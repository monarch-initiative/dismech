# Neonatal Botulism: A Case Series Suggesting Varied Presentations.
**Authors:** Horvat DE, Eye PG, Whitehead MT, Bharucha-Goebel D, Roth E, Anwar T, Tsuchida T, Kousa YA
**Journal:** Pediatr Neurol (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.pediatrneurol.2023.06.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pediatrneurol.2023.06.004)

## Content

Infantile botulism is an uncommon diagnosis and rarer still in the neonatal period. We describe three cases of neonatal-onset botulism that presented with symptoms typically (hypotonia, constipation, facial diplegia) or atypically seen in older infants (encephalopathy, seizures, and hypothermia). Our series shows a wider spectrum of clinical presentations in patients with neonatal-onset botulism. Our report also suggests that neonatal-onset botulism should be considered more broadly in the hypotonic infant, especially as the condition is treatable with intravenous botulinum-specific immune globulin.

Botulism is a rare and potentially lethal condition caused by exposure to a toxin produced by the Clostridium botulinum bacterium, and can affect individuals of all ages. Of the condition’s subtypes (infantile, foodborne, wound, or iatrogenic), infantile is the most common, accounting for 70% of botulism cases annually. 1 , 2 In infants, very low doses of the toxin (1mcg/kg) can be lethal. 2 The classic presentation includes constipation, feeding difficulties, and pupillary abnormalities. However, other features can include facial diplegia, weak suck and cry, appendicular weakness, and hypotonia, which can present as overall lethargy or even encephalopathy. 3 Neonatal onset botulism is defined by disease onset at or before 44 weeks postmenstrual age and accounts for only 15% of the infantile subtype. 3 To date, the available literature largely consists of sporadic case reports or series. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 The neonatal subtype has the least characteristic presentation, often including vague symptomatology that can delay diagnosis and treatment. As a result, according to the California Department of Public Health, the average time to treat infantile botulism with intravenous botulinum specific immune globulin (BIG-IV) in the USA from 2013-2022 is 2.3 days while the time to treat neonatal onset botulism is 4.2 days. 8 Therefore, more descriptions of neonatal botulism are needed in the literature, especially those with atypical features to broaden our understanding of the disease spectrum at onset and facilitate timely treatment. As such, here we present a case series of three patients with neonatal botulism with varied presentations. We conclude that among other conditions, infantile botulism should be considered in the differential diagnosis of the hypotonic neonate with poor feeding who is not improving with treatment for an infection, even when there are central features, including seizures or the appearance of encephalopathy.

A 13-day-old full term male presented with two days of poor feeding and diarrhea. His initial exam was notable for diffuse hypotonia, quadriparesis, congestion, ptosis, and mild encephalopathy. He underwent a sepsis and meningitis evaluation. Blood cultures and a respiratory viral panel were negative. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was notable for mild leukocytosis (22 cells/mcL) and elevated protein (97mg/dL), with negative infectious work-up. (A full list of diagnostic labs and imaging is shown in Table 1). EEG was notable for mild encephalopathy. Venous blood gases showed a mild acidosis and hypercarbia. His neurologic exam evolved to include facial diplegia, progressive hypotonia, and a progressive loss of reflexes, including loss of the gag reflex. Pupillary testing was notable for positive fatigability testing. He had a normal abdominal exam without distention, loss of bowel sounds, or development of ileus, throughout his course. Progressive worsening toward areflexia raised concern for infantile botulism. Stool studies were sent, and the California Department of Health was notified. Given the presumptive diagnosis, the patient was given a course of human intravenous (IV) Botulism immune globulin (BIG-IV) on hospital day two. Stool studies were later positive for botulism toxin A. The patient was discharged on hospital day 22. He was seen at a three-month follow-up visit (about four months of age) and was found to have mild axial hypotonia; his reflexes and strength was otherwise normal for age. Feeding and developmental evaluations were normal.

A three-week-old male twin delivered at 36-week gestation presented with three days of poor feeding, emesis, apneic events, and hypothermia to 35.2° Celsius. The initial exam was notable for encephalopathy, but his reflexes were normal, and he did not have appendicular or bulbar weakness. He required intubation for recurrent apneic events; it was not clear if the apnea was related to a central or peripheral etiology based on data available at the time. Blood, urine, respiratory, HSV testing, and CSF cultures were all negative. He had an abnormal EEG due to mild encephalopathy and electrographic seizures. He was treated with phenobarbital, which effectively controlled his seizures. Phenobarbital was discontinued on hospital day three, per our protocol for acute symptomatic seizures. Head ultrasound, brain MRI, and brain MR spectroscopy were normal. Despite effective treatment of seizures, his clinical presentation worsened to include facial diplegia without encephalopathy and progressive respiratory failure. Throughout the duration of his hospital course he had reactive pupils without fatigability, with normal size. There was no clear evidence for autonomic dysfunction as the patient did not have constipation or temperature instability. On hospital day five, he started a four-day course of gentamicin for presumed late onset neonatal culture-negative sepsis, but he did not improve. Upon further disease progression, including bulbar weakness, ptosis, and hypoxic respiratory distress requiring mechanical ventilation, there was concern for a peripheral etiology, including botulism. We notified the California Department of Health and started a course of BIG-IV on hospital day 13. Stool botulinum toxin evaluations confirmed a positive result, with unknown serotype. The patient also underwent electromyography and nerve conduction studies (EMG/NCS) that were technically challenging, but demonstrated electro-decrement (20%) with slow repetitive stimulation at 2Hz and incremental response with fast repetitive stimulation at 20Hz (42.9%) (Figure 1). Needle EMG was conducted of the left deltoid which demonstrated small motor unit potentials with normal recruitment and no abnormal spontaneous activity. These electrodiagnostic studies were consistent with a presynaptic disorder of neuromuscular transmission and confirmed the diagnosis of infantile botulism. The patient was extubated on hospital day 27, with improvement in clinical symptoms. He was discharged home on hospital day 43. The patient was seen in follow-up two months later, at which time he achieved age-appropriate developmental milestones. He had mild axial hypotonia with an otherwise normal neurologic exam for age.

A four-week-old ex-full-term female delivered at term presented to the hospital with one day of poor feeding associated with coughing and gagging, a weak cry, lethargy, and new onset constipation. Her general exam was notable for normal vital signs, with no temperature instability, normal abdominal exam, with no distention, and normal bowel sounds. Her neurological exam was notable for sluggishly reactive pupils, facial diplegia, flaccid quadriparesis, and areflexia. Social history was notable for a construction site near the family’s home. Blood, urine, and CSF cultures and respiratory viral PCR panel were all negative. An arterial blood gas was normal. A four-hour EEG was obtained and demonstrated mild encephalopathy. The patient had worsening respiratory status and was intubated on hospital day two. Given the clinical presentation and progression of symptoms, without a central etiology and social risk factors, stool studies were sent and the California Department of Health was notified. The patient received BIG-IV on hospital day two, prior to confirmatory stool studies which were positive for botulism toxin B. She had gradual improvement in symptoms and was discharged home on hospital day 19. At six-month follow-up, the patient had a neurologic examination only notable for slight right sided preference with otherwise typical development. At nine-month follow-up the right sided preference resolved.

Here we present three cases of neonatal-onset botulism, which is underreported in the literature but requires timely recognition and treatment to prevent potentially serious complications. Infantile botulism is an uncommon condition that results from ingestion of Clostridium botulinum spores that are sometimes present in soil, dust, and some food sources, including home-canned foods and honey. 1 While the source of the toxin is at times difficult to determine, a less mature intestinal microbiome may facilitate colonization of botulism spores in infants and younger children. 3 This conceptual framework is supported by the time periods at which infants are most likely to contract botulism, including after birth and during transitions from formula/breast feeding to table foods.

Botulism is less common in the neonatal period, and the presence of additional atypical findings at time of presentation can make the diagnosis more challenging. In these cases, initial exams were concerning for central or infectious etiologies, especially given associated encephalopathy, seizures, hypothermia, congestion, and diarrhea, among other symptoms. Furthermore, the exposure history may not be clear, and the presentation might not be typical, which can impact timely diagnosis, treatment, and length of hospitalization. For example, Case two was initially treated with gentamicin, an aminoglycoside that can potentiate the action of botulinum toxin at the neuromuscular junction. 9 Thus, consideration of neonatal botulism may impact even needed, standard, and early treatment choices.

Atypical and central nervous system features can lead to a delay in accurate diagnosis. For example, among our cases, seizures and mild encephalopathy, would otherwise suggest a central etiology. It is not clear how botulism exposure can lead to seizures, but the affect might be indirect (through transient metabolic changes from systemic illness) or direct (although the toxin is not known to cross the blood brain barrier). In our case, seizures were easily controlled with phenobarbital without recurrence. Evolution of the exam enabled localization to a peripheral etiology, including the neuromuscular junction.

These cases highlight some features and learning points that may help others with timely diagnosis and treatment of neonatal-onset botulism. First, it is important to take a thorough toxic exposure history, especially in neonates who present from home. Second, facial weakness, ptosis, and poor feeding may be misinterpreted as encephalopathy in infancy but mild encephalopathy on EEG, like Cases one and three, suggest these features result from weakness rather than encephalopathy. In such cases, we recommend considering an EEG to distinguish between encephalopathy and bulbar weakness. Further, brain MRI exams were normal and CSF testing was unrevealing for a cause. If available, the classic EMG/NCS pattern described above can be diagnostic of botulism, and arrive more quickly at the diagnosis, as stool toxin detection may take a week or more to result. However, if there is high clinical suspicion for botulism, initiation of treatment is recommended.

Access to BIG-IV is managed by the Infant Botulism Treatment and Prevention Program in the Division of Communicable Disease Control in the California Department of Public Health. The California Department of Public Health recommends empiric treatment before laboratory confirmation is obtained because BIG-IV has the greatest efficacy if administered before hospital day seven. 9 A relatively safe treatment, timely administration can potentially decrease morbidity and mortality in affected neonates and infants. Additionally, early diagnosis of botulism can also reduce overall healthcare costs by avoiding unnecessary diagnostic procedures and testing as well as decreasing the duration of hospitalization.

We present a case series of neonatal-onset botulism, further expanding our understanding of the spectrum of presentation in this age group. Given the significant benefits of treatment with BIG-IV, botulism should be in the differential of the hypotonic neonate, even in those with central features such as mild encephalopathy and seizures.
