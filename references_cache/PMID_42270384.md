Abstract:
Atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) is a rarecause of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). When
aHUS cannot be excluded, diagnostic treatment with anti-C5 antibody agents should be considered. We report
a patient with a novel heterozygous complement factor H (CFH) variant, p.Glu1198Asp (c.3594A>T), who
received ravulizumab three months after disease onset. Ravulizumab was administered for relapse prevention
in the chronic phase, and no recurrence of thromboticmicroangiopathy was observed during the follow-up.

Introduction
Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is characterized by
microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), consumptive
thrombocytopenia, and organ dysfunction caused by in-
travascular platelet thrombi. The most common TMA-
associated disorders are hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS)
and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP).
In Japan, atypical HUS (aHUS) is diagnosed based on
one of the following criteria: (1) the presence of pathogenic
mutations in seven complement-related genes-complement
factor H ( CFH), complement factor I ( CFI), CD46, C3,
complement factor B, thrombomodulin, and diacylglycerol
kinase ε-identified as congenital complement abnormalities;
(2) the presence of anti-CFH antibodies (acquired aHUS); or
(3) TMA without any of the above abnormalities, in which
no alternative cause can be identified.
Genetic abnormalities associated with aHUS can be
broadly classified into loss-of-function mutations affecting
complement regulatory proteins and gain-of-function muta-
tions involving complement-activating factors. Loss-of-
function mutations include those in CFH, CFI, CD46,a n d
thrombomodulin, as well as a functional impairment of CFH
due to the development of anti-CFH antibodies. In contrast,
gain-of-function mutations have been described as comple-
ment factors B and C3.
Approximately 10-20% of patients with aHUS have circu-
lating anti-CFH autoantibodies. These antibodies recognize
the C-terminal domain of CFH and inhibit its binding to the
surface of autologous cell membranes, thereby impairing the
cytoprotective function of CFH. However, pathogenic muta-
tions are identified in only 40-60% of aHUS cases. There-
fore, a diagnosis of aHUS cannot be excluded, even in the
absence of any detectable genetic or immunological abnor-
malities (1).

lytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) revised in 2023, when no ob-
vious cause is identified after a diagnosis of thrombotic mi-
croangiopathy, aHUS is diagnosed clinically, and the prompt

We report a rare case of aHUS associated with a CFH
gene mutation, which is known to be associated with severe
disease and a high risk of recurrence. The acute phase was
managed with plasma exchange, prednisolone, and hemo-
dialysis. However, ravulizumab was initiated during the
chronic phase to prevent TMA recurrence, thus resulting in
a favorable outcome.
Case
A 40-year-old man presented with a 5-day history of ab-
dominal pain and nausea. He visited our hospital after fail-
ing to improve with symptomatic treatment. His bowel
movements were normal. His medical history was notable
only for suspected dyslipidemia, which was identified during
a routine health examination. There was no family history of
thrombophilia, chronic kidney disease, or end-stage renal
disease requiring hemodialysis.
On admission, the patient was afebrile, with a pulse rate
of 81 beats/min and blood pressure of 159/122 mmHg.
Physical examination revealed mildly pale conjunctivae, but
no other significant abnormalities were observed in the pa-
tient. The laboratory findings at presentation are summarized
in Table. Normocytic anemia, increased reticulocyte count,
marked presence of schistocytes, and elevated lactate dehy-
drogenase (LDH) levels were consistent with microan-
giopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA). Thrombotic microan-
giopathy (TMA) was suspected based on the presence of
MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and renal dysfunction.
The clinical course during hospitalization is shown in
Fig. 1A, and the overall clinical course is summarized in
Fig. 1B. Emergency continuous plasma exchange with dialy-
sis (cPED) (5) and high-dose corticosteroid therapy (predni-
solone 60 mg/day; 1 mg/kg/day) were initiated immediately
after admission. After 8 days of cPED, the platelet count
and LDH levels improved. However, renal dysfunction per-
sisted.
Subsequent testing revealed normal ADAMTS13 activity
and the absence of an ADAMTS13 inhibitor, excluding a di-
agnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). Ac-
cordingly, the prednisolone dosage was rapidly tapered and
discontinued. Stool cultures were negative for Shiga toxin-
producing Escherichia coli (STEC). Other potential causes
of secondary TMA, including collagen vascular diseases,
malignancy, drug-induced reactions, and severe infections,
were also excluded. Because the blood urea nitrogen and
creatinine levels remained elevated, hemodialysis was con-
tinued three times per week after the completion of plasma
exchange. A renal biopsy was performed on day 15 of hos-
pitalization to investigate the cause of persistent renal im-
pairment.
The renal biopsy findings are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. The
cortex-to-medulla ratio was approximately 6:4 in both kid-
neys. Eight glomeruli were identified, six of which were
collapsed, showing prominent wrinkling of the glomerular
capillary tufts. Low-power examination with periodic acid-
Schiff (PAS) staining (Fig. 2A) revealed marked tubular at-
rophy and interstitial expansion with inflammatory cell infil-
tration in the cortex. Elastica-Masson staining (Fig. 2B) re-
vealed mild fibrous intimal thickening of the interlobular ar-
teries and mild hyaline thickening of the afferent arterioles,
without vascular changes suggestive of malignant hyperten-
sion. A high-power examination with periodic acid
methenamine silver (PAM) staining (Fig. 2C) revealed one
glomerulus with a hypertrophic tuft measuring 230 μm in
diameter and segmental duplication of the glomerular base-
ment membrane, consistent with thrombotic microangiopa-
thy. High-power PAS staining (Fig. 2D) demonstrated tubu-
litis with the interstitial infiltration of lymphocytes, plasma
cells, neutrophils, and eosinophils, suggesting drug-induced
hypersensitive tubulointerstitial nephritis. Electron micros-
copy (Fig. 2E) revealed widening of the subendothelial
space with mesangial interposition without extensive podo-
cyte foot process effacement, findings consistent with the
chronic phase of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome. Im-
munofluorescence studies revealed no immunoglobulin
deposition in the glomeruli, whereas nonspecific C3 deposi-
tion was observed in the segmental mesangial areas
(Fig. 3A-E). Although no overt thrombotic lesions were
identified in the kidney specimens, these findings are com-
patible with the chronic phase of aHUS, in which such le-
sions may be inconspicuous (6).
Based on these findings, neither intraglomerular fibrin
thrombi nor inflammatory cell infiltration characteristic of
the acute phase was observed, and there was no evidence of
sclerosis or hyalinization of the glomerular capillary tufts,
which are typically seen in advanced chronic lesions. In
contrast, duplication of the glomerular capillary basement
membrane on light microscopy and widening of the suben-
dothelial space with mesangial interposition on electron mi-
croscopy were consistent with chronic thrombotic
microangiopathy-related endothelial injury.

served in the absence of any identifiable infectious etiology,

In light of previous reports that aHUS may cause acute
tubulointerstitial nephritis, we speculated that aHUS could
have been involved in the tubulointerstitial lesions observed

To evaluate complement dysregulation, the patient’s
plasma was sent to the Department of Nephrology at Na-
goya University for a quantitative hemolysis assay using
sheep red blood cells. The assay demonstrated marked
hemolysis, reaching 321.4% of that observed in the positive
control using an inhibitory anti-CFH murine monoclonal an-
tibody, consistent with CFH-related complement amplifica-
tion.
Genetic testing performed at the Kazusa DNA Research
Institute identified a heterozygous CFH mutation. The p.
Glu1198Asp (c.3594A>T) variant has not been previously
described. The location of this mutation is shown in Fig. 4.
Based on these complementary functional and genetic find-
ings, the patient was definitively diagnosed with aHUS.

Ravulizumab therapy was continued for more than two
years, during which no clinical or laboratory evidence of
disease recurrence was observed (Fig. 1B). In the sheep red
blood cell hemolysis assay, hemolysis exceeding 75% rela-
tive to that of the positive control was classified as severe.
In this patient, severe hemolysis persisted for three months
after disease onset, suggesting a sustained functional impair-
ment of CFH.
Following the initiation of ravulizumab therapy, hemolysis
was completely resolved. This finding is consistent with the
pharmacodynamic complement blockade under anti-C5 ther-
apy (Fig. 1B).
The patient’s renal function has gradually improved over
time during follow-up.

ravulizumab, are effective therapies for aHUS associated
with genetic abnormalities in complement regulatory fac-
tors (2). However, because genetic testing is time-
consuming, the Japanese practice guidelines for aHUS rec-
ommend the initiation of anti-C5 antibody therapy at the
time of clinical diagnosis in patients with TMA in whom no

In the present case, the renal impairment was prolonged.
However, the patient was eventually weaned off hemodialy-
sis and achieved transfusion independence with plasma ex-
change therapy, corticosteroid therapy, and supportive care.
Although it is not possible to determine whether earlier in-
itiation of ravulizumab at the time of clinical diagnosis
would have resulted in a more favorable clinical course, this
case suggests that earlier consideration of anti-C5 antibody
therapy after the diagnosis of TMA may have been war-
ranted, with close monitoring of the treatment response.
A positive sheep red blood cell hemolysis assay has been
reported to be suggestive of functional impairment of
CFH (3). In many patients, hemolysis assay results normal-
ize after plasma exchange. However, in the present case, se-
vere hemolysis persisted despite plasma exchange and be-
came completely negative only after initiating ravulizumab.
This finding is consistent with persistent complement dys-
regulation, which may not have been sufficiently controlled
by plasma exchange alone.
In this case, the serum C3 level was within the normal
range (Table). Although low C3 levels are more frequently
observed in patients with aHUS, particularly those with
CFH mutations, decreased C3 levels are reported in only ap-
proximately 35-50% of aHUS cases (9). Therefore, while
hypocomplementemia may support the diagnosis of aHUS,
normal C3 levels do not.
Regarding the efficacy of anti-C5 antibody therapy be-
yond the acute phase, previous studies have reported the
long-term benefits of ravulizumab in adult patients with
aHUS (4). In these reports, the platelet counts, hemoglobin
levels, and renal function improved as early as 8 days after
treatment initiation, with further improvements observed at
183 days. In addition, 77.8% of patients who were not re-
ceiving dialysis at baseline remained dialysis-free until day
183 (10). However, an important limitation of these studies,
including the present case, is the lack of prospective trials
with appropriate control groups.
In our patient, the renal function gradually improved even
before the initiation of ravulizumab, and hemodialysis was
discontinued on hospital day 57 when the serum creatinine
level was <5 mg/dL. After completion of plasma exchange

Only a limited number of reports have described the

count, which was consistently maintained at approximately
200,000/μL, and a gradual improvement in the renal func-
tion over time. These findings suggest that anti-C5 antibody
therapy may be effective not only for acute disease control
but also for the maintenance of remission in aHUS without
any apparent adverse effects.
Although our patient has shown no evidence of disease
recurrence to date, we consider continued ravulizumab ther-
apy necessary, as CFH mutations are associated with a
higher risk of recurrence than other genetic abnormali-
ties (11). Moreover, patients with heterozygousCFH muta-
tions have been reported to be at increased risk of progres-
sion to end-stage renal disease due to persistent or recurrent
renal injury in aHUS (12).
The renal histopathological findings in aHUS are typically
characterized by endothelial cell injury and thrombus forma-

tion within the glomerular microvasculature (13, 14). Al-
though the renal pathological features of TTP, STEC-HUS,
and aHUS may overlap, thrombi in STEC-HUS and aHUS
are predominantly fibrin-rich, whereas those in TTP are pri-
marily platelet-rich (15). In the acute to subacute phases, tu-
bulointerstitial inflammation and acute tubular injury may be
observed, which are generally considered nonspecific find-
ings (7, 16). As the disease progresses, inflammatory cell in-
filtration of the tubulointerstitium becomes more pro-
nounced, followed by the development of interstitial fibro-
sis (13). In the chronic phase, tubular atrophy and tubu-
lointerstitial fibrosis are often accompanied by glomerulo-
sclerosis and are closely associated with the long-term renal
prognosis (7).
In the present case, the classical histopathological features
of acute-phase aHUS, such as overt thrombus formation or
glomerular paralysis, were absent. A sclerotic glomerulus
was initially suspected to be crescentic. The dominant
pathological finding was prominent tubulointerstitial nephri-
tis, and at the time of the initial evaluation, there were few
features strongly suggestive of TMA. However, subsequent
detailed assessments, including the identification of
glomerular basement membrane duplication on light micros-
copy and ultrastructural evaluation by electron microscopy,
revealed endothelial injury consistent with chronic aHUS-
related TMA.
The heterozygous CFH mutation identified in this patient
has not been previously reported and is therefore considered
novel. Although more than 100 heterozygous CFH muta-
tions associated with aHUS have been described, genotype-
phenotype correlations and mutation-specific disease severity
remain poorly understood (17). According to ACMG/AMP
guidelines, the p.Glu1198Asp variant was classified as likely
pathogenic. Notably, p.Glu1198is a mutational hotspot, with
several reported variants, including p.Glu1198Ala, p.Glu
1198Lys, and p.Glu1198Val, located within short consensus
repeat 20, a region critical for interaction with C3b (18).
These findings support the pathogenic role of this variant in
the present case (19). In general, heterozygousCFH muta-
tions are associated with a poorer prognosis than other ge-
netic abnormalities, thus necessitating a careful and long-
term follow-up. Although the possibility of carrier status
among blood relatives was considered, genetic testing could
not be performed because informed consent could not be
obtained from the family.
In this case, a definitive diagnosis of aHUS was estab-
lished approximately three months after the onset. Plasma
exchange was initially performed to stabilize the patient and
exclude TTP. A renal biopsy revealed chronic TMA with
concomitant tubulointerstitial nephritis, thus prompting the
initiation of corticosteroid therapy for the interstitial compo-
nent. Treatment with plasma exchange, prednisolone, and in-
termittent hemodialysis resulted in the resolution of active
TMA and gradual improvement in renal function. Therefore,
ravulizumab was not introduced during the acute phase. Al-
though the patient was eventually able to discontinue hemo-
dialysis, subsequent genetic testing revealed aCFH mutation
associated with a high risk of recurrence and severe disease.
Accordingly, ravulizumab was initiated during the chronic
phase for relapse prevention after identifying a high-risk
CFH variant.
Conclusion
In the case of aHUS with a pathogenicCFH variant, the
anti-C5 monoclonal antibody ravulizumab was initiated after
remission of the active disease for relapse prevention. No re-
currence of thrombotic microangiopathy was observed dur-
ing the follow-up period.
The sheep red blood cell hemolysis assay demonstrated
the suppression of hemolysis during ravulizumab therapy.
However, this assay reflects the pharmacodynamic comple-
ment blockade and does not directly indicate the disease ac-
tivity.
Because this is a single case, the efficacy and feasibility
of long-term maintenance therapy with ravulizumab in the
chronic phase cannot be established.
