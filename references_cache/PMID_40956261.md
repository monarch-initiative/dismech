# Inflammatory Features in Homozygous DSG2 Cardiomyopathy Mimicking Cardiac Sarcoidosis.
**Authors:** Parianos D, Pingree R, Medeiros-Domingo A, Flammer AJ, Giannopoulos AA, Chiodini A, Maccio U, Ruschitzka F, Duru F, Saguner AM
**Journal:** JACC Case Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaccas.2025.105345](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaccas.2025.105345)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Homozygous variants in DSG2, a desmosomal protein, are associated with a severe form of biventricular arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM). To date, myocardial inflammation mimicking isolated cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) and responding well to immunosuppressive therapy (IST) has not been described in this entity.

CASE SUMMARY: A young man with sustained ventricular tachycardia was diagnosed with ACM. Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography raised suspicion on isolated CS with left ventricular inflammation. Endomyocardial biopsies only showed interstitial fibrosis. Empirical IST with corticosteroids and azathioprine reduced inflammation and led to flare-ups when tapered. Post-transplant histology showed no granulomas, but extensive fibrofatty replacement typically seen in ACM. Genetic testing identified a novel homozygous DSG2 variant (DSG2 c.523+2T>C (splice donor)).

DISCUSSION: IST may be effective in controlling inflammation, arrhythmias, and disease progression in severe desmosomal disease.

TAKE-HOME MESSAGE: We highlight the presence of severe biventricular ACM associated with a novel homozygous DSG2 variant with chronic inflammatory features mimicking CS responding to IST.

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM) is often associated with genetic variants encoding cardiac desmosomes. Although heterozygous variants in cadherins (DSG2 and DSC2) may present as right-dominant ACM, multiple variants in these cadherins such as homozygous desmoglein variants often cause a severe phenotype with biventricular involvement, ventricular arrhythmias, and progressive heart failure.1,2 The association of ACM with acute myocardial inflammation and myocarditis has been reported, for example for desmoplakin variants,3, 4, 5, 6 but an ACM phenotype presenting with chronic inflammation resembling isolated cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) has, to our knowledge, not been reported thus far. We report a case of homozygous DSG2 cardiomyopathy presenting with recurrent sustained ventricular arrhythmias from the right ventricle (RV), biventricular heart failure, and myocardial inflammation responsive to immunosuppressive therapy (IST), as demonstrated by repetitive fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) scans.Take-Home Messages•Homozygous DSG2 variant carriers may present as ACM with cardiac inflammation mimicking ICS.•This case underlines the importance of thorough genetic testing in patients with suspicion of ACM.•18F-FDG PET/CT imaging can be a useful tool in identifying inflammatory activity in patients with ACM.

A 35-year-old man presented to the emergency department with dizziness. Chest pain, dyspnea, or syncopal events were denied. Physical examination revealed a diaphoretic patient with blood pressure of 95/62 mm Hg and heart rate of 146 beats/min with normal oxygen saturation. No signs of hypervolemia were apparent with normal lung auscultation, and no jugular vein extension or limb edema. Electrocardiogram showed a regular wide complex tachycardia (Figure 1B), confirming sustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) by implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) interrogation. ICD storage revealed several VTs successfully treated by antitachycardia pacing and ICD shocks over the last days. Serial troponin measurement was not significantly elevated (troponin stable at 16 ng/L); electrolytes, inflammatory signs, and thyroid-stimulating hormone were normal. There was a mildly elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (600 ng/L) and normal kidney function. Because of the ongoing ventricular arrhythmia at the emergency department, amiodarone was started with successful conversion in sinus rhythm. A few weeks later, successful endocardial VT ablation in the RV was performed.

Eight years before, the patient had been diagnosed with ACM with biventricular involvement based on 2010 Task Force Criteria7 (2 major, 1 minor) after having had a first episode of VT with syncope while playing soccer. The electrocardiogram in sinus rhythm showed large P waves, normal PR interval with peripheral low voltage, a fragmented QRS interval, and T-wave inversions in V1 to V5 (Figure 1A). Transthoracic echocardiography already revealed a dilated LV with moderately reduced LVEF of 38% and a dilated RV with reduced function, apical right ventricular akinesia, and moderate tricuspid regurgitation. Cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed severe right ventricular dilatation and dysfunction with apical akinesia and diffuse late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) of the RV without signs of a shunt. The LV was dilated with moderately reduced systolic function and lateral/posterior LGE. Coronary angiography was normal. Family history was negative for sudden cardiac death and cardiomyopathy. A secondary prophylactic ICD had been implanted. Medical therapy with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and beta-blocker had been initiated, and restriction to light-moderate physical activity had been advised.

Because the etiology of this nonfamilial severe biventricular cardiomyopathy with recurrent VTs was not clear at this time, further investigations were performed. Repeated cardiac magnetic resonance was not possible due to a non–magnetic resonance imaging-compatible ICD. Because of possible sarcoidosis with a PR interval at the upper limit of normal, septal involvement, and recurrent VTs, an 18F-FDG PET/CT was performed. To avoid false-positive results, the scan was performed several weeks after the arrhythmia and after ablation. It revealed intense focal uptake in the septum, the apex, and anterolateral left ventricular LV segments (Figure 2A). Laboratory work-up for sarcoidosis showed mildly elevated neopterin (4.01 ng/mL); angiotensin-converting enzyme and soluble interleukin-2 receptor were normal. No extracardiac sarcoidosis manifestations were detected. Multiple right ventricular and left ventricular endomyocardial biopsies (Supplemental Figure 1) in regions with 18F-FDG uptake on 18F-FDG PET/CT revealed patchy interstitial fibrosis, without granulomas or inflammatory infiltrates. Despite nondiagnostic histology, the patient fulfilled 4 of 5 major Japanese criteria8 for isolated CS (VT, left ventricular contractile dysfunction, 18F-FDG PET/CT tracer activity, and cardiac magnetic resonance with LGE). Therefore, IST with prednisone and azathioprine was initiated. Inflammation subsided on a follow-up 18F-FDG PET/CT scan after 3 months (Figure 2B), and no further sustained arrhythmias occurred thereafter. ICD box exchange was performed 2 months later, and to reduce the risk of infection and due to azathioprine intolerance, IST was interrupted. The third 18F-FDG PET/CT scan after 6 months showed a relapse of inflammation (Figure 2C). Prednisone was reintroduced and later combined with methotrexate, achieving remission (Figure 2D).

About 3 years later, inflammation reappeared after steroid tapering (Figure 2E), with worsening right ventricular function (Video 1, Video 2, Video 3, Video 4, Video 5, Video 6), severe tricuspid regurgitation (Video 1, Video 2, Video 3, Video 4, Video 5, Video 6), progressive exercise intolerance (peak oxygen uptake: 14 mL/kg/min), and low cardiac index (1.7 L/min/m2). Steroid doses were increased, and methotrexate was retained. The patient was listed for heart transplantation and subsequently (after 6 months) underwent orthotopic heart transplantation. Repeated genetic testing using state-of-the art next-generation sequencing techniques with a custom panel identified the novel presumably Swiss founder homozygous DSG2 variant (DSG2 c.523+2T>C (splice donor)). The first genetic testing 6 years before might have been negative due to low coverage of the extent region of the DSG2 gene. Cascade family screening was performed proving that his parents and sister were heterozygous for this variant; none of them show signs of cardiomyopathy.

Gross macroscopic examination and thorough histology of the explanted heart showed extensive fibrofatty replacement of the RV, LV, and right atrium, without granulomas and no acute inflammatory infiltrates (Figures 3A to 3D). Subepicardial fibrosis predominantly of the LV was present. This case exemplifies an inflammatory phenotype of homozygous cadherin cardiomyopathy due to a novel DSG2 splice site variant. Although classical right-sided arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is regarded as noninflammatory, acute myocarditis-like inflammation may accompany disease progression in ACM, particularly in desmoplakin cardiomyopathy.4 However, chronic inflammation mimicking CS has not been reported in patients with desmosomal ACM.

Our patient met 4 of 5 major Japanese criteria for isolated CS, including ventricular arrhythmias, focal 18F-FDG uptake, LGE on magnetic resonance imaging, and left ventricular dysfunction. IST led to both full inflammatory and clinical remission, with inflammatory recurrence on cessation or tapering of IST. Despite the absence of granulomas on endomyocardial biopsy and explant histology, therapeutic response suggested underlying inflammation.

The finding of biventricular fibrofatty infiltration with subepicardial predominance, a common feature of ACM, without granulomas suggested desmosomal disease. Of interest, because of the severe nature of these homozygous variants, the septum was involved as well; however, this feature has been used to differentiate classical ARVC from sarcoidosis, and is usually characteristic of the latter.9 Here, we confirm that severe multigenic cadherin variant–associated cardiomyopathy can involve the septum and present with chronic inflammation well responding to IST mimicking CS.1 Therefore, repeat genetic testing including a large panel with high coverage is crucial to establish the correct diagnosis, as was done in this case.

Interestingly, the identified variant affects a canonical splice donor site in DSG2, raising the question of how a homozygous state in such a critical gene might be compatible with life. Several mechanisms may account for this observation, including 1) complete skipping of an in-frame exon, potentially resulting in a partially functional or unstable protein; 2) leaky splicing with residual production of correctly spliced transcripts, indicative of a hypomorphic allele; or 3) activation of cryptic splice sites, leading to alternative transcripts with partial exon retention. These mechanisms may permit sufficient residual DSG2 function to allow viability, despite the severe cardiac phenotype observed. This is consistent with other reported homozygous DSG2 variants with suspected residual function.10,11

This case highlights the presence of severe biventricular cardiomyopathy associated with a homozygous DSG2 variant with chronic inflammatory features mimicking isolated CS. It underlines the importance of thorough broad genetic testing and suggests that IST may be beneficial in these patients.
