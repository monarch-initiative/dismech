# Ocular Manifestations of Wilson Disease: Pathophysiology, Clinical and Paraclinical Features.
**Authors:** Coviltir V, Sabo C, Nicula AP, Marinescu MC
**Journal:** J Gastrointestin Liver Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.15403/jgld-7494](https://doi.org/10.15403/jgld-7494)

## Content

Wilson disease (WD) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the ATP7B gene, resulting in impaired biliary copper excretion and progressive copper accumulation in multiple tissues. Ocular manifestations represent some of the most characteristic and clinically valuable features of the disease, contributing to diagnosis, monitoring, and assessment of neurological involvement. This narrative review summarizes current knowledge regarding the pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and imaging characteristics of ocular involvement in WD. Copper deposition within the eye occurs primarily through the aqueous humor, leading to accumulation in the corneal Descemet membrane and lens capsule. Kayser-Fleischer rings remain the most prevalent ocular sign being strongly associated with neurological disease, while sunflower cataracts represent a less common but highly characteristic manifestation. Anterior segment optical coherence tomography and in vivo confocal microscopy have recently improved the detection and monitoring of these lesions. Beyond copper deposition, growing evidence indicates that WD is associated with retinal and optic nerve neurodegeneration. Optical coherence tomography studies consistently demonstrate thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer, ganglion cell complex, and macular structures, particularly in patients with neurological involvement. Electrophysiological investigations, including visual evoked potentials and electroretinography, reveal delayed neural conduction and retinal dysfunction, supporting the concept of widespread neuro-ophthalmological impairment. Optical coherence tomography angiography further identifies microvascular alterations affecting retinal and peripapillary capillary networks. Importantly, several ocular abnormalities correlate with neurological severity and may serve as non-invasive biomarkers of disease progression. Current treatments, including copper chelators and zinc therapy, can induce regression of Kayser-Fleischer rings and sunflower cataracts. Ocular assessment therefore provides a valuable window into systemic and neurological disease activity, highlighting the importance of multidisciplinary management and the potential role of emerging imaging biomarkers in Wilson disease.

The epidemiology of WD is significantly variable as well,
depending on the geographical area and risk of consanguinity;
isolated communities report higher prevalence: 1 case per
2,600 individuals in the Canary Islands and 1 case per 7,000
individuals in Sardinia [3]. However, a recent systematic
study has gathered data from 136 studies and revealed that
WD prevalence is higher in Southern Europe and Asia and
lower in Northern/Eastern Europe, most countries revealing
a disease prevalence under 4.99 per 100,000 inhabitants
[2]. Forecasts suggest a modest, gradual global increase in
prevalence, of around 2-3% per year, the authors attributing
this mainly to improved diagnostics [2]. Another recent
study, based on insurance data and diagnostic codes in
Germany, estimates a mean prevalence of 20.3 patients per
million (with a range between 17.8 and 24.4) [4]. A genetic
study, based in the UK, reveals a calculated prevalence of
carriers of two mutant pathogenic ATP7B alleles of 1 in 7026,
considerably higher than the prevalences reported above of
clinical manifest disease. Thus, it was suggested either that the
disease is still underdiagnosed, or there is a larger proportion
of asymptomatic or mild cases of WD [5].
For the hepatologist and gastroenterologist, recognizing
extrahepatic manifestations is critical for comprehensive
disease staging. Ocular findings serve as accessible, non-
invasive indicators of systemic copper overload and impending
neurological risk. The aim of this narrative review is to
summarize the ocular findings specific to Wilson disease,
with a particular focus on their pathophysiology and their
clinical utility in monitoring disease progression and treatment
efficacy.
A non-systematic literature search was performed, using
the PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect databases. Articles
published in English through 2026 were considered, of any study
methodology (review, meta-analysis, case-report, cohort study,
case report and case series). The manuscripts were identified
by using the combination of keywords “Wilson disease” and
“cornea” , “Kayser-Fleischer ring” , “sunflower cataract” , “optical

P ATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF COPPER
DEPOSITION IN OCULAR TISSUES
The connection between WD and copper metabolism
has been suggested in the 1940, with the finding of markedly
elevated concentration of copper in the liver and brain of
deceased WD patients. Definitive proof has been established
in 1985 with the identification of the location on chromosome
13 of the gene responsible. Further, in 1995 the ATP7B gene
and its mutations was described [6].
Copper plays the role of a micronutrient in the human
body, functioning primarily as a cofactor for metalloenzymes,
particularly oxidases and their role in reducing molecular
oxygen. Copper deficiency has a profound impact on
growth, neurodevelopment, the immune system, and
connective tissues. Specifically, copper is a cofactor for brain
dopamine β-hydroxylase (DBH) (catecholamine synthesis
and neurotransmission), cytochrome c oxidase in the
mitochondria, Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase, SOD1 and SOD 3
(elimination of reactive oxygen species), lysyl oxidase (collagen
and elastin cross-linking), tyrosinase (melanin synthesis),
hephaestin and ceruloplasmin (iron transport and oxidation)
[7, 8]. The ATP7B gene is the code for a protein with the role
of transmembrane copper- transporting ATPase. Mutation may
be homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations (both
alleles modified, but with different mutations). As the protein
has the role of excreting copper into bile and transporting
copper for ceruloplasmin synthesis, the presence of a defective
ATP7B protein leads to copper overload in hepatocytes, from
the dietary intake copper. Further, copper is released in excess
in circulation, and it accumulates in other tissues, particularly
the brain [3].
The ATP7B is essential in several steps of the copper
absorption and function: dietary copper is reduced to Cu+
and absorbed in the enterocytes, further passing through the
basolateral ATP7B into the portal blood. Once in the liver,
ATP7B is present both in the membrane of the Golgi apparatus
in the apical membrane of the hepatocyte, transporting copper,
supporting its binding to ceruloplasmin and its excretion into
the biliary ductule. In the brain, it has been shown that in the
presence of excess copper, ATP7B has the role of passing it
through the blood brain barrier, from the choroidal plexus
into the blood [7, 9].
The excess copper which defines WD is deleterious by
causing cell death (mainly hepatocytes) through the formation
of free radicals by the copper ions - these hydroxyl radicals
causing oxidative stress to aminoacids, DNA bases and fatty
acids [10].
Regarding ocular deposits, copper enters the eye through
the secretion of the aqueous humor. Following this, the
convection currents lead to the contact of copper with the
innermost layer of the cornea, and as it has affinity for the
basement membrane, copper forms a complex with sulphur
and deposits. As well, aqueous humor convection currents lead
to the deposit of copper in the crystalline lens, particularly in
the anterior and posterior capsule [11] (Fig. 1).
Anterior Segment Manifestations
The disease has first been described by Kinnier Wilson in
1912 [12], and while the pathognomonic Kayser-Fleischer ring
(KFR) has been described around the same time, by Kayser in
1902 and Fleischer in 1903, the connection between the disease
and the sign has only been suggested by Wilson in the 1930s [6].
Kayser-Fleischer ring is the manifestation of extracellular
copper deposits in the Descemet’s membrane of the cornea
(the basement membrane of the corneal endothelium - the
innermost monolayer of cells in the cornea - separating it
from the corneal stroma). It appears at slit-lamp examination
as a green–brown circular discoloration at the periphery of
the cornea, near the limbus, almost always bilateral, starting
in the superior quadrant, then inferior, then becoming
circumferential [13, 14]. When the ring is not visible, the

The presence of KFR is variable; it has been described in
36–62% of patients with hepatic involvement, in 77.8–85.2%
of patients with neurological manifestations and in 10–30% of
asymptomatic WD patients [15]. In a recent pooled prevalence
study, the presence of the KFR ring was found as a highly
sensitive sign, present in 88% [2]. The KFR is well correlated
with other disease markers, such as seric ceruloplasmin and
copper, liver disease and brain MRI findings, and is reversible
if the patient undergoes treatment [15].
The structure of KFR has also been investigated using in
vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM), an ocular imaging technique
able to accurately describe microscopic, layered structures such
as the cornea [15, 16]. Thus, it becomes visible that KFR is
formed of bright granules, with higher density at the level of the
Descemet’s membrane and lower density of keratocytes [15].
Another ocular imaging technique, anterior segment optical
coherence tomography (AS-OCT) supports KFR detection; it
appears as a hyperreflective layer in the Descemet’s membrane
in the peripheral cornea. It is considered that this sign is more
easily identifiable than the KFR on slit-lamp, thus is suggested
as useful aid in the diagnosis and follow-up of patients [15].
Another corneal characteristic which appears in WD is the
alteration of corneal nerves, particularly the sub-basal plexus
- alteration which is related to the neurological aspect of WD
and its toxicity on nerve cells [17].
Another typical ocular sign of WD is the sunflower cataract.
The slit-lamp aspect is of a thin opacified area in the center
of the crystalline lens, just posterior to the anterior capsule. It
occupies between 30% and 50% of the anterior lens surface.
Around it, needle-shaped opacifications are visible radially,
leading to its resemblance to a sunflower [18]. It has a variable
prevalence (between 1.2% and 17%), rarely affects the visual
acuity and is reversible with WD chelation treatment [17-
19] [17][18]. Interestingly, when KFR is present a sunflower
cataract may or may not be present, but no sunflower cataract
has been reported in the absence of KFR [18]. An electron
microscopy study was performed on the anterior capsule
excised from a WD patient (during the lens extraction surgical
intervention), and it was shown that the lens presents granular
deposits of varying size, in linear patterns with scattered dots,
mainly in the posterior one third of the anterior capsule. These
particles were absent from the epithelial cell layer of the lens.
Elemental analysis revealed the presence in the granules of
copper and sulfur, in a variable ratio [20].
Posterior Segment, Neuro-ophthalmological and
Electrophysiological Manifestations
Beyond the anterior segment signs of WD, posterior pole
and neuro-ophthalmological manifestations have also been
described, conceptually, as part of the neurological spectrum of
the disease. While copper deposits have also been described in
the vitreous body and retina, the main mechanisms proposed
in posterior pole WD dysfunction is intracellular copper
toxicity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury and ultimately,
neurodegeneration [17, 21]. Due to copper deposits in the
axons, there is degeneration of the axon and neuron body,
along the optic nerve and tract [17]. This is supported by
the fact that the ocular tissues present, normally, one of the
highest concentrations of copper in the body and, also, one of
the highest metabolic activities (relying on the mitochondrial
function) [22]. As such, if the copper levels are too high in the
eye, it is very likely that regulated cell death will be triggered
[21]. Copper circulates bound to albumin, is released through

Firstly, one of the most accessible methods of investigating
the structure of the macula and optic disc structures is the
optical coherence tomography (OCT), a non-invasive imaging
technique, based on low-coherence interferometry, which
allows for the quantification of the retinal nerve fiber layer
(RNFL) thickness peripapillary, the ganglion cell complex
at the macular level (GCC) thickness [formed of RNFL, the
ganglion cell layer (GCL), and the inner plexiform layer (IPL),
housing the body, axon and dendrites of the ganglion cell]
[23]. Secondly, the electrophysiological response of the optic
pathways can be investigated using visual evoked potentials
(VEP), in which the eye is stimulated with a standardized light
(either a checkerboard pattern or a flash) and the occipital
cortex’s electrical signal is measured [23].
One of the first studies in this regard, published in
2012, combine the morphological and electrophysiological
approaches, and assessed 42 WD patients in comparison
with 76 matched controls, regarding the peripapillary RNFL
thickness, total macular thickness, the thickness of the retinal
layers at the level of the fovea, latencies of the negative and
positive waves distinguished on pattern stimulation VEP [24].
It became evident that the peripapillary RNFL was significantly
thinner in WD, and so was the mean total macular thickness
(particularly in the inferior quadrant). The comparison of
individual retinal layers revealed that the retinal ganglion
cell- and inner plexiform layer complex (GCIP) and the inner
nuclear layer (INL) are reduced in WD. Furthermore, the
N75 and P100 latencies of the VEPs were prolonged, while
N140 latency and the wave amplitude remained unchanged.
Significant correlations between the OCT morphology and
VEP function were also detected  [24].
A previous study has investigated the electrophysiological
response in 10 recently diagnosed WD patients with
neurological symptoms and KFR, using both VEP and
electroretinography (ERG). It was described that pattern
VEP was significantly modified, with prolonged latency of
the P100 wave and normal amplitude. Regarding the ERG
responses, significant latency prolongation of the photopic A
wave and the amplitude reduction of both the photopic A and
B waves were detected [25]. Following this seminal study, in
2015 research combining OCT, VEP and electroretinography
confirmed these alterations, in 58 WD cases and 30 healthy
controls. Interestingly, the WD cases were divided based on
whether the patient presented cerebral alterations on MRI.
As expected, total RNFL was thinner in the neurological
subgroup, particularly the superior and temporal quadrants.
Total macular thickness was also lower. These parameters did
not present significant differences between the normal controls
and the MRI-negative subgroup. Furthermore, VEP latencies
were significantly longer in the MRI-positive subgroup of the
N75, P100 and N135 waves, with no differences in amplitude.
Only the N135 wave presented a significant difference
between controls and MRI-negative WD patients. Not least,
electroretinography latencies were prolonged and amplitudes
were lowered in the MRI-positive subgroup, when compared
to the MRI-negative group [26].

Several other case reports involve optic nerve dysfunction
in WD, suggesting that the spectrum of disease may be more
varied than previously thought. For instance, an acute onset
of WD in an adolescent, with neurological and abdominal
symptoms, also presented sudden bilateral painless vision loss,
attributed to acute optic neuropathy [32], while slow evolving
optic neuropathy has also been documented [33, 34]. Cases
have been documented with normal fundus aspects as well, but
decreased color vision [35], and also in middle-aged patients,
with end-stage liver disease [36]. Another case of subacute
bilateral painless vision loss was reported in an adolescent, who
in the absence of severe neurological symptoms, complied to
ophthalmological testing. A diagnosis of bilateral optic atrophy
was established, based on fundus temporal disc pallor, delayed
waves on evoked potentials, reduction in the thickness of the
ganglion cell complex and retinal nerve fiber layer on OCT,
peripheral visual field defects, and MRI revealing bilateral
thinning of the intraorbital and intracanalicular segments of
the optic nerves [37].

CONCLUSIONS
While KFR and sunflower cataracts remain the hallmark
ophthalmic manifestations of Wilson disease, useful both in
diagnosis and in treatment monitoring, emerging evidence
suggests that retinal and optic nerve alterations detected by
OCT may serve as valuable biomarkers of copper-induced
neurodegeneration and neurological disease severity, acting as
a “window” into the neurological aspect of the disease. Wilson
disease is increasingly viewed as a neurodegenerative disorder,
more broadly than a copper storage disease. Future research
focusing on AI-assisted image analysis, the identification and
documentation of OCT or VEP biomarkers of disease progression,
better correlation with neurology-specific parameters and
correlation between ocular parameters and treatment response
may significantly improve the management and detection
of WD cases and their many, complex, manifestations. A
multidisciplinary approach to the WD patient is essential, and
ophthalmologists have an important role in diagnosis and follow-
up, alongside the gastroenterologist and neurologist.
