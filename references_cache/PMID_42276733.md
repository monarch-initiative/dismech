BACKGROUND: Thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAA) are predominantly degenerative.
Non-infectious inflammatory aortitis, including giant cell arteritis (GCA),
Takayasu arteritis and clinically isolated aortitis (CIA), represents a less
understood aetiology with poorly characterised growth patterns. While GCA is
linked to higher TAA risk, preoperative growth data for inflammatory TAA are
scarce. This study compares growth rates in histologically proven inflammatory
aortitis.
METHODS: We conducted a retrospective case-control study, including patients
with histologically proven aortitis and matched controls with degenerative TAA.
Cases and controls were matched for age, sex, surgical year and procedure type.
Aneurysm growth rate (mm/month) was calculated from serial imaging.
RESULTS: This study included 23 patients with histologically confirmed aortitis
(13 GCA, 9 CIA) and 42 matched controls with degenerative TAA. The mean growth
rate was significantly higher in the aortitis group (0.177±0.01 mm/month;
2.12 mm/year) compared with controls (0.039±0.02 mm/month; 0.47 mm/year),
representing a 4.5-fold acceleration (p<0.001). Among inflammatory etiologies,
CIA demonstrated faster growth (0.338±0.06 mm/month; 4.06 mm/year) than GCA
(2.1 mm/year; p<0.01). Patients with CIA more frequently required Bentall
procedures (33% vs 0%, p=0.047). No differences were observed in baseline
demographics, cardiovascular risk factors or baseline aortic diameters between
groups. Histologically, 70% of aortitis cases exhibited granulomatous
inflammation.
CONCLUSION: Inflammatory aortitis is associated with a 4.5-fold faster
preoperative TAA growth rate compared with degenerative aneurysms, with CIA
exhibiting two times the growth rate of GCA. Prospective studies are warranted
to validate these results and optimise surveillance and management strategies
for this high-risk population.

INTRODUCTION
Thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAA) represent a
serious cardiovascular condition with signifi-
cant morbidity and mortality. While degener-
ative atherosclerotic processes account for the
majority of TAA cases, non- infectious aortitis
constitutes an important subset of aetiologies
that remains incompletely characterised.
Aortitis encompasses several forms of large
vessel vasculitis (LVV), including giant cell
arteritis (GCA), Takayasu arteritis and clini-
cally isolated aortitis (CIA), all characterised
by inflammatory infiltration of the aortic wall.
The pathophysiology of aneurysm forma-
tion in inflammatory aortitis differs funda-
mentally from degenerative processes. In

PATIENTS AND METHODS
Study design and population
We conducted a retrospective, monocentric, case–con-
trol study at Nantes University Hospital, France,
including all patients with histologically proven aortitis
between January 1997 and February 2024. Cases were
defined as adult patients (≥18 years) with non- infectious
inflammatory aortitis histologically confirmed according
to consensus criteria, requiring inflammatory infiltrate
involving the media and/or intima.8 Each aortic histology
was reviewed by a specialist in cardiovascular pathology
(CT).
Exclusion criteria comprised infectious aortitis, hered-
itary aneurysm syndromes (Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-
Danlos syndrome, etc), bicuspid aortic valve disease,
non- inflammatory histology, isolated periaortitis or medi-
astinal tumour invasion.
Finally, aortitis cases were included in the analysis
if at least one matched control could be identified as
described below.
To assess aneurysm growth rate, we included patients
who had undergone at least two measurements of
maximal thoracic aortic diameter at minimum 3- month
intervals using validated imaging modalities: CT angiog-
raphy (CTA), MRI or transthoracic echocardiography.
Aortitis classification
GCA was diagnosed according to the 2022 American
College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European Alliance of
Associations for Rheumatology classification criteria. 9
Takayasu arteritis was diagnosed according to ACR
criteria10 and/or modified Ishikawa criteria.11
CIA was defined as a histologically proven aortitis
without an increase in biological inflammatory markers
before or after aortic surgery, with no other vascular
abnormalities evident on histological or imaging tests,
with the absence of any identifiable underlying inflamma-
tory disease, in accordance with the 2012 revised Chapel
Hill Consensus Conference Nomenclature, which classi-
fies CIA as a single- organ vasculitis.12 CIA is therefore a
clinical, exclusion- based diagnosis that may share over -
lapping histopathological features with GCA, notably
granulomatous inflammation with giant cells.
Histological classification distinguished granuloma-
tous aortitis (presence of epithelioid macrophages, giant
cells and granulomas) from lymphoplasmacytic aortitis
(absence of granulomas with abundant mononuclear
lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate).
Control selection
Control subjects were identified from the cardiac surgery
database (Cordabase) at Nantes University Hospital,
France, between January 2010 and March 2024. Controls
were matched 1:1 to 1:4 with cases for age at surgery,
sex, surgical year and surgical procedure type. Control
inclusion criteria required: (1) no genetic syndrome or
bicuspid valve- related aneurysm; (2) at least two aortic
diameter measurements at minimum 3- month intervals
preoperatively; (3) appropriate matching parameters.
Data collection
We retrospectively collected comprehensive clinical data
from electronic medical records, including demographic
characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension,
dyslipidaemia, diabetes, obesity, smoking status), personal
and family cardiovascular history, aortic aneurysm topog-
raphy, surgical details, histological findings and preoper-
ative treatments (corticosteroids, immunosuppressants,
antiplatelet agents and statins). C reactive protein (CRP)
measured within 3 months preoperatively was recorded
when available, with inflammatory syndrome defined as
CRP>10 mg/L.
Aortic diameter measurement
When CT images were available, aortic diameters were
centrally reviewed (AB and MDG). Measurements of
maximal diameters were performed at the level of (1)
the sinuses of Valsalva, (2) the sinotubular junction,
(3) the ascending aorta, (4) the aortic arch and (5) the
descending aorta posterior to the left atrium, perpendic-
ular to the aortic axis at maximal diameter. When images
were unavailable, diameters from official radiology
reports were used.

Growth rate calculation
Aneurysm growth rate (mm/month) was calculated
as: Growth rate = (D_max − D_baseline)/time interval
(months). D_max represents the preoperative diameter
and D_baseline represents the earliest measurement.
Statistical analysis
Continuous variables were expressed as means with SD
or medians with IQRs 25–75 (Q1–Q3). Quantitative vari-
ables were compared using Student’s t- test for normally
distributed data or Wilcoxon rank- sum test otherwise.
Qualitative variables were compared using Fisher’s exact
test or χ2 test depending on expected frequencies.
Growth rates were compared using a mixed linear
regression model with independent samples, accounting
for the matched design. Results were considered statisti-
cally significant at p<0.05 (two tailed). All analyses were
performed using R statistical software.
RESULTS
Study population and baseline characteristics
Between January 1997 and February 2024, we identified
90 patients with thoracic aortitis diagnosed on surgical
pathology. Of these, 26 patients (28.9%) had at least
two imaging studies at minimum 3- month intervals and
met inclusion criteria. Three patients with aortitis were
excluded due to inability to identify suitable matched
controls (two with extreme ages: 36 and 91 years; one
with combined ascending and arch replacement).
From 1683 potential control patients who underwent
TAA surgery, we included 42 controls after applying all
matching criteria. The mean number of controls per
case was 1.8 (median 2); 11 patients (48%) had only one
matched control.
Table 1 presents baseline characteristics. Both groups
demonstrated female predominance (74% in cases, 60%
in controls; p=0.37) with similar mean age of 73.7 and
73.5 years, respectively. No significant differences existed
regarding body mass index (25.7 vs 26.2 kg/m², p=0.62),
cardiovascular risk factors or cardiovascular history.
Hypertension prevalence was comparable (74% of cases,
79% of controls; p=0.91), as was hypercholesterolaemia
(57% vs 43%, p=0.43).
Surgical and imaging characteristics
All patients in both groups had ascending TAA. However,
aneurysmal extension to the aortic arch and descending
aorta or synchronous descending aneurysm occurred
exclusively in the aortitis group (n=3, 13% vs 0%;
p<0.001).
Surgical procedures were also matched: supracoronary
tube graft in 70% of cases and 64% of controls (p=0.88);
Bentall procedure in 13% vs 24% (p=0.35). Control
patients more frequently underwent concomitant cardiac
interventional procedures (57% vs 0%; p<0.001) (valve
replacement, atrial fibrillation ablation or coronary
artery bypass grafting).
The median number of preoperative imaging examina-
tions was four in both groups (IQR 3–5 for cases, 3–4 for
controls; p=0.48). The median interval between diagnosis
and surgery was 19 months (Q1–Q3: 9–32) for cases and
18 months (Q1–Q3: 9–61) for controls (p=0.48). Mean
baseline ascending aortic diameter was similar: 49.0 mm
vs 49.3 mm (p=0.85).
Surgical indications differed markedly. Aortitis
patients were predominantly operated for asymptomatic
aneurysms reaching critical size (96%, n=22), whereas
controls were more often symptomatic (69%, n=29;
p<0.001), primarily with dyspnoea (57%). Aortic regur -
gitation was present in 55% of cases. Despite different
indications, mean aneurysmal diameter at surgery was
similar: 56.0 mm in cases vs 54.6 mm in controls (p=0.24).
Aortic diameters were centrally measured on CT in
52% of cases and 62% of controls. No significant differ -
ence was observed regarding diameters at the sinuses
of Valsalva, sinotubular junction, ascending aorta or
descending aorta (table 1). Mean aortic arch diameter
was greater in controls (34.3 mm) compared with patients
with aortitis (31.9 mm; p=0.044).
Aortitis characteristics
Among the 23 aortitis cases, GCA was the most common
aetiology (n=13, 57%), followed by CIA (n=9, 39%) and
IgG4- related disease (n=1, 4%); the latter was excluded
from subgroup analysis to focus on differences between
CIA and GCA. Among patients with GCA, 77% (n=10)
had received corticosteroids at the time of surgery,
and one had received immunosuppressive therapy. No
patient with CIA had received corticosteroids or immu-
nosuppressive treatment prior to surgery (p=0.002) as
the diagnosis was established postoperatively on patho-
logical examination.
Preoperative CRP levels were available for 9 patients
(69%), all within the GCA group; of these, 33% exhib-
ited a persistent inflammatory syndrome (table 2). For
patients with CIA, no preoperative inflammatory markers
were available.
Histological examination revealed predominantly
granulomatous inflammation (n=16, 70%) with lymph-
oplasmacytic pattern in 30% (n=7). Distribution did not
differ significantly between CIA (56% granulomatous)
and GCA (79% granulomatous; p=0.36).
Aneurysm growth rate comparison
The preoperative aneurysm growth rate was signifi-
cantly higher in the aortitis group (figure 1A). The

0.039±0.02 mm/month (0.47 mm/year) in degenerative
aneurysms. This represents approximately 4.5- fold faster
growth in inflammatory cases, highly statistically signifi-
cant (p<0.001).

DISCUSSION
This case–control study presents the first comparison
of preoperative aneurysm growth rates between inflam-
matory and degenerative TAA using matched controls.
Our key finding—that aortitis accelerates growth by 4.5-
fold—carries significant clinical and pathophysiological
implications.
Our control group growth rate of 0.47 mm/year aligns
closely with a 2025 meta- analysis reporting degenerative
TAA growth at 0.33 mm/year.13 This meta- analysis also
documented rates of 0.45 mm/year for Marfan syndrome
and 0.81 mm/year for Loeys- Dietz syndrome. Inflamma-
tory aortitis growth rate of 2.12 mm/year substantially
exceeds even hereditary connective tissue disorders,
representing the fastest non- infectious TAA progression
documented.
Limited prior data support our findings. Kebed et al
reported 1.3 mm/year in GCA- associated TAA,4 somewhat
lower but similar magnitude. Quimson et al observed a
non- significant trend towards faster CIA growth (2.9 mm/
year) versus unmatched controls (−1.6 mm/year),5 but
methodological limitations restrict interpretation. Our
matched design provides substantially stronger evidence.
Pathophysiological mechanisms
Accelerated growth likely reflects distinct pathophysi-
ology. Over aortitis group, we observed a lymphoplas-
macytic pattern in seven cases (30%), consistent with
reported frequencies (22–30%).6 14–17 In GCA, the inflam-
matory cascade begins with dendritic cell antigen pres-
entation activating CD4+ T lymphocytes, which differen-
tiate into Th1 and Th17 subpopulations producing inter-
feron-γ and interleukin 17. The inflammatory process
begins in the adventitia, where an increase in the density
of the vasa vasorum is observed. Circulating monocytes
differentiate into macrophages that migrate to arterial
intima and fuse into multinucleated giant cells.1 18 These
cells secrete matrix metalloproteinases degrading elastin-
rich extracellular matrix while inducing smooth muscle
cell apoptosis. Medial smooth muscle cells migrate to the
intima, transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts and prolif-
erate, replacing elastic matrix with disorganised collagen

However, the nosological status of CIA remains
debated. According to the CHCC2012 nomenclature, it
cannot be determined whether CIA represents a truly
distinct entity or a limited manifestation of GCA or
Takayasu arteritis, and several studies have suggested that
many patients with CIA may harbour subclinical GCA.19 20
In our cohort, 56% of patients with CIA had granuloma-
tous inflammation indistinguishable from GCA, while
16–26% of patients with CIA eventually develop features
of a systemic disease (predominantly GCA) during
follow- up.6 14 Subtle differences in inflammatory media-
tors, genetic predisposition or antigen recognition could
nonetheless influence disease behaviour. 6 17  Therefore,
the CIA versus GCA comparison in our study should be
understood as a comparison based on clinical classifica-
tion at the time of surgery rather than between defini-
tively distinct pathological entities.
The faster growth observed in CIA must also be inter -
preted in light of a major confounding factor: 77% of
patients with GCA received corticosteroids preopera-
tively compared with 0% of patients with CIA. Treatment
timing relative to aortic involvement onset is critical:
GCA is usually diagnosed prior to surgery, allowing for
earlier immunosuppressive therapy. Although there is
no direct evidence that corticosteroids reduce aneurysm
growth rate in GCA, immunosuppression has been shown
to decrease the appearance of new vascular lesions, 21
suggesting a potential attenuating effect on disease
progression. In contrast, CIA typically presents as an
isolated aneurysm, with diagnosis established only after
surgery, precluding any preoperative treatment. The
observed growth rate difference may therefore reflect,
at least in part, the suppression of aortic wall inflamma-
tion by corticosteroids rather than an intrinsically faster
growth rate in CIA. Separating the treatment effect from
a potential pathophysiological difference is not possible
in this observational design.
Postoperative outcomes were not endpoints of the
present study but in a multicentre study of 217 patients
with noninfectious surgical thoracic aortitis, Espitia et
al6 identified the main prognostic factors for adverse
vascular outcomes, including aortic dissection at initial
presentation and descending thoracic aorta involvement.
Notably, there was no significant difference in the risk
of vascular complications, second vascular procedure or
mortality between CIA and other non- infectious aortitis
patients. Furthermore, granulomatous aortitis was asso-
ciated with a worse long- term prognosis compared with
lymphoplasmacytic aortitis.15 In the present study, limited
statistical power due to the small number of patients
with lymphoplasmacytic aortitis precluded any definitive
conclusion regarding a difference in aneurysm growth
rate according to histological pattern.
Selection bias inherent in our design—CIA diagnosis
requires surgery and sufficient preoperative imaging—
may have enriched our CIA cohort for rapidly progres-
sive cases, whereas systemic disease patients benefit from
systematic surveillance potentially detecting slower-
growing aneurysms. However, this would emphasise
rather than invalidate recognising rapid growth as a
potential CIA indicator.
Clinical implications and recommendations
Our findings have immediate clinical relevance. Rapid
TAA growth—particularly exceeding 3 mm/year—should
prompt consideration of inflammatory aortitis, especially
in women over 60 years, even without overt symptoms or
elevated inflammatory markers. Such patients warrant
comprehensive evaluation, including a systematic review
for GCA symptoms (headache, jaw claudication, visual
symptoms and polymyalgia), vascular imaging (CTA or
MRI) to assess aortic wall thickening, [ 18F]-fluorodeox-
yglucose positron emission tomography/CT if available
and temporal artery biopsy if GCA suspected clinically.22 23
If aortitis is identified prior to surgery, the use of immu-
nosuppressants or corticosteroids is considered on a case-
by- case basis to minimise surgical complications, particu-
larly difficulties in suturing an inflamed aortic wall.
Faster growth in CIA suggests particularly intensive post-
operative surveillance, as these patients show high rates
of new aortic complications (47% develop subsequent
aneurysms vs 7% of degenerative controls) and reoper -
ation (40–46% in major series). 6 14 24  Whether empiric
postoperative immunosuppression could improve CIA
outcomes remains an open question requiring prospec-
tive investigation.6 14
Strengths and limitations
Study strengths include a rigorous matched case–control
design, standardised measurement protocols with 58%
of scans centralised review, comprehensive clinical data
from a tertiary referral centre with recognised expertise,
long preoperative imaging follow- up (median 18–19
months) and robust statistical methodology using mixed
linear regression validated in previous aneurysm growth
studies.
Important limitations warrant consideration. The
retrospective single- centre design and sample size limit
subgroup analyses and generalisability. Matching difficul-
ties in order to have a large number of controls arose from
population differences: controls were predominantly
younger men with associated cardiac pathology requiring
complex procedures, whereas aortitis patients were older
women with isolated ascending disease requiring simpler
operations.
The shorter observation period in CIA (median 11
months) compared with GCA (median 30 months) may
raise concerns regarding the reliability of growth rate esti-
mation. However, this shorter surveillance period is itself
a consequence of the faster aneurysm growth, leading
to earlier achievement of surgical criteria. Moreover,

CONCLUSION
This matched case–control study demonstrates that
inflammatory aortitis is associated with 4.5- fold faster
preoperative TAA growth compared with degenerative
aneurysms (2.1 vs 0.5 mm/year). These findings establish
inflammatory aortitis as the fastest non- infectious cause
of TAA progression documented.
Among inflammatory aetiologies, preoperatively undi-
agnosed CIA shows approximately twofold faster growth
than mostly corticosteroid- treated GCA (4.1 vs 2.1 mm/
year), although this design does not allow distinguish
between distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and a
treatment effect.
Rapid TAA growth should prompt clinical suspicion
of underlying inflammatory aortitis, particularly in older
women, warranting comprehensive evaluation even
without classical inflammatory symptoms or biomarkers.
Prospective multicentre studies are warranted to validate
our findings, elucidate pathophysiology of accelerated
inflammatory aneurysm growth and optimise manage-
ment algorithms for this high- risk population.
