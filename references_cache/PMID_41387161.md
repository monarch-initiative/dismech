# Treatment of primary adult-onset neurodegenerative cerebellar ataxias.
**Authors:** Ariello LE, Rastall DPW, Rosenthal LS
**Journal:** Neurotherapeutics (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.neurot.2025.e00805](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neurot.2025.e00805)

## Content

Primary adult-onset neurodegenerative cerebellar ataxias (PANCA) are a clinically and genetically diverse group of disorders for which disease-modifying therapies remain limited. In this review, we provide a comprehensive analysis of therapeutic strategies for PANCA, with a primary focus on clinical trials-randomized controlled and open-label-that have evaluated pharmacological agents, rehabilitation programs, and neuromodulatory interventions. Where clinical trial data are lacking, we incorporate relevant observational studies, expert consensus, and mechanistic rationale to contextualize current practices. Rehabilitation and multidisciplinary care remain foundational across all subtypes and are supported by growing clinical trial evidence. Pharmacological approaches, including omaveloxolone for Friedreich's ataxia and off-label agents such as riluzole, 4-aminopyridine, and varenicline, demonstrate subtype-specific benefits. Neuromodulation techniques, such as transcranial direct current stimulation and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, have shown early promise in improving motor outcomes. In parallel, molecular and gene-based therapies-including antisense oligonucleotides, viral vector delivery systems, and CRISPR-based strategies-are advancing into preclinical and early-phase clinical studies. This evolving therapeutic landscape underscores a shift toward personalized, multimodal care for cerebellar ataxias and highlights the need for continued translational efforts to bridge mechanistic insights with clinical impact.

Cerebellar ataxia encompasses a heterogeneous group of neurological disorders marked by impairments in coordination, balance, and eye movements resulting from cerebellar dysfunction. An early understanding of the cerebellum’s motor role emerged from Sir Gordon Morgan Holmes’ observations of traumatic cerebellar injuries in World War I soldiers wearing the flawed “Brodie” helmet [1]. Since then, it has become clear that the cerebellum coordinates not only limb and trunk movements but also eye movements, sensory integration, and emotional regulation [2]. These diverse functions are unified by the cerebellum’s core role: ensuring precision by measuring targets, detecting error, and enabling rapid adjustments.

Classification of cerebellar ataxias varies according to etiology and onset. Broadly, they are categorized into primary and secondary forms [3]. Secondary ataxias result from external insults including stroke, autoimmune disorders, infections, tumors, and trauma, among others. Their management centers on identifying and treating the underlying cause, followed by rehabilitation [4]. This review focuses on those arising in adulthood and from intrinsic cerebellar pathology, the primary adult-onset neurodegenerative cerebellar ataxia (PANCA).

Primary cerebellar ataxias comprise both sporadic and hereditary forms. In this review we introduce the acronym PANCA as a pragmatic shorthand to collectively describe non-acquired ataxias of adult onset, encompassing both sporadic and hereditary forms. Among the sporadic forms, the two most common causes are idiopathic late-onset cerebellar ataxia (ILOCA) and multiple system atrophy (MSA). Among the hereditary forms, autosomal dominant conditions such as the spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) and episodic ataxias (EAs) predominate. Less frequently, numerous autosomal recessive cerebellar ataxias (ARCAs), as well as X-linked and mitochondrial disorders, may also present with cerebellar ataxia as a defining feature in adults [5].

Among the autosomal-dominant ataxias of adulthood, the SCAs represent the most prevalent group, currently encompassing more than 50 genetically and clinically heterogeneous subtypes. They follow a progressive course and are frequently accompanied by extracerebellar features that can aid in diagnosis. In contrast, EAs are characterized by transient ataxic episodes, frequently associated with vertigo, double vision and slurred speech, and tend to manifest in young adults, before the age of 20 [6,7]. ARCAs include disorders such as, ataxia-telangiectasia, Autosomal Recessive Spastic Ataxia of Charlevoix–Saguenay and Friedreich’s ataxia (FA), which typically present during young ages [8]. Among the autosomal-recessive forms with adult onset, cerebellar ataxia with neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) has more recently been recognized as one of the more frequent conditions. It combines a characteristic triad of cerebellar dysfunction, bilateral vestibulopathy, and sensory neuronopathy, which together define its clinical phenotype [9].

In the past two decades, advances in genetics and molecular biology have deepened our understanding of PANCA pathophysiology. Yet, effective treatments remain largely elusive [10]. The marked clinical and genetic heterogeneity of PANCA has long hindered therapeutic development. However, recent progress in trial design, biomarker identification, and disease modeling is enabling more targeted and mechanistically informed interventions [11]. Promising symptomatic therapies have emerged, and a growing number of trials now explore disease-modifying approaches, including drug repurposing, gene-based treatments, and neuromodulation techniques [12].

In this review, we present a focused and pragmatic overview of current and emerging therapeutic strategies for PANCA. We synthesize evidence from clinical trials, delineate expert-guided management approaches where data are scarce, and highlight evolving directions in pharmacological, genetic, and neuromodulatory interventions that may shape future treatment paradigms.

This review focuses on currently utilized and emerging therapeutic options for PANCA. We conducted a targeted literature search in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, complemented by ClinicalTrials.gov for ongoing or unpublished trials. The search covered studies published between 1983 and 2025 and prioritized articles published in peer-reviewed, high-impact journals or representing pivotal clinical contributions in the field.

To ensure comprehensive coverage, searches were performed separately for the three main therapeutic domains: pharmacological, neuromodulatory, and rehabilitative approaches. Disease-related terms (“cerebellar ataxia,” “spinocerebellar ataxia,” “episodic ataxia,” “multiple system atrophy,” “late-onset cerebellar ataxia,” “Friedreich ataxia”) were combined with intervention-related keywords (“treatment,” “therapy,” “clinical trial,” “neuromodulation,” “rTMS,” “tDCS,” “rehabilitation,” “physiotherapy”).

Clinical trials, open-label interventional studies, and expert consensus reports most relevant to PANCA were prioritized. Case reports and publications without a therapeutic focus were excluded. The review followed a targeted, rather than fully systematic, approach aimed at capturing the most influential and clinically informative studies across all three therapeutic domains.

The treatment of most cerebellar ataxias remains primarily symptomatic and supportive. For the majority of patients, therapeutic strategies are not yet tailored to specific sporadic or genetic subtypes but instead aim to mitigate motor and non-motor symptoms, preserve functional independence, and enhance quality of life [9]. In this context, multidisciplinary care, encompassing symptomatic pharmacological treatment and physical, occupational, and speech therapies, is essential and often forms the backbone of management.

Although pharmacological treatments for cerebellar ataxias have historically been limited to these symptomatic approaches, recent progress marks a potential shift in the therapeutic landscape. One example is the recent approval of omaveloxolone (Skyclarys) for FA, making FA the first primary ataxia to benefit from a U.S. Food and Drug Agency (FDA) - and European Medicines Agency (EMA)-approved disease-modifying therapy [13]. Omaveloxolone, an Nrf2 activator that reduces mitochondrial oxidative stress, has demonstrated sustained benefit in trials such as the MOXIe Extension Trial [14], establishing a pivotal milestone and positioning FA as a model of translational success in the field.

Encouragingly, this landmark approval has catalyzed a wave of new therapeutic candidates now advancing through late-stage development. Examples include Lu AF82422, an anti-α-synuclein monoclonal antibody in Phase 3 trials for MSA, and SLX-100 (immediate-release 4-aminopyridine), which is slated for Phase 3 evaluation in SCA27B [15,16]. These efforts reflect growing momentum toward disease-modifying pharmacological interventions across the spectrum of neurodegenerative ataxias [16,17].

Despite the recent progress, most pharmacologic interventions for cerebellar ataxias remain off-label. Clinical decision-making is frequently guided by small studies involving heterogeneous patient populations and reporting only modest effect sizes. The 2018 consensus guidelines offer a structured framework for symptomatic pharmacologic management, addressing features such as spasticity, tremor, dystonia, fatigue, and depression [17]. However, real-world practice often extends beyond these recommendations, reflecting the limitations of the current evidence base [18].

Given the limited availability of pharmacological treatments and the need for a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach, non-pharmacological interventions are central to the management of cerebellar ataxias, evolving substantially in both scope and sophistication in recent years. Advances in rehabilitation science, coupled with improved access to multidisciplinary care, have enhanced the delivery and impact of interventions such as physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and swallow therapy, balance training, and coordination-focused regimens [19,20]. These approaches are increasingly guided by structured protocols and emerging clinical data, and continue to offer meaningful symptomatic benefits when tailored to individual patient needs.

More recently, neuromodulatory interventions have been added to the non-pharmacological therapeutic arsenal for cerebellar ataxias. Techniques such as transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) are being explored as adjunctive and potentially disease-modifying, strategies [21]. Building on the foundation of supportive care, these non-invasive approaches align with the broader shift toward integrated, pharmacological target and, multimodal management. Although evidence remains preliminary, their growing promise has sparked increasing research interest. These interventions will be further discussed in the Future Perspectives section.

In this section, we outline therapeutic strategies first in general for all PANCAs and then applied to the most prevalent and clinically relevant disorders within the PANCA spectrum. Particular emphasis is placed on conditions that have recently attracted attention due to advances in targeted therapeutic development. Given the rarity and recent characterization of many of these disorders, disease-specific clinical trials are often lacking. Consequently, management frequently relies on extrapolated trial data, limited evidence, and expert clinical judgment. For each condition, we summarize key clinical features, review available therapeutic studies, and—where data are scarce—provide insights derived from our own and others’ clinical experience.A.General PANCA Treatment Considerations

General PANCA Treatment Considerations

When possible and appropriate, the first-line treatment for any of the PANCAs is a drug that is approved for the specific ataxia subtype identified. To date though, no disease-modifying therapies have been approved for the majority of PANCA subtypes, so a more general approach is often used. The guiding principle is that there is almost always something that we are able to do to improve function or quality of life amongst our patients. Comprehensive ataxia care is based on screening for the symptoms of each subtype and then symptomatic management of each of those symptoms as needed, with the goal of optimizing patient function. Non-pharmacological support including physiotherapy, balance training, and speech therapy has demonstrated significant benefit in improving functional outcomes and quality of life, even in advanced stages of disease [22]. Looking ahead, precision-medicine-based strategies, guided by advances in neuroimaging, biomarker discovery, and longitudinal natural history studies, are expected to play a central role in accelerating the development of individualized therapies for PANCAs [29,30]. Experimental molecular approaches are being developed to reduce the expression of mutant proteins, including antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) [31], RNA interference (RNAi) [32], and gene silencing strategies. Although most of these remain in preclinical or early-phase clinical trials [33], they represent a promising avenue for disease modification.B.Idiophatic Late-Onset Cerebellar Ataxia

Idiophatic Late-Onset Cerebellar Ataxia

In many individuals with PANCA, no genetic etiology is identified despite extensive evaluation, leading to a working diagnosis of idiopathic late-onset cerebellar ataxia (ILOCA). ILOCA refers to a group of progressive, adult-onset cerebellar ataxias—typically beginning after age 40—with no clear family history or secondary cause at the time of diagnosis, even after comprehensive workup including genetic testing [34]. Importantly, the expansion of genetic testing technologies has led to the reclassification of a growing number of cases originally labeled as ILOCA. Some of these have been linked to repeat expansions or mutations in genes such as RFC1, SACS, SPTBN2, and FGF14. [35,36].

Therapeutic trials in ILOCA have been limited, and no disease-modifying treatments have yet demonstrated consistent efficacy. The ALCAT trial, which included both hereditary and sporadic cerebellar ataxias, found that acetyl-dl-leucine did not significantly improve ataxia severity compared to placebo [37]. Spinocerebellar Ataxias (SCAs)

SCAs are a diverse group of autosomal dominant inherited neurodegenerative disorders characterized by progressive cerebellar dysfunction. With over 50 identified subtypes, SCAs present with a wide spectrum of clinical features, including ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus, vertigo and often a variety of extra-cerebellar signs [62]. The significant genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity among SCAs poses a considerable challenge for the development of universally effective treatments. As discussed in Table 3, a number of pharmaceuticals have been tested in a wide range of SCAs, with inclusion criteria sometimes requiring specific genetic diagnoses and at other times simply requiring a diagnosis of cerebellar degeneration. Out of all these investigations, riluzole remains the only medication recommended by the American Academy of Neurology for a wide range of SCAs based on evidence from 1 Class I study (86). Further, a meta-analysis indicated a potential benefit of riluzole in hereditary ataxias in general, although the strength of evidence remains limited [63].

The prodrug of riluzole, troriluzole has been under investigation (NCT02960893 and NCT03701399) for a diverse range of SCA subtypes ​though it was not approved by the FDA [44,64,64]. While the Phase III randomized controlled trial that enrolled individuals with SCA 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, and 10 failed to meet its primary endpoint of change in the modified functional SARA score at 48 weeks, other aspects of the longtime investigation are more promising. The troriluzole real-world evidence study (NCT06529146) suggests that troriluzole may slow disease progression by 50–70 ​% over three years, as measured by the functional SARA (fSARA), across multiple SCA genotypes [65], [66]. Details of the troriluzole randomized trial findings are discussed within each SCA subtype as relevant. Acetyl-dl-leucine and 4-aminopyridine have also demonstrated symptomatic benefit in selected genetic or episodic ataxias [15,67,68].

Spinocerebellar ataxia type 1 (SCA1) is an autosomal dominant polyglutamine disorder caused by CAG repeat expansion in the ATXN1 gene, leading to toxic gain of function of the ataxin-1 protein [69]. SCA 1 is often included in pharmaceutical trials as it is one of the more common SCAs along with SCA 2 and SCA 3 (Table 3). Therapeutic development for SCA1 has recently included gene-targeting approaches, most notably ASOs that reduce ATXN1 expression and have shown robust benefits in preclinical models [70]. SCA2 is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder caused by CAG repeat expansions in the ATXN2 gene. It is characterized by progressive cerebellar ataxia, slow saccades, pyramidal signs, and frequent extrapyramidal features. Currently, no disease-modifying therapies are approved for SCA2, and treatment remains primarily symptomatic. The ATRIL trial specifically investigated riluzole in SCA type 2 did not demonstrate a significant clinical benefit [84]. Troriluzole did not demonstrate statistically significant clinical efficacy in primary outcomes in SCA2 (Table 3), with again neither placebo nor troriluzole participants progressing significantly over the course of the investigation [85]. Emerging disease-modifying approaches for SCA2 include ASOs targeting ATXN2, which have demonstrated promising preclinical results, reducing mutant transcript levels, improving motor coordination, and preserving Purkinje cell integrity [31]. ARO-ATXN2, another gene therapy based emerging approach, utilizes small interfering RNA molecules targeting the ATXN2 mRNA and is currently undergoing a Phase I study. Another investigational avenue involves small-conductance calcium-activated potassium (SK) channel modulators, such as chlorzoxazone and NS13001, which aim to restore cerebellar neuronal excitability [86]. These agents have shown motor improvements in SCA2 animal models and are currently under early-phase clinical investigation [32]. Non-pharmacological interventions, including intensive rehabilitation and tDCS, have also demonstrated functional benefits and may be included as supportive therapies (Table 3, Table 4) [87].

SCA3, also known as Machado-Joseph Disease, does have specific trials underway. It is the most common autosomal dominant ataxia globally and therefore has attracted attention for a specific treatment. Clinically, SCA3 is highly pleomorphic, typically presenting with cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal signs, peripheral neuropathy, ophthalmoparesis, and various non-motor symptoms such as REM sleep behavior disorder, cognitive impairment, and urinary dysfunction [88].

To date, no disease-modifying therapies have been approved for SCA3 in published trials, however multiple pre-published results are available. Among them, troriluzole, has shown promise. A post hoc analysis of the Phase 3 trial (NCT03701399) in adult patients with multiple SCA subtypes, including SCA3, reported significant improvement in gait stability—as measured by the Pose Dispersion Index (PDI)—after 48 weeks of treatment compared to placebo [89]. In addition, a post-hoc analysis of the same phase III study showed that benefit of taking troriluzole over placebo in the SCA 3 subgroup [63].

In the preclinical domain, therapeutic approaches targeting protein homeostasis and autophagy have yielded encouraging results. In murine models of SCA3, inhibition of Hsp90, ameliorated motor deficits and neuropathological changes, highlighting proteostasis modulation as a therapeutic avenue [90]. Another emerging therapeutic strategy for SCA3 involves chlorzoxazone. In preclinical studies using the SCA3-84Q mouse model, long-term administration of chlorzoxazone significantly improved motor coordination, as demonstrated by normalized performance on beam-walk tasks. This benefit is attributed to restoration of Purkinje cell pacemaking fidelity, suggesting that SK-channel modulation may correct underlying cerebellar dysfunction. These findings support the rationale for advancing chlorzoxazone into early-phase clinical trials as a potential symptomatic therapy for motor impairment in SCA3 [91]. Additionally, repurposing of the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor citalopram has shown promise in murine models of SCA3. In YACMJD84.2 mice, chronic citalopram administration reduced mutant ATXN3 aggregation by approximately 50 ​% in brainstem regions. These findings further support the therapeutic potential of enhancing proteostasis in SCA3 and nominate citalopram as a candidate for drug repurposing [92].

Moreover, a particularly promising line of investigation involves ASOs targeting mutant ATXN3mRNA. These molecules are designed to reduce the synthesis of the toxic polyglutamine-expanded protein [93]. Although early ASO programs, such as Ionis Pharmaceuticals’ IONIS-ATXN3Rx, were discontinued due to an unfavorable risk–benefit profile in early-phase trials (terminated in 2022), the therapeutic rationale remains valid. New ASO candidates with improved chemistry and CNS delivery are in preclinical or early clinical stages, with growing interest in allele-selective strategies that minimize off-target effects on wild-type ATXN3 [33]. To mentioned, Biogen has put forward an ASO therapy for SCA3 (NCT05160558), representing one of the most advanced genetic approaches in this field [94]. The study however was terminated after review of nonclinical safety data and other data.

Beyond pharmacological approaches, DBS has been explored in selected SCA3 patients with prominent extrapyramidal features, such as tremor. In small pilot series, dentate nucleus DBS improved tremor and coordination with acceptable safety, while isolated reports describe sustained benefit of subthalamic DBS in parkinsonian SCA3 and short-term improvement in ataxia and dystonia with combined dentate and pallidal stimulation [95,96]. Although evidence remains limited, these findings suggest that neuromodulation may offer symptomatic relief in carefully selected cases.

Noninvasive stimulation techniques have also gained traction. In a randomized trial of low-frequency rTMS (1 ​Hz, 900 pulses, 30 ​min), Chen et al. reported significant improvements in ICARS, with greater effects among SCA3 patients [81]. Sikandar et al. (2023) further confirmed that LF-rTMS led to consistent gains in SARA and ICARS scores [82]. More recently, Zhou et al. showed that a two-week course of high-frequency (10 ​Hz) rTMS also improved both scales in SCA3 patients compared to sham [83]. Taken together, these studies suggest that SCA3 may be particularly responsive to cerebellar rTMS, positioning this subtype as a leading candidate for future trials of noninvasive neuromodulation in degenerative ataxias (Table 4).

SCA6 is a late-onset, slowly progressive disorder characterized by a relatively “pure” cerebellar syndrome, with predominant gait and limb ataxia, dysarthria, and nystagmus, but minimal extra-cerebellar involvement. It is caused by CAG repeat expansion in the CACNA1A gene, which encodes the α1A subunit of the P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channel [97]. Clinically, SCA6 tends to have milder severity and a longer disease duration compared to other SCAs, often with preserved ambulation for many years [98].

To date there are no approved disease-modifying therapies for SCA6 with genotype-specific efficacy or lack thereof not known for troriluzole. Emerging signals from pooled SCA cohorts (e.g., gait stability and slower progression) support continued evaluation, but do not constitute a formal therapeutic recommendation for SCA6. Ongoing efforts to stratify troriluzole outcomes by genotype may further clarify its role in this population [66].

Given the channelopathy mechanism of CACNA1A, acetazolamide has also been explored as a symptomatic treatment. In a small open-label study by Yabe et al., six SCA6 patients treated with acetazolamide (250–500 ​mg/day) for 88 weeks demonstrated significant improvement in Ataxia Rating Scale scores and stabilometric measures, suggesting a transient symptomatic benefit (Table 3). While results were short-term and limited by sample size, they support potential responsiveness in patients with intermittent or fluctuating symptoms resembling episodic ataxia [54]. To our knowledge, there are no other currently being planned RCTs specifically for SCA 6.

SCA27B is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a slowly progressive cerebellar syndrome [99]. The underlying genetic cause of SCA27B is a (GAA) repeat expansion located in the first intron of the FGF14 gene [100]. It is characterized by oculomotor signs, including downbeat nystagmus and oscillopsia. As it was first described in 2022, there have yet to be dedicated clinical trials, however our firsthand experience is that dalfampridine and its immediate-release form, 4-aminopyridine, may offer significant symptomatic relief for downbeat nystagmus and the accompanying visual instability in individuals with SCA27B [60]. This is supported by preliminary studies in a small number of SCA27B patients, where placebo-controlled video-oculography data demonstrated a significant reduction in the slow phase velocity of downbeat nystagmus following treatment with 4-aminopyridine, with no such reduction observed in the placebo group [60].

Building on these early findings, a Phase 3 randomized, placebo-controlled trial of SLX-100 (immediate-release 4-aminopyridine) is now being planned by Solaxa [101]. Friedreich’s Ataxia (FA)

FA is the most common hereditary ataxia, characterized by an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern and caused by a GAA repeat expansion in the FRDA gene, leading to a deficiency in the mitochondrial protein frataxin [102]. Symptoms typically manifest before the age of 25, but can develop late in life, and include progressive gait ataxia, dysarthria, loss of tendon reflexes, and are often accompanied by hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, diabetes mellitus, and skeletal abnormalities including scoliosis [103,104].

Significant progress has been made in the development of pharmacological treatments for FA. Omaveloxolone (SKYCLARYS), an Nrf2 activator, is the first FDA-approved treatment for FA in adults and adolescents aged 16 years and older and is thought to be disease modifying [13]. The approval was based on the results of the MOXIe trial, which demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in neurological function, as measured by the modified Friedreich’s Ataxia Rating Scale (mFARS), compared to placebo at 48 weeks [14,105]. In addition to the randomized data, a post hoc analysis comparing patients from the MOXIe extension study with a propensity score–matched cohort from the Friedreich’s Ataxia Clinical Outcome Measures Study (FACOMS) provided further support. Over a three-year period, patients treated with omaveloxolone showed significantly slower progression, an average increase of only 3.0 mFARS points versus 6.6 in the matched natural history group, corresponding to a 55 ​% reduction in disease progression [106]. A post-marketing registry, SKYCLARYS PASS, is tracking the long-term safety and patient experiences with omaveloxolone and research is underway regarding the potential role for Omaveloxolone in the treatment of younger FA patients [107].

Several other pharmacological therapies are under investigation for FA. Vatiquinone, a lipid peroxidation inhibitor targeting mitochondrial dysfunction, was among the most advanced candidates for FA. Its pivotal randomized, placebo-controlled Phase 2/3 trial (MOVE-FA) reported a mean 3.7-point benefit in mFARS over 144 weeks relative to a matched natural history cohort, suggesting disease-modifying potential. However, the study did not meet its primary endpoint, and the subsequent FDA review resulted in a Complete Response Letter, stating that approval was not granted and further trials will be required [56,68,108].

Research is also focusing on treatment for the cardiac effects of FA. A clinical trial assessed the safety and effectiveness of Epicatechin. It showed good safety, was well tolerated over 24 weeks at up to 150 ​mg/d and improvement was observed in cardiac structure and function in a subset of subjects with FA without statistically significant improvement in primary neurological outcomes [109]. Other studies are still under investigation. LX2006, an adeno-associated virus (AAV) based gene therapy, is being evaluated in a Phase 1/2 study for its safety and efficacy in treating cardiomyopathy associated with FA. Interim data from this study have shown promising improvements in cardiac biomarkers [110]. Given that cardiac complications are the leading cause of death in FA, comprehensive therapy must both slow neurological progression and address the underlying cardiomyopathy to achieve meaningful long-term outcomes. The NICOFA trial, a Phase 2 study, is investigating the effects of Nicotinamide (Vitamin B3) on frataxin protein production in individuals with confirmed FA aged 18–50 years [111]. CTI-1601 (Nomlabofusp) is undergoing an open-label extension study to evaluate its long-term safety and clinical effects in subjects with FA [112], [113]. Given the similarity of the GAA repeat expansion in the FGF14 gene (associated with a type of late-onset ataxia) to that in the FRDA gene, 4-aminopyridine has been suggested as a potential therapeutic option for FA as well [114]. Finally, additional approaches are being tested, including frataxin replacement, mitochondrial enhancers, and histone deacetylase inhibitors [115]. Notably, Design Therapeutics is conducting the Phase 1/2 RESTORE-FA trial of DT-216P2, a gene-targeted therapy aimed at restoring frataxin expression, which represents an innovative strategy now in early clinical evaluation [116]. Collectively, the landscape of pharmacological research in FA is rapidly evolving, offering increasing hope for effective treatments.D.Niemann-Pick Type C

Niemann-Pick Type C

Niemann–Pick type C (NPC) is a rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder, usually manifesting in childhood but occasionally in adulthood with ataxia, cognitive decline, or psychiatric features. It is caused by mutations in NPC1 or NPC2, leading to impaired intracellular lipid trafficking and progressive neurodegeneration [117]. Miglustat, an iminosugar that inhibits glycosphingolipid synthesis, remains the only approved therapy in several countries. Randomized controlled trials and long-term observational data have shown that miglustat can stabilize or slow neurological progression, particularly in younger patients and when started early in the disease course [118].

More recently, N-acetyl-dl-leucine, a modified amino acid with effects on neuronal energy metabolism and membrane stabilization, has been tested as a novel therapeutic approach. The phase 2b/3 IB1001-201 trial, published in The New England Journal of Medicine in 2024, evaluated N-acetyl-dl-leucine in NPC. While the study did not meet its primary endpoint (SARA score), subgroup analyses suggested potential benefit in younger and less advanced patients [67]. N-acetyl-dl-leucine was approved by the FDA for treatment of the neurological symptoms of NPC in September 2024 under the name Aqneursa (levacetylleucine).E.Episodic Ataxias (EA)

Episodic Ataxias (EA)

EAs are a group of inherited neurological disorders characterized by recurrent, discrete episodes of ataxia often accompanied by vertigo, with periods of normal function between attacks [119]. Several types of episodic ataxia have been identified (EA1 through EA8), each with distinct genetic causes, triggers, and associated symptoms [1]. EA1 and EA2 are typically responsive to medication as discussed in detail below [120].

EA1 is often associated with muscle twitching or stiffness (myokymia) and is caused by mutations in the KCNA1 gene. The attacks of ataxia are typically brief, lasting from seconds to minutes, and can be triggered by stress, sudden movement, or being startled [121]. While no clinical trials have been completed in EA1, carbamazepine, a drug that stabilizes the inactivated state of voltage-gated sodium channels, is considered the treatment of choice for EA1, effectively reducing the frequency and severity of ataxic episodes [122,123].

EA2 is caused by mutations in the CACNA1A gene which is also implicated in familial hemiplegic migraine type 1 and SCA6 [124]. Attacks of ataxia in EA2 are longer than those in EA1, typically lasting from minutes to hours, and can be triggered, mostly by stress and physical exertion [125]. Acetazolamide, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, has been shown to reduce the frequency and severity of attacks in many individuals with EA2 [126,127]. 4-Aminopyridine, a potassium channel blocker, is another effective treatment option that has demonstrated a reduction in the frequency and duration of ataxic attacks in EA2 [47]. The EAT2TREAT trial, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial, compared the efficacy of 4-Aminopyridine and acetazolamide to placebo in patients with EA2 and related episodic ataxia. The results showed that both significantly reduced the number of attacks compared to placebo, with 4-Aminopyridine potentially being better tolerated at the tested dosages [47].

Other recognized types of episodic ataxia include EA3, EA4, EA5, EA6, EA7, and EA-Other. Acetazolamide may be beneficial in reducing the frequency and severity of attacks in some of these rarer forms as well [128]. Genetic testing is available for some episodic ataxia types, aiding in diagnosis. However, treatment options for these less common types are not as well-established as for EA1 and EA2, highlighting the need for further research.F.Cerebellar Ataxia, Neuropathy, and Vestibular Areflexia Syndrome (CANVAS)

Cerebellar Ataxia, Neuropathy, and Vestibular Areflexia Syndrome (CANVAS)

CANVAS is a recently described, late-onset cerebellar ataxia disorder characterized by the triad of cerebellar ataxia, peripheral neuropathy, and vestibular areflexia [129]. The underlying cause of CANVAS is typically biallelic intronic AAGGG repeat expansions in the RFC1 gene [130]. Currently, there are no disease-modifying pharmacological treatments approved for CANVAS. Therapeutic strategies are primarily symptomatic and tailored to the individual components of the syndrome. A clinical trial is currently investigating the efficacy of pregabalin for chronic cough associated with CANVAS [131]. Notably, this symptom can be particularly debilitating and is often managed with agents used for chronic refractory cough, such as low-dose morphine or γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) analogues [132]. Overall, management remains supportive, aiming to improve quality of life through targeted symptom control.G.Dentatorubral-Pallidoluysian Atrophy (DRPLA)

Dentatorubral-Pallidoluysian Atrophy (DRPLA)

DRPLA is a rare inherited neurodegenerative disorder caused by an expansion of a CAG trinucleotide repeat in the ATN1 gene and characterized by progressive late-onset cerebellar ataxia with an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. The clinical presentation typically includes cerebellar ataxia, involuntary movements such as chorea, cognitive decline progressing to dementia, and often seizures and myoclonus, particularly in individuals with an earlier age of onset [133].

Currently, there is no cure for DRPLA, and treatment is primarily focused on managing symptoms. An experimental treatment approach involves testing an ASO specific for DRPLA [134]. Additionally, the DRPLA Natural History and Biomarkers Study is an ongoing international project that aims to characterize the natural history of DRPLA in both juvenile- and adult-onset patients and to identify clinical, genetic, fluid, and imaging biomarkers. Multiple System Atrophy (MSA)

MSA is a sporadic, progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by a combination of motor and autonomic dysfunctions. It is categorized into two main subtypes: parkinsonian type (MSA-P) and cerebellar type (MSA-C), with MSA-C being characterized by prominent cerebellar ataxia [136].

Currently, there is no preventive or curative treatment for MSA. Treatment strategies focus on managing the various symptoms [137]. Levodopa, a common medication for Parkinson’s disease, is often used to treat parkinsonian features in MSA, but the response is typically poor or unsustained and actually the poor response to this drug is used as diagnostic criteria [138,139]. Droxidropa, [138] midrinone [139] and fludrocortisone [140], can be used to treat neurogenic orthostatic hypotension; the first two being FDA-approved, while the later used as off-labeled [141].

Several clinical trials have recently been completed or are ongoing to evaluate potential disease-modifying therapies for MSA. Earlier candidates have included agents such asRifampicin [142], Sirolimus [143] and Mesenchymal Stem Cells [144], although results have been largely inconclusive. TAK-341, a selective LRRK2 inhibitor [145], and BHV-3241 (verdiperstat) [146], an oral myeloperoxidase inhibitor, have completed Phase 2 studies. Lu AF82422 [16], an α-synuclein monoclonal antibody, has advanced to Phase 3 following encouraging Phase 2 signals and a previous published Phase 1 [147]. Additional Phase 2 programs have recently entered the clinical pipeline: ONO-2808, an oral sphingosine-1-phosphate 5 (S1P5) receptor agonist developed by Ono Pharmaceutical, progressed from a first-in-human study in healthy volunteers (NCT04578028) to an ongoing placebo-controlled Phase 2 trial in MSA (NCT05923866) [148]; YA-101 (Yoda Therapeutics) is under investigation in the RESTORE-MSA Phase 2 study (NCT06848231) [148], and ATH434 (Alterity Therapeutics), a small-molecule iron chelator with anti-aggregation properties, has now completed Phase 2 evaluation in MSA, with randomized trial results reporting a slower progression of clinical symptoms and evidence of target engagement [149]. In parallel, Teva has initiated early-phase development of emrusolmin (TEV-56286), a novel investigational therapy recently granted Fast Track designation by the FDA for MSA [150]. Despite the growing breadth of this therapeutic pipeline, no candidate has yet demonstrated definitive clinical efficacy, underscoring the urgent need for disease-modifying treatments in MSA.

All modifiable contributors to disability should be systematically addressed. Fatigue, sleep disturbances, and mood disorders are common but under-recognized [151,152]. Approved

Omaveloxolone represents the beginning of a pharmacological shift in the treatment of cerebellar ataxias. It became the first FDA-approved therapy for a primary neurodegenerative ataxia in 2023, specifically indicated for FA in patients aged 16 years and older. The approval was based on the Phase 2/3 MOXIe trial. Off-Label and completed Phase II/III studies

While no other agents are formally approved for PANCA, several medications have demonstrated symptomatic or functional benefit in specific subtypes or clinical contexts (Table 3). In our practice, we typically recommend a therapeutic trial of at least 3 months to assess response; if no meaningful improvement is observed, a gradual discontinuation is advised.

Acetazolamide and 4-aminopyridine are established therapies for episodic ataxia and SCA6, and 4-AP has also shown benefit for downbeat nystagmus and oscillopsia in SCA27B [126,154]. In practice, many patients with any forms of nystagmus or oscillopsia symptoms report some benefit to 4-aminopyridine or its long-acting formulation dalfampridine.

Riluzole has demonstrated modest improvement in motor scores in hereditary ataxias in randomized trials and is recommended with level B evidence. In practice, while we have tried riluzole on many of our patients, only a few have remained on the medication due to a small number of patients actually perceiving symptomatic benefit.

Troriluzole did not meet primary endpoints in phase 3 trials but showed functional gains in SCA3 subgroups and slowed progression in long-term real-world comparison studies; it is currently under regulatory review [89] since it was not approved by the FDA is is currently only available through an Expanded Access Program at select sites.. Should it be approved, this would become on-label use likely for disease modification.

Baclofen may be considered in selected cases for spontaneous nystagmus and spasticity [[155], [156], [157]]. In practice, many patients report benefits with this medication but its success is sometimes limited by the fatigue side effect. Given its GABA activation, baclofen may also be helpful for the cough in individuals with CANVAS, though experts generally found other medications more effective (email communications with LSR).

Chlorzoxazone can also be used to help with spasticity. There is some basic science data that determined a combination of low-dose baclofen and chlorzoxazone will stabilize the purkinje cell neurons [158], so it may be reasonable to have patients on both medications though this approach may be limited by fatigue-related side effects.

Levodopa can be trialed in patients with overlapping parkinsonian features, particularly in SCA2 or MSA-C [159,160]. We have seen patients with other SCAs benefit significantly from levodopa use for spasticity while others report modest to no benefit. While its effect is often limited, for some patients it can make a significant difference in their symptoms and quality of life.

Gabapentin and pregabalin may be beneficial for the cough in individuals with CANVAS (email communications with LSR).

Rehabilitation: The Therapeutic Backbone

Rehabilitative care remains a foundational pillar of treatment. Physical therapy plays a key role in improving balance, gait, and reducing fall risk. Occupational therapy enhances independence in activities of daily living by addressing functional limitations. Speech and language therapy is essential for managing dysarthria, dysphagia, and cognitive-communication impairments [161]. In addition, regular structured exercise has been shown to support motor function, alleviate fatigue, and promote overall mental health [22].

Controlled clinical trials provide stronger evidence for rehabilitation efficacy in ataxias. Even limited periods as four weeks of intensive coordinative training have shown to significantly improved SARA scores, gait, and manual dexterity, with benefits partly sustained after three months [162]. Similarly, inpatient coordinative training followed by structured home-based exercises produced lasting gains in gait and daily function, with effects maintained for up to one year [163]. Together, these studies highlight that rehabilitation in cerebellar ataxia is not only supportive but can induce measurable and durable functional improvements when delivered in intensive, task-specific formats (Table 1).

While traditional management focuses on supportive therapies, the advent of non-invasive neuromodulation techniques offers a promising avenue for intervention in the cerebellar ataxias. Non-invasive brain stimulation techniques, including rTMS and tDCS, has demonstrated potential in modulating cortical and cerebellar excitability [164]. These techniques may facilitate neuroplasticity and functional recovery by inducing changes in synaptic efficacy and neuronal firing patterns within affected cerebellar networks [165].

Clinical investigations have explored the efficacy of non-invasive brain stimulation techniques in cerebellar ataxia. Manor et al. reported significant improvements in motor coordination following rTMS application to the cerebellum in SCA patients [79]. Benussi et al. have also examined the impact of TMS on cerebellar excitability and motor function in sporadic cerebellar ataxia and demonstrated that rTMS, can enhance cerebello-thalamo-cortical excitability and improve motor coordination in patients with sporadic cerebellar ataxia, with effects persisting up to several weeks [74,75]. Koch et al. showed that rTMS delivered over the cerebellum improved gait and balance in patients with several neurological diseases, further reinforcing clinical applicability [166]. A recent meta-analysis pooling eight randomized controlled trials (237 patients, mostly SCA3) confirmed that rTMS significantly improves motor outcomes, with mean reductions of −1.56 in SARA and −3.16 in ICARS compared with sham [167]. Importantly, low-frequency stimulation and iTBS showed the most consistent benefits, while high-frequency stimulation yielded more variable results (Table 4) [168].

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) has also emerged as a promising supportive therapy for cerebellar ataxias. França et al. demonstrated that cerebello-spinal anodal tDCS combined with intensive physiotherapy led to significantly greater improvements in SARA and ICARS scores compared to physiotherapy alone [80]. Moreover, additional trials involving mixed cohorts of hereditary and sporadic ataxias have reported sustained improvements in gait speed and upper-limb dexterity following a two-week regimen of daily cerebellar tDCS [25]. These clinical benefits are further supported by mechanistic insights from systematic reviews, which suggest that tDCS is safe, well-tolerated, and may enhance Purkinje cell excitability as well as cerebello-thalamo-cortical connectivity [169]. Although initial reports suggested possible benefits, the study by Maas et al. found no significant group-level improvement in individuals with SCA3 treated with tDCS administered five times per week over two weeks. Nevertheless, the authors noted potential gains in speech and other clinical features in a subset of patients, highlighting the need for further investigation in larger and more targeted cohorts [170]. Importantly, these RCTs did not couple the tDCS with intensive physical therapy and that may be a key component of the efficacy of tDCS.

More recently, DBS has also been explored in selected cases of cerebellar ataxia, particularly for refractory tremor or dystonia [171]. Although more invasive and less commonly applied than non-invasive techniques, isolated reports have demonstrated symptom-specific benefits. In patients with SCA2 and SCA17, for instance, DBS targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus or globus pallidus internus led to reductions in tremor and dystonia, respectively, though core ataxic features typically remained unchanged, these findings suggest a potential role for DBS as an adjunctive strategy in carefully selected phenotypes [172,173]. A recent review of 23 genetically confirmed SCA patients undergoing DBS highlighted that dystonia and tremor were the most frequent indications, with variable improvement and only rare benefit for core ataxia symptoms [174]. Research is ongoing into a possibly larger role for DBS in the SCAs, with investigators looking into different potential DBS targets and utilization of the newer adaptive DBS programming options. While the current findings are promising, further research is warranted to optimize TMS,tDCS, and DBS protocols, identify predictive biomarkers of treatment response, and establish the long-term efficacy of these techniques in diverse PANCA. Future studies should focus on randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and rigorous methodological designs. Additionally, investigations into the synergistic effects of combining TMS and tDCS with other therapeutic modalities, such as robotic rehabilitation or pharmacological interventions, may offer novel strategies for enhancing functional outcomes in patients with cerebellar ataxias.

The landscape of pharmacological treatment for primary ataxias is rapidly evolving, driven by a deeper understanding of their genetic and molecular underpinnings. Current research is focused on three key domains: gene therapy, neuroprotective strategies, and symptomatic pharmacologic interventions.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have also emerged as a potential therapeutic for individuals with PANCA. There have been at least 3 open label, non-RCT type studies that demonstrated overall safety or MSCs in this population but no improvement in clinical endpoints [175]. More recently, two Phase II multicenter RCT in SCA 3 noted possible disease stabilization in a subgroup of patients with more severe disease after 1 year following repeated IV administration of MSCs compared to placebo [176]. The potential efficacy of MSCs is believed to be based on decreased inflammation through modulation of the microglial and astrocyte activation and increases in neurotrophic factors in the cerebellum [177] as well as possible strengthening of the GABAergic pathways [178]. Functional improvement then relies on these factors specifically targeting the cerebellum and Purkinje cell neurons.

Looking ahead, genetic therapies are emerging as one of the most transformative strategies for disease modification in inherited ataxias. Among these, ASOs have reached the most advanced stage of development. In SCA1 and SCA2, early-phase clinical trials have already demonstrated target engagement and safety in humans, marking a critical milestone in silencing mutant transcripts that drive polyglutamine toxicity [93,179]. Similarly, in SCA3, both ASOs and RNA interference techniques have shown robust efficacy in preclinical models, reducing ATXN3 aggregation and ameliorating motor dysfunction in vivo [31].

Complementary to transcript suppression, AAV-mediated gene therapies are under active development. These approaches aim not only to deliver therapeutic genes to compensate for loss-of-function mutations—as in recessive ataxias such asFA, but also to silence toxic gene products or deliver neuroprotective molecules in dominant SCAs. Several AAV vectors have been optimized to target cerebellar Purkinje cells or modulate transcriptional regulators, offering localized and sustained therapeutic effects [180]. The preclinical success of AAV-based strategies in multiple ataxia models has paved the way for translational efforts, including the first human trials in Friedreich’s ataxia [110].

More recently, genome-editing technologies such as CRISPR–Cas9 and base editing have garnered attention as powerful tools for allele-specific correction [181]. Although still in preclinical phases, these platforms have demonstrated proof-of-concept in several ataxia models. In SCA1, CRISPR-mediated disruption of ATXN1 reduced pathogenic transcript levels and improved coordination in mice [182]. In SCA3, gene excision and alternative editing methods—such as polyadenylation site modulation—have effectively suppressed ATXN3 aggregation and rescued neuronal phenotypes in vitro [183]. Likewise, in FA, ex vivo CRISPR-mediated excision of expanded GAA repeats restored frataxin expression and mitochondrial function in hematopoietic stem cells [184]. These findings support the feasibility of precision gene editing as a future therapeutic strategy for inherited ataxias.

To summarize, the main gene-based approaches include: (1) AAV-mediated gene replacement for recessive forms, (2) transcript silencing via ASOs or RNA interference to reduce toxic protein expression in dominant ataxias, (3) genome editing techniques like CRISPR-Cas9 for allele-specific correction, and (4) splicing modulation to restore functional protein output. Emerging combinatorial approaches aim to integrate these modalities to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, these strategies represent the core of modern genetic therapeutics, offering the potential for durable disease modification [185]. These developments mark a paradigm shift—from exclusively symptomatic treatment toward genotype-targeted interventions, and provide renewed hope for disease-modifying therapies in inherited ataxias. As biomarker discovery and delivery technologies continue to advance, genetic therapy is increasingly positioned as a cornerstone of future treatment for PANCA.

PANCA comprise a heterogeneous group of neurodegenerative disorders, yet they share common management principles. Physical therapy and multidisciplinary rehabilitation remain foundational and consistently beneficial. In recent years, non-invasive brain stimulation and select pharmacological agents, such as omaveloxolone for FA, have expanded the therapeutic landscape. Emerging gene-based strategies, including AAV-mediated gene replacement, antisense oligonucleotides, genome editing, and splicing modulation, offer growing promise for disease modification. As our understanding of genetic mechanisms improves, these approaches are likely to shape the future of personalized therapy for inherited ataxias.
