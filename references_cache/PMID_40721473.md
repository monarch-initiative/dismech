Laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) is a malignant tumor with limited
treatment options and poor prognosis in advanced stages. Arsenic trioxide (ATO),
a drug well-known for treating acute promyelocytic leukemia, has shown potential
antitumor effects in several solid tumors. This study aimed to investigate the
role of ATO on LSCC proliferation and its underlying molecular mechanisms. LSCC
cell lines (TU212, TU686, and AMC-HN-8) were treated with varying concentrations
of ATO, and cell proliferation was evaluated using CCK-8, colony formation, and
EdU assays. miRNA-sequencing identified differentially expressed miRNAs after
ATO treatment, and bioinformatics tools predicted hsa-miR-573 target genes. The
interaction between hsa-miR-573 and dynactin-associated protein (DYNAP) was
validated by dual-luciferase reporter assays. Additionally, a xenograft tumor
model was established to examine the in vivo effects of ATO on tumor growth. ATO
significantly inhibited LSCC cell proliferation in a dose- and time-dependent
manner. miRNA-sequencing identified hsa-miR-573 as significantly upregulated
following ATO treatment, and functional studies demonstrated that hsa-miR-573
suppresses LSCC cell proliferation by directly targeting DYNAP. Overexpression
of DYNAP promoted LSCC cell proliferation, while DYNAP knockdown reversed this
effect. In vivo, ATO treatment suppressed tumor growth in nude mice without
significant nephrotoxicity or cardiotoxicity. Mechanistically, ATO reduced the
expression of DYNAP and inhibited the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. ATO inhibited
LSCC progression by upregulating hsa-miR-573, which directly targets DYNAP to
suppress cell proliferation and disrupt the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. These
findings supported the potential of ATO as a therapeutic agent for LSCC.

Laryngeal cancer is a prevalent malignancy that arises from the tissues of respiratory tract, presenting a 
significant public health burden worldwide 1. Despite advancements in treatment modalities such as surgery, 
radiation therapy, and chemotherapy, the prognosis for laryngeal cancer patients remains poor, with high rates 
of recurrence and metastasis 2. The 5-year survival rate for laryngeal cancer has remained largely unchanged 
for patients with advanced-stage disease 3. Therefore, there is an urgent imperative to investigate innovative 
therapeutic strategies for laryngeal cancer and unravel their underlying mechanisms to improve patient 
outcomes.
Arsenic trioxide (ATO), a naturally occurring compound with a long history in traditional medicine, has 
emerged as a potent chemotherapeutic agent in modern oncology. Initially recognized for its remarkable efficacy 
in treating acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), ATO has subsequently demonstrated promising therapeutic 
potential across a broad spectrum of malignancies, including various types of squamous cell carcinoma 4,5. The 
anticancer mechanisms elicited by ATO are multifaceted, encompassing the induction of apoptosis via reactive 
oxygen species generation, perturbation of cellular redox homeostasis, and the inhibition of angiogenesis 
through the targeting of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway 6. Additionally, ATO regulates 
gene expression via microRNAs (miRNAs), affecting key cancer-related processes 7–9. Understanding these 
mechanisms could enhance ATO’s therapeutic applications across various cancers.
MiRNAs are short, non-coding RNA molecules that play pivotal roles in the post-transcriptional regulation 
of gene expression 10. miRNAs can direct their degradation or repress their translation, thereby fine-tuning a 
wide array of cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis by regulation of downstream 
target genes 11,12. Accumulating evidence suggested that miRNAs can serve as both therapeutic targets and 
biomarkers in cancer treatment13,14. Among the miRNAs implicated in oncogenesis, miR-573 has been found to 
suppress tumorigenesis in several solid tumors15–17. Its influence on critical cancer pathways, such as cell cycle 
regulation and apoptosis, underscores its importance in cancer biology. However, the potential role of miR-573 
in mediating ATO’s anticancer effects remains largely unexplored.
In this study, we aimed to explore the therapeutic potential of ATO in laryngeal cancer and elucidate the 
underlying molecular mechanisms. Our results indicated a significant inhibition of laryngeal cancer cell 
proliferation by ATO. Dysregulation of Dynactin-associated protein (DYNAP), a crucial regulator of vesicular 
trafficking that plays essential roles in cellular processes including endocytosis and signal transduction 18, may 
disrupt cellular homeostasis, potentially contributing to tumor development and progression. Through a series 
of in vitro and in vivo experiments, we revealed that ATO-induced upregulation of hsa-miR-573 and subsequent 
downregulation of DYNAP are pivotal for its anti-proliferative effects on laryngeal cancer cells. Collectively, 
our study provides novel insights into ATO’s anti-cancer effects and suggests the hsa-miR-573/DYNAP axis as a 
promising therapeutic target for laryngeal cancer.
Methods
Cell culture
Human laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma cell lines TU212, TU686, and AMC-HN-8 were obtained from 
Hunan Fenghui Biological Co. and Saihongrui Co. All cell lines were authenticated by STR (short tandem repeat) 
profiling and stored at the Extramural Center Laboratory of the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical College, 
Jining, China. TU212 cells were cultured in DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium) supplemented with 
10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), while TU686 and AMC-HN-8 cells 
were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium, also supplemented with 10% FBS. All cultures were incubated at 37 °C 
in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO. Cells were used for subsequent experiments once they reached 
the logarithmic growth phase. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of 
Jining Medical University(2023-10-B002).
Animals
Six female BALB/C nude mice (4–5 weeks old, 18–20 g) were obtained from Slac Experimental Animal Company, 
Shanghai, China. Animals were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions (24 °C, 55% humidity, 12-h light 
cycle) with ad libitum access to standard chow and water.AMC - HN − 8 cells were subcutaneously inoculated 
into BALB/C nude mice, and tumor growth was allowed for 5 weeks. Subsequently, two groups were set up in 
the experiment: In the control group (Ctrl group), normal saline was intraperitoneally injected daily; in the 
experimental group (ATO group), arsenic trioxide was intraperitoneally injected daily with an initial dosage of 
0.8 mg/kg. After continuous administration for 5 days, the dosage was increased to 1 mg/kg for 15days. After 20 
days, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and the subcutaneous transplanted tumors, as well as heart 
and kidney tissues of the mice, were dissected and collected.All procedures have been approved by the Ethics 
Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical University (Approval No.: 2023-10-B002) and comply with 
the requirements of the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.
miRNA-seq sequencing
To investigate the effect of ATO on the miRNA expression profile in laryngeal cancer cells, the TU212 cell line 
was selected as the experimental model. The experiment was divided into a control group (Ctrl) and an ATO 
treatment group, with three biological replicates per group. Initially, 2 mL of TU212 cell suspension was evenly 
seeded into each well of a 6-well plate, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ until they reached 
approximately 70% confluence. At this point, the ATO treatment group was treated with complete medium 
containing 2 µM ATO for 24 h. After the treatment, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were trypsinized 
and resuspended in 1 mL of culture medium. The cell suspension was gently mixed and transferred to a 1.5 mL 
EP tube, followed by slow-speed centrifugation to pellet the cells. The collected cells were stored on dry ice and

sent to Seqhealth (Wuhan, China) for miRNA library preparation, high-throughput sequencing, and subsequent 
miRNA-seq data analysis.
Mimic, plasmid, and SiRNA transfection
The hsa-mir-573 mimics, DYNAP plasmid, and DYNAP si-RNA were synthesized by GenePharma Co., Ltd. 
(Shanghai, China). For cell culture and grouping, laryngeal cancer cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density 
of 5 × 105 cells per well in 2 mL of complete medium. When the cell density reached approximately 70%, 
transient transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to 
the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were divided into the following groups: mimic NC (transfected with 
hsa-mir-573 mimic negative control), mimic (transfected with hsa-mir-573 mimics), OE-NC (transfected with 
DYNAP plasmid negative control), OE-DYNAP (transfected with DYNAP plasmid), Si-NC (transfected with 
DYNAP siRNA negative control), and Si-DYNAP (transfected with DYNAP siRNAs).
Transfection mixtures were prepared by adding Lipofectamine 3000 to 250 µL serum-free medium, and the 
respective nucleic acids (5 µg for mimics or 2 µg for plasmids/siRNAs) to another 250 µL serum-free medium. 
After a 5-minute incubation, the nucleic acid mixtures were combined with Lipofectamine 3000 and incubated 
for 20 min. The transfection complexes were then added dropwise to the cells in 1 mL serum-free medium. 
Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 6 h, after which the medium was replaced with 2.5 mL complete 
medium and cultured for an additional 36–48 h before subsequent analyses.
Cell proliferation assay
The cytotoxicity and proliferation of laryngeal cancer cells were assessed using the CCK-8 assay. For the 
cytotoxicity assay, cell suspensions were prepared with 6000 cells/well for TU212, 4000 cells/well for TU686, 
and 5000 cells/well for AMC-HN-8. Each suspension (200 µL) was inoculated into a 96-well plate and incubated 
at 37 °C with 5% CO 2 for 24 h to achieve approximately 70% confluence. The medium was then replaced with 
fresh complete medium containing different concentrations of ATO (Heilongjiang Harbin Medical University 
Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), with each concentration tested in quintuplicate. After 48 h of treatment, fresh medium 
mixed with CCK-8 reagent (GLPBIO) at a ratio of 10:1 was added (110 µL per well). Blank wells were included 
for calibration.
For the CCK-8 cell proliferation assay, a similar approach was employed. Cell suspensions were prepared in 
complete medium and inoculated into the 96-well plates at a volume of 200 µL per well. The plates were incubated 
at 37 °C with 5% CO 2, with each experimental condition represented by three replicates. Optical density (OD) 
values were measured over five days to monitor changes in cell proliferation for both control and drug-treated 
groups. Additionally, for specific time points, OD values were assessed at 0, 24, and 48 h for the hsa-mir-573 
mimics and negative control groups (purchased from Gemma Genetics). The plates were incubated for 1 h in a 
light-protected environment, after which absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a UV spectrophotometer.
EDU proliferation assay
To assess cell proliferation, appropriate amounts of cell suspension were prepared for each group, with a final 
volume of 500 µL per well using complete medium. The suspensions were inoculated into 24-well plates and 
incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO 2 to allow for cell adhesion and growth. Following the incubation, 
the EdU proliferation assay was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions provided in the Cell 
Proliferation Imaging Analysis Kit (Orange Fluorescence) from Abbkine. After the EdU labeling process, images 
were captured for subsequent analysis to quantify the proliferative capacity of the cells.
The samples are observed and the images are collected using a fluorescence microscope. After obtaining the 
original images, the single-channel images of nuclear staining and Edu staining are superimposed to generate a 
merged image. On this basis, the manual counting module of the ImageJ software is used to accurately count the 
total number of cells and the number of fluorescent positive cells frame by frame in the merged image.
Each experiment has three biological replicates. The merged images obtained from the three independent 
repeated experiments are manually counted respectively, and the average value is calculated as the final result of 
the data of this group. Finally, statistical analysis is carried out through the GraphPad Prism 8 software.
Colony formation assay
Cell suspensions (2 mL per well) were prepared in complete medium and inoculated into 6-well plates. The 
plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 7 to 10 days, with the medium replaced every two days.
In the experiment to explore the effect of arsenic trioxide on the ability of cells to form colonies, the medium 
of the ATO group was replaced with complete medium containing arsenic trioxide each time the medium 
was changed, while the ctrl group had its medium replaced with complete medium without arsenic trioxide 
synchronously. In the experimental part studying the effect of transfection on the ability of cells to form colonies, 
the medium of all cells in the transfected group was replaced with complete medium during the culture process.
After 7 to 10 days, distinct colonies were visible. Each well received 1.5 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde 
(Biosharp) for 20 min. After discarding the paraformaldehyde, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Next, 
1 mL of crystal violet staining solution (KGI) was added to each well. The plates were incubated in the dark for 
20 min, then the staining solution was discarded. Plates were rinsed with deionized water for 1 min, inverted to 
air-dry, and images were taken for analysis.

Context + + score, Context + + score percentile) to select possible downstream target genes.

Immunofluorescence
A total of 2.5 × 10^5 cells per well were plated in 500 µL of complete medium in a 24-well plate. The plate was 
pre-warmed in a cell incubator, and cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ overnight until reaching 50–70% 
confluence. Following this, the medium was removed, and the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 
15 min. After three washes with PBS, the cells were permeabilized with Triton X-100 for 20 min, followed by 
another three washes with PBS. To block non-specific binding, the cells were incubated with 2% bovine serum 
albumin (BSA) for 20 min. Subsequently, the Ki-67 antibody (purchased from Affinity Company) was diluted 
with 2% BSA at a ratio of 1:400 and used to incubate the cells, which were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. washed three 
times with PBS, and then incubate the cells with Alexa Fluor 647 goat multimer + rabbit IgG (H + L) (purchased 
from Beyotime Biotechnology) diluted with 2% BSA at a ratio of 1:600 for 45 min at room temperature. After 
three additional washes with PBS, the coverslips were mounted on slides, and nuclei were stained with DAPI. 
Fluorescence microscopy was used for observation and imaging.
Dual luciferase assay

Both plasmids were constructed based on human-derived genes and were both built in the pmirGLO vector.
Using a 96-well plate for chemiluminescence, each well was inoculated with 100ul of cells, while a negative 
control group (without cells) was set up, and when the cell density reached 60%, the cells were co-transfected 
with hsa-mir-573 NC, MIMICS, and DYNAP WT, DYNAP MUT (purchased from Gemma Genetics), and the 
subsequent interventions were carried out after 48 h of transfection. Preparation of sea kidney luciferase assay 
working solution: Dual-LumiTM Sea Kidney Luciferase Assay Substrate (100×): Dual-LumiTM Sea Kidney

and sea kidney luciferase assay were performed according to the instructions of Dual-Lumi Dual Luciferase

Western blotting

Statistical analysis
ImageJ software was used for image analysis, including data quantification and area calculations. Experimental 
data were organized using Microsoft Excel. GraphPad Prism 8 was employed for statistical analysis and data

Results
Arsenic trioxide inhibits the proliferative ability of laryngeal cancer cells
To evaluate the effects of arsenic trioxide (ATO) on the proliferation of human laryngeal cancer cells, we treated 
three laryngeal cancer cell lines (TU212, TU686, and AMC-HN-8) with ATO at concentrations ranging from 
0 to 4 µmol/L for 48  h. A dose-dependent reduction in cell proliferation was observed across all three cell 
lines, with calculated IC50 values of 0.982 µmol/L for TU212, 2.500 µmol/L for TU686, and 1.767 µmol/L for 
AMC-HN-8 (Fig. 1A–C). Additionally, a dose-dependent inhibitory effect on proliferation was recorded, with 
prolonged exposure to ATO leading to progressively greater suppression of cell growth (Fig. 1D–F). Furthermore,

the inhibitory effect on cell proliferation was more pronounced with prolonged exposure to ATO (Fig.  2A-C). 
These findings were further corroborated by the EDU assay and colony formation assay, which demonstrated 
significant reductions in DNA replication activity and colony formation ability in the ATO-treated groups, 
particularly in TU686 and AMC-HN-8 cells (Fig.  2D–G). Taken together, the proliferation of TU212, TU686, 
and AMC-HN-8 laryngeal cancer cells was significantly inhibited by ATO in both a dose- and time-dependent 
manner, highlighting the potential of ATO as an effective anti-proliferative agent in laryngeal cancer cells.
Arsenic trioxide upregulates hsa-mir-573 expression in laryngeal cancer cells
To identify the differentially expressed miRNAs induced by ATO, we performed miRNA-Seq experiments on 
ATO-treated TU212 cells. A total of 1,423 previously known hsa-miRNAs and 345 newly identified novel miRNAs 
were detected in TU212 cells through alignment with the human genome reference sequence.Compared to the 
control group, arsenic trioxide (ATO) treatment induced 25 differentially expressed microRNAs (miRNAs), 
including 13 upregulated miRNAs (9 annotated human miRNAs [hsa-miRNAs] and 4 novel miRNAs) such as 
hsa-miR-573, and 12 downregulated miRNAs (9 annotated hsa-miRNAs and 3 novel miRNAs) (Fig.  3A–C). 
To further validate the sequencing results, we performed quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) experiments 
using AMC-HN-8 and TU686 cells to compare the relative expression levels of hsa-miR-573 between the control 
and ATO-treated groups. In ATO-treated AMC-HN-8 cells, the expression of hsa-miR-573 was significantly 
upregulated compared to the control group (Fig.  3D). This upregulation was further confirmed in TU686 
laryngeal cancer cells, where hsa-miR-573 expression was markedly higher in the ATO-treated group than in 
the control group (Fig. 3E).

The role of DYNAP in laryngeal cancer cell proliferation
To investigate the role of DYNAP in the proliferation of laryngeal cancer cells, we utilized a DYNAP plasmid 
to enhance DYNAP expression (Fig.  5A) and employed siRNA to knock down DYNAP expression (Fig.  5B). 
Following successful transfection, we assessed the impact of DYNAP on cell proliferation through Ki-67 
immunofluorescence, colony formation, and EdU assays.
Our results demonstrated that elevating DYNAP expression led to an increase in Ki-67 levels (Fig.  6A), 
indicating enhanced cell proliferation. Additionally, both DNA replication and colony formation abilities were 
significantly improved in laryngeal cancer cells with increased DYNAP expression (Fig. 6B and C). Conversely, 
following the knockdown of DYNAP expression, we observed a marked reduction in both DNA replication and 
colony formation capabilities (Fig. 6D and E). These findings collectively indicated that there was a significant 
effect of DYNAP on the proliferation ability of laryngeal cancer cells.
Arsenic trioxide affects laryngeal cancer progression through the hsa-mir-573/DYNAP axis
We next investigated the in vivo effects of ATO on laryngeal cancer progression. AMC-HN-8 cells were 
subcutaneously inoculated into BALB/C nude mice, and tumor formation was allowed for 5 weeks. Mice were 
then intraperitoneally injected with either saline (control group) or ATO (ATO group) for 20 days. At the end 
of the treatment, tumor growth was significantly slower in the ATO-treated group compared to the control 
group .As the medication time continues to extend, the volume changes of the transplanted tumors in mice 
show gradually significant inter-group differences(Fig. 7A). Importantly, ATO treatment did not induce notable 
nephrotoxicity or cardiotoxicity in the mice (Fig.  7B). Quantitative PCR analysis confirmed that hsa-mir-573 
expression was significantly upregulated in the ATO group (Fig. 7C), while immunoblotting revealed a marked 
reduction in DYNAP protein levels in the same group (Fig. 7D).
The PI3K/Akt pathway are crucial for cell proliferation and survival, and their inhibition can lead to reduced 
tumor growth. Given the well-known role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in tumorigenesis, we further examined the 
effect of ATO on this pathway in human LSCC cell lines. Western blot analysis demonstrated that phosphorylated 
AKT (P-AKT, Ser473) levels were significantly reduced in the ATO-treated group compared with the control 
group, while total AKT levels remained unchanged (Fig. 7E). Consistent with these results, western blot analysis 
of tumor tissues from nude mice showed decreased levels of both P-AKT and phosphorylated PI3K (P-PI3K) in 
the ATO group compared to the control group (Fig. 7F). In conclusion, our findings suggested that ATO inhibits 
laryngeal cancer progression by modulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

challenges to patient quality of life and survival outcomes. The subtle and nonspecific symptoms during the 
early stages of laryngeal cancer often result in delayed diagnoses, with the majority of patients presenting 
with advanced-stage disease 19. At these stages, the primary treatment modalities, such as extensive surgical 
resections, can result in severe impairments to vital functions, including swallowing and speech 20. Despite 
advances in modern medical care and the implementation of individualized treatment approaches through 
multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions 21,22, the prognosis for advanced-stage laryngeal cancer remains

bleak23. Additionally, the risk of lymph node metastasis in supraglottic laryngeal cancer further complicates the 
prognosis24, underscoring the urgent need for more effective and less invasive therapeutic options.
ATO, a well-known chemotherapeutic agent, has shown promising anti-cancer effects in hematological 
malignancies and some solid tumors. For example, ATO has been shown to inhibit breast cancer cell proliferation 
in a time-dependent manner, with prolonged exposure leading to more pronounced effects25. Similarly, a study 
on TC-1 cells revealed enhanced cytotoxicity of ATO with longer intervention times26. In our study, we observed 
that ATO significantly reduced the proliferative capacity of TU686 and AMC-HN-8 laryngeal cancer cells in 
a time- and dose-dependent manner. These in vitro findings were supported by in vivo experiments using a 
nude mouse xenograft model, where ATO treatment markedly suppressed tumor growth. Together, these results 
indicated the potential of ATO as an effective therapeutic agent for laryngeal cancer.
ATO has been found to inhibit the growth and development of various solid tumors through different 
mechanisms. For instance, ATO can alter the expression of the CYP1A enzyme in mouse hepatocellular 
carcinoma Hepa-1c1c7 cells27 and reactivate structurally mutated p53 to inhibit solid tumor growth in vivo 28. 
In laryngeal cancer, dysregulated miRNAs have been implicated in malignant transformation, invasion, and 
chemoresistance. Aberrant miRNA expression contributes to uncontrolled proliferation and metastasis, 
hallmarks of laryngeal cancer 29,30. Interestingly, emerging evidence suggests that ATO can modulate miRNA 
expression to exert its anti-tumor effects 7,9,31. In our study, we identified a significant alteration in the miRNA 
expression profile, particularly in hsa-miR-573, after ATO intervention in laryngeal cancer cells. Notably, hsa-
miR-573 has been identified as a tumor-suppressive miRNA in several cancers 32,33. It was found to inhibit the 
proliferation, migration, and invasion of pancreatic cancer cells 15,16. Based on these findings, we hypothesized 
that hsa-miR-573 mediates the anti-proliferative effects of ATO in laryngeal cancer. To test this, we transfected 
TU686 and AMC-HN-8 cells with hsa-miR-573 mimics and observed a significant reduction in their proliferative 
capacity, suggesting that hsa-miR-573 acts as a key mediator of ATO’s anti-tumor effects in laryngeal cancer.
To further investigate the mechanism, we identified potential downstream target genes of hsa-miR-573, 
including DYNAP , a transmembrane protein that highly expressed in various cancer cell lines 18. Previous 
study has linked DYNAP to cell proliferation and tumor progression through its activation of protein kinase B 
(AKT)18. Our experiments demonstrated that ATO treatment significantly downregulated DYNAP expression 
in laryngeal cancer cells at both the mRNA and protein levels. Correspondingly, DYNAP protein expression 
was markedly reduced in tumors from ATO-treated xenograft mice. Co-transfection with hsa-miR-573 mimics 
resulted in a significant reduction in luciferase activity, confirming that DYNAP is a direct target of hsa-miR-573. 
Functional analyses confirmed DYNAP’s role in laryngeal cancer proliferation, showing that its overexpression 
promotes cell growth, while its knockdown inhibits it. These findings establish DYNAP as a downstream effector 
of hsa-miR-573 and a vital regulator of laryngeal cancer progression.
The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is a well-documented regulator of cell survival, proliferation, and 
metabolism34,35. Aberrant activation of this pathway has been implicated in numerous cancers, including 
laryngeal cancer36,37. In our study, ATO treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and 
AKT in laryngeal cancer xenografts, indicating effective inhibition of this pathway. Given that DYNAP is closely 
associated with the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade 38, we propose that ATO exerts its anti-proliferative effects, at 
least in part, by targeting the hsa-miR-573/DYNAP axis and suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway.
Conclusions
In conclusion, our study revealed the anti-tumor mechanisms of ATO in laryngeal cancer, demonstrating that 
it inhibits cancer proliferation via the hsa-miR-573/DYNAP axis and modulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. 
These findings enhance our understanding of ATO’s action and identify hsa-miR-573 and DYNAP as potential 
therapeutic targets. Further research is needed to assess the clinical potential of these findings and develop ATO-
based combination therapies to improve patient outcomes.
