# Neuropathological Findings in a Case of IFIH1-Related Aicardi-Goutières Syndrome.
**Authors:** Gilani A, Adang LA, Vanderver A, Collins A, Kleinschmidt-DeMasters BK
**Journal:** Pediatr Dev Pathol (2019)
**DOI:** [10.1177/1093526619837797](https://doi.org/10.1177/1093526619837797)

## Content

Aicardi-Goutières syndrome (AGS) is a rare syndrome characterized by calcification, diffuse demyelination, and variable degree of brain atrophy. The syndrome is genetically heterogeneous with mutations in 7 genes, including TREX1, RNASEH2A, RNASEH2B, RNASEH2C, SAMHD1, ADAR1, and IFIH1 (interferon-induced helicase c domain-containing protein 1) associated with the syndrome, so far. These mutations lead to the overproduction of α-interferon within the central nervous system. Mutations in IFIH1 have been recently described in a subset of AGS, with only 1 previous report of neuropathological findings. We report neuropathological findings in a second case of AGS with a known mutation in IFIH1 gene. The patient is a 16-year-old adolescent boy with early-onset symptoms that progressed to profound loss of cognitive and motor functions. The patient experienced sudden cardiopulmonary arrest at the age of 16 years. At autopsy, the cause of death was determined to be pulmonary thromboembolism. Neuropathological examination revealed microcephaly (brain weight: 916 g) with relatively mild brain atrophy on gross examination. Microscopic examination revealed multifocal calcifications limited to small to medium central nervous system arteries (no evidence of calcification in other organs), involving bilateral cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, thalamus, and cerebellum. Ultrastructural examination showed Calcospherules limited to the vessel walls and the perivasulcar area without evidence of neuronal ferrugination or tubuloreticular bodies. The extent of calcifications was variable across different brain regions, resembling findings in previously reported cases and correlated with the extent of IFIH1 protein expression (data derived from Allen Brain Institute). AGS is a rare cause of brain calcifications that can closely mimic congenital and neonatal infections such as Rubella and similar infections.

The patient was a 16-year-old adolescent boy who presented with cardiopulmonary arrest following a long hitory of neurologic deficits secondary to IFIH1 (interferon-induced helicase c domain-containing protein 1)-related Aicardi–Goutiéres Syndrome (AGS). A complete autopsy was conducted.

The patient was born at term to nonconsanguineous Caucasian parents; there were no peripartum complications. Progressive hypotonia, motor weakness, and cognitive delay were noted first at approximately 10 months of age. Over the subsequent years, he experienced a slow neurologic deterioration. He was wheelchair-dependent and relied on basic gestures and sounds to communicate. Systemic symptoms included marked gastrointestinal discomfort with carbohydrate intolerance, for which the patient was placed on a high-lipid diet. His laboratory testing was consistent with a chronic hepatitis and pancreatitis. He was also affected by hypercholesterolemia and an intermittent eczematous skin rash. The patient suffered from severe growth retardation, achieving a body length and weight of 7 to 8 years by 16 years of age.

His initial magnetic resonance imaging of the brain at 10 months of age showed multiple, discrete T2 hyperintense perivascular white matter lesions throughout the cerebrum, with possible calcifications in bilateral globus pallidi, cerebral cortex, and cerebellar dentate nuclei. A subsequent computed tomography scan confirmed the presence of calcifications. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed mild pleocytosis with an increase in leukocytes with activated monocytes, macrophages, and lymphocytes. The CSF analysis was notable for elevated inflammatory markers such as tetrahydrobiopterin and neopterin. No evidence of a viral, bacterial, or parasitic infection was found by culture studies or by targeted testing. In particular, tests for cytomegalovirus, varicella, herpes simplex virus, BK virus, and JC were all negative. A muscle biopsy showed neurogenic atrophy (pathologic slides are unavailable for review). He was ultimately diagnosed at the age of 12 years with a de novo heterozygous mutation in IFIH1 [c.1009A>G; p.Arg337Gly], a known autosomal dominant pathogenic mutation associated with AGS. 1 , 2

Immediately preceding his death at 16 years, the patient began to experience projectile vomiting and a “dry” cough. Liver and pancreatic enzymes (aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyltransferase, lipase, and amylase) were mildly elevated. The patient eventually succumbed after an episode of vomiting followed by cyanosis and cessation of breathing.

General gross findings on systemic autopsy were notable for marked growth retardation, with body weight (20.6 kg) and length (116 cm, crown heel) equivalent to the average for 7 to 8 years of age. Cyanosis was noted in the periorbital, perioral regions, and bilateral nail beds. Pericardial (25 mL, clear amber fluid) and pleural effusions (clear amber fluid; right 10 mL, left 40 mL) were found, consistent with right-sided heart failure. The lungs were grossly unremarkable and within normal weight parameters for body length. Gross examination revealed mild dilatation of the right side of the heart.

General microscopic findings included the stigmata of pulmonary arterial hypertension and multifocal thrombi within medium-sized proximal and distal pulmonary arteries. 3 A large number of pulmonary arteries, of variable diameters, ranging from medium to large caliber, showed arteriolar muscularization, increased intimal thickness, and reduced lumen areal diameter due to thickening of the intimal layer. No significant abnormalities of the airways or alveoli were seen. Several arteries in the lungs and elsewhere in the body showed marked lipidization of the intimal layers with increase in lipid-laden foam cells, which were positive for the macrophage marker CD163 on immunohistochemical staining. Only rare regions showed mild lymphocytic infiltration into the vessel walls. No erosion, ulceration, or fissuring of a plaque’s surface was seen. There was no indication of advanced atherosclerotic lesions such as extracellular lipid deposits or atheroma associated calcifications. No significant changes were seen in the venous system. Microscopic examination of visceral organs showed no significant pathology. No diagnostic abnormalities were found in the liver and the pancreas, despite raised chronically elevated liver and pancreatic enzymes.

Gross neuropathological findings showed microcephaly (brain weight: 916 grams, normal expected for body size: 1351 g). The cerebral hemispheres were symmetrical with minimal narrowing of the gyri or widening of the sulci (Figure 1(A) and (B)). Coronal sections showed mild hydrocephalus ex vacuo without marked atrophy of the cortical ribbon, deep brain nuclei or white matter tracts including corpus callosum, anterior commissure or internal capsule (Figure 1(C)). Multiple discrete regions of gray-white gritty deposits, measuring 0.5 to 1 cm, were present at the cortical gray-white junction and subcortical white matter, the caudate nucleus, putamen, thalamus and cerebellum (Figure 1(D) to (G)). The vessels of the circle of Willis were unremarkable except the basilar artery, which showed 40% luminal narrowing with yellow plaque formation.

Microscopic examination of the lesions showed psammoma-body like microcalcifications involving small to medium-sized arteries (Figure 2(A) to (E)). Calcifications which were PAS-positive, were seen throughout the wall of the vessels, including the media and adventitia (Figure 2(A) to (B)), attached to the external surface of the vessels, and within the perivascular space (Figure 2(A) to (D)). Ultrastructural examination further proved concentric lamellated calcification (Figure 2(G)). Mild loss of myelin was seen in the affected areas, highlighted by Luxol Fast Blue-Periodic Acid Schiff (LFB-PAS) stain (Figure 2(B) to (D)). Mild increase in perivascular space was present with neuropil vacuolation (Figure 2(D) and (E)) and mild gliosis. Overall, only rare scattered blood vessels showed perivascular mild lymphocytic inflammation (with CD3+ T-lymphocytes) with occasional hemosiderin-laden macrophages (Figure 2(F)). No fibrinoid necrosis or significant inflammation of the vessel walls, the brain parenchyma or the leptomeninges was present. Several cortical sections show mild cortical layer-I and white matter vacuolation but neuronal density was only mildly reduced and tangential and radial cortical architecture was preserved (Figure 2(F)). The microscopic changes corresponded to the lesions appreciated on gross examination, with the most severe changes being limited to the putamen, the thalamus and the dentate nucleus of the cerebellum (Figure 1(E)). The basilar artery showed intimal thickening and fibrosis with increase in lipid laden macrophages (Figure 2(H)).

Aicardi Goutiéres syndrome (AGS) is a rare genetic auto-inflammatory disorder that results in interferon overproduction and systemic symptomatology. 4 AGS is genetically heterogeneous with causal mutations identified in 7 genes associated with the nucleic acid processing machinery, including TREX1, RNASEH2A, RNASEH2B, RNASEH2C, SAMHD1, ADAR1, and IFIH1. 5 It is not uncommon for the diagnostic odyssey to last for years, as described in this case. Mutations in IFIH1 have been more recently described in a subset of AGS, with only 1 previous report of neuropathological findings. 3 Of note, the diagnosis of AGS was initially missed in this patient because IFIH1 was not available at the time of the initial AGS genetic panel.

Although there is considerable variation in the symptoms, age of onset, and evolution of the disease, typically, the onset of the disease is early—with a majority of patients presenting within the first year of life. The subset of patients who present prenatally or soon after birth exhibit many features common to a congenital infection, including microcephaly, CSF pleocytosis, hepatitis, thrombocytopenia, and fevers. 5– 9 In addition to central nervous system dysfunction, systemic symptoms are common as well. This may include skin lesions such as chilblains, sterile pyrexia, pulmonary hypertension, auto-antibody production, and hepatitis. 3 , 5 , 7– 11

Imaging studies are equally variable, but often reveal cerebral atrophy, myelination abnormalities, a thin corpus callosum, and enlarged ventricles. 12 , 13 Calcifications are typically most prominent in the basal ganglia and white matter. 13 On microscopic examination, the calcospherites are positive for Periodic Acid Schiff (PAS), iron, and calcium and are present in the wall of the blood vessels or present in the perivascular space in close proximity to a vessel. Mineralization of neurons, especially of the cerebellar Purkinje cells, has also been documented. Myelin stains (such as Luxol fast blue) show loss of myelin. These changes are often accompanied by gliosis in the affected regions of the brain. Slight lymphocytic infiltrate in the leptomeninges can also be seen. Vascular pathology including vasculitic changes of the cerebral and leptomeningeal vessels, calcification of the walls of small blood vessels, and ruptured aneurysm has also been described. Similar to prior reports, this case demonstrated severe calcifications and demyelination but deepens our understanding of the pathologic changes founds in AGS.

Many individuals affected by AGS exhibit microcephaly, which may be present at birth or develop in the first year of life. This microcephaly, which ranges from two-thirds to one-half of the expected size, 14 is believed to be secondary to progressive cerebral damage. In contrast to the present case, previous cases have shown markedly atrophic and friable gyri with pronounced ventricular dilation, shrunken central white matter, thin corpus callosum, and atrophic basal ganglia and thalamus. The brain stem may be slender and firm and the cerebellum small, with marked cortical atrophy. Although significantly microencephalic, brain atrophy was less pronounced in this case.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the second reported case of AGS resulting from a mutation in IFIH1 gene. 15 The overall neuropathology is similar to the previously reported case with IFIH1 (15) mutation and AGS with other mutations. 4 , 14 The neuropathologic findings were however much subtler than the previously reported case 15 underscoring the inherent heterogeneity found within AGS. While there was overall brain atrophy, it was milder than that seen in the case reported by Marguet et al. 15 Neuronal depletion and loss of lamination as reported in the first reported case of INIH1 mutation, 15 were not seen in the present case. Widely varying degrees of severity in clinical symptoms and pathologic findings may hint at genetic heterogeneity or the modulating effect of environmental factors. Other novel findings from this case include extensive histologic evidence of pulmonary hypertension and extensive lipid accumulation in the blood vessels. The origin of the early atherosclerotic-like changes (Figure 2(H)) in this patient is unclear, but it is likely multifactorial with high-lipid diet playing a part. The patient also showed evidence of pulmonary embolism affecting wide areas of pulmonary circulation, the exact cause of which is uncertain. 3 Some of the well-known factors of thromboembolism such as vascular damage and decreased mobility were seen in this patient. Thrombocytosis has previously been reported in patients of AGS, but it was not observed in this patient.

In summary, AGS is a heterogeneous disorder that results in severe neurologic dysfunction. AGS should be considered in children presenting with motor symptoms and developmental delay who show brain calcifications and CSF pleocytosis in the absence of any evidence of infection.
