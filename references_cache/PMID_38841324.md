Acyl-CoA binding domain containing 5 (ACBD5) is a peroxisomal protein which is involved in the uptake and transfer of very-long-chain fatty acyl-CoA (VLCFA-CoA) in peroxisomes via its cytosolic N-terminal acyl-CoA-binding domain and is highly specific for C26-CoA. In peroxisomes, C26-CoA is then presented to ABCD1 and β-oxidized by the sequential action of peroxisomal β-oxidation enzymes. Depending on its crucial role in the transfer process of VLCFA-CoA, deficiency of ACBD5 results in defective peroxisomal β-oxidation of very-long-chain fatty acid (VLCFAs) [1–3].

ACBD5 deficiency is a peroxisomal disorder that has only recently been described and was associated with retinal dystrophy and neurologic deterioration when it was first described [2–4]. The clinical spectrum is now mainly characterized by ophthalmologic signs, including abnormal pupillary light reflex, progressive visual impairment, nystagmus associated with neurodevelopmental delay, pyramidal system findings, intentional tremor, and dysarthria [5, 6]. To date, only 7 patients with ACBD5 deficiency have been reported. The clinical, biochemical, and genetic spectrum of the disease remains unclear, and new patients are needed to expand knowledge. Here, we report a 6-year-old male patient with retinal dystrophy, neuromotor regression, and white matter involvement on brain magnetic resonance imaging, whose first symptoms appeared at 7 months of age and who was finally diagnosed with ACBD5 deficiency with a novel homozygous intronic splice site variant (c.936+2T > G) in ACBD5.

A 6-year-old male patient was admitted to our outpatient clinic with complaints of neuromotor regression and visual disturbances. He was the second child of consanguineous parents, and his prenatal, natal, and postnatal history was uneventful. It was learned that he could sit unassisted at 7 months of age but never acquired the ability to walk. He also had delayed expressive language development as his holophrastic stage did not appear until 4 years of age. At 2 months of age, he developed horizontal nystagmus. At age 4 years, his neuromotor skills began to deteriorate and he lost the ability to sit. His visual impairment was detected at age 5 years.

Neurologic examination at the time of admission revealed spastic paraparesis characterized by the axial hypotonic posture. He had increased tendon reflexes and a positive Babinski sign. He had head control and could sit with support. On examination, he was found to have a generalized tremor that was particularly pronounced in the arms and hands. He had horizontal nystagmus accompanied by horizontal gaze palsy. Audiometric testing revealed normal hearing.

On ophthalmologic examination, visual acuity could not be assessed because of low compliance with the Snellen chart. Biomicroscopic examination was normal. Fundus examination revealed atrophy of the fovea and peripheral retina in both eyes. Retinal atrophy consistent with retinal dystrophy is observed in the fovea of both eyes. Retinal thinning, loss of the subfoveal outer retinal layer, and atrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium are observed in both eyes, suggesting retinal dystrophy. The visual field test could not be evaluated because of the patient’s poor cooperation. Hematologic and biochemical examinations, including complete blood count, renal functions, liver functions and transaminases, lipid profile, plasma ammonia and lactate levels, were normal. Biotinidase activity, plasma acyl-carnitine profile, urinary organic acid analysis, and plasma quantitative amino acid analysis were normal. Plasma VLCFA analysis which is performed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry revealed a moderately elevated C26:0/C22:0 ratio, but plasma C24:0 and C26:0 concentrations were normal [7]. Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid neurotransmitter metabolites were in normal range. His brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed posterior white matter involvement, bilateral signal alterations in the internal capsule, and atrophy of the cerebellar hemispheres and vermis.

The patient was first evaluated for the mitochondrial diseases, and his mitochondrial DNA sequencing analysis revealed no pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant. Chromosomal analysis of the patient was normal (46,XY). For clinical exome sequencing, genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) with a QIACube (QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and concentration of DNA were examined with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). In the present study, clinical exome sequencing was performed using Twist Customize CES Probe (Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco) which screens 4,899 genes known to cause hereditary disease. Sequencing was done using the DNBSEQ-2000RS (MGI, Chine) sequencer. Genemaster (Istanbul, Turkey) (https://egenemaster.com/) bioinformatic analysis tool was used for filtering and interpretations of variants. Clinical exome sequencing results displayed a novel homozygous intronic splice site variant (c.936+2T>G) in the ACBD5 (NM_145698.5) gene (Fig. 2a). To the best of our knowledge, this variant has not been detected previously and is defined as likely pathogenic according to ACMG criteria (PVS1, PM2) in in silico databases [8]. As a result of segregation analysis, parents were found to be heterozygous for this variant (Fig. 2b, c).

Here, we reported a patient with ACBD5 deficiency who was diagnosed after a long and complicated diagnostic process that began at 2 months of age when he first presented with horizontal nystagmus. Progressive neuromotor deterioration, nystagmus, and visual impairment dominated the clinical phenotype. Detection of VLCFAs within normal ranges with a slightly elevated C26:0/C22:0 ratio proved to be a pitfall in the diagnosis of this newly identified peroxisomal disorder. The patient was found to have a novel homozygous intronic splice site variant (c.936+2T>G) in ACBD5. Because ACBD5 deficiency is an extremely rare metabolic disorder with only 7 patients reported to date, we emphasize here that knowledge of novel phenotypic and genotypic features contributes to the medical literature and may prevent diagnostic delays.

Acyl-CoA binding domain-containing proteins are a large family specified by a particular acyl-CoA binding design and involved in lipid degradation and signaling pathways via regulation of activated fatty acid concentrations [9, 10]. The main biochemical consequence of ACBD5 deficiency is increased concentrations of VLCFAs in body fluids and tissues [1, 2].

Because retinal dystrophy occurs in many inherited metabolic diseases, it is also a cardinal finding in our patient with a newly defined ACBD5 variant. Retinal dystrophy has been shown to be an ocular finding in other patients described in the literature. Ocular findings such as nystagmus, progressive decrease in visual acuity, retinal dystrophy, optic nerve pallor, attenuation of retinal vessels, and diffuse granularity of retinal pigment epithelium have been reported in patients with ACBD5 deficiency [6]. Our patient had nystagmus, decreased vision, and atrophy in the foveal and peripheral retina. In the macular OCT imaging of the patient, there were marked retinal thinning, subfoveal outer retinal layer loss, and retinal pigment epithelial atrophy in both eyes, consistent with macular dystrophy. To the best of our knowledge, for the first time in the literature, we demonstrated macular dystrophy with OCT imaging in a patient with ACBD5 deficiency.

To date, only 7 patients of ACBD5 deficiency have been reported in the literature. The phenotypic features of the patients were dominated by neuromotor dysfunction and progressive visual impairment. Clinical findings, physical examination, and laboratory and imaging tests do not provide specific data for a definitive diagnosis. The gold standard for diagnosis is molecular analysis of the ACBD5 gene [2–6]. In conclusion, ACBD5 deficiency should be included in differential diagnosis of patients with nystagmus, retinal dystrophy, and neuromotor regression. Normal plasma VLCFAs levels should not exclude the diagnosis, and isolated C26:0/C22:0 elevation may be the only pathologic finding. Our patient’s intronic splice site variant (ACBD5; c.936+2T>G) has not been previously reported. Reports of the new patients are needed to expand the knowledge about the clinical, biochemical, and genetic spectrum of the disease.
