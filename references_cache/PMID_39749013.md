S-Sulfocysteine (SSC) is a metabolite derived from the metabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids. It has been implicated in neurotoxicity observed in children with sulfite oxidase deficiency. The aim of our study was to confirm the neurotoxic effects of SSC using a mouse hippocampal cell line (HT-22) and to investigate the role of apoptosis in these effects, especially in terms of caspase-3 activation and genotoxicity. Based on the viability graph obtained following increasing concentrations of SSC, we determined the LC50 dose of SSC to be 125 µM by probit analysis. The cytotoxic effects of SSC were not reversed by glutamate receptor blocker administration. However, SSC treatment did not induce caspase-3 activation or induce DNA damage. Our results showed that SSC has a cytotoxic effect on neurons like glutamate, but glutamate receptor blockers reversed glutamate-induced toxicity, while these blockers did not protect neurons from SSC toxicity. The absence of caspase-3 activation and DNA fragmentation, which are indicative of apoptosis, in SSC-induced cell death suggests that alternative cell death pathways, such as necrosis and oxytosis may be implicated. Further research is necessary to fully elucidate SSC-induced cell death. Sulfite, a hazardous molecule with the potential to interact with various cellular structures and induce cellular toxicity, is produced in large quantities during the metabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids in the human body (Hayatsu and Miller1972; Meng and Zhang1999; Akdogan et al.2011). Despite its origin, sulfite, whether produced internally or consumed externally, requires detoxification in mammalian tissues because of its hazardous properties. To achieve this goal, mammalian tissues containing sulfite oxidase (SOX, EC. 1.8.3.1), an enzyme that contains molybdenum and is located within the intramembranous region of mitochondria (Cohen and Fridovich1971), plays a crucial role. This enzyme facilitates the conversion of sulfite to sulfate through a two-electron oxidation process, thereby safeguarding cells from the harmful effects of sulfite (Mudd et al.1967). The significance of detoxification is evident in the context of sulfite oxidase deficiency, a rare genetic disorder that is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. This uncommon condition is characterized by cerebral damage, including seizures, spastic quadriplegia, and cerebral atrophy, cognitive impairment, dislocation of ophthalmic lenses, and visual impairment (Mudd et al.1967; Hoffmann et al.2007). Infants diagnosed with SOX deficiency often die within the first two years of their life because of the absence of appropriate therapy for the condition (Johnson2003). Consequently, inefficiency of the enzyme results in increased levels of sulfite in body fluids. Moreover, individuals affected by this condition exhibit elevated concentrations of sulfur-containing amino acid metabolites, including taurine, thiosulfate, and S-sulfocysteine (SSC), in their body fluids such as plasma and urine (Olney et al.1975).

SSC is a highly potent NMDA receptor agonist formed by an unidentified reaction between inorganic sulfite and cystine. Owing to its structural similarity to glutamate, it has been hypothesized that SSC is the primary cause of neurotoxicity in sulfite oxidase deficiency (Olney et al.1975; Belaidi et al.2012). However, the toxic mechanism of SSCs in sulfite oxidase deficiency is not well understood, and the role of this metabolite in the neurotoxic effects observed under these conditions is a significant area of research. Kumar et al. conducted the most comprehensive study of this metabolite and its neurotoxicity. Kumar demonstrated that SSC increases excitotoxicity through NMDA-R-mediated calcium signaling and calpain activation. In addition, in a mouse model of sulfite oxidase deficiency induced by tungsten administration, SSC accumulated primarily in the brain tissue, and substantial neuronal cell death was detected in the cortical and hippocampal regions of the brains of these animals (Kumar et al.2017). In addition, they found that SSC/NMDA-R/calcium signaling resulted in calpain-dependent cleavage of the postsynaptic scaffolding protein gephyrin, causing destabilization of GABAergic synapses (Belaidi et al.2012).

Based on our current understanding, the type of cell death caused by the toxic effects of SSC has not yet been examined in academic literature. In the present study, we aimed to confirm the cytotoxic effects of SSC and determine whether apoptosis is involved in the resulting cytotoxicity. Apoptosis is characterized by chromatin condensation, caspase activation, and DNA fragmentation. If SSC induces apoptosis in these cells, it is necessary to observe the genotoxicity effects of this process. To determine the presence of genotoxicity processes in the cytotoxicity of SSC, comet analysis, which is the gold standard for genotoxicity measurement, was performed on HT-22 cells exposed to SSC.

All experiments were performed using HT-22 mouse hippocampal cells generously donated by the Medical Biochemistry Department of Marmara University. These cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (GIBCO, USA) supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, penicillin (20 units/mL), streptomycin (20 g/mL), and 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. For all experiments, cells were differentiated for 24 h in Neurobasal Medium.

In a 96-well plate, HT-22 cells were seeded at 5000 cells/well density and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2 to allow adherence. The cultures were treated with either the vehicle control or SSC at 5 to 300 µM concentrations. The LC50 dose of SSC was determined using the WST-1 assay and calculated to be 125 µM using probit analysis. This dose was subsequently used in further experiments.

Given the structural similarity between SSC and glutamate, we investigated whether blocking ionotropic and metabotropic glutamate receptors could prevent SSC-induced cytotoxicity. For this purpose, we used memantine (at doses ranging from 0,1 µM to 20 µM) to inhibit ionotropic glutamate receptors andLY341495(at doses ranging from 1 µM to 10 µM) to inhibit metabotropic glutamate receptors. We used the most effective doses of both inhibitors reported in the scientific literature (20 µM for memantine and 10 µM forLY341495) to assess potential synergistic effects in mitigating SSC-induced cytotoxicity. For this purpose The cells were categorized into five groups: (1) C group: control group (no treatment); (2) SSC group: treatment with 125 µM SSC; (3) SSC + M group: treatment with 125 µM SSC and increasing doses of memantine from 0.1–20 µM; (4) SSC + LY group: treatment with 125 µM SSC and increasing doses ofLY341495from 1 µM to 10 µM; and (5) SSC + M + LY group: treatment with 125 µM SSC, 20 µM memantine, and 10 µMLY341495. All agents mentioned above were administered to the medium 24 h after cell seeding. Subsequent analyses were conducted 24 h after the treatment.

Cell viability was determined using a WST-1 Cell Proliferation Kit (Cayman, USA). To assess cell viability, HT-22 cells (5,000 cells/well) were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h, followed by treatment with the specified concentrations. After 24 h of exposure, cell viability was evaluated by adding 10 µl of cell proliferation WST-1 reagent (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Solna, Sweden) to each well, according to the kit's instructions. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan ™ GO microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The plates were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 1 h and the absorbance was measured again at 450 nm. All graphs represent the results of at least three separate experiments, and the absorbance values following treatment were compared with the PBS control values.

Caspase-3 enzyme activity was determined using a colorimetric assay from Biovision following the manufacturer's instructions (Caspase-3/CPP32, Biovision). After treating the cells with SSC and Glutamate receptor blockers, Memantine andLY341495, for 24 h, the cells were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min. The cell lysate was obtained by adding 50 µL of cold lysis buffer to the pelleted cells, which were then incubated on ice for 10 min and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 1 min. Subsequently, the protein levels of the supernatants were measured using BCA, and the diluted lysate was made up to 100 µg of protein in 50 µL of Cell Lysis Buffer. To ascertain caspase-3 activity, 50 µL of the sample was placed in each well, followed by 50 µL of Reaction Buffer containing 10 mM DTT and 5 µL of 4 mM DEVD-pNA substrate (200 µM final concentration). The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, after which the absorbance of the samples was measured at 405 nm using a microtiter plate reader. The fold increase in caspase-3 activity was determined by comparing the pNA absorbance of the apoptotic sample with that of the control.

DNA damage and any genotoxicity of SSC were determined using Comet Assay IV Version 4.3.2 for Basler FireWire in HT-22 cells. DNA damage was evaluated based on the percentage of DNA in the tail of the comet (% tDNA) and the tail intensity. HT-22 cells were then incubated with these agents for 24 h. Then the cell mediums were removed, and cells were washed in 0.1 M PBS for three times, separated, and then suspended in 0.1 M PBS (2 × 103 cells in 75 µl PBS) with low melting agarose (LMA). The cells in all groups were divided into three layers. The first layer contained 1% normal melting agarose. The cells were suspended in the second layer and 1% LMA was added to the solidified first layer. Subsequently, a third layer, containing 1% LMA, was added. All the stages were performed under cold conditions. After solidification, all slides were kept in the lysing solution for 1 h at 4 °C. Next, the slides were placed in an electrophoresis buffer for 20 min. The slides were electrophoresed at 25 V (approximately 300 mA). 0.74 V/cm) for 30 min at 4 °C. The samples were washed three times with neutralization buffer for 5 min, and then the slides were immersed in methanol for 5 min at -20˚C. To prevent exogenous DNA damage, these processes were performed in dark medium. Slides were then placed in a smooth area for drying. The slides were saturated with ethidium bromide (60 µL), and 590 nm emission and 510–560 nm excitation filters were monitored using a Nikon fluorescence microscope. Images of 50 randomly chosen comets on each slide were captured for sample at × 20 magnification.

The total glutathione (GSSG + GSH) levels in HT-22 cells were measured using a commercial kit (CS0260; Sigma-Aldrich). Briefly, according to the kit instructions, the samples obtained from the groups were first deproteinized with a 5% 5-sulfosalicylic acid solution. The glutathione content of the sample was then determined using a kinetic assay, in which the amount of catalytic glutathione caused a continuous reduction of 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic) acid (DTNB) to TNB. Oxidized glutathione is recycled by glutathione reductase and NADPH. The TNB product was analyzed colorimetrically at 412 nm. To calculate the results, the values of the glutathione standards were used to plot the standard curve and to calculate ΔA412/min equivalent to 1 mol of reduced glutathione per well.

Data were analyzed using SPSS 24.0. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard error. One-Way Analysis of Variance was used to compare independent group differences. If the difference between groups was statistically significant, the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test was used as a Post Hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

SSC was administered to cells at a dose range of 5–300 µM, and the test was carried out at the end of 24 h. While no significant change was noted at doses of 5, 10 and 20 µM compared to the control group, a statistically significant decrease in vitality was observed at doses of 100, 200, and 300 µM compared to the control group. Catabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids leads to the formation of a metabolite known as SSC, which has harmful properties (Curtis and Watkins1960; Pullan et al.1987). Although the concentration of SSC in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is typically low in healthy individuals (< 1 µmol/L), studies have shown that children with sulfite oxidase deficiency exhibit significantly higher levels of SSC (19 micromol/L) (Jones et al.2006). In pediatric patients with SOX deficiency, it has been observed that there is a notable increase in sulfite levels in both urine and blood, which is attributed to the impaired enzymatic conversion of sulfite to sulfate (VE et al.1977; Pramio et al.2023). The neurological impairments observed in children with this deficiency have been attributed to sulfite, which is widely recognized for its neurotoxic effects (Mudd et al.1967; Öztürk et al.2006). However, recent research suggests that the neurotoxicity observed in these individuals may be primarily caused by SSC formation through the interaction between sulfite and cystine (Olney et al.1975; Kumar et al.2017; Plate et al.2019). For example, to examine the toxicity of sulfite and SSC, cortical neurons were cultured in cystine-containing (normal neuronal culture media) or cystine-free artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF). The results revealed that sulfite toxicity decreased by 58% in neurons cultured in ACSF compared to those cultured in cystine-containing normal neuronal culture media (Kumar et al.2017). This suggests that sulfite is cytotoxic, as previously reported (Zhang et al.2004; Küçükatay et al.2006). However, when it reacts with cystine to produce SSC, it develops more aggressive NMDA-R-dependent neurotoxicity. Therefore, SSC may mediate the neurotoxic effects of sulfite.

Although a limited number of studies have demonstrated the cytotoxic effect of SSC and the possible mechanisms underlying this effect (Abbas et al.2008; Kumar et al.2017; Plate et al.2019), the precise mechanism by which SSC induces cell death remains unknown. In this study, we aimed to confirm the cytotoxic effects of SSC and investigate whether caspase-3 activation and genotoxicity-mediated apoptosis mechanisms are implicated in the possible cytotoxicity of SSC observed in the HT-22 cell line. To confirm the cytotoxicity of SSC, we used HT-22 cells, an immortalized mouse hippocampal cell line commonly used in glutamate toxicity research. Our results, similar to those reported in the literature (Kumar et al.2017), showed that SSC decreased cell viability in a dose-dependent manner. Using probit analysis of the concentration-viability graph, we determined 125 µM to be the optimal dose for subsequent investigations.

To assess the potential efficacy of glutamate receptor blockers in mitigating SSC-induced toxicity, we administered memantine, an NMDA antagonist, andLY341495, a metabotropic glutamate receptor blocker, either individually or in combination. We used five doses, with increasing concentrations ranging from 1 to 20 µM for memantine and from 1 to 10 µM forLY341495. The literature has reported these doses as concentrations that effectively inhibit the relevant glutamate receptors for both blockers (Kingston et al.1998; Rozumna et al.2023; Boehringer et al.2023). Our results indicate that administration of these blockers, either alone or in combination, at any dose used, did not prevent the SSC toxicity observed in HT-22 cells. The differential responses of cortical neurons and HT-22 cells to SSC-induced cytotoxicity may be attributed to the distinct structural features of HT-22 cells (Kritis et al.2015). In addition, by this finding, it has been suggested that receptor blockers cannot prevent glutamate toxicity in anoxia and there may be a non-receptor-dependent pathway in the observed neurotoxicity (Newell et al.1995). These results suggest that the potential cause of glutamate-like toxicity in these cells may be attributed to intracellular uptake of SSC rather than receptor-mediated. Indeed, studies showed that CHO cells exhibited cellular absorption of SSC through the cystine/glutamate antiporter (xc-) mechanism owing to the structural resemblance between SSC and cystine and glutamate (Zimmermann et al.2021; Nguyen et al.2023). It has been shown that SSC can be taken up into the cell by a glutamate-like transport system, although at a much lower rate than glutamate uptake (Bouvier et al.1991). Consistent with these findings, it has been demonstrated that glutamate-treated cells morphologically exhibit plasma membrane blebbing and cell shrinkage, not DNA fragmentation. At the ultrastructural level, the nucleus is stable, whereas mitochondria and other organelles are damaged (Rozumna et al.2023). However, a complete mechanism for the detoxification of SSC has not been elucidated in the current literature. Therefore, further investigation of SSC's intracellular toxicological effects is necessary.

The next phase of our study was to determine whether SSC triggered cell death through apoptosis. Caspase-3 plays a crucial role as an executive caspase in the process of apoptosis. This enzyme is responsible for managing inflammatory and apoptotic signaling pathways, and orchestrating the breakdown of cellular components (Porter and Jänicke1999). These functions include the fragmentation of DNA and the breakdown of proteins within the cytoskeleton. we examined two well-known indicators of apoptotic cell death, caspase-3 activation and DNA damage (Taatjes et al.2008). To this end, we assessed the levels of this enzyme to see if the cell death induced by SSC administration was facilitated by caspase 3. In addition, we used comet analysis to investigate whether DNA damage could occur due to a possible increase in caspase 3 activity. Our results showed that SSC did not affect these parameters, suggesting that these two mechanisms did not mediate cytotoxicity caused by SSC. The scientific community is still discussing the mechanism of glutamate-induced cell death in HT-22 cells, with ongoing debates as to whether it occurs by apoptosis, necrosis, or some previously unidentified form of cell death (Fukui et al.2009). Research has shown that glutamate-triggered cell death in HT-22 cells displays characteristics reminiscent of both apoptosis and necrosis (Shirlee Tan et al.2001; Fukui et al.2009). However, glutamate-induced cell death lacks typical apoptotic markers such as DNA laddering, chromatin condensation, and caspase-3 activation. Additional studies have demonstrated that glutamate can induce both necrotic and apoptotic responses. Moreover, the preference for necrotic or apoptotic cell death following glutamate exposure was found to be time-dependent and partially influenced by glutamate concentration (Xu et al.2010).

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are short-lived and highly reactive molecules. At low doses, ROS are thought to be essential for the regulation of normal physiological functions involved in development, such as cell cycle progression and proliferation, differentiation, migration and cell death. Excessive increases in ROS levels can damage many biological structures such as proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, cell membranes and organelles, leading to the activation of cell death processes such as apoptosis (Redza-Dutordoir and Averill-Bates2016). ROS production in cells is balanced by various antioxidant defence mechanisms. An imbalance in favour of ROS leads to a pathological process called oxidative stress (Poljsak et al.2013). Conversely, up-regulation of the antioxidant defence system can prevent the occurrence of potential harmful consequences mediated by ROS (Rauf et al.2023). ROS play a central role in cell signalling and in the regulation of the main apoptotic pathways mediated by mitochondria, death receptors and the endoplasmic reticulum (Redza-Dutordoir and Averill-Bates2016). Since we did not observe changes in parameters such as DNA fragmentation and Caspase-3 activation, which are indicators of apoptotic processes in SSC-induced cytotoxicity, we wanted to measure glutathione levels, a component of the cellular antioxidant system. GSH is the most abundant cellular thiol and plays a central role in maintaining cellular redox status. Increased GSH levels are expected to reduce ROS levels and antagonise apoptotic signalling (Dalton et al.2004). In our study, there was a twofold increase in total glutathione levels upon administration of SSCs to HT-22 cells. examples of increased glutathione levels against various cell lines and cytotoxic agents can be found in the literature (Du et al.2009). A research study demonstrated that when gliotoxin induced cytotoxicity in SH-SY5Y neuronal cells, it was accompanied by an elevation in both total and reduced glutathione forms. This increase was linked to enhanced activity of γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase and glutathione reductase, enzymes involved in glutathione production. Notably, the same investigation revealed that decreasing glutathione levels using L-buthionine-sulfoxamine (BSO) diminished the cytotoxic impact of gliotoxin (Axelsson et al.2006). We think that the total glutathione increase we found in our study is the weakness of our study, because the method we use is used to measure the level of total glutathione (GSSG + GSH) in HT22 cells. The origin of this increase remained unclear, as it could not be established whether elevated levels of reduced GSH or its oxidized counterpart, GSSH, were responsible.

Although oxidative stress was not measured in our study, this finding may indirectly indicate that SSC induced oxidative stress. If the increase in intracellular glutathione is in the direction of reduced glutathione (GSH), it may be due to a compensatory increase in antioxidant mechanisms to protect cells against the toxic effects of SSC. If the increase is in the oxidized form (GSSH), another mechanism for the toxic effects of SSC can be speculated. NF-κB plays an important role in several physiological and pathophysiological processes (Luo et al.2005). One of these mechanisms is promotion of cell viability. Since it has been shown that the accumulation of GSSH in the cell causes inhibition of NF-κB signaling, which in turn decreases cell viability (Dou et al.2018). Thus, SSC may exert its toxic effects in this manner.

In conclusion, our research results corroborated the excitotoxic nature of SSC, which exhibited effects comparable to those of glutamate. The absence of two well-established indicators of apoptosis, caspase-3 activation and DNA fragmentation, in SSC-induced cell death suggests the involvement of alternative pathways. These include necrosis, oxytosis, and other mechanisms of cell death. Further research is required to understand the precise process underlying SSC-induced cell death comprehensively.
