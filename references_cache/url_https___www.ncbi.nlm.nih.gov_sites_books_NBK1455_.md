</div><div id="craniosynostosis.Clinical_Characteristic"><h2 id="_craniosynostosis_Clinical_Characteristic_">1.  Clinical Characteristics of <i>FGFR</i> Craniosynostosis Syndromes</h2><div id="craniosynostosis.Clinical_Description"><h3>Clinical Description</h3><p>To date, more than 500 individuals with an <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome have been reported. The spectrum of severity ranges from severe prenatal multisuture craniosynostosis with feeding and airway issues to <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isolated/">isolated</a> unicoronal craniosynostosis. Included in this overview are the following <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis phenotypes:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Apert syndrome</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Beare-Stevenson cutis gyrata syndrome</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Bent bone dysplasia</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Crouzon syndrome</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Crouzon syndrome with acanthosis nigricans</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Jackson-Weiss syndrome</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Muenke syndrome</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Pfeiffer syndrome</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Isolated coronal synostosis</div></li></ul><p>Considerable phenotypic overlap notwithstanding, discriminating features can aid in the specific diagnosis (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK1455/table/craniosynostosis.T.distinguishing_charac/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobcraniosynostosisTdistinguishingcharac">Table 1</a>). The following individual phenotypes are recognized.</p><p>
<a href="/sites/books/n/gene/apert/">
<b>Apert syndrome</b>
</a>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Craniofacial.</b> Head shape is determined by the sutures involved and the timing of premature fusion; the majority of individuals have some degree of turribrachycephaly. Midface retrusion is moderate to severe, with a greater degree of vertical impaction of the midface than most individuals with Crouzon syndrome [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.forte.2014.285">Forte et al 2014</a>]. Additional common features include: ocular anomalies (e.g., proptosis, strabismus, refractive error, anisometropia), cleft palate, dental anomalies (crowding, delayed eruption, crossbite, missing teeth), and hearing loss (80%) that is most often conductive.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Respiratory.</b> Multilevel airway obstruction is common, including choanal stenosis, tongue-based airway obstruction, and tracheal anomalies.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Extremities.</b> Findings include soft tissue and bony ("mitten glove") syndactyly with or without polydactyly of fingers and toes often involving fusion of the second, third, and fourth digits with variable inclusion of the first and fifth digits; synonychia (a single nail for the second, third, and fourth digits) more commonly involving the upper extremities; synostosis of the radius and humerus in some individuals; and occasional rhizomelia [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.cohen.1995.82">Cohen &#x00026; Kreiborg 1995</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.wilkie.1995.165">Wilkie et al 1995</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Neurologic.</b> Variable developmental delay and/or intellectual disability (50%) is possibly related to the timing of craniofacial surgery [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.renier.1996.66">Renier et al 1996</a>]; ventriculomegaly is common, progressive hydrocephalus is less common (2%); structural brain malformations (e.g., Chiari I malformation, absent septum pellucidum, agenesis of the corpus callosum) have been reported.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Gastrointestinal anomalies.</b> Malrotation is the most common [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.hibberd.2016.3215">Hibberd et al 2016</a>]. Congenital diaphragmatic hernia has been reported in five infants [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.witters.2000.404">Witters et al 2000</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.bulfamante.2011.910">Bulfamante et al 2011</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.sobaih.2015.106">Sobaih &#x00026; AlAli 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.kosi_ski.2016.830">Kosi&#x00144;ski et al 2016</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.kaur.2019.78">Kaur et al 2019</a>]. Distal esophageal stenosis, pyloric stenosis, esophageal atresia, and ectopic anus have all been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.cohen.1993.758">Cohen &#x00026; Kreiborg 1993</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.pelz.1994.91">Pelz et al 1994</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.zarate.2010.638">Zarate et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.hibberd.2016.3215">Hibberd et al 2016</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Integument.</b> Hyperhidrosis, acneiform lesions, and nail dystrophy have been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.cohen.1993.758">Cohen &#x00026; Kreiborg 1993</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.cohen.1995.82">Cohen &#x00026; Kreiborg 1995</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.bissacotti_steglich.2016.e596">Bissacotti Steglich et al 2016</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>
<b>Other</b>
</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Fused cervical and/or thoracic vertebrae (68%), usually C5-C6</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Cardiac anomalies (10%) (e.g., ventricular septal defect, overriding aorta)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Ovarian dysgerminoma (1 individual) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.rouzier.2008.696">Rouzier et al 2008</a>]. A single instance of low-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma was reported. It is unclear if these tumors are related to Apert syndrome.</div></li></ul></li></ul><p><b>Beare-Stevenson</b>
<b>cutis gyrata syndrome</b></p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Craniofacial.</b> Multisuture craniosynostosis with cloverleaf skull is the most common skull configuration. Moderate-to-severe midface retrusion, proptosis, abnormal ears, cleft palate, conductive hearing loss, natal teeth, and relative prognathism are seen.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Respiratory.</b> Multilevel airway obstruction includes choanal stenosis, tongue-based airway obstruction, and tracheal anomalies, with survivors requiring endotracheal intubation with mechanical ventilation and/or tracheostomy.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Extremities.</b> Hands and feet are normally formed aside from cutis gyrata.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Neurologic.</b> Intellectual disability is present in all affected individuals who have survived (neonatal mortality is common). Hydrocephalus and Chiari I malformations are common.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Integument.</b> Widespread cutis gyrata and acanthosis nigricans are usually evident at birth; hirsutism, skin tags, prominent umbilicus with redundant tissue, and accessory nipples are also seen.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Other</b> findings include genitourinary anomalies (e.g., bifid scrotum, prominent labial raphe, rugated labia majora), pyloric stenosis, and anterior anus.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Bent bone dysplasia</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Craniofacial.</b> Variable features include hypomineralization of the calvarium, coronal craniosynostosis, open metopic suture, hypertelorism, megalophthalmous, midface hypoplasia, low-set posteriorly rotated ears overfolded superior helix, hypoplastic ears, gingival hyperplasia, prenatal teeth, and micrognathia [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.merrill.2012.550">Merrill et al 2012</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Respiratory.</b> Perinatal lethal skeletal dysplasia with bell-shaped thorax</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Extremities.</b> Bent long bones, osteopenia, irregular periosteal surfaces (especially the phalanges), brachydactyly</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Gastrointestinal.</b> Hepatosplenomegaly, extramedullary hematopoiesis</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Integument.</b> Hirsutism.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Other.</b> Osteopenia, hypoplastic clavicles, narrow ischia, hypoplastic pubis, clitoromegaly</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Crouzon syndrome</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Craniofacial.</b> Craniosynostosis in most individuals. Head shape depends on the sutures involved and the timing of premature fusion, ranging from normal head shape to cloverleaf skull. Infants without craniosynostosis may have normal facial features at birth with craniofacial features developing over the first year or two of life including: significant proptosis, external strabismus, midface retrusion, convex nasal ridge, and relative prognathism. Facial features can be highly variable among affected family members. High arched palate is common; cleft palate is less common. Hearing loss occurs in 74% and is most often conductive.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Respiratory.</b> Variable from no airway issues to multilevel airway obstruction including choanal stenosis, tongue-based airway obstruction, and tracheal anomalies</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Extremities.</b> Normal (although shortened phalanges compared to unaffected family members have been identified on radiographs) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.murdochkinch.1997.61">Murdoch-Kinch &#x00026; Ward 1997</a>]</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Neurologic.</b> Structural brain malformations are uncommon; Chiari I malformation, progressive hydrocephalus (30%) often with tonsillar herniation have been reported. Most individuals have normal intelligence, although there is a risk for developmental delays, especially in individuals with hydrocephalus and increased intracranial pressure. A study of 31 adults with Crouzon syndrome reported a lower level of education, lower chance of having a romantic partner, and fewer children. There were no differences in housing type, and affected individuals' estimation of their overall health was similar to healthy controls with the exception of a higher use of anti-seizure medication. Depressed mood was more common in individuals with Crouzon syndrome, but overall positive attitude to life was similar to control individuals. There was significant variability among affected individuals [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.fischer.2014.244">Fischer et al 2014</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Integument.</b> Linear skin rugations, deep creases, and redundant scalp skin (similar to those seen in Beare-Stevenson cutis gyrata syndrome) were reported in individuals with pathogenic variants c.Ser267Pro and c.Val274_Glu275delinsLeu [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.leblanc.2018.149">LeBlanc et al 2018</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Other.</b> Approximately 25% have vertebral fusion, most often C2-C3. Sacrococcygeal appendage has also been described [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.lapunzina.2005.61">Lapunzina et al 2005</a>].</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Crouzon syndrome with acanthosis nigricans</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Craniofacial.</b> Craniosynostosis with variable head shape depending on involved sutures. Significant proptosis, external strabismus, prognathism. Hearing loss is reported in 14%.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Respiratory.</b> Variable from no airway issues to multilevel airway obstruction including choanal stenosis, tongue-based airway obstruction, and tracheal anomalies</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Extremities.</b> Normal (although shortened phalanges compared to unaffected family members have been identified on radiographs) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.murdochkinch.1997.61">Murdoch-Kinch &#x00026; Ward 1997</a>]</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Neurologic</b>. Intellect is typically normal, although there is a risk for developmental delay especially in children with increased intracranial pressure as a result of hydrocephalus.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Integument.</b> Acanthosis nigricans (pigmentary changes in the skin fold regions) can be present in the neonatal period or appear later.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Other.</b> Odontogenic tumors have been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.xu.2018.296">Xu et al 2018</a>].</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Jackson-Weiss syndrome</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Craniofacial.</b> Multisuture craniosynostosis with proptosis and prognathism; hearing loss (68%) is usually conductive.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Respiratory.</b> Variable from no airway issues to multilevel airway obstruction including choanal stenosis, tongue-based airway obstruction, and tracheal anomalies</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Extremities.</b> Broad and medially deviated great toes, with 2/3 toe syndactyly, and normal hands; short first metatarsal, calcaneocuboid fusion, and abnormally formed tarsals; genu valgum</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Neurologic.</b> Intellect is typically normal.</div></li></ul><p><a href="/sites/books/n/gene/muenke/"><b>Muenke syndrome</b></a><b>.</b> Some individuals have no apparent features and are only identified after they have a child diagnosed with Muenke syndrome.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Craniofacial.</b> Variable features including uni- or bicoronal craniosynostosis; unicoronal synostosis, more often seen in males [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.honnebier.2008.919">Honnebier et al 2008</a>]; in some individuals, absence of craniosynostosis and normal or macrocephalic head shape; mild-to-significant midface retrusion; hypertelorism; bilateral, symmetric, low- to mid-frequency sensorineural hearing loss (61%) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.honnebier.2008.919">Honnebier et al 2008</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Extremities.</b> Variable. Carpal and tarsal fusions are diagnostic when present but are not always present. Brachydactyly, carpal bone malsegregation, or coned epiphyses may occur.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Neurologic.</b> Intelligence ranges from normal to mild intellectual disability; social difficulties and mild neuropsychiatric issues (e.g., attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) are more common compared to unaffected sibs. Seizures are reported in 20% of individuals.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Other.</b> Osteochondroma [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.barbosa.2009.260">Barbosa et al 2009</a>]</div></li></ul><p><b>Pfeiffer syndrome</b> shares significant phenotypic overlap with Crouzon syndrome, and individuals with the same <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> have been diagnosed with either Pfeiffer or Crouzon syndrome, although some <i>FGFR</i> pathogenic variants have been reported only in individuals with Pfeiffer syndrome.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Craniofacial.</b> Multisuture craniosynostosis in most individuals. Head shape depends on the sutures involved and the timing of premature fusion, ranging from normal head shape to cloverleaf skull. Individuals without craniosynostosis have been described. Midface retrusion is moderate to severe, with a greater degree of vertical impaction of the midface compared to individuals with Crouzon syndrome [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.forte.2014.285">Forte et al 2014</a>]. In individuals with severe craniosynostosis with shallow orbits, eyes are very prominent and there is a risk for subluxation of the globe. Hearing loss occurs in 92% and is most often conductive. Hearing loss may be associated with stenosis or atresia of the external auditory canal. Some individuals have cleft palate [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.stoler.2009.1375">Stoler et al 2009</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Respiratory.</b> Some individuals have multilevel airway obstruction, including choanal stenosis/atresia, laryngotracheal abnormalities including tracheal cartilaginous sleeve, and tongue-based airway obstruction.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Extremities.</b> Thumbs and great toes are broad and medially deviated, with a variable degree of brachydactyly. Synostosis of the radius and humerus occurs in some individuals particularly those with <i>FGFR2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> p.Trp290Cys. Ankylosis of the knees has been reported. In one family, involvement of the feet was the only clinical feature [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.rossi.2003.269">Rossi et al 2003</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Neurologic.</b> Intelligence ranges from normal to severe intellectual disability. Seizures and an increased risk for early death are reported. Early surgery to prevent cephalocranial disproportion and intervention to manage sleep apnea may promote improved cognitive outcomes in children with severe presentations [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.wenger.2019.471">Wenger et al 2019</a>]. Approximately 28% of children require surgical intervention for hydrocephalus [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.cinalli.1998.209">Cinalli et al 1998</a>]. Approximately 50% of individuals with cloverleaf skull have Chiari I malformation [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.cinalli.1995.575">Cinalli et al 1995</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Other.</b> Sacrococcygeal eversion/appendage has been described [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.oliveira.2006.629">Oliveira et al 2006</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.lai.2008.73">Lai et al 2008</a>]. Prune belly has been reported in two infants [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.bracero.1988.239">Bracero et al 1988</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.pe_apadilla.2019.412">Pe&#x000f1;a-Padilla et al 2019</a>].</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Isolated coronal synostosis</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Intellect. Normal</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Craniofacial. Unilateral or bilateral coronal synostosis, asymmetric brachycephaly and/or orbital hypertelorism</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Extremities. Unless otherwise indicated, the following general descriptions do not include Muenke syndrome or <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isolated/">isolated</a> coronal synostosis.</p><p><b>Craniosynostosis.</b> The majority of individuals with an <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome have <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/congenital/">congenital</a> craniosynostosis. However, craniosynostosis can also develop later in infancy or childhood, and individuals with congenital craniosynostosis can prematurely fuse additional sutures over time. Individuals with Muenke syndrome are more likely to have premature fusion of coronal sutures only, while other <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndromes (e.g., Apert, Crouzon, Pfeiffer) are associated with progressive postnatal premature fusion of sutures.</p><p>Unicoronal craniosynostosis results in asymmetric forehead with nasal twist and harlequin eye deformity. Bicoronal craniosynostosis results in turribrachycephaly.</p><p>Multisuture craniosynostosis results in a variable head shape determined by the involved sutures, presence of hydrocephalus, and timing of premature fusion. The shape of the skull results from the pressure of the developing brain expanding outwards into the space allowed by the skull. There is typically expansion perpendicular to the fused suture. When multiple sutures are fused, expansion occurs into the portion of the skull with least resistance, resulting in predictable head shapes. Prenatal pansynostosis results in a cloverleaf (<i>Kleeblatschadel</i>) head shape. Pansynostosis that occurs later in infancy or childhood does not result in a cloverleaf head shape, and may only be identified on head CT with an arrest in head circumference growth. Affected individuals may or may not have microcephaly.</p><p><b>Feeding issues</b> can be <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multifactorial/">multifactorial</a>, and can be caused by any of the following:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Palatal anomalies affecting the quality of suck (e.g., high arched palate, narrow palate, cleft palate)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Respiratory difficulties due to airway obstruction (e.g., choanal stenosis, choanal atresia, tracheomalacia, laryngomalacia). Infants with choanal stenosis or atresia attempt to latch but abruptly unlatch to breathe through their mouth. The degree of narrowing of the bony passage correlates with the amount of time an infant can attempt to suck before unlatching.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Ascending and/or descending aspiration</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Coordination difficulties with sucking, swallowing, and breathing, which may be seen without other signs of neurologic dysfunction</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Severe neurologic dysfunction (e.g., severe hydrocephalus, symptomatic Chiari I malformation)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Gastrointestinal issues (e.g., pyloric stenosis, malrotation, volvulus)</div></li></ul><p><b>Multilevel airway obstruction.</b> Most individuals have some degree of airway obstruction, though contributing factors can vary with age:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Narrowed nasal passages as a result of bony atresia or stenosis, including choanal atresia or stenosis. For infants with choanal stenosis respiratory difficulty can increase over time as the bony passage remains relatively stable but the lung tidal volumes increase with growth. Infants with choanal stenosis may gradually require more time to finish a smaller volume. Nasal flaring, retractions, and repeated unlatching during a feeding with mouth breathing can be seen.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Tongue-based airway obstruction, which may be exacerbated in infants with cleft palate after palate repair</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Tracheal anomalies including fused rings and tracheal cartilaginous sleeves. Tracheal cartilaginous sleeves are often asymptomatic but pose a significant risk of sudden death caused by obstruction of the airway with mucous during illness. Tracheal cartilaginous sleeves can be identified during operative airway evaluation, as recommended for all children with a multisuture craniosynostosis syndrome [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.pickrell.2017.696">Pickrell et al 2017</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.wenger.2017.62">Wenger et al 2017</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Airway inflammation as a result of chronic aspiration</div></li></ul><p><b>Sleep apnea.</b> Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), caused by multilevel airway obstruction, may develop or worsen during childhood or adulthood. The majority of individuals with <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndromes have midface retrusion, which can contribute to obstructive sleep apnea. This can be challenging to treat, as continuous-positive-airway-pressure masks place pressure on the maxillae and can worsen midface retrusion with consistent wear during childhood, which can produce more airway resistance and potentially worsen OSA [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.driessen.2013.538">Driessen et al 2013</a>].</p><p>Central sleep apnea is more common in children with Chiari I malformation and/or significant hydrocephalus. Children with Pfeiffer syndrome as a result of <i>FGFR2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> p.Trp290Cys appear to be particularly susceptible to central sleep apnea, and 100% of reported surviving individuals have required mechanical ventilation during sleep for at least some period of time [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.wenger.2019.471">Wenger et al 2019</a>].</p><p><b>Ocular abnormalities.</b> Coronal craniosynostosis and underdevelopment of the maxillary arches results in decreased depth of the bony orbit and proptosis. Some individuals with particularly shallow orbits have globe subluxation (i.e., eyelids retract behind the globe). Individuals with proptosis often have difficulty keeping their eyes closed fully while asleep leading to exposure keratopathy and corneal scarring.</p><p>Individuals with mild involvement of the bony orbit may have downslanted palpebral fissures. Other ophthalmologic abnormalities include strabismus, refractive error, anisometropia, iris hypoplasia, and posterior embryotoxon [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.mccann.2005.278">McCann et al 2005</a>].</p><p>In individuals with increased intracranial pressure &#x02013; particularly if there is not prompt and aggressive intervention &#x02013; papilledema can occur, leading to optic atrophy and loss of vision.</p><p><b>Hearing loss.</b> Conductive hearing loss is more common than sensorineural for all <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndromes except Muenke syndrome.</p><p><b>Dental anomalies.</b> Tooth agenesis, enamel opacities, and abnormal patterns of tooth eruption are common. Dental maturation is more significantly delayed in individuals with Apert than Crouzon syndrome [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.reitsma.2014.639">Reitsma et al 2014</a>]. There is often dental crowding, especially in the maxillary arch. Most children develop malocclusion as a result of progressive maxillary retrusion and/or abnormalities in mandibular growth [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.kolar.2017.709">Kolar et al 2017</a>].</p><p><b>Limb anomalies.</b> Synostosis of the radius and humerus occurs in some individuals, most commonly in those with Apert syndrome, occasionally in those with Pfeiffer syndrome, especially in those with <i>FGFR2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> p.Trp290Cys. Upper-arm mobility may also be limited by glenohumeral dysplasia, leading to progressive decrease in forward flexion and abduction of the upper arm, limiting the ability to perform overhead tasks. Some individuals have an increased susceptibility to fractures, including femoral fractures [Author, unpublished data]. Broad, medially deviated thumbs and great toes are characteristic of Pfeiffer syndrome. Mildly broad thumbs have been reported in individuals with other <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndromes. Preaxial and/or postaxial polydactyly are rare [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.mantillacapacho.2005.403">Mantilla-Capacho et al 2005</a>].</p><p><b>Vertebral anomalies.</b> Vertebral fusions are more common in individuals with Apert syndrome than Crouzon syndrome. Approximately half of individuals with vertebral fusions have multiple fusions. This can result in scoliosis and/or instability [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.shotelersuk.2002.4">Shotelersuk et al 2002</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.lin.2019.e843">Lin et al 2019</a>]. Cervical spine instability has been reported. Some children have been reported to have atlanto-axial subluxation and C1 spina bifida occulta [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.breik.2016.833">Breik et al 2016</a>].</p><p><b>Neurologic.</b> Hydrocephalus is a prominent feature of Crouzon and Pfeiffer syndromes and may occur at any time. Many children with Crouzon and Pfeiffer syndromes who have multisuture craniosynostosis require a surgical treatment for obstructive hydrocephalus (e.g., ventriculoperitoneal shunt, endoscopic third ventriculostomy) within the first two to three years of life, and some require intervention early in infancy. The foramen magnum develops differently and intra-occipital synchondroses may fuse early in each of the <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndromes, which may contribute to hydrocephalus and abnormal head shape [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.rijken.2015.1042">Rijken et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.coll.2018.e460">Coll et al 2018</a>]. Stable ventriculomegaly is seen in more than half of children with Apert syndrome, and these children are much less likely to require surgical interventions for hydrocephalus.</p><p>Structural brain anomalies are more common in individuals with Apert syndrome, including abnormalities of the corpus callosum, absent septum pellucidum, posterior fossa arachnoid cyst, and limbic malformations. Chiari I malformations and/or low-lying cerebellar tonsils can be seen, and 73% of those with Crouzon syndrome have been reported to have chronic tonsillar herniation. This is in stark contrast to Apert syndrome, where only 2% have chronic tonsillar herniation.</p><p><b>Neurodevelopment</b> ranges from normal to severe intellectual disability. Most children with significant impairments have had cloverleaf skull, structural brain anomalies, and/or significant hydrocephalus. For children with multisuture craniosynostosis, early and aggressive surgical intervention to address increased intracranial pressure may prevent intellectual disability [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.wenger.2019.471">Wenger et al 2019</a>]. Neurobehavioral and developmental challenges may also be as a result of hearing impairment, vision impairment, physical limitations (e.g., limb anomalies), and sleep apnea.</p><p><b>Cardiovascular.</b> Structural cardiac defects occur in approximately 10% of individuals with Apert syndrome but are uncommon in individuals with Crouzon and Pfeiffer syndromes. Complex <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/congenital/">congenital</a> heart disease is associated with an increased risk of morbidity and mortality because of the cardiac lesion as well as with other procedures (e.g., positive pressure ventilation via tracheostomy can contribute to poor outcomes in children with single-ventricle physiology). Cardiac defects that result in atrial shunts can increase the risk of embolic stroke during craniosynostosis surgery. Children with severe, untreated obstructive sleep apnea can develop right ventricular hypertrophy and pulmonary hypertension.</p><p>
<b>Other</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Gastrointestinal.</b> Structural malformations include malrotation, pyloric stenosis, and esophageal atresia.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Genitourinary.</b> Hydronephrosis and cryptorchidism have been reported.</div></li></ul><p><b>Prognosis.</b> Multigenerational families with Crouzon and Apert syndromes have been reported. Many adults with Crouzon syndrome and some with Apert syndrome are fully independent, though some individuals have physical or cognitive limitations that require assistance.</p></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Differential_Diagnosis"><h3>Differential Diagnosis</h3><p>Craniosynostosis can be primary or secondary. In primary craniosynostosis, abnormal biology of the suture causes premature suture closure, as in <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndromes. Primary craniosynostosis can be <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isolated/">isolated</a> or part of a syndrome.</p><p>In secondary craniosynostosis, the suture biology is normal, but abnormal external forces result in premature suture closure.</p><div id="craniosynostosis.Isolated_Primary_Cranio"><h4>Isolated Primary Craniosynostosis</h4><p>Single-suture craniosynostosis results in recognizable head shapes: metopic (trigonocephaly), sagittal (scaphocephaly), lambdoid (posterior asymmetric flattening with vertical displacement of one ear and tilt of skull base), unicoronal (asymmetric forehead with nasal twist and harlequin eye deformity), and bicoronal (turribrachycephaly).</p><p>Among 204 individuals with apparently nonsyndromic and nonfamilial single-suture craniosynostosis, the likelihood of finding an underlying genetic difference varied by suture involvement [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.wilkie.2010.e391">Wilkie et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.mathijssen.2015.1735">Mathijssen 2015</a>].</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Isolated unicoronal craniosynostosis.</b> Among individuals with apparently nonsyndromic unicoronal craniosynostosis the prevalence of any syndrome was 17%; Muenke syndrome was identified in 10%.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Isolated bicoronal craniosynostosis.</b> Among individuals with apparently <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isolated/">isolated</a> bicoronal craniosynostosis, Muenke syndrome was diagnosed in 38%; no other syndromes were identified.</div></li></ul><p>Note: (1) Those with apparently <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isolated/">isolated</a> synostosis of the lambdoid, sagittal, or metopic sutures had no pathogenic variants identified [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.wilkie.2010.e391">Wilkie et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.mathijssen.2015.1735">Mathijssen 2015</a>]. (2) A study in individuals with either <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/syndromic/">syndromic</a> or nonsyndromic metopic craniosynostosis found no pathogenic variants in <i>FGFR1</i>, <i>CER1</i>, or <i>CDON</i>, suggesting that analysis of these genes is not warranted in persons with metopic craniosynostosis [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.jehee.2006.148">Jehee et al 2006</a>].</p></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Syndromic_Primary_Crani"><h4>Syndromic Primary Craniosynostosis</h4><p>Craniosynostosis is a finding in more than 150 genetic disorders. Evaluation Strategies to Identify the Genetic Cause of an <i>FGFR</i> Craniosynostosis Syndrome in a Proband</h2><p>An <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome should be suspected in fetuses and individuals presenting with classic or suggestive findings of uni- or bicoronal craniosynostosis or cloverleaf skull, characteristic facial features, and/or variable hand and foot findings (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK1455/table/craniosynostosis.T.distinguishing_charac/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobcraniosynostosisTdistinguishingcharac">Table 1</a>). Note that these features can vary from severe and life threatening to extremely mild, and may not be apparent in an affected neonate. Features typically become more prominent with age.</p><p>Establishing a specific genetic cause of an <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">Can aid in discussions of prognosis (which are beyond the scope of this <i>GeneReview</i>) and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a>;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">Is based on clinical and radiologic findings and the identification of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in <i>FGFR1, FGFR2,</i> or <i>FGFR3</i>, and involves medical history, physical examination, imaging, family history, and genetic testing.</div><div class="half_rhythm">Note: As no formal clinical diagnostic criteria exist, specific diagnosis should be confirmed by genetic testing.</div></li></ul><div id="craniosynostosis.Medical_History"><h3>Medical History</h3><p>An <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome should be suspected in a fetus with prenatal ultrasound findings of craniosynostosis involving the coronal sutures, especially cloverleaf skull, polysyndactyly, midface retrusion, and growth restriction. Bent bone dysplasia should be suspected in a fetus with features of a skeletal dysplasia including hypoplastic thorax with short ribs, short limbs, curved femurs, or skull deformity.</p></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Physical_Examination"><h3>Physical Examination</h3><p>A physical examination should include standard growth parameters (height, weight, head circumference) and address the following key issues:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Abnormal head shape to evaluate for craniosynostosis as well as bulging fontanelle, which could suggest increased intracranial pressure</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Orbital protection and particular attention to whether the lids fully cover the eyes during sleep</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Nasal flaring, retractions or other signs of obstructive breathing, or inability to pass a nasogastric tube or suction catheter, suggestive of choanal stenosis/atresia</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Careful examination of hands and feet for polysyndactyly or thumb anomalies</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Range of motion of elbows and knees to evaluate for radioulnar synostosis and/or joint contractures</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Genitourinary exam for sacral appendage or other anomalies</div></li></ul></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Family_History"><h3>Family History</h3><p>A three-generation family history should be taken, with attention to relatives with clinical and radiographic manifestations of an <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome (e.g., specific questions about individuals with abnormal head shapes, prominent eyes, midface retrusion, skeletal dysplasia, and/or other structural birth defects. Relevant findings from direct examination or review of medical records (including results of <a href="#craniosynostosis.Molecular_Genetic_Testi">molecular genetic testing</a>) must be documented.</p></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Molecular_Genetic_Testi"><h3>Molecular Genetic Testing</h3><p>Approaches include <b><a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a>-targeted testing</b> (targeted analysis, single-gene testing, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multigene-panel/">multigene panel</a>) or <b>comprehensive</b>
<b><a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing</b> (<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exome-sequencing/">exome sequencing</a>, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exome-array/">exome array</a>, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genome-sequencing/">genome sequencing</a>). Gene-targeted testing requires that the clinician determine which gene(s) are likely involved, whereas genomic testing does not. Options for testing include the following.</p><p><b>Targeted analysis or single-<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> testing</b> can be considered in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with clinical features characteristic of a specific <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK1455/table/craniosynostosis.T.distinguishing_charac/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobcraniosynostosisTdistinguishingcharac">Table 1</a>).</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Apert syndrome.</b> Targeted analysis of <b><i>FGFR2</i></b> pathogenic variants p.Ser252Trp and p.Pro253Arg</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Beare-Stevenson cutis gyrata syndrome</b>. <b>Neurodevelopment</b>

Evaluations can include the following:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Molecular genetic testing if the <i>FGFR</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in the family is known</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Assessment based on clinical and radiographic criteria (Note: Manifestations may not be readily evident in all affected individuals.)</div></li></ul><p>See <a href="#craniosynostosis.Related_Genetic_Counsel">Genetic Counseling</a> for issues related to testing of at-risk relatives for <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> purposes.</p></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Pregnancy_Management"><h3>Pregnancy Management</h3><p>Pregnant women carrying fetuses affected by <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndromes should be monitored during pregnancy for features that can affect early morbidity and mortality, and should be encouraged to deliver in a hospital with ready access to pediatric otolaryngology, plastic surgery, neurosurgery, and pulmonary medicine. Because of the high rate of respiratory obstruction such as choanal atresia, a health care provider skilled in endotracheal intubation and resuscitation should be present at the delivery.</p><p>See <a href="https://www.mothertobaby.org/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">MotherToBaby</a> for further information on medication use during pregnancy.</p></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Therapies_Under_Investi"><h3>Therapies Under Investigation</h3><p>Search <a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">ClinicalTrials.gov</a> in the US and <a href="https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/ctr-search/search" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">EU Clinical Trials Register</a> in Europe for access to information on clinical studies for a wide range of diseases and conditions. Note: There may not be clinical trials for this disorder.</p></div></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Genetic_Risk_Assessment"><h2 id="_craniosynostosis_Genetic_Risk_Assessment_">5.  Genetic Risk Assessment</h2><p><i>Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with information on the nature, mode(s) of inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them make informed medical and personal decisions. The following section deals with genetic risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or ethical issues that may arise or to substitute for consultation with a genetics professional</i>. &#x02014;ED.</p><div id="craniosynostosis.Mode_of_Inheritance"><h3>Mode of Inheritance</h3><p>The <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndromes are inherited in an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> manner.</p></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Risk_to_Family_Members"><h3>Risk to Family Members</h3><p>
<b>Parents of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>An individual with an <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome may have an affected parent or may have the disorder as the result of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>.</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>With a milder <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> &#x02013; as can be seen in <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/muenke/">Muenke syndrome</a>, Crouzon syndrome, Pfeiffer syndrome, and Jackson-Weiss syndrome &#x02013; inheritance of the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> from an affected parent is common; in the most severe forms (e.g., bent bone dysplasia), <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> pathogenic variants are common.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><i>FGFR3</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/isolated/">isolated</a> coronal synostosis is usually inherited from a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> parent who may or may not be affected.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Molecular genetic testing and clinical and radiographic evaluations are recommended for the parents of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with an apparent <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a></div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> found in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> cannot be detected in leukocyte DNA of either parent, possible explanations include a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> pathogenic variant in the proband or <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline-mosaicism/">germline mosaicism</a> in a parent.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The family history of some individuals diagnosed with an <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome may appear to be negative because of failure to recognize the disorder in family members or reduced <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a>. Therefore, an apparently negative family history cannot be confirmed unless <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> and clinical and radiographic evaluations have been performed on the parents of the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the parent is the individual in whom the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> first occurred, the parent may have somatic and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline-mosaicism/">germline mosaicism</a> for the variant and may be asymptomatic or mildly/minimally affected. To date, Crouzon syndrome is the only <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome in which parental mosaicism has been reported.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Note: Advanced paternal age has been shown clinically to be associated with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> pathogenic variants for Crouzon syndrome, Apert syndrome, Pfeiffer syndrome [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.glaser.2000.768">Glaser et al 2000</a>], Beare-Stevenson cutis gyrata syndrome, and Muenke syndrome [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.rannaneliya.2004.200">Rannan-Eliya et al 2004</a>]. Paternal age effect in <i>de novo</i> mutation has been conclusively demonstrated at the molecular level in Apert syndrome [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.moloney.1996.48">Moloney et al 1996</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.yoon.2009.e1000558">Yoon et al 2009</a>]. It has been proposed that <i>FGFR</i> pathogenic variants are paradoxically enriched in the male <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> because they confer a selective advantage to the spermatogonial cells in which they arise [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.goriely.2003.643">Goriely et al 2003</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.choi.2008.10143">Choi et al 2008</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.bochukova.2009.204">Bochukova et al 2009</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.yoon.2009.e1000558">Yoon et al 2009</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b>Sibs of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> The risk to sibs of the proband depends on the genetic status of the proband's parents:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If a parent is affected and/or is known to have the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>, the risk to the sibs of inheriting the pathogenic variant is 50%.</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis categorical <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> is usually consistent within family members <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for the same <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, though the severity and specific manifestations can vary widely among affected individuals. For example, if the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> has the clinical findings of Pfeiffer syndrome, heterozygous sibs of the proband will likely have clinical findings that are also consistent with Pfeiffer syndrome rather than Crouzon, Jackson-Weiss, or Apert syndrome. Nonetheless, rare examples of varied phenotype among affected individuals in a given family have been reported: <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.moko.2001.22">Moko &#x00026; Blandin de Chalain [2001]</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.aravidis.2014.1502">Aravidis et al [2014]</a>, and <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.bessenyei.2014.3176">Bessenyei et al [2014]</a> describe families in which some family members had findings suggestive of Pfeiffer syndrome, whereas others had findings suggestive of Jackson-Weiss or Crouzon syndromes.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Significant differences in clinical severity of a given type of <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome may be observed in <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> sibs. For example, the sutures that are fused at birth and degree of respiratory support required may vary significantly in families with Crouzon syndrome. In families with Muenke syndrome, there is decreased <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> of craniosynostosis and variability in hearing loss.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the <i>FGFR</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> found in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> cannot be detected in the leukocyte DNA of either parent, the risk to sibs is slightly greater than that of the general population because of the possibility of parental <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline-mosaicism/">germline mosaicism</a>. Parental germline and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/somatic-mosaicism/">somatic mosaicism</a> in Crouzon syndrome has been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.goriely.2010.2067">Goriely et al 2010</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the parents have not been tested for the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> but are clinically unaffected, sibs are still presumed to be at increased risk for an <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome because of the possibility of reduced <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a>/<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/variable-expressivity/">variable expressivity</a> in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> parent or parental <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline-mosaicism/">germline mosaicism</a>.</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>This risk appears to be low for Apert, Beare-Stevenson cutis gyrata, bent bone dysplasia, and Pfeiffer syndromes.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>For Crouzon and Muenke syndromes, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/variable-expressivity/">variable expressivity</a> between family members necessitates careful clinical evaluation of apparently unaffected relatives as affected parents with mild manifestations (e.g., mild hearing loss, prominent eyes) may be unaware that they have features of the condition.</div></li></ul></li></ul><p><b>Offspring of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> Each child of an individual with an <i>FGFR</i> craniosynostosis syndrome has a 50% chance of inheriting the <i>FGFR</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>.</p><p><b>Other family members.</b> The risk to other family members depends on the status of the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>'s parents: if a parent has the <i>FGFR</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, the parent's family members may be at risk.</p></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Related_Genetic_Counsel"><h3>Related Genetic Counseling Issues</h3><p>See Management, <a href="#craniosynostosis.Evaluation_of_Relatives">Evaluation of Relatives at Risk</a> for information on evaluating at-risk relatives for the purpose of early diagnosis and treatment.</p><p><b>Considerations in families with an apparent <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>.</b> When neither parent of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> condition has the pathogenic variant identified in the proband or clinical evidence of the disorder, the pathogenic variant is likely <i>de novo</i>. However, non-medical explanations including <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/alternate-paternity/">alternate paternity</a> or maternity (e.g., with assisted reproduction) and undisclosed adoption could also be explored.</p><p>
<b>Family planning</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The optimal time for determination of genetic risk and discussion of the availability of prenatal/<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> is before pregnancy.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is appropriate to offer <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> (including discussion of potential risks to offspring and reproductive options) to young adults who are affected or at risk.</div></li></ul><p><b>DNA banking.</b> Because it is likely that testing methodology and our understanding of genes, pathogenic mechanisms, and diseases will improve in the future, consideration should be given to banking DNA from probands in whom a molecular diagnosis has not been confirmed (i.e., the causative pathogenic mechanism is unknown). For more information, see <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.huang.2022.389">Huang et al [2022]</a>.</p></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Prenatal_Testing_and_Pr"><h3>Prenatal Testing and Preimplantation Genetic Testing</h3><p><b>High-risk pregnancies.</b> Once the <i>FGFR</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> has been identified in an affected family member, prenatal and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> are possible.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The finding of an <i>FGFR</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> cannot be used to predict the occurrence or severity of clinical manifestations for most <i>FGFR</i> phenotypes.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The long-term prognosis of individuals with syndromes previously associated with poor outcomes is difficult to predict as advances in surgical management have dramatically changed expected outcomes from those suggested by historical data in the literature. For example, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.wenger.2019.471">Wenger et al [2019]</a> reported that all individuals with <i>FGFR2</i> p.W290C previously described in the literature had severe intellectual disability and/or early death but that three affected individuals who underwent aggressive surgical techniques designed to manage cephalocranial disproportion all had roughly normal neurocognitive development later in childhood.</div></li></ul><p><b>Low-risk pregnancies.</b> In a pregnancy not previously identified to be at risk for craniosynostosis in which an abnormal head shape is detected on prenatal ultrasound examination, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-testing/">prenatal testing</a> is more difficult.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Molecular genetic testing.</b> While testing for pathogenic variants in <i>FGFR1</i>, <i>FGFR2</i>, or <i>FGFR3</i> is possible, the yield is likely to be low. Furthermore, identification of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in one of these genes would not clarify the prognosis, which is determined by clinical findings (e.g., the prognosis for cloverleaf skull is generally poor regardless of the molecular defect or nature of hand and foot findings).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Prenatal imaging.</b> Prenatal testing of various craniosynostosis syndromes may be possible if physical findings including abnormal biparietal diameter and ventriculomegaly are apparent on prenatal imaging. Three-dimensional ultrasound examination, three-dimensional CT scan, and MRI have proven useful in some cases to further delineate suspicious ultrasound findings and assess for underlying brain abnormalities. Prenatal MRI is often used to accurately diagnose suspected craniosynostosis syndromes such as Pfeiffer or Apert syndromes. Findings detectable by MRI may include agenesis of the corpus callosum, hydrocephalus causing increased biparietal diameter, or cloverleaf skull [<a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.tonni.2011.909">Tonni et al 2011</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.ketwaroo.2015.453">Ketwaroo et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#craniosynostosis.REF.helfer.2016.378">Helfer et al 2016</a>].</div></li></ul><p>Differences in perspective may exist among medical professionals and within families regarding the use of prenatal and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a>, particularly if the testing is being considered for the purpose of pregnancy termination rather than early diagnosis. While most health care centers would consider use of prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing to be a personal decision, discussion of these issues may be helpful.</p></div></div><div id="craniosynostosis.Resources"><h2 id="_craniosynostosis_Resources_">Resources</h2><p>
