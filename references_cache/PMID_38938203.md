# The impact of individual clinical features in cold agglutinin disease: hemolytic versus non-hemolytic symptoms.
**Authors:** Berentsen S, Vos JMI, Malecka A, Tjønnfjord GE, D'Sa S
**Journal:** Expert Rev Hematol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1080/17474086.2024.2372333](https://doi.org/10.1080/17474086.2024.2372333)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: During the last decades, the pathogenesis of cold agglutinin disease (CAD) has been well elucidated and shown to be complex. Several documented or investigational therapies have been made available. This development has resulted in major therapeutic advances, but also in challenges in choice of therapy.

AREAS COVERED: In this review, we address each step in pathogenesis: bone marrow clonal lymphoproliferation, composition and effects of monoclonal cold agglutinin, non-complement mediated erythrocyte agglutination, complement-dependent hemolysis, and other effects of complement activation. We also discuss the heterogeneous clinical features and their relation to specific steps in pathogenesis, in particular with respect to the impact of complement involvement. CAD can be classified into three clinical phenotypes with consequences for established treatments as well as development of new therapies. Some promising future treatment approaches - beyond chemoimmunotherapy and complement inhibition - are reviewed.

EXPERT OPINION: The patient's individual clinical profile regarding complement involvement and hemolytic versus non-hemolytic features is important for the choice of treatment. Further development of treatment approaches is encouraged, and some candidate drugs are promising irrespective of clinical phenotype. Patients with CAD requiring therapy should be considered for inclusion in clinical trials.

1. Introduction
Cold agglutinin disease (CAD) is an autoimmune hemolytic ane -
mia mediated by monoclonal cold agglutinins (CA), a term 
describing the ability of the autoantibody to agglutinate erythro -
cytes at optimum temperatures below normal central body tem -
perature [ 1–4 ]. CA in CAD are usually of the immunoglobulin 
M (IgM) class, and they are produced by clonal B-lymphocytes in 
the bone marrow, to some extent probably also by mature 
plasma cells [ 5–8 ]. Binding of CA to their antigen on the cell 
surface causes erythrocytes to agglutinate and, at the same time, 
induces complement-mediated hemolysis [ 4 , 9 ]. Therefore, the 
pathogenesis can result in heterogeneous clinical and hemato -
logic features: non-hemolytic, non-complement mediated symp -
toms and signs as well as hemolytic, complement-mediated 
manifestations, with considerable individual variations [ 2 , 10 ].
Recent literature distinguishes between CAD (previously 
termed primary or idiopathic CAD) and secondary CA syndrome 
(CAS) [ 11 , 12 ]. While CAD is considered a well-defined clinico -
pathologic entity as will be discussed below, CAS is a similar, 
cold hemolytic syndrome arising from a variety of underlying 
clinical diseases, such as specific infections ( Mycoplasma pneu -
moniae pneumonia, Epstein-Barr virus infection, SARS-CoV2 
infection, and others), polyclonal immunologic disorders, or 
overt malignancies (mostly non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphomas) 
[ 12–15 ]. Only CAD will be further addressed in this review. 
Defining criteria are listed in Table 1 [ 5 , 6 , 16 ]. The prevalence 
and incidence of patients diagnosed with CAD seem higher in 
cool or cold climates as compared to warmer regions [ 2 ]. In 
Norway and Denmark, the prevalence is 18–20 per million inha -
bitants, and the incidence is 1.9 per million inhabitants per year, 
while the corresponding figures for North Italy have been calcu -
lated to ¼ of these [ 2 , 17 ]. CAD is more common in women than 
in men (male:female ratio 0.56), and the median age at clinical 
disease onset is 65–67 years [ 2 , 3 , 18 ].
Today, several established and investigational therapies are 
available for the treatment of CAD [ 10 , 12 , 19–23 ]. As different 
treatment approaches target specific steps in the pathogen -
esis, the clinical and hematologic features of CAD will raise 
individual challenges and require individualization of therapy. 
This review will address the respective clinical features as 
related to the pathogenesis and their impact on existing 
therapy and further drug development.

In clinical practice, a clonal LPD is not detected in all patients. 
Appropriate precautions are critical for obtaining sufficient sen -
sitivity in finding the monoclonal Ig; blood samples must be 
drawn in prewarmed tubes and handled at 37–38°C until serum 
has been removed from the clot or plasma removed from the 
cell pellet ( Table 1 ) [ 12 , 28 , 29 ]. Furthermore, a systematic study of 
232 patients showed that histopathologic signs of a clonal B-cell 
disorder, in particular the CAD-specific features, were more fre -
quently disclosed by central, highly specialized pathology 
departments as compared to unselected pathology laboratories 
[ 2 ]. In a study of 20 patients among whom four had no mono -
clonal protein or evidence of lymphoma, molecular evidence of 
clonality could be detected in three of these four [ 30 ]. When 
both heavy and light chain gene sequences were tested, clonal 
rearrangement was shown for at least one of them; and in the 
vast majority, clonality was evident for both chains [ 31 ]. If sensi -
tive enough molecular techniques are used, clonality is expected 
to be demonstrated in all CAD bone marrow samples.
The diagnosis of CAD relies on the clinical and hematolo -
gic features and immunologic tests ( Table 1 ). However, bone 
marrow biopsy and flow cytometry should be obtained in all 
patients [ 7 , 12 , 19 , 22 ]. Typical differential diagnoses to CA- 
associated LPD are lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL), mar -
ginal zone lymphoma (MZL), small lymphocytic lymphoma, 
unclassified lymphoproliferation, and normal bone marrow 
[ 2 , 5 ]. Most CAD patients can also be classified as having 
a monoclonal gammopathy of clinical significance (MGCS) 
[ 32 ]. Histomorphologic, immune phenotypic, cytogenetic, 
and molecular features that may help differentiating CA- 
associated LPD from LPL and MZL have been described in 
detail by Guenther and colleagues [ 7 ].
Exome sequencing revealed non-synonymous mutations 
in KMT2D in over 66% of CAD patients, with most of the 
mutations expected to be inactivating mutations [ 33 , 34 ]. 
Non-synonymous mutations were found in CARD11, CXCR4 
and IGLL5 in over 20% of cases. The CARD11 and CXCR4 
mutations, expected to affect the regulation of the nuclear 
factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, correlated with lower hemo -
globin (Hb) levels [ 33 ]. The majority of samples showed 
mutations in genes involved in the regulation of the NF-κB 
pathway. In addition, the most affected pathways are related 
to chromatin modifying enzymes and chromatin organiza -
tion. Gain of chromosome 3 was detected in almost all the 
samples. Gain of chromosome 12 or 18 was detected in most 
of the cases and was associated with response to chemoim -
munotherapy [ 35 ]. RNA sequencing revealed top 20 differen -
tially expressed genes in clonal CAD B cells compared to 
normal memory B cells, including 19 upregulated genes: 
SLC4A1, SPTA1, YBX3, TESC, HBD, AHSP, TRAF1, HBA2, RHAG, 
CA1, SPTB, IL10, UBASH3B, ALAS2, HBA1, CRYM, RGCC, KANK2, 
IGHV4–34, and one down-regulated gene CR1 [ 36 ]. The 
MYD88 L265P mutation, characteristic for LPL, is not present 
in CAD [ 5 , 7 , 31 ].
2.2. Cold agglutinin in CAD
CA in CAD are usually specific for the I antigen, a carbohydrate 
antigen expressed on erythrocytes and most other cells in the 
vast majority of humans aged >1.5 years [ 37–39 ]. In rare cases, 
the antigen specificity can be Pr (a protein surface antigen) or 
IH [ 39 ]. The thermal amplitude, i.e. the temperature range 
within which the CA will agglutinate erythrocytes, is specific 
for each individual CA [ 40 ].
The immunoglobulin class is nearly always IgM, but can be 
IgG in up to 5% of the patients [ 2 , 41 ]. The light chain type is 
usually κ, whereas λ light chain restriction is found in 5–6% of 
the patients [ 2 , 25 ]. The encoding gene sequence for the heavy 
chain variable region is IGVH4–34 in most cases, while the light 
chain is encoded by IGKV3–20 (or the very similar gene IGKV3– 
15 ) in more than half of the cases [ 31 , 42 ]. In the IGVH4–34 
corresponding protein structure, termed VH4–34 ( Figure 2 ), 
the hydrophobic patch of framework region 1 as well as the 
complementarity-determining region 3 are essential for speci -
ficity and affinity of the antigen binding [ 31 , 43 , 44 ]. 
Additionally, in IGHV4–34 , inactivating mutations of the 
N-glycosylation site that is located in complementary- 
determining region 2 and mutations in hot spot located in 
framework region 3 correlated with lower hemoglobin levels 
[ 31 ]. The VH4–34 structure is also expressed on the surface of 
the CA-producing B-cells [ 45–47 ].

2.3. Agglutination of erythrocytes
In the cooler, acral parts of the circulation, CA with 
a sufficiently high thermal amplitude will bind to the surface 
I antigen on the erythrocytes and cause them to agglutinate, 
while the antigen-antibody complex will induce complement- 
dependent hemolysis as further described below ( Figure 3 ) 
[ 2 , 4 , 53 ]. The aggregates are readily visible by microscopy of 
peripheral blood smear, usually, even macroscopically in the 
tube [ 54 ]. With a diameter several times the erythrocyte dia -
meter, these agglutinates will impair passage through the 
capillaries and also explain the marked, partly spurious macro -
cytosis seen in patients with CAD [ 55 ]. After rewarming in the 
central circulation, the CA detaches from the cell, and the 
agglutinates disintegrate.
CA titers of more than 100 000 may be observed even in 
the absence of markedly elevated IgM, and there is no correla -
tion between IgM levels and CA titer [ 1 , 2 ]. Furthermore, none 
of these variables correlates with Hb levels or biochemical 
markers of hemolysis [ 1 , 2 , 39 ]. These observations indicate 
that the biologic activity of the autoantibody is variable, but 
very high in some patients. IgM also exists in a hexameric 
form, which is still more biologically active than the common, 
pentameric form [ 56 , 57 ]. Hexameric IgM has been identified in 
serum from patients with CAD, but any clinical consequences 
remain elusive [ 58 ].
Although the I antigen is also expressed on leukocytes and 
platelets, patients rarely have leukopenia or thrombocytope -
nia [ 2 , 59 , 60 ]. However, neutrophil aggregation can be seen in 
the blood smear in occasional patients with CAD [ 61 , 62 ].
2.4. Complement-mediated hemolysis
Complement involvement in the pathogenesis of CAD was 
demonstrated before 1960 [ 9 ], and during the last decades, 
the classical complement pathway dependent hemolysis 
has been extensively elucidated [ 63–65 ]. Comprehensive 
descriptions have been published elsewhere [ 65–67 ]. In 
short, antigen-bound IgM is a potent classical pathway 
trigger that activates the C1qrs complex, resulting in activa -
tion of the C1s serine protease, which in turn splits C4 and 
C2. Next, C3 convertase (C4b2a) is formed and splits C3, 
leaving erythrocytes coated with C3b ( Figure 3 ). C3b- 
opsonized cells are prone to phagocytosis by the mono -
nuclear phagocytic system, mainly in the liver (extravascular 
hemolysis) [ 53 , 65 ]. On the surviving cells, C3b is further 
cleaved and eventually replaced by its end product, C3d. 
As macrophages have no receptor for this membrane- 
bound split product, C3d-opsonized erythrocytes escape 
further attack by the mononuclear phagocytic system, but 
can be detected in the laboratory by the monospecific 
direct antiglobulin test (DAT). In severe hemolytic disease

and during acute exacerbations, C3b will further act by 
combining with C4b2a to form C5 convertase, which in 
turn leads to cleavage of C5, activation of the terminal 
complement cascade, and cell membrane destruction by 
the C5b-9 complex (intravascular hemolysis) [ 68 , 69 ]. The 
complement activation also results in release of soluble 
split products, of which the anaphylotoxins C3a and C5a 
have proinflammatory properties [ 39 , 68 ].
Because of continuous consumption, patients often have 
low serum concentration of classical pathway components, in 
particular C4. In a study of 232 unselected patients, median C4 
level was 0.07 g/L (lower limit of reference range [LLN]: 0.13 g/ 
L), and levels were below LLN in 72% of the patients [ 2 ]. In 
24% of the patients, even C3 levels were below LLN. Therefore, 
C4 depletion is probably rate-limiting for complement- 
mediated hemolysis [ 4 , 69 ]. If an acute phase reaction occurs, 
the production of complement components increases; more 
complement is available, and hemolysis exacerbates [ 69 ]. In 
theory, other mechanisms of classical pathway enhancement 
may also contribute, for example, additional activation by 
microbial antigens or by acute-phase proteins bound to 
pathogens or affected cells [ 70 ].
Complement receptor 1 (CR1, CD35) is a glycoprotein 
expressed on several types of blood cells including erythro -
cytes, neutrophils, and lymphocytes [ 71 ]. It is a receptor for 
C3b and C4b and also functions as a complement inhibitor 
by acting as a cofactor for inactivation of these components 
[ 72 ]. Furthermore, CR1 has anti-B-cell activity by inhibiting 
proliferation as well as differentiation and Ig production 
[ 73 , 74 ]. A recent gene expression study in which clonal 
B-cells from 12 CAD patients and IgM-expressing memory 
B-cells from 4 healthy subjects were analyzed, found 11-fold 
downregulation of CR1 expression in CAD B-cells as com -
pared to normal memory B-cells, both at the RNA and 
protein level [ 36 ]. Thus, downregulated CR1 may increase 
proliferation, activation, and antibody production in clonal 
B-cells in CAD.
3. Clinical features of CAD
3.1. Complement versus non-complement mediated 
disease features
Table 2 provides an overview of hemolytic versus non- 
hemolytic manifestations and the role of complement for the 
respective clinical and hematologic features of CAD 
[ 1 , 2 , 18 , 75 , 76 ].
3.2. Hemolytic anemia
In a multinational, observational study of 232 patients with 
CAD, median Hb level was 9.2 g/dL (range, 4.5–15.3 g/dL) [ 2 ], 
which is in line with other studies [ 3 , 18 ]. Twenty-seven percent 
of the patients had severe anemia, defined as Hb <8 g/dL, 
while 38% presented with mild anemia (10 g/dL-LLN) or com -
pensated hemolysis [ 2 ]. Details are shown in Figure 4 , which 
also shows that Hb levels correlated inversely with levels of 
total bilirubin and lactate dehydrogenase but not with IgM 
levels [ 2 , 39 ].
3.3. Cold-induced circulatory symptoms
Symptoms from the acral circulation, as listed in Table 2 , were 
described already in the earliest reports on CAD and related to 
impaired passage of cell agglutinates through the capillaries 
as explained in Section 2.3 ( Figure 3 ) [ 1 , 77 ]. Such symptoms 
have been reported in 57–91% of unselected patients [ 2 , 18 ] 
and can occur even in the absence of particularly low ambient 
temperatures. Although the frequency may vary with climate 
and region, the circulatory symptoms are likely underreported 
because of methodologic issues, such as study design, the lack 
of a validated scoring tool, semantic unclarity, and the fact 
that these symptoms are not part of the Common 
Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [ 78 ]. In a large obser -
vational study of CAD, we classified the circulatory symptoms 
in grade 1 (acrocyanosis only), grade 2 (Raynaud-like symp -
toms interfering with daily living), and grade 3 (ulcerations or 
gangrene); a scale that has not been validated, but seems 
robust for use in therapeutic trials [ 2 , 79 ]. Typical patients 
with grade 2 symptoms will have difficulties in staying out -
doors even in moderately cool weather and taking food from 
the refrigerator without wearing gloves, and even isolated 
acrocyanosis may sometimes be disabling [ 80 ]. Therefore, cir -
culatory symptoms constitute an independent indication for 
treatment in some patients [ 10 , 19 ], and they should be 
assessed in therapeutic trials.
As explained in Section 2.3, the circulatory symptoms are 
not complement-mediated. Furthermore, the impaired pas -
sage through capillaries hardly explains the large inter- 
individual variation in these symptoms. Coexistence of CA 
and cryoglobulin has been observed already in early reports 
[ 77 ], probably best explained as two distinct properties of the 
same Ig, but has not been systematically explored until 
recently. However, a recent retrospective study of 134 patients 
with type I cryoglobulinemia seen at two third-line reference

3.4. Fatigue
In a questionnaire-based study on symptom burden in CAD, 90% 
of the respondents reported fatigue, although this cohort might 
be selected [ 76 ]. In a group of patients who needed treatment, 
the mean pretreatment Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness 
Therapy (FACIT) fatigue scale score was 32.5, which is similar to 
scores seen in patients with advanced cancer or severe rheu -
matic disease [ 83 , 84 ]. It might be tempting to explain fatigue as 
caused by the anemia. However, fatigue also occurs among CAD 
patients with mild anemia, and studies of therapeutic comple -
ment inhibition have shown even faster improvement in FACIT 
scores than in Hb levels [ 84 , 85 ]. Therefore, fatigue is probably 
also associated with the complement activation by itself, via 
release of proinflammatory split products as described in 
Section 2.4. Additional causes, such as chronic bone marrow 
compensation or split products of the hemolysis itself, cannot 
be excluded but remain unproven. Because of fatigue and circu -
latory symptoms, CAD often implies a bad quality of life, and 
many patients report a considerable psychosocial impact [ 76 , 84 ].
3.5. Acute exacerbations
During or following infectious diseases, major trauma, major 
surgery, or occasionally after vaccination, patients with CAD 
can have acute exacerbations of the hemolytic anemia because 
of additional complement activation as described in section 2.4 
[ 69 , 86–89 ]. The exact percentage of patients who experience this 
phenomenon is unknown, as such exacerbations are likely 
underreported because of the retrospective design of most 
descriptive studies. Therefore, the best estimate at 74% [ 18 ] 
should be regarded as a minimum figure. Exposure to low ambi -
ent temperatures can also trigger exacerbations [ 17 , 19 , 90 ]. In 
most cases, the severity of hemolysis will gradually return to 
the patient’s usual level after removal of the cause of exacerba -
tion, but sometimes the worsening can persist [ 19 , 69 ].
3.6. Thrombosis
The estimated risk of thrombosis varies between studies, prob -
ably due to methodological differences. Based on the largest 
studies, the overall relative risk can be calculated to 2–3 as 
compared to age-matched, healthy people [ 2 , 75 ]. Severely 
hemolytic patients, in particular those with marked intravas -
cular hemolysis, seem to have a higher risk [ 75 , 84 , 91 , 92 ]. 
Studies from the USA and Europe have indicated a higher 
risk of venous versus arterial thromboembolic events, while 
a Japanese study found the opposite [ 75 , 93 ].
As multiple points of interaction have been identified 
between the complement and the coagulation cascades, the 
complement activation can explain the risk of thrombosis, at 
least in part [ 94 , 95 ]. Data indicating that the elevated risk is 
more or less eliminated by therapeutic complement inhibition 
are consistent with this explanation [ 84 , 85 ]. The hemolysis by 
itself is also thought to contribute, as AIHA are associated with 
a risk of thrombosis [ 96 ].

4. Established and investigational therapies
4.1. Treatment options
Not all patients with CAD require drug treatment. Thermal 
protection remains a cornerstone in supportive care, at least 
in patients who are not in complete remission (CR) after 
therapy [ 1 , 19 , 97 ]. Patients and health care professionals 
should be explained that the purpose is not only relief of 
circulatory symptoms but also protection against exacerbation 
of hemolysis [ 90 ]. In addition, management includes specific 
precautions in transfusion, avoidance of complement-rich 
blood products, avoidance of cold infusions, and prompt 
treatment of bacterial infections, as described elsewhere 
[ 12 , 19 , 69 , 97 ].
With the increasing number of available therapies, how -

prevention of thrombosis in CAD. Tentatively, we recommend 
administration of low-molecular heparin in prophylaxis doses 
or a direct oral anticoagulant in patients with additional risk 
factors, severe hemolytic anemia that is not under control, or 
acute exacerbations [ 12 , 19 , 22 , 75 ].

discussed in this subsection should ideally be confirmed 
across different demographics by real-life studies. Such studies 
have only been performed to a small extent and with rela -

bility of large real-life studies remains uncertain in such a rare 
disease. It is hoped that CAD patient registries such as the 
Cadence registry ( https://coldagglutininnews.com/health- 
insights/cadence-registry/ ) may turn out to be helpful.
4.2. Future prospects
First, considering the advantages and drawbacks of chemoim -
munotherapy, B-cell directed approaches should be further 
developed. The promising results of the retrospective ibrutinib 
study ( Table 4 ) [ 103 ] should warrant a prospective trial of 
a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi) in CAD. In the ibruti -
nib study, patients received 420 mg/day, and responses were 
quick (<4 weeks) with improvement of hemolytic anemia as 
well as circulatory symptoms. Indeed, a prospective study with 
zanubrutinib, a second-generation non-covalent BTKi is 
expected to open in the first half of 2024 (HOVON 169 CAD 
study; ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06067048; https://hovon.nl/en/ 
trials/ho169 ). This study will be opened in the Netherlands, 
Denmark, Belgium, and Norway; and patients can be included 
not only based on the presence of hemolytic anemia but also 
based on severe circulatory symptoms alone (at least grade 2 
symptoms).
Case reports have described successful use of the anti- 
CD38 monoclonal antibody daratumumab in patients failing 
chemoimmunotherapy [ 80 , 119 ]. In a recent, retrospective 
study of 19 patients with refractory AIHA, daratumumab at 
various regimens induced a response in 4 of 7 patients with 
CAD [ 104 ]. Responders achieved relief of circulatory symptoms 
as well as hemolytic anemia. The therapeutic effect might be 
explained in part by activity against long-lived plasma cells, as 
most of these patients were heavily pretreated [ 8 , 108 ]. The 
study also demonstrated an immune modulatory effect via 
CD38 positive T-cells [ 104 ].
Second, we will probably also see new complement- 
directed therapies. Riliprubart (BIVV020, SAR445088) is 
a modified monoclonal IgG4 that selectively targets activated 
C1s and contains mutations that increase its binding to the 
neonatal Fc receptor, thereby providing improved recircula -
tion of the drug with increased biologic half-life [ 120 , 121 ]. In 
a phase 1B trial, 12 patients with CAD received a single dose of 
riliprubart and were subsequently followed for 15 weeks [ 122 ]. 
Rapid improvements in Hb and bilirubin, classical complement 
pathway blockade, and normalization of C4 levels were 
observed and were sustained throughout the observation 
period. If confirmed by further clinical studies, this substance 
will have an efficacy and safety similar to that of sutimlimab 
with a much less frequent dosage [ 122 ]. Candidate drugs also 
include the C1q inhibitor ANX005 and the C2 inhibitor ARGX- 
117, none of which has been tested in published clinical trials 
[ 123–125 ].
Third, entirely new principles of therapy are being developed. 
HLST-01 (HMBD-011) is a first-in-class, monoclonal IgG1 specific 
for the VH4–34 protein structure of the CA heavy chain ( Figure 2 ; 
see Section 2.2) [ 45 , 126 ]. As this novel monoclonal antibody has 
the potential to selectively target VH4–34 expressing B-cells as

Physiologic modulators of B-cell development, differentia -

family cytokines ‘B-cell activating factor’ (BAFF) and ‘a prolif -

and sibeprenlimab are monoclonal antibodies developed to 
inhibit this cytokine system, but their efficacy in autoimmune 
disorders is limited by specificity only for either BAFF or APRIL 
[ 128 ]. Povetacicept (ALPN-303) is an engineered molecule con -
sisting of a modified ‘TNF receptor superfamily 13B’ (TACI) 
receptor domain, which inhibits both BAFF and APRIL. Potent 
inhibition of class-switched B-cells and plasma cells has been 
shown in vitro , and testing in animal models has demon -
strated suppression of serum Ig, clinical effect on inducible 
systemic lupus erythematosus, and, probably, clinical tolerabil -
ity [ 127 ]. Although preclinical studies have focused on poly -
clonal autoimmune disorders, a possible role of this drug has 
also been suggested in the treatment of CAD [ 129 ]. In theory, 
however, such an effect may be limited by autonomy of the 
clonal B-cells. An open-label ‘basket’ trial has been activated, 
studying the safety and efficacy of povetacicept in autoim -
mune cytopenias including CAD (ClinicalTrials.gov, 
NCT05757570) [ 129 ].
5. Choice and development of therapy: impact of 
clinical features
Considering the different targets of therapeutic approaches 
and the variation in clinical features, therapy for CAD should 
be individualized [ 10 , 20 , 23 ]. The Expert Opinion section 
( Section 7 ) will address the consequences for clinical practice 
and further research.
6. Conclusion
The well-elucidated, but relatively complex pathogenesis in 
CAD consists of several steps, non-complement mediated as 
well as entirely complement-dependent that explain 
a variety of clinical features. Today, several therapeutic 
options are available and novel approaches are in develop -
ment, each targeting specific steps in pathogenesis. Some 
are fixed-duration treatments, while others require long- 
term maintenance therapy. These heterogeneities will affect 
the choice of therapy in individual patients. 
A subclassification of CAD into clinical phenotypes is useful 
for the choice of existing therapy, for clinical studies, and 
for further drug development. Patients with CAD requiring 
therapy should be considered for clinical trials.

administration of sutimlimab [ 84 , 110 ], although it has not 
been systematically tested in fulminant cases. Unfortunately, 
the availability and affordability of this fast-acting comple -

ably depends on indefinite continuation of therapy, transition 
to a time-limited, B-cell directed treatment offering long 
response duration might be considered after having obtained 
control of the hemolytic anemia. Although there are no pub -
lished, hard data to support such ‘bridging,’ it will probably be 
a good approach provided sufficient overlap and close mon -
itoring in the transition phase [ 22 , 130 ]. It could be argued that 
complement blockade might reduce the efficacy of B-cell 
depleting agents, in particular monoclonal antibodies, by elim -
inating complement-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. The 
favorable effect of rituximab in CAD patients who are already 
C4 depleted by consumption as explained in Section 2.4, how -
ever, indicates activity of other mechanisms of action, for 
example, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity [ 99 , 131 ].
As complement inhibition does not improve the non- 
complement mediated, non-hemolytic disease features and 
may even provoke or worsen such symptoms in single cases 
(Section 4.4) [ 85 ], B-cell directed therapy is preferred in the 
first line in patients with prominent circulatory manifestations. 
The minority of patients in whom circulatory symptoms are 
the only indication for treatment should not receive comple -
ment inhibitors.
In a global perspective, challenges in choice of therapy also 
arise from differences in healthcare settings. In many low-to- 
middle income countries, even rituximab is not reimbursed for 
the treatment of CAD [ 116 , 132 ]. Sutimlimab, at an estimated 
cost for one-year treatment of more than $260 000 in the USA, 
has been approved in relatively few countries: the USA, the 
European Union, and Japan [ 110 ]. Even in several European 
countries and Canada, this drug is not reimbursed or available 
outside clinical trials, and in low-to-middle income countries 
worldwide, such expensive therapies will be practically una -
vailable. Solving these problems will be outside the scope of 
this article.
The clinical phenotypes proposed in Section 3.7 ( Table 3 ) will 
also be relevant for future drug development. Further studies of 
specific B-cell targeting agents, such as BTKi, may improve ther -
apy for many patients, including those with phenotype 2 (hemo -
lytic anemia with circulatory symptoms grade 2–3) and 3 
(circulatory symptoms with compensated hemolysis) [ 103 ]. 
Effective against complement-mediated as well as non- 
complement mediated disease features, daratumumab may 
become a useful option in refractory CAD [ 104 ]. The novel mono -
clonal antibody HLST-01, selectively targeting VH4–34 heavy 
chain CA and VH4–34 expressing B-cells may also, if supported 
by further studies, become an interesting therapeutic tool in all 
clinical phenotypes of CAD [ 45 ]. Finally, there is a need for sys -
tematic studies in the rare subpopulation of patients with fulmi -
nant (life-threatening) CAD, who probably have more terminal 
complement involvement [ 92 , 133 ].
