# A randomized controlled trial (RCT) of Juniver, a new digital intervention for eating disorder symptoms
**Authors:** Qi L, Faure E, Allen K
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-10127439/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10127439/v1)

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>Juniver is an app-based program for eating disorder symptoms combining neuroscience, CBT, an AI support tool configured specifically for eating disorder symptoms, and moderated peer groups. This study evaluated Juniver’s efficacy using a randomized controlled trial design, assigning participants aged ≥16-years to Juniver immediately or after 12-weeks (wait-list control). Participants completed measures of eating disorder symptoms, depression, anxiety, psychosocial impairment, and stress at baseline and 4-, 8-, 12-, 16-, 20 -and 24-weeks. The primary outcome was global eating disorder symptomatology at 12-weeks. Analyses used baseline-adjusted mixed models for repeated measures, with restricted maximum likelihood. N=357 participants were randomized. Juniver participants (n=179) showed significantly greater reductions than control (n=178) on the primary outcome as well as binge eating, clinical impairment, depression and anxiety. Adverse events were n=18 for Juniver and n=32 for control, including 1x control participant emergency hospitalization. Results support the efficacy and safety of Juniver for eating disorder symptoms.</p>

IntroducƟon 69 
Eating disorders are characterized by persistent disturbances in eating behavior and 70 
related cognitions that significantly impair physical health, psychological wellbeing, 71 
interpersonal relationships, and quality of life (1). They include anorexia nervosa (AN), 72 
bulimia nervosa (BN), binge eating disorder (BED) and ‘other specified feeding or eating 73 
disorders’ (OSFED). Prevalence rates are high and growing (2). Approximately 15% of 74 
women and 2% of men meet full diagnostic criteria over their lifetime (3) and up to half of 75 
female university students report clinically significant eating disorder symptoms (4). Eating 76 
disorders frequently persist and they occur across ages, body sizes, racial and cultural 77 
groups, socioeconomic backgrounds, and sexual orientations. Only 6% of those diagnosed 78 
with an eating disorder are medically underweight (5). BED accounts for approximately two-79 
thirds of all eating disorder cases (6) but has the longest delays before treatment access, 80 
approximately 5 years on average (7).  81 
Whilst early detection is a key factor in outcomes (7), shame, stigma, and the 82 
complexity of navigating the care system delay diagnosis and treatment. Up to 70% of 83 
individuals with an eating disorder never access specialist treatment, and this figure has 84 
remained unchanged for the last decade (8). When treatment is sought, wait times for 85 
specialist care are long (e.g., between 8 and 12 months in the UK) (9). During this time, 86 
symptoms often worsen (10,11).  87 
The efficacy of eating disorder treatment is also limited. Cognitive-behavioral 88 
therapy (CBT) is the treatment of choice for most eating disorders (BN, BED and related non-89 
underweight eating disorders) (12-14) but is moderately effective at best, with post-90 
treatment remission rates of 29–43% (14). Again, these outcomes haven’t changed over the 91 
last decade (15). Further to this, the costs associated with eating disorders are substantial 92 
and growing (16). Many affected individuals require recurrent treatment either for their 93 
eating disorder or related physical or psychological comorbidities. Healthcare costs for 94 
individuals with eating disorders are four times higher than for those without (16,17).  95 
In this context, there is an urgent need for new eating disorder treatments that are 96 
more accessible and efficacious. Scalable approaches are also essential, because current 97 
treatment approaches are resource-intensive and unable to meet demand (8, 11, 18). 98 
Updated treatment approaches need to provide support in the moment where someone 99

IntervenƟon 205 
The Juniver app features content modules, micro-intervention tools, daily check-ins 206 
and meal tracking, moderated peer groups, and an AI assistant configured specifically for 207 
eating disorder symptoms.  208 
The program is designed to support self-management of eating disorder symptoms 209 
through skill-building (e.g. cognitive reframing, engaging with longer-term goals, increasing 210 
behavioral alternatives to bingeing, purging, or restriction), emotional regulation, tailored 211 
support (e.g. to users’ individual goals and preferred coaching style), and the reduction of 212 
isolation and shame through shared experiences. 213 
Daily check-ins and meal tracking are framed to support self-efficacy by helping users 214 
to identify strategies that support their management of urges, and the common triggers 215 
that contribute to setbacks. Optional notifications in the form of emails, in-app push 216 
notifications and text messages are also used. 217 
The AI Assistant is grounded in Juniver's curriculum and refined through scenario-218 
specific decision trees and prompt engineering to deliver clinically appropriate guidance. It 219 
explicitly does not provide medical advice; when users seek it, they are directed to a medical 220 
professional. When suicidal ideation is detected, the user is sent crisis resources localized to 221 
their region. 222
