# An unusual Co-S bond links B(12) chaperones in an interprotein complex.
**Authors:** Mascarenhas R, Ruetz M, Heitman N, Hall RL, Brunold TC, Banerjee R
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2507700122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2507700122)

## Content

Chaperones in trafficking pathways ensure specificity of transition metal loading and protection against adventitious side reactions. In B12, an essential cofactor for humans, a cobalt ion is coordinated to a >1.3 kDa tetrapyrrolic scaffold, posing logistical challenges for its translocation. In this study, we report the 3.4 Å crystal structure of the human MMACHC and MMADHC B12 chaperones, tethered via a rarely seen covalent cobalt-sulfur bond. B12 is bound in the base-off state to MMACHC, with Cys-261 on MMADHC serving as the upper axial ligand; the lower-axial position is vacant. The propensity of thiolato-cobalamin derivatives bound to MMACHC to undergo spontaneous decomposition via general acid catalysis or reduction/oxidation chemistry is averted in the interprotein complex with MMADHC. An exposed face in the complex suggests an exit route for B12. No known clinical variants localize to the interprotein interface, consistent with the cobalt-sulfur bond being key to forming the high-affinity complex.

Metal cofactors are essential partners for approximately a third of the proteome ( 1 ) and trafficked to client proteins via dedicated chaperones that promote specificity and avert adventitious side reactions. Cobalamin (or vitamin B12) is an organometallic cofactor that is needed by just two human enzymes ( 2 ) but handled by an army of intracellular chaperones, which exploit redox-linked coordination chemistry to translocate and concurrently convert the cofactor to its biologically active forms ( 3 – 5 ). Moving oversized cargo like B12 (>1.3 kDa) poses logistical challenges, and the use of cobalt–sulfur (Co–S) coordination is emerging as a strategy for translocating B12 in the trafficking pathway ( 6 ).

The MMACHC (for methylmalonic aciduria type C and homocystinuria, also known as CblC) chaperone is notable for its lax specificity at the β- (or upper axial) ligand position of cobalamin; it is postulated to be the cytoplasmic receiver as the cofactor exits the lysosome ( 7 ). The α- (or lower axial) dimethylbenzimidazole (DMB) ligand is sequestered in a hydrophobic pocket flanking the MMACHC active site, which gives rise to the “base-off” state ( 8 – 14 ). The β-ligand transferase activity of MMACHC processes incoming alkyl- and cyano-cobalamins to a common cob(II)alamin intermediate (Fig. 1A) ( 8 , 9 , 15 ). Removal of the β-ligand frees up the upper axial site for coordination by a cysteine thiolate ligand on MMADHC (methylmalonic aciduria type D and homocystinuria, also known as CblD), resulting in an interprotein complex held together via an uncommon Co–S bond (Fig. 1A) ( 16 ). Cys-261 in MMADHC serves as the upper axial ligand to MMACHC-bound cobalamin. The MMACHC•B12–MMADHC complex is postulated to sit at the branchpoint between the cytosolic and mitochondrial trafficking routes.

Co–S bonds, which are susceptible to oxidation, reduction, and ligand exchange reactions, are generally rare in nature and are poorly studied. Two bacterial proteins have been reported to use a cysteine thiolate as α-axial ligand to B12: the BtuM transporter ( 17 ) and HgcA, which methylates mercury ( 18 ). In both cases, the thiolate ligand is presumed to influence chemical reactivity at the β-face via trans effects. In contrast, MMADHC exploits β-axial Co–S coordination for cofactor loading onto methionine synthase ( 6 ). In principle, the α- or β-axial ligand can function as a coordination handle for moving the cofactor between chaperones in the trafficking pathway ( 5 ). A sulfur ligand is well suited for ligand exchange; sulfur coordination to cobalt induces significantly different electronic properties compared to the coordination of carbon and oxygen β-ligands that are more commonly found in cobalamins ( 19 , 20 ).

In this study, we present a structural snapshot of an interprotein complex tethered via a Co–S bond, which reveals how Cys-261 on human MMADHC reaches into and coordinates the cobalt ion in the active site of MMACHC. Mutagenesis of the interfacial residues does not disrupt the complex, revealing the primacy of the Co–S bond for stabilizing the interprotein complex. To better understand the role of an interprotein complex versus low molecular weight (LMW) thiolato-ligands in stabilizing the Co–S bond, we synthesized a series of cobalamin derivatives with sulfur ligands of varying bulk and characterized them in the “base-on” (i.e. in solution) and “base-off” (i.e. bound to MMACHC) states. We found that cobalamins with LMW sulfur ligands are susceptible to spontaneous decomposition when bound to MMACHC. In contrast, when the thiolato-ligand is donated by MMADHC, the Co–S bond is protected from reduction/oxidation chemistry and formation of aquocobalamin, which would be resistant to translocation from MMACHC, is averted.

The crystal structure of the interprotein complex between human MMACHC and MMADHC with B12 bound was solved at a 3.4 Å resolution by molecular replacement (Table 1). Each molecule in the asymmetric unit contained four copies of MMACHC and MMADHC. B12 is bound to each MMACHC monomer in the base-off state with the DMB tail extended away from the corrin ring in a side pocket (Fig. 1 B and C) as seen in previous MMACHC structures ( 10 , 14 , 21 ). The upper axial face of the corrin ring is largely open and can accommodate a variety of ligands. In the complex, a β-hairpin (residues 254–266) on MMADHC, which houses the conserved vicinal cysteines, Cys-261 and Cys-262, is wedged into the MMACHC pocket, and Cys-261 is positioned to coordinate the cobalt ion via its sulfur atom (Fig. 1C). The vicinal Cys-262 residue was mutated to serine to avoid nucleophilic attack on the Co–S bond resulting in a disulfide bond on MMADHC and loss of the interprotein complex. The orientation of the Ser-262 side chain is unresolved in our structure. Continuous electron density between the cobalt and the Cys-261 sulfur suggests that the Co–S bond is intact; its bond length was restrained to 2.5 Å based on a prior EXAFS analysis of this complex ( 16 ). The Co–S bond in the complex is considerably longer than the corresponding bond in B12 coordinated to Cys-261 on MMADHC alone (2.2 Å) where the cofactor is bound in the base-on state. For comparison, the Co–S distances in HgcA ( 18 ) and BtuM ( 17 ) are 2.469 Å and 2.4 Å, respectively.

An overlay of MMACHC (PDB code: 3SC0, RMSD = 0.7Å) and MMADHC (PDB code 5CV0, RMSD = 0.5Å, Cα) on the MMACHC–MMADHC complex revealed no major conformational changes in either protein (Fig. 1D). The only notable change is in the β-hairpin loop (between residues 258–264), which is positioned for axial coordination by Cys-261 to cobalt bound to MMACHC. The protein–protein interface is restricted to a relatively small patch of residues that reside in loops on MMACHC (104–116) and MMADHC (186–197) (Fig. 1E). Salt bridges connect Arg-111 on MMACHC and the backbone of Met-186 and the side chain of Glu-244 on MMADHC, in addition to the salt bridge between the backbone of Asn-110 and Arg-197, and a hydrogen bond between the side chains of Asn-110 and Glu-194. The sidechain of Arg-112 on MMACHC is within 4 Å of Glu-194 on MMADHC and likely forms another salt bridge. Interestingly, the protein–protein interactions are confined to one face of the B12-binding pocket while the opposite face is “open”, exposing the corrin ring and the DMB tail to solvent (Fig. 1B). The open face suggests a possible route for B12 translocation from the MMACHC–MMADHC complex to the cytoplasmic target, methionine synthase.

The Co–S bond and a small set of residues are involved in the interaction between the two proteins. While MMACHC is a hotbed of cobalamin disorder mutations, fewer clinical mutations have been described in MMADHC ( 22 , 23 ). Interestingly, none of the clinical variants maps directly onto the protein–protein interface (Fig. 2A). The contribution of the interfacial residues to stabilizing the MMADHC–MMACHC complex was evaluated by alanine scanning mutagenesis of Glu-194, Arg-197, and Glu-244 on MMADHC. Additionally, the N110A/R111A MMACHC double mutant was evaluated since these two residues make multiple interactions. MMADHC was previously reported to form a tight complex with wild-type MMACHC with a KD of 40 ± 20 nM, as determined by isothermal titration calorimetry ( 16 ). In this study, the complex was generated by mixing MMADHC-B12 with MMACHC (Fig. 2B). The MMADHC mutations were introduced in the C262S background to avoid having a mixture of species with B12 coordinated to either Cys-261 or Cys-262. Surprisingly, high-affinity binding (KD <0.2 µM, i.e., at the lower limit of detection of the spectrophotometric assay) was observed between MMACHC and the E194A, R197A, and E244A variants of MMADHC-B12 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). In each case, the blue shift in the spectrum of MMADHC-B12 (λmax = 554 nm) to 424 and 505 nm in the presence of MMACHC signaled conversion of base-on to base-off thiolato-cobalamin (RSCbl). A decrease in affinity was observed for the interaction between N110A/R111A MMACHC and MMADHC (0.3 ± 0.1 µM) and E194A MMADHC (0.6 ± 0.1 µM) but not R197A MMADHC (Fig. 2 C–F). Thus, while the individual mutations do not affect complex formation, the N110A/R111A MMACHC–E194A MMADHC complex, lacking five hydrogen bonds at the interface, exhibits an ~15-fold decrease in affinity. In contrast, complex formation is not detected between wild-type MMACHC•cob(II)alamin and C261S MMADHC, which is unable to form a Co–S bond ( 16 ). The modest effect of the individual alanine substitutions reveals a relatively minor role for the interfacial residues and indicates that the Co–S bond is the primary contributor to the high-affinity interaction between the two proteins.

To understand the rationale for why MMADHC rather than an LMW thiol is used as a cobalt ligand for MMACHC-bound B12, a series of thiolato-cobalamins (RSCbls) was prepared by ligand exchange reactions between aquocobalamin (H2OCbl+) and the corresponding thiol (Fig. 3A). We specifically selected simple alkane thiols to exclude the possibility of hydrogen bond formation, an interaction that could arise with more complex thiols containing additional functional groups, such as GSH, when bound to MMACHC. As these alkane thiolato-cobalamin derivatives have not been previously reported, we also undertook a detailed characterization of their structural and spectroscopic properties. Addition of thiols with linear alkyl chains (ethyl-, n-propyl-, and n-butyl) resulted in a shift in the absorbance spectrum of H2OCbl+ to a spectrum resembling that reported for glutathionyl-cobalamin (GSCbl) ( 24 , 25 ) (Fig. 3B). The spectra of RSCbl with bulkier ligands (iso-propyl, sec-butyl, and tert-butyl) showed subtle differences in the γ-band (330 to 345 nm) which corresponds to π-π* transitions in the corrin ring (Fig. 3C). In iso-propyl- and sec-butyl-SCbl, the 336 nm peak was of higher intensity relative to the 330 nm peak as seen with the linear thiolato- ligands, while tert-butyl-SCbl, with the bulkiest ligand in this series, exhibited an even more pronounced increase in intensity (λmax = 344 nm).

Addition of methanethiol to a solution of H2OCbl+ led to a complex spectrum with peaks at 311 nm (shoulder at 323 nm) and 364 nm (Fig. 3D); HPLC analysis revealed that the product was a mixture of two species (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Based on the absorption spectra, the more polar compound (λmax = 539, 516, 365, 322, and 311 nm) was assigned as sulfitocobalamin (SO3Cbl)( 26 , 27 ) and the less polar compound (λmax = 540, 516, 363, and 326 nm) as CH3SCbl (Fig. 3D). Formation of SO3Cbl likely resulted from sulfite contamination in sodium methanethiolate and the 10-fold higher equilibrium constant for the reaction of sulfite (2 × 107 M−1) versus thiol (cysteine, 2 × 106 M−1) with H2OCbl+ ( 28 ).

RSCbls with bulky ligands (isopropyl-, sec-butyl-, and tert-butyl-SCbl) remained intact during HPLC purification. Of these, only isopropyl- and sec-butyl-SCbl yielded high-quality crystals suitable for structure elucidation. Crystals of iso-propyl-SCbl and sec-butyl-SCbl belonged to space group P212121 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) with Co–S bond lengths of 2.243 Å and 2.242 Å, respectively (SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2). For comparison, the Co–S bond lengths in N-acetylcysteinyl-SCbl ( 27 ), γ-glutamylcysteinyl-SCbl ( 24 ), and GSCbl ( 29 ) range between 2.250 and 2.295 Å (SI Appendix, Table S3). Thus, the ~0.05 Å increase in Co–S bond length from a propyl/butyl ligand to the tripeptidyl ligand in GSCbl appears to be correlated with an increase in bulkiness. The S–Co–N1 bond angles in iso-propyl-SCbl and sec-butyl-SCbl are 86.3° and 86.7°, respectively, similar to the values for N-acetylcysteinyl-Cbl (87.7°), γ-glutamylcysteinyl-Cbl (87.4°), and GSCbl (88.8°). A maximal difference of only 3.4° is seen within this group of thiol ligands.

The reactivity of base-off RSCbls, pertinent to the B12 coordination state in MMACHC, is poorly understood. We were however, unable to synthesize the corresponding base-off derivatives starting with cobinamide, which lacking the DMB tail, is perforce, base-off. Addition of ethanethiol to aquocobinamide (H2OCbi2+) led to the formation of cob(II)inamide (λmax = 472 nm) followed by reoxidation to H2OCbi2+ (λmax = 493 and 517 nm) in air (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). An exception to this behavior was seen with tert-butanethiol, which led to an increase in intensity at 413 nm (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). The stability of tert-butyl-SCbi could be due to the bulkiness of the ligand, which prevents dethiolation by a second equivalent of the thiol, or could result from the increase in electron donation of the tert-butyl ligand, stabilizing the Co–S bond.

The B12 cofactor in the MMACHC–MMADHC complex is bound in the base-off state in which the DMB tail is found in a hydrophobic side pocket of MMACHC (Fig. 1A) ( 10 , 21 ). To establish the role of MMADHC versus an LMW thiol as the preferred ligand donor, the stabilities and spectroscopic properties of the RSCbl model compounds bound to MMACHC were characterized. MMACHC was reconstituted with methyl-, iso-propyl-, sec-butyl-, and tert-butyl-SCbl and SO3Cbl (Fig. 4A). A blue shift of the αβ bands in the 500 to 600 nm range, characteristic of a base-on to base-off conversion, was observed for each derivative. RSCbls with bulky thiol ligands (iso-propyl-SCbl, sec-butyl-SCbl, tert-butyl-SCbl) exhibited a major absorption band between 413 to 417 nm and a lower intensity maximum at ~502 nm (Fig. 4 B–D).

Most base-off RSCbls bound to MMACHC were unstable and decomposed to H2OCbl+ to varying degrees upon prolonged incubation. Decomposition of MMACHC-bound CH3SCbl to H2OCbl+, which was indicated by the slow increase in absorption at 350 nm, was accelerated by GSH (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–C). Dethiolation of CH3SCbl by GSH presumably leads to elimination of the mixed disulfide and the transient formation of cob(I)alamin, which is oxidized to H2OCbl+. Similar chemistry has been invoked during the dethiolation of GSCbl bound to MMACHC by a second equivalent of GSH, which promotes a thiol oxidation cycle ( 15 ). In contrast to the RSCbls, SO3Cbl, which displayed a large blue shift in its absorption spectrum upon binding to MMACHC, was relatively stable even in the presence of GSH (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 D and E). The spectrum exhibited a broad absorption maximum at 430 nm, similar to the spectrum of free base-off SO3Cbl in acidic solution ( 30 ). The stability of the MMACHC–SO3Cbl complex allowed estimation of its KD (<0.2 µM) and revealed a 1:1 stoichiometry of binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

The instability of the other RSCbls bound to MMACHC at pH 7.4 was characterized by a loss in intensity of the 413 to 415 nm peak with corresponding increases at 35 to 356 and 500 to 502 nm, characteristic of H2OCbl+ (SI Appendix, Figs. S7A and S8A). Lowering the temperature from 20 °C to 10 °C or the pH from 7.5 to 6.5 (SI Appendix, Figs. S7 B–D and S8 B–D) enhanced stability.

To rule out MMACHC-induced corrin ring deformations of RSCbls that could potentially lead to changes in axial bond lengths as a mechanism of destabilization ( 31 ), we turned to resonance Raman spectroscopy, which is an excellent tool to investigate conformational changes in the corrin ring. Features in the ∼1,500 and 1,550 cm–1 regions have been assigned to vibrational modes representing stretching motions of conjugated C–C and C–N bonds oriented along the long axis (νLA) and the short axis (νSA) of the corrin ring. The position of these features, particularly νSA, is influenced by the cobalt oxidation state ( 32 , 33 ). The RSCbls examined in this study exhibited resonance Raman spectra that were typical for the Co3+ oxidation state, and binding to MMACHC induced only small changes in νLA or νSA, ruling out significant corrin ring distortions (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A–G). The band at ~1,160 cm−1, which is sensitive to DMB coordination, is shifted toward lower energy in RSCbls bound to MMACHC (1,144 to 1,155 cm−1), consistent with these species being base-off (SI Appendix, Table S4). The low-energy region of the resonance Raman spectrum contains information on axial bond vibrations. The Co–S bond vibration in GSCbl and cysteinyl-Cbl has been reported at 325 cm−1 ( 19 ). Unfortunately, we were unable to detect this vibration in the resonance Raman spectra of our samples due to an overlapping ice peak. Therefore, we also collected the spectrum for a sample of cobalamin bound to the MMACHC•MMADHC complex prepared in 60% glycerol. In this spectrum, two peaks were observed at 246 and 257 cm−1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9H), suggesting a significant weakening of the Co–S bond in the complex.

The deployment of Co–S chemistry to traffic cobalamin exploits the facility of this cofactor for undergoing ligand exchange, but also holds perils. In the base-off state in solution, RSCbls undergo spontaneous decomposition to H2OCbl+ ( 34 ), which is also seen with base-off RSCbls bound to MMACHC (Fig. 4). The instability of base-off RSCbls raises the question as to how the Co–S bond is stabilized in the trafficking pathway. In this study, we have captured the interprotein structure of the MMACHC–MMADHC complex, which provides insights into stabilization of the Co–S bond, and the rationale for a protein versus an LMW thiolato ligand.

The B12 cofactor is bound tightly, albeit noncovalently, to MMACHC. Our study establishes that the covalent Co–S bond with the Cys-261 thiolate on MMADHC underwrites the high-affinity interaction between the two proteins, which is consistent with the lack of a stable complex in the absence of B12 ( 35 ) or when C261/C262 on MMADHC are mutated to serine ( 16 ). The importance of the Co–S bond in the high-affinity interaction between the chaperones is supported by the minimal effects of single-point mutations at interfacial residues (Fig. 2). Interestingly, despite being the most frequent locus of mutations associated with cobalamin disorders ( 22 ), none of the patient mutations reported in MMACHC to our knowledge map at its interface with MMADHC (Fig. 2A).

Since the high-affinity MMADHC–MMACHC complex appears to be largely driven by an interprotein coordination bond, we tested the utility of Chai-1, a multimodal structure prediction tool, to generate a model of the interprotein complex ( 36 ). An excellent alignment was obtained between the highest-ranking model obtained with GSMe and cobalamin as ligands and the experimentally obtained structure (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). In the predicted model, the β-hairpin (254–266) on MMADHC is positioned closer to B12 bound to MMACHC and the Co–S bond distance is 2.3 Å, which is 0.2 Å shorter than in the X-ray structure (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). The model predicts a binding site for GSMe, the product of demethylation of MeCbl (Fig. 1A), and positions the sulfur atom within 4.3 Å to the thiol group of Cys 261 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). While GSMe was present in our crystallization conditions, electron density for this ligand was not seen, indicating that it is likely not required for complex formation. Instead, in one of the MMACHC chains, difference electron density adjacent to Cys-261 was observed that can be modeled as a sulfate ion, which was present during crystallization. The distance between the sulfur in the sulfate ion and the thiol on Cys261 is 6.3 Å, and likely too far to mimic the position of the thiol group when GSMe or GSH is bound (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C).

To elucidate why MMADHC serves as the preferred thiol ligand donor, we synthesized a series of cobalamin derivatives featuring simple thiols of varying bulkiness as axial cobalt ligands. When bound to MMACHC, these RSCbls mimic the Co–S bond present in the MMACHC•B12–MMADHC complex. Notably, unlike the MMACHC•B12–MMADHC complex, which is stable over time ( 16 ), the base-off RSCbls bound to MMACHC were unstable at neutral pH as previously seen with thiolato-derivatives with β-axial cysteamine and mercaptopropionylglycine thiol ligands ( 37 ). Superposition of the structure of sec-butyl-SCbl onto CH3Cbl bound to MMACHC did not reveal steric clashes between the bulky thiol ligand and active site residues (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). However, this is not surprising since MMACHC can accommodate large β-axial ligands, including the 5′-deoxyadenosyl group and bulky groups of antivitamin B12 derivatives ( 21 , 38 , 39 ).

Alternative mechanisms for the spontaneous decomposition of RSCbl bound to MMACHC can be considered. In the first (Fig. 5A, path a), protonation at the sulfur atom of the RS- ligand precedes decomposition, akin to model chemistry described for RSCbls in acidic solution ( 34 ). An amino acid side chain in the vicinity of the sulfur atom could act as a general acid; however, the observation that iso-propyl- and sec-butyl-SCbl bound to MMACHC were stabilized when the pH was lowered from 7.5 to 6.5 (SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8) argues against this mechanism. In the second mechanism (Fig. 5A, path b), electron donation from the thiol ligand to the cobalt leads to a radical pair (RS• and Co2+Cbl), as previously invoked for Cys-SCbl and GSCbl ( 40 , 41 ). Subsequent oxidation leads to the formation of H2OCbl+ and an oxidized thiol species (e.g. RSOH or RSO2H). Although a 6-coordinate geometry is preferred in the Co3+ oxidation state, an α-axial ligand to cobalt is not seen in the MMACHC crystal structures ( 10 , 14 , 21 ), which would contribute to destabilizing RSCbls. EPR spectroscopy reveals that in the Co2+ oxidation state, the cofactor is in its preferred 5-coordinate oxidation state ( 8 ) with a water molecule coordinated either at the upper or lower axial position. Thus, stabilization of Co2+Cbl relative to the RSCbl ground state via coordination chemistry control could drive the spontaneous decomposition observed with MMACHC-bound RSCbls. We posit that formation of the radical pair is disfavored in the complex with MMADHC either by limiting access of a water ligand to cobalamin or O2 to quench the radicals. Instead, if formed, the thiyl and Co2+Cbl radicals rebound, stabilizing the RSCbl state where R represents MMADHC.

A third route for the decomposition of RSCbl, albeit not spontaneous, is via nucleophilic displacement of the thiolate ligand by GSH (Fig. 5A, path c), which was observed with CH3SCbl but not SO3Cbl (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and E). The expected products of this reaction are a mixed disulfide and H2OCbl+, formed by rapid oxidation of the initially formed Co2+Cbl. The active site of MMACHC is designed to accommodate GSH, a cosubstrate for the dealkylation and H2OCbl+ reductase reactions ( 9 , 11 ). The affinity of sulfite for cobalamin (K = 2 × 107 M−1) is surpassed only by cyanide (K = 1 × 1012 M−1) ( 28 ). However, while CNCbl can undergo reductive elimination ( 11 ), SO3Cbl is stable in the presence of GSH, which could be due to steric hindrance or the high redox potential of the GS•/GSH couple (+920 mV at pH 7.4) ( 42 ) compared to SO3•/ SO3- (+730 mV) ( 43 ). Interestingly, SO3Cbl has been detected in biological samples ( 44 ); the wide use of sulfites as food additives to prevent browning and oxidation ( 45 ) could contribute to SO3Cbl formation, potentially limiting the bioavailability of vitamin B12 ( 46 ).

GSCbl has been proposed as a direct precursor for the coenzyme in methionine synthase and methylmalonyl CoA mutase ( 47 ). Although GSCbl is rather stable when bound to bovine MMACHC, low micromolar concentrations of free GSH led to its rapid dethiolation and formation of H2OCbl ( 48 ). Given the high intracellular concentration of GSH (1–10 mM) ( 49 ), GSCbl is unlikely to replace MMADHC as the thiol ligand donor for B12 trafficking.

While B12 coordinated to Cys-261 on MMADHC can load onto methionine synthase in vitro ( 6 ), it is unlikely to be a physiologically relevant pathway due to the low affinity of MMADHC for H2OCbl+ (estimated to be 1.0 ± 0.4 µM) ( 16 ) and the low concentration of free intracellular B12 (estimated to be 0.03 to 0.7 µM in humans) ( 50 ). Our crystal structure of the MMACHC–MMADHC complex reveals how the Co–S bond tethers the interprotein complex and offers a framework for understanding the mechanism by which B12 might be translocated to methionine synthase. The DMB tail is relatively solvent exposed in the “open face” of the complex, which we speculate is important for translocating the cofactor from the MMACHC–MMADHC complex onto methionine synthase (Fig. 5B). The DMB tail also serves as a key handle for B12 loading onto methylmalonyl-CoA mutase from its chaperone ( 51 ). We have recently captured methylmalonyl-CoA mutase in complex with its G-protein chaperone MMAA, in which a large 180˚ rotation of the B12 domain exposes it to solvent, facilitating cofactor loading ( 52 ). As in methylmalonyl-CoA mutase ( 53 ), B12 is sequestered from solvent in the active site of methionine synthase ( 54 ). We posit that the B12 domain in methionine synthase, which is a multidomain and dynamic protein ( 54 ) undergoes a conformational change to accept the cofactor from MMACHC–MMADHC in a three-protein complex (Fig. 5C). Once the cofactor is transferred onto methionine synthase, MMACHC leaves, and nucleophilic displacement by the vicinal Cys-262 on Cys-261 releases the cofactor from MMADHC. The reaction results in the formation of a disulfide bond between Cys-261 and Cys-262 on MMADHC and the cofactor in the cob(I)alamin form on methionine synthase, which is converted to MeCbl in the presence of S-adenosylmethionine.

In summary, the structure of the interprotein complex reveals how the β-hairpin on MMADHC plugs the large, open upper face of the corrin in the MMACHC active site and protects the cofactor from undergoing unwanted side reactions. Additionally, since the Co–S bond of B12-bound MMADHC is a handle for loading B12 onto methionine synthase ( 6 ), the protection also extends to its preservation until the cofactor is ready for translocation. Direct B12 transfer from MMACHC to methionine synthase would expose the cofactor to solvent, rendering it susceptible to reduction and/or decomposition as seen with model RSCbls with LMW thiol ligands.

The C262S MMADHC expression clone (lacking the N-terminal disordered region extending from residues 1–115) in a pGEX vector, containing an N-terminal GST-tag was used as a template for introducing the E194A, R197A, and E244A mutations. Spectroscopic and resonance Raman characterization of the MMACHC–MMADHC interaction in the presence of cobalamin or RSCbls was performed as described in the SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

Detailed protocols for the synthesis of RSCbls and their spectroscopic, crystallographic, and resonance Raman characterization in solution and bound to MMADHC are described in the SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.

For crystallography, the MMACHC–MMADHC complex was purified in the presence of B12 and GSMe using ΔN133 MMADHC harboring the C262S mutation, and the previously described ΔC237 truncation variant of MMACHC ( 10 ). Following gel filtration chromatography, fractions corresponding to the complex were pooled, concentrated (10 mg/mL), and used for screening crystallization conditions. Crystals were obtained in well solutions containing 25% PEG 3350, 0.1 M Bis-Tris, pH 6.5, and 0.325 M ammonium sulfate incubated at 20 °C. Crystals were further optimized by varying the concentrations of PEG 3350 (20 to 30%) and ammonium sulfate (0.1 to 0.5 M) and the pH of Bis-Tris (from 5.5 to 7). Crystals were obtained using the hanging drop vapor diffusion method from a 2:1 mixture of protein (20 mg/mL) to well solution in 2 to 3 µL drops. X-ray data were collected at LS-CAT (21-ID) at Argonne National Laboratory. Molecular replacement with Phaser ( 55 ), using previously solved structures of MMACHC (PDB code 3SC0) and MMADHC (PDB code 6X8Z), was used to obtain the structure of the MMACHC•B12–MMADHC complex.
