# A data assimilation framework for predicting the spatiotemporal response of high-grade gliomas to chemoradiation.
**Authors:** Miniere HJM, Hormuth DA 2nd, Lima EABF, Farhat M, Panthi B, Langshaw H, Shanker MD, Talpur W, Thrower S, Goldman J, Ty S, Chung C, Yankeelov TE
**Journal:** BMC Cancer (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12885-025-14557-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-025-14557-3)

## Content

BACKGROUND: High-grade gliomas are highly invasive and respond variably to chemoradiation. Accurate, patient-specific predictions of tumor response could enhance treatment planning. We present a novel computational platform that assimilates MRI data to continually predict spatiotemporal tumor changes during chemoradiotherapy.

METHODS: Tumor growth and response to chemoradiation was described using a two-species reaction-diffusion model of enhancing and non-enhancing regions of the tumor. Two evaluation scenarios were used to test the predictive accuracy of this model. In scenario 1, the model was calibrated on a patient-specific basis (n = 21) to weekly MRI data during the course of chemoradiotherapy. A data assimilation framework was used to update model parameters with each new imaging visit which were then used to update model predictions. In scenario 2, we evaluated the predictive accuracy of the model when fewer data points are available by calibrating the same model using only the first two imaging visits and then predicted tumor response at the remaining five weeks of treatment. We investigated three approaches to assign model parameters for scenario 2: (1) predictions using only parameters estimated by fitting the data obtained from an individual patient's first two imaging visits, (2) predictions made by averaging the patient-specific parameters with the cohort-derived parameters, and (3) predictions using only cohort-derived parameters.

RESULTS: Scenario 1 achieved a median [range] concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) between the predicted and measured total tumor cell counts of 0.91 [0.84, 0.95], and a median [range] percent error in tumor volume of -2.6% [-19.7, 8.0%], demonstrating strong agreement throughout the course of treatment. For scenario 2, the three approaches yielded CCCs of: (1) 0.65 [0.51, 0.88], (2) 0.74 [0.70, 0.91], (3) 0.76 [0.73, 0.92] with significant differences between the approach (1) that does not use the cohort parameters and the two approaches (2 and 3) that do.

CONCLUSIONS: The proposed data assimilation framework enhances the accuracy of tumor growth forecasts by integrating patient-specific and cohort-based data. These findings show a practical method for identifying more personalized treatment strategies in high-grade glioma patients.

High-grade gliomas (HGG) are aggressive malignancies characterized by their invasive nature and heterogeneous composition. Even with the current combination of surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy, these tumors are highly likely to recur and progress within the brain [1]. The wide variety of observed patient responses to these established interventions is due to the underlying, and well-recognized, biological heterogeneity between individual tumors [2, 3]. One approach to improve treatment efficacy has been to use patient-specific data, including the application of highly precise radiotherapy tailored to the patient [4]. A particularly promising development is the use of biologically-guided treatment that seeks to target the areas where tumor progression and treatment resistance are expected to occur [5, 6]. Currently, evaluating how a patient responds to therapy depends on monitoring radiological and clinical changes over weeks to months after treatment has been completed [7]. This approach delays the identification of tumor progression and directly impedes the switch from ineffective treatment plan to a potentially more effective one.

There is a crucial need to more definitively assess and characterize—even predict—the quality and outcome of a therapeutic intervention in its early stages to maximize positive responses and reduce detrimental side effects. Mathematical oncology [8] provides a structured framework to describe and predict the complex dynamics of cancer progression [9–13], notably by leveraging biologically-derived models characterizing biological phenomena such as cell movement, proliferation, and response to therapy [14, 15]. Computational approaches are employed to inform and calibrate these models using patient-specific data, thereby enabling personalized predictions of tumor growth and paving the way for the systematic optimization of therapy plans [16].

The mathematical modeling of gliomas is a well-established area of research, covering topics such as resection planning [17], response to radiation therapy [18, 19], angiogenesis [20, 21], and mass effect [22]. In particular, in vivo imaging data has been instrumental in initializing and constraining these models [23]. Of specific relevance to the current study, are spatio-temporally models built on systems of partial-differential equations (PDE) enabling accurate characterization and forecasting of both cell count and cell distribution [24–26]. Consequently, this approach facilitates the identification of regions within the tumor that are progressing or showing suboptimal response to treatment. In [24], Hormuth et al. developed and compared a family of PDE models that featured various ways to capture cell proliferation, mechanically determined cell movement, and response to treatment to characterize and predict glioma dynamics in a clinical context using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data to inform the model. In a preliminary study of nine patients undergoing standard-of-care treatment, they were able to forecast tumor growth with a low median error in tumor volume forecasts below 2.5% using a two-species model distinguishing the enhancing and non-enhancing regions of gliomas. The small patient cohort coupled with a relatively limited number of imaging timepoints (four visits per patient) motivates a larger-scale investigation to validate and extend those findings. In particular, the three imaging visits at which MRI data were gathered (1, 3 and 5 months after tumor resection surgery) leave little opportunity for repeated and dynamic treatment optimization during the course of radiotherapy and the one-and-a-half-month post-surgery MRI visit. However, in contexts with greater access to imaging data (e.g., clinical trials with frequent image-based assessments), the techniques of data assimilation provide a systematic framework for shorter-term predictions guided by recurrent measurements. Commonly used in weather forecasting [27], data assimilation has been successfully applied to predict response to radiotherapy and develop personalized treatment plans in head and neck cancer [28, 29], or to perform forecasts of glioblastoma growth while accounting for model parameter errors and uncertain data [30]. Especially noteworthy for the current investigation is the work by Liu et al. [31]. who implemented a data assimilation protocol to forecast the growth of in vitro glioma cell populations. They generated predictions by weighting parameters obtained by calibrating a two-species model on the measured time-resolved cell counts of individual cell colonies with parameters calibrated on a training dataset. This weighting scheme helped to maintain prediction accuracy in the early stages of treatment when little data is available on the individual replicate on which a prediction is being performed. This approach was then replicated in more recent work from our group, where predictions of in vitro breast cancer cell growth were performed on a weekly basis following chemotherapy treatment, guided by parameters calibrated on a training cohort [32].

In the present contribution, we aim to refine the modeling work proposed by Hormuth et al. [24]. by implementing a data assimilation pipeline similar to that devised by Liu et al. [31]. and Miniere et al. [32] to enable weekly predictions for 21 glioma patients undergoing standard-of-care chemoradiation with weekly assessment of tumor status. Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted and T2-weighted FLAIR (fluid attenuated inversion recovery) MRI are used to characterize the heterogeneous makeup of the tumor, thus allowing specific examination of the non-enhancing and enhancing areas using a two-species model. As predictions are informed and guided by weekly imaging visits during therapy, our approach enables frequent model updates as additional patient-specific data becomes available and assimilated into the calibrating framework. Moreover, we also investigate predictions performed without recurrent model updates to address situations where imaging protocols of this frequency could not be implemented. In this case, we leverage model parameters trained on the patient cohort to guide predictions performed at the early stages of therapy on new patients—an in vivo implementation of the in vitro approach utilized in [31] and [32]. We hypothesize that despite the heterogeneity in tumor morphology and dynamics found between patients, population trends can still be employed to significantly increase prediction accuracy. By demonstrating accurate predictions of the spatio-temporal development of tumors, biology-based mathematical models such as the one used in this work can potentially be used to optimize therapeutic plans.

This study includes 21 patients who had been diagnosed with histologically confirmed, high-grade gliomas enrolled on an institutional review board approved prospective study at M.D. Anderson Cancer Center (NCT# 04771806). Informed consent was collected from all patients that were enrolled into this trial. All research methods adhered to the proper guidelines and regulations. All patients received a maximal safe surgical resection prior to radiotherapy. On this prospective study, MRI was conducted at baseline, and then once per week during the six weeks of radiotherapy. This duration thus corresponds to the timeframe of our analysis.

Table 1 provides a summary of the clinical characteristics of the patient population. Each patient received a total dose of 60 Gy, delivered in 2 Gy fractions on each weekday for a period of 6 weeks. Radiotherapy was administered concurrently with oral temozolomide at a dose of 75 mg/kg per day, seven days a week. After the completion of radiation therapy, temozolomide was given with a minimum of six cycles at a dose of 150–200 mg/kg, delivered orally for 5 days during each 28-day cycle.

While a comprehensive description can be found in [24], here we provide only the salient details on image acquisition and analysis. We utilized data from three different MRI sequences obtained during each scan session: (1) a T1-weighted image, (2) T2-FLAIR (fluid attenuated inversion recover), and (3) DW-MRI (diffusion weighted MRI). Note that the baseline, middle of radiotherapy, and follow-up scans also included a Gadolinium-based contrast agent injection. On these visits, T1-weighted images were acquired before and after the injection of the contrast agent.

A rigid registration algorithm using the imwarp and imregtform functions in MATLAB R2022a (Mathworks, Nattick, MA) was employed to align all images to the T2-FLAIR image acquired at the first visit. On visits that included a contrast-agent injection, we segmented the enhancing and non-enhancing tumor volumes, the latter of which is defined as the non-enhancing, T2-hyperintense region. This segmentation was accomplished using a semi-automated method that combined thresholding techniques with manual adjustments performed by a trained expert. Additionally, a senior radiation oncologist conducted a secondary quality review of the results. On visits without contrast-agent injection, the enhancing and non-enhancing tumor volumes correspond to those drawn at the most recent contrast-injection visit. To further aid in image analysis, we employed k-means clustering of signal intensity to segment the white matter, gray matter, and cerebrospinal fluid from the T2-FLAIR images.

We first suppose that we have already gathered MRI data on an individual patient at visit 0 (i.e., the time point between surgery and initiation of radiotherapy) and visit 1. Those initial two data points allow an initial model calibration using Eqs. (1)–(8), yielding the parameter set Ppatient. A prediction for visit 2 is then performed by running the model forward from week 1 using Ppatient. MRI data from visit 2 is then measured, and Ppatient is updated by calibrating Eqs. (1)–(8) on all data available (i.e., visits 0, 1, and 2). A new prediction is now similarly made for visit 3, and the process is repeated until the conclusion of radiotherapy.

We have developed and systematically assessed a clinical-computational approach that integrates widely available multiparametric MRI data into a biology-based mathematical model to characterize and predict the growth and development of high-grade glioma in a cohort of 21 patients. Our pipeline allows an accurate and quantitative description of tumor progression during the course of standard-of-care chemoradiation therapy. First, a two-species reaction-diffusion model, as described by Hormuth et al. [24]. was calibrated on individual patients using cellularity estimates provided by DW-MRI. T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR images were used to discriminate between the non-enhancing and enhancing areas of the tumor, thus placing tumor heterogeneity at the core of our modeling effort. Our proposed data assimilation prediction pipeline achieved high levels of accuracy on a week-to-week basis, providing a pathway for the systematic and dynamic optimization of radiation delivery for individual patients. Moreover, as weekly imaging visits are currently not part of standard-of-care practice, and may not be widely available, we also tested the performance of predictions performed from the second week of treatment without weekly updates. Different scenarios were investigated including predictions supported by parameters trained on the patient cohort, thereby interrogating the potential of leveraging population trends to better guide longer-term predictions in the absence of patient-specific, recurrent imaging that allows for model updates. Our findings show that in spite of the significant disease heterogeneity found among the patients, parameters calibrated on the cohort can be reliably used to increase the quality of long-term predictions of treatment response performed in the early stages of therapy. Nonetheless, it is unsurprising that this approach quantitively underperforms the data assimilation pipeline presented in scenario 1 given the greater access and integration of medical images that allow frequent model updates.

The potential of mathematical oncology to characterize and predict the response of tumors to treatments using models of cell growth, diffusion, and treatment efficacy is now well established in both the pre-clinical [9, 33, 36–39] and clinical settings [12, 13, 40–42]. Considering both spatial and temporal dynamics represent a crucial step for the optimization of locally-delivered therapies (such as radiation therapy) as uneven treatment efficacy due to areas of resistance throughout the tumor can lead to disease progression [43, 44]. Moreover, under the current standard-of-care treatment of high-grade glioma, radiation delivery maps are established following surgery and remain unchanged during the course of the treatment, irrespective of any changes in the patient’s individual response to therapy or the temporal evolving spatial characteristics of the tumor within this interval [45]. Consequently, the current approach potentially limits the precision and effectiveness of the treatment strategy. Thus, our approach could allow for the dynamic adaptation of radiation delivery to further target areas of predicted progress or resistance using mathematical modeling, as others have proposed [46]. Corwin et al.. developed a systematic platform that integrates a patient-specific biomathematical model of glioma proliferation, invasion, and radiotherapy with a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm designed for optimizing intensity-modulated radiation therapy [47]. Implementing such a technique on a recurrent and dynamic basis by accounting for on-going changes in the tumor could assist in more effectively—and more frequently—tailoring therapy plans, hopefully enhancing the precision and efficacy of glioma treatment. More generally, there is a growing recognition of the need for adaptive treatment protocols that can be tailored based on real-time assessments of both the patient’s condition and the tumor’s behavior [44, 48, 49], fostering a more personalized and responsive approach to improve outcomes in the management of malignancies. By providing weekly forecasts of tumor progression guided by recurrent imaging, our data assimilation pipeline is a promising tool for the implementation of locally-focused strategies such as adaptive radiotherapy [5, 6], focal radiotherapy [50, 51], or laser interstitial thermal therapy [52].

Our study also demonstrates the added value of employing population trends to better inform predictions in situations where a recurrent imaging procedure cannot be implemented. As Fig. 9 demonstrates, scheme T outperforms (or performs similarly) to the other two schemes for all metrics except the percent error in tumor volume for the non-enhancing region, thus corroborating our hypothesis. A similar pipeline was implemented in an in vitro context by Liu et al. [31]., where the population parameter set was weighted with parameters calibrated on individual glioma growth time courses to perform short-term predictions. By giving more weight to the population parameter set in the early stages of the time course, they were able to keep predictions “on track” when little data was available from the individual time course being predicted. Do note that we have also implemented a similar approach for Scenario 1, but no differences were observed between the T, T + P, and P schemes in this case. We hypothesize that the restricted time frame (one week) on which the model is evaluated in this scenario does not allow significant differences in the cell count and distribution between the three weighting schemes to become observable. Nonetheless, these findings potentially suggest the use of a more robust data-based approach to perform early predictions based on patterns found in patient cohorts—and do so within the confines of a readily interpretable, mechanism-based mathematical model. Given a much larger data set, it may be possible to construct a physics-informed neural network [53] that explicitly incorporates the physical principles within Eqs. (1)–(8) into the training of the network. These models offer significant advantages over classical convolutional neural networks including, importantly, the increased interpretability of the weight-training process and smaller data requirements for training provided by the embedded physical principles. Such approaches have already been applied to cancer treatment and diagnosis [51–55].

Our work invites various opportunities for improvement. First, as described in refs [21, 24], it is assumed that the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), and therefore the cell count, derived from DW-MRI measurements is mostly driven by cell density. In reality, DW-MRI is sensitive to various factors such as tissue microstructure, perfusion, and cell membrane integrity, which may contribute to variations in ADC measurements. Additionally, the presence of edema, necrosis, or hemorrhage within the tumor can impact the accuracy of ADC values, potentially leading to an overestimation or underestimation of cancer cell count [21]. To minimize these effects, voxels with extreme ADC values (indicative of necrosis or edema) were assigned a minimal cellularity following Eq. (3). Future work could involve more refined segmentation by dividing the domain into tumor, edema, and necrosis and applying such masks to the ADC measurements (as seen in the BraTS challenge [34]). Second, our modest cohort (n = 21) may affect the generalizability of the proposed framework, notably in the case of scenario 2 where the patient cohort is leveraged to perform predictions at the individual level. In this case, systematic errors in predictions can be expected if the model parameters from the training cohort do not reflect the values in the testing population. As stated in the Introduction, the pipeline in this study was inspired by previous in vitro work [31, 32], and our results now motivate application on larger clinical datasets to confirm the added value of cohort-trained parameters. Moreover, a larger cohort would allow sub-group analysis based on molecular, genetic, or histochemical markers (e.g., MGMT and IDH status, Ki-67, or GFAP levels), or patient characteristics (e.g., sex or age). Selecting cohort-trained model parameters based on such characteristics, and on real-time observed similarities in treatment response (as in [32]), could potentially improve prediction accuracy. Lastly, As briefly stated in Sect. 3.2, contrast injections given at the middle of radiotherapy (fourth visit for most patients) are used by the clinicians to redraw the borders of the non-enhancing and enhancing regions. As the contrast agent is not used at visits 2 and 3, the borders at those time points correspond to those drawn after visit 1 (see Sect. 2.2 above). Thus, our model having been trained on the first three visits to predict the fourth, is unable to predict the new borders of the domain that will be redrawn at visit 4, leading to prediction errors at this time point. Moreover, an inherent limitation to a mathematical model such as the one presented here lies in the “reactive” rather than “pro-active” nature of the prediction approach. As the model leverages patterns captured between week 0 and t and projects them forward to make a prediction for week t + 1, it is unable to forecast the appearance and development of tumor hot spots between week t and t + 1. This is almost certainly due to limitations in the biology that our model captures. In its current form, our model utilizes past cell count dynamics to infer future cell count dynamics, and only implicitly captures the underlying biological phenomena driving the changes in cell count through the proliferation and death parameters (kp, e, kp, n, SFRT, SFCT in Eqs. 1, 2 and 6). This would require the acquisition of a wider range of data and the integration of a more complete description of glioma biology including, for example, molecular data, gene expression profiles, and more advanced imaging measures [56–58]. Moreover, emphasizing the role of microenvironmental factors, such as immune responses and nutrient gradients, could provide crucial insights into the observed spatial heterogeneity [59]. The inclusion of these nuanced aspects in our model would undoubtedly elevate its predictive capabilities. However, it is essential to acknowledge that this enhanced model may introduce heightened model complexity, demanding careful consideration of validation and interpretation methodologies. The compromise between the dynamics of a particular disease, the measurement and imaging protocols available and the ability to adapt the course of an ongoing treatment is thus a crucial consideration for optimally implementing data assimilation.

We have introduced a clinical-computational platform that accurately characterizes and predicts the diverse spatiotemporal dynamics of high-grade glioma tumors and their response to treatment with chemoradiation. Our approach, which utilizes standard-of-care data to constrain a biologically-derived mechanistic model, demonstrates low volume errors and high levels of voxel agreement. Moreover, our work supports leveraging population trends to guide long-term predictions in cases where recurrent model updates are not readily available. These findings contribute to ongoing efforts to adapt and personalize therapies for individual patients in real time to improve treatment outcomes.
