loss-of-function variants in the transcriptional regulator methyl-CpG binding 
protein-2 (MECP2) gene. These variants were historically believed to be 
incompatible with life in males; however, recent advances in genetic testing 
have revealed significant clinical heterogeneity. The current study aimed to 
improve our understanding of diagnostic experiences in males with confirmed 
pathogenic alteration of MECP2. An international sample of caregivers completed 
a survey of diagnostic experiences (N = 47) and phenomenological interviews 
(n = 32). Median [interquartile range; IQR] age of genetic diagnosis was 3 years 
[1.08, 6.75]. Multivariate analysis showed that for every year increase in year 
of birth, age of diagnosis (in years) decreased by 0.31. Qualitative findings 
demonstrate that medical biases and widespread misconceptions contribute to 
delays in accurate clinical diagnosis, which negatively impacts child health and 
family functioning. As genetic testing becomes more widely available, age of 
genetic diagnosis is decreasing, resulting in more providers and families with 
unexpected results and a notable lack of male-specific anticipatory guidance and 
clinical recommendations. Diagnostic providers must increase their awareness of 
males with confirmed pathogenic alterations of MECP2 and be prepared to deliver 
the diagnosis with empathy and accurate, up-to-date information on prognosis and 
treatment options.

Rett syndrome (RTT) is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder caused primarily by loss-of-function pathogenic variants in the transcriptional regulatorMethyl-CpG Binding Protein-2(MECP2) gene.1-4RTT is distinct fromMECP2-duplication syndrome, which is caused by duplication of the entireMECP2locus as well as several surrounding genes.5As an X-linked condition, RTT was historically believed to be incompatible with life in males.6,7Previously, limited earlier research showed males with loss-of-function pathogenic variants inMECP2had early-onset severe encephalopathy universally resulting in early death.8,9However, recent advances in genetic testing have revealed more males with loss-of-functionMECP2variants (referred to broadly as male RTT for this manuscript) than previously known.10Clinical outcomes from thirty males enrolled in the Rett Syndrome Natural History Study fell within four broad categories of male RTT: 1) classic RTT, 2) atypical RTT which can be progressive, 3) neonatal encephalopathy, and 4) X-linked intellectual disability.11The majority (60%) had abnormal initial development, 43% had neonatal or progressive encephalopathy, and ~30% were either ventilator dependent or died (mean age of death: 8.9 years).11This broadly distributed phenotype in a rare disorder makes phenotypic recognition difficult in male RTT, and clinicians may be less likely to pursue or interpret supportive genotypic studies. Therefore, male RTT is at high risk for non-diagnosis, diagnostic delays, and inaccurate estimates of incidence.

Over 300 causative loss-of-function alterations ofMECP2are associated with RTT and the spectrum ofMECP2-related disorders, including an expanding identification of male RTT.12-17However, despite its genetic underpinnings, RTT remains a clinical diagnosis based on the observation of regression and signature medical and developmental findings.18There is a high predictive power that people with a clinical diagnosis of RTT will have a loss-of-functionMECP2mutation),19but the converse is unknown. As genetic testing capable of revealing pathogenic variants inMECP2is now common, genetic results may precede the recognition of regression or other clinical features characteristic and diagnostic of classic or atypical RTT, especially in the male phenotypic spectrum. To date, there have been no studies investigating important diagnostic experiences in male RTT, such as average age of diagnosis or what the diagnosis means for family caregivers. As diagnoses are likely to increase with expanded genetic testing, it is critical that clinicians and researchers have a nuanced understanding of barriers and facilitators to accurate and timely diagnoses, the impact of diagnostic delays, and the subjective experiences and priorities of families receiving the male RTT diagnosis.

To fill this gap, we conducted an exploratory mixed methods study to describe diagnostic experiences in male RTT. A primary quantitative aim was to describe the timing of diagnoses and potential associations with clinical and sociocultural factors. We hypothesized increased diagnostic delays for male RTT compared with previously documented timing of RTT diagnosis in females and a significant negative correlation between age of diagnosis and year of birth. Our qualitative aim was to provide rich descriptions of how male children ultimately come to receive a RTT diagnosis, parent perceptions of how the diagnosis is delivered, and the meaning parent caregivers ascribe to receiving a male RTT diagnosis for their child. A third aim of the study was to merge the quantitative and qualitative data strands to provide a robust understanding of these phenomena to inform clinical care. Our ultimate goal is to foster more timely diagnoses of male RTT, delivered with evidence-based counseling and clinical recommendations tailored to the needs of the male RTT community.

The current findings are part of a larger convergent parallel mixed-methods phenomenological research20study of male RTT. All study procedures were approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB: 23-0075).Figure 1shows the overarching study design including a pilot phase with caregivers of five males with RTT who provided feedback on the content and sensitivity of novel data collection instruments. After refinement of the instruments, participants were recruited to complete a battery of electronic surveys and in-depth phenomenological interviews. Qualitative and quantitative data analyses occurred separately and concurrently. Qualitative data collection stopped after 32 interviews, when preliminary analyses revealed saturation of meaning, or the ability to adequately describe depth, content, nuance, and variability within the phenomena.21,22

Participants were recruited from a private social media platform group which includes approximately 80 affected families, the International Rett Syndrome Foundation, andclinicaltrials.gov(NCT06346106). Inclusion criteria were intentionally broad to document the broad variability in males with pathogenic and presumed pathogenic loss-of-functionMECP2variants.11Consenting participants were included if they were parents/caregivers of males with a loss-of-functionMECP2variant (seeSupplemental Table 1), living or passed, and the ability to complete an online survey and interview in English. Exclusion criteria were MECP2 duplication syndrome, female sex, or inability to provide genetic proof ofMECP2variant. Genetic eligibility was confirmed by a pediatric neurologist with extensive expertise in RTT, and in complicated cases, by study team consensus. When available, parent genetic reports were also collected to determine inheritance patterns within our cohort but did not influence inclusion or exclusion of participants. Participants were invited to complete the survey and interview alone or with partner caregivers. Informed electronic consent was obtained for all participants per IRB protocol and participants who completed all parts of the study were compensated $50 USD. Survey data collection is ongoing; data were extracted for this analysis in November 2024.

Surveys were completed on respondents’ personal computers or phones between May 2023 through August 2024 using the online General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliant Qualtrics platform. Respondents were able to start and stop the survey as needed to accommodate the intensive and time-consuming caregiving burdens.

Results from the Rett Syndrome Natural History Study (U54 HD061222) suggesting a broad and diverse male RTT phenotype11were used by a multidisciplinary team (including a parent advocate, qualitatively trained behavioral health researchers, and physician experts from RTT Centers of Excellence) to develop a novel survey of clinical characteristics and diagnostic experiences in male RTT. Questions were slightly modified for parents/caregivers of living boys versus those deceased at the time of survey completion, resulting in two comparable but distinct questionnaires. The survey included a mixture of true/false and multiple-choice questions, with options for free-text responses if predetermined choices did not capture participant experiences. Regarding diagnostic experiences, participants reported on the child’s age at the time of diagnosis, who delivered the diagnosis, the time between onset of symptoms and the diagnosis, any misdiagnoses received prior to the identifiedMECP2variant, and a question asking parents to rate their overall diagnostic experience on a Likert scale (1=negative, worst experience ever, 2=on the whole, very negative, 3=mostly negative, but with some positives, 4=truly neutral, equally good and bad, 5=mostly positive, but with some negatives, 6=on the whole, very positive, and 7=positive, helpful, best possible). Participants were asked to select and rank their top five concerns23from a list of 52 RTT-related features and problems, both at the time of diagnosis and at present (in the last six-months of life for parents of deceased boys). A sociodemographic survey was also developed to capture key contextual information on an international sample. Parents of boys in the alive cohort completed an additional standardized questionnaire on quality of life.24We report here on diagnostic experiences and top five concerns at the time of diagnosis. Clinical outcomes, top concerns at present (last six-months of life), and quality of life results will be reported in subsequent manuscripts. SeeSupplemental 2for the alive and passed versions of the novel male RTT survey.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample and summarize the range and variability within diagnostic experiences. Top five concerns at the time of the diagnosis were analyzed per previously completed work in females with RTT.12Items were weighted by the participant assigned ranking (weighted rank=1/rank order), where features listed as the First Concern were given a value of 1, Second Concern a weighted rank of .5, Third Concern a weighted rank of .33, Fourth Concern a weighted rank of .25, and Fifth Concern a weighted rank of .2.13Weighted scores were summarized and ranked (higher weighted scores = higher rank order), and percentages were calculated by dividing each item’s weighted score by the total of weighted scores. Univariate analyses were performed using linear regression to assess associations with age at diagnosis. Variables found to be associated at the p < 0.05 level were included in the final multivariate analysis. All p-values were based on two-sided statistical tests and p<0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in SAS 9.4 copyright (c) 2020 by SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA or R 4.3.2 copyright (C) 2023 The R Foundation for Statistical Computing.

Upon survey completion, participants were scheduled for in-depth, semi-structured individual or dyadic interviews. Interviews were conducted via video conference at a time convenient to the participants and lasted up to 90 minutes (Mean=82 minutes, SD=7.8). Audio recordings were professionally transcribed and uploaded to Atlas.ti (version 23) for data storage and management. All qualitative procedures were conducted by two researchers with extensive qualitative experience (TGT licensed psychologist and qualitative methodologist with rare disease expertise, DG qualitative analyst with a master’s degree in public health) and results were refined with the larger study team comprised of a parent advocate and RTT physician specialists.

The interview guide was developed to follow best practice in descriptive phenomenology.25Several broad, open-ended questions allowed participants to guide the conversation toward topics they deemed most important. The present manuscript focuses on parent responses to the questions, “Please describe your son and his day-to-day life in as much detail as possible,” “How does Rett syndrome affect your lives?” and “Please describe your experience with getting the Rett diagnosis for your child. Please provide as much detail as you can.” Follow-up probes asked participants to provide specific examples, reflect on how experiences made them feel, and describe the meaning they ascribed to their lived experiences. Guides were slightly modified for parents of living and deceased children to provide more sensitive questions and probes for bereaved parents.26SeeSupplemental Table 3for the alive and passed versions of the interview guide.

The qualitative team followed Colaizzi’s seven-step systematic approach,27,28adapted to incorporate qualitative data analysis software. Two members of the investigative team highly experienced in qualitative data analysis familiarized themselves with the data set by listening to audio and reading transcripts. Each transcript was open-coded in Atlas.ti (version 23) to identify significant statements related to the diagnostic experience. Roughly 10% of transcripts were double coded to foster consistency, after which transcripts were independently coded and reviewed. The team met frequently to discuss and refine formulated meanings emerging from the transcripts and associated exemplary quotes (significant statements). Formulated meanings and associated significant statements were stored in Excel documents. Once all transcripts were coded and reviewed, the qualitative team met several times to further distill formulated meanings into clusters of themes. Using these clusters, the team reviewed data and formulated a comprehensive description of the diagnostic experience in male RTT, including overarching themes and subthemes.

Statistical results and qualitative themes were linked through an interpretive matrix (joint display)29to assess the relationships between the datasets and provide new insights relevant for clinical practice.

Sixty families expressed interest in the study and uploaded their child’s genetic reports for screening (Figure 1). Of those, 8 were deemed ineligible (female sex, genetic reports indicated a different genetic condition) and 5 were lost to follow-up prior to survey completion. The final sample includes family caregivers to 47 boys withMECP2variations, including 36 living and 11 deceased. Living boys ranged in age from 7-months to 31.5-years and age of death for boys who had passed ranged from 1 to 16 years. Univariate associations (Supplemental Table 5) showed age of diagnosis was negatively correlated with child’s year of birth and father’s age at time of birth, but no other sociodemographic variables. Age of diagnosis was positively correlated with age of symptom onset, but not with any specific clinical features characteristic of RTT (such as developmental regression, hand stereotypies, breathing problems, seizures). Children with one or more prior diagnoses (misdiagnoses or under-diagnoses) on average, received the male RTT diagnosis three-years later than those who did not receive any prior diagnoses. However, the type of provider who diagnosed (geneticist versus neurologist) was not associated with age of diagnosis. Multivariate analysis showed that for every year increase in year of birth, the age of diagnosis (in years) decreased by 0.31 years after adjusting for paternal age at time of child’s birth and age at which developmental concerns began (Figure 2).

Parents quantitative ratings of the diagnostic experience varied; nearly half of participants rated the diagnosis as a negative experience, over one-third as a positive experience, and ~17% as truly neutral (Figure 3). There were no significant associations between parent ratings of the diagnostic experience and year of birth (p=0.403), age of diagnosis (p=0.078), U.S. versus non-U.S. (p=0.754), or the provider delivering the male RTT diagnosis (geneticist versus neurologist (p=0.207), geneticist versus other (p=0.276)).

Parents rated their overall impressions of the diagnostic experience on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from negative, worst ever to positive helpful, best possible.

The diagnostic experience in male RTT (Figure 4) can be conceptualized as a three-phase phenomenon beginning with a difficult and demanding journey to the diagnosis ranging from long diagnostic odysseys to more sudden diagnoses. The journey eventually leads to a diagnosis delivered by providers who are usually unfamiliar with male RTT, working within an unaware medical community, providing insufficient data and, at times, misinformation. The experience results in mixed emotional reactions from parents, characterized by grief and despair alongside feelings of relief and resolve. Three broad themes portray salient experiences for each phase of the diagnostic experience that were common across participants. Subthemes and illustrative quotes (Supplemental Table 6) describe the unique contextual factors that influence lived experiences and the subjective meaning participants assign to their children receiving a diagnosis of male RTT.

The diagnostic experience in male RTT is conceptualized as a three-phase phenomenon beginning with a difficult, demanding, and diverse journey to the diagnosis, leading to the delivery of the male RTT diagnosis from an uninformed medical community, culminating in mixed emotional reactions from parents.

Interviews revealed many paths to the diagnosis of male RTT. The immense variability in diagnostic journeys captured in our dataset reveals that broader sociocultural and contextual factors, such as healthcare systems, insurance policies, currently available technology, physician awareness of male RTT, physician attitudes toward the potential of identifying a variant of uncertain significance (VUS), and parent advocacy can alter the diagnostic experience significantly, regardless of disease severity. Most participants’ diagnostic stories fell within two categories: longer diagnostic odysseys and sudden diagnoses.

The majority of participants described a long, arduous journey to an accurate diagnosis of male RTT. Notably, the length of time between first symptoms of concern and a final male RTT diagnosis differed greatly; some parents perceived a journey of eight-months as prolonged, while some children experienced symptoms for over a decade before they were accurately diagnosed. Parents of older children described very different journeys from the parents of younger boys. Participants whose children were born prior to more routine genetic testing (circa. 2014) and those living in countries where genetic testing was still difficult to obtain, waited years to gain access to testing that could accurately identify theMECP2variant. Furthermore, several parents of older boys with mosaicism described repeated instances of early inconclusive genetic testing as multiple samples failed to reveal the pathogenic variant. In general, availability and use of genetic testing was more limited outside of the US, and some parents travelled outside their home countries to obtain testing.

Even when genetic testing was available, many parents still reported a need to advocate fiercely for whole exome sequencing to confirm their suspicions that an underlying genetic condition was the cause of their sons’ neurodevelopmental and medical issues. Participants reported feeling dismissed by providers and recounted stories of repeatedly being told to “wait and see,” with pediatricians incorrectly attributing reports of delayed milestones to inexperienced first-time parents or misdiagnoses. Many children received multiple secondary diagnoses (e.g., failure to thrive, intellectual disability) or misdiagnoses (e.g., autism spectrum disorder or cerebral palsy), including by medical providers familiar with RTT in females. Harms of inaccurate diagnoses included delaying proper treatment, providing false hope, or in some cases leading to counter-productive behavioral interventions (e.g., applied behavioral analysis therapies to curb RTT-related hand wringing behaviors or stereotypies). Participants described providers as hesitant to order genetic testing when symptoms did not suggest a specific condition, or they suspected results would reveal a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). This was particularly problematic for our participants’ sons, as clear signs of classic and atypical RTT were often ignored or dismissed because of the pervasive misconception that males cannot have RTT. Some parents resorted to paying out of pocket for expensive testing when they were denied by insurance companies or providers actively discouraged testing.

For a smaller minority of our participants, the diagnosis of male RTT felt unexpectedly sudden. Some, but not all, parents of younger males described providers who proactively ordered whole exome sequencing after an initial diagnosis of autism or an epilepsy gene panel with initial seizure activity. For these families, cost was not an issue, as testing was covered under their insurance policies. Sudden diagnoses occurred almost exclusively in the U.S., and mostly with boys born after 2019. As sudden diagnoses were more common for younger boys with increased disease severity (e.g., seizures, breathing abnormalities), these diagnoses were often delivered during very high stress moments in families’ lives, sometimes within critical care settings. In general, families with sudden diagnoses experienced a shorter journey to an accurate diagnosis, with little need to push their providers for testing or appeal rejected insurance claims. Regardless, their journeys were equally difficult, as the traumatic news of a male RTT diagnosis while managing their child’s severe medical needs caused extreme distress.

While journeys to a male RTT diagnosis varied considerably, a nearly universal theme expressed across all interviews was the challenge of receiving the diagnosis from an uninformed medical community. Many participants described eventually benefiting from the expertise of RTT specialists and the male RTT parent community. However, at the time of their sons’ diagnoses, participants described being counseled by providers with a near total unawareness of male RTT, often receiving alarming and, sometimes, harmful misinformation on the condition.

The lack of knowledge and understanding of male RTT delayed diagnoses as providers were unable or unwilling to entertain the possibility of male RTT. Often, after theMECP2variant was identified, providers and parents acknowledged that, while not initially suspected because of the child’s male sex, all symptoms had pointed to RTT. Even after a correct diagnosis was made, some providers continued to express confusion and disbelief, even openly questioning the possibility of the RTT diagnosis in a male. Parents reported fighting an uphill battle just to have their child’s condition acknowledged. Due to lack of provider awareness and the absence of current data on male RTT, diagnostic conversations included little to no information on what to expect regarding the child’s prognosis or recommendations for clinical care. Family-friendly RTT educational resources were specific to females and were unable to answer participants’ most pressing questions. Parents had to determine the significance of the male RTT diagnosis without the support of an informed specialist care team. Feelings of isolation from being the sole expert on their child’s medical condition were common, feelings especially pronounced by families living in areas with fewer RTT specific resources (e.g., rural families, families in countries where only one known boy with male RTT lives).

Despite being unable to provide sufficient data on male RTT at the time of the diagnosis, we heard frequent stories of providers cautioning parents not to search online to fill the gaps in their knowledge. Online sources were rife with misinformation, including blanket statements thatMECP2variants are incompatible with life in males or that boys who survive gestation will almost certainly die within the first few years of life. However, misinformation about a universally short lifespan was also shared, at times, directly with families from their medical team. Participants recalled stories of the male RTT diagnosis being delivered with grim implications and no hope for a cure or treatment. Parents of very young boys were told not to expect their child to survive past age three, while parents of older children were told that their child is an anomaly living far beyond expectations. Several parents reflected in interviews that they now know the broader spectrum of outcomes for male RTT and wish that would have been conveyed at the time of their sons’ diagnoses, as they believed this would have provided them with hope for their son’s future.

Emotional responses to the male RTT diagnosis were complicated and mixed both within and between participants. Parental reactions were highly influenced by the length of the diagnostic journey, the delivery of the diagnosis (e.g., amount of information provided with the diagnosis, the providers attitudes and knowledge), the child’s current health status, and parent’s existing social support systems (e.g., family, friends, neighbors, religious community). For most parents, a male RTT diagnosis held multiple meanings. Primarily, parents viewed male RTT as a death sentence for their sons which led to immense anticipatory grief and despair. However, the accurate diagnosis simultaneously provided relief and renewed focus for families seeking answers.

The majority of parents described that learning their sons had male RTT meant a loss of hope. The limited information (including misinformation) families were provided on male RTT revealed an incurable and chronic condition. Parents’ hopes that genetic testing might reveal an abnormality with a known cure were dashed; rather the male RTT diagnosis meant their son would experience a loss of skills over time and would likely die at a young age. Multiple participants described learning about their son’s genetic variation as the worst day of their lives, equating receiving the diagnosis to receiving a death sentence for their child. Parents of younger children with more severe medical problems were especially vulnerable to despair, as they had often already experienced near-death medical emergencies such as cardiac arrest, severe seizures, and respiratory disfunction. The male RTT diagnosis confirmed their worst fears, indicating they had limited time left with their sons.

The emotional toll on parents was highly influenced by their existing social support systems. Many parents described relying heavily on their partners or extended family and friends for comfort and encouragement in their time of grief. Some found strength to carry on from their spiritual lives and religious communities, who helped them to make sense of the diagnosis and what theMECP2variation meant for their child and family. Others were more isolated at the time of diagnosis, as intense caregiving needs for their sons had forced them to give up careers and abandon friendships over time. These families often described intense grief and stress.

Several parents expressed regret about learning the diagnosis, or gratitude that the diagnosis was not found until later in life, as they believed a male RTT diagnosis highly influenced attitudes toward their sons. For this subset of parents, the genetic diagnosis with the subsequent loss of hope for recovery or improvement meant medical teams stopped looking for solutions and the focus shifted toward more palliative care and/or monitoring for regression.

Despite the intense sadness often associated with a male RTT diagnosis, parents frequently reported that having clarity about theMECP2variant provided some relief as it allowed them to stop searching for the cause of their son’s illness and move forward. The most frequently cited positive outcome of the diagnosis was finding an online community of male RTT parent caregivers. In this setting, other parents helped newly diagnosed families to feel less alone and find resources and information they had been unable to find elsewhere.

The diagnosis opened doors for some families to increased support services, such as hospice care which relieved some of their caregiving stressors with around-the-clock nursing, mobility devices, respite, and case management. These services were highly variable across our sample, depending greatly on local healthcare and insurance policies as well as family advocacy. While some families reported these benefits of the accurate diagnosis, others reported no change in service provision after the diagnosis. Some families, particularly those living in the U.S., gained access to RTT specialists, where they developed more clear plans for disease management (e.g., recommendations for seizure medication and sedation, surveillance plans), helping parents to feel more focused in their caregiving activities.

As the first ever FDA approved pharmaceutical treatment for RTT was released during our data collection period, several families reported the accurate diagnosis allowed their sons to begin this treatment, providing them with a new sense of hope. It is important to note that this drug was not available to all families in our study at the time of data collection, as some insurance policies working under the widespread misconception that boys do not have RTT denied coverage, and many participants lived in countries where the drug was not yet available.

For participants with sons diagnosed later in adolescence, who had experienced much longer diagnostic journeys and whose sons were clearly living long past the abbreviated lifespan described in literature, the diagnosis was experienced as a more benign or exclusively positive experience. These parents did not see the new diagnostic label as fundamentally changing their son, yet found utility in RTT-specific literature and clinical care recommendations. They were more likely to dismiss the predictions of dire outcomes in males as they did not align with their own lived experiences. Overall, while the male RTT diagnosis was never a welcome finding, there were many documented benefits to correctly identifying the sources of illness, and most parent participants reported experiencing some relief with the diagnosis.

We present the first mixed-methods investigation of diagnostic experiences in male RTT. Boys in our study were diagnosed between 3 months and 16 years, with a median [IQR] age of 3 years [1.17, 6.50], which is slightly older and more variable than the reported age of diagnosis in females with RTT.30Caregivers identified common concerns at the time of diagnosis, such as seizures, communication issues, and motor problems, overlapping with concerns seen in females with classic RTT.31However, “prognosis/longevity” was the second-highest concern for parents of males, reflecting the lack of informed guidance from providers about how the diagnosis might impact their child’s life.

Caregivers also ranked poor weight gain and breathing problems as critical concerns, emphasizing the importance of earlier diagnosis for this high-risk population. Earlier diagnosis can facilitate aggressive treatment strategies for gastrointestinal issues (e.g., gastrostomy placement),32tailored therapeutic interventions (e.g., assistive technology, physical therapy),33and RTT-specific sedation recommendations to avoid unnecessary interventions such as tracheostomy.34These findings underscore the need for pediatricians and neurologists of boys presenting with unexplained epilepsy, developmental delays, regression, repetitive hand movements, and/or breathing abnormalities to order developmental/intellectual disability panels or exome sequencing. Referral to a neurogeneticist is recommended for non-diagnostic results, as mosaicMECP2variants may require retesting with updated technologies.35

Qualitative findings point to lack of awareness and medical biases as a primary barrier to timely diagnosis. Unlike females, who are often diagnosed clinically and subsequently confirmed genetically,12,36,37males in our sample were exclusively diagnosed through genetic testing. Parents frequently reported that providers did not suspect RTT, hesitated to order genetic tests due to cost concerns, and assumed results would be inconclusive.38These biases and misconceptions contribute to diagnostic delays, highlighting the need for increased provider awareness and education about the broad spectrum of outcomes associated withMECP2variations in males.

The availability of genetic testing is rapidly improving, and as it becomes standard of care for symptoms such as seizures and developmental delays,39,40the age of male RTT diagnosis is expected to decline. However, this trend introduces new challenges, including the need for male-specific anticipatory guidance and updated clinical care recommendations. Historically, males were excluded from RTT research due to low incidence, leaving providers with limited resources for diagnostic conversations and prognostic guidance. Our study of thirty males from the RTT Natural History Study highlights the variability within male RTT phenotypes, including classic and atypical RTT, encephalopathy, and related neurodevelopmental disorders.11Diagnostic conversations should reflect the broad spectrum of outcomes forMECP2mutations in males rather than outdated assumptions that males universally die in infancy.

Parents in our study frequently ignored provider warnings to avoid internet searches about RTT in males, consistent with prior research showing that families of children with rare conditions often rely on the internet and social media for information and peer support.41While some parents reported distress from inaccurate or outdated online content, many found significant value in male RTT-specific online communities. These private social media groups provided social support, research updates, and shared experiences about medical decision-making and care resources.42Providers should guide families toward trustworthy online communities, prepare them for potential misinformation, and offer reliable, up-to-date resources on male RTT rather than discouraging internet use altogether.

Parents value diagnostic sessions that provide emotional support, clear follow-up plans, and hopeful messaging.43However, many participants described insensitive comments and provider confusion during diagnosis, leaving them feeling isolated and unsupported. Structured communication tools such as SPIKES or EMPATHY may help guide providers in delivering this rare and life-altering diagnosis with a more sensitive approach.44-46Interestingly, for some parents, receiving the diagnosis brought relief and closure after a lengthy diagnostic odyssey, illustrating the broader benefits of an accurate genetic diagnosis for the family system.47,48Notably, specific terminology was also important to our participants; some caregivers preferred “RTT” to “male RTT” or other (more or less specific) descriptors, as naming impacts their access to support communities, therapeutic options (e.g., FDA-approved medications), and research participation.

Study limitations include a relatively small sample size that limits generalizability to the broader male RTT population. Although we enrolled nearly half of known males with loss-of-functionMECP2mutations, under-ascertainment likely occurred due to provider misconceptions and limited access to genetic testing. Recruitment through a private male RTT social media group may also have introduced sampling bias, as these families’ experiences may differ from those not connected to such communities. The cross-sectional design captured lived experiences at various life stages but lacked longitudinal follow-up. Additionally, the rapidly evolving genetics landscape (e.g., earlier diagnosis, gene therapies, increased awareness) may reduce the prevalence of diagnostic delays or feelings of despair for families in the near future. The retrospective nature of caregiver reports introduces the potential for recall bias, especially for events in the distant past. Further, differences in genetic companies’ reporting thresholds (seeSupplemental Table 1) limit our understanding of individuals with mosaicism. We also did not collect information on maternal phenotype, limiting our understanding of those with maternally inherited variants. Finally, the study was limited to English-speaking participants due to team and funding constraints. Future research should incorporate multilingual approaches to improve diversity and representation.

Earlier diagnosis of male RTT is critical for enabling targeted care plans and expanding families’ social support networks. However, medical biases, misconceptions, and low incidence have historically delayed diagnosis. As genetic testing becomes more accessible, providers must improve their understanding ofMECP2variations in males, deliver diagnoses with empathy, and provide accurate, up-to-date prognostic and treatment information. This approach can help families adapt more positively to this life-altering diagnosis. Our findings likely generalize to the growing number of rare neurodevelopmental disorders (>800).49With the rapid expansion of genetic testing and recognition of broader phenotypes, clinicians face increasing challenges in staying informed. Rather than faulting clinicians, empathetic alternatives—such as longer or more frequent visits, expert consultations, and annual updates on advances in the field—should be explored, though these solutions are often constrained by workload and financial limitations.
