# Spatial and graded heterogeneity of GD2 expression in neuroblastoma revealed by whole-slide immunohistochemistry.
**Authors:** Fujikawa T, Inoue S, Nishimura A, Nakatani N, Yamamoto N, Hyodo T, Yoshida M, Hasegawa D, Nozu K, Tamura A
**Journal:** Cancer Immunol Immunother (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00262-026-04508-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00262-026-04508-7)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Anti-GD2 immunotherapy has improved outcomes in high-risk neuroblastoma; however, therapeutic responses remain heterogeneous. Although GD2 is generally assumed to be uniformly expressed in neuroblastoma, its spatial distribution within clinical tumors has not been systematically characterized. We therefore investigated intratumoral GD2 heterogeneity using whole-slide immunohistochemistry and explored its molecular regulation.

METHODS: GD2 expression was evaluated in 45 FFPE neuroblastoma tumors using standardized whole-slide immunohistochemistry and independently assessed by two pathologists. GD2 surface expression was analyzed by flow cytometry in 12 neuroblastoma cell lines. Functional roles of ganglioside biosynthetic enzymes were evaluated using siRNA-mediated knockdown.

RESULTS: Whole-slide immunohistochemistry demonstrated frequent intratumoral GD2 heterogeneity. Fourteen tumors (31%) contained <90% GD2-positive tumor cells, with spatial coexistence of GD2-positive and GD2-negative tumor cell populations within the same lesion. One tumor showed minimal GD2 expression with only rare GD2-positive tumor cells. Consistent with these tissue-level findings, GD2 surface expression varied widely across neuroblastoma cell lines and within clonally related subpopulations. Among ganglioside biosynthetic enzymes, ST8SIA1 showed the strongest association with GD2 expression, and siRNA-mediated suppression of ST8SIA1 significantly reduced GD2 surface expression.

CONCLUSIONS: GD2 expression in neuroblastoma is frequently heterogeneous, with spatial coexistence of GD2-positive and GD2-negative tumor cells within individual tumors. These findings suggest that GD2 exists along a biological continuum rather than a uniform phenotype and provide a framework for future studies investigating the biological and clinical significance of GD2 heterogeneity. Neuroblastoma is the most common extracranial solid tumor of childhood and is characterized by marked biological and clinical heterogeneity [ 1 2 ]. Despite advances in multimodal therapy, high-risk neuroblastoma continues to have a poor prognosis, with long-term survival rates remaining below 50% [ 3– 5 ].

Disialoganglioside GD2 is a glycosphingolipid abundantly expressed on the surface of neuroblastoma cells and has emerged as a critical therapeutic target [ 5 6 ]. The introduction of anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody–based immunotherapy has significantly improved outcomes for patients with high-risk neuroblastoma and is now incorporated into standard post-consolidation maintenance therapy, with recent studies evaluating its use in frontline treatment [ 7– 9 ]. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of patients experience relapse or disease progression despite anti-GD2 therapy, indicating that the therapeutic efficacy of GD2-targeted immunotherapy remains incomplete [ 10 11 ].

At the molecular level, ganglioside biosynthesis is a tightly regulated, multistep process mediated by glycosyltransferases and sialyltransferases that are spatially organized within the Golgi apparatus (Fig. 1) [ 12 13 ]. Fig. 1 Schematic overview of the ganglioside biosynthetic pathway

ST3GAL5 (GM3 synthase) initiates this pathway by converting lactosylceramide to GM3, which is subsequently converted to GD3 by ST8SIA1 (GD3 synthase), a key branching point toward b-series ganglioside synthesis. GD3 is further converted to GD2 by B4GALNT1 (GM2/GD2 synthase), with B3GALT4 contributing to downstream ganglioside elongation [ 12 13 ]. Through this coordinated enzymatic cascade, GD2 is synthesized and trafficked to the plasma membrane, where it functions as a bioactive glycolipid involved in cell signaling and membrane organization, with emerging evidence suggesting roles in regulating cellular behaviors associated with invasion and metastasis rather than serving as a passive structural component [ 14 15 ].

Key enzymes involved in ganglioside biosynthesis are shown in boxed labels, including ST3GAL5 (GM3 synthase), ST8SIA1 (GD3 synthase), B4GALNT1 (GM2/GD2 synthase), B3GALT4, and ST3GAL2/3. Solid outlines indicate the 0-series, a-series, and b-series ganglioside branches. Arrows represent individual enzymatic conversion steps leading to the generation of complex gangliosides.

Although GD2 has traditionally been regarded as uniformly expressed in neuroblastoma, emerging evidence suggests that its expression may be more variable than previously appreciated [ 16– 18 ]. However, the extent, spatial organization, and biological significance of intratumoral GD2 heterogeneity remain unclear. In this study, we investigated the spatial distribution and abundance of GD2 expression in neuroblastoma by integrating whole-slide immunohistochemistry of patient tumors with complementary molecular and functional analyses in neuroblastoma cell lines, aiming to clarify the biological basis of GD2 variability.

Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples were obtained from patients with newly diagnosed neuroblastoma or ganglioneuroblastoma, nodular type, whose tumor specimens were available and analyzable for research purposes between 2007 and 2024 at Kobe University Hospital and Kobe Children’s Hospital. Ganglioneuroblastoma, nodular type was included because its clinical behavior is primarily determined by the neuroblastoma component according to the International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC) [ 19 ]. Normal brain tissue used as a positive control was obtained from tissue resected during epilepsy surgery. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kobe University, Kobe, Japan (IRB numbers: B230009 and B250120).

All tumor samples were fixed in 10–20% formalin and embedded in paraffin. Antigen retrieval was carried out using citraconic anhydride (Immunosaver, 1:200, Nisshin EM) for 45 min at 95 °C in a temperature-controlled water bath. After washing in distilled water, endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. The sections were then washed again in distilled water and Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), followed by incubation at room temperature for 60 min with Blocking One Histo (Nacalai Tesque). After washing with TBST, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-mouse monoclonal GD2 primary antibody (Clone 14G2a, 1:100, BioLegend). In selected cases, adjacent serial sections were additionally stained with an anti-synaptophysin antibody (Synaptophysin [D8F6H] rabbit monoclonal antibody, 1:200; Cell Signaling Technology). Synaptophysin staining was performed to confirm the neuroblastoma component and to verify that GD2 immunoreactivity corresponded to tumor cells rather than surrounding stromal elements. After three washes with TBST, the sections were incubated for 30 min with a secondary detection reagent (Dako REAL™ EnVision™ Detection System, HRP-labeled polymer anti-rabbit/mouse; Agilent Technologies). Positive labeling was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) (Agilent Technologies). Each section was counterstained with hematoxylin. Normal brain tissue was used as a positive control, and a mouse IgG2a, κ (clone eBM2a, 1:100, eBioscience™, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as an isotype control. The stained slides were digitized using a slide scanner (Slideview VS200, Olympus).

Immunohistochemical analysis of GD2 expression was independently performed by two pathologists based on visual assessment and was restricted to the neuroblastoma component identified on whole-slide images. GD2 expression was evaluated at the whole-slide level. Tumors were classified according to the proportion of GD2-positive tumor cells throughout the neuroblastoma area of the slide as either ≥90% or <90%. The 90% threshold was selected as a predefined criterion to distinguish tumors with near-uniform GD2 expression from those with <90% GD2-positive tumor cells.

Inter-observer agreement for classification according to the ≥90% versus <90% threshold was assessed using percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Cases with discrepant initial assessments were subsequently reviewed jointly by the two pathologists, and a consensus classification was assigned.

The human neuroblastoma cell lines LA-N-1, LA-N-5, CHP-134, and NB-69 were obtained from the RIKEN BRC through the National Bio-Resource Project of the MEXT, Japan. NH-12, TN-2, NB-1, and SCCH-26 were obtained from Cell Resource Center for Biomedical Research Institute of Development, Aging and Cancer Tohoku University. SK-N-AS, SH-SY5Y, and IMR-32 were obtained from the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC). BE(2)-C was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. All cell lines were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free and authenticated by STR profiling. MYCN amplification status of neuroblastoma cell lines was obtained from the original characterization studies or information provided by the cell repositories. BE(2)-C, CHP-134, LA-N-1, LA-N-5, NB-1 and IMR-32 are MYCN-amplified cell lines, whereas SH-SY5Y, SK-N-AS, NB-69, and NH-12 lack MYCN amplification [ 20– 23 ]. Information regarding MYCN amplification status was not available for TN-2 and SCCH-26.

LA-N-5 xenograft tumors were established in 4-week-old NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid/J (NOD/SCID) mice (The Jackson Laboratory Japan). A total of 2.0 × 107 LA-N-5 cells suspended in Matrigel Matrix (Corning) were injected subcutaneously into the left flank. Tumors were harvested 34 days after implantation, fixed in 10% formalin, and embedded in paraffin. FFPE tumor sections were subjected to GD2 immunohistochemistry using the identical protocol applied to patient tumor specimens. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kobe University (approval number: 210601-R2).

Flow cytometric analyses were performed to evaluate the surface expression of disialoganglioside GD2 in neuroblastoma cells. Cells were stained on ice with an APC-conjugated anti-human GD2 antibody (clone 14G2a, BioLegend) or an APC-conjugated isotype control antibody (mouse IgG2a, κ; clone MOPC-173, BioLegend), protected from light. Dead cells were excluded using Zombie Aqua Fixable Viability Dye (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After two washes with FACS buffer, cells were subjected to flow cytometric analysis or cell sorting as described below.

GD2 surface expression was analyzed in twelve human neuroblastoma cell lines by flow cytometry using a BD LSRFortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10.10.1).

GD2 surface expression in SK-N-AS cells was analyzed seven days after siRNA transfection. GD2 expression levels were quantified as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the GD2-APC signal.

To isolate GD2-high and GD2-low subpopulations, SK-N-AS cells were stained as described above and sorted using a BD FACSMelody cell sorter (BD Biosciences). Live single cells were gated, and the top 20% and bottom 20% of GD2-expressing cells were defined as the GD2-high and GD2-low populations, respectively. Sorted cells were then processed for RNA extraction, followed by reverse transcription to generate cDNA and subsequent quantitative real-time PCR analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using a QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer sequences for ST3GAL5, ST8SIA1, B4GALNT1, B3GALT4, and GAPDH are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s, and a dissociation stage. All samples were analyzed in triplicate. Cq values were determined using QuantStudio Design and Analysis Software (version 1.5.2).

For gene silencing experiments, SK-N-AS cells were transfected with siRNAs targeting ST3GAL5, ST8SIA1, B4GALNT1, and B3GALT4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). siRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2. A non-targeting siRNA was used as a negative control (Silencer Select Negative Control #1, Cat. No. 4390843, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells/well in 12-well plates and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). siRNAs were transfected at a final concentration of 5 nM, and cells were maintained for up to 7 days post-transfection.

Knockdown efficiency was evaluated by RT-qPCR and ranged from 61.7 to 92.9%, with approximately 90% knockdown achieved for ST3GAL5, ST8SIA1, and B4GALNT1 (Supplementary Table S3).

Associations between GD2 immunohistochemical expression patterns (≥ 90% versus <90% GD2-positive tumor cells) and clinicopathological variables were assessed using Fisher’s exact test with odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. In neuroblastoma cell line analyses, correlations between GD2 surface expression (MFI) and mRNA expression of ganglioside biosynthetic enzymes were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation, and differences between GD2-high and GD2-low groups, including FACS-sorted SK-N-AS subpopulations, were analyzed using Welch’s t-test. Comparisons of GD2 MFI following siRNA-mediated knockdown were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction. Fisher’s exact test was conducted in R, and all other analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software). All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 45 neuroblastoma samples were included in this study (Table 1). At diagnosis, 16 patients (36%) were younger than 18 months and 29 (64%) were 18 months or older. Most tumors were histologically classified as poorly differentiated or undifferentiated neuroblastoma (35 cases, 78%), followed by ganglioneuroblastoma, nodular type (9 cases, 20%), and differentiating neuroblastoma (1 case, 2%). MYCN amplification status was available for 42 cases, with amplification detected in 13 tumors (29%) and absent in 29 tumors (64%). The mitosis-karyorrhexis index was low, intermediate, and high in 18 (40%), 18 (40%), and 9 cases (20%), respectively. According to the INPC, 12 tumors (27%) were classified as favorable histology and 33 (73%) as unfavorable. Based on the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group Staging System (INRGSS), most patients had metastatic disease (stage M, 33 cases, 73%), while 6 (13%) and 4 (9%) patients had L1 and L2 disease, respectively; stage information was unavailable in 2 cases (4%). At last follow-up, 30 patients (67%) were alive and 15 (33%) had died. The median follow-up period was 112 months (range, 6–211 months).

To validate the specificity and reliability of GD2 immunohistochemistry, normal brain tissue was stained as a positive control, and mouse IgG2a, κ was used as an isotype control. In normal brain tissue, neuronal cell bodies showed strong GD2 immunoreactivity, whereas axons exhibited weaker staining (Fig. 2a). In neuroblastoma specimens, the isotype control showed minimal background staining (Fig. 2b), whereas GD2 immunostaining of a corresponding section from the same specimen demonstrated strong tumor-cell staining (Fig. 2c). In addition, GD2 immunostaining showed no staining in vascular endothelial cells and a lack of GD2 expression in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (Fig. 2d, e). Furthermore, GD2 immunohistochemistry performed on FFPE xenograft tumors generated from LA-N-5 cells demonstrated diffuse and strong tumor-cell staining, whereas the corresponding isotype control showed minimal background staining (Supplementary Fig. S1). These findings further support the specificity and robustness of the staining protocol. Fig. 2 Validation of the specificity and reliability of GD2 immunohistochemistry. a Normal brain tissue showing GD2 immunoreactivity in neuronal cell bodies (black arrows). b Isotype control staining of a neuroblastoma specimen using mouse IgG2a, κ, showing minimal background staining. c GD2 immunostaining of a corresponding section from the same neuroblastoma specimen, showing strong tumor-cell staining. d GD2 immunostaining showing absence of staining in vascular endothelial cells (red arrow). e GD2 immunostaining demonstrating lack of GD2 expression in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (red arrow).

Using this validated staining protocol, we next evaluated GD2 expression patterns in FFPE tumor sections from 45 patients. Clinical information of the cases and the corresponding immunohistochemical findings are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Inter-observer agreement for classification according to the ≥ 90% versus < 90% threshold showed an overall agreement of 82.2% and a Cohen's kappa coefficient of 0.56. Initial disagreement occurred in 8 of 45 cases, all of which were resolved by joint review and consensus assessment. Based on whole-slide assessment, 31 tumors (69%) were classified as having ≥90% GD2-positive tumor cells (Fig. 3), whereas 14 tumors (31%) were classified as having <90% GD2-positive tumor cells (Fig. 4). In tumors with <90% GD2-positive tumor cells, GD2-positive and GD2-negative tumor cells coexisted within the same lesion, demonstrating marked spatial heterogeneity of GD2 expression (Fig. 4). Among the tumors classified as having <90% GD2-positive tumor cells, one case showed only minimal GD2 expression, with almost complete absence of GD2 staining in tumor cells (Fig. 5). We next examined whether GD2 expression patterns were associated with clinicopathological characteristics. Patients younger than 18 months accounted for 11 of 31 cases (35%) in the ≥90% GD2-positive tumor cell group and 5 of 14 cases (36%) in the <90% GD2-positive tumor cell group. GD2 expression patterns were not associated with age at diagnosis (odds ratio [OR] = 0.99, 95% CI 0.22–4.75; Fisher’s exact test, p = 1.00). MYCN amplification was detected in 8 of 31 tumors (26%) in the ≥90% group and in 5 of 14 tumors (36%) in the <90% group. No significant association was observed between MYCN status and GD2 expression patterns (OR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.16–2.60; p = 0.52). Similarly, unfavorable histology according to the INPC classification was present in 21 of 31 tumors (68%) in the ≥90% group and in 12 of 14 tumors (86%) in the <90% group. GD2 expression patterns were not significantly associated with INPC classification (OR = 2.86, 95% CI 0.52–28.3; p = 0.28). INRG stage M disease was observed in 22 of 31 patients (71%) in the ≥90% group and in 11 of 14 patients (79%) in the <90% group. No significant association was detected between GD2 expression pattern and INRG stage M disease (OR = 1.50; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.73).

Among patients classified as high risk according to the INRG classification (n = 27), survival differed according to the pattern of GD2 expression. Deaths occurred in 7 of 17 patients in the ≥90% group compared with 8 of 10 patients in the <90% group. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, a higher proportion of deaths was observed in the <90% group (OR = 5.34, 95% CI 0.74–66.88; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.11).

To establish a quantitative framework for GD2 expression heterogeneity across neuroblastoma cell lines, we first measured GD2 surface expression by flow cytometry in a panel of 12 neuroblastoma cell lines. Flow cytometric analysis of 12 neuroblastoma cell lines demonstrated marked differences in GD2 surface expression across the cell lines (Fig. 6A). SK-N-AS, LA-N-1, LA-N-5, CHP-134, IMR-32, TN-2, NH-12, NB-1, and SCCH-26 showed clear GD2 positivity. In contrast, NB-69 showed dim GD2 expression, whereas BE(2)-C and SH-SY5Y showed minimal to no GD2 expression. To examine whether heterogeneity of GD2 expression across neuroblastoma cell lines is associated with transcriptional regulation of ganglioside biosynthetic enzymes, we quantified the expression levels of ST3GAL5, ST8SIA1, B4GALNT1, and B3GALT4 by RT-qPCR. Because GD2 surface expression measured by flow cytometry spanned a broad and continuous range, correlations between enzyme expression and GD2 MFI were assessed using Pearson’s correlation (Fig. 6B). GD2 MFI showed a strong positive correlation with ST8SIA1 expression (r = 0.83, p = 0.007), whereas no significant correlations were observed for ST3GAL5 (r = 0.24, p = 0.46) or B4GALNT1 (r = 0.01, p = 0.96). B3GALT4 expression showed a moderate but non-significant correlation with GD2 MFI (r = 0.51, p = 0.09).

Among the enzymes examined, ST8SIA1 showed the strongest association with GD2 expression status across neuroblastoma cell lines.

To determine whether GD2 expression heterogeneity within a single genetic background is linked to transcriptional regulation of ganglioside biosynthetic enzymes, SK-N-AS cells were sorted into GD2-high and GD2-low subpopulations by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). The expression of ST3GAL5, ST8SIA1, B4GALNT1, and B3GALT4 was quantified by RT-qPCR (Fig. 7A). Compared with GD2-high subpopulations, GD2-low subpopulations showed reduced ST3GAL5 expression (p = 0.0162) and an even greater reduction in ST8SIA1 expression (p = 0.0045), whereas B4GALNT1 and B3GALT4 expression did not differ significantly between the two subpopulations (p = 0.3795 and p = 0.117, respectively). Collectively, these results show that GD2-high and GD2-low subpopulations coexist within a single neuroblastoma cell line and are distinguished by differential expression of ganglioside biosynthetic enzymes, with ST8SIA1 showing the strongest association with GD2 expression.

To directly assess the functional contribution of each ganglioside biosynthetic enzyme to GD2 expression, siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments were performed in SK-N-AS cells. GD2 surface expression was quantified by flow cytometry and compared across treatment groups (Fig. 7B). A significant difference in GD2 MFI was observed among groups (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.0082). Post hoc Dunn’s multiple comparisons test demonstrated that ST8SIA1 knockdown resulted in a significant reduction in GD2 MFI compared with negative control siRNA-treated cells (adjusted p = 0.0056). In contrast, knockdown of ST3GAL5, B4GALNT1, or B3GALT4 did not significantly alter GD2 expression.

Collectively, these results demonstrated that GD2 surface expression is specifically reduced by ST8SIA1 knockdown, whereas knockdown of other ganglioside biosynthetic enzymes has no significant effect.

Disialoganglioside GD2 has long been regarded as a uniformly and abundantly expressed surface antigen in neuroblastoma and serves as a principal target of GD2-directed immunotherapy [ 24 25 ]. Although variability in GD2 expression has been suggested in neuroblastic tumors, its spatial organization within tumors has not been systematically characterized using whole-slide evaluation of FFPE tissue sections [ 13 14 ].

In contrast to the prevailing assumption of uniform GD2 expression, our immunohistochemical analyses of clinical neuroblastoma specimens demonstrated that intratumoral GD2 heterogeneity is a common feature of neuroblastoma. Approximately one-third of tumors contained <90% GD2-positive tumor cells, in which GD2-positive and GD2-negative tumor cells coexisted within the same lesion. We also identified one tumor showing only minimal GD2 expression, with virtually all tumor cells lacking detectable GD2 immunoreactivity. Together, these findings indicate that intratumoral GD2 expression is more complex than a simple binary “on/off” pattern of antigen expression. Although GD2 is generally considered a broadly expressed antigen in neuroblastoma, our findings suggest that rare GD2-low or GD2-negative tumors may occur.

While lipid extraction during FFPE processing may theoretically affect membrane-associated glycolipids such as GD2, the extent to which this influences immunohistochemical detection remains unclear. In the present study, appropriate positive and isotype controls together with additional validation using FFPE xenograft tumors supported the specificity and reproducibility of our staining protocol. Furthermore, the reproducible and spatially organized staining patterns observed across multiple tumors would be difficult to explain solely by uniform lipid loss during FFPE processing. In addition, tumors with both ≥90% and <90% GD2-positive tumor cells were observed throughout the specimen collection period, suggesting that the observed heterogeneity is unlikely to be explained solely by prolonged storage of older archived FFPE specimens.

Importantly, the ≥90% versus <90% classification was not associated with established clinicopathological risk factors, including MYCN amplification status, INPC histology, or metastatic stage. This independence suggests that GD2 heterogeneity may capture a biological dimension distinct from conventional risk stratification. Although GD2 signaling has been implicated in tumor cell proliferation and metastatic behavior [ 26– 28 ], we observed a trend toward poorer survival in the <90% group. While this association did not reach statistical significance in our cohort—likely reflecting the limited sample size—these findings are hypothesis-generating and warrant validation in larger independent cohorts.

Consistent with the tissue-level findings, neuroblastoma cell lines exhibited a continuous spectrum of GD2 surface expression. Among ganglioside biosynthetic enzymes, ST8SIA1 showed the strongest association with GD2 expression across cell lines and within clonally related subpopulations. FACS-isolated GD2-low SK-N-AS cells displayed marked ST8SIA1 downregulation, and siRNA-mediated suppression of ST8SIA1 specifically reduced GD2 surface expression, supporting its role as a key regulator of GD2 expression. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for the observed intratumoral GD2 heterogeneity. Our findings are consistent with previous studies identifying ST8SIA1 as a major determinant of GD2 expression in neuroblastoma cells [ 29 30 ].

Taken together, our findings support the concept that GD2 expression is dynamically regulated in neuroblastoma. Previous studies have suggested that transcriptional and epigenetic regulation of glycosyltransferases contributes to the diversity of ganglioside expression profiles [ 29 30 ], and our results are consistent with this framework. Because anti-GD2 antibody therapy was approved in Japan only in 2021 for patients with neuroblastoma following high-dose chemotherapy and autologous stem cell transplantation, only three patients in our cohort received this treatment. Therefore, the present study was not designed to evaluate the relationship between GD2 expression patterns and clinical response to anti-GD2 immunotherapy. Instead, our survival analysis was intended to explore whether GD2 heterogeneity itself was associated with the intrinsic biological behavior of neuroblastoma.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Analyses of GD2 expression dynamics were constrained by the limited availability of longitudinal tumor specimens and the mechanistic studies were limited to a single neuroblastoma cell line. Furthermore, clinical survival analyses were underpowered to detect statistically significant differences between tumors with ≥90% and <90% GD2-positive tumor cells. In addition, because this retrospective study relied on archived FFPE specimens collected over an extended period, differences in antigen preservation related to specimen age cannot be completely excluded. Future studies using larger patient cohorts, longitudinal sampling, and additional neuroblastoma models, including MYCN-amplified cell lines, will be important to further clarify the biological and clinical significance of GD2 heterogeneity.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that GD2 expression in neuroblastoma is frequently heterogeneous and appears to be independent of established clinicopathological risk factors. By integrating pathological and cellular analyses, we show that GD2 expression exists along a biologically regulated continuum rather than as a uniformly expressed phenotype. These findings extend current knowledge regarding the biological regulation of GD2 expression and establish a foundation for future studies investigating the biological and clinical significance of GD2 heterogeneity.
