# Expanding the Epidemiological and Phenotypic Spectrum of MEGDEL Syndrome: The First Case Report From Egypt.
**Authors:** Sadek AA, Aladawy MA, Mansour TM, Fouad MF, Magdy RM, Elmoursy MM, Abdelkreem E
**Journal:** Clin Med Insights Pediatr (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1177/11795565251348345](https://doi.org/10.1177/11795565251348345)

## Content

MEGDEL syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disease characterized by 3-methylglutaconic aciduria, deafness-dystonia, hepatopathy, encephalopathy, and leigh-like syndrome, which results from biallelic pathogenic variants in SERAC1 gene. The diagnosis is commonly challenging due to the diverse clinical manifestations. Herein, we report the first case of MEGDEL syndrome from the Egyptian population. This is a 7-year-old boy born to first cousins Arab parents from Egypt with family history of unexplained deaths of 3 siblings during the neonatal period. He presented with developmental regression since the age of 2 years resulting in marked muscle weakness with no head support, generalized spasticity more prominent in lower limbs, and aphonia, but intact hearing. This report expands the epidemiological and phenotypic spectrum of MEGDEL syndrome by reporting the first case from the Egyptian population who had relatively delayed onset and no evident hepatopathy or deafness.

Abstract

MEGDEL syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disease characterized by 3-methylglutaconic aciduria, deafness-dystonia, hepatopathy, encephalopathy, and leigh-like syndrome, which results from biallelic pathogenic variants in SERAC1 gene. The diagnosis is commonly challenging due to the diverse clinical manifestations. Herein, we report the first case of MEGDEL syndrome from the Egyptian population. This is a 7-year-old boy born to first cousins Arab parents from Egypt with family history of unexplained deaths of 3 siblings during the neonatal period. He presented with developmental regression since the age of 2 years resulting in marked muscle weakness with no head support, generalized spasticity more prominent in lower limbs, and aphonia, but intact hearing. The child had excessive urinary excretion of 3-methylglutaconic acid, and his brain magnetic resonance imaging showed characteristic basal ganglia affection with “Putaminal eye sign.” Whole-exome sequencing demonstrated a likely pathogenic homozygous c.1404-2A>G variant in SERAC1 gene. This report expands the epidemiological and phenotypic spectrum of MEGDEL syndrome by reporting the first case from the Egyptian population who had relatively delayed onset and no evident hepatopathy or deafness.

Keywords: MEGDEL syndrome, 3-methylglutaconic aciduria, SERAC1, variant, Egypt

Introduction

MEGDEL syndrome (OMIM #614739), also known as 3-methylglutaconic aciduria type VI, is a rare autosomal recessive mitochondrial disease characterized by 3-methylglutaconic aciduria (MEG), deafness-dystonia (D), encephalopathy (E), and leigh-like syndrome (L); the acronym MEGD(H)EL is also used since most patients manifest hepatopathy (H).
1
 Patients with MEGDEL syndrome exhibit a broad phenotypic spectrum that involves not only the brain, ears, and liver but also the eyes, heart, endocrine glands, peripheral nerves, and skeletal muscles.
2
 Most common is the severe infantile type, which typically presents in the neonatal period with hypoglycemia and sepsis-like condition of unidentified cause. During the first year of life, patients develop feeding troubles, failure to thrive, truncal hypotonia, and transient hepatopathy of variable severity. This is followed in the second year of age by progressive spasticity, dystonia, hypoacusis, and aphonia, resulting in neurodevelopmental delay and regression. Survival is usually shortened, and patients may succumb during neonatal period or at a later age.1,2 While rare, patients with milder forms of juvenile-onset complicated hereditary spastic paraplegia and adult-onset generalized dystonia have been also described.3,4

MEGDEL syndrome results from biallelic pathogenic variants in the serine active site-containing protein 1 (SERAC1) gene (OMIM *614725), which exists on chromosome 6q25.3, spans approximately 59 kb, and contains 17 exons.1,5 SERAC1 is a phosphatidylglycerol remodeling protein located at the mitochondria-endoplasmic reticulum interface and plays a critical role in mitochondrial functions and intracellular cholesterol trafficking.
2
 More than 40 variants have been associated with MEGDEL syndrome,
6
 most of which exist within the serine-lipase domain.
7
 No correlation has been recognized between the genotype and clinical phenotype.
2

To date, more than 100 patients with MEGDEL syndrome have been reported worldwide.
2
 Its prevalence is estimated as 0.9 per million
8
 with approximately 27 new cases being born every year.
7
 To our knowledge, no cases with MEGDEL syndrome have been described from Egypt.

Herein, we report the first case with MEGDEL syndrome from the Egyptian population, who was diagnosed by whole-exome sequencing (WES).

Case Description

A 7-year-old boy presented to our hospital with developmental problems since the age of 1 year. He is the last of 9 children born to first cousins parents of Egyptian Arab descent (Figure 1). Family history revealed 3 siblings’ deaths during the early neonatal period: the eldest sister died on the third day of life due to acute hepatic encephalopathy, the fifth brother passed away on the fourth day of life secondary to severe respiratory distress, and the sixth brother succumbed on the sixth day of life owing to acute encephalopathy. Both parents and other living siblings had no neurodevelopmental abnormalities. The proband was delivered uneventfully by CS at full term after smooth pregnancy. His birth weight was 3.2 kg and APGAR scores of 6, 9, and 10 at 1, 5, and 10 minutes, respectively. There was no history of neonatal jaundice, admission to neonatal intensive care unit, or any remarkable events during the early neonatal period. During the first 12 months of life, the boy achieved normal growth and developmental milestones up to walking supported, mature pincer grasp, 2 to 3 spoken words, and imitating bye-bye. He did not experience jaundice, abdominal pain, or any significant health problems during infancy. At the age of 13 months, the child had acute febrile illness associated with an episode of generalized tonic-clonic seizures that resolved uneventfully in less than 5 minutes and was diagnosed as a simple febrile seizure. However, the mother noticed that her son could not walk independently till 2 years of age, after which he lost his ability to stand and manifested repetitive twisting movements. Developmental regression continued as the child could no longer sit independently after 3 months, and he became unable to support his head at the age of 31 months. By the age of 3 years, the child developed progressive feeding difficulty and could no longer speak any words, and his communication was restricted to some uncomprehensive sounds and mild facial expressions. Nevertheless, the child could hear and respond to his name.

Evaluation at the age of 28 months revealed dystonia that was more prominent than spasticity, but serial assessment showed progressive spasticity. The parents were reluctant to do advanced investigations and missed follow-up for a long time. On examination at the age of 7 years, the patient appeared bedridden and in a flexion position although alert with good eye contact. His weight (14 kg), length (92 cm), and head circumference (48 cm) were all below third centile. The child could only produce uncomprehensive sounds with no understandable words. Neurological exam showed generalized spasticity that was more prominent in the lower than upper limbs in association with exaggerated deep tendon reflexes and positive Babinski sign. Dystonic movement was also observed.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed bilateral symmetrical T2 signal hyperintensities in basal ganglia involving mainly the lentiform nucleus with mid-dorsal putamen sparing (putaminal eye sign) in association with mild periventricular deep white matter hyperintensities and brain atrophic changes (Figure 2). Auditory brain stem response test, fundus exam, electrocardiogram, echocardiography, and electroencephalography were normal. Laboratory tests at the age of 6 years showed alanine aminotransferase of 22 U/l, asparate aminotransferase 18 U/l, total bilirubin 0.9 mg/dl, direct bilirubin 0.1 mg/dl, creatinine 0.6 mg/dl, ammonia 38 μmol/l, and lactate 2.1 mmol/l. Lipid profile was within normal range. Acylcarnitine profile by tandem mass spectrometry showed no abnormalities, while urinary organic acids analysis by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry revealed elevated 3-methylglutaric acid of 14.65 mg/gm creatinine (normal range 0-6.7 mg/gm creatinine). Whole-exome sequencing (WES) demonstrated a homozygous splice site variant in SERAC1 gene (NM_032861.3:c.1404-2A>G). The parents declined to undergo genetic testing for segregation analysis. The child was diagnosed with MEGDEL syndrome and managed by a multidisciplinary team, including pediatric neurologist, metabolic pediatrician, dietician, physiotherapist, and orthotherapist. The child received baclofen and physiotherapy for spasticity, nasogastric tube feeding, and regular audiological and ophthalmological monitoring.

Discussion

This case report describes the first case of MEGDEL syndrome in the Egyptian population. The case was suspected based on the presence of developmental regression, spasticity, and dystonia coupled with excessive urinary excretion of 3-methylglutaconic acid and characteristic “putaminal eye” appearance in brain MRI, and the diagnosis was confirmed by WES.

Interestingly, the proband has a family history of 3 siblings who passed away during the neonatal period due to unexplained encephalopathy or respiratory distress, which is highly suggestive of MEGDEL syndrome. This is consistent with prior reports of neonatal deaths of cases with MEGDEL syndrome or their siblings.6,7,9
-11 SERAC1 deficiency impairs the biosynthesis and remodeling of phosphatidylglycerol, which is the latest expressed surfactant whose deficiency interferes with lung maturity.
9
 Moreover, phosphatidylglycerol is the precursor of cardiolipin and bis (mono-) acylglycerolphosphate, which are essential for mitochondrial functions and intracellular cholesterol trafficking, respectively.
5
 These pathophysiological abnormalities explain the commonly observed severe manifestations in neonatal-onset MEGDEL syndrome, including hypoglycemia, severe liver dysfunction, lactic acidosis, and respiratory distress, which could contribute to mortality.7,9 In contrast, the proband displayed no remarkable health problems during the first year of life, after which he developed gradual neurodevelopmental delay and regression. This underscores the previously described lack of genotype-phenotype correlation in MEGDEL syndrome.2,7 Indeed, patients with MEGDEL syndrome who have the same SERAC1 variant may manifest different phenotypes in terms of age at onset and clinical severity.1,7

Another important aspect of the current case report is the lack of some typical features of MEGDEL syndrome, particularly neonatal manifestations, hepatopathy, and deafness. Among previously published 101 cases of MEGDEL syndrome, 73% had neonatal manifestations, 82% liver affection, and 78% deafness (Table 1).4,6,7,9,10,12
-27 Therefore, these manifestations are not universal in all cases, but their combined absence seems rare and would impose diagnostic challenges. Nevertheless, our case was diagnosed at the age of 7 years, which is the same median age at diagnosis in the published international cohort of 67 cases of MEGDEL syndrome.
7
 Similar to our proband, other reports have described cases of MEGDEL syndrome with no hearing impairment.4,12,13,20 However, this does not absolutely exclude the development of deafness at a later age. Deafness is an important feature of MEGDEL due to its association with impaired speech, which is a common feature as only 11% could speak some words by the age of 8 years.
7
 In the present study, the proband could say 2 to 3 words by 1 year of age but lost his speaking ability later. Interestingly, our case developed aphonia despite of yet intact hearing, which indicates that hearing impairment is not the only reason for speech loss; other suggested causes are intellectual impairment, extrapyramidal tongue movement, dysphagia, drooling, and basal ganglia affection.2,7 On the other side, the onset of neurodevelopmental regression and dystonia at 24 months seems modestly delayed compared with their described median range of 12 and 18 months, respectively.1,7

One of the important diagnostic clues in our case was the “putaminal eye” sign in brain MRI. Patients with MEGDEL syndrome exhibit characteristic brain MRI pattern involving the basal ganglia, particularly the putamen. Wortmann et al
28
 postulated 5 stages of brain MRI patterns over the clinical disease course. The first stage includes pallidal T2 signal changes. The second stage involves swelling of the caudate nucleus and putamen, sparing the dorsal putamen which appears as a normal area encircled by T2 hyperintensity (putaminal eye). The next stages involve gradual disappearance of the “putaminal eye,” basal ganglia degeneration, and cortical/cerebellar atrophy. Indeed, symmetrical affection of basal ganglia is frequent in inborn errors of metabolism, particularly acute phase of organic acidurias and mitochondrial diseases.
29
 For instance, acute alteration of the putamen and caudate nucleus is a typical feature of glutaric aciduria type 1 (GA1). However, GA1 usually affects the dorsal putamen, whereas MEGDEL typically involves the anterior to mid-dorsal putamen. Furthermore, GA1 is characterized by wide Sylvian fissure and extrastriatal abnormalities (eg, white matter changes), which are absent in MEGDEL syndrome.28,30 In another example, putaminal and caudal abnormalities have been also reported in a patient with 3-hydroxybutric aciduria, but this is not a typical feature in other patients.28,31 Finally, Leigh syndrome subcategory of mitochondrial diseases is associated with basal ganglia abnormalities, but not following the pattern sequence and “putaminal eye” of MEGDEL syndrome. For instance, a patient with a coenzyme Q10 deficiency due to PDSS2 mutations was described to have putaminal lesions in the mid-dorsal part with sparing of other regions, which is the reverse pattern seen in MEGDEL syndrome.
32
 This suggests a differential regional affection of putamen in various mitochondrial disorders.
28

Another clue to the diagnosis of MEGDEL syndrome is the excessive urinary excretion of 3-methylglutaconic acids, which has been described in 99% of published cases (Table 1).4,6,7,9,10,12
-27 However, this is not pathognomonic for MEGDEL and may occur secondary to other causes, such as 3-methylglutaconyl-CoA hydratase deficiency (MIM #250950), Sanger syndrome (# 212350), Barth syndrome (#302060), and Costeff syndrome (MIM #258501); these disorders depart from MEGDEL syndrome by distinctive clinical and laboratory features as well as genotyping.
1
 Importantly, studies have shown that urinary excretion of 3-methylglutaconic acid does not correlate with the clinical course of MEEGDEL syndrome.
2

The diagnosis in this reported case was confirmed by WES, which revealed a homozygous c.1404-2A>G SERAC1 gene. This variant is registered in the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD; www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk) in association with MEGDEL syndrome and has been previously described in 2 cases.33,34 The first case is from China, who experienced liver affection since neonatal period and psychomotor retardation since late infancy with hyperlactatemia and abnormal basal ganglia; there was no formal audiovisual examination.
33
 The other case was from Turkey, who had hypotonia, neuromotor retardation, and growth retardation; no other phenotypic data are available.
34
 The SERAC1 c.1404-2A>G is classified as likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics guidelines.
35
 This variant is predicted by in silico tools to disrupt the splice acceptor site of intron 13 with skipping of exon 14, as shown by reduced scores in Human splicing finder (HSF; https://genomnis.com/hsf) from 88.61 to 60.74 and MaxEntScan (http://hollywood.mit.edu/burgelab/maxent/Xmaxentscan_scoreseq.html) from 7.9 to 0.05. Molecular testing does not only confirm the diagnosis of MEGDEL syndrome but also allows for proper genetic counseling, carrier screening among married relatives, and prenatal genetic studies.

We acknowledge some limitations of this case report. First, we could not perform genetic testing for parents and other family members for segregation analysis. Another limitation is the lack of advanced facilities to confirm the pathogenic effect of c.1404-2A>G SERAC1 variant in vitro. Finally, some clinical and laboratory documentations were missed, which did not allow a better depiction of patient’s clinical and radiological course over time.

Conclusion

We report the first case with MEGDEL syndrome from the Egyptian population who had relatively delayed onset and no evident hepatopathy or deafness. This expands the epidemiological and phenotypic spectrum of MEGDEL syndrome and underscores the importance of WES in confirming the diagnosis.
