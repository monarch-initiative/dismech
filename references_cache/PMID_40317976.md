# Clinical Features That Increase Suspicion of Brucella canis Infection in Dogs With Discospondylitis: A Multicenter Case-Control Study.
**Authors:** Moeller CM, Bartges JW, Brust KD, Kass PH, Kelly RF, Lashnits EW, Li CF, Merkel LK, Pritchard JC, Sykes JE
**Journal:** J Vet Intern Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/jvim.70112](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.70112)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Brucella canis is an important cause of discospondylitis in dogs, and brucellosis can be challenging to diagnose. Early recognition could aid in early implementation of precautions to limit zoonotic transmission.

HYPOTHESIS/OBJECTIVES: To determine whether specific clinical features exist that differentiate Brucella canis infection from other causes of discospondylitis in dogs.

ANIMALS: Fifteen dogs with Brucella canis discospondylitis (cases) and 25 dogs with non-Brucella discospondylitis (controls) evaluated between 2010 and 2021.

METHODS: Multi-institutional retrospective case-control study. Clinical features in cases were compared to those in temporally matched controls using conditional logistic regression analysis. Neurologic examination findings and radiographic findings were documented by a board-certified neurologist and radiologist who were masked to group identity.

RESULTS: No case had a history of being bred or kennel housing. Cases were younger (median, 2.6 years) than controls (median, 5.7 years) (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.66-1.00). There were no differences in sex or breed. Compared to controls, cases had a lower odds of neurologic deficits (odds ratio [OR] = 0.03; 95% CI 0.00-0.85), higher odds of C2-C5 involvement (OR = 13.0; 95% CI 1.6-106.7), and higher odds of multiple disc site involvement (OR = 12.0, 95% CI 1.5-95.8). They had a lower odds of neutrophilia (OR = 0.11, 95% CI 0.01-0.92), a higher median hematocrit (50.6% versus 43.7%; OR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.09-2.03), and a higher median lymphocyte count (1.8 × 1000/μL vs. 1.1 × 1000/μL; OR = 2.39, 95% CI 1.05-5.45). There was no difference in the radiographic appearance of lesions between groups.

CONCLUSIONS AND CLINICAL IMPORTANCE: Brucella canis infection should still be considered in cases of discospondylitis regardless of neuter status, sex, and breeding history. Clinical features exist that should increase suspicion for brucellosis.

Brucella canis
 is a gram‐negative bacterium characterized as a “rough” Brucella species based on cytologic characteristics [1]. 
Brucella canis
 is a leading cause of infertility in dogs worldwide but can also cause chronic nonspecific illness and discospondylitis [2, 3, 4]. Transmission can occur through contact with vaginal discharges, semen, aborted materials, urine, milk, saliva, feces, and blood. Case numbers appear to be on the rise in Europe and North America as a result of widespread international and inter‐state trade of subclinically infected dogs from regions where infection is endemic [5, 6, 7]. 
Brucella canis
 is a zoonotic pathogen and infection in humans is reportable to public health authorities. The increased recognition of cases in dogs represents an emerging public health threat [5].



Brucella canis
 infrequently causes discospondylitis in dogs compared to other bacterial and fungal pathogens [8, 9], but it is an important differential because of its zoonotic potential. Diagnosis is challenging because of vague clinical signs such as episodic spinal pain, gait abnormalities, and nonspecific laboratory abnormalities [3, 8]. Because of intermittent bacteremia and pathogen evasion of the host immune response, blood culture and serologic tests might be negative despite chronic infection [2, 10, 11]. Because of these challenges, veterinary workers, laboratory personnel, and dog owners might be unknowingly exposed before the diagnosis is made.

Whether clinicopathologic features, such as thrombocytopenia—which is associated with Brucella infection in humans [12]—could raise suspicion for involvement of Brucella in dogs with discospondylitis is unknown. In previous studies, dogs with 
B. canis
 discospondylitis were often intact males [2], young dogs [7], dogs with multiple disc involvement [7], or dogs with signs of chronic disease [7]. In a 2022 case series of 33 dogs with 
B. canis
 discospondylitis, it was suggested that central lytic (“hole punch”) lesions in vertebral endplates might be a distinguishing feature of infection [7]. However, these studies did not include a control group for comparison.

We hypothesized that dogs with Brucella discospondylitis have a higher odds of exhibiting specific clinicopathologic features than dogs with other causes of discospondylitis. Such features could allow early implementation of public health precautions, including appropriate treatment protocols.

This study was a multi‐institutional retrospective case–control study of dogs evaluated at academic veterinary teaching hospitals at the University of California‐Davis, University of Georgia, University of Minnesota, and University of Wisconsin‐Madison between January 2010 and December 2021. To be eligible for inclusion, dogs had to have an imaging diagnosis of discospondylitis made based on partial or complete images of the spine performed using radiography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or computed tomography (CT). A complete spinal image series was defined as the availability of images from the first cervical vertebra through to the lumbosacral region (C1‐LS). Images had to have been obtained within 60 days of initial diagnosis. If images were available at multiple time points or outside of this timeframe, images obtained closest to the time of initial diagnosis of discospondylitis were selected for inclusion. In addition, the results of a CBC and serum chemistry panel had to be available in association with clinical signs present at the time of diagnosis. Results reported at the time closest to the time of diagnosis were used for analysis. Laboratory reference ranges from the University of California‐Davis were used for analysis.

To be classified as a case, a dog had to have no evidence of a non‐Brucella cause of discospondylitis together with one or more of: (1) detectable antibodies to Brucella (using either rapid slide agglutination test [RSAT], tube agglutination test [TAT], agar gel immunodiffusion [AGID], indirect fluorescent antibody test [IFA], or a lateral flow assay [LFA]), (2) a positive PCR test result for 
B. canis
 on blood or material aspirated from an intervertebral disc, or (3) a positive culture for 
B. canis
 from blood or material aspirated from an intervertebral disc.

For every case, at least one hospital‐matched control dog with non‐Brucella discospondylitis was identified. To be classified as a control, a dog had to have one or more of: (1) a negative serologic test for 
B. canis
, (2) negative blood cultures for 
B. canis
, or (3) a positive blood culture for a non‐Brucella pathogen. In order to reduce time‐varying confounding, temporal matching was performed by selecting control dogs with a diagnosis of discospondylitis made within a 6‐month period before or after a case (i.e., a 1‐year window). If a suitable control could not be found in this window, the next most temporally associated control was selected.

Eligibility for enrollment was initially determined by one or more investigators at each center, then verified by investigators at the University of California‐Davis (CM and JES), including review by a board‐certified internal medicine specialist with expertise in infectious disease (JES). Information extracted from the medical record included age, breed, sex, neuter status, body weight, information about breeding history and kennel exposure, clinical signs, and comorbidities. The United States Postal Service ZIP code of owner residence at the time of diagnosis was obtained and mapped using ArcGIS Pro v3.3 (Esri, Redlands, CA). For cases, information on origin at the time of adoption was obtained to the most precise location available by contacting owners. The duration of four categories of clinical signs before diagnosis was documented: (1) apparent pain, including vocalization; (2) abnormal mobility, including refusal or inability to ambulate, abnormal gait, difficulty rising, exercise intolerance, or paresis; (3) inappetence; and (4) lethargy. Breeds were categorized based on size (small, medium, large, giant) as previously described [7, 13]. In order to obtain enough dogs for breed analysis, dogs were also grouped into related breeds. Rectal temperature at the time of examination at the participating site was recorded. Rectal temperature was considered elevated if above 103.0°F; when reported as “normal”, a value of 101.6°F was assigned [14]. Where performed, results of urinalysis, aerobic bacterial urine culture, abdominal imaging (ultrasound, radiographs, CT), and neurological examination were recorded.

When available, neurological examination findings were reviewed by a single board‐certified neurologist (CFL) who was masked to case–control status and participating center. The first neurologic examination performed at the teaching hospital closest to the date of diagnosis was used, regardless of whether it had been performed by a board‐certified neurologist. A complete neurologic examination was defined as an examination that documented cranial nerve assessment, postural reactions, segmental reflexes, pain perception, and neuroanatomic localization. Information evaluated included whether the dog was ambulatory, the presence and number of limbs with paresis, mentation status, cranial nerve deficits, pelvic reflexes, the cutaneous trunci reflex, pain perception, and the presence of spinal pain, lateralization signs, and the reported neuroanatomic localization.

Images of the spine were deposited by an investigator at each participating institution on an online platform (Ambra Health, Intelerad, Raleigh, NC, USA) so they could be accessed for collaborative review by a single board‐certified radiologist (KB) and a single board‐certified neurologist (CFL). Images were de‐identified such that reviewers were masked to case–control status and institution. Information gathered included the number of disc sites affected; location of affected discs (cervical, cervicothoracic, thoracolumbar, or lumbosacral); whether the process present was proliferative or lytic; presence of endplate destruction; and presence or absence of lymphadenomegaly, spondylosis, sclerosis, disc space narrowing, or isolated central lytic (“hole punch”) lesions [7]. Lesions that consisted of “smooth, round, central end plate lysis,” based on the description by Long et al. [7] were considered central lytic (“hole punch”) lesions, regardless of the presence or absence of concurrent spondylitis. Endplate destruction, spondylosis, and sclerosis were scored based subjective assessment as either absent (0), partial (i.e., < 100% of disc space affected) (1), or complete (100% disc space affected) (2). In dogs with > 3 lesions, the three most severely affected sites (based on subjective assessment by both reviewers) were assessed. Additional information evaluated on MRI included degree of hyperintensity on short tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequences and T1‐weighted post contrast sequences, compression of the spinal column, and presence of empyema and/or paravertebral muscular changes.

Conditional logistic regression was used to evaluate associations between potential risk factors and a diagnosis of 
B. canis
 discospondylitis, with each case–control matched set representing a unique conditional risk set. Variables evaluated were breed, body weight (kg), sex, age at diagnosis, duration of clinical signs, evidence of pain, rectal temperature, selected CBC parameters (hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), total white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, platelet count), selected biochemistry parameters (concentrations of serum bicarbonate, urea nitrogen [SUN], creatinine, glucose, and albumin; activity of serum alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and alkaline phosphatase [ALP]); location of radiographic lesions (C1‐C5, C6‐L3, L4‐caudal), presence or absence of multiple disc site involvement, and total number of radiographic lesions. Variables were nominal, ordinal, or continuous. For models that contained continuous measures, the assumption of linearity in the natural log odds was assessed using a link test. When there were multiple observations taken from the same dog, a robust method of variance estimation was used. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Conditional logistic regression analyses were performed using the Stata software package (Stata/BE 17.0, College Station, TX).

In this case–control study of dogs diagnosed with discospondylitis at academic veterinary hospitals, we identified several clinical findings that, if present, could raise suspicion for Brucella infection over other causes of discospondylitis before the results of blood culture and serological tests are available. These included young age at diagnosis, a normal neurologic examination, C1‐C5 involvement, multiple disc site involvement, an absence of neutrophilia, and higher median lymphocyte counts and hematocrit when compared with other causes of discospondylitis. Such findings, especially if present in combination, could prompt implementation of appropriate handling precautions in the hospital environment such as use of personal protective equipment (gloves, gown, and face shield), reducing movement around the hospital, and posting of warning signs on runs. Warning labels could be placed on specimens submitted to the laboratory and owners counseled regarding potential zoonotic risk and referred to public health resources for more information. Early treatment with appropriate antimicrobial therapy could be commenced, limiting the potential for transmission to humans and other dogs. Specifically, combination protocols that include doxycycline are recommended; a 2024 meta‐analysis of humans with brucellosis supported combination therapy with doxycycline and rifampin with or without streptomycin or levofloxacin [15]. These recommendations also apply to spinal brucellosis, the most frequent complication of Brucella infection in humans [16]. Conversely, the presence of an abnormal neurologic examination, marked neutrophilia, and a low lymphocyte count, especially in combination, might reduce the need for strict handling precautions, with associated costs of care and contributions to environmental waste.

Despite the generally accepted association between brucellosis, breeding establishments, and reproductive disorders, as well as recent kennel outbreaks [17, 18, 19], none of the cases in our study had a history of being bred or had current exposure to kennel environments. Although shepherds and shepherd mixes appeared to be over‐represented in the cases when compared to controls, the number of dogs in each group was too few to conclude that a difference—if present—existed. We also found no association with sex or neutering status, in contrast to a 1992 study of dogs with 
B. canis
 discospondylitis from the United States, where an intact male sex predisposition was suggested [2]. The association that we found with younger age was consistent with observations made within the last 5 years by others [7, 20]. Given the slow replication rate of Brucella and the chronicity of signs relative to other causes of discospondylitis, the association with younger age could reflect infection in the perinatal period, either transplacentally or during lactation [7]. Eleven of the 15 cases had been acquired from regions outside the U.S. state where the teaching hospital was located. Thus, although information on the origin of control dogs was not available for comparison, in light of concerns regarding the rising incidence of brucellosis in association with dog movement and dog trade [5, 20], obtaining a detailed travel history that includes source might also prompt consideration of brucellosis.

The clinical picture in dogs with brucellosis was one of chronic pain lasting months to years; controls often had more obvious signs of neurologic disease that were rapid in onset and progression. For example, signs such as abnormal pelvic limb placing reactions and paresis were present in only one case but over half of the control dogs. The relative lack of signs of neurologic disease in cases likely reflects the slow rate of progression of Brucella infections when compared with other causes of discospondylitis. The greater likelihood of multiple disc involvement in cases compared with controls might also be related to a more insidious clinical course that provides opportunity for lesion development at multiple sites. The reason for preferential targeting of cervical disc spaces by Brucella when compared with other bacteria is unclear but underscores the need to consider brucellosis as a differential in young adult dogs with cervical pain. Such dogs have the potential to be misdiagnosed with steroid‐responsive meningitis‐arteritis (SRMA) given the overlap in signalment and clinical findings; treatment of infected dogs with glucocorticoids could result in devastating outcomes and increase the potential for zoonotic transmission [21]. Cervical and multifocal spinal pain were observed in 9/33 and 7/33 dogs with Brucella discospondylitis, respectively, in another study [7]. In contrast, only 1/33 and 3/33 dogs in that study had thoracolumbar and lumbosacral pain, respectively. Multiple disc involvement was identified in 21 of the 33 dogs in that study and 20% of lesions were in the cervical spine [7]. However, in the same study, over 80% of dogs with Brucella discospondylitis had thoracolumbar disc involvement, so despite the association detected in the study reported here between Brucella infection and cervical disc involvement, Brucella infection remains an important differential in dogs with thoracolumbar lesions.

The more chronic and insidious course of disease in cases when compared with controls was also reflected by the relative absence of neutrophilia and lymphopenia on blood work. Neutrophilia was also uncommon in the 2022 case series of 33 dogs with Brucella discospondylitis [7]. The finding of normal serum globulin concentrations in most of the cases was unexpected given the potential for chronic immune stimulation by 
B. canis
. The reason for the higher median hematocrit in cases also was unclear but could relate to the ability of Brucella to evade host responses (and associated anemia of inflammatory disease). In contrast to humans, where thrombocytopenia is reported in 3% to 26% of cases [12], we did not associate lower platelet counts with brucellosis. Only 1/18 dogs with Brucella discospondylitis was thrombocytopenic in another study, and only 3/17 dogs had mild hyperglobulinemia [7].

Although a 2022 case series suggested a possible association between “hole punch” or central lytic lesions and Brucella discospondylitis [7], we identified such lesions in both Brucella‐infected and non‐Brucella‐infected dogs.

The most important limitations of this study were its retrospective nature and the small size of the study sample. Analysis was dependent on findings documented in the medical record at each of the participating institutions and the availability of images of suitable quality. Assessment of clinical findings such as pain was subjective. Use of validated pain scoring measures currently is not routinely used by veterinary neurologists for neurological examinations, and to our knowledge, no scoring measure has yet been developed for discospondylitis. However, all cases were evaluated by specialists at academic teaching hospitals, medical records were detailed, and diagnostic investigations were comprehensive. The study sample size potentially limited our ability to detect differences between cases and controls, despite enrollment of dogs from four different institutions. Therefore, we compared case and control groups and presented findings as odds ratios and confidence intervals to avoid overstating the existence of differences (or lack thereof) based on p values [22]. Nevertheless, the findings provide a basis for power calculations for future studies. Animal numbers also limited our ability to temporally match cases and controls.

For some controls, diagnosis of the cause of discospondylitis was based on detection of a likely pathogen at a distant site such as urine; it is possible that a different pathogen was the cause of discospondylitis. No serologic test has 100% sensitivity and specificity [11], and the performance of serologic tests can vary among different laboratories. However, given that all cases were positive for 
B. canis
 using multiple diagnostic tests and all controls were seronegative for 
B. canis
, misclassification was considered unlikely. The sensitivity of blood culture for 
B. canis
 discospondylitis is unknown, but it might be higher than in subclinical 
B. canis
 infection or infections limited to the reproductive tract given that all blood samples were positive whenever they were done. Findings relating to disc involvement should be interpreted cautiously as images of the cervical spine were not available for 6 cases and 15 controls, and discospondylitis can be present in the absence of radiographically detectable lesions. However, this applied equally to cases and controls, and for dogs where only partial spinal images were obtained, it was likely that sites chosen for imaging were based on findings of physical and neurological examinations. Larger studies that include complete spinal images would be difficult to conduct given the rarity of Brucella discospondylitis and potentially increased costs to the animal owner for complete spinal imaging compared with partial spinal imaging. Nevertheless, we clearly identified findings associated with Brucella discospondylitis and given the relatively young age of the entire study sample, most of the dogs in our study had no comorbidities that could influence clinical findings.

In conclusion, 
Brucella canis
 infection should be considered in cases of discospondylitis regardless of neuter status, sex, and breeding history. In accordance with Brucella's ability to cause chronic infections and evade the host immune response, heightened suspicion for brucellosis might be warranted in young adult dogs with chronic spinal pain that lack signs of neurologic disease, lack neutrophilia, and have robust hematocrits and lymphocyte counts. Studies that include larger numbers of dogs are needed to confirm these findings, and because of overlap in findings between cases and controls, no variable should be used in isolation to predict whether discospondylitis is caused by Brucella or some other infectious agent.
