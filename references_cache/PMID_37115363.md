# Homozygous RMRP Promoter Duplications Cause Severely Reduced Transcript Abundance and SCID Associated with Cartilage Hair Hypoplasia.
**Authors:** Tan R, BC Children’s Hospital Members, Rozmus J, Turvey SE, Biggs CM
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-023-01489-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-023-01489-5)

## Content

To the Editor,

Cartilage hair hypoplasia (CHH) is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by skeletal dysplasia, hypotrichosis, anemia, and immunodeficiency [1]. Both skeletal and extra-skeletal manifestations can be highly variable among individuals with CHH: some exhibit subtle immune defects, while others have severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) [1]. Identifying the typical metaphyseal skeletal abnormalities may also be challenging at younger ages. CHH is part of a spectrum of skeletal dysplasias caused by variants in the RMRP gene, which encodes for the untranslated RNA component of the endoribonuclease mitochondrial RNA Processing (RNase MRP) complex. Phenotypic heterogeneity of RMRP-associated skeletal dysplasias has been linked to the particular location of the pathogenic variant and its impact on the diverse functions of the RNase MRP complex. Specifically, bone dysplasia has been linked to impaired ribosomal RNA processing and protein synthesis, while immunodeficiency is associated with genetic variants impacting messenger RNA cleavage and cell cycle regulation [2].

While most pathogenic RMRP variants occur in the transcribed gene, variants in the promoter region upstream of RMRP have also been implicated in CHH [2]. Promoter insertions or duplications cause reduced transcript abundance by increasing the distance between the TATA box and the transcription start site [2]. Historically, promoter variants have only been found in a compound heterozygous state with an intragenic variant, causing speculation that homozygous promoter duplications or insertions are embryonically lethal due to gene silencing [1]. Recently, there have been two cases reported in the literature of biallelic promoter variants causing CHH; however, RMRP transcript levels were not assessed [1, 3].

SCID in a CHH setting can be managed by allogenic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) which can effectively treat the immunodeficiency [4]. Generally, conditioning is recommended to ensure full donor chimerism. A recent report on reduced intensity conditioning HSCT in 6 patients with CHH showed an 83% survival rate, with 1 patient achieveing full donor chimerism and the remaining 5 demonstrating donor T-cell reconstitution of > 90% but lower and variable donor myeloid (11–80%) and B-cell (1.3–5%) chimerism [4]. To our knowledge, there are no reports of engraftment of non-T cell lineages after unconditioned HSCT.

Here, we describe a case of CHH presenting as SCID caused by a homozygous RMRP promoter duplication resulting in severely reduced RMRP transcript. Furthermore, we report on the surprising finding of the patient achieving full myeloid (CD33) and lymphoid (CD3) donor chimerism following an unconditioned bone marrow HLA-matched sibling HSCT.

The patient is a female born at 37 weeks gestational age to healthy, non-consanguineous parents. Her 32-week prenatal ultrasound revealed a femur length in the 10th percentile with other parameters in the 50th percentile. The patient was well until 2 months of age when she developed diarrhea following the administration of vaccines including live rotavirus. She presented at 3 months of age with fever, oral lesions, and cough progressing to respiratory failure requiring intubation. Examination revealed weight and length both below the 3rd percentile for age, normal appearing hair, and no skin rashes. CT scan of the chest showed ground glass opacities. Immune evaluation (Fig. 1A) showed a near absence of T cells with normal B cell numbers, low NK cells, low IgG, and absent IgA/IgM, in keeping with T−B+NK− SCID. Her infectious workup confirmed Pneumocystis jirovecii (PJP) via bronchoalveolar lavage staining. She had disseminated cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection and tested positive for rotavirus antigen in her stool. The patient was treated with IV sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim and corticosteroids for her PJP pneumonia. Despite receiving IV ganciclovir, foscarnet, and CMV hyperimmune globulin (HIG), the CMV viral load in the serum, initially close to 2 × 106 copies/mL, remained elevated, and the patient exhibited end-organ disease with CMV encephalitis and retinitis requiring intravitreal ganciclovir injections. She also had anemia, requiring red blood cell transfusions every 10 days, intermittent neutropenia, and hyponatremia attributed to syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion.

A primary immunodeficiency gene panel revealed a homozygous 8-nucleotide duplication in the promoter region of RMRP c.-13_-6dup in the affected patient (see supplemental materials for methods). Both parents were found to be heterozygous carriers of the variant (Fig. 1B). The variants and zygosity were confirmed with targeted Sanger sequencing (Fig. 1C). To confirm the diagnosis of CHH, we compared the expression of the RMRP transcript between the patient, her parents, and a healthy control (see Supplemental Material for methods). The patient was found to have severely reduced RMRP transcript abundance (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1D).

Because of the patient’s ongoing refractory infections, the team proceeded with an unconditioned stem cell transplant from her 10/10 HLA-matched brother. The product was volume reduced bone marrow with a CD34 count of 6.08 × 106/kg and a total nucleated cell count of 5.24 × 108/kg. Her post-transplant course was complicated by skin graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), encephalitis from CMV/immune reconstitution, episodes of severe hyponatremia, and acute heart failure secondary to myocarditis and pericardial effusion. These complications were managed with a range of treatments including corticosteroids, broad-spectrum antimicrobials, daily CMV HIG, IVIG and pericardiocentesis. She is now clinically stable at 13 months of age, with diagnoses of developmental delay, visual impairment, and infantile spasms, attributed to her history of CMV encephalitis and retinitis. Her weight is at the 15th percentile for age, and length remains below the 3rd percentile/Z-score − 3.45, corresponding to approximately 50th length percentile on the CHH growth curve, and she has mildly fine and thin hair.

From a laboratory perspective, the patient developed neutropenia which progressively worsened to an absolute neutrophil count of 0 on day + 33, accompanied by monocytopenia followed by thrombocytopenia. Then, on day + 46, her myeloid cytopenias and anemia started improving coinciding with a marked reticulocytosis. By day + 53, she was transfusion independent and had normal neutrophil and platelet counts (Supplemental Fig.1). Peripheral blood chimerism performed on day + 94 showed 97% (± 7%) lymphoid (CD3) donor and 95% (± 7%) myeloid (CD33) donor chimerism, with repeat analysis on day + 229 showing ongoing full donor chimerism, with > 95% of cells in peripheral blood corresponding to donor. Repeat T and B cell phenotyping on day + 229 showed improving CD4 T cell lymphopenia, CD8 T cell lymphocytosis, and normalization of her switched memory B cell population (Fig. 1A). In addition, the patient’s naïve CD4+ T cell percentage increased from 2.2% on day + 52 to 12.7% on day + 229. T cell proliferation was not assessed. Her last dose of IVIG was on day + 44, after which she was able to maintain her IgG levels > 7 g /L.

Here, we report a case of CHH secondary to homozygous RMRP promoter duplications causing SCID and transfusion-dependent anemia. This study is the first to demonstrate the impact of biallelic promoter variants on RMRP expression in a patient, which in the described case led to severely decreased transcript levels. This study provides further evidence that homozygous RMRP promoter duplications reduce but do not abrogate transcript expression and are not universally embryonically lethal. We also describe the surprising finding of full donor chimerism in both lymphoid and myeloid lineages despite no pre-transplant conditioning. Our lymphoid chimerism marker (CD3) identifies T cells; therefore, B cell chimerism has not been evaluated. The reconstitution of non-lymphoid lineages without prior conditioning is surprising and to our knowledge has not been previously described in CHH.

Biallelic RMRP promoter variants causing CHH are rare, with our case representing only the third reported in the literature. One unifying feature of all 3 affected patients has been a severe immune phenotype. The first reported case was compound heterozygous for RMRP promoter variants (c.-4_-1dup and c.-21_-14trip) and presented with CID and CD8 lymphopenia without skeletal abnormalities and underwent a successful HSCT [1]. Case 2 was homozygous for an RMRP promoter duplication (-19_-25dup) and presented with sparse hair, disproportionate short stature, and combined B and T cell defect [3]. This patient did not undergo HSCT, however, died from infection at 23 months. Collectively, these cases support the hypothesis that RMRP promoter variants cause severe immunodeficiency but variable skeletal manifestations, ranging from no skeletal abnormalities to, as seen in our patient, severe short stature [1, 3]. This suggests that reduced RMRP transcript levels may more consistently affect the cell cycle regulation function of the RNase MRP complex and warrants further study.

We also report full donor chimerism in our patient despite no conditioning. As our patient had myeloid cells prior to transplant, it is surprising that conditioning was not required for this lineage to reconstitute. One hypothesis for our patient’s successful engraftment is that severely reduced RMRP profoundly impacts the RNase MRP’s role in cell cycle regulation, creating a significant survival advantage of donor stem cells over the recipient patient’s. Another possibility is that space was created for the donor stem cells to engraft from either a graft-versus-host effect against marrow stem cells or from secondary effects of the patient’s concurrent therapies. The patient had been receiving antimicrobials associated with bone marrow suppression (ganciclovir, beta lactams) at the time, which may have had a disproportionate effect on the recipient stem cell precursors than the donor’s. Additionally, bone marrow suppression due to intrinsic defects is common in CHH patients with anemia, with a prior study showing markedly defective erythroid colony formation and granulocyte–macrophage growth [5]. Future work will seek to understand the mechanisms and conditions by which donor stem cells were able to achieve full lymphoid and myeloid engraftment without a conditioning regimen.
