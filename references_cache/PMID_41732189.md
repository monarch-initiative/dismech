# Glycogen storage disease type IX: Long-term follow-up of 52 patients from three European countries.
**Authors:** Magner M, Šáhó R, Slavíková P, Bakaľár R, Dvořáková L, Pešková K, Ramadža DP, Barić I, Ilic N, Čechová A, Řeboun M, Vlášková H, Kelifová S, Ješina P, Procházková D, Hansíková H, Honzík T, Zeman J
**Journal:** Mol Genet Metab Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymgmr.2026.101297](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymgmr.2026.101297)

## Content

Glycogen storage disease type IX (GSD IX) arises from hepatic phosphorylase b kinase (PhK) deficiency attributable to pathogenic variants in the PHKA2, PHKB, and PHKG2 genes. This multicenter retrospective study evaluated clinical and biochemical data from 52 patients diagnosed across three European countries, with a median follow-up of 9.3 years (range: 1-49). In the cohort, 86.5% were classified as GSD IXa, whereas GSD IXb and IXc accounted for 7.7% and 3.8%, respectively; one diagnosis was based solely on enzymatic testing. Null variants in PHKA2 consistently resulted in severe PhK deficiency, whereas missense variants and in-frame deletions were associated with variable enzymatic impairment (8/19 tested cases). The median age at symptom onset was 1.6 years, and the mean age at diagnosis was 2.0 years. Predominant manifestations included hepatomegaly (82%), elevated aminotransferases (81%), hypertriglyceridemia (71%), hypercholesterolemia (67%), hypoglycemia (46%), hyperlactatemia (38%), and short stature (30%). Aberrant apolipoprotein C-III glycosylation was detected in 80% of analyzed samples. Nutritional intervention was associated with improved growth (height SD score - 0.8 ± 1.3 vs -0.2 ± 1.65; p = 0.031) and fewer documented fasting hypoglycemia episodes (20/44 vs 9/44; p = 0.012), although hepatomegaly frequently persisted. Liver biopsies showed steatosis, fibrosis, and/or chronic hepatitis in 52% of examined cases. A single hepatic adenoma was identified in a 14-year-old male. Overall, the clinical course of GSD IX was favorable, with hepatomegaly, elevated liver enzymes, and dyslipidemia as the most prevalent features. Severe hypoglycemic episodes were uncommon, and no clear genotype-phenotype correlation emerged.

Liver glycogen storage diseases (GSDs) are a heterogeneous group of metabolic disorders caused by defects in enzymes involved in glycogen synthesis or degradation, resulting in impaired glycogen synthesis, storage, or utilization [1]. The estimated incidence of all GSDs ranges from 1 in 20,000 to 1 in 43,000. GSD IX is among the more commonly reported hepatic GSD subtypes; however, published prevalence estimates vary by population and study design, and precise incidence data remain limited [2]. Available sources suggest that overall prevalence is on the order of 1:100,000 and that GSD IX may account for approximately 25%–30% of GSD cases [3], [4], [5]. GSD IX results from deficiency of phosphorylase b kinase (PhK), a heterotetramer composed of four copies each of the α, β, γ, and δ subunits. Pathogenic variants in PHKA2, PHKB, and PHKG2 are associated with hepatic GSD IXa (OMIM 306000), IXb (OMIM 261750), and IXc (OMIM 613027), respectively. Pathogenic variants in the muscle isoform of the α-subunit (PHKA1) have been reported in GSD IXd (OMIM 300559) [6]. Inheritance is X-linked for PHKA2-related disease, which accounts for approximately 75% of reported cases [7], and autosomal recessive in patients with biallelic variants in PHKB or PHKG2.

The clinical course of GSD IX is usually mild [6], but some patients require continuous nighttime feeding [7]. Few studies, mostly with small cohorts, have been published to date [7], [8], [9], [10], and a recent review summarizing published cases reported that GSD IX typically manifests in infants or toddlers with hepatomegaly, growth delay, elevated aminotransferases, dyslipidemia, and episodes of fasting hypoglycemia [11].

The aim of our study was to compare the natural course of GSD IX with biochemical and molecular data in 52 previously unreported patients diagnosed in three European countries and followed for a median of 9.3 years (range: 1–49).

Fifty-two patients with GSD IX from 30 families diagnosed between 1973 and 2023 in metabolic centers in the Czech Republic (41 patients), Croatia (6), and Serbia (5) were included in this retrospective study. Diagnosis was established using molecular genetic analysis and/or enzyme assay in patients evaluated before the era of molecular genetic testing. Clinical and laboratory data were collected and analyzed.

Hepatomegaly was defined as a liver edge >2 cm below the costal margin on physical examination in children, consistent with American Academy of Pediatrics guidance [12]. Short stature was defined as <3rd percentile for age and sex.

Reference ranges and definitions of abnormal values were based on the central laboratory of the General University Hospital in Prague. The reference range for aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was 9–48 IU/L, for alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 7–56 IU/L, and for lactate <2.3 mmol/L. Elevated triacylglycerols (TAG) were defined as >2 mmol/L, and hypercholesterolemia as total cholesterol >4.9 mmol/L. Normal uric acid was defined as ≤360 μmol/L in girls and prepubertal boys and ≤ 410 μmol/L in adolescent boys.

Based on internal consensus, hypoglycemia was defined as plasma glucose <55 mg/dL (<3.0 mmol/L), which in older children and adults typically triggers a search for food or assistance and represents an important defense against hypoglycemia [13]. This threshold also corresponds to the laboratory criterion used in Whipple's triad [14].

Glycogen concentration in erythrocytes was determined using amyloglucosidase, and free glucose was subsequently measured by the hexokinase/glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase method [17]. Phosphorylase b kinase activity in erythrocytes was measured using added phosphorylase; activated phosphorylase cleaves inorganic phosphate (Pi) from glucose-1-phosphate, and Pi was determined photometrically [15]. Monosialo- (ApoC-III1), disialo- (ApoC-III2), and asialoapolipoprotein C-III (ApoC-III0) were analyzed by isoelectric focusing [16].

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). A custom capture-based target enrichment (SeqCap EZ Choice, Roche NimbleGen) was designed to capture all exons, splice sites, and adjacent intronic sequences (30 bp) of 29 genes associated with disorders of glycogen metabolism (AGL, ALDOA, ALDOB, ALDOC, ENO3, FBP1, G6PC, GAA, GBE1, GYG1, GYS1, GYS2, KHK, PC, PFKM, PGAM2, PGM1, PHKA1, PHKA2, PHKB, PHKG2, PRKAB1, PRKAB2, PRKAG2, PYGL, PYGM, RBCK1, SLC2A2, SLC37A4). Libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer's protocol (SeqCap EZ Library SR User Guide, Roche NimbleGen, Inc.) and sequenced on a MiSeq (Illumina) using MiSeq Reagent v2 (2 × 150) or v3 (2 × 75) kits. Sequencing data were processed using an in-house bioinformatics pipeline based on BWA, GATK tools, and CNVkit [17], [18], [19]. Reads were mapped to the hg38 reference genome. ANNOVAR was used for variant annotation [20].

Variant interpretation used in silico tools VarSome Premium [21] and Franklin by Genoox [22], and databases ClinVar [23], gnomAD v4.1.0 [24], LOVD [25], and HGMD Professional [26]. Variants were classified according to ACMG/AMP guidelines [27].

Variants identified by panel sequencing were confirmed by Sanger sequencing using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit on an ABI 3500xL Genetic Analyzer according to the manufacturer's protocols (Thermo Fisher Scientific, South San Francisco, CA, USA). Sanger sequencing was also used for genotyping of family members. Large deletions were confirmed by qPCR (SYBR Green, Bio-Rad) and/or by Sanger sequencing of PCR products spanning deletion breakpoints to refine position and extent. PCR conditions and primers are available upon request. X-inactivation patterns were assessed using methylation-specific probes (AR and RP2) as summarized previously [28].

Continuous data are presented as mean or median and standard deviation (SD). For categorical variables, percentages are reported. Shapiro–Wilk testing was used to assess normality. For group comparisons, t-tests were used for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for data that were not normally distributed. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All information was accessed in accordance with applicable laws and ethical requirements for the study period and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised 2000). All patients and/or their legal guardians provided written informed consent for genetic testing and for use of anonymized data for publication.

In four patients with GSD IXb, six different PHKB variants were identified, including a novel missense variant c.1741G>A p.(Asp581Asn), a frameshift variant c.256_260del p.(Asp86ProfsTer18), and a deletion of exons 2–13. One of two patients with GSD IXc was homozygous for a novel PHKG2 variant c.996_999dup p.(Asn334Glnfs*56), and the other was compound heterozygous for c.996_999dup p.(Asn334Glnfs*56) and a novel variant c.458 A>T p.(Asp153Val).

All patients were advised to follow an anti-hypoglycemic regimen with frequent meals throughout the day. Most used uncooked cornstarch after cornstarch-free meals during the day and an additional dose after the second supper or at bedtime; a minority used one cornstarch dose during the night. Total daily cornstarch intake ranged from 0.5 to 3.8 g/kg/day. The maximum fasting interval overnight ranged from 6 to 8 h. One 20-month-old boy (P11), initially extremely premature, still requires continuous nighttime feeding to prevent hypoglycemia and is among the few patients managed with continuous glucose monitoring.

Other dietary recommendations (high-protein diet, 2–3 g/kg/day; low-fat diet; and reduced dairy intake) varied among patients and were largely based on local practice and clinician preference. Recommendations for management of hypoglycemia were routinely included in medical reports, and an emergency link (https://www.emergencyprotocol.net) has recently been implemented.

Mean height SD score (relative to the 50th percentile for age) indicated shorter stature at diagnosis (−0.8 ± 1.3) and improved significantly during follow-up (−0.2 ± 1.65; p = 0.031). This improvement was accompanied by an increase in BMI Z score from 0.0 ± 1.3 at diagnosis to 0.3 ± 1.44 at the last examination (p = 0.027). Liver size at the most recent assessment (cm below the right costal margin) decreased in 16 patients, remained stable in 17, and increased in 12 despite dietary treatment. Mean liver size decreased over follow-up but did not reach statistical significance when comparing diagnosis with last follow-up.

Neurologically, three patients had hypotonia, two had muscle weakness, and three had delayed psychomotor development; all had GSD IXa. Cardiologically, one patient with GSD IXb had total anomalous pulmonary venous return. No patients had renal involvement.

Twenty-one patients (40%) underwent liver biopsy at a median age of 3 years (range: 1.25–12 years) (18 with PHKA2, 2 with PHKB, and 1 with PHKG2). Glycogenic hepatopathy was described in 11/21 (52%). Steatosis was present in 7 (33%), and fibrosis in 7 (33%). Chronic hepatitis was described in two biopsies (one patient with GSD IXb and one with GSD IXc). A single liver adenoma on MRI was observed in one patient with GSD IXa at age 14 years (P16). This patient underwent biopsy at age 10 years, which showed progressive cytosolic glycogen accumulation. Follow-up MRIs showed no progression of the adenoma.

GSD IX typically manifests in early childhood, and hepatomegaly, elevated aminotransferases, dyslipidemia, a history of hypoglycemia, and short stature are the most common reasons for metabolic evaluation in specialized centers [7], [10], [29], [30]. Mean age at diagnosis in our cohort was 2 years, earlier than the mean age of 4.4 years reported in a recent review [11], which included reports published up to 43 years ago. Similar to that review, hepatomegaly (82%) and elevated aminotransferases (81%) were the most common findings in our cohort; however, hypertriglyceridemia (71% vs 37%), hypercholesterolemia (67% vs 17%), and fasting hypoglycemia (46% vs 31.7%) were more frequent. In contrast, short stature was less frequent (30% vs 41.3%) [11]. These findings suggest that no single symptom is diagnostic; rather, the combination of features should raise suspicion for GSD.

In most cases, cholesterol and triacylglycerols were only mildly elevated; however, some patients with more severe phenotypes had total cholesterol >11 mmol/L. Our data suggest that neither routinely measured laboratory markers (e.g., uric acid and lactate) nor specialized ones (e.g., erythrocyte glycogen) were particularly informative at diagnosis, as abnormalities were present in fewer than one-third of patients. These biomarkers were also of limited value for longitudinal monitoring. Monitoring of morning urine ketones, recommended by Kishnani et al. [31], was not routinely performed in most patients and could not be evaluated.

In our group of males with GSD IXa, secondary aberrant apolipoprotein C-III glycosylation with decreased disialoapolipoprotein C-III and increased monosialoapolipoprotein C-III was detected in 79.5% of analyzed samples. We previously hypothesized that ApoC-III hypoglycosylation in GSD IX may result from reduced availability of nucleotide monosaccharides, particularly UDP-GalNAc, for glycosylation reactions [16].

The phenotype of GSD IX is poorly defined, and this study adds to the growing evidence describing a more involved phenotype than previously appreciated [9], [29]. A minority of patients remained asymptomatic or had only mild manifestations managed with modest dietary measures. Most had an intermediate phenotype with good metabolic control under frequent meals supplemented with uncooked cornstarch. Only a small subset required sustained nocturnal enteral feeding to maintain euglycemia. Identification of pathogenic variants in individuals with mild features suggests that GSD IX may be underdiagnosed in some patients, underscoring the need for clinical vigilance and broader genetic testing, especially in subtle or atypical presentations. Hepatomegaly persisted in some patients despite dietary therapy. The increase in BMI Z scores observed in our cohort may plausibly reflect inadvertent overtreatment with cornstarch in some individuals, highlighting the need for individualized nutritional monitoring.

In the literature, GSD IXc has been associated with a more severe course, including increased risk of cirrhosis or adenoma in childhood, sometimes improving with age [32]. The two girls with GSD IXc in our cohort had a relatively severe course with recurrent hypoglycemia, marked hepatomegaly, and/or progression during follow-up. A systematic review also suggested that GSD IXc is typically more severe than IXa, IXb, and IXd [11]. However, the small number of patients with IXb and IXc in our cohort precluded robust comparisons between subtypes.

GSD IXa was the most common subtype (88% of our cohort), consistent with prior reviews [11]. Because IXa is X-linked, most affected individuals are male; however, females may also have manifestations ranging from mild hepatomegaly to more severe features. In the study by Morava et al., one girl had elevated serum cholesterol, short stature, and failure to thrive, whereas another had hepatomegaly, short stature, failure to thrive, elevated serum cholesterol, and elevated aminotransferases [6]. Cho et al. also reported a 29-year-old female with hepatomegaly [33]. In our cohort, female carriers were clinically asymptomatic or only mildly affected, with occasional mild hypoglycemia (P18), mild hepatomegaly (P10, P18), or reduced enzyme activity (P10, P45). The possible impact of X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) and specific variants on the phenotype of female heterozygotes should be considered; however, XCI status measured in peripheral leukocytes did not correlate with phenotype or with erythrocyte enzyme activity (Table 4, footnote).

Liver histology findings included hepatic glycogen accumulation, steatosis, fibrosis, and, in some cases, features of chronic hepatitis. The frequencies of these findings were comparable to those reported in other published cohorts [2], [7]. Although a definitive genotype–phenotype correlation has not been established in phosphorylase kinase (PhK) deficiency, variants in PHKG2 have been associated with a higher likelihood of developing childhood liver cirrhosis [29]; however, we observed no cirrhosis in our cohort. A hepatic adenoma was identified in only one patient, a 14-year-old boy with GSD IXa (P16), without progression during follow-up. This patient was metabolically well compensated, with aminotransferase levels only slightly above the reference range during that period. He was advised to consume a late evening meal and received uncooked cornstarch supplementation with cornstarch-free meals (total cornstarch dose, 1.15 g/kg/day). In the literature, eight cases of liver adenoma have been reported across all GSD IX subtypes [2], [9], [34], including cases present as early as 4 years of age, as well as three cases of hepatocellular carcinoma [34], [35]. These observations support the need for long-term follow-up. Noninvasive methods, such as transient elastography (FibroScan) and contrast-enhanced ultrasonography, may be considered for long-term monitoring of liver disease progression [7].

Unique complications such as interventricular septal hypertrophy or renal tubular acidosis have been reported [9], [36], but were not observed in our cohort. One patient with GSD IXb (P48) had total anomalous pulmonary venous return, considered unrelated to GSD IX.

Although GSD IX is often viewed as relatively mild, severe presentations resembling GSD Ia can occur. For example, P11 (PHKA2 c.3336 + 2del) required continuous nighttime feeding to prevent hypoglycemia, and P37 (PHKA2 c.3210_3212del) had marked hepatomegaly, elevated liver enzymes, and short stature. Genotype–phenotype relationships were inconclusive: the same variant may be associated with similar phenotypes (e.g., siblings 4 and 5, 8 and 9, and 16 and 17 carrying c.3402del, c.2908 + 1G > A, and c.134G > A, respectively; or unrelated patients with c.134G > A, such as P15, P19, and P20), but also with markedly different clinical manifestations (e.g., siblings 2 and 3 with the null variant c.1174C > T, and the family comprising P41–P45 with c.3629G > A). Notably, P37 had more severe clinical manifestations than P36 and P38 despite carrying the same variant. Interestingly, P37 was also heterozygous for a pathogenic variant in PYGL. Because phosphorylase b kinase activates liver glycogen phosphorylase encoded by PYGL
[31], a modifying contribution of this variant to the phenotype can be considered.

In P12, a deletion of the terminal exon 33 was detected. Exon 33 is part of the second calcineurin B-like (CBL-2) domain and contains a binding site for the gamma subunit, which mediates the catalytic activity of the enzyme complex [37]. P12 had extremely low enzyme activity, yet the clinical presentation was relatively mild. Overall, residual enzyme activity did not correlate with clinical severity. Moreover, the practical utility of erythrocyte enzyme assays for confirming the diagnosis is limited, because a number of pathogenic missense variants are not associated with measurable enzyme deficiency in vitro. In addition, this method does not distinguish among different GSD IX subtypes.

In P46, the PHKB variant c.39G>A p.(Trp13Ter) was detected in the homozygous state together with a previously undescribed variant, c.1741G>A p.(Asp581Asn), classified as a VUS according to ACMG/AMP criteria [27]. According to ClinVar and ACMG criteria, the nonsense c.39G>A p.(Trp13Ter) variant is classified as benign/likely benign and has a reported frequency of 0.0016 in 1,613,112 control chromosomes in gnomAD v4.1.0, including 24 homozygotes. Given the clinical findings (mild hepatomegaly and borderline hypertriglyceridemia), it is plausible that this nonsense variant, in combination with another variant, may contribute to a mild GSD IXb phenotype. In PHKG2, we report two novel likely causal variants, both apparently severe: a frameshift variant c.996_999dup p.(Asn334Glnfs*56) and a missense variant c.458 A>T p.(Asp153Val) affecting the enzyme active site [37].

Our study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, variability in clinical protocols across participating centers, and the absence of systematic monitoring of ketone bodies. Nonetheless, these limitations are partly offset by the relatively large sample size and the pragmatic nature of the data, which reflect real-world clinical practice and thereby enhance the external validity of our findings.

Glycogen storage disease type IX (GSD IX) is characterized by a constellation of findings, including hepatomegaly, fasting hypoglycemia, elevated aminotransferases, and dyslipidemia. Aberrant apolipoprotein C-III glycosylation is frequently observed. Although the disease course is poorly defined, a more involved phenotype—with occasional overlap with GSD Ia—has been reported, including among patients with the IXa subtype. In oligosymptomatic presentations, clinical signs may be subtle and readily overlooked, contributing to underdiagnosis. Measurement of phosphorylase b kinase activity in erythrocytes does not reliably correlate with disease severity and has limited diagnostic utility. Contemporary diagnosis relies on molecular genetic testing, which is essential for subtype delineation; however, genotype–phenotype relationships remain complex. Treatment should be individualized based on clinical severity and metabolic profile. Importantly, medical care for GSD IX has changed substantially over time, affecting diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes. Advances in nutritional management, including amino acid supplementation, may contribute to differences across the cohort. Patients in this cohort were diagnosed after symptom onset, reflecting older diagnostic practices. With increasing use of newborn screening and prenatal genomic testing, earlier diagnosis and treatment are becoming possible and will likely alter the natural history of the condition in the future.
