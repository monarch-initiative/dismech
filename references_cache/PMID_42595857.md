# Clinical and genetic characteristics of hypoparathyroidism, deafness, and renal dysplasia syndrome in a chronic kidney disease cohort.
**Authors:** Tanaka Y, Nagano C, Asagai Y, Hanafusa H, Horinouchi T, Sakakibara N, Yamamura T, Ishimori S, Kaito H, Iijima K, Nozu K, Morisada N
**Journal:** Pediatr Nephrol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00467-026-07499-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00467-026-07499-w)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Hypoparathyroidism, deafness, and renal dysplasia (HDR) syndrome is a rare autosomal dominant disorder caused by GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA3) haploinsufficiency. Although the clinical triad is well recognized, the phenotype is often incomplete, making clinical diagnosis challenging. This study aimed to elucidate the clinical and genetic characteristics of HDR syndrome in a kidney disease cohort.

METHODS: We retrospectively analyzed patients with absent or mild urinary abnormalities who underwent genetic testing for chronic kidney disease (CKD) between 2010 and 2025. Targeted next-generation sequencing-based kidney disease panels were used, and copy number variations were assessed using array comparative genomic hybridization.

RESULTS: Among 1254 families with CKD, pathogenic or likely pathogenic GATA3 variants were identified in 10 patients (seven families). One additional patient with a clinically compatible phenotype carried a missense variant of uncertain significance. The median age at diagnosis was 5 years (IQR, 2-23; range, 0.7-44 years). GATA3 variants included missense (n = 3, including one variant of uncertain significance), nonsense (n = 2), short insertion/deletion (n = 2), and a whole-gene deletion (n = 1). Regarding the classical triad, hypoparathyroidism, deafness, and renal anomalies were observed in 45, 100, and 90% of patients, respectively. Only 40% exhibited the complete triad, whereas half presented deafness and renal manifestations.

CONCLUSIONS: HDR syndrome identified in kidney disease cohorts often presents with incomplete clinical features. In patients with CKD and sensorineural deafness, HDR syndrome should be considered, and comprehensive genetic testing, including copy number analysis, is essential for accurate diagnosis.

caused by GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA3) haploinsufficiency. Although the clinical triad is well recognized, the phenotype 
is often incomplete, making clinical diagnosis challenging. This study aimed to elucidate the clinical and genetic character-

chronic kidney disease (CKD) between 2010 and 2025. Targeted next-generation sequencing–based kidney disease panels 
were used, and copy number variations were assessed using array comparative genomic hybridization.

Introduction
GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA3) is a zinc-finger tran-
scription factor consisting of 444 amino acids that con-
tains two zinc-finger domains and plays an essential role 
in embryonic development, including the formation of the 
kidneys, parathyroid glands, and auditory system [1 –3]. 
Heterozygous pathogenic variants of GATA3  are the major 
cause of hypoparathyroidism, deafness, and renal anomalies 
(HDR) syndrome (also known as Barakat syndrome; OMIM 
#146255) [4]. In addition to pathogenic sequence variants in 
GATA3, chromosomal deletions involving the 10p14 region 
that includes GATA3  have been reported as genetic causes 
of HDR syndrome [5]. Although the classical triad of HDR 
defines the disease, incomplete phenotypes involving only 
one or two features are frequently observed. Therefore, 
diagnosis based solely on clinical manifestations or strict 
application of diagnostic criteria can be challenging [6 ]. 
Additionally, GATA3 haploinsufficiency is associated with 
variable penetrance and a broad phenotypic spectrum, indi-
cating that this condition may be under-recognized in clini-
cal practice [7]. Recent large cohort studies and systematic

most previous studies have focused on patients with hear -

Notably, HDR syndrome may be overlooked in patients 
specifically with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and minimal 
urinary abnormalities. Therefore, re-assessing HDR syn-
drome from a nephrological perspective is of considerable 
clinical significance. Additionally, because both sequence 
variants (single-nucleotide variants and indels) and chromo-
somal deletions can cause HDR syndrome, it is crucial to 
clarify the genetic and clinical identification of this disorder 
within kidney disease cohorts in real-world clinical settings. 
In this study, we identified patients with HDR syndrome 
caused by pathogenic or likely pathogenic GATA3 variants, 
as well as one clinically compatible HDR-spectrum case 
with a GATA3  variant of uncertain significance, within a 
cohort undergoing genetic testing for CKD with absent or 
mild urinary abnormalities, and delineated the clinical and 
genetic spectra of the disorder.
Methods
Patients
We conducted genetic testing in 1254 families with CKD 
and absent or mild urinary abnormalities between 2010 and 
December 2025. This cohort consisted of patients referred 
for genetic testing using our kidney disease panel for con-
genital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), 
nephronophthisis-related ciliopathies, polycystic kidney dis-
ease (PKD), autosomal dominant tubulointerstitial kidney 
disease, or unexplained CKD, in whom urinary abnormali-
ties were absent or not the primary clinical feature. Because 
this was a referral-based genetic testing cohort and some 
patients had more than one suspected clinical diagnosis at 
referral, these categories were not mutually exclusive and 
were therefore not analyzed as discrete disease categories. 
Patients whose main indication for genetic testing was 
suspected hereditary nephrotic syndrome, persistent pro-
teinuria, or Alport syndrome were analyzed using separate 
disease-specific panels and excluded from this cohort. In 
this retrospective referral-based study, absent or mild urinary 
abnormalities were determined from referral information 
and clinical records, and no uniform quantitative proteinuria 
threshold was applied. Clinical data were collected from the 
medical records provided by clinicians during testing. All 
samples and clinical data were obtained from the Japanese 
referral hospitals. This study was approved by the Institu-
tional Review Board of Kobe University School of Medicine 
(approval numbers 86, 301, and 1210) and adhered to the 
1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or 
comparable ethical standards. Written informed consent was 
obtained from all patients or their parents before genetic 
analyses.
Genetic analysis
Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leu-
kocytes using the QuickGene Mini 80 system (Wako Pure 
Chemical Industries, Tokyo, Japan) or QuickGene-Auto 12S 
(Kurabo Industries Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), according to the 
manufacturer’s protocol. Targeted short-read next-genera-
tion sequencing (NGS) was performed using the Haloplex or 
SureSelect target enrichment system (Agilent Technologies, 
Santa Clara, CA, USA), followed by paired-end sequenc-
ing on a MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). 
The gene panels included genes associated with CAKUT, 
nephronophthisis-related ciliopathies, PKD, and other inher-
ited kidney disorders, curated from the OMIM and PubMed 
databases (Supplementary Table 1, Table 2). The sequence 
reads were analyzed using SureCall software (version 4.2; 
Agilent Technologies). Variants detected by NGS were 
confirmed by Sanger sequencing using a 3130 or SeqStu-
dio Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, 
Japan). Family segregation analysis was performed when 
parental samples were available. In all cases where copy 
number variations were suspected based on NGS read depth 
analysis, array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH) 
was performed using the SurePrint G3 Human CGH Micro-
array 60 K platform (Agilent Technologies) according to 
the manufacturer’s protocol. Variant nomenclature followed 
the Human Genome Variation Society guidelines, using the 
NCBI Reference Sequence NM_001002295.2 for GATA3. 
In silico predictions were assessed using multiple compu-
tational tools, including CADD, SIFT, MutationTaster, and 
PROVEAN. Variants were classified based on the American 
College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines [12].
Statistical analysis
Continuous variables are presented as medians with ranges 
and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables are presented 
as counts and percentages with exact 95% confidence inter-
vals, where appropriate. Kaplan–Meier analysis for renal 
survival was not performed because only one patient reached 
kidney replacement therapy and follow-up duration was not 
uniformly available.

Results
All patients
Among the 1254 families with CKD and absent or mild uri-
nary abnormalities, pathogenic or likely pathogenic GATA3 
variants were identified in 10 patients from seven families. 
One additional patient with a phenotype compatible with 
HDR syndrome carried a missense variant of uncertain sig-
nificance (VUS), c.1016G > T p.(Cys339Phe), located in the 
zinc-finger domain, and was included in the descriptive anal-
yses but considered separately from genetically confirmed 
cases. The median age at diagnosis among patients with 
available data was 5 years (IQR, 2–23; range, 0.7–44 years), 
and eight patients were female (Table 1).
Genetic characteristics
Among the seven families with pathogenic or likely patho-
genic GATA3 variants, six were identified by NGS and 
one whole-gene deletion was identified using aCGH. The 
additional missense VUS was detected by NGS. The eight 
distinct GATA3 variants comprised three missense variants, 
including one VUS, two nonsense variants, two short indels, 
and one whole-gene deletion. All missense variants were 
located in exons 3 or 4, corresponding to the zinc-finger 
domains. Segregation analysis identified GATA3  variants 
in all three families. None of the identified variants was 
reported in the Human Gene Mutation Database (Table  2, 
Fig. 1).
Clinical characteristics
Among the major features of HDR syndrome, hypoparathy-
roidism, hearing impairment, and renal manifestations were 
present in 45.5% (5/11; 95% CI, 16.7–76.6), 100% (11/11; 
95% CI, 71.5–100), and 90.0% (9/10; 95% CI, 55.5–99.7) 
of patients, respectively. Among the 11 patients, hearing 
impairment was documented through newborn hearing 
screening in two patients, whereas none of the patients was 
genetically diagnosed with HDR syndrome directly through 
newborn hearing screening. Information on newborn hearing 
screening was unavailable for the remaining nine patients. 
The phenotypic combinations were HDR in 40.0% (4/10), 
deafness and renal anomalies in 50.0% (5/10), and isolated 
deafness in 10.0% (1/10). Among patients with renal mani-
festations, hypoplastic kidney was the most common renal 
phenotype, observed in 33.3% (3/9), followed by multicystic 
dysplastic kidney (MCDK) in 22.2% (2/9), vesicoureteral 
reflux (VUR) in 22.2% (2/9), and reduced kidney function 
without structural abnormalities in 22.2% (2/9). Unilateral 
kidney agenesis and hydronephrosis were observed in 11.1% 
(1/9) of patients. Among patients with available data, the 
median eGFR was 76.5 mL/min/1.73  m2 (IQR, 70.1–82.5; 
range, 63.0–99.0) and the median UPCR was 0.16 g/gCr 
(IQR, 0.07–0.42; range, 0.05–0.79). One patient, SC230, 
who had bilateral MCDK, required kidney replacement ther-
apy beginning in the neonatal period and underwent living-
donor kidney transplantation from her father at 10 years of 
age. At the last follow-up, she was 28 years old and had 
preserved graft function. Clinical information for the two 
affected mothers was incomplete. SC532m had hearing 
impairment, and decreased kidney function was reported; 
however, detailed renal function data were unavailable, and 
she was therefore excluded from phenotype classification. 
SC1125m had hearing impairment and decreased kidney 
function with a serum creatinine level of 1.79 mg/dL, but 
eGFR could not be calculated because age at evaluation was 
unavailable. Among the extrarenal manifestations, female 
genital tract anomalies were present in 37.5% of female 
patients (3/8), whereas cataract, acetabular dysplasia, short 
stature, auricular anomalies, and syndactyly were observed 
in 10.0% (1/10) (Table 1).
Discussion
This cohort study identified HDR syndrome caused by 
pathogenic or likely pathogenic GATA3  variants, as well 
as one patient with a clinically compatible HDR-spectrum 
phenotype who harbored a GATA3  variant of uncertain 
significance, in a kidney disease cohort. Our findings char -
acterize HDR syndrome from a nephrology perspective 
and show that patients referred for renal manifestations 
may lack the complete triad. Although HDR syndrome is 
a well-recognized autosomal dominant disorder character -
ized by hypoparathyroidism, sensorineural deafness, and 
renal anomalies, not all three features are always present in 
clinical practice, and diagnosis cannot rely solely on clini-
cal manifestations. Therefore, it is crucial to clinically sus-
pect HDR syndrome and direct these patients to appropriate 
genetic testing.
HDR syndrome is attributed to GATA3 haploinsufficiency 
[13], and various pathogenic variants have been reported, 
including nonsense, frameshift, splice-site, and missense 
variants, along with deletions involving the 10p14 region 
[6]. In our cohort, in addition to single-nucleotide variants 
and short indels, a pathogenic whole-gene deletion was iden-
tified in one of the eight families described in this study 
(12.5%), indicating that copy number variants occur at a 
frequency comparable to that reported previously. These 
findings indicate that comprehensive genetic testing, includ-
ing copy number and single-nucleotide variant analyses, is

were located within the zinc-finger domain. Numerous mis-
sense variants have been reported to cluster in or around 
these domains [8 ]. Functional analyses have demonstrated 
that variants affecting these domains can impair the tran-
scriptional activity and DNA-binding capacity of GATA3 
[10, 14]. One missense variant in our cohort, c.1016G > T, 
p.(Cys339Phe), was classified as a variant of uncertain sig-
nificance according to the ACMG/AMP criteria. Although 
its absence from population databases, location within the 
zinc-finger domain, predicted deleterious effects using 
multiple in silico tools, and the compatibility of the pheno-
type of the patient with HDR syndrome revealed potential 
clinical relevance, this evidence was insufficient to establish 
pathogenicity.
Regarding the renal phenotype, hypoplastic kidneys were 
the most common finding, followed by MCDK, VUR, and 
reduced kidney function without structural abnormalities. 
Previous studies have reported hypoplastic kidneys, VUR, 
and MCDK in 25, 20, and 18% of patients, respectively [8], 
and a similar trend was observed in our cohort. Nine percent 
of patients developed kidney failure, with age at onset rang-
ing from 9 to 61 years [7]. In our cohort, kidney dysfunction 
was absent or mild in most patients; only one patient, who 
had bilateral MCDK, progressed to kidney failure. Addition-
ally, reduced kidney function was observed in some patients 
without CAKUT. Previous reports have described patients 
with HDR syndrome presenting with nephrotic syndrome, 
hematuria, proteinuria, proximal or distal renal tubular aci-
dosis, and nephrocalcinosis [15–17]. These findings indicate 
that the renal manifestations of HDR syndrome are broader 
than those of CAKUT alone.
The complete triad of HDR syndrome was observed in 
only 40.0% of patients in our cohort, whereas half exhibited 
a DR phenotype, defined by deafness and renal manifesta-
tions without hypoparathyroidism. In large cohort studies, 
the complete triad is present in only 20–70% of patients, 
indicating that the phenotype of HDR syndrome is often 
incomplete [9 –11]. Additionally, selection bias among 
cohorts has been indicated as a possible explanation for the 
differences in the frequencies of these three major features 
[8]. This kidney disease cohort predominantly included 
patients presenting with renal manifestations at a young age, 
resulting in a relatively early diagnosis. This may partially 
explain the relatively low frequency of hypoparathyroidism 
in our cohort. Because hypoparathyroidism develops in an 
age-dependent manner [6 ], patients who initially present 
with only renal manifestations or hearing impairment may 
subsequently develop hypoparathyroidism. This finding indi-
cates that long-term endocrine follow-up is warranted, even 
in patients with DR phenotypes. A comparison of the present 
cohort with previous studies is presented in Supplementary 
Table 3.
From a nephrological perspective, our findings high-
light that HDR syndrome should be included in the dif-
ferential diagnosis of patients with the DR phenotype. In 
such cases, the major differential diagnoses include Alport 
syndrome, branchio-oto-renal syndrome, and mitochon-
drial nephropathy [7 , 18, 19]. However, hypoparathy -
roidism may remain unrecognized because it has neither 
developed nor been assessed. Therefore, HDR syndrome 
should be considered in this clinical setting to facilitate 
the assessment of serum calcium and parathyroid hor -
mone levels, along with comprehensive genetic testing, 
including copy number analysis. A proposed diagnostic 
approach for HDR syndrome from a nephrology perspec-
tive is shown in Fig.  2.

observed in several patients, consistent with previous 
studies [8 ]. Additionally, we observed various other con-
genital anomalies, including acetabular dysplasia, short 
stature, auricular anomalies, and syndactyly. GATA3 is 
a transcription factor involved in the development of the 
parathyroid glands, inner ear, kidneys, genitourinary tract, 
and central nervous system [6 ]. Therefore, the phenotypic 
diversity of HDR syndrome may reflect the broad devel-
opmental roles of GATA3. In patients with copy number

Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, this was a retro-
spective, single-center cohort study with a limited number 
of patients. Therefore, caution is required when general-
izing the frequency of renal phenotypes and clinical course 
of HDR syndrome. In addition, referral diagnoses were not 
systematically coded as mutually exclusive disease catego -
ries, limiting the calculation of percentage distributions 
for each suspected disease group. Because hearing status

becomes available. Further large-scale multicenter studies 
with long-term follow-up are needed to better define the 
renal spectrum and clinical course of HDR syndrome.
Conclusion
In this study, kidney disease gene panel analysis of patients 
with unexplained CKD identified seven families with HDR 
syndrome caused by pathogenic or likely pathogenic GATA3 
variants and one additional family with a clinically compat-
ible HDR-spectrum phenotype carrying a GATA3  variant 
of uncertain significance. Among patients diagnosed with 
renal manifestations, the complete triad was uncommon, and 
many exhibited a DR phenotype. These findings indicate 
that HDR syndrome should be included in the differential 
diagnosis of patients with deafness and renal manifestations, 
and comprehensive genetic testing, including copy number 
analysis, may facilitate an earlier diagnosis.
