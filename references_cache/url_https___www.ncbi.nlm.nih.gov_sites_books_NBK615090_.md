itemprop="description"><h2 id="_chek2-cpd_Summary_">Summary</h2><div><h4 class="inline">Clinical characteristics.</h4><p><i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition is predominantly characterized by an increased risk of female breast cancer. To a lesser extent there is an association with increased risk of prostate cancer. Associations for other cancers are less well established or conflicting. Cancer risks in those with <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition can vary considerably depending on modifying factors such as family history.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Diagnosis/testing.</h4><p>The diagnosis of <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition is established in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in <i>CHEK2</i> identified by <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a>.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Management.</h4><p><i>Treatment of manifestations:</i> Standard cancer treatments are recommended. Breast cancer risk-reducing contralateral mastectomy is generally not recommended but may be considered in some situations.</p><p><i>Surveillance:</i> Breast cancer surveillance in females typically includes breast self-exam training and symptom awareness; mammogram every 12 months beginning at age 40 years; breast MRI recommendations vary by location. Prostate cancer surveillance in males includes serum PSA to be considered annually beginning at age 40 years especially in those with a strong family history of prostate cancer.</p><p><i>Evaluation of relatives at risk:</i> Molecular genetic testing of at-risk female and male adult relatives can identify those family members who also have the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/familial/">familial</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> and thus may benefit from increased cancer surveillance.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Genetic counseling.</h4><p><i>CHEK2</i>-related cancer predisposition is an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> disorder (i.e., individuals who have one copy of a <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> have the disorder). Most individuals with <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition have a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> pathogenic variant. Rarely, affected individuals have <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic variants. The vast majority of individuals with a heterozygous <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variant inherited the pathogenic variant from a parent who may or may not have had a cancer diagnosis. The offspring of an individual identified as having a heterozygous germline <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variant have a 50% chance of inheriting the pathogenic variant. All offspring of an individual identified as having biallelic germline <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variants will inherit a pathogenic variant. Once the <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variant(s) have been identified in an affected family member, predictive testing for at-risk relatives is possible.</p></div></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Diagnosis"><h2 id="_chek2-cpd_Diagnosis_">Diagnosis</h2><div id="chek2-cpd.Suggestive_Findings"><h3>Suggestive Findings</h3><p><i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition <b>should be suspected</b> in probands with the following clinical findings, family history, and/or laboratory findings.</p><p>
<b>Clinical findings</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Estrogen receptor (ER)-positive breast cancer, particularly young onset and/or bilateral breast cancer in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with a family history of breast cancer in close relatives</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Prostate cancer, particularly in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with a family history of prostate and/or breast cancer</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Results from a risk assessment model incorporating family history, personal risk factors, ER status of breast cancer, and breast cancer diagnoses in a family indicates an increased likelihood of identifying a <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> (e.g., <a href="https://www.canrisk.org/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">CanRisk</a>)</div></li></ul><p><b>Family history</b> can be consistent with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> inheritance (e.g., affected males and females across multiple generations). However, as <i>CHEK2</i> is associated with intermediate <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a>, the presence of individuals in a family without cancer is common and a typical autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance is often not observed.</p><p><b>Laboratory findings.</b> Identification of a <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> on tumor tissue testing may indicate the presence of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline-variant/">germline variant</a>. Note: In an analysis of 45,472 non-hypermutated solid malignancy tumor samples from individuals treated at a single oncology center, a high proportion of filtered <i>CHEK2</i> variants (89.9%, 187/208) were of true germline origin. Variants filtered for inclusion in the overall analyses were those with (1) minor <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/allele-frequency/">allele frequency</a> (MAF) &#x0003c;1%, (2) tumor observed variant allele frequency (VAF) &#x0003e;30% (single nucleotide variants) or &#x0003e;20% (small insertions/deletions), and (3) predicted to result in protein truncation and/or classified as pathogenic&#x000a0;/ <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.kuzbari.2023.215">Kuzbari et al 2023</a>].</p></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Establishing_the_Diagnosis"><h3>Establishing the Diagnosis</h3><p>The diagnosis of <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition <b>is established</b> in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> pathogenic (or <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a>) variant in <i>CHEK2</i> identified by <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.molecular_genetic_testing_us/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTmoleculargenetictestingus">Table 1</a>).</p><p>Note: (1) Per American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics&#x000a0;/ Association for Molecular Pathology variant interpretation guidelines, the terms "<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>" and "<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a> variant" are synonymous in a clinical setting, meaning that both are considered diagnostic and can be used for clinical decision making [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.richards.2015.405">Richards et al 2015</a>]. Reference to "pathogenic variants" in this <i>GeneReview</i> is understood to include likely pathogenic variants. (2) Identification of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> <i>CHEK2</i> variant of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/uncertain-significance/">uncertain significance</a> does not establish or rule out the diagnosis. (3) Some <i>CHEK2</i> variants historically reported as pathogenic&#x000a0;/ likely pathogenic, in particular <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.470T&#x0003e;C (p.Ile157Thr)</a> and <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.1283C&#x0003e;T (p.Ser428Phe)</a>, are now widely considered to be lower-<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> variants that in isolation are not associated with a level of clinically actionable risk. In addition, a third variant, <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.1427C&#x0003e;T (p.Thr476Met)</a>, has also been suggested to be associated with a smaller increased risk [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.bychkovsky.2022.1598">Bychkovsky et al 2022</a>] (see <a href="#chek2-cpd.GenotypePhenotype_Correlations">Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</a>). Guidelines from the ENIGMA group for standardized reporting of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> cancer susceptibility variants suggest that only variants associated with a twofold or greater risk should be clinically reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.spurdle.2019.347">Spurdle et al 2019</a>]. (4) Identification of low-level <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/mosaicism/">mosaicism</a> for a <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variant in leukocytes is most suggestive of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/postzygotic/">postzygotic</a> (acquired) pathogenic variant associated with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) due to aging, cytotoxic therapies, an underlying hematologic malignancy or premalignancy, or circulating tumor cells [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.slavin.2019.31">Slavin et al 2019</a>]. (2) Some multigene panels may include genes not associated with the condition discussed in this <i>GeneReview</i>. (3) In some laboratories, panel options may include a custom laboratory-designed panel and/or custom phenotype-focused <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exome/">exome</a> analysis that includes genes specified by the clinician. </p></div></dd><dt>3. </dt><dd><div id="chek2-cpd.TF.2.3"><p class="no_margin">
<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.liang.2018.397">Liang et al [2018]</a>
</p></div></dd><dt>4. </dt><dd><div id="chek2-cpd.TF.2.4"><p class="no_margin">See Cancer Research UK, <a href="https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/health-professional/cancer-statistics/risk" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">Cancer risk statistics</a> (accessed 5-19-25).</p></div></dd></dl></div></div></div><p><b>Breast cancer.</b> Large case-control studies have reported an increased risk of breast cancer for females <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.1100delC (p.Thr367MetfsTer15)</a>, with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.66 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.27-3.11). An OR of 2.13 (95% CI: 1.60-2.84) is reported for other <i>CHEK2</i> truncating pathogenic variants, and an OR of 2.47 (95% CI: 2.02-3.05) is reported for all individuals combined with a germline <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variant [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.dorling.2021.428">Dorling et al 2021</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.hu.2021.440">Hu et al 2021</a>].</p><p>The magnitude of increased risk of breast cancer in <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes is highest in females under age 40 years (OR = 4.54, 95% CI: 2.87-7.17) compared to females older than age 60 years (OR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.72-2.86) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.dorling.2021.428">Dorling et al 2021</a>].</p><p>Similar to breast cancers occurring in the general population, breast cancers occurring in <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes are more likely to be estrogen receptor (ER) positive than ER negative or triple-negative subtypes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.dorling.2021.428">Dorling et al 2021</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.hu.2021.440">Hu et al 2021</a>]. An association with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive breast cancers has also been suggested by some studies [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.agaoglu.2024.101829">Agaoglu et al 2024</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.de_baumont.2024.1395970">de Baumont et al 2024</a>]. Unlike breast cancers associated with other breast cancer predisposition genes, breast cancers occurring in <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes do not typically display <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> features of homologous <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/recombination/">recombination</a> repair deficiency (HRD).</p><p>There have been mixed reports about the impact of <i>CHEK2</i> status on cancer outcomes. Some studies have not demonstrated any difference in outcomes for individuals with <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.kilpivaara.2005.575">Kilpivaara et al 2005</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.huzarski.2014.397">Huzarski et al 2014</a>], while some studies have demonstrated significantly adverse outcomes (measured as overall survival [OS], breast cancer-specific survival, and incidence of contralateral breast cancer) for <i>CHEK2</i> c.1100delC (p.Thr367MetfsTer15) heterozygotes, with or without the administration of adjuvant chemotherapy and/or hormone therapy [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.de_bock.2004.731">de Bock et al 2004</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.meyer.2007.349">Meyer et al 2007</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.schmidt.2007.64">Schmidt et al 2007</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.weischer.2012.4308">Weischer et al 2012</a>]. A study specifically focused on 2,344 individuals diagnosed with breast cancer under age 40 years, including 53 <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes, reported that overall survival and distant disease-free survival were significantly worse in <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes compared to individuals without a <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, even when adjusted for other known prognostic factors (OS hazard ratio [HR] = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.01-2.48, P=0.043) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.grevilleheygate.2020">Greville-Heygate et al 2020</a>]. Given that most cancers in <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes are ER-positive cancers, generally associated with more favorable outcomes, these observations need additional studies to investigate these findings further.</p><p><b>Contralateral breast cancer (CBC).</b> An increased risk of CBC has been reported in <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes. Two large studies, Breast Cancer Association Consortium's (BCAC) Breast Cancer Risk after Diagnostic Gene Sequencing (BRIDGES) and Cancer Risk Estimates Related to Susceptibility (CARRIERS), demonstrated similar risks: HR = 2.25 (95% CI: 1.55-3.27) and HR = 2.0 (95% CI: 1.0-4.2), respectively [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.morra.2023.475">Morra et al 2023</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.yadav.2023.1703">Yadav et al 2023</a>]. In the CARRIERS study, the risk of CBC was reported to be greatest among females whose initial breast cancer occurred at a young age. Among premenopausal females, the ten-year cumulative incidence of CBC was estimated to be 13%, compared to postmenopausal females, who were found to have a ten-year cumulative incidence of CBC of 4%.</p><p>Radiotherapy has not been demonstrated to impact CBC risk for <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes in a small number of studies undertaken to date [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.reiner.2020.1275">Reiner et al 2020</a>].</p><p><b>Male breast cancer.</b> An increased risk of male breast cancer has been reported in <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes, although published studies have been limited to small single-country studies and predominantly focused on c.1100delC (p.Thr367MetfsTer15) heterozygotes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.hallamies.2017.620">Hallamies et al 2017</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.schreurs.2024.101171">Schreurs et al 2024</a>]. A metanalysis including 26 studies reported an increased risk of male breast cancer for <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes (OR = 3.13, 95% CI: 1.94-5.07) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.liang.2018.397">Liang et al 2018</a>]. <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes developed male breast cancer at younger ages than <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sporadic/">sporadic</a> male breast cancer or male breast cancer due to pathogenic variants in other genes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.pritzlaff.2017.575">Pritzlaff et al 2017</a>]. No clear histopathologic associations have been described for breast cancers occurring in male <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes.</p><p><b>Prostate cancer.</b>
<i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variants are associated with approximately twofold increased risk of prostate cancer; cumulative lifetime risk figures have not been published. Studies have identified a higher prevalence of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variants in males with metastatic prostate cancer, suggesting that <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygosity is associated with more aggressive disease [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.pritchard.2016.443">Pritchard et al 2016</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.wu.2018.607">Wu et al 2018</a>].</p><p><b>Other cancers with unconfirmed association with <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variants.</b>
<i>CHEK2</i> has been proposed to be associated with many cancers and to potentially act as a low-to-moderate-risk multitumor predisposition <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a>. Other cancers, particularly colorectal, renal, and papillary thyroid cancer, as well as many others such as sarcoma, pancreatic, stomach, melanoma, endometrial, bladder, testicular, and hematologic malignancies have been suggested to be increased in <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes, but further data are required to confirm any clear associations with clinically actionable risks [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.hanson.2023.100870">Hanson et al 2023</a>]. Note: Early studies suggested that variants in <i>CHEK2</i> were associated with Li-Fraumeni syndrome. However, subsequent studies firmly refuted this, and it is now widely acknowledged that variants in <i>CHEK2</i> do not cause Li-Fraumeni syndrome.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Colorectal cancer (CRC).</b> Studies on the association of <i>CHEK2</i> with CRC have been conflicting. A study focusing on <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> c.1100delC (p.Thr367MetfsTer15) in a Dutch population found an increased CRC risk in heterozygotes compared to the general population from age 45 in females and age 55 in males, with an overall standardized incidence ratio (SIR) of 1.43 (95% CI: 1.14-1.76, absolute excess risk = 1.4); SIR was 3.44 in individuals age 45-50 years [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.schreurs.2024.101171">Schreurs et al 2024</a>]. In comparison, a retrospective cohort study of individuals with cancer or family history of cancer who had <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multigene-panel/">multigene panel</a> testing did not find an association between <i>CHEK2</i> and CRC. In fact, individuals with a <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variant (n=3,783) had fewer instances of CRC, compared with individuals without a <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> pathogenic variant (n=33,034) (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.51-0.76, P &#x0003c;0.001) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.bychkovsky.2022.1598">Bychkovsky et al 2022</a>]. A further commercial laboratory-based study also did not find an association of either truncating or <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a> <i>CHEK2</i> variants with CRC risk in more than 6,000 heterozygotes (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 0.91-1.33, P=0.30600). Consequently, there is insufficient data to support a clinically meaningful increased risk of CRC among <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes. Further research is needed to determine if enhanced surveillance is of benefit to specific <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Thyroid cancer.</b> An increased risk of papillary thyroid cancer has been suggested in <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes. However, studies reporting a statistically significant increased risk are limited to a single country or specific variant (e.g., the Polish <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/founder-variant/">founder variant</a> <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.444+1G&#x0003e;A</a>) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.sio_ek.2015.548">Sio&#x00142;ek et al 2015</a>]. In fact, studies from other countries have not identified statistically increased risks or have reported lower risks (OR &#x0003c;2) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.n_slundkoch.2016.1208">N&#x000e4;slund-Koch et al 2016</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.bychkovsky.2022.1598">Bychkovsky et al 2022</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.brock.2024.477">Brock et al 2024</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Renal cancer.</b> Studies of an association of <i>CHEK2</i> with renal cancer have also been conflicting. Some studies have reported an association [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.carlo.2018.1228">Carlo et al 2018</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.hartman.2020.13518">Hartman et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.bychkovsky.2022.1598">Bychkovsky et al 2022</a>], whereas others have not [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.n_slundkoch.2016.1208">N&#x000e4;slund-Koch et al 2016</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.schreurs.2024.101171">Schreurs et al 2024</a>]. In a large series of unselected individuals with renal cell cancer (n=1336) and controls (n=5834) recruited to the UK 100,000 Genomes Project, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/whole-genome-sequencing/">whole-genome sequencing</a> data demonstrated a significant excess of <i>CHEK2</i> variants in individuals with renal cancer compared to controls (P=0.0019); the mean age of individuals was reported to be 65.5 years, suggesting a potential age-related association, but overall lifetime cancer risk was not studied [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.yngvadottir.2022.3001">Yngvadottir et al 2022</a>]. Although current studies are largely supportive of a possible increased risk of renal cancer, additional studies are required to confirm an association and determine if there is a predisposition for a specific histopathologic subtype to inform surveillance recommendations.</div></li></ul></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Homozygous__Compound_Heterozyg"><h3>Homozygous&#x000a0;/ Compound Heterozygous Individuals</h3><p>Individuals <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/homozygous/">homozygous</a> for <i>CHEK2</i> c.1100delC (p.Thr367MetfsTer15) are reported to have a higher risk for both unilateral and bilateral breast cancer [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.rainville.2020.503">Rainville et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.hini_.2024.101101">Hini&#x00107; et al 2024</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.schreurs.2024.101171">Schreurs et al 2024</a>]. In one study of individuals identified through <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multigene-panel/">multigene panel</a> testing that included 6,473 <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes and 31 individuals with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variants, individuals with biallelic <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variants had a significantly higher risk for invasive breast cancer (OR = 8.69, 95% CI: 3.69-20.47), were more likely to be diagnosed at or before age 50 years, and were more likely to have multiple primary breast cancers compared to <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes. The higher reported cancer risks were only statistically significant for breast cancer [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.bychkovsky.2022.1598">Bychkovsky et al 2022</a>].</p><p>A small number of case reports have described a cancer predisposition syndrome with early adult onset and/or multiple primary cancers with somatic <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosome/">chromosome</a> instability and multiple <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/cytogenetic/">cytogenetic</a> abnormalities identified in tumor tissue in individuals with <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic variants. One report described two unrelated individuals <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/homozygous/">homozygous</a> for <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.499G&#x0003e;A (p.Gly167Arg)</a>, one with multiple primary tumors and one with early-onset leukemia [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.paperna.2020.500">Paperna et al 2020</a>]. Another reported an individual homozygous for <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.793-1G&#x0003e;A</a> and an individual <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/compound-heterozygous/">compound heterozygous</a> for c.1100delC (p.Thr367MetfsTer15) and <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.1312G&#x0003e;T (p.Asp438Tyr)</a>, both with multiple primary tumors [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.bottillo.2023.474">Bottillo et al 2023</a>].</p><p>The largest study of individuals with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variants ascertained via the ERN GENTURIS network (n=294) confirmed an association of biallelic <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/loss-of-function/">loss-of-function</a> variants with early-onset breast cancer and multiple breast and other cancers [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.hini_.2024.101101">Hini&#x00107; et al 2024</a>]. More than half of females with biallelic loss-of-function <i>CHEK2</i> variants developed at least two cancers, with approximately half developing cancers other than breast cancer. A higher risk of CRC when compared with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/monoallelic/">monoallelic</a> carriers was also suggested.</p></div><div id="chek2-cpd.GenotypePhenotype_Correlations"><h3>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</h3><p>Cancer risk estimates for <i>CHEK2</i> heterozygotes with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a> variants have been reported to be lower than estimates for <i>CHEK2</i> truncating variants in some studies [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.sutcliffe.2020.12">Sutcliffe et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.dorling.2021.428">Dorling et al 2021</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.dorling.2022.51">Dorling et al 2022</a>]. A large study from BRIDGES estimated an OR &#x0003c;1.5 (OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.28-1.58, P= 2.5 &#x000d7; 10<sup>&#x02212;11</sup>) for rare missense variants (defined as variants with a population frequency of &#x0003c;0.001) and found no correlation with the variant position in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a>, while the OR for truncating variants was 2.66 (95% CI: 2.27-3.11) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.dorling.2021.428">Dorling et al 2021</a>].</p><p>However, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genotype-phenotype-correlations/">genotype-phenotype correlations</a> have been difficult to determine due to inclusion of low-<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a> variants in studies of cancer risk, thus influencing the risk for missense variants as a whole. Some missense variants historically considered pathogenic are now recognized as low-penetrance variants that in isolation do not reach a level of clinical actionability. The most notable examples are <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.470T&#x0003e;C (p.Ile157Thr)</a> and <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.1283C&#x0003e;T (p.Ser428Phe)</a>. In a large population-based case-control study of 32,247 women with breast cancer and 32,544 unaffected women, the OR for both these variants when separately analyzed fell below 1.5 (p.Ile157Thr: OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.06-1.59, P=0.01 and p.Ser428Phe: OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 0.76-2.12, P=0.37) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.hu.2021.440">Hu et al 2021</a>].</p><p>To further compare cancer risk associated with truncating versus <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a> variants, a commercial laboratory-based study of 6,000 <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variants excluded both c.470T&#x0003e;C (p.Ile157Thr) and c.1283C&#x0003e;T (p.Ser428Phe) from the analysis of missense variants and reported no statistically significant differences when truncating pathogenic variants (P &#x0003c;0.001) and missense pathogenic variants (P &#x0003c;0.001) were evaluated separately [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.mundt.2023.84">Mundt et al 2023</a>]. Similar findings were reported in other retrospective analyses of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multigene-panel/">multigene panel</a> testing for these two variants [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.bychkovsky.2022.1598">Bychkovsky et al 2022</a>]. It is therefore suggested that these two variants are best considered comparable to other common breast cancer risk single-nucleotide polymorphisms within a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/polygenic/">polygenic</a> risk score, rather than a single-<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> cause [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.ivanov.2022">Ivanov et al 2022</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.mundt.2023.84">Mundt et al 2023</a>].</p></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Penetrance"><h3>Penetrance</h3><p>The <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> of breast and other cancers associated with pathogenic variants in <i>CHEK2</i> is typically in the range of 20%-30%, but overall individual lifetime risk can be lower or higher when modifying factors are taken into consideration. <i>CHEK2</i> is considered a moderate risk or intermediate penetrance cancer predisposition <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.cancer_risk_in_chek2_heteroz/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTcancerriskinchek2heteroz">Table 2</a> for associated cancer risks).</p></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Genetic_Modifiers"><h3>Genetic Modifiers</h3><p>Individuals containing pathogenic variants in both <i>CHEK2</i> and <i>ATM</i> (excluding low-risk variants) were found to be diagnosed with breast cancer at a younger age compared to those with a single <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in either <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a>. This significant difference is primarily driven by the age of breast cancer onset. Additional studies are necessary to validate these findings [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.agaoglu.2024.101829">Agaoglu et al 2024</a>].</p><p>A cohort study of 3,783 individuals <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for pathogenic&#x000a0;/ <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a> (P/LP) and low-risk <i>CHEK2</i> variants demonstrated that low-risk variants such as c.470T&#x0003e;C (p.Ile157Thr), c.1283C&#x0003e;T (p.Ser428Phe), and <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.1427C&#x0003e;T (p.Thr476Met)</a>, when found in combination with a P/LP variant, were associated with a more penetrant cancer <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>. These included increased incidence of cancer, multiple primary cancers, and bilateral breast cancer. Further investigation of this potential modifier effect is warranted [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.bychkovsky.2025.e2451361">Bychkovsky et al 2025</a>].</p></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Prevalence"><h3>Prevalence</h3><p><i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> <a prevalent in northern European populations, with a prevalence of 1% reported in the Netherlands [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.schreurs.2024.101171">Schreurs et al 2024</a>].</p><p>The <i>CHEK2</i> low-risk variants <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.470T&#x0003e;C (p.Ile157Thr)</a>, <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.1283C&#x0003e;T (p.Ser428Phe)</a>, and <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.notable_chek2_pathogenic_var/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTnotablechek2pathogenicvar">c.1427C&#x0003e;T (p.Thr476Met)</a> have been observed with varying frequency across populations: 0.2% in non-Finnish Europeans, 1.1% in <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/ashkenazi-jewish/">Ashkenazi Jewish</a> individuals, and 0.05% in the broader European population [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.bychkovsky.2025.e2451361">Bychkovsky et al 2025</a>].</p><p>Several other <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variants have been reported to be more common in specific populations (see <a id="chek2-cpd.Genetically_Related_Allelic_Di"><h2 id="_chek2-cpd_Genetically_Related_Allelic_Di_">Genetically Related (Allelic) Disorders</h2><p>No phenotypes other than <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition are known to be associated with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> pathogenic variants in <i>CHEK2</i>.</p><p><b>Low-level <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/somatic-mosaicism/">somatic mosaicism</a></b> for a <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in leukocytes is a common finding and is suggestive of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/postzygotic/">postzygotic</a> (acquired) pathogenic variant associated with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) due to aging, cytotoxic therapies, an underlying hematologic malignancy or premalignancy, or circulating tumor cells [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.slavin.2019.31">Slavin et al 2019</a>]. Individuals with a <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variant that is not present in non-hematopoietic tissue and is confirmed through ancillary evaluation to be the result of CHIP or other forms of aberrant clonal expansion in hematopoietic cells do not have <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition.</p><p>Many types of <b><a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sporadic/">sporadic</a> tumors</b> contain somatic pathogenic variants in <i>CHEK2</i>. Taking into consideration the resulting impact on clinical management of an individual or family, ancillary evaluation may be performed to determine <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/constitutional/">constitutional</a> or somatic status of a <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> and may include <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> on non-hematopoietic tissue such as cultured skin fibroblasts [<a class="bk_pop" href="#chek2-cpd.REF.sutcliffe.2022.86">Sutcliffe et al 2022</a>].</p></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Differential_Diagnosis"><h2 id="_chek2-cpd_Differential_Diagnosis_">Differential Diagnosis</h2><p><b>Syndromic breast cancer.</b> Individuals with the cancer susceptibility syndromes listed in <a href="/sites/books/NBK615090/table/chek2-cpd.T.genes_associated_with_cancer/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobchek2cpdTgenesassociatedwithcancer">Table 3</a> have an elevated breast cancer risk and may have features overlapping those associated with <i>CHEK2</i>-related cancer predisposition (e.g., Estrogen receptor (ER)-positive breast cancer and moderate family history). It is not possible to distinguish <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition from these other cancer susceptibility syndromes based solely on the constellation of tumors and ages of cancer onset present in the family. Standard cancer treatments are recommended.</p><p><b>Breast cancer.</b> In those with breast cancer, risk-reducing contralateral mastectomy is generally not recommended but may be considered in some situations. 2025</a>].</p></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Surveillance"><h3>Surveillance</h3><p>To monitor existing manifestations and the emergence of new manifestations, the evaluations summarized in <a number of ongoing studies are investigating novel approaches to the treatment of <i>CHEK2</i>-associated breast and prostate cancer. The majority of these studies involve poly [ADP-ribose] polymerase (PARP) inhibitors. However, to date there is insufficient evidence to recommend specific cancer treatment based on identification of <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition.</p><p>Search <a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">ClinicalTrials.gov</a> in the US and <a href="https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/ctr-search/search" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">EU Clinical Trials Register</a> in Europe for access to information on clinical studies.</p></div></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Genetic_Counseling"><h2 id="_chek2-cpd_Genetic_Counseling_">Genetic Counseling</h2><p><i>Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with information on the nature, mode(s) of inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them make informed medical and personal decisions. The following section deals with genetic risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or ethical issues that may arise or to substitute for consultation with a genetics professional</i>. &#x02014;ED.</p><div id="chek2-cpd.Mode_of_Inheritance"><h3>Mode of Inheritance</h3><p><i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition is an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> disorder (i.e., individuals who have one copy of a <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> have the disorder).</p><p>Most individuals with <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition have a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>. Rarely, individuals with <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition have <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic variants (see Clinical Description, <a href="#chek2-cpd.Homozygous__Compound_Heterozyg">Homozygous&#x000a0;/ Compound Heterozygous Individuals</a>).</p></div><div id="chek2-cpd.Risk_to_Family_Members"><h3>Risk to Family Members</h3><p>
<b>Parents of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The vast majority of individuals with a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> inherited the pathogenic variant from a parent. The parent with the pathogenic variant may or may not have had a cancer diagnosis depending on <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> of the pathogenic variant, modifying factors (e.g., <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/polygenic/">polygenic</a> risk score, hormonal factors, and/or lifestyle factors), sex of the parent, age of the parent, cancer risk reduction in the parent as a result of screening or prophylactic surgeries, and early death of the parent.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is appropriate to offer <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> to both parents of an individual with a <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> to determine which side of the family is at risk. Generally, the pattern of cancers seen in the family guides which parent is tested first. Note: If an individual with <i>CHEK2-</i>related cancer predisposition has <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> germline pathogenic variants, it is likely that both parents are <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for a <i>CHEK2</i> pathogenic variant.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> is not identified in either parent and parental identity testing has confirmed biological maternity and paternity, the following possibilities should be considered:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">The <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> has a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">The <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> inherited a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> from a parent with gonadal (or somatic and gonadal) <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/mosaicism/">mosaicism</a>.</div><div class="half_rhythm">Note: Testing of parental leukocyte DNA may not detect all instances of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/somatic-mosaicism/">somatic mosaicism</a> and will not detect a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> that is present in the germ (gonadal) cells only.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>An apparently negative family history cannot be confirmed unless <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> has demonstrated that neither parent is <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for the <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</div></li></ul><p><b>Sibs of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> The risk to the sibs of the proband depends on the genetic status of the proband's parents:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If one parent has the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline/">germline</a> <i>CHEK2</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>, the risk
