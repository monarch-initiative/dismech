# Therapeutic restoration of miR-96 prevents hearing loss in mice through modulation of noise-induced and genetic pathways.
**Authors:** Xia R, Jin C, Fei S, Dong T, Wen T, Zhu F, Shi Y, Zhou Q, Tao Y, Peng C
**Journal:** iScience (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.isci.2025.112355](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.112355)

## Content

Hearing loss often arises from impairments in multiple genes, complicating therapeutic development. MicroRNAs, as master regulators, offer promising targets for complex diseases. We explored miR-96's roles in hair cell (HC) function and noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL), finding that miR-96 -/-, not miR-96 +/- , mice exhibited progressive hearing loss due to gene regulatory network dysregulation from miR-96 loss in HCs not spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs). Viral-mediated delivery of miR-96 into the inner ear partially rescued hearing of miR-96 -/- mice. Tamoxifen-induced depletion of miR-96 in adult UBC CreERT2/+ ; miR-96 fl/fl mice led to hearing loss, with Bach2, Gabra2, Gabra4, and Grk1 upregulation and Tnn, Col11a1, Gjb3, and Hnf4a downregulation. Furthermore, noise trauma reduced miR-96, altering Bach2, Bcl2l1, Slc26a9, Gabrb1, Grk1, Nos2, and Cyp1a1 expression, whereas miR-96 overexpression protected hearing against noise by reversing the expression of Bach2, Bcl2l1, and Cyp1a1. Our findings underscore miR-96's essential role in adult hearing maintenance and NIHL prevention, presenting it as a promising therapeutic target.

Disabling hearing loss impacts approximately half of a billion individuals worldwide and has genetic and/or environmental causes.1 Up to 60% of early-onset hearing loss cases are congenital.2,3 Over 500 genes/loci are associated with hearing loss, with approximately 6,000 causative variants identified across 150+ genes (https://hereditaryhearingloss.org).4,5 Age-related hearing loss (ARHL) is influenced by both environmental factors like noise exposure and genetic susceptibilities.2,6 Examples include oxidative-stress-related genes (NAT2, GST, UCP2) and the neurotransmitter-related gene (GRM7).7 Genes like KCNQ4 and ACTG1 are crucial in both ARHL and early-onset hearing loss.2,8,9,10,11,12 Given the involvement of numerous genes in hearing loss, identifying hub genes that regulate multiple hearing-loss-related genes could facilitate the development of pharmacological interventions for hearing loss prevention and treatment.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have the capacity to regulate hundreds to thousands of direct targets through imperfect complementary paring to the 3′UTR of mRNAs,13 making them pivotal regulators in multi-gene diseases. For example, the miR-183-96-182 cluster regulates ∼80% of genes in the network linked to neuropathic pain induced by peripheral injury, including sodium and calcium channel networks.14,15,16
MiR-183-96-182 cluster is selectively highly expressed in multiple sensory organs, including inner hair cells from embryonic to adult stage.17,18,19,20,21 It is known that homozygous miR-183-96-182 and miR-183-96 knockout mice are deaf, whereas heterozygotes retain normal hearing.22,23,24
MiR-96 mutations in humans cause nonsyndromic progressive hearing loss.25,26 In mice, a single nucleotide mutation in the seed sequence of miR-96 causes deafness in the homozygous individuals and progressive hearing loss beginning from 4 weeks in the heterozygous.27,28,29 However, the hearing loss phenotype observed in the miR-96 mutant mice implicates both gain- and loss-of-function mechanisms,24,27,28,29 and the precise role of miR-96 in auditory system and its regulation of the hearing-related genetic network remains unclear.

Here, we show that only homozygous, not heterozygous, miR-96 knockout mice exhibit progressive hearing loss. Moreover, we found that miR-96 is crucial for adult hearing maintenance and confers resistance to NIHL by regulating a critical genetic network that encompasses multiple hearing-loss-related genes, neurotransmitter receptors, and transcription factors in mice.

The miR-183 cluster, composed of miR-183, miR-96, and miR-182, is highly conserved across vertebrates and invertebrates, with specific and synergistic expression in sensory neurons and sensory epithelial cells.30
In situ hybridization revealed miR-96 expression in the auditory system during the embryonic stage, which became undetectable in the organ of Corti from postnatal day 4 (P4) onward in mice.20 To confirm whether miR-96 is expressed in the organ of Corti from postnatal to adult stages, we used miR-96LacZ-2A-Tomato/+ and miR-96CreERT2-2A-GFP/+ knockin reporter mice14 in which the reporters have the same expression pattern in multi-sensory organs as the LacZ gene in the gene-trap miR-183CGT/GT mice.18 X-gal staining showed specific miR-96/LacZ expression in the auditory system at embryonic day 10.5 (E10.5), E11.5, E12.5, E14.5, E16.5, and E18.5, and postnatal days P4, P14, and P28, as well as in 3-month-old miR-96LacZ-2A-Tomato/+ mice, with high abundance in HCs and SGNs (Figures S1A–S1K). Immunostaining data from miR-96CreERT2-2A-GFP/+ mice also demonstrated specific miR-96/GFP expression in HCs (marked by Myosin VI) and SGNs (marked by Tuj1) of adult mice (Figures S1L–S1M), both crucial sensory cell types in the auditory system. These data are in line with previously reported findings that detection of miR-96 and miR-183 in HCs and SGNs in embryonic mice and detection of miR-183 in HCs and SGNs of P0 and P37 mice by in situ hybridization,19,20 because miR-183-96-182 cluster is believed to derive from the same one transcript. We further employed RT-qPCR to quantify miR-96 expression in inner ear of WT mice from E16.5 to 38 weeks of age. The expression of miR-96-5p peaked at 4 weeks of age, then declined to approximately one-third of its peak by 26 weeks, with a gradual decrease thereafter (Figure 1A). In contrast, miR-96-3p expression peaked at E16.5, followed by a significant decrease from 4 to 38 weeks of age (Figure 1B). We used a previously described knockout allele of miR-9614 to investigate miR-96 loss-of-function effects in the auditory system. RT-qPCR confirmed miR-96 depletion in the inner ear of miR-96−/− mice and about 50% decrease in miR-96+/− mice (Figure 1C). We then examined the auditory function of P21, P28, 2-month-old, 3-month-old miR-96−/−, miR-96+/−, and WT littermate mice using auditory brainstem response (ABR) and distortion product otoacoustic emission (DPOAE). The results demonstrated that miR-96−/− mice exhibited impaired hearing at P21 compared to WT littermates, progressing to complete hearing loss by 3 months of age (Figures 1D–1N and S2A–S2E). This phenotype contrasts with that of the homozygous miR-96-mutated (Dmdo/Dmdo and Mir96+14C>A homozygotes) mice, which exhibit congenital deafness.27,28,29 Interestingly, miR-96+/− mice displayed normal hearing, with ABR amplitude and wave 1 latency comparable to WT littermate mice at P21, maintaining normal hearing through 3 months of age (Figures 1D–1N and S2F–S2J). In contrast, Dmdo/+ and Mir96+14C>A heterozygous mice27,28,29 exhibit progressive hearing loss starting at P15 and 4 weeks of age, respectively. Our findings, consistent with prior reports,31 indicated that miR-96 plays a critical role in postnatal development, and our data additionally suggested its involvement in maintaining hearing in adults. Furthermore, our findings and others27,28,29 suggested that progressive hearing loss in Dmdo/+ and Mir96+14C>A heterozygous mice, and early-onset complete hearing loss in Dmdo/Dmdo mice, primarily result from gain-of-function mutations, effectively transforming the mutated miR-96 into a novel miRNA. The normal hearing in miR-96+/− and Mir96+/13G>A29 mice confirms that miR-96 is haplosufficient, not haploinsufficient, in auditory system.

Given that HCs are frequently implicated in early-stage hearing loss, we investigated their survival rate and synaptic connections. Double immunofluorescence staining for Myosin VI (an HC marker) and CTBP2 (a pre-synaptic marker) showed significant HC loss in the outer layer 1 of the cochlear basal turn in miR-96−/− mice at P28, with a marked decrease in the pre-synapse number (CTBP2) of inner HCs (IHCs) throughout the entire cochlea (Figures 2A–2C and S3A–S3D). By 3 months, miR-96−/− mice had lost most outer HCs (OHCs) in the basal and middle turns, with a moderate proportion lost in the apical turn. IHC numbers were similar between miR-96−/− mice and WT littermates; however, the number of IHC synapses in miR-96−/− mice was further reduced to approximately one-third of WT level (Figures 2A–2D, 2E, S3A, and S3E–S3G). At 6 months of age, OHC decline continued, and a significant loss of IHCs was also observed in miR-96−/− mice (Figures 2A–2F, 2G, S3A, and S3H–S3J). MiR-96+/− mice exhibited no significant difference in the number of HCs and IHC synapses (Figures S4A–S4E), which is in line with their normal hearing. These data suggested that functional defects in HCs, rather than the loss of HCs, contribute more significantly to hearing loss of miR-96−/− mice at early stage. The progressive HC loss indicates that miR-96 is essential for HC maintenance beyond postnatal hearing development. Given that miR-96 is also highly expressed in SGNs, we quantified Tuj1-positive cells (an SGN marker) in miR-96−/− mice at P28, 3 months, and 6 months of age. Based on the predicted protein-protein functional interaction network, gene enrichment analysis, and pertinent literatures, a subset of differentially expressed genes (upregulated: Cidea, Cidec, Slc12a5, Slc1a1, Slc34a2, Slc26a9, Cyp1a1, and Bcl2l1 and downregulated: Kcnq4, Ptprq, Calb1, Calb2, Ptpn5, Slc24a2, Hcn4, Hcnb2, Fgf12, Chrnb3, and Gria2) were selected for further validation. RT-qPCR confirmed significantly increased expression of Cidea, Cidec, Slc12a5, Slc1a1, and Slc34a2 and decreased expression of Kcnq4, Ptprq, Calb1, Calb2, Ptpn5, Slc24a2, Hcn4, Fgf12, and Chrnb3 in the cochlea of miR-96−/− mice compared to WT littermates (Figures 3F and 3G). Cidea and Cidec belong to cell-death-inducing DFFA-like effector (CIDE) family, which can initiate cell death and promote cell apoptosis.36,37
Slc1a1 encodes a high-affinity glutamate transporter crucial for maintaining low synaptic glutamate level in the cochlea.38
Slc12a5 encodes the K+-Cl− co-transporter KCC2, which modulates GABA(A)-receptor-mediated transmission by regulating chloride concentration in the nervous system.39 SLC34A2 plays a vital role in maintaining ion-dependent metabolism of the inner ear homeostasis.40
KCNQ4 and PTPRQ are crucial deafness genes involved in potassium homeostasis and stereocilium function, respectively.41,42,43 The calcium-binding proteins CALB1 and CALB2, along with the neuroprotective protein PTPN5,44 are associated with the KEGG pathway of long-term synaptic enhancement. Slc24a2, Kcnb2, and Hcn4 are potassium transporters or channels, whereas Chrnb3 and Gria2 are related to excitatory extracellular-ligand-gated ion channel activity in KEGG pathway. Fgf12 deficiency elevates hearing threshold in mice.45 These findings suggest that progressive hearing loss in miR-96−/− mice is attributed to the mis-regulation of a genetic network encompassing numerous deafness genes, cell death genes, calcium binding proteins, neurotransmitter transporters, and ion channels due to deficiency of miR-96. This indicates that miR-96 plays a systemic regulatory role in hearing.

MiR-96 is specifically expressed in HCs and SGNs in inner ear. To evaluate its function in these cells, we employed conditional knockout approaches. Since SGNs derived from Shh-expressing cells and Atoh1 is only expressed in HCs, not in SGNs,46,47 we generated ShhCre/+; miR-96fl/fl mice and Atoh1Cre/+; miR-96fl/fl mice in which miR-96 was ablated in SGNs or HCs,47,48 respectively. Auditory assessments showed no significant difference in hearing between ShhCre/+; miR-96fl/fl mice and ShhCre/+ mice at both P28 and 6 months of age (Figure S7A, S7B, 4A, and 4B). SGN-specific miR-96 depletion did not affect SGN or HC survival in ShhCre/+; miR-96fl/fl mice (Figure 4C, 4D, and S7C–S7G). In contrast, miR-96 knockout in HCs severely compromised hearing ability in Atoh1Cre/+; miR-96fl/fl mouse at P28 (Figures 4E and 4F) and led to progressive loss of HCs and synapses (Figures 4G–4K and S8A–S8G). To investigate how miR-96 maintains hearing in adulthood, we conducted RNA sequencing analysis on cochleae from 6-month-old tamoxifen-treated UBCCreERT2/+ and UBCCreERT2/+; miR-96fl/fl mice, 4 weeks post-treatment. RNA expression profiling revealed 535 upregulated and 538 downregulated genes |log2 fold-change| > 0 and p value <0.05) in the cochlea following inducible knockout of miR-96 (Figures 5G; Table S4). Using the STRING database, we identified genetic regulatory networks for up- and downregulated genes (Figures S10F and S10G). Through subsequent literature searches and bioinformatics analysis, 17 genes in the up- and downregulated genetic regulatory networks attracted our interest: deafness genes Gjb3, Col11a1, and Pax1; hearing-loss-related genes Nos2 and Tnn (Table S5); neurotransmitter receptors Gabra2, Gabra4, Gabrb1, and Chrm4; transcription factors Bach2, Hnf4a, Foxa1, Elf3, Nfe2l1, and Neurod2; rhodopsin-kinase-related gene Grk1; and potassium channel Kcnj4. RT-qPCR confirmed significant upregulation of Gabra2, Gabra4, Bach2, and Grk1 (predicted miR-96 targets by TargetScan) and downregulation of Tnn, Col11a1, Gjb3, and Hnf4a in cochlea of tamoxifen-treated UBCCreERT2/+; miR-96fl/fl mice compared to tamoxifen-treated UBCCreERT2/+ mice. Additionally, trends were observed in the expression of Kcnj4, Chrm4, Nos2, Neurod2, Pax1, Elf3, and Foxa1 (Figures 5H and 5I). These data suggest that acute miR-96 depletion in adult mice de-represses transcription factors and neurotransmitter receptors, subsequently affecting the expression of deafness and hearing-loss-related genes, ultimately leading to hearing loss.

Given that miR-96 expression in the inner ear increases from P4 to P28 and its deficiency in HCs counts for the progressive hearing loss in miR-96−/− mice, we speculated that viral overexpression of miR-96 may restore hearing. To test this hypothesis, we constructed an AAV8-CMV-miR-96-GFP virus (hereafter called AAV8-miR-96) and injected it into the inner ear via scala media of P1 WT pups. The results of a serial validation experiments showed that GFP/miR-96 was robustly expressed in HCs (both IHCs and OHCs) and SGNs with high efficiency in mice at 28 days post-injection (Figures S11A and S11B). RT-qPCR showed that miR-96-5p and miR-96-3p levels in the cochlea of AAV8-miR-96-injected WT mice increased 4–6 times compared to naive and AAV8-CMV-GFP-injected WT mice at 28 days post-injection (Figure S11C). Moreover, the hearing of WT mice injected with AAV8-miR-96 were unaffected compared to untreated control WT mice (Figure S11D). These data indicate that AAV8-miR-96 effectively overexpressed miR-96 in HCs and SGNs, with no observable side effects.

Consequently, we injected the AAV8-miR-96 virus into inner ear of P1 miR-96−/− pups and observed a significantly reduced ABR threshold in treated mice compared to untreated miR-96−/− mice at P28 (Figure 6A). Moreover, the restorative effects were maintained in 50% and 38% of AAV8-miR-96-treated miR-96−/− mice at 2 and 3 months of age, respectively, in two independent experiments (Figures 6B and 6C). Additionally, ABR waveforms were also significantly improved in miR-96−/− mice at 28 days and 3 months post-injection (Figures 6D and 6E). Immunostaining showed that AAV8-miR-96-treated miR-96−/− mice had significantly more HCs and IHC synapses than untreated miR-96−/− mice at P28 and 3 months of age (Figures 6F, 6G, S12A–S12E, and S13A–S13D). By 3 months of age, untreated miR-96−/− mice had lost many OHCs, with the remaining OHCs displaying shrunken and fused stereocilia. Given that miR-96 regulates a genetic network encompassing genes with diverse functions to maintain hearing in adult mice, it is conceivable that miR-96 may also play a vital role in protecting against environment-induced hearing loss, such as NIHL. To explore this hypothesis, we first investigated miR-96 expression in noise-exposed mice. Noise exposure at intensities of 90 dB, 96 dB, and 100 dB, within the frequency range of 2–20 kHz, for a duration of 2 h, significantly downregulated miR-96-5p and miR-96-3p expression in the cochlea of WT mice, as determined 12 h after noise exposure (Figure 7A). As expected, the expression of miR-183 and miR-182 were also downregulated in response to noise exposure (Figure S15A). We next assessed the expression of genes altered in cochlea of miR-96−/− mice or UBCCreERT2/+; miR-96fl/fl mice, finding that the expression levels of 7 of 25 examined genes (Bcl2l1, Slc26a9, Cyp1a1, Gabrb1, Bach2, Grk1, and Nos2) were significantly changed in WT mice after exposure to 100 dB noise (Figures 7B and S15B). Many of the upregulated genes are targets of miR-96-5p and/or miR-96-3p predicted by TargetScan (Figure S10F). To confirm their direct regulation by miR-96-5p and/or miR-96-3p, we selected representative genes (Slc12a5, Gabrb1, Gabra4, and Bach2) for dual luciferase reporter assay. The results demonstrated that Slc12a5, which contains a conserved binding site exclusively for miR-96-5p, was directly repressed by miR-96-5p via the binding site on its 3′ UTR. Gabrb1 was repressed by both miR-96-5p and/or miR-96-3p via the conserved binding sites on its 3′ UTR. Gabra4, which harbors a binding site for miR-96-3p, but none for miR-96-5p, was repressed by miR-96-3p via its conserved binding site on its 3′ UTR. Bach2 was repressed by miR-96-5p via the conserved binding sites on its 3′ UTR (Figures 7G–7K). BACH2 can function as either a transcriptional repressor or a activator, depending on the binding site orientation and sequence on the downstream gene promoters, thereby regulating numerous environmentally responsive genes.50 So the upregulation of Bach2 may have contributed to the repression of downregulated gene in miR-96 CKO mice. Through bioinformatics analysis (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/AnimalTFDB4/#/TFBS_Predict and https://www.genecards.org/), we identified that there are putative binding sites for BACH2 on the promoter of Hnf4a, which was downregulated in miR-96 CKO. Additionally, we found HNF4A-binding sites on the promoter of Gjb3, Tnn, and other downregulated genes. Dual luciferase reporter assay further confirmed BACH2 represses Hnf4a expression, whereas HNF4A activates Gjb3 and Tnn expression (Figures 7L–7N). These findings suggest that miR-96-5p and miR-96-3p target genes directly via binding sites on target genes’ 3′ UTR and indirectly through transcription factors Bach2 and Hnf4a.

Genetic factors account for up to 60% of early-onset hearing loss,2,3 with over 500 genes associated with auditory functions.4 Our study reveals that miR-96 deficiency triggers progressive hearing loss in mice, reaching profound impairment by 3 months of age. This phenotype arises from a mis-regulation of multiple genetic regulatory networks, encompassing genes related to deafness, apoptosis, calcium signaling, glutamate transport, neuroprotection, and ion channels (Figure S16). Importantly, viral-mediated miR-96 reintroduction partially restored hearing of miR-96−/− mice, supporting miR-96’s therapeutic potential in mitigating genetic hearing impairments (Figure S16).

MiR-183, miR-96, and miR-182 are a cluster locating together closely in chromosome and co-expressed in mouse cochlea from embryonic stage to adult stage17,19,20 (Figures 1A and 1B). The miR-96−/− mice retain residual hearing till P28 (Figures 1D–1I), which is less severe than the hearing deficits in homozygous miR-183-96-182 knockout mice and miR-183-96 knockout mice, the latter exhibiting profound hearing loss at birth.22,23,24 The miR-183-96-182 knockout mice and miR-183-96 knockout mice also have more severe defects in HC morphogenesis than miR-96−/− mice.22,23,24 These together suggests that miR-183 and miR-182 also play roles in development of hearing, in addition to miR-96. Indeed, miR-182−/− mice exhibited only a mild hearing loss starting at higher frequencies and progressing with age to lower frequencies.24 So further investigation of miR-183 knockout mice is needed to decipher the contribution of miR-183 in auditory system.

Our transcriptomic analysis identified 33 deafness-associated genes with over 1.5-fold changes in expression in miR-96-deficient inner ear tissue, with further validation showing significant downregulation of key deafness genes, Kcnq4 and Ptprq. Given miRNAs’ role in post-transcriptional regulation, miR-96 likely influences additional hearing-related genes beyond those identified, indicating its status as a top-level regulator in auditory network modulation.

Interestingly, although miR-96−/− mice retain HCs at P28, mutants with miR-96 gain-of-function mutations (Dmdo/Dmdo, Mir96+14C>A homozygotes and Mir96+13G>A homozygotes) exhibit rapid HC degeneration and hearing loss.27,29 This difference highlights that the gain-of-function mutation in miR-96 may introduce novel regulatory interactions, whereas miR-96−/− mice show a purer loss-of-function phenotype. Lewis et al.24 found only six shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the four mutants (Mir96+13G>A, Mir96+14C>A, Mir96Dmdo, and Mir183/96dko).24,29,51 The more limited overlap in DEGs among these four mutants at P4 and miR-96−/− mice at P28 may be due to differences in developmental timing, HC survival, and network compensation, as well as genetic background variations and sample size limitations.

AAV8-miR-96-mediated rescue was moderate, likely due to the complex regulatory role miR-96 plays across multiple deafness-related genes and due to time point of miR-96 expression from virus. Given that miR-96 expression in mouse inner ear starts from E10.5, and the gene delivery and expression by AAV typically requires about 7 days, it is conceivable that earlier delivery of miR-96 into HCs could obtain favorable rescue effects.

A broad spectrum of genetic and environmental factors is involved in ARHL and NIHL, often mediated by genetic regulatory networks associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, calcium overload, glutamate excitotoxicity, and energy metabolism.7,52,53 Therefore, a systemic regulatory approach is essential for any drug aiming to wide-scale efficacy across populations with diverse genetic backgrounds and environmental exposures. AAV-mediated gene therapy targeting a single gene causing hearing loss has shown success in animal models of hearing loss,54,55 which suggests that gene therapy could also be used for targeting multiple genes involved in ARHL and NIHL, provided a pivotal target is identified. Our data showed that miR-96 regulates multiple genetic regulatory networks encompassing oxidative stress, neurotransmitter receptors and transporters, metabolism, and deafness-related genes in inner ear, protecting hearing against age-related and noise-induced changes (Figure S16). Besides direct target repression, miR-96 regulates additional genes indirectly through transcription factors, such as Bach2 and Hnf4a. BACH2 also plays an important role in mediating oxidative stress, innate immunity, and the adaptive immune response.56,57 Our data showed that Bach2 represses the expression of Hnf4a; the latter can activate hearing-loss-related genes, such like Gjb3 and Tnn.

These findings highlight miR-96’s role as a master regulator of auditory networks, bridging environmental resilience with genetic stability. By controlling gene networks critical for auditory maintenance and resilience to noise exposure, miR-96 presents a promising therapeutic target for treating early-onset, age-related, and noise-induced hearing loss.

The function of miR-96 has been only examined in mouse model in this study. Whether overexpression of miR-96 can protect hearing against noise-induced damage need to be tested in clinic.

Decalcified was performed using 0.5 M EDTA solution, with the duration of decalcify adjusted based on the age of the mice. Further dissection was applied to expose the sensory epithelium. Samples were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M PB for 2 h, then washed twice with PB solution. Dehydration was carried out through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%) for 10 min each at 4°C. The samples were then dried by a critical point drying instrument and were coated with 10–12 nm gold using Quorum Q150T ES. The SEM images were collected by Zeiss GeminiSEM 300.

P28 WT C57BL/6J mice with or without injection of AAV virus were exposed to noise in a small wire cage in a soundproof room. The mice were exposed to a sound intensity of 90 dB or 96 dB or 100 dB for 2 h within the frequency range of 2–20 kHz. A maximum of 6 awake mice could be placed in the cage for each noise exposure experiment. The sound used for overexposure was emitted by a TDT System3 RZ6 Processor (Tucker DAVIS Technologies, Alachua, FL USA), filtered, amplified by a Crown power amplifier, and delivered to a loudspeaker securely fixed to a hole in the top of the cage. Sound levels were adjusted with a condenser microphone (PCB PIEZOTRONICS, 480C02), variations within the cage were less than 1 dB SPL. After 12 h, or 3 days, or 7days, or 14 days of noise exposure, mice were anesthetized for auditory testing or for dissecting cochleae samples for subsequent RT-qPCR experiment.
