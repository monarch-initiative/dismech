# Esophageal Atresia, an Anomaly of VACTERL Association or Novel Feature of the FGF10 Gene: A Case Report.
**Authors:** Escalante-Reyes M, García-Payá E, Re VA, Sirera PS, Navarro de Miguel M, Martínez RS
**Journal:** Mol Syndromol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000549201](https://doi.org/10.1159/000549201)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: VACTERL association (VA) is defined as the nonrandom co-occurrence of at least three of the following six features: Vertebral anomalies (V), Anal atresia (A), Cardiac defects (C), Tracheo-esophageal fistula (TE), Renal defects (R), and Limb anomalies (L). The genetic basis of VA remains undiscovered.

CASE PRESENTATION: In this study, we report a 22-year-old male patient suspected of VA at birth (TE: esophageal atresia (EA), C: dextrocardia, without heterotaxy, and L: hypoplasia of thumb phalanges). Additionally, the patient presented lacrimal gland aplasia, xerostomia, and pulmonary hypoplasia (PH). Whole exome sequencing identified a novel loss-of-function FGF10 variant: c.2T>C; p.(Met1Thr). Pathogenic variants in the FGF10 gene are known causes of lacrimo-auriculo-dento-digital syndrome 3 or aplasia of lacrimal and salivary glands, but their association has been scarcely described in the literature. This FGF10 variant was also detected in other family members exhibiting a wide range of clinical variability; however, PH and EA were observed only in our index case.

CONCLUSION: This report supports the involvement of the FGF10 gene in EA and PH, and expands the phenotypic spectrum of pathogenic FGF10 variants. We hypothesize that while FGF10 contributes to the development of PH or VACTERL association (VA), a yet unidentified second hit is likely necessary.

Pathogenic variants in the FGF10 gene lead to two disorders with vast clinical variability and incomplete penetrance: lacrimo‐auriculo‐dento‐digital syndrome 3 (LADD3) and aplasia of lacrimal and salivary glands (ALSG).

There is an emerging association between FGF10 pathogenic variants and lung disease.

The genetic basis of VACTERL association remains unknown today.

We report the first documented human case report of coexisting esophageal atresia, an anomaly of VACTERL association, and a pathogenic FGF10 mutation.

This case broadens the mutational spectrum of FGF10 variants and reinforces their involvement in lung disease.

Fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10) is essential during cell proliferation and multi-organ development. The exact role of the FGF10 gene and the mechanisms underlying the associated phenotypic heterogeneity remain incompletely understood [1].

Deleterious gene variants in the FGF10 gene lead to two disorders that share some clinical traits: lacrimo‐auriculo‐dento‐digital syndrome 3 (LADD3, #620193) and aplasia of lacrimal and salivary glands (ALSG, #180920), both with autosomal dominant inheritance. LADD3 syndrome is a congenital anomaly disorder characterized by aplasia, atresia or hypoplasia of the lacrimal and salivary systems, cup-shaped ears, hearing loss, and dental and digital anomalies. ALSG is a rare disease characterized by irritable eyes, epiphora, xerostomia, dental caries, periodontal disease, and aplasia or hypoplasia of lacrimal glands. Patients with LADD3 present a more severe phenotype than those with ALSG [2–5]. Both disorders exhibit substantial clinical variability, even within the same families, and reduced penetrance has been reported [6, 7].

FGF10 plays a well-established function in lung branching morphogenesis [8]. Several studies have associated pathogenic variants in FGF10 (chromosome 5p12) with various lung disorders: (i) the FGF10 deletion variant in a family with pulmonary acinar hypoplasia and LADD3 syndrome [9]; (ii) several cases of compound inheritance involving the FGF10/TBX4 genes in lethal lung development [10]; (iii) patient cohort reported by Entesarian et al. [11] with ALSG and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [12]; (iv) four children affected by severe interstitial lung disease during childhood with or without evidence for LADD3/ALSG [13].

Another condition that requires further research related to the FGF10 gene is the association of esophageal atresia (EA) or duodenal atresia (DA). Several studies with FGF10 knockout animal models suggest an association between the FGF10 gene and EA/DA [14–17]; however, to date, no cases of gastrointestinal tract atresia have been reported in humans. VACTERL association (VA) is defined as the nonrandom co-occurrence of at least three of the following six features: Vertebral anomalies (V), Anal atresia (A), Cardiac defects (C), Tracheo-esophageal fistula (TE), Renal defects (R), and Limb anomalies (L). The genetic basis of VA remains unknown today, but several genetic associations have been reported, including the involvement of the FGF8-FGF10 pathway [18].

We report three affected individuals from a two-generation family. Their clinical phenotypes included FGF10-related manifestations in all three cases and lung disease and EA in one individual, all harboring a novel mutation in the FGF10 gene. This represents the first documented human case of EA associated with a pathogenic FGF10 mutation. Our data expand the phenotypic spectrum linked to FGF10 variants and reinforce its emerging role in lung disease or EA/DA.

We present the case of a patient born to non-consanguineous parents after 36 gestational weeks by cesarean section due to suspected polymalformation syndrome: EA and polyhydramnios. At birth, he weighed 2,250 g (P10–P25) and required mechanical ventilation for 5 days due to grade II–III hyaline membrane disease. He also presented dextrocardia without evidence of heterotaxy or other situs ambiguus anomalies, along with hypoplasia of thumb phalanges, cone-shaped epiphyses of the phalanges, and hemangioma on the right arm. VA was suspected based on the presence of TE: esophageal atresia, C: dextrocardia, and L: hypoplasia of the thumb phalanges. He underwent EA surgery twice shortly after birth, once at 4 years and again at 18 years. At the age of 4 years, he had his first episode of pneumonia associated with asthma. From then to the present day (22 years old), he has experienced recurrent respiratory infections and repeated hospitalizations (1 or 2 per year) for bilateral pneumonia. At present, he has severe lung restriction with left pulmonary hypoplasia (PH) and left diaphragmatic paralysis and requires supplemental oxygen at night. At the age of 5 years, he was consulted for multiple dental caries, generalized periodontitis and xerostomia. By the age of 6 years, he was diagnosed with punctate keratitis, dry conjunctival mucosae, and lacrimal gland aplasia. At 9 years of age, he presented with scoliosis and Madelung’s deformity. During the last EA surgery (at 18 years), he developed ptosis of the eyelid, which resolved within a few weeks. Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed on the patient at 22 years with suspected polymalformative syndrome, revealing a novel loss-of-function variant in the FGF10 gene.

A family pedigree was conducted to evaluate segregation of the variant within the family (shown in Fig. 1). His father exhibited epiphora, xerostomia, dental caries, periodontal disease, hypoplasia of lacrimal glands, and hearing loss. His brother presented xerostomia and lacrimal gland hypoplasia. No other family members showed lung involvement or EA.

DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood using the MagNA Pure Compact System (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Germany). WES was performed using the SureSelect XT Human All Exon V8 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 500/550 instrument (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data were aligned and mapped to the human genome reference sequence (hg38) using the Alissa Reporter software (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The interpretation of sequence variants was done in agreement with the standards and guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [19]. The FGF10 gene variant was validated by Sanger sequencing using the ABI 3500 platform (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA). The patient gave his informed consent before the studies began.

A novel heterozygous mutation in the FGF10 gene (NM_004465.2) c.2T>C; p.(Met1Thr) was identified by WES (shown in Fig. 2) and subsequently confirmed by Sanger sequencing (shown in Fig. 3). This FGF10 variant was classified as a “pathogenic mutation” according to ACMG guidelines (PVS1, strong, null variant in a gene where loss of function is a known mechanism of disease; PM2: supporting, no observation of this variant in the gnomAD 4.0 version database; PS1, strong, alternative variant chr5:44388682 T>C classified as a likely pathogenic variant in the ClinVar database – variation ID: 547371; PP1, cosegregation with disease in three affected family members, supporting). No loss-of-function variants in the FGF10 gene have been identified in large-scale control population databases, such as gnomAD, indicating their rarity and potential pathogenicity. The identified FGF10 variant is deposited in the Leiden Open Variation Database (#00450521, LOVD, https://www.lovd.nl/). The father and his brother were heterozygous for the same FGF10 variant. We report a family exhibiting FGF10-related features harboring a novel pathogenic variant in the FGF10 gene. This case expands the phenotypic spectrum associated with FGF10 variants and reinforces its role in lung disease. Notably, this is the first documented human case of coexisting EA and a pathogenic FGF10 mutation.

LADD3 syndrome and ALSG are characterized by considerable clinical variability, though LADD3 syndrome tends to manifest more severely. The diverse clinical presentations within this family challenge clear phenotype-genotype correlation: the father fits the LADD3 profile, the brother corresponds to ALSG, and the index case exhibits LADD3 features along with pulmonary disease and EA. Several studies suggest that LADD3 syndrome and ALSG represent a continuum within the same phenotypic spectrum: (i) Milunsky et al. [2] identified a FGF10 variant in a mother diagnosed with ALSG and her daughter with LADD3 syndrome; (ii) Zhang et al. [7] described a family affected with LADD3 syndrome exhibiting extensive phenotypic variability alongside two unaffected family members; (iii) Freund et al. [6] reported six generations of ALSG-affected individuals with epiphora in addition to a variable xerostomia with one asymptomatic carrier.

Our reported case presented severe lung restriction with PH. Further studies support an association between the FGF10 gene and lung disease and animal studies corroborate the clinical role of FGF10 during lung development [15]. Karolak et al. [10] described two unrelated families with patients presenting severe PH who had inherited the variant from parents with LADD3 syndrome but no evidence of lung disease. Similarly, Wade et al. [9] reported a case of PH associated with heterozygous FGF10 deletion inherited from a mother with LADD3 syndrome. These findings suggest that FGF10 pathogenic mutation alone may be insufficient to cause lung disease and that additional, as yet unidentified, genetic modifiers may be required.

Four T-box genes (TBX2, TBX3, TBX4, TBX5) have been implicated in lung development [21]. Karolak et al. [10] identified TBX4 variants as another hypothetical contributor to PH and reported additional variants in genes known to play roles in lung development (TCF21, BTBD7, DSPP, BCLAF1). WES in our patient did not reveal any rare variant (population frequency gnomAD Exomes Version: 4.1 <1%) in these mentioned genes.

The presence of EA, dextrocardia, and hypoplasia of the thumb phalanges in our patient raised the suspicion of VA. Although an association between the FGF10 gene and gastrointestinal malformations has been demonstrated in animal models, no cases of gastrointestinal tract atresia have been reported in humans to date. Krüger et al. [20] found no relevant FGF10 variants in 20 patients with VA, which corroborates that FGF10 gene is unlikely to play a role in digestive tract malformations.

Our case involves a patient with an inherited pathogenic FGF10 variant presenting with LADD3 syndrome, pulmonary disease, and EA. While the association of the pathogenic FGF10 variant (c.2T>C) with LADD3 syndrome is well established, its relationship with PH and EA remains unclear. Additional genetic factors may contribute to the development of EA and pulmonary disease, including variants in other genes within the FGF10 signaling pathway, variations at other genetic loci, or currently unidentified regulatory or noncoding regions. Environmental influences affecting phenotypic expression should also be considered. Further investigation of the FGF10 gene is essential to advance understanding of its role in lung development and disease.

To conclude, our report expands the phenotypic spectrum identified in individuals with FGF10 gene variants and describes the first documented human case of EA linked to pathogenic FGF10 mutation. Our findings support a potential association between FGF10 gene and pulmonary disease or digestive tract malformations; however, it remains uncertain whether these manifestations fall within the FGF10-related spectrum. Additional studies are required to clarify the mechanisms underlying incomplete penetrance, the marked phenotypic variability observed, and the exact role of the FGF10 gene in other conditions such as PH and EA.
