Abstract
Sickle cell disease (SCD) is an inherited hematologic disease caused by sickle hemoglobin as the predominant RBC hemo -
globin or by sickle hemoglobin in combination with other abnormal β-hemoglobin variants like HbC, HbD and others. 
Sickling of erythrocytes under deoxygenated conditions is the basis of inflammatory and thrombotic cascades which result 
in multiple serious complications, leading to early morbidity and mortality. While HLA-matched allogeneic bone mar -
row transplantation is potentially curative, it has considerable limitations due to potential severe toxicities. Despite slow 
progress towards novel therapeutic strategies for SCD and hydroxyurea being the sole medication that is shown to reduce 
vaso-occlusive events and mortality for almost 20 years, several pharmacological agents targeting different mechanisms 
have been examined in clinical trials and recently US- US-FDA-approved, including L-glutamine and crizanlizumab. 
V oxelotor was previously US-FDA-approved but has been voluntarily withdrawn from the market as the overall benefit did 
not outweigh the risks. Gene therapies based on CRISPR-Cas9 and lentiviral vectors have been very recently approved. 
However, excessive costs are a barrier to widespread use. Nonetheless, there is still a large area of unmet needs for patients 
with SCD, and further research towards better care is warranted.

Hydroxyurea
Hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide) has been used to tackle 
sickle cell disease (SCD) for the past four decades. It has 
emerged as a promising modality and the primary disease-
modifying agent. Hydroxyurea’s predominant and well-
established mechanism of action is the reversible inhibition 
of ribonucleotide reductase, an enzyme essential for synthe-
sizing and repairing DNA [24]. Inhibition of ribonucleotide 
reductase halts the progression of cellular division. Through 
once-daily dosing, intermittent suppression of erythroid pro-
genitors and cell stress signaling takes place, which favors 
the recruitment of erythroid progenitors with increased lev-
els of HbF [ 25, 26]. However, the effects of Hydroxyurea 
are not limited to HbF induction [27].
The proof-of-principle studies led to a key open-label 
Phase I/II trial in 32 adult patients suffering from SCD that 
confirmed the increase in HbF levels (mean: 11.2% ±5.2%) 
without significant bone marrow suppression [28]. This was 
followed by the landmark Phase III randomized multicenter 
study of hydroxyurea in sickle cell anemia (MSH), compris-
ing 299 adult SCD patients (patients with B0/B+ thalassemia 
genotype were excluded) with a history of greater than three 
painful crises per year. This prospective study demonstrated 
a highly significant reduction in the annual rates of painful 
crises in the patients treated with hydroxyurea compared to 
the placebo group (2.5 vs. 4.5 painful crises/year, P < 0.001) 
and thus was terminated early [ 17]. In addition, the MSH 
trial also highlighted the reduced frequency of ACS, blood 
transfusions and hospitalizations in hydroxyurea recipients. 
A 17.5-year follow-up study of the MSH trial participants 
suggested that long-term hydroxyurea was associated with 
improved overall survival without any concerns for adverse 
events caused by increased drug exposure [29].
The compelling results obtained from the studies in adult 
patients stimulated prospective studies to be performed in 
the pediatric population. Foremost, two open-label studies 
described the short-term efficacy of Hydroxyurea in chil -
dren with severe SCD [ 30, 31]. Subsequently, the HUG-
KIDS study, a phase I/II trial, denoted Hydroxyurea’s 
toxicity profile as mild, transient, and reversible, with simi-
lar laboratory changes observed in the adult population [32]. 
HUSOFT trial, a multicenter open-label 2-year pilot study, 
reported good tolerability of Hydroxyurea in infants without 
any short-term adverse events and potentially delayed func-
tional asplenia [ 33]. These pilot studies paved the path for 
the multicentric BABY HUG study, a phase III randomized 
control trial. The study included infants aged 9 to 18 months 
with no inclusion criteria for clinical severity. Although 
the primary endpoints of preventing splenic and renal dys -
function were not met, infants treated with hydroxyurea 
significantly reduced the frequency of dactylitis and other 
vaso-occlusive events with fewer blood transfusions and 
hospitalizations. These clinical effects were manifested at 
low doses of hydroxyurea (20 mg/kg/day), unlike previous 
studies that demonstrated clinical and laboratory improve -
ment at the maximum tolerated dose [ 34]. Long-term use 
of Hydroxyurea in children with SCD has been associated 
with sustained hematologic benefits, improved growth, pre-
served organ function, and decreased mortality [ 35–37]. 
Hydroxyurea has also been studied in the prevention of 
cerebrovascular disease. The results from a single-insti -
tution pilot study supported hydroxyurea and phlebotomy 
as a practical approach for secondary stroke prevention 
and managing transfusion-associated iron overload [ 38]. It 
failed to elicit the desired results in the SWiTCH trial, a 
multicentric phase III study [39]. However, the results from 
the TWiTCH trial, a multicenter phase III randomized trial, 
suggested that hydroxyurea can be a substitute for chronic 
transfusions in pediatric patients with abnormal transcranial 
Doppler velocities for preventing primary stroke [40].
Despite the consistent evidence regarding its efficacy and 
safety, hydroxyurea remains underutilized in SCD. Its usage 
is limited to high-resource settings with inadequate utility in 
Sub-Saharan Africa where the disease burden is the greatest.

The SUSTAIN trial was a double-blind, randomized, con-
trolled phase II trial that evaluated the efficacy and safety of 
crizanlizumab for the first time in SCD [ 51]. 198 patients 
aged 16–65 years with SCD genotypes homozygous hemo-
globin S (HbSS), sickle hemoglobin C (HbSC) and sickle-
thalassemia (HbSB0/ HbSB+) with 2 or more vaso-occlusive 
events in 1 year before enrolment, were randomly assigned 
to receive placebo, low dose crizanlizumab (2.5 mg/kg) 
or high dose crizanlizumab (5 mg/kg). The annual rate of 
VOCs was 2.01 per year in the low-dose crizanlizumab 
group (p = 0.18) and 1.63 per year in the high-dose crizan -
lizumab group ( p = 0.01) compared to 2.98 in placebo [ 51, 
52]. This represented around a 45% reduction in the high-
dose group versus placebo. The percentage of patients who 
had no VOCs was almost the same in the placebo (17%) and 
low-dose treatment group (18%), but it was nearly doubled 
in the high-dose group (36%) [51, 52]. The group receiving 
high-dose crizanlizumab had lesser rates of annual VOCs 
compared to the placebo group, irrespective of hydroxyurea 
use, prior history of VOCs, or genotype. All groups had sim-
ilar patients with serious adverse events (AEs). Side effects 
were more common in the high-dose crizanlizumab group 
compared to placebo [51, 52].
A post hoc analysis of the SUSTAIN showed that a larger 
portion of patients receiving crizanlizumab 5 mg/kg had no 
hospitalizations during the study period (46% vs. 35%), and 
the time to first hospitalization was increased compared to 
placebo (6.3 vs. 3.2 months) [ 52]. Another post hoc sub -
group analysis of the original SUSTAIN trial evaluated the 
effect of high-dose crizanlizumab (5 mg/kg) vs. placebo 
on specific secondary efficacy endpoints and safety [ 52, 
53]. The subgroups were classified according to the VOC 
frequency in the past year (< 4 vs. >=5), genotype (HbSS 
vs. non-HbSS), and hydroxyurea use. More patients in the 
crizanlizumab group were VOC event-free compared with 
placebo (35.8% vs. 16.9%, respectively) irrespective of 
subgroups [52, 53]. The time to first and second VOC was 
increased using crizanlizumab most significantly in patients 
not on hydroxyurea (5.68 vs. 2.86 months) and the HbSS 
genotype (4.07 vs. 1.12 months). However, it was noted in 
all other subgroups [ 52, 53]. The annual rate of uncompli -
cated VOCs was lower in the crizanlizumab versus placebo 
arms across all subgroups. Treatment-related AEs were 
comparable in all the subgroups [52, 53].
The SUCCESSOR trial retrospectively assessed the sub-
sequent occurrence of VOCs in patients who completed the 
SCD from an early age, as it could lead to an increased inci-
dence of reduced fertility among males prior to the onset of 
puberty. In low-income countries, the overall advantages, 
notably the decrease in vaso-occlusive events and mortality 
rates, may outweigh this risk [44]. Further research is essen-
tial to provide clarity on this issue.
L-glutamine
L-glutamine is a naturally occurring amino acid, required 
for the synthesis of the pyridine nucleotides [45, 46]. It was 
approved by the FDA in July 2017 for SCD patients 5 years 
and older to reduce the frequency of vaso-occlusive crises 
[46]. The mechanism of action of L-Glutamine in sickle 
cell disease is not entirely understood, but it’s believed to 
be related to the antioxidant effect [ 45, 46]. The NADH 
(redox) ratio in sickle red blood cells (RBCs) is lower than 
in normal RBCs. L-glutamine increases the availability of 
reduced glutathione in sickle red blood cells and subse -
quently increases NAD redox [45, 46]. In a multicentre, ran-
domized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, phase 3 trial, the 
efficacy of L-glutamine (0.3 g per kilogram of body weight 
per dose) administered twice daily by mouth, was compared 
with placebo among patients with SCD (HbSS or HbSB 0). 
It showed that patients in the L-glutamine group had fewer 
pain crises and hospitalizations than those in the placebo 
group [ 47]. It showed lesser ACS events (8.6%) in the 
L-Glutamine group versus placebo (23.1%) [47]. However, 
the study didn’t improve hemolysis, anemia, or retic count 
[47]. The study showed more adverse events like nausea and 
arm and leg pain in the L-glutamine group, but there was no 
increase in the overall adverse events [ 47]. The Commit -
tee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) of the 
European Medicines Agency (EMA) has issued a recom -
mendation against the approval of L-glutamine for treating 
sickle cell disease. The primary concern of the CHMP was 
that the principal study did not demonstrate the efficacy of 
L-glutamine in reducing the frequency of sickle cell crises 
or the need for hospitalizations. A large number of patients, 
more who were taking L-glutamine than taking placebo 
(36.2% vs. 24.4%), dropped out of the study before it was 
finished, and information on how the medicine worked for 
those patients was not available.

Osivelotor
Osivelotor (GBT021601) is a second-generation HbS 
polymerization inhibitor, with higher potency than voxelotor 
[67]. Two phase 1 studies (NCT05036512, NCT04983264) 
showed no serious treatment-related events after drug 
administration, with improvements in Hb level, hemolysis 
markers, and erythrocyte morphology [68–70]. A phase 2/3, 
randomized study (GBT021601-021; NCT05431088) is 
ongoing to explore this drug further [71].

Riociguat
Chronic thromboembolic disease (CTED) and pulmonary 
hypertension (PH) are both common in sickle cell disease 
[82]. The nitric oxide (NO) signaling pathway is disrupted 
in sickle cell disease, which leads to pulmonary artery endo-
thelial dysfunction and smooth muscle proliferation [ 82]. 
No disease-modifying drugs have been shown to decrease 
the risk of PH.
Riociguat potentiates guanylate cyclase enzyme, which 
produces cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) upon 
activation by NO. cGMP functions as a smooth muscle 
relaxant and thus causes vaso-dilation. This may reduce pul-
monary arterial pressures and right heart strain and improve 
exercise tolerance. A case series of six SCD patients with 
PH or CTED who were administered riociguat reported sig-
nificant improvements in the six-minute walk test, World 
Health Organization functional class scale, right ventricular 
systolic pressure, and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic pep -
tide (NT-proBNP) levels in 3 out of 6 patients [83]. Further-
more, riociguat was well tolerated in 4 out of 6 patients [83]. 
A randomized, double-blind phase 2 trial (NCT02633397) 
is ongoing to validate these findings further.
Vamifeport
Vamifeport (VIT-2763) is a small-molecule inhibitor of 
ferroportin. It was initially developed for thalassemia to 
modulate iron homeostasis in erythroid precursors, which 
boosts cell differentiation rather than proliferation and lim -
its ineffective erythropoiesis [ 84]. In SCD, iron deficiency 
is known to decrease mean corpuscular HbS concentra -
tion, intravascular hemolysis, and pain crisis. A preclinical 
mouse model has shown reduced erythrocyte-endothelial 
adhesion and enhanced intravascular hemodynamics [ 85]. 
A phase 2a, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled 
trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety profile of Vamifeport 
is ongoing (NCT04817670).

Selectin inhibitors
Inclacumab
Inclacumab is a recombinant monoclonal antibody directed 
against P-selectin glycoprotein. Inclacumab, by binding to 
P-selectin and preventing its ligand binding activity, inter -
feres with the adhesion of sickle red blood cells, leucocytes, 
and platelets to the vascular endothelium, hence reducing 
the incidence of vaso-occlusive crises (VOCs) [ 92]. In the 
phase I study, Inclacumab was well tolerated in healthy 
adult patients [ 93]. In the THRIVE-131 (NCT04935879) 
trial, a randomized, double-blind, multicenter trial, patients 
aged ≥ 12 years experiencing 2 to 10 VOCs in the previ -
ous 12 months were randomized 1:1 to receive either Inc -
lacumab or placebo every 12 weeks for 48 weeks. The 
study’s primary endpoint is to assess the rate of VOCs, and 
the results are eagerly awaited.
Other selectin inhibitors
Since adhesion molecules such as P-selectin, E-selectin, and 
L-selectin are strongly implicated in the pathophysiology of 
SCD, studies of other agents targeting this pathway were 
carried out. A phase 3 study (NCT02187003) of rivipan -
sel, a pan-selectin inhibitor, failed to meet the primary and 
secondary endpoints [94]. Sevuparin is a modified form of 
heparin that inhibits both P and L selectin. However, a sig -
nificant reduction in VOCs was not shown in a phase II trial 
(NCT02515838) [95]. The failure of these drugs may be due 
to a lack of substantial inhibition of cell adhesion during 
acute VOC episodes.

Hemopexin
The breakdown of sickle cell red blood cells releases heme, 
which activates inflammatory proteins that result in vaso-
occlusion. In a mouse study, the injection of free hemoglobin 
led to vascular stasis in about 40% of subcutaneous blood 
vessels. Administering intravenous hemopexin decreased 
this stasis in a dose-dependent manner and proved effec -
tive against stasis caused by hypoxia-reoxygenation [ 86]. 
Repeated intravenous administration of hemopexin was 
well tolerated in rats and primates without adverse effects 
[86]. A phase 1 trial of CSL889 (hemopexin) showed excel-
lent safety and tolerability when administered in patients 
with stable SCD and patients with VOC [87].
Tocilizumab
ACS is not fully understood, and no definitive treatments are 
available. Elevated interleukin-6 (IL-6) plays a role in the 
inflammation associated with ACS [ 88]. Tocilizumab tar -
gets the IL-6 receptors and reduces inflammation. A Phase 
IIA trial (NCT05640271) is currently assessing the effects 
of low-dose tocilizumab in adult and adolescent patients 
with SCD experiencing ACS. Tocilizumab has been effec -
tively used in two instances of hyperhemolysis syndrome 
(HHS) and merits further research [89].
rADAMTS13
Sickle cell patients often have high levels of significant von 
Willebrand factor (VWF) multimers, which can obstruct 
blood vessels and cause pain. rADAMTS13 may break 
down these multimers and improve blood flow. In a human-
ized mouse model of SCD, increased VWF activity and a 
reduced ADAMTS13 to VWF activity ratio were found. 
Administering recombinant ADAMTS13 to SCD mice sub-
jected to hypoxia/re-oxygenation stress reduced hemolysis 
and inflammation in the lungs and kidneys, helping to alle -
viate sickle cell-related organ damage [ 90]. This suggests 
that rADAMTS13 may be an effective treatment for acute 
SCD events. A phase 1 clinical trial (NCT0399760) is eval-
uating the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and phar -
macodynamics of rADAMTS13 in SCD patients, focusing 
on adverse events and immunogenicity [91].

Discussion
Vaso-occlusion of small vessels, as well as extravascular 
hemolysis, are implicated in SCD-related complications 
[99]. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation 
has curative potential for SCD. Still, its use is limited due 
to a lack of suitable donors, the prohibitive cost of treat -
ment and serious short- and long-term complications [ 13, 
100]. Autologous stem cell-based gene therapies utilizing 
the CRISPR-Cas9 system and lentiviral vectors have shown 
encouraging results and are recently approved. These thera-
pies have substantial cost and logistic hurdles that may pre-
clude widespread use, especially among underprivileged 
populations. Allogeneic red cell transfusion is also used to 
treat and prevent specific complications related to SCD such 
as cerebrovascular events. Still, it has its array of long-term 
complications like allo-immunization including hyperhe -
molysis, iron overload and ongoing shortage of blood prod-
ucts [101]. For the next decade or so, pharmacotherapeutic 
agents will continue to play a significant role in improving 
the outcomes for SCD patients.
Several investigational agents were poised to influence 
the management of SCD but did not show benefit in clinical 
trials and are no longer being studied further. Canakinumab 
(interleukin 1-beta blocker) did not meet the primary end -
point in a phase 2 study [ 102]. A phase 2 clinical trial 
showed that memantine was safe and led to a significantly 
improved quality of life, but it is no longer being evaluated 
further [103]. Ticagrelor is an anti-platelet agent that did not 
meet the primary endpoint of reduction in the number of 
VOCs in the phase 3 HESTIA trial [ 104]. Senicapoc was a 
highly selective Gardos channel blocker [ 105]. A phase 3 
randomized controlled trial, unfortunately, showed no sig -
nificant improvement in the rate of sickle cell pain crises 
and the study was terminated early [ 106]. However, sev -
eral other investigational agents we have delineated in this 
review currently hold promise for SCD and are being evalu-
ated further.
Hydroxyurea has shown a reduction in the incidence

transfusion [ 107]. Hydroxyurea prevents or reduces the 
progression of chronic organ damage like cerebrovascular
