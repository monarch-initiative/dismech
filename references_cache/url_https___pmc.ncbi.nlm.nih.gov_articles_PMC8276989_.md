</div></section></section><section><section><section><h2>ABSTRACT</h2>
<p>Intramembranous ossification, which consists of direct conversion of mesenchymal cells to osteoblasts, is a characteristic process in skull development. One crucial role of these osteoblasts is to secrete collagen-containing bone matrix. However, it remains unclear how the dynamics of collagen trafficking is regulated during skull development. Here, we reveal the regulatory mechanisms of ciliary and golgin proteins required for intramembranous ossification. During normal skull formation, osteoblasts residing on the osteogenic front actively secreted collagen. Mass spectrometry and proteomic analysis determined endogenous binding between ciliary protein IFT20 and golgin protein GMAP210 in these osteoblasts. As seen in <em>Ift20</em> mutant mice, disruption of neural crest-specific GMAP210 in mice caused osteopenia-like phenotypes due to dysfunctional collagen trafficking. Mice lacking both IFT20 and GMAP210 displayed more severe skull defects compared with either IFT20 or GMAP210 mutants. These results demonstrate that the molecular complex of IFT20 and GMAP210 is essential for the intramembranous ossification during skull development.</p>
<section><p><strong>KEY WORDS:</strong> Collagen, Cranial neural crest cells, Golgi microtubule associated protein 210, Intraflagellar transport 20, Skull, Mouse</p></section></section><section><hr/>
<p><strong>Summary:</strong> Synergistic molecular regulation of IFT20 and GMAP210 is required for intramembranous ossification, which has not been previously characterized in skull formation.</p></section><section><h2>INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p>The skull vault, one of the most important craniofacial bones, is formed through intramembranous ossification (<a>Chai and Maxson, 2006</a>; <a>Szabo-Rogers et al., 2010</a>; <a>Wilkie and Morriss-Kay, 2001</a>). In humans, intelligence and mental capacities highly depend on long-term expansion of the skull, which allows brain growth (<a>Morriss-Kay and Wilkie, 2005</a>). If the skull does not develop appropriately owing to variable ossification defects and/or premature cranial suture fusions, individuals will present diverse medical conditions such as hearing and vision loss, which require long-term care. Therefore, it is crucial to understand how craniofacial bones develop.</p>
<p>In mice, skull development (e.g. frontal bone) is initiated at embryonic day (E) 12.5 when osteogenic precursors derived from cranial neural crest (CNC) cells spread upward to the top of the skull (<a>Ishii et al., 2005</a>; <a>Noden and Trainor, 2005</a>). Experimental evidence shows that growth factor signaling, such as fibroblast growth factor (FGF) and bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling, controls osteogenic proliferation and differentiation (<a>Iseki et al., 1997</a>, <a>1999</a>; <a>Kim et al., 1998</a>; <a>Komatsu and Mishina, 2016</a>; <a>Komatsu et al., 2013</a>; <a>Rice et al., 2000</a>). These developmental processes occur at the unique niche between the cranial sutures and the edge of skull bones. Several studies reveal that aberrant osteoblast growth and differentiation frequently result in skull defects (<a>Rice et al., 1999</a>; <a>Yu et al., 2005</a>). These studies suggest that the initial step of osteogenesis is essential. However, it is unknown how collagen secretion, another essential step in osteogenesis, contributes to intramembranous ossification during skull formation.</p>
<p>We have previously reported that intraflagellar transport 20 (IFT20), known to be required for assembling the primary cilium, is important for intramembranous ossification in the skull (<a>Kaku and Komatsu, 2017</a>; <a>Noda et al., 2016</a>). Ciliary-mediated signaling regulates osteogenic cell proliferation and survival, but ciliary-independent IFT20 function is also crucial to control collagen secretion (<a>Noda et al., 2016</a>; <a>Reiter and Leroux, 2017</a>). Because craniofacial bone defects in <em>Ift20</em> mutant mice cannot be simply explained by modest cell survival defects, we hypothesized that ciliary-independent IFT20 function may have a primary role in the regulation of craniofacial osteogenesis. However, it is unclear how IFT20 is involved in collagen trafficking. Interestingly, previous studies have shown that Golgi microtubule-associated protein 210 (GMAP210) anchors to IFT20, and that both proteins function together in the trafficking of proteins destined for the ciliary membrane (<a>Finetti et al., 2009</a>; <a>Follit et al., 2008</a>). Although <em>IFT20</em> mutation(s) in humans have not yet been identified, an absent GMAP210 (encoded by the <em>TRIP11</em> gene) results in lethal skeletal dysplasia (<a>Smits et al., 2010</a>). In addition, null mutations of <em>Trip11</em> cause mineralization defects of craniofacial bones in zebrafish (<a>Daane et al., 2019</a>). Therefore, it may be reasonable to speculate that both IFT20 and GMAP210 function together to regulate skeletal formation. Importantly, a recent phenotypic analysis of GMAP210 in mice revealed that it is essential for trafficking-specific cargo in chondrocytes, but dispensable in other secretory cells such as osteoblasts (<a>Bird et al., 2018</a>). However, human fetuses with lethal skeletal dysplasia because of the GMAP210 mutation display a severe mineralization defect in the skull, in which chondrocytes are not present (<a>Ornitz and Marie, 2002</a>; <a>Smits et al., 2010</a>). These observations raise an important question: Although GMAP210 appears to function as a standalone for the regulation of chondrogenesis during endochondral ossification, does GMAP210 interact with other molecule(s) to participate in skull development?</p>
<p>In this study, we demonstrate the role of the molecular complex of IFT20 and GMAP210, which is crucial for orchestrating collagen trafficking in CNC-derived osteoblasts. Our study highlights that molecular regulation of IFT20 and GMAP210 is required for intramembranous ossification, which has not been previously characterized in skull formation.</p></section><section><h2>RESULTS</h2>
<section><h3>Osteoblasts near the osteogenic front are crucial for intramembranous ossification</h3>
<p>During skull development, growth at the osteogenic fronts (OFs) occurs via intramembranous ossification, in which mesenchymal osteoprogenitors proliferate and differentiate directly into osteoblasts (<a>Iseki et al., 1997</a>, <a>1999</a>; <a>Rice et al., 2000</a>). A recent elegant study using single cell RNA-seq analysis demonstrated that one of the most significant OF-associated genes is <em>Col22a1</em>, which encodes cross-linking extracellular matrix (ECM) collagen (<a>Holmes et al., 2020</a>). This prompted us to hypothesize that osteoblasts residing on OFs may be actively involved in production of type I collagen (<a>Fig. 1</a>A), thus directly contributing to intramembranous ossification during skull development (<a>Fig. 1</a>B). To test this possibility, we examined the cellular localization of procollagen in CNC-derived osteoblasts in the mouse skull. Interestingly, only osteoblasts near the OFs of wild-type skulls were enriched for procollagen in the Golgi (<a>Fig. 1</a>C,D). At embryonic day (E) 18.5, nearly 80% of CNC-derived osteoblasts in the OF contained procollagen in the Golgi (<a>Fig. 1</a>E). A detailed quantitative imaging analysis revealed that procollagen was abundantly present in cis-Golgi labeled with GM130 (<a>Fig. 1</a>F,G). We also quantified Golgi size from the OF zone to non-OF zones. Although there was a tendency of decreased Golgi size toward non-OF zones, no statistical differences in Golgi size were found in the wild-type skull (<a>Fig. S1</a>). These results demonstrate that osteoblasts near the OFs are key in the regulation of spatiotemporal collagen secretion required for the intramembranous ossification in skull development.
</p>

N.S., not significant.</p></figcaption></figure></section><section><h3>IFT20 interacts with GMAP210 during collagen secretion in CNC-derived osteoblasts</h3>
<p>To characterize the function of intraflagellar transport proteins (IFT) in craniofacial development, we previously deleted IFT20, known as the smallest IFT, using <em>Wnt1-Cre</em> mice (hereafter <em>Ift20</em> cKO). Although deletion of <em>Ift20</em> moderately affects proliferation and cell survival in CNC-derived osteoblasts (<a>Noda et al., 2016</a>), <em>Ift20</em> cKO mice displayed severe skull defects (<a>Fig. 2</a>A). Thus, there must be a reason that explains loss of skull mineralization in <em>Ift20</em> cKO mice. Also, it was not clear how loss of IFT20 leads to defective collagen structure. To address these points, collagen structure was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) <em>in vitro</em>. Compared with controls, the collagen fibrils were thin and disorganized in <em>Ift20</em> cKO osteoblasts (<a>Fig. 2</a>B). To characterize the collagen structure at the nanoscale level, atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis was also performed. Consistent with the SEM analysis, the collagen fibril network in <em>Ift20</em> cKO osteoblasts was severely damaged, with thinly formed collagen layers (<a>Fig. 2</a>C). To further examine ultrastructure of collagen <em>in vivo</em>, super-resolution microscopy analysis was performed. Compared with controls, the number of collagen fibrils was reduced in skull tissue from <em>Ift20</em> cKO mice (<a>Fig. 2</a>D). These results suggest that IFT20 is essential for collagen biosynthesis, which is crucial for intramembranous ossification in the skull. Although the primary role of the IFT complex is to assemble primary cilia, recent studies have highlighted the importance of the extraciliary function of IFTs (<a>Reiter and Leroux, 2017</a>; <a>Singla and Reiter, 2006</a>). IFT20 participates in the regulation of intracellular membrane trafficking, Cbl-mediated ubiquitination and nucleation of Golgi-derived microtubules (<a>Finetti et al., 2009</a>; <a>Nishita et al., 2017</a>; <a>Schmid et al., 2018</a>). However, little is known about the cellular function of IFT20 during collagen biosynthesis. To determine the molecules that interact with IFT20 during intramembranous ossification, cell lysates from control and <em>Ift20</em> cKO osteoblasts were immunoprecipitated using an IFT20 antibody and subjected to non-biased mass spectrometry (MS) and proteomic analysis (<a>Fig. S2</a>). As a result, GMAP210 was identified as a potential molecule binding with IFT20 (<a>Fig. 2</a>E). A co-immunoprecipitation assay confirmed the MS results by showing molecular binding between IFT20 and GMAP210 in CNC-derived osteoblasts (<a>Fig. 2</a>F). To examine whether IFT20 interacts with GMAP210 during skull development, immunocytochemical analysis was performed using wild-type CNC-derived osteoblasts. Consistent with results from the MS analysis (<a>Tables S1-S5</a>), IFT20 and GMAP210 colocalized in the cis-Golgi (<a>Fig. 2</a>G). To further define the molecular interaction between IFT20 and GMAP210 at the cellular level, a proximity ligation assay (PLA), which allows the <em>in situ</em> immunodetection of protein-protein complexes under endogenous circumstances, was performed. PLA determined the functional binding activity between IFT20 and GMAP210, but its localization is distinct from the presence of primary cilia (<a>Fig. 2</a>H), demonstrating the intracellular binding activity of IFT20 and GMAP210 as single proteins. These findings are consistent with a previous study showing that GMAP210 anchors IFT20 to the Golgi complex (<a>Follit et al., 2008</a>). Together, these results suggest that, as an extraciliary function, IFT20 interacts with GMAP210 to control collagen trafficking during skull development.
</p>

<p>Loss-of-function mutations in GMAP210 result in lethal skeletal dysplasia in humans and mice (<a>Bird et al., 2018</a>; <a>Smits et al., 2010</a>). Although these studies do not focus on addressing the molecular function of GMAP210 in intramembranous bones, patients with GMAP210 mutations display skull defects (<a>Smits et al., 2010</a>). The function of GMAP210 has been well studied in chondrocytes during endochondral ossification (<a>Bird et al., 2018</a>), but its molecular function during intramembranous ossification is unclear. To address this question, we disrupted <em>Trip11</em> using <em>Wnt1-Cre</em> mice (hereafter <em>Trip11</em> cKO). Interestingly, <em>Trip11</em> cKO mice displayed craniofacial defects (<a>Fig. 3</a>A). The disruption was confirmed by absence of GMAP210 proteins in the skull (<a>Fig. 3</a>B). Although the majority of <em>Trip11</em> cKO mice died after birth (<em>n</em>=9, &gt;66.7%; <a>Fig. S3A</a>), this was not because of cardiac defects, because the structure of the cardiac outflow tract effectively labeled by <em>Wnt1-Cre</em> was normal in <em>Trip11</em> cKO mice (<a>Fig. S3B</a>). Skeletal staining and histological analysis revealed that CNC-derived bones (e.g. nasal and frontal bones) were less mineralized in <em>Trip11</em> cKO mice (<a>Fig. 3</a>C,D). These observations highlight the newly discovered function of GMAP210 in intramembranous bone formation.
</p>

<p>Because GMAP210 may play a pivotal role in the onset of craniofacial osteogenesis, we first determined proliferation activity in CNC-derived skulls using a BrdU assay and found that the ratio of BrdU incorporation was comparable between control and <em>Trip11</em> cKO mice (<a>Fig. 4</a>A). Although the wild-type osteoblasts formed solid nodules, which positively stained with Alizarin Red, no cell clusters developed and few osteogenic nodules stained in <em>Trip11</em> cKO cells (<a>Fig. 4</a>E). Because skull defects in <em>Trip11</em> cKO mice are very similar to those observed in <em>Ift20</em> cKO mice (<a>Fig. 2</a>), levels of IFT20 were examined by immunohistochemical staining and western blot analysis. Compared with controls, IFT20 production was reduced in <em>Trip11</em> mice (<a>Fig. S4</a>), suggesting that stability of IFT20 may depend on the presence of GMAP210. Together, these results demonstrate that GMAP210 is important for regulating collagen biosynthesis by controlling collagen trafficking during skull development.

<p>Although <em>Col1a1</em> was normally expressed in <em>Trip11</em> cKO mice (<a>Fig. 4</a>B), the amount of type I collagen was decreased in skull tissues (<a>Fig. 4</a>F). These results suggest that, although procollagen was produced, it was not secreted extracellularly, resulting in the attenuation of collagen fibril formation. This raised an important question about how GMAP210 functions in Golgi to control collagen trafficking. To examine intracellular protein transport from endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to Golgi, we employed a plasmid encoding vesicular stomatitis virus G protein tagged with EGFP (VSVG-GFP) (<a>Presley et al., 1997</a>). VSVG-GFP has been used to study membrane transport because of its reversible misfolding and retention in the ER at 40°C, and its ability to move out of ER and into Golgi upon reducing the temperature to 32°C. VSVG-GFP plasmid was transfected into control and <em>Trip11</em> cKO osteoblasts, and GFP movement was examined by immunocytochemical co-staining with GM130 (GOLGA2), a marker of cis-Golgi. After temperature reduction, similar to the controls, GFP in <em>Trip11</em> cKO osteoblasts was well co-localized with GM130 (<a>Fig. 5</a>A,B). Although a decreased Golgi size was found in <em>Trip11</em> cKO osteoblasts, this did not prevent the transport of VSVG-GFP into Golgi (<a>Fig. 5</a>B). Time-lapse live imaging analysis confirmed these observations <em>in vitro</em> (<a>Movies 1</a> and <a>2</a>). To examine the process of collagen trafficking, control and CNC-derived <em>Trip11</em> cKO cells were treated with ascorbic acid (AA) (<a>Noda et al., 2016</a>). After 2 h of AA stimulation, ∼80% of control and <em>Trip11</em> cKO cells showed smooth collagen transport into the Golgi (<a>Fig. 5</a>C, second panels from the left, <a>Fig. 5</a>D). Starting from 4 h of AA stimulation, many of the control osteoblasts actively secreted collagen from the Golgi, suggesting that dynamic collagen trafficking occurs (<a>Fig. 5</a>C, third panels from the left, <a>Fig. 5</a>D). On the other hand, over 80% of <em>Trip11</em> cKO osteoblasts still showed an insufficient collagen trafficking from the Golgi, suggesting that collagen trafficking is significantly slower in <em>Trip11</em> cKO osteoblasts compared with controls (<a>Fig. 5</a>C, third and fourth left lower panels, <a>Fig. 5</a>D). Western blot analysis confirmed that intracellular collagen in <em>Trip11</em> cKO osteoblasts remained much longer than in controls (<a>Fig. 5</a>E,F), indicating that collagen trafficking is hampered by GMAP210 dysfunction. To examine whether collagen trafficking was attenuated in <em>Trip11</em> cKO skulls, the levels of collagen were examined by immunohistochemistry (<a>Fig. 5</a>G). Although <em>Col1a1</em> was expressed normally (<a>Fig. 4</a>B), compared with controls, type I collagen was less presented in <em>Trip11</em> cKO skull (<a>Fig. 5</a>G). Quantification analysis further confirmed that type I collagen in cis-Golgi was less present in <em>Trip11</em> cKO compared with controls (<a>Fig. S5</a>). With these data, one might think that there is a discrepancy observed in cellular localization of collagen <em>in vitro</em> (<a>Fig. 5</a>C-F) and <em>in vivo</em> (<a>Fig. 5</a>G). However, this can be explained by the following reasons. Recently, we have reported that IFT20 plays a pivotal role in regulation of collagen quality (<a>Yamaguchi et al., 2020</a>). Because IFT20 directly interacted with GMAP210 (<a>Fig. 2</a>E-H), it is reasonable to infer that GMAP210 may also be involved in regulation of collagen quality. Therefore, in addition to a lesser amount of extracellular collagen secretion, intracellular collagen could not be detected due to the low quality of collagen in <em>Trip11</em> cKO skulls. On the other hand, although the pattern of collagen kinetics in the Golgi was distinct compared with control cells, intracellular collagen was still detectable in <em>Trip11</em> mutant cells (<a>Fig. 5</a>C-F). This is probably because cell culture experiments enabled us to examine collagen trafficking without being affected by abnormal collagen post-translational modifications, which may occur in <em>Trip11</em> cKO skulls. Together, these results suggest that GMAP210 is required for the transport of collagen through the Golgi, thereby directly contributing to the intracellular trafficking of collagen during skull development.

<p>Intramembranous ossification gives rise to many craniofacial bones, including the skull. One of the important roles of osteoblasts is to secrete type I collagen. However, it remains unclear how collagen trafficking contributes to form complex skull structures. Here, we show that ciliary protein IFT20 and golgin protein GMAP210 interact together to regulate collagen trafficking and control the process of intramembranous ossification in mice. This is the first study to demonstrate that both ciliary and golgin proteins participate in the regulation of collagen biosynthesis during skull development.</p>
<p>Between adjacent skull bones, OFs are present within the developing suture consisting of fibrous joints (<a>Morriss-Kay and Wilkie, 2005</a>; <a>Rice et al., 2000</a>; <a>Wilkie and Morriss-Kay, 2001</a>). Growth at the OFs occurs through intramembranous ossification, by which osteoprogenitors proliferate and differentiate directly into osteoblasts (<a>Iseki et al., 1997</a>, <a>1999</a>). Subsequently, osteoblasts secrete type I collagen required for bone mineralization. It has been well studied that the balance of transcriptional regulations and growth factor signaling is essential for regulating the proliferation and differentiation of these osteoblasts (<a>Chai and Maxson, 2006</a>; <a>Noden and Trainor, 2005</a>). However, it remains unknown how exactly osteoblasts participate in the collagen secretion process during skull development. We found that CNC-derived osteoblasts near the OF of wild-type skulls are enriched for procollagen in the cis-Golgi (<a>Fig. 1</a>). Because the Golgi apparatus in skeletogenic cells expands when collagen matrix is secreted in skeletogenic cells (<a>Cameron, 1968</a>; <a>Kitami et al., 2019</a>), we hypothesized that the process of collagen secretion in these osteoblasts is highly activated when they need to build bone ECM rapidly. Although the cellular function of osteoblasts residing on OFs has not been well characterized, our study may define the OF as a ‘center of collagen secretion’ required for the intramembranous ossification in the skull (<a>Fig. 7</a>).</p>
<p>The fundamental role of IFTs is to assemble primary cilia (<a>Kozminski et al., 1993</a>; <a>Pazour and Rosenbaum, 2002</a>). Once overlooked as an evolutionary vestige, primary cilia are now considered to be one of the crucial organelles required for many developmental aspects and tissue homeostasis (<a>Eggenschwiler and Anderson, 2007</a>; <a>Goetz and Anderson, 2010</a>). Therefore, understanding the complete role of IFTs will be very helpful in dissecting the complex etiology of human diseases linked to defective primary cilia formation and function, termed ‘ciliopathies’ (<a>Chang et al., 2015</a>; <a>Reiter and Leroux, 2017</a>). To date, ciliopathies have emerged as broad and serious clinical conditions, and considerable efforts have been made to understand how their pathogeneses attribute to alteration of Hedgehog signaling. However, skull defects vary in skeletal ciliopathies and their etiology cannot be solely explained by ciliary Hedgehog signaling mechanisms. Therefore, it is essential to understand how IFT functions in the regulation of skeletal development.</p>
<p>In humans, mutations in ciliary genes often affect the skeletal system. For example, skeletal ciliopathies are frequently observed in Verma-Naumoff syndrome, Majewski syndrome, Jeune syndrome and Ellis-van Creveld syndrome (<a>Ashe et al., 2012</a>; <a>Duran et al., 2017</a>; <a>Miller et al., 2013</a>). Consistent with the phenotypic features of these conditions, loss-of-function of IFT in animal models has clearly demonstrated that IFTs are crucial for ciliogenesis and that they orchestrate growth factor signaling to control skeletogenic cell proliferation, survival and differentiation (<a>Serra, 2008</a>; <a>Yuan et al., 2015</a>). However, at present, little is known about how IFTs function when organizing bone ECM. In this regard, we have recently reported that IFT20 plays a pivotal role in collagen biosynthesis by regulating, in part, telopeptidyl lysine hydroxylation and cross-linking in the craniofacial bone (<a>Yamaguchi et al., 2020</a>). This demonstrates that IFT20 participates in collagen post-translational modifications to control the ‘quality’ of collagen. In addition, we have also reported that IFT20 functions in collagen trafficking to regulate the ‘quantity’ of collagen (<a>Noda et al., 2016</a>). These findings are significant because elucidating the role of IFT20 will provide a novel insight in the understanding of the etiology of skeletal ciliopathies. However, it remains unclear what the primary role of IFT20 during collagen biosynthesis is. To address this, we performed a non-bias MS analysis to identify the binding partner of IFT20. Utilizing both genetic and proteomic approaches, we determined that both IFT20 and GMAP210 function together in CNC-derived osteoblasts. Our finding is consistent with those from a previous study showing that GMAP210 anchors IFT20 to the Golgi complex (<a>Follit et al., 2008</a>). In that study, the authors generated stable mouse kidney cell lines overexpressing both FLAG-tagged IFT20 and GMAP210 and found that GMAP210 functions as an IFT20 binding protein. Our study shows, for the first time, the importance of the IFT20-GMAP210 molecular complex in type I collagen secretion <em>in vivo</em>. Through our study, we are aware that disruption of IFT20 and GMAP210 causes craniofacial bone defects including micrognathia. Although the mandible is also formed by intramembranous ossification, its developmental mechanism is very different compared with the skull. For example, mandible formation is required for Meckel's cartilage, but skull is formed by the direct conversion process of mesenchymal cells to osteoblasts. We will examine the etiology of mandible defects in both <em>Ift20</em> and <em>Trip11</em> mutants in the future.</p>
<p>Our results support recent elegant studies that showed how hypomorphic mutations in <em>TRIP11</em> cause odontochondrodysplasia, highlighting the important role of the IFT20-GMAP210 complex in the development of a clinical condition (<a>Medina et al., 2020</a>; <a>Wehrle et al., 2019</a>). Although <em>IFT20</em> mutations have not been identified in humans, one might expect to observe phenotypic resemblance, at least in part, between <em>IFT20</em> and <em>TRIP11</em> mutations. Supporting this prediction, deleting either <em>Ift20</em> and <em>Trip11</em> using <em>Wnt1-Cre</em> leads to similar osteopenia-like skull defects, and the double knockout mice developed much more severe skull defects compared with either IFT20 or GMAP210 mutants (<a>Fig. 6</a>). Because cellular trafficking from the ER to the Golgi was comparable in both control and GMAP210 mutant osteoblasts (<a>Fig. 5</a>), the primary cause of bone mineralization defects was most likely attributed to collagen trafficking abnormalities through the Golgi onwards to the plasma membrane. This is well supported by the cellular localization of GMAP210 in the cis-Golgi (<a>Follit et al., 2008</a>, <a>2006</a>), further reinforcing our idea that the molecular association between IFT20 and GMAP210 in the Golgi is essential for collagen trafficking. However, this cellular machinery may not be generally conserved; rather, it may function in cell origin- and/or developmental stage-specific manners. For example, deletion of GMAP210 in CNC-derived osteoblasts resulted in craniofacial bone defects (<a>Fig. 3</a>), but disruption of GMAP210 in mesoderm-derived osteoblasts did not (<a>Bird et al., 2018</a>). This suggests that the IFT20-GMAP210 complex may predominantly function in CNC-derived skeletogenic precursors. In fact, null <em>Trip11</em> mutations in mice and loss-of-function of <em>TRIP11</em> in humans results in lack of mineralization in the frontal area of skull where CNC-derived osteoblasts contribute to bone formation (<a>Smits et al., 2010</a>). It would be interesting to further examine the function of both <em>Ift20</em> and <em>Trip11</em> using a mesodermal Cre-driver such as <em>Mesp1-Cre</em> to determine whether these genes control skull development.</p>
<p>Chondrocytes in mice lacking <em>Trip11</em> show ER swelling and stress, which is also the case in patients with achondrogenesis type 1A caused by mutations in GMAP210 (<a>Bird et al., 2018</a>; <a>Smits et al., 2010</a>). Interestingly, chondrogenesis in these mutant mice is severely affected; however, the secretion of major chondrocyte-related ECM proteins, including type II collagen or aggrecan, is normal. This suggests that GMAP210 may not function in all of the cellular trafficking processes. If this is the case, it may be reasonable to predict that GMAP210 specifically interacts with IFT20 to regulate type I collagen trafficking in CNC-derived osteoblasts. At this time, it is unclear how the molecular mechanism of the IFT20-GMAP210 complex participates in collagen trafficking. It is noteworthy though that GMAP210 possibly functions when collagen molecules tether in the Golgi (<a>Roboti et al., 2015</a>; <a>Sato et al., 2015</a>), suggesting that the IFT20-GMAP210 complex may play a role in the tethering process of type I collagen.</p>
<p>Collagen trafficking is an essential cellular process during craniofacial bone development (<a>Lang et al., 2006</a>). In humans, mutations in the SEC23A and SEC24D genes, which encode components of the coat protein complex II (COPII) required for anterograde transport from the ER to the Golgi, cause cranio-lenticulo-sutural dysplasia (CLSD), characterized by open cranial sutures and defects in craniofacial bones (<a>Boyadjiev et al., 2006</a>, <a>2003</a>). Coat protein complex I (COPI) vesicles are required for the retrograde transport from the Golgi to the ER, and mutations in ARCN1, which encodes a component of the COPI, also cause craniofacial abnormalities in humans (<a>Izumi et al., 2016</a>). Our study further demonstrates the importance of collagen trafficking regulated by IFT20 and GMAP210, which is crucial for skull development.</p></section><section><h2>MATERIALS AND METHODS</h2>

<p>Skull tissues were fixed for at least 24 h in Karnovsky's fixative, decalcified in 10% EDTA for one week, then refixed for at least 24 h in Karnovsky's fixative. Samples were then secondarily fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h, dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol, embedded in epoxy resin, sectioned at 100 nm thickness using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7), stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and imaged at 80 kV using a JEOL JEM-1230 TEM equipped with a Gatan digital camera.</p></section><section><h3>Section <em>in situ</em> hybridization</h3>
<p>A digoxygenin-labeled RNA probe was generated by <em>in vitro</em> transcription (Sigma-Aldrich). Section <em>in situ</em> hybridization was performed according to standard procedures (<a>Komatsu et al., 2014</a>).</p></section><section><h3>Western blot analysis</h3>
<p>Osteoblast lysates or tissue lysates were prepared in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer. After centrifugation at 15,000 <strong><em>g</em></strong>, the supernatants were separated by SDS/PAGE, blotted onto a PVDF membrane, analyzed with specific antibodies, and visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence. The antibodies used were as follows: GMAP210 (1:500, LS Biosciences, LS-<a>C20059</a>), IFT20 (1:500, Proteintech, 13615-1-AP), collagen type I (1:1000, Cedarlane, CL50151AP), α-tubulin (1:5000, Abcam, ab7291) and GAPDH (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology, 2118). The Clarity Max ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad) was used for chemiluminescent detection, and signals were quantified with Image Lab Version 5.0 (Bio-Rad).</p></section><section><h3>LC/MS/MS analysis</h3>
<p>The gel band samples were subjected to in-gel digestion using a previously described procedure (<a>Shevchenko et al., 2001</a>). The tryptic digested samples were analyzed by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS) using an Orbitrap Fusion™ Tribrid™ mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific™) interfaced with a Dionex UltiMate 3000 Binary RSLCnano HPLC System. We loaded 1 μg of each sample for the analysis. Peptides were separated with an AcclaimTM PepMap TM C18 column (75 µm ID×15 cm) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. Gradient conditions were: 3%-22% of buffer B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) versus buffer A (0.1% formic acid in water) for 12 min, 22%-35% buffer B for 10 min, 35%-90% buffer B for 10 min, followed by 90% buffer B for 10 min. The peptides were analyzed using the data-dependent acquisition (DDA) method. The survey scan was carried out at 120 K resolution at 400 m/z from 350 to 1500 m/z, with AGC target of 2e5 and maximum injection time of 50 msec. Monoisotopic masses were then selected for further fragmentation for ions with 2+ to 4+ charges within a dynamic exclusion range of 35 s. Fragmentation priority was given to the most intense ions. Precursor ions were isolated using the quadrupole with an isolation window of 1.6 m/z. The rapid scan speed in the ion trap was used for CID fragmentation (NCE 35), and an AGC target of 1e4 and maximum injection time of 35 ms were applied. The DDA cycle was limited to 3 s.</p></section><section><h3>MS data processing and analysis</h3>
<p>Raw data files were processed using Thermo Fisher Scientific™ Proteome Discoverer™ software version 1.4. The spectra were searched against the Uniprot <em>Mus musculus</em> database using both the Mascot (v2.3.02, Matrix Science) and Sequest HT search engine. The database search was restricted to the following parameters: allowing up to two missed cleavages, 10 ppm mass tolerance for MS1, 0.8 Da mass tolerance for MS/MS, carbamidomethylation on cysteine residues as fixed modification, oxidation of methionine and phosphorylation of serine, threonine and tyrosine as variable modifications. The search results were validated and trimmed to a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) using Percolator.</p></section><section><h3>Proximity ligation assay</h3>
<p>CNC-derived osteoblast from wild type at E18.5 were cultured and used to perform PLA using the Duolink PLA Kit (Millipore Sigma, DUO92101). More detailed information is described in <a>supplementary Materials and Methods</a> [Proximity ligation assay (PLA)].</p></section><section><h3>VSVG-GFP time-lapse imaging and protein transport assays and immunocytochemistry</h3>
<p>CNC-derived osteoblasts were transfected with the plasmid containing VSVG-GFP using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To shift the temperature from 40°C to 32°C, we used a Thermo Plate (Tokai Hit) for immunocytochemistry and a Stage Top Incubator (Tokai Hit) for time-lapse imaging. For quantification, ten images were captured from one coverslip with at least 33 GFP-positive cells. More detailed information is described in <a>supplementary Materials and Methods</a> (VSVG-GFP time-lapse imaging). To analyze intracellular COL1A1, cells were cultured for 12 h after seeding and then treated with 50 µg/ml ascorbic acid or vehicle. The primary antibodies used were as follows: IFT20 (1:500, Proteintech, 13615-1-AP), collagen type I (1:500, Cedarlane, CL50151AP), GM130 (1:500, BD Biosciences, 610822), GFP (1:2000, Abcam, ab13970) and GMAP210 (1:500, LS Biosciences, LS-<a>C20059</a>). More information is described in <a>supplementary Materials and Methods</a> (Protein transport assays and immunocytochemistry).</p></section><section><h3>Statistical analysis</h3>
<p>An unpaired <em>t</em>-test (two-tailed) or one-way ANOVA with Tukey's HSD post-hoc test was performed in the statistical analysis. A <em>P</em>-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.</p></section></section><section><h2>Supplementary Material</h2>
