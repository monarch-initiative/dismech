# A mouse model of GRIN2D developmental and epileptic encephalopathy recapitulates the human disease.
**Authors:** Yam M, Nassir J, Galber D, Quinn S, Gal R, Ovadia M, Bordeynik-Cohen M, Peled E, Makinson CD, Hausman-Kedem M, Fattal-Valevski A, Frankel WN, Avraham KB, Rubinstein M
**Journal:** Brain (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/brain/awaf149](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awaf149)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in GRIN2D, encoding one of the subunits of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), are associated with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs). Unusual for de novo mutations, the recurrent, de novo, gain of function, missense mutation c.1999G>A (p.Val667Ile) was discovered in multiple patients. We characterized a mouse model carrying the orthologous Grin2d mutation, using behavioural paradigms, electrophysiological recordings in acute brain slices focusing mainly on the activity of Purkinje neurons in the cerebellum, and electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings monitoring brain activity and the response to several drugs. Grin2d mutant mice exhibit a range of phenotypes that closely mirror the human disease, including premature mortality, spontaneous seizures and early onset of motor deficits followed by cognitive impairment. In addition, we observed complex developmental changes in Purkinje neurons with reduced spontaneous firing in immature mice and augmented synaptic response to NMDA application in older mice. ECoG recordings demonstrated profound and continuous abnormal brain activity, with altered spectral properties and a prominent narrowband activity in the theta, alpha and beta bands, paralleling the patterns seen in a patient with the same GRIN2D pathogenic variant. Acute administration of ketamine at a low dose (0.5 mg/kg) had a limited effect on the spectral properties, and higher dosages (4 or 10 mg/kg) caused seizures. Conversely, memantine (10 mg/kg) and phenytoin (30 mg/kg) demonstrated a small corrective effect on ECoG properties. Together, Grin2d mutant mice recapitulate key phenotypes of patients with pathogenic GRIN2D variants, including unique, abnormal brain oscillations, which may serve as a biomarker for quantifying drug responses and guiding future research efforts.

Developmental epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs) are a group of childhood-onset disorders characterized by severe and often refractory epilepsy, accompanied by developmental and behavioural comorbidities. Genetic aetiology is identified in 30%–50% of cases, with pathogenic variants recognized in genes encoding for proteins that are important for developmental functions or neuronal activity, such as ion channels and synaptic proteins.1 Among these genes are those encoding for the different subunits of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), a glutamate ligand-gated cation channel mediating the slow component of excitatory synaptic transmission in the brain.

NMDARs are assembled as heterotetramers, composed of the obligatory GluN1 subunit encoded by GRIN1, together with one of the isoforms of GluN2A-D or GluN3A-B subunits, encoded by GRIN2A–D or GRIN3A–B, respectively. In agreement with their pivotal role in brain activity, dysfunction of NMDARs is associated with various neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders.2,3 Whole exome sequencing of patients with developmental delay, with or without epilepsy, identified de novo variants in GRIN1, GRIN2A, GRIN2B or GRIN2D as the underlying genetic cause.4-6 According to The GRIN database, variants in GRIN2D have been identified in ∼30 patients globally. When combined with data from the ClinVar database, over 25 different pathogenic variants have been documented, many of which were recognized as de novo variants.7-12 Unusual for de novo mutations, several recurrent missense variants were found, including the recurrent missense mutation c.1999G>A (p.Val667Ile) that was discovered in at least four unrelated children presenting with early-onset epileptic encephalopathy associated with severe developmental delay and movement disorder.7-10,13 Functional evaluation of this variant in expression systems demonstrated an increased open probability, slower kinetics of deactivation, and decreased sensitivity to negative modulators, suggesting that this mutation causes a gain of function effect.8 Based on these findings, treatment with NMDAR antagonists was suggested, yet with variable clinical therapeutic effects.7-10,13

Research using mouse models has significantly advanced our understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms underlying neurodevelopmental disorders and has been instrumental in developing therapeutic approaches for complex neurological diseases.14,15 Several GRIN-related mouse models have been developed, recapitulating haploinsufficiency in GRIN1,16,17 haploinsufficiency or DEE-associated missense mutation in GRIN2A18-20 and haploinsufficiency of GRIN2B.21,22 Here, we characterized a mouse model carrying the Grin2d p.Val664Ile mutation (Grin2dV664I/+), an orthologous mutation to the recurrent human GRIN2D Val667Ile pathogenic variant. These mutant mice recapitulate many features of the human disease, including pronounced epileptic activity and premature mortality with cognitive and motor deficits. Recordings of Purkinje neurons (PNs) in acute cerebellum brain slices showed developmental changes in spontaneous firing and augmented response to NMDA, correlating synaptic and network changes with the global severe phenotype. Electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings in these mice confirmed sustained abnormal activity, reminiscent of that of a patient carrying the same GRIN2D pathogenic variants, with limited response to acute administration of NMDAR antagonists. Together, these data highlight this mouse model as a valuable tool for further studies to develop and test novel treatments for GRIN2D-DEE.

Both males and females were used for the behavioural experiments performed, as previously described,24,25 with some modifications as detailed in the, Supplementary material.

Electrode implantation, recordings and analyses were performed as previously described.24-26 Briefly, the mice were anaesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg), xylazine (10 mg/kg) and carprofen (5 mg/kg). A midline incision was made above the skull, and fine silver wire electrodes (130 μm diameter bare; 180 μm diameter coated) were placed at visually identified locations, bilaterally above the somatosensory cortex; a reference electrode was placed on the cerebellum and a grounding electrode was placed subcutaneously behind the neck, towards the left shoulder. The connector was secured with dental cement, and the skin was closed with sutures if needed. Mice were allowed at least two nights to recover prior to recording.

Video-ECoG recordings lasting 2–5 h, between 8 a.m. and 6 p.m., were obtained from freely behaving mice, connected to a T8 Headstage (Triangle BioSystems), using PowerLab 8/35 acquisition hardware and LabChart 8 software (ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia). After 2–3 h of recording, some of the mice were injected [intraperitoneal (IP)] with ketamine (Vetoquinol; 10 mg/kg, 4 mg/kg or 0.5 mg/kg), magnesium sulphate (Thermo Scientific; 50 mg/kg), memantine (Acros Organics; 10 mg/kg) or phenytoin (Alomone Labs; 30 mg/kg). The electrical signals were recorded and digitized at a sampling rate of 1 kHz with a notch filter at 50 Hz. The analysis was performed using LabChart 8 (ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia). ECoG signals were processed offline with a 0.5–100 Hz bandpass filter, and the EMG signal filtered with a 0.5 Hz highpass filter. We used a custom-written threshold algorithm in Python to extract artefact-free epochs where the mice were awake and not moving, as we did before.26 In addition, to verify the data, we also used manual analysis in which 5 to 10 30-s segments devoid of movement artefacts but following movement were analysed and averaged. The power bands were defined as: delta, 0.9–3.9 Hz; theta, 4.8–7.8 Hz; alpha, 8.7–11.7 Hz; beta 12.6–29.2 Hz; gamma, 30.2–99.6 Hz.

All analyses were performed as part of clinical care reported according to Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, and consent was obtained from the parents. Clinical EEG were acquired at a sampling rate of 500 Hz using 18 channels positioned according to the international 10–20 system. Recordings were obtained from the patient at different ages (1, 2, 3, 5 and 7 years). Data preprocessing was performed with Brainstorm27 and EEGLAB toolbox for MATLAB.28 Power spectral densities (PSD) of channels C3-P3 and C4-P4, corresponding to the somatosensory cortex, were computed for each recording using Welch’s method with a 50% overlap Hann window using a custom-written MATLAB script. Periodic oscillatory peaks were extracted from the normalized PSD using the FOOOF toolbox.29

Cerebellum brain slices were prepared from WT and Grin2dmut littermates at different ages, as we did before,30,31 with some modifications. Mice were deeply anaesthetized with isoflurane and decapitated. The brain was quickly removed into an ice-cold slicing solution containing (in mM) 75 sucrose, 87 NaCl, 25 NaHCO3, 25 glucose, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 0.5 CaCl2 and 7.0 MgCl2; 300 μm sagittal cerebellar vermis slices were cut using a Leica VT1200S vibratome (Leica Biosystems). After sectioning, slices were transferred to a storage chamber with fresh artificial CSF (ACSF) [containing (in mM) 125 NaCl, 3 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3 and 10 glucose] and incubated for 45 min at 37°C, followed by a 30 min recovery period at room temperature. All solutions were saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. The cells were visualized under oblique illumination with near-infrared LED and an upright microscope (SOM; Sutter Instrument). Recordings were obtained at a temperature of 32°C, using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices) and Clampex 10.7 software (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Loose-patch extracellular recordings were made from the soma of visually identified PNs. Only one cell was recorded from each lobe, and no more than two cells from each slice. The electrode pipettes (resistance of 2–4 MΩ) were filled with ACSF. For whole-cell voltage-clamp recordings the membrane potential was set to 40 mV, and the pipettes were filled with an internal solution containing (in mM): 150 CsCl, 4.6 MgCl2, 0.1 CaCl2, 4.6 Na-ATP, 0.4 Na2-GTP, 10 HEPES, 1 EGTA and 5 QX-314. For current clamp recordings, the pipette was filled with (in mM): 145 K-gluconate, 2 MgCl2, 0.5 EGTA, 2 ATP-Tris, 0.2 Na2-GTP, 10 HEPES, pH to 7.2. In certain experiments, NMDA (15 μM) and picrotoxin, (40 μM) (Alomone Labs) were used.

In some experiments, biocytin (2 mg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the recording electrodes. After the recordings, slices were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h and processed as previously described.30 Visualization was performed using a fluorescent microscope (Olympus). Analysis of the soma size and dendritic arborization was performed using ImageJ software (NIH).

Entorhinal cortex slices were prepared from Grin2dmut mice at 2 to 4 weeks of age. To preserve connectivity between the cortex and the hippocampus, the brain was placed at a 12° angle, and 400 μm horizontal slices were obtained. After sectioning, slices were transferred to a storage chamber with fresh ACSF [containing (in mM) 125 NaCl, 4 KCl, 1.5 CaCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, and 25 glucose] and maintained at room temperature in this magnesium-free solution for at least 90 min before recordings began. Extracellular field potential recordings (glass microelectrodes, resistance 0.5–1 MΩ, filled with magnesium-free ACSF) were performed at 32°C from layer II of the entorhinal cortex, in a magnesium-free solution with high potassium concentration (8 mM KCl). Sporadic spontaneous short bursting activity was observed in some slices, but not all, at 4 mM KCl, while continuous epileptic discharges were observed in all slices at 8 mM KCl. After 15 min of baseline recordings, ketamine (5 μM) or memantine (100 μM) were added to the external solution, and the recording continued for an additional 15 min.

Data are reported as mean (standard error; SE). All statistical tests were carried out via GraphPad Prism, and details of the specific test and the P-values for the different panels are listed in Supplementary Table 1. In general, the statistical analysis between WT and mutant mice used an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test for measurements that followed a normal distribution, and the Mann–Whitney U-test for measurements that failed to demonstrate normal distribution. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

The de novo DEE-associated GRIN2D p.Val667Ile missense mutation was identified in multiple patients.8,9,13 To generate a murine model, we introduced the homologous Val664Ile in Grin2d using CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats)-Cas9. Four founders were identified following several rounds of electroporation, but all died prematurely between 3 and 9 weeks of age. Sperm was recovered from one of the mice soon after its death and was used to produce a total of nine additional Grin2dV664I/+ mutant mice, eight of which were produced through in vitro fertilization and only one through breeding. Grin2dV664I/+ mutants showed profound mortality starting from postnatal Day (P)16 (Fig. 1A). ECoG recordings, performed at the end of the third week of life, on P18–20, demonstrated continuous abnormal and severe epileptic activity (Fig. 1B).

Owing to difficulties in maintaining this line, a conditional mouse was generated using a ‘knockout-first’ approach as described in (Teoh et al.23). Conditional heterozygous (Grin2d+/−) mice, were crossed with Sox2-Cre mice,32 to generate mice carrying the global heterozygous Sox2-CreV664I/+ pups (Grin2dmut) and Sox2-Cre+/+ (WT) littermates. Similar to the CRISPR-generated Grin2dV664I/+, Grin2dmut demonstrated profound mortality, with the highest risk during the third week of life (P14–20, Fig. 1C and D). While most of the deaths were unwitnessed, continuous video recordings of several mice revealed that their death was preceded by a terminal and severe generalized convulsive seizure (Supplementary Video 1). Similar to many other models of DEE, Grin2dmut mice showed a growth delay and were smaller throughout their development (Fig. 1E). Nevertheless, the normalized increase in weight was comparable between WT and Grin2dmut [WT: 142.6% (2.3%); Grin2dmut: 138.2% (4.1%); P = 0.4], indicating reduced well-being and developmental delay, rather than a failure to thrive.

As for many rare diseases, the developmental trajectory and disease progression of GRIN2D-DEE in patients are understudied,33 yet it is clear that severe phenotypes are presented early in life.7-10,13 Aiming to examine the disease progression, we tested various behavioural phenotypes in mice at different ages. The second week of life in a mouse, while they are still nursing, most closely resembles infancy in humans.34 Motor function at the beginning of the second week of life (P8–9), measured as the time to turn in response to vestibular gravitational cues, was similar between WT and Grin2dmut mice (Fig. 2A). However, at P10, Grin2dmut mice showed reduced locomotor abilities in an open arena with a decreased voluntary movement, indicating impaired crawling (Fig. 2A). Mice at their third week of life (P14–21), are still partially nursing, while staring to eat on their own, which can be correlated to children at around 6 months of age. At this age, motor deficits in Grin2dmut mice manifested as distinct hindlimb clasping (Fig. 2B), and reduced ability to balance on the rotarod (Fig. 2C). Mice gain full independence of their feeding and motor abilities during their fourth week of life, analogous to the developmental stage of human toddlers. Sexual maturation begins at around 5 weeks of age, which is comparable to early teen years, and mice are considered adults after 6 weeks.34 Motor deficits, exhibited as reduced performance on the rotarod, were also evident in Grin2dmut mice after their fifth week of life (Fig. 2C). In addition. Grin2dmut mice in their fourth week of life showed reduced ability to hold the position in the catalepsy bar test (Fig. 2D).

GRIN2D patients also demonstrate intellectual disability and attention deficits. To test these behaviours in the mice, we first used the open-field test, which examines the free exploration of a novel arena. Grin2dmut mice in their fourth week of life showed increased spontaneous locomotor activity, which was also evident in mice at 5 weeks of age. However, while 4-week-old Grin2dmut mice showed increased anxiety levels, as indicated by a preference to avoid the centre of the arena (Fig. 2E), it was similar to that observed in WT mice at 5 weeks (Fig. 2F). To test the cognitive functions, we used the novel object recognition test, in which both WT and Grin2dmut mice demonstrated a similar preference toward exploration of the novel object at the age of 4 weeks (Fig. 2G). Likewise, in the Y maze spontaneous alternation test, in which natural curiosity promotes the mice to alternate between the arms of the maze, WT and Grin2dmut mice in their fourth week of life explored the maze similarly, alternating between the arms in a directed manner. Conversely, older Grin2dmut mice, at 5–7 weeks of age, showed random alternations between the arms of the maze (Fig. 2H), indicating an impaired spatial working memory.

Together, these data confirm that Grin2dmut demonstrate multifaceted phenotypes reminiscent of patients carrying the pathogenic GRIN2D variants, with a high risk for premature death, early presentation of motor deficits and hyperactivity, followed by the presentation of cognitive impairments.

Next, to establish an electrophysiological correlation to the observed disease-related phenotypes, we recorded neuronal activity in the cerebellum, focusing on the activity of PNs. PNs, the sole output of the cerebellum, integrate neuronal changes within the cerebellum, and their activity has been shown to be altered in multiple forms of movement disorders, and neurodevelopmental disorders, including epilepsy and autism spectrum disorders.35,36

Using extracellular recordings in acute brain slices, we measured the rate of spontaneous firing of PNs at various ages. At the second week of life, the frequency of spontaneous firing was reduced in Grin2dmut mice. However, over development, this difference dissipated, and in mice older than 4 weeks of age, the frequency of spontaneous firing was similar between WT and Grin2dmut mice (Fig. 3A–C). Changes in spontaneous firing frequency can be caused by altered intrinsic properties or by changes in the amplitude or frequency of synaptic inputs.37 The intrinsic firing properties of PNs at the second week of life showed similar properties of the action potentials or overall firing frequency in response to the injection of a depolarizing current injection (Fig. 3D and E and Supplementary Table 2), suggesting comparable intrinsic properties in WT and Grin2dmut mice at this age. Nevertheless, morphological examination of biocytin-labelled PNs suggested an overall smaller somata of Grin2dmut mice, yet with an overall similar dendritic arborization (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Examination of synaptic currents, focusing on spontaneous postsynaptic currents (sPSCs) on PNs and their modulation by bath application of NMDA, revealed a developmental-related decrease in the frequency of PSCs onto PNs. However, similar amplitudes and frequencies of sPSCs were observed in WT and Grin2dmut mice in the varying age groups (Fig. 3G and H and Supplementary Fig. 2). The addition of NMDA (15 μM) produced a marked increase in the frequency of sPSCs in both WT and Grin2dmut mice. However, the response to NMDA was more robust in Grin2dmut mice after 4 weeks of age (Fig. 3G–J). The addition of picrotoxin (40 μM) at the end of the recording completely abolished the synaptic inputs in both WT and Grin2dmut mice, confirming that these synaptic effects were inhibitory, mediated by GABAAR (Fig. 3G). The lack of NMDA effect on the sPSCs amplitudes (Supplementary Fig. 1), together with the conformation that these inputs are governed by GABAAR, indicates that the augmented response to NMDA, under these conditions, was mostly mediated by the effect of NMDA on presynaptic cerebellar interneurons that target PNs, and not the recorded, postsynaptic PNs. Together, these data demonstrated complex developmental neuronal and network changes in PNs in Grin2dmut mice, with reduced spontaneous firing in young immature mice, and augmented synaptic response to NMDA in older mice.

With profound mortality (Fig. 1C) and terminal convulsive seizures (Supplementary Video 1), ECoG recordings were further used to examine neuronal oscillations in Grin2dmut mice. Notably, consistent abnormal activity was observed in Grin2dmut mice throughout the recording, with some spike-wave discharges, but mostly prolonged starches of abnormal high-amplitude activity without convulsions, and rare sections of normal background activity (Fig. 4A). To quantify the properties of these oscillations we used spectral analysis, focusing on segments of quiet wakefulness that were devoid of movement artefacts (see methods26). The overall total power was similar in WT and Grin2dmut at their fourth week of life, however, with a tendency for increased power in the theta frequency range (Fig. 4B). Examination of the normalized power density, which is often used for clinical analyses,38 further demonstrated increased, narrowband rhythmic activity in the theta to beta range in Grin2dmut mice (Fig. 4C). Analyses of the specific contribution of different frequency bands to the overall power showed a reduced contribution of the lower delta range frequencies and the high gamma frequency band and a greater contribution of the theta, alpha and beta frequency bands, together with a higher median frequency in Grin2dmut mice (Fig. 4C–E). Analyses of older Grin2dmut (5–7 weeks) revealed some alterations of the spectral properties in mice that survived to this age. Specifically, the median frequency was similar in WT and Grin2dmut mice, and so was the contribution of the alpha and gamma frequency bands. Nevertheless, the reduced contribution of delta frequencies, as well as the increased impact of theta and beta frequencies, persisted (Fig. 4F–J).

Next, we compared the spectral profile of the mice, to that of a patient carrying the homologous GRIN2D V667I pathogenic variant. While convulsive seizures were not observed in this patient, the EEG demonstrated diffusely slow and disorganized high-voltage background activity.13 Notably, spectral analysis of the background activity, despite the varying settings and conditions (EEG versus ECoG, recording systems, filters, etc.), showed prominent activity at the theta and alpha frequencies (Fig. 4K–L). To identify the peaks of abnormal narrowband GRIN2D-related oscillatory activity in the mice and the patient, we used the previously described fitting oscillations and one-over-f (FOOOF) algorithm.29 This algorithm detects peaks of activity that rise above the typical background aperiodic activity, which follows the 1/f distribution with exponentially decreasing power with increasing frequencies.39 This analysis highlighted similar peaks of narrowband, abnormal rhythmic activity around 5 and 10 Hz in Grin2dmut mice, as well as in the EEG recordings from the patient (Fig. 4M), further suggesting that these are linked to GRIN2D-related abnormal brain activity.

Together, ECoG recordings in Grin2dmut mice demonstrated unusual and consistent abnormal activity with an altered spectral signature and prominent narrowband activity at the theta, alpha and beta bands, similar to that of a patient carrying a GRIN2D pathogenic variant, indicating that this model faithfully recapitulates GRIN2D abnormal brain activity.

Drugs that modulate brain function can also affect the spectral pattern of ECoG oscillations, and these modulations can be used to assess the therapeutic potential of pharmacological interventions.26,40 Using this method to examine the effect of various drugs in Grin2dmut mice is particularly compelling as the quantification of the isolated epileptic activity (i.e. the frequency of the interictal spikes) is convoluted by the continued abnormal ECoG patterns.

As the GRIN2D V667I pathogenic variant was shown to have a gain of function effect on the activity of NMDAR,8 NMDAR antagonists, such as ketamine and memantine, were proposed as a potential therapy in GRIN2D patients.8,9,13 Ketamine is a non-competitive NMDAR antagonist that acts by an open channel blocker mechanism. However, it is not selective to NMDAR and can also inhibit other types of ion channels and receptors, including the ionotropic glutamate AMPA receptors, hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated cationic channels, nicotinic acetyl-choline ion channels, L-type Ca2+ channels, metabotropic glutamate receptors, dopamine D2 receptors and 5-HT2 serotonin receptors.41-44 When given acutely to WT mice, ketamine at 10 mg/kg, had no effect on the power or spectral properties of the ECoG signal (Supplementary Fig. 3). Conversely, in Grin2dmut mice, the same dose of ketamine caused a generalized convulsive seizure that lasted minutes, a clear indication for an adverse response at this dose (four mice, Fig. 5A and B). Of note, the spectral signature of ketamine-provoked seizures differed from the consistent abnormal activity in these mice, with a prominent delta activity and reduced power in higher frequency bands (Fig. 5B).

Magnesium sulfate was reported to have anti-epileptic properties, especially when combined with ketamine.8 Magnesium sulfate alone was also proposed to improve the clinical EEG of GRIN2D patients, likely acting by increasing the magnesium block of NMDAR.8,9,13 Therefore, we designed an experiment in which, following 2 h of baseline recordings, magnesium sulfate (50 mg/kg) was injected, and after two additional hours, still within the therapeutic range of magnesium sulfate, ketamine was given at 4 mg/kg. Magnesium sulfate did not cause convulsive seizures in Grin2dmut mice. However, quantitative analysis of the spectral properties demonstrated that magnesium sulfate decreased the contribution of the delta frequency range to the overall oscillations in both WT and Grin2dmut mice (Fig. 5E–H and Supplementary Fig. 3). As reduced delta power is a prominent feature in Grin2dmut mice, further reduction in this frequency band may offer a limited therapeutic effect. However, ketamine, despite its lower dose and the consecutive administration following magnesium sulfate, still caused convulsive seizures in three of the five mice that were tested (60%), with no further effect on the spectral properties in either WT or Grin2dmut mice (Fig. 5E–H and Supplementary Fig. 3). Finally, ketamine at a low dose (0.5 mg/kg) was reported to have a rapid ameliorating effect on the symptoms of major depressive disorder, specifically among patients carrying single nucleotide polymorphisms in GRIN2D.45 In Grin2dmut mice, ketamine at this dose did not cause seizures. However, it had no positive effect on the ECoG properties of Grin2dmut mice (Supplementary Fig. 4A and B). The effect of ketamine was also examined in entorhinal cortex slices from Grin2dmut mice. Consistent epileptic discharges were observed in all the slices in a magnesium-free and high potassium concentration (8 mM). Similar to the lack of effect in vivo, ketamine application (5 μM) had no effect on the epileptic activity ex vivo (Supplementary Fig. 4D–G). Together, these analyses suggested the lack of an acute therapeutic effect of ketamine in Grin2dmut mice, with a potential for pro-convulsive properties.

Memantine is another NMDAR open-channel blocker with high affinity to GluN2D-containing NMDAR.46,47 The binding site of ketamine and memantine on NMDAR is similar, but the effects on other ion channels and receptors differ. In addition to NMDAR, memantine also inhibits multiple types of acetylcholine receptor subtypes and the cation conducting 5-HT3 serotonin receptors.43,48 At 10 mg/kg, memantine had no significant effect in WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 5). In Grin2dmut mice, in contrast to ketamine, memantine did not provoke seizures. Moreover, it caused an increase in the contribution of delta range frequencies, also reducing the median frequency, yet with no direct effect on the abnormal activity at the theta and beta range (Fig. 6A–D). Despite that, memantine did not affect the motor abilities of Grin2dmut mice (Supplementary Fig. 5). Ex vivo, in entorhinal cortex slices from Grin2dmut mice, memantine (100 μM) showed a partial corrective effect, shortening the duration of the epileptic burst and reducing the overall duration of epileptic activity. Together, the positive effect of memantine on the contribution of the delta frequencies in Grin2dmut mice and the ameliorating effect ex vivo indicate a partial yet corrective effect of memantine.

Lastly, we tested the effect of the anticonvulsant phenytoin. The mechanism of action of phenytoin involves the inhibition of voltage-gated sodium channels, and T-type Ca2+ channels, with no direct effect on NMDAR, but the inhibition of Ca2+ channels can contribute to reduced glutamate release49-51 and suppression of spike-wave-discharge events in the ECoG of mice.52,53 Similar to the effect of memantine, acute administration of phenytoin (30 mg/kg) increased the contribution of the lower delta frequency band and reduced the median frequency of ECoG oscillations in Grin2dmut mice (Fig. 7A–D).

Together, our data indicates that quantitative analysis of brain oscillation can be a useful biomarker in GRIN2D, demonstrating an adverse effect of ketamine, and the partial corrective properties of memantine and phenytoin administration.

In patients, GRIN2D pathogenic variants are associated with severe developmental epileptic encephalopathy and poor prognosis. Here, we characterize a mouse model that recapitulates key phenotypes of this human neurodevelopmental disorder, presenting with profound mortality, sustained abnormal brain activity, impaired locomotor abilities, as well as hyperactivity and cognitive impairment, and altered neuronal and synaptic function in the cerebellum.

Examination of the developmental trajectory of the disease in Grin2dmut mice revealed the varying presentation of disease-related phenotypes at multiple ages. Premature death, low body weight and motor deficits were observed in immature mice and persisted into adulthood (Figs 1 and 2). Hyperactivity in a novel arena and abnormal ECoG activity were observed in juvenile mice at 4 weeks of age (Figs 2 and 4), but may also be presented earlier in life. Conversely, cognitive abilities seemed to decline progressively with age (Fig. 2H), indicating the potentially adverse effect of abnormal epileptic brain activity.54 On the other hand, focusing on balance and coordination functions through development, although Grin2dmut mice had reduced motor abilities throughout their development, older Grin2dmut mice improved their abilities to balance on the rotarod, and their improvement was comparable to that of WT mice, suggesting an impairment, yet with no stagnation or progressive loss of motor abilities (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Our electrophysiological investigations, focusing on cerebellar PNs, revealed several developmental changes in spontaneous firing frequency and synaptic response to NMDA. We focused on the cerebellum due to: (i) its role in motor coordination, motor learning and cognitive functions; (ii) its implication in multiple diseases, spanning from cerebellar ataxias Huntington’s disease, schizophrenia and neurodevelopmental disorders35,36,55; (iii) the levels of Grin2d mRNA are relatively high in PNs and molecular layer interneurons in adult cerebellum56-58; and (iv) spontaneous firing rates of PNs are modulated by cellular properties, as well as synaptic inputs, and were shown to be modulated in multiple animal models of neurological diseases, mostly featuring reduced spontaneous firing of these cells.25,35,59-61 In Grin2dmut mice, we observed developmental changes in spontaneous firing frequency. At the second week of life, in correlation with the observed motor deficits in Grin2dmut mice (Fig. 2A), the frequency of spontaneous firing was reduced (Fig. 3). Intrinsic firing properties at this age were overall similar between WT and Grin2dmut mice, indicating that the reduced spontaneous firing is governed by synaptic or structural mechanisms. The main morphological change we observed was a smaller PNs somata in Grin2dmut mice, yet with comparable membrane capacitance and dendritic morphology to WT mice. Smaller cell bodies were also observed in patients with autism and schizophrenia.62,63 As the soma increases its size over development, this structural difference may be related to developmental delay in Grin2dmut mice.64 Along this line, as the firing rates of PNs increase through development,65 reduced firing at 2 weeks of age may be attributed to developmental delay in these circuits (Fig. 3A and B). Interestingly, our recordings of PSCs did not reveal clear differences between 2-week-old WT and Grin2dmut mice, with similar frequencies of synaptic inputs, as well as comparable increased activity following bath application of NMDA (Fig. 3G–J).

In older mice, despite the persistence of behavioural deficits and abnormal brain activity (Figs 2 and 4), spontaneous firing rates of PNs were comparable between WT and Grin2dmut mice, possibly indicating homeostatic mechanisms that stabilize the firing rates of these neurons (Fig. 3). Conversely, we observed an age-dependent increase in the frequencies of PSC in response to bath application of NMDA, with no change in the amplitude or the baseline frequency of these inputs (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2), probably attributed to the effect of NMDA on synaptic release from presynaptic interneurons within the cerebellum that target PNs.66,67 Considering that the GRIN2D V667I pathogenic variant was shown to increase the open probability of GluN2D containing NMDARs,8 is it possible that the augmented response in Grin2dmut mice is mediated by increased NMDAR activity in cerebellar stellate or basket cells, which form inhibitory synapses with PNs and harbour NMDA channels containing the GluN2D subunit .66,68

Detailed examination of the hippocampal changes in Grin2dmut mice, performed by Teoh et al.,23 revealed dendritic and synaptic structural changes, elevated inhibitory tone onto CA1 excitatory neurons, as well as enhanced excitatory evoked response in the CA3 region. These intricate hippocampal changes are likely to contribute to the severe epileptic phenotypes in these mice.

Brain oscillations in Grin2dmut mice were characterized by distinct spectral properties with diminished power in the delta and gamma frequency bands and prominent activity in the theta, alpha, and beta frequency range at 4 weeks of age, and the theta and beta frequencies in older mice (Fig. 4). Does this age-related change in the contribution of the alpha frequency bands in Grin2dmut mice reflect an improvement in brain activity during maturation? This is one possibility. However, as the risk for premature mortality was not further reduced after the third week of life, spatial cognitive functions reduced over time (Fig. 2H), and the contribution of alpha oscillations decreased over normal development,69,70 we suspect that developmental compensatory correction of brain activity in Grin2dmut mice is limited.

The spectral alterations observed in these mice are not unique, and corresponding changes in spectral background ECoG properties were observed in other models of epilepsy and neurodevelopmental disorders.71-75 However, the unique feature of Grin2dmut mice is their sustained abnormal brain activity that lasted throughout the recording, such that discriminating between background and epileptic activity was overly challenging in Grin2dmut mice (Figs 4–7). This exceptionally sustained abnormal activity diverges this model from many other models of DEE, in which there are segments of normal or close-to-normal background activity with sporadic distinct sections of epileptic activity with a clear onset and termination.15

Notably, prominent abnormal rhythmic activity with peaks at the theta and alpha frequencies was also observed in a GRIN2D patient carrying the homologous GRIN2D pathogenic variant (Fig. 4), despite the lack of documented seizures.13 Thus, while it remains to be seen if this spectral signature is shared by other GRIN2D patients, it underscores the potential and accuracy of this mouse model and may serve as a clinically useful biomarker for future translational and therapeutic research. Interestingly, although increased sensitivity to seizures was observed in other GRIN models,16-22 such profound abnormal brain activity, as well as high mortality (with heterozygous mutations mimicking the human genetic condition), were not observed before. This is in line with the more severe and early-life clinical presentation in most GRIN2D patients,5 compared with other GRIN-related disorders.

The pharmacological interventions we tested demonstrated limited abilities to rectify the abnormal brain activity in Grin2dmut mice (Figs 5–7). In human GRIN2D-DEE patients, ketamine was reported to have anti-epileptic properties, especially when combined with magnesium sulfate.8 Nevertheless, in Grin2dmut mice, it had a pro-convulsive effect (Fig. 5). Furthermore, following seizure, in our hands, ketamine had no effect on the spectral properties of WT or Grin2dmut mice. Ketamine and memantine have similar binding sites and activities on NMDARs, as well as lower affinity to the mutant V667I GluN2D.8 However, ketamine caused seizures, while memantine did not. This difference may indicate that the pro-seizure activity of ketamine is governed by ketamine’s action on other types of ion channels or receptors.41-44

Magnesium sulfate, which is thought to involve the inhibition of NMDAR, was reported to have some anti-seizure properties and is well-tolerated.76 Our data show that magnesium sulfate reduced the power of the delta frequency band in both WT or Grin2dmut mice (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 3). However, as the contribution of delta power to the overall oscillations of Grin2dmut mice was lower at baseline, further reduction of this frequency band may represent a limited corrective effect on brain oscillations. Nevertheless, in Angelman syndrome, lower delta power correlated with better cognitive functions,77 indicating that additional parameters need to be considered when examining the overall efficacy of a specific pharmacological intervention.

Conversely, memantine and phenytoin increased the contribution of the delta frequency band, demonstrating partial correction of the ECoG oscillations of Grin2dmut mice (Figs 6 and 7). The effect of memantine was specific for Grin2dmut mice, with a lack of comparable memantine-induced delta power modulation in WT mice. Previous studies indicated that memantine can increase gamma oscillations,78,79 or reduce delta frequencies,80 further suggesting that the memantine-induced increase in delta power observed in Grin2dmut mice is specific to this model (Fig. 6), offering a partial corrective effect, which was also suggested based on clinical reports.8,9,13

Lastly, despite the vast difference in the mechanism of action, phenytoin had a similar effect to that of memantine in Grin2dmut mice, with increased delta contribution (Fig. 7). However, this effect was reported before,81,82 and is therefore unlikely to be specific for Grin2dmut mice. Together, these data indicate that drug-induced alteration in brain oscillations may be used as a potential biomarker in GRIN2D-DEE, providing a quantitative tool to test drugs or drug combinations. Here, this tool was applied to test the acute effect of drugs, but it can also be modified to test the effect of chronic treatments.

Nevertheless, one limitation of this approach is that the drugs were given acutely to the mice in contrast to chronic administered in humans. While this approach allowed us to assess the immediate effects of the drugs, chronic exposure could produce different results, via additional effects on gene expression or function over time, such that acute administration may not capture the full potential outcome associated with chronic use.

In conclusion, the new Grin2dmut mouse model recapitulates many features of the human disease: abnormal brain oscillations, susceptibility to convulsive seizures, profound premature mortality, and motor and cognitive deficits (see also Teoh et al.23). Moreover, we found neuronal and developmental changes in the activity of PNs in this model and highlighted the properties of background ECoG activity as a possible biomarker to quantify the effect of drug response, providing clear and quantitative readouts for further drug development.-
