# A review and analysis of the clinical literature on Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease caused by mutations in neurofilament protein L.
**Authors:** Stone EJ, Kolb SJ, Brown A
**Journal:** Cytoskeleton (Hoboken) (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1002/cm.21676](https://doi.org/10.1002/cm.21676)

## Content

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (CMT) is one of the most common inherited neurological disorders and can be caused by mutations in over 100 different genes. One of the causative genes is NEFL on chromosome 8 which encodes neurofilament light protein (NEFL), one of five proteins that co-assemble to form neurofilaments. At least 34 different CMT-causing mutations in NEFL have been reported which span the head, rod, and tail domains of the protein. The majority of these mutations are inherited dominantly, but some are inherited recessively. The resulting disease is classified variably in clinical reports based on electrodiagnostic studies as either axonal (type 2; CMT2E), demyelinating (type 1; CMT1F), or a form intermediate between the two (dominant intermediate; DI-CMTG). In this article, we first present a brief introduction to CMT and neurofilaments. We then collate and analyze the data from the clinical literature on the disease classification, age of onset and electrodiagnostic test results for the various mutations. We find that mutations in the head, rod, and tail domains can all cause disease with early onset and profound neurological impairment, with a trend toward greater severity for head domain mutations. We also find that the disease classification does not correlate with specific mutation or domain. In fact, different individuals with the same mutation can be classified as having axonal, demyelinating, or dominant intermediate forms of the disease. This suggests that the classification of the disease as CMT2E, CMT1F or DI-CMTG has more to do with variable disease presentation than to differences in the underlying disease mechanism, which is most likely primarily axonal in all cases.

Hereditary Motor and Sensory Neuropathy, more commonly known as Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (CMT), is one of the most common inherited neurological disorders (Shy et al. 2005a; Saporta and Shy 2013). CMT is estimated to affect an average of 1 in 2,500 people (Skre 1974), though this varies geographically (Barreto et al. 2016). The diagnosis is based upon clinical symptoms, electrophysiological studies and genetic testing. Generally, patients present in childhood with slowly progressive weakness and sensory disturbances in a distal-predominant pattern (Holmes and Hansen 1993; Pareyson et al. 2006). As the disease progresses, patients may develop foot deformities and gait disturbance (Tazir et al. 2014).

CMT is classified electrophysiologically by measuring nerve conduction velocity (NCV) and compound muscle action potential (CMAP), typically in the median nerve (Pease et al. 2007). Patients that exhibit low median NCV (generally attributed to myelin loss) and CMAP within the normal range are classified as CMT type 1, sometimes referred to as “demyelinating” CMT. Patients with normal median nerve NCV and low CMAP (generally attributed to axon atrophy and/or loss) are classified as CMT type 2, sometimes referred to as “axonal” CMT (Pareyson et al. 2006). If the electrodiagnostic results from patients fit neither type 1 or type 2 but lie somewhere in between, these patients are sometimes given the designation of DI-CMT, or dominant intermediate CMT (Davis et al. 1978; Pareyson et al. 2006). Additional subtypes of the disease have also been proposed, including CMT types 3, 4, recessive intermediate CMT (RI-CMT), and CMT type X. A summary of these disease classes, their inheritance patterns, electrodiagnostic and genetic nomenclature, and where they overlap is presented in Table 1.

Presently, there are more than 100 unique genes that have been implicated in CMT (Laura et al. 2019). Regardless of the population studied, approximately 1% of genetically diagnosed cases are caused by mutations in NEFL, variably classified as either type 2 (CMT2E), type 1 (CMT1F), or dominant-intermediate CMT, DI-CMTG (Sivera et al. 2013; Saporta et al. 2011; Murphy et al. 2012). NEFL encodes the low molecular mass neurofilament protein (NEFL), which is a component of neuronal intermediate filaments, also known as neurofilaments (Shaw 1998). Mature neurofilaments are heteropolymers consisting of up to five different subunit proteins, which can co-assemble in various combinations with variable stoichiometry (Perrot and Eyer 2013). These proteins are NEFL, NEFM and NEFH, often referred to as the “neurofilament triplet”, as well as α-internexin and peripherin (Yuan and Nixon 2011; Yuan et al. 2012).

All intermediate filament proteins, and therefore all neurofilament subunit proteins, share three major structural domains: a central alpha-helical “rod” domain flanked by unstructured amino-terminal “head” and carboxy-terminal “tail” domains (Herrmann and Aebi 2016). It is the differing lengths of the tail domains which account for the considerably different molecular weights of the neurofilament triplet proteins. The three domains are known to have different functions (Laser-Azogui et al. 2015). The head domain of neurofilament proteins, and especially that of NEFL, is thought to be critical for the initiation of neurofilament assembly (Heins et al. 1993; Nakagawa et al. 1995; Sun et al. 1997; Ching and Liem 1999). The rod domain makes up the backbone of the neurofilament polymer and is necessary for neurofilament integrity (Gill et al. 1990; Wong and Cleveland 1990; Chin et al. 1991). The alpha helices of the rod domains dimerize in parallel to form a coiled-coil quaternary structure (Eldirany et al. 2020). The basic subunit of the neurofilament is a tetramer, comprised of two coiled-coil dimers in a staggered antiparallel arrangement (Laser-Azogui et al. 2015; Eldirany et al. 2020). The tetramers associate laterally and through end-to-end interactions to form the filament backbone, which measures about 10 nm in diameter. The tail domains are highly charged and intrinsically disordered polypeptide chains known as sidearms that project several tens of nanometers outward from the filament backbone giving the filament a brush-like appearance. The sidearms create a zone of exclusion around the filaments, thereby maximizing the inter-filament spacing (Troncoso et al. 1990; Brown and Hoh 1997).

The principal known function of neurofilaments is as space-filling structures that contribute to the expansion and maintenance of axonal caliber, thereby increasing axonal conduction velocity (Perrot and Eyer 2013; Yuan et al. 2017). This accumulation is dependent on the axonal transport of neurofilaments into and along axons from their site of assembly in the neuronal cell body (Hoffman 1995). Live-cell imaging studies in cultured nerve cells have revealed that the filaments move along microtubule tracks in a stop-and-go manner, alternating between short bouts of rapid movement and prolonged pauses (Brown 2000; Brown 2014). In addition to their role in maintaining axon caliber, neurofilaments are presumed to contribute to the structural integrity of the axon and have been proposed to function as mechanical shock absorbers for axons subjected to mechanical stress (Kornreich et al. 2016) as well as scaffolds for anchoring of synaptic vesicles, endosomes, and endoplasmic reticulum within axons and synapses (Yuan et al. 2017). Axonal neurofilament proteins are very long-lived (Millecamps et al. 2007; Bott and Winckler 2020) but studies in cultured nerve cells have revealed surprising dynamics including intercalary subunit exchange and antagonistic regulation of polymer length by severing and end-to-end annealing mechanisms (Colakoglu and Brown 2009; Uchida et al. 2013).

The molecular mechanism by which mutations in NEFL cause disease is unknown. However, it is interesting that CMT can be caused both by dominantly inherited missense mutations as well as recessively inherited nonsense mutations, which appear to be null (Yum et al. 2009). This indicates that both the presence of mutated NEFL protein as well as the absence of wild type NEFL protein can cause disease. There are many reports that missense mutations in NEFL disrupt neurofilament assembly in cultured cells (Brownlees et al. 2002; Perez-Olle et al. 2002; Perez-Olle et al. 2004; Sasaki et al. 2006). Careful examination of a panel of such mutants in our laboratory revealed that all perturb the ability of the human NEFL to form homopolymers in a heterologous cell line, though it should be noted that homopolymers do not form in vivo. However, when co-expressed with other neurofilament proteins most of the mutants co-assembled to form neurofilaments (Stone et al. 2019). Whether or not a given mutant assembled into neurofilaments was dependent on what other neurofilament subunits were available for co-assembly. These studies do not exclude the possibility that a disruption or perturbation of neurofilament assembly or stability could underlie the disease mechanism, but they do suggest that to resolve this question it will be important to study the effects of the mutant proteins in an in vivo context, where the complete complement of neurofilament proteins are expressed at their normal endogenous levels.

Electron microscopy of sural nerve biopsies from patients with CMT caused by missense mutations in NEFL have revealed axon atrophy and loss, a paucity of neurofilaments in the atrophied axons, and occasional axonal swellings packed with neurofilament polymers (Fabrizi et al. 2004; Zuchner et al. 2004; Fabrizi et al. 2007; Benedetti et al. 2010; Doppler et al. 2017). These observations imply a problem with neurofilament organization or distribution along the axons. Electron microscopy of sural nerve biopsies from patients with the E210X nonsense mutation in NEFL have also revealed axon atrophy and loss, with an absence of neurofilaments throughout (Yum et al. 2009).

Further insight has come from mouse models. A transgenic mouse model expressing human NEFL with the P22S mutation exhibited a proximal-to-distal decline in neurofilament protein levels along the sciatic nerve without any axon loss, though puzzlingly this was not accompanied by changes in axon morphology or neurofilament density (Dequen et al. 2010). A transgenic mouse model expressing human NEFL with the E396K mutation exhibited aberrant neurofilament phosphorylation in motor neuron cell bodies, a reduction in the caliber of the largest myelinated axons in the sciatic nerve, and a 10% decrease in conduction velocity (Shen et al. 2011; Villalon et al. 2017). However, these mice exhibited only subtle changes in axonal neurofilament organization and no apparent reduction in axonal neurofilament content. In contrast, a knock-in mouse model of CMT bearing the N98S mutation in the endogenous mouse Nefl locus exhibited accumulations of neurofilaments in the cell bodies and/or proximal axons of motor and sensory neurons and an absence of neurofilaments in peripheral myelinated axons, resulting in a failure of these axons to expand in caliber (Adebola et al. 2015; Zhao et al. 2017; Lancaster et al. 2018). Functionally, these mice exhibit profound defects in the conduction velocity and amplitude of compound nerve action potentials (Lancaster et al. 2018). Myelinated axons are also devoid of neurofilaments and fail to attain normal caliber in the peripheral nerves of the Nefl−/− homozygous null “knockout” mouse (Zhu et al. 1997), which can be considered a model for recessive CMT caused by null mutations in the NEFL gene.

Collectively, these observations on patient nerve biopsies and mouse models suggest that a common feature in the etiology of the dominant and recessive forms of the disease may be a loss of neurofilaments from axons. In CMT caused by recessive nonsense mutations, neurofilaments are absent because they fail to assemble in the absence of NEFL protein (Lee et al. 1993; Ching and Liem 1993). How dominant missense mutations in NEFL result in the absence of neurofilaments from axons is less clear, but it seems likely to involve defective neurofilament assembly and/or transport.

NEFL is one of eight genes that give rise to more than one form of CMT disease (see Table 1). For mutations in NEFL the disease is variably classified as type 1, 2 or DI-CMT. Overall, 47% of the 78 published sporadic or familial cases of CMT caused by NEFL mutations were classified as CMT2E, 27% as CMT1F, 9% as DI-CMTG, and 17% were unspecified. In some cases, this variable classification is even encountered for different patients with the same mutation. For example, the P8R, P22S, N98S and E396K mutations have each been associated with all three forms of the disease (Fig. 1). Since the distinction between disease subtypes is based primarily on measurements of NCV and CMAP, the explanation for this variable classification appears to be the considerable variability in the electrophysiological manifestations of the disease. Specifically, we do not find evidence to support the idea that specific mutations in the NEFL protein map to specific forms of the disease. Thus, the variable classification of the disease as CMT2E, CMT1F or DI-CMTG is unlikely to reflect differences in the underlying pathological mechanisms.

The most common CMT-causing mutations in NEFL are missense mutations, however nonsense, frameshift, and deletion mutations have also been reported (Fig. 2) (Stone et al. 2019). The reported mutations span the entire length of the protein, although there are clusters at the N- and C-terminal ends of the rod domain and within the rod and head domains that may represent sites of particular vulnerability (Fig. 2B). For example, the C-terminal end of the rod domain contains an amino acid sequence motif that is conserved across all intermediate filaments and is believed to be important for the stability of the coiled coils (Wu et al. 2000). Where the inheritance pattern of the mutations is known, it is always autosomal dominant for the in-frame deletion and missense mutations and autosomal recessive for the nonsense mutations located in the rod domain. The recessive inheritance of these rod-domain nonsense mutations is thought to be due to the fact that they result in unstable truncated proteins that are functionally nulls (Yum et al. 2009). Individuals who are heterozygous for the null allele are asymptomatic, indicating that there is no haploinsufficiency. The two nonsense mutations that lie outside the rod domain, T21Xfs and R421X, are dominantly inherited, suggesting that they give rise to truncated proteins that are stable and dominantly alter neurofilament structure and/or function (Leung et al. 2006; Agrawal et al. 2014).

Clinical reports of CMT linked to mutations in NEFL are limited, often retrospective, and the specific data reported for each patient varies from study to study. The most consistently reported data are the ages of the patients at disease onset. The second-most consistently reported data are the electrodiagnostic measures used to classify the CMT subtype. Of these, the most commonly reported are the nerve conduction velocities (NCVs) and compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) from the median nerve, followed by the same measures in the ulnar nerve. To investigate whether there is any correlation between disease severity and mutation location, we collated and graphed these data from all published reports to date and plotted each measure versus position in the primary structure of the protein. Given the known importance of the rod domains for neurofilament assembly and integrity, we hypothesized that mutations in the rod domain might have the greatest disease severity.

The data from the published reports (39 publications representing data from 168 patients) were extracted and compiled into a single data set. When NCV and CMAP measurements were provided as a range or were reported separately for the left and right side, we used the average. When both motor and sensory NCV measurements were provided, the motor NCV values were used for this analysis. Some studies reported patient age in years and others in decades. To permit comparison, any ages reported in decades were converted to years by assuming the age to be the midpoint of that decade (e.g. a patient in their fifth decade was considered to have an age of 45 years). Any “less than” or “less than or equal to” values were included at their maximum values. If the paper recorded any electrodiagnostic data as “not evoked” or “no response”, those measures were considered to have a value of zero. The data were then categorized by type and either plotted versus mutation location within the amino acid sequence or grouped by structural domain. For the data grouped by domain we excluded nonsense and frameshift mutations that have unknown or autosomal recessive inheritance patterns due to the likelihood that those mutations produce truncated, unstable proteins; such mutations are believed to result in a null phenotype for which the site of the mutation within the protein does not have the same relevance as, for example, a missense mutation. The boundaries of the rod domain were based on sequence alignment with vimentin and the structural analysis of Herrman and Aebi (2004) as refined by Strelkov and colleagues (Chernyatina et al. 2015) as shown in Fig. 2.

Some clinical reports provided their own reference values for the normal or healthy range, but not all included this information and the ranges were not consistent. For our analysis, specifically in Figs. 5 & 6, we used the uniform reference values averaged for all ages reported by Chen et al. (2016). Since age can affect electrodiagnostic results, that study also provided reference values for different age groups. Given the limited amount of patient data available, we elected not to further subdivide the data by age. Reassuringly, however, we found that this was unlikely to bias our analysis because the range of ages of the patients at the time of testing was similar for each of the NEFL protein’s functional domains (Fig. 3). Therefore, on average, any differences in the data between the NEFL protein domains are unlikely to be attributable to differences in patient age.

Age of onset of disease symptoms was reported for 85% of the patients in the clinical reports, resulting in a total sample size of 142. In Fig. 4 this data is plotted versus mutation location within the protein (Fig. 4A) or binned by domain (Fig. 4B). It can be seen that the age range of disease onset is extremely broad, spanning more than 5 decades. This reflects the extraordinary variability in the clinical presentation of this disease. The rod domain mutations exhibit the greatest range (0.3–55 years, average = 15.0 years, n = 46 mutations), followed closely by the tail domain mutations (1–50 years, average = 13.6 years, n = 50 mutations). The head domain mutations exhibit a tighter range and lower average age of onset (2–25 years, average = 9.8 years, n = 46 mutations). It is important to keep in mind that the age of onset can be somewhat subjective because it often depends on the ability of patients and families to recall when symptoms (which are only later attributed to the disease) first arose. Nonetheless, the data suggest that mutations in the head domain of NEFL were associated with an earlier age of onset than mutations in the rod and tail domains, which may indicate that, on average, head domain mutations result in greater disease severity.

Fig. 5 shows the available NCV and CMAP data for the median nerve in CMT patients with NEFL mutations arranged by mutation location within the protein sequence (Fig. 5A,C) or binned by domain (Fig. 5B,D). NCV in the median nerve was reported for 65% of the patients, resulting in a total sample size of 109 (Fig. 5A,B). For these data, the head domain has only two patients within the normal range (above 49 m/s) (5%) and the rod domain has three (8%), while the tail domain has six (21%). The mean NCV values for all three domains are below the normal range with the head domain having the lowest average value (32.7 m/s), the tail domain having the highest (43 m/s), and the rod domain in between (37.2 m/s) (Fig. 5B).

CMAP in the median nerve was reported for 51% of the patients, which is somewhat fewer than for the NCV, resulting in a total sample size of 86 (Fig. 5C,D). For these data, which exhibit a much wider variability among healthy individuals, more than 50% of the patients are within the normal range (above 4.1 mV) for both the rod domain and tail domain mutations (60% and 55%, respectively), whereas only 13% are within the normal range for the head domain mutations (Fig. 5D). It is therefore not surprising that the median nerve average CMAP values for the rod and tail domain mutations are within the normal range. The mean CMAP values for each domain are: 2.2 mV for the head domain, 6.3 mV for the rod domain, and finally, 6.0 mV for the tail domain. A caveat for this NCV and CMAP analysis is that the measurements are not controlled for either age or degree of disease progression. However, the range of ages at the time of analysis is similar for the three domains (Fig. 3). Thus, the data show that mutations in all three domains can give rise to marked neurological deficits, but on average there is a trend towards greater disease severity for mutations in the head domain compared to mutations in the rod and tail domains.

To assess the consistency of the median nerve data when compared by domain, we also analyzed the data for the ulnar nerve, when available. Fig. 6 depicts the NCV and CMAP values for the ulnar nerve in CMT patients with NEFL mutations arranged either by mutation location within the protein sequence (Fig. 6A,C) or binned by domain (Fig. 6B,D). NCV in the ulnar nerve was reported for 49% of the patients, resulting in a total sample size of 83 (Fig. 6A,B). For these data, the head domain has only one patient in the normal range (above 52 m/s) (4%), the rod domain has two (6%), and the tail domain has four (15%) (Fig. 6B). Similar to the median nerve, the average ulnar nerve NCV values are below the normal range for all protein domains. These values were 34.3 m/s for the head domain, 36.9 m/s for the rod domain, and 38.0 m/s for the tail domain. Thus, the conclusion that the head domain mutations tend to have lower NCV values than the rod and tail domains is consistent between the two nerves tested, though the difference is more pronounced for the median nerve.

CMAP in the ulnar nerve was reported for 49% of the patients, resulting in a total sample size of 83 (Fig. 6C,D). For these measurements, the head domain has no patients in the normal range (above 7.9 mV) (0%), while the rod domain has nine (29%) and the tail domain has seven (26%) (Fig. 6D). The tail domain mutations have a particularly large spread in this data set due to two patients with recordings at the upper end of the normal range (Fig. 6D). In contrast to the median nerve, all the average CMAP values for the ulnar nerve are below the normal range for each domain. However, similar to the median nerve, the measurements were lower for the head domain (2.6 mV) than for the rod and tail domains (5.9 mV each). Thus, the NCV and CMAP recordings for both the median and ulnar nerves are lower for the head domain than for the rod and tail domains, implying that mutations in the head domain, on average, result in greater neurological deficits.

One final set of data collected from these clinical reports was the Charcot-Marie-Tooth Neuropathy Score (CMTNS), which was reported for 22% of the patients, resulting in a total sample size of 37. This scoring system, first introduced in 2005 (Shy et al. 2005b), is based on the Total Neuropathy Score (TNS) which measures the composite disability in length-dependent neuropathies (Cornblath et al. 1999). The TNS has been validated in both diabetic and toxic neuropathies, which are syndromes that result primarily in a reduction in sensory nerve function, so the CMTNS is adjusted to account for the motor nerve dysfunction typical in CMT (Shy et al. 2005b). The CMTNS scores patients on nine criteria, with each criterion receiving a rating between zero and four. The ratings for each criterion are summed for a total maximum score of 36. There are three criteria for sensory and motor symptoms, four for sensory and strength measurements, and two for electrodiagnostic assessments. Those patients with scores between 0 and 10 are considered “mild”, between 11 and 20 are considered “moderate”, and greater than 20 are considered “severe”. The utility of this measure is that it accounts for the impact of muscle weakness and atrophy on function, rather than relying solely on electrodiagnostic measurements, and it accounts for the length-dependent progression of disease symptoms (with regions innervated by the longest peripheral nerves being the most affected). We are aware that the CMTNS has been modified to improve sensitivity (CMTNSv2) (Murphy et al. 2011; Sadjadi et al. 2014) and superseded by newer clinical and functional outcome measures (Rossor et al. 2020). In addition, new biomarkers of disease progression such as serum neurofilament protein and muscle fat fraction show promise (Sandelius et al. 2018; Morrow et al. 2016). However, most of the clinical reports on CMT associated with mutations in NEFL either predate these more recent measures or do not report them, and only four studies have reported CMTNSv2 scores (total of 13 patients) (Hashiguchi et al. 2014; Pisciotta et al. 2015; Werheid et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2016), so we focus our analysis here on the original CMTNS score.

Fig. 7 depicts the available CMTNS scores arranged either by mutation location within the protein sequence (Fig. 6A,C) or binned by domain (Fig. 6B,D). The head domain has a sample size of only 5 patients, whereas the rod and tail domains have 19 and 13 patients, respectively. There is considerable spread in the data, particularly for the rod and tail domain mutations, ranging from mild to moderate for the head domain and from mild to severe for the rod and tail domains. The average CMTNS scores were 12.9, 12.2 and 14.1 for the head, rod and tail domains, respectively. Thus, on average mutations in all three domains result in CMTNS scores that indicate moderate disease severity. However, more patient data is clearly needed, particularly for the head domain.

We identified and analyzed the patient data for all CMT-causing mutations in NEFL that have been reported in the clinical literature. We observed no apparent correlation between the location of the mutation and the disease categorization; all three structural domains of NEFL (head, rod, tail) contained mutations associated with all three forms of the disease (CMT2E, CMT1F, DI-CMTG). Moreover, in six cases, different patients or families with the same mutation were classified as having different forms of the disease. These observations call into question whether CMT2E, CMT1F, and DI-CMTG are indeed distinct forms of CMT or simply different presentations of the same disease that may manifest differently in different patients depending on genetic and environmental factors. While to some extent this might seem to be a question of semantics, it can have very real consequences in terms of how we think about the mechanisms and treatment of this disease.

A source of confusion in the literature is that a significant proportion of cases of CMT caused by mutations in NEFL are classified as CMT type 1, a demyelinating form of the disease, even though NEFL is a neuronal protein and is not expressed in myelinating cells. The basis for this classification is an electrophysiological measure, specifically a slow median nerve NCV, which is considered indicative of demyelination. However, it is important to note that axonal conduction velocity is also influenced by other factors including axonal caliber (Waxman 1980). Thus, a widespread reduction in axonal caliber could also result in a reduction in NCV. Some precedent for this can be found in studies on the NeflN98S/+ mouse model of CMT2E (Adebola et al. 2015), which exhibits an impaired nerve conduction velocity that is due not to demyelination, but rather to a failure of the axons of the peripheral nerves to expand in caliber due to an absence of axonal neurofilaments (Lancaster et al. 2018). Importantly, these mice exhibit electrophysiological symptoms of CMT type 1, yet histology of the nerves indicates that they are myelinated normally. This highlights the limitations of using electrodiagnostic measures to classify the disease. Onion bulbs, a morphological feature of demyelination, have been reported in sural nerve biopsies from patients with CMT1F (Shin et al. 2008; Horga et al. 2017; Benedetti et al. 2010), but these could be a secondary consequence of axonal atrophy rather than a result of a primary demyelination defect. Thus, we propose that all CMT caused by mutations in NEFL is axonal in nature (i.e. CMT type 2) and should be classified as such.

The mechanism by which mutations in NEFL cause CMT is not known, but if it involved some specific structural feature of NEFL, then we might expect to observe a correlation between mutation location and disease severity. Given the known importance of the rod domain of neurofilament proteins for filament formation and integrity, we hypothesized that mutations in the rod domain might be associated with more severe disease. Our analysis revealed that there is considerable variation in age of onset, electrodiagnostic measures and CMT neuropathy score among different individuals with the same mutation and among mutations within each domain. However, mutations in the head, rod and tail domains are all capable of causing disease with early onset and profound neurological impairment in some patients, with a trend towards greater impairment for mutations in the head domain compared to the rod and tail domains, as assessed by these measures. Moreover, with the exception of the median nerve NCV, mutations in the tail domain are, on average, as severe as those in the rod domain. Thus, we do not find evidence to support the hypothesis that mutations in the rod domain cause more severe disease.

There are several caveats to this analysis. First, our subdivision of the NEFL protein into three domains is a simplistic view of the protein structure and ignores the likelihood that there are particular residues, motifs or sub-domains within each domain that are critical for certain specific and perhaps unknown functions of NEFL, and others which are less critical. For example, we noted above that there appear to be “hot spots” of mutations in the head and rod domains, such as in the highly conserved region at the C-terminus of the rod domain (Fig. 2). It is also possible that certain mutations could affect mRNA stability in ways that could alter protein expression. However, for the most part there are not sufficient number of patients with any given mutation to permit a more granular analysis.

A second caveat is that the most commonly encountered NEFL mutation, E396K, lies only a few residues outside the rod domain yet represents 70% of the published reports for tail domain mutations. If this mutation were considered to be part of the rod domain, this could change the results of our analysis for the tail domain. Unfortunately, it is not possible to test this because without the E396K mutation, there are only 2 or 3 patients with tail domain mutations for which we have CMAP, NCV and/or CMTNS data. However, we feel confident that the E396 residue should be considered part of the tail domain because the boundaries of the rod domain are defined by its coiled coil structure and the E396 residue disrupts the heptad repeat that defines that structure (Chernyatina et al. 2015).

A third caveat is that the data were not acquired in a controlled manner with standardized protocols, and thus there are undoubtedly differences in the data collection between reports that are sources of error in our analysis. For example, electrodiagnostic recordings are known to be highly susceptible to slight differences in factors such as skin temperature, electrode positioning, and the measurements of the length of the nerve (Mallik and Weir 2005). Nonetheless, such measures are widely used because they provide an objective and quantitative assessment of disease state.

Finally, a confounding challenge that applies to all forms of CMT is the profound heterogeneity of the disease phenotype between patients with the same mutation, even within family lineages (Boerkoel et al. 2002). In the case of CMT disease caused by mutations in NEFL, this likely reflects our poor understanding of neurofilament structure and function and the disease mechanism as well as of genetic and environmental factors that may modulate the disease severity for a given NEFL mutation. Ideally, there would be a prospective, comprehensive, natural history study for CMT patients across a range of mutations. This is challenging given the rarity of the mutations. Nonetheless, there is a pressing need to understand the causes of the diverse phenotypic manifestations of this disease in order to develop better diagnostic and prognostic capabilities.

As discussed above, there is conflicting evidence in the field regarding the extent to which CMT mutations in NEFL disrupt neurofilament assembly. Our finding here that mutations in the rod domain, which is critical for neurofilament assembly, are not associated with poorer electrodiagnostic measures, earlier age of onset or higher neuropathy score, underscores that this is still an open question. While the head domains have been implicated in the regulation of neurofilament assembly, the fact that mutations in the tail domains cause disease with comparable severity to mutations in the rod domain suggests that the disease mechanism may involve other properties of neurofilaments or other aspects of their function. One possibility is that mutations in different domains of NEFL may cause disease via different upstream mechanisms that converge on a common downstream event. For example, CMT could result from an absence of axonal neurofilaments but this could be caused by either a disruption of neurofilament assembly or by a perturbation of neurofilament transport. Another possibility is that disease is caused not by impairment of domain-specific functions of NEFL or neurofilaments but rather by general perturbations in protein folding or stability leading to pathological changes in neurofilament subunit stoichiometry, or to protein aggregation and cellular stress. Indeed, such mechanisms have been implicated in a large number of other neurodegenerative diseases (Wang and Kaufman 2016). A substantial impediment to distinguishing between these possibilities is that we still have a limited understanding of neurofilament biology and function, as well as a limited understanding of the effects of CMT mutations on NEFL and neurofilaments in vivo. Such studies need to be a priority moving forward.
