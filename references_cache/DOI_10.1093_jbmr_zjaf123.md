# Fractures are highly correlated with bone density and inversely correlated with bone turnover markers in autosomal dominant osteopetrosis
**Authors:** Michael J Econs, Stuart J Warden, Ziyue Liu, Paul J Niziolek, Corinne Parks-Schenck, Netsanet Gebregziabher, Rita L Gerard-O'Riley, Marian Hart, Lynda E Polgreen, Erik A Imel
**Journal:** Journal of Bone and Mineral Research (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/jbmr/zjaf123](https://doi.org/10.1093/jbmr/zjaf123)

## Content

Abstract
Autosomal dominant osteopetrosis (ADO) is a rare osteosclerotic disorder usually caused by missense variants in the CLCN7 gene, which results in impaired osteoclastic bone resorption. Penetrance is incomplete, and disease severity varies widely, even among relatives within the same family. Although ADO can cause visual loss, osteonecrosis, osteomyelitis, and bone marrow failure, the most common complication of ADO is fracture. We are conducting a natural history study to characterize disease progression and determinants of disease severity. We hypothesized that baseline BMD and bone turnover markers would correlate with self-reported fracture history. We report cross-sectional analysis of baseline data from the natural history study in 54 individuals (42 adults, 12 children). In adults, Z-scores for both volumetric (r = 0.87, p &lt; .001) and areal BMD (aBMD) of the LS, and Z-scores for FN, and TH aBMD (r = 0.77 to 0.78; p &lt; .001) were correlated with lifetime fracture number. Tartrate resistant acid phosphatase, a marker of osteoclast number, correlated positively with fracture (r = 0.52, p = .004) consistent with an adaptive response of higher numbers of osteoclasts among more severely affected individuals. However, fracture number correlated inversely with the bone resorption markers serum C-telopeptide (r = −0.60, p &lt; .001) and urine N-telopeptide/creatinine ratio (r = −0.35, p = .047), suggesting that ADO subjects who have the most reduced osteoclast activity have a greater tendency to fracture. Correlation coefficients between fractures, BMD, and bone turnover markers were similar when limited to the 37 adults with disease-causing CLCN7 variants. There were no statistically significant differences between subjects with the most common CLCN7 variant (G215R), the most common variant in our cohort, compared to other CLCN7 variants with respect to fracture, bone density measures, or biochemical markers of bone turnover. These data demonstrate that bone density and biochemical bone turnover markers are indicators of ADO severity as defined by fracture number.

Autosomal dominant osteopetrosis (ADO) is a rare genetic bone disorder characterized by abnormally dense but fragile bones. The severity of the condition can vary significantly, even among individuals within the same family. This natural history study aims to better understand the progression of ADO and identify factors that influence disease severity. Unlike osteoporosis, individuals with ADO who exhibit the highest bone density are paradoxically at the greatest risk for fractures. Furthermore, lower levels of biochemical markers of bone turnover were associated with both increased bone density and a higher incidence of fractures. Importantly, the severity of ADO did not correlate with the specific genetic variants responsible for the condition. In summary, ADO patients with the highest bone density have the most fractures. These findings suggest that both bone density and biochemical markers of bone turnover can serve as meaningful clinical endpoints in future trials evaluating potential therapies for ADO.

Autosomal dominant osteopetrosis (ADO, also known at Albers Schonberg or “marble bone” disease) is the most common form of osteopetrosis, with an incidence of approximately 1:20 000.1,2Autosomal dominant osteopetrosis occurs due to impaired osteoclastic bone resorption and is usually caused by heterozygous loss-of-function missense variants inCLCN73,4and, rarely,TCIRG1.5The defective osteoclast activity leads to dense but brittle bones. However, penetrance is incomplete, with approximately one-third of individuals withCLCN7variants being nonpenetrant carriers.6The mechanism of nonpenetrance remains unknown and disease severity varies widely, even within the same family.

Autosomal dominant osteopetrosis can cause visual loss, osteonecrosis, osteomyelitis, and bone marrow failure, but the most common complication is fracture.6,7There are currently no medical therapies for ADO. Management is symptomatic, limited to surgical repair of fractures, dental procedures and antibiotics for osteonecrosis and osteomyelitis, as well as bone marrow supporting therapies and transfusions as needed for bone marrow failure. Recent studies in ADO mouse models point to potential novel therapeutic targets for this disease.8–10With new therapies on the horizon, it is important to define and understand markers of current disease severity, predictors of future disease severity, and potential surrogate endpoints for clinical trials.

The relationship between bone density and fractures is well established in osteoporosis; however, the relationship in high bone mass disorders is less established and likely varies by condition. In osteoporosis, lower bone density is associated with higher risk of fractures. In some high bone mass disorders, such as sclerosteosis, the higher bone density appears to be protective against fractures. In contrast to osteoporosis, osteopetrosis is characterized by skeletal fragility in the setting of high bone density.2,6,7

We are conducting a longitudinal natural history study to characterize disease progression and determinants of disease severity using multiple modalities in individuals with ADO. The current data represent a cross-sectional analysis of baseline data from the natural history study. Based on clinical observations that individuals with ADO having severe symptoms tend to have highest bone density, we tested the hypothesis that ADO subjects with higher bone density have greater numbers of self-reported fractures than those with lower bone density. We also sought to test the hypothesis that persons with ADO with lower bone turnover markers would have more fractures than those with higher turnover.

All individuals in this report are participants in an ongoing longitudinal observational natural history study of ADO, designed to collect longitudinal data to determine the natural progression of the disease over time. Subjects were recruited in a variety of different ways. Many were known to the investigators due to participation in prior studies. Some were recruited after seeking advice about their disease or by finding out about the study through social media or web-based searches, while others were referred for clinical care.

All individuals in the current analysis attended a study visit for sample collection and measurements at the Indiana University Clinical Research Center and the Function, Imaging and Testing Core of the Indiana Center for Musculoskeletal Health. A variety of health information is collected, including medical records (when available), medical history, genetics, physical exams, and radiographic images. Images include plain radiographs, DXA for areal bone density (aBMD) of the hip and spine, and computed tomography for volumetric bone density (vBMD) of lumbar vertebrae 1–3 (L1–3). Some of the participants in this study also participated in a separate online registry assessing patient-reported outcomes, with the results reported separately.11

Criteria for study participation were deliberately broad to allow for a wide spectrum of disease severity. Inclusion criteria were adults and children ≥1 yr old with a diagnosis of ADO either by gene testing or by radiographic features combined with family history. Carriers (nonpenetrant individuals without radiographic findings of ADO) having ADO-causing pathogenic variants were also included.

Exclusion criteria were defined as the presence of any condition that, in the opinion of the investigators, would interfere with the collection or accuracy of data, or would put the patient at excessive risk of injury or harm from participation. No subjects met exclusion criteria.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Indiana University. All study subjects (or their parents for participants under age 18) gave written informed consent, and assent was obtained from all participants who were 7–17 yr old.

Due to the expected normal variation in bone density values by age, sex, and race, Z-scores were calculated for bone density measurements. Volumetric BMD was measured in subjects over 10 yr of age using a helical CT scanner (Ingenuity 128 Channel and iCT 128 Channel CT scanners; Philips Medical Systems), as previously described.12A calibration phantom was included containing calcium hydroxyapatite (HA) standards embedded in water-equivalent resin (QCT-Bone Mineral Phantom; Image Analysis, Inc.). In brief, the subject was supine with the calibration phantom underneath them. CT imaging was performed with continuous axial slices from L1 through L3 based on scout images. CT imaging parameters used included 120 kV, 300 mAs, 2 mm slice thickness, and no dose modulation. Image analysis was performed manually with the center axial slice of each vertebral body (L1, L2, and L3) selected and manual region of interest drawn within the vertebral body. The linear relationship between Hounsfield Units and densities of the standards within the phantom were used to determine voxel density values (mgHA/cm3). Z-scores for spine QCT were calculated based on data from Cann et al.,13with Z-scores for subjects below 20 yr of age extrapolated from the curve fit for the Z-scores.

Spine, TH, and FN aBMD was assessed using either a Norland Elite (Norland at Swissray) or Hologic Horizon A (Hologic Inc.) scanner. The manufacturers’ recommended imaging protocols were utilized. Sites containing orthopedic metal were not imaged. Z-scores obtained in adults (age ≥18 yr) and aBMD obtained in children (age < 18 yr) using the Norland Elite scanner were converted to Hologic Horizon equivalent values using regression formulae derived by scanning 45 individuals on each scanner, including 15 individuals with ADO. Height-adjusted Z-scores in children were calculated from the converted aBMD values using reference data from the Bone Mineral Density in Childhood Study.14

Fracture history was self-reported by participants and recorded by study personnel. Lifetime fracture number was calculated by counting individual fractures up to their enrollment in the natural history study, excluding finger and toe fractures. As the baseline fracture reports included all lifetime fractures, we were not able to obtain radiographs to verify reported fractures.

Bone turnover markers were measured for serum C-telopeptide (Serum CrossLaps (CTX-I) ELISA, Immunodiagnostic systems), serum N-telopeptide (OSTEOMARK NTx Serum, Zeus Scientific), and urine NTX/Creatinine ratio (OSTEOMARK NTx Urine, Zeus Scientific). We also measured serum Tartrate resistant acid phosphatase 5b (BoneTRAP (TRAcP 5b) ELISA, Immunodiagnostic systems), which is a marker of osteoclast number.

Genetic testing was performed primarily using commercial panels (28 of 54 subjects using the Invitae Skeletal Disorders Panel, which screens for 358 different genes for skeletal disorders, Invitae Corp.; and 13 subjects using various other commercial panels measured clinically before study enrolment and provided from the patient records). An additional 11 subjects underwentCLCN7testing conducted in the principal investigator’s laboratory as part of prior studies. Two did not have genetic testing at the time of data analysis.

In general, numeric variables were summarized by descriptive statistics, including minimum (min), first quartile, median, third quartile, maximum (max), mean, and SDs. The variables were compared between groups by Student’st-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test depending on their normality, which were assessed by Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and QQ-plot. Categorical variables were summarized by counts and percentages and were compared between groups by Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Given a small sample size of pediatric participants (N= 12) and the wide variation in the normal range in outcome markers (particularly bone turnover markers) between children of different ages, the primary analyses focused on adult participants. However, summary data from pediatric participants are provided in theresults. The associations among total fracture numbers, bone density measurements, and bone turnover markers were quantified by Spearman correlations and scatter plots, given the highly skewed distributions of total fracture numbers and potentially nonlinear associations. Since the total fracture numbers accumulate with age and may depend on sex, the age- and sex-adjusted partial Spearman correlations were presented as the main results. Spearman correlations without adjustments for age and sex were also calculated, which were similar to the adjusted ones and not presented. Williams’t-test was used to compare correlations sharing a common variable. We also compared phenotypic traits between G215R, which is the most commonCLCN7pathogenic variant in our cohort, and all otherCLCN7pathogenic variants.

To test for genotype/phenotype correlations, we compared fractures and bone density in adult subjects with confirmedCLCN7variants (N= 37), excluding patients who did not have genetic testing, and the three previously reported subjects with ADO from aTCIRG1variant,5by comparing the most commonCLCN7variant (G215R,N= 14) to the otherCLCN7variants (N= 23, across 10 different variants inCLCN7: 5 R286W, 4 R762W, 4 R767W, 3 L213F, 1 for each of F318L, G292R, R286Q, S290F, Y99N, S317P, and a 22 bp deletion in exon 22, which spanned the exon intron boundary). Subjects with the G215R variant did not have a statistically significant increase in fractures compared to those with other variants (p= .362). There were no statistically significant differences between G215R and the other variants for Z-scores of spine QCT (p= .554), DXA of LS (p= .271), FN (p= .966), or TH (p= .898). Similarly, there were no statistically significant differences for any of the bone turnover markers.

In this study, higher BMD Z-scores were positively associated with higher numbers of reported fractures (as a marker of disease severity) in adults with ADO. Autosomal dominant osteopetrosis is an “osteoclast rich” osteopetrosis, where there are increased numbers of osteoclasts15(as indicated by TRAP), but the osteoclasts have impaired ability to resorb bone (as indicated by bone resorption markers). Previous studies by our group demonstrated that this decreased resorption was due to osteoclast impairment rather than the bone microenvironment and that osteoclasts from carriers, who also have the sameCLCN7disease-causing variants but are without apparent clinical disease, resorb bone normally in culture.16In the current study, individuals with the highest bone density—and presumably the greatest impairment in osteoclastic bone resorption—also had the highest fracture rate. This concept is further supported by the bone resorption marker data (at least in regard to serum CTX), where CTX was inversely correlated to both bone density and fractures. Conversely, the serum TRAP, which is a marker of osteoclast number, was directly correlated to bone density and fracture. Of note, greater increases in serum TRAP in the more severely affected individuals may indicate that these individuals “sense” that their bone density is too high and increase osteoclast number in an attempt to increase resorption.

Our findings are consistent with the concept that ADO patients with the most severe impairment in osteoclast activity will have the highest bone density, the greatest number of fractures, and the most severe clinical disease. In this regard, potential therapies that increase biochemical markers of osteoclast activity and decrease bone density are likely to decrease fractures and, potentially, the rates of other complications of ADO, such as bone marrow failure.

This study has implications for clinical trial design to test potential therapies for ADO. Our data are consistent with the concept that therapies that increase markers of bone resorption, such as CTX, and decrease BMD are likely to rescue the ADO phenotype and that these could be used as surrogate endpoints in a clinical trial. Numerically, correlation between fracture and spine QCT vBMD was the greatest, although this was not statistically significant from the correlations with DXA aBMD measures. Additionally, the range of Z-scores for spine QCT was twice that of DXA measures. Therefore, spine QCT may be the better surrogate endpoint. Additionally, CTX may be very useful in a shorter-term dose finding study.

Our study also has implications for the clinical care of these patients. Clearly ADO patients with very high densities and low bone turnover are at markedly increased risk for fracture and should be counseled appropriately. Conversely, patients with normal bone density may not be at increased risk for fracture. For those with bone density, Z-scores in the +2 to +5 range the risk appears less clear but intermediate, while fracture occurrence does appear to be even higher at Z-scores >+5. The associations between Z-scores are monotonic, making establishment of “cutoffs” difficult. Additionally, establishing a “cutoff” below which patients are unlikely to fracture would require a validation dataset. The importance of this study is in establishing a strong relationship between high BMD and fracture in osteopetrosis, opposite of the relationship in osteoporosis.

There are limited studies in the literature with which to compare our results. Benichou et al.7studied 42 ADO patients and found that 78% had fractures and over half required orthopedic procedures. However, they did not report bone density or biochemical markers in their study. Thus, it is not possible to determine whether there was an association between these variables and fracture. We previously conducted a cross-sectional study in 94 subjects withCLCN7pathogenic variants (62 affected and 32 nonpenetrant carriers), which demonstrated high fracture rates in affected individuals.6Fracture occurred in 53% of affected children and 98% of affected adults and “severe fractures” (defined as 10 or more fractures of any type and/or greater than one hip/femur fracture) occurred in 16% of children and 49% of adults.6Although a subset of study participants in our previous study had DXA measured, the numbers were too small to analyze for correlation to fractures. However, there was a difference between spine aBMD Z-score in carriers (2.41; range: −0.4 to 4.61) and affected individuals (9.84; range: 5.9–12.4), with the latter group having substantially more fractures than carriers. These findings are consistent with the present study. Del Fattore et al.17studied both recessive and dominant osteopetrosis and found that TRAP concentrations were elevated in all osteopetrotic patients consistent with increased osteoclast number compared to controls, but they did not correlate these values with fracture and did not report BMD. Butscheidt et al.18retrospectively studied 8 ADO patients withCLCN7variants and 8 subjects with high bone mass but withoutCLCN7variants. Mean Z-scores on DXA were +10 to +11. Six of the eight patients had fractured; however, they also did not compare fractures to BMD.

For the subset of adults with confirmedCLCN7variants, the number of fractures was not statistically significantly different between the most common variant in our cohort, G215R (N= 14), vs otherCLCN7variants (N= 23), and there were no differences between individuals with G215R variant and other variants for any bone density variable. It should be noted that although power was limited to detect differences between genetic variants, there was great variation in phenotype between subjects with the same disease-causing variant, even among members of the same family. This marked variation between members of the same family has consistently been observed by us and others.6,7,16Therefore, for practical purposes, such as genetic counseling, there is no evidence for a strong genotype-phenotype association in ADO.

Our study has several limitations. It has a cross-sectional design with people of different ages with different lengths of time to fracture in their lifetime, although there was not a statistically significant correlation between age and fracture. Because we did not have access to medical records or radiographs for most subjects, we used self-reported fractures, which introduced the risk of over and under reporting.19–21Furthermore, we used bone turnover markers instead of direct assessment of bone histomorphometry to assess bone turnover. However, bone biopsies in ADO patients are difficult to perform and ADO patients are at increased risk of infection. Therefore, we have avoided bone biopsies in persons with osteopetrosis. Strengths of our study include a sample size of 54 subjects, which is large compared to most similar studies in osteopetrosis. Additionally, the effect sizes that were obtained led to clear-cut, statistically significant evidence of association. Indeed, our cohort may be the largest ADO cohort with in-depth phenotyping in the world. Additionally, our study encompasses a range of ADO genotypes and a range of disease severity as indicated by both fracture rates and bone densitometry.

Our results demonstrate that in adults with ADO bone density, as measured by QCT of the spine or DXA of the spine and hip, is strongly associated with fracture and that biochemical markers of bone turnover are inversely correlated to fracture. These data are consistent with the concept that bone density and biochemical bone turnover markers are indicators of disease severity and indicate that bone turnover markers and bone density may be useful as surrogate endpoints in clinical trials aimed at testing potential therapeutics for efficacy.
