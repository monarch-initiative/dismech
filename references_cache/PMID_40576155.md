# Neuronal oscillatory imbalances in GNAO1-related disorders associated with disease severity.
**Authors:** Wang T, Domínguez-Carral J, Ludlam WG, Segarra MJ, Marti MF, Bruining H, Martemyanov KA, Linkenkaer-Hansen K, Ortigoza-Escobar JD
**Journal:** Epilepsia (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/epi.18513](https://doi.org/10.1111/epi.18513)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: This study investigates excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) imbalances in GNAO1-related disorders (GNAO1-RD), linking neuronal dysfunction to clinical severity using E/I-sensitive electroencephalography (EEG) analyses.

METHODS: We conducted an observational study involving 12 children with GNAO1-RD caused by pathogenic variants and 36 age-matched, typically developing children (TDC). EEG was recorded during eyes-closed rest. Clinical evaluations included scales for epilepsy, movement disorders, motor and language development, and an overall clinical severity score. Molecular assessments of GNAO1 variants used bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) assays. Quantitative EEG measures included spectral power, aperiodic exponent, long-range temporal correlations (LRTCs), and functional E/I (fE/I) ratio. Statistical analyses incorporated permutation tests and cluster-based enhancements.

RESULTS: Children with GNAO1-RD exhibited elevated delta power and reduced alpha power compared to TDC. Higher delta power correlated with more severe epilepsy and pronounced molecular dysfunction, whereas lower alpha power was associated with overall clinical severity. Stronger alpha- and beta-band LRTCs were observed in GNAO1-RD, reflecting altered network dynamics. Reduced alpha-band fE/I ratios suggested a network state dominated by inhibition, potentially compensating for hyperexcitability. Developmental differences were evident, as the age-related decreases in delta power observed in TDCs were absent in GNAO1-RD.

SIGNIFICANCE: This study identifies quantitative EEG abnormalities in GNAO1-RD, characterized by increased delta power, decreased alpha power, and disrupted network dynamics indicative of an inhibition-dominant state. These findings align with molecular dysfunction caused by GNAO1 variants, highlighting the role of GNAO1 in maintaining E/I balance. The results provide neurophysiological insights into GNAO1-RD pathophysiology and suggest potential biomarkers for assessing disease severity and therapeutic interventions.

The GNAO1 gene (OMIM*139311) encodes a subclass (Gαo) of the Gα subunit of a heterotrimeric guanine nucleotide‐binding protein expressed predominantly in the brain. This protein plays a crucial role in regulating neuronal excitability and neurotransmission.
1
 Pathogenic variants in GNAO1 are associated with a spectrum of both epileptic and non‐epileptic phenotypes, ranging from mild to severe developmental delay, hypotonia, and movement disorders. Despite the fact that affected individuals are identified with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy 17 (DEE‐17) (OMIM#615473) or neurodevelopmental disorder with involuntary movements (OMIM#617493), we currently prefer the term GNAO1‐related disorders (GNAO1‐RD) to encompass the mild or atypical phenotypes. To date, more than 282 cases have been documented.

 Understanding how the function of GNAO1 cascades into neurodevelopmental disturbances is essential for developing targeted interventions. A central focus is the excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) ratio within neural networks, which has been studied in other DEEs like STXBP1‐related disorders and various monogenic neurodevelopmental conditions.

 Imbalances in the E/I ratio at the synaptic level are known to impact neuronal network organization and information processing, with disruptions contributing to the pathogenesis of neurodevelopmental disorders.

, 
12
 Given the essential role of GNAO1 in neuronal circuit formation and neurotransmission, pathogenic variants in this gene are likely to disrupt the E/I balance at the synaptic level.

However, the relationships among molecular and cellular deficits, neuronal circuit disturbances, clinical symptoms, and treatment responses remain complex and not fully elucidated. Quantitative electroencephalography (qEEG) analyses offer a promising approach to exploring these network‐level alterations and may help bridge this knowledge gap.
8
 Genetic disorders such as GNAO1‐RD provide a valuable model for investigating how specific genetic alterations influence E/I dynamics, thereby offering insights into neurodevelopmental processes and their implications for therapeutic strategies.
4
, 
10
 By combining spectral power analysis with long‐range temporal correlations, the aperiodic exponent, and the functional E/I (fE/I) ratio, qEEG can further elucidate the neurophysiological underpinnings of GNAO1‐RD. Different levels of E/I studies may yield varying results due to homeostatic interactions within cellular and network topologies. Our study aims to apply these concepts to GNAO1‐RD by investigating neuronal network imbalances related to distinct oscillatory processes and their correlation with clinical severity, thus contributing to a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms of GNAO1‐RD.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institut de Recerca Sant Joan de Déu, Barcelona, Spain (PIC‐134‐23 and PIC‐40‐24). Written informed consent was obtained from all guardians of participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

We recruited 12 individuals with GNAO1‐RD from the ongoing study “Prospective and retrospective study of phenotypic and genotypic characterization of individuals affected by GNAO1‐related disorders” at the Hospital Sant Joan de Déu, Barcelona, Spain. EEG recordings were obtained retrospectively from the long‐term video‐EEG monitoring (lasting from 6 to 72 h) performed as part of this study.

For comparative purposes, EEG was prospectively recorded from 36 carefully selected, age‐matched, typically developing children (TDC), with three controls selected for each GNAO1‐RD individual to ensure comparable age distribution across groups. Subsequently, EEG analyses were conducted by comparing the GNAO1‐RD and TDC cohorts at the group level, without relying on individual patient–control matching. TDCs underwent video‐EEG due to syncope or other paroxysmal non‐epileptic disorders or as part of a polysomnography for suspected obstructive sleep apnea syndrome or other non‐neurological indications. The demographic and clinical characteristics of TDCs are summarized in Table S1. A brief interview was conducted with TDCs prior to inclusion to exclude known or suspected neurodevelopmental disorders, learning disabilities, or ongoing follow‐ups with neurologists, psychiatrists, or early intervention services.

For GNAO1‐RD children, we conducted a comprehensive clinical assessment to evaluate characteristics such as epilepsy, movement disorders, motor development, and language development. Clinical measures included the Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM‐88) and the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Second Edition (Vineland‐II), for motor and overall development. We also used the recently developed GNAO1‐RD severity score
6
 to quantify the severity of the disorder. Molecular characterization of pathogenic missense variants in the GNAO1 gene was performed using bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) assays. These assays allowed us to evaluate five categories: Gαo expression, heterotrimer formation, loss‐of‐function, dominant negative activity, and receptor interaction. Each category was assigned a score from 0 to 3, where 0 represented no deviation from wild‐type values, and 3 indicated the most significant deviation. The scores of these five categories were then integrated into the experimental measurement score (EMS), ranging from 0 to 15. Detailed BRET methodology is described in Dominguez‐Carral et al.
6



Eyes‐closed rest EEG recordings lasting 2–15 min were analyzed. These recordings were obtained using BrainQuick Software, version 1.01.0013 (Micromed SpA), with 19 electrodes (Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, F7, F8, T3, T4, T5, T6, Fz, Cz, Pz) placed according to the 10–20 system and referenced to the average of all electrodes.

EEG data were processed using MNE‐Python
13
 and custom‐made scripts. Preprocessing included filtering, downsampling, noisy channel identification and interpolation, re‐referencing, and artifact removal. A detailed description of the preprocessing steps is provided in the Appendix S1.

Power spectral density (PSD) was computed for all channels using Welch's method (10‐s Hamming windows, 50% overlap). The length of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) was determined as the sample frequency divided by .1 to obtain a frequency resolution of .1 Hz for all recordings. Five conventional frequency bands were defined: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–45 Hz). Relative power was calculated as the power in a frequency band as a percentage of the total power between 1 and 45 Hz, expressed as a percentage.

To investigate whether the temporal structure of brain oscillations was altered in children with GNAO1‐RD compared with TDC, long‐range temporal correlations (LRTCs) in the amplitude modulation of oscillations were calculated using detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA).
14
, 
15
 First, we obtain the amplitude envelope by taking the absolute value of the Hilbert transform. The envelope was then demeaned, and its cumulative sum was calculated. The cumulative sum of the envelope time series was split into 50% overlapping windows. A linear trend was fitted and removed from each window, and the fluctuation of the resulting signal was computed as the standard deviation (SD) per window. The average fluctuation was calculated as the mean (SD) across windows. This process was repeated for windows of different logarithmically spaced time scales between 5 and 30 s. The DFA exponent was defined as the exponent of the least‐squares linear fit of mean fluctuation over time scales on a log–log scale.

For the spectrum of LRTCs in the frequency domain, the EEG signal at each channel was decomposed into frequency bins of .5 Hz using a multi‐taper filter to minimize spectral leakage.
16
 The DFA exponent was then computed for the envelope time series at each frequency bin. For LRTCs in a specific frequency band, the EEG signal at each channel was filtered within the specified frequency band before computing the DFA exponent.

The network excitation‐inhibition ratio was quantified using the fE/I ratio, an algorithm derived from the Critical Oscillations (CROS) model of ongoing neuronal activity.
17
, 
18
 fE/I ratio is estimated from the covariance of amplitude and a fluctuation function that correlates strongly with the DFA exponents. At each window, the amplitude is calculated as the mean of the envelope of a narrow frequency band (e.g., alpha band), whereas the fluctuation is calculated as the SD of the cumulative sum of the envelope. fE/I ratio is calculated based on the correlation between the windowed amplitudes and fluctuation calculated as 1 minus the correlation. A balanced network activity (fE/I ≈ 1) indicates no correlation between amplitude and fluctuation. A positive correlation (fE/I < 1) suggests an inhibition‐dominated state, whereas a negative correlation (fE/I > 1) reflects an excitation‐dominated state. A detailed account of the fE/I algorithm in relation to a broad range of frequencies can be found in ref. [19].

The power spectrum of neural data consists of a 1/f‐like aperiodic component. The aperiodic component has a 1/fβ decay of power with frequency, where β is the aperiodic exponent that determines the slope of the power spectrum. Recent findings indicate that the aperiodic exponent decreases with age during early and middle childhood.
20
 We used the FOOOF algorithm
21
 to extract the aperiodic exponent within the frequency range of 1 to 45 Hz. The average fitted r
2 value was .99, indicating a satisfactory fit.

It also describes the use of Fisher r‐to‐z transformation and z‐tests to compare independent correlations, ensuring robust analysis.
22
,

,

 Statistical analyses were performed using custom Python scripts. In all analyses, statistical significance was set at p < .05, with p values between .05 and .07 considered marginally significant.

For comparison, we recruited 36 age‐matched typically developing children (TDC) with an age range of .5–15.1 years (16 female, 8.0 ± 4.2 years). Ten GNAO1‐RD children had missense variants in the GNAO1 gene, which were analyzed in our previous work.

 Seven of the 12 children had epilepsy and were taking antiseizure medications when EEG was performed, and all experienced movement disorders and motor and language developmental delay. In contrast, the GMFM‐88 and the Adaptive Behavior composite score from Vineland‐II, where lower scores indicate greater severity, show a correlation with EEG markers in the opposite direction (Figure 1C,G,O).

A strong negative correlation between age and both relative (r = −.83, p < .001) and absolute power (r = −.49, p = .002) in the delta band is observed in the TDC cohort. Of interest, this age‐related decrease of absolute delta power is not significant in children with GNAO1‐RD (r = −.44, p = .15) (Figure 1D,H). A direct test of the difference between the correlation coefficients revealed a strong trend (p = .053). The reduced age‐related decrement of delta power in GNAO1‐RD could explain the higher delta power observed in this group (Figure 1A,B,E,F).

Aside from EEG power, the aperiodic exponent has been found to correlate with age in many studies. In our study, we observed age‐related correlations among aperiodic exponents in both GNAO1‐RD and TDC. However, no statistically significant difference was found between the correlation coefficients (Figure 2A,B). In addition, the higher delta power (Figure 1A,B,E,F) and lower alpha power (Figure 1M–P) observed in children with GNAO1‐RD are unlikely to be caused by a steeper 1/f trend or a larger aperiodic exponent in the power spectrum, given that these differences are non‐significant. Figure S1 presents representative power spectra for both groups and clearly illustrates the frequency peaks, helping to differentiate between the effects of a steepened 1/f component and delta activity.

To further investigate this intriguing crossover phenomenon, we computed the ratio of DFA exponents in the high‐beta band (20–30 Hz) to the low‐gamma band (30–45 Hz) between GNAO1‐RD and TDC. Figure 3J illustrates that children with GNAO1‐RD exhibit a near‐equal ratio of DFA exponents, whereas TDC show lower ratios, indicating that the DFA exponent for beta oscillations is generally lower than for gamma oscillations. This pattern is altered in GNAO1‐RD, with more pronounced alterations correlating with higher experimental molecular scores (Figure 3K).

To determine whether excitation or inhibition dominates brain network activity in GNAO1‐RD, we assessed the functional excitation/inhibition ratio using the fE/I algorithm (see methods). Whereas the TDC showed a balanced network activity of alpha oscillations (fE/I = .94 ± .03), the children with GNAO1‐RD presented with inhibition‐dominated dynamics of alpha oscillations (fE/I = .79 ± .03, p = .01) (Figure 4A,B). Beta oscillations had similar and balanced dynamics in both groups (Figure 4D,E). Neither in the alpha nor the beta band did we observe significant correlations with clinical scores (Figure 4C,F).

This study examined the EEG of 12 children with GNAO1‐RD compared to 36 age‐matched, typically developing children (TDC). Key findings include a correlation between elevated delta power and severity at both the clinical and molecular level, alongside impaired age‐related decreases in delta power, indicating disrupted neurodevelopmental maturation. These findings highlight the potential neurophysiological indicators for disease severity and provide insights into how GNAO1 variants impact brain network dynamics, informing diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

In TDCs, alpha power increases with age, reflecting maturation of cortical inhibition, and is most prominent during relaxed wakefulness.
28
 Conversely, delta power tends to decrease as children grow, as it is associated with slower brain rhythms typical of early development and deep sleep.

Our study reveals significant alterations in EEG dynamics in GNAO1‐RD, including increased delta power and reduced alpha power, alongside an impaired age‐related decline in delta power, which suggests altered neuronal processing. In addition, the blunted decrease in delta power with age suggests disruptions in neuronal maturation processes. This aligns with a recent study on patient‐derived induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) with the p.G203R variant, which showed impaired functional activity, including loss of spontaneous Ca2+ transient activity and lower neurotransmitter responsiveness, suggesting a slower maturation of the molecular machinery necessary for proper synaptic connections.
7
 In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the aperiodic components between GNAO1‐RD and TDC, indicating that GNAO1 pathogenic variants have a minimal impact on EEG background activity compared to oscillatory dynamics. These EEG findings are consistent with previous research showing that deviations in spectral power may reflect underlying pathophysiological processes associated with DEE.
8
, 
18
 Notably, the EEG abnormalities were consistent across the cohort, similar to observations in STXBP1‐related disorders, suggesting that these patterns are linked intrinsically to the mechanisms underlying GNAO1‐RD, despite genetic variability. However, further investigation is needed to determine whether these findings reflect specific pathophysiological processes unique to GNAO1‐RD or represent broader disruptions in neurophysiological development. In addition, their potential as biomarkers for distinguishing between typical and atypical brain maturation warrants further exploration. Moreover, to assess the sensitivity of delta and alpha power alterations to disease progression, longitudinal studies tracking EEG alongside clinical changes (e.g., seizure frequency, movement disorder episodes, or developmental milestones) would be necessary.

LRTC refers to the temporal structure of fluctuations in brain activity, reflecting the complexity and self‐organization of neuronal networks.
15
, 
29
, 
30
 In our study, children with GNAO1‐RD displayed significantly stronger LRTCs in the alpha and beta bands compared to TDCs, suggesting altered oscillatory dynamics and reduced network flexibility. LRTCs in resting‐state cortical activity are widely recognized as a hallmark of normal functioning of the brain
30
, 
31
; therefore, their exaggeration in GNAO1‐RD may indicate impaired network flexibility. Although previous studies have associated elevated LRTCs with epilepsy,
32
 we did not find a correlation between LRTC strength and epilepsy severity in our cohort. However, the tendency for epilepsy may contribute to the generation of more complex neural activity patterns than typically seen. Research using iPSCs has revealed that Gαo regulates neuronal morphogenesis, polarity acquisition, and functional connectivity organization in the postnatal brain. Defects in Gαo expression during development can impair neuronal firing and trans‐synaptic communication.
33
 Increased LRTCs may arise from intermittent interictal spiking activity, burst‐suppression patterns, or hypsarrhythmia, but they are also observed in profoundly inhibition‐dominated brain states, such as anesthesia and coma.
8
, 
34
 Of interest, in our cohort, the qualitative review of EEG recordings from Patients P4 and P6, described a pattern during sleep characterized by periods of diffuse low‐voltage activity alternating with nearly normal slow activity—independent of epileptiform abnormalities—resulting in a fragmented EEG appearance. These findings underscore the potential of DFA ratios as candidate EEG markers of abnormal neural dynamics in GNAO1‐RD.

Our findings of lower E/I ratios in the alpha band among children with GNAO1‐RD compared to TDC suggest a predominance of inhibitory activity in their neuronal networks. These lower ratios may reflect underlying disruptions in γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic signaling, similar to those identified in animal models of various neurodevelopmental disorders and epilepsy, where elevated GABA or reduced glutamate levels and increased depolarizing GABAergic signaling may contribute to the E/I imbalance.
35
 A comparable pattern is seen in autism spectrum disorder, where children with EEG background slowing also exhibit reduced fE/I in the alpha band.
18
 Studies on a Gnao1 cerebellum mouse model with a monoallelic loss‐of‐function mutation (G203R) have revealed reduced frequencies of spontaneous and miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents in Purkinje cells, whereas excitatory postsynaptic currents remained unaffected.
36
 These results support the idea that GNAO1 variants may disrupt normal inhibitory processes in both cortical and cerebellar neurons, contributing to the altered neuronal dynamics seen in the mouse model and human subjects.

This study identifies EEG patterns in GNAO1‐RD, including increased delta power, decreased alpha power, and altered LRTC and fE/I ratio, which suggest altered neuronal dynamics. However, these findings are preliminary, and their potential as neurophysiological indicators for disease severity requires further validation. Not all correlations were statistically significant, and these markers lack specificity, as similar patterns have been observed in other forms of DEEs. Future studies with larger cohorts and direct comparisons to other conditions are needed to confirm these observations. Of interest, although EEG markers showed stronger associations with epilepsy severity, they were less correlated with movement disorder severity. We hypothesize that the difference may be due to greater involvement of subcortical or cerebellar circuits in movement disorders, which might not be as directly reflected in cortical EEG. Future research exploring EEG patterns during movement disorder episodes (e.g., dyskinetic crises or dystonia) may provide additional insights into the role of EEG biomarkers in reflecting the severity of movement disorders in GNAO1‐RD. Moreover, the observed trends between EEG parameters and experimental measurement scores suggest a possible link to disease severity at a molecular level; however, this relationship remains to be confirmed in larger studies. If validated, these markers might eventually contribute to assessing responses to future treatments.

Factors influencing brain maturation, such as neuronal connectivity and neurotransmission, are crucial for understanding the EEG alterations observed in neurodevelopmental disorders. GPCRs (G protein–coupled receptors) are pivotal in regulating neuronal connectivity and synaptic signaling. Gαo, the protein encoded by GNAO1, acts as a key signaling mediator for multiple GPCRs, modulating pathways that regulate synaptic strength, neurotransmitter release, and the formation of neuronal circuits.

, 
37
,

,

,

 In GNAO1‐RD, pathogenic variants disrupt Gαo‐mediated signaling, leading to altered neural network dynamics and contributing to the distinctive EEG abnormalities observed in affected individuals.
33
 Both structural and functional connectivity are impaired in GNAO1‐RD. Structural plasticity disruptions may involve impaired neurite outgrowth or retraction and abnormal synaptic connectivity.
33
 Meanwhile, synaptic dysfunction—manifesting as altered synaptic strength and neurotransmission—disrupts neural communication efficiency.
7
 This molecular disruption aligns with the EEG phenotype observed in GNAO1‐RD, particularly the altered delta and alpha power, highlighting the link between synaptic abnormalities and altered neuronal oscillations.

Although few studies have comprehensively compared neural circuit flexibility across different DEEs, our findings in GNAO1‐RD can be considered alongside existing data on other genetic DEEs, such as STXBP1‐RD.
8
 Both GNAO1‐RD and STXBP1‐RD exhibit significant alterations in quantitative EEG; however, the specific patterns differ. In STXBP1‐RD, changes in the E/I ratio emerge at higher frequency ranges and involve different aspects of the 1/f component of the power spectrum, which are not prominent in GNAO1‐RD. In contrast, GNAO1‐RD is characterized by increased delta power and decreased alpha power, reflecting global slowing of brain activity and a predominance of inhibitory states. Circuit‐level alterations, prevalent in movement disorders such as those in GNAO1‐RD, may also shape these EEG findings. Disruptions in basal ganglia‐thalamocortical loops, common in dystonia and chorea, could enhance inhibition‐dominated states (lower fE/I, Figure 4), by reducing cortical oscillatory input from subcortical structures. Similarly, increased network rigidity (stronger LRTCs, Figure 3) might reflect inflexible motor circuit dynamics, whereas persistent delta power could stem from immature or aberrant connectivity in these networks. These differences underscore the necessity for further research to elucidate the specific effects of various genetic DEEs. Subcortical recordings or movement‐specific analyses may help elucidate how the motor phenotype in GNAO1‐RD influences brain maturation, network stability, and E/I balance.

Although our study offers valuable insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings, and additional research with larger cohorts is desired to confirm these results. In addition, transient artifacts, noise, and the inclusion of interictal discharges (IEDs) in the EEG analyses—deliberately retained to capture the full spectrum of the GNAO1‐RD phenotype—may affect the accuracy of spectral measures, particularly in the delta and alpha bands. Due to the short duration of the recordings, we were unable to analyze IED‐free segments. Furthermore, the potential influence of pharmacological treatments on EEG patterns was not controlled for and represents another limitation.

This study represents an initial step toward identifying EEG patterns as potential neurophysiological indicators for GNAO1‐RD, pending validation in larger and comparative studies. The observed decrease in alpha power and increase in delta power, together with altered LRTCs and fE/I ratio, point to a brain state dominated by inhibition and a reduced network flexibility. This pattern may indicate dysfunction in synaptic plasticity and neural communication. By associating EEG patterns with severity at both clinical and molecular levels, the study offers an integrated perspective on how altered brain dynamics correlate with disease severity. This enhanced understanding could ultimately support the development of targeted therapeutic strategies and improve clinical management.
