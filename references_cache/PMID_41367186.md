# Risk factors for shoulder dystocia-related brachial plexus injuries: A case-control study.
**Authors:** Heinonen K, Saisto T, Gissler M, Sarvilinna N
**Journal:** Acta Obstet Gynecol Scand (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/aogs.70116](https://doi.org/10.1111/aogs.70116)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Brachial plexus injury (BPI) is one of the major complications associated with shoulder dystocia. This study aimed to identify risk factors for shoulder dystocia-related BPI (SD BPI) in the Finnish population.

MATERIAL AND METHODS: The study included all deliveries in the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa between 2006 and 2015 (n = 181 352). Singleton deliveries complicated by shoulder dystocia, BPI, and clavicle fracture were identified using ICD-10 codes O66.0, P13.4, P14.0, and P14.1 (n = 1708). Following a thorough review of medical records, 374 cases of BPI were identified, 259 of which were related to shoulder dystocia. The background-matched control group (n = 566) consisted of singleton deliveries without BPI.

RESULTS: The incidences of both BPI with and without shoulder dystocia declined significantly during the study period, from 0.16% to 0.05% and from 0.09% to 0.03%, respectively. Multivariate analysis identified the following independent risk factors for SD BPI: birthweight ≥4000 g (adjusted odds ratio, aOR 15.3), maternal diabetes (aOR 4.0), vacuum extraction (aOR 3.7), and maternal height ≤160 cm (aOR 2.7). A predictive model based on these four risk factors demonstrated a positive predictive value of 48.2% and a negative predictive value of 92.9% for SD BPI cases. The odds of permanent BPI were 2.5-fold for cases associated with shoulder dystocia compared to those without. Antepartum and intrapartum variables were not predictive of permanent SD BPI. Among all BPI cases, 69.3% were associated with shoulder dystocia and 64.2% with birthweight ≥4000 g.

CONCLUSIONS: Birthweight ≥4000 g, maternal diabetes, height ≤160 cm, and vacuum extraction independently increased the risk of SD BPI. Notably, 14.7% of all BPI cases occurred in the absence of both shoulder dystocia and birthweight ≥4000 g.

The purpose of this study was to identify antepartum and intrapartum risk factors for shoulder dystocia‐related BPI (SD‐BPI) and evaluate their predictive value for this complication. Additionally, we aimed to identify potential differences between SD‐BPI cases and BPI cases without shoulder dystocia, as well as between permanent and transient SD‐BPI cases.

A total of 181 352 deliveries in the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa between 2006 and 2015 were included in this register‐based study. As outlined in the flow chart (Figure 1), we identified singleton deliveries complicated by shoulder dystocia, BPI, or clavicle fractures using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes O66.0, P13.4, P14.0, and P14.1 from the Finnish Medical Birth Register and the Hospital Discharge Register, both maintained by the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare. This search yielded 1708 deliveries. Diagnosis codes for the children were traced up to the age of 1 year. These registers, known for their high data quality,

, 
29
 include all live births and stillbirths with a gestational age of ≥22 weeks or a birthweight of ≥500 g, as well as all inpatient and outpatient hospital episodes. Since 1996, the ICD‐10 classification has been used in Finland without major revisions that could influence our results.

In Finland, all neonates are examined by a pediatrician before discharge from the delivery hospital or earlier, if required. Pediatricians are responsible for diagnosing newborn conditions. BPI is diagnosed through physical examination, with typical signs including an asymmetrical Moro reflex or impaired movement of an upper extremity. Other potential causes for these symptoms, such as clavicle or humeral fractures, are excluded. In our hospital district, if a pediatrician suspects or diagnoses BPI after delivery, a follow‐up is arranged at a specialized multiprofessional unit responsible for treating BPI cases, including those classified as permanent. In this study, permanent BPI was defined as BPI persisting at the age of 1 year.

After a detailed review of the medical records of the 1708 identified parturients by clinically experienced obstetricians (K.H., T.S., and N.S.), 374 BPI cases were confirmed. Of these, 259 were related to shoulder dystocia, while 115 occurred without any indications of shoulder dystocia or other difficulties in their deliveries, according to medical records. Data collection for cases and controls was conducted using a standardized Excel file, and the assessors discussed the criteria for shoulder dystocia both prior to and during data collection to ensure as uniform data quality as possible. Each case of shoulder dystocia was assessed by one of the authors. In cases of uncertainty, three authors discussed to reach a final agreement. The definitions and diagnostic features of shoulder dystocia are given in detail in our previous publication.
30
 All 374 BPI cases were diagnosed before the newborn was discharged from the hospital. From the total of 1708 parturients, 131 cases were excluded due to P13.4 and/or P14.0 diagnoses being associated with cesarean sections, stillbirths, breech deliveries, or insufficient or incorrect data. The remaining 1203 parturients showed no evidence of BPI in their medical records. The control group consisted of 566 singleton vertex deliveries with none of the above‐mentioned ICD‐10 codes. These controls were matched to the originally identified 1708 parturients, as the number of verified BPI cases was unknown prior to the medical record review. The matching was based on the year of the index birth, mode of delivery, parity, maternal age, and gestational age. A thorough review of the medical records for these 566 controls revealed no evidence of missed diagnoses.

We analyzed maternal characteristics and potential antepartum and intrapartum variables by comparing SD‐BPI cases (n = 259) to controls (n = 566). We further examined antepartum and intrapartum variables, as well as neonatal complications, by comparing SD‐BPI cases to BPI cases without shoulder dystocia. Additionally, permanent SD‐BPI cases were compared to transient ones. No forceps deliveries were recorded in our study population; therefore, we use the term vacuum extraction rather than instrumental vaginal delivery. All collected body mass index (BMI) values represent pre‐pregnancy BMIs. Maternal diabetes refers to both gestational diabetes mellitus and pre‐pregnancy diabetes. A prolonged second stage of labor was defined as ≥60 min, as obstetricians in Finland typically evaluate the situation after 60 min of active, midwife‐led second‐stage labor. All episiotomies were performed prior to the delivery of the fetal head.

For categorical variables, the chi‐square test, the test of relative proportions, and Fisher's exact test were used to assess statistical significance. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non‐normally distributed continuous data, while the independent samples t‐test was used for normally distributed continuous data, with birthweight being the only normally distributed variable. To evaluate the statistical significance of trends in incidences, the test for relative proportions between two samples was employed. Crude odds ratio (OR) values with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are presented for the univariate analysis. For multivariate analysis, logistic regression analysis was conducted to calculate the adjusted odds ratio (aOR). In addition, negative and positive predictive values (NPV and PPV) were calculated for the variables. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with the area under the curve (AUC) was used to assess the diagnostic performance of the multivariate model. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28).

Figure 2 illustrates the incidences of SD‐BPI (total and permanent) and BPI without shoulder dystocia among all deliveries in the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa from 2006 to 2015. The overall incidence of SD‐BPI decreased significantly from 0.16% to 0.05% (p < 0.001), while the rate of permanent SD‐BPI declined from 0.03% to 0.01%, though this reduction did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.08). Concurrently, the incidence of BPI without shoulder dystocia decreased by two‐thirds, from 0.09% to 0.03% (p = 0.009).

Intrapartum variables for SD‐BPI cases and controls are shown in Table 2. SD‐BPI cases had 1.7‐fold odds of labor induction and 2.6‐fold odds of vacuum extraction compared to controls. The mean duration of the first stage of labor was significantly longer in the SD‐BPI group (556 vs. 472 min, p < 0.001), as was the second stage of labor (34 vs. 24 min, p < 0.001). Prolonged second‐stage labor was also more common among SD‐BPI cases (OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.7–4.0). The odds of episiotomy were significantly higher for SD‐BPI cases (OR 2.4, 95% CI 1.8–3.3). Mean birthweight was notably higher for SD‐BPI infants (4223 vs. 3582 g, p < 0.001). Additionally, the rates of infants weighing ≥4000 and ≥4500 g were significantly elevated in the SD‐BPI group compared to controls, with ORs of 9.9 (95% CI 7.0–13.9) and 21.6 (95% CI 10.6–44.1), respectively.

A multivariate analysis was conducted, including dichotomous antepartum and intrapartum variables identified as significant risk factors in univariate analyses (maternal height ≤160 cm, BMI ≥30, maternal diabetes, labor induction, vacuum extraction, prolonged second stage of labor, use of oxytocin, and birthweight ≥4000 g). Four variables remained independent risk factors for SD‐BPI in the logistic regression analysis: maternal height ≤160 cm (aOR 2.7, 95% CI 1.7–4.1), maternal diabetes (aOR 4.0, 95% CI 2.4–6.6), vacuum extraction (aOR 3.7, 95% CI 2.5–5.6), and birthweight ≥4000 g (aOR 15.3, 95% CI 10.2–23.0). A predictive model based on these variables demonstrated a PPV of 48.2% and an NPV of 92.9% for SD‐BPI cases. A ROC analysis showed an AUC of 0.85 (95% CI 0.82–0.87).

We also compared permanent SD‐BPI cases to transient SD‐BPI cases (Table 5). No statistically significant differences were found in either antepartum or intrapartum variables between the groups. Notably, there was no statistically significant difference in the use of obstetric maneuvers (OR 1.1, 95% CI 0.6–1.9) or in cases where more than three maneuvers were used (OR 1.5, 95% CI 0.6–3.4). Among neonatal complications, permanent SD‐BPI cases had a significantly lower risk of clavicle fractures (OR 0.3, 95% CI 0.1–0.7), but rates of other complications were similar. Regarding maternal complications, no significant differences were observed. The mean head‐to‐body delivery interval did not differ significantly between groups (260 vs. 184 s, p = 0.26), although this interval was reported for only six (10.2%) of the permanent cases and 22 (11.3%) of the transient cases.

Among all BPI cases, 69.3% were associated with shoulder dystocia, while 64.2% had a birthweight exceeding 4000 g. Furthermore, 48.1% of cases involved both shoulder dystocia and birthweight ≥4000 g, whereas 14.7% occurred without either of these risk factors. We performed the same analyses for antepartum and intrapartum variables on all BPI cases (including those without shoulder dystocia) as were conducted for SD‐BPI cases, comparing them to controls. The results remained consistent with those previously presented in Tables 1 and 3, with no changes observed after including BPI cases without shoulder dystocia.

The incidences of both SD‐BPI and BPI without shoulder dystocia decreased over the study period. Birthweight ≥4000 g, maternal diabetes, maternal height ≤160 cm, and vacuum extraction were independently associated with an increased risk of SD‐BPI. SD‐BPI cases had 2.5‐fold odds of becoming permanent and were more often associated with vacuum extraction and birthweight ≥4000 g. No significant differences in antepartum or intrapartum variables were observed when permanent SD‐BPI cases were compared to transient SD‐BPI cases. The majority of BPI cases were associated with shoulder dystocia (69.3%) and birthweight ≥4000 g (64.2%), while 14.7% (55/374) occurred in the absence of both factors.

The declining rates of SD‐BPI (from 0.16% to 0.05%) and BPI without shoulder dystocia (from 0.09% to 0.03%) observed in this study were lower than the previously reported incidence rates of BPI in the general obstetric populations in Finland and other countries, which range from 0.04% to 3.3%.

There are, however, important considerations regarding the diagnosis of shoulder dystocia. First, although shoulder dystocia must be treated as a dichotomous variable (yes or no) for research purposes, the clinical reality is not always so clear‐cut. As demonstrated in our previous publication,
30
 diagnosing shoulder dystocia is complex and inevitably includes a degree of subjectivity. Moreover, one‐author assessment based on medical records also carries an element of interpretation, even though it offers more detailed insight than studies relying solely on diagnosis codes. These diagnostic challenges inherently influence the findings related to BPI.

Our comprehensive dataset encompassed all deliveries in Finland's largest hospital district over a 10‐year period. The number of BPI cases included was relatively high for such a rare complication. To ensure the accuracy of the data, we meticulously reviewed all medical records for BPI cases and their matched controls, allowing us to reliably confirm BPI diagnoses and distinguish cases associated with shoulder dystocia from those that were not. This detailed approach enabled us to compare SD‐BPI with BPI cases without shoulder dystocia, providing new insights into this phenomenon.

However, our study has some limitations. Its retrospective design, while well‐suited to studying a rare obstetric complication such as BPI, inherently limits the ability to draw causal inferences. For example, the exact mechanism of BPI without shoulder dystocia and its possible differences to the SD‐BPI mechanism remain unknown. Additionally, any register data may be subject to coding errors and incomplete documentation. For example, maternal weight gain was recorded in only 23% of cases in this dataset and therefore could not be reliably used as a risk factor. The matching of controls precluded an assessment of maternal age, gestational age, and parity as potential risk factors. Moreover, we were unable to evaluate if shoulder dystocia is a risk factor for BPI, as controls and BPI cases without shoulder dystocia were, by definition, not exposed to it. Finally, diagnosing shoulder dystocia or identifying it from medical records always involves some degree of subjectivity,
30
 which may have affected our results.

SD‐BPI cases share risk factors with shoulder dystocia, including birthweight ≥4000 g, maternal diabetes, vacuum extraction, and maternal height ≤160 cm. Antepartum and intrapartum variables, including the use and number of obstetric maneuvers, cannot reliably predict permanent SD BPI. A minority (14.7%) of all BPI cases occurred in parturients without both shoulder dystocia and a birthweight exceeding 4000 g.
