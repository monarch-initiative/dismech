GOALS: To clarify the characteristics of desmoid tumors in Japanese patients 
with familial adenomatous polyposis after colectomy.
BACKGROUND: Few comprehensive reports have been published on desmoid tumors in 
Asian patients with familial adenomatous polyposis.
STUDY: This retrospective study included the data of 81 patients with familial 
adenomatous polyposis who underwent surgery between 1978 and 2021. The 
adenomatous polyposis coli gene mutation sites, risk factors, and long-term 
outcomes associated with desmoid tumors in Japanese patients with familial 
adenomatous polyposis after colectomy were analyzed.
RESULTS: No association was observed between the gene mutation sites and desmoid 
tumor development in 40 patients who underwent genetic analyses. The rate of 
desmoid tumor development was 30.3% in 66 patients. Multivariate analysis 
revealed that age below 32 years at colectomy (hazard ratio = 5.491, 95% 
confidence interval 1.820-16.50, P < 0.001) and familial adenomatous 
polyposis-related malignancies other than colorectal cancer (hazard ratio = 
5.574, 95% confidence interval 2.075-14.98, P < 0.001) were independent risk 
factors for desmoid tumor development following colectomy. The 10-year 
disease-specific survival and overall survival rates for desmoid tumors were 
92.9% and 76.9%, respectively. The median surveillance duration was 90 months.
CONCLUSIONS: Adenomatous polyposis coli gene mutation sites alone were not 
considered a factor for delaying or avoiding colectomy to prevent desmoid tumors 
in Japanese patients with familial adenomatous polyposis. The timing of 
colectomy and careful surveillance should be considered for managing patients at 
a high risk of developing desmoid tumors. Desmoid tumors in patients with 
familial adenomatous polyposis did not significantly impact prognosis, and 
pharmacological treatments are important for disease control.

Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is the most common inherited polyposis syndrome, which is caused by germline mutations in the adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) gene on the long arm of chromosome 5 in band q21.1,2 It is accompanied by various benign and malignant extracolonic manifestations, including gastric and duodenal tumors, osteomas, retinal pigmentation, thyroid tumors, and desmoid tumors (DTs).1–4 DTs are rare, nonmetastatic, benign soft tissue tumors characterized by aggressive fibroblast proliferation in an abundant collagen extracellular matrix.5,6 The incidence of DTs among patients with FAP is ∼850 times higher than that among the general population; moreover, 10% to 25% of the patients with FAP develop at least one DT in their lifetime.7–10 FAP-related DTs have a higher prevalence of intra-abdominal location, risk of recurrence, and mortality rate than sporadic DTs.11,12 Although DTs do not metastasize, they demonstrate aggressive local invasion, which can be life-threatening.13 Therefore, DTs are associated with significant morbidity and mortality and are the second most common cause of death among patients with FAP.14,15 Although their development and growth are associated with surgical trauma, genetic predisposition, and hormonal factors,16,17 few detailed studies have been conducted on the development of DTs in Asian patients with FAP, compared with Westerners. In particular, a possible relationship between specific APC gene mutations (codons 1445 and 1578) and DT development has been reported in Westerners.17–20 Whether APC gene mutation sites may be a critical risk factor for DT development remains controversial,8,20–23 and this relationship has not been investigated in detail in Asians. In addition, only a few studies have reported the long-term outcomes of DTs in patients with FAP even in Westerners. Therefore, we aimed to clarify the APC gene mutations sites, risk factors, and long-term outcomes associated with DTs in Japanese patients with FAP after FAP-colectomy, including total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis (TC-IRA), restorative proctocolectomy with ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (RPC-IPAA), and total proctocolectomy with ileostomy (TPC-I) in this study.

Data were collected from the institutional database of the Hamamatsu University School of Medicine between January 1978 and April 2021 and analyzed retrospectively. Patients of all ages with FAP who underwent colectomy were included. FAP was defined as the presence of APC gene mutations or >100 colorectal adenomas. DTs were diagnosed based on pathologic findings or a combination of clinical course, surgical findings, and radiologic evaluation, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).20,24 In Japan, genetic testing for APC mutation is not performed in all cases because genetic verification of FAP is uncommon and not covered by health insurance. Only patients who consented to genetic testing were eligible for analysis for the APC gene mutation sites. In the analysis for risk factors of DTs and long-term outcomes, cases with incomplete data were excluded as diagnosing and evaluating the clinical course of DTs requires detailed data. A total of 81 patients with FAP who underwent surgery were enrolled. Consequently, 66 patients from 48 pedigrees who underwent analysis for risk factors of DTs and long-term outcomes and 40 from 37 pedigrees who underwent analysis for APC gene mutations were analyzed (Fig. 1). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Hamamatsu University School of Medicine (IRB number: 17–132). The need for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Flowchart of patient selection.

Patients who underwent genetic counseling provided informed consent for the APC genetic testing. We analyzed the noncoding region of APC promoter 1B, all coding exons of APC, and their border regions. Multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification method was used to analyze relatively large exon-level deletions and duplications.

The standard institutional surgical procedure for patients with FAP has been described previously.25 The DTs were evaluated based on the location, tumor size, growth rate, and symptoms.26 Intra-abdominal DTs were categorized according to the Church’s classification.26 Surgical resection for DTs was indicated for patients with tumors located in the abdominal wall that could be completely resected or tumors diagnosed concurrently with FAP-colectomy. Patients with life-threatening complications underwent palliative treatment if necessary.27 All surgeries were performed under the supervision of surgeons with sufficient experience in performing FAP-colectomy. To control tumor growth, pharmacological treatments, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and antihormonal agents, were indicated for all patients, excluding those with pregnancy, allergies, or stable tumor size.6,27 Patients unresponsive to NSAIDs and antihormonal agents were treated with chemotherapy, including methotrexate plus vinorelbine and pazopanib.6,27


Postoperative surveillance of patients who underwent FAP-colectomy has been described previously.25 Most patients underwent imaging examinations, with esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy performed once a year. To detect DTs, especially during the first 2 to 3 years following FAP-colectomy, palpation, ultrasound, and computed tomography (CT) were carefully performed, and clinical symptoms, such as abdominal pain, distension, and mass, were investigated.27 Because our institution has been using CT since March 1980, DTs were diagnosed based on palpation and ultrasound examinations before this time. Five cases underwent FAP-colectomy before the introduction of CT. Following DT diagnosis, imaging examinations using CT or MRI were performed for ∼3 to 6 months until the tumor size stabilized.

Data on patient characteristics and clinical factors of DTs, including location site, date of diagnosis after FAP-colectomy, treatments, sites of germline mutation, and outcomes, were collected for retrospective analysis. FAP-related malignancies include colorectal cancer (CRC), hepatoblastoma, medulloblastoma, and ileal pouch, gastric, duodenal, and thyroid cancers. APC gene mutations, risk factors, and long-term outcomes associated with DTs in patients with FAP after FAP-colectomy were analyzed.

Parametric variables are expressed as medians. The Fisher exact, χ2, and Mann–Whitney U tests were used to assess the significance of between-group differences. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify the independent risk factors for DT development in patients with FAP after FAP-colectomy. The cut-off value for age at FAP-colectomy was determined based on the median value. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate the time-to-event curves. Cumulative incidence was used to estimate the rates of DT development among patients with FAP, with death as a competing risk, and the hazard ratio was estimated using the Gray test. Statistical significance was set at a P-value of < 0.05. All the statistical analyses were performed using R software version 3.5.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Forty patients from 37 pedigrees, including 12 with DTs who underwent genetic testing for APC mutations, were analyzed. Table 1 and Figure 2 show the influence of the APC gene mutation sites on DT development. DTs developed in 12 of 27 (44.4%) patients with APC gene mutations proximal to codon 1400 and none of the 4 patients with APC gene mutations distal to codon 1400. No association was observed between the APC gene mutation sites and DT development.

Sixty-six patients from 48 pedigrees who underwent FAP-colectomy were analyzed. Table 2 shows the characteristics of patients with and without DTs after FAP-colectomy. DTs developed in 20 (30.3%) of the 66 patients. Thirteen (19.6%) patients had intra-abdominal DTs, 5 (7.5%) had abdominal-wall DTs, and 2 (3.0%) had both intra-abdominal and abdominal-wall DTs. Only one patient reported a family history of DTs (a sister having experienced DTs). There were no differences in terms of sex, the total number of polyps <100, surgical approach, total colectomy, or CRC incidence between the 2 groups. However, the age at FAP-colectomy was lower in the DT than in the no DT group (27 vs. 37 y, P = 0.015). The incidence of FAP-related malignancies other than CRC was significantly higher in the DT than in the no DT group (40.0% vs. 13.0%, P = 0.022). Following FAP-colectomy, 16 deaths were reported among patients with FAP, of which 7 were due to CRC, and one was attributed to complications of DTs.

Figure 3 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves illustrating the cumulative risk of DT development after FAP-colectomy in patients with FAP. At the 5-year follow-up, the cumulative probability of DT development in patients with FAP was 32.4%. Table 3 shows the results of univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses of the risk factors for DT development in patients with FAP after FAP-colectomy. Age <32 years at FAP-colectomy (P<0.001) and FAP-related malignancies other than CRC (P<0.001) were independent risk factors for DT development in patients with FAP following FAP-colectomy. In addition, FAP-related malignancies other than CRC and gastric cancer were also independent risk factors for DT development (Supplemental Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JCG/B137). At the 5-year follow-up, the cumulative risk of developing DTs significantly differed between patients aged <32 and ≥32 years at FAP-colectomy (43.2% vs. 13.8%, P=0.019) and between patients with and without FAP-related malignancies other than CRC (52.9% vs. 22.9%, P=0.017) (Fig. 3). The median follow-up duration was 121 months. Sex, family history of DTs, and osteoma were not risk factors for DT development.

Table 4 presents the association between DT locations and patient characteristics after FAP-colectomy. Fifteen patients had intra-abdominal DTs, whereas 5 had only abdominal-wall DTs. There were 8, 0, 3, and 4 patients with stages I, II, III, and IV in the intra-abdominal DT group, respectively. The median age at DT diagnosis after FAP-colectomy was 30 years, and the median time between FAP-colectomy and DT onset was 16 months. Seven patients exhibited symptomatic DTs, and 2 patients with intra-abdominal DTs required palliative surgery. Patient characteristics did not differ between the groups. The prevalence of resection tended to be higher in the abdominal-wall DTs than in the intra-abdominal DT group. Surgical resection was performed in 5 (25%) cases, of which four (20%) resulted in complete resection. In cases of complete resection, no patients showed recurrence in the abdominal-wall DT group, and two patients showed recurrence in the intra-abdominal DT group.

We investigated the genetic analysis, risk factors, and long-term outcomes associated with DTs in Japanese patients with FAP after FAP-colectomy. No association between the APC gene mutation sites and DT development was observed in Japanese patients with FAP following FAP-colectomy. Age <32 years at FAP-colectomy and FAP-related malignancies other than CRC were independent risk factors for DT development in these patients. The 10-year DSS rate for DTs among these patients was 92.9%. Our findings revealed the characteristics of DTs in Japanese patients with FAP and will contribute to the development of treatment strategies for DTs in patients with FAP.

There are only a few studies on DTs in Asians,28,29 especially regarding APC gene mutations compared with those in Westerners.19,20,23,30
APC gene mutation sites 3’ to codon 1399 and between codon 1445 and 1578 are associated with a higher risk of developing DTs in Westerners.17,18 However, several studies have reported no correlation between DTs and APC gene mutation sites.8,23 In a report on DTs in Japan, Enomoto et al29 reported no association between the APC gene mutation sites and DT development when the APC gene mutation site was divided into 2 groups: mutations from codon 157 to 416 and those from codon 564 to 1465. Shimamoto et al28 indicated that Japanese patients with APC codons mutations (1398–1580) exhibited a correlation with extracolonic aggressive phenotypes, including DTs ≥stage III. We found that the APC mutation site and total number of polyps were not associated with DT development. The reason for no association between the APC gene mutation sites and DT development in this study could be attributed to the small number of cases after APC codon 1400. Another contributing factor could be that surgery itself represents a significant risk factor for the development of DT. In this study, all patients underwent surgery, compared with the 28% reported by Shimamoto et al.28 Previous surgical trauma is considered the main risk factor for DTs.16,17 Nieuwenhuis et al23 reported no correlation between the APC gene mutation sites and DT development, with many patients undergoing surgery. As DTs have a multifactorial etiology, more detailed studies for Asians, including Japanese patients, are warranted to clarify interethnic differences. Several studies have suggested that mutation analyses play a crucial role in therapeutic decisions, guiding whether to delay or avoid surgery to prevent DTs.7,30 However, the present data suggest that diagnosing risk for developing DTs after FAP-colectomy by analyzing the APC gene mutations and the total number of polyps is not necessarily appropriate for Japanese patients.

In the present study, the incidence of DTs was 30.3%, which is slightly higher than that reported in previous studies.7–9 Previous studies have described risk factors for DT development, including female sex, trauma, a distal germline APC variant, and family history of DTs, proctocolectomy, open surgery, and previous abdominal surgery.17,20,31 In a report on DTs in Asians, Saito et al10 indicated female sex and proctocolectomy to be independent risk factors after surgery in Japanese patients with FAP, which was similar to Westerners. Herein, we found that age <32 years at FAP-colectomy was an independent risk factor for DT development after FAP-colectomy. Whether undergoing FAP-colectomy earlier in life is a risk factor for DT development remains controversial.20,22,23,31 DTs, including their treatment, significantly impact the quality of life and pregnancy in patients with FAP. Our findings suggest that FAP-colectomy timing should be considered to avoid the impact of DTs and their treatment.

In addition, in the present study, FAP-related malignancies other than CRC were independent risk factors for DT development after FAP-colectomy. The APC codon mutation regions in Western patients with FAP who develop hepatoblastoma or osteoma overlap with the regions where DTs develop.18 A possible association has been observed between gastric tumor development and DTs in Western patients, possibly due to sharing a common region on the APC gene or being independent of the germline mutation.32 Shimamoto et al28 indicated that Japanese patients with APC codon mutations (1398–1580) have a high rate of severe phenotypes for DTs, fundic gland polyps, and gastric neoplasms. Japanese patients with FAP have a gastric cancer incidence ∼3 times higher than that of Western patients, making it a notable characteristic among Japanese patients with FAP.33 It remains unclear whether this increased risk is due to genetic causes in FAP; however, racial and environmental factors may be contributing to the carcinogenic potential inherent in FAP.34,35 This study found no association between gastric cancer and DT development, which may be due to different environmental factors and the proportion of patients who underwent surgery in previous studies. Since DT development was associated with FAP-related extracolonic malignancies in this study, even when excluding gastric cancer, which occurs more frequently in Japan than in many other countries, there may be a strong relationship between gastric cancer and DTs despite uncertain causes. Therefore, further studies are warranted to investigate the association between FAP-related malignancies other than CRC and DT development independent of germline mutations.

Furthermore, although it is controversial whether laparoscopic surgery decreases the risk of DT development in patients with FAP after colectomy,10,20 we observed that laparoscopic surgery was not associated with DT development. Laparoscopic surgery may reduce DT development in the abdominal wall; however, this finding was unclear in this study. Moreover, proctocolectomy is a risk factor for DT development compared with IRA10,20; however, the surgical procedure was not relevant in this study.

Other than CRC, DTs in patients with FAP are associated with high mortality rates.13 DTs accounted for ∼10% of the deaths in patients with FAP.14,15 Church et al36 reported that no patients with stages I or II, 15% of the patients with stage III, and 44% of the patients with stage IV died from DTs among those with FAP. However, a recent study reported a reduced mortality rate.13 Jung et al24 reported that the 5-year OS rate was 88.9% in cases of FAP with DTs. Another study that included 45 patients with FAP reported zero DTs-related deaths.37 In this study, we similarly found that DTs did not impact the long-term outcomes of patients with FAP, even with observation periods longer than those reported in previous studies.24,38 Pharmacological treatments, especially chemotherapy, contribute to improving the prognosis of DTs in patients with FAP.38–42 Doxorubicin plus dacarbazine results in a disease-control rate of 80%,38 whereas methotrexate plus vinblastine results in a disease-control rate of 94.6%.42 Furthermore, several tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as imatinib, sorafenib, and pazopanib, are effective in treating sporadic DTs.39–41 In this study, disease control for DTs was possible with NSAIDs and antihormonal agents in many cases, although not a complete response. Even in cases where those treatments were ineffective, chemotherapies were found to be effective. A recent study recommended an initial observation strategy for DTs in patients with FAP following sporadic DTs practice.43 Complete surgical resection with wide margins was considered the best treatment previously, especially for patients with extra-abdominal or abdominal-wall DTs.11,44 However, high recurrence and morbidity rates have been reported following surgical resection of DTs.45,46 Regarding intra-abdominal DTs, conservative approaches and surgery showed comparable outcomes.47 Based on these reports and our findings, it is recommended to initially follow-up with pharmacological treatment for DTs in patients with FAP. In addition, although there is no evidence for prophylactic treatments against DTs in patients with FAP, it may be considered for high-risk cases following FAP-colectomy. Some DTs progress rapidly, invade adjacent organs, and cause life-threatening complications requiring palliative surgery. DTs remain major postoperative complications in patients with FAP, and disease control with pharmacological treatment is crucial.

This study had some limitations. First, it was a retrospective study performed at a single institution. The sample size was small, and the required sample size was not calculated. However, our findings are considered valuable because only a few reports published on DTs in Asian patients with FAP exits. Second, a bias regarding the date of surgery for FAP was present. Therefore, diagnosis based on imaging examinations and treatment for DTs may differ among patients. However, since treatments for DTs in this study were almost similar pharmacotherapy among patients (Table, Supplemental Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JCG/B138) and few patients received chemotherapy, the presence of an external factor affecting the conclusions is doubtful. Third, the genetic analysis results were limited because we did not perform genetic analysis for all patients. Finally, the choice of surgical and/or pharmacological treatments may have been influenced by surgeon preference. Despite these limitations, our study provides a practical approach for DTs in Japanese patients with FAP. Further genetic analyses and investigations of treatment strategies for DTs in patients with FAP are required on an international scale.

In conclusion, APC gene mutation analyses alone were not observed to be a factor in delaying or avoiding surgery to prevent DTs in Japanese patients with FAP. Patients with FAP should carefully consider the timing of colectomy. DT development in patients with FAP did not significantly impact the prognosis with a long observation. In addition, disease control is important, and careful postoperative surveillance and the initiation of pharmacological treatments can be beneficial.
