Rapid development of antibody therapeutics is often hindered by dependencies on
recombinant protein production, both for antigen generation and for antibody
manufacturing. To overcome these bottlenecks, we established a self-amplifying
replicon RNA (repRNA) immunization and therapeutic delivery platform that
enables an end-to-end RNA-to-antibody-to-RNA workflow. In this approach, alpacas
are immunized with repRNA encoding virus-like particles to elicit antibody
responses, peripheral blood mononuclear cells are harvested to construct phage
display libraries, and broadly neutralizing heavy-chain-only antibodies (VHHs
[variable heavy domain of the heavy chain]) are identified through
high-throughput screening. Lead VHHs are then re-encoded into repRNA for in vivo
delivery as therapeutic constructs, with engineering options for valency,
potency, and serum half-life. As proof of concept, we applied this platform
against enterovirus D68 (EV-D68), an emerging pathogen associated with severe
respiratory disease and acute flaccid myelitis in children for which no vaccines
or treatments exist. repRNA-encoded VHHs protected mice from EV-D68 challenge in
both lungs and nasal cavities, and cryoelectron microscopy revealed the
capsid-binding footprint and mechanism of neutralization. Together, these
findings demonstrate a modular platform for rapid discovery and delivery of
antiviral biologics, with EV-D68 serving as a prototype application.

The success of RNA-based vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic has demonstrated how rapidly nucleic acid platforms can be used to generate and test candidates with only an antigen’s sequence. We previously reported a self-amplifying replicon RNA (repRNA) vaccine encoding antigens from six divergent enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) subclades, designed to generate virus-like particles (VLPs) in vivo that could elicit protective immunity against EV-D68 infection and disease in mice when delivered with a cationic nanocarrier called LION.1

EV-D68 is a non-enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus spread via the respiratory route. While most infections cause mild cold-like symptoms, EV-D68 can induce severe asthma-like illness, and acute flaccid myelitis (AFM) in children.2 No approved vaccines or therapeutics exist, although several platforms—including inactivated virus, VLPs, and protease inhibitors—are in development.3–5 The genetic variability and rapid antigenic drift of RNA viruses like EV-D68 present a major challenge to durable protection.6 Indeed, we observed that just a few amino acid changes in two of the six subclades diminished vaccine-induced neutralization.1 Thus, eliciting or delivering broadly neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) that target conserved epitopes will be essential.

One strategy to bypass the limitations of vaccination; such as delayed induction of protective titers, reduced responsiveness in immunocompromised individuals, and the need for antigenic matching in rapidly evolving viruses, is the direct delivery of protective antibodies. nAbs can be used prophylactically or therapeutically, especially in immunocompromised individuals who may not respond well to vaccines.7 Animal studies and human epidemiological data support the role of nAbs in protecting against EV-D68 infection and disease.8–10

Recent work has explored encoding antibodies using RNA, enabling in vivo expression while avoiding recombinant protein production, purification, and intravenous infusion.10–16 RNA-encoded antibodies can be delivered intramuscularly (IM), and for self-amplifying RNA platforms like repRNA, protein expression can be longer-lasting than standard mRNA formats.13,17,18 This extended expression may reduce dosing frequency and enhance therapeutic durability. We previously demonstrated that repRNA encoding an anti-Zika virus antibody resulted in ~32-fold higher serum titers than conventional mRNA in mice, with protection observed when delivered 7 days before or 1 day after challenge.12

Here, we describe a platform workflow to rapidly generate an RNA therapeutic against an antigen of interest using EV-D68 as a model target. Alpacas were immunized with repRNA encoding EV-D68 VLPs. We then isolated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and constructed a phage display library to screen for broadly neutralizing VHHs (Variable Heavy domain of the Heavy chain) derived from alpaca heavy-chain-only antibodies using a high-throughput neutralization assay. Top candidates were sequenced and re-encoded into our repRNA platform for continued development.

VHHs offer several advantages over conventional antibodies: they are smaller, more stable, easier to express, and exhibit better tissue penetration.19 Their main limitation is a shorter in vivo half-life (~2 hours).20 While continual expression from repRNA can partially offset this limitation, we also sought to enhance persistence by fusing the VHH to a human IgG1 Fc domain (huFc), which prolongs circulation and potentially enables Fc-mediated effector functions. In this study, Fc fusion increased the duration of detectable serum expression relative to VHH alone, however, the present model is not suitable for determining pharmacokinetic half-life, which will require future studies in Fc-appropriate mouse strains or NHPs.

This approach highlights a modular and rapid workflow—from immunization to therapeutic design—leveraging RNA technologies to generate and deliver broadly protective biologics against emerging EV-D68 variants.

Previously, we optimized repRNA delivery of a human anti-Zika virus (ZIKV) IgG monoclonal antibody (mAb) called ZIKV-117.12 However, a 40 μg dose of formulated repRNA delivered intramuscularly (IM) in mice could only achieve maximum circulating concentrations of ~0.8–4 μg/ml of the encoded mAb, depending on the mass of the animal. Due to the role of the CH1 domain of IgG in controlling antibody assembly through the regulation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress21,22, we next asked if removal of the CH1 domain could enhance expression of mAbs in vivo. First, in the context of ZIKV-117, we created a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) fused to the CH2-CH3-bearing Fc domain of IgG1, removing the CH1 domain (Figure S1A). After IM injection in C57BL/6 mice of formulated repRNA (40 μg) encoding either the IgG or the scFv-Fc versions of ZIKV-117, we measured 7- to 12-fold higher concentrations of ZIKV-117 when encoded as the latter, CH1-deleted, version (Figure S1B). Additionally, in transfected BHK cells, scFv-Fc constructs caused less cytotoxicity than full-length IgGs (data not shown). Next, we compared the ZIKV-117 full length IgG-encoding repRNA to a repRNA encoding an anti-Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV) VHH-83 fused to an Fc domain, as these single domain antibodies exist in nature natively devoid of a CH1 domain.23 By 7 days post IM injection in C57BL/6 mice the VHH-Fc encoded construct achieved 17-fold higher concentrations compared to the IgG-encoded mAb, persisting for at least 9 days longer in serum (Figure S1C).

Alpacas naturally generate heavy-chain only antibodies devoid of CH1 domains, making them an ideal source for VHH antibody discovery. We immunized two alpacas with repRNA constructs encoding virus-like particles (VLPs) derived from six divergent EV-D68 strains (Figure 1A). Each repRNA included the P1 and 3CD genes separated by an IRES to allow in vivo VLP assembly and release from transfected cells.1 Serum collected from alpacas post-prime and -boost showed neutralizing activity against EV-D68 isolate MO-14–18947, and the alpaca serum showed neutralizing activity against isolates from all six EV-D68 subclades at timepoints corresponding to PBMC collection for phage library generation (Figure 1B–C). Neutralizing antibody titers declined in the time window between days 60 and 95 (Figure 1C). The study design prioritized collection of PBMCs for VHH library construction (days 88 and 95), and therefore was not optimized to capture the peak serologic response after the second boost. It is possible the responses may be approaching a plateau in magnitude, and the additional boost and late day timepoint PBMC collection (D95) was intended to refine VHH affinity rather than push neutralizing antibody titers higher.

A VHH phage display library was generated from pooled PBMCs harvested after the second and final boost and panned against live EV-D68 (Figure S2). We adapted an impedance-based cytopathic effect assay for high-throughput screening, identifying 13 neutralizing VHH hits from an initial screen of 92 candidates. To validate the method, we then selected 20 different VHHs with a range of area-under-the-curve (AUC) values falling roughly into three groups: low (purple outline), medium (orange outline), and high (red outline) neutralizing activity. We then evaluated EV-D68 binding activity by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), against live EV-D68, so that neutralizing antibody (nAb)-to-binding antibody (bAb) ratios could be calculated to normalize for variability in soluble VHH concentrations present in crude bacterial supernatants (Figure 2A–B). We reasoned that sequencing the hits in our VHH library could provide useful information through phylogenetic analysis; including identification of conserved regions that could inform the design of better antibodies, gain insight into the mechanism of antibody-mediated neutralization, predict future VHH hits to be cross reactive against other EV-D68 variants, and reduce redundancy in the library. As the CDR3 loop of VHHs tends to engage most with a target antigen’s epitope, we sanger sequenced the twenty VHH colonies and identified five different CDR3 families (Figure 2B). One family, containing A2 and D1 VHHs, did not bind or neutralize the MO-14–18947 EV-D68 variant (clade B1), but the other four exhibited EV-D68 neutralizing and binding phenotypes. We cloned one VHH sequence from each of the three CDR3 neutralizing families as well as a VHH from the non-neutralizing CDR3 family as a negative control, into an expression vector to purify recombinant VHHs. Recombinant expression and functional testing confirmed two nanobodies—G12 and F9—with IC50 values of 1.5 and 11 nM, respectively (Figure 2C). Since these initial hits we have similarly panned against EV-D68 from the subclades A1 and A2 (isolates USA-WI-2009–23230 and US-KY-14–18953) and expanded our phylogenetics analysis to include 61 VHH sequences across 7 phylogenetic families (Figure S3).

Purified recombinant G12 exhibited neutralization potencies of 1–20 nM across representative isolates from all six EV-D68 subclades (Figure 3A). Since G12 remained our most potent VHH neutralizer against isolates from all six subclades, we moved forward optimizing G12. We engineered and purified recombinant bivalent G12 constructs with variable-length glycine-serine linkers but observed no improvement in neutralization potency against EV-D68 (clade A1 isolate USA-WI-2009–23230) (Figure 3B). To enhance serum half-life and neutralization potency, we fused G12 to a human IgG1 Fc domain using linkers of varying lengths and encoded each construct in repRNA. The linker lengths compared were 10AA, 15AA, 27AA, and 33AA long (Figure 3C, Figure S4). The 33AA linker derived from the llama IgG2a hinge, the 27AA linker from the llama IgG2b hinge, the 15AA linker from the human IgG1 hinge, and the 10AA linker is a truncated version that only contains the sequence from the core and lower hinge regions of human IgG1.22 We also constructed G12 fused to the huFc domain (via the 33AA linker) with an ESCRT- and ALIX-binding region (EABR) tag at the C-terminus, which has been shown to drive vesicle formation and secretion of multivalent particles through utilization of the ESCRT pathway.24 We hypothesized that multivalent particles could improve the construct’s neutralization potency through enhanced virus particle aggregation. Each repRNA-encoded Fc fusion construct was transfected into BHK cells and crude supernatants were harvested for downstream analyses. Protein expression levels and neutralization potencies were measured by biolayer interferometry (BLI) and the impedance-based cytopathic effect assay, respectively (Figure 3C). The two longest linkers had improved neutralization potencies against the A1 subclade isolate. The 15AA linker exhibited the weakest potency, which was likely due to the large decrease in protein expression. Interestingly, the EABR tag designed to display G12-Fc on vesicles also decreased protein expression, but the potency was similar to G12-Fc connected by the 10AA long linker that expressed 3x better. Constructs with longer linkers (27AA and 33AA) demonstrated improved potency and expression (Figure 3C). We decided to move forward with the 33AA long linker derived from the llama IgG2a hinge due to its relatively higher expression and potency compared to the other designs. Recombinant G12-Fc tethered via the 33AA linker exhibited enhanced neutralization potencies by 10–100-fold across the virus panel (IC50 < 0.5 nM) (Figure 3D), compared to recombinant G12 monomer (Fig. 3A), overcoming potency differences seen with monomeric and bivalent G12 against some subclades.

To uncover the binding footprint and investigate the mechanism of G12-mediated virus neutralization as well as the enhanced potency conferred by Fc fusion, we solved the 2 Å cryo-EM structures of EV-D68 bound to G12 and G12-Fc (Figure 4, Figure S5). G12 binds 60 sites per virion. The 2D class averages of the virus bound to G12-Fc illustrates the flexibility of the linker connecting the G12 and Fc domains as can be seen by a halo effect around the virus surface (Figure S5A). This increased steric obstruction of the virus surface likely explains the increased potency of G12-Fc compared to G12. Inclusion of the Fc domain did not alter the binding footpring of G12 as can be seen in the EM reconstruction (Figure S5B), however, the resolution of the Fc domain was low, likely due to the flexibility of the long linker.

All three G12 complementary determining loops (CDR1–3) engage a quaternary epitope that spans VP1 and VP3 (Figure 4A, Figure S4, Table S2). Zooming in on the epitope reveals that G12 binds outside the canyon that surrounds the five-fold axis of symmetry formed at the interface of five VP1 subunits, which is thought to be the receptor binding site.2526 Four main antigenic sites (NIm or neutralizing immunogenic sites) have been defined across enteroviruses; NIm-IA, NIm-IB, Nim-II, and NIm-III that mostly lie in disordered loops connecting the eight beta strands in VP1–3.27 The majority of the G12 epitope lies outside these more traditionally defined immunogenic sites.28 Interestingly, the disordered N-terminus of G12 contacts two residues (A84, R85) that lie in the VP1 B-C loop that is part of the NIm-IA site. The VP1 BC and DE loops are thought to be more variable immunogenic sites that may contribute to immune evasion.26 The BC loop is variable enough that it is used to define EV-D68 serotypes.29 In particular, A2 viruses have been shown to exhibit reduced neutralization by a VLP B1-based vaccine sera and to accumulate distinctive substitutions in the VP1 BC and DE loops during recent outbreaks, underscoring the value of testing whether G12 can target an epitope that remains conserved within this antigenically evolved subclade.3,30 Notably all three G12 CDR loops avoid these sites considered to be the most variable parts of enteroviruses. The CDR3 loop binds the C beta strand (residues K92, T94, N96) and the disordered EF loop (residues T150, G151) in VP1. All three of the CDR loops can engage the G-H loop in VP1 (residues N206, L208, I211, and P213). Additionally, The G-H loop has been shown to be conformationally dynamic and guards the entrance to a hydrophobic pocket inside the VP1 jellyroll fold where a fatty acid pocket factor typically sits.26

Outside of VP1, the CDR1 and CDR3 loops of G12 form binding contacts with the C-terminus of VP3, which is proximal to the sialic acid binding site.31 To directly test whether G12 interferes with receptor engagement, we performed a competition ELISA experiment to assess EV-D68 binding to increasing concentrations of G12-Fc in the presence of recombinant MFSD6 and sialic acid (Figure S6). In contrast to receptor-blocking antibodies, G12 binding was unaffected by the presence of either receptor, indicating that its neutralization mechanism does not involve direct competition at these receptor interfaces. These data are consistent with the structural observation that G12 binds outside the canonical canyon receptor docking site and suggest that neutralization likely occurs through steric hindrance and/or stabilization of capsid conformations incompatible with productive viral uncoating.

Sequence conservation analysis of isolates from six subclades revealed minor epitope variation at three positions that may influence neutralization sensitivities. While the G12 epitope is highly conserved among currently sampled isolates, there are two residues in VP3 and one residue in VP1 that are variable (Figure 4B, Figure S7). A change in the epitope at the C-terminus of VP3 (H240D and A241E) in isolates USA-WI-2009–23230 (A1), USA-MD-2003–23226 (C), and US-KY-14–18953 (A2 or D) causes a change in the epitope net charge that may explain the reduced sensitivity to G12 mediated neutralization (Figure 3A). In the VP1 subunit a change in the amino acid residue N/S206T in the G-H loop does not alter the net charge but may cause a more subtle change in the epitope-paratope sterics or the ability of the G12 CDR2 loop to bind this region, and impacts the neutralization sensitivity of the USA-2018–23087 isolate (B3 subclade). Notably, Fc fusion mitigated any differences in strain specific neutralization sensitivities to G12.

To extend this analysis to contemporary strains, we assembled a panel of twelve publicly available A2(D) isolates collected between 2020 and 2024, selecting one representative from each major branch of the Nextstrain phylogeny (Figure S8). Alignment of these sequences with US-KY-14–18953 showed that amino acid substitutions clustered predominantly in the VP1 B–C loop, whereas residues comprising the core G12 footprint were invariant across the panel. Among eighteen residues within 1.4 Å of the modeled G12 interface, only VP1 position 84 (A→E/K) differed among recent isolates, and this residue lies in the hypervariable B–C loop outside the core CDR-mediated paratope, a region contacted only by the flexible N terminus of G12.

Because substitutions outside direct contact residues could in principle alter the epitope allosterically, we next used AlphaFold3 to predict capsid structures for two recent A2 isolates (SH2020–3310 and Ita-MI-4069–2024) and for KY-18953, and aligned these models with our EV-D68–G12 cryo-EM structure (Figure S9). The three predicted structures were highly superimposable over the VP1–VP3 scaffold, with differences confined to loop regions, including the VP1 B–C loop, that AlphaFold3 assigns low confidence (low pLDDT). Importantly, the backbone and side-chain orientations of residues within the G12 epitope were preserved across all three models and matched those observed in the experimental map. Taken together, these sequence and structural analyses support the likelihood that G12 engages a structurally constrained, highly conserved epitope that has remained unchanged in circulating A2 isolates sampled from 2020–2024, while acknowledging that functional neutralization of these strains remains to be tested experimentally.

After settling on our lead construct design, repRNA encoding G12-Fc using the 33AA linker composition, we wanted to better understand the expression kinetics and serum neutralization levels in vivo to inform a virus challenge study. We delivered repRNA encoding both the G12 VHH monomer and G12-Fc (Figure 5A) IM in mice at a dose of 40 or 10 μg.

Serum neutralization and ELISA data showed rapid expression of G12-Fc post-IM injection, with detectable neutralization activity through day 12. G12 monomer serum neutralization potency waned by day 6 (Figure 5B). At day 1, the 10 μg repRNA dose led to higher G12-Fc expression than the 40μg dose, possibly due to reduced innate immune activation. However, by later timepoints, the 40μg dose consistently produced higher serum levels. G12 monomer VHH expression levels, however, were below the LLOQ.

Next, to determine the optimal RNA dose that protects mice from virus infection we delivered repRNA encoding G12-Fc at four different doses (40, 10, 1, and 0.1μg) as well as an irrelevant repRNA via the IM route. Four days later mice were intranasally challenged with a mouse-adapted virus (B1 subclade isolate 18949), then euthanized the next day, so that lungs and nasal cavities could be harvested for measurement of EV-D68 viral loads in lower and upper respiratory tracts, respectively. 40 μg of G12-Fc repRNA conferred robust protection in the lungs, with viral titers below the limit of detection in 4 of 5 mice, and reduced viral burden in the nasal cavities. Lower doses did not confer significant protection (Figure 5C).

To expand upon the neutralization data we reported, we conducted another virus-challenge study with the US-MO-14–18947 isolate (B1 subclade) EV-D68 challenge four days after IM delivery of 40 μg of repRNA. In addition to the G12-Fc treatment candidates, we also included repRNA encoding the G12 monomer and a G12 dimeric IgA construct which we hypothesized may aid in antibody translocation to mucosal surfaces and improve protection in the upper airways.32 Notably across all groups the virus titers were lower compared to the mouse-adapted virus in the nasal cavities by about an order of magnitude. However, G12-Fc consistently offered better protection compared to dimeric IgA and the G12 VHH monomer (Figure 5D).

Since 2014, EV-D68 outbreaks have occurred in a biennial pattern, with a notable resurgence in 2024 marked by elevated detection in U.S. wastewater and confirmed AFM cases. 2,33,34 While typically underdiagnosed due to sequence overlap with rhinovirus, EV-D68 poses a significant risk to infants and young children, potentially causing asthma-like symptoms, or AFM.33 The recent outbreaks, primarily driven by B3 and A2 strains, underscore the need for effective therapeutics.35 Given the 5–7-day window between respiratory symptom onset and neurological complications, there may be an opportunity for early therapeutic intervention.2 Although EV-D68 has not been a major cause of neonatal sepsis, unlike other enteroviruses such as echoviruses and coxsackieviruses, the ability to rapidly deploy RNA-encoded antibodies may also have value for pathogens that pose risks to pregnant individuals or neonates. 36–40 We therefore view the G12-Fc study as a demonstration of a flexible, rapid, platform with applications beyond EV-D68. To provide additional context for the term “rapid” as applied to this workflow, we outline here realistic timelines for each stage of the repRNA-to-VHH pipeline based on our experience with EV-D68. Once the viral genome sequence is available, cloning the P1 and 3CD genes into the repRNA template and preparing high-quality plasmid DNA typically requires 1–2 weeks. Plasmid linearization, in vitro transcription, analytical characterization, and formulation with LION add approximately 4–5 days, enabling generation of a formulated repRNA vaccine candidate in ~2 weeks from sequence acquisition. This compares favorably with inactivated picornavirus vaccine production, which requires isolation, propagation, purification, and quality testing of high-titer virus stocks and can take significantly longer even under optimized laboratory conditions.

The antibody discovery phase requires additional time owing to the biological kinetics of affinity maturation. In this study, alpacas were immunized over a 95-day schedule to enrich for high-affinity VHHs, but shorter immunization regimens or use of smaller camelids would reduce this interval, and synthetic or semi-synthetic VHH libraries can eliminate immunization entirely. Following PBMC collection, phage display library generation (approximately 3 weeks), panning and enrichment (3–5 weeks), functional screening (~1 week), and repRNA construction of lead VHH candidates (~2 weeks) result in an end-to-end timeline of roughly 20–24 weeks for an immunization-based workflow. Accelerated versions of this pipeline, particularly those leveraging pre-existing VHH libraries, can compress antibody discovery and lead optimization to 8–10 weeks. Together, these timelines illustrate that while immunization-based VHH discovery introduces a biological time component, the repRNA platform enables rapid sequence-to-vaccine transitions and flexible integration of antibody discovery strategies, supporting its potential utility in rapid response settings.

Neutralizing monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have shown protective efficacy against EV-D68 in both epidemiological studies and animal models.10 Notably, mAb EV68–228 improved survival in mice when administered up to 3 days post-infection.10 Here, we describe a broadly neutralizing VHH, G12, capable of neutralizing isolates derived from all circulating EV-D68 subclades. G12 was discovered from repRNA-immunized alpacas, and was encoded into our repRNA platform and rapidly expressed in vivo following intramuscular delivery, providing a modular alternative to recombinant mAb therapies.

Our in vivo experiments were conducted in immunocompetent adult mice, in which EV-D68 replication is naturally curtailed early by the innate immune response. As a result, this model does not allow assessment of whether RNA-encoded G12 can therapeutically suppress ongoing viral replication or prevent subsequent dissemination to the lower respiratory tract, neurological tissues, or other sites that contribute to severe disease. The two challenge studies presented here, one using the mouse-adapted EV-D68 isolate and a separate experiment using the U.S. clinical isolate US-MO-14–18947, were designed to measure nasal and lung viral titers following intranasal infection, but they did not include monitoring of survival, weight loss, or neurological signs. Thus, while these experiments demonstrate that IM delivery of repRNA leads to expression of a neutralizing VHH capable of reducing detectable viral titers, they do not establish whether repRNA-encoded G12 can function as a true therapeutic in models where virus replication proceeds unchecked. Future studies in permissive models such as neonatal or IFN-α/β receptor-deficient mice will be necessary to determine whether repRNA-encoded antibodies can halt viral replication, prevent dissemination, and ameliorate clinical disease.41 Additionally, The requirement for a 40 μg dose to achieve robust protection highlights an opportunity for further optimization of repRNA-encoded antibody delivery. Antibody formats may require higher intracellular RNA abundance to achieve therapeutically relevant serum levels compared to viral antigens, and our current LION formulation was not specifically optimized for maximal expression of secreted IgG-like molecules. More recently we have tested LION co-formulated with small molecules that suppress innate immune responses and signals that shut down protein expression, and observed significant increases in antibody expression levels in vivo (manuscript in submission). In future work, improvements in formulation efficiency, codon and UTR engineering, enhanced replicase activity, or alternative delivery routes may substantially reduce the required dose. These findings underscore that while repRNA is capable of driving in vivo antibody expression sufficient for antiviral efficacy, additional optimization will be needed to fully realize the low-dose potential of repRNA for antibody therapeutics.

Unlike previously characterized mAbs that bind canonical neutralization sites (NIms), G12 recognizes a distinct epitope involving the VP1 G-H loop, a region linked to viral uncoating due to its role in pocket factor displacement.27,31 Interestingly the small molecule Pleconaril, which is a known potent inhibitor of rhinovirus and EV-D68, has been shown to displace the pocket factor, bind in the VP1 hydrophobic pocket and alter the conformation of the G-H loop.26 Mechanistically Pleconaril stabilizes the virus capsid making it more difficult for the virus to undergo conformational changes required to release the viral genome. While Pleconaril was never approved as a viable treatment due to unwanted side effects in women on birth control, an antibody therapeutic that protects via a similar mechanism may prove a better alternative.26 Similarly, the G-H loop in EV-71, which has a capsid with similar structural homology, has been described as an “adaptor-sensor” that controls the uncoating process, where presumably locking this loop in a certain conformation could block uncoating.42 Outside of the binding footprint in VP1, we hypothesized that G12 may block receptor binding via interactions with the C-terminus of VP3 that is proximal to the sialic acid receptor binding site at eastern side of the canyon, and near a positively charged patch in VP1 (residues K268, K270, K271, and R272) posited to be the binding site of sulfated glycosaminoglycans that may act as an alternative receptor.43 Although the G12 footprint lies proximal to receptor engagement regions, competition ELISAs demonstrated that MFSD6 and sialic acid binding does not directly inhibit G12 (Figure S5). Instead, neutralization may result from steric obstruction of conformational transitions required for uncoating or post-attachment entry steps. The G12 binding footprint information provided here may guide future studies further investigating the mechanism of protection. For example, in a mechanistic study, Zheng at al. demonstrated that two murine derived mAbs can be specific for different conformational states and also induce the conformational transition from the mature virion to the A-particle state through interactions with the VP2 HI loop and VP3 BC loops. 9 Unlike these two murine mAbs and two well-characterized human mAbs, we note G12 binds a distinct epitope, and the virus structure does not resemble an expanded conformational state (Figure S5 and S10).10 Of note, EV68–228 is currently in clinical trials, and an exceptionally potent antibody.41 For rapidly evolving pathogens, complementary antibodies that target non-overlapping epitopes are often required for durable antiviral control, either as standalone therapeutics or in combination “cocktail” strategies. Therapeutic antibodies can also be paired with decoy receptors, such as the MFSD6-Fc microbody recently developed, which neutralizes EV-D68 my mimicking the putative cellular entry receptor.44
45 Whereas receptor decoys act by competitively blocking attachment, pairing them with antibodies or VHHs that neutralize through distinct mechanisms could offer complementary strengths and alternative paths to breadth or escape resilience. The repRNA immunization pipeline described here enables rapid elicitation of such candidates and expands the pool of epitope-diverse antiviral molecules available for therapeutic development. Our sequence and structural analyses indicate that the G12 footprint lies in a structurally constrained region that has remained conserved among A2(D) isolates collected between 2020 and 2024 (Figure S8), despite ongoing diversification in adjacent hypervariable loops. Nevertheless, future studies will be required to directly benchmark G12-Fc against established clinical candidates, map escape mutations, and to measure neutralization potency against newly emerged clinical isolates from the most recent outbreaks.

To extend in vivo duration and potentially enhance Fc-mediated effector functions, G12 was fused to a human Fc domain. The G12-Fc fusion showed markedly improved neutralization potency, which appears attributable to steric hindrance rather than increased avidity, as bivalent homodimer formats of G12 without Fc did not improve potency.

Safety and immunogenicity considerations are important for any RNA-encoded VHH-Fc or multi-valent VHH based therapeutic. Although VHHs are camelid-derived, clinical experience with humanized VHH–Fc biologics demonstrates that these molecules can be safely administered in humans. Notably, caplacizumab (a bivalent VHH–Fc fusion) is FDA-approved for acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and has an established systemic safety profile, and additional VHH therapeutics such as ozoralizumab (anti-TNFα) have been approved or advanced through late-stage clinical trials with favorable tolerability.46,47 Additionally ALX-0171 is is a VHH-Fc derivative against RSV designed to be inhaled. ALX-0171 demonstrated no significant increase in adverse events or anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) in study participants, but was discontinued post Phase 2 trials due to a lack of efficacy.48 These examples suggest that VHH–Fc scaffolds can be engineered to minimize immunogenicity and are compatible with repeated dosing in humans.

For repRNA-encoded antibodies, two theoretical risks warrant acknowledgment. First, because the VHH domain is foreign, vaccine-like priming could induce anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) over time, potentially limiting the durability of expression or complicating re-treatment. Second, intracellular expression of a foreign antibody could theoretically lead to antigen presentation and recognition of producer cells by CD8+ T cells that reduces expression levels following repeated doses. While the present and prior saRNA/repRNA studies have not reported evidence of widespread cytotoxic elimination of producer cells in vivo, detailed mapping of how innate and adaptive responses shape the longevity of VHH-Fc expression will be an important focus of future work.49–52 Importantly, these risks can be mitigated through humanization of the VHH framework, Fc engineering, and dose optimization, and the platform is compatible with encoding updated or alternative VHHs should anti-drug immunity emerge. Together, these considerations support the feasibility of advancing repRNA-encoded VHH-Fc therapeutics while highlighting several safety questions, particularly immunogenicity and repeat dosing, that will be essential to evaluate in future preclinical and clinical studies.

RNA-based antibody delivery platforms offer a key advantage over traditional protein therapeutics: rapid construct design and deployment, which is critical for addressing fast-evolving RNA viruses like EV-D68.6 This strategy is also adaptable, RNA constructs encoding multiple nAbs or VHHs targeting distinct epitopes could mitigate viral escape. Cross-reactive antibody responses in polyclonal sera capable of neutralizing diverse enteroviruses (e.g., EV-D68, EV-A71, rhinovirus, coxsackievirus) have been identified, suggesting that shared structural motifs may enable pan-enterovirus targeting. 27,42,53–55

In the absence of a single pan-reactive sequence, multimeric constructs combining VHHs against distinct serotypes can expand breadth. For example, Mukherjee et al. demonstrated that a repRNA-encoded VHH hetero-hexamer provided protection against multiple botulinum toxin serotypes.52 Similarly, our high-throughput workflow—from repRNA immunization to phage display screening, therapeutic protein optimization, and back to repRNA—offers a flexible and scalable approach for generating RNA-encoded VHH therapeutics.

Altogether, this study demonstrates that repRNA can be used to 1) encode and vaccinate against multiple antigenic targets, 2) raise immune responses in alpacas to enable VHH discovery, and 3) rapidly express a potent, broadly neutralizing VHH-Fc therapeutic against a virus of interest. This platform may be adapted to address other rapidly evolving or emerging pathogens, offering a promising avenue for fast-response biologic development.
