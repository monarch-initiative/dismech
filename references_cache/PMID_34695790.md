In rural India, since 2014, the Swachh Bharat Abhiyan (Clean India Mission) has ensured construction of more than 100 million toilets and is now focusing on reinforcement of sanitation behaviors. We report a cholera outbreak in a remote village in western India where open defecation was implicated in causation. A water pipeline was damaged in the vicinity of a stream flowing from a site of open defecation. Despite the availability of a toilet facility in the majority of households (75%), open defecation was widely practiced (62.8%). Many reported not washing hands with soap and water before eating (78.5%) and after defecation (61.1%). The study emphasizes the need for focused health behavior studies and evidence-based interventions to reduce the occurrence of cholera outbreaks. This could be the last lap in the path toward achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6, which aims to “ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all.”

Contaminated water and poor sanitation is linked to transmission of diseases such as cholera, hepatitis A, and typhoid.1,2The burden of these diseases is high in India.3,4Cholera is endemic in 19 districts, with 34 additional districts reporting multiple outbreaks.5Since 2014, a major initiative for universal sanitation has been the Swachh Bharat Abhiyan, or Clean India Mission, with more than 100 million toilets constructed in rural India (https://swachhbharatmission.gov.in/sbmcms/index.htm). The mission in its second phase is focusing on “open defecation-free plus” activities in rural areas to reinforce open defecation-free behaviors, and to provide interventions for solid and liquid waste management in villages (https://swachhbharatmission.gov.in/sbmcms/index.htm). We report a cholera outbreak in a remote village in western India where open defecation was implicated in the causation.

Cholera is an acute diarrheal disease caused by the ingestion of food or water contaminated byVibrio cholerae.6Globally, there are ≈1.3 to 4.0 million cholera cases a year, and 21,000 to 143,000 deaths.7ToxigenicV. choleraestrains belong to serogroups O1 and O139. Serogroup O1 has two biotypes: classical and El Tor. O1 serogroup strains are categorized based on the cholera toxin (CTX) prophage they harbor.8Recently, atypical El Tor strains have emerged, harboring the classical cholera toxin allele (ctxB1).9The Haiti outbreak in 2010 highlighted the emergence and spread of the ctxB7 allele,10which was first documented in an outbreak in Orissa, India.11Traditionally, cholera was reported along the eastern Gangetic plains of India, but Maharashtra in western India has been recently classified as endemic for cholera, with outbreaks reported in at least 3 of the past 5 years.12

Sawarpada is a notified village in the hills of the Nashik District, with an agrarian population of 919. After the detection of an outbreak of acute watery diarrhea on October 17, 2019, an investigation was carried out by the district rapid response team in coordination with epidemiologists and microbiologist from the Indian Council of Medical Research–National Institute of Virology, Pune, Maharashtra, India, starting October 18, 2019. After confirmation of the cholera outbreak, a study was undertaken to identify risk factors and suggest control and prevention measures. The investigation included active case detection and case management, characterization ofV. choleraeisolated, and determination of the source of the outbreak. An epidemic graph and spot map were prepared to understand temporal and spatial distributions, and probable mode of transmission. Age-specific attack rates were calculated to identify the most affected age group. Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, Indian Council of Medical Research–National Institute of Virology, Pune, and written informed consent was obtained from the respondents. Surveys were conducted to understand the water supply and storage practices in the village, and the defecation habits of the population. Information on different potential exposures and risk factors were collected during house-to-house visits. Inputs were sought from community gatekeepers, village elders, and local health workers.

Nine representative fecal specimens were tested for common bacterial and viral causative agents responsible for diarrheal outbreaks. Organisms that produced yellow colonies on thiosulfate citrate bile salt sucrose agar (BD Difco, Sparks, MD), and were oxidase positive were confirmed serologically by agglutination with specificV. choleraepolyvalent O1 antisera, followed by Ogawa and Inaba serotype-specific antisera (Denka Seiken, Tokyo, Japan). Biotyping was carried out by the recommended phenotypic tests.13Antimicrobial susceptibility testing for azithromycin (15 μg), tetracycline (30 μg), nalidixic acid (30 μg), ampicillin (10 μg), and cefotaxime (30 μg) (BD BBL™Sensi-Disc; Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD) was performed on Mueller Hinton agar by the Kirby Bauer disk diffusion method. Genomic DNA was extracted from theV. choleraeisolates using the QIAamp DNA kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and polymerase chain reaction assays were performed for specified targets using the AccuPrime Taq DNA Polymerase System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA), primers, and polymerase chain reaction conditions as described in the Supplemental Material.

In four fecal samples, a non-hemolytic polymyxin B-sensitive El Tor variant ofV. choleraeO1 Ogawa was confirmed phenotypically. The isolates were susceptible to azithromycin and tetracycline, and resistant to nalidixic acid, ampicillin, and cefotaxime. All the fecal samples were negative for other bacterial or viral etiology. Molecular characterization of various virulence genes confirmed an altered El Tor wave 3 strain belonging to cholera toxin (CT)– genotype 7 (Supplemental Material). A water sample from the leakage site grew thermotolerant coliforms, indicating fecal contamination. Within the population of 919, 288 cases of cholera were identified. The majority (278 of 288, 96.7%) had mild illness. There were two deaths (case fatality ratio, 0.7%, 2 of 288). The index case presented on the night of October 16, 2019. He was referred to a hospital in a nearby village and discharged after 3 days of treatment. Neither the index case nor any of the early cases reported visiting any outbreak area or any travel history outside the village. The epidemic graph (Figure 1) suggested a point source outbreak, which began on October 16, peaked October 17, and lasted until October 20. Based on the median incubation period for El Tor Ogawa strains,14and the recommended recall period, it is estimated that exposure probably occurred between October 15 and 16, 2019.

The cholera cases were distributed across the village (Figure 2). Attack rates were similar among male (31.8%) and female (30.8%) residents, and highest among young adults (42.8%). Villagers in the age group of 18 to 60 years were symptomatic in greater proportions (42.8%) than other age groups; children younger than 5 years old were least affected (1.3%). Compared with the 18- to 60-year age group, the RR was lowest for children younger than 5 years (RR, 0.03; 95% CI, 0.002–0.124) followed by the 6- to 17-year age group (RR, 0.26; 95% CI, 0.17–0.39). Although cholera outbreaks have been reported from Nashik previously,15Sawarpada itself had no history of cholera outbreaks (local health records). There were no gatherings in the previous week. It was found that a shallow plastic pipeline carrying water from the source well to the storage tanks had been damaged during civil work on October 15, 2019. A stream in the vicinity flowed from an area used by the villagers for open defecation and mixed with the piped water through the break in the pipeline. The village had a relatively good water supply, with almost all the responders (99.3%) availing themselves of piped drinking water, supplied by the local body. Open defecation was widely practiced (62.8%) despite the availability of toilet facilities (75%). Of the 216 people who had toilets available at home, more than half (58.8%) did not use them. Although 10% of the respondents preferred not to answer the question on open defecation, only four individuals indicated use of public toilets (Table 1). The toilets were being used frequently for other purposes, such as storage and bathing by female members of the household. Open defecation, lack of handwashing with soap and water before eating and after defecation, and no processing of drinking water were identified as the major areas where behavioral interventions were required.

The occurrence of cholera is common in rural areas of India, which have limited access to safe water and good sanitation practices. Our household survey demonstrated a population that fulfilled several risk factors for cholera including poverty, illiteracy, and overcrowding. Despite the construction of toilets, their use was inadequate and open defecation was a preferred practice. A study from Rajasthan State in northwestern India also noted that ≈21% of rural households with access to toilet facilities through the Swachh Bharat Abhiyan continued to practice open defecation.16The majority of the respondents freely admitted to not washing hands before eating or after defecation, indicating the acceptability of this practice. Open defecation is a public health issue worldwide. The most common consequence is infectious diseases such as diarrhea, which can potentially lead to devastating outbreaks. Hygienic disposal of feces of infected persons is crucial to prevent the spread of cholera.

Our study had some limitations.Vibrio choleraecould not be isolated from the water source. This could be because of the occurrence of a viable but non-culturable state, in which the organism is known to exist in the aquatic environment.17Although we could not pinpoint how the index case became ill, our investigation suggests that it was probably a result of ingestion of contaminated water. Only a minority (10–20%) of cholera infections are symptomatic, and it is possible that an asymptomatic case prior to the pipe leakage had contaminated the water source.7

Our findings underscore the need to go beyond infrastructure development and engage with the community to encourage socio-behavioral changes in hygiene practices. A review of behavioral change models for infectious disease transmission shows that most of the models are not based on actual data, but rather on multiple sources of information, including social media.18A model for the influence of human behavior on cholera dynamics has been proposed that considers rational human behavior.19Validation of such models in the real world are not common. Very few data on actual behavioral interventions for improving sanitation and hygiene are available from India.

In conclusion, focused health behavior studies and evidence-based interventions are needed to reduce the occurrence of outbreaks of fecal-orally transmitted diseases such as cholera. This could be the last lap for achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6, in letter and spirit, of “ensuring availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all.”
