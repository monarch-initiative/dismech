# Muscle cell and motor protein function in patients with a IIa myosin missense mutation (Glu-706 to Lys).
**Authors:** Li M, Lionikas A, Yu F, Tajsharghi H, Oldfors A, Larsson L
**Journal:** Neuromuscul Disord (2006)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.nmd.2006.07.023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nmd.2006.07.023)

## Content

The pathogenic events leading to the progressive muscle weakness in patients with a E706K mutation in the head of the myosin heavy chain (MyHC) IIa were analyzed at the muscle cell and motor protein levels. Contractile properties were measured in single muscle fiber segments using the skinned fiber preparation and a single muscle fiber in vitro motility assay. A dramatic impairment in the function of the IIa MyHC isoform was observed at the motor protein level. At the single muscle fiber level, on the other hand, a general decrease was observed in the number of preparations where the specific criteria for acceptance were fulfilled irrespective of MyHC isoform expression. Our results provide evidence that the pathogenesis of the MyHC IIa E706K myopathy involves defective function of the mutated myosin as well as alterations in the structural integrity of all muscle cells irrespective of MyHC isoform expression.

Myosin is a major structural component of skeletal muscle and is considered                to be the molecular motor that converts free energy derived from the hydrolysis of                ATP into mechanical work. It is a hexamer comprised of two myosin heavy chain (MyHC)                subunits of approximately 220 kD molecular weight and four myosin light chain (MyLC)                subunits, two regulatory and two essential MyLC, of approximately 20 kD each. The                MyHCs found in mammalian skeletal muscle myofibrils are all isoforms of the class II                myosin family. At least ten MyHCs have been identified in striated muscles from                mammals, i.e., ß/slow (I), α-cardiac, slow-tonic, embryonic,                fetal, IIa, IIx (IId), IIb, a super-fast extraocular and masticatory myosin isoform                    (1, 2). Three of these are expressed in normal adult extrafusal human limb and                trunk muscles, i.e., ß/slow or type I, and the two fast MyHCs, IIa and                IIx (1).

The coding sequence for the adult MyHC isoforms is among the most heavily                amplified in mammalian species and the resulting polypeptide generally accounts for                15-25% of the total body protein content. In spite of the abundance of myosin and                its critical role in motor function, there are only a few reports in the literature                describing neuromuscular disorders where the motor protein myosin is the primary                cause of the disease. However there is an increasing awareness of both acquired and                hereditary myosin myopathies, i.e., “myosinopathies” (3). The acute quadriplegic myopathy (AQM), or                critical illness myopathy, is an example of an acquired myosinopathy. It is                associated with generalized muscle paralysis and partial or complete loss of myosin                and myosin-associated proteins caused by a dramatic down-regulation of myosin                synthesis at the transcriptional level and enhanced protein degradation in response                to pharmacological treatment in modern anesthesia and intensive care (4). The recently described selective myosin loss                in experimental studies of cancer cachexia may represent another acquired                myosinopathy caused by altered cytokine activation of myosin synthesis at the gene                level and/or proteolysis via the ubiquitin-dependent proteasome pathway (5). The very small number of myopathies                identified in humans that have been associated with myosin mutations need not be due                to the absence of myosinopathies. Indeed, the large size of the 17p13.1 locus within                the MyHC gene would predict a prominent role for MyHC mutations in human                myosinopathies (3). There is accordingly                reason to believe that this group of myopathies may have been misdiagnosed and                lumped together with other neuromuscular disorders. Experimental animal studies have                shown distinct patterns of muscle dysfunction in IIx and IIb MyHC null mice, while                heterozygous mice are clinically normal (6).                Missense mutation in the β/slow MyHC gene causes a familial hypertrophic                cardiomyopathy-like disease in heterozygous mice, indicating that missense mutations                are more deleterious than nulls (7). In                humans, numerous mutations have been reported in the β/slow MyHC gene in                patients with familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and the vast majority of these                mutations are located in the motor domain of the myosin molecule (8). In these patients, a central core disease                has also been observed in skeletal muscle, i.e., in the type I fibers expressing the                β/slow MyHC (9). However, pure                skeletal muscle myosinopathies have not been reported until recently (10, 11).                These include different mutations in the distal region of the β/slow MyHC                affecting primarily skeletal muscle and a mutation of the IIa MyHC (10, 12-16).

An “autosomal dominant myopathy with congenital contractures,                ophtalmoplegia and rimmed vacuoles” linked to the MYH2 gene on chromosome                17p13.1 has been reported by Darin and co-workers (15). This missense mutation is in a highly conserved region of the motor                domain of the type IIa MyHC isoform and gives rise to an amino acid shift from a                negatively (Glu-706) to a positively charged (Lys) residue (14, 17) and the                progression and severity of the disease correlates with type IIa MyHC expression                    (18).

It is hypothesized that this missense mutation in the motor domain causes a                malfunction of the actin myosin interaction in fibers expressing type IIa MyHC,                which might be manifested by reduced cross-bridge force generating capacity and/or                slowing of actin propagation over myosin. To test this hypothesis, the function of                the adult MyHC isoforms expressed in human skeletal muscle will be explored at the                cellular and molecular levels in patients with the IIa MyHC mutation and compared                with healthy controls.

Muscle fibers from four patients carrying the MYH2 E706K                    mutation were investigated and compared with results from 26 healthy male and                    female controls ranging in age between 18 and 80 years (46±26yrs)                        (18, 19). Patient 1 was a 63-year-old man with joint contractures at                    birth. He had slowly progressive proximal muscle weakness since childhood. At                    investigation he had moderate muscle weakness and external ophthalmoplegia, and                    was able rise from sitting position with hand support. The muscle biopsy showed                    marked variation in the diameter of the fibers, which frequently showed numerous                    internalized nuclei. There was a moderate increase in interstitial connective                    tissue and occasional regenerating muscle fibers. Patient 2 was a 38-year-old                    woman with multiple joint contractures at birth. Since childhood mild, proximal                    muscle weakness had persisted without obvious progression. She also had external                    ophthalmoplegia and hand weakness. She could rise from a sitting position                    without support. The muscle biopsy showed moderate variation in muscle fiber                    size. Fibers frequently showed numerous internalized nuclei. There was slight                    increase in interstitial connective tissue, but no regenerating fibers. Patient                    3 was a 36-year-old woman with multiple joint contractures at birth. Since                    childhood she had mild proximal muscle weakness with slow progression. There was                    a predominant back insufficiency and she also had external ophthalmoplegia. She                    was able to rise from a sitting position without support. The muscle biopsy                    showed moderate variability of fiber size and slight increase of interstitial                    connective tissue. Muscle fibers with internalized nuclei were frequent, but                    there were no regenerating fibers. Patient 4 was a 47-year-old man with slight                    muscle weakness with onset in adulthood. His major complaint was back                    insufficiency and he also had hand weakness and external ophthalmoplegia. He was                    able to rise from a sitting position without support. The muscle biopsy showed                    slight but pathologic variation in fiber size and many fibers showed                    internalized nuclei. There were no regenerating fibers and no increase of                    interstitial connective tissue.

Muscle samples were taken from the lateral portion of the quadriceps                    (vastus lateralis) muscle in both patients and controls. Muscle samples were                    obtained by open biopsy in the patients and by the percutaneous conchotome                    method in the control subjects with the understanding and consent of the                    patients/subjects. The muscle sample was immediately placed in an ice-cold relax                    solution (in mmol/l: 100 KCl, 20 Imidazole, 7 MgCl2, 2 EGTA, 4 ATP,                    pH 7.0; 4°C). Small bundles of ∼25-50 fibers were                    dissected free from the muscle and tied to a glass microcapillary tube at                    ∼110% resting length. The bundles were then placed in a skinning                    solution (relax solution containing glycerol; 50:50 v/v) at 4°C for                    24 h and subsequently stored at −20°C for use within 3                    weeks, or treated with cryoprotectant (sucrose) solution for long-term storage                    at −80°C as described earlier (20). At the beginning of a set of experiments, a sucrose                    treated frozen bundle was desucrosed by stepwise lowering the sucrose                    concentration of the relax solution until no sucrose remained. The bundle was                    thereafter stored in relax solution with glycerol at –20°C                    for a maximum of two weeks. Muscle samples were treated in an identical way in                    patients and controls. Short muscle fiber segments were used in all biochemical                    and contractile analyses and identical results are obtained irrespective of if                    samples are taken by the open or percutaneous muscle biopsy technique. The                    Ethical Committee at Gothenburg University and the Pennsylvania State                    University's Institutional Review Board approved all aspects of this study.

On the day of an experiment, the fibers were placed for 30 min in a                    relaxing solution containing 0.5% Brij-58 (polyoxyethylene 20 cetyl ether; Sigma                    Chemical Co.). The fibers were mounted in an experimental apparatus similar to                    one described previously (21, 22), leaving an average fiber segment                    length of 2.02 ± 0.51 mm (mean ± SD, range 1.05-3.60 mm)                    exposed to the solution between connectors to a force transducer (Model 403,                    Cambridge Technology, Inc., U.S.A.) and a DC torque motor (Model 300H, Cambridge                    Technology, Inc.). The apparatus was mounted on the stage of an inverted                    microscope (Zeiss Axiovert-35, Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). While the fiber                    was in the relaxing solution, the sarcomere length (SL) was set to 2.76                    ± 0.04 μm (range 2.66-2.85 μm) by adjusting the                    overall segment length. The segments were observed at a magnification of                    320×. Prints of the fiber segments were taken with a videoprinter                    (P71E, Mitsubishi Electric Corp., Japan). The sarcomere length, the segment                    width and the length of segment between the connectors were measured directly                    from the microscope via a TV-overlay with the aid of a digitizer connected to a                    microcomputer (Videoplan, Kontron Bildanalyes Gmbh, Munich, Germany). The final                    magnification with the image analysis system on the TV screen was                    1,480×. Fiber SL was measured routinely in the fibers during maximal                    activation. Fiber depth was measured by recording the vertical displacement of                    the microscope nosepiece while focusing on the top and bottom surfaces of the                    fiber. Cross-sectional fiber area (CSA) was calculated from the width and depth,                    assuming an elliptical circumference, and was corrected for the 20% swelling                    that is known to occur during skinning (22). Specific tension was calculated as maximum tension (Po)                    normalised to CSA.

Relaxing and activating solutions contained (in mM): 4 MgATP, 1 free                        Mg2+, 20 imidazole, 7 EGTA, 14.5 creatine phosphate and                    sufficient KCl to adjust the ionic strength to 180. The pH was adjusted to 7.0.                    The free Ca2+ concentrations were 10−9 M                    (relaxing solution) and 10−4.5 M (maximum activation                    solution) and are expressed as pCa (−log[Ca2+]). Apparent                    stability constants for Ca2+-EGTA were corrected for temperature and                    ionic strength (23). The computer program                    of Fabiato(23) was used to calculate the                    concentrations of each metal, ligand and metal-ligand complex. Immediately                    preceding each activation, the fiber was immersed for 10-20 s in a solution with                    a reduced Ca2+-EGTA buffering capacity (24). This solution was identical to the relaxing solution                    except that EGTA was reduced to 0.5 mM, which resulted in more rapid attainment                    of steady tension during subsequent activation and helped to preserve the                    regularity of cross-striations during activation.

V0 was measured by the slack-test procedure (25). Fibers were activated at pCa 4.5 and                    once steady tension was reached, various amplitudes of slack (ΔL)                    were rapidly introduced (within 1-2 ms) at one end of the fiber. The time                    (Δt) required to take up the imposed slack was measured from the                    onset of the length step to the beginning of tension redevelopment. For each                    amplitude of ΔL, the fiber was re-extended while relaxed in order to                    minimize non-uniformity of sarcomere length. A straight line was fitted to a                    plot of ΔL versus Δt, using a                    least-squares regression, and the slope of the line was recorded as                    V0 for that fiber. Maximum active tension (P0) was                    calculated as the difference between the total tension in the activating                    solution (pCa 4.5) and the resting tension measured in the same segment while in                    the relaxing solution. All contractile measurements were carried out at                    15°C. The contractile recordings were accepted in subsequent analyses                    if a V0 value was based on linear regressions including four or more                    data points, and data were discarded if r for the fitted line                    was less than 0.97, if P0 changed more than 10% from first to final                    activation, or if SL during isometric tension development changed by more than                    0.10 μm compared with SL while the fiber was relaxed (22).

The single fiber in vitro motility system has been                    described in detail elsewhere (26, 27). In brief, a short muscle fiber segment                    was placed on a glass slide between two stripes of grease and a                    nitrocellulose-coated coverslip was placed on top, creating a flow-cell of                    approximately 2 μl volume. Myosin was extracted from the fiber                    segment through addition of a high-salt buffer (0.5 M KCl, 25 mM Hepes pH 7.6, 4                    mM MgCl2, 4 mM EGTA, 2 mM ATP, 1% β-mercaptoethanol).                    After a 30 minute incubation on ice, a low-salt buffer (25 mM KCl, 25 mM Hepes                    pH 7.6, 4 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 1% β-mercaptoethanol) was                    applied, followed by BSA (1 mg/ml). Non-functional myosin molecules were blocked                    with fragmentized F-actin and rhodamine-phalloidin (Rh-Ph; Molecular Probes                    Inc., OR) labeled actin filaments were subsequently infused into the flow-cell                    followed by motility buffer (2 mM ATP, 0.1 mg/ml glucose oxidase, 23                    μg/ml catalase, 2.5 mg/ml glucose, 0.4% methyl cellulose in low-salt                    buffer) to initiate movement. The pH of the buffers was adjusted with KOH and                    the final ionic strength of the motility buffer was 71 mM. The flow-cell was                    placed on the stage of an inverted epifluorescence microscope (Olympus IX 70,                    Olympus America Inc. NY) and thermostatically controlled at 25°C.                    Actin movements were filmed with an image intensified SIT camera (SIT 66,                    DAGE-MIT Inc., USA) and recorded on tape with a VCR.

From each single fiber preparation, 20 actin filaments moving with                    constant speed in an oriented motion were selected for speed analysis.                    Recordings and analysis were only performed from preparations in which                    >90% of the filaments moved bi-directionally. The center of a                    filament was tracked and the speed was calculated from 20 frames at an                    acquisition rate of 5 or 1 frame(s)/s depending on the fiber type, using an                    image analysis package (OPTIMAS 6.0, Optimas Corp., USA). The average speed and                    standard deviation of the 20 filaments were calculated. Since the standard                    deviation in this group of filaments was small (between 10-15% of the mean), the                    average velocity was taken as representative for the muscle fiber.

The MyHC composition of a muscle fiber or muscle biopsy sample was                    determined by 6% SDS-PAGE. The total acrylamide and Bis concentrations were 4%                    (w/v) in the stacking and 6% in the running gel, respectively, and the gel                    matrix included 30% glycerol. Electrophoresis was performed at 120 V for 24 h                    with a Tris-glycine electrode buffer (pH 8.3) at 15°C (SE 600                    vertical slab gel unit, Hoefer Scientific Instruments, USA).

The gels were silver-stained and subsequently scanned in a soft laser                    densitometer (Molecular Dynamics, Sunnyvale, CA) with a high spatial resolution                    (50 μm pixel spacing) and 4096 optical density levels (21, 28, 29). The volume integration                    function (ImageQuant software v3.3, Molecular Dynamics) was used to quantify the                    amount of protein on the 6% gels.

Means, standard deviations of means (SD) and correlation coefficients                    (r) of linear regressions were calculated from individual values by standard                    procedures. A two-tailed t-test was used for comparisons of two groups and                    one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among more than                    two groups. Differences were considered significant at p<0.05.

In previous studies on patients with the IIa MyHC missense mutation,                    morphological muscle biopsy analyses at the light and electron microscopic level                    have shown an irregular intermyofibrillar network and rimmed vacuoles in type 2A                    fibers, increased variability in size of muscle fibers, increased interstitial                    fat and connective tissue and internalized myonuclei (14, 15, 18). A short summary of the clinical                    picture and histopathological findings are listed in Materials &                    Methods. In this context it is interesting to observe that the structural                    abnormalities were predominantly in 2A fibers, but central nuclei also observed                    in other fiber types as well (Fig. 1). In                    individual membrane permeabilized muscle fiber segments, the size of individual                    muscle fibers can be determined and related to MyHC isoform expression. The                    advantage with this approach is that muscle fiber size is measured at a fixed                    sarcomere length. The β/slow (type I) and the two fast (type IIa and                    IIx) MyHC isoforms were expressed in all patients (Fig. 2). In both male and female patients, increased muscle fiber                    size variability was observed independent of MyHC isoform expression and there                    was no sign of muscle atrophy. On the contrary, the average size of fibers                    expressing the type I MyHC isoform was approximately twice as large as the                    corresponding cell type in age- and gender-matched controls. Muscle cells                    expressing different fast MyHC isoforms in patients were of the same size or                    slightly larger than in the controls (Table                    1). One muscle cell segment in a male patient co-expressed all adult                    human limb muscle MyHC isoforms (IIIa-IIx). This MyHC isoform expression was not                    observed in any of the muscle fiber segments from the controls and it is                    anticipated that this rare MyHC combination reflects a MyHC isoform                    transformation (2).

The study presents data on myosin function at the protein and cellular level                in patients carrying a missense mutation in the gene coding for the type IIa myosin.                Analyses at the motor protein level demonstrate a significant negative effect on the                function of the IIa MyHC isoform while other myosin isoforms had normal function.                Analyses at the cellular level, on the other hand, indicate that the IIa MyHC                mutation is associated with impairments in muscle cell function, which goes beyond                structure-function changes in the IIa MyHC isoform. These findings suggest complex                mechanisms underlying the symptoms of “autosomal dominant myopathy with                congenital contractures, ophtalmoplegia and rimmed vacuoles”.

Patients with the autosomal dominant myopathy, first described by Darin and                co-workers (15), are characterized by                multiple joint contractures at birth, mild myopathy at childhood and adolescence,                and a progressive course at middle age with proximal muscle weakness, ophtalmoplegia                and elevated creatine kinase serum levels. The histopathological abnormalities in                muscle tissue increase during the progression of the disease and include central                nuclei, fiber size variability and focal disorganization of myofilaments (inclusion                bodies) in type 2A fibers (14, 15, 18).                By screening the MyHC-IIa gene, a mutation was detected in affected family members,                i.e., a negatively charged glutamate replaced by a positively charged lysine residue                in the SH1 helix of the myosin molecule (14).                This mutation is in a region of the myosin molecule which is 100% conserved in both                conventional and unconventional myosins (14).                This region is close to the catalytic domain that undergoes a conformational change                during ATP hydrolysis. The altered charge in this region, induced by the mutation,                may have a significant effect on motor protein function. The present observations                demonstrating a significant decline in motility speed as well as an increased number                of dysfunctional IIa myosin molecules in the in vitro motility                assay support this hypothesis. The slowing of motility speed together with a                relatively well maintained specific tension at the cellular level indicate that the                impaired muscle function in these patients is not primarily caused by an impaired                thick filament assembly or myosin-actin binding. An altered conformational change of                the IIa myosin molecule during ATP hydrolysis appears to be a more likely cause                underlying the impaired IIa myosin function.

By introducing probes to the Dictyostelium discoideum                myosin class II motor domain, myosin function has been studied in detail and                interpreted in relation to motor domain crystal structures. Zeng and co-workers                    (30) recently constructed the equivalent                mutation to the E706K mutation in human IIa MyHC in Dictyostelium                discoideum myosin converter region, E683K. It was predicted that the change                in amino acid charge would result in charge repulsion instead of attraction. This                would in turn lead to a kinked and/or more unstable helix region and a reduced range                of motion of the converter domain. Studies on nucleotide binding and hydrolysis                indicated a 3-fold reduction of the hydrolysis step and slowing of the acto-myosin                dissociation leading to a higher duty ratio in the cycle and a reduced contractile                speed (30). Thus, the slowing observed in the                    in vitro motility speed in the mutated IIa myosin from the                patients is consistent with the “braking” action suggested by                the Dictyostelium E683K mutation, but the reduction in motility                speed was less than the 3-fold reduction anticipated from the                Dictyostelium experiments. However, the smaller reduction in                motility speed is most probably related to the fact that in the heterozygous                subjects the motility speed is determined by the combined action of both the native                and mutated IIa MyHCs.

Contractile recordings at the muscle cell and motor protein levels were                included only when specific criteria for acceptance were fulfilled (see Materials                and Methods). At the single muscle fiber level, the criteria originally introduced                by Moss (22) were used for acceptance.                According to these criteria, high reproducibility, e.g., when comparing two segments                of the same muscle fiber from both rabbit (21) and human skeletal muscle (31),                has been reported in contractile recordings from single membrane permeabilized                muscle fibers. In the single muscle fiber in vitro motility assay,                originally developed within our group (27,                    32, 33), motility speed was measured from actin filaments which were only moving                parallel or anti-parallel to the flow in the experimental chamber. These filaments                move slightly faster, but more importantly, they show significantly less variable                motility speed than randomly moving filaments (27). When restricting analyses to actin filaments moving parallel or                anti-parallel, reproducibility equal to that seen at the single muscle fiber level                    (21, 31) has been reported, e.g., when comparing motility speed between two                halves of the same muscle fiber (32, 34). Due to the necessary strict acceptance                criteria, numerous preparations were discarded from both patient and control                subjects. The risk associated with eliminating preparations representing a                subpopulation of normal muscle cells or motor proteins by excessively stringent                criteria is negligible compared with the advantage of removing false measurements.                However, an increased number of preparations failing to reach the level of                acceptance may reflect pathological changes at the muscle cell or motor protein                levels, thus we have compared the success rate in preparations from patients and                controls in an attempt to monitor this possibility.

The mutated and native alleles of the IIa MyHC isoform are equally expressed                at the transcript level in patients with the E706K mutation (18). In patients carrying the E706K mutation, the IIa MyHC mRNA                levels consistently exceeded the IIa MyHC protein levels, while IIa myosin mRNA and                protein levels were roughly the same in healthy controls (18). It was therefore suggested that the disproportionately                high IIa MyHC transcript levels reflected an increased turnover of the mutated IIa                myosin (18). The amount of mutated versus                native IIa myosin may accordingly vary between different 2A muscle fibers. The                relative amounts of mutated versus native myosin in the different muscle cells                investigated in this study are not known. A considerable effort was made to separate                the mutated vs. the native myosin using 2D gel electrophoresis and mass                spectrometry, but we were not able to obtain reproducible results using either                approach. The slowing of actin filament speed propelled by the motor protein and the                high rate of unacceptable recordings in preparations expressing IIa or co-expressing                IIa and IIx myosin indicate a significant amount of mutated IIa MyHC in all these                preparations.

Proteolytic degradation products of myofibrillar proteins have been                suggested to have a toxic effect on muscle cell structure leading to impaired                function and eventually apoptotic effects (35). The increased turnover of the mutated IIa myosin (18) may accordingly result in an increased amount of toxic                byproducts that may have a general effect on muscle cell structure and function                leading to the increased number of preparations failing to reach the level of                acceptance.

At the motor protein level, the disproportionately high number of                unsuccessful recordings was restricted to preparations expressing the IIa MyHC                isoform. At the cellular level the number of preparations that fulfilled the                criteria for acceptance was also very low (4%). However, in contrast to recordings                at the motor protein level, the poor success rate at the cellular level affected all                fiber types irrespective of the MyHC isoform expression. This 4% success rate should                be compared with the 75% success rate typically obtained when performing contractile                recordings from normal human muscle fiber segments by the experienced scientist                conducting all contractile recordings in this study and having more than eight years                experience with this experimental setup. The extremely low success rate in all fiber                types suggests that the mechanisms underlying the phenotype of the inclusion body                myopathy with the E706K mutation is not restricted to structural and functional                changes in the converter region of the IIa myosin molecule. The increased frequency                of structural abnormalities during activation with high calcium concentrations, such                as uneven cross-striation pattern, partial or complete disruption of the muscle                fiber segments, suggest alterations in cytoskeletal elements. The involvement of                other proteins is supported by the progressive nature of the disease in adulthood                and the mild phenotype in children. While the missense mutation of the                MYH2 gene is causing the myopathy, other genes and proteins may                play important roles as modifiers of the symptoms. The polygenic nature of                phenotypes such as muscle mass, varying according to the age (36, 37), may contribute                to certain deviations from the mendelian type of inheritance of this myosinopathy in                some patients, such as the conversion of recessive to a dominant effect over the                life span. It can be hypothesized that allelic variations of other genes influenced                the ability of muscle tissue to adapt to the primary mutation. Adoption of the                polygenic concept might help to identify pathways that could be targeted to delay                and/or alleviate symptoms of the disease.

The involvement of muscle fiber types other than 2A, expressing the IIa MyHC                isoform, is supported by pathological changes in all muscle fiber types in severely                affected muscles from the oldest patients carrying the E706K mutation, although                rimmed vacuoles were restricted to muscle fibers expressing the IIa MyHC isoform                    (14). In young patients with a milder                phenotype, on the other hand, morphological changes were restricted to 2A fibers.                The muscle samples in this study were taken from affected muscles in adult patients,                albeit not with a severe phenotype. The present observations suggest that changes in                muscle cell function precede changes in muscle fiber morphology, representing a                later stage in the pathological process.

The four muscle cells that fulfilled the criteria for acceptance expressed                the β/slow (type I) MyHC isoform had average shortening velocities that                were significantly faster than in controls. We have observed a similar increase in                shortening velocity in hypertrophic muscle cells expressing the β/slow                MyHC isoform in overused slow-twitch motor units from patients with a prior polio                lesion (38, 39). It is suggested that the faster V0 and larger size of                fibers expressing the type I MyHC isoform in patients carrying the IIa myosin                missense mutation than in the control subjects are secondary to a functional                overload (38, 39). In vitro motility observations indicate that the                faster shortening velocities at the muscle cell level were not secondary to changes                in the function of the β/slow MyHC isoform.

In conclusion, the MYH2 E706K mutation has a significant                negative effect on the function of the IIa myosin isoform consistent with the                “braking” action suggested by the                Dictyostelium E683K mutation. However, single fiber contractile                measurements indicate that the phenotype of this autosomal dominant myopathy is                caused by changes that go beyond the IIa MyHC and probably affects structural                elements in all muscle fiber types.
