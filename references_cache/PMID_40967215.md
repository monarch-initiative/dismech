A significant complication of Crohn's disease (CD) is intestinal fibrosis, which
narrows the bowel lumen to form a stricture. Creeping fat (CF) is the wrapping
of mesenteric adipose tissue around diseased bowel, of which the role in CD
stricture progression is unclear. By constructing a human single-cell CD
fibroblast atlas, we identified CF-derived, CTHRC1+ fibroblasts enriched for
Yes-associated protein (YAP)/transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif
(TAZ) signatures and localized to a fibrotic CF-bowel wall interface within the
stricture. We further showed that analogous Cthrc1+ mouse fibroblasts derive
from mesenteric adipose tissue stromal cells, infiltrate fibrotic bowel, and
deposit extracellular matrix in a YAP/TAZ-dependent manner in a mouse model of
intestinal fibrosis. Our findings identify CF as a key source of pro-fibrotic
fibroblasts and raise the possibility of improving future clinical management of
stricture progression by targeting not only the bowel but also CF.

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a subtype of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterized by cycles of patchy, transmural inflammation primarily in the bowel. Chronic inflammation triggers intestinal fibrosis, wherein thick scar tissue replaces the normal bowel parenchyma, narrowing the lumen to cause a stricture.1 Although strictures present in both adult and pediatric patients, pediatric CD strictures are understudied and may have a different course of disease progression.2 An estimated 30–50% of pediatric patients develop an intestinal stricture within five years of diagnosis,3,4 approximately 28% will require intra-abdominal surgery within ten years of diagnosis,5 and 22–47% of strictures recur after surgery.5–7 Finally, no anti-fibrotic therapies have been approved.8 Thus, strictures cause significant morbidity in CD patients.

Creeping fat (CF) is the expansion and wrapping of mesenteric adipose tissue (MAT) around diseased bowel and is pathognomonic for CD.9 CF has long been utilized by surgeons as a landmark for strictures during intestinal resections, with an estimated 70–90% of intestinal strictures containing CF.10,11 CF is associated with recurrence after bowel resection, and removal of CF reduces stricture rates.9–13 Histologically, CF wrapping correlates with fibrosis severity and increased bowel wall thickness.9,11 Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies revealed that CF contains networks of pro-fibrotic immune and stromal cells that stimulate extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, aberrant adipocyte differentiation, and smooth muscle hypertrophy.14–17 However, the contribution of the CF-derived stromal cells to CD stricture progression remains largely understudied.

While inflammation plays a critical role in CD fibrosis, mechanical properties of diseased tissue such as mechanical tension, ECM composition, and stiffness also promote CD fibrosis progression.8,18 Despite the introduction of immune-modulating treatments, stricture incidence and surgical intervention rates for CD have remained relatively unchanged.19–21 Furthermore, strictures resolve in only 8% of patients with medical therapy alone, and the 5-year risk of surgery after initiating biologics remains unchanged.18,21–23 Finally, CD strictures largely manifest at the ileocecal junction, where the relatively small diameter of the ileocecal valve causes intestinal contents to exert high mechanical stress, potentially making this location susceptible to fibrosis. 8,18,24,25

Large-scale transcriptomic studies have revealed that fibroblasts, the cells primarily responsible for ECM synthesis and deposition, exhibit transcriptional and functional heterogeneity in multiple organs and disease states.26–28 A subset of fibroblasts known as mechanosensitive fibroblasts is particularly sensitive to mechanical tension in the wound microenvironment and produces high levels of ECM.27–32 These fibroblasts transduce mechanical signals via the Hippo/yes-associated-protein (YAP)/transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) signaling pathway and are hypothesized to play a key role in fibrosis progression.27–32 In the context of CD, YAP signaling is necessary for bowel fibroblast activation and ECM deposition in mouse models of IBD.33–35 However, the sources of mechanosensitive fibroblasts and the contribution of CF-derived fibroblasts to intestinal fibrosis remain unclear due in part to the difficulty of modeling CF in vivo.

We hypothesized that CF-derived mechanosensitive fibroblasts promote intestinal fibrosis in a YAP/TAZ-dependent manner. To identify mechanosensitive fibroblast populations in strictures, we constructed a CD fibroblast atlas by performing a meta-analysis of CD scRNA-seq studies comprising of both bowel and MAT. We then integrated spatial transcriptomics (ST) and ECM ultrastructure analysis30,32 to show that these mechanosensitive fibroblasts occupy fibrotic niches at the interface between CF and the bowel wall. To explore the role of these fibroblasts in vivo, we developed a colotomy-based mouse model that consistently generates both CF and intestinal fibrosis, mimicking human CD strictures. Using this model, we show that CF-derived mechanosensitive fibroblasts migrate into the stricture site to promote fibrosis, and that blocking YAP/TAZ signaling in CF can significantly reduce fibrosis. Our study identifies CF-derived fibroblasts as a key contributor to CD stricture progression and underscores the importance of targeting both the bowel and CF to achieve anti-fibrotic therapeutic efficacy.

CF wrapping correlates with fibrosis severity in Crohn’s disease (CD), suggesting that mesenteric adipose tissue (MAT) may participate in stricture progression (Figure 1A).9,36 We initially hypothesized that a subset of CF-derived, pro-fibrotic fibroblasts migrate into the bowel wall (BW) and promote stricture progression. To explore this possibility, we constructed a pan-study CD single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) fibroblast atlas to identify pathogenic fibroblasts as follows (Figure 1B). First, we curated publicly available scRNA-seq studies on bowel and MAT samples obtained from biopsies or full-thickness resections of patients with or without CD (Figure S1A, Table S1). We simultaneously performed in-house scRNA-seq on full-thickness, paired bowel and MAT samples in pediatric patients from CD strictures and uninvolved, adjacent tissue, as well as ostomy takedowns from 5 non-CD pediatric patients (Table S2). We then assigned disease states based on disease presentation and integrated all studies to obtain a CD atlas containing approximately 1.8 million cells (80,327 from MAT and 1,729,759 from bowel) from 426 patients. After identifying global cell types, we iteratively subclustered the fibroblasts, resulting in ten global fibroblast clusters with uniform representation across all study parameters except for mucosal biopsies, which lacked the complete fibroblast repertoire of full-thickness samples (Figure 1C, Figure S1B–D; Table S3).

To characterize fibroblast phenotypes, we measured the expression of gene signatures that infer sources of fibroblast activation (YAP/TAZ signaling, inflammation, and antigen-presentation), ECM production, or migration capacity (Table S4). CTHRC1+ fibroblasts had the highest expression of ECM genes, exhibited high levels of YAP/TAZ target genes, and expressed components of the YAP/TAZ pathway and migration genes (Figure 1D, Figure S1E–G). We then assessed global fibroblast enrichment from our CD fibroblast atlas across tissues and disease states by confining our analysis to non-CD histologically normal tissue, CD uninvolved tissue adjacent to a stricture, and CD strictured tissue from full-thickness bowel and MAT samples (Figure 1E). CTHRC1+ fibroblasts were the only population that significantly increased both globally and in strictured bowel and MAT compared to normal and uninvolved tissue (Figure 1F, Figure S1H–J). Intriguingly, we found that treatment modality did not significantly impact CTHRC1+ fibroblast enrichment in strictures (Figure S1K). CTHRC1+ fibroblasts strongly activated ECM and YAP/TAZ signatures in uninvolved and strictured tissue, especially in CF, compared to other disease states, suggesting that these fibroblasts become activated early in stricture pathogenesis (Figure 1G). To more accurately assess fibroblast tissue heterogeneity, we independently subclustered global fibroblasts by tissue, annotated the populations using tissue-specific markers,37–40 and measured fibroblast enrichment (Figure S2A–H, Table S3). As expected, CTHRC1+ fibroblasts were enriched in both strictured bowel and CF. Furthermore, anchor label transfer between MAT and bowel fibroblasts assessing gene expression similarity revealed that CTHRC1+ fibroblasts were similar between tissues (Figure S2I).

Because our CD fibroblast atlas consisted of a wide variety of disease states, sampling methods, and tissue sources, we sought to validate our initial findings by exclusively analyzing our pediatric dataset containing paired bowel and MAT samples from stricture and uninvolved, adjacent tissue. We subsetted the CD atlas for labeled fibroblasts from our study, independently re-clustered them at high resolution, and mapped them back to the original CD atlas by anchor-label transfer (Figure S2J, Table S3). As expected, CTHRC1+ fibroblasts were present in both bowel and MAT, expanded in stricture compared to uninvolved tissue, and strongly resembled CTHRC1+ fibroblasts identified in our CD atlas (Figure S2K–L). Interestingly, we also identified pediatric fibroblast populations that differed from our CD atlas populations (predominantly from adult patients), suggesting that there may be differences in fibroblast heterogeneity between pediatric and adult strictures. Nonetheless, the expansion of CTHRC1+ fibroblasts in both patient populations reinforces that CTHRC1+ fibroblasts are integral to CD fibrosis. Altogether, these results demonstrate that mechanosensitive CTHRC1+ fibroblasts are a stricture-associated fibroblast present in both bowel and CF (Figure 1H).

We next sought to determine what transcriptional networks regulate CTHRC1+ fibroblast identity and pathogenicity using single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) to discover significant transcription factors and their target genes (regulons) (Table S5).41 Performing SCENIC on our independently re-clustered MAT fibroblasts from our CD atlas, we identified key transcription factor regulons upregulated in CTHRC1+ fibroblasts from CF compared to those from normal MAT, including KLF6, REL, EGR3, SOX4, and previously reported TWIST1 (Figure S2M).39,40 Target gene analysis revealed networks that regulated CTHRC1 and POSTN, as well as other genes responsible for ECM deposition, cytoskeletal reorganization, and migration (Figure S2N). These results further underscore that CF-derived CTHRC1+ fibroblasts are pro-fibrotic and pathogenic.

We next reasoned that CTHRC1+ fibroblasts would localize to fibrotic regions within strictures. To identify these areas, we utilized a multi-modal approach by integrating ECM ultrastructure analysis with Visium spatial transcriptomics (Figure 2A).30,32 We initially observed that the MAT-BW interface (IF) contained a thick zone of fibrosis below the muscularis propria (MP) (Figure 2B). Thus, we partitioned stricture or uninvolved bowel histology into three distinct regions: uninvolved and stricture BW (lumen through the MP) and the IF (stroma between the MP and CF), which was only present in strictured bowel. We then imaged samples stained with PicroSirius Red, quantified 294 parameters of ECM ultrastructure, performed dimensionality reduction, and assigned increasing values from normal to fibrotic ECM, which we denoted as fibrosis pseudotime. ECM ultrastructure analysis revealed that IF ECM ultrastructure tended to cluster further away from uninvolved ECM ultrastructure and had significantly higher fibrosis pseudotime values than other regions (Figure 2C–D). In agreement with a prior ultrastructure analysis, these findings reveal that the CF/BW interface may be the primary location of fibrosis in advanced strictures.42

We then performed spatial transcriptomics (ST) on a pediatric stricture from our cohort by dissecting a stricture sample into two regions: Inner BW (spanning lumen to the superficial IF), and Outer BW (spanning the inferior MP, IF, and CF) (Figure 2E, Figure S3A). We found that the IF was strongly enriched for ECM, mechanotransduction, migration, and pathogenic fibroblast signatures and genes compared to other regions (Figure 2F, Figure S3B–D). To confirm that the IF is a transcriptionally unique region, we subclustered the Visium spots using a low resolution. Of the four neighborhoods identified, Stromal 3 spots clustered independently, overlapped with IF spots, and transcriptionally resembled the IF spots (Figure S3E–G, Table S3). Stromal 3 was also enriched for mechanosensitive gene signatures and ECM remodeling pathways compared to other stromal spots (Figure S3H–I). Finally, quantification of nuclear YAP, a readout for mechanical fibroblast activation, demonstrated that CF and IF had the most nuclear YAP, further corroborating that both CF and IF are mechanosensitive regions in human strictures (Figure S3J).

After confirming that the IF is a fibrotic area within strictures, we sought to determine if CTHRC1+ fibroblasts localized to this region. We performed Xenium on five pediatric strictures and three paired uninvolved bowel samples (Figure S4A) with associated MAT using a custom probe set (Table S6). We deconvoluted the spatial data using the scRNA-seq CD global cell atlas containing an additional adipocyte dataset and inferred cell neighborhoods using Niche Covariation (Figure 3A).43,44 As expected, predicted CTHRC1+ fibroblasts were enriched in CF and the IF, and a subset were triple positive for transcripts of CTHRC1, POSTN, and YAP target gene CCN1 (Figure 3B–D). Further corroborating these data, we found nuclear YAP+POSTN+ cells enriched in the CF and IF of strictures compared to uninvolved tissue, suggesting that these cells are the pro-fibrotic CTHRC1+ fibroblasts we identified previously in our CD fibroblast atlas (Figure S4B). To determine where CF-derived CTHRC1+ fibroblasts become activated, we quantified YAP target gene transcripts in mapped CTHRC1+ fibroblasts at three distance ranges away from the BW, starting in the IF through distant CF (Figure 3E). Surprisingly, target gene transcript counts were lowest in the IF and highest in CF furthest away from the BW, suggesting that CTHRC1+ fibroblasts become mechanically activated far from the stricture (Figure 3F–G).

Given ECM heterogeneity within a stricture, we sought to elucidate the cellular composition of CTHRC1+ neighborhoods. We calculated cell-cell interaction scores by identifying neighboring cells with a high predictive capacity for CTHRC1+ fibroblasts and vice versa. Analysis of CTHRC1+ neighborhood interactions by region revealed that CTHRC1+ fibroblasts occupied distinct fibrotic niches (Figure 4A–B, Figure S4C, Table S7). Overall, CTHRC1+ fibroblasts interacted strongly with macrophages along with PI16+ and GREM1+ fibroblasts in perivascular regions. CF-CTHRC1+ neighborhoods exhibited immune cell (macrophages and neutrophils) and fibroblast (PI16+ and CD74+) interactions. IF-CTHRC1+ neighborhoods consisted of macrophage, plasma cell, pericyte, and fibroblast interactions in perivascular regions (GREM1+, THBS1+). Finally, BW-CTHRC1+ neighborhoods primarily had fibroblast interactions (GREM1+, MMP3+, PI16+). Thus, ECM heterogeneity paralleled changes in CTHRC1+ fibroblast niches.

We then extrapolated major cell-cell communication pathways from ligand-receptor gene expression in CTHRC1+ neighborhoods using our CD cell atlas as a reference. To specifically pinpoint pro-fibrotic pathways, we measured differentially upregulated pathways in stricture compared to uninvolved and normal cells. Subsequently, we isolated interactions occurring between CTHRC1+ fibroblasts and their neighbors identified earlier. Corroborating our neighborhood analysis, CTHRC1+ fibroblasts strongly communicated with most predicted neighbors (Figure S4D–E). Ligand-receptor analysis revealed that CTHRC1+ fibroblasts predominantly interacted via ECM-integrin pathways with most neighbors, along with smaller contributions from pro-fibrotic, macrophage-derived cytokines (TNF, TGF-β, and OSM), and angiogenic, pericyte-derived factors (FGF, JAG1) (Figure 4C–F). These data suggest that CTHRC1+ fibroblasts primarily interact with their microenvironment via the ECM.

Although we identified pro-fibrotic CTHRC1+ fibroblasts in human CD strictures, we were unable to determine where they originated from or their contribution to bowel fibrosis. An initial survey of mouse models of IBD and CF revealed that most did not consistently generate CF except for an endoscopic punch biopsy model, which required specialized equipment.42,45–47 Thus, we sought to design a consistent and accessible mouse model of intestinal fibrosis and CF with the goal of specifically addressing how mechanical tension activates CTHRC1+ fibroblasts in late-stage strictures. To this end, we generated a surgical mouse model whereby we created a longitudinal, anti-mesenteric incision in the colon (colotomy) (Figure 5A, Figure S5A). We then effaced the mucosa of surrounding bowel to induce transmural injury, closed the colotomy transversally under tension to create a pouch, and stretched the colotomy horizontally for an additional fifteen minutes at maximal tension. Recognizing that strictures primarily occur in the terminal ileum, we chose the proximal colon because its larger diameter and thicker BW compared to ileum allowed the BW to expand without causing a bowel obstruction and the ability to apply greater mechanical tension. For initial comparisons, we harvested bowel from sham surgeries (sham) or from bowel adjacent to the colotomy without fat wrapping (uninvolved). Both sham and colotomy mice experienced similar changes in weight and returned to baseline by post-operative (POD) 6 (Figure S5B). Grossly, we observed that MAT exclusively wrapped around colotomy sites as early as POD 7, peaked at POD 14, and persisted through POD 90 but did not wrap around sham or uninvolved bowel (Figure 5B, Figure S5C) Histologically, the BW exhibited transmural fibrosis at the colotomy site, including a fibrotic area between MAT and the BW similar to the IF in human strictures (Figure 5C). Finally, we found that nuclear YAP+ cells were enriched in MAT and IF in colotomies, mimicking our YAP findings in human CD strictures (Figure S5D). Thus, our model phenocopied grossly and histologically features of CD strictures and CF.

To determine how CF formation and fibrosis evolve over time in our model, we harvested tissue at five timepoints from colotomy or uninvolved tissue between POD 3 through POD 90, as well as sham from POD 14. We then quantified BW thickness, normalized trichrome-positive area, and MP thickness in bowel covered with MAT and found that all these metrics peaked between POD7 and POD 14 and decreased through POD 90 (Figure 5D–E, Figure S5E). Furthermore, MAT coverage reached fifty percent of the bowel circumference, and the angle between bowel and MAT was significantly reduced during this interval, meeting the clinical definition of CF (Figure 5F, Figure S5F).9,48 Despite some of these metrics returning to baseline, pseudotime analysis of ECM ultrastructure revealed that fibrosis continued to increase through POD 90, suggesting that the fibrosis was long-lasting (Figure 5G–H). Furthermore, we found that colotomy BW surrounded by MAT was more fibrotic than BW without MAT (Figure 5I–J). Altogether, these findings demonstrate that our model generates long-lasting, transmural fibrosis and CF that phenocopies key features of CD strictures. Because we observed no significant histological differences between sham and uninvolved samples, we performed all downstream histological analyses using uninvolved and involved tissue at POD 14, when fibrosis metrics were maximal.

After confirming the congruency of our model with CD strictures, we performed scRNA-seq on bowel and MAT from colotomy and sham mice at POD 14 to see if our model could also capture CTHRC1+ mechanosensitive fibroblasts (Figure 6A). We subclustered the fibroblasts and identified ten major global clusters that we subsequently assessed for enrichment and phenotype (Figure 6B, Figure S5G–H, Table S3). We identified Cthrc1+ fibroblasts enriched in colotomies with high expression of ECM, mechanical, migration, and pathologic fibroblast signatures (Figure 6C–F, Figure S5I–J, Table S3). To determine if mouse Cthrc1+ fibroblasts were analogous to human CTHRC1+ mechanosensitive fibroblasts, we performed anchor label transfer between our mouse fibroblast dataset and our human CD fibroblast atlas. Mouse Cthrc1+ fibroblasts mapped strongly onto the human CTHRC1+ fibroblasts, suggesting these fibroblast populations are conserved between species (Figure 6G). To confirm our mouse scRNA-seq findings at the protein level, we co-stained for YAP1 and Cthrc1+ fibroblast marker POSTN, which revealed the increased presence of nuclear YAP1+POSTN+ cells in colotomy MAT and IF compared to uninvolved BW and MAT (Figure 6H), mimicking CTHRC1+ fibroblast expansion in human strictures.

We then studied if altering mechanical tension in our model could promote fibrosis and Cthrc1+ fibroblast activation. To interrogate this possibility, we closed colotomies under minimal tension without stretching and compared them to the standard colotomy model of fifteen minutes under maximal tension (Figure S6A). We observed that BW thickness, normalized trichrome-positive area, and MAT circumference significantly increased in stretched compared to unstretched colotomies and uninvolved bowel (Figure S6B–E). Concurrently, ECM ultrastructure analysis revealed that stretched colotomies had more fibrotic architecture compared to unstretched colotomies (Figure S6F–G). Immunostaining for YAP and Cthrc1+ fibroblast marker POSTN revealed more nuclear YAP+POSTN+ cells in stretched IF and MAT compared to unstretched regions or BW (Figure S6H). To exclude a role for accessory immune cells in inducing YAP activation following stretching, we plated MAT fibroblasts in either stretched or unstretched hydrogels and quantified nuclear YAP. We observed that stretched MAT fibroblasts significantly increased nuclear YAP compared to unstretched controls, indicating that mechanical tension is sufficient to activate MAT fibroblasts ex vivo (Figure S6I–K). These findings support the possibility that mechanical tension in concert with inflammation play an important role in CF formation and intestinal fibrosis progression.

To determine if Cthrc1+ fibroblasts are broadly associated with bowel fibrosis, we examined the SAMP1/YitFc (SAMP) mouse strain, an IBD model which spontaneously develops progressive, ileal-specific inflammation that recapitulates human CD in regard to disease location, the presence of patchy, discontinuous inflammatory lesions, and response to conventional clinical therapies.49 Furthermore, SAMP mice develop a severe, fully established ileal fibrosis by 30–40 weeks of age, during which time pro-fibrotic genes were elevated, collagen deposition within the lamina propria and submucosa were increased, and striking hypertrophy of the MP was observed compared to their parental control strain AKR (Figure S6L–N).50,51
Cthrc1+ fibroblast marker Postn expression was significantly increased in fibrotic ilea of SAMP compared to AKR controls (Figure S6O). Immunostaining revealed increased POSTN expression within the subepithelial compartment and submucosa of SAMP mice (Figure S6P–Q). Furthermore, we confirmed that some of these POSTN+ cells expressed nuclear YAP (Figure S6P) and fibroblast marker PDGFRA (Figure S6Q), suggesting that these fibroblasts resemble Cthrc1+ fibroblasts in the colotomy model. These results demonstrate that Cthrc1+ fibroblasts correlate with bowel fibrosis across multiple mouse intestinal fibrosis models, suggesting that they may be a universal target in bowel fibrosis.

Given the presence of mechanosensitive fibroblasts in the MAT-colotomy IF, we sought to determine if some of these fibroblasts came from MAT. Because we could not identify any MAT-specific fibroblast markers by scRNA-seq, we initially employed a localized lineage tracing strategy to label MAT-derived fibroblasts. We encapsulated 4-hydroxytamoxifen in liposomes (LiTMX) as done previously29,52 and injected LiTMX into the mesentery of Col1a2-CreERT2; ZsGreen+ mice that underwent sham or colotomy surgeries (Figure S7A). To determine the specificity of our lineage-tracing system, we compared the labeling of Col1a2+ cells in LiTMX to Col1a2-CreERT2; ZsGreen+ mice that received no liposomes or systemic tamoxifen in uninvolved and colotomy bowel at POD 14. Systemic tamoxifen delivery yielded widespread labeling of uninvolved and colotomy BW and MAT fibroblasts, impeding our ability to distinguish between MAT- and bowel-derived fibroblasts. Conversely, injection of LiTMX yielded robust labeling of MAT fibroblasts and sparse labeling of BW fibroblasts, with notable GFP+ fibroblast expansion occurring at the IF. For these reasons, we chose to utilize LiTMX to label MAT-derived fibroblasts in our mouse model.

To assess the contribution of CF-derived fibroblasts to intestinal fibrosis, we generated Col1a2-CreERT2; YAPlox/lox;TAZlox/lox; ZsGreen+ (YAP/TAZ KO) and Col1a2-CreERT2; YAPlox/+;TAZlox/+; ZsGreen+ (YAP/TAZ Het) as controls, injected the mice one time with LiTMX, created colotomies, and harvested the mice at POD 14 (Figure 7A). Grossly, we observed that YAP/TAZ KO mice reduced MAT wrapping around colotomies compared to YAP/TAZ Het mice (Figure 7B–C). We then separately quantified fibrosis parameters in the total bowel ring as well as bowel exclusively covered with MAT within the colotomy site or uninvolved regions. In total bowel, there were no significant differences in BW thickness and normalized trichrome-positive area between YAP/TAZ Het and KO mice in either uninvolved or colotomies (Figure S7B–D). Conversely, when we exclusively investigated bowel surrounded by MAT, we found that YAP/TAZ KO mice exhibited reduced BW thickness and normalized trichrome-positive area, but no differences in uninvolved regions. Furthermore, YAP/TAZ KO mice exhibited reduced MAT coverage and less fibrotic ECM ultrastructure at the IF compared to YAP/TAZ Hets (Figure 7D–H). These findings suggest that the effects of CF-driven fibrosis are localized to the BW adjacent to MAT and are not due to global BW thinning.

We then wanted to confirm that the histological findings correlated with MAT-derived Cthrc1+ fibroblasts. To rule out the possibility that the reduction in fibrosis was due to widespread ablation of YAP/TAZ, we quantified nuclear YAP in MAT, the MAT-Colotomy IF, and the BW adjacent to MAT. YAP/TAZ KO mice had very low levels of nuclear YAP in MAT and the IF without significant differences in the BW, reinforcing that the liposome injections were localized to MAT (Figure S7E). Co-staining for YAP with POSTN revealed the presence of nuclear YAP+GFP+POSTN+ and GFP+POSTN+ cells that infiltrated the colotomy site at the MAT-Colotomy interface in YAP/TAZ Het mice (Figure 7I). Conversely, YAP/TAZ KO mice had reduced levels of infiltrating nuclear YAP+POSTN+ cells and no GFP+YAP+POSTN+ cells, suggesting that YAP/TAZ KO also reduced the expansion of MAT-derived mechanosensitive fibroblasts. These findings demonstrate that MAT-derived Cthrc1+ fibroblasts promote fibrosis in a YAP/TAZ-dependent manner and that inhibition of YAP/TAZ signaling in MAT fibroblasts is sufficient to ameliorate intestinal fibrosis.

To confirm that our LiTMX lineage tracing studies were not confounded by bowel fibroblast labeling, we grafted MAT from YAP/TAZ Het and YAP/TAZ KO ZsGreen+ donors into ZsGreen− recipients that underwent a colotomy (Figure S7F). In uninvolved, adjacent regions, we did not observe any infiltration of MAT-derived GFP+ fibroblasts into the BW (Figure S7G). In the colotomy, however, we found that MAT-derived GFP+POSTN+ fibroblasts from YAP/TAZ Het donors migrated deeper into the BW than those from YAP/TAZ KO donors (Figure S7H). To exclude the possibility that our results were confounded by poor engraftment in KO mice, we stained our grafts for apoptosis marker Caspase3 (CASP3) (Figure S7I). We found no differences in CASP3 staining, suggesting that the difference in migration is not due to poor engraftment in KO mice. Consequently, YAP/TAZ signaling also plays a key role in the migration of MAT-derived Cthrc1+ fibroblasts and the establishment of the fibrotic IF.

It is well known that CF associates with intestinal strictures in CD, but its contribution to intestinal fibrosis has remained elusive. Most studies characterizing fibroblast heterogeneity in CD strictures have focused understandably on the bowel, the primary location of ECM deposition. Recent scRNA-seq studies demonstrated that CF contains pro-fibrotic fibroblasts, but their connection to stricture progression was not fully addressed.14–16 The central goal of this study was to elucidate how CF-derived fibroblasts contribute to stricture progression and fibrosis in CD. We hypothesized that mechanosensitive, CF-derived fibroblasts migrate into the BW and deposit excessive ECM deposition in a YAP/TAZ-dependent manner. Using a combination of multi-omic analyses of human strictures and a lineage tracing in a mouse model of CF, we identified a CF-derived, pro-fibrotic, mechanosensitive fibroblast population marked by CTHRC1 that participates in intestinal fibrosis.

Our findings provide further evidence that profound changes in fibroblast heterogeneity, tissue architecture, and cell-cell networks underly fibrosis. CTHRC1+ fibroblasts in particular have been previously found in multiple organ fibroses and resemble FAP+ bowel-derived, ECM-producing fibroblasts recently identified in the submucosa of CD strictures.39,42,53–57 Most studies characterizing fibroblast heterogeneity in CD strictures have focused understandably on the bowel. Our study complements these findings by demonstrating that the CF-BW interface also undergoes fibrotic remodeling. Although we focused on CTHRC1+ fibroblasts, others such as ECM-producing GREM1+ and antigen-presenting CD74+ fibroblasts likely also participate in this remodeling. Given possible differences between pediatric and adult fibroblast heterogeneity, understanding the roles of other fibroblast populations may help to explain differences in disease progression between the two patient groups.

To determine if CF is a source of CTHRC1+ fibroblasts, we developed a mouse model of intestinal fibrosis and CF that phenocopies human CD strictures histologically and molecularly. Although other mouse models of CF have been developed recently,58 one significant advantage of our model is the ability to modulate mechanical tension to study Cthrc1+ fibroblast activation, which may be critical component of stricture progression. To isolate the effects of mechanical tension on Cthrc1+ fibroblast activation, we chose initially not to create colotomies in inflamed bowel. Nonetheless, the presence of Cthrc1+ fibroblasts in the SAMP1/YitFc model of IBD suggests that these cells are not confined to our disease model but rather are broadly applicable to fibrosis. Further validation in another model of CF recently developed58 may further demonstrate that these fibroblasts may represent a universal, evolutionarily conserved, pathogenic fibroblast for therapeutic targeting in strictures and other fibroses. Thus, incorporation of an inflammatory background for this model may provide further insight into mechanical-inflammatory crosstalk that may underly stricture resistance to biologics.

Recently, there has been an increased appreciation for the role of adipose tissue remodeling in wound healing and fibrosis.15,59–63 Several studies, including one on CF, suggest that adipose tissue migration may be beneficial for augmenting barrier function and preventing pathogenic bacterial translocation.15,63 However, our study suggests that over time, activation of CTHRC1+ fibroblasts may shift the balance of CF from a wound healing response toward fibrosis. Although we did not address the origins of CTHRC1+ fibroblasts, the proximity of CTHRC1+ fibroblasts to PI16+ stromal progenitors, pericytes, and adipocytes raises the intriguing possibility that they may transdifferentiate into CTHRC1+ fibroblasts, given prior evidence showing that pericytes and adipocytes can give rise to fibroblasts.16,59–65 These observations underscore the importance of understanding the connection between adipose tissue plasticity and CF remodeling.

Finally, our study provides evidence that CF-driven stricture progression can occur in an “outside-in” manner in the intestine. After CF formation, transmural inflammation may induce local stromal progenitors in CF to become CTHRC1+ fibroblasts or amplify mechanotransduction and migration in tissue-resident CTHRC1+ fibroblasts. In response to changes in the local ECM and tissue stiffness, these fibroblasts migrate from CF into the BW, where they deposit collagen and establish a fibrotic interface that thickens over time, leading to stenosis. This possibility may provide an explanation for why CD patients present with intestinal strictures at a higher rate than ulcerative colitis patients where the inflammation is usually limited to the mucosa without transmural inflammation. This model could also provide another explanation for aberrant smooth muscle expansion, which has been shown to be the primary cause of BW thickening.66 Enteric SMCs undergo hypertrophy and proliferate in response to ECM proteins such as fibronectin and collagen.17 Consequently, the accumulation of CF-derived subserosal ECM could also promote smooth muscle expansion. Finally, this model could also explain the high rate of post-operative stricture recurrence. Because CF is a source of mechanically active CTHRC1+ fibroblasts, it is possible that these fibroblasts could migrate into the remaining bowel and re-establish a fibrotic IF. Indeed, recent attempts to resect stricture with associated CF hold promise, and the addition of CF-targeted therapeutics inhibiting YAP/TAZ signaling locally could provide another avenue to lower stricture recurrence.13,25,48

In conclusion, our work demonstrates that CF-derived, mechanosensitive fibroblasts are important contributors to stricture progression. These findings raise important questions about surgical management of intestinal strictures and raise the possibility that future anti-fibrotic therapies will need to address both bowel and CF-derived fibroblasts to achieve stricture remission.

Despite our comprehensive characterization of CF-derived CTHRC1+ fibroblasts in stricture progression, we acknowledge that there are limitations to this study. Our scRNA-seq, Visium, and Xenium studies had low patient numbers, limiting the scope of our findings. Most scRNA-seq studies in our CD atlas were performed on biopsies, which do not sample submucosal layers of the bowel wall where fibrosis predominates, forcing us to limit disease state comparisons to full-thickness samples. Furthermore, low probe number, gene selection limitations to prevent optical crowding, and cell segmentation inaccuracies reduced our cellular resolution, ECM gene localization, and increased unmapped cells, respectively. Although our mouse model showed that CF is a source of CTHRC1+ fibroblasts, the lack of a chronic inflammatory background makes it challenging to determine when they emerge during inflammation and fibrosis. Future model iterations could consider utilizing colitis-inducing agents or genetic models of IBD to determine the spatiotemporal dynamics of these cells. In addition, we did not address the role of the microbiome, which is known to play a key role in CF initiation and fibroblast activation, and this area can be the focus of future studies. The inability to repeatedly label MAT fibroblasts hampered the ability to capture mechanosensitive fibroblasts that became active after LiTMX administration. Finally, our study focused on CTHRC1+ fibroblasts and YAP/TAZ signaling but did not address the role that other cells like SMCs and mechanotransduction pathways such as Piezo signaling play in stricture progression. Future studies could utilize our model with different gene drivers to lineage trace and target MAT-derived populations and assess their contribution to intestinal fibrosis.

A total of 14 human datasets were manually screened to identify datasets that met the study inclusion criteria: (1) datasets must contain at least 3 scRNA-seq samples from full resections or biopsies; (2) the scRNA-seq platform must be droplet-based (e.g., 10x Genomics, Drop-Seq, etc.); and (3) the scRNA-seq sample must include stromal cells.

Identified scRNA-seq datasets were downloaded from their public repositories and analyzed individually for quality control using the Seurat single-cell analysis pipeline (v4.9.9.9050)43 in R (v4.2.2). To ensure comparability between the human datasets, ENSEMBL transcript IDs and gene synonyms were converted to human gene symbols using biomaRt (v2.56), and only genes found in the human reference genome GRCh38.p14 were retained. Afterwards, the raw counts matrices for each dataset were confirmed to contain only integer values. Each properly formatted scRNA-seq sample was then annotated with the relevant sample-level metadata. The orig.ident, study name, hashing status, digest method, age, sex, scRNA-seq platform, treatment, tissue type, disease state, patient ID, and specimen type were recorded. These metadata were consistently annotated across all human samples. For each properly formatted Seurat object, low quality cells with nFeature_RNA ≤ 200 or ≥20% mitochondrial reads were then filtered out. To remove potential doublets, cells with nFeature_RNA ≥95th percentile were excluded because the expected multiplet rate when loading 10,000 cells using the 10x Genomics single-cell platform is 5%.92

Before integration, the counts data for 1,810,086 human cells from 804 individual human scRNA-seq samples from 15 total human datasets were merged. After merging, the resulting object was re-deployed using bit-packing compression with BPCells to minimize disk read/write requirements. The layers in the object were flattened using the JoinLayers function. The human datasets were separately integrated following the same steps: Using Seurat v4.9.9.9050, merged data were split by dataset (study), the raw RNA counts were log-normalized, and the top 2,000 variable features (highly variable genes [HVGs]) were identified. To improve the computational efficiency of analyzing datasets spanning millions of cells, we selected a representative sketch of 1,000 cells per dataset based on the leverage scores calculated for each cell43. Using the sketched datasets, variable features were identified, expression levels were scaled, and PCA was performed (npcs = 30). Next, integration of the sketched data was performed using Harmony. The batch key for this integration was “study”, and no covariate regression was performed. After each integration approach, UMAP dimensionality reduction was performed (dims = 1:30, min.dist = 0.001), neighbors were found (dims = 1:30), and initial clusters were identified (resolution varied based on dataset). The integration results and clusters from the sketched dataset were then projected onto the full dataset. All steps were performed using Seurat v5 functions (NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, SketchData, ScaleData, RunPCA, IntegrateLayers, RunUMAP, FindNeighbours, FindClusters) with default parameters, except where mentioned. Distinct cell types in the Harmony atlas were identified and grouped into broad categories using canonical marker genes plotted with the Seurat v5 function FeaturePlot in conjunction with EnrichR. Heat maps were generated using z-score normalized average expression of top 100 differentially expressed genes in clusters of interesting and plotted using the R package ComplexHeatmaps.79 Harmony was selected as the approach for all subsequent downstream integration based on its performance in biological conservation and batch metrics using scIB.50

To generate the human CD fibroblast atlas, cells identified to be fibroblasts were extracted in silico for further analysis. Using Seurat v4.9.9.9050 and BPCells,44 these putative fibroblasts were split by the key “orig.ident”, the raw RNA counts were log-normalized, the top 2,000 variable features (highly variable genes [HVGs]) were identified, expression levels were scaled, and PCA (npcs = 30) was performed. Next, integration of the data was performed using Harmony, UMAP dimensionality reduction was performed (dims = 1:30, min.dist = 0.001), neighbors were found (dims = 1:30), and clusters were identified. Distinct cell types were annotated using the top 100 marker genes for each cluster and contaminating non-fibroblast clusters were removed. Fibroblasts with similar marker genes were then pooled together. This computational pipeline was iterated four total times to generate the final human pan-fibroblast atlas. Each subpopulation in the final human fibroblast atlas was annotated in a semi-supervised fashion using canonical fibroblast markers (PDGFRA, DPT, LUM) as well as those previously reported in CD fibrosis scRNA-seq studies.

CD disease states were stratified for bowel and MAT as follows: First, non-CD tissue, which included histologically normal or ostomy tissue, was distinguished from CD tissue. Second, CD patients were subdivided according to the Montreal Classification of CD Severity (B1 for no stricture present, B2 for stricture present, and B3 for penetrating disease). Third, stricture samples were subdivided based on whether the sample was taken distal to the stricture or from the stricture itself. And fourth, all samples were subdivided based on whether histological inflammation was detected. This stratification yielded the final twelve disease states (Table S1). The top differentially expressed genes in CTHRC1+ fibroblasts were used to perform Gene Ontology analysis using Single Cell Pipeline with GO Biological Pathways.

MAT, bowel, and in-house fibroblast atlases were generated by iteratively subclustering the cells of interest and removing low quality and contaminating cells using the Seurat standard pipeline until achieving relatively pure populations. For fibroblast subclustering, associated non-fibroblast stromal cells not observed in the global fibroblast clusters were included in the fibroblast subclustering.

A comparison dataset was generated to characterize fibroblast proportions and phenotype unless otherwise specified. Biopsy samples were excluded, as these lacked the full representation of fibroblasts (Figure S1D). Subsequently, samples obtained from ostomy takedowns were also excluded, as these were mechanically active at baseline (data not shown), and from the colon, where CD strictures tend not to occur. Thus the final comparison dataset consisted of normal, uninvolved, and involved bowel and MAT samples from full-thickness resections.

Mechanosensitive fibroblasts were identified by constructing gene signatures and using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function to calculate gene signature score (Table S2). YAP Target Genes: Top genes from four studies of YAP target genes in fibroblasts and cancer were consolidated generate an initial list of 195 genes. To prevent lowly expressed genes from introducing noise, genes expressed in less than 10% of cells in each fibroblast cluster were removed, resulting in a final list of 111 YAP target genes.93–96 YAP Pathway Genes: Genes that encode for major components of the YAP/TAZ/Hippo pathway, as well as associated transcription factors and integrins were selected to obtain a final list of 19 genes.97 These two signatures were combined to form the Mechanical signature. ECM Signature: 31 genes were selected from three broad categories of known pro-fibrotic ECM genes: Collagens, basement membranes, and core matrisome.98 For antigen-presentation and inflammatory signatures, GO:0019882 (GOBP Antigen_Processing_and_Presentation) and M5932 (GSEA, “Hallmark_Inflammatory_Response) were used. For migration, GO:0010761 (Fibroblast_Migration) in combination with other known fibroblast migration genes were used.99,100 Finally, the pathogenic fibroblast signature was constructed using previously described markers from scRNA-seq datasets in CD.8,39,40 All module scores were calculated using fibroblasts from the comparison dataset.

For analysis of colotomy and sham mouse bowel and MAT scRNA-seq datasets, a similar workflow was followed as the human meta-analysis. Briefly, each sample was annotated with the relevant sample-level meta-data. The orig.ident, hashing status, tissue type, and condition were recorded. Cells with nCount_RNA > 200, cells with nFeature_RNA ≤95th percentile, and percent mitochondrial genes < 10%, were filtered. Global cells were integrated using Harmony and annotated using canonical markers. Fibroblasts were integrated using SCTransform and CCA alignment and iteratively subclustered three times as previously described. Annotation of subclusters was based on previously described mouse fibroblast and human immune population markers. For fibroblast phenotyping, we used mouse-specific YAP target genes to generate the YAP Target Genes signature (Table S2).101 YAP Pathway genes, ECM genes, migration, antigen-presentation, and inflammation signatures were converted to mouse genes from the human gene signatures.

To accommodate the size constraints of the Visium chip, a 1cm-thick wedge from an intestinal stricture was harvested and divided into two sections: 1) mucosa through the muscularis propria (Inner Bowel Wall), and 2) the muscularis propria (MP) and surrounding fat (outer bowel). A 6.5 by 6.5mm sample was placed in a cryoblock (TissueTek) with pre-chilled OCT and processed according to the Visual Spatial Protocol-Tissue Preparation Guide by 10x Genomics. Briefly, tissue samples were flash frozen in OCT using a Thermo-Flask benchtop container filled with liquid nitrogen and a small, round, metal bowel with 1-inch height worth of 2-methylbutane. A pair of needle-drivers was used to maneuver the 2-methylbutane bowl in and out of the Thermo-Flask. The OCT block was placed in the 2-methylbutane bowl and lowered into the liquid nitrogen until the tissue was completely frozen. The tissue was then placed on dry ice and transferred to a −80°C freezer. Tissues were cryosectioned at −20°C onto gene expression slides. The Gene Expression Slide & Reagent kit was followed per the 10x Genomics protocol and used to produce sequencing libraries. The libraries were sequenced using NextSeq (Illumina) and Bcl files were demultiplexed. Raw FASTQ files and histology images were processed by sample with the Space Ranger software, which uses STAR v2.5.1b for genome alignment against the Cell Ranger mm10 reference genome, available at http://cf.10xgenomics.com/supp/cell-exp.92
Domain assignment: To assign the different domains, the MP was used as a landmark because it was the most distinguishable feature in both intestinal strictures and adjacent, uninvolved bowel. Tissue from the MP through the mucosa to the lumen was designated as BW, stroma between MAT and the MP as IF, and associated MAT as MAT or CF. Uninvolved, adjacent bowel did not have any appreciable stroma between the bowel wall and MAT, and so this category was omitted in these samples. Domains were assigned to the Outer and Inner Bowel section spots using the 10X Loupe Browser. Processing and integration: Quality control was performed by removing spots with less than 200 features and greater than 20% mitochondrial reads. After merging the objects, samples were integrated using the following Seurat v5 pipeline: SCTransform, RunPCA, IntegrateLayers with Harmony by sample, RunUMAP (dims = 1:30), FindNeighbors, and FindClusters using the lowest resolution that differentiated the spatial domains. Domain assignment and similarity comparison: The top 100 differentially expressed genes in spots and domains were calculated using the FindAllMarkers function. To measure spot-domain similarity, the cosine similarity scores between the respective groups was calculated to generate a heatmap using ComplexHeatmap.

A custom-designed 471 gene (Table S3) panel was generated using global gene markers of global cell populations previously described for all cell types identified in our CD cell atlas, as well as approximately five fibroblast subpopulation-specific marker genes identified by differential gene expression per subpopulation using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function. Additionally, genes in upregulated CellChat and YAP/TAZ mechanotransduction pathways were also included. To avoid optical crowding and maximize cell type identification, highly expressed ECM genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, etc.) were omitted. The custom probes were hybridized to the Xenium slides following the user guide for “Xenium In Situ Gene Expression” (CG000582, Rev A, 10x Genomics). Sample preparation: Approximately 10mm by 10mm samples of bowel and associated MAT from five strictures along with three paired adjacent, uninvolved sections were placed in a cryoblock (TissueTek) with pre-chilled OCT and processed as previously described (Sample Preparation for Visium). Samples were sectioned using a cryostat at 10-μm thickness and placed on the Xenium slide according to the manufacturer’s instructions (CG000579, Rev A, 10x Genomics). Samples were subsequently fixed, permeabilized (CG000581, Rev A, 10x Genomics), and decoded using the Xenium decoding reagents (1000487, 1000461, 10x Genomics) on a Xenium Analyzer instrument (10x Genomics). Domain Assignment: Xenium data was imported into the Xenium Explorer software (10x Genomics). Domains were annotated based on the MP as described previously (see Quantitative analysis of extracellular matrix ultrastructure in human and mouse) and exported.

The scRNA-seq reference was constructed by selecting cell populations from our initial global populations as well as our fibroblast, lymphoid, and myeloid subclustering. To label adipocytes, a snRNA-seq atlas of adipocytes44 was merged into the global atlas to generate a reference dataset, downsampled to 1000 cells per cell type, and Seurat’s NormalizeData and ScaleData functions were run. For deconvolution, NiCo 1.1.0 pipeline was implemented as recommended by filtering and normalizing the reference dataset and Xenium ST datasets, finding commonly shared genes, performing SCTransform from Scanpy (Alternative 1), and Leiden clustering the Xenium data at a resolution of 0.7. Then find_anchor_cells_between_ref_and_query and nico_based_annotation were run to find anchors between the reference and Xenium ST datasets with a spatial dispersion cutoff of 0.15. Mappings were subsequently visualized using the visualize_umap_and_cell_coordinates_with_selected_celltypes function. Neighborhood Analysis: Using a radius of 30um, spatial_neighborhood_analysis was run on all cell types to generate the expected neighborhood composition matrix, which contained the regression coefficients that quantify a given cell type’s ability to predict the presence of a target cell. Thus, cells that are more likely to predict a target cell will have higher regression coefficients and vice versa. Neighborhood plots were generated using plot_niche_interactions_with_edge_weight and the normalized regression coefficients were extracted. Network plots for CTHRC1+ fibroblasts were generated by creating directed network graphs for each domain, setting the edge weights to be the normalized regression coefficients, and excluding weights less than 0.05 using the networkx Python package. To identify significant interactions with CTHRC1+ fibroblasts, the maximum normalized regression coefficient between CTHRC1+ fibroblasts and all other cell types were selected.

The raw number of representative YAP target transcripts (CCN1, SERPINE1) in deconvoluted CTHRC1+ fibroblast were quantified in the IF and two CF subdivisions in pediatric stricture samples: 1) superficial CF, which contained CF up to 300μm below the IF, and 2) deep CF, which consisted of all CF below superficial CF.

CellChat was performed with default parameters using secreted signaling, ECM–receptor, and cell–cell contact human CellChatDb databases on the global CD atlas with the fibroblast subpopulation labels. The atlas was downsampled to 1000 cells maximum per cell population. Individual circle plots of ligand-receptor pairs were generated using netVisual_chord_gene. Differential interactions between stricture and uninvolved (fibroblasts combined from normal and uninvolved) were calculated using netVisual_diffInteraction. Heatmaps of differentially upregulated ligand-receptor pairs in strictures were generated using netAnalysis_signalingRole_heatmap.

Anchor label transfer (ALT) was used to determine the similarities between cell populations in a query dataset and reference dataset. Datasets to be compared were assigned as the reference and query datasets, respectively. For mouse-human comparisons, mouse genes were converted to human genes using the convert_mouse_to_human_symbols function from nichenetR. Then Seurat’s pipeline for anchor label transfer was run to align biological features across datasets: FindTransferAnchors (dims = 1:30) and MapQuery using standard parameters. Scaled ALT scores were then visualized with Seurat’s DotPlot function.

SCENIC was used to calculate the AUC (area under the recovery curve) for the enrichment of regulons in human re-clustered MAT fibroblasts (Table S5). Seurat objects were generated with AUC scores and clustered by running the standard Seurat pipeline. Differentially expressed regulons were identified by running the FindAllMarkers function. The network graph of transcription factors and their target genes was generated by extracting representative predicted target genes in transcription factor regulons using SCope.82

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v.10 or RStudio. Unless otherwise noted, all values are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. To determine whether the data were parametric or non-parametric, the normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and verified by inspection of Q–Q plots. Statistical significance between two experimental groups was determined using an unpaired Student’s t-test (two-tailed) for parametric data or a Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data. For comparisons involving three or more groups, a one- or two-way ANOVA was performed followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Fibroblast proportions were quantified by dividing the total number of fibroblasts in a subpopulation by the total number of fibroblasts in each analysis or per patient from the comparison dataset and a Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to statistically significant differences. To determine if treatment effects on CTHRC1+ fibroblast proportion, the proportion of CTHRC1+ fibroblasts was calculated per sample of uninvolved and involved bowel and MAT, since some patients contributed both bowel and MAT samples. Adjusted means (least-squares means) were obtained from the ANCOVA model using the aov and emmeans packages in R studio with treatment as a covariant and compared to unadjusted means computed from the raw data. Results were visualized as point estimates with 95% confidence intervals. For all statistical tests, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance in figures and supplemental figures is indicated as follows: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****). The value of n refers to the number of biological samples used in the study and at least three independent experiments were performed unless otherwise specified. Histological analyses were performed blinded to experimental condition and independently of the surgeon for mouse experiments. ImageJ software was used for all image quantification.
