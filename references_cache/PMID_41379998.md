# TAPT1 interacts with SUCO to maintain the homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins and brain development in mice.
**Authors:** Gao J, Yang F, Jiang Y, Zheng Y, Cai L, Huang X, Yuan L, Wang Y, Wang Y, Xu Z
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2501361122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2501361122)

## Content

Genetic mutations in Tapt1 cause complex skeletal dysplasia and structural brain abnormalities. Although the pathogenesis underlying skeletal dysplasia has been explored, the functions and potential mechanisms of transmembrane anterior-posterior transition 1 (TAPT1) during brain development have not been reported. Here, we show that the brains of Tapt1 conditional knockout mice exhibit severe neurodevelopmental defects, including impaired proliferation and differentiation of neural progenitor cells and defects in dendritic and synaptic development, leading to severe microcephaly, motor dysfunction, and early death. Mechanically, we reveal that TAPT1 interacts with SUCO in the endoplasmic reticulum to maintain newly synthesized proteins, including those important for brain development. The TAPT1-SUCO complex plays an essential role in the homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins, and its loss causes overactivated protein degradation, as well as impaired endoplasmic reticulum-to-Golgi trafficking and organelle structures. Our results thus provide insights into the pathogenesis of TAPT1 and SUCO mutation-associated diseases that share similar pathologies.

The proliferation and differentiation of neural progenitor cells (NPCs) are pivotal to the development of the cerebral cortex, ensuring the generation of an appropriate number of neurons and glial cells (1–4). Following neurogenesis, neurons undergo significant cellular and molecular transformations, culminating in the growth and patterning of axons and dendrites (5, 6). The development of dendrites is crucial for establishing neuronal connections (7). Dendrites contain various proteins that receive, process, and transmit neural signals, impacting the processing of synaptic information within neural circuits (7). Any disturbance in developmental processes can cause structural and functional abnormalities of the cerebral cortex and brain disorders (8, 9).

During and after neurogenesis, there is an augmented demand for protein synthesis to express proteins required for neuron development, particularly those related to the generation of axons, dendrites, and synapses (10–12). The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) serves as a central hub for protein homeostasis, influencing various cellular processes essential for neural development (13–15). The ER provides an optimized environment for protein folding and maturation (13). Newly synthesized proteins enter the ER during translation, where they are properly folded and modified to achieve their native conformation (13). To ensure high fidelity of protein synthesis and maturation in the ER, cells have evolved the ER protein quality control system, which facilitates the rapid degradation of abnormally folded proteins (16). Only correctly folded proteins are allowed to leave the ER and reach their final destination (16).

The unfolded protein response (UPR) and endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD) are two key quality control mechanisms (17, 18). The UPR is activated by the accumulation of misfolded proteins (17), and the ERAD is responsible for clearing misfolded proteins from the ER (18). During ERAD, misfolded proteins are ubiquitinated by enzymes and then translocated from the ER to the cytoplasm, where polyubiquitinated ERAD substrates are recognized and degraded by proteasomes (18). The defects in ERAD are potentially associated with several neurological disorders such as epilepsy and cerebellar ataxia (19, 20).

Molecular chaperones play a crucial role in substrate recognition for ERAD (21). These chaperones include BiP, calnexin, calreticulin, glucose-regulated protein 94, and ERp57 (21–23). Even minor deviations from the native conformation can lead to protein binding by these factors, resulting in its retention in the ER (16). These molecular chaperones also participate in the protein folding process, raising a pertinent question: If the folding intermediates use the same molecular chaperones as ERAD, how to prevent the unfolded proteins from being treated as misfolded proteins by ERAD and subsequently degraded?

Transmembrane anterior–posterior transition 1 (TAPT1) is named because Tapt1 mutant mice exhibit a posterior-to-anterior transformation of the midsegment of the spine (24). Genetic mutations in TAPT1 cause complex lethal skeletal dysplasia, characterized by severe hypomineralization of the entire skeleton and intrauterine fractures (25). The morphology of ER, Golgi apparatus, and the protein trafficking from plasma membrane to Golgi apparatus are also disturbed in fibroblasts isolated from patients (25). MRI of the brain implicates the white matter deficits with pachygyria in patients with TAPT1 mutations, which may represent a form of leukodystrophy (26). However, the function of TAPT1 during brain development and its molecular mechanism to regulate ER function remain unclear.

In this study, we established a mouse model carrying brain-specific knockout of Tapt1. These mice exhibited severe neurodevelopmental defects in both NPCs and postmitotic neurons, coming to a premature end. The normal function of TAPT1 is crucial for the neurogenesis and the development of both dendrites and spines. We further revealed that TAPT1 interacted with SUCO in the ER to maintain the homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins. Deficiency of TAPT1 could result in abnormal ER-to-Golgi trafficking and overactivation of ERAD signaling pathways.

We also observed that deletion of Tapt1 caused a thinner cerebral cortex, which may result in the smaller brain size shown above (Fig. 1E). However, immunostaining of the neural marker NeuN revealed no significant differences in neuron density between Tapt1 cKO (hereafter referred to as homozygotes) and WT mice (Fig. 1E). Meanwhile, the activation of astrocytes in the cerebral cortex of cKO mice was revealed by immunostaining and WB of GFAP (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). These results suggested that Tapt1 knockout significantly reduced the number of neurons and probably induced sustained impairment in the neural system.

The balance between proliferation and differentiation of NPCs determines the number of neurons produced during brain development (27). To inspect the proliferation of NPCs, we injected the thymidine deoxynucleotide analog EdU intraperitoneally for 2 h at embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5) to track cells undergoing DNA synthesis (28). Compared to WT, the density of EdU-positive cells, as well as the density of Ki67-positive proliferating cells, was significantly reduced in cKO mice (Fig. 1G). We also isolated the NPCs from E15.5 brains and cultured neurospheres in vitro (Fig. 1F). The sizes of neurospheres formed from cKO mice were significantly smaller, indicating a declined proliferation without Tapt1.

We next investigated whether the differentiation of NPCs was also affected. EdU labeling was prolonged to 24 h and the cell cycle exit index was inferred by the percentage of Ki67−/EdU+ cells among EdU+ cells. Compared to WT brains, the index was increased, suggesting the disturbed differentiation of NPCs in Tapt1 cKO brains (Fig. 1H). The above findings demonstrate that Tapt1 is required for the normal development of NPCs, including both the proliferation and differentiation.

The maturation of synapse is vital for the function of neural circuits (29). Since the period of synapse maturation overlapped with the death of our Tapt1 cKO mice, we inspected whether the synapses were also affected. Thy1-GFPm-labeled neurons from Tapt1 cKO mice were cultured, and a significant decrease in spine density was observed (Fig. 2A). Among four different subtypes of dendritic spines, the stubby form is the prominent kind during postnatal development (30). Detailed classification revealed a significant reduction in the number and percentage of stubby spines in Tapt1 cKO neurons (Fig. 2B), indicating that synaptic maturation was impaired.

The synapse development is highly coordinated with the elaboration of dendritic arbors (31). To assess whether dendritic complexity was affected, cultured neurons were stained with the dendrite marker MAP2. Imaris software was used to reconstruct the dendrites, and Sholl analysis was applied to analyze the dendritic complexity. Both total length and number of dendritic branches were significantly reduced in the cKO neurons, suggesting a simpler dendritic morphology (Fig. 2C). The same perturbation of dendritic morphology was observed in vivo according to the analyses of Thy1-GFPm-labeled neurons in cKO mice (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that Tapt1 knockout also impairs the dendritic development.

Taken together, the above results demonstrate that Tapt1 is crucial for neuronal development, with Tapt1 knockout leading to defects in synapse maturation and dendrite complexity.

To investigate how Tapt1 knockout affected the development of the cortex, we performed quantitative proteomic analyses with cortical samples collected from WT and cKO mice. We focused on two different timepoints, P20 and E14, which represented the relative mature cortex and the cortex undergoing active neurogenesis (Fig. 3 A–C and Dataset S1). Consistent with the impaired synaptic and dendritic development shown above, the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis of Biological Process (GSEA-BP) suggested the decreased proteins in the Tapt1 cKO brains were likely to participate in the dendrite development and the synapse organizations (Fig. 3 D and E and Dataset S2). Many synaptic proteins such as SYT2 (32), SHANK1 (33), and SH3RF1 (34) were significantly reduced when Tapt1 was ablated (Fig. 3F). The increase of GFAP levels in P20 proteomic data is consistent with the evidence for astrocyte activation described above (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B).

We then analyze the proteomic data from E14 to explore the function of Tapt1 during neurogenesis. The levels of many proteins involved in stem cell maintenance and cell cycle were altered, which were correlated with the observed disruption in NPC development (Fig. 3G and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). The decreased protein levels of several important neuronal differentiation regulators, such as Ptbp1 and Yap1, were further validated (Fig. 3G). We hypothesized that the most dramatically changed proteins at the early stage of the knockout might be more relevant to the cellular response to Tapt1 ablation, implicating the molecular functions of TAPT1. As revealed by Gene Ontology enrichment analysis, significantly reduced proteins tended to be involved in protein synthesis and transport, while increased proteins were associated with peptidase activities (Fig. 3H and Dataset S3). These results suggest the knockout of Tapt1 might disturb the protein homeostasis and probably ER functions regarding protein synthesis and degradation.

TAPT1 has been reported to be enriched in the Mito/ER/Golgi fraction of human fibroblasts (26). To confirm the subcellular localization of TAPT1, immunostaining of TAPT1 and markers of different organelles was performed. TAPT1 was more likely to colocalize with the ER membrane protein calnexin and the translocation protein SEC61B than with the ER luminal protein calreticulin, PDI, and BIP (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). It seemed that TAPT1 did not colocalize with ER–Golgi intermediate compartment marker ERGIC53, endosome marker Rab5a and Rab7c, Golgi apparatus marker GM130, lysosome marker LAMP1, mitochondrial marker TOMM20, or plasma membrane protein Na/K ATPase (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–D). These results indicate that TAPT1 is mainly localized in the ER and probably adjacent to translocon complex.

Since more disperse and distended Golgi apparatus was observed in Tapt1-deficient neurons as previously reported in patients (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E) (25), we wondered whether ER–Golgi trafficking was also affected. The forward ER–Golgi transport was inspected by tracking the temperature-sensitive VSVG-GFP (35). VSVG-GFP would undergo ER–Golgi transport when the temperature decreased from 40 °C to 32 °C, and its transport was significantly impaired indicated by reduced colocalization with GM130 in Tapt1 cKO neurons after 30 min incubation (Fig. 4B). Further investigation of the impaired ER–Golgi trafficking of Cst3 was applied in TAPT1-KO HEK293 cells by retention using selective hooks (RUSH) (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). Delayed ER–Golgi transport of Cst3 was revealed by RUSH (Fig. 4C). These results indicate that Tapt1 knockout leads to defective forward vesicle trafficking from the ER to the Golgi apparatus.

Our proteomic analysis suggested that protein synthesis might be affected in the Tapt1 cKO brains, and we further examined this speculation by employing the methionine analog HPG to label the newly synthesized proteins (Fig. 4D). A dramatic reduction of newly synthesized proteins was observed in cultured Tapt1-deficient neurons (Fig. 4D). Since calnexin is an important molecular chaperone involved in the maturation of nascent proteins (36), the reduced expression of calnexin in Tapt1 cKO cells also indicates the impairment of the homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F).

Taken together, we demonstrate that Tapt1 knockout would result in structural and functional deficiency of the ER, including impaired homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins and ER–Golgi transport.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms of how the ablation of TAPT1 causes ER impairment, we carefully checked the significantly changed proteins detected in our proteomic data. Among them, we noticed the significantly decreased protein SUCO (SUN domain-containing ossification factor) (Fig. 5A). Mutations in SUCO have been linked to skeletal dysplasia and epilepsy (37), and Suco knockout mice displayed phenotypes similar to those of Tapt1 KO mice, including impaired bone formation and spontaneous fractures (38). Suco knockdown also impaired dendritic growth and development (37). In addition, SUCO, as well as translocation protein SEC62 and SEC63, were found to be potential TAPT1-interacted proteins via proximity label-MS data from BioGRID or IntAct (39, 40) (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2H). Therefore, we hypothesized that TAPT1 might interact with SUCO and thus participate in protein synthesis or maturation. Their colocalization was first confirmed by coexpression (Fig. 5C), and as expected, the coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay also revealed that SUCO interacted with TAPT1 (Fig. 5D).

To decipher the detailed interaction domains between TAPT1 and SUCO (SI Appendix, Fig. S2I), we examined their interactions between different truncated variants through Co-IP. The results revealed that the deletion of the SUN domain in SUCO abolished its interactions with TAPT1 (Fig. 5D). Meanwhile, the deletion of the N-terminal domain in TAPT1 abolished its interactions with SUCO. Therefore, the N-terminal domain of TAPT1 and the SUN domain of SUCO are important for their interaction with each other (Fig. 5D).

We also inspected the related changes in the protein levels of TAPT1 and SUCO in cells. When equal amounts of TAPT1 and varying doses of SUCO were co-overexpressed in HEK293 cells, the levels of TAPT1 varied with SUCO and vice versa (Fig. 5E). It seems that the interaction between SUCO and TAPT1 could stabilize each other, which may explain the decreased protein level of SUCO in our proteomic data of Tapt1 cKO brains (Fig. 5A).

Since neither 3D structure of TAPT1 nor SUCO has been validated, we attempted to investigate the topology of TAPT1 on the ER membrane by utilizing the selective permeability of digitonin compared to Triton X-100. The luminal domain of a membrane protein could only be detected after treatment with Triton X-100 but not after treatment with digitonin (41, 42). As revealed in the immunofluorescent results, the C-terminal signal of TAPT1 but not the N-terminal signal was detected after treatment with digitonin, while both of them were detected after Triton X-100 treatment (Fig. 5F). These results suggest the N-terminal of TAPT1 and probably the interaction between TAPT1 and SUCO is likely to be in the luminal side of the ER, where protein folding occurs (Fig. 5G). Additional Co-IP experiments revealed the interactions between TAPT1 and SEC61B or calreticulin (Fig. 5H), further suggesting its association with protein synthesis and maturation.

According to the interaction results, we postulated that TAPT1 and SUCO shared the similar function in the homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins. HEK293 cell line with SUCO knockout was generated, and it was found that the newly synthesized proteins labeled by HPG were also markedly reduced in SUCO knockout cells as that in TAPT1 knockout cells (Fig. 6 A–D).

To further investigate whether the interaction between TAPT1 and SUCO is essential for the homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins, we overexpressed different deletion or truncated variants in knockout cell lines and performed HPG assays. Consistent with the interaction domains we found, only WT TAPT1 and TAPT1 with the N-terminal domain (TAPT1ΔC) could partially rescue the deficiency in TAPT1 knockout cells (Fig. 6 A and D). Meanwhile, WT SUCO, SUCOΔTM, and even the SUN domain solely could partially rescue the deficiency in SUCO knockout cells (Fig. 6 B–D). Therefore, the TAPT1–SUCO interaction is essential for the homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins.

During translation, some nascent proteins would be translocated into the ER lumen for proper folding. When the accumulation of misfolded protein exceeds the ER’s quality control capacity, ER stress is induced, and misfolded protein would undergo degradation (17). Considering the essential role of TAPT1–SUCO in the homeostasis of protein synthesis, we hypothesized that the loss of TAPT1–SUCO may trigger stress responses, leading to excessive protein degradation and resulting in the decrease of HPG signals. When the accumulation of misfolded protein exceeds the ER’s quality control capacity, ER stress is induced, and the unfolded protein response (UPR) would be activated (43). We found that several UPR signaling pathways were likely to be activated in TAPT1 knockout HEK293 cells, cultured neurons, and cortical samples of Tapt1 cKO mice (Fig. 7A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). We further demonstrated the activation of UPR in TAPT1 knockout HEK293 cells, as evidenced by the increased level of Xbp1s, increased ratio of P-elF2α and ATF6p50 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Stress pathways relevant to proteostasis imbalance like heat shock response were also found to be activated in the cortex of cKO mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and C). All of these results indicated that knockout of TAPT1 would result in stress responses. Excessed misfolded proteins would be degraded by ER-associated degradation (ERAD) or lysosomal degradation (18). Therefore, we examined whether the ERAD pathways were activated when Tapt1 was ablated. WB results revealed a significant increase of many ERAD-related proteins including the critical SEL1L-HRD1 complex (44), ER-resident lectins OS9 (45), and key sensor of ER stress BiP (46) (also known as GRP78) in both TAPT1 knockout HEK293 cells, cultured Tapt1-KO neurons, and cKO cortex (Fig. 7A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). All of these indicate that there is an increase in the amount of protein being degraded. We speculated that during protein synthesis, nascent proteins might be liable to the failure of protein folding without the facilitation of TAPT1–SUCO complex, resulting in the activation of ERAD and finally reduction of newly synthesized proteins as detected in the HPG test.

While TAPT1 deficiency leads to a reduction of newly synthesized proteins, it may also cause indiscriminate degradation of proteins: impairing the ERAD function of recognizing and degrading misfolded proteins, thus leading to incorrect degradation of normally folded/mature proteins. To test this postulation, we performed cycloheximide (CHX) chase experiments to observe the fate of mature proteins. We inspected both PTBP1 and YAP in cultured NPCs and neuronal morphology-related proteins Tau and MAP2 in primary cultured neurons (47). We found that the stability of PTBP1, YAP, Tau, and MAP2 was similar between WT and Tapt1 KO cells, indicating that the mature proteins may not be degraded by mistake (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D).

To test whether overactivation of ERAD disturbed proteostasis in TAPT1 or SUCO knockout cells, we treated cells with ERAD inhibitor Eeyarestatin I (EerI) (48) followed by HPG assays (Fig. 7B). As expected, EerI treatment led to increased HPG signals in WT HEK293 cells, indicating the inhibition of ERAD pathways under normal conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). When normalized to WT cells, EerI treatment significantly restored HPG signals in both TAPT1 or SUCO knockout cells, indicating the partial rescue of the imbalanced proteostasis caused by TAPT1 or SUCO knockout. To further investigate whether the inhibition of ERAD could rescue the defects of NPCs, we treated cultured NPCs with EerI. After 10 h of treatment, it partially rescued the cell proliferation defects of NPCs from Tapt1-cKO mice (Fig. 7C). These findings indicate that ERAD overactivation contributes to the defects caused by Tapt1 knockout.

In this study, we showed that mice with Tapt1 cKO in the brain display severe neuronal developmental defects, culminating in a significant reduction in brain size and early death. We unveiled that TAPT1 interacts with SUCO, and loss of TAPT1 or SUCO would cause dramatic decrease of synthesized proteins. Overactivation of stress responses and ERAD pathways was observed when TAPT1 was knocked out, resulting in imbalanced proteostasis. Such impairment could be partially rescued by the ERAD inhibitor EerI. We hypothesized that TAPT1–SUCO safeguarded newly synthesized proteins from being erroneously identified as misfolded proteins and subsequently degraded by ERAD, thereby maintaining normal protein maturation and ER-to-Golgi trafficking during brain development (Fig. 7D).

Previous studies of TAPT1 mainly focused on the cartilage and skeletal dysplasia, although patients with brain abnormalities harboring TAPT1 mutations were also reported (26, 49). The expression of many proteins involved in neurogenesis, dendrite development, and synaptic organization was significantly reduced in Tapt1 cKO brains. Neural differentiation regulators such as Ptbp1 and Yap1 were also decreased in the E14 cortex. The phenotypes of decreased proliferation and an increased cell cycle exit index were similar to Yap1 knockout, likely due to exhaustion of stemness and premature switch of neurogenesis. In addition, many proteins involved in protein synthesis and transport were also significantly reduced. Further investigation revealed defects in forward vesicle trafficking from the ER to the Golgi apparatus and homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins in Tapt1 KO neurons.

We further elucidated the molecular mechanism by which Tapt1 knockout results in defects in ER structure and function. SUCO levels were significantly reduced in Tapt1 cKO brain, and interestingly, the phenotypes of patients or knockout mice with TAPT1 and SUCO mutations were very similar (37, 38). Most patients with Tapt1 mutations do not survive to adulthood, with some dying during pregnancy and others in adolescence (25, 26). Suco knockout in mice primarily leads to neonatal lethality (37), and it was also observed in our Tapt1 cKO mice. This led us to verify the interaction between TAPT1 and SUCO in the ER. The TAPT1–SUCO interaction stabilizes each other and is essential for the homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins. Both TAPT1 and SUCO are ubiquitously expressed in various organs (24), suggesting a housekeeping function of the TAPT1–SUCO complex.

We came across a report of the Slp1–Emp65 complex in yeast (23), which are homologues of mammalian SUCO (or OPT) and TAPT1, respectively. The Slp1–Emp65 complex binds unfolded proteins and protects them from promiscuous degradation during folding (23). The study of Slp1–Emp65 complex in yeast elucidates a mechanism regarding ER protein folding quality control (ERQC) which may also fit for mammalian TAPT1–SUCO complex, presiding over cellular homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins. We proposed a model in which the TAPT1–SUCO complex protects newly synthesized proteins in the ER from being mistakenly recognized as misfolded proteins and degraded by ERAD (Fig. 7D). When the TAPT1–SUCO complex is dysfunctional, newly synthesized proteins fail to fold correctly, leading to excessive activation of UPR and ERAD (Fig. 7C). Specifically, the autophosphorylation of PERK was not detected, suggesting the phosphorylation of eIF2α might be carried out by other kinases. This mechanism applies to numerous proteins including those involved in various functions and signaling pathways, which may explain the phenotypic diversity observed in different systems upon Tapt1 or Suco knockout. For example, consistent with previous studies that TAPT1 regulates SMAD2/3 and BMP signaling (50), we found that SMAD proteins were also significantly reduced in Tapt1 cKO brains (Dataset S1). That may explain the simultaneous occurrence of skeletal and neurological syndromes when TAPT1 does not function properly. The ER dysfunction in TAPT1 or SUCO knockout cells is most likely due to the defects of ER protein folding quality control which lead to overactivation of UPR and ERAD. Since the estimation of ERAD activation by quantifying half-life of exogenous ERAD substrates may be compromised by the activation of UPR and heat-shock response, we applied an ERAD inhibitor to prove the activation of ERAD in TAPT1-KO cells. Inhibition of ERAD pathways by EerI can partially rescue imbalanced proteostasis and the cell proliferation defects in NPCs.

In addition to those decreased proteins, we also found the elevation of several ER proteins, such as Arl6ip1, Mt3, and Ssr4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). Translocon-associated protein (TRAP) subunit delta Ssr4 was increased in both E14 and P20 proteomic data. Ssr4 directly interacts with Sec61, ribosome, and nascent proteins to regulate the maturation of nascent polypeptides. This elevation may indicate a compensatory response to the ER protein folding defect caused by Tapt1 knockout. All of these results indicate that TAPT1 and SUCO are both essential for the proteostasis. The abnormal ER-to-Golgi vesicle trafficking and organelle structures in Tapt1 knockout cells are likely to be the secondary effect of disturbed homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins. Considering that TAPT1 may interact with the SEC complex, whether TAPT1 directly participates in the translocation of nascent proteins into the ER remains to be studied.

In summary, our findings provide insights into the pathogenesis of diseases associated with Tapt1 or Suco mutations, which lead to disrupted homeostasis of newly synthesized proteins in the ER, resulting in abnormal ER function and structure, and subsequently abnormal brain development.

The sample was lysed in a buffer containing 4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6. After being incubated at 100 °C for 10 min, the lysates were centrifuged to remove insoluble debris (12,000 × g, 10 min). The protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL). Protein digestion was performed using the filter-aided proteome preparation (FASP) method (54). Briefly, protein was reduced by 100 mM DTT, and the buffer was exchanged by UA (8 M urea and 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) using the Microcon YM-30 centrifugal filter units (EMD Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA). After being alkylated by 55 mM iodoacetamide (IAA, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO), a buffer containing 0.1 M triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO) was used to replace the denaturing buffer of the sample. Proteins were then digested with trypsin (1:50, w:w) at 37 °C overnight, and the resultant tryptic peptides were labeled with acetonitrile-dissolved TMT reagents (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL), and equal amounts of labeled samples were mixed together before prefractionation with reversed phase (RP)-high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

The peptides were fractionated on an ACQUITY UPLC Peptide BEH C18 Column, 2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.7 μm 130 Å (Waters, PN:186003556) using a Waters ACQUITY Arc UHPLC system. All samples were dried with a Speed-Vac concentrator and desalted by StageTip (55) before use. The LC-MS/MS/MS analysis was performed using an Orbitrap Fusion™ Lumos™ Tribrid™ mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, Waltham, MA) coupled online to an Easy-nLC 1000 in the data-dependent mode. Detailed parameters could be found in SI Appendix, Extended Methods.
