Labyrinthitis ossificans (LO) is a rare pathological ossification of the 
membranous labyrinth, typically arising after inflammatory or infectious insults 
to the inner ear. We present a case of a 65-year-old male with type 2 diabetes 
presenting with progressive unilateral sensorineural hearing loss and a history 
of recurrent childhood ear infections treated with repeated lavages. Imaging 
revealed advanced ossification of the right cochlea, vestibule, and semicircular 
canals, despite no history of meningitis or trauma. Magnetic resonance imaging 
(MRI) revealed near-complete loss of the normal fluid signal in the affected 
labyrinth, while high-resolution CT confirmed extensive bony obliteration with a 
small residual segment of the basal turn spared. The absence of vestibular 
symptoms despite semicircular canal ossification highlights the variable 
clinical presentation of LO. This case illustrates how advanced LO can closely 
mimic congenital inner ear malformations on imaging, making accurate diagnosis 
particularly challenging. Differential diagnoses such as cochlear otosclerosis, 
temporal bone fractures, and autoimmune inner ear disease must be carefully 
excluded based on clinical and radiological features. Treatment options depend 
on the ossification stage; while cochlear implantation remains feasible in early 
or partial ossification, advanced cases may require alternative rehabilitation 
strategies. Early diagnosis is crucial for optimizing hearing outcomes. This 
report emphasizes the importance of combining clinical history, audiometry, and 
multimodal imaging in the diagnosis and management of LO.

Labyrinthitis ossificans (LO) is a pathological process characterized by abnormal bone formation within the membranous labyrinth of the inner ear, often following infection, inflammation, or trauma [1]. This condition can lead to progressive sensorineural hearing loss and poses significant challenges for audiological rehabilitation, particularly cochlear implantation. Early recognition and accurate imaging assessment are vital for timely intervention [2]. This report presents a case of advanced unilateral LO in an adult patient with a history of childhood otitis media and diabetes, highlighting its atypical presentation and diagnostic complexities.

A 65-year-old male with a history of type 2 diabetes presented with progressive right-sided hearing loss that had developed insidiously over several months. The patient reported multiple episodes of ear discharge during childhood, which were managed with repeated ear lavages. There was no history of head trauma, meningitis, or recent ear infections. Clinically, the patient denied vertigo and exhibited no signs of facial nerve palsy. Otoscopic examination was unremarkable, and the left ear was normal both clinically and audiologically.

Audiometric testing revealed a severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right side, with preserved hearing on the left. Given the unilateral and progressive nature of the hearing loss, imaging was requested to explore possible inner ear pathology.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with an internal auditory canal protocol demonstrated a significant loss of normal high T2 fluid signal in the right inner ear. The cochlea exhibited markedly decreased fluid signal compared to the contralateral side, with near-complete loss of fluid signal intensity. The vestibule also showed reduced signal intensity, indicative of fibrous or ossified changes. The semicircular canals on the right were hypointense compared to the normal left side, suggesting advanced labyrinthine involvement. The combination of unilateral progressive sensorineural hearing loss, the absence of vestibular symptoms or facial nerve palsy, and imaging findings of advanced ossification of the membranous labyrinth led to the diagnosis of LO.

Given the advanced stage of ossification, which limits the possibility of medical reversal, the patient was referred to the otolaryngology department for further evaluation. Management was planned to mainly focus on audiological rehabilitation.

LO refers to abnormal bone formation within the membranous labyrinth, most commonly following previous inflammation, infection, or trauma affecting the inner ear. It represents the end-stage of labyrinthitis, wherein inflammatory processes induce fibrous tissue formation followed by osseous transformation of the labyrinthine spaces. This condition has significant clinical and radiological implications [1]. In our case, LO presented atypically, closely mimicking congenital inner ear malformations due to its advanced stage, thus posing a diagnostic challenge.

The incidence of LO is relatively low in the general population but may be underreported due to its often asymptomatic nature in early stages or in patients with bilateral profound deafness where inner ear ossification may not be pursued radiologically. However, it becomes highly relevant in the evaluation of patients with progressive or sudden hearing loss. Post-meningitic cases are more widely recognized due to their rapid and extensive ossification [3], but chronic otitis media–related LO can also present, albeit more insidiously and unilaterally, as in the present case [2].

Labyrinthitis can be broadly classified into serous (toxic) and suppurative (bacterial) forms. Serous labyrinthitis is a reversible inflammatory response to nearby infection or trauma, whereas suppurative labyrinthitis involves direct bacterial invasion of the membranous labyrinth, leading to irreversible damage and predisposing to subsequent ossification.

LO can also be classified etiologically into meningitic, tympanogenic, and traumatic types. Meningitic LO typically progresses rapidly and diffusely, often showing early bilateral involvement on MRI and extensive ossification on CT. Tympanogenic LO tends to be more localized and unilateral, commonly affecting the basal turn and vestibule, while traumatic LO may present as focal ossification associated with fracture lines or post-surgical changes. Recognizing these patterns can help narrow the differential and guide prognosis.

These etiological patterns also correlate with the major risk factors for LO, which include prior bacterial meningitis, chronic or recurrent otitis media, temporal bone fractures, autoimmune inner ear disease, and previous inner ear surgery [4]. In our patient, there was no history of meningitis or trauma, but his history of recurrent childhood otorrhea and repeated ear lavages suggest a likely infectious etiology. Chronic suppurative otitis media, especially when inadequately treated or complicated by labyrinthine fistula or bony erosion, can cause translocation of pathogens or inflammatory mediators into the inner ear structures. Repeated mechanical ear lavage may have inadvertently facilitated such translocation. Moreover, the patient's type 2 diabetes mellitus is known to impair local immune responses, hinder tissue repair, and promote chronic inflammation, potentially accelerating fibrotic and ossific changes within the inner ear.

Pathologically, labyrinthitis progresses through 3 stages: an acute inflammatory stage (with hyperemia, leukocyte infiltration, and increased labyrinthine fluid protein content), a fibrous stage (characterized by fibroblast proliferation and replacement of the membranous labyrinth with fibrous tissue), and finally an ossification stage (with new bone formation within the labyrinthine spaces).

Radiologically, the acute stage may appear normal or show subtle enhancement on post-contrast MRI; the fibrous stage shows loss of T2 hyperintensity on MRI without CT changes; and the ossification stage shows dense hypointensity on MRI with corresponding hyperdense bony replacement on CT [5]. MRI, particularly high-resolution T2-weighted sequences, plays a key role in the early detection of fibrotic transformation and ossification, manifesting as loss of the normal hyperintense T2 signal within the cochlea, vestibule, or semicircular canals. In our patient, MRI showed near-complete loss of T2 signal intensity throughout the right cochlea, with marked hypointensity also involving the vestibule and semicircular canals. These changes reflect the presence of either dense fibrosis or ossification but do not allow clear differentiation between the two [6].

CT, on the other hand, remains the gold standard for confirming ossification. High-resolution temporal bone CT allows for detailed visualization of fine ossific encroachment within the labyrinth. In our case, CT demonstrated near-complete ossification of the semicircular canals and cochlea, with only a small residual basal turn visible. Ossified septations in the vestibule were noted too. The absence of mastoid sclerosis was notable, despite the patient’s history of recurrent otitis media, indicating that middle ear disease can resolve radiologically while inner ear damage persists or progresses.

Audiometrically, LO typically results in profound or severe sensorineural hearing loss, often with a rapid onset in post-infectious cases such as meningitis [3], or a more gradual decline in cases linked to chronic middle ear disease [1]. In our patient, audiometry confirmed severe unilateral sensorineural hearing loss on the right, which was progressive.

One of the critical diagnostic pitfalls illustrated by this case is the potential confusion between advanced LO and congenital inner ear malformations. On imaging, cochlear aplasia or common cavity malformations may appear similar to ossified cochleae, particularly when ossification is extensive and obliterates the internal architecture [7]. However, key distinguishing features exist. In congenital malformations, the bony contours of the otic capsule are often abnormal from the outset, with absent or hypoplastic cochlear turns, dysmorphic vestibules, or lack of proper segmentation. In contrast, LO typically maintains the external contour of the cochlea and vestibule but shows internal obliteration by abnormal tissue or bone [1]. Additionally, a history of prior hearing and the delayed onset of symptoms strongly favor an acquired etiology.

The differential diagnosis in such scenarios must also include cochlear otosclerosis (which predominantly affects the otic capsule rather than the membranous labyrinth) [8], temporal bone fractures, autoimmune inner ear disease, and inflammatory processes such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis [9]. These can often be excluded based on clinical context and imaging patterns.

Treatment options for LO depend on the stage of ossification and the degree of cochlear patency. Medical therapy has limited benefit once ossification has occurred. Audiological rehabilitation remains the cornerstone of management. In early stages or partial ossification, cochlear implantation may still be feasible. The presence of a unossified basal turn in some cases can allow successful insertion of an electrode array [10]. However, advanced ossification may necessitate drill-out procedures or even preclude cochlear implantation, necessitating alternative amplification strategies such as bone-anchored hearing aids [2].

The prognosis is variable and closely linked to the timing of diagnosis and intervention. Early identification of fibrotic changes before ossification offers a therapeutic window [2]. Subtle loss of fluid signal on high-resolution T2 MRI or patchy post-contrast enhancement in the membranous labyrinth in patients with sudden or progressive sensorineural hearing loss—especially with a history of meningitis, chronic otitis media, or trauma—should raise suspicion for early LO. Detecting the fibrous stage offers a potential window to prevent progression to permanent ossification and irreversible hearing loss.

In chronic cases such as ours, where ossification is advanced, the focus shifts to optimizing quality of life through assistive hearing technologies and counseling.

In summary, LO is a rare but important cause of acquired sensorineural hearing loss that can simulate congenital anomalies on imaging, especially in advanced stages. Accurate diagnosis requires a combination of clinical suspicion, detailed MRI and CT evaluation, and correlation with audiologic findings. Recognition of characteristic radiologic features and understanding the etiologic context are essential in avoiding misdiagnosis and guiding appropriate management.
