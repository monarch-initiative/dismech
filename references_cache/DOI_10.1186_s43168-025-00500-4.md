# A lobe lost and a vein misplaced: an adult’s tale of scimitar syndrome
**Authors:** Anil Kumar Singh, Huda Shamim, Rahul Sharma, Rajat Kumar Mishra
**Journal:** The Egyptian Journal of Bronchology (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s43168-025-00500-4](https://doi.org/10.1186/s43168-025-00500-4)

## Content

Abstract

Background
Scimitar syndrome is a rare congenital cardiopulmonary anomaly characterized by partial anomalous pulmonary venous return (PAPVR), most often involving drainage of the right pulmonary veins into the inferior vena cava (IVC) instead of the left atrium. It is commonly associated with right lung hypoplasia, dextroposition of the heart, and other cardiovascular or bronchopulmonary abnormalities. While usually diagnosed in infancy or childhood, adult presentations are uncommon and often manifest with vague or non-specific respiratory symptoms.


Case presentation
We report the case of a 60-year-old female who presented with intermittent dyspnea and was found to have right upper lobe agenesis, middle lobe aplasia, and anomalous right pulmonary venous drainage to the IVC. The diagnosis of Scimitar syndrome was confirmed based on imaging studies. This case is unique in highlighting the diagnostic complexity and subtlety of adult presentations, in which anatomical abnormalities may be overlooked or misattributed to other causes of dyspnea.


Conclusion
Scimitar syndrome, though typically diagnosed early in life, should be considered in adults presenting with unexplained respiratory symptoms and radiological anomalies. Awareness of this rare condition and its variable presentations is crucial for timely diagnosis and management. We also present a concise review of the available literature outlining diagnostic approaches and therapeutic considerations in adult cases of Scimitar syndrome.

Case presentation
A 60-year-old female presented to our pulmonary outpa -
tient clinic with complaints of breathlessness occurring 
intermittently over the past year. She reported exertional 
dyspnea, with no associated chest pain, palpitations, 
syncope, cough, or hemoptysis. There was no history 
of orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fever, or 
recent respiratory infections. Her past medical history 
stated intake of antitubercular therapy for 6 months, 2 
years back, based on radiological abnormality on chest 
radiograph, and she was a lifelong non-smoker. On physi-
cal examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable 
with an oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. Respira -
tory examination revealed decreased breath sounds and 
mild dullness to percussion over the right lower lung 
field. Cardiac examination was normal, with no mur -
murs or added sounds. Routine laboratory investiga -
tions, including complete blood count and metabolic 
panel, were within normal limits. Chest radiograph 
showed decreased right lung volume with mediastinal 
shift toward the right and a curvilinear opacity along 
the right cardiac border (Turkish sword)-scimitar sign, 
raising suspicion for Scimitar syndrome (Fig. 1). High-
resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the tho -
rax demonstrated agenesis of the right upper lobe and 
aplasia of the middle lobe, with compensatory hyperin -
flation of the right lower lobe and the left lung (Fig. 2). 
Additionally, CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) and 
3D reconstructive images showed delineated hypoplasia 
of the right pulmonary artery and systemic arterial sup -
ply to the right lower lobe from the abdominal aorta, 
suggesting the presence of a partial anomalous pulmo -
nary venous return (PAPVR) from the right lung drain -
ing into the inferior vena cava, consistent with scimitar 
syndrome(Figs. 3 and 4). A subsequent bronchoscopy 
revealed no identifiable orifice at the usual location of the 
right upper lobe bronchus, bronchus intermedius con -
tinues distally, giving rise to only lower lobe bronchi and 
its segments concluding absence of the middle lobe and 
its segments (Figs. 5 and 6). Echocardiography revealed 
mild right atrial and right ventricular enlargement, with 
no significant pulmonary hypertension or associated 
congenital cardiac defects. Right heart catheterization 
was planned, but patient did not consent. Pulmonary 
function tests showed mild restrictive ventilatory defect. 
The patient was diagnosed with adult-type scimitar syn -
drome with a relatively benign course. Given the absence 
of significant pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, or 
recurrent infections, a conservative approach with regu -
lar follow-up was advised. The patient was counselled 
regarding symptom monitoring, and periodic imaging 
and echocardiography were planned to assess for any 
progression in subsequent follow-ups.
Discussion
Scimitar syndrome remains an uncommon but well-
documented entity in the literature with diverse pre -
sentations that depend on the extent of pulmonary and 
cardiovascular anomalies. The syndrome is primarily 
defined by the presence of PAPVR, with the right pulmo -
nary venous return connecting abnormally to the IVC, 
resulting in a left-to-right shunt. This shunt is respon -
sible for variable hemodynamic consequences, including 
right heart volume overload and pulmonary hyperten -
sion, particularly when the shunt is large [ 1–4]. Several 
studies have provided insight into the pathophysiology

detection depends heavily on the quality of the image and 
the clinical suspicion. Advanced imaging modalities such 
as CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provide 
detailed anatomical and functional information. Masrani 
et al. [ 7] have noted that CT and MRI are essential not 
only for confirming the anomalous venous drainage but 
also for evaluating associated lung parenchymal abnor -
malities and the degree of lung hypoplasia. In our case, 
CT and CTPA were invaluable for characterizing the 
extent of lobar agenesis and for confirming the course of 
the anomalous vein.
Echocardiography plays a complementary role by 
assessing cardiac chamber sizes and function, and iden -
tifying any secondary intracardiac anomalies. Fur -
thermore, cardiac catheterization may be employed 
to measure shunt fractions and pulmonary pressures 
when non-invasive studies provide inconclusive results 
[4]. Najm et al. [ 4] demonstrated in a 20-year study that 
detailed hemodynamic evaluation via catheterization 
was critical in guiding therapeutic decisions, especially 
in symptomatic patients with significant left-to-right 
shunts. The therapeutic approach depends on the sever -
ity of symptoms and the degree of the shunt. In patients 
with minimal symptoms, conservative management with 
regular follow-up may be sufficient. Conversely, symp -
tomatic cases with significant pulmonary over-circulation 
or pulmonary hypertension may necessitate surgical 
intervention. Surgical techniques vary from re-routing 
the anomalous pulmonary venous drainage to the left 
atrium, to segmental lung resection or even lobectomy 
in cases where the affected lung is non-functional [ 5, 8]. 
Seymour E and Wang H et al. [ 8, 9] in a recent multi-
centre study reported favourable outcomes in pediatric 
and adult patients undergoing surgical correction, not -
ing a reduction in right heart strain and improvement in 
functional status. Nonetheless, the decision for surgery 
must weigh potential benefits against the inherent risks 
of cardiothoracic procedures, especially in older adults. 
Another point of discussion is the relationship between 
Scimitar syndrome and pulmonary infections. Several 
reports have observed that structural abnormalities in 
pulmonary architecture predispose patients to recurrent 
infections [ 2, 6]. In our patient, a history of anti-tuber -
cular treatment prior to the final diagnosis suggests that 
misinterpretation of radiographic findings may delay 
the identification of congenital anomalies. Kalpana et al. 
[6] highlighted that in regions with a high prevalence of 
pulmonary tuberculosis, atypical radiographic features 
should prompt further evaluation to exclude congenital 
anomalies such as Scimitar syndrome (Fig. 7).

Conclusion
Scimitar syndrome, although rare in adults, should be 
considered in the differential diagnosis when encounter -
ing unexplained respiratory symptoms and characteristic 
imaging findings. Comprehensive evaluation using chest 
radiography, CT/CTPA, and echocardiography is essen -
tial for proper diagnosis. The decision between conserva -
tive management and surgical intervention relies on the 
extent of the left-to-right shunt, pulmonary hyperten -
sion, and overall symptom burden. A systematic review 
of the literature suggests that timely identification and 
customized treatment strategies can yield favourable out-
comes, even in older patients.
