# Expanding the Clinical Spectrum of RERE-Related Disorders: A Case Report of Neurodevelopmental Disorder with Brain Malformations Including Chiari Type I.
**Authors:** Pachajoa H, Gutierrez-Obando AM, Leal AF
**Journal:** Mol Syndromol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000550194](https://doi.org/10.1159/000550194)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Neurodevelopmental disorder with or without anomalies of the brain, eye, or heart (NEDBEH) is a rare genetic condition caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the RERE gene, which encodes a transcriptional co-repressor essential for embryonic development. Most reported cases result from de novo variants and show a broad spectrum of neurodevelopmental and structural abnormalities. This report expands the phenotypic spectrum of NEDBEH by describing the first association with a Chiari type I malformation.

CASE PRESENTATION: We report a 26-year-old Colombian male presenting with global developmental delay, progressive spasticity, mild dysmorphic features and a Chiari type I malformation, a finding not previously linked to RERE variants. Whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous de novo missense variant in RERE (NM_001042681.3:c.815A>G; p.Tyr272Cys), initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance but supported as likely pathogenic according to ACMG criteria (PP3, PM2). The RERE gene functions as a retinoic acid-dependent transcriptional cofactor, a pathway critical for hindbrain segmentation and morphogenesis, providing a plausible mechanism for the cerebellar anomaly observed.

CONCLUSION: This case broadens the clinical spectrum of RERE-related NEDBEH, suggesting potential cerebellar involvement secondary to disrupted retinoic acid signaling. It emphasizes the importance of genomic testing for patients with neurodevelopmental delay and structural brain malformations, enabling accurate diagnosis, genetic counseling, and deeper understanding of rare developmental disorders.

RERE-related neurodevelopmental disorder (NEDBEH) is caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants inRERE, a gene essential for embryonic development.

Most reported cases are due to de novo variants and present with developmental delay, hypotonia, intellectual disability, and variable structural anomalies of the brain, eye, or heart.

The retinoic acid-dependent function of RERE is critical for early brain patterning, particularly in forebrain and midbrain development.

This report describes the first association of a RERE-related disorder with Chiari type I malformation, expanding the known phenotype of NEDBEH.

The identified de novo missense variant p.Tyr272Cys supports its likely pathogenicity according to ACMG criteria.

The findings suggest that RERE dysfunction may also affect hindbrain segmentation, providing new insights into the developmental mechanisms underlying cerebellar anomalies in NEDBEH.

Neurodevelopmental disorders with or without anomalies of the brain, eye, or heart (NEDBEH; MIM: 616975), also known asRERE-related disorders, are caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in thearginine-glutamic acid dipeptide repeats(RERE) gene. This gene encodes a protein that functions as a transcriptional co-repressor during embryonic development and is located on chromosome 1p36.23. This condition follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, but most pathogenic variants reported to date occur de novo [1,2].

In 2016, it was reported that heterozygous mutations in theREREgene were responsible for a group of patients with NEDBEH. Previously,REREdisorders were considered part of the 1p36 deletion syndrome spectrum, given the gene’s location on chromosome 1p36.23. However, subsequent studies have established NEDBEH as a distinct clinical and molecular entity specifically associated with pathogenicREREvariants [3,4].

The prevalence of NEDBEH remains unknown. Currently, approximately 23 clinically characterized cases have been described in the medical literature. However, research registries such as Simons Searchlight have documented more than 60 individuals withREREvariants as of 2024 [1,5]. This difference reflects the limited number of published case descriptions and the increasing identification of individuals through research registries.

NEDBEH is characterized by a neurodevelopmental disorder with or without structural anomalies of the brain, eyes, heart, and genitourinary (GU) tract, as well as sensorineural hearing loss. These conditions show marked clinical heterogeneity, and many of their features overlap with those observed in the 1p36 deletion syndrome. It has been proposed thatREREhaploinsufficiency contributes to several of the phenotypes observed in this syndrome, suggesting that pathogenic variants inREREalone may account for many of the clinical manifestations observed [4,6].

Here, we present the case of a patient with a neurodevelopmental disorder, progressive spasticity, and Chiari type I malformation, who carries a de novo variant in theREREgene. This report contributes to the phenotypic characterization and expansion of the clinical spectrum associated withRERE-related disorders, highlighting the variability of their neurological and structural manifestations.

We present the case of a 26-year-old Colombian male patient of mixed ancestry. He is currently unemployed and lives with his family. The patient was born to non-consanguineous parents, and the pregnancy was well controlled, with normal prenatal ultrasounds. He was born by cesarean section at term and developed respiratory distress and neonatal jaundice requiring hospitalization for 5 days. The mother had a history of a previous miscarriage due to cervical incompetence. Family history was unremarkable for neurological or genetic disorders.

From early childhood, he exhibited global developmental delay, with initial hypotonia followed by progressive spasticity. Speech was limited, and independent walking was achieved late. At 6 months of age, a muscular ventricular septal defect with mild hemodynamic repercussion was diagnosed, which closed spontaneously during adolescence. During childhood, he presented moderate cognitive impairment (total intellectual quotient, IQ: 39) without major behavioral disorders. He required special educational support, as well as speech and occupational therapy. He also had strabismus, surgically corrected twice.

At 24 years of age, brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed descent of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum by approximately 2 cm, compatible with Arnold-Chiari malformation type I (Fig. 1). One year later, he underwent occipital decompressive craniotomy, with postoperative magnetic resonance imaging showing residual descent of 16 mm, without signs of intracranial hypertension or bulbar-medullary abnormalities. The ventricular system was normal. In the same year an echocardiogram revealed structurally normal mitral and tricuspid valves, trivial functional insufficiency, no shunts, and no evidence of pulmonary hypertension.

Currently there has been a progression of weakness and spasticity in the lower limbs, with increased frequency of falls and he was diagnosed with mild mixed dystonic-spastic paralysis and language development disorder. At the moment, he receives physical, occupational, and speech therapy, with mild improvement in gait.

Physical examination revealed mild dysmorphic features: triangular face, low anterior hairline, prominent nasal bridge (shown inFig. 2), and high-arched palate. Additional findings included cubitus valgus, camptodactyly, and clinodactyly of the left fifth finger, and nail hypoplasia of the fifth toes bilaterally. Muscle strength was 4+/5 in both upper and lower limbs, with generalized hyperreflexia and increased tone in the lower extremities.

Complementary diagnostic tests included a normal abdominal ultrasound, audiometry, and speech audiometry. Trio-based whole-exome sequencing was performed using Illumina paired-end technology with GenSKits reagents (IVD-CE), achieving a mean coverage of 84.6×. Bioinformatic analysis was conducted using the GeneSystems (IVD-CE) pipeline, which includes variant calling, mitochondrial genome analysis, and exome-based CNV detection. No pathogenic, likely pathogenic, or uncertain copy-number variants were identified, including a targeted review of the 1p36 region.

Whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous de novo missense variant of uncertain significance in theREREgene: (NM_001042681) c.815A>G (p.Tyr272Cys). This gene is associated with NEDBEH. During the preparation of this manuscript, the variant was reclassified as likely pathogenic based on updated ACMG criteria, supported by PP3 (strong) and PM2 (moderate) evidence levels [7]. The variant is located in a conserved region of the protein, and multiple in silico tools predicted a deleterious effect (SIFT, Provean, CADD, REVEL, FATHMM, MutationAssessor). No additional pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in genes associated with posterior fossa development or Chiari type I malformation, and all other variants detected in the proband were either unrelated to the phenotype or involved genes associated with recessive conditions.

Initially, several differential diagnoses were considered, including Kabuki syndrome, CHARGE syndrome, and 1p36 deletion syndrome, due to overlapping neurodevelopmental and craniofacial features. However, these conditions were ruled out based on the absence of their defining features and normal results from targeted analyses. The identification of theREREvariant through trio-based sequencing redirected the diagnostic hypothesis toward NEDBEH as the patient’s phenotype – including developmental delay and brain anomalies – was consistent with previously describedRERE-related disorders.

The patient is currently followed by medical genetics, neurology, psychiatry, otorhinolaryngology, and gastroenterology. He receives comprehensive rehabilitation therapy and botulinum toxin injections approximately every 6 months for spasticity control with no adverse or unanticipated events observed. At his most recent genetics evaluation, he showed partial improvement in gait and good adherence to follow-up. The clinical and genetic findings support a correlation between the observed phenotype and the spectrum of neurodevelopmental disorders associated withREREvariants. The overall prognosis is stable, with no evidence of disease progression, and early multidisciplinary management is expected to optimize functional outcomes and quality of life. Adherence to rehabilitation sessions and follow-up appointments was monitored through clinical records and caregiver reports.

NEDBEH is a genetic syndrome caused by pathogenic variants in theREREgene. This condition has autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, but most pathogenic variants reported to date occur de novo [1]. In the case presented here, a de novoREREvariant was consistently identified and found to be absent in both parents.

Given that phenotypic characterization is essential for assessing concordance with RERE-related disorders, a comparative table was constructed to contrast the clinical manifestations previously reported with those observed in the present case (Table 2). Many of the clinical features of NEDBEH overlap with those observed in 1p36 deletion syndrome, supporting evidence thatREREhaploinsufficiency alone may account for many of the manifestations previously attributed to that condition [3,6,9]. Although patients with NEDBEH do not exhibit a consistent dysmorphic pattern, a broad range of physical features have been described, including frontal prominence, low-set and posteriorly rotated ears, epicanthal folds, anteverted nares, and broad eyebrows. In addition, some cases have reported micrognathia and downslanting palpebral fissures [3,4].

In the patient, subtle but recognizable facial features were noted, consistent with those previously described in individuals withRERE-related disorders [3]. These included a broad forehead, prominent nasal bridge, and short nose with a bulbous tip (Fig. 1). No major craniofacial anomalies or prominent dysmorphisms were identified, which is consistent with the variable facial gestalt observed among affected individuals.

This syndrome is characterized by marked clinical heterogeneity and primarily involves neurodevelopmental impairment, with or without structural abnormalities of the brain, eyes, heart, kidneys, and GU tract, as well as sensorineural hearing loss. The most frequently reported manifestations include global developmental delay, intellectual disability from mild to profound, and autism, hypotonia, seizures, and behavioral problems [3,10,11]. The patient described in this report shares several of these features, particularly global neurodevelopmental delay with initial hypotonia evolving into progressive spasticity, consistent with the neurological findings described in most reported cases carrying pathogenicREREvariants.

Structural brain abnormalities associated with NEDBEH include agenesis and/or thinning of the corpus callosum, reduced white matter volume, abnormal cerebellar vermis, and ventriculomegaly [2–4]. The present case documented a Chiari type I malformation. To our knowledge, Chiari type I malformation has not been previously reported in patients with pathogenicREREvariants, suggesting that this finding may represent an expansion of the phenotypic spectrum of NEDBEH.

The p.Tyr272Cys variant identified in our patient is located within the N-terminal repressor region ofRERE, a domain known to mediate interactions with transcriptional regulators and nuclear receptor complexes involved in retinoic acid (RA)-dependent signaling. This region is required for the recruitment of corepressor machinery that modulates RA-responsive gene expression during hindbrain development. Disruption of this pathway in murine Rere-deficient models leads to abnormalities in rhombencephalic patterning, delayed cerebellar foliation, reduced proliferation of granule cell precursors, and impaired maturation of Purkinje cells [12].

Although foramen magnum compression has not been documented in animal models, functional evidence suggests that cerebellar dysgenesis and posterior fossa hypoplasia could lead to a secondary downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils, explaining the coexistence of a Chiari type I malformation in patients withREREmutations, as observed in the case. This finding supports a potential association between altered RA-dependentREREfunction and the development of posterior fossa abnormalities, further expanding the known clinical spectrum of NEDBEH.

Additionally, pathogenic variants inREREare thought to cause disease through disruption of its role as a coregulator of RA-dependent transcription, a pathway essential for hindbrain patterning, cerebellar development, and neuronal maturation [12]. Reported pathogenic variants include frameshift mutations (the most frequent), followed by missense and nonsense variants, all of which support a mechanism consistent with partial loss of function or impaired RA-mediated signaling. The missense variant identified in our patient (c.815A>G; p.Tyr272Cys) is not currently listed in ClinVar, and no previously reported cases with this specific change were found. However, its location within a functional domain and the presence of similar pathogenic missense variants inREREsupport its biological plausibility as a disease-causing alteration in the context of NEDBEH.

Reported congenital heart defects include ventricular and atrial septal defects, as well as, less frequently, patent foramen ovale, patent ductus arteriosus, and truncus arteriosus [13]. In agreement with previous observations, the patient presented with a ventricular septal defect, reinforcing the association betweenREREvariants and structural cardiac anomalies. Ophthalmologic findings described in NEDBEH include coloboma, myopia, anisometropia, astigmatism, exotropia, esotropia, ptosis, microphthalmia, and optic nerve hypoplasia [4,14]. In this context, the strabismus observed in our patient is consistent with the spectrum of ocular manifestations previously reported.

GU anomalies described in the literature include vesicoureteral reflux, hypospadias, cryptorchidism, and, in a few cases, enuresis [2]. Isolated cases have also reported scoliosis, congenital hip dysplasia, cleft lip, and cleft palate [15]. The patient did not present any of these anomalies, which align with the described phenotypic variability and demonstrate that predominantly neurological phenotypes can occur in the absence of major malformations. Altogether, these findings reinforce the multisystemic involvement of this gene and highlight the importance of comprehensive evaluation and follow-up in patients with NEDBEH.

This case emphasizes the importance of consideringRERE-related disorders in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with neurodevelopmental delay and progressive spasticity. Moreover, it underscores the value of genetic testing as a diagnostic tool to confirm the underlying etiology, guide clinical management, and provide appropriate genetic counseling for affected families. The identification of a Chiari type I malformation in this patient contributes to expanding the known clinical spectrum ofRERE-related disorders and adds further evidence of the phenotypic variability and central nervous system involvement associated with these variants. Additionally, this case highlights that dysmorphic features alone are insufficient for diagnosis; rather,RERE-related disorders should be considered within the context of a broader spectrum of findings, particularly those involving abnormalities of the brain, eyes, and heart. A limitation of this report is the absence of functional studies to confirm variant pathogenicity; however, the phenotypic correlation and de novo status provide strong supportive evidence.
