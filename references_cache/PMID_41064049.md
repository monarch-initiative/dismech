Introduction

Hypoparathyroidism, sensorineural deafness, and renal dysplasia (HDR) syndrome, formerly known as Barakat syndrome, is caused by haploinsufficient GATA3 variants. GATA3 acts as an enhancer-binding protein through its zinc finger domain, with haploinsufficiency, leading to localized degeneration of the cochlea [1], abnormal differentiation and development of parathyroid progenitor cells [2], and deficits in nephric duct cell differentiation [3]. The diagnosis can be confirmed molecularly or clinically in patients with 2 or more impacted symptoms and a positive family history [4].

Clinical diagnoses can be complicated by variable expressivity, with a review of 180 cases finding hearing loss present in 96.7%, hypoparathyroidism in 93.3%, and renal dysfunction in 72.2% of individuals, though only 64.4% of patients presented with all three features [4]. 40% of renal disease in HDR patients is due to congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract [5], while remaining cases demonstrate variably progressive disease potentially including nephrotic syndrome, benign hematuria, renal tubular acidosis, or nephrocalcinosis. Overall prognosis is primarily dependent upon the severity of renal disease.

Current treatment prioritizes symptomatic management. Hypocalcemia due to hypoparathyroidism is treated acutely with intravenous calcium gluconate and controlled long-term with oral calcium and calcitriol. Sensorineural deafness can be treated with hearing amplification and cochlear implantation [4]. Routine monitoring and standard treatments for chronic kidney disease are chiefly important for improving prognosis, with kidney disease generally progressing over time at highly variable rates. Four patients with end-stage renal disease due to molecularly confirmed HDR syndrome have had successful renal transplants; however, there are minimal long-term data regarding the efficacy of renal transplants in these patients. Here we present a 74-year-old male with genetically confirmed HDR syndrome and include long-term data on the outcomes of this patient having undergone a living-related donor renal transplant 27 years prior.

Case Presentation

This 74-year-old was born at term from an uneventful pregnancy. His hearing loss was first noted in early childhood, and he needed to be at the front of the class to hear his teacher starting in early elementary school. His hearing loss was previously attributed to recurrent middle ear infections, excess cerumen, and tympanic membrane perforations, though he was found to have bilateral sensorineural hearing loss as an adult. He required bilateral hearing aids in his 50s. His audiogram at age 66 demonstrated moderate and severe sensorineural hearing loss on the right, and severe and profound mixed hearing loss on the left. He had excessively foamy urine as a child, potentially suggestive of early-onset proteinuria, though he was not formally evaluated and found to have proteinuria until age 30. This evaluation additionally revealed an atrophic right kidney and ∼25% remaining left renal function. He maintained sufficient renal function for roughly 12 years, though worsening recurrent renal stones caused acute on chronic renal damage, leading to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) requiring dialysis by age 46. He received a living related donor transplant at age 47, which was well tolerated. He was initially on prednisone, cyclosporine, and mycophenolate, though was able to discontinue prednisone after 5 years without complication. His transplant was well tolerated for the next 26 years, which he attributes in part to at least 60 min of workouts per day to maintain his health.

At age 73, he was found to have declining renal function with workup revealing acute renal failure with acute tubular necrosis, advanced global glomerulosclerosis, moderate arteriosclerosis, and severe interstitial fibrosis/chronic tubular interstitial changes. He was restarted on dialysis. He has never been diagnosed with hypoparathyroidism or hypocalcemia, though he notes difficulties maintaining his calcium levels and severe paresthesias during dialysis. He is currently listed for a repeat kidney transplant. A renal disease gene panel confirmed that he shared his familial pathogenic GATA3 (NM_001002295.2) c.608_609del, p.Gly203Glufs*100 variant, which results in a frameshift variant in exon 3 of 6, leading to haploinsufficiency due to presumed nonsense-mediated decay.

His family history is notable for multiple relatives with hematuria, hearing loss, and kidney failure with a widely variable age of onset (Fig. 1). Individuals II-2 and III-1 had congenital anomalies of the kidneys and urinary tract and passed away within 1 year. Individual II-3 passed away from end stage renal disease in late adulthood of unclear cause. Individual III-3 was diagnosed with mild hearing loss at age 2 and has had hematuria associated with recurrent urinary tract infections since she was a teenager. She maintains functional hearing without hearing aids and has had creatinine, urinalysis, and calcium levels within normal limits in the past year. Her children, individuals IV-1 and IV-2, were both found to have congenital hearing loss, and individual IV-1 additionally had recurrent urinary tract infections that partially resolved following a ureteral implantation. IV-1 and IV-2 have otherwise showed no clinical evidence of renal disease or hypoparathyroidism. Individuals III-3, IV-1, and IV-2 were confirmed to share the familial pathogenic GATA3 (NM_001002295.2) c.608_609del, p.Gly203Glufs*100 variant.

Discussion

The reported family presented to clinic with a presumptive diagnosis of Alport syndrome. However, the congenital hearing loss reported in IV-1 and IV-2 suggested an alternative diagnosis, as “hearing loss in Alport syndrome is never congenital” [6]. Additionally, the limited medical information available for individuals II-2 and III-1 suggested they passed away from complications of congenital anomalies of the kidneys and urinary tract. While renal involvement in HDR syndrome presents as congenital anomalies of the kidneys and urinary tract in 40% of patients [5], the kidney disease in Alport syndrome is instead expected to be slowly progressive over years to decades [7]. While both conditions should be on the differential for patients with progressive hearing loss and renal disease, the age of onset can help clinically distinguish these conditions.

Though HDR syndrome can present with congenital anomalies, there is significant variable expressivity and intrafamilial variability, suggesting the renal and hearing loss could present at any age and progress at any pace. The largest available HDR syndrome cohort reported 73.2% of patients have renal disease [4], while a second cohort suggested 9% of patients with HDR syndrome will progress to ESRD [5]. GATA3’s role in nephric duct differentiation likely explains the congenital renal anomalies seen in HDR syndrome [3]. This impaired cellular differentiation likely leads to the progressive disease in HDR syndrome as well, as mice with GATA3 haploinsufficiency had impaired glomerular maturation and decreased ability to proliferate mesangial cells to heal injured glomeruli [8].

Despite progressive renal involvement in a syndrome in which ∼10% of patients will develop ESRD, there are limited information regarding kidney transplant outcomes. Only 4 patients with a confirmed molecular HDR syndrome diagnosis have been reported to have undergone kidney transplantation, and no follow-up data after 1 year have been reported (Table 1) [5, 9, 10]. In this family, individual II-4 had a successful living related renal transplant from individual III-4 which remained functional for 26 years, despite the fact that the median renal graft survival from a living related donor from 1995 to 1999 was only 12.1 years with a 42.2% predicted 10-year graft survival rate [11]. This suggests his current renal failure is due to the expected lifespan of a transplanted kidney as opposed to GATA3-related dysfunction. While the patient from Nishimura et al. [9] demonstrates that treatment of the hypocalcemia and vitamin D deficiency caused by hypoparathyroidism in HDR syndrome can pose a risk to kidney health, known pathophysiology suggests that kidney disease is primarily driven by intra-renal GATA3-related changes.

Our limited data and knowledge about GATA3 pathophysiology suggest that this genetic disorder will not have a directly negative impact on a grafted kidney. Given the variable expressivity in HDR syndrome, any potential living-related donor should receive familial variant testing to determine if the potential transplanted kidney carries the same GATA3 variant. Detection of a GATA3 pathogenic variant should not be an absolute contraindication to kidney transplantation, as not all HDR syndrome patients develop clinical renal disease. However, an abundance of caution and attempts to find alternative donors would be merited to avoid removing a kidney in an individual who is at increased risk for future ESRD and grafting a kidney with an increased risk of progressive renal disease. Guidelines for the genetic evaluation of living donors are available [12]. In this case, the patient and other affected family members did not have a confirmed molecular diagnosis at the time of transplant; thus, the related donor was not sequenced. Confirmatory testing for individual III-4 is currently pending at this time.

In conclusion, the reported family highlights clinical distinctions between Alport syndrome and HDR syndrome. Additionally, individual II-4 demonstrates that long-term successful renal transplants are possible in HDR syndrome patients. The limited data available in the literature suggest that kidney transplantation should be readily offered to individuals with ESRD due to HDR syndrome, and molecular confirmation of a clinical presentation can offer a more precise diagnosis and allow for targeted screening for living related donors.
