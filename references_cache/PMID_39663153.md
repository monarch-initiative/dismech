BACKGROUND: Immunosuppressants are widely used as the preferred treatment for 
primary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (pFSGS). Nevertheless, controversies 
persist regarding the effectiveness and side effects of different 
immunosuppressive medications.
METHODS: From July 2023 until June 2024, we systematically searched PubMed, 
Cochrane Library, Web of Science, clinicalrials.gov, SinoMed, Chinese 
Biomedical, Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang, and VIP 
information. Randomized controlled trials comparing different immunosuppressants 
were included in adult patients with pFSGS, with total remission (TR) and 24-h 
urine total protein (24-h UTP) as the main outcome measures.
RESULTS: We identified 20 RCTs comparing nine different immunosuppressants for 
the final analysis. Most immunosuppressants showed better therapeutic effects in 
TR compared to non-immunosuppressive therapies (NIT), with risk ratios (RRs) of 
2.22 (95% CI 1.41-3.50) for cyclosporin, 2.10 (1.57-2.80) for 
leflunomide-combined steroids, 2.01 (1.24-3.27) for chlorambucil-combined 
steroids, 1.98 (1.17-3.33) for tacrolimus-combined steroids, 1.89 (1.36-2.63) 
for cyclosporin-combined steroids, 1.67 (1.28-2.18) for mycophenolate 
mofetil-combined steroids, and 1.47 (1.21-1.80) for steroids. Only mycophenolate 
mofetil-combined steroids (SMD -11, 95% CI -21 to -0.64) showed significant 
superiority in reducing 24-h UTP when compared with NIT. The subgroup analyses 
of steroids-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) patients showed that CSA + STE 
was significantly superior than the NIT group, with RR of 10.5 (95% CI 
2.28-44.35).
CONCLUSION: Steroids remain the recommended initial treatment for pFSGS. For 
those patients with SRNS, CSA + STE might be the best choice for improving the 
rate of TR. LEF + STE and MMF + STE also appear to offer a steroid-saving 
alternative to high-dose glucocorticoids for patients.

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) is a common cause of adult nephrotic syndrome (NS), accounting for 35% of cases of nephrotic syndrome undergoing biopsy [1]. In recent decades, the incidence rate of FSGS has gradually increased worldwide [2]. According to Sim et al. [3], FSGS is the most common primary glomerular disease among all primary glomerulonephropathies. Also, patients with FSGS have an elevated risk of progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) compared to other types of glomerulonephritis [4]. Among these cases, ∼16–25% of patients are classified as primary FSGS (pFSGS), with an annual incidence rate of 2–18 cases per million population [2,5]. The progression to ESRD imposes a significant economic burden and health-related morbidity on patients with pFSGS [6]. Proteinuria is the most common clinical manifestation of pFSGS patients, which can range from sub-nephrotic to nephrotic levels [7]. It has been known the relief of proteinuria can significantly reduce the risk of progression to ESRD in patients with pFSGS [8,9]. Therefore, achieving the relief of proteinuria in pFSGS patients as soon as possible and delaying the progression of renal failure is a common goal of nephrologists.

The most commonly used therapy to induce the remission of proteinuria in patients with pFSGS is immunosuppressive drugs, including glucocorticoids, calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), etc. [10]. Although nephrologists have recognized the importance of early initiation of immunosuppressive therapy for the management of pFSGS patients, however, the efficacy and adverse effects of various immunosuppressive drugs are still the subject of controversy. At present, it is difficult to identify the optimal immunosuppressive treatment plan based on the ­pathological results of kidney biopsy alone. On the other hand, excessive immunosuppression inevitably leads to immunosuppression-related drug toxicity. On this basis, understanding the therapeutic effects and safety of different immunosuppressants, individualizing treatment based on the patient’s specific situation, and maximizing patient benefit are the major challenges facing clinicians today.

Nevertheless, there are currently few head-to-head randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing the advantages of different immunosuppression regimens. Due to this, we performed a pairwise and network meta-analysis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of different immunosuppressants in FSGS patients, aiming to provide a reference for clinical practice.

Our work was performed following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) checklists and guidelines (see Supplementary File 1 for details). Also, the network meta-analysis has been registered at PROSPERO (CRD42023435525). From July 2023 to June 2024, two reviewers (YZ and BC) conducted an extensive search of PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, clinicaltrials.gov, SinoMed, Chinese Biomedicine, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, WanFang, and VIP Information using a predetermined retrieval strategy, aiming to select eligible studies evaluating the therapeutic effectiveness of various immunosuppressive medications in patients with pFSGS. The search involved a combination of free terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms across the databases: [(Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis) OR (Segmental glomerulosclerosis) OR (Glomerulosclerosis, Focal Segmental) OR (Segmental Glomerulosclerosis, Focal) OR (Focal segmental sclerosis)] AND [(Tacrolimus) OR (Cyclophosphamide) OR (Cyclosporin) OR (Steroids) OR (Mycophenolate mofetil) OR (Leflunomide) OR (Chlorambucil) OR (Adalimumab)]. The specific search terms and strategies of different databases were detailed in Supplementary File 8. Studies included in relevant publication references were also reviewed to avoid omissions. Additionally, the search process did not establish any language restrictions.

The selection criteria for including publications were as follows: (1) the study design should be RCTs (see Supplementary Table 1 for selection criteria); (2) participants enrolled must be biopsy-proven pFSGS; (3) the number of patients who achieved total remission (TR) and/or 24-h urine total protein (24-h UTP) should be reported by each study. TR was described as either CR or PR, meaning complete or partial remission. CR was defined as 24-h urine total protein <0.3 g, while PR was defined as 24-h urine total protein <3.5 g and a reduction of at least half from the crest value, both these definitions were confirmed by two text results obtained more than a week apart [11,12]; (4) interventions for studies should include Adalimumab, CSA (cyclosporin), CSA + STE (cyclosporin-combined steroids), CTX + STE (cyclophosphamide-combined steroids), LEF + STE (leflunomide-combined steroids), CLB + STE (chlorambucil-combined steroids), MMF + STE (mycophenolate mofetil-combined steroids), TAC + STE (tacrolimus-combined steroids), NIT (non-immunosuppressive therapies), and STE (steroids). Publications that fulfilled the subsequent criteria were not included: (1) participants were not adults (under the age of 15); (2) participants were hereditary FSGS, secondary FSGS, unexplained FSGS and FSGS recurrence after kidney transplantation; (3) the study was not aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of different immunosuppressants for patients with pFSGS; (4) medications mentioned in the publication were not included in our study, or publications compared the effect of different dosages or administration routes of a certain drug.

The retrieved material was managed using EndNoteX9 software. Articles that satisfied the inclusion criteria were acquired for assessment and data extraction after the title and abstract were screened. Based on a standardized data collection table, two reviewers (YZ and BC) independently extracted data from the included studies. The included information was as follows: baseline characteristics of studies (author, publication year), information of study subjects (country, sample size, gender, mean age), interventions (different immunosuppressive therapies, duration of treatment, and follow-up period), baseline criteria (initial estimated glomerular filtration rate, initial proteinuria, blood pressure, and coexisting treatment), as well as interested outcomes (TR, 24-h UTP, and adverse events). Data were mainly extracted from tables and text descriptions. If there is missing data or data shown in the figures, we tried to contact the author to request the original data. All included studies were assessed for quality. We used the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool [Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 5.4.0] to assess the risk of bias. Each domain could be classified as ‘low risk of bias’, ‘unclear risk of bias’, or ‘high risk of bias’ [13,14]. YZ and BC assessed the quality of the studies respectively. If there is any inconsistency, the third reviewer (GSX) will arbitrate.

The primary outcomes were TR, 24-h UTP, and adverse events (AEs), all measured at the last follow-up visit for each included study. The follow-up durations for each study are detailed in Supplementary Table 3. We conducted a network meta-analysis (NMA) to compare the efficacy and safety of different immunosuppressants. NMA offers a significant advantage over traditional meta-analysis as it enables the simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions against a common comparator. This approach allows for the ranking of numerous treatments at once by combining the outcomes of direct and indirect comparisons [15], which can improve the accuracy and reliability of the final conclusion [16]. To describe the effect sizes, we used risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for dichotomous variables and standard mean differences (SMD) with 95% CI for continuous variables. We only included the study with the most comprehensive and informative data when it came to studies that were published more than once, or duplicates. When the literature only reported the median and range or quartile, we used the median as a substitute for the mean and used the range/4 and interquartile range/1.35 to estimate the standard deviation [17]. To analyze the effects of various immunosuppressive drugs, a pairwise meta-analysis was conducted using STATA 15.0 software. Before statistical analysis, STATA 15.0 (‘mvmeta’ and ‘network’ packages) was installed for network map plotting, league tables making, and publication bias assessment. R 4.3.1 (‘ggplot2’, ‘JAGS’, and ‘gemtc’ packages) was applied to draw forest plots and regression analysis. The R 4.3.1 was employed for a Bayesian frame structure, while STATA 15.0 was applied for a frequentist framework. For each of the two sets of starting values, we ran 1,000,000 simulations, discarding the first 50,000 as the burn-in period. Then, the Brooks-Gelman-Rubin diagnostic and trace plots were used to analyze the density and convergence diagrams. If the 95% CI of the SMD does not include zero, or the 95% CI of the RR does not include one, it can be considered that the difference between the two comparisons is statistically significant.

We utilized STATA to calculate the mean rank and the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) of each outcome to rank the treatment regimens. By this approach, we assessed the probability of each treatment regimen being ranked at all possible positions and determined the superiority of the treatment regimens accordingly. The SUCRA value represents the likelihood that a treatment regimen will be the most effective for the outcome, expressed as a percentage. This probability approaches zero when the treatment is considered the worst, and approaches one when the treatment is considered the best [18]. Higher SUCRA values implied higher treatment grades. Then we used R software to conduct a statistical evaluation of global consistency using the design-by-treatment model [19]. We also adopted the node-splitting method for local evaluation to assess statistical consistency for each closed loop. The p-values were used in the node-splitting method to evaluate the difference between the evidence from direct and indirect comparisons [20]. A p-value >0.05 indicated that the heterogeneity is not significant in direct and indirect comparisons. Then the consistency model would be used for the following statistical analysis. We used I2 to evaluate the heterogeneity of pairwise and network comparisons. Significant heterogeneity was considered if I2 was more than 50%. Nonetheless, the I2 values of TR and 24-h UTP were all <16% when utilizing the consistency or inconsistency model, suggesting low heterogeneity overall (see Supplementary Table 2 for details). Moreover, subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis were performed to look into the possible explanation of heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on different regions and types of response to steroids to estimate the effectiveness of different interventions in clinically relevant patient subgroups. Additionally, the comparison-adjusted funnel plots were utilized to investigate publication bias.

The search identified 3816 articles in total, among which 298 potentially eligible articles were screened in full text (Figure 1). Only 20 [21–40] RCTs involving 920 patients were included in the final analysis. These publications observed nine different immunosuppressive protocols, including Adalimumab, CSA (cyclosporin), CSA + STE (cyclosporin-combined steroids), CTX + STE (cyclophosphamide-combined steroids), LEF + STE (leflunomide-combined steroids), CLB + STE (chlorambucil-combined steroids), MMF + STE (mycophenolate mofetil-combined steroids), TAC + STE (tacrolimus-combined ­steroids) and STE (steroids) for patients with pFSGS.

Of these 20 publications, the mean study sample size was 46 participants, and the average treatment period was 7.86 months (range: 4–23.5 months). All 20 RCTs reported detailed data of TR (including CR and PR), 13 of which reported the baseline of 24-h UTP and final 24-h UTP after pertinent therapy. Only seven studies simultaneously reported changes in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) before and after treatment (Supplementary Table 7) [22,28,29,31,33,36,39]. The baseline characteristics of the enrolled studies are shown in Supplementary Tables 3 and 6. Eighteen studies reported a randomized group allocation method; eight articles reported specific methods of random sequence generation, mostly including random number tables and randomized blocks. All studies exhibited a ‘low risk’ of bias in terms of blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. Nevertheless, due to a lack of sufficient information, most RCTs were considered to be of ‘uncertain risk’ of selection bias, performance bias, and other biases. In total, the included studies varied in quality from high to moderate. The quality evaluation results of the included studies are shown in Supplementary File 2.

The size of the nodes indicates the sample size of the interventions. Different treatment regimens were connected by a straight line whose thickness matched the test number of the direct comparison. The sample size and comparative times of the various therapies varied, as Figure 2 illustrates. Among them, NIT and STE-related studies have the largest sample size.

The diagnostic and trace plots indicated that this NMA has satisfactory convergence. The consistency analysis was performed using the node-splitting method, and all the p-values were more than 0.05, indicating a high degree of dependability and consistency (Supplementary File 3). In the heterogeneity analysis (Supplementary File 4), significant heterogeneity could be revealed in the comparison of NIT and Adalimumab for TR, NIT and Adalimumab, STE and NIT, and TAC + STE and CTX + STE for 24-h UTP. Therefore, we performed the network meta-analysis using the random-effects model. We also conducted meta-regression and sensitivity analysis to identify the sources of heterogeneity.

Thirteen studies reported the 24-h UTP at the last follow-up visit for a total of 580 patients [22,25,26,28–30,32,33,35–37,39,40]. Following were the involved treatment schedules: NIT (6 trials,123 patients), CSA + STE (3, 82), CTX + STE (4, 70), STE (4, 137), LEF + STE (2, 69), TAC + STE (2, 34), Adalimumab (2, 17), CLB + STE, MMF + STE, and CSA. Figure 2 shows the network plot. The analysis demonstrated that MMF + STE was a more successful treatment, with a significantly superior effect on 24-h UTP than NIT [SMD −11, 95% CI (−21, −0.64)]. While compared to NIT, the results of other treatment regimens did not show statistically significant (Figures 3 and 4). Of the results of NMA on 24-h UTP, MMF + STE had the highest rate of reducing 24-h UTP (SUCRA of 98.2%). It was followed by TAC + STE (74.1%), CTX + STE (64.6%), LEF + STE (62.8%), STE (50.5%), CSA + STE (42.5%), CLB + STE (35.1%), CSA (32.2%), and NIT (27.6%). Adalimumab was rated as the poorest treatment (SUCRA of 12.3%) (Figure 5, Supplementary Table 4).

Including 573 FSGS patients, 12 studies have described the TR rate in the Asian region [21,26,30,31,33–40]. Supplementary File 9 details the subgroup analysis for the Asian and non-Asian populations. In the Asian population, the TR for LEF + STE [RR 1.95, 95% CI (1.44, 2.64)], TAC + STE [RR 1.92, 95% CI (1.14, 3.25)], MMF + STE [RR 1.63, 95% CI (1.23, 2.15)], CSA + STE [RR, 1.68, 95% CI (1.18, 2.40)], and STE [RR, 1.43, 95% CI (1.16, 1.75)] were significantly higher than for NIT. However, the favorable outcome for CTX + STE did not reach statistical significance. The SUCRA rankings for the treatment protocols were as follows: LEF + STE at 87.6%, TAC + STE at 79.8%, MMF + STE at 58.1%, CSA + STE at 63.2%, and STE alone at 30.2%. In the 347 non-Asian FSGS patients [22–25,27–29,32], CSA + STE [RR 5.96, 95% CI (1.43, 24.81)], and CLB + STE [RR 4.29, 95% CI (1.04, 17.74)] were the only treatments with a significantly higher TR than NIT. The SUCRA values for CSA + STE, CLB + STE, CSA alone, and MMF + STE are 86.8, 74.9, 49.9, and 35.9%, respectively.

Totally eight studies evaluated TR rates in steroid-resistant pFSGS patients [21–25,28,29,33], with interventions including CSA + STE, CSA, MMF + STE, CLB + STE, STE, Adalimumab, and NIT, across 357 patients (Supplementary File 10). In this patient cohort, CSA + STE, CLB + STE, STE, and CSA emerged with superior SUCRA values of 86.7, 86.0, 66.3, and 40.4%, respectively. Furthermore, both CSA + STE and CLB + STE showed significant statistical superiority over the NIT group, with risk ratios (RR) of 10.5 (95% CI 2.28–44.35) and 10.61 (95% CI 1.80–62.69), respectively.

Supplementary Table 5 listed the incidences of adverse events for each of the 10 treatment regimens. The majority of immunosuppressants had a combined adverse event list that included infections (such as respiratory tract infections, urinary tract infections, etc.), gastrointestinal symptoms, bone marrow suppression, hyperglycemia, and impaired liver function. Gastrointestinal symptoms and infections were the most frequently reported adverse events across all patient cohorts, which was similar to the findings of Caster et al. [41]. In terms of gastrointestinal symptoms, LEF + STE (41.18%), CTX + STE (30.34%), and MMF + STE (21.37%) are the top three immunosuppressants with the largest proportion. In contrast, the three immunosuppressants linked to a lower incidence of bone marrow suppression were Adalimumab (0%), STE (0%), and TAC + STE (0%). Besides, STE (36.00%), TAC + STE (31.25%), and CTX + STE (25.84%) were linked to a higher incidence of infection.

The investigation into the cause of heterogeneity was done using a meta-regression. Univariate covariates included patient age, study duration, sample size, and follow-up time were adjusted for TR and 24-h UTP. The findings demonstrated that none of the covariates mentioned above had a significant effect on the results of the study (Supplementary File 5). The comparison-adjusted funnel plots showed that all studies are basically distributed on both sides of the central line in the middle, with a roughly symmetrical distribution on the left and right, suggesting a lower risk of publication bias [42]. A few studies deviate significantly from the regression line, indicating the possible presence of small sample studies (Supplementary File 6).

The sensitivity analysis results are shown in Supplementary File 7. For the outcomes of TR and 24-h UTP, excluding any individual studies from the sensitivity analysis had no discernible impact on the total effect sizes, demonstrating the robustness of our results.

Primary FSGS is an essential cause of clinically refractory nephrotic syndrome that affects both adults and children [10]. Patients who receive inappropriate or delayed treatment have a high risk of deterioration in renal function and possibly even advance to ESRD. Immunosuppressive therapy remains the preferred treatment for pFSGS patients [43]. Nevertheless, there is still controversy over the efficacy and safety of different immunosuppressants. In this review, we focus on the outcomes that are most probably to be meaningful for patients when making treatment decisions, such as TR, 24-h UTP, and AEs, aiming to provide more references for clinical practice. As shown in our pooled results (Figure 4), the NMA showed that CSA, LEF + STE, CLB + STE, TAC + STE, CSA + STE, MMF + STE, and STE have better therapeutic effects on TR than NIT. However, in our analysis of 24-h UTP, only MMF + STE showed significantly better therapeutic effects than NIT. In terms of bone marrow suppression, the immunosuppressive drugs that occurred with low frequency were TAC + STE, STE, and Adalimumab.

The recently updated Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) practice guidelines recommend that patients with pFSGS should receive high-dose corticosteroids as initial treatment [43]. In our pooled NMA, STE did not seem to show a more significant advantage in TR compared with other immunosuppressants (Figure 5). However, the region subgroup showed that STE plays an important role in TR (Supplementary File 9). In Asian subgroup, whether STE used alone or in combination with other immunosuppressants, the majority of treatments demonstrated superior therapeutic effects compared to NIT. CSA + STE also shower better effect than CSA alone in non-Asian subgroup. The 2021 KDIGO guidelines clearly recommend the initial treatment course of high-dose steroids should not <16-week, unless the complete remission achieve [43]. Some early researches have used an irregular treatment regimens duration, leading to the manifestation of pseudo-steroid resistance in numerous FSGS patients. Those patients then response positive outcomes when additional immunosuppressive agents are administered, concealing the efficacy of corticosteroids [37,38]. Hence, we believe that patients with pFSGS should still considered steroids as initial treatment, with duration based on treatment response.

Steroid-resistant patients with FSGS (usually defined as persistent proteinuria after receiving prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily or 2 mg/kg every other day for 4 months) have a significant risk of sustained progression of kidney disease [44]. Hence, we did a subgroup analysis of pFSGS with SRNS (Supplementary File 10). The results suggested that CSA + STE may represent the optimal pharmacological treatment for patients with pFSGS complicated by SRNS. The effects of STE and CSA used individually for inducing TR all did not reach statistical significance in this subgroup. Similarly, a study by Jiang et al. mentioned that prednisolone plus TAC or CSA might be the best choices for patients with SRNS [45]. The KDIGO2021 guidelines recommended CSA and TAC as a second-line treatment for high-dose steroid-dependent patients, steroid-resistant or steroid-intolerant (diabetes, severe bone disease, etc.) pFSGS patients [43]. Our pooled results also showed that CSA, TAC + STE, and CSA + STE all have better therapeutic effects in TR compared to NIT (Figure 4). However, the adverse reactions, such as infection, gastrointestinal reactions, and hypertension caused by CSA, TAC + STE, and CSA + STE should not be ignored (Supplementary Table 5). Notably, patients receiving long-term CSA and TAC treatment have a high risk of chronic nephrotoxicity, which may lead to a progressive decline in renal function [46]. We should be careful to avoid possible AEs when using calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs).

In our pooled result, MMF + STE demonstrated not only effective in improving TR but also a greater reduction in 24-h UTP than NIT (Figure 4). However, the SRNS subgroup showed that MMF + STE may not benefit to patients when compared with NIT, raised our consideration about its potential effect. Using MMF + STE as the initial treatment for pFSGS will mask a subset of patients who are responsive to steroids [31,39]. And AEs of gastrointestinal symptoms, muscular pain, dermatosis can also happen frequently in MMF + STE regimen. Thus its potential effect and safety, still needs to be further evaluated [43]. Similar views have also been expressed in the review by Lau et al. [47].

Notably, region is an important confounding factor in pFSGS research. Accordingly, we conducted subgroup analyses based on region. We noticed a phenomenon where patients with pFSGS seemed to respond well to LEF + STE in TR in our pooled result (Figure 4). It even showed a more significant advantage than STE, which was also seen in the Asian subgroup. However, only a few Chinese scholars have attempted to explore its efficacy in pFSGS [33,38,40], which is less robust research support in larger population. The highest incidence of gastrointestinal reactions, impaired liver function, and leukopenia among patients treated with the LEF + STE regimen are 41.28, 23.53, and 23.53%, respectively. This has also raised our concerns about the safety of this treatment regimen. Its efficacy and safety require further confirmation and be treated cautiously.

CTX and CLB, as alkylating agents, have been used to treat pFSGS for over 30 years [48]. However, we observed that compared with NIT, only CLB + STE had an advantage in TR, whereas the CTX + STE showed no notable effects either in TR or 24-h UTP (Figure 4). Due to the gonadal and hematological toxicity caused by CTX and CLB, CINs have replaced them as a second-line treatment for SDNS or SRNS patients [43]. Nevertheless, since it is affordable, CTX is still widely used in developing countries, which also alleviates the progression of the disease in these patients to some extent. In addition, our NMA indicated that Adalimumab had no significant advantage over NIT either in TR or 24-h UTP. Other new targeted agents are currently under development to explore novel treatment approaches for pFSGS [49]. Better and more adequate studies are still needed to demonstrate the efficacy of these drugs in patients with pFSGS.

To our knowledge, this is the first NMA to compare the efficacy and safety of different immunosuppressive treatment regimens in pFSGS patients. A meta-analysis by Caster et al. provided data only on treatment regimens and lacked comparisons between different immunosuppressive regimens [41]. Our study fills this deficiency by combining evidence from direct and indirect comparisons between different immunosuppressants. Our research aims to provide more references for clinicians when dealing with the different symptoms and needs of patients with pFSGS. Subgroup analyses were also conducted to estimate the effectiveness of different interventions in clinically relevant patient subgroups. We emphasize that the existing literature is limited and that better and fully controlled long-term studies should be carefully designed. Especially trials based on patient stratification according to the response to steroids and other drugs are needed to reliably evaluate the treatments of pFSGS patients.

In addition, there are still several limitations to this study. At first, heterogeneity in the included studies is inevitable. Although subgroup analyses indicate that regions and the response to steroids of patients are sources of heterogeneity, our analyses results cannot be considered confirmatory evidence for assessing efficacy. Caution is still needed in interpreting the results. Our interpretations can only serve as exploratory analyses to provide references for further investigation. Likewise, differences in trial design, patient populations, and treatment regimens among the included studies may also be sources of heterogeneity. Future studies should aim to employ standardized protocols to minimize these such inconsistencies. Especially for the initial treatment of pFSGS, it is recommended to use the standard corticosteroid regimen as recommended by the KDIGO 2021 guidelines. Secondly, there is a lack of RCTs that meet our inclusion criteria for several interventions of interest. Our analysis did not evaluate the application of RTX, which has been used as a consolidation therapy to reduce the risk of relapse in FSGS patients [50,51]. But literature search only found one RCT which included both MCD and FSGS patients simultaneously. We also did not find any studies comparing RTX with other immunosuppressants. The lack of research precluded our ability to evaluate the efficacy of RTX in the treatment of pFSGS. Thirdly, the number of studies on each specific drug is very limited. There is often only a single study for each agent, and there is a lack of validation studies to confirm the findings. This may exacerbate the design constraints inherent in individual studies, thereby compromising the robustness of our analysis outcomes. Another limitation is that the observation period of included studies was short, with an average observation period of 11 months. Although our results provide valuable insights into immediate and short-term effects, they cannot predict long-term outcomes or sustained benefits. While our meta-analysis included the latest studies, some of the studies were published earlier, and as a result, the administration of medication may be outdated. We emphasize the need for more carefully designed long-term trials to investigate the use of these drugs in pFSGS. Also, some studies have small sample sizes, which may lead to an overestimation of the efficacy of immunosuppressive therapy. Additionally, our explanation for patients with sub-nephrotic is limited, since it is an uncommon pFSGS patient.

For those patients with SRNS, CSA + STE might be the best choice for improving the rate of TR. While LEF + STE and MMF + STE appeared to offer a steroid-saving alternative to high-dose glucocorticoids for patients, the outcomes between the subgroup analyses results showed inconsistencies. Their adverse effects cannot be ignored. Caution is still needed when treating the results.
