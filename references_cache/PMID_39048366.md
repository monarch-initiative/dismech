Cowden syndrome (CS) is an autosomal dominant syndrome characterized by the 
development of hamartomas and an increased cancer risk. Most CS patients harbor 
mutations in the phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) gene. We herein report a 
70-year-old patient with CS who presented with lower extremity weakness caused 
by multiple thoracic dural arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs). Genetic testing 
revealed a truncated PTEN mutation (c.485_487delACAinsCC and p.D162Afs*5). 
Vascular malformations are common in CS, particularly in the extremities. 
However, spinal dural AVFs are extremely rare. Furthermore, in our case, the 
number of AVFs increased, and both lower limbs became flaccid four months after 
embolization. Therefore, we suggest that physicians carefully observe the 
changes in symptoms for prolonged periods after embolization.

Cowden syndrome (CS) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder characterized by multiple hamartomas and has a high risk of developing benign and malignant tumors in various organs, including the breast, thyroid, skin, and gastrointestinal tract (1,2). In 1997, phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) gene mutations were found to be responsible for CS (3,4).

Various abnormalities of the central nervous system, including mental retardation, developmental delay, macrocephaly, meningioma, and vascular malformations, can occur in patients (1,2). However, spinal arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) are extremely rare.

We herein report a CS patient with a PTEN mutation who presented with progressive paraplegia due to multiple thoracic spinal dural AVFs.

A 70-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital with progressive motor weakness of the lower extremities during walking and urinary and fecal incontinence. Her medical history included a multinodular goiter, gastrointestinal polyposis (Fig. 1), tubulovillous adenoma of the colon, and trichilemmoma of the forehead. Her family history was notable for multiple neoplasms and gastrointestinal polyposis in her grandfather, father, sisters, and son (Fig. 2).

Colonoscopy showed polyposis in the colon.


Family tree of the patient. c.485_487delACAinsCC (p.D162Afs*5) was identified in two patients (III-4, IV-2). The patient’s grandfather (I-1) and father had malignancies. The patient’s sister (III-5) had gastrointestinal polyposis. The patient’s son (IV-2) had gastrointestinal polyposis and gingival papules.


On a neurological examination, the patient was alert and had a normal cerebral function. All of the cranial nerves were intact. Both lower extremities were weak, and the patient could not walk. Deep tendon reflexes were absent in the knees and ankles. The Babinski and Chaddock signs were negative.

Laboratory findings were as follows: leukocyte count 4200 /μL (reference range, 3600-8900); C-reactive protein, 0.81 mg/dL (reference range, ＜0.29); prothrombin time-international normalized ratio, 1.11 (reference range, 0.85-1.15); and D-dimer, 7.7 μg/mL (reference range, 0-1). Thyroid function test results were normal. Tests for anti-thyroid peroxidase, anti-thyroglobulin, anti-nuclear, anti-SSA, anti-SSB, and anti-aquaporin antibodies were negative. Tests for proteinase-3 and myeloperoxidase anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies were negative. In a cerebrospinal fluid examination, the number of cells was slightly increased, and protein was significantly increased: cell count, 13 /μL (mononuclear cells 11 /μL, polynuclear cells 3 /μL); protein, 145.9 mg/dL; and glucose, 52 mg/dL. The patient's serum glucose level was 105 mg/dL. The immunoglobulin G index was 0.66 and oligoclonal bands were not detected. Cerebrospinal fluid cultures showed no bacterial growth. Genetic testing was performed because the patient had a history of multiple malignancies. Direct PTEN sequencing by polymerase chain reaction showed a c.485_487delACAinsCC mutation (p.D162Afs*5) in exon 5.

Chest computed tomography revealed multiple lung nodules. Spinal magnetic resonance imaging revealed intramedullary hyperintensity from T3 to T11 on sagittal T2-weighted sequences (Fig. 3B). On coronal T1-weighted post-contrast sequences, a dilated vessel was observed in the lower thoracic spine (Fig. 3C). Angiography demonstrated dural AVFs on the right side at T10 and on the left side at T10 and T11. The major arterial feeder radicular branches of the left T11 segmental artery drained into the perimedullary veins via the epidural vein (Fig. 4A). Endovascular glue embolization of the left fistula (Th11) was performed using 33% N-butyl-cyanoacrylate lipoid and coiling (Fig. 4B, white arrow). The right T10 segmental artery was embolized by coiling (Fig. 4B, white arrowhead). The left T10 segmental artery was not embolized because it was too small to be treated. Angiography after embolization showed a glue cast at the site and no arterial feeder opacification (Fig. 4B).

Her lower extremity weakness improved slightly after embolization, and she was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital. Four months after embolization, the patient returned to our hospital because of increased lower-extremity weakness and urinary retention during rehabilitation. Sagittal T2-weighted imaging revealed a new intramedullary hyperintensity at T12. Angiography showed dural AVFs on the right side at T8, T9, T10, and T12 and on the left side at T9, T10, and T12. These fistulas were too small to be embolized. Unfortunately, her lower limbs became flaccid, and she was discharged in a wheelchair.

At 72 years old, the patient underwent right lung upper lobectomy for the resection of an invasive adenocarcinoma (T1bN0M0). The patient died at 74 years old, after liver failure.

According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network diagnostic criteria for CS, our patient met two major criteria (multiple gastrointestinal hamartomas and trichilemmoma) and two minor criteria (colon adenoma and vascular anomalies) (2,5). Up to 80% of patients with CS have heterozygous germline pathogenic PTEN mutations (1). Our patient harbored a c.485_487delACAinsCC mutation in exon 5. This mutation creates a premature translational stop signal at 162 (p.D162Afs*5) and is expected to result in an absent or disrupted protein product. PTEN consists of 403 amino acids and has 5 functional domains (6): a phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PtdIns (4,5) P2)-binding domain (PBD), phosphatase domain, C2 domain, carboxy-terminal tail, and PDZ-binding domain. The phosphatase domain plays an important role in the enzyme activity (6). Since exon 5 encodes the phosphatase domain, this truncation mutation (c.485_487delACAinsCC, p.D162Afs*5) might decrease the enzyme activity of the PTEN protein. In fact, similar truncation mutations, such as c.441 insAdelGG (stop at 152) and c.330delAT (stop at 178), also induce CS (7).

Previous reports have revealed that vascular anomalies are present in approximately 56% of patients with PTEN mutations associated with CS (8). Vascular anomalies involving the spinal cord are extremely rare. A literature search of Japanese reports of CS patients between 1983 and 2017 revealed that only 1 of the 122 reported patients exhibited spinal vascular malformations (9). There are only five case reports that describe the detailed clinical features of CS with spinal AVFs in the English literature (10-14; Table). According to previous case reports, the age at first neurological presentation in patients with CS ranges from 38 to 70 years old (10-14). The most common initial symptoms of spinal AVFs in CS were progressive paraplegia (4/6), dysesthesia (2/4), urinary retention (3/6), and back pain (1/6). These symptoms are consistent with those of spinal AVF patients without CS (15,16). Unusual symptoms included neck and upper limb weakness. In the CS group, spinal AVFs developed at any site, from the cervical to the lumbar region (Table). Furthermore, spinal AVFs in the CS occur at multiple sites, so symptoms vary greatly. Almost all spinal AVF cases with CS are treated with transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE). The prognosis of spinal AVF in CS varies from partial improvement to complete paraplegia.

One important clinical feature in our case was that the number of AVFs increased after TAE and lower limb weakness worsened. PTEN is a negative regulator of the phosphoinositol-3-kinase/Akt pathway, which promotes the cell survival, proliferation, and motility. In CS patients with PTEN mutations, the protein loses its function, resulting in a predisposition to cancer development and tissue overgrowth across multiple developmental lineages. Loss of the PTEN function is associated with the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells (17) and leads to the overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which induces hypervascularization (18). It is possible that the increasing number of AVFs after TAE in our case may have been associated with an increase in VEGF. In fact, VEGF increase four months after embolization of a pelvic arteriovenous malformation was reported in a previous CS patient (13).

In conclusion, vascular malformations are common in CS; however, spinal dural AVFs are extremely rare. In our case, the number of AVFs increased four months after embolization. Therefore, we suggest that physicians carefully observe changes in symptoms for prolonged periods after embolization.
