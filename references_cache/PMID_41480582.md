# Rat bite fever presenting as localized cellulitis: The first documented presumptive case from Nepal and a call for clinical awareness.
**Authors:** Chaudhary S, Adhikari S, Sharma K
**Journal:** IDCases (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.idcr.2025.e02440](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.idcr.2025.e02440)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Rat bite fever (RBF) is a rare but potentially fatal zoonotic disease caused by Streptobacillus moniliformis or Spirillum minus. In Asia, it is often caused by Spirillum minus and may present with atypical clinical features that complicate diagnosis.

CASE PRESENTATION: A 35-year-old female from Far-Western Nepal developed fever and progressive localized swelling of the right hand eight days after a rat bite. Classic systemic features like rash, polyarthritis, and lymphadenopathy were absent. Laboratory tests showed neutrophilia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated CRP, while blood culture was negative. Despite negative microbiological result, a presumptive diagnosis of S. minus RBF was made based on epidemiological context, incubation period, and clinical presentation. She was treated with a combination of intravenous ampicillin, doxycycline and surgical wound debridement. She completed a course of oral antibiotics and recovered fully.

CONCLUSION: This case represents the first documented presumptive case of Spirillum minus RBF in Nepal, which presented atypically. It underscores the importance of clinical suspicion over microbiological confirmation in diagnosing RBF and emphasizes considering RBF in the differential diagnosis of febrile patients with cellulitis and a history of rodent exposure. Early initiation of empiric antibiotic therapy and timely surgical intervention can prevent serious complications. This case highlights RBF as an under-recognized zoonotic disease in Nepal and the need for greater clinical awareness and strengthened public health measures focused on rodent control.

Rat bite fever (RBF) is an uncommon zoonotic infection caused by Streptobacillus moniliformis or Spirillum minus [1], [2]. S. moniliformis is prevalent in the Americas and Europe, whereas S. minus, also known as Sodoku, is more common in Asia [3]. Transmission occurs primarily through rodent bites, scratches, or contact with rodent secretions [4].

Although the global incidence is low, untreated RBF can carry a mortality rate of up to 13 % [5]. The clinical features of S. minus infections differ from S. moniliformis in terms of incubation period, rash frequency, arthritis, and regional lymphadenopathy [3]. The diagnosis of S. minus is challenging as the bacteria is fastidious, it cannot be grown on standard artificial media. Moreover, atypical presentations, particularly with only localized lesions, are often under-recognized, leading to delayed diagnosis [6].

In Nepal, no cases have been formally reported in the medical literature, however, national news reports have highlighted suspected outbreaks, suggesting it is an emerging public health concern [7]. We report the first documented case of presumptive S. minus RBF from Nepal, which presented as localized cellulitis without systemic features such as rash, arthritis, or lymphadenopathy.

Patient Profile: A 35-year-old female farmer from Kailali District, Far-Western Nepal.

This report describes the first documented presumptive case of Spirillum minus related rat bite fever (RBF) in Nepal. Although rare globally, the disease is most frequently reported in Asia [1]. The patient's presentation was atypical, as she developed localized cellulitis without the systemic features like rash, joint involvement, or lymphadenopathy, making initial diagnosis challenging.

The clinical features are highly suggestive of Spirillum minus RBF (common in Asia) rather than Streptobacillus moniliformis (common in the Western countries). Key clues included the longer incubation period (8 days), the persistent and worsening wound at the bite site, and the absence of a rash or arthritis [1], [3], [10]. A definitive microbial diagnosis of S. minus is difficult because the bacteria is fastidious and cannot be cultured on standard media [6]. Therefore, diagnosis depends on recognizing the clinical features and a history of rodent exposure, as in this case. The negative tests for other diseases like leptospirosis supported RBF as the most likely diagnosis. This diagnostic challenge contributes to under-reporting, creating a gap between true incidence and published cases in Nepal [7]. Overall, our case suggests that RBF may be an under-recognized yet locally common zoonotic infection in Nepal rather than a truly rare disease.

The management of S. minus RBF requires prompt antibiotics therapy. Penicillin-based drugs like ampicillin are first-line, with doxycycline as a key alternative [6], [8]. Our patient improved on this combination. This case also highlights that surgical intervention may be necessary if severe tissue damage, as debridement was essential for her recovery [9].

This case suggests RBF may be an emerging and under-recognized zoonotic threat in Nepal, as hinted by national news reports [7], especially in agricultural settings where rodent exposure is common. Public health efforts should focus on educating communities about rodent control and the importance of immediate and proper wound cleaning following rodent exposure as essential preventive measures.

Differential diagnoses included bacterial cellulitis (e.g., S. aureus or Streptococcus pyogenes), S. moniliformis RBF, and leptospirosis.

This report describes a presumptive case of Spirillum minus rat bite fever (RBF) from Nepal. The atypical presentation as localized cellulitis without systemic features posed a significant diagnostic challenge and underscores the need for high clinical suspicion. Therefore, any patient with fever and cellulitis following a history of rodent bite should prompt consideration of RBF in the differential diagnosis. The patient’s successful outcome highlights the importance of early antibiotic therapy and timely surgical intervention in achieving complete recovery. Ultimately, preventing future cases requires a comprehensive approach that includes enhancing clinician awareness, strengthening public health measures, and implementing rodent control programs to minimize the risk of this under-recognized zoonosis in Nepal.
