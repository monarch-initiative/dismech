# Heart Failure Phenotypes Across Age Groups in the Danish Administrative Registers.
**Authors:** Garred CH, Friis C, Elmegaard M, Christensen DM, Wolsk E, Køber L, Schou M
**Journal:** Clin Epidemiol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.2147/CLEP.S622033](https://doi.org/10.2147/CLEP.S622033)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The Danish administrative registers are a powerful tool for heart failure (HF) research. However, the coding system has historically does not distinguished between HF phenotypes. We aimed to identify the distribution of HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF), and preserved EF (HFpEF) within the Danish National Patient Register overall and across age groups.

METHODS: We manually reviewed electronic health records of all patients with an incident primary diagnosis of HF (ICD-10: I50) at two large university hospitals in the Capital Region of Denmark between June 2021 and May 2024. HFrEF was defined as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤40%, HFmrEF as 41-49%, and HFpEF as ≥50%. Patients without available echocardiographic measurements were classified together with the HFpEF group. The phenotypic distribution was stratified by age groups (<65, 65-79, and ≥80 years) and six clinical subgroups (female sex, obesity, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation/flutter, diabetes, and ischemic heart disease).

RESULTS: Following manual review of 1888 patients with a primary diagnosis of HF (median age 74, 67% male), 1687 (89%) had HFrEF, 72 (4%) HFmrEF, and 129 (7%) HFpEF. HFrEF was dominant across all ages but was lower with advancing age: 95% in patients aged <65 years, 90% in those aged 65-79, and 83% among those aged ≥80. In all clinical subgroups, the proportion of HFrEF remained high, ranging from 83 to 90%.

CONCLUSION: Patients with an incident hospital diagnosis of HF at two Danish university hospitals predominantly presented with HFrEF. This phenotype distribution may reflect that of patients diagnosed with HF in the Danish National Patient Register. Even among those aged ≥80 years, fewer than one in five patients presented with HFmrEF or HFpEF. These findings provide a necessary reference for interpreting Danish registry-based HF data and inform the design of future registry-based clinical trials.

The Danish national health registries are widely recognised for their nationwide coverage and high quality.1 Using a unique civil identification number, individual-level data can be linked across the entire healthcare system, making these registries a primary tool for epidemiological research and registry-based clinical trials.2,3 Accurately identifying patients with heart failure (HF) is fundamental to cardiovascular research. While the ICD-10 code for HF has a high positive predictive value in Danish registries, it has historically not distinguished between HF phenotypes.4,5 A Danish study addressed this partially, showing that combining a HF diagnosis with prescriptions for HF medication could identify HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) with high precision.6 However, while effective for isolating HFrEF, this approach leaves a significant portion of the HF population unclassified. Moreover, the lack of granular diagnostic coding is increasingly problematic as clinical guidelines and therapeutic strategies for HFrEF and HFpEF continue to diverge, particularly following the introduction of evidence-based therapies for HFpEF.7 This limitation extends beyond registry interpretation, as misclassification of HF phenotypes may affect epidemiological studies, healthcare planning, and registry-based clinical trials.

Phenotype-specific ICD-10 subcodes were introduced and recommended by the Danish Society of Cardiology in late 2025, yet systematic adoption remains limited and have not been applied retrospectively.8

To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically described the distribution of HF phenotypes through manual review of electronic health records among patients with an incident hospital diagnosis of HF in Denmark. To address this issue, we manually reviewed electronic health records of HF patients. Our objective was to establish the distribution of HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF among patients with an incident hospital diagnosis of HF across age groups to support more accurate interpretation of Danish registry-based HF data in future research.

This study was conducted as part of a clinical quality assurance initiative and evaluated the phenotypic distribution of HF at the time of initial diagnosis. We retrieved a cohort of all patients assigned a first-time, primary diagnosis of HF (ICD-10 code: I50) at Herlev and Gentofte University Hospitals in the Capital Region of Denmark between June 1, 2021, and May 31, 2024. Incident HF was defined as the first recorded primary HF diagnosis at these hospitals, and patients with a previous HF diagnosis before referral to Herlev and Gentofte University Hospitals were excluded. Patients could be diagnosed both during a hospitalisation or a visit in an outpatient clinic. Clinical data were obtained through manual review of electronic health records performed between March and June 2025. Reviews were performed by two investigators using a standardized data collection form with predefined variable definitions and uncertain cases were resolved by discussion. Formal assessment of inter-reviewer agreement was not performed. HFrEF was defined by the presence of HF symptoms and a documented left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤40%. When multiple echocardiographic assessments were available, the LVEF measurement closest to the incident diagnosis date was used to determine HF phenotype, ensuring the data reflected the patient’s clinical state at presentation. When LVEF was reported as a range estimate (eg 15–20%), the lower bound was recorded. In cases where LVEF was reported as a borderline range spanning the HFrEF and HFmrEF thresholds (40–45%), phenotype classification was guided by referral to a specialised HF outpatient clinic. This approach was chosen because referral to these clinics in Denmark is generally reserved for patients considered to have HFrEF and eligible for guideline-directed HFrEF management. Referred patients were classified as HFrEF (lower bound recorded), whereas non-referred patients were classified as HFmrEF (upper bound recorded). Patients with an LVEF between 41% to 49% were defined as HFmrEF, and patients with an LVEF ≥50% or those without echocardiographic measures were grouped with the HFpEF category as a pragmatic approach. The following variables were collected at time of HF diagnosis: age, sex, diagnostic setting, comorbidities (diabetes, ischemic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, active cancer, and atrial fibrillation/flutter), and clinical measurements (LVEF, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), NT-proBNP, New York Heart Association (NYHA) Functional Classification). When NYHA class was documented as a range (eg NYHA I–II), the higher class was recorded. In cases where no explicit NYHA classification was documented, functional descriptions of symptoms were used to assign NYHA class. For eGFR and NT-proBNP, values obtained up to one months after the date of diagnosis were considered acceptable if not measurements were available at the time of diagnosis. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the dry weight in kilograms divided by the square of the height in meters. Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥30 kg/m2, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) as an eGFR <60 mL/min per 1.73m2. Additionally, if death had occurred at the time of electronic health record review, the date of death was noted.

Baseline characteristics are presented as frequencies and percentages (%) for categorical variables. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) or means with standard deviations (SD), as appropriate. Missing data are reported relative to the group of patients with data available. The distribution of HF phenotypes was analysed across age groups (<65, 65–79, ≥80) and within six prespecified subgroups based on patient characteristics at baseline (female sex, obesity, history of chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation/flutter, diabetes, and ischemic heart disease). A sensitivity analysis excluding patients without echocardiographic assessment was performed. To assess the representativeness of the study cohort relative to the nationwide HF population, two-year mortality across age groups was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier estimator. As this was a descriptive study, no formal statistical comparisons between age groups or clinical subgroups were performed.

All analyses were performed using R.9

This project was approved by the legal department and hospital management at Herlev and Gentofte University Hospital (WorkZone no. 24034115) as a clinical quality assurance initiative. In accordance with Section 42d of the Danish Health Act (Sundhedsloven), informed consent for review of patient medical records was not required. All patient data were handled confidentially and in accordance with applicable Danish data protection legislation. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

From a retrieval of 2475 patients with a HF diagnosis, we identified 1888 patients with incident HF. Patients were excluded if they had a pre-existing HF diagnosis, miscoded diagnoses, or duplicate recordings (Figure 1). In the included study cohort, the median age was 74.0 (63.0–82.0) years, 53% were diagnosed in an outpatient setting and 67% were male. By age group, 512 (27%) were <65 years, 780 (41%) were 65–79 years, and 596 (32%) were ≥80 years. Patients aged ≥80 years were more symptomatic, with 19.3% classified as NYHA class IV, approximately double that of younger age groups, and had a higher proportion of women. Among the entire study cohort, 1687 patients (89.4%) were identified as HFrEF, 72 (3.8%) as HFmrEF, and 129 (6.8%) as HFpEF (Figure 2). Baseline characteristics of each HF phenotype are provided in S-Table 1. The distribution of HF phenotypes varied significantly by age group. Among patients aged <65 years, 486 (94.9%) had HFrEF, 13 (2.5%) had HFmrEF, and 13 (2.5%) had HFpEF. In the 65–79 age group, 705 (90.4%) had HFrEF, 30 (3.8%) had HFmrEF, and 45 (5.8%) had HFpEF. Among those aged ≥80 years, 496 (83.2%) had HFrEF, 29 (4.9%) had HFmrEF, and 71 (11.9%) had HFpEF (Figure 3). Across the six clinical subgroups, HFrEF remained the dominant phenotype (Figure 4). Excluding patients without echocardiographic measurements did not materially change the distribution of HF phenotypes (S-Figure 1). Of the total study cohort, 373 deaths (19.8%) were recorded over a median follow-up of 2.2 years (IQR 1.4–3.0). The two-year mortality risk was significantly higher with advancing age: 6.0% (95% CI: 3.8–8.1) in patients aged <65 years, 14.2% (11.7–16.7) in those aged 65-–79 years, and 31.6% (27.6–35.5) in those aged ≥80 years (S-Figure 2).

In this manual review of nearly 2000 individual electronic health records, we found that HFrEF is the dominant HF phenotype among patients diagnosed in a contemporary hospital setting in Denmark. While the proportion of HFrEF was lower at higher ages and subgroups traditionally associated with HFpEF, including atrial fibrillation/flutter, chronic kidney disease, and female sex, it remained notably higher than global estimates.10,11

The distribution of HF phenotypes varies across settings and populations, making direct comparisons challenging. Data from the European Society of Cardiology HF III Registry suggest a distribution of approximately 58% HFrEF and 42% HFmrEF/HFpEF.12 Likewise, a population-based study from Southwest Sweden reported 35% HFrEF, 27% HFmrEF, and 38% HFpEF among patients with a conclusive echocardiographic assessment.13 In contrast, HFrEF accounted for nearly nine out of ten of HF diagnoses in our cohort.

Several factors may contribute to this observation, although our study was not designed to determine the underlying mechanism. In 2002, the Danish HF Clinic Network was founded.14 One possible explanation is the organisation of specialised HF clinics in Denmark, which may contribute to differential referral and diagnostic capture across phenotypes. Other factors may also contribute, including differences in diagnostic coding practices, thresholds for assigning a primary HF diagnosis, referral patterns, and access to echocardiography.

The Danish HF clinics are predominantly designed to manage patients with HFrEF,14 focusing on optimisation of guideline-directed medical therapy and patient education. Consequently, patients with suspected HF may be more likely to be referred and formally diagnosed when HFrEF is suspected or confirmed. Patients with HFmrEF/HFpEF, who often present with more subtle symptoms and fewer clear therapeutic targets, may therefore be underdiagnosed or may never receive a diagnosis code. Furthermore, patients presenting with dyspnoea, normal LVEF, and concomitant conditions such as valvular heart disease or atrial fibrillation may be coded according to these conditions rather than receiving a primary HF diagnosis, despite having phenotypic HFpEF.

The Diamond-CHF study, which screened patients between 1993 and 1996, found that a LVEF ≤35% was present in only 41% of patients hospitalised with HF across 34 Danish hospitals.15,16 Importantly, the study was conducted before the establishment of HF clinics. Our data, reflecting contemporary coding between 2021 and 2024, suggest that this evolution in the organisation of HF care has profoundly shaped which patients receive a formal hospital-based diagnosis and, consequently, what the Danish National Patient Register captures. Jensen et al reported a HFmrEF/HFpEF prevalence of 26% in a nationwide study of patients with HF followed in primary care.17 Although higher than our results, this suggests that HFmrEF and HFpEF are underrepresented across all settings in Denmark, whether hospital-based or in primary care.

Taken together, these factors suggest that the predominance of HFrEF observed in our study reflects how HF is diagnosed and coded in Danish healthcare system rather than the true population distribution of HF phenotypes. Accordingly, our findings are most relevant to the interpretation of Danish registry-based HF research rather than the epidemiology of HF phenotypes in Denmark.

The data were collected from two large university hospitals serving a broad and diverse patient population. Importantly, baseline characteristics and mortality risk in our cohort were comparable to those observed in nationwide registry data, supporting the relevance of our findings to Danish hospital-based HF populations.18,19 In addition, the study period (2021–2024) provides a contemporary overview during a time of increasing clinical awareness and evolving therapeutic guidelines for HFpEF. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our cohort was derived from patients living in the Capital Region of Denmark. Therefore, regional differences in referral patterns, and coding may limit the generalizability of our findings across Danish hospitals. Nevertheless, the similarity to nationwide data supports the relevance of our phenotypic distribution findings. Second, patients without echocardiographic data were classified as having HFpEF to ensure a conservative, inclusive approach. This approach may have introduced some misclassification and marginally underestimated the proportion of patients with HFrEF. However, given that only 1% of the cohort lacked echocardiographic data, the overall impact of this limitation is likely minimal. Furthermore, classification of borderline LVEF values (40–45%) was guided by referral to a specialised HF outpatient clinic as a pragmatic approach, which may have introduced some degree of misclassification. Finally, our findings reflect the phenotypic distribution of patients assigned an I50 diagnosis in a hospital setting, rather than the overall distribution in the general population. Additionally, only patients with a primary HF diagnosis were included. Consequently, patients in whom HF was recorded as a secondary diagnosis may have been missed, particularly those presenting with competing primary diagnoses. While this provides valuable insight into diagnostic coding and clinical practice in Danish hospital care, it does not capture patients managed exclusively in primary care or those who remain undiagnosed.

Among patients with an incident hospital diagnosis of HF at two Danish university hospitals, HFrEF accounted for 89% of the cases, remaining dominant even in older age groups and clinical subgroups. These findings provide an important reference point for interpretation of Danish registry-based HF data in hospital-diagnosed HF patients and inform the design of future registry-based clinical trials.
