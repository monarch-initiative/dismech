# A multicentre analysis of efficacy, safety and molecular response correlates of fostamatinib in warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and Evans syndrome.
**Authors:** Ruiz Lopez JN, Shao L, Jiang D, Bailey LS, Bikkani T, Boothby A, Moore DC, Murakhovskaya I, Piatek CI, Pineda-Roman M, Jin P, Poston JN, Stolla M, Stroncek DF, Hudson KE, Bussel JB, Panch SR
**Journal:** Br J Haematol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/bjh.70434](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjh.70434)

## Content

Fostamatinib had 46% durable response, with 73% steroid reduction, in this multicentre retrospective study of refractory wAIHA/ES. Hypertension, gastrointestinal (GI) distress and neutropenia occurred in 23%. Only one patient required drug discontinuation and one patient dose reduction. In an eight-patient subset, migration inhibitory factor (MIF) levels, naïve (CD62Lhi) T-cell and HLA-DRhi monocyte subsets each correlated with treatment response.


To the Editor,


Warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (wAIHA), a rare, acquired disorder characterized by macrophage‐mediated phagocytosis of autoantibody‐opsonized red blood cells (RBCs), may be idiopathic or secondary (infection, autoimmune disease, immune deficiency and/or lymphoproliferative disorder). wAIHA occasionally occurs with other autoimmune cytopenias, for example, immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), termed Evans syndrome (ES). Currently, there are no US‐FDA‐approved therapies for wAIHA or ES. Treatment‐related toxicities and mortality remain high.
1



Fostamatinib, a spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk) inhibitor, prevents autoantibody and macrophage‐mediated RBC/platelet destruction by blocking Fc receptor (FcR)‐initiated cellular signalling pathways. It is approved in chronic ITP with overall response rates (ORRs) of 43% and durable responses (DRs) of 17%.
2
,
3
 In recent phase II trials, 46% of patients with wAIHA achieved DR with fostamatinib.
4
,
5
 However, frequent placebo responses were seen in Eastern European subjects in phase III. Post hoc analysis suggested that this may have been due to enrolment of patients with anaemia not attributable to wAIHA.
4
 Given prior approval in ITP, signal towards drug response in post hoc analyses of Western European and US sites
4
 and dearth of targeted therapies, we undertook a real‐world evidence study to further explore clinical data. We performed biomarker assessments in a single‐centre subset to identify fostamatinib response predictors.

Following institutional review board approval, this multicentre US‐based retrospective study evaluated adults (≥18 years) with wAIHA/ES who received fostamatinib off‐label after ≥1 failed therapy. Consent was waived. Demographics and laboratory results were obtained 3 months before and up to 6 months after fostamatinib. Data were entered on REDCap. Safety and efficacy were evaluated: response was defined as Haemoglobin (Hgb) >10 g/dL or an increase of >2 g/dL from baseline on two consecutive visits and DR as Hgb >10 g/dL at 6 months. Additional outcomes included steroid dose changes, concomitant therapies, transfusions, hospitalizations and adverse events (AEs). In a single‐centre subset, eight patients consented to evaluation of germline mutations associated with inborn errors of immunity (IEI), plasma cytokines (Bio‐Rad), peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) transcriptomics (NanoString) and single‐cell ribonucleic acid sequencing (scRNAseq) (CITE‐seq). When available, bone marrow (BM) myeloid next‐generation sequencing (NGS) data were included. Healthy blood donor controls were age‐ and sex‐matched. Paired t‐tests compared responders versus non‐responders. Descriptive statistics evaluated other end‐points (Table S1).

Between January 2016 and July 2024, 22 patients' data from six US sites were collected (Figure S1). Demographics, clinical characteristics and outcomes in Table 1 include 13 patients (59%) with wAIHA and 9 (41%) ES (8 ES/ITP, 1 autoimmune neutropenia). Median age was 55 years, females (64%) and 55% non‐Hispanic Whites. Mean disease duration was 4.8 years (median 2.5 years, range: 0–19) at study initiation with median three prior treatments. Baseline median Hgb was 8.9 g/dL.

Regarding efficacy, 14 of 22 patients (64%) responded to fostamatinib (9 wAIHA and 5 ES). All cases of ES were categorized as secondary wAIHA. Median time to response was 3 weeks; DR was achieved in 10 responders: 6/13 wAIHA and 4/9 ES (Table 1). Prednisone was tapered to <20 mg daily in 16/22 (73%). Eleven required corticosteroid rescue for wAIHA relapses. Ten of 22 (45%) required at least monthly transfusions prior, but only 4 of 22 (18%) after fostamatinib initiation. Six of seven Hispanics responded (p = 0.19). Response did not differ by splenectomy status, ES versus wAIHA, or other demographics (Table 1). Using FIT1/2 criteria (response as platelet count ≥50 × 109/L without rescue therapy after 12 weeks), 5/8 patients (62%) with ES/ITP responded to fostamatinib; 3/8 continued corticosteroids or other concomitant therapies (Figure 1A–D).

Regarding safety, AEs were documented in 5/22 patients (23%): two hypertension (grade 1), one abdominal discomfort, one diarrhoea and one severe neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count [ANC] ≤500/μL, grades 1–2). Drug discontinuation occurred in one neutropenic, dose reduction in one hypertensive patient. Small increases occurred in mean diastolic blood pressure (5 mmHg; p = 0.03) on fostamatinib. Average monthly emergency department (ED) visits and hospitalizations decreased by −0.4 and −0.2 respectively (p's >0.05).

In five responders and three non‐responders, targeted germline sequencing identified four variants/patient in IEI genes, not different by response status (Figure 1E) or compared to healthy controls (Figure S2a). In 15 patients, BM NGS identified one responder and one non‐responder with pathogenic variants, and one each with a variant of uncertain significance (Table S2). Post‐treatment PBMC transcriptomics revealed downregulated FcR phagocytosis (p < 0.01) and nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB) pathways (p < 0.05) in non‐responders compared to responders (Figure 1F); responders were similar to healthy controls (Figure S2b). Plasma cytokine levels revealed higher macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) levels in responders versus non‐responders (p < 0.01) (Figure 1G) and responders versus healthy controls (p < 0.01) (Figure S2c). scRNAseq demonstrated differences in monocytes, CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell populations (Figure 1H). Compared to responders, non‐responders had more CD62Llo T cells (reduced L‐selectin/CD62L occurs following transition to activated or effector cell status) and T‐effector memory cells (TEMRA) (Figure 1I; Figure S2e). Responders had higher CD62Lhi (more naïve) T cells (Figure 1I) and more HLA‐DRhi (expressed on antigen‐presenting cells) classical monocytes while non‐responders had more HLA‐DRlo monocytes. Reduced human leukocyte antigen DR (HLA‐DR) expression is noted in pathological states.
6
 Cellular clusters among responders and healthy controls showed overlap, whereas non‐responders formed distinct clusters (Figure S2d,e). For CD14, CD16 and HLA‐DR expression, see Figure S3.

In this multicentre retrospective real‐world evidence study with chronic wAIHA and ES patients who had failed prior therapies, fostamatinib demonstrated 64% ORR and 46% DR (Hgb increase 6 months after) with responses observed within 3 weeks. Among 14 responders, three had undergone splenectomy, suggesting fostamatinib's mechanism of action extends beyond inhibition of splenic macrophage‐mediated phagocytosis. Inhibition of B‐cell receptor signalling and autoantibody production may contribute.
7
 DR was similar to the phase II trial; ORR was higher,
4
,
5
 possibly because of 50% non‐Whites in our cohort compared to ~20% in earlier wAIHA studies or inclusion of ES patients. Responses in this study, both by Hgb increase and platelet increase in ES, were higher than in the licensing trials of chronic refractory ITP
8
 and similar to real‐world retrospective studies of chronic ITP from Spain, France and Italy (where they note earlier use may contribute to higher responses).
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
 Steroid reduction and transfusion‐sparing effects were notable; ED visits and hospitalizations trended down but were inconsistently captured. Other limitations include inconsistent capture of steroid dosage (dichotomized as high‐dose vs. <20 mg daily of prednisone equivalents), small number of patients in the biorepository and potential for selection bias. The latter addressed by screening all patients with documented fostamatinib use.

GI side effects and hypertension were similar to prior studies.
4
,
10
 Two of 22 patients required dose reductions (for neutropenia) or drug discontinuation (for hypertension) occurring within the first 12 weeks after fostamatinib initiation. No serious infections were identified. More specific syk inhibitors,
13
 or bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors that act downstream of syk,
14
 may demonstrate improved toxicity profiles with retained or even enhanced efficacy.
13
,
14



Transcriptomics and cytokine profiling distinguished responders from non‐responders. NF‐κB, FcR and phagocytic pathways
15
 were relatively upregulated in responders compared to non‐responders and mirrored healthy controls. Relative upregulation of NF‐κB and FcR pathways may indicate increased macrophage‐mediated phagocytic activity facilitating response to inhibitors of these pathways. Changes in the balance of activating and inhibitory FcRs can indicate a successful therapeutic effect.
16
,
17
 Conversely, non‐responders may rely less on FcR‐driven phagocytosis, explaining their non‐responsiveness. The lack of baseline data precludes definitive conclusions. Nonetheless, similar expression levels among responders and healthy controls suggest that fostamatinib may reset pathogenically downregulated pathways in certain patients.

Responders demonstrated higher MIF levels ~12 weeks after treatment with fostamatinib versus non‐responders. MIF plays a role in acute inflammatory and autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis,
18
 with persistently elevated MIF levels in chronic states associated with protective functions and anti‐inflammatory/reparative effects over time.
19
 scRNAseq revealed higher CD62Lhi CD4 and CD8 T cells among responders versus non‐responders, associated with naive and some memory T cells, less effector function and (paradoxically) better antitumor/immune activity.
20
 Non‐responders demonstrated higher TEMRA cells, similar to chronic refractory ITP patients.
21
 Differential monocytic populations among responders (HLA‐DRhi classical monocytes) and non‐responders (HLA‐DRlo classical monocytes) suggest an anti‐inflammatory effect of syk‐inhibitors among responders. This is consistent with a study in severe COVID‐19, wherein investigators corroborated improved outcomes following fostamatinib with a shift in classical monocyte populations from HLA‐DRlo to HLA‐DRhi phenotypes.
22
 There were no apparent differences in somatic or germline variants among responders versus non‐responders.

A dearth of both approved treatments and biomarkers remains a crucial gap in personalizing therapies for wAIHA/ES. Patients with refractory wAIHA/ES are typically exposed to multiple sequential therapies resulting in cumulative toxicities and potentially worsening immunologic aberrations over time. The burgeoning rise in newer therapies for wAIHA
23
,
24
 renders biomarker‐based patient profiling and pharmacotyping even more relevant. Several omics platforms have identified promising molecular response predictors in ITP,
25
 lymphoma
26
 and cancers.
27
 Ours is the only study to perform multiomic profiling to identify fostamatinib response predictors.

Overall, this real‐world evidence study indicates safety and substantial efficacy of fostamatinib in refractory wAIHA/ES, reinforcing its role in difficult‐to‐treat populations that lack FDA‐approved therapies. Molecular profiling suggests that FcR‐phagocytosis pathways, MIF levels as well as certain cellular subsets are involved in response. While aligning with fostamatinib's mechanism and other studies characterizing T‐cell and monocyte phenotype and function, confirmatory data are needed in more patients. Incorporating biomarker profiling into future clinical trials and real‐world studies may enable rational therapy sequencing.
