INTRODUCTION: Long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCHAD) deficiency, 
typically inherited as a recessive trait, is a genetic condition predominantly 
observed in Central and Eastern Europe, with birth prevalence in Poland 
amounting to 1/118,336. In most European countries, e.g., in Poland since 2014, 
this disorder is included in newborn screening.
CASE PRESENTATION: This paper presents the ophthalmic symptoms of long-chain 
3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCHAD) deficiency in three pediatric patients. 
Visual acuity testing, fundus photography, and optical coherence tomography 
(OCT) were performed and data were collected over several years (2017-2022). In 
case 1, a female born in 2010, exhibited abnormalities in the central part of 
the posterior pole, mainly in the macula, and included choriocapillaris atrophy 
and severe disruption of the outer retinal layer. Case 2, a female born in 2012, 
presented with progressive shortsightedness and choroid atrophy documented with 
angio-OCT. Case 3, a male born in 2013, experienced recurrent hospitalizations 
due to metabolic decompensations and presented with mild myopia, thinning of the 
choroid layer, and slight pigment dispersion with macular sparing.
CONCLUSION: The main ophthalmic symptoms of LCHAD deficiency were choroidal 
atrophy, disorganization of the outer retinal layer, and myopia. Choroidal 
atrophy and pigment dispersion were consistently the earliest signs of 
LCHAD-associated chorioretinopathy. Although the progression of 
chorioretinopathy in each case resulted from metabolic decompensation, one 
documented case revealed that not every metabolic crisis results in 
ophthalmological changes. Nonetheless, strict adherence to a low-fat, 
high-carbohydrate diet remains crucial to prevent gradual deterioration and 
vision loss.

Long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCHAD) deficiency is a rare genetic disorder caused by a mutation in the HADHA gene, which is in most cases inherited as a recessive trait [1] that leads to an impairment of mitochondrial fatty acid beta-oxidation. The prevalence of deficiency carriers is high in Estonia [2], Finland [3], and Poland, where it accounts for 1/118,336 [4] and is common in the Pomeranian region, totaling 1/91,700 [4]. The deficiency is lethal if a low-fat, high-carbohydrate diet is not administered early in life [5]. Therefore, LCHAD is among the other metabolic disorders tested in newborn screening [6].

During a highly stressful event, fasting, consumption of high-fat food, insufficient calorie intake, excessive physical activity, or infection with dehydration caused by diarrhea or emesis, the body is unable to metabolize long-chain fatty acids. Their toxic intermediates accumulate in cells [7] and cause metabolic decompensation. Clinically, this state manifests as nonketotic hypoglycemia, myopathy, and symptoms resembling Reye syndrome, and it may even result in sudden infant death syndrome [8]. Even if this life-threatening condition is managed promptly by intravenous administration of 10% glucose, it catalyzes the progression of long-term complications such as cardiomyopathy, liver defects, rhabdomyolysis, peripheral neuropathy, and retinopathy [9].

As long-chain fatty acid intermediates accumulate, they cause retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) degeneration. Without a functioning RPE, photoreceptors atrophy. As a secondary effect of this process, there is an increase in the visibility of the choroid on optical coherence tomography (OCT) [10]. LCHAD-associated chorioretinopathy is categorized into four stages [3]. Stage 1 presents with a normal fundus, stage 2 presents with the dispersion of pigmentation within the RPE, stage 3 presents with circumscribed chorioretinal atrophy and decline of central vision, and stage 4 presents with chorioretinal atrophy within the macula and development of a central scotoma [3].

This paper presents the ophthalmic symptoms of LCHAD deficiency in three cases from the Provincial Specialist Hospital, Wroclaw, Poland, depicting disease progression using visual acuity testing, fundus photography, OCT, angio-OCT, and visual field testing in one of the cases. The CARE Checklist has been completed by the authors for this case report, attached as supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000537895).

A female patient born in 2010 from a second pregnancy complicated by maternal gestational cholestasis was diagnosed with LCHAD deficiency at the Children’s Memorial Health Institute in Warsaw at the age of 3 months because of severe life-threatening metabolic decompensation. In 2015, at 5 years of age, she was admitted to our ophthalmology clinic. The patient had a visual acuity of 0.8 in both eyes. The right eye showed a myopia of −1.25 and the left eye of −0.5 SPH. Fundus examination revealed choroidal atrophy with prominently large vessels and submacular regrouping of the pigment (Fig. 1). These changes led to the categorization of LCHAD-associated with chorioretinopathy to stage 2. Consecutive annual examinations revealed a slow progress of shortsightedness and chorioretinopathy that were not beyond stage 2 LCHAD-associated retinopathy. Metabolic decompensations that required hospitalization frequently occurred throughout the years. Because the patient was obese, an uncontrolled self-restriction of calories also exacerbated the progression of LCHAD-associated symptoms.

Case 1: fundus photography, 2015: decay of the choriocapillaris with visible large choroidal vessels and submacular accumulation of the pigment. OCT, 2015: despite the abnormalities in the extrafoveal choriocapillaris, the other layers appear unaffected.

In 2022, at the age of 12, the patient was hospitalized because of sudden pain in the back and upper and lower extremities and myoglobinuria. Blood tests showed significantly high creatine kinase and liver function parameters (alanine aminotransferase and alanine transaminase), confirming metabolic decompensation. The patient was treated with continuous 10% glucose infusion with electrolytes and oral glucose. On ophthalmological examination, visual field testing revealed a central scotoma and decreased peripheral sensitivity to light in both eyes. Fundus photography revealed a pale optic disk surrounded by atrophic choriocapillaris, narrow capillaries, maculopathy, and irregular regrouping of the pigmentation (Fig. 2). OCT showed destruction of the photoreceptor layers, hyperreflective material situated submacularly, a generalized decrease in the thickness of the retina, and disorganization of the outer retina (Fig. 2), categorizing the retinopathy as stage 3. The myopia of the right eye was −4.00 spherical diopters, whereas that of the left eye was −2.00 spherical diopters. The patient had a visual acuity of 0.4 in the right eye and 0.5 in the left eye.

Case 1: fundus photography, 2022: pigment accumulation submacularly, generalized atrophy of the choriocapillaris of the posterior pole, and pallor of the optic disk. OCT, 2022: destruction of the photoreceptor layers demonstrating progressive atrophy in LCHAD deficiency, generalized decrease of thickness of the retina, outer retinal atrophy showing patchy loss of the ellipsoid zone and subjacent RPE, scrolling of the outer retinal elements, and outer retinal tubulations with hyperreflective material situated submacularly.

A female patient born in 2012 in the 33rd week of gestation by cesarean section due to HELLP syndrome was diagnosed with LCHAD deficiency at the age of 2. Before the diagnosis, the patient was hospitalized because of hepatitis at the age of 5 months. She was consulted by an ophthalmologist for the first time in 2014, when she was 2 years old. Funduscopic examination revealed generalized moderate choroid atrophy with prominent large vessels (Fig. 3); no retinal changes were observed on OCT. From 2018 to 2022, when the patient was between 6 and 10 years old, consecutive examinations confirmed the gradual progression of shortsightedness and choroid atrophy. In 2022, OCT detected thinning of the choroidal layer with peripheral attenuation of the choriocapillaris (Fig. 4) categorizing LCHAD-associated retinopathy as stage 2. The patient was diagnosed with myopia of −2.75 in the right eye and −3.0 in the left eye, with a visual acuity of 1.0.

Case 2: fundus photography, 2017: choroidal layer thinning with visible large vessels; OCT, 2017: normal retinal layers showing no abnormalities in the ellipsoid zone and RPE.

Case 2: angio-OCT, 2022: normal superficial and deep vasculature of the retina, delicate choriocapillaris atrophy with visible large vessels. OCT and FF, 2022: delicate pigment dispersion peripherally.

A male patient born prematurely in 2013 by cesarean section because of intrahepatic cholestasis was diagnosed with LCHAD deficiency at the age of 4 months. Three months later, he was hospitalized because of poor appetite and had to be intragastrically fed with 10% glucose. A permanent percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy was performed because of impaired swallowing reflex to ensure consistent nutrition and protection from metabolic crisis. Funduscopic examination in 2014 revealed attenuation of the choroid (Fig. 5). As a consequence of divergent strabismus, convergence exercises were introduced. In 2016, at the age of three, the patient was hospitalized because of acute gastritis and was diagnosed with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The following month, the patient was readmitted, this time with features of decompensation with elevated creatine phosphokinase, alanine transaminase, alanine aminotransferase, and C-reactive protein levels and hypokalemia. Consequently, 10% glucose and 15% KCl infusions were administered. Between 2017 and 2022, when the patient was between 4 and 9 years old, four episodes of metabolic crises occurred; each was promptly addressed using the methods described above. In 2017, when 4 years old, the child was qualified for surgical treatment of strabismus. Despite the metabolic crises, fundoscopy and annual OCT showed slight pigment dispersion peripherally and choroidal layer thinning (Fig. 6). Myopia of −1.0 spherical diopters was observed in both eyes, with a visual acuity of 1. Despite several metabolic decompensations, no regrouping of the pigment has been detected, thereby categorizing LCHAD-associated retinopathy as stage 1.

Case 3: fundus photography, 2015: delicate pigment dispersion peripherally, choroidal layer thinning with visible large vessels. The yellowish vertical abnormality in the left eye is a result of a light reflex that occurred during the examination.

Case 3: fundus autofluorescence, 2022: peripheral dispersion of the pigment with sparing of the macula. OCT, 2022: normal retinal layers showing no abnormalities in the ellipsoid zone and RPE and perifoveal thinning of the choroid layer.

At least 50% of individuals with LCHAD deficiency experience LCHAD-associated retinopathy [11]; however, the precise incidence remains undetermined. The pathophysiology of this process involves RPE atrophy [12], which is a crucial component of visual transduction, maintenance of homeostasis, and appropriate extraphotoreceptor matrix [13]. As excessive free fatty acids, which are unable to enter the beta-oxidation process, surpass the limited lipid storage capacity of nonadipose RPE cells, they induce peroxidation, which disrupts cell signaling and leads to cellular dysfunction and apoptosis [14]. RPE atrophy secondarily affects the choriocapillaris and further retinal layers [14], including the photoreceptor layer, with cones showing atrophy before rods [11]. The in vitro model of patient-specific RPE monolayers with LCHAD deficiency further confirmed that retinal changes are linked to the overaccumulation of free fatty acids, reduced pigmentation, decreased melanosomes, and impaired cell-to-cell adhesion [14]. Long-chain fatty acids have also been linked to changes in the composition of mitochondrial cardiolipins, impairing respiration and functioning of the mitochondria [15]. Moreover, Fletcher et al. [16] found a correlation between elevated concentrations of 3-hydroxy acylcarnitines, a byproduct of the long-chain fatty acid β-oxidation process, observed after metabolic decompensation, and progression of retinopathy.

The main ophthalmic symptoms of LCHAD deficiency were choroidal atrophy, disorganization of the outer layer of the retina, and mild myopia. In each case, choroidal atrophy and pigment dispersion were the earliest signs of LCHAD-associated chorioretinopathy. In case 1, the most severe of the presented cases, these abnormalities were observed in the central part of the posterior pole, mainly in the macula, and included choriocapillaris atrophy, RPE degeneration, and severe disruption of the outer retinal layer. These findings align with other cases of LCHAD-associated chorioretinopathy reported in the literature [11, 17]. Similar to other studies concerning LCHAD deficiency, no retinal tears were observed [11, 17, 18]. In each case, myopia progressed gradually, although in none of the cases so far has it surpassed −5 diopters, which would qualify it as a high myopia [19]. Notably, these are pediatric cases (the oldest patient was born in 2010); therefore, myopia will most likely progress, as according to the literature, it tends to progress even in adulthood [17]. Visual acuity was significantly decreased in case 1. In the other cases, visual acuity remained unchanged. Notably, the optic disks in case 1 were pale. Similar findings were reported by Boese et al. [17]. According to Wongchaisuwat et al. [18], choroidal neovascularization may occur later in life; however, no CNV has yet been detected in the 3 cases presented. We assume that the strabismus in case 3 was not related to the deficiency because there has been no link between strabismus and LCHAD deficiency in the literature.

Each progression of retinopathy and myopia occurred after a metabolic decompensation; however, not each decompensation deteriorated these symptoms, as shown in case 3. In this case, immediate application of high-percentage interventional fluid and present percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy prevented severe complications of the decompensation just as its first symptoms started to emerge.

Furthermore, the deficiency manifested differently in each child. Symptoms that dominated in the first case were mainly ophthalmological, in the second case hepatic, and in the third case cardiological. The differences in the manifestations may stem from the different genotypes of the patients.

Unfortunately, none of the patients were diagnosed during neonatal screening because they were born before 2014, the year LCHAD deficiency was added to the newborn screening program. Early introduction of a proper diet, avoidance of highly stressful events, and prompt remedying once indicators of decompensation occur are crucial to arrest the advancement of the deficiency, including neurological and ophthalmological manifestations [9]. To date, no treatment for the root cause of the deficiency has been established. However, articles on a novel therapy using triheptanoin have begun to emerge over the last few years [20]. Considering that retinopathy is an irreversible process in contrast to hypertransaminasemia and cardiomyopathy, regular ophthalmological assessments are mandatory for monitoring retinopathy progression. All patients featured herein are currently undergoing preparatory measures for a life characterized by partial or complete loss of sight because the disease is lifelong.
