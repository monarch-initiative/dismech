# Myelin lesion in the aspartoacylase (Aspa) knockout rat, an animal model for Canavan disease.

Canavan disease (CD) is a fatal hereditary neurological disorder caused by a
mutation in the aspartoacylase (ASPA) gene and characterized by neurological
signs and vacuolation in the central nervous system (CNS). The mutation inhibits
the hydrolysis of N-acetyl-aspartate (NAA) resulting in accumulation of NAA in
the CNS. A new Aspa-knockout rat was generated by transcription activator-like
effector nuclease (TALEN) technology. Herein we describe the pathological and
morphometrical findings in the brain and spinal cords of Aspa-knockout rats.
Although Aspa-knockout rats did not show any neurological signs, vacuolation
with swollen axons, hypomyelination, and activated swollen astrocytes were
observed mainly in the brainstem reticular formation, ascending and descending
motor neuron pathway, and in the olfactory tract. Morphometrical analysis
revealed no obvious change in the number of neurons. These changes in the CNS
are similar to human CD, suggesting that this animal model would be useful for
further study of treatment and understanding the pathophysiology of human CD.

Canavan disease (CD) is a rare neurodegenerative disease characterized by developmental delays and mental retardation in humans [1,2,3,4,5]. Onset of CD typically occurs between the age of 2
to 6 months, after which it progresses, and most patients die by the age of 5 years [6]. The prevalence of CD is much higher in children of Ashkenazi
Jewish and Saudi Arabian descent than in other ethnic groups [1, 2, 7]. There
are still no effective treatments for CD [8]. CD is inherited as a recessive trait caused by a mutation in the aspartoacylase (ASPA) gene
that results in ASPA enzyme deficiency, and is pathologically characterized by vacuolation in the central nervous system (CNS) [4, 5, 9,10,11,12,13]. The ASPA protein is an enzyme highly expressed in oligodendrocytes and is involved in the hydrolysis of N-acetyl-aspartate (NAA) into acetate and
aspartate [12]. Therefore, deficiency of ASPA leads to accumulation of NAA, and decreases in the products of acetate and aspartate, resulting in a lack
of myelin-associated lipids [1, 2, 10, 12,13,14,15].

The Aspa gene knockout mouse (Aspa-knockout mouse) shows symptoms similar to patients with CD such as tremors, seizures, and ataxia [16]. Furthermore, the Aspa-knockout mouse develops vacuolation in the white matter of the brain and shows high levels of NAA in the urine and brain, which
is typical of patients with CD [17,18,19]. Therefore, the mouse model is
thought to be useful to understand the pathogenesis of human CD, and new therapeutic approaches, including gene therapy, have been studied with this mouse model [4].

A new Aspa-knockout rat was generated by using transcription activator-like effector nuclease (TALEN) technology; this rat model also showed accumulation of NAA in the CNS
[20]. Pathological changes similar to human CD were described in a study using 12 weeks old Aspa-knockout rats, showing potential
utility as an animal model for CD [20]. But detailed pathology of Aspa-knockout rats remains to be investigated. Here we describe the
pathological and morphometrical studies on Aspa-knockout rats from 4 to 40 weeks of age to address further potential utility.

Aspa-knockout rats (F344-Aspaem34Kyo, NBRP Rat No: 0806) were supplied by the National BioResource Project-Rat, Kyoto University (Kyoto, Japan).
The Aspa-knockout rat was established by genome editing using TALEN technology [20]. Supplied rats were fed and bred at Osaka
Metropolitan University.

The homozygous Aspa-knockout rats (4, 8, 12, 24, 26, 28, and 40 weeks old), F344/DuCrlCrlj rats (4, 8, and 12 weeks old, Charles River, Hino, Japan) and F344/NSlc rats (24
weeks old, Japan SLC, Hamamatsu, Japan) were used (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). All animals were handled according to the
Guidelines for Animal Experimentation of Osaka Metropolitan University, and approved for animal experiment.

For histological analysis, brains and cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spinal cords were collected. Tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. Brains
of 4, 8, 12, 26, 28, 40 weeks old Aspa-knockout rats and 4, 8, and 12 weeks old F344 rats were cut into 2 sections: cerebrum and cerebellum/pons/medulla oblongata. Brains of
24 weeks old Aspa-knockout and F344 rats were cut into 14 sections of approximately 1 mm width using a brain matrix. To examine the effects of post-mortem changes or
fixation, perfusion fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde was also conducted for 28 weeks old Aspa-knockout rats. Sections were cut 3 µm in thickness and
stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Cervical and thoracic spinal cords of 4, 8, and 12 weeks old Aspa-knockout and F344 rats were removed and fixed in 2.5%
glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PBS), postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide at room temperature overnight, and embedded in epoxy resin. Semi-thin (1 µm) sections were
cut and stained with toluidine blue.

Immunohistochemistry against NeuN, Calbindin, GFAP, Olig2, and Iba-1 was conducted on paraffin sections of Aspa-knockout and F344/NSlc rats at 24 weeks. Sections were cut 3
µm in thickness, dewaxed and incubated with 5% skimmed milk in PBS for 15 min and with primary antibodies for 1 h, followed by 1 h incubation with secondary antibody
(Histofine Simple Stain rat MAX-PO, Nichirei, Tokyo, Japan) (Supplementary Table 3). Immuno-reactions were visualized with
diaminobenzidine (Nichirei). Sections were lightly counterstained with hematoxylin and analyzed using a virtual slide scanner with image analysis software (VS-200 TruAI, Olympus, Tokyo,
Japan). Segmentation from hand-labeled virtual slide images of F344 rats based on its morphological features was used as labeled training data. The neural network learned the labeled
training data, and it applied the same methodology to the other images. Segmented images were analyzed to count negative and positive cells, and to identify quantitative morphological
changes. The area for image analysis was selected based on the distribution of lesions and the results of immunohistochemistry, where the morphological change was thought to be severe. The
numbers of Iba-1, NeuN, and Calbindin positive cells in the pons and cerebellar nucleus, thalamus, and cerebellar cortex (Purkinje cells) were determined in one control F344 rat, and the
intensity of signals in both control and Aspa-knockout rat groups were measured. As for Olig2 positive cells, the numbers of Olig2 positive cells of the pons and medulla
oblongata in a control were determined, and the intensity was measured to classify cells into strongly and weakly positive cells. As for GFAP positive cells, image analysis was not performed
because appropriate training data could not be obtained. Image analyzed data are shown as average values (mean ± SE), and no statistical analysis was performed.

None of the Aspa-knockout rats showed any neurological signs.

Aspa-knockout rats can live over 12 weeks without any neurological signs but severe vacuolation in the CNS similar to human CD and mouse CD models was observed [16, 17, 21]. Some Aspa-knockout mouse strains were reported to
die shortly after weaning and the others exhibited poor growth, ataxia, and macrocephaly [16, 22]. The mechanism
of species differences in survival and neurological signs between the rat and mouse Aspa-knockout strains remains unclear. These findings indicate that knockout of the
Aspa gene induces vacuolation in the CNS, but not neurological signs in Aspa-knockout rats. Previous reports showed that a single Aspa or
single Hcn1 mutation does not cause tremor, whereas double mutations in Aspa and Hcn1 do [20, 23]. Based on this, it was considered that the rats analyzed in this study may had a single Aspa mutation, which is why they did not develop
any neurological signs.

Histopathological examination showed vacuolation mainly located in the brainstem reticular formation, pons and medulla oblongata. Brainstem reticular formation functions to control breathing,
heartbeat and blood pressure, and access the gray matter of the spinal cord forming somatic motor cortex (pyramidal tract)–tractus reticulospinalis pathway. Vacuolation was also detected in
the somatic motor cortex (pyramidal tract)–lateral corticospinal and spinothalamic tract pathway, cerebellar nuclei, cerebellar granular and molecule layer, and piriform cortex and anterior
commissure. These results demonstrated that vacuolation in the CNS of Aspa-knockout rats mainly occurs in the brainstem reticular formation, ascending and descending motor
neuron pathway, and in the olfactory tract, and has no or minimal effect on neurological functions.

The myelin vacuolation was observed around the axons on toluidine blue-stained sections in the Aspa-knockout rats. The cause of myelin vacuolation in CD is still uncertain
[3]. Acetate is supplied by hydrolysis of NAA in oligodendrocytes for lipid synthesis, which is an important constituent of the myelin sheath [24,25,26,27]. ASPA is involved in the
hydrolysis of NAA, and decreased ASPA leads to high NAA accumulation and might cause myelin degeneration [28]. Although the accumulation of NAA in the
CNS was not examined in this study, increased urine NAA levels in the Aspa-knockout rats is reported [20]. These results suggest that
myelin vacuolation in the Aspa-knockout rats might be a change related to excessive NAA accumulation. However, no obvious change in the number of oligodendrocytes in the
Aspa-knockout rats was observed in immunohistochemical image analysis using the Olig2 antibody in areas where severe vacuolation was observed.

It is reported that NAA accumulation can be associated with neuronal dysfunction as well as neuronal death [29]. Analysis of immunohistochemical images
using NeuN and Calbindin antibodies revealed no obvious change in the number of neurons. As for immunohistochemistry against GFAP, histopathological examination resulted in increased
immunoactivity indicating activated astrocyte in the Aspa-knockout rats. Regarding Iba-1 staining, activation was observed histopathologically, but no obvious change was
observed in quantitative analysis using image analysis. Therefore, it was considered that microglia activity was not increased. It is reported that activated astrocytes are not only changes
associated with degeneration and necrosis of nerve cells, but also might be involved in the removal of extracellular NAA and the synthesis of NAA [30,31,32,33,34,35]. The increased immunoactivity of astrocytes observed in the Aspa-knockout rats might be related to NAA accumulation.

Mineralization was observed in the thalamus, pons, and medulla oblongata older than 24 weeks old Aspa-knockout rats. It is known that brain mineralization occurs
spontaneously in older rats, but it was not observed in 24 weeks old F344 rats, and it was observed frequently in the same region in Aspa-knockout rats aged older than 24
weeks [36]. Therefore, it was considered a strain-specific change. The cause and effect of mineralization against brain function in the
Aspa-knockout rat brain was also unclear, but similar pathological changes were reported in human patients with idiopathic basal ganglia calcification (IBGC) [37]. IBGC is a rare neurodegenerative disorder and associated genes are known. Mutation in the gene of SLC20A2 which encodes the type III
sodium-dependent inorganic phosphate transporter PiT2 is involved [37]. Patients with IBGC show various symptoms, ranging from none to Parkinson’s
disease like, and marked mineralization with glial reaction is also detected in the brainstem [37, 38]. The
Slc20a2-knockout mouse is reported as an IBGC model animal, and showed marked mineralization in the hypothalamus, thalamus, midbrain, and pons [39]. Brain mineralization in Aspa-knockout rats was similar to the human IBGC and mouse IBGC model, but the human IBGC and mouse IBGC model showed no
vacuolation in the brain. Further analysis would be necessary to characterize the pathophysiology of mineralization in the Aspa-knockout rats.

Human with CD exhibit neurological signs such as tremor, vacuolation in the white matter of the cerebrum, and myelin degeneration [1,2,3,4, 7,8,9, 13]. The Aspa-knockout rat showed neither neurological signs nor changes in neuron
number, but had some characteristics similar to CD such as neuropathological changes including severe vacuolation and abnormal myelination. Studies have been reported regarding the usefulness
of the Aspa-knockout mouse as a human CD model and for gene therapy with recombinant adeno-associated virus [19]. As for the application
of gene therapy for human CD, it showed no long-term adverse effects [14]. The Aspa-knockout rat is expected to have the potential to be
another human CD animal model and for use in gene therapy studies.
