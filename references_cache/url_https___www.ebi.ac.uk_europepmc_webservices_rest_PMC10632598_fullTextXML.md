The neoplasm is characterized by a specific chromosomal translocation, der (17) t(X; 17)(p11.2;q25), that results in <italic>ASPSCR1–TFE3</italic> gene fusion. The natural history of alveolar soft part sarcoma describes indolent behaviour with slow progression in deep soft tissues of the extremities, trunk and head/neck in adolescents and young adults. A high rate of detection of distant metastasis at presentation has been reported, and the most common metastatic sites in decreasing order of frequency are the lung, bone and brain. Complete surgical resection remains the standard treatment strategy, whereas radiotherapy is indicated for patients with inadequate surgical margins or unresectable tumours. Although alveolar soft part sarcoma is refractory to conventional doxorubicin-based chemotherapy, monotherapy or combination therapy using tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors have provided antitumor activity and emerged as new treatment strategies. This article provides an overview of the current understanding of this ultra-rare sarcoma and recent advancements in treatments according to the clinical stage of alveolar soft part sarcoma.</p><sec id="kwd-group1" sec-type="kwd-group" disp-level="2"><p><bold>Keywords:</bold> alveolar soft part sarcoma, surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy</p></sec></abstract><abstract id="abstract2" abstract-type="teaser"><p>Alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS), a newly defined ultra-rare sarcoma, is characterized by specific gene fusions including <italic>ASPSCR1–TFE3</italic>. Alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) is a very rare sarcoma of uncertain histogenesis that accounts for &lt;1% of all soft-tissue sarcomas and belongs to a newly defined category of ultra-rare sarcomas (<xref rid="ref3" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>). ASPS was originally described in 1952 as ‘malignant myoblastoma’ or ‘granular-cell myoblastoma’ and defined histologically as consisting of cell ‘nests’ loosely arranged along connective tissue containing sinusoidal vascular channels lined by flattened endothelium with characteristic intracytoplasmic rod-shaped crystals (<xref rid="ref4" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>). These nests separated by capillaries appeared to resemble lung alveoli, which is the origin of the name of this sarcoma (<xref rid="ref4" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="ref5" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>). ASPS is characterized by a specific translocation, der (17)t(X;17)(p11.2;q25), which results in <italic>ASPSCR1–TFE3</italic> gene fusion (<xref rid="ref6" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>). Although ASPS is refractory to conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy and radiotherapy, recent reports have discussed the possible benefits of targeted therapy, such as antiangiogenic drugs and immune-stimulating therapy (<xref rid="ref7" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>). This review article aimed to summarize the clinicopathological characteristics of ASPS and to update current views regarding its diagnosis and treatment.</p></sec><sec id="sec4" disp-level="1"><title>Epidemiology</title><p>ASPS is an ultra-rare sarcoma with an incidence rate of only one diagnosis per 10 million population per year, accounting for 0.2–0.9% of all soft-tissue sarcomas (<xref rid="ref8" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>). From the Bone and Soft Tissue Tumor Registry (BSTTR) database in Japan, diagnosis of ASPS was recorded in 51 (2.1%) of 2474 patients with soft-tissue sarcomas registered from 1985 to 1994 (<xref rid="ref9" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>). ASPS most commonly occurs in adolescents and young adults (age range, 15–39 years), with a slight predominance in females. In the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, the patients had a median age of 25 (range, 1–78) years, 72% were &lt;30 years old and 58% were females (<xref rid="ref2" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>,<xref rid="ref10" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="sec5" disp-level="1"><title>Clinical presentation</title><p>ASPS presents asymptomatically as a slow-growing tumour (<xref rid="ref4" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="ref11" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>) and commonly arises in deep soft tissues of the extremities (61%), trunk (20%), head and neck (9%), and internal organs (8%) (<xref rid="ref2" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>,<xref rid="ref10" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>). Uncommon sites include bone (<xref rid="ref12" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>,<xref rid="ref13" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>), brain (<xref rid="ref14" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>,<xref rid="ref15" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>), orbit (<xref rid="ref16" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>), tongue (<xref rid="ref17" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>), lung (<xref rid="ref18" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>), mediastinum (<xref rid="ref19" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="ref20" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>), bladder (<xref rid="ref21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>), prostate (<xref rid="ref22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>), uterus (<xref rid="ref23" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>) and vagina (<xref rid="ref24" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>). Because of the lack of associated symptoms or functional impairment, patients with ASPS often present with metastatic disease (<xref rid="ref4" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>). At presentation, 28% of patients have localized disease and 72% have metastatic disease in the BSTTR database (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>), which was similar to the finding in the National Cancer Data Base (NCDB) in the USA (<xref rid="ref26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>). The most common metastatic sites are the lung, bone, brain and liver (<xref rid="ref3" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>). In an analysis of the BSTTR database, 45% (<italic>n</italic> = 13/34) of patients with localized ASPS developed distant metastases in the lung (<italic>n</italic> = 12, 92%) and brain (<italic>n</italic> = 2, 15%), whereas the sites of metastasis in patients with stage 4 disease at diagnosis (<italic>n</italic> = 86) were lung (<italic>n</italic> = 85; 99%), bone (<italic>n</italic> = 12; 14%) and brain (<italic>n</italic> = 9; 11%) (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="sec6" disp-level="1"><title>Radiological features</title><p>On computed tomography (CT) imaging, ASPS is observed as an enhancing mass lesion with prominent feeding vessels (<xref rid="ref27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>). Prominent intratumoural blood vessels are observed on contrast-enhanced CT (<xref rid="ref28" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>). CT is also used to detect distant metastases to the brain, lung and bone for initial tumour staging. On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ASPS typically shows high signal intensity in T1- and T2-weighted imaging and features internal and external multilobulated signal changes (<xref rid="ref27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>). Crombé et al. analysed MRI features in 25 patients with ASPS and concluded that deep-seated tumours presenting with mainly high signal intensity on T1-weighted imaging, an absence of fibrotic component, ill-defined margins without aponeurotic extension, and more than five central and peripheral flow-voids are very likely to be ASPS (<xref rid="ref29" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>). The mass shows intense enhancement and multiple peritumoral and intratumoural tortuous signal voids on contrast-enhanced MR images (<xref rid="ref27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>) (<xref rid="f1" ref-type="fig">Fig. 1</xref>). ASPS may be misdiagnosed as haemangioma or arteriovenous malformation, which occur in the same age group (<xref rid="ref29" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>). ASPS comprises organoid nests outlined in sinusoidal vessels (<xref rid="f2" ref-type="fig">Fig. 2a</xref>) (<xref rid="ref29" ref-type="bibr">29–31</xref>). The tumour nests show central degeneration and loss of cellular cohesion, resulting in a characteristic pseudoalveolar pattern (<xref rid="f2" ref-type="fig">Fig. 2b</xref>) (<xref rid="ref32" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>,<xref rid="ref33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>). Tumour cells have a well-defined border, abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, round vesicular nucleus and prominent nucleolus (<xref rid="ref32" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>,<xref rid="ref33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>). Histochemically, PAS stain shows varying amounts of cytoplasmic glycogen and characteristic rod-shaped crystals (<xref rid="f2" ref-type="fig">Fig. 2c</xref>) (<xref rid="ref34" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>). This tumour is characterized by aberrations in <italic>TFE3</italic> genes, showing nuclear immunoreactivity for this protein product in most cases (<xref rid="f2" ref-type="fig">Fig. 2d</xref>) (<xref rid="ref33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>,<xref rid="ref35" ref-type="bibr">35</xref>,<xref rid="ref36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>).</p><fig id="f2" position="float"><?disp-level 2?><label>Figure 2</label><caption><p>Histopathological features of ASPS. Detection of this fusion transcript, <italic>ASPSCR1–TFE3</italic>, through real-time polymerase chain reaction or fluorescence <italic>in situ</italic> hybridization for <italic>TFE3</italic> rearrangements are considered useful methods for diagnosis (<xref rid="ref33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>,<xref rid="ref37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>). This fusion protein acts as an aberrant transcription factor resulting in activation of the MET signalling pathway believed to promote angiogenesis and cell proliferation (<xref rid="ref2" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>,<xref rid="ref33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>). Although the presence of <italic>ASPSCR1–TFE3</italic> fusion is highly specific to ASPS, the same gene fusion is also seen in a small but unique subset of renal cell carcinomas (<xref rid="ref2" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>). Recently, novel alternative rearrangements, including <italic>HNRNPH3–TFE3</italic>, <italic>DVL2–TFE3</italic> and <italic>PRCC–TFE3</italic> gene fusions, have been identified, highlighting genetic diversity in ASPS (<xref rid="ref3" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>,<xref rid="ref38" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="sec9" disp-level="1"><title>Natural history and prognostic factor</title><p>ASPS is characterized by its indolent behaviour with slow progression (<xref rid="ref3" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>). However, the metastatic potential appears to be greater for ASPS than for other soft-tissue sarcomas; patients often present with a metastatic stage at the time of diagnosis (<xref rid="ref37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="ref39" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>). The NCDB (USA) retrospective study included 293 patients ≥18 years who were diagnosed between 2004 and 2015, among whom 59% (<italic>n</italic> = 172/293) had a metastatic stage (<xref rid="ref26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>). Patients with head and neck tumours were least likely (40%) to present with distant disease (<xref rid="ref26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>). In BSTTR (Japan), 34 (28%) patients presented with localized disease (stage II, 13%; stage IIIA, 11%; stage IIIB, 4% by American Joint Committee on Cancer staging) at diagnosis and 86 (72%) with metastatic disease (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). Patients who were &gt;25 years old, had deep-seated tumours and tumours &gt;5 cm were more likely to have metastatic disease (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). The common metastatic sites are the lung, brain, bone and liver (<xref rid="ref3" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>). Of note, ASPS is characterized by higher rates of brain metastasis than other STSs. In the BSTTR study, distant metastases to the lung and brain developed in 12 (35%) and 2 (6%) of 34 patients with localized disease at diagnosis, respectively, and the sites of metastasis in 86 patients with metastatic disease at diagnosis were the lung in 85 patients (99%), bone in 12 (14%) and brain in 9 (11%) (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). Therefore, intracranial imaging should be added to routine imaging studies, as mentioned in the current clinical practice guidelines (<xref rid="ref1" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>,<xref rid="ref40" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>,<xref rid="ref41" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>).</p><p>The prognostic factors previously reported in the literature include age at presentation, tumour size, bone involvement and presence of metastasis at diagnosis (<xref rid="TB1" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>). For localized ASPS, the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate is 60–88%, which was reported by previous studies, excluding those with &lt;30 patients (<xref rid="TB1" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>). A survival rate of 60% reported in 1989 by Lieberman et al. for patients with localized disease appears to have improved slightly to 73–87% for that group. This might be because of improvements in surgical techniques; Hagerty et al. reported in the NCDB study that the margin status (positive/negative) was univariable associated with OS. For metastatic ASPS, the 5-year OS rate is 20–62%, as reported by previous studies, excluding those with &lt;30 patients (<xref rid="TB1" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>) (<xref rid="ref10" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>,<xref rid="ref26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>,<xref rid="ref42" ref-type="bibr">42–45</xref>). The 5-year OS rates reported before 2010 were 22% by Lieberman et al. (<xref rid="ref43" ref-type="bibr">43</xref>), 20% by Portea et al. (<xref rid="ref45" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>) and 46% by Ogose et al. (<xref rid="ref44" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>), whereas the rate was 61% by Flores et al. (<xref rid="ref42" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>), 46% by Hagerty et al. (<xref rid="ref26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>) and 62% by Fujiwara et al. (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>) (<xref rid="TB1" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>). The improvement in survival outcome might be because of the introduction of targeted therapy. In the BSTTR analysis, a comparison of survival outcomes before and after the approval of pazopanib was performed, and a trend toward superior disease-specific survival (DSS) was observed in patients who had a diagnosis and/or treatment for metastatic ASPS after 2012 (5-year DSS, 65%) than before 2012 (5-year DSS, 58%) when the clinical use of pazopanib was approved in Japan (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). The standard surgical procedure is complete resection with wide margins. In a multi-institutional, retrospective study from the Japanese Musculoskeletal Oncology Group (JMOG), Ogose et al. reported that the rates of local recurrence following surgery alone were 0% (<italic>n</italic> = 0/36) with wide margins, 57% (<italic>n</italic> = 4/7) with marginal margins and 100% (<italic>n</italic> = 1/1) with intralesional margins (<xref rid="ref44" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>). Complete resection may be curative in some patients, but metastases are common with long-term follow-up after resection of the primary tumour (<xref rid="ref37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>). In the BSTTR database study, the 5-year metastasis-free survival was 19% after wide resection for localized ASPS (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="sec12" disp-level="2"><title>Radiotherapy</title><p>There is no consensus on the current role of adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) because of the lack of evidence of improved local control and survival rates. In the SEER database analysis, Wang et al. reported that OS was better for surgery plus RT (<italic>n</italic> = 54) than for surgery alone (<italic>n</italic> = 64) in patients with localized ASPS (<xref rid="ref10" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>). Although patients with a larger tumour size (&gt;5 cm) were more likely to receive RT (<xref rid="ref10" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>), it was unclear if the indication of RT was determined on the basis of the surgical margins of the primary tumour. Casanova et al. suggested the use of RT in patients with inadequate surgical margins (<xref rid="ref46" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>), but they did not recommend the use of RT in all children to prevent delayed morbidity since local control is probably unnecessary if the tumour is adequately excised (<xref rid="ref46" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>). In the JMOG study, patients who underwent marginal excision and RT (<italic>n</italic> = 3) had no local recurrence, whereas local recurrence occurred in 4 (57%) of 7 patients who underwent marginal excision alone (<xref rid="ref44" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>). Patients who underwent wide excision (<italic>n</italic> = 36) and amputation with wide margins (<italic>n</italic> = 2) had no local recurrence (<xref rid="ref44" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>). Although these data suggest a clinical benefit for adjuvant RT with inadequate surgical margins, further investigation with a larger cohort of patients is necessary, which should be considered to assess the role of surgical margins and systemic treatments.</p><p>Definitive RT using carbon-ion RT may be one treatment option for unresectable ASPS. Nakao et al. reported a case of a 9-year-old girl with localized ASPS arising in the upper third of her vagina (<xref rid="ref47" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>). Carbon-ion RT with 67.2 Gy in 16 fractions was delivered to the residual tumour adhered to the posterior pubis after partial resection of the tumour, which decreased gradually in size without tumour recurrence over 20 months (<xref rid="ref47" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>). Okamoto et al. reported a case of a 24-year-old woman with unresectable ASPS arising in the right pelvic lesion and right lower leg (<xref rid="ref48" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>). Carbon-ion RT with 67.2 Gy in 16 fractions was delivered to the pelvic tumour followed by anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibody (pembrolizumab). Both the irradiated pelvic tumour and nonirradiated leg tumour decreased remarkably in size (80%), which was confirmed on the MRI taken 10 months after carbon-ion RT (<xref rid="ref48" ref-type="bibr">48</xref>). Long-term efficacy of carbon-ion RT is awaited.</p></sec><sec id="sec13" disp-level="2"><title>Chemotherapy</title><p>The efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy on ASPS reportedly has been ineffective to date. Conventional anthracycline-based chemotherapy is largely inactive, with response criteria in solid RECIST rates &lt;10% (<xref rid="ref37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="ref45" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>,<xref rid="ref49" ref-type="bibr">49</xref>,<xref rid="ref50" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>). In the JMOG study by Ogose et al., 21 patients with primary tumours underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy; none of 14 patients who received systemic chemotherapy based on doxorubicin-, ifosfamide- and cisplatin-based regimens showed a clinical response of stable disease (SD, <italic>n</italic> = 12) or progressive disease (PD, <italic>n</italic> = 2), and 5 (71%) of 7 patients who underwent intra-arterial chemotherapy mainly with cisplatin showed no clinical response (<xref rid="ref44" ref-type="bibr">44</xref>). In the EpSSG NRSTS 2005 study, 4 of 22 patients received chemotherapy with ifosfamide and doxorubicin, but there were no clinical responses (<xref rid="ref49" ref-type="bibr">49</xref>). In a review of the published literature by Reichardt et al., the response to first-line chemotherapy (anthracycline alone, anthracycline plus ifosfamide and others) in 68 patients was PD in 51%, SD in 41%, partial response (PR) in 3% and complete response (CR) in 4% (<xref rid="ref50" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>). Although these studies involved small series and it is necessary to collect more data from systematic analyses and clinical trials, standard regimens of cytotoxic chemotherapy agents as adjuvants appear to have no clinical benefit.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec14" disp-level="1"><title>The management of advanced/metastatic ASPS</title><sec id="sec15" disp-level="2"><title>Surgery</title><p>In patients with advanced/metastatic ASPS, the effect of surgery on the primary lesion is controversial because of conflicting results. In the BSTTR database study, surgical resection of the primary site did not affect DSS; the 5-year DSS for the surgery (<italic>n</italic> = 57) and no-surgery (<italic>n</italic> = 29) subgroups were 68% and 51%, respectively (<italic>p</italic> = 0.559) (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). Contrarily, in the SEER database study by Wang et al., survival was significantly better for patients who underwent surgery for the primary lesion than for those without surgery; the 5-year OS rates for the surgery (<italic>n</italic> = 61) and no-surgery (<italic>n</italic> = 44) subgroups were 41% and 10%, respectively (<italic>p</italic> &lt; 0.001) (<xref rid="ref10" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>). Of note, in the NCDB study by Hagerty et al., patients with metastatic ASPS who underwent surgical resection of the primary tumour had longer OS (median OS: 48 months) than an identically selected population of patients with common histological sarcoma subtypes, including synovial sarcoma (median OS: 21 months), liposarcoma (median OS: 18 months), rhabdomyosarcoma (median OS: 11 months) and desmoplastic small round cell tumour (median OS: 28 months) (<xref rid="ref26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>). Since these survival outcomes differ among the databases in Japan and the USA, further prospective analysis with international collaborations would clarify the effect of surgical resection of the primary site in patients with advanced/metastatic ASPS.</p><p>Resectable metachronous (disease-free interval ≥ 1 year) lung metastases of soft-tissue sarcoma without extrapulmonary disease are managed with metastasectomy as standard treatment (<xref rid="ref1" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>,<xref rid="ref51" ref-type="bibr">51</xref>). However, studies investigating the effect of metastasectomy of ASPS are limited, probably because the incidence of oligometastatic ASPS is rare. In the BSTTR database study, the surgical resection of the metastatic site did not affect survival outcome: the 5-year DSS rates in patients with (<italic>n</italic> = 11) and without (<italic>n</italic> = 75) metastasectomy were 67% and 62%, respectively (<italic>P</italic> = 0.143) (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). Zhang et al. investigated 1184 patients with STS having metastasis at diagnosis and reported that surgery for metastasis was an independent factor associated with better survival (<xref rid="ref52" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>). Although this was confirmed for a group of seven common histological subtypes (undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, myxoid liposarcoma, ASPS, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour and dedifferentiated liposarcoma), the survival benefit of metastasectomy in patients with ASPS remained unclear (<xref rid="ref52" ref-type="bibr">52</xref>). Kodama et al. reported the clinical course of 4 patients who underwent aggressive excision of multiple metastases from ASPS. These patients underwent surgery of the primary tumour, followed by 8 pulmonary surgeries that excised 333 metastatic tumours (<xref rid="ref53" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>). Although 3 of 4 patients died of tumour progression 40, 46 and 68 months after surgery of the primary tumour, 1 patient had been alive for 98 months after excision of the primary lesion (<xref rid="ref53" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>). Of note, this was reported in 1997 when targeted therapy was not available, and the decision-making for metastasectomy should be multidisciplinary considering the accumulating evidence of currently available systemic agents.</p></sec><sec id="sec16" disp-level="2"><title>Radiotherapy</title><p>For the brain metastasis of ASPS, gamma-knife radiosurgery has been a reasonable option for local control. Flannery et al. reported satisfactory results in 21 patients who underwent gamma-knife stereotactic radiosurgery for intracranial sarcomatous metastases, including ASPS (<italic>n</italic> = 2) (<xref rid="ref55" ref-type="bibr">55</xref>). The local control rate was 88%, the median survival after diagnosis was 16 months and the 1-year survival rate was 61% (<xref rid="ref55" ref-type="bibr">55</xref>). Ogura et al. described satisfactory local control in 4 patients with brain metastasis of ASPS who underwent gamma-knife radiosurgery, with a median progression-free survival of 12 (range, 9–30) months (<xref rid="ref56" ref-type="bibr">56</xref>). Lim et al. suggested the use of gamma-knife stereotactic radiosurgery with a single-dose ≥25 Gy for all brain metastases of ASPS. For large (&gt;1.5 cm<sup>3</sup>) brain metastases of ASPS, all tumours treated with a low dose (&lt;25 Gy) recurred, requiring surgical removal within 2 months following stereostatic radiosurgery, whereas the large tumour treated with a high dose (≥25 Gy) recurred after 11 months (<xref rid="ref57" ref-type="bibr">57</xref>). For small (≤0.5 cm<sup>3</sup>) brain metastases of ASPS, 5 of 6 tumours treated with high doses ≥25 Gy were controlled, whereas the remaining tumour required additional treatment (<xref rid="ref57" ref-type="bibr">57</xref>). Palliative whole-brain RT has been administered for several cases with multiple brain metastases, but the prognoses of these patients have been poor (<xref rid="ref55" ref-type="bibr">55</xref>,<xref rid="ref58" ref-type="bibr">58</xref>).</p><p>For the lung metastasis of ASPS, whole-lung irradiation is generally not performed as for other subtypes of STS except Ewing sarcoma. Strategies combining metastasectomy and RT may be used; a case report indicated the use of hyperfractioned local RT with a total dose of 44.8 Gy (2 × 1.6 Gy daily) following pulmonary metastasectomy (<xref rid="ref59" ref-type="bibr">59</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="sec17" disp-level="2"><title>Chemotherapy</title><p>The standard treatment for patients with advanced/metastatic soft-tissue sarcomas is systemic chemotherapy with doxorubicin. For advanced/metastatic ASPS, however, previous studies have shown limited efficacy of doxorubicin-based chemotherapy. In a series from MD Anderson Cancer Center, 26 patients with metastatic ASPS at diagnosis were treated with systemic chemotherapy; doxorubicin-based chemotherapy was used in 17 (65%) of 26 patients (median, 4 cycles) (<xref rid="ref45" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>). The majority of patients treated with chemotherapy (58%) developed disease progression and no partial or minor responses were noted (<xref rid="ref45" ref-type="bibr">45</xref>). In the BSTTR analysis, administration of systemic therapy (conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, 29%; targeted therapy, 40%; conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy + targeted therapy, 23%; others, 8%) did not affect survival outcomes in patients with metastatic ASPS (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). Among these patients, patients who received doxorubicin-based regimens had significantly inferior DSS; the 5-year DSS rates were 39% and 75% in patients with and without doxorubicin-based chemotherapy regimens, respectively (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). Patients who did not receive doxorubicin-based regimens were mostly treated with targeted agents (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). To date, pazopanib and sunitinib have been recommended as preferred regimens for ASPS.</p><p>Pazopanib is a small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) exhibiting a selective activity against VEGF receptors (<xref rid="ref60" ref-type="bibr">60</xref>). In a phase III study of metastatic STS (PALETTE study), the median progression-free survival (PFS) in patients receiving pazopanib was improved to 4.6 months compared with 1.6 months in those receiving placebo (<xref rid="ref61" ref-type="bibr">61</xref>). For metastatic ASPS, a phase II study demonstrated that 1 of 6 patients enrolled achieved a PR, and 5 showed SD (<xref rid="ref62" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>). The median PFS was 5.5 months, and the 6-month PFS rate was 50% (<xref rid="ref62" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>). A retrospective study using the BSTTR database demonstrated that the median survival period in patients who received systemic therapy, including pazopanib, was 70 months (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). A trend towards improved survival was observed after 2012 when pazopanib was approved for metastatic STS in Japan (<xref rid="ref25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>). The mechanisms underlying the response to pazopanib in ASPS remain under evaluation. The antiangiogenic effect of pazopanib may target the peculiar vasculature of ASPS, sustained by the translocation-related activation of the lactate pathway in the tumour microenvironment (<xref rid="ref63" ref-type="bibr">63</xref>,<xref rid="ref64" ref-type="bibr">64</xref>). Kim et al. analysed the transcriptome of ASPS before and after pazopanib treatment; the top differentially expressed genes were related to angiogenesis and signalling pathways such as mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and wingless-type MMTV integration site family (WNT) (<xref rid="ref62" ref-type="bibr">62</xref>). These data indicated that pazopanib may modulate multiple signalling pathways in a simultaneous manner in ASPS.</p><p>Sunitinib has also shown promising efficacy for metastatic ASPS, although this agent is not approved for STSs in Japan. As a summary of multiple studies, administration of sunitinib demonstrated PR in 19 patients and SD in ≤24 of 46 evaluable patients (<xref rid="ref37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="ref54" ref-type="bibr">54</xref>,<xref rid="ref65" ref-type="bibr">65–68</xref>). In addition, neoadjuvant use of sunitinib for primarily unresectable ASPS provided a change of complete surgical resection (<xref rid="ref67" ref-type="bibr">67</xref>).</p><p>Other targeted drugs, including cediranib (<xref rid="ref69" ref-type="bibr">69–72</xref>), crizotinib (<xref rid="ref73" ref-type="bibr">73</xref>,<xref rid="ref74" ref-type="bibr">74</xref>), cabozantinib (<xref rid="ref75" ref-type="bibr">75</xref>), axitinib (<xref rid="ref76" ref-type="bibr">76</xref>), bevacizumab (<xref rid="ref77" ref-type="bibr">77</xref>,<xref rid="ref78" ref-type="bibr">78</xref>), sorafenib (<xref rid="ref79" ref-type="bibr">79</xref>), dasatinib (<xref rid="ref80" ref-type="bibr">80</xref>) and tivantinib (<xref rid="ref81" ref-type="bibr">81</xref>), have also shown therapeutic advantages in patients with advanced/metastatic ASPS (<xref rid="TB2" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>).</p><p>Trabectedin binding to the minor groove of DNA and blocking of DNA-repair machinery has also been approved for advanced/metastatic STSs. This agent was proven to be effective in translocation-related sarcomas (<xref rid="ref82" ref-type="bibr">82</xref>). Stacchiotti et al. retrospectively reviewed the efficacy of trabectedin in 23 patients with ASPS, which revealed limited activity; 1 PR, 13 SDs and 9 PDs were observed, with a median PFS of 3.7 months and a median OS of 9.1 months (<xref rid="ref64" ref-type="bibr">64</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="sec20" disp-level="2"><title>Immunotherapy</title><p>Immunotherapy is a promising area of drug development for ASPS. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as anti-PD-1, anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) have been tested in patients with ASPS, although the number of patients has been limited (<xref rid="TB2" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>).</p><p>In the NCCN guideline, pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1 inhibitor) is recommended for treating ASPS. This recommendation is based on the results of the retrospective study by Grossberg et al., which studied 50 patients with advanced sarcoma who were referred to the phase I clinic at the MD Anderson Cancer Center (USA) and received immunotherapy (<xref rid="ref83" ref-type="bibr">83</xref>). Among these, all 4 patients with ASPS showed a clinical benefit from use of ICIs; 2 patients had PR bordering CR lasting 8 and 12 months, and 2 achieved SD (<xref rid="ref83" ref-type="bibr">83</xref>).</p><p>The efficacy of nivolumab (anti-PD-1 inhibitor) was reported by Kuo et al. and Paoluzzi et al.; one patient had PD after 3 months (<xref rid="ref84" ref-type="bibr">84</xref>) and another had a slight PD after 9 cycles (<xref rid="ref37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>). However, nivolumab + ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4 inhibitor) resulted in PR in a 29-year-old man with metastatic ASPS (<xref rid="ref85" ref-type="bibr">85</xref>).</p><p>On December 2022, the US Food and Drug Administration approved atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1 inhibitor) for adult and paediatric patients ≥2 years old with unresectable or metastatic ASPS. This agent was reportedly effective in a 13-year-old girl with multiple brain metastases; the radiological studies suggested PD after 6 doses (15 mg/kg IV every 21 days), but pathological evaluation revealed a nonviable tumour in the resected specimens, which suggested that the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria may be unsuitable for evaluating ICI efficacy (<xref rid="ref86" ref-type="bibr">86</xref>).</p><p>Recently, promising results of a phase II trial of durvalumab (anti-PD-L1 inhibitor) + tremelimumab (anti-CTLA-4 inhibitor) were reported; the overall response rates in 10 patients with ASPS by immune-related RECIST and immune-related response criteria were 40% and 50%, respectively, with 2 patients reported to have a CR (<xref rid="ref87" ref-type="bibr">87</xref>). Somaiah et al. confirmed pseudoprogression in a few patients in this clinical trial, which was most prominent in patients with ASPS, highlighting the need for a longer duration of therapy and confirmatory scans (<xref rid="ref87" ref-type="bibr">87</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="sec21" disp-level="2"><title>Combined therapy</title><p>In addition to ICI combination therapy, several clinical trials using the combined regimen of a targeted agent and an ICI have shown promising results. In a phase Ib/II trial of sunitinib (multitargeted receptor TKI) + nivolumab (anti-PD-1 inhibitor), 4 (57%) of 7 patients with ASPS had a PR (<xref rid="ref88" ref-type="bibr">88</xref>). In a phase II trial of axitinib (anti-VEGF receptor TKI) + pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1 inhibitor) in patients with advanced sarcomas, 6 of 11 patients with ASPS achieved a PR (54.5%) and 2 of 11 (18%) achieved SD; the proportion of patients who achieved a clinical benefit was 72.7% (<italic>n</italic> = 8/11) (<xref rid="ref76" ref-type="bibr">76</xref>). The median time to PR in patients was 25.1 weeks (<xref rid="ref76" ref-type="bibr">76</xref>). Therefore, a combination of VEGF and PD-1 blockade appears feasible and promising in patients with advanced/metastatic ASPS (<xref rid="ref76" ref-type="bibr">76</xref>).</p><p>Ongoing clinical trials are exploring the efficacy and safety of targeted agents (such as bevacizumab and selinexor, an XPO1 inhibitor) and ICIs (such as atezolizumab and nivolumab) given alone or in combinations of these agents (<xref rid="TB3" ref-type="table">Table 3</xref>). A relatively higher rate of metastasis to the brain means that clinicians should include intracranial imaging in their routine imaging studies. The overall cure rate remains unsatisfactory because of the greater metastatic potential of ASPS than of other STSs. However, an overall trend towards improved survival in patients with advanced/metastatic ASPS after introducing TKIs and ICIs supports continuing efforts to develop novel therapeutic options. Given the indolent behaviour of the tumour, clinical trials of the combination of targeted therapy and immunotherapy potentially can provide evidence that these treatments can further prolong survival in patients with this type of ultra-rare sarcoma.</p></sec><sec id="sec23" disp-level="1"><title>Funding</title><p>This study was supported by JSPS KAKENHI Grant Numbers 21K16709 and 22H03202.</p></sec><sec id="sec24" disp-level="1"><title>Conflict of interest statement</title><p>None declared.</p></sec><sec id="_ci93_" xml:lang="en" sec-type="contrib-info" disp-level="1"><title>Contributor Information</title><p>Tomohiro Fujiwara,
