Retrograde upper esophageal sphincter function… and dysfunction.

Kahrilas PJ(1).

Author information:
(1)Department of Medicine, Feinberg School of Medicine, Northwestern University, 
Chicago, Illinois, USA.

Upper esophageal sphincter (UES) function has been extensively studied in the 
context of swallowing and antegrade bolus transit, but relatively little 
attention has been paid to its retrograde function to facilitate belching. 
Indeed, prior to 2019, there were only three case reports in the world 
literature of individuals with a physiologically demonstrated inability to belch 
due to failure of the UES to relax after gas venting from the stomach and 
ensuing low-pressure esophageal distention. In this minireview, that disorder is 
termed retrograde UES dysfunction (R-UESD). The associated symptom complex 
includes some combination of chest pain, gurgling noises in the chest, bloating, 
nausea, hiccups, and flatulence. The obscurity of R-UESD has recently been 
challenged by two large series published in the ENT literature of 
circumstantially demonstrated R-UESD that was resolved by botulinum toxin (BT) 
injection to the cricopharyngeus. The field has now further advanced with the 
publication in this issue of the Journal by Oude Nijhuis et al. of 8 cases of 
R-UESD demonstrated by high-resolution impedance manometry (HRIM) with a 
provocative challenge of carbonated water. These individuals were also treated 
with BT and at 3-month follow-up had both an encouraging symptomatic response 
and HRIM demonstrated resolution of R-UESD.

Clinical esophageal manometry has substantially evolved since the widespread adoption of high-resolution platforms and color topography plots. Appropriately, most of this evolution has focused on esophageal motility disorders, especially achalasia and its various subtypes. However, high-resolution manometry (HRM) and, by extension, high-resolution impedance manometry (HRIM) are powerful technologies well suited not only for defining the contractility of the distal esophagus but also to interrogate the contractility of the pharynx, upper esophageal sphincter (UES), and proximal esophagus. Additionally, the application of topographic plotting and the addition of impedance sensors make it possible to detect the antegrade or retrograde movement of fluid and air through the esophagus and across its sphincters. An excellent clinical application of this technology appears in this issue of the Journal describing a case series of 8 individuals with long histories of an inability to belch who were shown to suffer from “partial belching” with air escaping the stomach but becoming trapped within the esophagus on account of failed UES relaxation in response to esophageal gaseous distention.1 Associated symptoms included chest pain, gurgling noises within the chest, bloating, nausea, hiccups, and flatulence. This dysfunction was subsequently treated with botulinum toxin injection to the cricopharyngeus which both restored the ability to belch and provided symptom relief. Both the impaired belch reflex and its post-treatment recovery were demonstrated with HRIM and provocative testing using carbonated water. For the purposes of this minireview, this disorder will be branded retrograde UES dysfunction (R-UESD).

The UES is an unusual structure with both muscular and cartilaginous components. Its principle contractile component is the cricopharyngeus muscle which inserts to the lateral aspects of the cricoid cartilage in a C-shaped configuration with the plate of the cricoid cartilage forming the anterior wall of the UES and closing the C. The cricopharyngeus is striated muscle but contains about 40% connective tissue, much of which is elastic2 such that it has a passive residual tone even when devoid of neural excitation and that tone is augmented as it is “stretched” open.3 Unlike other sphincters that have a constant tone generated either by neural input or by intrinsic contractility of the muscle, the UES does not exhibit a constant neurogenic or myogenic tone; cricopharyngeal electromyography of chronically instrumented awake animals exhibit periods of very low activity when the animal is calm and resting its head.4 Similarly in awake humans undergoing prolonged UES recordings with a miniature Dent sleeve manometric catheter, UES pressure during long inter- swallow intervals was sufficiently low (6– 18 mmHg) to suggest that it was mainly attributable to the elastic properties of the cricopharyngeus rather than active contraction.5

Adding to the complexity of UES physiology is the multiplicity of its innervation and extreme responsiveness to a wide array of stimuli. Innervation is through the pharyngeal plexus supplied by three nerves: multiple branches of the vagus, the glossopharyngeal, and sympathetics through the cranial cervical ganglion.3 This complex innervation partially explains how the UES participates in so many reflexive responses and contracts to such an array of stimuli from the pharynx, esophagus, airways, and CNS (Table 1). Taken together, these observations suggest that most manometrically recorded UES tone is attributable to various reflex responses stimulated by the recording technique itself. Devoid of these extrinsic influences UES tone falls to very low values as seen during anesthesia6 or sleep.7 It is also worth noting that UES contraction and to a lesser degree relaxation are subject to volitional control as demonstrated with HRM- driven biofeedback.8

UES relaxation is not synonymous with UES opening opening. For opening to occur, one of two conditions needs to be met: Either the luminal pressure above or below the sphincter needs to exceed its residual tone or the anterior wall of the sphincter (the cricoid cartilage suspended by the hyolaryngeal strap muscles) needs to be actively pulled forward. The latter mechanism is clearly at play during swallowing wherein both laryngeal airway closure and UES opening occur through the coordinated contraction pattern of the pharyngeal swallow.9,10 UES opening also occurs during belching11 and vomiting4 when its residual relaxation tone is overcome by intra-esophageal pressure; belching being of particular interest with respect to the work of Oude Nijhuis, et al published in this issue of the Journal.1

The UES belch reflex was initially described in normal volunteer subjects in experiments using a custom-built UES sleeve manometric assembly and gaseous distention of the stomach.11 Belching was observed to occur by the following sequence of events: (1) transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxation (tLESR); (2) gastroesophageal gas reflux, manometrically evident as a gastroesophageal common cavity phenomenon and pressurization of the esophagus to intra-gastric pressure; (3) complete UES relaxation to intra-esophageal pressure; (4) esophago-pharyngeal gas reflux; and (5) restoration of intra-esophageal pressure to baseline by a peristaltic contraction, usually secondary peristalsis. Further experimentation in which boluses of air were abruptly injected into the mid-esophagus established that it was the gaseous distention of the esophagus that triggered UES relaxation rather than it being a “programmed” component of a tLESR.11,12 The larger the volume of air injected, the greater the likelihood of eliciting UES relaxation and the more prolonged the period of relaxation. With 5 ml injections <20% UES relaxations averaging 0.34s; with 30 ml injections >80% UES relaxations averaging 0.8s. In the same experiments, injecting saline into the mid-esophagus did not elicit UES relaxation, but rather, caused either no change in UES pressure or UES contraction. Slowly injecting air to replicate the rate at which saline could be injected had an inconsistent effect. Most commonly, the slowly injected air boluses did not affect UES pressure, but in four instances, the slow injection of a 30-ml gas bolus elicited UES relaxation.

The relationship between tLESRs and gastric venting was further explored in another set of experiments on normal volunteer subjects using HRM, a distal esophageal pH electrode, and a high- fat test meal.13 Subjects were monitored for a 2-h postprandial period in a sitting posture. More than 200 tLESRs were observed in these experiments and 79% of these were associated with brief periods of UES relaxation, usually after gas reflux as evident by a gastroesophageal common cavity. These UES relaxations were often very short and not associated with audible burps leading the investigators to call them “microburps.” The pattern of UES response was no different during tLESRs with or without acid reflux as detected by the distal esophageal pH electrode. As for timing, on average microburps occurred about 0.4s after gas reflux. However, this was widely variable ranging from 1.3 s after proximal esophageal pressurization to 0.3s before proximal esophageal pressurization. In other words, in some instances UES relaxation occurred in anticipation of gas reflux to the proximal esophagus suggesting some degree of volitional control.

To summarize observations made in normal individuals regarding UES relaxation during belching: (1) UES relaxation is a component of the normal physiology of belching, (2) UES relaxation occurs with a substantial fraction of postprandial tLESRs implying that gas venting of the stomach is the physiological “purpose” of tLESRs, (3) UES relaxation is elicited by abrupt gaseous distention of the proximal esophagus, (4) the threshold gas volume for triggering UES relaxation varies widely among individuals, (5) when threshold parameters for eliciting UES relaxation are not achieved, UES contraction and/or secondary peristalsis are often observed, (6) the duration of UES relaxation during belching is proportional to the volume of esophageal gaseous distention, and (7) the latency of UES relaxation relative to gaseous distention of the proximal esophagus is highly variable both within individuals and among individuals implying that this involves a complex neural pathway.

The first report of dysfunction of the belch reflex (R- UESD) came shortly after the description of esophageal distention- induced UES relaxation as a component of belch physiology.14 The case was of a young woman troubled by a lifelong inability to regurgitate, vomit or belch, gurgling noises in her chest, and severe episodic chest pains. She was studied both fluoroscopically with a meal challenge and with the same custom- built UES sleeve manometric assembly mentioned above. Manometrically, she had normal UES function with swallowing, but it was not possible to elicit UES relaxation with repeated 30-ml air injections into the mid-esophagus. Rather, she exhibited only partial UES relaxation and no instance of gas venting. Eventually, after injecting a total of 210 ml of air, she began having spontaneous gastroesophageal gas reflux events. These caused common cavity pressures of 10–15 mmHg in the esophagus but failed to trigger complete UES relaxation and no esophago-pharyngeal gas venting occurred. Rather, gas was repeatedly cleared from the esophageal body by secondary peristalsis and then immediately refluxed back into the esophageal body. The reflux events were accompanied by an audible gurgling sound that the patient identified as the noise prompting her to seek our attention. She was then studied fluoroscopically and was noted to have a large gastric air bubble that persisted for 2 h after the test meal. When this was further expanded by the oral administration of gas-producing crystals, spontaneous gastroesophageal gas reflux was triggered. However, this did not result in esophago-pharyngeal gas venting. Rather, there was profound esophageal distention, distress, and then secondary peristalsis that swept the gas back into the stomach. During the manometric study, an oscillating system of gastroesophageal gas reflux followed by secondary peristalsis followed by repeated reflux was evident (Figure 1). Gurgling noises were clearly associated with gastroesophageal gas reflux. She was then persuaded to pass a nasogastric tube, resulting in gagging and a loud belch that immediately relieved both her pain and emptied the gas bubble from the gastric fundus.

Two years after the initial report, a second case report appeared of a 21-year-old law student also with a lifelong inability to belch or vomit presenting with chest pain and manometrically shown to have incomplete UES relaxation in response to esophageal air injections.15 In neither of these cases did the patient respond to reflux- directed treatment and in neither case were they open to the option of inducing belching by passing nasogastric tubes. A third case of R- UESD was described in 2001.16 This was of a 22-year-old man with a lifelong inability to belch resulting in “unendurable bloating” to the point that he would digitally stimulate his pharynx to induce vomiting (which he apparently was capable of). This man was studied manometrically with concurrent recordings from both UES and LES Dent sleeve assemblies. When challenged with air bolus injections distal to the UES, he exhibited only UES contraction and secondary peristalsis, but no UES relaxation and no gas venting.

The next chapter in the story of R-UESD involves the description of botulinum toxin (BT) injection to the cricopharyngeus as a treatment, an innovation attributable to Dr Robert Bastian.17 Prior to that, BT had been used in an uncontrolled fashion with mixed results to treat a variety of dysphagic conditions attributable to UES hypertonicity, including post-laryngectomy dysphagia, post- CVA dysphagia, myositis, and isolated cricopharyngeal spasm.18 Bastian described a patient he encountered in 2015 with a constellation of symptoms (inability to belch, bloating, gurgling, flatulence) who he reasoned must have “retrograde cricopharyngeus dysfunction” and successfully treated him with a 50-unit BT injection to the cricopharyngeus. The diagnosis was made based on the symptom complex alone; there were no confirmatory physiological studies. That patient apparently posted his miraculously successful outcome on the Internet site “Reddit.” That posting resulted in numerous self-diagnosed patients subsequently presenting to Dr Bastian for BT treatment. Indeed, as testimonial to the power of the Internet (and the subjective efficacy of BT for presumed R-UESD), by the time of his initial 2019 report, Dr Bastian had treated 121 such self-identified patients. This amounts to 40× the number of prior cases in the world literature. His report was limited to the first 51 of these individuals coming from 20 US states and 3 foreign countries who were at least 6 months post- treatment. Fifty of 51 patients were reportedly relieved of their abdominal bloating, and all but 4 were relieved of their gurgling noises and excessive flatulence. The only adverse effect reported was of dysphagia to solid food beginning the second or third day after the injection. This problem was successfully managed with diet modification.

Following the Bastian report, a second large series of “abelchia” was reported out of London.19 Very similar to the Bastian series, Karagama describes a case series of 72 patients with an inability to burp, retrosternal pain after eating or drinking, bloating, gurgling noises, and excessive flatulence. As with the Bastian series, the diagnosis was made based on the symptom complex alone; there were no confirmatory physiological studies. These patients were treated over a 4-year period with of BT injected laryngoscopically into the cricopharyngeus; initially, the author used 50 units of BT but eventually concluded that 100 units was the optimal dose. All patients were able to burp within the first 4 weeks of the injection, and this persisted beyond 3 months in 96% of cases.

As described above, the accumulating ENT literature strongly supports the existence of R-UESD as an important, albeit rare, clinical entity and BT as an effective treatment for it. However, there is an obvious need for an objective way of diagnosing this disorder and monitoring its response to treatment. The symptoms of bloating, flatulence, chest pain, gurgling, and an inability to burp are very common with functional GI disorders, most of which are unlikely to respond to BT injection to the cricopharyngeus. Illustrative of this, since 2020, this author has encountered two patients treated with BT for presumptive R-UESD who experienced no benefit from that therapy and, in fact, developed dysphagia for solid food as a result of it. In both cases, the dysphagia resolved after about a year, an interval consistent with the expected recovery of function after a BT injection. The pattern of dysphagia described by these individuals was consistent with loss of the “grabbing effect” wherein the UES reflexively contracts during and after passage of the pharyngeal contraction in conjunction with laryngeal descent after swallowing to assure transfer of luminal contents from the level of the laryngeal vestibule to a position 2–3.5 cm below it thereby facilitating hypopharyngeal clearance and providing protection against aspiration when the airway reopens at the conclusion of the pharyngeal swallow.20 The magnitude of this clearing traction force attributable to UES contraction was measured in normal volunteers with a Teflon ball attached to a force transducer. The traction force acting on the ball suspended within the UES was 12–28× the traction force observed with the ball suspended above it in the oropharynx or hypopharynx or beneath it in the cervical esophagus. With loss of this reflexive grabbing effect, individuals would be vulnerable to having post-swallow bolus residue at the laryngeal inlet and exhibit post-swallow aspiration and cough necessitating compensatory eating behaviors as described by Bastian.17

Given this combination of the rarity of R-UESD, the non-specific nature of the associated symptoms, and the potential morbidity associated with misdirected BT treatments, it is welcome news that there is a relatively simple confirmatory test for R-UESD as described by Oude Nijhuis et al. These authors have carefully studied 8 young patients encountered over a two-year period with chest pain, gurgling noises, and/or bloating at least three times a week, in combination with a self-reported inability to belch. All patients were studied with stationary HRIM incorporating a belch provocation test with 500 ml of carbonated water and by an ambulatory 24-h pH-impedance study. This was followed by a 50-unit BT injection to the UES. They were then again studied with the HRIM protocol and ambulatory pH-impedance study three months after treatment. Symptoms and health- related quality of life were evaluated before and after treatment. The major findings of the study were as follows: (1) that R- UESD was demonstrated in all 8 patients by the provocative HRIM study using carbonated water prior to BT treatment, (2) that no normal belching was demonstrable during the ambulatory 24-h pH-impedance study, (3) BT injection to the UES reduced UES basal pressure and restored belching ability in all subjects on follow up provocative HRIM, and (4) esophageal symptoms improved in all subjects. Of note, although all patients eventually exhibited UES relaxation during provocative HRIM studies and were able to belch audibly, 3 of the 8 subjects needed to be taught additional maneuvers (abdominal contraction, head turning) to force out air.

Over the years, this author has encountered several patients with self-diagnosed R-UESD, usually following Internet searches that unearthed the initial case report from 1987.14 However, the number of such patients has increased substantially since the publication by Bastian and Smithson in 201917 and is likely to further increase now with the publication by Oude Nijhuis et al.1 The word is spreading among the afflicted, first through Reddit as noted by Bastian and more recently on TikToc. Of course, not all these self-diagnosed individuals will prove to have R-UESD making it so timely that we now have a diagnostic test to both verify the dysfunction and to monitor response to BT treatment.

So, I will end this story with another case history, the final chapter of which has yet to be written. In the fall of 2021, a self-referred 25-year-old patient came to see me reporting that she was unable to belch, experienced strange gurgling noises in her chest, and had lifelong severe bloating. She had had consulted with several physicians about it over the years with no satisfying outcome. However, she had seen a TikToc video of another young woman with “exactly the same problem” whose problem was solved by a BT injection in Chicago (www.tiktok.com/@brittcurls/video/6906340552904609029?lang=en). Astonishingly, “brittcurls” identifies herself as one of an over 14,000 such persons in an on-line forum. In any event, we did an HRM study with carbonated water provocation and demonstrated paradoxical UES contraction in response to gas venting as described by Oude Nijhuis et al (Figure 2), findings that we took to be diagnostic of R-UESD. She then underwent a 100-unit BT injection to the CP. However, at 6-week follow-up, she did not have a miraculous response. She still had the gurgling noises, she reported only belching a little, and she was experiencing some dysphagia… but her bloating was gone. So, the plan (Covid permitting) is to now restudy her with HRM and carbonated water provocation to see whether she has had a partial response, perhaps now needing some “belch training” similar to the 3/8 patients described by Oude Nijhuis et al.
