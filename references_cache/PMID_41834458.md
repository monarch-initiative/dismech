Severe obesity is positively associated with the risk of multiple myeloma and 
mortality; thus, obesity has emerged as a target for multiple myeloma 
prevention. Established dietary-based mouse models have proven useful in 
recapitulating obesity-related diseases in humans, but the multifaceted nature 
of obesity, cancer, and mouse models has led to a knowledge gap about which 
myeloma models can capture obesity-accelerated cancer. Thus, we tested the 
effect of obesity on multiple myeloma in three high-fat diet (HFD) murine 
multiple myeloma models: a SCID-beige MM.1SGFP+/Luc+ xenograft, a C57BL/6J 
Vk*MYC syngeneic model, and a C57BL/6J 5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ semisyngeneic model. 
Only the third model recapitulated obesity-accelerated multiple myeloma, in 
which incidence rates, serum IgG levels, and bioluminescent tumor signals in 
HFD-fed mice were significantly higher than in controls. This HFD-C57BL/6J 
5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ model is the first bioluminescent assessment of myeloma 
engraftment in obese mice and allows for noninvasive spatiotemporal tumor 
tracking of features such as tumor growth and clearance. It can now be used to 
test the role of the immune system or other factors in obesity-accelerated 
myeloma. Finally, our analyses of Multiple Myeloma Research Foundation CoMMpass 
clinical data showed associations of moderate and severe obesity at diagnosis 
with increased patient mortality and revealed novel gene expression and pathway 
signatures that differed between multiple myeloma cells from obese and normal 
patients. Overall, our work supports the hypothesis that obesity contributes to 
myeloma disease progression in humans and provides a novel mouse model with 
which to study this.
PREVENTION RELEVANCE: Obesity is associated with an increased risk of developing 
multiple myeloma; however, the underlying mechanisms are unknown. We 
demonstrated that obesity-accelerated multiple myeloma is coupled with immune 
disruptions in mice and that gene expression is altered in myeloma cells from 
patients with obesity, further implicating obesity as a critical modifier for 
prevention.

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a fatal plasma cell dyscrasia characterized by bone marrow (BM) tumor infiltration and lytic lesions. MM is the second most common hematologic malignancy in the US and is highly dependent on support from the BM (1-3). Obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) or obesogenic behaviors may be new targets for MM prevention or treatment, as obesity is a risk factor for MM and its precursor states of smoldering MM (SMM) and monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance (MGUS) (4-8), and a risk factor for MM progression and mortality (9,10), but our understanding of obesity’s effects on MM is limited. The most recent epidemiological study on this found that patients who were either underweight (BMI <18 kg/m2) or living with severe obesity (BMI ≥35 kg/m2) at the time of diagnosis exhibited significantly lower median progression free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared to patients with BMI values in the “normal” range (18-24 kg/m2), representing many patients who received inadequate treatment and who could benefit from patient-specific therapy (11). However, MM patients living with modestly increased BMI have not previously been found to have a greater risk of death, and changes in gene expression and signaling pathways in obese versus normal BMI patients with MM are also unknown despite holding potentially invaluable information for establishing novel, patient-centric treatment approaches. Herein, we conducted an analysis of Multiple Myeloma Research Foundation (MMRF) CoMMpass clinical data to characterize the differences in patient outcomes, and tumor gene expression in MM from patients with BMIs in the obesity designated range compared to those within the normal BMI range.

In vivo mouse models as well as human data suggest that obesity may contribute to myeloma through numerous mechanisms, including effects on immune cell function and composition, vasculogenesis, serum protein, lipid, and fatty acid content, metabolic syndromes (linked to insulin and glucose levels and tolerance), and other factors (6,12). Associations have also been found between MM risk and weight cycling (WC) (intentional weight loss followed by weight regain) in two large prospective cohorts (13). Similar to obesity, weight cycling can induce metabolic dysfunction (14-16) and alter immune cell phenotypes in adipose tissue (17,18). Weight cycling can negatively affect immune function by reducing natural killer cell cytotoxicity, thereby dampening the ability to survey and control tumor initiation (19). Few reports have utilized murine diet-induced obesity (DIO) myeloma models, and none have assessed weight cycling or used non-invasive optical imaging to monitor tumor growth or regression (20,21). To address the need for accurate models of the obesity-myeloma axis, we developed several DIO mouse models combining different genetic backgrounds, myeloma cells, and diets. Our models examined diet contributions to the full spectrum of MM disease progression from the initial stages of tumor cell engraftment to the end stages of the disease, thus informing possible mechanisms of prevention as well as intervention.

The human myeloma cell line MM.1SGFP+/Luc+ (MM.1S, Biological Sex: Female; RRID:CVCL_8792; modified to express reporter genes) (generously provided by Dr. Ghobrial, Dana-Farber Cancer Institute in 2015) and mouse myeloma cell line 5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ cells (generously provided by Dr. Roodman, Indiana University in 2016; (5TGM1, Biological Sex: Female; RRID:CVCL_VI66; modified to express reporter genes and TK)) were maintained in standard MM cell media: RPMI-1640 medium, 10% FBS (Atlanta Biologicals, Flowery Branch, GA), and 1X Antibiotic-Antimycotic (100 U/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin, 0.25 μg/ml fungizone) (ThermoFisher Scientific, Grand Island, NY). The mouse myeloma Vk*MYC line (Vk12598, Biological Sex: Male) (Dr. Ghobrial in 2015; no RRID available) was passaged through mice (22). MM.1SGFP+/Luc+ was validated with simple sequence length polymorphism characterization at the Vermont Integrative Genomics Resource (Burlington, VT) in 2022. Validation of 5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ cells was performed using the Mouse Cell STR Profiling Service through the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA) in 2023. Vk*MYC cells were not tested as they are passaged through subcutaneous tumors in vivo. Mycoplasma testing was conducted on all cells kept in culture every 6 months using the MycoAlert Detection Kit (Lonza, Portsmouth, NH); cells were used between passages 1-21 for all studies. Most recently, MM.1SGFP+/Luc+ and 5TGM1-TkGFP+/Luc+ cells were tested and negative for mycoplasma in 2025.

All experimental studies and procedures involving mice were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the MaineHealth Institute for Research (Scarborough, ME). Survival analyses were performed using the endpoints approved by the IACUC. Mice were group housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled space (20-25°C, 45%-64% humidity). The light was regulated (12 h light/dark) and throughout the experiments, body weights were recorded, and overall health status and body conditioning score were monitored following tumor cell inoculation. “Survival endpoints” were mouse death or euthanasia as required by IACUC, based on body conditioning score including weight loss and impaired hind limb use. AquaPak HydroGel (Clear H2O, South Portland, ME) and moistened grain were provided on the bottom of all cages if needed. For exsanguination, mice were anesthetized using 100 mg/kg ketamine and 15 mg/kg xylazine, and blood was collected into MiniCollect K3EDTA (Greiner Bio-One, Monroe, NC) and sterile microcentrifuge tubes after surgical removal of their eye for serum collection. All diets described herein were ordered from Research Diets Inc. (New Brunswick, NJ) and used within 4 weeks of expiration dates.

In the MM.1SGFP+/Luc+ study, male Fox Chase SCID-beige mice (CB17.Cg-PrkdcscidLystbg-J/Crl, 6 weeks old; Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA; RRID:IMSR_CRL:250) were fed either a control diet (sucrose-matched control diet, CD; D12450J, 10% kcal from fat; n=11), a cycling diet (diet cycling, DC; 4 weeks/cycle; one diet per cycle; n=9), or a high-fat diet (HFD; D12492, 60% kcal from fat; n=11) for 16 weeks. Then, 2x106 human MM.1SGFP+/Luc+ myeloma cells were intravenously (i.v.) injected and dietary regimens were continued as before, although diet cycling occurred every 2 weeks. Tumor burden was measured using in vivo bioluminescence imaging in an IVIS Lumina LT (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA) as previously described (23).

In the Vk*MYC study, 13-week-old male C57BL6/J mice (strain #000664, The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME; RRID:MGI:8253427) were fed a sucrose-matched Control Diet (CD; D12450Ji, 10% kcal from fat), a cycling diet (diet cycling, DC), or a high-fat diet (HFD; D12492i, 60% kcal from fat) for 16 weeks, followed by intravenous injection of 5x105 Vk12598 murine myeloma cells isolated from subcutaneous tumor passaging. Mice were humanely euthanized at a pre-determined “early” timepoint (T2 n=10; 4 weeks after tumor injection) or used for survival analysis (T3). At T1 (n=5) and T2, the mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation and cervical dislocation. Mice were closely monitored after T2 and were humanely euthanized with CO2 and cervical dislocation individually, based on body conditioning scores and weight loss. Mice from T2 were used to measure tumor burden and whole marrow gene expression (C57BL/6J-Vk*MYC n=10). Following sacrifice, the mouse tibia and femora were fixed, sectioned, and stained, as described previously (24).

For the C57BL/6J-5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ study (cohort 1 (n=20) and cohort 2 (n=21-22)), male C57BL6/J Strain#000664, The Jackson Laboratory; RRID:MGI:8253427) were fed a control diet (CD; #380056, D12450B,10% kcal from fat) or a high-fat diet (HFD; #:380050, D12492, 60% kcal from fat) starting at 6 weeks of age throughout the study. At ~22 weeks old, mice were injected with 1x106 5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ cells (cohort 1) or 2.5 x106 5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ cells (cohort 2) intravenously. For both cohorts, weights were tracked throughout the experiment and body composition was assessed via NMR. Tumor burden was assessed using IVIS (both cohorts) and serum IgG2b ELISA (cohort 1). Cohort 1 mice were euthanized 5.5 weeks post-tumor injection and blood was collected. The cohort 1 end-point (56 days post-inoculation) was chosen based on estimated time for tumors to be present but before any mice would require euthanasia due to IACUC-approved tumor limits. For cohort 2, survival analysis was run for 100 days, using a survival “cutoff” of either: 1. mouse had IVIS saturated tumor signal at 30 second exposures, 2. mouse was found dead, or 3. mouse had to be euthanized based on IACUC-approved endpoints. For both cohorts, tumor incidence was defined as an IVIS signal >1x106 photons/sec total flux.

Dual energy X-ray absorptiometry for whole body composition excluding the head were performed on SCID-Beige mice throughout the duration of the study prior to tumor cell inoculation using PIXImus (GE Lunar, Fairfield, CT) as described previously (25). Body composition in C57BL6/J mice was assessed utilizing quantitative nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) as previously described (25). Data was gathered and analyzed using the minispec Plus NF software (Billerica, MA). NMR was executed at the end of each cycle in the C57BL/6J-Vk*MYC study and once prior to tumor cell injection in the C57BL/6J-5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ study.

GTT was performed after 16 weeks of diet administration in both the SCID-Beige-MM1S and C57BL/6J-Vk*MYC models as described (25). Briefly, mice were fasted for 6 hours followed by establishment of basal glucose level by using the OneTouch Ultra Glucometer (LifeScan, Inc, Milpitas, CA) to detect glucose in tail vein blood sample. Mice were then injected IP with glucose (1g/kg with a 100 mg/mL solution; Sigma Chemical, St. Louis, MO). Glucose levels were measured 20, 60, and 120 minutes after glucose injection using OneTouch UltraMini test strips. For ITT, SCID-Beige mice were fed ad libitum and basal glucose levels were measured using OneTouch UltraMini test trips (representing time point 0). Mice were then injected IP with glucose (1g/kg) and 2 minutes later, they were then injected with insulin (1U/Kg; Patterson Veterinary Supply, Loveland, CO). Glucose levels were measured 20, 40, 60, and 120 minutes later.

Blood was extracted via removal of the eyes from the retroorbital vein. The first two drops were collected for Procyte in a MiniCollect K3EDTA (Cat. No. 450475, Greiner Bio-One, Monroe, NC) and the remaining sample was used for serum collection as previously described (25). To assess monoclonal protein production from Vk*MYC tumors, serum protein electrophoresis was used to determine the presence or absence of a monoclonal protein spike (M-Spike). A 2-fold dilution of serum was made in 1X PBS prior to running the samples on QuickGel Split Beta SPE Gels and following the QuickGel Serum Protein procedures (Helena Laboratories, Beaumont, TX). To quantify serum IgG levels, ELISA kits were used in two models: for the Vk*MYC studies ( Mouse IgG ELISA Kit; Cat. No. ab151276; Abcam) and the 5TGM1 studies (Mouse IgG2b ELISA kit; Bethyl Laboratories, Cat. No. E99-109; Fortis Life Sciences, Waltham, MA). For the C57BL/6J-5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ study, mouse leptin (MOB00B) and adiponectin (MRP300) levels were quantified using quantikine ELISA kits from R&D systems (Minneapolis, MN) per the manufacturers’ instructions.

For hematoxylin and eosin staining, tissues were fixed in 10% formalin for 3 days followed by decalcification of bones until soft in 20% EDTA at 4°C. EDTA solution was changed weekly over the course of a three-to-four-week incubation. Bones were then rinsed with water 3 times and submitted to MaineHealth Institute for Research Histology Core for embedding, processing, staining and sectioning. Slides were imaged in brightfield using an EVOS M5000 microscope (Invitrogen) with representative images taken for each mouse adjacent to the femoral growth plate.

Total RNA was harvested from C57BL/6J mice through excision of the long bones and collection of bone marrow (BM) by syringe flush. Bone marrow was resuspended and lysed in QIAZOL and RNA was isolated via Qiagen miRNEASY Kit with DNase On-column digestion (Qiagen; Hildnen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was quantified and tested for quality and contamination using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 260/230>2 and 260/280>1.8). qPCR was executed as previously described (24). For analysis, target genes were normalized to beta actin (Actb). All 20 samples were able to be used per group for leptin (Lep) and interleukin 6 (Il6), however only n=11 per group exhibited amplification in the luciferase (Luc) qRT-PCR assay, as Luc would only amplify in samples containing enough tumor cells to generate transcript amplification in the whole bone marrow sample.

Tibiae were collected from mice at all timepoints, fixed overnight in 10% formalin and stored in 70% ethanol until scanning. Microcomputed tomography was performed by the Small Animal Imaging Core of MaineHealth Institute for Research. Bone samples from the B6-Vk*MYC study were scanned by a high-resolution desktop micro-tomographic system (vivaCT 40, Scanco Medical AG, Brüttisellen, Switzerland) using a 10.5 μm3 isotropic voxel size, 70 kVp peak x-ray tube intensity, 114 mA x-ray tube current, 250 ms integration time, and were subjected to Gaussian filtration and segmentation. Analyses were done with the Scanco software μCT V6.1, and trabecular and cortical bone analyses both used a specific for region of interest (ROI) and segmented bone from soft tissue using a mineral density threshold (MDT). A control sample was run with each scan and the inter measurement coefficient of variability was 2.649.

The trabecular bone ROI started 105 μm (10 transverse slices) distal to break in the growth plate and extended 1050 μm (100 slices). The bone was segmented using a MDT of 400 mg HA/cm3. Trabecular bone was analyzed for bone volume fraction (Tb.BV/TV,%), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, mm), trabecular number (Tb.N, mm−1), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mm), connectivity density (Conn.D, mm−3), and trabecular bone mineral density (Tb.BMD, mgHA/cm3).

The cortical bone ROI started at 1,995 μm (190 transverse slices) proximal to the fibular junction and extended 525 μm (50 slices). The bone was segmented used a MDT of 700 mg HA/cm3.Cortical bone was analyzed for bone area (Ct.Ar, mm2), medullary area (Ma.Ar, mm2), bone area fraction (Ct.Ar/Tt.Ar,%), cortical thickness (Ct.Th, μm), cortical tissue mineral density (Ct.TMD, mgHA/cm3), polar moment of inertia (J, mm4), and maximum and minimum moment of inertia (Imax, Imin, mm4).

Bone samples from the B6-5TGM1 study were scanned using the SkyScan 1276 CMOS (Bruker, Billerica, MA) with a resolution pixel size of 6 μm; camera pixel binning of 2X was applied. SkyScan CT-Analyser software was used to select the region of interest (ROI), and conduct the ROI analyses. Trabecular analyses commenced approximately 0.18 mm from the growth plate in the direction of the metastasis and extended 1.05 mm (175 slices) towards the diaphysis. Cortical bone ROI was selected with reference to the fibular junction, approximately 248 image slices in the proximal direction, and extended for 0.51 mm (85 image slices). Trabecular bone was assessed using an adaptive threshold with a lower grey value of 130; cortical bone porosity threshold was set to 100 and 170.

Femurs were fixed in 10% Neutral-buffered formalin and stored in 70% ethanol at MHIR before being transferred to the University of New England microscopy core for further processing. Femurs were then embedded in methyl methacrylate and sectioned at 5 microns. Von Kossa/Toluidine Blue staining was used for measuring bone area and osteoblast number, and TRAP staining was used to determine osteoclast number. Six adjacent 20x fields (2.4 mm total length) beginning 200 microns below the center of the growth plate were analyzed. Representative images were collected using an Olympus VS200 slide scanner.

For sorting and quantification of tumor cells in the C57BL/5J-Vk*MYC study, splenic and bone marrow tissues were isolated following sacrifice. To obtain a single cell solution, spleens were gently agitated (circular motion) and pressed through a cell strainer (70 μm pores) using the rubber end of a syringe and suspended in 1X PBS. To obtain a single cell solution from bone marrow, the long bones from 1 leg were dissected, and the marrow cavity was opened and flushed with 1X PBS prior to centrifugation. After pelleting (5 minutes at 300 RCF), red blood cells from spleen were lysed using Red Blood Cell Lysing Buffer (Sigma, Cat. No. R7757-100ML) and RBC lysis for bone marrow was done with ACK Lysing Buffer (Lonza, Cat No. 10548E). Cells from both tissue types were stained using a protocol adapted from Biolegend (San Diego, CA). Cells were labeled with 1 μL antibody per 10 million cells (in 100 μL Cell Staining Buffer) with APC anti-mouse CD138 (syndecan 1; Biolegend, Cat. No.142506; RRID:AB_10962911) for 1 hour followed by two washes in Cell Staining Buffer (Biolegend) prior to sorting on the FACS Aria Multi Parameter Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). The CD138 positive and negative fractions from each cell suspension were saved in QIAzol (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) for downstream gene expression analyses.

BM biopsies from newly diagnosed MM patients (N = 754) in the Multiple Myeloma Research Foundation (MMRF) CoMMpass study (NCT01454297, version IA23) were collected and analyzed as we previously described (26). These data involve 1150 patients from 76 sites worldwide, where participants are surveyed every 6 months for 8 years. Patients were newly-diagnosed, symptomatic for MM, and candidates for systemic treatment. They were treated with a proteasome-based, IMID-based, or, most commonly, a proteasome and IMID-based regimen as decided by the patients’ physician or a clinical trial. Patients were grouped by BMI into 5 World Health Organization (WHO)-defined (27) categories: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2); normal (18.5 to < 25 kg/m2); overweight (25 to <30 kg/m2); moderately obese (30 to < 35 kg/m2); or severely obese (≥ 35 kg/m2), or 4 categories (with a collapsed obese groups, e.g., BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression calculated hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) to examine the association of BMI categories with time to death (overall survival, OS). Both OS models adjusted for selected patient characteristics at baseline, including age, sex, race, ISS stage, therapy class, and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score. ECOG Performance Status ranges from 0 (fully active, able to carry on all pre-disease performance without restriction) to 4 (completely disabled; cannot carry on any selfcare; totally confined to bed or chair).

Differentially expressed gene analysis was performed using the limma model comparing obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) vs normal (18.5 <BMI< 25 kg/m2) MMRF patient CoMMpass bone marrow samples. The most significantly different genes (FDR corrected limma moderated t-test p-value<0.05; log2fold change > 0.2) are highlighted as color-coded. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed in the Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) R package (28) using Hallmark gene set collections from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (29). Pathway GSVA enrichment scores were calculated for each gene set using the GSVA function with default settings. Cell type enrichment analysis was performed using xCell (30). Pathway and cell type enrichment scores were compared using limma moderated t-test using a FDR corrected p-value of 0.05 as the cutoff.

GraphPad Prism (v.6 or above) was used to create all graphs and statistical significance was determined using one-way, two-way ANOVA, Mixed-effects analysis, or Student’s T-tests as described in figure legends. Survival analysis in mice was performed with Kaplan-Meyer analysis. Data represent mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. Significance is indicated as: *, p<0.05; **, p<0.01; ***, p<0.001; ****, p<0.0001.

We first developed a model of diet-induced obesity (DIO) using an MM xenograft mouse model. Male SCID-beige mice were randomly divided into three groups and fed a control diet (CD), a high-fat diet (HFD), or a diet alternated (cycled) between the control and HFD every 4 weeks for 16 weeks (diet cycling, DC) (Fig. 1A). Individual body weights, food consumption, glucose tolerance testing (GTT), body composition, and bone parameters were measured. SCID-beige mice on HFD weighed significantly more than controls only on days 39, 46, and 55, and had subtle weight cycling in response to diet cycling, demonstrating a greater resistance to DIO than immunocompetent mice (Fig. 1B). HFD mice consumed more than CD mice in two of the six measured periods, and mice with diet cycling consumed more food than the other groups in the third and fifth cycles (that is, when they were fed the HFD), demonstrating the development of HFD preference (Supplementary Figure S1A). DC mice had more lean mass than control mice at each cycle, while HFD mice had more fat mass than controls at each cycle (Fig. 1C, D). Glucose handling was impaired in HFD mice, although it was not altered by diet cycling, by the end of the 16-week period (Supplementary Figure S1B). Finally, mice in the diet cycling group had the lowest average bone mineral density (BMD) at the end of the 16-week regimen, despite starting with the highest BMD, suggesting a potential detriment to bone health induced by diet or weight cycling (Supplementary Figure S1C). After 16 weeks, mice were injected with MM.1SGFP+/luc+ human myeloma cells. Mice in both the HFD and diet cycling groups had greater tumor burden compared to CD at week 2, as assessed with IVIS, but these differences were lost by week 3 (Fig. 1E-F) and no survival difference was detected (Fig. 1G). Interestingly, there was large inter-animal variability in response to diet; some mice fed a HFD weighed the least or gained the least, and the opposite was true for certain mice fed a CD. Thus, as a post-hoc analysis, we examined whether body weight at the time of injection affected tumor burden. No differences in tumor burden were observed when mice were stratified by body fat (Supplementary Figure S1D) or weight gain (Supplementary Figure S1E), but at 3.5 weeks, tumor burden was significantly elevated in the heaviest versus lightest mice at the time of injection (Supplementary Figure S1F), although this did not translate into survival differences (Supplementary Figure S1G). Overall, this model did not replicate the obesity-driven MM observed in humans, suggesting that the immune system, which is compromised in these mice, may be an intermediary in the obesity-accelerated myeloma pathway.

We next tested the effects of DIO on C57BL/6J male mice injected with Vk*Myc murine myeloma cells (Fig. 2A). These immunocompetent mice show large responses to both HFD and diet cycling and were expected to better recapitulate effects of obesity on MM observed clinically(17). Mice were fed a HFD, a control diet (CD) or a diet-cycling (DC) regimen, alternating high fat diet and control diet every 4 weeks, for 16 weeks, and then assessed at T1 (before tumor injection), T2 (3 weeks after tumor injection), or T3 (at IACUC-determined survival endpoints). HFD mice weighed more than CD mice at every time point starting from day 7 of diets (Fig. 2B), and had more lean mass starting at cycle 2, and fat mass starting from cycle 1 (Fig. 2C,D). Diet cycling mice showed intermediate phenotypes and cycling responses in body weight, lean mass, and fat mass (Fig 2B-D). HFD and diet cycling regimens both lowered glucose tolerance (Supplementary Figure S2A) and insulin tolerance (Supplementary Figure S2B).

We next examined myeloma burden differences 3 weeks after cell inoculation (T2) and at survival endpoints (T3). CD138+ tumor cell percentages were not affected by diets in the bone marrow (BM), or spleen (Fig. 2E), or in the BM plus spleen combined (Supplementary Figure S2C). There was also no difference in Scd1 (encoding CD138) gene expression in whole BM (Supplementary Figure S2D) at T2, circulating IgG levels (Fig. 2F) at T2, or spleen weights at T2 or T3. (Supplementary Figure S2E). Survival did not differ based on diet (Fig. 2G) or based on weight gain (low- versus high-gainers) (Supplementary Figure S2F) (which, unlike in the prior model, did segregate by diet), and did not correlate with body weight at the time of tumor inoculation (Supplementary Figure S2G). Bone parameters examined by microCT showed lower trabecular bone volume fraction (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) in the HFD and DC groups compared to controls at T3 (Supplementary Figure S2H, I, J). Additionally, trabecular number was decreased by diet-cycling (Supplementary Figure S2K), and both cortical thickness (Supplementary Figure S2L) and cortical area (Supplementary Figure S2M) were decreased by HFD-feeding at T3. Overt phenotypic changes in BM adiposity were not observed in either HFD or DC groups as assessed by gross histology (Supplementary Figure S3). Complete blood count at T2 revealed no changes between groups except a decrease in monocytes in the DC group, and a decrease in mean platelet volume in HFD and DC groups (Supplementary Figure S4). Taken together, HFD and diet-cycling both negatively impacted bone health in myeloma-injected mice, but neither HFD nor DC affected myeloma progression in this model.

Finally we created a third myeloma mouse DIO model in C57BL6/J mice, which improved upon similar models (20,21) through the use of luciferase-labeled tumor cells. The premise of this model is that 5TGM1 murine myeloma cells, which were derived from a C57BL6 sub-strain, are not able to engraft or grow in healthy C57BL/6J mice on a normal diet, but are able to when mice are fed a HFD, perhaps due to the creation of a more tumor-permissive microenvironment. In our model, mice were fed a control diet or HFD from 6 weeks of age for 16 weeks ad libitum (Fig. 3A). Body composition assessed via NMR revealed that HFD-fed mice had increased fat mass, body fluid mass, and lean mass compared to control mice after 16 weeks of feeding (Fig. 3B). Similarly, HFD-fed mice consistently gained weight and sustained these gains throughout the study, weighing significantly more than control mice at the time of tumor cell injection and at the terminal endpoint (Fig. 3C).

In a first cohort, a pre-determined endpoint study, mice ~22 weeks of age were injected with 1x106 5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ cells via the tail vein. As early as two weeks later, tumor growth in several mice on HFD was detected on IVIS, and tumor incidence remained higher in HFD than in controls throughout the study (Supplementary Figure S5A,B). At the endpoint (56 days post-tumor injection), only 1 of 20 mice with CD developed cancer (5% incidence), while 4 of 20 mice (20% incidence) developed cancer on HFD (Supplementary Figure S5B). However, this difference was not significant by Fisher’s Exact Test, perhaps due to such overall engraftment. Still, HFD mice had significantly elevated circulating IgG2b levels (Supplementary Figure S5C) and exhibited a >2-fold increase in serum leptin levels compared to CD mice, as expected with obesity (Fig. 3D). No difference in adiponectin was observed (Supplementary Figure S5D). HFD mice also had significantly larger spleens (Fig. 3E) and complete blood count differences including increased neutrophils (Fig. 3F), decreased lymphocytes (Figure 3G) and eosinophils, and no changes in monocytes or reticulocytes (Supplementary Figure S5E-G). qPCR of the femoral BM from these mice revealed increased expression of two pro-myeloma factors (leptin (Lep, 3.7-fold; Supplementary Figure S5H), and Il-6 (1.7-fold, but non-significant; Supplementary Figure S5I), as well as Fabp4 (Supplementary Figure S5J) in the HFD versus control mice.

Although most of the cohort 1 mice had no tumor engraftment, their tibia were still assessed by microCT to quantify HFD effects on skeletal properties. Similar to the B6-Vk*MYC model above, the B6-5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ model also showed mice on HFD had lower trabecular bone volume fraction and bone mineral density (Supplementary Figure S6A,B) compared to controls. Trabecular number was lower in HFD mice as well, but no trabecular thickness differences were seen (Supplementary Figure S6C,D). Cortical thickness was also decreased in HFD mice (Supplementary Figure S6E,F). The few mice who had tumors by BLI were identified as black dots on the graphs and although there were not enough for a formal comparison, the tumor-bearing and non-tumor bearing mice were not distinguishable. Representative images of microCT are shown in Supplementary Figure S6G,H. To explore the mechanism of this bone phenotype, we then performed static bone histomorphometry on the cohort 1 femurs. This analysis confirmed that trabecular bone volumes fraction was decreased, due to decreased trabecular number (Supplementary Figure S7A-D) in HFD mice. Importantly, osteoblast and osteoclast numbers were both diminished by high fat feeding (Supplementary Figure S7E, F), demonstrating that their coupling was intact, and suggesting that decreased bone formation was likely the cause of the bone loss in HFD-fed mice. Representative TRAP and von Kossa stained images (3 per group) used for static bone histomorphometry are shown in Supplementary Figure S8. Overall, the negative effect of HFD on bone volumes we observed suggest that MM patients with obesity may be even more at risk for skeletal-related events than non-obese MM patients due to worse baseline bone health.

Finally, because tumor incidence rates were so low in cohort 1, we decided to repeat the study with the modification of injecting more tumor cells to increase overall tumor engraftment rates. Thus, in cohort 2, we injected 2.5x106 5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ cells via the tail vein and assessed mice with BLI and with survival endpoint. As with cohort 1, HFD mice had more fat and lean mass, and weighed more than controls at the time of tumor injection (Supplementary Figure S9). Whole body tumor quantification by BLI was significantly increased at week 5 (Fig. 3H) and MM incidence was significantly higher at week 7 in HFD versus control mice (Fig. 3I), a trend that continued through the study. Interestingly, despite having greater tumor engraftment, HFD mice did not show worse outcomes in the survival curve analysis (Fig. 3J).

To examine the relationship between obesity and MM progression in humans, we analyzed the updated CoMMpass study dataset. In multivariate Cox proportional hazards analyses adjusted for other patient baseline characteristics (age, sex, race, International Staging System [ISS] stage, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group [ECOG] score, and therapeutic class), we found that patients with BMI values in the obese range at diagnosis (e.g., BMI ≥30 kg/m2) had a statistically significant 63% increased risk of death compared to patients with a normal BMI (18.5-<25 kg/m2) at diagnosis (hazard ratio [HR]=1.63, 95% confidence interval [CI]=1.13-2.35, p-value=0.009; Fig. 4). Further subclassification of patients with obesity revealed similarly elevated mortality risks among both patients with moderate obesity (BMI of 30 to < 35 kg/m2: HR=1.60, 95% CI=1.06-2.43, p =0.026) and severe obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2: HR=1.66, 95% CI=1.07-2.58, p =0.023), with no change to the respective covariable associations with mortality (Fig. 4).

We then compared the gene expression (for 39,145 genes) of CD138+ myeloma cells isolated from MM patients with BMI values in the obese or normal range at diagnosis (Fig. 5, Supplementary Figure S10). Overall expression profiles were altered in myeloma cells from patients with obesity compared with those of normal weight at diagnosis (Fig. 5). Notably, gene expression profiles were enriched for several critical signaling pathways (Supplementary Figure S10A), including the hallmark adipogenesis pathway (Supplementary Figure S10B) and hallmark oxidative phosphorylation pathways (Supplementary Figure S10C). Furthermore, acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family members 1 and 2 (ACSS1 and ACSS2) were significantly increased in tumor cells from obese patients compared to those from normal BMI patients, as well as acyl-CoA synthetase family members 3 and 4 (ACSL3 and ACSL4) (Supplementary Table S1). Overall, expression of many genes related to an array of cellular functions, such as intracellular vesicle trafficking and mitochondrial function, differed between patients with obese and normal BMI designations.

Given that obesity affects immune cells, we also used XCell analysis to examine immune and other non-tumor microenvironment cells that were pulled down with CD138+ selection during the MMRF collection process (Supplementary Figure S11). We found significant differences in granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMP), basophils, natural killer T cells (NKT), eosinophils, CD8 naïve T cells, epithelial cells, megakaryocytes, common lymphoid progenitors (CLP), megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEP), osteoblasts, and cells that were identified as smooth muscle cells but likely have an expression profile consistent with bone marrow stromal cells, including mesenchymal stem cells. Collectively, these data suggest that obesity at diagnosis is associated with alterations in both myeloma microenvironment and myeloma cell gene expression profiles.

We developed three murine myeloma models to test whether aspects of energy balance play a role in myeloma disease progression. Using an immunocompromised xenograft model, an immunocompetent syngeneic model, and a semi-syngeneic (slight immune mismatch) model, we demonstrated important differences in early and late myeloma tumor burden levels in response to diet. The comparison between the three models highlights the complexity of obesity-accelerated disease, with implications for a diet by immune by tumor cell interactions.

In the MM.1Sgfp+luc+ model, neither HFD nor diet cycling significantly affected the endpoint tumor burden or overall survival. However, there was a positive relationship between mouse weight at the time of tumor cell injection and late-stage tumor burden, suggesting that while SCID-beige mice exhibit variable responses to HFD, there may be a link between increased weight and tumor burden advancement. Further investigation into this correlation is needed. In the second model (Vk*MYC cells injected into syngeneic C57BL/6J mice, which exhibited a robust response to HFD), there was no difference in CD138+ tumor cell counts or survival with diet cycling or obesity. However, in the C57BL6/J-5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+ model, DIO created a more MM-permissive environment, similar to prior reports with non-Luc-5TGM1 cells; results that have not been observed in syngeneic C57BL/KaLwRij-5TGM1 experiments, where all the mice exhibit engraftment and tumor growth on both control and HFD (20,21).

While others have reported aberrant adiponectin levels as a major contributor to this phenomenon (20), we did not observe any differences in adiponectin levels between the control and HFD groups in our model. However, we detected suppressed lymphocytes in the C57BL6/J-5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+-DIO model (a more immunogenically “hot” model with a slight genetic mismatch), but not the C57BL6/J-Vk*MYC-DIO (a more immunogenically “cold” model), suggesting that the combination of DIO and a non-syngeneic model allowed for tumor cell engraftment and expansion. This is similar to findings in other obesity-driven tumor models, showing that obesity-driven cancer is evident only in immunogenically hot tumors and implicates immune suppression in obesity-driven tumor development(31). We also detected differences in bone parameters in both the C57BL6/J-Vk*MYC-DIO and C57BL6/J-5TGM1-TKGFP+/Luc+-DIO models, suggesting that bone health may also be negatively impacted by obesity. Knowing that both bone and immune strength are critical to responses to therapies in MM (32), investigating the mechanistic links governing the impact of DIO on bone, immune system, and tumor growth are critical next steps in this field.

Interestingly, a recent publication found that HFD, which correlated with decreased BM T and B cells, promoted myeloma using the Vk*MYC transplantable model with six weeks of HFD (33). A few differences between that study and ours could explain our differing results (eg. that work used female mice, started diets earlier, injected younger mice (11 weeks old), used the Vk12653 Vk*MYC subline, and injected fewer cells/mouse), but their work aligns with ours in suggesting that obesity-induced immune changes could contribute to DIO-driven MM. Others have seen obesity accelerate Vk12598 growth in syngeneic mice when tumor cells are directly injected into the femur, but not intravenously, which is an interesting model we would like to explore in future work (21). Additional studies using DIO over a longer time course, such as with the Vk*MYC transgenic mice (34) or C57BL/KaLwRij (35) which both develop myeloma with advantaged age, may further delineate the effects of obesity on myeloma disease development. Additionally, obesity-associated abnormalities in the secretion of adipokines, such as leptin, and cytokines can lead to the activation of a multitude of oncogenic intracellular molecular networks, including NF-kB, JAK2/STAT3, and PI3K/AKT (36,37). Since all DIO mouse models did not show accelerated MM, and none showed worse survival outcomes, more research integrating the vast physiological effects of obesity on myeloma, and cancer generally, into a cohesive picture is necessary. This will become increasingly important to determine which anti-obesity/anti-diabetic therapies may hold promise for MM, especially as they become more widely available and used.

There are many limitations to our in vivo work. Firstly, we did not examine the effects of HFD on myeloma or bone health in the context of chemotherapy or other anti-myeloma therapies, despite many of these having known detrimental effects on bone. Also, the SCID-beige mouse model was not fully characterized, though it suggested a link between elevated body weight and tumor burden, independent from diet regimen which should be further explored. More analyses of these mice (eg. μCT bone data, paraprotein or CD138+ levels, spleen weights) would be useful. Other limitations include: the lack of control age-matched tumor-naïve mice for many parameters (eg. to be able to detect cancer-induced bone loss), the use of only males (chosen because they typically show a larger DIO phenotype than females), and the lack of mechanistic data proving a role for immune cells in obesity-accelerated myeloma. Finally, human obesity is very different from DIO in mouse models; human obesity often starts decades before MM development and includes many social, genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors that contribute to BMI designations (38). We hope to address some of these limitations in future work, and plan to continue characterizing immune cell populations in spleens and BM of HFD and control mice, with and without myeloma, to better identify potential immune cell types or exhaustion pathways that link obesity and myeloma.

In human data, we found novel clinical evidence that patients with MM who are living with moderate obesity, not only severe obesity, have an increased risk of death. This reflects approximately 8 years of additional follow-up compared to the previously published analysis (interim analysis in 2015 versus 2023) (11). Since our analysis used slightly different patient categorizations (e.g., first-line therapies) and total numbers, the findings should not be directly compared to those of the previous study (11). A second caution to this work is that BMI is a deeply flawed metric of health and body composition for many reasons (39), but it remains the only tool currently available to systematically assess and compare obesity in patients with MM in and across datasets. Despite these limitations, our findings are consistent with the mounting evidence for the clinical significance of obesity in MM, the potential for targeting adiposity or obesity to improve outcomes in patients with MM, and the importance of elucidating the underlying mechanisms connecting obesity and MM.

Within the CoMMpass dataset, we found altered gene expression profiles in tumor cells from patients with obesity versus normal BMI, resulting in 35 differential hallmark signatures, including enrichment of adipogenesis and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. While expression fold changes for individual genes were relatively small, yet highly significant, the combined pathway analyses suggest that these are likely meaningful. Of note, our finding of increased expression of ACSS2 in obese MM patients confirmed a previously published smaller study of nine patients (21) and added new evidence of similar expression profiles for ACSS1, and other metabolically-linked enzymes such as ACSL4, which we recently found plays a direct role in myeloma cell viability (40). Using xCell analysis of the CD138+ fraction, we detected differences in non-MM and BM-resident cells between the two groups. Taken together, these findings suggest that obesity alters myeloma cells and their surrounding microenvironmental cell profiles. More work on dietary, weight, and body composition effects on immune function, tumor growth and resistance, and bone loss would benefit patients with MM. Comparisons between human and mouse myeloma cell gene expression, and other differences, in obese versus normal BMI conditions would also be beneficial for future investigations, which we believe would now be possible through the generation of the 5TGM1 obesity-accelerated model we developed. In summary, our study suggests a novel relationship between diet, obesity and myeloma that we hope will enhance precision medicine for MM.
