# Clinical management, epidemiology, and recurrence of human cystic echinococcosis in a secondary care level hospital in an endemic area of the Andes in Sicuani, Cusco, Peru
**Authors:** Roberto Pineda-Reyes, Miguel M. Cabada, Maria L. Morales, Jan Hattendorf, Paola Vergaray, Ruben Bascope, Jakob Zinsstag
**DOI:** [10.1101/2024.09.12.24313559](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.09.12.24313559)

## Content

Abstract
Peru has the highest incidence of human cystic echinococcosis (CE) in South America and most cases are reported in the Central and Southern Peruvian Andes, including the Cusco region. We reviewed medical records of patients with CE admitted between 2010-2019 to a level-2 hospital in Sicuani, which serves the Canchis province of Cusco. We collected data on surgical management and disease recurrence. Records from 115 patients were included, their median age was 25 years (IQR, 16-46) and 60% were female. Most patients (68.7%) presented with a single liver cyst. The Gharbi classification was used in 75/107 (70.1%) patients with an ultrasound and in 91/142 cysts (64.8%). Thirty-one (34.1%) were classified as Gharbi I, 33 (36.3%) Gharbi II, 21 (23.1%) Gharbi III, and 6 (6.6%) Gharbi IV. Pre-surgical complications were reported in 41.7%. One hundred two patients underwent surgery. In 39.2% a secondary CE prevention measure was documented, 5.9% had two, and one had the three recommended measures. Post-surgical cyst recurrence was reported in 16.5% at a median 32.3 months (IQR, 3.9-46.6) and readmissions in 12.7%. In the multivariable analysis, having ≥3 cysts (OR 6.26, 95% CI 1.10-39.85) was associated with post-surgical cyst recurrence. Advanced and complicated CE and post-surgical recurrence were common in Sicuani. Standardization of ultrasound staging-guided clinical management along with secondary CE prevention measures could effectively decrease the disease burden linked to clinical care, improving outcomes and decreasing costs. Ultimately, only integrated control at the source of infection including hand hygiene, secure disposal of offal, avoidance of dog feeding offal, and deworming of dogs can reduce transmission and lead to elimination.

Introduction 50 
Cystic echinococcosis (CE) is a zoonosis caused by the metacestode stage of the dog 51 
tapeworm Echinococcus granulosus (1). E. granulosus has a global distribution, being 52 
endemic in South America, North and East Africa, Central Asia and China (2). Peru has the 53 
highest incidence of CE in South America, accounting for 84.8% of all reported cases in year 54 
2018 (3). The district of Sicuani in the Cusco region is a highly endemic area in the Southern 55 
Peruvian Andes. Up to 45% of CE cases referred to tertiary care hospitals in Cusco are from 56 
Sicuani (4). A population survey using focus ultrasound to detect abdominal CE in four 57 
communities of the Cusco region found that 78% of patients diagnosed with CE were from 58 
Sicuani (5). The economic and urban expansion of the Sicuani city have created areas 59 
where unregulated livestock slaughter activities, stray dogs, and humans interact closely. 60 
Prompt diagnosis and appropriate treatment of cases in Sicuani are crucial to address the 61 
burden of CE in the healthcare system. 62 
The WHO-Informal Working Group on Echinococcosis (WHO-IWGE) ultrasound staging 63 
system is used to diagnose and guide the clinical management of liver CE (6). However, 64 
many low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) have not implemented the proposed WHO 65 
ultrasound staging which may expose patients to unnecessary procedures (7). In developing 66 
countries, CE has a high post-surgical recurrence risk, with rates that range between 4.7% 67 
and 16.4% (8-11). Clinical characteristics such as the cyst size, number of cysts, and 68 
presence of complications have been associated with disease severity and recurrence (10, 69 
12, 13). Treatment precautions to avoid cyst rupture and intraabdominal seeding may 70 
decrease the risk of recurrence related to surgical care. 71 
The recommendations to avoid cyst rupture and secondary CE include the use of surgical 72 
field protection with hypertonic saline-soaked drapes, injection of a scolicidal agent into the 73 
cyst, and prescription of pre-surgical albendazole (ABZ) (6). Increasing evidence shows that 74 
appropriate standardization of surgical procedures may be associated with lower risk of 75 
recurrence (14-16). A prospective study found no mortality and zero recurrence at 24 76

Methods 99 
Study design and setting 100 
This is a review of medical records from patients hospitalized at the Alfredo Callo Rodriguez 101 
(ACR) Hospital in Sicuani. This hospital is a level-2 public healthcare center that provides 102 
specialty medical care to patients from the Canchis and Canas provinces of Cusco (19). 103 
During March 2024, we collected data from all patients admitted to the hospital for CE 104 
between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2019. The pre-COVID-19 period was selected 105 
to avoid collecting information that could reflect gaps in care or practice of CE treatment in 106 
Sicuani because of the pandemic public health emergency response. This timeframe also 107 
coincides with the renovation and expansion of surgical services in the hospital in 2010.  108 
The city of Sicuani is the capital of the Canchis province in the Cusco region at an elevation 109 
of 3,550 meters above sea level. The Sicuani district has an urban population of 47,400 and 110 
a rural population of 10,400 habitants (20). Sicuani is a commerce center for farmers of the 111 
surrounding areas and is the second largest city in the Cusco region. 112 
Study procedures 113 
Medical record numbers of patients with CE were identified using ICD-10 codes (B67.0-114 
B67.9) assigned as the discharge diagnosis through the hospital's statistics department. 115 
Surgical logbooks were handsearched to ensure all surgical CE cases were retrieved. 116 
Demographic and clinical data were collected via ODK software (© 2022 Get ODK Inc., 117 
California, USA) using an electronic case report form, which was piloted at the start of data 118 
collection to ensure data accuracy.  119

Results 161 
Demographic and clinical characteristics 162 
One hundred and twenty-five medical record numbers were identified. Six were duplicated, 2 163 
were misrecorded as CE, and 2 had missing charts. In total, 115 unique medical records 164 
from patients with CE were included in the study (Table 1). The median age was 25 years 165 
(IQR, 16-46) and 60% were female. Most patients (103, 89.6%) were residents of the 166 
Canchis province and 73.9% lived in the Sicuani district. Fifty-six patients (48.7%) were 167 
referred from primary healthcare centers and 57.1% of these were referred from the 168 
Pampaphalla health center.  169 
One hundred eighty-five echinococcal cysts were diagnosed in the 115 patients, 169 were 170 
diagnosed upon presentation to the hospital and 16 during readmissions. These involved the 171 
liver, lungs, stomach, spleen, duodenum, gallbladder, diaphragm, adnexa, bladder, and 172 
colon. Most (142, 84%) were located in the liver, 16 within the abdominal cavity, and 11 in 173 
the lungs. Four patients had disseminated liver and intraabdominal cysts. The maximum 174 
number of lung cysts per patient was 2. The median diameter of the largest liver cyst per 175 
patient was 12.8 cm (IQR, 9.2-15.0) and for lung cysts was 8.5 cm (IQR, 4.5-12.6). Seventy-176 
seven (67%) patients had a liver cyst of ≥ 10 cm in diameter. 177 
Most patients (98, 85.2%) presented with cysts only in the liver, 7 (6.1%) with both liver and 178 
intraabdominal cysts, 5 (4.3%) with liver and lung cysts, and 5 (4.3%) with only lung cysts. 179 
Most patients presented with a single liver cyst (79, 68.7%). Forty-eight (41.7%) cases 180 
presented with complicated cysts before surgery, 31 (27%) had one pre-surgical 181 
complication, and 17 (14.8%) had two or more. The most common pre-surgical 182 
complications were infection of the cyst content (17, 14.9%), cysto-biliary fistulas (9, 7.8%), 183 
liver abscess (9, 7.8%), cyst rupture (8, 7%), and peritonitis (5, 4.3%) (Table 2). A summary 184 
of the clinical presentation characteristics and laboratory results is shown in Supplementary 185

Ultrasound diagnosis 187 
One hundred seven patients (93%) had a diagnostic ultrasound. In 75 (70.1%), a liver 188 
ultrasound was performed and used for staging according to the Gharbi classification (6), 189 
and in 20 (18.7%) a liver ultrasound was performed but no staging was reported. Twelve 190

total, 91 (64.8%) of the 142 liver cysts were staged using the Gharbi classification. Thirty-193 
one (34.1%) were Gharbi I, 33 (36.3%) Gharbi II, 21 (23.1%) Gharbi III, and 6 (6.6%) Gharbi 194 
IV. Seven (6.1%) patients were diagnosed with inactive cysts, including one who had a 195 
Gharbi IV cyst diagnosed intraoperatively.  196

Discussion 262 
Our study describes the clinical characteristics and management of CE at a low-complexity 263 
hospital in an endemic area of the Southern Peruvian Andes. Most CE patients requiring 264 
admission to this hospital were part of the economically active population and presented 265 
often with advanced disease (cysts ≥ 10 cm in diameter) and/or pre-surgical complications. 266 
Despite the common use of ultrasound for the evaluation of CE, the WHO-IWGE system was 267 
not used for the diagnosis and management of liver CE in any of the cases. Instead, the 268 
Gharbi classification was used on a limited number of cysts. Secondary CE prevention 269 
measures were documented in less than half of the surgical procedures. One in six patients 270 
had recurrence of CE after surgery and one in eight patients were readmitted to the hospital 271 
at least once. Our data suggest that educational interventions could improve the medical and 272 
surgical management of CE at the ACR Hospital of Sicuani, thus decreasing the morbidity 273 
associated with disease recurrence and surgical procedures. 274 
Between 2016-2018, the Peruvian Ministry of Health reported a CE cumulative incidence of 275 
29.2-45 cases per 100,000 in the Cusco region, which is one of the highest in the country 276 
(24). During the same period, our cumulative incidence calculation ranged between 8.4-11.2 277 
new admissions to the ACR Hospital per 100,000. These numbers are also higher than most 278 
other regions in Peru, even though they do not include outpatient consultations, cases 279 
diagnosed in primary healthcare centers, and cases from the Social Security Health System 280 
(EsSalud) or private healthcare centers. The ACR Hospital of Sicuani serves the populations 281 
of the Canas and Canchis provinces. These provinces have large young age populations, 282 
which are affected by CE. The period between infection and clinical presentation, or ‘latent 283 
period’, typically lasts many years in CE. This suggests that active transmission occurs in 284 
Sicuani and that it happens early in life. A large case series of hospitalized patients in two 285 
regions of Spain showed a distribution skewed towards the older age groups with only 2% 286 
cases in patients younger than 19 years (25, 26). The authors attributed this age distribution 287 
in part to the successful interruption of transmission by control programs based on stray 288

In our study, most patients had liver cysts and 41.7% presented to the hospital with 295 
complications. This is a high proportion of complications, similar to the 40.3% reported in a 296 
study in Salamanca, Spain (29). Compared to the latter, our study had slightly lower 297 
percentage of mechanical complications (20% vs 25%); however, we encountered a higher 298 
proportion of infectious complications (25.2% vs 17.8%), cyst rupture (8.7% vs 2.9%), and 299 
advanced disease (cysts ≥10 cm in 68.7% versus 45%) (29). These discrepancies could be 300 
due to a selection bias related to the hospital level of complexity, our lower proportion of lung 301 
cysts, or due to differences in healthcare access. Another retrospective series in the Junin 302 
region in the Central Peruvian Andes also reported over 50% cysts ≥10 cm, but their 303 
proportion of pre-surgical complications was significantly lower (4%) likely due to including 304 
cases from outpatient services and private hospitals (30). 305 
We could not establish a significant association between pre-surgical complications and CE 306 
recurrence. Other studies with larger samples were also unable to demonstrate associations 307 
between infectious, mechanical, or allergic complications and CE recurrence (9, 29). This 308 
could indicate that the risk of recurrence depends on other clinical variables associated with 309 
surgical or post-surgical care (9). Large cyst diameter has been proposed as a marker of 310 
severity and risk factor for recurrence (12). Despite the large cyst diameters found in our 311 
patients compared to other studies, an association between this variable and pre-surgical 312 
complications, recurrence, or readmission was not detected (11, 13, 29). We did observe an 313 
association between higher number of cysts and risk of post-surgical recurrence. Number of 314

Ultrasound staging was documented in two-thirds of those with liver cysts. Ultrasound CE 322 
diagnosis and staging is not yet implemented in many LMIC (7). The WHO-IWGE staging 323 
system was not used for the management of CE in our study sample. This classification 324 
serves as a clinical management guide to consider medical and invasive (percutaneous, 325 
surgical) interventions, or a ‘watch and wait’ approach (6). Patients with liver cysts who 326 
underwent surgery presented with Gharbi I or II in our study, and some could have been 327 
candidates for percutaneous treatment (31). However, this treatment option is not available 328 
in endemic areas of Peru. Patients with Gharbi III cysts in our study had a greater proportion 329 
of recurrence compared to the other stages. Surgery is the treatment of choice for Gharbi III 330 
cysts due to high recurrence rates reported in percutaneous treatment studies (31, 32). 331 
Multiple daughter cysts within a solid matrix, as seen in Gharbi III, may be more difficult to 332 
access and drain likely missing smaller cysts which may not get sufficient scolicidal drug 333 
exposure within the matrix, increasing the risk of treatment failure (32, 33). Early ultrasound 334 
screening along with implementation of WHO-IWGE staging and percutaneous procedures 335 
may decrease costs, unnecessary procedures, and hospitalization time in Sicuani. 336 
We found a higher post-surgical recurrence rate in comparison to other studies from different 337

interventions to prevent spillage of cyst contents during surgery. In some cases, surgical 339

medical charts with a small sample size. Missing information in certain variables such as 359 
ultrasound staging did not allow testing their association to the outcome. Second, as the 360 
hospital had a digitalized database of medical record numbers only since 2017, cases 361

hospitalized during this period who did not undergo surgery are missing in our cohort. Third, 363 
the description of symptoms, complications, and management of patients in our study 364

Peru. Practical measures including standardization of ultrasound-guided clinical 373 
management, secondary CE prevention measures, and follow-up could decrease the CE 374 
burden linked to clinical care, improving outcomes and decreasing costs. Ultimately, the 375 
contextual ecological cycle of transmission must be established in Sicuani, as only an 376 
integrated control at the source of infection involving interventions such as hand hygiene, 377 
secure disposal of offal, avoidance of dog feeding offal, and deworming of dogs can reduce 378 
transmission and lead to elimination. This requires a One Health approach and a socio-379
