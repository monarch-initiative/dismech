# A gene therapy approach to prevent dilated intercellular space, a hallmark of gastroesophageal reflux disease.
**Authors:** Schiralli Lester GM, Huang J, Barravecchia M, Whigham LN, Hooker JM, de Mesy Bentley KL, Young JL, Zhou Z, Dean DA
**Journal:** Gene Ther (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41434-026-00634-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41434-026-00634-0)

## Content

The incidence of esophageal disease ranging from gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) to cancer has dramatically risen over the last 10 years. However, there have been limited descriptions of effective gene delivery methods for the esophagus. We developed a simple and effective electroporation-based gene delivery procedure to target the distal esophagus in living animals. We have used this approach to prevent formation of dilated intercellular space (DIS), a pathological feature of GERD, which is characterized by disruption of tight junctions and loss of epithelial barrier function. We found that tight junction proteins and the Na+,K+-ATPase β1 subunit were decreased in biopsies from patients with GERD. Transfection of esophageal epithelial cells with plasmids expressing the Na+,K+-ATPase β1 subunit increased levels of tight junction proteins and transepithelial electrical resistance. Electroporation-mediated gene delivery to the distal esophagus resulted in no injury in either rat or rabbit models. Gene expression was detected within 2 days in the esophageal mucosa and persisted for at least 12 weeks. DIS developed by 12 weeks in a rabbit GERD model and was reduced by 70% when the Na+,K+-ATPase β1 subunit was delivered. These results are encouraging for future development of gene-based treatments for GERD and its downstream consequences.

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) affects almost one third of adults in United States and Western countries. This disease is characterized by a broad spectrum of typical symptoms, such as heartburn and acid regurgitation, and extra-esophageal manifestations, including asthma, chronic cough and laryngitis [1,2]. In addition, those with chronic GERD have a 13–20% life-time risk of progression to Barrett’s esophagus (BE) with a resulting 11 to 40-fold increased risk for development of esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) [3,4]. Although proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are currently the most effective treatment for GERD and its complications [5], up to 45% of patients with GERD remain symptomatic on standard therapy and the incidence of EAC has also increased 600-700% in the US and other Western Countries over the last 20–30 years [6]. In addition, long term use of PPIs can cause severe side effects including chronic kidney disease and myocardial infarction [7,8]. There is an urgent need to develop new methods to treat PPI refractory GERD, which could decrease occurrence of BE and EAC.

Dilated intercellular space (DIS) is a hallmark pathological feature of the esophageal squamous epithelium in individuals with GERD [9]. In reflux disease, gastro-duodenal contents regurgitate into the distal esophagus. In addition to stomach acid, bile acids are a major constituent of the gastro-esophageal refluxate, which causes formation of DIS and loss of barrier function through epithelial injury and disruption of cell junctions in the rabbit and human esophagus [10,11,12]. Studies in several animal models have shown that a number of proteins in the apical junctional complexes (AJC), comprised of tight junctions, adherens junctions, and desmosomes, decrease in abundance in the lower esophageal squamous epithelium with GERD and likely contribute to the increased paracellular permeability and DIS observed [13,14,15,16]. Indeed, decreased tight junctions in the squamous epithelium and DIS are an early and important feature of both erosive and nonerosive forms of GERD [17,18,19,20,21]. One consequence of the reduced barrier function and increased intercellular space in the esophageal squamous epithelium is that bile acids and other reflux constituents continually bathe the cells and decrease the intercellular pH, resulting in cell injury, inflammation, and in turn, tissue repair. Basal progenitor cells underlie the squamous epithelium and the adjacent metaplastic and transitional epithelium, and it is believed that these cells, in response to continual exposure to bile acids and reflux constituents, generate BE and lead to EAC. The prevailing paradigm is that if the barrier function of this tissue can be restored, cell injury, inflammation, and aberrant repair can be avoided, abrogating columnar epithelial metaplasia, BE, and EAC [22,23].

AJCs are multifunctional complexes that play many crucial roles in epithelial development and differentiation by providing adhesion, diffusion barrier, polarity and signaling functions, as well as maintain cell polarity and the diffusion of fluid between the apical and basolateral surfaces. Intriguingly, the tight junction proteins Claudin-1 and -4 have been shown to be significantly decreased in GERD patients and may form the mechanistic basis for DIS [13]. High dose deoxycholic acid and trypsin decreased both the electrical resistance of esophageal epithelium and expression of claudin-3, -4 and the adherens junction protein E-cadherin, which indicate that bile acids have the potential to disrupt esophageal barrier function by modulating AJC proteins [24]. As with several of the claudins, E-cadherin levels are reduced (in this case by cleavage) in squamous epithelium of patients with GERD and DIS [25]. Further, the disassembly or disappearance of desmosomes has also been associated with DIS formation in humans, animals, and cell models [26]. We have hypothesized that by restoring levels of these AJC proteins in the distal esophagus, we may be able to modulate development of DIS and GERD symptoms.

Unfortunately, there are no pharmacological drugs to upregulate individual tight or adherens junction genes or promote AJC formation and function. Gene therapy may provide an alternative approach to achieve this goal. In studies aimed at developing a gene therapy for acute lung injury, we have found that the regulatory β1 subunit of the Na+,K+-ATPase leads to increased abundance and function at the membrane of multiple tight and adherens junction proteins in the lung as well as increased barrier function [27]. As such, we sought to deliver this gene to the esophagus and ask whether it was able to mediate the same activity, thereby reducing DIS in appropriate model systems. However, there has been very little progress on developing methods for gene transfer to the esophagus, with only three publications reporting on the use of liposomes or adeno-associated virus, neither of which reported robust gene transfer and expression [28,29,30].

We have developed an approach using electric fields that effectively delivers genes to multiple cell layers in tissues without inducing damage to the tissue or surface cell layers of the lung and other internal organs [31,32]. The method requires delivery of DNA, typically in plasmid form, to the tissue to be transfected followed immediately by the application of a series of electric pulses. In this report, we have used this methodology to transfer the β1 subunit of the Na+,K+-ATPase to the living esophagus and found that its overexpression increases AJC expression and reduces DIS in a rabbit model of GERD.

Plasmids pCMV-GFP-DTS, pCMV-GFP-β1, and pCMV-lux-DTS express GFP, a GFP fusion protein with the β1 subunit of the Na+,K+-ATPase, and luciferase, respectively, from the CMV immediate early promoter (CMViep) and contain a DNA nuclear targeting sequence downstream of the transgene [33]. pUbC-lux, pUbC-GFP and pUbC-GFP-β1 express luciferase, GFP, and a fusion protein of GFP with the β1 subunit of the Na+,K+-ATPase, respectively, from the human Ubiquitin C (UbC) promoter and contain a DNA nuclear targeting sequence downstream of the transgene [34]. Plasmids were purified using Qiagen Giga-prep kits (Qiagen) and suspended in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and 140 mM NaCl.

Lentiviral particles were produced in Hek293T cells using the Lenti-vpak Lentiviral packaging Kit (Origene) along with a Lenti ORF clone of mGFP tagged Human ATP1B1 (Origene, RC200500L4) or a control mGFP tagged plasmid (Origene, PS100093). EPC1 cells, an immortalized primary esophageal cell line were a gift from Dr. Anil Rustgi (University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA, USA) [35]. EPC1 cells were transduced using 200ul of concentrated GFP-β1 Lentivirus or control mGFPvirus via spinoculation for 1 h at 1200xg at 25°C with 10ug Polybrene/ml (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-134220) [36]. The transduced EPC1 cells were plated (5×105cells) onto Transwell Permeable Supports and cultured in KSFM media supplemented with 50ug/ml Bovine Pituitary Extract and 1 ng/ml human recombinant EGF and Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco, Life Technologies 37000-015). Cultures were incubated at 37°C in 5%CO2for 48 h after which protein lysates were made using 1x Reporter lysis buffer (Promega) for Western blot analysis.

TEER was measured in transfected cultures of EPC1 cells grown on transwell dishes (n= 3) as previously described [37].

Sample size rationale was based on our prior experiments developing electroporation in the lung and for transfer and expression of β1 in this organ [27,31,38]. Since gene transfer of β1 to the lung results in 50% to 500% changes in various outcomes, the effect size is large. Using an on-line calculator, a detection power of 90% and a significance level of 0.05, a sample size of 3-4 animals per condition was estimated.

Female Sprague Dawley Rats (200–300 g) were fasted for 16 h and then given SR Buprenorphine 2 h prior to surgery. Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane administered with O2and the surgical site was shaved and cleaned and a ~ 5 cm midline abdominal incision was made from the xiphoid cartilage to the pubic bone. The upper lobes of the liver were exteriorized to expose the stomach which was manipulated to expose the distal esophagus and gastric junction. The distal esophagus was draped into a tube electrode, parallel to the conducting wires, bathed in 500 µl of plasmid DNA (2 mg/ml) in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA and 140 mM NaCl, and electroporated at 200 Volts/cm using 8 square wave pulses 10msec in length and 1 s apart. The tube electrode was removed and the organs were returned into the body cavity. Sterile phosphate-buffered saline was used to moisten the organs and the muscle layer and skin were each closed with absorbable sutures. Normal housing and feeding were resumed post-surgery. Animals were monitored closely for any sign of pain or discomfort for 48 h post-surgery and weekly until the end of the experiment.

Female New Zealand White rabbits (2.5–4 kg) were fasted for 16 h and then anesthetized by intramuscular injection of Ketamine 20 mg/kg, Dexmedetomidine 0.05 mg/kg, Meloxicam 0.6 mg/kg and Bupicaine 2 mg/kg. A Laryngeal mask was placed at the epiglottis and the rabbits were maintained on 1.5–3.5% isoflurane administered with O2for the duration of surgery. Rabbits were prepped for surgery and vitals were monitored and recorded throughout the duration of the procedure. An 8 cm midline abdominal incision was made and the liver was retracted to expose the esophageal gastric junction and stomach. The partial cardiomyectomy was performed, stripping 270° of muscle from ~2 cm of the distal esophagus using blunt dissection (n= 5 for each condition), as previously described [15,39]. In animals that also received gene transfer, the muscle-stripped distal esophagus was then draped into a tube electrode, bathed in 2 mg/ml plasmid DNA (n = 5 per plasmid) and electroporated at 200 V/cm, with eight pulses of 10 ms duration separated by 1 s each while plasmid DNA was continually dripped onto the exposed esophagus tissue to maintain an electrode filled with DNA solution. The electrode was removed and the muscle layer and skin were each closed using absorbable sutures. The rabbits were given SR Buprenex to manage pain and discomfort and were monitored multiple times daily for the first 3 days post surgery and then throughout the duration of the 12 week study for weight loss and outward signs of disease. While two of the animals lost 15% body weight and were supplemented by hand feeding until they regained their pre-operative weight, none of the other treated animals lost more than 10% body weight and recovered without incident. The person carrying out surgery and electroporation was blinded to the DNAs being added in any animal and the GFP and β1 DNAs were delivered in a randomized manner. Experiments were repeated once.

Rat distal esophagus (~2.5 cm) was collected at the indicated times post electroporation, weighed, frozen in liquid nitrogen, disassociated using a Tissue Pulverizer (BioSpec) in 1x Reporter Lysis buffer (Promega), and luciferase expression was measured as described in duplicate technical replicates [27].

As can be seen in Fig.1A, distal esophageal tissue from normal individuals shows a very clear stratified epithelium with little to no space between cells toward the luminal surface of the tissue. By contrast, in patients with severe GERD (Fig.1B), the intercellular junction appears dilated, the hallmark of the classic DIS phenotype. Long term GERD patients will progress to intestinal metaplasia (Barrett’s esophagus, BE, Fig.1C) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC, Fig.1D). The studies in several animal models have shown that DIS likely related with a number of proteins in the apical junctional complexes (AJC) decrease in abundance in the lower esophageal squamous epithelium with GERD [13,14,15,16,40]. While several studies have also shown reduction in tight junction abundance in biopsies from patients with GERD, BE, and EAC, all of these have focused solely on the expression of claudins, including claudin 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7 [12,13]. We stained sections from biopsies of normal and GERD patients for several tight (ZO-1 and occludin) and adherens (α-catenin and E-cadherin) junction proteins (Fig.2). Levels of all proteins are abundant in control normal samples, with the classic chicken wire staining pattern, indicating membrane localization of the complexes and continuous circumnavigation of the cells. By contrast, in biopsies from patients with GERD, staining of all four proteins is greatly reduced (Figs.2A and2B). In addition, for ZO-1, α-catenin and occludin staining, not only is the level of expression reduced, but the expression patterns of these proteins also changed to more punctate and intracellular or perinuclear, indicative of possible junctional complex disassembly, endocytosis and degradation. However, the reduced E-Cadherin level in GERD patients remains at the membrane, which may indicate a different functional change of E-cadherin.

Since gene transfer of the Na+,K+-ATPase β1 subunit increases levels of multiple AJC proteins at the same time in the lung and has been shown to be downregulated in diseases of barrier dysfunction, we assessed its levels in biopsies from GERD patients [27,41]. Levels of the β1 subunit were indeed decreased in GERD biopsies compared to healthy samples, as was MRCKα, a key the β1-interacting protein that mediates β1’s ability to upregulate abundance and activity of apical junction complexes (Figs.2A and2B) [37,42]. We next tested whether overexpression of β1 subunit could also be used in the esophageal epithelium to prevent barrier disruption seen in the transition to GERD. EPC1 esophageal epithelial cells were transduced with lentivirus particles expressing either GFP or a GFP-β1 fusion protein from the CMViepand analyzed two days later for expression of different tight junction proteins by Western blot (Fig.3A). Overexpression of the GFP-β1 subunit fusion protein led to increased expression of ZO-1, occludin, and claudins 1, 3, 4, and 18, similar to results we have seen using pulmonary epithelial and endothelial cells [27]. When monolayers of EPC1 cells were transfected with plasmids expressing the β1 subunit (in this case, not as a GFP fusion protein), transmembrane electrical resistance of the monolayers increased compared to cells transfected with GFP as a control (Fig.3B).

Our goal was to deliver the β1 gene to the intact esophagus and evaluate its ability to improve barrier function in vivo, but unfortunately, very few methods have been reported for gene transfer to this tissue. We have previously developed successful protocols for effective gene delivery to the vasculature and lungs using electroporation and applied these approaches to the rat esophagus [31,33]. Gene transfer is achieved by performing an abdominal mid-line incision to isolate the distal region of the esophagus, gastroesophageal junction and proximal stomach. The esophagus is externally bathed in 1 mg of plasmid DNA in saline and eight square wave electric pulses (200 Volts/cm) of 10 ms duration are delivered 1 s apart. Pulses are delivered with an electrode resembling a tube in which 1.5 cm of esophagus is draped and bathed in DNA solution with a wire running down each side of the device. The transfected esophagus is returned to the abdomen and the incision is closed (Fig.4A). Normal housing and feeding were resumed post-surgery and animals showed no sign of pain, discomfort, altered eating, or altered digestion following treatment.

To evaluate gene transfer and expression, we electroporated the distal esophagus with a plasmid carrying the CMViepdriving a Luciferase reporter gene and 48 h later measured between 11 and 110 pg Luciferase/g wet weight tissue (n= 7; Fig.4B). To determine the distribution and expression of the transgene, we similarly electroporated the distal esophagus with plasmid expressing GFP from the CMViep. Tissue was collected from the animals 48 h post-electroporation for paraffin embedding and GFP immunohistochemistry. GFP expression was identified throughout the full thickness of the esophageal mucosa and muscularis mucosa (Fig.4C) indicated by the dark brown staining. Basal epithelial cells lining the interface of the mucosal and submucosal regions show the strongest expression of GFP signal (indicated by white arrows). Submucosal fibrous and vascular tissue showed focal weak GFP expression. Thus, electroporation-mediated gene transfer results in robust reporter gene expression throughout the layered mucosal tissue. Similar distribution of DNA delivery and expression throughout multiple layers of tissue following electroporation has been seen in a number of tissues, including the lung and vasculature [32,33].

To investigate the duration of gene expression, we tested both short- and long-acting promoters. The CMViepelicits rapid and robust transgene expression but is subjected to silencing over time through DNA methylation, typically after 5-7 days. By contrast, the UbC promoter has been shown to provide robust, durable transgene expression for up to a year in many tissues [43]. Following electroporation of the esophagus, we detected luciferase expression at 6 weeks (12.5–89 pg Luciferase/g wet weight,n= 3) and 12 weeks (7–990 pg Luciferase/g wet weight,n= 5) post-electroporation using plasmids carrying the UbC promoter that were comparable to or better than that seen at the height of CMV promoter-driven luciferase expression on day 2 (11–110 pg Luciferase/g wet weight tissue, n = 7).

To test whether gene transfer of the β1 subunit of the Na+,K+-ATPase could lead to increased tight junction protein expression in the intact esophagus as it did in cultured cells, we electroporated the rat esophagus with pCMV-GFP-β1 or control pCMV-GFP and evaluated expression of β1, occludin, and ZO-1 three days later (Fig.4D). Both endogenous β1 and the GFP-β1 fusion protein were upregulated in the esophagi of rats electroporated with the pCMV-GFP-β1 plasmid compared to pCMV-GFP alone. It has been documented that GFP fusion proteins are often proteolyzed to remove the GFP moiety and can lead to increased levels of the target protein, in this case the β1 subunit, accounting for the increased levels of “full length” β1 alone. In two of the three animals receiving the GFP-β1 plasmid, levels of occludin and ZO-1 are markedly increased, similar to that seen in cultured cells that had been transfected with the same plasmids (Fig.3). Moreover, expression of these proteins appeared to parallel the expression of the β1 subunit, with more ZO-1 and occludin present when β1 was most overexpressed.

To test whether upregulation of tight and adherens junction protein complexes and barrier function could reduce the severity of DIS in vivo, we used the rabbit cardiomyectomy model that develops moderate DIS within 12 weeks of injury [15,39]. Prior to asking this question however, we investigated whether the gene transfer process itself interfered at all with the disease model. In this model, approximately 270° of the muscle surrounding the distal esophagus at the gastro-esophageal junction is removed and the animal is recovered. To carry out gene transfer, immediately following removal of the muscle, ~1 cm of the dissected and stripped distal esophagus was placed into the tube electrode and electroporated in the presence of pUbC-GFP plasmid in saline; the control included bathing the dissected muscle-stripped distal esophagus in saline containing no DNA for 1 min but without the addition of electric pulses. Animals were then recovered without incident and displayed no to minimal difficulties or changes in eating and drinking. Twelve weeks later, the esophagus was removed and portions were processed for electron microscopy. As can be seen in Fig.5and Supplementary Fig.1, naïve animals show very little, if any, intercellular space between cells. While the membranes between cells are extremely ruffled, they abut each other along the entirety of the plasma membranes. By contrast, animals that received partial cardiomyectomy but no DNA transfer had pronounced dilated intercellular space between all cells in the sections (Fig.5). Similarly, animals subject to partial cardiomyectomy that also received the GFP-expressing plasmids pUbC-GFP by electroporation were indistinguishable from those that received only the cardiomyectomy procedure alone. When DIS between cells was quantified using the EM images by measuring the width between cells, it was found that the gap between cells in the naïve esophagus was approximately eight-fold greater in animals receiving cardiomyectomy compared to naïve animals (0.137 µm ± 0.032 µm vs. 1.068 µm ± 0.372 µm;p< 0.001; Fig.5B). Electroporation of GFP-expressing plasmids to the cardiomyectomy-treated animals had no significant effect on the amount of DIS in the animals (0.927 µm ± 0.261 µm), suggesting that the application of DNA and electroporation of non-therapeutic plasmids had no effect on the development of DIS in this model.

Since overexpression of the Na+,K+-ATPase β1 subunit increased barrier function in vitro and in the lung, we tested whether it could decrease DIS formation in the rabbit GERD model. Plasmids expressing either GFP or the GFP-β1 fusion protein from the UbC promoter were delivered to the distal esophagus by electroporation immediately following muscle removal during the partial cardiomyectomy procedure. Histological analysis of the animals 12 weeks later reveals that animals that underwent cardiomyectomy and received the GFP control plasmid had pronounced dilated intercellular space and basal cell proliferation in the distal esophagus compared to Naïve control animals (Figs.6and7and Supplementary Figs.2 and 3). By contrast, animals that underwent cardiomyectomy and received the GFP-β1 plasmid had significantly less DIS or basal cell proliferation compared to the naïve control animals, suggesting that expression of the β1 subunit largely prevented cardiomyectomy-induced DIS formation.

To further investigate the effects of injury and treatment on tight and adherens junctions, immunofluorescent staining was carried out. Naïve animals show abundant membrane staining of α-catenin and ZO-1, both in a chicken wire staining pattern, consistent with that seen for apical junctional complexes (Fig.6). Further, naïve animals show abundant membrane-localized staining of the β1 subunit. In animals that received partial cardoimyectomy and a non-therapeutic GFP control plasmid, all three proteins show reduced abundance (Fig.6B). By contrast, transfer of β1 plasmid completely abrogated the reduction of both apical junctional complex proteins, restoring the levels and localization seen in naïve animals. Overexpression of β1 also restored endogenous levels of the protein based on staining intensity and membrane localization.

Electron Microscopy (EM) was performed on esophageal tissue harvested at 12 weeks post gene transfer to evaluate formation and degree of DIS. Five out of 5 animals receiving cardiomyectomy and GFP control plasmid showed clear DIS (Fig.7and Supplementary Fig.3). By contrast, 4 out of 5 animals that received cardiomyectomy and GFP-β1 showed markedly reduced DIS throughout the distal esophagus and the remaining animal showed slight DIS, although of much less severity than its GFP control plasmid-receiving counterparts. DIS was measured as width between cells (Fig.7B). DIS increased 1400% following cardiomyectomy (0.065 µm ± 0.003 µm vs 0.917 µm ± 0.042 µm, mean ± sem), and treatment with the pUbC-GFP-β1 plasmid decreased DIS width by 70% (0.278 µm ± 0.069 µm). Similarly, quantitation of DIS by area between cells (as measured by the fraction of total cell area) showed an almost 2200% increase upon injury (0.171 ± 0.092 vs. 3.751 ± 0.980) and was decreased 87% with pUbC-β1 (0.500 ± 0.177)(Fig.7C). These data clearly demonstrate that expression of the β1 subunit greatly reduced the occurrence of DIS in the rabbit GERD model.

GERD is a prevalent condition, affecting approximately 18-28% of adults in the United States [44]. Despite the widespread use of anti-acid treatments, a significant proportion of GERD patients, ranging from 17% to 45%, continue to experience reflux symptoms, leading to the classification of refractory GERD [44]. Addressing the needs of these resistant patients is imperative, necessitating the development of alternative treatments. In conceptualizing GERD as a football game, the acid functions as the offense, while the esophageal mucosa serves as the defense. Current anti-acid therapies predominantly focus on mitigating the offensive aspect by reducing acid production. However, there is a notable absence of treatments targeting the defensive mechanism—specifically, the damage inflicted on the esophageal mucosa by acid reflux. Our innovative approach seeks to introduce a novel treatment strategy that bolsters the esophageal squamous mucosal barrier, thereby enhancing the defensive capacity.

Building upon our previous work in the lung, where overexpression of the β1 subunit of the Na+,K+-ATPase increases the levels of tight and adherens junction proteins at the epithelial cell membrane and improves barrier function in healthy and diseased animal models, we explored the application of this approach in treating in vitro and in vivo GERD models. Our initial observations confirmed the presence of DIS and alterations in several tight and adherent proteins, as well as greatly reduced levels of the Na+,K+-ATPase β1 subunit and MRCKα, in biopsies obtained from GERD patients and in a rabbit model of GERD characterized by severe DIS. The chronic exposure of the squamous epithelium in the lower esophagus to gastric refluxate is well-established as a causative factor in impairing and destroying cellular junctions, thereby compromising the barrier function and inducing DIS [9]. Our studies unveiled a reduction in the abundance of intercellular proteins, including α-catenin, E-cadherin, ZO-1, and Occludin, in the lower esophageal squamous epithelium of both GERD patients and the GERD rabbit model and the developing DIS identified by electronic microscope in the rabbit model. These findings suggest a contributory role in the increased paracellular permeability and DIS associated with GERD [13,14,15,16,40]. Importantly, the diminished tight junctions in the squamous epithelium and the presence of DIS emerge as early and crucial features in both erosive and nonerosive forms of GERD [17,18,19,20,21]. The manifestation of DIS and the reduction of intercellular proteins collectively support the establishment of a robust rabbit GERD model for further investigations.

With the rabbit GERD model, we aimed to extend the applicability of the electroporation approach previously utilized in the lung to the distal esophagus. Notably, our work represents the pioneering application of electroporation to achieve high-level gene delivery in the esophageal mucosa. Through this innovative method, we demonstrated the efficacy of gene transfer involving the β1 subunit of the Na + ,K + -ATPase to the distal esophagus in a rabbit model of GERD. This intervention effectively prevents the downregulation of apical junctional complex proteins and reduces the formation of DIS. Histological and electron microscopic analyses further revealed a significant reduction in DIS width by 70% and DIS overall area by 87% between cells, respectively, compared to the rabbit GERD model control group. Utilizing immunofluorescence, α-catenin, Na+,K+-ATPase β1 subunit, and ZO-1 were all decreased in the control group of the rabbit GERD model, but no reduction was observed in the group pre-electroporated with the β1 subunit, compared to the Naïve-control.

Previous studies have used both non-viral and viral gene delivery strategies in the esophagus with mixed results. The use of cationic-liposome DNA complexes delivered by luminal instillation to the rat esophagus yielded transgene expression in epithelial cells, although expression was limited to areas of the esophagus lacking intact keratin layers, suggesting that the liposomes were unable to penetrate the intact keratin layer of the epithelium, which acts as a functional barrier for gene transfer [28]. Another study used DNA-Liposome formulations in a phase I clinical trial to deliver the human MnSOD gene to the esophagus of patients who had inoperable, unresectable stage III non-small-cell lung cancer to protect the esophagus from damage induced by chemoradiotherapy [29]. However, no transgene expression was detected in esophageal biopsies, leading the authors to suggest that patchy distribution of the plasmid-liposome complexes after swallowing or incorrect biopsy sites may have been responsible for the lack of detectable expression. All of these challenges underscore the barriers to esophageal gene transfer and the limitations of current methods of delivery.

An Adenovirus associated virus (AAV) based viral gene delivery strategy has also been used to target the esophageal epithelium. Fibrin glue containing replication-deficient adenovirus was sprayed directly through a catheter onto a patch of squamous epithelium in the porcine esophagus. Transgene expression was detected in the treated area of the esophagus 24 h after transduction but was limited to the superficial cells on the mucosal surface [30]. The absence of basal cell delivery and transgene expression even at one day suggests that this approach may not be applicable for long-term expression, since the squamous epithelium turns over regularly, with new epithelial cells being continually generated from the basal cell progenitors. Thus, for long-lived gene expression within the esophagus, transgenes must be delivered deep within the epithelium to reach the basal cells. The fact that we detect penetration of transgene expression through the entire esophageal epithelium suggests that electroporation may be a better approach for gene delivery and long-term expression. Further investigation will need to be performed to test if transfection of the basal cell progenitors contributes to the longevity of transgene expression seen with the UbC promoter in our electroporation studies.

Two limitations of the current research reported here are the fact that our gene transfer method is an invasive procedure and that our studies represent protection experiments as opposed to treatment ones. While we have obtained robust gene delivery and expression following electroporation-mediated transfer of plasmid DNA to the esophagus, the method currently requires surgical exposure of the lower esophagus in order for gene transfer to be carried out. Based on the design of our electrode, we currently deliver DNA and the electric field to approximately a 1.5 cm segment of the rabbit or rat esophagus. By repositioning the electrode, we can electroporate multiple segments of the distal esophagus to achieve gene delivery in a greater area. Alternatively, a larger electrode covering a longer segment of the esophagus could achieve a similar outcome. To apply this to patients or larger animal models, both length and diameter of the electrode would simply be increased accordingly. One great advantage of electroporation is that it delivers DNA to multiple cell layers in a tissue, even when applied topically (as opposed to by injection) so distribution of gene delivery and expression is largely controlled by electrode dimensions and design. A continuing goal of our work is to translate this to a non-invasive approach. The most obvious way to achieve this would be to deliver the DNA solution by gavage, perhaps using a balloon catheter, and then apply the electric field using pacemaker pads placed on either side of the chest external to the location of the esophageal sphincter. The second limitation of this study is that we carried out gene delivery at the same time that we induced injury to the esophagus. Thus, the experiments showing lack of DIS formation in animals following transfer of the Na+,K+-ATPase β1 subunit are preventative rather than therapeutic. Any future application of this to reverse DIS formation would require transferring the β1 subunit after injury and formation of DIS. Based on our studies showing that gene transfer of the β1 subunit can reverse barrier breakdown in previously injured lungs, β1 is likely to act therapeutically in the esophagus as well to reverse DIS formation and strengthen the barrier function of the injured esophagus [27].
