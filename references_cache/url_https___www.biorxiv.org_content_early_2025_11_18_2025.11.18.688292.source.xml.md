hwp:id="p-4">Biallelic AAGGG expansions in <italic toggle="yes">Replication Factor Complex Subunit 1</italic> (<italic toggle="yes">RFC1)</italic> are associated with cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy, vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) and are increasingly recognised as a common cause of adult-onset ataxia and sensory neuropathy. However, the disease-causing mechanisms remain unclear. Here we leveraged <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> assays, post-mortem brain tissue, patient-derived cell lines and a neuronal <italic toggle="yes">Drosophila</italic> model to demonstrate that AAGGG expansions are associated with tissue-specific reductions in the expression of <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> transcript, along with impaired RFC1 function and increased sensitivity to DNA damage from platinum-based drugs. CRISPR/Cas9 excision of the AAGGG repeat and flanking AluSx3 element normalized <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression in iPSC-derived neurons and rescued the DNA damage response, providing a framework for future therapeutic strategies. We also show that these biological findings are clinically relevant in heterozygous AAGGG expansion carriers, who display an increased risk and severity of neuropathy with platinum-based rid="c48" hwp:id="xref-ref-48-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-48">Tagliapietra et al. 2021</xref>) in the ageing population.</p><p hwp:id="p-7">To date, the mechanism by which AAGGG repeat expansions in <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> cause neuronal dysfunction remains unclear. Initial studies did not show reduced <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression or changes in <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> mRNA processing in replicating cell lines and in post-mortem brain tissue from a CANVAS patient (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c11" hwp:id="xref-ref-11-2" hwp:rel-id="ref-11">Cortese et al. 2019</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c21" hwp:id="xref-ref-21-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-21">Gisatulin et al. 2020b</xref>). These findings were puzzling considering the recessive mode of inheritance of this condition.</p><p hwp:id="p-8">More recent studies have suggested AAGGG repeat-derived RNA foci (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c54" hwp:id="xref-ref-54-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-54">Wada et al. 2024</xref>) and peptides (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c35" hwp:id="xref-ref-35-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-35">Maltby et al. 2024</xref>) as possible contributors to the disease pathogenesis. Indeed, patient-derived cortical motor neurons were shown to exhibit defects in neuronal development, reduced expression of synaptic genes, and diminished synaptic connectivity, which were partly rescued by CRISPR deletion of a single AAGGG expanded repeat, but not by <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> supplementation (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c35" hwp:id="xref-ref-35-2" hwp:rel-id="ref-35">Maltby et al. 2024</xref>), implying that RFC1-independent mechanisms may drive CANVAS pathogenesis.</p><p hwp:id="p-9">Yet, the identification of several CANVAS patients carrying nonsense variants in <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> in <italic toggle="yes">trans</italic> with an expanded allele provide strong genetic evidence in support of a loss or reduced function model of the disease (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c5" hwp:id="xref-ref-5-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-5">Benkirane et al. 2022</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c45" hwp:id="xref-ref-45-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-45">Ronco et al. 2023</xref>). Similarly, point mutations <italic toggle="yes">in trans</italic> have been observed in Friedreich’s ataxia (FRDA), another intronic repeat expansion disorder, in which the loss of function of the encoded protein has been clearly demonstrated (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c9" hwp:id="xref-ref-9-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-9">Campuzano et al. 1996</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c12" hwp:id="xref-ref-12-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-12">Cossée et al. 1999</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c6" hwp:id="xref-ref-6-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-6">Bidichandani et al. 1998</xref>).</p><p hwp:id="p-10">Moreover, clinical onset and severity of CANVAS appear to be more strongly dependent on the size of the smaller of the two expanded alleles, which may suggest an effect of the expanded repeat on residual gene expression and function (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c13" hwp:id="xref-ref-13-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-13">Currò et al. 2023</xref>), as also shown in Friedreich’s ataxia (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c46" hwp:id="xref-ref-46-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-46">Saccà et al. 2011</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c43" hwp:id="xref-ref-43-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-43">Plasterer et al. 2013</xref>).</p><p hwp:id="p-11">RFC1 is the largest of the five subunits of the Replication Factor Complex (RFC), which is responsible for loading Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA) onto the double-helix molecule during DNA replication and repair (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c27" hwp:id="xref-ref-27-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-27">Indiani and O’Donnell 2006</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c37" hwp:id="xref-ref-37-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-37">Mossi et al. 1997</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c2" hwp:id="xref-ref-2-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-2">Allen et al. 1998</xref>). Cells that are primarily affected in CANVAS, including cerebellar and sensory neurons, appear to be particularly sensitive to DNA damage, as showed by their primary involvement in other diseases linked to mutations in genes involved in DNA repair, including <italic toggle="yes">ATM</italic> causing ataxia-telangiectasia, <italic toggle="yes">APTX</italic> and <italic toggle="yes">SETX</italic> causing ataxia-oculomotor apraxia, <italic toggle="yes">TDP1</italic> causing spinocerebellar ataxia with axonal neuropathy-1, and several genes associated with xeroderma pigmentosum (reviewed in <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c32" hwp:id="xref-ref-32-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-32">Kulkarni and Wilson 2008</xref>).</p><p hwp:id="p-12">Prompted by these observations, we used cellular and animal models, post-mortem brain tissue, and clinical/genetic data from CANVAS patients, to study the effect of biallelic AAGGG expansions on neuronal <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression and function. Additionally, given the high frequency of <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expansion carriers (8% of the European population) (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c26" hwp:id="xref-ref-26-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-26">Ibañez et al. 2024</xref>), we gathered evidence supporting a role for heterozygous AAGGG expansions as modifiers of the prevalence and severity of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy.</p></sec><sec id="s2" hwp:id="sec-2"><title hwp:id="title-4">RESULTS</title><sec id="s2a" hwp:id="sec-3"><title hwp:id="title-5">Pathogenic RFC1 repeats form stable parallel G-quadruplex structures</title><p hwp:id="p-13">To investigate how <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expansion may affect cellular functions and contribute to neurological disease, we first analysed the repeat sequence for distinctive structural features. Despite their fundamental impact in increasing survival of cancer patients, chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is a common and dose-limiting adverse effect of both cisplatin and oxaliplatin (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c7" hwp:id="xref-ref-7-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-7">Bonomo and Cavaletti 2021</xref>).</p><p hwp:id="p-80">Platinum-induced neuropathy is primarily a sensory neuronopathy, and as such it presents with similar clinical manifestations to CANVAS (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c47" hwp:id="xref-ref-47-2" hwp:rel-id="ref-47">Staff et al. 2019</xref>). Obviously, we could not directly assess whether the detrimental effect of platinum compounds observed in CANVAS cell lines and <italic toggle="yes">Drosophila</italic> also occurs in CANVAS patients by giving them platinum drugs. However, under a theoretical loss-of-function model, it would be expected that each pathogenic expanded allele leads to reduced <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression and function, and therefore that heterozygous AAGGG expansion carriers may be more susceptible to CIPN compared to non-carriers. To test this hypothesis, we leveraged an international cohort of 361 colorectal cancer patients treated with oxaliplatin and assessed the association between <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expansion and presence and severity of the CIPN.</p><p hwp:id="p-81">We selected only patients without a previous history of neuropathy, no prior exposure to chemotherapy, and who had received a cumulative dose of at least 1,000 mg of oxaliplatin. All selected patients were clinically graded by a trained neurologist using the validated clinical version of the Total Neuropathy Score scale (TNSc) and genetically tested for the presence of one AAGGG expanded allele. Importantly, clinical teams were blind to patient <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> genotypes at the time of neurological assessment. We identified 34 heterozygous AAGGG expansion carriers (9.4%), in line with the expected allele frequency in the population, and no individuals carrying biallelic AAGGG expansions. Whereas 82% (269/327) of non-carriers developed any degree of CIPN (TNSc &gt; 0), 100% (34/34) carriers of AAGGG alleles were found to have some degree of clinically detectable neuropathy (P=0.003) (<bold>Table 1</bold>). Moreover, clinically relevant neuropathy (TNSc &gt; 7) was identified significantly more frequently in AAGGG expansion carriers relative to non-carriers (70.6% vs. 45.3%, P=0.006). Using logistic regression to correct for effects of age, sex, and centre, we confirmed that <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expansions were independently associated with a higher risk of developing clinically relevant neuropathy (OR: 2.86; CI 95%: 1.32-6.21; P=0.008).</p><table-wrap id="tbl1" orientation="portrait" position="float" hwp:id="T1"><object-id pub-id-type="other" hwp:sub-type="pisa">biorxiv;2025.11.18.688292v1/TBL1</object-id><object-id pub-id-type="other" hwp:sub-type="slug">T1</object-id><object-id pub-id-type="publisher-id">tbl1</object-id><label>Table 1</label><graphic xlink:href="688292v1_tbl1" position="float" orientation="portrait" hwp:id="graphic-7"/></table-wrap><p hwp:id="p-82">In conclusion, heterozygous AAGGG expansion carriers appear to be at increased risk of developing CIPN when treated with oxaliplatin and, when affected, tend to experience more severe symptoms and signs. These observations in post-mortem brain tissue and patient-derived cell lines were further supported by <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> studies showing the propensity of pathogenic AAGGG repeats to form G-quadruplexes, and the reduced transcription from AAGGG-containing templates, with more pronounced effects at longer repeat lengths and increased G-content of the pathogenic repeat motif.</p><p hwp:id="p-84">The initial observation of an apparent lack of reduction of <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> mRNA or protein in replicating cell lines (and one post-mortem brain) has puzzled researchers since the first identification of this novel repeat, challenging the paradigm linking recessive diseases to loss of function of the disease-causing gene. Our study offers a comprehensive explanation for this conundrum. Unlike Friedreich ataxia, where biallelic intronic GAA repeat expansions in <italic toggle="yes">FXN</italic> lead to a widespread and pronounced reduction of FXN (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c9" hwp:id="xref-ref-9-2" hwp:rel-id="ref-9">Campuzano et al. 1996</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c6" hwp:id="xref-ref-6-2" hwp:rel-id="ref-6">Bidichandani et al. 1998</xref>), in CANVAS <bold>1)</bold> expression of <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> appears to be only mildly reduced (∼20%), <bold>2)</bold> the effect of the repeat on gene expression is highly tissue-specific, as it could only be identified in affected cerebellum, but not in other unaffected brain regions or peripheral tissues, and <bold>3)</bold> in cell lines, a significant reduction of <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> transcript was only detected using biallelic isogenic lines differentiated into sensory or motor neurons, which minimised variability in <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression arising from replication and differences in the genetic background of non-isogenic lines.</p><p hwp:id="p-85">It is perhaps not surprising that a large reduction of <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> is not detectable, as complete RFC1 knockout is incompatible with life, both at the cellular level and in animal models, including mice (<ext-link l:rel="related" l:ref-type="uri" l:ref="https://www.informatics.jax.org/allele/MGI:5495286" ext-link-type="uri" xlink:href="https://www.informatics.jax.org/allele/MGI:5495286" hwp:id="ext-link-2">https://www.informatics.jax.org/allele/MGI:5495286</ext-link>), <italic toggle="yes">Drosophila</italic> (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c50" hwp:id="xref-ref-50-2" hwp:rel-id="ref-50">Tsuchiya et al. 2007</xref>), and a recent zebrafish model, where global <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> knockdown led to early lethality along with abnormal cerebellar development (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c39" hwp:id="xref-ref-39-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-39">Nobilleau et al. 2025</xref>). Importantly, we demonstrate that a ∼50% reduction of <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> in <italic toggle="yes">Drosophila</italic> neurons is sufficient to reproduce the late-onset motor phenotype observed in CANVAS patients, which was accompanied by progressive DNA damage accumulation in the brain.</p><p hwp:id="p-86">The basis for the marked tissue specificity of RFC1 downregulation in CANVAS remains unclear. Possible explanations include <bold>1)</bold> an interaction between the <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expansion and cerebellum-specific transcription factors, enhancers or chromatin accessibility pattern – potentially also common to sensory and vestibular neurons – an interpretation further supported by the observation that the repeat associated haplotype acts as a cerebellum-specific eQTL for <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression, <bold>2)</bold> the intense transcriptional, metabolic and electrical activity of these highly active cells, leading to the accumulation of DNA damage and creating a demand for RFC1 that exceeds the capacity of the transcriptional machinery to supply it, and <bold>3)</bold> a possible somatic expansion of the AAGGG repeat in this specific cell type, as showed in affected medium spiny neurons in Huntington’s disease (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c23" hwp:id="xref-ref-23-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-23">Handsaker et al. 2025</xref>), further decreasing <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression in single cells.</p><p hwp:id="p-87">The most important implication of our study is for the development of therapies for CANVAS patients. Indeed, biallelic excision of the repeat was sufficient to increase <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression by ∼20%, which matches the reduction observed in patient cerebellum. To this regard it is worth noting that, up to this time, only heterozygous corrected lines could be generated, leading to the hypothesis that the AluSx3 element and/or the flanking tandem repeat play an essential role in <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression and for cell survival (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c16" hwp:id="xref-ref-16-1" hwp:rel-id="ref-16">Davies et al. 2023</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c35" hwp:id="xref-ref-35-5" hwp:rel-id="ref-35">Maltby et al. 2024</xref>). However, here we show that complete excision of both repeat and AluSx3 element is tolerated and appears safe, at least at the cellular level.</p><p hwp:id="p-88">While we cannot currently rule out that additional mechanisms may contribute to CANVAS pathology, including repeat-derived peptides, RNA foci, or broader transcriptional changes impacting neuronal function (<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c54" hwp:id="xref-ref-54-2" hwp:rel-id="ref-54">Wada et al. 2024</xref>; <xref ref-type="bibr" rid="c35" hwp:id="xref-ref-35-6" hwp:rel-id="ref-35">Maltby et al. 2024</xref>), our data suggest that therapies aimed at increasing <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression/function may represent reasonable approaches, warranting further investigation.</p><p hwp:id="p-89">Finally, our study has implications for other common diseases, particularly CIPN. Although carriers of the AAGGG expansion do not typically develop neuropathy or ataxia within their natural lifespan, our data demonstrate that they have a significantly increased risk of developing moderate-to-severe neuropathy when treated with oxaliplatin for an underlying malignancy. Importantly, since oxaliplatin does not effectively cross the blood–brain barrier, it is unlikely to exert comparable toxic effects on Purkinje cells in the cerebellum. CIPN remains the leading cause of discontinuation of life-saving platinum-based chemotherapy in patients with high-prevalence cancers, such as colorectal cancer. While our findings suggest a potential clinical utility for <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> genotyping prior to the initiation of platinum-based chemotherapy to stratify neuropathy risk and possibly guide dose modification or treatment scheduling, independent validation in larger cohorts is required before clinical implementation.</p><p hwp:id="p-90">As a first-in-kind study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Due to the modest reduction of <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> mRNA observed in both post-mortem brain tissue and CANVAS cell lines, combined with the suboptimal performance of commercially available anti-RFC1 antibodies, we were unable to demonstrate a significant effect of the repeat expansion on RFC1 protein. Furthermore, the observation of an increased sensitivity of CANVAS cell lines to DNA damage was limited to testing of platinum-based drugs. A more comprehensive study assessing multiple types of DNA damage is therefore warranted to elucidate the intermediate steps linking reduced <italic toggle="yes">RFC1</italic> expression to DNA damage accumulation and subsequent neurodegeneration.</p></sec><sec id="s4" hwp:id="sec-11"><title hwp:id="title-19">METHODS</title><sec id="s4a" hwp:id="sec-12"><title hwp:id="title-20">Determination of the propensity of the AAGGG repeat to form G-quadruplexes</title><p hwp:id="p-91">Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were acquired on a J-1500 Spectropolarimeter (Jasco Corporation, Easton, MD, USA) equipped with a Peltier temperature controller set at 20°C. The measurements were performed in a 1 cm quartz cuvette using synthetic oligonucleotides 5’-(AAAAG)<sub>8</sub>-3’ and 5’-(AAGGG)<sub>8</sub>-3’. CD spectra were recorded over the 210 to 340 nm wavelength at a scan speed of 200 nm/min, with a 2s response time and a 1 nm bandwidth. Each spectrum represents the average of four scans and was baseline-corrected to account for contributions from the buffer and chloride salts. CD signals were expressed as Δε (M ¹cm ¹) as a function of wavelength, calculated using the formula Δε = / (32980 × C × l), where represents the observed ellipticity in millidegrees, C is the DNA molar concentration, and l is the path length in centimeters (1 cm).
