# Genetic Risk Factors in Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus: What We Know and What Is Next.
**Authors:** Piccinin CC, Anis S, Yu JRT, Salles PA, Chaparro-Solano HM, Kundrick A, Ivary S, Liao JY, Nagel SJ, Mata IF
**Journal:** Mov Disord (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mds.30206](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.30206)

## Content

Knowledge of the genetic factors in normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) is rapidly evolving, with significant advances in recent years. We conducted a systematic review examining genetic contributions to NPH risk. Ovid Embase, Ovid Medline, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central were searched from inception through October 14, 2024, for human studies in English reporting familial NPH cases, genetic variants associated with NPH, and associations with other neurogenetic disorders and exploring transcriptomics. Studies on secondary, obstructive, and congenital hydrocephalus were excluded, and findings were reported narratively. Of 2562 titles and abstracts screened, 56 met inclusion criteria, predominantly involving European populations. More than 30 familial cases were identified, and two cohorts found that 10%-16% of patients with NPH had relatives with NPH symptoms. Whole-genome/exome sequencing, copy-number variant analyses, and genome-wide association studies showed risk variants enriched in NPH cohorts in or near CFAP43, SFMBT1, CWH43, AK9, RXFP2, PRKD1, HAVCR1, OTOG, MYO7A, NOTCH1, SPG11, MYH13, FOXJ1, AMZ1/GNA12, and C16orf95, alongside protective variants near SLCO1A2 and MLLT10. These genes are associated with blood-brain and blood-cerebrospinal fluid barriers, cilia, and ependymal function. In addition, higher rates of pathological C9orf72 repeat expansions were observed in an NPH cohort compared with controls. NPH was also more prevalent in frontotemporal dementia cohorts without this expansion and co-occurred with myotonic dystrophy type 1 in several cases. Despite heterogeneity in outcome measures, this review highlights the genetic contribution to NPH risk. Future research should encourage collaborations for big data generation, identify genetic variants addressing diversity, and integrate clinical, environmental, and shunt-response data. © 2025 The Author(s). Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH), first described in 1965, is characterized by gait instability, cognitive decline, and impaired bladder control, collectively known as the Hakim triad.
1
, 
2
 It is currently divided into primary NPH, with no identifiable cause, and secondary NPH, linked to previous intracranial insults.

The diagnosis of primary NPH is guided by two major criteria sets. The Japanese guideline categorizes NPH as possible, probable, and definite, whereas the International guideline classifies it as unlikely, possible, and probable.
3
, 
4
 “Unlikely NPH” refers to cases where symptoms are better explained by other causes. For “possible NPH,” the Japanese guideline requires at least two symptoms from the triad, excluding atypical or secondary features, whereas the International guideline requires at least one symptom, which may be accompanied by atypical features. For “probable NPH,” both guidelines require at least two symptoms (particularly gait disturbance), normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) opening pressure, and specific neuroimaging features (or symptom improvement after a CSF drainage test in the Japanese guideline). “Definite NPH” refers to individuals who improve after shunt surgery.

The prevalence of NPH increases with age, ranging from 0.003% in individuals aged 50–59 years
5
 to 5.9% in those aged 80 years and older.
6
 Martín‐Láez et al
7
 suggested that 60 years could be considered the threshold for a population “at risk for NPH,” given its prevalence is 15‐fold higher in the seventh decade of life compared with the sixth. Although familial NPH has been underexplored, 4.8% of patients with NPH reported a relative who underwent shunt placement
8
 and approximately 7% of first‐degree relatives exhibit two or more NPH triad symptoms.
9
 Similarly, the genetic factors contributing to NPH risk or protection remain poorly understood and have not been systematically investigated.

In this context, the aim of this systematic review is to assess the contribution of genetics to NPH risk by analyzing the existing literature on familial cases, genetic variants linked to primary NPH, and co‐occurring neurogenetic disorders.

A comprehensive search was conducted following PRISMA reporting guidelines
10
 with a medical librarian (S.I.) designing the research strategy for the databases: Ovid Embase, Ovid Medline, Web of Science Core Collection, and Cochrane Central. These databases were searched from their inception (exact dates available in the Supporting Information) to October 14, 2024 (Fig. 1), using truncation, adjacency, Boolean, and indexing terms. The search strategy used for Medline was “Hydrocephalus, Normal Pressure [MESH] or (Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus or hakim*syndrome*or nphs or nph or normotensive hydrocephalus) [keyword] AND exp Family or exp Inheritance Patterns or exp Paternal Inheritance or exp Genes or exp Genetic Variation or exp Mutation or exp Genetics [MESH] or (famil*or inherit*or genetic*or variant*or mutat*or gene or geno*) [keyword] or (genetics adj1 (medical or human or population)) [keyword].” An equivalent search strategy was applied for the other databases as described in the Supporting Information. A manual review of the references was also conducted. The citations were uploaded into Covidence,
11
 where they were managed, screened, and deduplicated.

By NPH, we referred to studies whose subjects met the criteria outlined in existing diagnostic guidelines, as detailed in the introduction. Studies published before these guidelines were included if the authors identified subjects as having idiopathic/primary NPH with slowly progressive symptoms from the triad and accompanying communicating hydrocephalus. Manuscripts describing cases more likely attributable to other neurological conditions or involving obstructive, congenital, or arrested childhood hydrocephalus were excluded, along with review articles, guidelines, conference abstracts, and consensus statements.

Two authors (C.C.P. and S.A.) independently screened the titles and abstracts uploaded to Covidence, resolving inconsistencies through discussion until consensus was reached. Subsequently, the same authors reviewed and screened the full‐text articles for inclusion and data extraction. Disagreements were discussed, with a third author (I.F.M.) consulted for unresolved cases. A narrative synthesis approach was used to summarize the findings from the selected studies. Data were categorized into the following themes: familial NPH, whole‐exome and whole‐genome sequencing (WES/WGS), copy‐number variation studies (CNV), genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) and Mendelian randomization studies, candidate gene approach, and transcriptomic studies.

A meta‐analysis was not conducted because of the heterogeneity in study designs, settings, methodologies, and outcomes. For each manuscript, the number of subjects and controls analyzed, ethnicity/race, diagnostic probability, and shunt responsiveness were reported when available. Tables were created to summarize study characteristics and findings, and a figure depicting the main genetic results was generated using features of BioRender,
12
 then finalized and exported in Adobe Illustrator.
13

Of the 56 articles included in the review, 12 were categorized under “familial aggregation,” which includes reports of familial cases and a higher incidence of triad symptoms among NPH relatives. Although NPH has traditionally been classified into primary and secondary forms, these findings support the hypothesis that NPH may result from a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors, rather than fitting neatly into distinct categories. This introduces a nondichotomous model of etiology, similar to other neurological conditions such as Parkinson's disease, where genetic influences span a spectrum from predominantly genetic to largely environmental. In this context, genetic factors may also play a role in secondary NPH, albeit possibly to a lesser extent, explaining why not all patients develop secondary forms after intracranial insults.

This review identified that 10% (2/20) of NPH probands in a predominantly White non‐Hispanic cohort had a first‐degree relative with two or more NPH symptoms. Among relatives of NPH probands, 7.1% (10/140) had two or more symptoms, compared with 0.7% (1/151) of control relatives (odds ratio [OR], 11.53), with NPH relatives showing symptoms at a younger age.

Although half of the familial aggregation studies did not report ethnicity, those that did demonstrated that familial cases occur worldwide, including Finnish, Saudi, non‐Hispanic White, and Japanese families. Data on ethnicity are also presented in Table 1.

Of the 12 earlier familial aggregation studies, only 1 conducted WES/WGS. Notably, most were published before the 2020s, whereas WES/WGS studies on NPH have emerged since, with 5 published to date. In that one family, WES followed by Sanger sequencing identified a pathogenic heterozygous loss‐of‐function (LOF) variant in exon 35 of the CFAP43 gene (OMIM*617558) in the two affected individuals tested and in none of the three unaffected individuals tested. The affected members of this Japanese family included a female proband, two of her seven siblings, and their mother. It is unclear whether any family members underwent shunt surgery. Age of symptoms onset in these individuals ranged from 44 to 55 years. Notably, at least two affected individuals had recurrent respiratory infections and chronic sinusitis. CFAP43 encodes a protein involved in cilia and flagella function, possibly linked to these comorbidities. In a mouse model, the introduction of a 14‐bp deletion in exon 35 of this gene caused protein truncation, reduced expression, and ventricular dilation consistent with hydrocephalus.
22



WES was also performed in 53 unrelated, shunt‐responsive NPH individuals of unspecified ethnicity.
24
, 
25
 The authors reported only variants predicted to be damaging by at least two in silico algorithms. These variants were identified in 58% of patients and occurred in one or more of the following genes: CWH43 (OMIM*618561), AK9 (OMIM*615358), RXFP2 (OMIM*606655), PRKD1 (OMIM*605435), HAVCR1 (OMIM*606518), OTOG (OMIM*604487), MYO7A (OMIM*276903), NOTCH1 (OMIM*190198), SPG11 (OMIM*604360), and MYH13 (OMIM*603487). Nine individuals carried variants in two or more genes. The encoded proteins are primarily expressed in the choroid plexus and ependymal cells, with most being associated with cilia. The pathogenicity of the reported variants, based on currently available information and in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, is detailed in Supporting Information Table S1.

Specifically, heterozygous LOF variants in the CWH43 gene were found in 15% of the individuals (8/53), and for the AK9 gene, in 9.6% (5/53).
24
, 
26
 Although the ACMG currently classifies the reported CWH43 variants as benign, mouse models with heterozygous CWH43 mutations demonstrated decreased cilia number and altered distribution of anchored proteins.
24
 For the AK9 gene, the variants described are classified by ACMG as likely pathogenic and VUS (variant of uncertain significance), with heterozygous mutant mice exhibiting normal brain development but developing communicating hydrocephalus in adulthood.
26



In addition to the 10 genes listed, a likely pathogenic missense variant in FOXJ1 (OMIM*602291) was identified in one individual. Although this gene did not meet the criteria for being reported along with the other genes, FOXJ1 was further investigated because of its prior association with obstructive hydrocephalus. In mouse models, disruptions in one Foxj1 allele were associated with communicating hydrocephalus as a result of incomplete ependymal cell differentiation.
25



WGS was performed in a cohort of 94 unrelated NPH patients from multiple ethnicities and compared with 532 controls, but only the results on the CWH43 variants have been published so far. Ten patients harbored the previously reported LOF variants, which were associated with a 2.6‐fold increase in NPH risk. Another five patients presented with missense point variations, classified by ACMG as benign, likely benign, and VUS. All eight carriers who underwent shunting were responsive. In addition, the odds of having a head circumference greater than the 75th percentile were more than five times higher for CWH43 variant carriers compared with noncarriers, although it was not statistically significant (P = 0.057). Neuroimaging features such as disproportionately enlarged subarachnoid‐space hydrocephalus (DESH) and sulcal trapping were more common in noncarriers (P < 0.05).
27



A 12‐kb deletion in intron 2 of the SFMBT1 gene (OMIM*607319) was identified in four of eight Japanese elderly individuals exhibiting ventriculomegaly with NPH‐like features on imaging and in only 1 of 110 healthy individuals. CNVs were detected using a CNV‐targeted whole‐genome oligonucleotide microarray. Among those with the variant, one was asymptomatic, whereas three had cognitive decline, with one also experiencing gait dysfunction.
28



Using quantitative polymerase chain reaction, the same CNV was identified in 26% (13/50) of Japanese shunt‐responsive patients versus 4.2% (8/191) of controls (OR, 7.94; P = 0.000018).
29
 The variant was also present in 10% (56/567) of Finnish NPH patients versus 5.4% (27/508) of controls (OR, 1.9; P = 0.0078) and in 21% (79/377) of Norwegian NPH patients, which were compared with the same Finnish controls (OR, 4.7; P < 0.0001).
30
 Notably, the European cohorts included both shunted and nonshunted NPH patients, and the presence of the CNV on the SFMBT1 gene did not predict shunt response.
30
 SFMBT1 protein is present in the choroid plexus, ependymal lining of the ventricles, and the smooth muscle and endothelial cells of arteries.

The first large‐scale GWAS on NPH was recently published comparing 1055 idiopathic NPH patients with 451,091 controls, all of Finnish ancestry. Two loci associated with NPH risk and two with protection were identified (P < 5.0−8): rs798511 (OR, 1.28) near AMZ1/GNA12 (OMIM*615168/OMIM*604394), rs56023709 (OR, 1.28) near C16orf95 (no OMIM entry), rs7962263 (OR, 0.70) near SLCO1A2 (OMIM*602883), and rs10828247 (OR, 0.74) near MLLT10 (OMIM*602409). These loci remained statistically significant on meta‐analysis with a validation cohort from the UK biobank, which included 173 NPH cases and 419,453 controls. The first three sustained statistical significance when analyzing only the 736 shunted patients. Notably, the loci found near SLCO1A2 and MLLT10 were protective against NPH.
31



Notably, the replication cohort had no genome‐wide significant hits of its own nor yielded additional significant loci in the meta‐analysis. Interestingly, all loci are located near genes involved in blood‐brain and blood‐CSF barriers function, and were previously linked to increased lateral ventricle volume.
31

NPH has been shown to co‐occur with other neurological diseases that have genetic causes or risk factors, raising the question whether the genes or abnormal mechanisms in those diseases might also contribute to NPH risk.

The APOE gene (OMIM*107741) has been largely investigated in NPH, particularly regarding the ɛ4 allele. A small study of 13 patients with NPH and 108 controls found the ɛ4 allele significantly more common in NPH (P < 0.017),

NPH was first reported to occur alongside frontotemporal dementia (FTD) in a patient with its behavioral variant (bvFTD) and a pathological hexanucleotide expansion in the C9orf72 gene (OMIM*614260), which is found in approximately 25% of FTD cases.

Six of seven shunted patients showed improvements, suggesting that C9orf72 expansion does not preclude shunt response.

The angiotensin I–converting enzyme has been demonstrated to play a major role in vascular physiology and pathology. In NPH, which has been linked to vascular risk factors, the presence of a 287‐bp Alu deletion in the ACE gene (OMIM*106180) was associated to less cognitive improvement after shunt surgery. However, its distribution did not significantly differ between 112 patients with NPH and 124 controls, indicating the deletion alone is not a risk for NPH.

Although this review focuses on DNA methods, transcriptomic studies analyzing gene expression at the RNA level have also been reported in NPH. The mRNA expression of AD‐related genes was first analyzed in the frontal cortex of 22 patients with NPH compared with eight controls. mRNA levels of TTR (OMIM*176300) were 17‐fold lower, whereas those of APP and α‐secretase (ADAM10, OMIM*602192) were elevated in the NPH group. No correlation was found with disease severity or shunt response.

MAPT expression was elevated in both NPH and AD groups, but not in controls, whereas TRPV4 expression was increased only in NPH, suggesting it could be a potential marker for differentiating the two conditions.

The role of the adenosine system, previously associated with vascular protection and modulation of inflammatory reactions, was also investigated in NPH, demonstrating significantly lower mRNA levels of adenosine receptors A1R and A2AR in patients compared with controls.

Regarding shunt response, increased expression of MORN1 (OMIM*610156), N4BP1 (OMIM*619138) and TRIM52‐AS1 (OMIM*619266) was observed for gait, urinary, and cognitive improvements, respectively.

The expression of hsa‐miR‐4274 was lower in the parkinsonian‐NPH group, effectively distinguishing it from the NPH, AD‐NPH, and healthy elderly groups.

This review has several limitations. First, there are biases from the definition of NPH. The absence of a universally accepted definition and objective diagnostic measures has led to differing guidelines with criteria lacking high‐quality evidence.
71
 This variability in diagnosis may have affected patient recruitment and study outcomes. Second, there is overlap with other conditions. The core features of NPH often overlap with those of other neurodegenerative disorders, and copathologies may have influenced its pathogenesis and clinical presentation, acting as confounders in the included studies. Third, methodological heterogeneity is present. The varied molecular approaches and criteria for reporting genetic findings complicated direct comparisons and precluded quantitative analyses, such as meta‐analysis. Fourth, there are sample size limitations. Several studies focused on small case series or isolated families, limiting the generalizability of results. Fifth, a lack of population diversity exists. The underrepresentation of non‐European and non‐Japanese populations reduces the applicability of findings to broader groups.

The poor understanding of NPH's mechanisms contributes to controversies in the field, even leading to a lack of consensus on whether NPH is a distinct entity. Further identification of NPH risk variants/genes addressing diversity: Our review highlights that most studies originate from European cohorts. Additional large‐scale studies, such as GWASs, should target or guarantee the inclusion of diverse populations. This is crucial to ensure the applicability of findings across ethnicities, identify population‐specific variants that may have been missed, and understand the full spectrum of genetic variation underlying NPH, ultimately leading to more effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Collaborations for big data generation: Partnerships between institutions and national or multinational repositories are essential for these large‐scale genetic studies on NPH. These joint efforts enable the pooling of resources to create DNA biobanks and support big data generation, ultimately ensuring an adequate sample size for meaningful analysis.

Further characterization of familial cases: Sequencing large and diverse families using WGS will allow mapping genetic variants that cosegregate with the disease phenotype. It can lead to the discovery of novel genes and variants, and provide insights into their penetrance and expressivity.

Identification of variants/genes associated with prognosis and shunt responsiveness: Genetic variants may also influence disease progression and treatment responsiveness. The literature on shunt treatment effectiveness shows significant variability in reported outcomes. Understanding the genetic factors underlying treatment responsiveness will enhance patient care through more personalized therapy approaches.

Coupling research with clinical and environmental data: Like other neurological disorders, NPH likely results from interactions between genetic and nongenetic factors, making it essential to integrate genetic data with environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, and medication histories. Nongenetic factors are particularly valuable because they may be modifiable.

PRS for risk prediction: PRSs, derived from GWAS‐identified risk variants, have potential to improve disease risk prediction, particularly when combined with environmental and lifestyle factors. Identifying high‐risk individuals could aid in evaluating disease‐modifying therapies. Although the practical application of PRS in NPH remains a future goal, large‐scale GWASs with diverse, multiethnic cohorts are needed to establish its foundation.

Biomarkers and biological definition: A better understanding of the genetics and pathophysiology of NPH may enable future identification of biomarkers. This could lead to the development of a biological definition, solidifying NPH as a distinct clinical entity and advancing targeted treatments.

Our review synthesized the existing literature on the genetic contribution to NPH risk, identifying potential genetic factors through familial studies, WES, WGS, GWAS, CNV analysis, candidate gene approaches, Mendelian randomization, and RNA studies. Further research is needed to confirm these findings and address population diversity to ensure their applicability across different groups and identify population‐specific variants. Understanding the role of genetic and environmental factors can enhance the identification of individuals at risk, improve patient selection for shunt surgery, and support the development of targeted therapies to address compromised biological pathways.
