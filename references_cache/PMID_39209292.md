# Clinical, Laboratory, Molecular, and Reproductive Aspects of Combined Deficiency of Factors V and VIII.
**Authors:** Yakovleva E, Zhang B
**Journal:** Semin Thromb Hemost (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1055/s-0044-1789019](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0044-1789019)

## Content

Congenital combined deficiency of factor V (FV) and factor VIII (FVIII; F5F8D, OMIM 227300) is a rare hereditary coagulopathy and accounts for approximately 3% of cases of rare coagulation disorders. The prevalence of this disease in the general population is estimated to be 1:1,000,000 and is significantly higher in regions where consanguineous marriages are permitted, such as the Mideast and South Asia. The disease has an autosomal recessive mode of inheritance and therefore occurs with an equal incidence among males and females. Heterozygous mutation carriers usually do not have clinical manifestations. The molecular basis of this disease differs from that of stand-alone congenital deficiencies of FVIII and FV. F5F8D is caused by mutations in either LMAN1 or MCFD2, which encode components of a cargo receptor complex for endoplasmic reticulum to Golgi transport of FV and FVIII, leading to defects in an intracellular transport pathway shared by these two coagulation factors. Congenital combined deficiency of FV and FVIII is characterized by decreased activities of both FV and FVIII in plasma, usually to 5 to 30% of normal. Clinical manifestations in most cases are represented by mild or moderate hemorrhagic syndrome. The simultaneous decreases of two coagulation factors present complications in the diagnosis and management of the disease. In female patients, the disease requires a special approach for family planning, pregnancy management, and parturition. This review summarizes recent progress in clinical, laboratory, and molecular understanding of this disorder.

Combined factor (F) deficiency of FV and FVIII (F5F8D, OMIM 227300) is a congenital bleeding disorder associated with simultaneous decreases of both FV and FVIII, two coagulation factors that are important in the blood clotting cascade. It was first described by Oeri and coworkers in 1954 in two Swiss brothers1 and confirmed as an autosomal recessive disorder later.1,2 The disease mechanism had been an enigma for several decades. However, the 21st century has brought significant progress in clinical and mechanistic understanding of the disease.

Both FV and FVIII are indispensable cofactors of enzymatic complexes in the coagulation cascade (►Fig. 1).3 FV is encoded by a gene (F5) located on chromosome 1 that includes 25 exons.4 FVIII is encoded by a gene (F8) situated on the X chromosome that contains 26 exons.5 FV and FVIII have much in common: they are both high-molecular-weight proteins, are devoid of catalytic activity, and share similar domain structures (A1-A2-B-A3-C1-C2), with 40% amino acid sequence homology in A and C domains, but less than 15% homology in B domains (►Fig. 2).6

Approximately 80% of FV circulates in plasma, and 20% is concentrated in platelet α-granules. In humans, plasma FV is synthesized by hepatocytes and platelet FV is derived from plasma FV via endocytosis by megakaryocytes.7–9 In contrast, FVIII is synthesized in endothelial cells.10,11 Plasma concentration of FVIII (0.2 μg/mL) is much lower than that of FV (7 μg/mL). The two cofactors have different half-lives: 10 to 14 hours for FVIII, versus 20 hours on average for FV (according to various sources, its half-life can vary between 12 and 36 hours; ►Table 1). In the bloodstream, FVIII circulates in a stable complex with von Willebrand factor (VWF); this complex dissociates after FVIII activation.

FVIII is a cofactor of coagulation factor IX (FIX) in the tenase complex (►Fig. 1), which catalyzes the conversion of coagulation factor X (FX) to its activated form (FXa). FV is a cofactor of FX in the prothrombinase complex (►Fig. 1), which catalyzes the conversion of prothrombin to thrombin. Activated FV (FVa) and activated FVIII (FVIIIa) greatly enhance the catalytic activity of FXa and FIXa, respectively. The main activators of FV and FVIII are thrombin and FXa. Both cofactors are inactivated by activated protein C and its cofactor protein S-mediated limited proteolysis, giving rise to an inactivated form of FVIII (FVIIIi) and an inactivated form of FV (FVi; ►Fig. 2).3

For a long time, the etiology of F5F8D had remained unresolved. It was originally hypothesized that the cause of this disease was a defect in the gene encoding the common precursor of the two factors.1 However, no such precursor was identified. Further studies showed that F5F8D cannot be explained by a combination of mutations in F8 and F5. In 1997 to 1998, Nichols and colleagues described mutations in LMAN1 (encoding a protein called lectinmannose-binding 1 [LMAN1]; also named ERGIC-53) as a cause of F5F8D.12,13 Mutations of this gene were found to be present in 70% of the patients. In 2003, Zhang and colleagues reported a second gene involved in this pathology: MCFD2 (encoding a protein called multiple coagulation factor deficiency 2 [MCFD2]),14,15 accounting for the remaining 30% of cases. LMAN1 and MCFD2 participate in the intracellular transport of FV and FVIII from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the Golgi complex.16–19 LMAN1 is a type-1 transmembrane animal lectin (►Fig. 3A). It has a higher affinity for D-mannose than other sugars. Most of the LMAN1 protein is in the lumen, which contains a carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD) with sequence similarity to plant L-type lectins, and a stalk domain with helical structures. The short C-terminal cytosolic tail contains both ER exit and retrieval signals. LMAN1 exists as a homotetramer in cells via the interactions of the stalk, transmembrane, and cytosolic domains.20,21 MCFD2 is a 16-kDa soluble protein with two Ca2+-binding EF-hand domains and the N-terminal domain that binds Zn2+ (►Fig. 3B). Each LMAN1 subunit binds to one MCFD2 molecule to form a stable Ca2+-dependent complex.22 Schematically, the LMAN1–MCFD2 protein complex is presented in ►Fig. 3C. The EF-hand domains of MCFD2 interact with the N-terminal helical structures of the CRD.23 The MCFD2-binding site in LMAN1 is structurally and functionally separate from its carbohydrate-binding pocket.24–27 Cryoelectron microscopy structures of full-length LMAN1 in complex with MCFD2 revealed a helical stalk domain with an architecture of four-helix coiled-coils and a tetrameric cargo-binding head region.21 The stalk domain has three helical segments and two hinges and is unusually long (~340 Å) and highly flexible (►Fig. 3C), which may allow for more efficient capture of a variety of cargo proteins. Recent studies suggest that MCFD2 is the major recruitment factor for the FV/FVIII cargo,28 with LMAN1 serving primarily as a carrier that shuttles the receptor complex between the ER and the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC). It is not clear what features of FV and FVIII are recognized by the LMAN1–MCFD2 complex. B domain-deleted FVIII has diminished interactions with LMAN1 and MCFD2 compared with the full-length FVIII,22,29 suggesting that N-glycans in the B domain are important for this interaction. However, LMAN1 with mutations that abolish carbohydrate binding can still partially rescue FVIII secretion in LMAN1 knockout cells,28 suggesting that N-glycan binding is not sufficient for LMAN1-mediated ER–Golgi transport.

After cargo capture, the tertiary complex of LMAN1–MCFD2 cargo is packaged into vesicles coated with a protein complex called COPII (coat protein complex II) and bud away from the ER.30 The SEC24 subunit of COPII interacts with the diphenylalanine motif at the end of the cytosolic tail.31,32 COPII vesicles merge to the ERGIC, where FV/FVIII cargoes are released from the cargo receptor complex, likely triggered by the lower pH and Ca2+ conditions in the ERGIC.23,33 The LMAN1–MCFD2 complex is returned to the ER in COPI (coat protein complex I) vesicles via its dilysine ER retrieval signal for additional rounds of cargo transport.34,35 FV/FVIII cargoes move through the Golgi complex while undergoing further posttranslational modifications. After leaving the trans-Golgi network, FV is directly secreted from the hepatocytes, while FVIII is further sorted into two pathways in endothelial cells (►Fig. 4). In the constitutive pathway, FVIII is directly secreted. In contrast, in the regulated pathway, FVIII is stored in the Weibel–Palade bodies (WPBs) with VWF for regulated exocytosis in response to extracellular stimuli.36,37 Deficiencies of LMAN1 or MCFD2 lead to a disturbance of the secretory pathway of FV and FVIII and limit the release of these factors into circulating blood.30,38 Transport of VWF is independent of the LMAN1–MCFD2 complex. Both LMAN1 and MCFD2 are widely expressed in different tissues. Several other proteins are also known to be client cargoes of the LMAN1–MCFD2 receptor complex.30 One such cargo, α1-antitrypsin, showed delayed ER–Golgi trafficking and accumulation in the ER of LMAN1- or MCFD2-deficient cells.39–42

A substantial personal history of bleeding and possible hemorrhagic signs in relatives may lead clinicians to suspect a hereditary coagulopathy, including F5F8D. To objectively characterize hemorrhagic syndrome, it is advisable to use a bleeding assessment scale (International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis [ISTH] Bleeding Assessment Tool, Pediatric Bleeding Questionnaire, or others).43,44 The disease manifests itself as hemorrhages of various locations and severity levels. Mild or moderate hemorrhagic syndrome is more common. The most typical signs are spontaneous ecchymoses, epistaxis, gingival bleeding, traumatic bleeding, and bleeding during surgical operations or childbirth. Hemarthroses, muscle hematomas, hematuria, gastrointestinal bleeding, and hemorrhages in the central nervous system occur in rare cases.2,19,45 Peyvandi et al have demonstrated a strong correlation between the extent of FV and FVIII deficiency and the severity of clinical manifestations in 18 patients with F5F8D. It is known that such a relationship is not typical for all deficiencies of blood coagulation factors.45 In this study population, the mean factor activity level was 24% in patients with spontaneous minor bleeding and 14% in patients with spontaneous major bleeding.45 However, F5F8D is mainly associated with minor bleeding. Only 50% of patients exhibit spontaneous bleeding, with major bleeding occurring in just 5% of patients.45 Although rare, major bleeding could lead to other complications. For example, in a patient with 7 to 11% FV and FVIII levels, extensive bleeding led to severe anemia requiring multiple red blood cell transfusion.46 Another patient developed ischemic necrosis requiring hip replacement.47 ►Table 2 presents the spectrum of clinical manifestations of F5F8D in different populations.2,48–52

Females are more susceptible to a severe course of this disease owing to physiological characteristics and potential for bleeding during menstruation, ovulation, and childbirth. Spiliopoulos and Kadir have analyzed complaints and clinical manifestations of 86 women with F5F8D.53 The most typical were menorrhagias (49%), epistaxis (34%), ecchymoses (19%), postoperative (17%) and posttraumatic hemorrhages (15%), bleeding after tooth extraction (11%), and ovulatory hemorrhages (4%). The authors describe 19 pregnancies in 18 women: there were no miscarriages, and bleeding during childbirth was registered in 32% of the patients.

The characteristic anamnestic, clinical, and family data allow clinicians to proceed to the next stage: a laboratory diagnosis. In F5F8D, according to a screening coagulogram, prolongation of activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and prothrombin time (PT) is typical. Upon further analysis of the activity of blood coagulation factors, a decrease in the activities of both FV and FVIII is noted (usually 5–30% of the norm). Tests involving mixing with normal plasma can correct aPTT and PT, thereby ruling out the presence of inhibitors.54,55

Genetic testing can ultimately confirm the diagnosis but it is currently not routinely done. The testing should start with LMAN1, because in 70% of cases, mutations are found in this gene, followed by MCFD2 if no mutations are identified in LMAN1. The LMAN1 gene is located on the long arm of chromosome 18 (18q21.32) and includes 13 exons13 (►Fig. 3B). The MCFD2 gene is situated on the short arm of chromosome 2 (2p21) and contains four exons14 (►Fig. 3A). At present, according to the Human Gene Mutation Database, there are 38 known mutations in LMAN1 (mostly insertions/deletions, nonsense mutations, and splice site mutations) and 20 known mutations in MCFD2 (mostly insertions/deletions, missense mutations, and splice site mutations). Some mutations, such as c.89insG and c.2T > C (M1T) in LMAN1, which are prevalent in Iranian/Iraqi Jewish and Italian populations, may represent founder effects. Other mutations, such as the c.149 + 5G > A and c.407T > C in MCFD2, are recurrent mutations found in multiple populations.56 A string of mononucleotide repeat of nine adenines from nucleotide 904 to 912 in exon 8 of LMAN1 appears to be another mutation hotspot, with multiple mutations (c.912–913insA, c.912delA, and c.904A >T) found in this region in different populations.56–58 Mutations may occur in the regulatory regions of the gene, including a large deletion of 8.4 kb in the 5′-untranslated region in the MCFD2 gene.59 Other unusual mutations include a synonymous mutation in LMAN1 that creates an ectopic splice donor site leading to splicing defects.46 Rare missense mutations in LMAN1 lead to unstable protein or mannose and MCFD2-binding defects.15,60,61 Missense mutations in MCFD2 are more common, all of which disrupt LMAN1 binding.56,62 Interpretation of missense variants could be difficult without functional studies, as two such reported mutations were recently shown to be benign variants.28

As mentioned above, although FVIII exclusively resides in plasma, FV circulates in the bloodstream both in plasma and in α-granules of platelets. In F5F8D, the concentration of FV in both plasma and platelets is diminished. Of scientific and practical interest is research on correlations among the genotype, plasma activity of FV and FVIII, and clinical manifestations. These data are helpful for the prognosis of the disease, for determining the activities of blood coagulation factors that are sufficient to prevent bleeding, and for choosing a treatment strategy. In studies of 46 patients with MCFD2 mutations and 100 patients with LMAN1 mutations, mutations in MCFD2 are associated with lower plasma FV and FVIII activities as compared with mutations in LMAN1 (mean FV activity: 9.6 and 13.7% [p < 0.001], respectively; the mean FVIII activity: 10 and 16% [p < 0.001], respectively).56 Despite these data, the activity of FV and FVIII in plasma cannot be a predictor of a defective gene due to the large overlap in the ranges of factor levels. No differences in the activity of either FV or FVIII have been found between male and female patients. In contrast to the activity of VWF, there is no difference in the activity of either FV or FVIII among blood groups in F5F8D patients.56

The treatment strategy and choice of therapy are based on the severity of clinical manifestations. In the vast majority of cases, hemostatic therapy is performed “on demand,” i.e., after bleeding. If surgical interventions are necessary, then preventive hemostatic therapy is performed in the perioperative period.

The following hemostatic agents can be highlighted: fibrinolysis inhibitors (tranexamic acid and aminocaproic acid) are prescribed for oral, intravenous, and topical administration. For moderate mucocutaneous hemorrhagic syndrome, on an outpatient basis, the typical daily dosage of tranexamic acid is 1,500 to 3,000 mg divided into three doses. Intravenous administration of tranexamic acid at 15 to 20 mg/kg can be applied in hospital settings as monotherapy or as part of a combination treatment.63

There are sufficient accumulated data on the use of desmopressin (1-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin [DDAVP]) in patients with hereditary coagulopathies, including those with F5F8D.52,64–66 The mechanism of action of this drug involves a release of the stored VWF and FVIII from WPB and consequently an increase in the plasma levels of these coagulation factors. Desmopressin is not recommended for long-term use due to the depletion effect. In their study, Garcia et al showed that desmopressin causes a short-term (60–120minutes) increase in FVIII activity by twofold.64 A typical single dose of intranasal spray is 260μg, and that of a solution for subcutaneous administration is 0.3 μg/kg.

Patients with severe hemorrhagic symptoms and patients in the perioperative period (for prevention of bleeding) require hemostatic replacement therapy. Today, a wide variety of plasma-derived and recombinant FVIII concentrates are available. However, there are no available FV concentrates in clinical practice. Difficulties with the production of FV-based therapeutics are related to the instability of this factor and its high sensitivity to proteolysis at the initial stages of purification.67,68 Nevertheless, a recent article described the development of a plasma concentrate of FV and its testing in vitro.69

Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) is a source of both FV and FVIII and serves as a universal therapeutic option for patients with F5F8D. In case of bleeding or planned surgery, the therapeutic goal is to achieve a FV activity of 15% based on treatments of FV deficiency63,70,71 and a FVIII activity of at least 50% of the norm. The initial recommended dose of FFP is 15 to 25 mL/kg, and that of a FVIII concentrate is 15 to 30 IU/kg (depending on the severity of hemorrhagic syndrome and baseline activity of FV and FVIII). The second administration is conducted after 12 to 24 hours judging by coagulogram parameters and by the clinical effectiveness of the therapy. After that, the subsequent regimen of administration of hemostatic agents is determined based on needs. In the postoperative period, replacement therapy should be continued until the surgical wound heals.54,63 Laboratory screening tests for clotting parameters (aPTT, PT, and fibrinogen) are routine and performed ubiquitously. Nonetheless, not all laboratories are equipped to quantify FV and FVIII activities, and relying only on screening tests to guide therapy is a challenge. On the other hand, using perioperative management of a female patient as a clinical example, it has been demonstrated that in the postoperative period, full compensation of screening parameters is not necessary for preventing hemorrhagic complications.72

Possible complications of the therapy described above may be plasma volume overload, allergic reactions, the formation of alloantibodies against plasma proteins when FFP is used, or the formation of inhibitors when FVIII concentrates are administered. Although emicizumab is presumably an effective alternative to FVIII concentrates, particularly for patients with FVIII inhibitors,73 its use has not been reported in the literature for F5F8D patients. Cryoprecipitate may have advantages over FFP because of lower transfusion volume. Nevertheless, the use of cryoprecipitates as a source of FV in F5F8D remains a poorly investigated topic. Although it is generally thought that cryoprecipitates lack FV, a recent report showed that sufficient activity of FV was found in cryoprecipitates obtained from donors with different blood groups,74 suggesting that cryoprecipitate could replace FFP in some situations. There are also successful cases of rFVIIa administration in the absence of the effect of a FFP in combination with a FVIII concentrate therapy or in case of anaphylactic shock in response to FFP administration in the literature.75,76 An alternative may be the use of a platelet concentrate as a source of FV given presence in α-granules.73,77,78

Recently, it was reported that FV deficiency is associated with reduced tissue factor pathway inhibitor α (TFPIα) levels, which protects FV-deficient patients from severe bleeding.79 In addition to being a coagulation factor, FV also exhibits an anticoagulant activity by regulating the TFPIα and protein C pathways.79–81 Bleeding is generally mild in FV-deficient patients and rarely occurs in those with FV levels over 10%.70,71 Shao and colleagues demonstrated that FV levels (between 6 and 20% of normal in their cohort) in F5F8D patients already had optimal procoagulant activity.52 Increasing FV levels only led to an increase in anticoagulation effect. Restoring FVIII alone in F5F8D patient plasma led to normal thrombin generation. Thus, low FV levels may be beneficial for F5F8D patients and ameliorate their bleeding tendency. The authors suggested that DDAVP may be considered a potential substitute for FVIII concentrates, and FFP infusion may not be necessary for F5F8D patients with minor bleeding challenges.52 Based on these studies, it is reasonable to suggest that at least for F5F8D patients with FV levels >10%, no FV replacement may be required, which frees patients from complications associated with FFP infusion. This is a welcome observation, as FFP is not readily available in all hospitals.47 In a patient undergoing stent placement in the coronary artery, only FVIII concentrates were given in the postoperative period and the patient had no sign of local bleeding at the femoral access site.58 In this case, the patient was treated with antiplatelet therapy for 30 days and aspirin therapy long-term to prevent metal stent thrombosis without complications, suggesting that F5F8D patients can tolerate anticoagulation therapy.58 Additional patient studies are required to test whether FVIII infusion alone is sufficient in major bleeding challenges such as trauma and surgery.

Inheritance of this disease is autosomal-recessive and does not depend on sex.19,55 If one of the parents has F5F8D (with biallelic mutations), and the other parent is normal, all the offspring would be heterozygous carriers. There is a 25% chance that a child will be born with the disease if the parents are heterozygous carriers. During pregnancy, a healthy woman’s hemostatic system undergoes considerable physiological changes. These changes are aimed at maintaining placental function and preventing expected bleeding during childbirth. The hemostatic balance of healthy pregnant women shifts toward hypercoagulation and is characterized by elevated activity of most blood coagulation factors, lowered activity of anticoagulants, and inhibition of fibrinolysis. The shifts of hemostasis in pregnant women with congenital coagulopathies have some specific features. Assessment of hemostasis parameters should be performed, taking into account the trimester of pregnancy. During pregnancy and postpartum, under the conditions of physiological hypercoagulation, plasma activity of FV does not change, while the activities of VWF and FVIII go up by 200 to 250%82,83; consequently, the diagnosis of F5F8D in pregnancy is difficult, and in this case, family history is especially important.84,85

Postpartum hemorrhages are the leading cause of maternal mortality in the general population. Blood loss up to 500 mL during natural childbirth is considered physiological, whereas blood loss under 1,000 mL during surgical childbirth is acceptable. In the general population, delivery is complicated by primary bleeding (within 24 hours after childbirth) in 4 to 6% of cases and secondary bleeding (between 24 hours and up to 6 weeks after childbirth) in 1 to 3% of cases. Obstetric and gynecological risk factors for bleeding are polyhydramnios, multiple pregnancies, macrosomia, uterine fibroids, intrauterine infection, and a placental anomaly. Hereditary coagulopathies, including F5F8D, significantly raise the risk of massive blood loss, even more so in combination with obstetric adverse factors.86,87

In pregnant women with F5F8D, it is necessary to plan hemostatic therapy for the parturition period.53,88 It is advisable to determine the activity of FV and FVIII at the end of the third trimester; depending on these parameters, doses and administration regimens of hemostatic drugs are determined next. F5F8D is not a contraindication to natural childbirth. The method of delivery is determined by the obstetrician-gynecologist. It must be taken into consideration that prolonged labor, delivery via vacuum extraction of the fetus, or the application of forceps pose the highest risk of intracranial hemorrhage in the child and of hemorrhagic complications in the mother.87,88

For the parturition period, the target value of FV activity is above 15%, whereas that of FVIII activity is at least 50%. Transfusion of FFP is not enough to restore FVIII activity, therefore administration of a FVIII concentrate in addition is recommended. A concentration of FVIII at 10 to 30 IU/kg should be administered once a day, or less frequent administration of newer long half-life FVIII products. FFP administration is advisable at a dose of 15 to 25 mL/kg before natural or surgical childbirth, and at 10 mL/kg once every 12 hours for 3 days after natural childbirth to maintain the target activity levels of FV and FVIII. In cases of surgical delivery, it is recommended to continue the therapy until the wound heals.53,54,88 If FFP is ineffective or allergic reactions develop, it is possible to administer rFVIIa.75,76 These empirical practices notwithstanding, in light of the recent finding that a low FV level is beneficial to thrombin generation in F5F8D patient plasma,52 it will be interesting to determine whether FFP infusion to raise FV is necessary for childbirth.

DDAVP is prescribed during pregnancy and can be used in F5F8D. In rare cases, adverse effects may include convulsions due to a water imbalance as well as moderately increased uterine contractility in the third trimester of pregnancy. DDAVP is excreted into breast milk in small quantities.89 Tranexamic acid can also be prescribed to pregnant women with F5F8D. Several studies point to a reduction in blood loss during natural or surgical childbirth and in the postpartum period with the administration of tranexamic acid.89,90 The issue of the risk of thrombotic complications when this drug is administered remains debatable. A contraindication to tranexamic acid is a history of thrombosis.87,90–92 A woman with F5F8D should be under careful observation for 6 weeks after delivery.93

Regional anesthesia in women with F5F8D raises the risk of an epidural or spinal hematoma during catheter insertion or removal. In most cases, these complications of anesthesia take place when a diagnosis of hereditary coagulopathy is not yet made. If a woman’s diagnosis of F5F8D is verified, then safe regional anesthesia is possible in combination with an adequate preventive replacement modality. Each case must be assessed individually. If it is impossible to ensure hemostasis, regional anesthesia is contraindicated.87

In cases of a moderate-to-mild clinical phenotype, prenatal diagnosis is usually not performed. These diagnostics are worthwhile if it is known that both parents are heterozygous carriers or have clinical manifestations of the disease.16 Prenatal diagnostics include invasive and noninvasive methods.89,91 Invasive diagnostic procedures include chorionic villus sampling, amniocentesis, and cordocentesis. Chorionic villus sampling is performed at 8 to 12 weeks of pregnancy. Chorionic tissue is collected via puncturing of the uterus through the anterior abdominal wall or through the vagina and cervix with a puncture needle. The choice of the puncture site depends on the specific positioning of the chorion in the uterus. Amniocentesis is performed at 16 to 21 weeks of pregnancy and involves puncture of the amniotic membrane of the fetus through the woman’s anterior abdominal wall for obtaining amniotic fluid and for subsequent examination. Cordocentesis is performed no earlier than 18 weeks of pregnancy and involves collecting cord blood by puncturing the umbilical cord vessel using a puncture needle. The aforementioned invasive procedures are performed during hemostasis compensation in a hospital according to ultrasonography findings.89 The material for genetic testing is transported to a genetic laboratory.

Nowadays, noninvasive prenatal testing is increasingly introduced into clinical practice and is based on an analysis of a fetus’s extracellular DNA circulating in the blood of the pregnant woman after the 10th week of pregnancy.94,95 In the future, noninvasive prenatal testing may become awidespread tool for prenatal diagnosis of monogenic diseases.

In a newborn, F5F8D can be diagnosed by tests of cord or peripheral blood. The average FV activity in healthy full-term newborns is comparable to that in adults and rises slightly during the first week of life, then remains stable during the first 6 months.96 The average FVIII activity at birth in healthy newborns is the same as in adults. FVIII activity tends to diminish during the first 6 months. In healthy newborns, the activity of FV and FVIII positively correlates with parturition timing (pregnancy duration) and birth weight.97 F5F8D is rarely accompanied by bleeding in newborns; in some cases, cephalohematoma can develop.53 Cases of neonatal intracranial hemorrhage in this disease have not been reported. There are no data on a prophylactic replacement modality in newborns.63

Due to the rarity of the disease in question, differential diagnosis of coagulopathies should be aimed at excluding or verifying F5F8D. Timely diagnosis of F5F8D remains the main practical issue and makes it possible to avoid unforeseen life-threatening situations—such as bleeding during a surgical procedure or childbirth—and to improve the quality of life of patients via appropriate hemostatic therapy in a timely manner. The main problem is still the absence of available replacement therapies for FV. The main unanswered question is how much, if any, contribution of low FV levels to the bleeding phenotype in this disorder. Key future research interests are continued gathering of global data on the prevalence, molecular genetics, and clinical course of this disease in different countries and populations, which will allow for more detailed phenotype and genotype correlation studies, as well as further mechanistic understanding of FV and FVIII secretion defects caused by LMAN1 and MCFD2 mutations.
