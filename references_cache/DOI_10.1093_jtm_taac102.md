# Clinical characteristics and management of neurocysticercosis patients: a retrospective assessment of case reports from Europe
**Authors:** Dominik Stelzle, Annette Abraham, Miriam Kaminski, Veronika Schmidt, Robert De Meijere, Javier A Bustos, Hector Hugo Garcia, Priyadarshi Soumyaranjan Sahu, Branko Bobić, Carmen Cretu, Peter Chiodini, Veronique Dermauw, Brecht Devleesschauwer, Pierre Dorny, Ana Fonseca, Sarah Gabriël, Maria Ángeles Gómez Morales, Minerva Laranjo-González, Achim Hoerauf, Ewan Hunter, Ronan Jambou, Maja Jurhar-Pavlova, Ingrid Reiter-Owona, Smaragda Sotiraki, Chiara Trevisan, Manuela Vilhena, Naomi F Walker, Lorenzo Zammarchi, Andrea Sylvia Winkler

## Content

AbstractObjectivesNeurocysticercosis (NCC) is a parasitic disease caused by the larval stage of the tapeworm Taenia solium. NCC mainly occurs in Africa, Latin America and South-East Asia and can cause a variety of clinical signs/symptoms. Although it is a rare disease in Europe, it should nonetheless be considered as a differential diagnosis. The aim of this study was to describe clinical characteristics and management of patients with NCC diagnosed and treated in Europe.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of published and unpublished data on patients diagnosed with NCC in Europe (2000–2019) and extracted demographic, clinical and radiological information on each case, if available.ResultsOut of 293 identified NCC cases, 59% of patients presented initially with epileptic seizures (21% focal onset); 52% presented with headache and 54% had other neurological signs/symptoms. The majority of patients had a travel or migration history (76%), mostly from/to Latin America (38%), Africa (32%) or Asia (30%). Treatment varied largely depending on cyst location and number. The outcome was favorable in 90% of the cases.ConclusionsManagement of NCC in Europe varied considerably but often had a good outcome. Travel and migration to and from areas endemic for T. solium will likely result in continued low prevalence of NCC in Europe. Therefore, training and guidance of clinicians is recommended for optimal patient management.

INTRODUCTION 75 
Neurocysticercosis (NCC) is caused by the tapeworm Taenia solium, a zoonotic parasite which has a pig-76 
human-environment life cycle. Humans get NCC by ingesting parasite eggs, which are shed through 77 
feces of T. solium tapeworm carriers. Through environmental contamination and lack of hygiene these 78 
eggs can accidentally be ingested by humans. Once in the intestines, the larvae, which are released from 79 
eggs, cross the intestinal mucosa into the circulatory system through which they are transported to 80 
multiple organs, where they encyst (cysticerci). When these cysticerci affect the central nervous system 81 
(CNS), the disease is c alled NCC. Cysts can persist in the CNS for several years without causing any 82 
neurological signs/symptoms. Signs and symptoms are pleomorphic 1–4, often resulting from 83 
degeneration of the cysts and associated inflammatory host immune reaction. The most common 84 
neurological signs/symptoms are epileptic seizures , headache episodes, focal neurological deficits and 85 
signs of raised intracranial pressure.1  86 
Neurological sig ns and symptoms depend on number, size, location (e.g. intraparenchymal or 87 
extraparenchymal and location within the brain parenchyma), and cyst stage. When located in the 88 
parenchyma, degeneration of cysts  (colloidal and granular nodular stage ) is associate d with 89 
inflammation leading to perilesional oedema  which can cause neurological signs/symptoms.2,5 90 
Treatment options for intraparenchymal lesions include anthelmintic therapy with albendazole and/or 91 
praziquantel in co mbination with corticosteroids  for vesicular cysts , or corticosteroids alone  for 92 
degenerating cysts ; both accompanied by  anti-epileptic drugs , if necessary. For extraparenchymal 93 
lesions treatment options include ventriculoperitoneal shunting, or surgical removal of cysts.4,6–13 94 
According to the distribution map for NCC published by WHO, Latin America, South and South -East 95 
Asia and sub -Saharan Africa are considered endemic .14 In these areas, NCC accounts for around one 96 
third of all epilepsy cases.4,15 In Europe, although data are scarce, the main countries affected are Spain, 97 
Portugal and Eastern European countries, however only rarely .16–21 Until the 1990s, many 98 
autochthonous cases of T. solium  infection were reported in Portugal and Spain. 16 To date , many 99 
immigrants who are likely to have been infe cted outside Europe are also diagnosed in these two 100 
countries.16 In Eastern Europe, disease surveillance is only sporadic; a previous review  reported a 101 
particularly large number of cases from Serbia with probable infection in Eastern Europe.  102 
Disease presentation differs between world regions. While single en hancing intraparenchymal lesions 103 
are the predominant form of the disease in India, multiple lesions are common in Latin America which 104 
more commonly are also in the extraparenchymal space than in other world regions . For African 105 
populations, also multiple lesions which are mostly located in the parenchyma have been described.2,22–106 
25 For Europe, disease presentation has not yet been described.  107 
Knowledge of diagnostic work-up and management of patients presenting with symptomatic NCC may 108 
be scarce among clinicians in Europe where NCC cases are rare and most clinicians have never seen a 109 

 
 
NCC case .26 As knowledge about differences of disease manifestation due to geographical 110 
characteristics or certain risk factors may help to correctly diagnose and treat patients , the aim of this 111 
study was to summarize and update information about clinical characteristics and management of NCC 112 
patients diagnosed in Europe.   113 
METHODS 114 
Systematic literature search:  This project was part of CYSTINET (European Network on 115 
Taeniosis/Cysticercosis, COST Action TD 1302) .27 We conducted a systematic literature search of NCC 116 
case reports and case series, along with cases from the grey literature. Also reviewed were unpublished 117 
data collected via collaborating clinicians and laboratories. Moreover, experts familiar with NCC patient 118 
management in the European setting were consulted. The protocol for the conduct of systematic 119 
literature review followed the PRISMA -P outline and was registered on PROSPERO (registration  120 
number: CRD42016050729).28 Ethical approval was obtained where required. Ethical approval for the 121 
retrospective analysis of anonymized patient data was granted by the ethics committee of the Klinikum 122 
rechts der Isar at the Technical University of Munich, Germany (208/16S).  123 
Search methods:  PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Global Health (CABI), Global Index Medicus 124 
coupled with Aoister and Open Grey were searched for articles published between January 2000 to May 125 
2019. Suppl ement Table S1 contains the precise search terms and dates. Moreover, each CYSTINET 126 
researcher searched for grey literature in their native countries (25 countries; the list of countries can be 127 
found under this link: http://www.cystinet.org/the-action/participating-countries/); references of 128 
included literature were evaluated for relevance and included if they met the inclusion criteria. No 129 
language restriction was applied; only studies on humans were included. (System atic) reviews were 130 
screened for additional references.16,17,19 131 
Study selection criteria:  Inclusion and exclusion criteria were pre -defined in the study protocol 132 
(Supplement Table S2). Only data on NCC cases presenting in Europe (see list of included countries in  133 
Supplement Table S2) were included. Case reports and case series were considered for inclusion. NCC 134 
was defined as the presence of T. solium cysts/calcifications in t he CNS confirmed on neuroimaging. 135 
Studies were excluded if 1) reporting on another Taenia species (e.g. T. crassiceps, T. hydatigena, T. 136 
asiatica), 2) reporting on cysticercosis only outside the CNS (e.g. muscles, eyes etc.), 3) reporting on 137 
patients treated outside Europe, 4) reporting year before 2000 (even if published after 2000), 5) reporting 138 
on the same patients (when reporting on the same patient both articles were taken into account for 139 
additional information, but the patient was counted as one) and 6) reporting on animals. 140 
Study selection process:  The Covidence online tool ( https://www.covidence.org/) was employed to 141 
assess the published literature obtained from PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Global Health (CABI), 142 

 
 
and Global Index Medicus.29 Literature was screened independently by four reviewers (AA, JB, PS, CU). 143 
Each selection re quired two votes from the reviewers; in the event of disagreement, a third reviewer 144 
was consulted. First, the titles and abstracts were screened and a decision was made whether to include 145 
or exclude the publication.  146 
Next, publications were sorted by country name (Supplement Table S2), and if found suitable as per the 147 
inclusion criteria, retained. Following this, the complete texts of the papers were reviewed and the 148 
rationale for any exclusion specified. In order to confirm the validity and suitab ility of the 149 
inclusion/exclusion criteria, the procedure of study selection was test -run by all researchers. Searching 150 
Aoister and Open Grey in collaboration with CYSTINET members yielded grey literature , including 151 
doctoral theses, papers in languages other than English and conference abstracts17,19, based on the same 152 
selection process as described above. 153 
Collection of unpublished data: Attendees at the 3 -4 November 2015 CYSTINET international 154 
conference in Belgrade, Serbia, were surveyed by means of a questionnaire in order to obtain further 155 
grey literature and unpublished data. Details of patient data - with due regard to in -country ethical 156 
stipulations – as well as information sources and local experts’ contacts were also requested from the 157 
CYSTINET members. Three of the authors (EH, NFW, PLC) collated the primary source data for a series 158 
of 26 cases mana ged at H ospital of Tropical Diseases in London , United Kingdom, that were 159 
subsequently published after the present study was conceived and initiated.30 Those cases are described 160 
here as unpublished, which accurately reflects their status at the time the data for the present study 161 
were collated. Ethical approval was obtained where required  (Serbia, Portugal, R omania). At all 162 
subsequent CYSTINET meetings and conferences, reminders were issued and contributions were also 163 
solicited via email. Medical plausibility verifications were conducted (by AA, DS, MK and ASW) on the 164 
patient data, which were all anonymized. 165 
Data extraction: All variables for data extraction have been outlined in the research protocol; these were 166 
consequently utilized in the data extra ction process. Data were extracted by five independent 167 
researchers (DS, RM, AA, AF, MK) and in case of uncertainty another expert of the group (ASW) was 168 
consulted. Plausibility verifications were carried out on the data extracts saved in Excel sheets, by a  169 
different reviewer from the one who had extracted the data.  170 
Definition of variables:  Autochthonous cases were defined as not having migrated from or never 171 
having travelled to an area outside of Europe that is endemic for T. solium. Only if travel/migrati on 172 
history was specifically denied by the patient, was the case considered to be autochthonous; otherwise 173 
the information was considered to be not available. Epileptic seizure types were classified as focal onset 174 
or generalised onset seizures according to the latest International League against Epilepsy ( ILAE) 175 
definition.31 For the evaluation of diagnostic variables computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance 176 

 
 
imaging (MRI), soft tissue x -ray, and electroencephalography (EEG)  were recorded. Furthermore,  177 
variables on location (cerebral: intraparenchymal, intraventricular, subarachnoid; spinal : intra-178 
medullary/extra-medullary, and extra -neural) and stages of the cyst(s) (active: vesicular, colloidal, 179 
granular nodular; inactive: calcified) were extracted. In addition,  other diagnostic findings such as 180 
perilesional edema and hydrocephalus  were considered. Of note, neurological si gns/symptoms 181 
pertaining to intracranial hypertension (ICH) were not recorded as they are heterogenous by nature and 182 
were assumed to have been reported inconsistently  throughout the included  case reports . All 183 
information mentioned in the text or visible on pictures was included. Favourable outcomes were 184 
defined as “Cured” or “Improved”. If patients were free from symptoms and no active cysts were visible 185 
on follow-up imaging, the patient was considered as cured. Also, if it was specifically mentioned tha t 186 
the patient was cured. The patient was considered to have improved if at least one active cyst shrank in 187 
size or if symptoms after treatment were less intense or less frequent as before. 188 
Statistical analyses: Categorical variables were compared with Chi -square tests and Chi -square tests 189 
for trends where applicable. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon test when non -190 
normally distributed. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6.2.32  191

RESULTS 193 
Search Results 194 
Searching PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Global Health (CABI), and Global Index Medicus  195 
identified a total of 13, 264 publications. Through Aoister, Open Grey, CYSTINET presentations and 196 
through personal communication 52 additional publications were found. After de -duplication, 10,088 197 
remained. After title, abstract and full text screening, 145 publications on indi vidual cases or smaller 198 
case series (containing data of overall 211 patients with NCC) were included. The search process is 199 
presented in a flowchart in Supplement Figure S1. Through expert consultations we retrieved a further 200 
82 unpublished cases of NCC.  Most of the unpublished cases were from the United Kingdom (n=34, 201 
41%) and Romania (n=31, 38%), but we also received case descriptions from Austria, France, Germany 202 
and Italy. More than 50% of the published cases were diagnosed and treated in three western European 203 
countries, namely Spain (48/211), France (38/211) and Portugal (28/211). A further 16 countries also 204 
reported cases, of which four countries reported more than 10 cases (United Kingdom, Germany, Italy 205 
and Slovenia; Figure 1, Supplement Table S3).  206

Patient demographic and clinical  characteristics 213 
Demographics and migration details 214 
Among the published cases, 53% of those with recorded sex, were female. Median age at diagnosis was 215 
32 years (interquartile range 21 to 47); the youngest patient was two years old and the oldest patient 216 
was 82 years old. Nearly every fifth (n=38, 19%) patient was a child or an adolescent (aged <18 years; 217 
Supplement Figure S2). Most patients (85%) either originated from or travelled to areas highly endemic 218 
for T. solium. Only 15% of all patients denied having travelled outside Europe; these patients were 219 
considered autochthonous cases (Table 1). More than half of tho se cases either occurred in or had a 220 
travel/migration history to/from eastern European countries, e.g. Hungary or Romania. Among the 221 
“imported” cases, approximately half of all  patients migrated from or had travelled to Latin America, 222 
mostly from Ecuador, Colombia, Bolivia or Brazil. Most of these patients were treated in Spain (61%) . 223 
Most cases from Asia had a travel history from India (22/33); most cases from Africa were diagnosed in 224 


 
 
Portugal, the majority originated from C ape Verde (17/44) and Guin ea-Bissau (5/44). A substantial 225 
proportion of unpublished cases were reported from Romania where most patients had never left 226 
Europe or even their country. Hence, almost 50% (32/70) of the unpublished cases were autochthonous 227 
cases. Contrary to the publish ed cases, among unpublished cases with travel/migration history, most 228 
migrated/travelled from Asia (58%) or Africa (42%).  229

Neuroimaging and electroencephalography 231 
All patients reported in this paper had neuroimaging performed, but it was not always sp ecified 232 
whether this was a CT and/or an MRI. In the majority of cases, diagnostic imaging was performed with 233 
a combination of CT and MRI, usually including contrast medium; in only 1 6% of the cases, diagnosis 234 
was based solely on CT scanning. In approximately one quarter ofpatients soft tissue imaging was also 235 
performed. Indications for this were most often either extensive findings on neuroimaging or palpable 236 
rice corn like cysts/calcifications on clinical examination. Among the published cases, 24 (14%) ha d an 237 
EEG documented (15 with result); of those, 4 had a normal EEG, 3 showed epileptic activity and 8 238 
showed abnormal unspecified patterns. Those were mostly patients with epileptic seizures. 239

Staging of cysts 241 
Around 90% of all NCC patients (published 89% and unpublished 93%; Table 1) had viable cysts in their 242 
brain (most commonly in the parenchyma) or spine. Viable cysts were defined as cysts in vesicular or 243 
degenerative stage. The remaining patients presented with calcifications only. There is some indication 244 
that children more often had intraparenchymal NCC  (26/29, 90%), whereas adults scored higher on 245 
intraventricular (39/199, 20%) and subarachnoid NCC (25/197, 13%; Supplement Table S4). Ninety-one 246 
(41%) patients had only a single lesion (regardless of whether viable or calcified; Table 1). Of the patients 247 
with viable cysts, published and unpublished cases taken together, 108 patients (68%) had at least one 248 
cyst with ring enhancement and 8 5 (59%) had a scolex visible on imaging. Perilesional edema was 249 
present in 124 patients (57%; Table 1). There was one case report of a patient who only had one 250 
calcification – no viable cysts – but perilesional edema. Fourty-one patients had hydrocephalus, most of 251 
which were patients with extraparenchymal or spinal cysts. Five patients with hydrocephalus were 252 
described as having only intraparenchymal cysts. Among the published cases, the majority of patients 253 
had at least one cyst in the degenerative (colloidal or granular nodular) stage (57%). Twenty-one of the 254 
144 of the patients with available detailed information on the stage of the cysts had calcifications only 255 
(Table 1). Five of 27 patients with subarachnoid cysts also had spinal cysts.  256

Neurological signs/symptoms 267 
The most common presentation in both the published (55%) and the unpublished (72%) cases, were  268 
epileptic seizures . Children more commonly presented with epileptic seizures than adults (Supplement 269 
Table S 4). Most of the seizures were of generalised onset . Concomitantly, m any patients reported 270 
headache episodes (52%). Twenty-three patients presented only with headache (12%) without any other 271 
neurological signs/symptoms. More than half of all patients (54%) presented with other neurological 272 
signs/symptoms either alone or in combination  with headache and/or epileptic seizures (Table 1). Those 273 
signs/symptoms were most commonly unsteady gait (27%), cognitive impairment (21%), impaired 274 
consciousness (20%) or impaired vision (19%). Also reported were cases with cranial nerve lesi ons, 275 
speech difficulties, meningism and vertigo (Table 2).  276

Association of neurological signs/symptoms and neuroimaging results 278 
Neurological signs/symptoms varied depending on cyst location, number, stage and other additional 279 
findings (Table 3, Figure 2, Supplement Figure S3). Regarding cyst location, presentation with epileptic 280 
seizures was more common in patients with intraparenchymal cysts. Epileptic seizures were reported 281 
in 66% of patients with intraparenchymal cysts compared with 25% of pat ients with at least one 282 
intraventricular cyst and 36% of patients with at least one subarachnoid cyst. This difference was even 283 
more pronounced when excluding patients with cysts at various locations (72% versus 15%/14%, 284 
p<0.005; Table 3 and Figure 2 A). Patients with only intraventricular cysts were more likely to present 285 
with headache than patients with only intraparenchymal or subarachnoid cysts (86% versus 43%/57%; 286 
p<0.01; Table 3). Furthermore, patients with subarachnoid cysts, were significantly olde r than patients 287 
with cysts at other locations (median: 47 years [IQR 32–56 years] versus 31 years [IQR 24–45]; Wilcoxon 288 
test p<0.005, Supplement Figure S 4). The majority of patients who showed hydrocephalus also had 289 
headache (67% versus 46%, p=0.04) or oth er neurological signs/symptoms (85% versus 34%, p<0.005; 290 
Table 3 and Figure 2B).  291 

 
 
Regarding cyst stage, patients with cysts in the vesicular stage more commonly presented with 292 
headache compared to degenerative and calcified cyst stage (65% versus 44%/38%; p=0.06). Patients 293 
with degenerative (71%) or calcified (65%) cyst stage more commonly presented with epileptic seizures 294 
(p<0.005; Table 3, Figure 2 C). There was no significant difference in the number of patients with other 295 
neurological signs/symptoms betw een the cyst stage (viable/degenerative/calcified: 47%/48%/42%, 296 
p=0.84).  297 
With respect to cyst number, patients with single lesions more commonly presented with seizures than 298 
patients with multiple lesions (p<0.005; Table 3), but there was no difference fo r headache or other 299 
neurological signs/symptoms. Among the published cases, seven had spinal cysts only. Symptoms 300 
ranged from general disorientation and headache , which could indicate undetected cereberal 301 
involvement,  to brachialgia, brachial paralysis,  bladder dysfunction, L5 radiculopathy, steppage gait 302 
and cauda equina syndrome. Overall, 30 patients additionally had extra -neural lesions. Most common 303 
locations were ocular cysts (n=8), cysts in the thoracic/back muscles (n=10) and calcifications in the thigh 304 
muscles (n=7). 305

Laboratory tests 307 
As determined by the inclusion criteria, all patients had NCC confirmed on neuroimaging. In addition, 308 
one hundred and eighty-four patients (63%) had serological testing of which 131 (71%) were positive in 309 
any test (serum or CSF, antigen or antibody). Among  published cases 73% had a positive test, of the 310

Treatment and outcomes 321 
Anthelminthic therapy was used in the treatment of the majority of NCC cases: 191 patients (81%) were 322 
treated with anthelmintics . Most patients received albendazole, either alone (76%) or in combination 323 
with praziquantel (15%; Table 1).  The duration of anthelmintic treatment ranged from a single dose to 324 
three months (praziquantel) and from a single dose to nine months (albendazole). The most common 325 

 
 
treatment duration was 10–15 days (Supplement Figure S5), longer for patients with extraparenchymal 326 
lesions compared to those with intraparenchymal lesions (Supplement Table S9). Eleven patients with 327 
only extraparenchymal lesions were treated with ant helmintic medication, e.g. after extirpation of 328 
spinal cysts. 329 
One-hundred-fifty-five patients (73%) received corticosteroid therapy, either dexamethasone or 330 
prednisolone/prednisone. Of those, 147 (95%) patients were also treated with anthelmintics, and 8 (5%) 331 
patients were treated with steroids alone. Dexamethasone was more frequently used than 332 
prednisolone/prednisone (68% versus 41%; some used both; Table 1).  333 
Surgical treatment was performed in 7 1 patients (40%) , mainly patients with intraventricular cysts . 334 
Surgical treatment often involved extirpation of the cysts and drainage of the hydrocephalus through 335 
ventricular shunting . Twenty -three o f 34 (68%) patients with hydrocephalus received ventricular 336 
shunting. Extirpated cysts were usually analysed pathologically (Table 1).  337 
The majority of patients presenting with epileptic seizures were put on antiepileptic drugs (AED) unless 338 
they had been on AED already. Twenty-five patients (21%) did not receive AED despite presenting with 339 
epileptic seizures. The most common AED was carbamazepine (400mg/d), followed by levetiracetam 340 
(1000mg/d) and valproic acid (1000mg/d; Supplement Table S10).  341 
When reported, the treatment outcome was favourable in 139/155 (90%) patients, although only 68 (44%) 342 
patients were reported to have been cured from NCC. Ten percent of the patients did not have an 343 
improvement of symptoms or lesions — some even deteriorated and five patients, of which four were 344 
younger than 40 years old, died from the disease during or after treatment (Table 1). Two of the patients 345 
who died had intraventricular cysts, one patient concomitantly had a glioblastome multiforme, one 346 
patient was liv ing with HIV and developed bronchopneumonia during therapy, and the fifth patient 347 
died from epileptic seizures.  Supplement Table S 11 shows treatment outcome by various parameters. 348 
The outcome did not differ significantly for any of the parameters (p>0.05 for all). Children more 349 
commonly were cured through therapy than adults (20/35 [57%] versus 48/120 [40%]). 350

Discussion 351 
In this study, we present demographic and clinical details of almost 300 patients with NCC in the 352 
European context, based on a systematic literature search, including grey literature, in addition to 353 
unpublished NCC cases collected from colleagues through t he European network CYSTINET. We 354 
report several novel observations as well as observations that are in line with previously published 355 
literature.16–19,33,34 356 
 357 
Origins of infection 358 
 359 
We were able to show that most patients treated for NCC in Europe are migrants from countries 360 
endemic for T. solium. However, confirming the exact region of origin of infection is a challenge, as NCC 361 
often becomes symptomatic only several years after first exposure. There seems to be only few regions 362 
in Europe where the full lifecycle of T. solium is still present and in those regions, autochthonous NCC 363 
cases can still occur .18  Our search yielded relatively more autochthonous cases among unpublished 364 
NCC cases compared with published cases. This is likely due to reporting differences between countries 365 
of published and unpublished cases, e.g. especially the reported cases in Romania were mostly 366 
unpublished. The number of autochthonous cases reported should be interpreted with caution, as 367 


 
 
routine travel history reported usually included only a few non -standardized questions and d id not 368 
include a detailed epidemiologic interview. Also, it only requires one tapeworm carrier to infect other 369 
people with cysticercosis – for autochthonous cases, this person could either be another local person but 370 
could also be a migrant which makes it difficult to trace back the source of infection. In our review, we 371 
also found a considerably lower proportion of autochthonous cases compared with a  review on 372 
cysticercosis in Europe, by Zammarchi et al. in 2013 , which found 62% of cysticercosis cases being 373 
autochtonous cases which may suggest improvements in the disruption of the T. solium lifecycle. 16  374 
 375 
Clinical characteristics and related findings on neuroimaging 376 
 377 
The sites and stages of cysts influences how patients with NCC present. With regards to site, most 378 
symptomatic patients with intraparenchymal cysts presented with epileptic seizures compared to 379 
patients with extraparenchymal cysts who were more likely to present with non-specific symptoms like 380 
headache. Patients with extraparenchymal cysts were sign ificantly older at diagnosis. This may be 381 
because patients infected at an older age were more likely to develop extraparenchymal NCC e.g. due 382 
to comorbidities and immunological factors  or because symptoms were less specific and therefore 383 
diagnosis was made at a later stage.35 These findings, i.e. patients with extraparenchymal lesions being 384 
older and showing more non-specific signs/symptoms, concur with NCC data published from Mexico.5 385 
The number of patients with autochthonous cases was rather low so it was difficult to draw conclusions 386 
on disease presentation in comparison to patients you were infected in Latin America, Asia or Africa. 387 
Ring enhancing lesions and those with perilesional edema also seemed to predispose to presentation 388 
with epileptic seizures. However, although epileptic seizures occur red frequently under those 389 
conditions, around half of the cases were also accompanied by other neurological signs and symptoms, 390 
including headache. The latter, however, was frequently reported in extraparenchymal NCC 391 
(intraventricular and subarachnoid NCC) and when hydrocephalus was present. Looking at cyst stage, 392 
headache prevailed in the vesicular stage, where little to no inflammation was evident on radiology, the 393 
pathophysiology of headache under th ese conditions is not clear (ASW unpublished data), whereas 394 
epileptic seizures were the predominant neurological sym ptom during the degenerative stage of the 395 
cyst(s) 396 
It is well established that c linical manifestations of NCC can vary from completely asymptomatic 397 
infection (54% of NCC patients in a study of Monteiro de Almeida et al.36) to severe disease and death.2,37 398 
The major determinants of the characteristics of symptomatic NCC are the number of cysts, their 399 
location, their stage  and the degree of inflammation .35,38 It has been shown previously that NCC can 400 
mimic almost any neurological disorder39, but to date neurological signs/symptoms other than epileptic 401 
seizures or headache are thought to occur in the minority of patients.1 In concordance with previous 402 

 
 
studies, we found a high proportion of epileptic seizures among NCC patients .1,16,40 However, in our 403 
study of European presentations, we found that more than half of all NCC patients presented with other 404 
neurological signs/symptoms. This may reflect some publication bias, as unusual case presentations 405 
more likely come to the attention of a clinician and be published than patients presenting with well -406 
known signs/symptoms of NCC. Also, patients presenting in low-income and middle-income countries 407 
may be more likely to present later to health services than in high-income countries, often with more 408 
severe symptoms and subtle neurological signs and symptoms may not be identified or recorded as 409 
consistently. 410 
Regarding publications included in our study, the classification of neurological signs/symptoms other 411 
than epileptic seizures and headache was challenging as description of signs/symptoms could be vague 412 
and their origin remain ing uncertain (see Table 3). Remarkably, in our study, impaired cognitive 413 
function appeared to be one of the most frequent other neurological signs. In the included publications, 414 
EEG was rarely performed and predominantly only when epileptic seizures were present. Therefore, 415 
non-convulsive seizures that could account for impaired cognitive function  may not have been 416 
identified. A  high proportion of cognitive decline (87.5%), dementia (12.5%) or altered mental state 417 
(28%) in NCC patients has also been observed in previous studies.1,41,42  418 
 419 
Special considerations 420 
 421 
According to previously published studies, between 1.5% and 3% of NCC patients are estimated to have 422 
spinal cysts .43–48 The true proportion is likely higher than 3% as spinal imaging is not  routinely  423 
performed. In our dataset, this proportion was higher (18/171; 10.5%). For example, a  study in Peru 424 
found spinal cysts in 17 of 28 (61%) patients with basal subarachnoid cysts , supporting a  425 
recommendation to perform  spinal imaging in all patients with basal subarachnoid NCC .49 In our 426 
dataset, 5/27 (19%) patients with subarachnoid NCC also had spinal cysts, while not all 27 patients had 427 
cysts in the basal subarachnoid space. Combined cerebral and spinal imaging was not often done, or at 428 
least not often reported in our European cases and may result in an underestimation of the true burden 429 
of spinal NCC in the context of subarachnoid NCC in our study population.  430 
In addition to spinal NCC, we would like to highlight the identification of perilesional edema around a 431 
calcification. In our study population , we found one patient where perilesional edema around a 432 
calcification was present. Until recently , calcifications were considered inactive in terms of immune 433 
response. However, it seems that calcified cysticerci can temporarily release residual antigen that may 434 
trigger an immune response.50–52 Why this happens is still not entirely clear. It could make treatment in 435 
patients with concomitant viable cysts and calcifications challenging, as epileptic seizures may still 436 
occur after cyst resolution (after anthelmintic therapy or spontaneously) and therefore discontinuation 437 

 
 
of AED after resolution of viable cysts in patients with concomitant intermittent perilesional edema 438 
around calcifications  may carry risks . Clinicians should be aware that this may occur. Perilesional 439 
edema around calcifications should not be treated with anthelmintic drugs and corticosteroid therapy 440 
is not routinely recommended.53  441

The value of serology and neuroimaging  443 
 444 
In our study, serological testing of any kind for T. solium in CSF and/or serum was reported in only 61% 445 
of cases. It is likely that serological testing was performed as confirmation after neuroimaging, however, 446 
from our dataset we could not establish why serological testing was performed  in some cases and not 447 
others, nor the time point when it was performed in relation to presentation. Serological detection of T. 448 
solium antigen and antibodies is not yet widely available throughout Europe . There are only a few 449 
commercial tests available on  the market, and these are often supplied in a form that is not always 450 
suitable for laboratories that see only occasional cases. In addition, in -house assays are not easy to 451 
implement and validate for laboratories due to a lack of well -defined reference se ra. A commercial 452 
antigen test has only been available in the last few years . Until then, in-house assays relied on 453 
monoclonal antibodies which were not readily available. The low number of antigen tests  reported is 454 
particularly striking, as there is good e vidence that antigen follow -up is useful in the context of 455 
therapeutic outcome monitoring in symptomatic active NCC cases. Here, clear and easily available 456 
guidelines for T. solium serological testing seem desirable. Although antigen testing was performed 457 
much less frequently than antibody testing, it showed higher sensitivity. However, it must be taken into 458 
account that the proportion of patients with active stage lesions was rather high which may have 459 
influenced the sensitivity of the antigen ELISA.  Still, one-third of the confirmed NCC cases, more so 460 
those with single lesions, were not positive in any serological assay which demonstrates the need for 461 
establishing a standardized approach to immunodiagnostic testing in European laboratories.  462 
Regarding neur oimaging, a combination of MRI and CT imaging is recommended in the Clinical 463 
Practice Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of NCC by the Infectious Diseases Society of 464 
America (IDSA) and the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene (ASTMH) guidelines and 465 
in the WHO guidelines on management of Taenia solium neurocysticercosis, because MRI has a higher 466 
sensitivity for detecting active NCC lesions and CT imaging ha s a higher sensitivity for detecting 467 
calcifications.53,54 In our study, a third of all patients only had one type of neuroimaging performed, 468 
which may have lead to an underreporting of both active and inactive NCC lesions. 469

Treatment of symptomatic patients with neurocysticercosis 471 
 472 

 
 
In the IDSA/ASTMH guidelines, different recommendations for anthelmintic treatment are given for 473 
different manifestations of NCC.53 Surgical removal and/or ventricular shunting in the case of increased 474 
intracranial pressure is recommended for patients with intraventricular cysts. For patients with NCC -475 
related hydrocephalus or diffuse cerebral edema, corticosteroid therapy without anthelmintics is 476 
recommended. For patients with one or two intraparenchymal cysts, 15mg/kg/d ay albendazole is  477 
advised for 10-14 days with treatment extendended if no effect in terms of cyst resolution is shown; for 478 
patients with more than two viable intraparenchymal cysts it is recommended to combine albendazole 479 
with praziquantel 50mg/kg/day is recommended.53 Generally, treatment recommendations do not differ 480 
for children and adults, apart from an adjustment of anthelmintic drug dosage. In our dataset, which 481 
includes many cases that were diagnosed and treated before the above guidelines were published in 482 
2018, approaches to tre atment were extremely varied and  longer durations and higher dosages were 483 
given to patients with more severe manifestations or to non-responders. This reflects the importance of 484 
having clear consensus guidance for treatment in order to improve safety and e fficacy, and to better 485 
understand treatment outcomes. 486 
In our European population , corticosteroids were mostly administered for the same duration as 487 
anthelmintic therapy although surprisingly, many patients received corticosteroids for shorter time 488 
than anthelmintics. This had been recommended previous ly7,8, but is nowadays  not recommended . 489 
However, this could also be due to the fact that concurrent steroid therapy was not consistently 490 
reported. For corticosteroid therapy, the IDSA/ASTMH guidelin es recommend steroids for the entire 491 
duration of the anthelmintic therapy and even  starting 3 to 4 days in advance. Generally, 492 
dexamethasone and prednisolone are recommended. For dexamethasone, a dosage of 0.1mg/kg/d is 493 
used for patients with intraparenchy mal cysts and up to 0.2mg/kg/d ay for patients with 494 
extraparenchymal cysts in the basal cisterns or Sylvian fissure. Successful treatment is contingent on 495 
finding the right steroid dose which on the one hand prevents side effects of anthelmintic therapy (i. e. 496 
epileptic seizures, severe headache or increased intracranial pressure), but on the other hand also does 497 
not suppress the effect of anthelmintic drugs. This is particularly important when treating with a 498 
combination therapy of albendazole and praziquant el, as there are enzymatic interactions with 499 
dexamethasone, which may lower plasma levels of praziquantel.55 The IDSA/ASTMH guidelines do not 500 
specify whether steroids should be given even after anthelmintic therapy  has ended , but  do 501 
recommendthat steroids should be tapered if they have been given for more than two weeks .53 It is 502 
important to note that  the cysticidal effects of anthelmintic therapy may last  beyond the end of the 503 
treatment cycle, especially with combination therapy with albendazole and praziquantel, and reducing 504 
steroid doses over a longer period may be advisable in these cases, at least until disappearance of any 505 
perilesional oedema. 506 

 
 
We found different AEDs used in patients treated in our European population . Whilst AEDs are 507 
recommended for all patients with epileptic seizures according to  IDSA/ASTMH guidelines, no 508 
recommendation on the type is given; choice should be based on local availability, cost, drug -drug 509 
interactions, and potential side effects.53  510

Recommendations for diagnosis and treatment of neurocysticercosis 512 
 513 
With this publication we woul d like to raise the awareness of NCC among clinicians working in 514 
European countries . While a  travel history to or from endemic countries may support a suspected 515 
diagnosis of NCC, the absence of a relevant travel history does not exclude the presence of NCC .  In 516 
addition, NCC remains predominantly a neuroradiological diagnosis, which means that performing a 517 
neuroradiological examination, preferably CT and MRI combined, should be the first priority.  At 518 
present, serological testing plays only a supportive role in the case of suspicious cerebral lesions on 519 
imaging. In addition, the possibility of spinal NCC  and temporary perilesional edema around 520 
calcifications must be considered and neuroimaging tailored to answer these questions.  521 
With a few exceptions, mainly regarding the availability of serological tests and some drugs, until 522 
guidelines tailored to the European context may become available , the IDSA/ASTMH guidelines can, 523 
for the time being,  be applied to the European context, although there may be only limited access to 524 
MRI i n some areas of  Europe. Based on our experience with the  IDSA/ASTMH guidelines , we 525 
recommend that he next update  reconsiders the  treatment guidelines for (co-existing) hydrocephalus 526 
and the length and dose of concomitant steroid therapy in combination therapy with albendazole and 527 
praziquantel. 528

Neurocysticercosis in the context of migration and travel medicine 530 
We were able to demonstrate that NCC occurs in Europe albeit most of the cases occur red among 531 
migrants and travellers. With increasing travel and migration, an increase in NCC cases in Europe can 532 
also be expected , although globally the number of NCC cases is decreasing.56 This phenomenon has 533 
been described for other neglected tropical diseases before.57 Migrants often face inequities in access to 534 
healthcare which hampers diagnosis of infectious diseases, and which may have been even more 535 
pronounced during the COVID-19 pandemic.58,59 There has been increasing effort by the International 536 
Society on Travel Medicine to promote migrant health and to bring to attention diseases that more 537 
commonly occur among migrants. Hence, this study can also serve this purpose.  538

The strength of the current paper lies in the combination of original patient data from unpublished NCC 542 
cases combined with patient characteristics gathered through an exhaustive systematic literature search 543 
including grey literature , in several major European languages. Detailed clinical and demographic 544 
characteristics of NCC patients diagnosed and treated in Europe have not previously been so 545 
comprehensively summarized in one document, and this study will therefore be of value to any clinician 546 
in Europe engaged in the diagnosis or treatment of NCC. Europe is a unique setting for NCC as T. solium 547 
is not highly prevalent while diagnostic facilities, particularly neuroimaging, are widely available . 548 
Regardless, a number of important  limitations should be acknowledged.  Full text access to the huge 549 
number of identified NCC related publications worldwide was not possible, and neither was it possible 550 
to acquire access to all country-specific publications. Inherent publication biases are, however, likely to 551 
be of greater importance : a) only selected cases being published, and b) often no detailed clinical 552 
information being provided in  case reports. Also, sharing of unpublished cases, as well as grey 553 
literature, depends on research interests and cooperation, and identifyingall unpublished NCC cases in 554 
Europe exceeded our research capacity. Thus, the data presented in this study are prone to bias, 555 
potentially towards more over-representation of more unusual cases. In addition, a ssessment of 556 
outcomes is limited by l ack of standardisation of timing at which the outcome may have been 557 
determined. 558 
 559 
Conclusions 560 
The synthesis of knowledge of and information about NCC in the European context contained in this 561 
publication is unprecedented.  NCC represents a rare disease for European clinicians and hence clinical 562 
familiarity outside specialist centres is most likely scarce.  The current publication contributes to a better 563 
understanding of the origin of infection with T. solium, clinical characteristics, diagnosis and treatment 564 
of patients suffering from NCC in Europe. These data highlight that NCC causes considerable morbidity 565 
in patients diagnosed in Europe and that overall treatment outcomes can be improved.  In some 566 
patients, NCC is a cause of death.  Due to the complexity of the life cycle of T. solium and the latency 567 
until signs and/or symptoms appear, reliable mu lti-/interdisciplinary data are needed to establish 568 
standardized context -specific evidence -based management guidelines for practising clinicians in 569 
Europe and beyond.  570
