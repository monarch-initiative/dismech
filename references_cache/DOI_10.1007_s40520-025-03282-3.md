# Effectiveness of a multidomain lifestyle counseling intervention on intrinsic capacity in older women: a randomized clinical trial
**Authors:** Mahsa Rouhani-Otaghsara, Shabnam Omidvar, Mahdi Sepidarkish, Afsaneh Bakhtiari, Fatemeh Nasiri-Amiri

Intrinsic capacity (IC), the composite of physical and mental capacities, is crucial for health and well-being in older adults. With aging populations experiencing IC decline, effective interventions are needed to promote healthy aging. This study evaluated the effectiveness of a structured lifestyle counseling program on IC in older women.

In this 8-week cluster randomized controlled trial, 240 women aged ≥ 60 were equally allocated to intervention or control groups. The intervention group participated in an 8-session (30–45 min/session) counseling program delivered in small groups (10 participants each), focusing on five IC domains: nutrition (vitality), physical exercise (locomotion), psychological well-being (happiness/depression management), cognitive stimulation, and sensory health. Sessions included education, goal-setting, and interactive discussions. The control group received no intervention during the study period but obtained educational materials post-trial. IC domains were assessed at baseline and 1-week post-intervention using validated tools: SPPB and TUG (locomotion), Mini-Cog (cognition), GDS (Geriatric Depression Scale) (psychological), MNA (Mini Nutritional Assessment) (vitality), and self-reported sensory measures.

The intervention group showed significantly greater improvement in overall IC compared to controls (adjusted mean difference [aMD] = 2.07, 95%CI: 1.69, 2.44). Domain-specific improvements included locomotion (aMD = 0.92, 95%CI: 0.68, 1.16), vitality (aMD =1.01 , 95%CI: 0.67, 1.33), cognition (aMD = 0.80, 95%CI: 0.52, 1.09), psychological (aMD = 2.28, 95%CI:1.37, 3.19), and sensory (aMD = 0.29, 95%CI:0.13, 0.44).

An 8-week lifestyle counseling intervention significantly improved intrinsic capacity and its domains in older women. These findings support the implementation of targeted, multidomain interventions to promote healthy aging, though longer-term studies are needed to assess sustained benefits.

Modern societies face the significant challenge of population aging, with profound implications for healthcare systems worldwide [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines individuals aged 60 years and older as older adults [2]. The aging process is characterized by progressive physiological decline across multiple organ systems, including sensory, musculoskeletal, and cognitive functions [3,4].

Global demographic trends underscore the urgency of addressing aging-related challenges. United Nations projections indicate that by 2050, older adults will constitute over 20% of the world’s population, exceeding 2 billion individuals. This demographic shift necessitates evidence-based strategies to promote healthy aging and mitigate associated health burdens [5,6,7,8].

In response to these challenges, the WHO introduced the innovative concept of intrinsic capacity (IC) in its 2015 World Report on Aging and Health. IC represents “the composite of all the physical and mental capacities that an individual can draw upon” [9,10]. Growing evidence demonstrates IC’s clinical relevance. Longitudinal studies have established IC as a robust predictor of care dependency, chronic disease progression, and mortality risk [11,12,13]. Systematic reviews highlight IC’s value in guiding person-centered interventions for older populations, and mortality [14].

Lifestyle factors significantly influence IC trajectories. Meta-analyses indicate that multidomain interventions targeting physical activity, nutrition, and cognitive stimulation can enhance IC in older adults [15]. However, research gaps remain regarding the effectiveness of structured counseling approaches, particularly for older women in community settings [16,17]. This study aims to evaluate the impact of a structured counseling intervention on IC domains among older women. Our findings will contribute to the growing evidence base for IC-focused interventions and inform clinical practice for healthy aging promotion.

This was a cluster 8-week RCT, that was conducted in a university hospital affiliated with Babol University of Medical Science, Babol, Iran. This study targeted women aged 60 and above in Babol, who were referred to Babol city’s older daycare centers. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Local Ethical Committee (protocol n. 724134857). The study was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (registration number: IRCT20230328057780N1). Participants were recruited through advertising and direct referrals. The researcher introduced themselves to the center officials, outlined the research objectives, and sought their collaboration.

Eligible participants were provided with detailed information about the study and completed written informed consent forms. Inclusion criteria required participants to be healthy and without diagnosed neurological, motor, physical, or mental disorders. They must not have undergone hormone therapy in the past two months, have no history of substance abuse, drug addiction, smoking, or medication misuse, and no past eating disorders. Participants should not engage in intense physical activity or be concurrently enrolled in another study, nor should they follow specific dietary patterns such as vegetarianism or a raw food diet.

Ten eligible older women were initially selected, and after consulting with a design methodology expert, the intervention type, test group, and control group were determined. The study employed a cluster randomized design where groups of 10 participants were randomized together rather than as individuals. This approach was chosen to: [1] prevent contamination between intervention and control participants who might interact at the same community centers [2], maintain the fidelity of the group-based intervention delivery, and [3] reflect real-world implementation conditions where interventions are typically delivered to groups. Each cluster of 10 participants received the same allocation (either intervention or control) through block randomization using an online platform (www.sealedenvelope.com), with block sizes ranging from four to eight clusters to ensure balanced allocation.

The randomization envelopes were generated by one researcher (M.S.), who maintained the trial’s concealment, while another researcher (M.R.) enrolled the participants and assigned them to their respective treatment groups. The intrinsic capacity of each participant was assessed at the beginning of the study. Following the intervention, a reevaluation of intrinsic capacity was conducted one week after the sessions concluded.

The intervention group participated in an 8-week structured lifestyle counseling program developed by the Department of Older Health at the Iranian Ministry of Health. This evidence-based program was delivered through eight weekly group sessions (30–45 min each) to groups of 10 participants, employing a multidomain approach targeting five key components of intrinsic capacity. Each session integrated psychoeducation with interactive components, including didactic instruction, group discussions, and practical exercises. The nutritional component provided counseling on age-appropriate dietary patterns, emphasizing portion control, nutrient requirements for older adults, and practical meal planning strategies. Participants received personalized guidance on improving dietary habits while considering individual preferences and cultural food practices. For physical activity promotion, the program focused on counseling participants to incorporate sustainable increases in daily walking and simple home-based strength exercises. This included demonstration of proper form, safety considerations for older adults, and individualized goal-setting for gradual activity progression. The psychological well-being component utilized cognitive-behavioral techniques to address happiness and depressive symptoms. This involved thought-challenging exercises, behavioral activation through positive activity scheduling, and stress reduction strategies tailored for older populations. Balance education formed an important part of the intervention, with specific attention to fall prevention strategies. Certified instructors provided verbal instruction and demonstration of evidence-based techniques, including home safety modifications, center-of-gravity control exercises, and methods to improve proprioception during daily activities. While this did not include supervised balance training sessions, participants learned to incorporate these principles into their routines. The program also emphasized social connection through structured group discussions that fostered peer support and shared problem-solving about common aging challenges. Each session followed a standardized protocol combining education with practical application, including weekly goal-setting with progress reviews, barrier problem-solving exercises, and take-home materials to reinforce learning. All intervention components were delivered by trained facilitators using a participant-centered counseling approach, emphasizing practical implementation rather than theoretical knowledge. The program’s standardized manual ensured consistency across groups while allowing for individual adaptation based on participants’ specific needs and capabilities. Session fidelity was monitored through periodic observation and facilitator checklists. The control group received no active intervention during the study period, reflecting standard care conditions for older women in Iran. Upon completion of all follow-up assessments, educational materials were provided to the control group as part of ethical post-trial knowledge dissemination.

The primary efficacy endpoint was change from baseline in IC and its domains score. Endpoint of the present study were evaluated at baseline, and at 8-weeks follow-up visits. The different domains of IC proposed by the WHO were assessed, based on available data: (i) Locomotion was measured using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) and the Timed Up and Go (TUG) tests; (ii) Cognition was evaluated with the Mini-Cog questionnaire, a screening tool for older adults, combining a three-item recall test and a clock-drawing test (CDT). It requires minimal training and takes about three minutes to administer. The recall test involves remembering three words, while the CDT assesses visual-spatial skills by having the patient draw a clock. Scoring includes one point per correctly recalled word (maximum three) and one point for a normal clock. A total score of 0–2 suggests cognitive impairment, while 3–5 indicates lower likelihood, though further assessment is recommended if a score of 3 accompanies an abnormal clock; (iii) The psychological domain was assessed using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS). The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) screens for depression in older adults, using 30 yes/no questions (or 15-item short form). Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms, guiding further clinical evaluation. The psychological domain scores reversed (higher scores = better functioning) to maintain directional consistency with other intrinsic capacity domains where higher scores indicate improvement; (iv) The vitality domain was measured using the Mini-Nutrition Assessment questionnaire (MNA). The Mini-Nutrition Assessment (MNA) questionnaire, comprising 18 items, is a validated tool designed to assess the nutritional status of older individuals. It integrates dimensions such as dietary intake, anthropometric measurements, and functional status. The MNA uses a scoring system where a total score of 24–30 indicates normal nutritional status, 17–23.5 suggests risk of malnutrition, and a score below 17 identifies malnutrition. This comprehensive screening instrument aids in the early identification and management of malnutrition, improving health outcomes in geriatric populations. ; (v) The sensory domain was assessed through self-reported visual and hearing impairments. A score was calculated based on the presence of hypoacusia (yes = 1, no = 0) and reduced visual acuity (yes = 1, no = 0), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 2. To minimize bias, outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation and were not involved in intervention delivery. Participants were reminded not to reveal their assigned group during follow-up assessments. The intervention and assessment teams worked independently to maintain blinding integrity.

The sample size calculation, performed using NCSS PASS version 15 software based on Analysis of Covariance, determined that 120 individuals per group were required, accounting for a 20% dropout rate and a design effect of 2 due to the cluster design. The analysis aims for a power of 0.8, indicating a high probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis. The alpha level is set at 0.05, representing a 5% risk of committing a Type I error. The effect size (mean difference) of 0.1 is calculated as the ratio of the standard deviation of the group means under the alternative hypothesis to the within-group standard deviation, which is 0.04 and 0.35 respectively. Additionally, the analysis incorporates five covariates, which influence the outcome variable, and achieves a combined R2of 0.8, reflecting the proportion of variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the covariates. This meticulous approach ensures that the study is sufficiently powered to detect meaningful differences among group means, thus enhancing the validity and reliability of the statistical conclusions drawn from the ANCOVA.

Descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and frequency and percentages for categorical variables, were provided. Baseline clinical and demographic variables were compared between the intervention and control groups using independent t-tests, chi-square, and Fisher’s exact test. Mixed-effect linear models (MELMs) were employed to investigate the longitudinal progression of IC score and its domains across different randomization groups over a period of 8 weeks. Composite intrinsic capacity scores were calculated by first standardizing each domain score (converting to z-scores with mean = 0, SD = 1) to ensure equal weighting, then summing these standardized scores. Raw domain scores are additionally reported to maintain clinical interpretability of each measure. Results were reported as adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. The model included variables known to impact outcomes based on the literature. A significance level of 0.05 was applied to all tests. Data analysis was performed using Stata version 17.

We commenced the recruitment of study participants over the course of eight months between July 2023 and March 2024. We screened 253 women for eligibility; 240 women had inclusion criteria and included in the study (120 women in each group). There were 12 women who were not eligible; the most common reason was neurological, physical, or mental disorders (12 [4.74%] women). One participant did not enter the study due to personal reasons. There were no lost participants in either the intervention or control group during the study. The participant’s flow diagram is shown in Fig.1.

The BMI was similar between the two groups, with the intervention group having a mean BMI of 28.93 ± 3.89 kg/m² and the control group having a mean BMI of 28.72 ± 3.95 kg/m² (p= 0.672). Regarding education, 28.3% of the intervention group were illiterate compared to 39.2% in the control group. Participants with a diploma or under diploma constituted 61.7% of the intervention group and 55.1% of the control group. The academic level was 10.0% in the intervention group and 5.8% in the control group, with no significant difference in educational levels between the groups (p= 0.145). A significant difference was found in the place of residence, with 71.7% of the intervention group living in urban areas compared to 55.0% in the control group (p= 0.007). The prevalence of underlying disease was similar between the groups, with 79.2% in the intervention group and 77.5% in the control group reporting at least one underlying disease (p= 0.754).

Table2present baseline, post intervention and values of changes for IC and its domains in both groups. No significant difference was detected in intended outcomes between groups at baseline.

The 8-week lifestyle intervention significantly improved IC in the intervention group compared to controls (aMD: 2.07, 95%CI: 1.69, 2.44;p< 0.001). Post-intervention, the intervention group showed a 14.0% increase in IC (baseline: 54.68 ± 9.02; post: 62.35 ± 7.24), while controls had a marginal 3.9% change (55.43 ± 10.86 to 57.57 ± 10.68) (Table2).

The largest absolute improvement occurred in psychological domain (aMD = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.37, 3.19), reflecting a 25.7% reduction in depressive symptoms (intervention: Δ: 3.20 ± 4.92 vs. control: Δ: 0.78 ± 2.27). Due to its 30-point scale range, this domain contributed nearly half (42%) of the total IC score change.

The intervention group showed significantly greater improvement in locomotion than controls (change difference = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.69, 1.25,p< 0.001), with post-intervention scores substantially higher in the intervention group (9.50 ± 1.12 vs. 8.60 ± 1.78; aMD = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.68, 1.16,p< 0.001).

The intervention group experienced a significantly greater increase in vitality (mean change: 1.39 ± 1.51) compared to the control group (mean change: 0.40 ± 1.31,p< 0.001). The post-treatment adjusted vitality score was significantly higher in the intervention group (24.51 ± 2.29) than in the control group (23.77 ± 3.35,p< 0.001) (aMD: 1.01, 95%CI: 0.67, 1.33;p< 0.001).

The intervention group showed a significantly greater improvement in cognition (mean change: 1.70 ± 1.70) compared to the control group (mean change: 0.87 ± 1.19,p< 0.001). The adjusted cognition score post-treatment was significantly higher in the intervention group (9.10 ± 0.51) compared to the control group (8.21 ± 1.98,p< 0.001) (aMD: 0.80, 95%CI: 0.52, 1.09;p< 0.001).

Total sensory scores significantly increased at the end of treatment in both the intervention and control groups. However, the increment in sensory score was significantly higher in the intervention group (sensory score from 3.20 ± 1.09 to 3.25 ± 0.87) compared with the control group (sensory score from 3.26 ± 1.05 to 3.27 ± 1.06) (Change difference: 0.31, 95%CI: 0.13, 0.48;p< 0.001). After adjustment for basic value of sensory score and other potential confounders, the treatment was associated with a significant improvement of sensory score compared to the control group (aMD: 0.29, 95%CI: 0.13, 0.44;p< 0.001) (Table2).

Our 8-week lifestyle counseling intervention significantly improved intrinsic capacity (IC) and its domains in older women compared to controls. These results provide preliminary evidence that structured, multidomain lifestyle interventions may enhance key markers of healthy aging in the short term. However, as the reviewer rightly notes, the brief follow-up period limits our ability to determine whether these benefits translate to sustained improvements in health or functional independence. The observed improvements, particularly in psychological well-being, locomotion, and vitality, signal potential clinical relevance, aligning with prior studies showing that similar interventions can positively influence aging trajectories. However, longer-term studies are needed to confirm whether these gains persist or lead to measurable reductions in disability or care dependency.

Our findings demonstrate significant improvements across all intrinsic capacity domains, with particularly robust effects in psychological well-being (aMD = 2.28). These results extend the work of Yu et al. (2023) [18], who reported similar multidomain benefits but in a more heterogeneous population, suggesting our targeted approach may offer advantages for healthier older adults. The observed effect sizes compare favorably to those in the MAPT trial [19], where longer interventions showed smaller effects, potentially due to their inclusion of participants with greater comorbidity burden. Importantly, our psychological domain improvements (25.7% reduction in depressive symptoms) exceed those typically seen in community-based interventions [20], possibly reflecting our program’s structured group format and emphasis on goal-setting. However, unlike the TIGER trial [21], we did not measure biomarker changes, leaving open questions about biological mechanisms underlying our clinical improvements.

One such study is the randomized controlled trial by Yu et al. (2023) [18], which explored the effects of a structured lifestyle counseling intervention, including nutrition, physical activity, and psychological support, on intrinsic capacity in older adults. The study reported significant improvements in intrinsic capacity and its domains, aligning with the positive outcomes observed in our own trial.

In particular, Yu et al. found that the holistic approach of their intervention, which included similar components to those in our study—such as physical exercise, nutritional counseling, and psychological support—resulted in notable enhancements in mobility, cognition, and overall intrinsic capacity. This similarity reinforces the effectiveness of a multifaceted lifestyle intervention in promoting functional independence and well-being among older individuals [18].

Utilizing data from the Taiwan Integrated Geriatric Care (TIGER) study, a randomized controlled trial, the researchers compared the effects of a 12-month multidomain intervention with usual care. The study assessed changes in IC across five subdomains—locomotion, cognition, vitality, psychological, and sensory—at multiple follow-up points. The intervention group demonstrated significant improvements in IC, particularly in locomotion and cognition, compared to the usual care group. Additionally, changes in various biomarkers, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), serum albumin, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S), free androgen index (FAI), and vitamin D, were associated with improvements in IC [21].

The study investigated the effects of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid supplementation and a multidomain intervention on IC among community-dwelling older adults, as part of the Multidomain Alzheimer Preventive Trial (MAPT). This secondary analysis included 1,445 participants randomized into four groups: multidomain intervention plus omega-3, multidomain intervention plus placebo, omega-3 supplementation alone, and placebo alone. Over three years, the study assessed changes in IC across several domains using tools such as the Geriatric Depression Scale for psychological assessment, the Short Physical Performance Battery for mobility, cognitive tests for cognitive function, and handgrip strength for vitality. The results revealed a decline in IC across all groups, with no significant differences between the interventions and control. Sensitivity analyses identified significant declines within groups starting at 24 months, indicating that none of the interventions had a notable impact on improving the composite IC Z-score [19].

In contrast, our study, which employed a structured lifestyle counseling intervention, demonstrated significant improvements in intrinsic capacity among older women. Unlike the MAPT study, which found overall declines in IC, our intervention, which included physical exercise, nutrition, psychological support, and balance training, led to notable enhancements in locomotion, cognition, vitality, and psychological well-being. This discrepancy may be attributed to the more targeted and holistic nature of our approach compared to the MAPT study’s combined omega-3 and multidomain interventions, which did not yield significant improvements. Our findings suggest that a multifaceted lifestyle intervention, addressing a broader range of domains and incorporating direct lifestyle changes, may offer more substantial benefits for maintaining or improving intrinsic capacity in older populations.

The study evaluated the effects of a 12-week Vivifrail exercise program on IC among frail, cognitively impaired community-dwelling older adults. Conducted as a secondary analysis of a multicenter randomized clinical trial, the research involved 188 participants aged 84.06 years on average, who were classified as pre-frail or frail with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia. Participants were randomized to either the Vivifrail multicomponent exercise (MCE) program or usual care. The study measured IC using a composite index derived from z-scores of locomotion, cognitive function, psychological well-being, and vitality. Results showed significant improvements in the IC composite score and its subdomains—locomotion, cognition, and vitality—in the MCE group compared to usual care, indicating that the structured exercise program had a beneficial impact on these aspects of intrinsic capacity [20].

In comparison to our study, which focused on a structured lifestyle counseling intervention over an 8-week period, the Vivifrail study highlights the effectiveness of targeted exercise interventions in improving IC among frail older adults. While both studies reported positive outcomes in enhancing IC, our intervention involved a broader range of lifestyle modifications, including nutrition and psychological support, in addition to physical exercise. Our findings also demonstrated significant improvements across various IC domains, similar to the Vivifrail study. However, our intervention was shorter in duration and implemented at a different intensity compared to the 12-week Vivifrail program. The longer duration and specific focus of the Vivifrail program on exercise alone may explain the observed improvements in IC and its domains, suggesting that while our holistic approach was effective, longer-term and more intensive exercise regimens could further enhance outcomes in frail populations. This highlights the potential advantage of our structured lifestyle counseling intervention, which incorporates multiple lifestyle factors to achieve a more holistic enhancement of intrinsic capacity. The significant improvements observed in our study, including increases in locomotion, cognition, vitality, psychological well-being, and sensory function, reflect the broader benefits of integrating various lifestyle components in the intervention.

The mechanisms behind the observed improvements in our study are multifaceted. The nutritional component likely contributed to enhanced vitality and reduced risks of malnutrition, which is crucial for maintaining health in older adults. The incorporation of regular physical exercise is known to improve mobility, muscle strength, and balance, thereby reducing fall risk and enhancing functional independence. The psychological support aspect of the intervention aimed at increasing happiness and managing depression likely contributed to better mental health and overall life satisfaction. The balance training specifically addressed stability and fall prevention, crucial for maintaining sensory and motor functions [13,22,23].

The generalizability of our findings is an important consideration. While the study provides valuable insights into the benefits of lifestyle interventions for older women, its focus on a specific geographic area and population may limit the applicability of the results to other settings. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in diverse populations and geographic locations to confirm the effectiveness of the intervention across different contexts. Additionally, the relatively short follow-up period of our study may not fully capture the long-term effects of the intervention. Long-term studies are needed to evaluate the sustained impact of lifestyle modifications on intrinsic capacity.

While this randomized trial provides evidence for the efficacy of a structured lifestyle counseling intervention on intrinsic capacity in older women, several limitations must be acknowledged. The most significant constraint is the brief follow-up period of just one week post-intervention. This short timeframe, while demonstrating immediate effects, cannot establish whether the observed improvements in intrinsic capacity domains represent sustained behavioral changes versus transient motivational effects. Particularly for self-reported measures like psychological well-being and sensory function, early positive results may reflect participants’ enthusiasm following the intervention rather than durable improvements. The reliance on self-reported measures for several key domains introduces additional limitations, as responses may be influenced by social desirability bias, recall limitations common in older populations, and subjective interpretation of questions. While we included performance-based measures like SPPB for locomotion and Mini-Cog for cognition, the incorporation of more objective biomarkers (e.g., inflammatory markers, nutritional panels) and device-based activity monitoring could have strengthened the physiological validity of our findings.

The stringent inclusion criteria, while methodologically sound for establishing efficacy, limit the generalizability of results to the broader population of older adults. By excluding individuals with common comorbidities, neurological conditions, and significant functional limitations, we created a study population of relatively healthy agers that may not reflect the clinical complexity of typical older patients in community settings. This is particularly relevant given that intrinsic capacity is most clinically meaningful when assessed in populations with emerging functional limitations.

These limitations suggest several important directions for future research. First, longer-term studies with follow-up periods of 3–6 months are needed to determine whether the observed improvements persist beyond the immediate post-intervention period. Second, future trials should incorporate more robust outcome measures, including wearable activity monitors, performance-based assessments of daily functioning, and relevant biomarkers to complement self-report measures. Finally, implementation studies conducted in real-world clinical settings with more inclusive eligibility criteria are necessary to evaluate the intervention’s effectiveness across the spectrum of older adult health states. Despite these limitations, our findings provide important preliminary evidence that a relatively brief, structured lifestyle counseling intervention can simultaneously improve multiple domains of intrinsic capacity, laying the groundwork for more comprehensive studies of sustainable lifestyle approaches to healthy aging.

This randomized trial demonstrates that an 8-week, structured counseling intervention targeting multiple lifestyle domains can lead to short-term improvements in intrinsic capacity among community-dwelling older women. While these findings suggest the potential value of counseling-based approaches for health promotion, the intervention’s effects on long-term functional outcomes remain unknown. Future research should investigate: [1] whether these benefits persist beyond the immediate post-intervention period [2], how to effectively adapt the program for older adults with comorbidities, and [3] the optimal intensity and duration of counseling needed to sustain meaningful health impacts.
