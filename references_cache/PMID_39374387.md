# Impaired axon initial segment structure and function in a model of ARHGEF9 developmental and epileptic encephalopathy.
**Authors:** Wang W, Williams DJ, Teoh JJ, Soundararajan D, Zuberi A, Lutz CM, Frankel WN, Makinson CD
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2400709121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2400709121)

## Content

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE) are rare but devastating and largely intractable childhood epilepsies. Genetic variants in ARHGEF9, encoding a scaffolding protein important for the organization of the postsynaptic density of inhibitory synapses, are associated with DEE accompanied by complex neurological phenotypes. In a mouse model carrying a patient-derived ARHGEF9 variant associated with severe disease, we observed aggregation of postsynaptic proteins and loss of functional inhibitory synapses at the axon initial segment (AIS), altered axo-axonic synaptic inhibition, disrupted action potential generation, and complex seizure phenotypes consistent with clinical observations. These results illustrate diverse roles of ARHGEF9 that converge on regulation of the structure and function of the AIS, thus revealing a pathological mechanism for ARHGEF9-associated DEE. This unique example of a neuropathological condition associated with multiple AIS dysfunctions may inform strategies for treating neurodevelopmental diseases.

ARHGEF9 (Rho Guanine Nucleotide Exchange Factor 9) is an X-linked gene associated with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) and intellectual disability (ID) (1, 2). ARHGEF9 encodes a brain-specific protein commonly known as collybistin (CB), a guanine nucleotide exchange factor and an essential regulator of the inhibitory postsynaptic density. CB has three defined functional domains, directly binding to the GABAergic scaffolding protein gephyrin through its middle catalytic DH domain; it also binds to the α2 subunit of type A GABA receptors (GABAAR-α2) and neuroligin-2 (NL2) through its N-terminal SH3 domain (3–5). More recently, CB was hypothesized to function as an essential regulator of the axo-axonic synapses between chandelier cells and the axon initial segment (AIS) of pyramidal neurons (6, 7). However, little is known of the functional roles CB plays in controlling synaptic signaling and network excitability, and how these functions are altered in diseases.

The diverse clinical phenotypes reported in ARHGEF9 patients are heterogenous but may, in part, be explained by the domain of the protein that is affected. In most patients with loss-of-function (LOF) variants (e.g., deletions, complex rearrangements, nonsense mutations), the reported disease phenotypes are relatively mild and seizures are well controlled by antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) (1). Meanwhile, patients with missense mutations in certain protein domains show more complex and intractable disease phenotypes. For example, missense mutations in the C-terminal PH domain, while causing mild-to-moderate ID, do not cause epilepsy. Mutations in the DH domain and SH3 domain, however, are associated with moderate to severe ID, early-onset and drug-resistant epilepsy, and developmental delay (1, 2). These clinical reports point to a rich set of CB-related functions that are unlikely to be fully understood using LOF models or knockdown studies of the gene.

Previously reported Arhgef9 knockout mice (KO; Arhgef9−/−) had reduced postsynaptic gephyrin and GABAARs within the hippocampus and behavioral abnormalities including increased anxiety and impaired spatial learning (8, 9). These phenotypes are consistent with the relatively mild clinical features in patients carrying LOF ARHGEF9 variants. To address the role of ARHGEF9 at the severe end of the ARHGEF9-DEE spectrum, we investigated a SH3 domain missense mutation -G55A- identified in a severe case of DEE (10). This patient exhibited drug-resistant tonic seizures, hyperekplexia, and severe ID. Functional studies with heterologous expression of ARHGEF9G55A showed aggregation of CB protein as well as loss of gephyrin and GABAAR clusters, suggesting that the G55A mutation might act in a dominant-negative manner (10).

In this study, an assessment of clinically relevant behavioral, molecular, and electrophysiological phenotypes was performed in an Arhgef9G55A/Y mouse model. We found that the G55A variant causes disruption of inhibitory postsynaptic organization and AIS architecture, leading to impairment of both synaptic transmission and action potential (AP) generation. The G55A variant also causes behavioral impairment and the generation of multiple seizure types including convulsive generalized tonic-clonic, tonic, spike-wave discharges, as well as interictal spikes. The effects of Arhgef9G55A on neuronal functions are multidimensional, affecting both intrinsic and synaptic excitability, and preferentially impair the AIS. These results point to a novel pathological mechanism of DEE and represent a unique example of a neuropathological condition resulting from converging AIS dysfunctions.

The genetic variant G55A (c.164G>A; p.G55A) in ARHGEF9 was previously identified as a de novo mutation in a male with early infantile epileptic encephalopathy and severe ID (1). Among all pathogenic missense variants in ARHGEF9 reported, the G55A variant is the only one affecting the SH3 domain (Fig. 1A) and it is associated with one of the most severe disease phenotypes. The G55A mutation was introduced into the orthologous mouse Arhgef9 gene using CRISPR/Cas9 (Fig. 1B) to model this case of ARHGEF9 DEE. Cohorts of wildtype (Arhgef9+/+ and Arhgef9+/Y), heterozygous (Arhgef9G55A/+), and hemizygous (Arhgef9G55A/Y) littermates were generated. Mutant and wildtype alleles were inherited in the expected Mendelian ratio. No overt signs of developmental and neurological deficits were observed during routine handling. Since Arhgef9 is an X-linked gene, male Arhgef9G55A/Y mutant mice and Arhgef9+/Y wildtype littermates were used for further experiments to avoid the potentially confounding effects of X-inactivation in females (11). Whole brain homogenates from postnatal day 30 (PND 30) animals were used to examine the expression of CB protein. Quantitative western blot analysis of CB protein showed comparable levels in both genotypes (Fig. 1C).

Adult Arhgef9G55A/Y mice along with wildtype littermates were monitored using video electroencephalography (video-EEG) recordings (Fig. 1D) to identify and characterize spontaneous seizures. Individuals with pathogenic ARHGEF9 variants had heterogeneous seizure phenotypes, including generalized tonic-clonic, focal, myoclonic, and tonic seizures (1, 2). Consistent with this, Arhgef9G55A/Y mice exhibited electrographic seizures that varied in form and severity, including convulsive generalized tonic-clonic seizures (GTCSs) (Fig. 1E) and spike-wave discharges (SWD) (Fig. 1 E and G–I). Half of Arhgef9G55A/Y mice (5/10) had GTCSs during the recording period with each episode lasting around 30 s (32.0 ± 2.5) and occurring at an average frequency of 1.2 ± 0.2 GTCSs per day (Fig. 1F). No GTCSs were observed in wildtype littermate control animals (0/9, Fig. 1F). Arhgef9G55A/Y mice were observed to have a strikingly elevated susceptibility to spontaneous SWD events. All Arhgef9G55A/Y mice exhibited SWDs (10/10) with an average of 15 episodes per hour (15.6 ± 4.2, Fig. 1I), approximately 2 s in duration (2.0 ± 0.1), whereas SWDs were detected in a subset of wildtype animals (4/9) at a low rate (0.4 ± 0.2 SWDs per hour, Fig. 1I). Analysis of the power spectrum of SWD events in Arhgef9G55A/Y mice revealed a peak frequency of approximately 7 Hz (Fig. 1 G and H). Interestingly, video recordings revealed that while wildtype mice had no behavioral abnormalities during the infrequent SWD events, Arhgef9G55A/Y mice showed muscle twitches accompanying most SWD events, reminiscent of the intractable atypical absence seizures often observed in DEE.

Individuals with pathogenic ARHGEF9 variants exhibit behavioral and sensory abnormalities in addition to seizures (1). Hyperekplexia (excessive startle reaction) is among the most prominent psychophysical impairments associated with the G55A variant. Arhgef9G55A/Y mice were examined for hyperekplexia phenotypes using performance in the acoustic startle response test, which measures the amplitude of muscle contractions in response to different decibel (dB) levels of acoustic stimuli (12). Compared to wildtype mice, Arhgef9G55A/+ mice had increased response to acoustic stimuli, indicating that the G55A mouse model exhibits abnormalities consistent with an impairment in sensorimotor gating and clinical observations (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Overall, the Arhgef9-G55A mouse model recapitulates key phenotypes observed in humans, motivating an in-depth exploration of the pathological mechanisms of ARHGEF9-DEE.

To examine whether Arhgef9G55A/Y mice have defects in overall brain morphology, we first performed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining on paraffin sections to compare brain size and structure between genotypes. No overt neuronal loss or significant defects in brain structure were observed postnatally in Arhgef9G55A/Y slices (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), indicating that Arhgef9G55A does not alter brain development grossly.

It was proposed in prior studies that the G55A variant causes a conformational change of CB protein. The G55A variant impairs the ability of the SH3 domain to bind to its interactors and disrupts the intramolecular interaction between three functional domains, further altering the clustering pattern of CB (10). Immunostaining of brain slices for CB and its binding partner gephyrin from PND30 mice of both genotypes was performed to examine the localization of CB. In wildtype sections, both CB and gephyrin-positive puncta were clustered sparsely within the hippocampal pyramidal layer (Fig. 2 A, Top row). In Arhgef9G55A/Y brain sections, strikingly, CB formed large, elongated aggregates that colocalized with gephyrin aggregates within the pyramidal layer with small CB and gephyrin positive puncta barely visible (Fig. 2 A, Bottom row), without changes in protein levels (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). In contrast, aggregates were not observed in PND10 Arhgef9G55A/Y sections, indicating that the formation of protein aggregates begins at later developmental time points, possibly coinciding with synaptogenesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B).

To better visualize the subcellular localization of protein aggregates and to further assess their effects on neuronal function, we performed immunocytochemistry on primary hippocampal neurons after 14 d in vitro (DIV) using the AIS marker Ankyrin-G (ANK3). This revealed that CB and gephyrin protein aggregates in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons were localized exclusively at the AIS and sometimes also within the soma (Fig. 2 B and C). Most neurons had a single elongated protein aggregate (CB–gephyrin aggregate complex) at the AIS, sized 3.5 ± 0.2 μm and positioned 13.0 ± 0.8 μm away from the soma. Additionally, excitatory neurons had protein aggregates while inhibitory neurons did not, as shown following transduction of primary neurons with AAV8-CaMKIIa-eGFP or AAV1-mDlx-NLS-mRuby2. This finding is consistent with immunostaining results in brain slices, revealing that pyramidal neurons are most affected by Arhgef9G55A (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C).

Quantification of immunocytochemistry showed a dramatic decrease in CB and gephyrin puncta density across all neuronal compartments in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons (P < 0.001; Fig. 2E). Compared to somatic and dendritic compartments where there remained some clustering of CB and gephyrin puncta in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons, the AIS was almost completely devoid of normal sized CB and gephyrin puncta (Fig. 2 B–E). This set of observations indicates that trafficking and clustering of inhibitory postsynaptic proteins are disrupted across neuronal compartments in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons.

A closer examination of AIS structure in CA3 pyramidal neurons with transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons exhibited local “swelling” at the location of protein aggregates (Fig. 3A). Brain slices were probed for gephyrin because of the compatibility of gephyrin antibody (compared with CB antibody) with acrolein fixation used for TEM. In Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons, some smaller protein aggregates were associated with AIS microtubules. In wildtype neurons, gephyrin-positive puncta were in the vicinity of the electron-dense cisternal organelle (CO). Structurally, the outermost of CO is attached to axonal plasma membrane; functionally, axonal CO is associated with GABAergic synapses and can regulate Ca2+ dynamics by acting as a local Ca2+ store (13). We speculate that protein aggregates in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons could be contained within CO or between CO and axonal plasma membrane. Observations from TEM show that Arhgef9G55A not only impairs the clustering of proteins but also disrupts the structural organization of the AIS through the tight interactions between CB–gephyrin complex and AIS cytoskeleton proteins and microstructures. The proximity of protein aggregates to CO also raises the possibility that the G55A variant may lead to disrupted CO-mediated Ca2+ signaling.

The location of the AIS relative to the soma has previously been shown to change in an activity-dependent manner involving structural changes in its location or length to modulate neuronal excitability (14, 15). Given the possibility that persistent changes in neuronal excitability in the Arhgef9G55A model might lead to plastic changes in the AIS length and location, we examined AIS length and the distance between AIS and soma in primary hippocampal neurons. Despite the frequent occurrence of hyperexcitable seizure states in these animals, analysis revealed that neither AIS length nor structure was significantly changed in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons in vitro (P = 0.466 and P = 0.499, respectively; SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). To confirm these results in the brain, we measured AIS length in CA3 hippocampal brain slices collected from PND30 wildtype and Arhgef9G55A/Y animals (16). Detection of AISs in tissue section presents several technical hurdles including the presence of significant fluorescence signal in the neuropil and the convoluted paths that AISs follow as they exit the dense CA3 pyramidal layer. To reliably detect AISs in brain slices, we used a subset of wildtype and Arhgef9G55A/Y slices to train a machine learning-powered segmentation program to define the boundaries of each AIS (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). In agreement with in vitro results, analysis of the center path of each AIS showed comparable lengths between wildtype and Arhgef9G55A/Y sections (P = 0.879; SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). To assess the organization of channels at the AIS we analyzed NaV1.6 expression by immunofluorescence detection. NaV1.6 channels are highly enriched at the AIS and are tightly associated with AIS cytoskeletal proteins (17). Examination of the immunofluorescence intensity of NaV1.6 channels along the AIS was not found to be different in either primary neurons or brain slices (P = 0.699 and P = 0.549, respectively; SI Appendix, Fig. S4 D and E).

Overall, immunohistological studies on brain slices and primary hippocampal neurons reveal a disruption in the inhibitory postsynaptic density across neuronal compartments, while protein aggregation and related structural disruptions are localized mostly to the AIS in Arhgef9G55A/Y pyramidal neurons.

The AIS is the initiation site for AP as well as the diffusion barrier between the somatodendritic and axonal compartments (18, 19). The location of the AIS at the junction between somatodendritic inputs and axonal outputs places this structure in a prime location for regulation by local synaptic contacts. Recent experimental evidence shows that the AIS of pyramidal neurons receives GABAergic inputs via axo-axonic synapses from chandelier neurons which have been shown to fine-tune synaptic integration through local modulation of AIS excitability at or near the point of AP initiation (7, 20). Furthermore, GABAARs containing the α2-subunit are enriched at the AIS and are responsible for most, if not all, synaptic inputs at the AIS (21, 22). By binding directly to GABAAR-α2 with high affinity, CB potentially plays an outsized role at the AIS through its function as a scaffolding protein for GABAAR-α2 (23). To test the hypothesis that the G55A variant preferentially impairs AIS GABA receptor clustering, we probed primary hippocampal neurons from Arhgef9G55A and wildtype animals for GABAAR-α2 and the presynaptic active zone marker Bassoon (BSN). As a control to verify that GABAAR-α2 mediate synaptic inputs at the AIS, colocalization of BSN puncta and GABAAR-α2 puncta at the AIS was confirmed using wildtype neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A).

To test the hypothesis that the G55A variant preferentially impairs AIS GABA receptor clustering, we probed primary hippocampal neurons for GABAAR-α2 and vesicular GABA transporter (VGAT) as a marker for inhibitory presynapses. Consistent with results from CB and gephyrin staining (Fig. 2), there was a dramatic decrease in GABAAR-α2 and VGAT puncta (P < 0.001; Fig. 4 A–D) at the AIS in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons. Immunostaining of brain slices showed a slight decrease in VGAT fluorescence intensity in Arhgef9G55A/Y brains (P = 0.026; Fig. 4E). In primary hippocampal neurons at somatic and dendritic compartments, GABAAR-α2 puncta were significantly decreased in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons (P < 0.001; Fig. 4 A and C). In contrast, VGAT puncta levels were comparable at somatic and dendritic compartments between genotypes, indicating that most of the GABAARs on somatic and dendritic sites do not contain the α2 subunit that requires CB (P = 0.24 and P = 0.08, respectively; Fig. 4 B and D). Additionally, dendritic BSN puncta levels were comparable between genotypes (P = 0.55; SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Together, these results demonstrate that GABAergic synapses are disrupted by the G55A variant, particularly at the AIS.

To examine the functional consequences of the observed molecular phenotypes in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons on synaptic transmission, we measured miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs) and excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) in acute brain slices.

Slices were prepared from P30 to P40 Arhgef9G55A/Y mice and wildtype littermates. As prominent molecular phenotypes were observed within the CA3 hippocampal pyramidal layer, all recordings were conducted on CA3 pyramidal neurons. Interestingly, Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons exhibited decreased mIPSC amplitude but increased mEPSC amplitude compared to wildtype neurons (P < 2.2e-16 for both; Fig. 5 A–C). These effects on the size of synaptic responses were partially countered by a significant decrease (P = 4.231e-13) in the decay time constant of mEPSCs while the kinetics of mIPSCs remained unaffected (P = 0.178; Fig. 5 D and E). The summation of these changes to amplitude and decay time constant led to an overall increase (P = 0.0235 in the average total charge transfer calculated for mEPSC events, but no change in mIPSCs of Arhgef9G55A/Y compared to wildtype cells (P = 0.501; Fig. 5F). In Arhgef9G55A/Y slices, both mIPSCs and mEPSCs occurred less frequently compared to wildtype slices (P < 0.001 and P = 0.016, respectively; Fig. 5 A and G). Together, these results demonstrate a significant impairment in the balance of excitation and inhibition in Arhgef9G55A/Y pyramidal neurons.

Based on morphological identification of presynaptic active zones by immunostaining of primary hippocampal neurons, there was no overt difference in the number of excitatory synapses between genotypes. In the absence of changes in excitatory synaptic organization or synapse density, we suspected that reduced mEPSC frequency results from a change in presynaptic release probability, possibly via a mechanism involving reduced presynaptic GABAB activation (24–26). Paired-pulse ratio measurements of evoked EPSCs (eEPSCs) in CA3 pyramidal cells were performed to investigate this possibility in Arhgef9G55A/Y animals. Interestingly, our results showed no effect of genotype in the paired-pulse ratio measurements at three stimulus frequencies (interstimulus interval 10 Hz, 20 Hz, and 40 Hz) between genotypes (P = 0.414, P = 0.228, and P = 0.059, respectively; SI Appendix, Fig. S5), suggesting that presynaptic release mechanisms are not altered by the G55A mutation. Together these experiments point to upregulation of postsynaptic AMPA receptor expression consistent with homeostatic synaptic scaling (27), or alternatively may reflect neuronal subcompartment-specific regulation of synaptic input that is not apparent in the cumulative analysis of synaptic inputs (28).

To further investigate subcompartment-specific synaptic mechanisms, we performed acousto-optic two-photon uncaging of RuBi-GABA, a ruthenium-based caged GABA compound (29), while measuring the resulting outward currents by voltage clamp. RuBi-GABA was uncaged with 800 nm laser pulses adjacent to specific neuronal compartments including the AIS, soma, and dendrites to examine effects of Arhgef9G55A on GABAR activation at high spatial resolution. Three-dimensional volumetric scanning was used to stimulate serpentine ROIs conforming to the outline of target compartments. This method enabled the application of a consistent uncaging area (10 µm3) and laser intensity along the soma, proximal dendrite, and AIS of CA3 pyramidal neurons for direct comparison of the properties of the resulting responses (Fig. 5 H and I). When uncaging with 5 ms pulses, GABA receptor-mediated outward currents were reliably elicited (Fig. 5 I and J). Average evoked event amplitudes measured at the soma and dendrites were comparable between genotypes (P = 0.491 and P = 0.662, respectively; Fig. 5K). By contrast, in response to uncaging at the AIS, there was a nearly complete loss of GABA signaling in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons (P < 0.001; Fig. 5K). This result further confirms that the G55A variant leads to a significant and specific disruption to inhibition at the AIS.

The structural and molecular organization of the AIS has evolved for exquisite control of AP initiation and propagation (30). Protein aggregations observed within the AIS of Arhgef9G55A neurons may impair AIS function either by altering the active properties of the AIS through blockage of transport of axon-localized proteins, by disrupting the molecular arrangement of the AIS, or by affecting the passive properties of the AIS that govern AP signal conduction according to cable theory (31–33). Additionally, a lack of inhibitory signaling at the AIS could have a substantial impact on excitability and dynamic processes of synaptic integration that occur in this structure (34, 35). Experimental evidence has shown that localized GABA iontophoresis at the AIS inhibits AP initiation in cortical layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons, which is largely mediated by shunting inhibition through GABAARs (36).

Passive membrane properties and AP generation were measured in CA3 pyramidal neurons to investigate the functional consequences to AP signaling in the Arhgef9G55A model. Compared to wildtype neurons, Arhgef9G55/Y neurons fired fewer APs in response to low levels of current injection—smaller than 250 pA (Fig. 6 A and B). Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons were found to have an elevated AP threshold and elevated rheobase current, indicative of reduced excitability (P = 0.002 and P < 0.001, respectively; Fig. 6 C–E). There was no significant difference in passive membrane properties including resting membrane potential or input resistance between genotypes (P = 0.27 and P = 0.665, respectively; Table 1). No differences in AP overshoot or half-width were observed (P = 0.58 and P = 0.437, respectively); however, Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons had slightly increased afterhyperpolarization (AHP) amplitude compared to wildtype cells (P = 0.018; Fig. 6 F and G and Table 1). The increase in the AHP could be explained by the higher AP threshold observed in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons as the absolute negative peak after the AP peak was comparable between genotypes.

To evaluate the initiation and immediate propagation of the AP from the AIS to the somatodendritic compartment, we generated phase-plane plots of the first time derivative of the AP voltage as a function of voltage, and also analyzed the features apparent in the second time-derivative of fractionated APs (37, 38). These analyses enable precise identification of the point of AP initiation at the AIS and the backpropagation of the AP from the AIS to the somatodendritic compartment (39) (Fig. 6C). The temporal separation between these two components is enhanced by slowing of AP conduction due to the electrotonic mismatch between the AIS and the much larger capacitive load of the soma (40).

APs generated by Arhgef9G55A/Y and wildtype neurons at PND30 were fractionated and rendered according to the first and second time derivative of the voltage record as shown (Fig. 6H). While all (18/18) wildtype neurons displayed the expected AIS and somatodendritic peaks in the second time derivative of the somatic membrane potential (d2V/dt2), only 64% (16/25) of recorded Arhgef9G55A/Y APs produced distinguishable AIS and somatic peaks (Fig. 6I). Interestingly, no significant change in max d2V/dt2 was observed between genotypes indicating that the activation of voltage-gated sodium channels at the AIS is not affected (P = 0.255; Fig. 6J). The local minimum d2V/dt2 value after the first peak was used to quantify the strength of AIS→somatodendritic conduction. Compared to wildtype neurons, Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons had an increase in the minimum d2V/dt2 corresponding to the critical point of AIS to soma transition of the AP (P = 0.003; Fig. 6K). Additionally, in 16 out of 25 Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons that displayed two d2V/dt2 peaks, there was a significant decrease in the interpeak interval (P = 0.002; Fig. 6L). These observations demonstrate an impairment in AP propagation from the AIS in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons.

ARHGEF9 DEE is a severe and complex neurological disorder that is not well understood or modeled using available laboratory animals. Here, we generated a mouse model carrying the G55A missense variant identified in a patient with severe epilepsy and neurodevelopmental phenotypes. Consistent with ARHGEF9-DEE patients, Arhgef9G55A/Y mice were found to exhibit hyperekplexia and multiple seizure types, including generalized tonic-clonic, atypical absence-like SWD events accompanied by myoclonic jerks, and frequent interictal spiking.

The Arhgef9G55A/Y mouse model revealed striking ectopic aggregation of the GABAergic synapse organizing protein CB and gephyrin at the AIS of hippocampal pyramidal neurons. In-depth histological and electrophysiological evaluation of neuronal structure and function revealed impaired synaptic and intrinsic excitability at the AIS, providing an explanation for the severe phenotypes caused by the G55A variant compared to variants in other functional domains. In addition, this study reveals the special role of CB at the AIS in organizing axo-axonic synapses by recruiting GABAARs containing the α2 subunit together with gephyrin. These results point to CB as a key player in the molecular diversity of inhibitory synapses by recruiting and maintaining domain-specific GABAergic synaptic proteins.

Arhgef9G55A not only leads to a loss of normal SH3 domain function but generates additional phenotypes by promoting protein aggregation, likely as a result of disrupted intramolecular interactions of the affected protein. Here, the term “aggregation” is used simply to describe the observed phenomenon. Future biochemical studies are needed to classify the observed structures to investigate whether they are folding aggregates, inclusion bodies, or amyloid-like ordered aggregates.

Consistent with our study, others have shown that the G55A mutation disrupts binding between the SH3 domain and its interactors such that CB is not able to switch from a closed/inactive conformation to an open/activate state (5, 10). As a result, CB loses the ability to tether to plasma membrane phosphoinositides and translocate protein targets to submembrane compartments. A previous study utilizing the yeast-two hybrid system reported that the G55A mutation disrupted the direct binding between CB and GABAAR-α2 and did not affect the binding between CB and gephyrin (4). While in recombinant systems, the G55A mutation caused deficits in dendritic trafficking of CB (10), our study reveals that the G55A mutation leads to endogenous CB and gephyrin protein aggregation specifically at the AIS in vivo while simultaneously causing a global reduction in synaptic inhibition.

The functional significance of several other ARHGEF9 variants has been explored using heterologous expression systems. The R356Q variant on the PH domain was shown to impair PI3P binding and further disable CB to translocate gephyrin to submembrane microaggregates (41). The R290H variant on the DH domain also appeared to reduce the PI3P binding affinity of the PH domain by altering the strength of intramolecular interactions between DH and PH domains (42). G55A, R290H, and R356Q variants are located on different functional domains, however, they are all associated with a loss of GABAergic synapses caused by impaired membrane lipid binding activity, which is a central theme in the pathological mechanisms of ARHGEF9 DEE. Based on prior work and observations reported here we propose that the SH3 domain—a structure that is responsible for mediating the intramolecular interaction between other CB domains (43, 44)—is susceptible to forming inappropriate interactions between proteins when mutated, leading to pathological aggregations. The Arhgef9-G55A mouse model provides a unique opportunity to explore the endogenous CB protein behavior with pathogenic missense variants in an animal model and the functional consequences of CB complex aggregations in neuronal activity and diseases.

Arhgef9G55A/Y mice showed decreased GABAAR-α2 puncta across neuronal compartments as well as reduced frequency and amplitude in mIPSC, which is consistent with previous studies on CB KO mice (9). Reduced synaptic inhibition described previously provides an attractive model for seizure generation in ARHGEF9 DEE. In fact, variants in the GABRA2 gene encoding the GABAAR-α2 are associated with early onset epilepsy and developmental delay (45, 46), providing a direct link between DEE and GABAAR-α2 dysfunction. More closely related to the current study, mice with a mutation in the α2-subunit-CB binding region (Gabra2-1 mice) showed increased anxiety, increased seizure susceptibility, and early mortality (23), confirming that disrupted binding between CB and GABAAR-α2 is the central mechanism underlying seizure phenotypes in ARHGEF9 patients.

Interestingly, there were reductions in both excitatory and inhibitory synaptic responses in the G55A model that have not been observed in CB KO mice. Our experiments, including analysis of synaptic proteins by immunohistochemistry, miniature synaptic responses, and paired-pulse ratios together implicate postsynaptic mechanisms. While the increased mEPSC amplitude in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons could be explained by the loss of attenuation by postsynaptic inhibition, we speculate that the reduction in mEPSC frequency is the result of homeostatic regulation of synaptic excitation as a mechanism to compensate for increased network activity due to dysregulated excitation/inhibition balance and seizure activity. In addition, although no overt change in the density and clustering of excitatory synapses was observed in primary hippocampal neurons, there could be a mismatch between the number of functional synapses and morphologically identified synapses (47).

The majority of Arhgef9G55A/Y hippocampal pyramidal neurons recorded were found to have lost the distinctive slowing feature of AP conduction that occurs due to electrotonic mismatch as the AP passes from the confinement of the AIS to the somatodendritic compartment (Fig. 6) (40). Neurons recorded from quivering (Sptbn4qv3j/qv3j) mice lacking functional AIS organizing protein ßIV-spectrin and decreased NaV density were shown to fail to generate separate AIS and somatodendritic phases of the AP (48, 49). Although APs could still initiate at the AIS with low axonal NaV density, the onset rapidness of the AIS phase was reduced, leading to impaired spike timing (48). Studies focusing on understanding the consequences of AP shape heterogeneity across different cell types have revealed that an electrotonically proximal AP initiation site allows for greater sensitivity to synaptic inputs while more distal AIS initiation enables greater regulation of incoming synaptic responses by axo-axonic GABAARs, and facilitates back propagation (50). We therefore speculate that the observed changes in AP shape in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons, likely reflecting impaired axonal cable properties, will have a significant impact on neuronal information encoding.

These impairments in intrinsic properties, observed in CA3 Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons, appear to be discordant with synaptic analysis with respect to excitability. Paradoxically, intrinsic excitability is reduced while impaired synaptic inhibition is expected to increase excitability. Given that the animal model displays multiple classes of spontaneous seizures, we expect that the observed changes in intrinsic excitability are the result of homeostatic compensation triggered by elevated network activity due to the loss of synaptic inhibition at the AIS. Consistent with this model, a very recent study reported that ablation of GABAergic synaptic input to the AIS of cortical pyramidal neurons from chandelier cells was sufficient to drive homeostatic tuning of intrinsic excitability (51).

From immunostaining of primary hippocampal neurons, we noticed that in the wildtype group, the puncta size of CB and GABAAR-α2 was considerably larger at the AIS and perisomatic sites compared to their dendritic counterparts. Under normal conditions, large puncta are restricted to inhibitory synaptic sites, whereas the small puncta, less than 0.2 µm2, are found at extrasynaptic sites (52, 53). In Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons, the existence of VGAT puncta in somatodendritic compartments and the significant decrease in CB and GABAAR-α2, showed that the requirement of CB in gephyrin-mediated clustering of GABAARs differs across neuronal compartments.

Our study highlights that CB is essential for organizing GABAARs at axo-axonic synapses at the AIS. Disruptions in axo-axonic synapses have been associated with various neurological conditions, including epilepsy. In nonhuman primates with cortical focal epilepsy, axon terminals of chandelier-type interneurons (ChCs), which preferentially target the AIS of pyramidal cells in the hippocampus and cortex, were lost at epileptic foci (54). In a rodent kindling model of epileptogenesis, loss of axo-axonic and axo-somatic GABAergic interneurons was observed following induction of multiple seizures (55). The specific recruitment of ChCs activity in seizure emphasizes the critical role of ChCs in regulating cortical and hippocampal network excitability. We propose that in Arhgef9G55A/Y neurons the ChC inhibitory connection to the AIS is severed, leading to loss of adaptive control of the network by ChCs and promotion of hypersynchronous network states such as seizure and impairment of other cortical and hippocampal functions such as cognition, which rely on precise control of neuronal excitability.

Compared to inhibitory synapses at other locations, AIS axo-axonic innervation is uniquely positioned at the point of AP initiation where it may exert outsized control of the target cell. Studies of axo-axonic inputs have shown that they not only modulate synaptic integration and spiking probability but also control the precise timing of AP generation in pyramidal neurons (36, 56). The Arhgef9G55A/Y animal will be a useful model for assessing the contribution of axo-axonic inhibition to complex cortical and hippocampal network processes.

In conclusion, Arhgef9G55A/Y mice exhibit complex seizure phenotypes reminiscent of those reported in ARHGEF9 patients, including convulsive tonic-clonic seizures and spike-wave discharges. Neurons within the hippocampal pyramidal layer of Arhgef9G55A/Y animals formed large aggregates located at the AIS and exhibited altered excitability and near-complete abolishment of AIS inhibition. These results reveal a unique and unexpected role of Arhgef9 in controlling neuronal activity through AIS function and axo-axonic inhibition.

Chambers were cleaned between each trial with 70% ethanol.

Slices were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by 2 h incubation with secondary antibodies and DAPI at room temperature. Slices were washed three times with PBS between steps. Automated quantification of AISs and NaV1.6 in brain slices was performed using AIVIA software (Leica Microsystems) trained in-house on a subset of manually segmented images. Cumulative distributions were generated from 10 randomly selected AISs from each slice.

Mice were anesthetized, and 300 µM-thick coronal slices containing hippocampal CA3 were prepared.
