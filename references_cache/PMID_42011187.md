# The association between cerebrospinal fluid interleukin-6, soluble interleukin-6 receptor, and interleukin-6 trans-signalling binary complex with postoperative delirium: an observational cohort study.
**Authors:** Wu JG, Taylor J, Kunkel D, Maze M, Pearce RA, Sanders RD, Lennertz R
**Journal:** BJA Open (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.bjao.2026.100546](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bjao.2026.100546)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Postoperative delirium causes significant morbidity and mortality. Delirium is associated with the presence of plasma and CSF biomarkers, including interleukin-6 (IL-6). When IL-6 binds to the non-membrane-bound soluble IL-6 receptor (sIL-6R), the resultant binary complex, IL-6·sIL-6R, triggers pro-inflammatory processes called trans-signalling. While murine models implicate central IL-6 trans-signalling in perioperative cognitive dysfunction, there are few clinical reports addressing its role in delirium. The primary aim was to investigate whether postoperative changes in CSF IL-6, sIL-6R and IL-6·sIL-6R complexes were associated with the incidence and severity of postoperative delirium. We also explored whether CSF sIL-6R was related to peripheral inflammation and blood-brain barrier permeability.

METHODS: We performed an exploratory analysis of data from an ongoing observational cohort study on perioperative delirium in patients undergoing major surgery, with delirium incidence (3-Minute Diagnostic Confusion Assessment Method or Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit) and severity (Delirium Rating Scale-Revised-98), plasma, and CSF samples collected on postoperative days 1-7. Biomarkers were analysed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.

RESULTS: In the 24 patients included undergoing major surgery (median age 67 yr, interquartile range 61-73), 13 participants had paired pre- and postoperative CSF IL-6 and sIL-6R data. The peak postoperative change in log CSF sIL-6R was significantly correlated with delirium incidence (P=0.03) and peak delirium severity (ρ=0.68, P=0.02). The peak postoperative change in log CSF IL-6·sIL-6R, a surrogate of trans-signalling, was associated with peak delirium severity (ρ=0.70, P=0.01). CSF soluble sgp130, the natural inhibitor of the trans-signalling pathway, did not correlate with delirium. CSF sIL-6R was also significantly correlated with peripheral inflammation (plasma IL-6 and IL-8) and blood-brain barrier permeability (CSF:plasma albumin ratio and plasma S100B).

CONCLUSIONS: Interleukin-6 trans-signalling may be implicated in postoperative delirium, which raises the future possibility of targeted immunomodulatory therapy in susceptible individuals to prevent and/or reverse postoperative co gnitive decline.

CLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRATION: NCT02926417.

Postoperative delirium has been reported to cause considerable morbidity, prolong hospitalisation, and increase healthcare costs.1 Delirium and perioperative neurocognitive disorders are associated with peripheral inflammation and neuro-inflammatory changes, including interleukin-6 (IL-6)2 and IL-8,3,4 as well as products of injury such as S100 calcium-binding protein B (S100B),5 tau,6,7 and neurofilament light.3 The various signalling pathways of IL-6 and their relationship to the delirium process have, to date, been understudied. IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine that exhibits both anti- and pro-inflammatory effects via the ‘classic’ and ‘trans-signalling’ pathways, respectively, through two distinct forms of the IL-6 receptor (Fig. 1).1 The membrane-bound receptor, present on a limited number of cell types, including hepatocytes and immunocytes, transduces classic signalling, resulting in regenerative and anti-inflammatory outcomes. The soluble IL-6 receptor (sIL-6R), generated by proteolytic cleavage or alternative splicing of the membrane-bound IL-6 receptor on hepatocytes and immunocytes, binds with IL-6 to form the binary complex of IL-6·sIL-6R, which signals through the ubiquitous membrane-bound glycoprotein 130 (gp130), to transduce pro-inflammatory cascades.8 The exodomain of membrane-bound gp130 can be proteolytically cleaved to generate a soluble form, sgp130, which is an endogenous inhibitor of trans-signalling, as it can form the ternary complex IL-6·sIL-6R·sgp130, which prevents the binary complex from binding to membrane-bound gp130 to transduce its pro-inflammatory signal. Hence, sIL-6R and sgp130 form a buffer system with IL-6, where a significant increase in one or the other may promote or down-regulate IL-6 trans-signalling respectively.9Fig 1Interleukin-6 classical and trans-signalling pathways. Murine models have demonstrated the critical role of IL-6 trans-signalling in CA1 hippocampal neurons in effecting perioperative memory dysfunction after aseptic surgical trauma.10 Furthermore, fluctuating higher levels of plasma IL-6 and sIL-6R were significantly associated with postoperative delirium in older adults, supporting a role for the trans-signalling pathway in postoperative delirium.8 However, few studies have explored CSF concentrations of sIL-6R in delirium pathogenesis.11 If IL-6 trans-signalling were specifically implicated in human perioperative neurocognitive disorders, this would provide a potential opportunity for therapeutic intervention with a selective IL-6 trans-signalling blocker.1

Our primary aim was to determine whether the peak postoperative changes in CSF sIL-6R, IL-6, and IL-6·sIL-6R complexes were associated with postoperative delirium incidence and severity score using the Delirium Rating Scale-Revised-98. Biomarker concentrations were compared between participants who did and did not experience postoperative delirium using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while correlations between biomarker concentrations and delirium severity were examined using Spearman’s rho test. We hypothesised that higher postoperative increases in these inflammatory markers associated with IL-6 trans-signalling may be positively correlated with measures of delirium.

Our secondary aims were exploratory in nature. We aimed to determine whether postoperative changes in CSF sIL-6R were associated with blood–brain barrier permeability (quantified by CSF:plasma albumin ratio) or peripheral inflammation (quantified by plasma IL-6 or plasma IL-8). Four separate generalised additive mixed models for change in CSF sIL-6R were constructed against each of these biomarkers, with time (postoperative day) modelled as a non-linear variable using splines. Finally, we also sought to determine whether the peak postoperative change in CSF sgp130, the endogenous inhibitor of trans-signalling, was associated with postoperative delirium incidence and severity score.

We performed an exploratory analysis of data from an ongoing observational cohort study on perioperative delirium (IPOD-B2) from the University of Wisconsin–Madison (Madison, WI, USA). Ethics approval was provided by the University of Wisconsin–Madison Institutional Review Board (2015-0960) in December 2015, and the trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02926417). Recruitment commenced in June 2016. Data were reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Participants were recruited from the University of Wisconsin–Madison Health aged ≥21 yr (n=24), scheduled for major elective open thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm vascular surgery requiring general anaesthesia and placement of a spinal drain, with an anticipated hospital stay of at least 2 days. While the inclusion criterion for age was ≥21 yr, the actual median age of participants was 67 yr (interquartile range [IQR] 61–73 yr). Participants were excluded if they had a contraindication for a spinal drain, a documented history of dementia, were unable to communicate with research staff due to language barriers, were pregnant, were residing in a nursing home, were prisoners, or were otherwise deemed inappropriate by the principal investigator for the study, including a history of appointment cancellations (also see3,6 for inclusion and exclusion criteria). All participants gave written informed consent to participate in the study.

Before surgery, participants were assessed using the National Surgical Quality Improvement Project (NSQIP) surgical risk score. Plasma and CSF samples, demographic details, and clinical information were also collected at baseline. CSF was obtained from spinal drains in patients undergoing vascular surgery, stored at –80°C, and analysed for IL-6, sIL-6R, sgp130, IL-8, and albumin. Plasma samples were stored at –80°C and analysed for IL-6, IL-8, S100B, and albumin. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Eve Technologies, Calgary, AB Canada) was used to analyse IL-6, sIL-6R, sgp130, IL-8 and S100B; albumin concentrations were quantified by immunoturbidimetry on a Cobas instrument (Roche Diagnostics, Penzberg, Germany). Participants were assessed for delirium twice daily during their hospital stay using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)/3-Minute Diagnostic Confusion Assessment Method (3D-CAM) or the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) if intubated. The primary study interval was the first 4 days after surgery. However, if participants continued to have delirium, they were followed until postoperative day 7. Delirium severity was quantified using the Delirium Rating Scale-Revised-98 (DRS). Plasma and CSF samples were also collected on each postoperative day.

All biomarker concentrations (pg ml–1) were log10-transformed and normalised to baseline preoperative values. The concentration of the binary complex IL-6·sIL-6R in CSF was calculated from the CSF concentrations of sIL-6R and IL-6 using a dissociation constant (Kd) of 0.5 nM12 (Supplementary Methods for formula). The relationships between the maximum (peak) postoperative changes in CSF IL-6, sIL-6R, IL-6·sIL-6R, sgp130 and delirium severity were analysed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Furthermore, the relationships between these biomarker-derived variables and binarised delirium incidence were investigated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with continuity correction. As per previous work,4 a meaningful association with delirium was defined a priori as yielding P<0.05 for both Spearman’s and Wilcoxon tests outlined above. For the regression analysis, outliers were excluded by Cook’s distance, applying a conservative threshold of four times the mean Cook’s distance.

Summary data were expressed as median and interquartile range. Generalised additive mixed-effects models were constructed to analyse the trajectory of CSF sIL-6R across postoperative days 1–7, and its relationship with peripheral inflammation and blood–brain barrier permeability. Independent models were constructed for two measures of blood–brain barrier permeability: CSF:plasma albumin ratio13 and plasma S100B,5,14 and two biomarkers of peripheral inflammation: plasma IL-6 and plasma IL-8. Penalised splines were used for postoperative day to account for non-linear effects over time. The basis dimension k was gradually reduced to a minimum such that the model provided the most appropriate fit as per the Akaike information criterion15; this was achieved in all models with three basis dimensions. In each model, CSF sIL-6R was treated as the dependent variable, the biomarker covariate as a linear fixed effect, individual participant as a random effect, and smoothing functions were applied for postoperative day only. Approximate P-values for non-linear fitting of postoperative day were calculated using the Wald’s statistic; otherwise, Satterthwaite degrees of freedom were used for linear fixed effects. As part of sensitivity analyses, these models were adjusted for potential confounders: age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, stroke or transient ischaemic attack, and ever having smoked. Both the unadjusted and adjusted models were presented. The threshold for statistical significance was set at α=0.05.

An effect size equivalent to a 50% relative difference in CSF sIL-6R between patients with and without delirium was selected a priori as a reasonable ‘moderate’ threshold for this study. Based on the summary statistics of CSF sIL-6R in a previous study of delirium,11 we estimated that 24 participants were required to show a 50% difference in CSF sIL-6R between groups (80% power, α=0.05, assuming 33% incidence of delirium). Our study was therefore adequately powered for this primary objective.

The 24 participants with CSF sIL-6R data were aged between 47 and 82 yr, and 11 (46%) were female (Fig. 2). Sixteen (67%) participants developed delirium after surgery and eight (33%) did not. Nine participants were assessed at any point in time with the CAM-ICU, with seven participants on the first two postoperative days and three on the third postoperative day. Patients with delirium had higher NSQIP-D scores than patients without (median [IQR]): 5.2 (3-3–11.3) vs 0.9 (0.6–4.0), P=0.016; longer operating times: 548 (447–690) vs 335 (269–452) mins, P=0.007; and increased blood loss: 6300 (4625–7625) vs 175 (125–363) ml, P=0.001 (Table 1). At baseline, patients with delirium had similar CSF biomarker concentrations to patients without delirium across IL-6: 729 (599–871) vs 593 (438–768) pg ml–1 (P=0.24), sIL-6R: 3.15 (1.91–5.21) vs 3.76 (2.65–5.76) pg ml–1 (P=0.42), and sgp130: 46.2 (39.3–48.7) vs 42.4 (40.3–45.2) ng ml–1 (P=0.64). CSF sIL-6R increased from a median baseline of 722 (502–824) pg ml–1 to a peak postoperative value of 1385 (1034–1892) pg ml–1, with a larger increase observed in participants with delirium (Fig. 3). Generalised additive mixed models for CSF sIL-6R concentrations over time showed significant associations with two measures of blood–brain barrier permeability: CSF:plasma albumin ratio (β=0.200, P=0.005), and plasma S100B (β=0.145, P=0.002); and two peripheral inflammatory biomarkers: plasma IL-6 (β=0.079, P<0.001) and plasma IL-8 (β=0.094, P=0.001) (Supplementary Table 2). Each of the four models provided evidence for a non-linear temporal trajectory of CSF sIL-6R following surgery. The effect size for each biomarker was statistically significant in both the unadjusted models and those adjusted for potential confounders (Supplementary Table 3). Meanwhile, the peak postoperative change in CSF sgp130 was neither correlated with delirium severity (ρ=0.25, P=0.4) nor incidence (P=0.94) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

We report that CSF sIL-6R increased after surgery, and the magnitude of its maximal postoperative rise was correlated with both delirium incidence and severity. Levels of IL-6 and IL-6·sIL-6R binary complexes in CSF also increased after surgery and were associated with worse delirium severity scores, potentially supporting a role for the trans-signalling pathway in post-surgical deliriogenesis. CSF sIL-6R was also significantly correlated with markers of systemic inflammation (plasma IL-6 and IL-8) and blood–brain barrier permeability (CSF:plasma albumin ratio and plasma S100B); this may suggest that surgically provoked peripheral inflammation allows monocytes to migrate across a leaky blood–brain barrier.16,17 However, the natural trans-signalling inhibitor sgp130 was not implicated in delirium incidence or severity.

Elevated levels of plasma sIL-6R have been shown to increase the risk of postoperative delirium.8 However, there are few data on sIL-6R concentrations in CSF, and plasma concentrations of inflammatory mediators do not always reflect the local milieu. For instance, IL-6 and sIL-6R concentrations from biopsy specimens in patients with Crohn’s disease reflect disease activity much more accurately than plasma concentrations.18 While prior literature reports associations between elevated CSF sIL-6R and inflammatory neurological conditions such as meningitis and Guillain–Barré syndrome,17 and possibly Alzheimer’s disease (although contentious),19,20 only one prior study has been performed on the relationship between CSF sIL-6R and delirium. The authors reported that baseline CSF sIL-6R did not correlate with delirium status11; however, the investigators recruited patients with acute hip fracture, which may have complicated the inflammatory profile, and only obtained preoperative CSF samples. Our methodological design allowed for repeated sampling of CSF from spinal drains, which enabled a longitudinal assessment of CSF sIL-6R. Moreover, in a subgroup analysis of the aforementioned study, patients with worse baseline cognitive reserve who subsequently developed postoperative delirium tended to have a higher baseline CSF sIL-6R.11 This suggests that there may exist a susceptible subgroup of older individuals who are predisposed to perioperative upregulated IL-6 trans-signalling in the central nervous system.

The binary complex IL-6·sIL-6R activates membrane-bound gp130 to transduce trans-signalling and is therefore a sensitive marker of this pro-inflammatory pathway.9 Notably, the increase in IL-6·sIL-6R is a function of both the perioperative concentrations of CSF sIL-6R and CSF IL-6, the latter of which has been shown in our previous work to be highly associated with delirium.3,5,6 The binary:ternary complex ratio, that is, the ratio of the concentration of binary complex IL-6·sIL-6R to the concentration of the ternary complex IL-6·sIL-6R·sgp130, has been proposed as another indicator of IL-6 trans-signalling; however, there is uncertainty regarding the dissociation constant (Kd) of the ternary complex,9 making the calculations of some reports less certain.21 Hence, this putative indicator was not pursued in our study.

The endogenous trans-signalling inhibitor sgp130 is present in significant molar excess compared to sIL-6R, as previously reported in plasma,9,22 and also supported by our CSF data (Supplementary Fig. 1). Under physiological conditions, plasma levels of sgp130 (2.5–4.0 nM)9 are higher than sIL-6R (0.5–1.5 nM), which are again 10 000 times more concentrated than IL-6 (0.05–0.5 pM).22,23 While inflammatory, infectious, or neoplastic processes can elevate plasma IL-6 by more than one thousandfold and plasma sIL-6R by two- or three-fold,22 levels of plasma sgp130 do not change significantly with inflammation,9 which was corroborated in the CSF in our study (Supplementary Fig. 1). This supports the notion that the buffering capacity of sgp130 may be overwhelmed by a concurrent rise in IL-6, sIL-6R and the binary complex after surgery.9 Concentrations of IL-6, sIL-6R and IL-6·sIL-6R binary complexes in CSF all increase after surgery, with their maximal postoperative changes significantly correlated with the delirium severity score. Therefore, in the absence of a concomitant rise in sgp130, our data provide evidence for upregulation of central IL-6 trans-signalling in association with postoperative delirium.

Furthermore, we found that the postoperative increase in CSF sIL-6R was correlated with an increase in peripheral inflammatory cytokines (plasma IL-6 and IL-8) and blood–brain barrier permeability quantified by S100B5,14 and the gold standard CSF:plasma albumin ratio.13 Surgery-induced peripheral inflammation may induce an influx of monocytes across the blood–brain barrier, which has been demonstrated in animal studies24 and recently suggested in humans.5,25 Whether surgery-induced peripheral inflammation triggers proteolytic cleavage of membrane-bound IL-6R26 and upregulation of CSF sIL-6R has not been established.

There were several limitations of our study. Firstly, our study had a small sample size, with only 13 patients included in the final analysis. There were also some missing data, and the number of biospecimen values was not consistent among all study participants. In addition, the incidence of postoperative delirium in our study was higher than reported in a previous study (67% vs 33%),11 possibly suggesting that our cohort might have been more predisposed to postoperative cognitive insult or underwent procedures more likely to precipitate delirium, among other contributing factors. Our study design was observational, so we could not conclude causality. However, we analysed several biomarkers associated with IL-6 trans-signalling, peripheral inflammation, and blood–brain barrier permeability, and our hypothesis has a strong biological grounding in murine models. Furthermore, we identified convergence between endpoints of delirium severity and incidence defined a priori based on previous work, giving us confidence in our results. Our findings were also robust to outlier rejection, non-linear fitting for postoperative day, and potential confounders. Although the number of possible confounders included in the generalised additive models was large relative to our small sample size, both the adjusted and unadjusted models showed similar effect sizes and were statistically significant for the primary outcomes of interest. In addition, we did not measure levels of IL-6·sIL-6R binary complexes directly, although our mathematical approach to calculate their concentration is based on biochemical principles (Supplementary Methods).12 We nonetheless acknowledge that potential postoperative changes in binding kinetics could introduce measurement uncertainty to these model-derived values. Furthermore, delirium is a heterogeneous and multifactorial condition, and it is unlikely that any single biomarker or pathway could completely account for its pathophysiology. IL-6 trans-signalling should therefore be considered as one possible component within a broader network of inflammatory and neuroendocrine mechanisms contributing to delirium. Finally, as this was a sub-dataset analysis with several secondary aims, it is underpowered to investigate further questions of interest, such as the relationship between delirium incidence and several CSF biomarkers involved in the trans-signalling pathway (e.g. Fig. 4d and f), and future studies with larger sample sizes are needed.

We demonstrated that peak postoperative changes in CSF sIL-6R were correlated with delirium incidence and severity. Patients with higher CSF levels of IL-6 and pro-inflammatory IL-6·sIL-6R complexes had worse delirium severity scores. Our findings also suggest that the postoperative elevation in CSF sIL-6R might be related to an influx of monocytes across a damaged blood–brain barrier, but further studies are required to explore these hypotheses. We thereby propose that the IL-6 trans-signalling pathway may be implicated in deliriogenesis following surgery, which raises the future possibility of targeted IL-6 trans-signalling blockade in susceptible individuals to prevent postoperative cognitive dysfunction and to promote cognitive recovery.
