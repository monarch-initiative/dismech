# Impaired docking and recycling of synaptic vesicles in inherited lysosomal sphingolipidoses.
**Authors:** Zelada D, Saldivia N, Konjeti A, Samano S, Marshall MS, Rebiai R, Sutter P, Zurawski Z, Simchuk D, Muchiri R, van Breemen R, Shin D, Givogri MI, Crocker SJ, Alford S, Bongarzone ER
**Journal:** Cell Commun Signal (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12964-025-02560-x](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-025-02560-x)

## Content

Cognitive, memory, and learning impairments are common features of many lysosomal sphingolipidoses, yet the underlying synaptic mechanisms remain poorly defined. Here, we examined the impact of galactosylceramidase (GALC) deficiency on synaptic structure and function in the Twitcher (TWI) mouse model of Krabbe disease (KD). In vivo electrophysiological recording revealed significant cell autonomous reductions in paired-pulse facilitation and excitatory postsynaptic potential amplitude in hippocampal neurons of TWI mice. These functional impairments were accompanied by notable decreases in dendritic spine density, disrupted synaptic vesicle distribution, and reduced size of postsynaptic densities, affecting both excitatory and inhibitory synapses. Mechanistically, we found that psychosine, the pathological sphingolipid in KD, accumulated preferentially in presynaptic membranes and synaptic vesicles. Moreover, its higher biosynthetic rate in synaptosome fractions suggests local synthesis within the synaptic compartment. Deregulation of the SNARE protein SNAP25 and increased formation of SNARE complexes pointed to reduced vesicle docking and fusion at the presynaptic membrane. In vitro assays confirmed that psychosine disrupts synaptic vesicle cycling and SNARE-mediated fusion. Comparative analysis of other disease-associated sphingolipids-including gangliosides, sulfatides, glucosylsphingosine, globotriaosylceramide, sphingomyelin, and sphingosine-revealed distinct effects on vesicle trafficking, underscoring convergent yet lipid-specific mechanisms across inherited sphingolipidoses. Collectively, these findings identify presynaptic sphingolipid accumulation and impaired vesicle docking and fusion as shared mechanisms contributing to synaptic failure in several inherited sphingolipidoses, with potential relevance in adult-onset neurodegenerative diseases where lysosomal function is also compromised.

Glycosphingolipids are key structural and functional components of neuronal membranes, where they contribute to synaptic stability and signaling [28, 36, 38, 45, 52, 55, 61, 62]. Disruption of glycosphingolipid homeostasis is associated with a wide range of neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental disorders. For example, early-stage reductions in sulfatides and galactosylceramides are observed in Alzheimer disease and correlate with disease progression [16, 39, 40]. In lysosomal storage diseases (LSD) such as Krabbe disease (KD), Sandhoff disease, and Niemann-Pick disease, defective catabolism leads to the accumulation of glycosphingolipids, resulting in myelin damage, neuronal dysfunction, and cognitive decline deficits [8, 63]. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining appropriate lipid composition in synaptic membranes to support efficient synaptic transmission [58].

Although the pathological accumulation of glycosphingolipids is a hallmark of many neurodegenerative diseases, the specific mechanisms by which these lipids impair synaptic function remain poorly defined. Gangliosides such as GM1 have been shown to facilitate synaptic vesicle (SV) release and enhance synaptic plasticity at physiological concentrations [22, 76]. However, excessive accumulation—as seen in GM1 gangliosidosis—leads to progressive neurodegeneration [29]. Similarly, GM3 gangliosidosis results in severe epileptic phenotypes, highlighting the sensitivity of synaptic function to lipid imbalance [71]. Beyond gangliosides, the contribution of other sphingolipid classes to synaptic dysfunction is less well understood. Given their influence on membrane dynamics [11, 17, 87], high levels of these lipids may act as detergents, disrupting bilayer structure upon reaching critical micelle concentrations. Yet such late-stage toxic effects do not fully explain the early synaptic deficits observed in disease.

To address this gap, we investigated the presynaptic impact of glycosphingolipid accumulation in KD, a severe sphingolipidosis characterized by myelin loss and neuronal degeneration [12, 34, 41, 54, 72]. KD is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the gene encoding galactosylceramidase (GALC), a lysosomal enzyme responsible for degrading galactosylceramides and galactosylsphingosine (psychosine). Accumulation of psychosine disrupts lipid raft architecture and alters membrane stability [30, 82]. While psychosine has long been associated with oligodendrocyte toxicity and demyelination [33], we and others have shown that it also exerts direct, cell-autonomous effects on neurons. These include axonal damage [12], inhibition of axonal transport via PP1–GSK-3β signaling [7], neurofilament dephosphorylation via PP2A [9], impaired neuromuscular synapses [10], suppression of IGF1R–AKT signaling [75], and aggregation of α-synuclein, a presynaptic SNARE-associated protein that binds psychosine [1, 73].

Here, using GALC-deficient genetic models, we demonstrate that psychosine disrupts synaptic vesicle organization and release in hippocampal neurons. We show that psychosine is enriched in presynaptic terminals and synaptic vesicles, where it impairs vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release. These effects are associated with dysregulation of SNAP-25, PP2Ac, and GSK-3β. Furthermore, we found that gangliosides GM1 and GM2 and sulfatides also inhibited SV cycling, suggesting that multiple sphingolipid species exert direct presynaptic effects. Our findings reveal a previously underappreciated mechanism by which sphingolipid imbalance disrupts synaptic function in LSD and point to presynaptic lipid regulation as a key determinant of neuronal health.

Animal work in this study was performed in accordance with protocols from the Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Illinois at Chicago. All mice were maintained in a light/dark cycle of 12 h each at 25 °C and were fed with food and water ad libitum. Wild-type (WT, GALC+/+) or Twitcher (Twi, GALC−/−) mice were maintained on a C57BL/6J background and identified through PCR analyses as previously described [67]. The mouse lines Thy1-YFP-H (Jackson Laboratory, #003782) [23] and GALC+/twi were crossed to obtain Thy1-YFP-H; GALC+/twi, which were backcrossed to produce Thy1-YFP-H; GALC+/+ or Thy1-YFP-H; GALCtwi/twi (here forth referred to as YFP/WT or YFP/Twi, respectively). Specific deletion of GALC in postmitotic CAMKII+ neurons or in mature PLP+ oligodendrocytes was driven by crossing the GALC-flox mouse line (a kind gift from Dr. Laura Feltri, State University of New York at Buffalo, USA) [81] with the inducible mouse lines CAMKII-CreERT2 [46] or PLP-CREERT2 [42], respectively. The resulting CAMKII-CREERT2;GALC-flox (CAMKII/GALC-Flox) or PLP-CREERT2;GALC-flox (PLP/GALC-flox) genotypes were confirmed in newborn pups by PCR. To induce CREERT2 recombination, 4 intraperitoneal injections of 75 mg/kg of Tamoxifen (TMX, Sigma-Aldrich # T5648) were daily performed starting at P12. Controls received equal daily injections of corn-oil. Bone marrow transplantation or AAV-mediated gene therapy were performed in TWI pups as described by Marshall et al. [49]. For tissue collection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and then transcardially perfused with PBS. Brains were then processed for either biochemical or histological analyses.

Mice were anesthetized, transcardially perfused with ice-cold cutting solution (93 mM N-methyl-D-glucamine, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 30 mM NaHCO3, 20 mM HEPES, 10 mM MgSO4, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 25 mM D-glucose, 5 mM sodium ascorbate, 3 mM sodium pyruvate, 5–10 mM D-mannitol) and the brain was quickly dissected. Hippocampi were isolated and transverse slices (300 μm) were obtained by Vibratome (Leica VT1200S, Leica Microsystems Inc) in ice-cold aCSF solution (123 mM NaCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 3 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 11 mM D-glucose, 1 mM MgCl2 and 2 mM CaCl2) bubbled with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Hippocampal slices were allowed to recover for 30 min, transferred to the recording chamber and perfused with oxygenated aCSF at 2 mL/min and 30 °C. Dendritic field Excitatory Post Synaptic Potentials (fEPSPs) were evoked in the CA1 region upon extracellular stimulation of Schaffer collateral axons though twisted bipolar nichrome electrodes and recorded with aCSF-filled glass pipettes (< 5 MΩ). Paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) analyses were performed by evoking two consecutive stimulations (100-ms interstimulus interval) every 15 s. PPF values were calculated as the ratio between the second fEPSP against the first fEPSP. Recordings were acquired and analyzed by Axograph software.

Brains from P20 and P40 WT or Twi mice were disrupted in a Dounce homogenizer with 5 mL of Solution A (0.32 M Sucrose, 1 mM NaHCO3, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM CaCl2) supplemented with PIC. Brain lysates were then centrifuged at 1,400xg for 15 min at 4 °C. Supernatant (S1) was saved and the pellet (P1) was resuspended in 5 mL of Solution A and centrifuged at 710xg for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet (nuclear fraction, P2) was discarded, and supernatant (S2) was combined with S1. A 100 µL aliquot of S1 + S2, corresponding to total homogenate (TH), was reserved for further analyses. Combined supernatants were centrifuged at 16,000xg for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant (S3, cytosolic fraction) was stored, and the pellet (P3) was resuspended in 875 µL of solution B (0.32 M Sucrose, 1 mM NaHCO3, supplemented with PIC) and overlayed in a three-layer sucrose gradient (0.85 M, 1.0 M and 1.2 M Sucrose, respectively). Samples were centrifuged at 100,000xg for 2 h at 4 °C in an SW41Ti rotor in an Optima L-100 ultracentrifuge. The band of crude synaptosomes (SPM) formed between the 1.0 M-1.2 M sucrose interface and the myelin membranes (formed at the 0.32 M-0.85 M sucrose layer) were removed, and SPM were washed by adding 4 mL of Solution B and centrifuging at 35,000xg in a TLA 100.3 rotor for 30 min at 4 °C. Clean SPM fraction was solubilized in 1 mL of 20 mM Tris pH 7.4 and sonicated with three 10-second pulses (5 s on, 2 s off, 20% amp). Samples were stored at −80 °C or processed the next day for pre- and post-synaptic fractionation.

Crude SPM were combined with a triple detergent solution (final concentration 0.5% digitonin, 0.5% Na-Cholate, 0.5% NP-40) and incubated 1 h at 4 °C in an end-over-end shaker (SPM+ det fraction). A 500 µL aliquot of SPM+ det was supplemented with 1 mL of ice-cold 0.1 mM CaCl2, combined with 1.5 mL of 40 mM Tris pH 6.0 + 2% Triton X-100 and incubated in a rocker for 30 min at 4 °C. Samples were centrifuged at 35,000xg in a SW60Ti rotor in an Optima L-100 ultracentrifuge for 25 min at 4 °C. The supernatant, corresponding to the synaptic vesicles (SV) fraction was stored, and the pellet was air dried, resolubilized with 1 mL of 0.1 mM CaCl2, diluted with 1 mL of 40 mM Tris pH 8.0 + 2% Triton X-100 and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C in a rocker. Samples were centrifuged at 140,000xg for 30 min and the resulting supernatant was saved as the pre-synaptic membrane fraction (Pre-S), and the pellet, which correspond to the post-synaptic membranes (Post-S) was air-dried, resuspended in 100 µL of 20 mM Tris pH 7.4 and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Dissected hippocampi were diluted with 150–200 µL of Syn-Per™ extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing PIC. Hippocampi were disaggregated with a Dounce homogenizer making 10–12 gentle strokes. Homogenates were transferred to a centrifuge tube and spun down at 1,200xg for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet (P1) was discarded, and the supernatant (S1) was transferred to a new tube. S1 was centrifuged at 15,000xg for 20 min at 4 °C. The resultant supernatant (S2, cytosolic fraction) was stored for further analysis. Meanwhile, the pellet (P2) containing synaptosomes was washed by adding 100 µL of Syn-Per reagent and centrifuging at 15,000xg for 15 min at 4 °C. Synaptosomes were resuspended in ~ 100 uL of Syn-Per reagent and stored at −80 °C until required.

WT or Twi mice were perfused with 0.1 M Phosphate Buffer (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Brains were removed and fixed with 2.5% Glutaraldehyde/2.5% Paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M Phosphate Buffer (pH 7.4) for 24 h at 4 °C [68], and subsequently embedded in araldite. Coronal ultrathin Sect. (60 nm) were obtained, and serial grids were viewed using a 120 kV JEOL JEM-1220 equipped with a Gatan Es1000W 11MP CCD camera. The number and distribution of synaptic vesicles, length of postsynaptic densities (PSD), as well as the size of synaptic cleft and vesicles were quantified at the hippocampus or prefrontal cortex using ImageJ. Briefly, PSDs and synaptic cleft were delineated with the free-hand line tool, and the length was measured. For vesicles, free-hand selection tool was employed, and the area and perimeter were determined. ~100 synaptic terminals per animal and genotype were analyzed.

Brain sagittal cryosections (30 μm) were washed with PBS for 10 min 3 times, blocked for 4–5 h with PBS pH 7.4 containing 10% Donkey Serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 and incubated O.N at 4 °C with guinea pig anti-vGlut1 (1:1000, Synaptic Systems), guinea pig anti-vGAT (1:500, Synaptic Systems), rabbit anti-Homer-1 (1:500, Synaptic Systems), or rabbit anti-Gephyrin (1:500, Synaptic Systems) primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution [50]. Next day, brain sections were washed 3 times with PBS and incubated with respective secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor-488 or −565 (1:600, Invitrogren) for 2 h at room temperature. After three washes with PBS, the tissue was mounted with VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (H-1200-10, Vector Laboratories). Images were acquired by confocal microscopy (Leica TCS SPE, Wetzlar, Germany). The number and density of pre- and post-synaptic excitatory or inhibitory puncta were determined at the CA1 Stratum Radiatum (SR) region by acquiring images with a 63x-oil immersion objective and analyzed with Image J (NIH). For measurement of pre- and postsynaptic densities, we obtained 3 images for each brain section, where each image comprised eight equidistant planes, acquired 0.3 μm apart (119.06 μm x 119.06 μm), using 1024 × 1024 pixel resolution. Settings remain constant for acquisition of WT and TWI samples. For normalization, each independent value (for each pre- and post-synaptic marker) was obtained by calculating the average of each brain section, and then the fold of change respect to the average of WT group was estimated and displayed for each marker. At least 3 slices per animal/per genotype were analyzed. All analyses were performed blinded to each experimental group.

For analysis of dendritic spine density, brain sagittal sections (30 μm) of YFP/Twi mice were mounted and imaged by confocal microscopy, as previously described [44, 70]. Briefly, CA1 region was identified by 10x objectives and then, the SR layer was imaged with the 63x objective. The primary branch of dendrites (branch point located around 20–50 μm from the soma) was chosen as the area of acquisition and spines were imaged by 8–15 z-stacks (each 0.2 μm) with a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels. The maximal intensity projection of z-stacks was processed and the number of spines per dendrite was measured by Image J. At least 10 dendrites were analyzed per mouse/per genotype [70]. YFP/WT littermates were used as controls.

Psychosine was extracted from 50 to 200 µg of the different synaptosome fractions (TH, CYT, SPM, SV, PreS and PostS) as described in Saldivia et al. [68]. Briefly, lipids were extracted by adding a methanol-acetic acid mixture (0.5% acetic acid in methanol) and incubated for 1 h in mild agitation. Samples were then centrifuged at 16,000xg at 4 °C and supernatants were collected. Psychosine content was determined by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), as previously described [49]. Lactosyl-(β)-sphingosine (d18:1) was used in the methanol-acetic acid mixture as an internal reference for psychosine quantification.

Psychosine synthesis was performed as described in [43], but with slight modifications. Equal amounts of protein (150 µg) from WT or Twi synaptosomes were mixed with 2 µL of 25 mM GalactosylCeramide (Cayman Chemical), 100 µL of 0.2 M citrate buffer (pH 4.5), 14.5 µL of 2 M NaCl, 2 µL of 10% IGEPAL CA630 and 10 µL of 1% BSA, for a total 200 µL reaction. For t0, an aliquot of 100 µL of this reaction was immediately mixed with 0.5% acetic acid in methanol. The remaining 100 µL of reaction were incubated at 37 °C for 18 h (t1). Reaction was stopped by adding 0.5% acetic acid in methanol and psychosine levels were determined as described above. The rate of psychosine synthesis was calculated as the difference in psychosine content between t1 and t0 in 18 h.

For GALC activity, an equal amount of proteins (10–30 µg) of the different synaptosome fractions (i.e., CYT, SPM, SV, Pre-S and Post-S) obtained from WT or Twi brains were mixed with 20 µL of reaction buffer (0.2 M Na2HPO4, 0.1 M Citric Acid buffer pH 5.2 and 0.02% Na-Azide) containing the fluorescent GALC substrate (6HMU-beta-D-galactoside; Moscerdam Substrates). The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 17 h and subsequently stopped by adding 200 µL of stop buffer (0.2 M Glycine/NaOH buffer pH 10.7 + 0.2% Na-SDS + 0.2% Triton X-100). Enzymatic activity was measured via fluorescence using a Beckmann Coulter DTX 880 Multimode Detector (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, United States) using wavelengths of 370 nm and 535 nm for excitation/emission, respectively. As negative control, the same reaction was performed with 10 µL of 0.2% BSA, or with 10 µL of the respective buffer employed to obtain each synaptosome fraction.

For acid ceramidase (ACD) activity, 20 µg of SPMs were diluted in a 0.2 M sucrose solution (final volume of 25 µL) and combined with 74.5 µL of 25 mM Na-acetate buffer (pH 4.5) and 0.5 µL of 4 mM Rbm14-12. The reaction was incubated for 3 h at 37 °C and then stopped with 50 µL methanol and 100 µL of 2.5 mg/mL NaIO4 (dissolved in 100 mM glycine/NaOH buffer at pH 10.6). Samples were left for 2 h at 37 °C on the dark, and the released fluorescence was measured at 370λexc/465λem.

To assess FM2-10 release over time [48], 150 µg of SPM obtained from WT or Twi hippocampus, cortex or rest of the brain were spun at 10,000xg for 10 min at 4 °C and resuspended in 150 µL of HEPES-buffered medium (HBM) (140 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 5 mM NaHCO3, 1 mM MgCl2, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM D-Glucose and 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4), Ca2+ was added to a final concentration of 1.3 mM and the mixture was incubated for 3 min at 37 °C. Next, SPM were pre-incubated with FM2-10 (100 µM final concentration) for 5 min and endocytic uptake of membrane-bound dye was stimulated by adding 30 mM KCl for 3 min at 37 °C. Then, unbound FM2-10 was washed by two centrifugations at 10,000xg for 1 min each at 4 °C. Pelleted SPM were resuspended in 150 µL of HBM to allow repolarization and loaded into a 96-well black plate. Finally, FM2-10-labeled vesicles were released by a second round of stimulation with 1.3 mM of CaCl2 and 30 mM of KCl and the decay of fluorescence over a time period of 20 min was read each 30 s at 485λexc/625λem. For experiments using the different lipids, 150 µg of SPM obtained from WT cortices were prepared as indicated above, and lipids (100–500µM) or vehicle were added after the dye-loading step and incubated for 5 min at 37 °C before depolarization was induced with 30 mM KCl.

For the endocytosis assay, 400 µg of SPM from the hippocampus, cortex or ROB were used, FM2-10 uptake was stimulated using the same protocol described above, except that following the first KCl stimulation, SPM were lysed in a hypotonic solution (20 mM HEPES pH 7.4) for 10 min on ice and then vortexed for 10 s afterwards. Samples were centrifuged at 20,000xg for 2 min at 4 °C and the fluorescence in the supernatant was measured at 485λexc/625λem.

Small unilamellar liposomes containing t-SNARE complexes or VAMP2 were made using 55% POPC (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero 3-phosphocholine), 15% DOPS (1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-L-serine (sodium salt), 30% POPE (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine) and 15% psychosine in chloroform. Liposomes were dried to a lipid film in a glass vial under argon, followed by vacuum removal of residual chloroform. Liposomes containing VAMP2 included 1.5% NBD-PE and 1.5% N-(lissamine rhodamine B sulfonyl)−1,2-dipalmitoyl phosphatidylethanolamine (rhodamine-PE), along with 55% POPC/15% DOPS/27% POPE. t-SNARE dimer (0.4 mg) or 95 µl of VAMP2 was added to each tube of lipids in tandem with elution buffer (25 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.8), 400 mM KCl, 500 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 1% n-octylglucoside) to a final volume of 500 µl and subjected to mild agitation until the lipid film was fully dissolved. Liposomes were then formed by adding two volumes of reconstitution buffer (25 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.8), 100 mM KCl, 1 mM dithiothreitol, and 10% glycerol) in a dropwise manner, followed by additional mild agitation for 10 min. The solution was then dialyzed (10,000 molecular weight cutoff) twice for 6 h in 4 L of reconstitution buffer to remove residual detergent. Solutions were mixed with equivalent volumes of 80% iohexol (Accudenz, Accurate Chemical Co.) and were purified on a 0%−30%−40% iohexol gradient in a Beckman SW-55 swinging bucket rotor. Liposomes were harvested from the 0 to 30% interface and flash-frozen at − 80 °C. Lipid concentrations and recovery rates were obtained using the Beer-Lambert law with the NBD-PE absorbance at 460 nm from v-SNARE liposomes containing NBD-PE that were maximally dequenched through the addition of docdeylmaltoside to 0.5%. SNARE protein concentrations in VAMP2 and t-SNARE liposomes were determined by Coomassie brilliant blue R-250 staining of SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels containing a standard curve of bovine serum albumin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by densitometric analysis of pixel intensity of syntaxin1A bands using the Fiji distribution of ImageJ software. SNARE copy number in liposomes was determined according to previously published methods [77, 88].

To determine whether this phenotype is present in other models of GALC deficiency, we used the same approach to examine hippocampal synaptic transmission in GALCG41S/G41S mice, a late-onset KD model characterized by mild demyelination and progressive motor and memory deficits [64]. Like TWI mice, GALCG41S/G41S animals showed impaired PPF at 2 years of age (Fig. 1E, F).

To further investigate ultrastructural synaptic changes, we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on hippocampal sections. TWI neurons exhibited both pre and post synaptic defects. They showed a reduction in synaptic vesicle (SV) number and a decrease in postsynaptic density (PSD) length compared to WT (Fig. 2D). SV pool analysis revealed stage-dependent defects: at P20, the reserve vesicle pool (RR) was reduced, while at P40, all vesicle pools—including reserve (RR), proximal (PP), and docked SVs—were significantly diminished in TWI neurons (Fig. 2E, F). Quantification of total SVs per terminal and PSD length demonstrated significant reductions at both P20 and P40 (Fig. 2G, H), indicating that synaptic structural compromise emerges early in disease progression.

Furthermore, synaptic clefts were enlarged in most TWI synapses at later stages of the disease, suggesting impaired alignment between pre- and postsynaptic membranes (Fig. 2I). Similar alterations in dendritic spines, SV number, and PSD size were also observed in the prefrontal cortex of TWI mice (Fig. S1A-G), indicating that GALC deficiency broadly impacts synaptic organization across multiple brain regions.

Next, to better understand the impact of GALC deficiency on SV structure, we analyzed the area and perimeter of SVs. Measurement of these parameters showed larger TWI SVs at both P20 and P40 (Fig. 2J), with area and perimeter significantly increased compared with those of WT SVs (Fig. 2J, K). Remarkably, these SV alterations in TWI mice were dependent on GALC restoration. For example, gene therapy with AAV9-GALC normalized SV size (TWI + AAV-GALC). However, treatment with BMT (TWI + BMT) failed to prevent synaptic changes (Figure S1H–J), likely due to the limited ability of BMT to restore sufficient GALC levels in the TWI brain [49]. Together, these findings reveal lower number of TWI SV with enlarged size, likely with a reduced load of neurotransmitters. Furthermore, these changes appear preventable when sufficient GALC correction is provided.

We next asked whether psychosine could be locally synthesized within synaptic compartments. An in vitro synthesis assay [43] using SPM fractions revealed that psychosine production was ~ 6.5-fold higher in TWI SPM than in WT at P20, increasing to ~ 15-fold at P40 (Fig. 4D). GALC enzymatic activity in WT SPM remained stable across these time points (Fig. 4E), suggesting that the elevated psychosine levels in TWI synapses are not solely due to impaired degradation but also reflect enhanced local synthesis (Fig. S2A). To note, the increased synthesis of psychosine was not due to alterations in the activity of ACD (Fig.S2B), the main enzyme responsible for psychosine production [43]. Together, these findings indicate that psychosine accumulates primarily in the presynaptic compartment of TWI neurons, that synaptosomes have the synthetic machinery to generate psychosine, and that this capacity is heightened in TWI synaptosomes.

To explore underlying mechanisms, we analyzed signaling pathways known to regulate SNARE complex turnover. Psychosine has been reported to activate Ser/Thr protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A) and glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), both of which modulate presynaptic organization and development of active zones [7, 78, 79]. PP2A activity enhances SNAP-25 phosphorylation at Ser187 [35], promoting SNARE assembly [84], while GSK3β facilitates SNARE disassembly when active. Western blot analysis of TWI hippocampal SPM revealed elevated levels of methylated PP2Ac at Leu309 (the active form), increased phosphorylation of SNAP-25 at Ser187, and enhanced phosphorylation of GSK3β at Ser9 (the inactive form), indicating coordinated upregulation of SNARE-assembly-promoting signals (Fig. 6C–G). Additionally, phosphorylated CaMKII at Thr286 (p-CaMKII)—a kinase known to phosphorylate v- and t-SNARE proteins [80]—was significantly increased in TWI SPM (Fig. 6F, H).

Consistent with regional specificity, TWI cortical SPM also exhibited elevated levels of p-SNAP-25, methylated PP2Ac, and p-GSK3β, whereas SPM from the rest of the brain showed only increased p-GSK3β without changes in other markers (Fig. S6A–F). Furthermore, p-SNAP-25 was present in both SNAP-25 monomers and higher molecular weight SNARE complexes (~ 150–200 kDa) (Fig. 6I–J), supporting a model of aberrant SNARE stabilization.

Together, these findings suggest that GALC deficiency in TWI mice leads to dysregulation of multiple signaling pathways that converge to enhance SNARE complex assembly, resulting in their pathological accumulation at presynaptic terminals.

The buildup of SNARE complexes in TWI synaptic membranes could result from enhanced SNARE complex formation during vesicle docking or from impaired fusion and recycling steps that disrupt SV turnover. In contrast, sphingosine enhanced SV exocytosis (Fig. 7C, Fig. S8G). As expected, psychosine exerted an inhibitory effect on SV cycling (Fig. 7C, Fig. S8H). These findings support a lipid-specific modulation of SV cycling by disease-associated sphingolipids.

We next evaluated SV endocytosis by measuring FM2-10 uptake during depolarization. TWI hippocampal SPM incorporated significantly less FM2-10 than WT, indicating reduced endocytic activity (Fig. 7D), while SPM from cortex and the rest of the brain were unaffected (Fig. S7C–D).

To directly test whether psychosine impairs SNARE-mediated membrane fusion, we performed an in vitro liposome fusion assay using V-liposomes (bearing synaptobrevin) and T-liposomes (containing Syntaxin-1 and SNAP-25). The reaction was performed in buffer containing the cytoplasmic domain of synaptotagmin 1 (C2AB, 10 μm) [25]. Upon Ca²⁺ addition, robust fusion from ~ 2% to around 20% occurred in control conditions, as measured by FRET-based fluorescence (Fig. 7E). However, psychosine inhibited this fusion in a dose-dependent manner, reducing maximum fluorescence by ~ 40% at 15% psychosine (Fig. 7E–F).

Altogether, these results suggest that in KD neurons, psychosine accumulates in the presynaptic compartments, inhibiting SNARE-mediated SV fusion, resulting in the buildup of SNARE complexes, and leading to synaptic dysfunction (Fig. 7G).

The structural and functional integrity of synapses is essential for maintaining neuronal connectivity in the brain. Among various synaptic components, membrane-associated sphingolipids have emerged as critical modulators of neurotransmitter receptor activity, SNARE complex dynamics, and memory consolidation. Accordingly, synaptic dysfunction is a common feature across sphingolipidoses [45, 61, 63]. However, the precise mechanisms by which sphingolipid dysregulation disrupts synaptic homeostasis remain poorly understood. Lysosomes play a central role in metabolite clearance and recycling, which is essential for neuronal function and survival [21]. When lysosomal degradation is impaired, un-degraded substrates accumulate, leading to neurodegeneration and synaptic pathology—as observed in LSD and in age-related conditions such as Alzheimer disease, Parkinson disease, and frontotemporal dementia.

Sphingolipidoses represent a subset of LSD characterized by disrupted sphingolipid metabolism and marked changes in membrane lipid composition. To investigate the mechanisms underlying synaptic dysfunction in these disorders, we focused on KD, a prototypical neurodegenerative sphingolipidosis. In KD, loss of GALC activity leads to toxic accumulation of the sphingolipid psychosine, resulting in demyelination and neurodegeneration [33, 41, 82]. Our prior work demonstrated that psychosine alone is sufficient to induce dose-dependent neuronal abnormalities, including neurofilament disruption, reduced axonal transport, axonopathy, and membrane shedding. Using the TWI mouse—an aggressive GALC-deficient model with elevated psychosine levels [19, 34, 41]—we observed marked structural damage in hippocampal synapses. These included dendritic spine shrinkage, reduced spine density, diminished synaptic contact area, and depletion of SV. These abnormalities were evident by disease onset (P20) and were exacerbated by P40. Such morphological features resemble synaptic degeneration observed in other neurodegenerative diseases [4, 27, 32].

The reduction in presynaptic vesicle number and the increase in vesicle size suggest impaired probability of vesicle release [18, 37, 83], likely due to defects in vesicle trafficking and recycling [13, 20, 66, 69]. Electrophysiological recordings further confirmed decreased fEPSP and PPF responses in the TWI hippocampus at late disease stages. Importantly, synaptic transmission deficits were also observed in two additional GALC-deficient models (CamKII-CreERT2/GALCflox and GALCG41S/G41S), indicating a neuron-autonomous origin. This is also supported by the large effects on synaptic transmission in unmyelinated axons in the SCCP pathway. The similar vulnerability of both excitatory and inhibitory synapses, despite regional and temporal differences, is consistent with the ubiquitous expression of GALC. This heterogeneity suggests that synapses may differ in their metabolic demands and compensatory capacity during disease progression.

Furthermore, disrupted synaptic organization was observed for postsynaptic (Homer-1, Gephyrin) rather than for presynaptic (vGluT1 and vGAT) markers. Our results suggest that the accumulation of psychosine in the presynaptic compartment interferes with synaptic vesicle release, triggering reduced synaptic activity, ultimately leading to destabilization of postsynaptic scaffold proteins and their consequent disorganization. Thus, although presynaptic terminals remain structurally present, they are functionally impaired as disease progresses, negatively impacting on postsynaptic organization.

Although relatively few studies have examined synaptic function in other LSD, growing evidence supports a broader relevance of synaptic dysfunction. In mucopolysaccharidosis Type IIIC (Sanfilippo syndrome), neuronal cultures and in vivo models show reduced spine density, impaired transport of synaptic proteins, and altered synaptic currents [57]. Similarly, Tay-Sachs disease exhibits disrupted synaptic protein localization and neurotransmission [51], while Niemann-Pick type C demonstrates cholesterol trafficking defects that impair vesicle dynamics and long-term potentiation [60]. In multiple sulfatase deficiency, synaptic defects appear to arise from SNARE complex perturbation and lipid accumulation [24]. Notably, synaptic abnormalities in MPS-IIIA shares striking features with KD, including vesicle depletion and decreased synaptic density [69], potentially due to reduced α-synuclein and CSPα levels at axon terminals. Collectively, these observations support a convergent mechanism of synaptic degeneration across LSD [63].

Proper assembly of the SNARE complex is essential for SV fusion and neurotransmitter release. Disruption of this process—due to deficits in Syntaxin-1, SNAP-25, VAMP2, or α-synuclein—has been implicated in cognitive decline and impaired presynaptic plasticity [28, 38]. Intriguingly, whereas MPS-IIIA models show impaired SNARE assembly, TWI synaptosomes revealed excessive accumulation of assembled SNARE complexes, with elevated SNAP-25, VAMP2, MUNC-18, and α-synuclein levels. This may result from increased α-synuclein binding to the membrane, potentially driven by elevated psychosine concentrations [1]. In healthy neurons, α-synuclein exists in a cytosolic monomeric state and a membrane-bound multimeric form, the latter of which promotes SNARE complex formation [5, 6]. Our previous work demonstrated a direct interaction between psychosine and α-synuclein [1], supporting the hypothesis that psychosine enhances SNARE assembly via increased α-synuclein membrane association. Whether this effect extends to other SNARE partners such as VAMP2, which can also stabilize vesicle docking, remains to be determined [2].

Notably, regional differences in vesicle cycling between hippocampus and cortex suggest variable vulnerability across brain regions. We previously reported earlier and greater accumulation of psychosine and thioflavin-S in the hippocampus than in the cortex [73], potentially contributing to enhanced synaptic susceptibility in this area. Similar regional heterogeneity has been observed in Alzheimer disease models [3], reinforcing the idea that synaptic vulnerability is shaped by both lipid composition and local homeostatic mechanisms. Using whole-brain preparations, we were able to assess the overall distribution of psychosine among synaptic compartments and observe regional differences in synaptic vulnerability. Focusing specifically on hippocampal SPMs would be of considerable interest, as it could provide more region-specific insights into synaptic susceptibility. However, such an analysis would require large amounts of hippocampal tissue from TWI brains, which was not feasible in the present study. Additionally, no sex-dependent differences in synaptic dysfunction were observed across KD mouse models. Since KD is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, such differences were not expected; however, studies with larger cohorts may help clarify potential sex-specific effects on synaptic alterations.

At the molecular level, TWI SPM exhibited increased phosphorylation of SNAP-25 at Ser187, a modification mediated by PKC that facilitates SNARE assembly and SV exocytosis [26]. In contrast, PKA-dependent phosphorylation at Thr138 inhibits SNARE complex assembly [26]. Our findings are consistent with a PKC-mediated enhancement of SNARE assembly, potentially accompanied by decreased PKA signaling. Additionally, p-SNAP-25 at Ser187 suppresses neuronal excitability by inhibiting VGCCs [59], which may further reduce neurotransmitter release in TWI neurons.

While psychosine is known to activate protein phosphatases such as PP1 and PP2A [9] and p-SNAP-25 at Ser187 is a known substrate of PP2A [35], we did not observe a corresponding reduction in p-SNAP-25 at Ser187, despite increased PP2A activity. This suggests that in the TWI context, PP2A may instead act on alternative targets. For instance, PP2A dephosphorylates voltage-gated sodium channels, potentially impairing presynaptic depolarization [15], and is required for the organization of active zones [31]. Phosphorylation of SNARE domain residues can inhibit vesicle fusion [47, 53], and thus, increased PP2A activity in TWI synapses may drive the dephosphorylation of other SV fusion mediators, contributing to the accumulation of SNARE complexes.

In addition to the accumulation of assembled SNARE complexes, our in vitro fusion assay revealed a direct impairment in vesicle fusion efficiency, suggesting a defect at the late stage of synaptic vesicle exocytosis. This late-stage dysfunction could reflect compromised membrane fusogenicity or impaired zippering of the ternary SNARE complex, despite its assembly. One potential mechanism involves altered interactions between SNAREs and Ca2+-sensing proteins (such as Synaptotagmin-1), which plays a pivotal role in triggering vesicle fusion upon Ca2+influx [14, 74]. Disruption of Synaptotagmin–membrane binding—either due to lipid environment changes [82] or altered protein phosphorylation [47, 53]—can decouple Ca2+ sensing from membrane fusion, leading to hindered exocytosis despite intact SNARE assembly [65]. Furthermore, psychosine-induced changes in membrane curvature or composition may reduce the biophysical capacity of synaptic membranes to undergo proper fusion. This suggests that in KD, defects on vesicle trafficking extend beyond SNARE assembly and include a late-step impairment in the accomplishment of membrane fusion, potentially driven by both lipid-based and protein-based mechanisms.

Finally, TWI SPM exhibited increased phosphorylation (i.e., inactivation) of GSK3-β at Ser9. GSK3-β activity suppresses exocytosis by inhibiting P/Q-type VGCCs, decreasing Ca²⁺ influx, and disrupting SNARE interactions [86]. Although psychosine activates GSK3-β in axons [7], our results suggest a compartment-specific effect, with synaptic GSK3-β inactivation favoring SV exocytosis while potentially destabilizing dendritic spines [56, 85]. Whether GSK3-β directly contributes to SNARE complex accumulation or merely reflects a downstream consequence of psychosine elevation remains unclear. Modulating GSK3-β activity could therefore be a valuable strategy in future studies to clarify its causal role in synaptic alterations.

In summary, our findings delineate a multifaceted disruption of synaptic structure and function in KD, driven by psychosine accumulation and its downstream effects on vesicle dynamics, SNARE complex assembly, and synaptic signaling pathways. These alterations appear to converge on a common mechanism observed across several sphingolipidoses: lipid-driven dysregulation of presynaptic vesicle trafficking. Targeting this shared vulnerability may represent a promising therapeutic approach for mitigating synaptic failure and improving cognition in affected patients.
