# Prevalence and clinical relevance of systematically tested antimitochondrial antibodies in systemic sclerosis.
**Authors:** Tonutti A, Motta F, Isailovic N, Selmi C, Ceribelli A, De Santis M
**Journal:** Arthritis Res Ther (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13075-026-03771-4](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13075-026-03771-4)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Antimitochondrial antibodies (AMA), the hallmark of primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), have been inconsistently reported and poorly characterized in systemic sclerosis (SSc). Although recently linked to subclinical dysmotility in a cohort enriched for gastrointestinal (GI) involvement, data on the prevalence and clinical relevance of AMA in real-life SSc cohorts remain limited. METHODS: A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted on consecutive SSc patients followed at a referral center for the disease. Patients were tested for serum AMA according to reference standards, including HEp-2 immunofluorescent screening and ELISA confirmation (anti-E2 pyruvate dehydrogenase). Patients’ features were compared between AMA-positive and -negative cases. RESULTS: Among 165 SSc patients (20% diffuse cutaneous disease, 33% interstitial lung disease, 7.3% pulmonary arterial hypertension; 51% positive for anticentromere antibodies, 29% anti-topoisomerase I, 12% anti-RNA polymerase III), 37 (22%) were AMA-positive. While strongly associated with PBC, AMA were not linked to significant differences in cutaneous, vascular, pulmonary, or myocardial involvement. However, AMA were associated with GI vascular involvement (OR 13.3, 95% CI 2.53–95.0; p = 0.002), including gastric antral vascular ectasia (OR 7.99, 95% CI 1.05–72.5; p = 0.045), independently from PBC or symptoms of SSc-GI involvement. These complications are clinically relevant, as they may lead to GI bleeding and refractory iron-deficiency anemia. CONCLUSION: In our cohort of SSc patients systematically screened for autoantibodies, we found a high prevalence of AMA and identified a novel putative association with GI vascular involvement, which warrants confirmation in independent studies.

Antimitochondrial antibodies (AMA) primarily target the E2 subunit of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (E2-PDC), and can be efficiently detected using recombinant antigen-based assays [1]. AMA also produce a characteristic cytoplasmic reticular/AMA-like (AC-21) pattern according to the International Consensus on ANA Patterns (ICAP) when positive sera are tested using reference standard indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) on HEp-2 cells [2].

Being a cornerstone in the diagnosis of primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), AMA have been seldom reported in connective tissue diseases (CTD) [3] and, quite unexpectedly, in COVID-19 associated autoimmunity, often combined with autoantibodies shared with CTD [4]. Nonetheless, evidence regarding AMA in systemic sclerosis (SSc) remains limited and heterogeneous, as very few centers routinely assess their presence in the absence of suspected PBC overlap. Reported prevalence is highly variable, reaching up to 20%, and largely influenced by both the indication for testing and the adopted techniques [5–7]. Clinical associations have also been quite weak, until a recent study described an association with functional evidence of esophago-gastric dysmotility in a cohort of 147 SSc patients enriched for GI involvement, 12.9% of whom AMA-positive [8].

Due to research purposes, our center routinely screens for serum AMA in patients with SSc. Considering the paucity, heterogeneity, and inconclusiveness of available evidence—mostly derived from cohorts enriched for PBC or GI involvement—our real-life scenario provides a unique opportunity to evaluate the impact of systematic AMA screening in patients with SSc with highly reproducible techniques. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the prevalence and clinical relevance of serum AMA in an unselected cohort of patients with SSc from a tertiary care center.

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted on consecutively enrolled patients aged ≥ 18 years, fulfilling 2013 SSc classification criteria [9], and followed at our institution for at least three consecutive years between January 2014 and July 2025. All patients underwent standardized procedures for visceral involvement screening and evaluation, as detailed below.

Patient’s characteristics were collected at the last available follow-up visit. Demographic variables included age, sex, disease duration (i.e.: time from the first non-Raynaud’s phenomenon manifestation to the index visit), smoking history, and vital status (alive or deceased). Clinical variables included: the cutaneous subset (diffuse cutaneous—dcSSc, limited cutaneous—lcSSc, sine scleroderma) [10]; the presence or history of digital ulcers, calcinosis, arthritis, and telangiectasias; myositis (increased values of serum muscle enzymes in the absence of other explanation, with or without associated muscle weakness on physical examination); scleroderma renal crisis (SRC) according to classification criteria [11]. Esophageal (gastroesophageal reflux, dysphagia), gastric (early satiety, dyspepsia, refractory post-prandial nausea, vomiting) and intestinal (constipation, diarrhea, malabsorption syndrome) symptoms were systematically queried and patient reported, as per clinical practice. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) was diagnosed if high-resolution chest CT scan showed the presence of ground-glass opacities, reticulations, traction bronchiectasis, or honeycombing [12, 13]. Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) was diagnosed by right-heart catheterization performed upon clinical suspicion, according to established procedures [14, 15]. Primary heart involvement (pHI) was defined as evidence of cardiac inflammation and/or fibrosis on cardiac magnetic resonance performed upon clinical or instrumental suspicion, as detailed in previous studies and recommendations [16–18]. Overlap PBC was diagnosed in patients meeting the diagnostic criteria and upon hepatologists’ review [19]. Anticentromere (ACA), anti-topoisomerase I (TOPO1), and RNA polymerase III (POLR3) autoantibodies were assessed at the time of diagnosis in routine laboratory, as per clinical practice [9]. Patients testing negative for ACA, anti-TOPO1, and anti-POLR3 were addressed as ‘CTP-negative’.

During follow-up, all patients received at least annual comprehensive evaluation of clinical domains, along with routine chemistries (including full blood count). All patients also underwent periodic malignancy screening, including biennial fecal occult blood testing and mammography, annual PSA measurement or gynecologic evaluation (with periodic cervical cancer screening), according to local practice [20]. Gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy was performed as screening in patients with a family history of GI neoplasm, in case of positive fecal occult blood testing, unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, or “red-flags”—including overt/covert GI bleeding, unexplained weight loss, persistent epigastric or abdominal pain, refractory gastroesophageal reflux, dysphagia or food impaction, odynophagia, early satiety, dyspepsia, recurrent vomiting, or unexplained changes in bowel habits [21, 22]. Evidence of gastric antral vascular ectasia (GAVE) and GI telangiectasias on digestive endoscopy was recorded, and the term ‘GI vascular involvement’ will be used hereafter when referring to both conditions.

Study procedures were approved by the Ethical Committee of our Institution (reference n. 831).

All patients underwent autoantibody assessment at the local research laboratory. Serum samples were obtained at the first visit and tested for the presence of ANA on HEp-2 IIF, according to ICAP standards [2, 23] (https://anapatterns.org). Briefly, immunofluorescent antinuclear/cytoplasmic antibodies (HEp-2 ANA slides by INOVA Diagnostics, San Diego, CA, USA) were tested using serial dilutions of human sera (1:80, 1:160, 1:320, 1:640, and 1:1280). Patients’ sera were incubated on HEp-2 slides followed by AlexaFluor488 AffiniPure F(ab’)2 fragment goat anti-human IgG (1:400 dilution, Jackson Immunoresearch Europe Ltd., Suffolk, UK). After incubation, the slides were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (1:20 dilution), and cover glasses were fixed on the slides using nail polish. Images were acquired immediately using an Olympus BX53 Upright fluorescence microscope. ANA patterns were defined according to the ICAP classification [23] (https://anapatterns.org).

For sera displaying a cytoplasmic reticular/AMA-like (AC-21) pattern on HEp-2 IIF, the presence of AMA was confirmed using the QUANTA Lite® M2 EP (MIT3) ELISA kit (Inova Diagnostics, Inc; San Diego, California), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The kit recognizes autoantibodies directed to the primary autoantigens associated with AMA in PBC, including E2-PDC, E2-OGDC (2-Oxoglutarate Dehydrogenase Complex), and E2-BCOADC (dihydrolipoyl transacetylase subunit of the Branched-Chain 2-Oxo Acid Dehydrogenase Complex), [24].

Patients were defined as ‘AMA-positive’ in case of evidence of the AC-21 pattern on HEp-2 IIF and a positive ELISA result, or ‘AMA-negative’ in case of either the absence of the AC-21 pattern or a negative ELISA result.

The exposure of interest was the presence of serum AMA. Outcomes of interest included clinical and demographic features, defined as above.

After assessing for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviation or medians with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the two-tailed t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were recorded as numbers and percentages and compared with the Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

Candidate associations between serum AMA and outcomes were explored using multivariable logistic regression to account for potential confounders. These included age, sex, disease duration, and variables showing p < 0.10 in univariable analysis. Due to the limited sample size and low frequencies of selected outcomes, multiple models were fitted to test different combinations of covariates to explore consistency. Multivariable logistic regression with Firth’s penalization was constructed on the same sets of covariates to confirm the observed associations.

Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p-values < 0.05. All analyses were performed using RStudio (version 2024.12.0 + 467).

AMA were more frequently positive in patients with PBC (41% vs. 1.6%; p < 0.001) and with coexisting ACA (76% vs. 44%; p = 0.001). Compared with AMA-negative patients, AMA-positive patients had longer disease duration (14 years, IQR 7–18, vs. 8 years, IQR 4–14; p = 0.016) and tended to have less ILD (19% vs. 38%; p = 0.056). While the presence of GI symptoms was similarly distributed among the two groups, GI vascular involvement was more frequent in AMA-positive patients (19% vs. 3.9%; p = 0.006), with a similar trend specifically for GAVE. Comparable findings were reported after restricting the analysis on GI vascular involvement to patients with available endoscopy only. Remarkably, among the two patients with liver cirrhosis and GAVE, AMA were detected only in the case related to PBC. No association was seen between anti-POLR3 and GI vascular involvement, including GAVE (data not shown). The proportion of patients exposed to different vasoactive and immunosuppressive treatments did not differ according to serum AMA positivity.

These associations remained significant when adjusting for different sets of demographic variables (age, sex, disease duration), covariates showing p < 0.10 in univariable analysis (PBC overlap, ACA positivity), and factors linked to GAVE according to previous literature (anti-POLR3) [25]. Leveraging the unique setting of a referral SSc cohort in which autoantibodies were systematically tested, we report a prevalence of AMA as high as 22%. Previous studies described prevalences ranging from 7 to 24% in SSc, depending on cohort size and detection techniques [5–8, 26–30]. Our findings align with the upper range of these data, higher than what recently described in a similarly sized cohort enriched for GI functional abnormalities [8]. Therefore, our results confirm that AMA represent a common finding in SSc sera, provided they are systematically assessed with reference standard methods.

Systematic screening remains the only reliable method to evaluate the prevalence and clinical relevance of AMA in SSc. Our study applied a two-step strategy for their determination, consisting of HEp-2 IIF screening followed by ELISA confirmation for antibodies to E2-PDC, E2-OGDC, and E2-BCOADC in patients displaying a compatible cytoplasmic reticular/AMA-like (AC-21) pattern. This approach is in line with ICAP recommendations [2, 31] and underscores the feasibility of AMA testing in routine practice, where HEp-2 IIF remains the gold standard for ANA detection. Remarkably, a slight discrepancy was seen between patients showing an AMA-like pattern on HEp-2 IIF (25%) and those with confirmed AMA on solid-phase assay (22%), indicating that a minority of SSc patients may harbor autoantibodies targeting mitochondrial antigens other than the ones mentioned above. Notably, such antibodies are clinically relevant in the diagnosis of PBC; nevertheless, we might expect that additional antimitochondrial specificities could acquire significance in the context of SSc—and, in general, of CTD—warranting further investigation [24]. A proposed algorithm for systematic AMA screening and testing in patients with SSc is reported in Fig. 1 and, despite the clinical consequences remain speculative, this could represent a research tool to inform further validation in larger and independent cohorts.

In partial contrast to a recent study focusing on subclinical GI dysmotility [8], we did not observe any association between AMA and clinically defined GI involvement in our SSc cohort. This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences. Indeed, McMahan et al. systematically evaluated GI motility in a SSc cohort enriched for digestive complaints [8], whereas our study focused on an unselected, real-life SSc population where GI involvement was defined by patient-reported symptoms—although systematically collected. Notably, the association between AMA and PBC was confirmed in our cohort but not in the GI-enriched study [8], underscoring that the clinical setting and patient selection may influence the observed associations. Beyond the potential underestimation of subclinical functional alterations, prospective studies with more detailed phenotyping of GI involvement (e.g., through dedicated questionnaires) are warranted.

Our study identified a novel association between serum AMA and GI vascular involvement, including GAVE and GI telangiectasias (Fig. 1). These complications were relatively uncommon, but the association remained significant across multiple sensitivity analyses in multivariable logistic regression. This association was independent of PBC and liver cirrhosis, seldom causing GAVE. It is worth noting that GI vascular involvement predisposes patients to digestive bleeding which, although often occult, represents a major cause of iron-deficiency anemia and frequently requires endoscopic intervention and tailored follow-up. While previous studies reported an association between GAVE and anti-POLR3 autoantibodies in SSc [32–34], we were not able to replicate this finding. This could be due to the relatively low proportion of anti-POLR3-positive patients in our cohort, a common feature in European populations [35]. Moreover, geographical heterogeneity should be accounted [36], since no evidence of such association was found in Japanese and Australian cohorts [33, 34].

Remarkably, AMA were most frequently detected in SSc sera in association with other autoantibodies—primarily ACA—whereas isolated AMA positivity, or coexistence with less specific CTD-related antibodies such as anti-PM/Scl, was uncommon. In the ACA-positive subgroup, our sub-analysis confirmed the association between AMA positivity and gastrointestinal vascular involvement, driven by a higher prevalence of gastrointestinal telangiectasias, while the prevalence of GAVE was similar. By contrast, although numbers were very small, both AMA-positive patients lacking SSc-specific autoantibodies were diagnosed with pHI, compared with a lower proportion among CTP-negative and AMA-negative patients. Notably, AMA positivity has been identified as a risk factor for myocardial involvement in inflammatory myositis [37]; therefore, further studies are warranted to clarify whether AMA contributes to the risk of pHI in SSc [18, 38] across different autoantibody subsets.

Considering the exploratory nature of our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the monocentric cross-sectional design in a referral cohort limits generalizability, and independent replication is warranted. The type of design also limits inference on temporal relationships, preventing any conclusion on causality. Second, the sample size was relatively small, and the number of events limited, as reflected by the wide CIs of regression models, indicating a degree of statistical uncertainty. To mitigate this limitation, Firth’s penalization was adopted and multiple models were fitted on different subsets of covariates to assess consistency. No correction for multiple testing was applied, in line with the exploratory purpose of this study [39]. For the same reasons, results from sub-analyses examining AMA positivity within specific autoantibody subsets should be interpreted with caution and are best considered hypothesis-generating. Third, endoscopic examinations were available only in a subset of patients, although performed according to real-world clinical practice. Moreover, since all patients diagnosed with GI vascular involvement had presented with iron-deficiency anemia, a certain degree of selection bias cannot be excluded. Fourth, although the two-step approach to AMA testing reflects the gold standard for autoantibody determination in CTD, it cannot completely exclude the occurrence of AMA positivity in patients without a detectable AMA-like HEp-2 IIF pattern; nevertheless, this scenario appears very rare and should be interpreted with caution [3]. Finally, some clinical associations (e.g., SRC) could not be properly evaluated due to their low prevalence.

In conclusion, we describe a prevalence of serum AMA as high as 22% in a cohort of unselected SSc patients from a national referral center. We confirm the association with PBC, despite a relevant proportion of AMA-positive patients with SSc do not meet criteria for overlapping autoimmune cholangitis. We postulate a novel link between serum AMA and GI vascular involvement, including GAVE, warranting validation in larger independent cohorts and pathophysiological exploration. We propose a two-step systematic autoantibody screening as reliable to investigate the clinical relevance of AMA in patients with SSc.
