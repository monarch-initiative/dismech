Many inherited bone marrow failure syndromes (IBMFSs) present a high risk of 
transformation to myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia 
(AML). During transformation of IBMFSs, hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells 
(HSPCs) with poor fitness gain ectopic, dysregulated self-renewal secondary to 
somatic mutations via undefined mechanisms. Here, in the context of the 
prototypical IBMFS Fanconi anemia (FA), we performed multiplexed gene editing of 
mutational hotspots in MDS-associated genes in human induced pluripotent stem 
cells (iPSCs) followed by hematopoietic differentiation. We observed aberrant 
self-renewal and impaired differentiation of HSPCs with enrichment of RUNX1 
insertions and deletions (indels), generating a model of IBMFS-associated MDS. 
We observed that compared to the failure state, FA MDS cells show mutant 
RUNX1-mediated blunting of the G1/S cell cycle checkpoint that is normally 
activated in FA in response to DNA damage. RUNX1 indels also lead to activation 
of innate immune signaling, which stabilizes the homologous recombination (HR) 
effector BRCA1, and this pathway can be targeted to abrogate viability and 
restore sensitivity to genotoxins in FA MDS. Together, these studies develop a 
paradigm for modeling clonal evolution in IBMFSs, provide basic understanding of 
the pathogenesis of MDS, and uncover a therapeutic target in FA-associated MDS.

In older adults, transformation of healthy hematopoiesis to myeloid malignancy is driven by the accumulation of somatic mutations in HSPCs and is often preceded by age-related clonal hematopoiesis (CH) [1, 2]. However, children and younger adults with certain IBMFSs are predisposed to a precocious and accelerated course of CH with a high incidence of MDS and AML [3–5]. The reduced fitness of HSPCs in IBMFSs creates a positive selection pressure for clones acquiring somatic mutations that either adaptively improve function (such as in the case of somatic reversion) or that confer pathologically dysregulated self-renewal that drives clonal expansion [6–8]. Such clones acquire defective differentiation, accelerated proliferation, and morphologic dysplasia through additional somatic mutations and cytogenetic anomalies, leading to MDS [9, 10]. Although advances in sequencing technologies over the past decade have markedly improved understanding of the mutational profiles of human MDS, how somatic mutations perturb self-renewal pathways in HSPCs, particularly in IBMFSs and MDS more broadly, is incompletely understood due to the lack of tractable, sustainable, and disease-relevant human models [11].

Fanconi anemia (FA) is an IBMFS caused by inactivating mutations in genes functioning in DNA repair [12]. Individuals with FA typically experience HSPC failure within the first decade of life that is only curable by allogeneic HSPC transplantation. FA HSPCs undergo pronounced quiescence, differentiation, and cell death secondary to DNA damage-induced cell cycle checkpoint activation, leading to cytopenias [13–15]. The inherent defect in DNA repair enables the accelerated accumulation of secondary somatic mutations and cytogenetic anomalies [9, 10, 16]. Together with a strong selective advantage for HSPC clones with increased fitness, this ongoing genotoxicity translates to a markedly increased risk of MDS and AML [13, 17]. MDS and AML in FA present a therapeutic challenge due to the constitutional sensitivity of FA patients to chemotherapy [18]. Moreover, FA patients bear an elevated risk of cancer in other tissues that is exacerbated by late effects of chemotherapy and HSPC transplantation [19]. Therefore, non-genotoxic, targeted therapies are needed to optimize long-term outcomes for FA patients with CH, MDS and AML.

Here, we performed multiplexed editing in iPSCs derived from FA patients to identify somatic mutations that blunt the G1/S checkpoint to maladaptively reverse the HSPC dysfunction of FA, conferring aberrant self-renewal of myeloid-biased hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) with impaired differentiation, consistent with an MDS-like state [20]. In this system, we reproducibly observe enrichment of indels in RUNX1 in FA MDS-like cells, recapitulating the high prevalence of RUNX1 mutations reported in patients with FA MDS and AML [9, 10]. Relative to healthy or FA BMF HPCs, FA MDS cells activate nuclear factor kappa-light-chain enhancer of B-cells (NF-κB) signaling to engage the compensatory HR DNA repair pathway. Inhibitors of innate inflammatory signaling sensitize FA MDS cells and FA pathway-deficient AML cells to genotoxins and impair viability. Our findings develop a human model of clonal evolution in IBMFS that we use to define mechanisms of pathologically increased HPC fitness in IBMFS-associated MDS and uncover a therapeutic approach to MDS in FA.

FA HSPCs undergo pronounced p53/p21-mediated cell cycle arrest in response to unresolved DNA damage, which leads to the clinical manifestation of bone marrow failure (BMF) [13]. Given the limitations of FA mouse models which for the most part do not manifest BMF or MDS/AML and the limited availability of primary human FA HSPCs, we have developed a model of human FA BMF using iPSCs with biallelic FANCA mutations engineered to express a complementing FANCA cDNA upon doxycycline exposure [14, 26, 27]. This enables maintenance of human FA iPSC lines with an intact FA pathway, which is required for induction and maintenance of pluripotency, with on-demand withdrawal of complementation upon hematopoietic differentiation to model the HSPC failure of FA [14, 26, 28]. This system captures hallmarks of human FA HSPCs including an impaired DNA damage response, p53/p21-mediated cell cycle arrest, and HPC exhaustion with accelerated terminal differentiation [14].

We hypothesized that the HPC failure occurring in this system could be reversed by somatic mutations that drive evolution to MDS, as there would be strong selective pressure for clones with mutation(s) promoting increased fitness and that activate aberrant self-renewal programs in this system. To test this, we performed multiplexed CRISPR/Cas9 editing in human iPSCs with an adapted guide RNA (gRNA) library targeting mutational hotspots in a panel of genes recurrently mutated in MDS, including in FA MDS and AML (Fig. 1A) [9, 10, 20]. We observed effective editing across the library at the targeted loci across multiple experiments in lines derived from two independent donors (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1A).

To examine effects on HPC fitness, we employed methylcellulose colony formation assays using HPCs derived from edited iPSCs. After 14 days, we scored colony morphology, finding that FANCA deficiency impaired primary myeloerythroid clonogenesis in unedited FANCA-deficient cells, as expected, relative to complemented (i.e., doxycycline-exposed), edited or unedited FANCA-expressing cells (Fig. 1C). Based on this result, we performed serial colony formation assays to measure self-renewal of the clonogenic progenitor state. Whereas unedited control HPCs exhausted by the secondary and tertiary rounds of replating, multiplex edited cells reproducibly showed serial replating capacity, yielding myeloid-skewed colonies, an effect which was most sustained in FANCA-deficient cells, and which was consistently observed across independent experiments and iPSC lines (Fig. 1C, Tables S1 and S2). We designate these myeloid-skewed, aberrantly serially renewing HPCs as a model of low-grade MDS (MDS-low).

Since these results indicated that engineered ‘somatic’ mutations/indels reversed HPC dysfunction in FA and caused aberrant self-renewal of the clonogenic HPC state, we tracked edited variant alleles through serial colony forming assays. Across multiple experiments, we observed reproducible enrichment for variant RUNX1, BCORL1, TET2, and TP53 alleles in serially self-renewing colonies, advancing these indels as candidate drivers of aberrant HPC renewal (Fig. S1A, B). We observed that specific mutant alleles at these loci were progressively enriched in HPCs with sustained self-renewal consistent with oligoclonal outgrowth (Fig. 1D).

We next asked whether we could progress these multiplexed edited, serially self-renewing MDS-low cells further through the transformation process toward high-grade MDS by introducing activating oncogenes reported in FA MDS and AML – FLT3-ITD and KRASG12D [9, 10]. Introduction of oncogenes activated mitogenic signaling and enabled stable establishment of high-grade MDS-like cells in liquid culture with an immature myeloid morphology, expression of undifferentiated myeloid surface markers, a defined indel complement and impaired myeloid differentiation (Fig. 1E–H, Fig. S2A–D). These cells were consistent with a high-grade MDS model (MDS-high). MDS-low and MDS-high possessed the sporadic ability to engraft NSG or NSGS mice short-term at levels consistent with those reported previously with HPCs derived from iPSCs (Fig. S2E–F) [29].

We performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) across the normal, BMF, low- and high-grade MDS spectrum, observing transcriptional profiles specific to each state and loss of terminal erythroid signatures during the transition from BMF to MDS (Fig. 1I–J, Fig. S2G) [14]. Consistent with maintenance of the undifferentiated HPC state, FA MDS lost signatures of mature hematopoietic cells relative to FANCA-deficient HPCs (Fig. 1I) [30]. Together, these data indicate that this system captures the spectrum ranging from healthy hematopoiesis to FA BMF and low- and high-grade/in transformation MDS and suggest that somatic mutations can reverse the pronounced HSPC dysfunction of FA BMF to implement aberrant self-renewal programs.

We confirmed that FA MDS-high cells were highly proliferative relative to complemented or uncomplemented unedited FA HPCs (Fig. 2A–B). We hypothesized that this acquisition of dysregulated proliferation in FANCA-deficient HPCs could occur through three possible mechanisms: somatic reversion, abrogation of p53-driven cell cycle arrest, and/or recruitment of compensatory DNA repair pathways. To exclude the first possibility, we did not observe doxycycline-independent activation of FANCA expression or the ability to monoubiquitinylate FANCD2 in response to genotoxic stress in FA MDS-high cells, excluding somatic reversion or indirect activation of the FA core complex (Fig. 2C–D). By karyotype and copy number analysis, we observed acquisition of cytogenetic anomalies, most notably variable trisomy 1, which is recurrently reported in FA MDS and AML, as well as gain of 17q (Fig. 2E, Table S3) [10]. Notably, although these cells bore TP53 indels at allele frequencies consistent with heterozygosity, only a fraction of alleles were frameshifting, and there was no evidence of loss of heterozygosity at the copy number or protein levels (Fig. 2E–F, Table S3). FA MDS cells maintained the ability to stabilize p53 protein downstream of DNA damage and localize it to the nucleus but showed diminished levels of p21 protein compared to FANCA-deficient iPSCs and blunted p53 target gene expression (Fig. 2F–I, Fig. S3A–B). Consistent with previous reports, we found that mutant RUNX1 (G165S, used here as this recurrent mutation in the Runt domain is near the gRNA cleavage site used to generate MDS-low and -high cells) impaired p53 target gene expression when expressed in K562 cells engineered with p53 proficiency, providing a mechanism of checkpoint abrogation permissive of cell cycle progression mediated by RUNX1 indels in our FA MDS model and in FA patients (Fig. S3C–D) [24, 31].

Activation of compensatory DNA repair pathways can adaptively restore FA HPC fitness and has been proposed as a therapeutic approach to the BMF of FA, and so we hypothesized that such a mechanism could be pathologically co-opted to increase the fitness of FA MDS HPCs [32]. Supportive of this hypothesis, compared to complemented or uncomplemented FA HPCs, high-grade FA MDS cells showed marked mitomycin C (MMC) resistance (Fig. 3A). Moreover, by using immunofluorescence to quantify DNA lesions marked by phosphorylated histone H2-AX (γH2-AX), we found that FA MDS cells accumulated less HU-induced damage compared to HPCs on average (Fig. 3B–C, Fig. S3E). However, by gene expression analysis, we did not observe that FA MDS cells consistently increased the expression of transcripts functioning in various alternative DNA repair pathways, suggesting that the genotoxicity effect might be occurring through post-transcriptional mechanisms (Fig. 3D, Fig. S4A).

To further investigate alternative mechanisms indirectly enabling FA MDS to resist genotoxic stress, we compared the transcriptional profiles of failing FA HPCs to engineered low- or high-grade MDS cells. Although we and others have observed activation of inflammatory signatures in FA deficient HPCs compared to HPCs with an intact FA pathway [14, 33, 34], FA MDS displayed enhanced activation of several innate inflammatory signatures, including signatures of interferon-α/γ, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα), and NF-κB signaling (Fig. 4A). We found that several NF-κB target and inflammatory genes were activated in FA MDS relative to FANCA-complemented or deficient, unedited HPCs (Fig. 4B).

Innate inflammatory signaling has been implicated in the pathobiology of sporadic MDS, particularly aberrant activation of NF-κB signaling via interleukin-1 receptor associated kinases 1 and 4 (IRAK1/4) [35, 36]. IRAK1 and IRAK4 are regulators of cell proliferation and survival in myeloid malignancies [35, 37]. IRAK1 transcripts were increased in FA MDS-low and -high cells relative to unedited HPCs, while IRAK4 transcripts were increased in FANCA-deficient HPCs, as well as low- and high-grade MDS relative to complemented HPCs, suggesting that the inflammatory signatures activated upon progression to MDS are IRAK1-driven (Fig. 4C). By western blotting, we confirmed this increased IRAK1 expression in FA MDS-high compared to unedited HPCs and observed evidence of activation of NF-κB signaling including increased phosphorylation of p65, p38 MAP kinase, and IKKα/β in MDS-high cells driven by either FLT3-ITD or KRASG12D versus HPCs (Fig. 4D and Fig. S4B). To support these mechanistic studies and to reduce reliance on fastidious MDS-like cells, we developed a p53-proficient K562 model engineered with combinations of FANCA and RUNX1 mutations (Fig. S4C) [24]. In this K562 model, in iPSCs undergoing differentiation bearing RUNX1 truncations, as well in HPCs derived from iPSCs bearing a RUNX1 point mutation, mutation of RUNX1 was sufficient to increase IRAK1 and activate the NF-κB pathway (Fig. 4E, Fig. S4C–G). Activation of NF-κB signaling has been reported to post-transcriptionally stabilize the central HR component BRCA1, providing a potential mechanism of compensatory DNA repair [38, 39]. Accordingly, we found that BRCA1 protein accumulated in FA high-grade MDS cells and not unedited FANCA-deficient HPCs in response to HU treatment (Fig. 4F). Consistent with a connection between RUNX1 mutations and DNA repair, expression of mutant RUNX1 in FANCA-deficient HPCs was sufficient to confer MMC resistance (Fig. 4G). Together, these results indicate that innate immune signaling is activated in FA MDS relative to complemented or failing FA HPCs as a potential source of compensatory HR.

Aberrant activation of innate immune signaling has emerged as a therapeutic vulnerability in sporadic adult MDS and AML, and its activation in FA MDS could be therapeutically targeted by small molecules to minimize exposure to cytotoxic chemotherapy. To this end, we evaluated a small molecule inhibitor of UBE2N (UC-764864; UC-64), a key upstream mediator of leukemogenic innate immune signaling [25]. We found that UC-64 could effectively block phosphorylation of p65 and diminished BRCA1 protein in K562 cells with FANCA deletion without affecting BRCA1 transcript levels (Fig. 5A and Fig. S5A–C). UC-64 inhibited the clonogenesis of primary patient FA MDS cells and FA MDS-high cells derived from iPSCs with higher potency than in normal human HSPCs (Fig. 5B–D). Consistent with the observed decrease in BRCA1, in our FANCA-deficient, p53-proficient K562 model, UC-64 also sensitized FANCA deficient MDS cells to MMC, blunted the kinetics of DNA repair, and decreased HR-mediated repair of double strand breaks (Fig. 5E–F, J). In MDS-high cells, UC-64 decreased DNA damage-induced RAD51 focus formation as a marker of HR (Fig. 5G–I). Since transformed cells (both MDS-high cells and epidermal progenitors as an independent model) silenced the FANCA induction cassette, we could not directly compare the sensitivity of FANCA-expressing and -deficient MDS-high cells to UC-64 (Fig. S5D–E). Therefore, to further establish the therapeutic potential of targeting innate inflammatory signaling, we treated NSG mice bearing TP53-intact, FANCA-deficient, RUNX1 mutated K562 cells, with the related UBE2N inhibitor UC-764865 (UC-65) which is optimized for in vivo bioavailability, finding that this intervention diminished human chimerism within the bone marrow in two independent experiments with negligible effects on normal mouse hematopoiesis (Fig. 5K, Fig. S5F–H). Taken together, these findings provide a rationale for targeting innate inflammatory signaling in FA-associated myeloid malignancies.

Cancer risk is a well-known complication of FA due to the underlying defect in DNA repair [5]. Without allogeneic stem cell transplantation, approximately one third of FA patients will develop MDS or AML in the first three decades of life [40]. Risk of MDS and AML is a complication of many other IBMFSs, and these malignancies typically bear poor prognosis, highlighting the unmet need for their early detection [12, 18]. Therefore, both early recognition of commitment of HSPCs to myeloid transformation as well as more effective, non-genotoxic therapies are required to improve outcomes. A prerequisite for these objectives is better understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of IBMFS-associated MDS in a disease-relevant human model system. Since the discovery of its genetic basis, several mouse models of FA have been developed [27]. In many of these strains, spontaneous BMF does not occur, and mouse models of FA MDS have to date not been derived, highlighting the need for a human-based system [27, 34, 41].

Here, we leveraged our human iPSC-based model of FA BMF to investigate mechanisms of myeloid transformation in FA [14]. We approached this study with the premise that the fulminant p53/p21-driven quiescence signal typical of FA would provide positive selective pressure for HPC clones with combinations of engineered indels that promote their outgrowth [13, 14]. This feature of our model enabled an approach whereby a panel of candidate drivers of clonal evolution could be screened simultaneously [20]. This system contrasts with other human iPSC-based models of clonal hematopoiesis, MDS, and AML which sequentially engineer somatic mutations, or which reprogram patient MDS cells bearing defined sets of mutations [42, 43]. Since our system builds on a state of inherent HPC failure, we could readily track the emergence of HPC clones with pathologically increased fitness. Moreover, we can directly compare the properties of complemented, failing, and MDS-like cells in an otherwise isogenic background, mitigating effects of donor variability that limit studies of primary human cells [44].

In our model, we asked the crucial question as to how HPCs transition from a state of reduced to aberrantly increased fitness in IBMFSs. We found that this did not occur via genetic reversion as has been reported as an adaptive mechanism of rescuing FA HSPC function [8, 45]. Since the HSPC dysfunction of FA must be overcome for expansion of diseased progenitors in MDS, this must occur via modification of DNA repair pathways and/or defusing of the downstream p53/p21-mediated G1/S cell cycle checkpoint [13, 46, 47]. We observe that FA AML cells blunt the p53/p21 response likely via the activity of mutant RUNX1, which is recurrently mutated in FA MDS (while TP53 is perplexingly not) consistent with indirect mechanisms of p53 inhibition [9, 10, 31]. A limitation of our model is that we do not capture the roles of recurrent chromosomal anomalies in MDS pathogenesis, though the common anomaly of trisomy 1 occurs spontaneously in our FA MDS cells, which may also be important for MDS development [48]. It is therefore challenging to define the relative roles of point mutations and cytogenetic abnormalities in maintaining FA MDS using our system. We also reproducibly recovered indels in BCORL1 and TET2. This observation compared with sequencing data from primary patient FA MDS cells would suggest that dysregulation of chromatin structure may play a central role in the pathogenesis of FA MDS (as also occurs in sporadic MDS) and should be a focus of future study [9, 49]. More patient-based data on the timing of point mutations in the clonal evolution process will define the point at which RUNX1 mutations occur to blunt p53-driven HSPC quiescence.

Activation of innate immune signaling occurs in sporadic MDS and may be a therapeutic vulnerability, a process triggered by aberrant IRAK1 or IRAK4 signaling [35, 50, 51]. FA BMF is associated with activation of inflammatory gene expression signatures, and we find that these gene expression programs are further activated upon evolution to MDS [14, 33, 34]. Mutations in RUNX1 can activate innate inflammatory signaling in neutrophils and mouse HSPCs, as we have now reported in human HPCs [52, 53]. We find that inhibition of the NF-κB pathway by using small molecules working via independent mechanisms inhibited the growth and viability of FA MDS cells – presumably due to sensitization to endogenous genotoxic byproducts such as aldehydes - and diminished markers of compensatory HR and sensitized cells to exogenous genotoxicity. Since NF-κB signaling can activate compensatory HR to promote the fitness of FANCA-deficient cells, we conclude that inhibition of this pathway sensitizes FA MDS cells to endogenous and exogenous genotoxic stress. This finding falls in line with the precedent that downstream activation of the HR pathway can partially rescue cellular defects related to FA deficiency as a potential therapeutic approach [32]. However, in our system, this is maladaptive as it is coupled with unrestrained proliferation due to the action of oncogenes as well as effects of mutant RUNX1 on the G1/S checkpoint and myeloid differentiation blockade locking cells into a proliferative progenitor state. Since BRCA1 is likely epistatic to the FA core complex, the precise mechanism by which this compensation occurs remains unclear. Interestingly, in the non-FA setting, both RUNX1 translocations and alleles with dominant acting point mutations have been reported to increase DNA damage via inhibition of p53 signaling, though effects on HR were not implicated in these studies [54–56]. Future work will focus on understanding RUNX1’s regulation of DNA damage response pathways in healthy and FA HSPCs (where its roles may differ in the background of high levels of baseline genotoxicity) to differentiate the relative effects of translocations, dominant missense mutations, and null alleles. Moreover, RUNX1 has been suggested to interact with FA proteins to promote recruitment of monoubiquitylated FANCD2 to sites of DNA damage [57, 58]. It would be interesting to determine how this mechanism is perturbed in the absence of FANCD2 monoubiquitylation during clonal evolution in FA. Since a portion of our mechanistic work required engineered K562 AML cell lines due to the limited growth potential of primary and iPSC-derived MDS cells, more work is required to optimize models of human FA MDS and AML.

Overall, we find that engineering of somatic mutations/indels in a human iPSC-based model of FA BMF could recapitulate transformation to a high-grade MDS-like state. Since we implicate RUNX1 mutations in both blunting of the G1/S checkpoint and activation of innate inflammatory signaling, this aligns with the high prevalence of RUNX1 mutations in FA patients with FA MDS and AML (Fig. 5L). We expect that our findings can be translated to other forms of IBMFSs that bear a heightened risk of myeloid transformation to gain understanding of the stem cell biology underlying this process. This renewable, individualized human-based system can continue to serve as a platform for therapeutic discovery.
