Established Facts

Methylation disorders are all very rare and affect primarily the brain, muscles, and liver.

SAHH deficiency is multisystemic and antenatal onset.

Prominent elevation of AdoMet and AdoHcy is essential marker for diagnosis. However, they are not available in routine. Methionine and homocysteine elevation without other amino acids could be helpful for diagnose.

A methionine-restricted diet, creatine, and phosphatidyl choline supplements do not appear to be a curative or completely preventive treatment option. More studies about liver transplantation are needed.

Novel Insights

We present an 18-month-old female patient who had a novel pathogenic variant in the AHCY gene and dysmorphic findings which was previously not stated with SAHH deficiency.

Introduction

Methionine is an essential amino acid and is converted to homocysteine with transmethylation processes. Enzyme deficiencies in the transmethylation processes are defined methylation disorders. Currently, four methylation disorders have been described. The 4 disorders are methionine adenosyltransferase I/III (MAT I/III) deficiency, glycine-N-methyltransferase deficiency (GNMT), S-adenosylhomocysteine hydrolase (SAHH) deficiency and adenosine kinase (AK) deficiency. Methylation disorders are all very rare and affect primarily the brain, muscles, and liver [1]. SAHH deficiency and GNMT deficiency are even rarer within these methylation disorders. There have been 19 SAHH deficiency cases have been reported so far [2–5]. Dysmorphic findings have been well described only in AK deficiency in methylation disorders. AK deficiency is the first disease to be considered in the differential diagnosis of SAHH deficiency. However, it has been stated that the dysmorphic findings are in favor of AK deficiency [1]. Dysmorphic findings are not prominent, highlighted features and have not been reported in SAHH deficiency so far. Here, we describe a patient who is the 20th patient in the literature according to the best of our knowledge and also has prominent dysmorphic findings.

Case Presentation

An 18-month-old female patient was referred to our clinic due to cataract, elevated transaminase levels and coagulopathy. She was born to consanguineous parents at 35 gestational weeks by Cesarean section. Her birth weight was 1,730 g. Her father underwent cataract surgery. She had 3 healthy siblings. She had severe global developmental delay. She could not gain head control. She had no words. She had no eye contact. She had no response to her name being called and could not respond to commands. She required hospitalization after birth due to prematurity, tachypnea, and early neonatal sepsis. Cataract was noted at the age of 5 months and surgery was planned. She had her first seizure when she was 6 months old. Elevated transaminases and coagulopathy were noticed before cataract surgery. Her weight, height, and head circumference at 18 months old were 7 kg (SD, −3.25), 72 cm (SD, −2.77), and 40 cm (SD, −5.37), respectively. Bilateral cataracts, hepatomegaly, poor head control, hypotonia, and dysmorphic findings (Fig. 1) including to following microcephaly, low anterior hairline, synophrys, hypertelorism, epicanthus, downslanting palpebral fissures, long eyelashes, depressed nasal bridge, long and smooth philtrum, thin lips, abnormal teeth, low set ears, short neck, tapering fingers, fifth toe clinodactyly, prominent heels were noted in her physical examination. Tendon reflexes could not be elicited. In laboratory investigations complete blood count, electrolytes, alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, bilirubin, basic urine tests, blood gases, thyroid functions, lipid profile, ammonia, ferritin, and vitamin values including B12, folate, and 25-OH vitamin D were normal. Serum aminotransferase levels had been elevated since the age of 6 months. Aminotransferase levels ​​ before age of 6 months were normal. Alanine aminotransferase levels and aspartate aminotransferase levels were between 60 and 304 U/L (N: 10–37 U/L) and 81–501 U/L (N: 10–40 U/L), respectively. Creatine kinase (CK) levels were usually normal but were high at three times (330–523–679 U/L; N: 40–170 UL/L). Albumin level was slightly low (32 g/dL; N: 38–54 g/dL). Prominent prolongation of prothrombin time (2.1–2.9 INR; N: 0.8–1.2 INR), normal activated partial thromboplastin time (25.4–36.9 s), an elevated D-dimer concentration (700–800 ng/mL; N: 0–500 ng/mL) and decreased fibrinogen concentration (100–150 mg/dL; N: 238–498 mg/dL) were revealed in coagulation tests. Viral serology, autoimmune hepatitis markers, and celiac antibodies were negative. Alpha-1-antitrypsin level and ceruloplasmin levels were in normal range. In her inherited metabolic disease evaluation, galactosemia, and tyrosinemia were excluded with galactose-1-phosphate uridyl transferase activity (18.03 U/g Hb; N >3.00 U/g Hb) and blood succinylacetone screen (0.26 μm; N <5.42 μm). Urinary organic acid analyses by gas-chromatography mass-spectroscopy, acylcarnitine analysis, very long chain fatty acid analysis, pristanic, and phytanic acids were normal. In blood amino acid analysis, methionine (422–833–847 umol/L; N: 8–54 umol/L) levels were significantly elevated, homocysteine levels (18–22.4 umol/L; N: 3–10 umol/L) were mildly elevated. Apart from these, other amino acid levels were in normal range. S-adenosylhomocysteine (AdoHcy), and S-adenosylmethionine (AdoMet) could not be performed. Auditory brainstem response was normal. Echocardiogram was normal. Cavum septum pellucidum et vergae, asymmetric mild ventriculomegaly, and periventricular gliosis were detected by brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Her karyotype and array-CGH were normal. Singleton whole-exome sequencing was performed via DNBSEQ-G400 (MGI Tech Co., Ltd.) at a depth of 100X. Data were analyzed using Genomize SEQ platform (https://seq.genomize.com). All nonsynonymous, no-stop, no-start, indels, missense, nonsense, frameshift, splice-site, and in-frame variants and rare variants with minor allele frequency < 0.1% were filtered. Retained variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria [6]. Segregation analysis was also performed with DNBSEQ-G400 (MGI Tech Co., Ltd.). Homozygous c.1043G>T (p.Gly348Val) novel variant in the AHCY gene (NM_000687.4) and heterozygous c.431A>G (p.Tyr144Cys) novel variant in the PITX3 gene (NM_005029) were detected (Fig. 2). Parental segregation analysis revealed that her mother and her father were heterozygous carriers for the c.1043G>T (p.Gly348Val) novel variant in the AHCY gene and her father also had heterozygous c.431A>G (p.Tyr144Cys) variant in the PITX3 gene (Fig. 2). No other variant potentially associated with the patient’s clinic was detected. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria c.1043G>T (p.Gly348Val) variant in the AHCY gene and the c.431A>G (p.Tyr144Cys) variant in the PITX3 gene were evaluated as “Likely Pathogenic” (PP1: cosegregation, PP3: computational prediction tools unanimously support a deleterious effect, PP4: phenotype, PM2: extremely low frequency in gnomAD population databases). This dual molecular diagnosis (AHCY-PITX3) was consistent with the patient's phenotype. She was diagnosed with SAHH deficiency. She is 3 years old and has been followed with hepatomegaly, hypotonia, global developmental delay, and persistent dysmorphic features. We advised methionine-restricted diet, phosphatidylcholine, and creatine supplements. However, the family did not comply with the treatment.

Discussions

The first SAHH deficiency was described by Baric et al. [7] in 2004. SAHH plays a role in converting AdoHcy to homocysteine and adenosine. Although the pathogenesis of SAHH deficiency is not very well known, it is thought that accumulated AdoHcy is responsible for the clinical features. AdoHcy inhibits methyltransferase reactions such as DNA-RNA methylation, creatine, phosphatidylcholine, sarcosine, and neurotransmitter synthesis. AHCY is encoded by the AHCY gene which is located on chromosome 20q11.22 and SAHH deficiency is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait [1, 2, 4, 7–10]. SAHH deficiency may resemble other inherited metabolic disease such as congenital disorders of glycosylation, mitochondrial diseases or peroxisomal disorders because of multisystemic involvement [11]. SAHH deficiency is characterized in various combinations by fetal hydrops, hypotonia, myopathy, joint contractures, global developmental delay, intellectual disability, microcephaly, central nervous system abnormalities, hyporeflexia, strabismus, growth failure, respiratory distress, liver disease, coagulopathy, and elevated CK. Since neonatal problems and fetal hydrops have been reported in most patients, the disease is thought to have an antenatal onset [1, 2, 4, 7–12]. Our patient also had neonatal problems such as prematurity, tachypnea, and early neonatal sepsis. Frontal bossing, short stature, hypertelorism, strabismus, sparse hair, long and triangular face, slender hands and feet, and distorted teeth have been reported in AK deficiency which is the most important disease in the differential diagnosis of SAHH deficiency [1]. However, no dysmorphic findings except microcephaly and strabismus have been described in SAHH deficiency [1, 2, 7, 8]. Our patient had prominent dysmorphic findings such as microcephaly, low anterior hairline, synophrys, hypertelorism, epicanthus, downslanting palpebral fissures, long eyelashes, depressed nasal bridge, long and smooth philtrum, thin lips, abnormal teeth, low set ears, short neck, tapering fingers, fifth toe clinodactyly, and prominent heels. Although dysmorphic findings are better defined and more common in AK deficiency, dysmorphic findings can also be seen in SAHH deficiency. Our patient had significant dysmorphic findings. We could not find any other reason to explain these findings through genetic analysis.

Ocular findings other than strabismus were reported in a girl by Grudzinska Pechhacker et al. [3] She had vision loss, cataract, maculopathy, foveal schisis, and thickened retina. In addition to SAHH deficiency, as in our patient, a different genetic variant was shown to explain her ocular findings with a retinal dystrophy gene panel. The gene which was explained ocular findings revealed in the retinal dystrophy gene panel was Crumbs Cell Polarıty Complex Component 1 (CRB1). We thought that cataract finding in our patient was explained by the genetic variant in the PITX3 gene. In the literature neurodevelopmental abnormalities have also been associated with PITX3 variants [13]. Our patient’s father also had the same PITX3 variant and was neurologically normal. Therefore, we think that the neurodevelopmental problems in our patient cannot be explained by PITX3 variant. However, prematurity and neonatal problems may have contributed to neurodevelopmental problems.

Hepatocellular carcinoma is also reported in SAHH deficiency. Furthermore, carriers of SAHH deficiency have a risk for hepatocellular carcinoma. Therefore, patients and their families should be followed up for hepatocellular cancer [14].

Prominent elevation of AdoMet and AdoHcy are essential markers for diagnosis. AdoMet and AdoHcy have been elevated since infancy. Unfortunately, it is not always possible for AdoMet and AdoHcy to perform. Methionine and homocysteine levels are normal at birth and increases over time. But the elevation in homocysteine level is not as prominent as the elevation in methionine level [1, 2, 4]. Our patient had significant methionine elevation and mild homocysteine elevation. Unfortunately, we could not perform AdoHcy and AdoMet in our patient.

There is no sensitive screening test available for newborn screening [1]. Only 33% of the patients had high methionine levels at the initial evaluation that occurred within the first 2 months of life [4]. SAHH deficiency could be easily missed by methionine-based newborn screening. Therefore, newborn screening is currently not possible without being performed by AdoHcy and AdoMet. Furthermore, due to its rarity and lack of clear treatment, the disease is not suitable for newborn screening.

Methionine could be elevated in liver disease, premature birth, and protein-rich diet. Among inherited metabolic diseases, citrine deficiency, tyrosinemia type 1 and mitochondrial depletion syndromes such as deoxyguanosine kinase and mitochondrial inner membrane protein 17 could cause elevated methionine secondary to generalized hepatic dysfunction. However in these disease, elevated methionine is usually accompanied by elevated tyrosine. If methionine is elevated and tyrosine is within normal ranges, methylation disorders and cystathionine beta-synthase deficiency should be considered [12].

In SAHH deficient patients’ MRI white matter atrophy, abnormal myelination, pontine-cerebellar hypoplasia and hypoplastic corpus callosum have been reported. Normal brain MRI has also been reported [2, 9].

A high index of suspicion is required for diagnosis. In clinically suspected patients, methylation disorders should be kept in mind if there are elevated transaminase, elevated CK, coagulopathy, methionine, and homocysteine (without other amino acids elevation) elevation. The diagnosis could be confirmed by enzyme analysis or molecular genetic analysis. A methionine-restricted diet is recommended to decrease AdoMet and AdoHcy. Elevated methionine also competes with other large neutral amino acids (leucine, valine, isoleucine, tryptophan, threonine, tyrosine, phenylalanine, histidine, and glutamine) to pass the blood-brain barrier decreasing other amino acids passing to the brain. Creatine and choline could be also deficient in these patients because their syntheses are methylation-dependent. In addition to a methionine-restricted diet, creatine and phosphatidyl choline supplements are also given in the treatment. A methionine-restricted diet helps decrease plasma levels of AdoMet and AdoHcy, even normalizes. While an improvement in muscle strength, mental alertness and liver function tests have been reported with a methionine-restricted diet, there were no long-term follow-up results. Possible reason for treatment failure is the multisystem involvement and antenatal onset [1, 2, 4, 7–9, 11, 12]. Liver transplantation was performed in a girl at 40 months of age, based on the idea that the SAHH enzyme is mostly synthesized in the liver. Both clinical and laboratory improvement was reported in that patient after liver transplantation. More studies about liver transplantation are needed [14].

Methylation disorders may be more common than expected. Also dysmorphic findings have not been highlighted so far except in AK deficiency in methylation disorders. With the increasing use of whole-exome sequencing and genetic panels, patients with SAHH deficiency will be diagnosed more frequently. Thus, different findings and phenotypes of the SAHH deficiency will possibly be revealed. We believe that the novel variant and the new dysmorphic findings reported in this study contribute to the literature both genotypically and phenotypically.
