# Genome-wide analysis of spina bifida risk variants in a case-control study from Bangladesh.
**Authors:** Tindula G, Issac B, Mukherjee SK, Ekramullah SM, Arman DM, Islam J, Suchanda HS, Sun L, Rockowitz S, Christiani DC, Warf BC, Mazumdar M
**Journal:** Birth Defects Res (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/bdr2.2331](https://doi.org/10.1002/bdr2.2331)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Human studies of genetic risk factors for neural tube defects, severe birth defects associated with long-term health consequences in surviving children, have predominantly been restricted to a subset of candidate genes in specific biological pathways including folate metabolism.

METHODS: In this study, we investigated the association of genetic variants spanning the genome with risk of spina bifida (i.e., myelomeningocele and meningocele) in a subset of families enrolled from December 2016 through December 2022 in a case-control study in Bangladesh, a population often underrepresented in genetic studies. Saliva DNA samples were analyzed using the Illumina Global Screening Array. We performed genetic association analyses to compare allele frequencies between 112 case and 121 control children, 272 mothers, and 128 trios.

RESULTS: In the transmission disequilibrium test analyses with trios only, we identified three novel exonic spina bifida risk loci, including rs140199800 (SULT1C2, p = 1.9 × 10-7), rs45580033 (ASB2, p = 4.2 × 10-10), and rs75426652 (LHPP, p = 7.2 × 10-14), after adjusting for multiple hypothesis testing. Association analyses comparing cases and controls, as well as models that included their mothers, did not identify genome-wide significant variants.

CONCLUSIONS: This study identified three novel single nucleotide polymorphisms involved in biological pathways not previously associated with neural tube defects. The study warrants replication in larger groups to validate findings and to inform targeted prevention strategies.

Neural tube defects are common and often severe congenital malformations that occur when the neural tube, the embryonic precursor to the brain and spinal cord, fails to close during the first month of gestation. Estimates of neural tube defect prevalence vary widely and range from 0.3 to 199.4 per 10,000 births (Zaganjor et al., 2016). The severity of the neural tube defect is largely a function of position (Molloy et al., 2017). Cranial neural tube defects, including craniorachischisis and anencephaly, result in infant death (Molloy et al., 2017), while individuals with spina bifida may survive, albeit many individuals experience life-long health consequences such as neurological impairment and gastrointestinal disorders (Greene & Copp, 2014).

Determinants of neural tube defects include a combination of environmental, nutritional, and genetic risk factors (Greene & Copp, 2014; Harris & Juriloff, 2007). Over the past several decades, multiple studies have demonstrated that periconceptional folic acid intake, either through food fortification or supplementation, can reduce neural tube defect risk (Blencowe et al., 2010; Crider et al., 2022). However, the large variation in neural tube defect prevalence is not solely attributable to folic-acid based prevention strategies, and affected pregnancies occur in regions with ubiquitous folic acid fortification and to women known to have taken folic acid supplements (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2010). Possible explanations for the underperformance of folic acid fortification include genetic variation (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2010).

Animal and human studies have identified genetic variants that confer altered risk for neural tube defects and have been the focus of many reviews (Finnell et al., 2021; Greene et al., 2009; Lupo et al., 2017; Molloy et al., 2017; Wolujewicz & Ross, 2019; Wolujewicz et al., 2021). Genes associated with neural tube defect risk in animal models are involved in diverse signaling pathways such as the noncanonical Wnt/planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway, sonic hedgehog (SHH) signaling, epigenetic modifications, and maintenance of the cell cycle (Chen et al., 2018; Greene & Copp, 2014; Greene et al., 2009; Webber et al., 2015; Wolujewicz & Ross, 2019; Wolujewicz et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2019). Human studies suggest a genetic component that is multifactorial, involving polygenic or oligogenic models (Greene & Copp, 2014; Harris & Juriloff, 2007). Various candidate gene studies of neural tube defects in humans have examined one or a few genes (Finnell et al., 2021), particularly those that are orthologs of risk genes in mice or genes involved in folate pathways (Greene & Copp, 2014). Biological pathways examined in candidate gene studies of neural tube defects involve the non-canonical Wnt/PCP and SHH signaling pathways (Wolujewicz & Ross, 2019), similar to the animal genetic models of neural tube defects.

Genetic association studies, including case-control and child-parent trio designs, have been implemented to explore the relationship between genetic variants and neural tube defect risk in humans. Case-control genetic association studies compare the frequency of genetic variants between cases and controls and are helpful in studies of rare outcomes, including NTDs (Lupo et al., 2017). Trio designs, which are suited to studying health outcomes that develop early in life (Lupo et al., 2017), can be used to detect de novo mutations and permit assessment using the transmission disequilibrium test to determine if alleles are over-transmitted from parents to case children (Wolujewicz et al., 2021). Differences in allele frequencies for specific genetic variants have been observed in study populations from Bangladesh related to various diseases or health outcomes, including autism spectrum disorder (Azmerin et al., 2022), type 2 diabetes mellitus (Barman et al., 2023), Guillain-Barré syndrome (Jahan et al., 2019), and skin lesion risk (Pierce et al., 2019).

We previously performed candidate gene analysis to examine the interactions between polymorphisms in folate pathway genes and drinking water arsenic, and the influence of these interactions on spina bifida risk, in a separate, pilot study conducted in Bangladesh between April and November of 2013 (Mazumdar et al., 2015). Among 54 mothers of children diagnosed with myelomeningocele and 55 control mothers, we observed that water arsenic was significantly associated with increased risk of myelomeningocele in participants with 4 single nucleotide polymorphisms in genes involved in folate metabolism (MTHFD1, HGNC:7432), folate transport (SLC19A1, HGNC:10937), arsenic metabolism (AS3MT, HGNC:17452), and DNA methylation (DNMT3A, HGNC:2978). In the current study, we build upon this previous analysis, performing a genome-wide scan of variants to assess genetic contributors to neural tube defect risk in a larger case-control population. The genetic association analysis includes data from child-parent trios, allowing us to explore family-based, genome-wide association analyses in a unique study population with myelomeningocele and meningocele, subtypes of spina bifida, from Bangladesh, a country with relatively high prevalence of neural tube defects with a population that is underrepresented in genome-wide association studies (Gurdasani et al., 2019).

From December 2016 through December 2022, we enrolled cases, controls, and their parents in a case-control study based in Bangladesh (Tindula et al., 2021). A total of 321 case and 177 control children were enrolled over the study period. The primary aim of the study was to assess the relationship between environmental arsenic exposure and spina bifida risk, and the details regarding recruitment and evaluation have been previously published (Tindula et al., 2021). Cases included children diagnosed with myelomeningocele or meningocele who presented for evaluation at the National Institute of Neurosciences & Hospital (NINS). Cases were eligible to enroll in the study if they were diagnosed with myelomeningocele or meningocele following examination by a physician, were less than a year old, received medical care from NINS, and if parents were able to identify the primary water source used during early pregnancy. Neurosurgeons at NINS further classified cases based on examination, observations during surgery, and review of neuroimaging studies.

Controls were matched on age (±6 months) and were selected from individuals who presented to NINS or the adjacent children’s hospital, Dhaka Shishu Hospital (DSH), which has a similar catchment area to NINS. Control children were excluded from the main study if the child was diagnosed with cancer or a condition considered to be related to arsenic exposure (i.e. arsenic-related keratosis). Five children, two fathers, and three mothers included in at least one of the analysis models of the current study belonged to a case family and were later reassigned to be categorized as control families after surgery on the child revealed an updated diagnosis of lipomeningocele. We included individuals with lipomeningocele, which is characterized by fatty tissue connected to the spinal cord (Agopian et al., 2012), and their families in the control group as this defect is believed to occur at different developmental stages than myelomeningocele and meningocele (Agopian et al., 2012; Chan et al., 2017).

A subset of individuals from the case-control study had saliva DNA of sufficient quality and were included in the genetic analyses (Figure 1). The Bangladesh Medical Research Council and the Human Research Committees at Boston Children’s Hospital and NINS approved all study protocols, and informed consent was obtained from parents prior to enrollment.

The study involved a hospital visit that included a physical examination of the child and their parents, the administration of questionnaires, and the collection of biological samples. Trained study staff collected saliva samples from children and their parents using the Oragene® OGR-575 kit (DNA Genotek Inc., Ottawa, ON, Canada), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Saliva samples were shaken for 5 seconds and stored at room temperature until shipped to Boston for long-term storage and DNA extraction.

DNA extraction of saliva samples from children and their parents was performed using the Maxwell® RSC Buccal Swab DNA kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Seven hundred sixty saliva DNA samples were shipped to the Broad Institute (Cambridge, MA, USA) for DNA quantification and genetic analysis (Figure 1). These samples represented 723 unique individuals and a total of 37 repeat samples. Samples were randomized across chips to prevent batch effects. One HapMap control was included for every processing plate that comprised 95 samples.

Saliva DNA samples were quantified using picogreen technology. Of the 760 samples sent for analysis, four did not have sufficient quantities of DNA and were excluded from the genetic analysis. Samples with sufficient quantity and quality of DNA were analyzed using the Illumina Global Screening Array (GSA; GSAMD-24v1-0_20011747_A1 v1.11.2; Illumina, San Diego, CA). The GSA chip can accommodate 24 samples and includes an array of 688,032 total markers. In addition to delivering the raw and called genotype files, the laboratory provided a final data quality report, detailing sample failures. Following GSA analysis, an additional seven samples failed quality control (QC) requirements due to call rates less than 97% and/or as a result of a sex mismatch. Of the remaining 749 saliva samples with GSA data, 36 duplicates were removed from further analysis.

Genetic data processing and quality checks followed standard protocols. Specifically, genotypes were called against GRCh37/hg19 reference and made available for downstream analysis as VCFv4.2 format files. First, genotypes were converted to PLINK PED format through PLINK software using the --vcf parameter (Purcell et al., 2007). Data from all samples available after filtering for duplicates and failed samples were then merged into a single multi-sample PED file for GWAS analysis. Initial QC steps for PLINK were performed as per default for the software. Sample and variant markers were examined for missing data using PLINK. All samples passed the missingness test. However, 6,806 variants with more than 10% missing data were excluded from further analysis. Next, sex for each sample was cross-checked using the --check-sex parameter in PLINK and one sample with a sex mismatch was filtered after verification. Variants from only autosomes were retained for next steps in GWAS analysis. Minor allele frequency (MAF) was computed for each variant using PLINK and 256,304 variants with MAF < 0.01 were excluded from subsequent analysis. Next, the Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium test (--hwe 0.05) was performed to also exclude variants. Deviations from Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium can result from a variety of factors including genotyping errors, inbreeding, population substructure, purifying selection, and copy number variation (Chen et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2008; Wang & Shete, 2017). Pairwise inbreeding coefficients (IBS/IBD estimation) were performed on the remaining 386,800 variants to determine relatedness between samples. This estimation resulted in the removal of two parental samples with family relationship statuses that did not match what was reported, and one additional child’s sample was removed due to subsequent unavailability of parental information. After the four individuals were excluded following data processing QC (i.e., one due to a sex mismatch and three as a result of relatedness) and data from fathers not included in a trio were excluded (46 fathers of cases and 30 fathers of control children), the final count of participants in the statistical analyses included 233 children (112 cases diagnosed with myelomeningocele or meningocele and 121 controls), 272 mothers (131 mothers of cases and 141 mothers of controls), and 128 fathers (65 fathers of cases and 63 fathers of controls) (Figure 1).

Descriptive statistics were generated to examine study participant characteristics. Data for cases and controls were initially compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests for binary and categorical variables. Standard case/control allelic association tests were performed using PLINK software (Purcell et al., 2007) to determine the relationship between genetic variants and spina bifida case status. The three models include samples that are from mothers-only (N=272), children-only (N=233), and families where all trio members (both parents and children) were available (counts: families=128, individuals=384) (Table 1). Missingness, MAF and HWE were recomputed for each of the three models prior to association tests. Next, models including 1) mothers-only or 2) children-only were assessed with standard association (--assoc) one degree of freedom chi-square allelic tests to identify significant variants. In the mothers-only and children-only models, 372,963 and 354,095 variants were assessed, respectively. For family-based association, standard transmission disequilibrium chi-square tests (TDT; --tdt) were performed on trio samples for 370,617 variants. For each of the three test models, significant variants were identified with the nominal p-value threshold < 5E-7 (Chen et al., 2021). These significant variants were then annotated using the ANNOVAR software (Wang et al., 2010) against the hg19 version Ensembl human gene model (Martin et al., 2023). The effect of amino acid substitutions in the significant variants from the models was predicted using the PolyPhen-2 Web tool (Adzhubei et al., 2013), with a potential prediction outcome of probably damaging, possibly damaging, or benign. Visualization of significant single nucleotide polymorphisms from GWAS analysis was done using Manhattan plots (qqman (Turner, 2018) package in R).

All analyses were performed using version 17.0 of STATA (StataCorp, 2021), version 4.2.2 of the R statistical computing software (R Core Team, 2022), and version 1.90b3.40 of PLINK (Purcell et al., 2007).

Of the 112 children with spina bifida (cases) included in the study, 87 were diagnosed with myelomeningocele and 25 with meningocele (Table 1). On average, case children were younger than controls (mean: cases = 55.1 days, controls = 178.6 days; p-value <0.0001). Among the mothers included in the association analysis, there were no significant differences in measured serum folate among mothers of cases and controls (mean: cases = 20.5 nmol/L, controls = 17.9 nmol/L; p-value = 0.06). Mothers of controls were more likely to report giving birth to their first child compared to mothers of cases (52% of control mothers and 38% of case mothers; p-value = 0.02). Both maternal and paternal educational attainment differed significantly between parents of cases and controls (mothers: p-value = 0.007, association model; fathers: p-value = 0.009, TDT model). For example, 37% of mothers and 35% of fathers of controls had either a college or university level education, compared to the 18% of mothers and 17% of fathers of cases with the same level of education.

The participants included in the case/control allelic association analyses (models: standard association and/or transmission disequilibrium chi-square tests; count: children = 233, mothers = 272, fathers = 128) did not differ significantly from individuals that were excluded (count: children = 265, mothers = 226, fathers = 370) because they 1) did not have saliva DNA assessed with the GSA technology or 2) were fathers with GSA data who were not a part of a trio, with the exception of a higher percentage of mothers giving birth to their first child among the mothers included in the study (first births in included mothers = 45%, excluded mothers = 35%, p-value = 0.02).

Analyses were performed for the following comparisons: 1) case (individuals with myelomeningocele or meningocele) and control children (association analysis), 2) mothers of cases and controls (association analysis), and 3) trios only (transmission disequilibrium test analysis). In the children only analysis (Figure S1), none of the variants were significantly different between cases and controls at the genome-wide significance level (P-value < 5.0x10−7). This was similarly observed in the model comparing mothers of cases and controls (Figure S2). In the trios only TDT analysis, the following variants were significant at the genome-wide threshold: rs140199800 (risk allele = A, p = 1.9x10−7), rs45580033 (risk allele = A, p = 4.2x10−10), and rs75426652 (risk allele = A, p = 7.2x10−14) (Table 2, Figure 2). However, the odds ratios from the TDT analysis are difficult to interpret, given the distribution of the genotype frequencies in the study participants of the top variants; specifically, the "AA” genotype for the three variants is not found in any of the case or control children or in mothers or fathers for the rs45580033 and rs140199800 variants (Table 3). The results of the PolyPhen-2 analysis to predict the effect of amino acid substitutions in the significant variants from the TDT analysis on the function of human proteins are presented in Table S1. The variants rs75426652 and rs45580033 had predicted effects of amino acid changes that were benign and possibly damaging, respectively. The variant rs140199800 is a stop-gain exonic variant and did not yield a prediction result.

This study adds to the limited, but growing, research focusing on comprehensive scans of the genome, rather than examining a small subset of genes, for risk variants for spina bifida. In transmission disequilibrium test analysis including trios, we were able to identify novel variants associated with spina bifida risk, including rs140199800, rs45580033, and rs75426652. In the association analyses with children only or including mothers of cases and controls, we did not observe any significant genetic variants at the genome-wide significance level (P-value < 5.0x10−7).

Given the relationship observed between folic acid intake, either through folic acid fortification or supplementation, and reduced incidence of neural tube defects, human studies of genetic determinants of neural tube defects have focused on genes involved in folate pathways (Wolujewicz & Ross, 2019). Many studies have examined the role of variants in genes involved in folate and one-carbon metabolism in neural tube defect risk (Aranda-Sánchez et al., 2021; Cadenas-Benitez et al., 2014; Cai et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2018; Deb et al., 2011; Paul et al., 2018; Prasoona et al., 2016; van der Put et al., 1995; Wang et al., 2015; Yadav et al., 2015; Zheng et al., 2015), with findings often inconsistent across studies (Lupo et al., 2017). In our analysis including genetic variants spanning the genome, we did not observe significant differences in measured serum folate among mothers of cases and controls and did not identify any significant associations between genetic variants in genes relevant in folate pathways and case status in the child, maternal, or trio models.

In the TDT analyses including trios only, the rs45580033 variant was significant (p = 4.2 x10−10) at the genome-wide threshold. This nonsynonymous single-nucleotide variant is located on chromosome 14 in an exon of ASB2 (HGNC:16012), a gene with genetic variants previously explored in relation to cell composition (Astle et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2020; Kachuri et al., 2021; Vuckovic et al., 2020) and acute myeloid leukemia (Lv et al., 2017). Previous research has implicated ASB2 in defects of the heart. A study by Métais et al. (Métais et al., 2018) using Asb2 knockout embryos demonstrated that the absence of Asb2a, an isoform of Asb2, can lead to embryonic mortality in mice as a result of heart defects. Additionally, Yamak et al. (Yamak et al., 2020) found that Asb2 knockout in the first and second heart fields, which contribute to the developing heart, were embryonic lethal in mice. In addition to studies of the role of Asb2 in heart abnormalities, animal models have also observed a relationship between Asb2 and placental defects. Park et al. (Park et al., 2021) observed that Asb2 knockout mice had placental defects, inhibiting the formation of a functional cardiac septum and leading to embryonic lethality. Although our study is the first to our knowledge to report an association between a variant in the ASB2 gene and neural tube defect risk, this gene has previously been implicated in early developmental processes, specifically heart defects.

Another variant associated with spina bifida risk in the trio TDT model was rs75426652 (p = 7.2 x10−14). This nonsynonymous single-nucleotide variant lies within an exonic region of LHPP (HGNC:30042) on chromosome 10. Genetic variants in the LHPP gene have been associated with a wide array of diseases and phenotypes, commonly with major depressive disorder (CONVERGE consortium, 2015; Knowles et al., 2016; Neff et al., 2009; Yue et al., 2022) and specific subtypes of cancer (Lesseur et al., 2016; Vijayakrishnan et al., 2017). A study by Li et al. (Li et al., 2021) observed that LHPP overexpression inhibits the proliferation and invasion of glioblastoma cells and downregulates the activity of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. The canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, along with the sonic hedgehog and noncanonical WNT/planar cell polarity pathways, are critical in directing cell differentiation and convergent extension of the neural plate during neural tube closure (Wolujewicz & Ross, 2019). Follow-up analyses will be necessary to determine the effect of the alleles of rs75426652 on LHPP expression and subsequent impacts on downstream pathways, such as Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

The final variant that was significant at the genome-wide level in the trios-only analysis was rs140199800. Located on chromosome 2, rs140199800 is a stop-gain exonic variant located in SULT1C2 (HGNC:11456). The gene SULT1C2 belongs to a family of genes that encode sulfotransferase enzymes that catalyze the sulfate conjugation of endogenous and exogenous compounds, such as drugs and neurotransmitters (Iida et al., 2001; Weinshilboum et al., 1997). Genome-wide association studies have identified variants of SULT1C2 that are related to ear lobe size (Adhikari et al., 2015) and composite progression in Parkinson’s disease (Tan et al., 2021). To our knowledge, our study is the first to report an association between a variant in SULT1C2 and spina bifida risk.

Our study complements the recent trend of gene discovery analysis in neural tube defect research promoted by advances in cutting-edge next-generation sequencing technologies. Genome sequencing and exome sequencing allow for the genomic profiling of the small quantities of input DNA obtained from infants, with declining costs allowing more widespread utilization of the technologies (Ross et al., 2017). These methods have been used to identify novel and potential candidate genes for neural tube defects (Lemay et al., 2015; Singh et al., 2017; Spellicy et al., 2018), including spina bifida (Azzarà et al., 2021; Cao et al., 2020; Han et al., 2022; Lemay et al., 2017; Mohd-Zin et al., 2022; Shah et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2022), and allow for the identification of rare alleles in families (i.e., trios and quads, which include a trio and an unaffected sibling) (Lee & Gleeson, 2020). Our study is a contribution to the larger effort to consolidate data and samples to obtain the larger sample sizes needed to better characterize the genomic architecture underlying the risk of neural tube defects.

The main strength of the study includes access to trio data to explore family-based, genome-wide association analyses in a unique study population with spina bifida. We were able to identify novel variants outside of the traditionally explored biological pathways, such as folate metabolism. Another strength of the study involved exploring genetic profiles in individuals living in Bangladesh, a population underrepresented in genome-wide association studies, which have primarily been conducted in individuals with European ancestry (Gurdasani et al., 2019). As a result of the predominance of GWAS including European populations, risk alleles in GWAS databases skew towards those with high MAFs in populations with European ancestry (Gurdasani et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2017). Including diverse populations in GWAS and sampling from minority populations, such as the population from Bangladesh included in the current study, may detect novel variants related to disease risk due to improved power to identify associations for variants that may be more common in specific minority populations (Gurdasani et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2018). Due to the small sample size of the current study, we were not able to perform linkage analysis that could have provided greater insight into genomic regions affecting disease risk and to perform predictive statistical models that could indicate causal variants for disease. An additional limitation of the study involved the inclusion of children with lipomeningocele, another type of neural tube defect, and their families in the control group. We included families with children with lipomeningocele as controls because our study was primarily designed to investigate associations between arsenic and spina bifida (myelomeningocele and meningocele), and lipomeningocele would be present in the population that would give rise to cases. Upon determination of a diagnosis with lipomeningocele, these individuals were reclassified as controls. We did not exclude these individuals in our GWAS analysis because lipomeningoceles are considered to have different etiologies than that of myelomeningocele and meningocele, and also occur during a different developmental stage (Agopian et al., 2012; Chan et al., 2017). A challenge faced in conducting genomic research in the study population was a lack of a replication sample, preventing us from validating the current findings in another study population. We have shared biospecimens and data from the project with investigators focused on neural tube defect research and are planning to be involved in future genetic analyses across studies from multiple geographic areas.

Although we were not able to confirm previously identified associations between spina bifida risk and genetic variants restricted to certain biological pathways, such as folate metabolism, we were able to identify novel variants that may impact biological processes involved in early development. We hope to validate the findings from this study with future follow-up genetic studies in humans and animals through existing collaborations, and to explore how folate status and arsenic exposure potentially modify the relationship between genetics and spina bifida risk.
