# Clearance of p21 highly expressing senescent cells accelerates cutaneous wound healing.
**Authors:** Gasek NS, Yan P, Zhu J, Purushothaman KR, Kim T, Wang L, Wang B, Flynn WF, Sun M, Guo C, Huggins B, Sharafieh R, Zhou Y, Parizek V, Tchkonia T, Kirkland JL, Wyles SP, Xu M
**Journal:** Nat Aging (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s43587-024-00755-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43587-024-00755-4)

## Content

While senescent cells have detrimental roles in several contexts, they are highly heterogeneous. p16 highly expressing senescent cells have been reported to exert beneficial functions in wound healing. Here we use Xenium spatial transcriptomics to identify a distinct p21 highly expressing senescent population induced on wounding, with a pro-inflammatory profile. We find that clearing p21 highly expressing cells expedites wound closure and is partially mediated by NF-κB inhibition, thus enhancing our understanding of the multifaceted functions of senescence in tissue remodeling.

Cellular senescence is a stress-induced cell fate characterized by proliferative arrest and a pro-inflammatory senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)1. Cdkn1a (p21) and Cdkn2a (p16) are two major regulators and biomarkers for cellular senescence. Despite their detrimental roles in age-related conditions, p16 highly expressing (p16high) cells have a beneficial role in wound healing2,3. However, senescent cells exhibit substantial heterogeneity4, implying that p16high cells may only account for a fraction of the whole senescent cell population. We recently generated a p21Cre mouse model5, enabling us to track, eliminate or modulate p21 highly expressing (p21high) cells in vivo. p21high cells targeted by this model constitute 1.5–10% of cells in aged mice, but very few in young unstressed mice5. Clearance of p21high cells extends the lifespan and ameliorates age-related and obesity-related health issues5–7. p21high cells exhibit several key senescence features and are distinct from p16high cells in several contexts5,6,8, indicating potentially unique roles. In this Article, we leveraged p21Cre mice in an established dorsal cutaneous injury model to investigate the role of p21high cells in wound healing.

In line with previous work using the p16-3MR model2, we created full-thickness excisional wounds on the dorsum of young p21Cre/+ (P) mice using a 6-mm biopsy punch. After acute injury to the skin, p21 mRNA and protein levels were induced 1 day after injury (Extended Data Fig. 1a–c). We crossed the p21Cre mice with floxed knock-in tdTomato (tdT) mice and generated p21Cre/+; tdT/+ (PT) mice (Fig. 1a), allowing visualization of p21high cells by tdT at single-cell resolution. We found accumulation of tdT+ cells in wounded areas at 2 and 6 days after injury (Fig. 1b,c). We performed Xenium in situ profiling on 209,937 cells from wounded and unwounded tissues and identified 11 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 2a–c), including key populations involved in the wound healing process9 such as fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). We observed an enrichment of immune cell populations, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells in wounded tissues, whereas alternatively activated macrophages10 decreased (Extended Data Fig. 3b). We were also able to identify p21high cells by the presence of tdT transcripts (Fig. 1e–g and Extended Data Fig. 4a), as after tamoxifen (TAM) administration, tdT should be continuously and exclusively expressed in p21high cells. Both the number and ratio of tdT+ cells, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, granulocytes and lymphoid cells were increased in wounded PT compared to unwounded PT mice (Fig. 1f–h). Although present across cell types, the predominant populations of p21high cells were fibroblasts, endothelial cells, M1 macrophages, keratinocytes, granulocytes and lymphocytes (Fig. 1i). In contrast, p16high (p16+) cells constituted 0.5% of cells in the wounds and were primarily fibroblasts and endothelial cells (Fig. 1j,k and Extended Data Fig. 4a,b), which is consistent with previous work2. We found that p21high cells were primarily concentrated within the wound margins and granulation tissue (Fig. 1e). Notably, p21high keratinocytes were not induced by wounding (Fig. 1f,g and Extended Data Fig. 3a). Additionally, most keratinocytes located around the wound edge were tdT− (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 3b), indicating that they were in the active proliferative phase, which aids in wound re-epithelialization. tdT+ keratinocytes probably represent the activation of endogenous p21 in the hair bulb11.

We next determined differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between p21high (tdT+) and non-p21high (tdT−) cells. Among the four prevalent cell types, p21 transcripts were highly enriched in tdT+
p21high cells, which we confirmed using immunostaining (Fig. 1l and Extended Data Fig. 5a–c). However, p16 expression was not significantly different between tdT+ and tdT− cells (Fig. 1l), underscoring the distinction between p21high and p16high cells. Compared to tdT− cells, genes involved in aspects of wound healing, including inflammation (Ccl2, Cxcl12, Tnfaip6), tissue repair and remodeling12 (Tgfb1, Pdgfa and matrix metalloproteinases) were upregulated in tdT+ cells across multiple cell types (Fig. 1l). Conversely, the Mmp2 inhibitor Pi16 (ref. 13) was downregulated in tdT+ fibroblasts. Moreover, wound healing-promoting genes such as Serpina3n14 and Ccl6 (ref. 15) were downregulated in tdT+ fibroblasts, while genes associated with delayed wound healing, including S100a4 (ref. 16), Cav1 (ref. 17) and Sdc1 (ref. 18) were upregulated in tdT+ cells (Fig. 1l). We did not observe enrichment of genes related to inflammation and tissue remodeling in p16+ cells compared to p16− cells for most cell types, except for Mmp13, Mmp14 and Tgfb3 (Extended Data Fig. 4c). In addition, we compared p21+ with p21− cells marked by the presence of the p21 transcript, finding that p21+ cells displayed similar transcriptomic signatures to tdT+ cells, but were still distinct from p16+ cells (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d and Fig. 1l).

To enable elimination of p21high cells in vivo, we crossed PT mice with floxed knock-in diphtheria toxin A (DTA) mice and generated p21Cre/+; tdT/DTA (PTD) mice (Fig. 2a) to specifically express DTA in p21high cells on TAM administration, driving apoptosis. We confirmed the presence of tdT+
p21high cells in the wound margin and granulation tissue in PT mice (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b), which is consistent with our Xenium results (Fig. 1e), and validated the clearance of tdT+ cells in PTD mice (Fig. 2b,c). Notably, more than 90% of tdT+ cells did not show signs of proliferation (Fig. 2d). Moreover, p21high cells displayed increased DNA damage (Extended Data Fig. 6c–e) and enlarged cell size (Extended Data Fig. 6f–h). These data along with the SASP (Fig. 1l) suggest that p21high cells exhibit key senescence features.

We investigated the impact of p21high cell elimination on wound closure dynamics by treating both PT and PTD mice with TAM daily from day 0 to day 11 (Fig. 2e,f). We found that wound closure was markedly accelerated in PTD mice compared to PT mice during 1–8 days after injury. Despite both groups achieving complete wound resolution without defect within 12 days, the enhanced closure kinetics observed in PTD mice, particularly in the early and middle phases, resulted in a notable decrease in the area under the curve (a.u.c.) for the wound closure curves (Fig. 2g). However, TAM alone had little effect upon wound healing (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b).

Wound tissues underwent further histological examination by a board-certified dermatologist, with confirmation by an independent pathologist. The findings revealed less lymphocytic inflammation commensurate with normal fibrin matrix formation during early-stage wound healing in PTD mice compared to PT mice (Fig. 2h–j). In late-stage wound healing, reduced dermal fibrosis and collagen disarray was noted in PTD mice compared to PT mice 12 days after injury, which corresponds to wound closure without haphazard scar formation (Fig. 2h–l). The absence of giant cells in the wound tissues of both PT and PTD mice indicated an acute rather than chronic nature of the wounds. Furthermore, no pathological hyperkeratosis or parakeratosis was observed in the epidermal wound edges of either PT or PTD mice, indicating complete wound resolution.

Notably, we exclusively observed accelerated wound closure in female but not male PTD mice (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d), indicating a potential sex difference in the impact of p21high cells upon wound healing. To investigate the role of p21high cells during the early phase of healing (during which p16high cells accelerate wound closure2), we treated PT and PTD mice with TAM daily during 1–5 days after injury. Clearance of p21high cells during the first 5 days after injury was sufficient to accelerate wound healing (Extended Data Fig. 7e,f). These findings indicate a detrimental role of p21high cells during wound healing and fibrosis, as opposed to the beneficial role observed for p16high cells2,3.

We conducted bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on wound tissues. Through gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), we demonstrated enrichment of the cellular hallmarks associated with inflammation and senescence in PT wounds 2 days after injury, including inflammatory response, the NF-κB, JAK/STAT and TGFβ signaling pathways, and SenMayo gene set (Fig. 2m), suggesting that a partially reduced inflammatory response after p21high cell elimination in PTD mice could be potentially responsible for accelerated wound closure. As an initial inflammatory response is critical in mitigating pathogens and establishing the healing cascade9, it is noteworthy that PTD mice still exhibited an inflammatory response after wounding when compared to unwounded tissue (Extended Data Fig. 7g), potentially suggesting that achieving an ‘optimal’ level of inflammation for cutaneous wound healing may be possible through the clearance of p21high cells. Furthermore, hallmarks of adipogenesis, myogenesis and energy metabolism, all associated with accelerated wound healing, were enriched in the wound tissues of PTD mice (Fig. 2m), suggesting their contribution to accelerated wound healing.

Because p21high cells exhibit a pro-inflammatory signature (Figs. 1l and 2m), and their clearance reduces several inflammation-related hallmarks (Fig. 2m), we examined the impact of inflammation on the role of p21high cells in wound healing. We crossed p21Cre mice with RelaloxP/loxP mice, which have a loxP-flanked exon 1 of the Rela (v-rel reticuloendotheliosis viral oncogene homolog A or p65) to generate p21Cre/+; RelaloxP/loxP (PRela) mice (Fig. 2n), wherein TAM administration induces NF-κB pathway inactivation exclusively in p21high cells6. Compared to control P mice, the wound closure rate was partially accelerated in PRela mice (Fig. 2o,p), suggesting that the NF-κB pathway is among the key mechanisms through which p21high cells contribute to the delay of wound healing.

Collectively, our study demonstrates a detrimental role of a distinct p21high senescent cell population during the acute cutaneous wound response, which is consistent with the observation that lower p21 expression is associated with faster healing in human wounded tissues19. Despite limitations and remaining questions (discussed in the Supplementary Discussion), our findings challenge the view that senescent cell elimination might be inherently detrimental to wound resolution. Current evidence for a beneficial role of senescent cells in cutaneous wound healing is primarily limited to p16high cells2,3. In this Article, we showed that p21high cells are distinct from p16high cells in skin wounds, similar to other tissues5,6,8. We found that p21high cells were at least ten times more abundant than p16high cells (7% versus 0.5%) in wound tissues, with minimal overlap between the two populations (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b) and the transcriptomic signature (with elevated SASP in p21high cells; Extended Data Fig. 4d). These distinctions probably contribute to the divergent roles fulfilled by p21high and p16high cells in cutaneous wound healing. Importantly, no existing model is capable of completely, or with perfect selectivity tracing, eliminating senescent cells. Interpretations derived from these models should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

In summary, our findings provide evidence for the notion that senescent cells exhibit substantial heterogeneity, with different roles across physiological or pathological conditions6,20. Considering the likely diversity of senescent cells in vivo, it is clinically essential to thoroughly evaluate the impact of several senolytic agents across a broad spectrum of conditions, including wound healing.

P mice were generated on a C57BL/6 background and genotyped as described previously5,6. The p21Cre mice were crossed with floxed knock-in tdT mice21 (strain no. 007914, The Jackson Laboratory) to get the p21Cre/p21Cre; tdT/tdT mice, which were further crossed with floxed DTA mice22 (strain no. 009669, The Jackson Laboratory) to generate PT and PTD littermates. Similarly, p21Cre mice were crossed with RelaloxP/loxP mice23 (strain no. 024342, The Jackson Laboratory) to get PRela mice. All mice were used as the result of in-house matings and maintained under a 12:12 light–dark cycle (7:00 to 19:00 light on) at 25 ± 2 °C and 30–60% humidity with free access to water and regular chow diet containing 18 kcal% fat, 58 kcal% carbohydrate and 24 kcal% protein (Teklad global 18% protein rodent diet, ENVIGO). Unless noted otherwise, all experiments were conducted in young lean 8–12-week-old female mice. All procedures complied with relevant ethical regulations and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at UConn Health.

The dorsal cutaneous wound was made in accordance with previous reports2. After dorsal shaving, depilation and betadine application, two full-thickness excision wounds were created on each animal’s left and right dorsum, caudal to the scapular fulcrum, using a 6-mm biopsy punch while animals were maintained under 2% isoflurane anesthesia. The pain relief drug ketoprofen was administered at a dose of 4 mg kg−1 via subcutaneous injection immediately after wound creation, with a second dose 1 day later, totaling two doses. After daily measurements with digital calipers (Thermo Fisher Scientific) across the major and minor axes of the wound and imaging, wounds were covered with a fresh semi-occlusive dressing (Tegaderm, 3M). The wound area was calculated as a percentage of baseline wound size (day 0) at each indicated time point. Animals were treated with 2 mg TAM (cat. no. 13258, Cayman Chemical) and corn oil solution (Sigma-Aldrich) delivered intraperitoneally daily after skin excision to induce the Cre-ERT2 system. Unless noted otherwise, all female mice were treated with TAM daily throughout wound closure.

Wound samples were collected at the indicated time points after wounding. After animal euthanasia using CO2, wounds were immediately excised with a 3-mm margin and briefly immersed in sterile saline before fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 4–8 h. Samples were then transferred to 70% ethanol solution and paraffin-embedded before sectioning (8 μm section size) on a microtome. For Xenium, sample cores were collected from paraffin blocks and embedded in a tissue microarray before sectioning at 5 μm and slide transfer. After Xenium staining, the imaged tissue microarray was stained with H&E. For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin-embedded skin sections were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated in alcohol gradients. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate retrieval buffer (10 mM sodium citrate, pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min and cooled down to room temperature. After washing with PBS and blocking with buffer (5% normal goat serum, 1% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 30 min at room temperature, sections were incubated with primary antibodies (anti-tdT, 1:200 dilution, cat. no. ab62341, Abcam; anti-Ki-67, 1:200 dilution, cat. no. 14–5698-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific; anti-γH2A.X, 1:100 dilution, cat. no. MA1–2022, Thermo Fisher Scientific; anti-p21, 1:200 dilution, cat. no. sc-6246, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) in dilution buffer (5% normal goat serum, 1% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS) overnight at 4 °C. Sections were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated (cat. no. A-32723, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated (cat. no. A-11036, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated (cat. no. A-21247, Thermo Fisher Scientific) secondary antibodies, each diluted 1:500 for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were counterstained with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (cat. no. P36962, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged on an Olympus APX100 microscope. For H&E and Masson trichrome staining, paraffin-embedded skin sections were stained with Epredia Shandon Gill Hematoxylin (cat. no. 6765007, Thermo Fisher Scientific), eosin (cat. no. LC140302, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Epredia Richard-Allan Scientific Masson Trichrome Kit (cat. no. 22–110-648, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Histological examination of the wound tissues was performed by a board-certified dermatologist (S.P.W.) using a previously validated grading system24, with confirmation by an independent pathologist (K.P.). The degree of fibrin matrix formation was measured as none (0), low (1), intermediate (2) or high (3) from wound edge-to-edge in wound closures. The degree of lymphocytic inflammation was measured as none (0), sparse (1), moderate (2) or diffuse (3) in the wound bed. The degree of dermal fibrosis was measured as none (0), mild (1), moderate (2) or severe (3) in the wound bed. The degree of collagen disarray was measured as none (0), low (1), intermediate (2) or high (3) in the wound bed. Complete wound resolution was indicated by the absence of pathological hyperkeratosis or parakeratosis that are typically found in nonhealing ulcers25.

Skins were collected and minced with scissors and digested in PBS containing 1 mg ml−1 type I collagenase (cat. no. C9891, Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 μg ml−1 of DNase I (cat. no. D5025, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 1 h. After digestion, cells were washed with PBS and 2% FCS and filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer. Cells were imaged using ImageStreamX Mark II (Amnis) and analyzed using the IDEAS 6.2 software (Amnis). To focus cells, samples were gated using the gradient root mean square values of the bright field (BF) channel. Cells were further gated using the BF area and aspect ratio to select single cells. tdT− and tdT+ skin cells were measured for cell areas using the BF area.

Skins were homogenized and lysed in buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM dithiothreitol, 0.5% SDS) with protease inhibitors (cat. no. A32965, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20 min on ice. The extracted proteins were boiled with Laemmli SDS sample buffer (cat. no. J60015.AC, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated on SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and then transferred to a 0.45-mm polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 and then sequentially incubated with primary antibodies (anti-p21, cat. no. 14–6715-81, Thermo Fisher Scientific; anti-β-actin, cat. no. 4967S, Cell Signaling Technology) and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (anti-rabbit IgG, cat. no. 7074, Cell Signaling Technology). The immunoblots were imaged with the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System after developing with the Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (cat. no. WBKLS0050, Merck Millipore).

Total skin RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), purified with chloroform, isopropanol and 75% ice-cold ethanol, dissolved in RNase-free water and then reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA with the M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase Kit (cat. no. 28025013, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Real-time quantitative PCR was conducted using the PerfeCTa FastMix II (cat. no. 95118, Quantabio) on the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The relative mRNA level of target genes was normalized to TATA-binding protein (TBP) and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primers and probes for TBP and p21 were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies: TBP (assay ID: Mm.PT.39a.22214839); p21 (assay ID: Mm.PT.58.17125846).

Wound samples were freshly collected from mice at days 2, 4 and 12 after injury or unwounded animals, as indicated, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), purified with chloroform, isopropanol and 75% ice-cold ethanol, and dissolved in RNase-free water; 500 ng total RNA per sample was submitted to the Center for Genomic Innovation at the University of Connecticut for library preparation (TruSeq Stranded mRNA) and sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic using a quality threshold of 30 and length threshold of 45. Then, trimmed reads were mapped to the GRCm39 reference genome with HISAT2 v.2.1.0. The resulting SAM files were converted into BAM format using SAMtools v.1.9; gene expression was estimated by generating a count matrix with htseq-count. GSEA was then conducted using the GSEA v.4.3.3 software. Analysis was conducted using both the mouse Molecular Signatures Database hallmark gene sets and the SenMayo cellular senescence gene set26 (a recently validated gene set to identify and assess cellular senescence) using the ‘phenotype’ permutation. Gene sets were considered significantly enriched at an FDR cutoff of 0.25.

Tissue microarrays were prepared from unwounded skin and wounds 6 days after injury (both treated with six doses of TAM) collected from PT mice as this time point corresponded to the high accumulation of p21high cells. After sectioning at 5 μm and transfer to the 10X Genomics Xenium sample slide, tissue was deparaffinized, decrosslinked and stained using a customized DNA probe panel and Xenium Mouse Tissue Atlassing panel supplemented with a custom gene panel encompassing approximately 500 genes according to the manufacturer’s guidelines (CG000580, conducted by The Jackson Laboratory Single Cell Biology Laboratory). After probe hybridization, ligation and amplification, slides were imaged; subsequent probe decoding and cell segmentation were performed onboard the Xenium (CG000582). Total transcriptomes were obtained from 209,937 cells (172,422 from wounded tissues and 37,515 from unwounded tissues). The results were visualized using the Xenium Explorer software. H&E images were converted to pyramidal format using QuPAth v.0.5.1 and overlayed on the dataset. Seurat v.5.0.3 in R v.4.3.3 was used to analyze the spatial transcriptomics data. Cells with zero counts were filtered out and the remaining data were normalized using a regularized negative binomial model using the SCTransform() function. tdT+ cells were defined as those that expressed the tdT transcript (normalized value > 0). p21+ cells were defined as those that expressed the p21 transcript (normalized value > 0). p16+ cells were defined as those that expressed the p16 transcript (normalized value > 0). For the downstream analysis, the first 40 principal components were retained. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were conducted using the first 19 principal components, selected based on the elbow plot, through the UMAP algorithm (RunUMAP (dims = 1:19)). Unsupervised clustering was achieved using the FindNeighbours () and FindClusters (resolution = 0.2) functions. Specific gene markers for each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers (only.pos = TRUE) function. Cell type assignment was conducted manually using the top ten DEGs referencing the Mouse Skin Single-cell RNA-seq Atlas27. The MAST test28 was used to identify DEGs between tdT+ and tdT− cells, as well as p16+ and p16− in each cell type. MAST tests were two-sided and multiple comparisons were adjusted using the FDR correction. Visualization of DEGs was performed using the ggplot () function to create bubble plots.

Details of the biological replicates and statistical methods can be found in the figure legends. All data underwent a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test in Prism 9 (GraphPad Software) to assess the normal distribution. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were analyzed using a two-tailed unpaired or paired Student’s t-test. Before the analysis, an F-test was conducted to assess whether variances were equal. If variances were equal, a t-test assuming equal variance was used; otherwise, a t-test assuming unequal variance was used. For data not normally distributed, comparisons were conducted using a two-tailed Mann–Whitney U-test. Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 9 or Microsoft Excel. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size; sample sizes were chosen based on a previous publication2. No animals or data were excluded from the analyses. No randomization method was used. Mice were assigned to experimental groups based on their genotypes. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during the experiments and outcome assessments, but the experiments were replicated by independent personnel to avoid bias. A difference was considered to be significant if P < 0.05.
