 Recent studies (6) have shown that B19 can cause acutearthritis or a chronic arthropathy both in children and adults.
In particular, B19 is suggested to be a causative agent for RA
because B19 DNA has been detected in the synovial tissue of
RA patients (7). Murai et al reported 3 cases who developed
RA after acute B 19 infection, which may also support that B 19
can be one of the possible pathogenic factors of RA (8). Other
studies also indicate a connective tissue disease-like syndrome
with parvoviral infections (9). The common features of this
syndrome are malar eruption, arthritis, and autoantibody pro-
duction such as antinuclear antibody and rheumatoid factor ( 1 0).
These clinical manifestations of adult B 19 infection may mimic
SLE (fever, malar rash, polyarthralgia and cytopenia);
rheumatologists must be aware of its infection and it is very
important to make a differential diagnosis between lupus ne-
phritis and B 19 glomerulonephritis, central nervous system
lupus and B 19 meningoencephalitis, or lupoid (autoimmune)
hepatitis
 and B 19 hepatitis.
 There is a report that B19 specific NS1 protein could in-
duce TNFa and IL-6 production (1 1), which would be involved
in the pathogenetic mechanism of RA and/or SLE. These stud-
ies and reports may lead to the hypothesis that B19 might be
the cause of some autoimmune diseases such as RA and SLE,
or at least, it may be involved in the pathogenesis of these dis-
orders. Further studies are necessary to elucidate the relation-
ship between systemic autoimmune diseases and B 19 infec-
tion.
