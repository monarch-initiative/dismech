Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) are a group of autoimmune muscle disorders characterized by muscle weakness caused by muscle tissue inflammation, as well as lung and skin symptoms. Their pathophysiology and exacerbation are primarily associated with the tissue infiltration of immune cells, such as T cells and macrophages, revealing a strong connection with the immune system. Based on the clinical and pathological features of IIMs, polymyositis, which is characterized by CD8 T-cell infiltration around muscle fibers, and dermatomyositis, which is characterized by CD4 T-cell infiltration with complement infiltration around blood vessels, along with distinctive skin symptoms, have been traditionally distinguished. However, recent classifications based on autoantibodies and gene expression have proposed new categories, such as antisynthetase syndrome, clinical amyopathic dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, and inclusion body myositis, resulting in the concept of IIM as a spectrum of diseases including these subtypes. Furthermore, advancements in next-generation sequencing have analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics using human patient samples, demonstrating the detailed characteristics of immune cell subsets, contributions of new immune cells, and interactions with effector cells in each disease subtype. A variety of IIM mouse models have been developed by activating the immune system through different methods, reflecting distinct myositis classifications within IIM. Recently, polymyositis models have used a humanized immune system, enabling the evaluation of therapeutics across species. This review provides an overview of the latest insights into the immunopathology of IIM and myositis models, reflecting various subtypes, advancing nonbiased and in-depth understanding using omics technologies.

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) are a group of inflammatory muscle disorders involving autoimmune mechanisms, with muscle tissue damage resulting in muscle weakness as the main symptom. In addition to muscle involvement, disease manifestations occur in tissues other than the muscle, such as interstitial lung disease and skin [1]. The incidence of malignant tumors is high among patients with IIM [1]. Originally, IIMs was classified based on muscle weakness patterns and clinical subtypes, such as heliotrope rash and Gottron’s sign on the skin, collectively known as polymyositis (PM), dermatomyositis (DM), and inclusion body myositis (IBM) [2,3]. However, recent classification advancements based on muscle-specific autoantibodies and pathological findings have led to new subtypes, namely, clinical amyopathic dermatomyositis (CADM) characterized by anti-MDA5 antibodies [3], immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) characterized by scattered muscle fiber necrosis and regeneration with minimal inflammatory cell infiltration [4], and anti-synthetase syndrome (ASS) associated with interstitial lung diseases (ILD) [5,6,7]. Based on the onset before or after the age of 18 years, IIMs were classified as juvenile dermatomyositis and other pediatric myositis [7,8]. Furthermore, genetic expression-based subtype classification identified genes specific to each subtype, in which has contributed to understanding the pathophysiology of the disease [9,10,11]. Immune abnormalities play a role in the pathophysiology of each IIM subtype, and different immune cells and patterns are involved. Notably, in PM, CD8 T-cell infiltration occurred around muscle fibers, leading to direct cellular damage through interaction with muscle cell MHC class I, whereas in DM, CD4 T-, and B-cell infiltration developed around blood vessels with complement deposition, suggesting autoantibody contribution [12]. Advances in the omics analysis of small human tissue samples at the single-cell level have enhanced the detailed pathophysiological understanding of myositis. Novel disease-modifying drugs with low side effects are required to address the needs unmet by current therapies. The contribution of type I interferon (IFN)-related signaling to DM pathophysiology has resulted in the active development of therapies targeting this pathway and controlling B-cell responses [13,14,15]. Despite efforts using mouse models, a gap remains in the full spectrum of human pathophysiology, posing challenges to drug development. Further bridging analysis between IIM mouse models and human pathophysiology using omics technologies is warranted. Subsequent sections will outline the immunological characteristics of known disease subtypes, recent insights based on single-cell omics technology, and provide a comprehensive overview of mouse models, their immunological characteristics, similarities with human disease subtypes, and advancements in constructing state-of-the-art mouse disease models.

PM involves a pathophysiology primarily affecting skeletal muscles and is characterized by symmetric proximal muscle weakness [16]. The predominant immunological mechanism is cytotoxic T-cell-mediated inflammation, with CD8 T-cell infiltration around nonnecrotic muscle fibers being a distinct characteristic [12]. Pathological analysis of human PM tissues revealed upregulated MHC class I expression on muscle fibers, with direct attack by CD8 T cells causing muscle tissue damage [12]. In PM patients, an increase in CD28nullT cells that do not express CD28 is also a characteristic in peripheral blood and muscle tissue [17]. In 2023, through RNA sequencing analysis of muscle biopsies from PM patients, four MHC class I-related genes, PDIA1, HLA-C, TAP-1, and B2M, were identified as hub genes that characterize PM patients [18]. These findings provide even stronger evidence for CD8 T-cell involvement in PM. In addition, autoantibodies associated with PM, such as FHL-1 and TRIM72, were recently reported [19]. FHL-1 is a muscle-specific antigen abundantly present in the skeletal and cardiac muscles [20]. The proportion of patients positive for anti-FHL1 antibodies is approximately 20% among those with IIM, and the frequency is known to be higher in PM and IBM [21,22]. TRIM72 belongs to the TRIM family like TIF1γ and is an important protein in the sarcolemmal repair process [23,24]. More than 10% of patients with PM and DM are positive for anti-TRIM72 antibodies [25]. Myopathy due to impaired sarcolemmal repair caused by TRIM72 deficiency has been reported in mice [26,27]. However, the relationship with symptoms in humans and the causal involvement in PM pathology are unclear. The autoantibodies detected in PM have not been established as myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs), as they do not accurately reflect clinical characteristics or prognosis, nor can they be used to classify patient subgroups. Based on this background, the involvement of specific autoantibodies is generally considered lower in PM compared with other types of myositis. Some patients were diagnosed with IMNM as PM without muscle biopsy or autoantibody testing [28,29]. Therefore, an accurate pathophysiological understanding and analysis are crucial for extracting PM cases alone to obtain an accurate understanding of the disease.

DM is a type of myositis characterized by perifascicular atrophy, in which the muscle fibers at the edges of the muscle bundles become thinner [16]. Accumulated pathological analysis of patient muscle tissues revealed B- and CD4 T-cell infiltration around blood vessels, along with deposition of C5b-9 membrane attack complexes, making this subtype the most associated with autoantibodies specific to myositis [12]. Various autoantibodies, such as anti-Mi-2, anti-TIF-1γ, anti-NXP-2, anti-SAE, and anti-MDA5 antibodies, are reportedly involved in DM [16]. Patients positive for anti-Mi-2 antibodies frequently exhibit typical skin symptoms, whereas those positive for anti-TIF-1γ antibodies are strongly associated with concurrent malignancies [16,30]. In DM patients as well, similar to PM, it is known that CD28nullT cells increased in peripheral blood and muscle tissue [17]. DM is also reported to involve type I IFN signaling [31,32,33,34]. Several studies have emphasized the importance of IFN signaling in DM, with bulk RNA sequencing (RNAseq) analysis of muscle tissue biopsies in patients with severe DM showing type I IFN signaling upregulation [35]. Izuka et al. also reported the possibility of monocyte infiltration into the muscle tissue, activation of type I/II interferon signaling, and complement system activation in DM, based on comparative analysis of cell types using bulk RNAseq and deconvolution of muscle tissue from patients with DM and PM [36]. The current drug development strategy for IIM, particularly for DM, targets this signaling pathway and B-cell-related pathways [14].

Within DM, subsets including amyopathic DM with skin symptoms but no muscle involvement and hypomyopathic DM with mild muscle symptoms are reported [16]. These subsets, along with CADM, which are characterized by anti-MDA5 antibodies, demonstrate a contribution of this signaling pathway to CADM [16]. Recent studies have reported specific features of autoantibodies in DM, revealing increased cytoskeleton expression and muscle regeneration-related genes in Mi-2, TIF-1γ, and NXP-2-positive cases [37]. Macrophage involvement in addition to B and CD4 T cells has been suggested in DM, with increased classical monocytes in MDA5 + DM and CD68 + macrophage infiltration in Mi-2, TIF-1γ, and NXP-2-positive cases [37]. T-cell profiles in muscle tissues of different IIM subtypes have been studied, revealing increased CXCL13 + tissue resident memory T (Trm) cells in DM, which attract CXCR5 + B cells and follicular helper T (Tfh) cells to promote antibody production, indicating their involvement in the disease pathophysiology [38]. From the analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing in MDA5 + DM patients, it has been observed that antibody-producing cells and Ki-67 + CD8 T cells increase in peripheral blood, and there is an enhanced proliferative signal in lung tissue [39]. Furthermore, protein analysis of peripheral blood plasma has identified CXCL10 and inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 as characteristic factors in anti-MDA5 antibody-positive cases, along with LAMP3, HSD11B1, and KRT19 [40,41]. Additionally, the fractalkine CX3CL1 has been reported to correlate with CK and disease activity [41].

ASS is characterized by myositis-specific antibodies known as ARS antibodies in IIM subtypes [16]. ARS antibodies target aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, with Jo-1 being a representative [16,42]. They are associated with muscle symptoms, interstitial lung disease, arthritis, and skin lesions known as “mechanic’s hands” [16]. Despite the existence of comparative reports on the dynamics of immune cells in ASS, a considerable degree of diversity in knowledge exists, and a definitive consensus on specific doctrines remains elusive. In a bulk RNAseq analysis of muscle biopsies of various IIM subtypes, ASS demonstrated a strong indication of contribution from intermediate monocytes [35]. T-cell profiling of muscle tissues of various IIM subtypes revealed GzmB + effector memory T cells as a distinctive T-cell subset in ASS [38]. However, reports on the contribution of CD8 T cells to the pathophysiology are limited, necessitating further analysis. Another analysis of the patient’s muscle tissue also reported that infiltration of Th1 cells and a shift in intracellular metabolism toward oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) occur in ASS [36]. Single-cell RNAseq analysis of PBMCs in patients with ASS indicated a potential lead in understanding immune contributions in ASS, such as decreased mucosal-associated invariant T cells and intracellular metabolic abnormalities observed in natural killer T cells [43], which could be relevant for future investigations.

IBM is a type of myositis characterized by a tendency to affect older individuals and involves the finger flexor and quadriceps muscles [16]. While it is similar to PM, IBM is distinct because of the presence of degenerated muscle fibers with rimmed vacuoles. In addition, the accumulation of proteins, such as TDP-43 and amyloid β, typically seen in Alzheimer’s disease, has been observed within muscle cells in IBM [44,45,46]. Moreover, the recent study has conducted single-cell nucleus RNAseq and spatial transcriptome analysis to compare the muscle tissues of patients with IBM, IMNM, and noninflammatory controls [47]. The results demonstrated a selective decrease in type 2 myonuclei and an increase in CD8 T cells and DC1 cells in patients with IBM, revealing the potential contribution of these cells to IBM tissue damage. In bulk RNAseq analysis of muscle biopsies of various IIM subtypes, T-cell-related genes were identified as a distinct cluster specific to IBM [35]. Another report demonstrated HOBIT + Trm-cell infiltration in the muscle tissues of patients with IBM [38]. Spatial transcriptome analysis and single-cell RNA sequencing have revealed that the upregulation of stress markers such as GADD45A, NORAD, and ACHE was characteristic [47,48]. In addition, it has been identified the characteristic infiltration of KLRG1 + T cells, which are markers of effector memory cells, in the muscle tissues of patients with IBM [49]. Based on this finding, clinical trials are underway to assess the efficacy of cytotoxic antibodies targeting KLRG1-expressing cells in a phase 2 clinical trial [50].

In 2004, ENMC proposed IMNM as a subtype characterized by predominant muscle fiber necrosis with minimal inflammatory cell infiltration based on histopathological findings [51]. The protein is classified based on muscle-specific autoantibodies that target heterogeneous muscle fiber necrosis and regeneration. Activation of the complement system by characteristic autoantibodies, such as anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR antibodies, is considered the main pathogenic mechanism [4,52,53]. A model has been established in which IgG administration derived from patients with IMNM positive for anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR antibodies leads to the onset of symptoms, supporting the validity of this model due to the clinically observed complement system tissue deposition [54]. Unlike PM and DM, IMNM is characterized by minimal lymphocyte infiltration, such as CD8 and CD4 T cells, into the muscle tissue, whereas macrophage infiltration has been reported [52,55].

An analysis of muscle tissues revealed minimal immune cell infiltration in the muscle tissues of patients with IMNM compared with IBM, indicating a lower contribution of immune cell infiltration to pathophysiology [56]. Bulk RNAseq analysis of muscle biopsies also revealed significantly lower inflammatory features by immune cells in IMNM than in other diseases [35].

In IBM, analyses of patient muscle tissue have revealed the contribution of CD8 T cells and DC1. ILD is a high-mortality condition that commonly complicates various autoimmune diseases, presenting a challenging pathophysiology without effective treatments [16,57]. Hence, the ILD associated with IIMs can be classified into acute/subacute and chronic types [57,58,59]. In IIM-associated ILD, the presence or absence of antibodies against MDA5 or ARS is crucial for determining treatment strategies [16]. The majority of ILD associated with IIM, known as rapid (RP)-ILD, are characterized by MDA5 antibody positivity and are termed MDA5-ILD [60]. Conversely, ASS-ILD, which is associated with ASS, typically progresses chronically. ILD can also occur in classical DM, PM, and IMNM [57]. Research on immunological differences in ILD based on the underlying conditions is currently advancing. Nakazawa et al. conducted an immunophenotype analysis using bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from patients with IIM-ILD, rheumatoid arthritis (RA)-ILD, and drug-induced ILD [61]. They found that compared with other ILD types, IIM-ILD showed significant CD8 T-cell infiltration and alveolar macrophages. Furthermore, activated markers, such as PD-1 and TIM-3, were notably higher in CD8 T cells in IIM-ILD, revealing their potential contribution to pathophysiology. RNAseq analysis of alveolar macrophages in various ILD revealed that IIM-ILD macrophages were more activated than those in drug-induced ILD, indicating their potential role in the disease. Furthermore, studies analyzing immune cells in the BALF of steroid-resistant patients revealed significant activation of CD8 T cells, CD4 T cells, macrophages, and neutrophil infiltration in patients with PM/DM [62]. Immune cell immunophenotyping in the BALF of various ILD using mass cytometry reported increased TIM-3 + CD8 T cells in patients with DM [63]. PBMC and lung tissue analysis in patients with MDA5 + ILD using single-cell RNAseq revealed increased numbers of plasma cells, which are the primary antibody-producing cells in PBMCs, consistent with the contribution of autoantibodies in MDA5 + ILD [39]. Proliferative CD8 T cells, type I IFN upregulation, and fibrotic signals were observed in the lung tissue of patients with MDA5 + ILD, revealing their involvement in direct lung tissue damage. These findings indicate that in addition to classical autoantibodies, targeting CD8 T cells and macrophages may be crucial for controlling ILD.

In addition to steroids, the combination of azathioprine or methotrexate is commonly selected as the first-line therapy for muscle symptoms [16,64]. In cases of first-line resistance or relapse, second-line treatments, such as calcineurin inhibitors (e.g., tacrolimus or cyclosporine) or mycophenolate mofetil, are frequently selected [64,65]. In patients who did not achieve control, third-line options, such as intravenous immunoglobulin therapy (IVIG), intravenous cyclophosphamide (IVCY), or rituximab (used off-label), may be considered as adjunctive therapies [16,64,65]. In addition, treatment strategies are individualized based on clinical subtypes classified by MSAs. In cases of acute MDA5-ILD, triple therapy consisting of high-dose steroids, IVCY, and calcineurin inhibitors is recommended, and the addition of JAK inhibitors may be considered in the presence of poor prognostic factors [16,66]. In chronic or subacute ASS-ILD, the combination of steroids and calcineurin inhibitors is commonly used, and in case of insufficient effects, treatment with IVCY may also be considered [67,68,69]. In IMNM positive for anti-SRP or anti-HMGCR antibodies, high doses of steroids with azathioprine or methotrexate are often insufficient; thus, early combination with IVIG or rituximab is also considered [4,16]. For anti-Mi-2 antibody-positive DM, steroids are highly effective, and the initial treatment with high-dose steroids leads to recovery of muscle symptoms in many cases [16]. In TIF-1γ-positive DM, the incidence of concomitant malignancy is high; thus, screening for malignancy is performed for new patients, and if present, tumor progression due to immunosuppressive therapy should be considered [70]. As described above, the usefulness of MSAs and organ-specific treatment is well established in the therapeutic strategy for IIMs. Despite the differences in treatment methods depending on MSAs and other factors, immunosuppressive therapy centered on steroids remains the mainstay IIM treatment. However, long-term use of steroids, the primary treatment choice, can cause muscle weakness as a side effect [71]. This posed the challenge that despite the suppression of inflammation, treatment may inadvertently worsen symptoms, highlighting the need for new first- and second-line treatment options.

Currently, drug development targeting IIMs primarily focuses on biologics that inhibit B cells and antibody production, with a specific focus on DM [14,72]. Given the reported contribution of type I IFN to DM pathophysiology, clinical trials are underway to assess drugs, such as anifrolumab targeting IFNAR1, dazukibart targeting IFNβ1, and brepocitinib targeting JAK1/Tyk2, with DM/ASS [31,73,74,75,76]. Clinical trials are also underway for FcRn inhibitors, such as efgartigimod and nipocalimab, which inhibit IgG production, as well as complement C2 inhibitors, including empasiprubart, targeting complement reduction [77,78,79]. Moreover, the concept of deep depletion of tissue-infiltrating B cells using CAR-T cells has gained attention as a novel treatment approach for autoimmune diseases, including IIM, in which local disease control is crucial [80]. Trials using CAR-T-cell therapy are being explored as an initial treatment for IIM, along with other diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus and systemic sclerosis [81,82]. These treatment concepts target B cells, autoantibody production, and complement deposition, and various options are being investigated, particularly for DM subtypes within IIM. Conversely, for IIM subtypes, such as PM and IBM, which are characterized by CD8 T-cell involvement and substantial tissue changes, clinical options are limited. Efforts to develop KLRG1-depletion antibodies targeting effector memory cells have focused primarily on IBM [50]. Moreover, the development of treatment strategies for disease manifestations that are not dependent on B-cell control or complement system control, such as PM, IBM, and lung involvement, is expected to increase in importance in the future.

In IIM mouse disease models, pathological changes in muscle tissue have been extensively reported. Each model exhibited different immune responses and immune cells, reflecting various IIM subtypes. The characteristics of each model in relation to the IIM subtypes and immunological features are depicted in Fig.2. Although myositis induced by immunizing against antigen proteins associated with muscle disorders has been well investigated, the features of the induced pathology vary depending on the type of antigen used. For example, the CIM model induced using myosin-binding C protein and TIF-1γ-immunized myositis (TIM) reflect PM-like pathology mediated by CD8 T cells [83,84,85], whereas experimental autoimmune myositis (EAM) is reported to represent DM-like pathology involving CD4 T cells, autoantibodies, and the complement system [86]. Furthermore, in clinical models, such as the Jo-1-immunized myositis model targeting Jo-1 or the MDA5-immunized myositis model targeting MDA5, lung complications not effectively controlled by existing treatments are reflected [87,88]. A lung symptom model using a TLR9 agonist administered systemically has also been revealed to reproduce myositis-related lung complications up to the fibrotic stage [89]. Genetically modified mice have been investigated, with models inducing PM-like pathology through muscle-specific MHC class I overexpression. In IBM models, the overexpression of muscle-specific pathogenic genes, including APP, gelsolin, and TDP-43, induces IBM-like pathology [44,90,91]. The detailed mechanisms and immunological contributions of each model, as well as their similarities to different disease subtypes, are further discussed in the following sections.

The CIM model is a myosin-binding protein C-induced myositis model in mice and rats, such as C57BL/6 mice, which was introduced by Kosaka et al. in 2007 [83]. The model is induced by immunizing animals with C protein using CFA and pertussis toxin adjuvants administered intraperitoneally, resulting in onset within 2–3 weeks. Pathologically, CD8 T-cell infiltration into muscle fibers and abnormal MHC class I expression were observed. The condition is CD8 T-cell-dependent, as evidenced by the attenuation of symptoms upon the knockout of MHC class I or GzmB, which resembles PM. Recently, increased PD-L1 expression in muscle tissue via IFN-γ and the interaction with PD-1 + CD8 T cells plays a crucial role in pathophysiology [92]. Symptoms commonly observed in PM, such as infiltration of macrophages and dendritic cells and symmetrical inflammation, are also noted. The onset of disease is suppressed in IL-1, MHC class I, and perforin KO mice, whereas TNFαnullmice exhibit a similar onset as that of the wild type [83,93]. The involvement of IL-6, IL-1, TNFα, IL-23, CXCL10, and CD80/86 has also been reported [94,95,96,97,98]. Due to differences in the binding affinity to MHC class I of C protein, myositis pathology occurs in B6, NZB, and SJL/J mice but not in Balb/c and C3H mice, among others [83]. Treatments targeting T cells, such as abatacept, IL-23R antibodies, and KZR-616 (LMP7 inhibitor), were effective in this model [96,98,99]. In addition to CK elevation, muscle weakness, gait abnormalities, and reduced activity levels were observed. Considering the induction mechanism and phenotypic similarity based on the activation of the MHC class I-CD8 T axis, this model closely mimic human PM.

The EAM model is the most classical antigen-induced autoimmune myositis model. After Pearson et al. published their report on immunizing rats using adjuvants in 1956 [86], various studies were conducted on methods to induce autoimmune myositis. In 1987, Kotzin et al. reported the EAM model induced in SJL/J mice by multiple subcutaneous injections of muscle homogenate and CFA emulsion [100]. SJL/J mice exhibit dysferlin gene defects, which cause secondary macrophage and T-cell infiltration following degeneration and necrosis of muscle fibers, as observed in humans with dysferlin gene abnormalities [101]. Subsequent studies have reported EAM models using different strains of mice, such as C57BL/6 mice [102,103] or Balb/c mice [104], indicating that the antigens present in the muscle homogenate contribute to the pathophysiology. Pathologically, macrophage and CD4 T-cell infiltration into muscle fibers and muscle fiber necrosis were observed [105]. Furthermore, similar to PM/DM, ICAM-1 expression was observed in infiltrated lymphocytes and inflamed endothelial cells [106,107]. Activation of autoantibodies, complement, and muscle weakness characteristic of DM were also observed [108]. Methylprednisolone, tacrolimus, IVIG, anti-CX3CL1 antibodies, retinoids, and rapamycin improved the pathology of this model [106,108,109,110,111,112], indicating its utility in drug efficacy evaluation. Although traditionally considered a classical PM/DM model, it is now thought to be more similar to DM due to the predominant infiltration of CD4 T cells and the observed activation of autoantibodies and complement.

The TIM model is a myositis model induced by immunizing C57BL/6 mice with human TIF-1γ, reported by Fujimoto et al. in 2021 [84,85]. Anti-TIF-1γ antibodies are autoantibodies specific to myositis and are known to be associated with cancer-associated DM [113]. Induction of pathology was achieved by immunization with recombinant human TIF-1γ and CFA emulsion subcutaneously on the back three times a week for 1 week, followed by additional immunization on the footpad and intraperitoneal administration of pertussis toxin on the fourth immunization. Pathologically, cell infiltration (primarily CD8 T cells) into muscle tissue and increased MHC class I expression were observed. In experiments involving the transfer of isolated CD8 T cells, CD4 T cells, and IgG containing anti-TIF-1γ antibodies from immunized mice, pathology induction was confirmed only in mice transferred with CD8 T cells, indicating the importance of CD8 T cells in the model’s pathophysiology rather than anti-TIF-1γ antibodies. Furthermore, the upregulation of the type I IFN-related genes Mx1 and Osa3 and the improvement of pathology with the JAK inhibitor tofacitinib demonstrate the model’s characteristic activation of the type I IFN pathway. Although immunizing with an antigen was closely correlated with autoantibodies associated with cancer-associated DM, the model’s phenotype did not necessarily align with the characteristics of cancer-associated DM. With CD8 T cells playing a crucial role in pathology induction, low involvement of CD4 T cells, B cells, and autoantibodies, along with distinctive activation of the type I IFN pathway, the model is considered to exhibit PM/DM features.

Transgenic mice overexpressing MHC class I, specifically in skeletal muscle, were previously reported by Nagaraju et al. in 2000 to induce myositis [114]. The upregulation of MHC class I expression is a characteristic feature of patients with autoimmune myositis (particularly PM) and is believed to be involved in the pathophysiology through CD8 T-cell activation [115]. The size of MHC class I Tg mice is smaller than that of wild-type mice, revealing minimal lymphocyte infiltration, but exhibiting reduced activity and muscle strength. Some mice also exhibited anti-Jo-1 antibodies, including anti-ARS antibodies. Pathologically, ICAM-1 expression is increased, and MIP-1α, MCP-1, and IL-15 production is enhanced as related molecules. Furthermore, common features of patients with PM/DM include Grp78 upregulation, a molecule associated with the endoplasmic reticulum stress response, and activation of the NF-κB pathway, revealing a pathogenic mechanism related to endoplasmic reticulum stress, different from models directly activating antigen–antibody reactions [116]. Although further investigation is needed to determine the contribution of lymphocytes, this model is considered to be similar to PM in terms of pathology.

The APP model is an IBM model that reflects muscle degeneration within the pathophysiology of autoimmune myositis. In 2002, Frank M LaFerla’s group focused on the abnormal accumulation of amyloid-β, known in Alzheimer’s disease and skeletal muscle diseases, similar to the brain [90]. They created transgenic mice overexpressing APP, specifically in skeletal muscles, and reported its usefulness as an IBM model. In this Tg mouse model, mild-to-moderate neutrophil infiltration was observed, whereas T cells and macrophages commonly found in patients with IBM with extensive infiltration were almost absent. However, the characteristic pathological features of IBM, such as rimmed vacuoles representing muscle degeneration and amyloid deposition within muscle fibers, were observed, causing muscle weakness and atrophy. Molecular mechanisms contributing to pathophysiology include abnormal TDP-43 accumulation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and autophagy dysfunction. Furthermore, reports have demonstrated that presenilin 1 overexpression, involved in amyloid-β production from APP, accelerates CD8 T-cell infiltration, enhancing pathophysiology [117]. Despite differences in the immunological mechanism with a predominant involvement of neutrophils rather than CD8 T cells observed in patients with IBM, the phenotype of this model closely resembles that of patients with IBM.

In 2009, Jeffery’s group reported that transgenic mice expressing human gelsolin D187N, specifically in the muscles, exhibited IBM-like symptoms [91]. At 9 months of age, muscle fiber size variations due to necrosis and regeneration, as well as degeneration and accumulation of intracellular protein aggregates, were observed. By 18 months of age, these features had become more widespread, including the presence of rimmed vacuoles. In addition to pathological features, muscle weakness was evident in Tg mice based on grip strength testing. The pathophysiology is thought to involve disruption of proteostasis in mouse muscles due to abnormal accumulation of the easily misfolded D187N gelsolin protein and its fragments. Lymphocyte infiltration is minimal, and the extent of its contribution remains unclear.

VCP is a protein that forms hexamers and possesses two ATPase domains. It is expressed in various tissues and is involved in regulating cellular functions, such as apoptosis, transcription factor activation, nuclear membrane reconstruction, endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation, and regulation of the ubiquitin–proteasome system [118]. Identified for the first time in 2004 by Watts et al., VCP is a causative gene for autosomal dominant diseases, such as inclusion body myopathy with Paget’s disease of bone and frontotemporal dementia [119]. The main mechanism of pathophysiology involves the inhibition of binding with ubiquitin and various adapter proteins due to VCP gene mutations. In mice overexpressing muscle-specific or systemic R155H or A232E VCP, pathological changes resembling inclusion body myositis, such as muscle fiber size variations and rimmed vacuoles, were observed [120,121]. In addition, lesions positive for TDP-43 and ubiquitin, as well as muscle atrophy and weakness, are present. One known mechanism is the VCP involvement in the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation of IkB-α. Furthermore, in vitro evaluations using muscle cells revealed that A232E VCP enhances the activation of the NF-kB pathway, indicating the involvement of the innate immune system in pathophysiology [122]. Although some inflammatory cell infiltration was observed, many aspects remain unanalyzed, and the extent of contribution from the acquired immune system is unclear.

TDP-43 is an RNA-binding protein that is primarily localized in the nucleus, and it is involved in the transcription and splicing of various genes. Moreover, it is a causative gene for familial ALS. Furthermore, TDP-43-positive fibers were observed in 25–32.5% of skeletal muscle biopsies from patients with IBM [44]. In 2018, Ando’s group reported on transgenic mice expressing TDP-43 specifically in the muscles [45]. In TDP-43 Tg mice, increased serum CK, LDH, and AST levels were observed at 18 months of age. Furthermore, aggregates were formed in the muscle fibers, indicating an IBM-like myositis model. Immune cell infiltration is considered minimal, and its contribution is assumed to be low; however, the extent of analysis in this regard remains insufficient.

In 1979, Johnson’s group reported a model with Coxsackievirus B1 infection in mice, such as BALB/c-induced PM-like pathology [123]. Since then, several models that induce myositis-like symptoms by infecting mice with various viruses have been reported. Coxsackievirus B1 infection in BALB/c, Swiss C3H, or COH mice rapidly induces a pathology characterized by muscle fiber necrosis and cell infiltration, which was reported to be T cell-dependent by Yitterberg et al. [124]. Furthermore, the Ross River and Chikungunya viruses induce moderate myositis, arthritis, meningitis, and other conditions [125,126]. Although Rag1 knockout mice showed no impact on pathology, C3 knockout mice exhibited symptom alleviation, indicating a complement-dependent model. These virus infection models progress rapidly, allowing the evaluation of acute to chronic conditions; however, challenges include the occurrence of fatalities related to infection and difficulties in handling the viruses. Due to the activation of the acquired immune system by viruses, T-cell involvement, and the complement system, these models encompass the PM/DM features.

The IMNM model, proposed by Boyer et al. in 2019., involves inducing the disease by transferring IgG derived from patients with IMNM-positive anti-SRP or anti-HMGCR antibodies into C57BL/6 mice [127]. Pathologically, similar to IMNM, the model exhibited a characteristic histopathological profile with necrosis-like muscle cell damage accompanied by macrophage infiltration and complement deposition. In addition, this model allows the evaluation of muscle weakness. The importance of autoantibodies and complement in pathophysiology is highlighted by alleviating symptoms in C3 knockout mice, accelerating pathophysiology by transferring human complement along with IgG, and the therapeutic effects of C5 and FcRn inhibitors [128]. Although the requirement to transfer IgG derived from human patients poses a challenge, this model is considered important for its resemblance to human IMNM in terms of the significance of autoantibodies and complement in pathophysiology and its phenotypic similarities.

In 2007, Ascherman’s group reported a myositis model induced by immunizing with the Jo-1 protein as an antigen [87]. Jo-1 is an ARS protein, and anti-Jo-1 antibodies are known autoantibodies specific to myositis [58]. Despite the absence of direct evidence of the pathogenicity of anti-Jo-1 antibodies, some studies have reported a correlation between anti-Jo-1 antibody titers and disease activity [129,130]. C57BL/6 or NOD mice were immunized on the tail base with human or mouse Jo-1 protein emulsified with CFA, leading to the onset within 8 weeks. Pathologically, sporadic foci of inflammatory cells were observed in the muscle tissue, with CD44 + CCR5 + T-cell infiltration. Inflammation of the perimysium/epimysium surrounding the blood vessels, as well as inflammation and infiltration/degeneration of the muscle fibers within the endomysium, were also observed. Furthermore, immunization of C57BL/6 mice with mouse Jo-1 protein induced significant interstitial pneumonitis, characterized by T-cell-centered lymphocyte infiltration mainly around blood vessels and bronchioles, extending to some alveoli. In C57BL/6 mice, lung lesions were induced only when the mice were immunized with mouse Jo-1 protein, not with human Jo-1 protein, indicating the importance of epitopes unique to mice. In NOD mice, co-stimulation via OX40 is necessary for disease induction. However, disease induction in Rag2 knockout mice, as observed in wild-type mice, indicates that activation of the innate immune system rather than antigen-specific T-B responses may be the trigger for pathophysiology. Although chemical-induced models have been predominant in ILD models, they are not always suitable for evaluating autoimmune ILD that causes fibrosis through inflammation. However, this model, which induces inflammation and ILD through antigen induction with T-cell activation, is useful as an ASS-ILD model.

In 2024, Okiyama’s group developed a model in which C57BL/6 J mice were immunized with MDA5 protein as an antigen [88]. MDA5 is a protein that functions as an intracellular virus infection sensor belonging to the RIG-I-like receptor family. Anti-MDA5 antibodies are known markers of DM with RP-ILD, especially CADM, which has a poor prognosis among patients with IIMs [131]. In the model, C57BL/6 J mice were immunized with recombinant MDA5 protein and CFA emulsion subcutaneously on the back, followed by intraperitoneal injection of pertussis toxin, and subsequent immunizations with MDA5 and CFA emulsion on the back at weekly intervals three times were administered to induce disease. Pathologically, 35 days after the initial immunization, inflammatory cell infiltration lesions were frequently observed in the lungs, whereas no inflammation was observed in the ears, muscles, or joints. The predominant types of infiltrating inflammatory cells were macrophages and T cells, with CD4 T cells being more dominant than CD8 T cells. Moreover, intranasal administration of poly(I:C) induced acute lung injury, followed by fibrosis after approximately 2 weeks. Experimental antibody administration indicated CD4 T cell-dependent pathology involving type I IFN and IL-6. The high contribution of CD4 T cells and type I IFN indicates that this model is useful as a DM-ILD model.

In 2025, Sigong Zhang’s group developed the myositis-associated interstitial lung disease (MAILD) model [89]. In this model, Balb/c mice were immunized subcutaneously on the back with rat muscle homogenate and CFA emulsion and intraperitoneally with the TLR9 agonist ODN2395 at weekly intervals, leading to the onset within 4–5 weeks. The muscle pathology is mild, characterized by mild immune cell infiltration and muscle fiber sizes, with some mice showing the production of myositis-related autoantibodies, such as anti-SRP and anti-PL-7 antibodies. In contrast, the lung symptoms progressed to severe fibrosis in the MAILD model. Increased TLR9 and IRF3 expression was observed in the lungs of the MAILD model and patients with DM-ILD, indicating the importance of the NET–TLR9–IRF3 pathway in the progression of human DM-ILD lung pathology. Furthermore, NET stimulation increased the expression of TLR9 and IRF3 in A549 human lung cancer epithelial cells. The use of a TLR9 agonist in this MAILD model is considered to closely reflect the DM-ILD pathology. As the newest IIM model, further studies are expected to elucidate the detailed relationship between immune cells and pathology.

In recent years, interest in using humanized mouse models to study disease pathologies has increased. These models involve reconstituting the human immune system in mice by transplanting hPBMCs or hematopoietic stem cells into immunodeficient mice. This approach has gained attention due to the increasing reconsideration of the use of higher-order animals, such as cynomolgus monkeys, in nonclinical trials, as well as the demand for alternative methods. Humanized mouse models are valuable for evaluating nonclinical development products that do not cross-react with rodents, such as mice and rats, and they are considered to have high human extrapolation potential as they allow direct evaluation of in vivo effects on human cells. In autoimmune myositis, our group reported in 2025 a model using NSG mice into which hPBMCs were intravenously transferred, inducing myositis-like pathology [132]. In this model, immune cell infiltration, predominantly CD8 T cells, in the muscle tissue was observed 20–35 days post-transplantation, resembling the pathology observed in patients with PM. Furthermore, increased serum CK, ALT, and AST levels and gene upregulation, such as Icam, Vcam, and Saa1, were observed. The pathology was CD8 T cell-dependent, as it was abrogated in humanized mice in which CD8 T cells were removed from PBMCs. Furthermore, lung pathology was also observed in this model, and prednisolone or tacrolimus treatment suppressed disease onset, indicating its high human extrapolation potential and suitability for evaluating development products, such as antibody formulations.

In myositis mouse models, disease induction by immunization with MSAs autoantigens has long been attempted. However, due to differences in the underlying immune systems, these models have not always accurately reflected the pathological conditions observed in humans. For instance, in the Jo-1 model, a representative ASS model, experiments have indicated that the innate immune system may play a more prominent role than the T–B-cell response. Furthermore, in the TIM model, adoptive transfer experiments have indicated that autoreactive antibodies and CD4 T cells are not essential for disease onset and that the model depends on CD8 T cells, revealing a pathogenic mechanism similar to that of PM. To address these discrepancies, novel models utilizing patient samples or employing humanized mice should be established. Notably, the IMNM model, wherein patient-derived autoantibodies are administered, closely replicates human pathology not only in terms of histological features, such as necrosis-like muscle cell injury, but also in pathogenic mechanisms involving complement activation, autoantibodies, and macrophages. Humanized mouse models established using patient-derived PBMCs can elevate autoantibody levels in circulation, potentially overcoming the limitations of previous disease models. In addition to models involving the administration of autoantibodies and humanized mice, the range of available myositis models is expanding. In particular, models that recapitulate pulmonary manifestations have been reported and are expected to contribute to the development of therapeutic drugs to address unmet medical needs in patients with IIM. Moreover, utilizing pathogenic models in humanized mice enables the evaluation of therapeutic antibodies specifically targeted to human immune cells. Nevertheless, existing DM models that reflect cutaneous symptoms or vasculopathy, as well as those reflecting human cell types other than T cells, remain suboptimal. To improve these models, generating humanized mice reconstituted with a broader array of human immune cells, such as dendritic cells, may be beneficial, which play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of skin symptoms through human stem cell transplantation.

Accurately identifying symptoms, such as muscle weakness and behavioral changes caused by myositis, is challenging because of disease severity and the instability of evaluation metrics, which indicate areas for future improvement. For instance, the overexpression of muscle-specific HLA-DR in our hPBMC mouse model may enhance disease severity. In addition, advancements in single-cell analysis promote a better understanding of the pathophysiology of complex and diverse myositis, such as in the myositis spectrum. Moreover, comparative analyses among the different subtypes have yielded more robust evidence, such as the involvement of type I IFN in DM and CD8 T cells in ILD. Although the molecular mechanisms associated with each subtype of IIM are gradually being elucidated, understanding them in mouse models remains insufficient, and further research is needed. The maturation of comparative analysis methods for omics data from human and mouse pathologies is crucial for improving disease models. A comprehensive gene analysis comparing similarities between our newly established hPBMC-induced myositis mouse models and various subtypes have proposed a methodology. Integrating not only the transcriptome but also the proteome and tissue omics data for similarity comparisons may enhance accuracy and potentially result in the development of methods applicable for patient differentiation in clinical settings. Expectations are high for further understanding the immunological mechanisms in the IIM field and the improvement of mouse models that better reflect human pathologies.
