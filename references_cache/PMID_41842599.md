# Hearing characteristics of Branchio-oto-renal syndrome in Japan.
**Authors:** Goto SI, Sasaki A, Nishio SY, Morita SY, Ogasawara N, Kobayashi Y, Amano A, Shinkawa C, Oda K, Wada T, Ikezono T, Matsuda H, Fujisaka M, Nagai K, Yoshimura H, Kashio A, Nishiyama N, Ito T, Tajima S, Oka SI, Kaga K, Takeda H, Kobayashi M, Sano H, Arai Y, Nakanishi H, Koizumi H, Obara N, Yoshida T, Esaki T, Takeuchi K, Yamazaki H, Horie R, Ohta Y, Morimoto C, Uehara N, Naito Y, Maeda Y, Ishino T, Egusa K, Sugahara K, Teraoka M, Kondo E, Tsuchihashi N, Kihara C, Kanda Y, Nakamura T, Miyanohara I, Kondo S, Usami SI
**Journal:** Acta Otolaryngol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1080/00016489.2026.2635665](https://doi.org/10.1080/00016489.2026.2635665)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome is characterized by branchiogenic malformation, hearing loss, and renal anomalies, with EYA1, SIX1, and SIX5 known as the causative genes. As BOR syndrome presents with various clinical phenotypes, its characteristics and genotype-phenotype correlations remain unknown.

AIMS/OBJECTIVES: In this study, we aimed to clarify the detailed hearing loss phenotypes and genotype-phenotype correlations of BOR syndrome.

MATERIAL AND METHODS: In this study, we performed an etiological analysis of 169 BOR syndrome patients from 129 families. We also performed genetic testing for 78 probands.

RESULTS: In all, 66.7% of BOR patients carried EYA1 variants, whereas 17.9% carried SIX1 variants. We also clarified the detailed clinical features including the prevalence of major and minor symptoms, asymmetrical hearing loss, type of hearing loss, severity of hearing loss and detailed clinical characteristics of auricular, external ear, and middle ear and inner ear anomalies. In terms of genotype-phenotype correlations, patients with SIX1 variants had no kidney anomalies and fewer middle ear anomalies.

CONCLUSIONS AND SIGNIFICANCE: We clarified the detailed hearing loss phenotypes of BOR syndrome patients. Our study results will contribute to a better understanding and clinical management of BOR syndrome patients.

Introduction
Branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome is an autosomal 
dominant (AD) disorder characterized by branchiogenic 
malformation, hearing loss (HL), and renal anomalies 
[1,2]. The presence of branchiogenic malformation and 
HL without renal anomalies is defined as branchio-otic 
(BO) syndrome. BOR syndrome and BO syndrome are 
considered to be disorders within the same spectrum 
[3]. Therefore, they are collectively referred to as BOR 
syndrome here. Chang et  al. reported the diagnostic cri-
teria of BOR syndrome in 2004 [ 4]. The major criteria 
are HL, second branchial arch anomalies, preauricular 
pits, auricular malformation, and renal anomalies. The 
minor criteria are external auditory canal anomalies, 
middle ear anomalies, inner ear anomalies, preauricular 
tags, and others (facial asymmetry, palate abnormali -
ties). Definitive BOR syndrome is defined as (1) the 
presence of at least three major criteria, (2) two major 
criteria and at least two minor criteria, and (3) one 
major criterion with at least one affected first-degree 
relative meeting the criteria for BOR. The prevalence of 
BOR syndrome has been reported to be 1:40,000 births 
in European populations [ 5], and about 250 Japanese 
patients were diagnosed with BOR syndrome between 
2009 and 2010 [ 2].
BOR syndrome is genetically heterogeneous, and 
three genes have been reported to be associated with 
BOR syndrome. The disease-causing gene for BOR1 
and BOS1 is EYA1 (OMIM #113650, #601653), that 
for BOR2 is SIX5 (OMIM #610896), and that for 
BOS3 is SIX1 (OMIM #608389). EYA1 is the most 
common causative gene of BOR syndrome, with 93% 
of BOR syndrome cases for which the causative gene 
is known being attributable to EYA1 variants [ 6]. 
Morisada et  al. reported the results of genetic analysis 
for 12 Japanese families with BOR syndrome and 
showed that an EYA1 variant was identified in 11 
families [ 2]. In contrast, SIX1 is a rare causative gene 
of BOR syndrome, with only 2.4% of BOR syndrome 
cases being attributable to SIX1 variants [ 6].
With regard to the hearing loss phenotypes for BOR 
syndrome, mild-to-profound HL is reported in 64%–
100% of BOR syndrome cases [ 2–4,6,7]. Most cases 
present with congenital HL, but some cases become 
aware of HL post-lingually [8]. The HL presents as con -
ductive (13.0%), sensorineural (30.4%), or mixed 
(56.5%), with anomalies affecting the external auditory 
canal, and middle and inner ear [ 9]. Although most HL 
is non-progressive (70%), a non-insignificant portion of 
HL is known to be progressive (30%) [ 10]. When mid -
dle ear surgery is performed for conductive or 
mixed-type HL, only about one-third of cases show 
clinical benefit. In contrast, cochlear implantation (CI) 
is effective, and good performance has been reported 
[8,11]. The major anomalies affecting the pinnae and 
external auditory canal include preauricular pits, lop ear 
malformation, preauricular tag, and atresia or stenosis 
of the external auditory canal [ 12]. The major anoma -
lies affecting the middle ear include malformation, mal -
position, dislocation, fixation of the ossicles, and 
reduction in the size of the middle ear space [ 3,9, 
11,12]. The major anomalies of the inner ear include 
cochlear hypoplasia, dilated internal auditory canals, 
enlarged vestibular aqueduct (EV A), and hypoplasia of 
the lateral semicircular canal (LSCC) [ 3,9–13]. 
Furthermore, an unwound cochlea is a commonly 
observed characteristic of BOR syndrome [ 14–16]. 
However, there are no reports about the relationship 
between HL type and anomalies of the middle or inner 
ear. Although BOR syndrome presents with various 
phenotypes, genotype-  
phenotype correlations in BOR syndrome remain 
unclear, even within the same family [ 7].
We performed a nationwide epidemiologic survey 
of BOR syndrome in Japan to clarify its detailed clin -
ical features and associated genetic variants in patients. 
Here, we report the main clinical characteristics, caus -
ative gene variants, detailed HL characteristics, and 
anomalies associated with BOR syndrome.

Materials and methods
Subjects
The clinical data for 169 patients with BOR syndrome 
were registered using a multicenter electronic data col -
lection form (EDCF) between September 2021 and 
August 2023. Forty-five university hospitals and medi -
cal centers participated in this epidemiologic survey. 
Written informed consent was obtained from all 
patients (or from their next of kin, caregiver, or legal 
guardian in the case of minors or children) prior to 
undertaking the genetic analysis. This study was 
approved by the Shinshu University Ethics Committee 
(approval number 6106) as well as the respective Ethics 
Committees of the other participating institutions.
Clinical evaluations
Clinical information, including age at awareness, pro -
gression of HL, details of characteristic symptoms of 
BOR syndrome, hearing thresholds, and treatments 
and outcomes, were obtained from medical charts. 
Pure-tone average (PTA) was calculated from the 
audiometric thresholds at four frequencies (0.5, 1, 2, 
and 4 kHz). The severity of hearing loss was divided 
into four categories: mild (PTA: 26–40 dB HL), mod -
erate (41–70 dB HL), severe (71–90 dB HL), and pro -
found (>91 dB HL). Asymmetrical HL was defined as 
a difference in PTA of over 20 dB between the right 
and left ears. Types of HL were defined as follows: (1) 
conductive HL: normal bone-conduction thresholds 
(< 20 dB), and an air–bone gap ≥ 15 dB HL averaged 
over 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz; (2) sensorineural HL: an air–
bone gap < 15 dB HL averaged over the three fre -
quencies; and (3) mixed HL: bone–conduction 
threshold > 20 dB HL, together with an air–bone gap 
≥ 15 dB HL averaged over the three frequencies [ 13]. 
We adopted Chang’s criteria to diagnose BOR syn -
drome [ 4]. Furthermore, we also included patients 
presenting with two major criteria and possessing 
causative gene variants. Statistical analyses were per -
formed using Fisher’s exact test or the Chi-square test 
with IBM SPSS Statistics ver.29 (IBM Corp. Armonk, 
NY). A p-value <0.05 was considered significant.
Next‑generation sequencing and bioinformatic 
analysis
An amplicon library was prepared with an Ion 
AmpliSeq Custom Panel (ThermoFisher Scientific, 
Waltham, MA, USA) for 158 target genes reported to 
cause non-syndromic hearing loss and syndromic hear -
ing loss, which included all three known causative 
genes ( EYA1, SIX1, and SIX5) for BOR syndrome. We 
referred to the Hereditary Hearing Loss homepage 
(https://hereditaryhearingloss.org) to select these genes. 
The emulsion PCR and next-generation sequencing 
were performed with an Ion Proton system or Ion S5 
system (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the 
manufacturer’s instructions. The sequence data were 
mapped against the human genome sequence (build 
GRCh37/hg19) with the Torrent Mapping Alignment 
Program. After sequencing mapping, the DNA variants 
were piled up with Torrent Variant Caller plug-in soft -
ware. After variant detection, variant effects were ana -
lyzed using the ANNOV AR software [ 17,18]. After the 
filtering process described previously [ 19], we per -
formed confirmation of the identified variants and 
family segregation analysis by Sanger sequencing. CNV 
analysis was performed using NGS read depth data 
according to a previously described method [ 20]. The 
pathogenicity of identified variants was analyzed in 
accordance with the American College of Medical 
Genetics (ACMG) standards and guidelines [ 21] with 
the ClinGen Hearing Loss Clinical Domain Working 
Group expert specification [ 22].
Results
Patient background and identified variants
Detailed clinical information for 169 BOR syndrome 
patients from 129 independent families was registered 
in the electronic data collection database from 45 par -
ticipating institutions. Seventy-seven were male 
(45.6%) and 87 were female (51.5%), and gender 
information was unavailable for five patients. Among 
the 169 patients, we could obtain family history infor -
mation for 133 patients. AD was the most common 
inheritance pattern, accounting for 60.4% of cases, 
whereas 18.3% were sporadic cases without any 
affected family member. Regarding the age at aware -
ness of symptoms, prelingual onset (0–5 years old) 
was most common, with 63.9% of cases presenting 
with some symptoms under age 6. In addition, 13.0% 
of cases reported childhood to adolescence onset 
(6–18 years old) and 7.1% of cases reported adult 
onset (over 19 years old).
Among the 129 families, genetic testing was per -
formed for 78 probands (60.5%). EYA1 (BOS1, BOR1) 
was the most commonly identified causative gene, 
with 52 probands (66.7%) carrying EYA1 variants. 
SIX1 (BOS3) gene variants were observed in 14 pro -
bands (17.9%), and we could not identify any cases 
with SIX5 variants. We identified 27 variants (includ -
ing one copy loss) from 52 probands with

Discussion
In the present study, we investigated 169 Japanese 
BOR syndrome patients, making this one of the larg -
est studies of BOR syndrome to date. Through this 
large cohort, we clarified the causative gene variants, 
clinical phenotypes, and detailed clinical features, 
including anomalies of the auricle, external ear, mid -
dle ear and inner ear in BOR syndrome.
The causative gene variants were identified in 
84.6% of the 78 probands who received genetic test -
ing. The detection rates in previous reports ranged 
from 36.3% to 72.2% ( Table 2 ) [ 6,7,9,12]. These dif -
ferences might reflect the inclusion criteria of each 
study. Unzaki et  al. reported a higher diagnostic rate 
(72.2%) in definitive BOR cases fulfilling Chang’s cri -
teria [ 7]. On the other hand, Krung et  al. and Masuda 
et  al. reported relatively lower diagnostic rates as their 
cohorts included atypical BOR syndrome patients 
who only partially fulfilled the diagnostic criteria 
[6,9]. In this study, we included definitive BOR syn -
drome patients as well as suspected cases; however, 
81.6% of this study cohort were definitive cases. We 
speculate that the relatively higher genetic diagnostic 
yield in our study reflects the higher prevalence of 
definite cases. EYA1 is known to be the most fre -
quently observed causative gene, detected in 33.9 to 
88.4% of BOR patients in previous reports [ 4,6,7,9, 
12,13,23]. We also identified EYA1 as a frequent caus -
ative gene, with 66.7% of probands carrying EYA1 
variants. Most of the identified EYA1 gene variants 
were loss-of-function variants (nonsense, splicing, 
frameshift insertion, frameshift deletion, and one copy 
loss), suggesting that haplo-insufficiency is the 
disease-causing mechanism for EYA1-associated BOR 
syndrome. SIX1 gene variants were identified in 17.9% 
of probands in this study, and the prevalence of SIX1 
gene variants in our cohort was relatively higher  
than in previous reports ( Table 2 ). Interestingly, 
SIX1-associated BOR cases showed relatively milder 
phenotypes, and none of the SIX1-associated BOR 
cases in this study had renal anomalies, second bran -
chial arch anomalies, or external ear anomalies. In 
addition, the prevalences of fistulae, auricle anomalies, 
middle ear anomalies, and inner ear anomalies for 
SIX1-associated cases were lower than those for 
EYA1-associated cases ( Table 2, Table 3). According to 
a previous report, BOR patients with SIX1 variants 
did not have renal anomalies [ 24] and have fewer 
temporal bone anomalies compared to those with 
EYA1 variants [ 25]. Although the associated symp -
toms could vary in BOR syndrome patients, even 
within the same family [ 6,18, 26–28], the absence of 
renal anomalies (BO syndrome) may represent a clin -
ical feature of SIX1-associated cases.
We also analyzed the prevalence of major symp -
toms of BOR syndrome and compared these with 
previous reports ( Table 2). HL was the most common 
symptom (93.3%), followed by fistulae (80.4%). The 
prevalences of HL and fistulae were comparable to 
those in previous reports [ 2,6,7,9,12]. The prevalence 
of second branchial arch anomalies was lower than 
that in previous reports, while the prevalence of renal 
anomalies was 27.8% in this study, which is also rel -
atively lower than that in previous reports. However, 
the prevalence of renal anomalies varies among previ -
ous reports ( Table 2 ) [ 2,6,7,9,12]. Plausible explana -
tions for the lower prevalence of renal anomalies in 
this study include the fact that our cohort was enrolled 
through 45 participating otorhinolaryngology depart -
ments. In general, patients with normal renal function 
tend to attend otolaryngology departments, and 
patients with renal dysfunction tend to visit pediatric 
or nephrology departments. One of the limitations of 
our study was that we could not obtain detailed infor -
mation regarding renal anomalies for 72 patients. 
Cooperation among multiple clinical departments is 
necessary to accurately reveal the prevalence of renal 
anomalies.
In the most recent study, BOR syndrome was diag -
nosed using Chang’s criteria [ 4]. These criteria cover 
four major and five minor symptoms of BOR syn -
drome but do not include the results of genetic test -
ing. As BOR syndrome exhibits various phenotypes, 
even among patients with the same variant, there are 
several cases that only partially fulfill the diagnostic 
criteria [ 26]. Now, as genetic testing is commonly 
used as a method for the accurate diagnosis of many

In one of the remarkable results of this study, we 
accurately showed the prevalence of asymmetrical HL, 
progression of HL, type of HL, severity of HL, and 
detailed clinical characteristics of auricular, external 
ear, middle ear and inner ear anomalies. Most cases 
in the study cohort received CT or MRI imaging, 
enabling us to show more accurately the prevalence of 
middle ear and inner ear anomalies. In this study, 
over 60% of BOR syndrome patients had middle and 
inner ear anomalies (63.8% and 71.1%, respectively). 
These findings suggest that middle and inner ear 
anomalies should be reconsidered as potential major 
diagnostic criteria. With regard to the details of mid -
dle ear anomalies, malformation and fixation of the 
ossicles were commonly observed and many patients 
had multiple anomalies of the middle ear [ 3]. 
Furthermore, low tegmen, dysplasia of the incus short 
process, and poor pneumatization of the mastoid were 
reported [ 29,30]. In our study, one case presented 
with malformations of the middle ear and congenital 
cholesteatoma. Although there are few reports, BOR 
syndrome with congenital cholesteatoma has occa -
sionally been observed [ 31,32]. In such cases, surgery 
must be performed with caution as complications 
related to middle ear malformations are relatively 
common. Regarding the details of inner ear anoma -
lies, cochlear hypoplasia was prevalent and some cases 
had multiple anomalies, including cochlear hypoplasia 
with EV A or hypoplasia of the LSCC and other anom -
alies [ 29,30,33]. We also investigated the relationship 
between middle and inner ear anomalies and type of 
HL. BOR syndrome patients with only middle ear 
anomalies tend to have conductive (41.7%) or mixed 
(37.5%) HL. On the other hand, BOR patients with 
only inner ear anomalies tend to have sensorineural 
HL (66.7%). Further, patients with both middle and 
inner ear anomalies tend to have mixed HL (55.7%). 
To our knowledge, no previous studies have examined 
the relationship between middle or inner ear anoma -
lies and types of hearing loss for BOR patients.
As for the treatment for HL, it has been reported 
that the results of middle ear surgery such as tympa -
noplasty, stapedotomy, and stapedectomy were not 
beneficial [ 8,34,35]. One proposed explanation for the 
limited benefit of middle ear surgery is complications 
related to middle ear malformations. As several mid -
dle ear malformations may be present in one patient; 
for example, irregular stapes angle or a thickened 
long process, this could interfere with prosthesis 
fixation or sound transmission. Inner ear malforma -
tion may also potentially contribute to poor results 
following middle ear surgery. In particular, EV A was 
strongly related to the poor results of exploratory 
tympanotomy, which may act as a pathological ‘third 
window’ , preventing effective energy transmission in 
the inner ear despite proper middle ear reconstruc -
tion [ 36].
In this study cohort, 13 cases received middle ear 
surgery, but only three cases showed improved hear -
ing after surgery. Some patients received a 
bone-anchored hearing aid (BAHA) due to insuffi -
cient hearing improvement after middle ear surgery. 
Based on these findings, middle ear surgery for con -
ductive or mixed HL in BOR syndrome patients is 
not recommended. Furthermore, beneficial responses 
were reported after CI and BAHA in BOR syndrome 
patients [ 8,11, 37,38]. Further study is needed to clar -
ify the benefits of hearing implants including CI, 
middle ear implantation, and BAHA, for BOR syn -
drome patients.
Conclusion
Although BOR syndrome is a rare syndrome, we were 
able to identify many clinical characteristics based on 
registration data obtained through a nationwide col -
laboration in Japan. This study is one of the largest 
studies of BOR syndrome patients to our knowledge 
and we were able to clarify a number of clinical char -
acteristics associated with this syndrome. The pre -
dominant type of HL reflects the region of 
malformation in the middle and/or inner ears. It may 
be possible to reconsider the diagnostic criteria of 
BOR syndrome to include genetic testing, and desig -
nate middle and inner ear anomalies as major criteria. 
We also revealed that BOR patients with SIX1 gene 
variants may lack renal anomalies and have fewer 
middle ear anomalies.
