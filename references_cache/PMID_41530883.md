# Thoracic infective native aortic aneurysm and T9-T10 spondylodiscitis complicating recurrent Salmonella enterica bacteremia in a 95-year-old: a case report.
**Authors:** Sarr A, Houari S, Nghiem HT, Tounkara S, Diab H, Arrouasse Y, Mezghani A, Diop M, Rouyer M, Diamantis S
**Journal:** J Med Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13256-025-05769-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13256-025-05769-z)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Non-typhoidal Salmonella bacteremia in elderly patients is uncommon but carries a high risk of vascular and skeletal complications. Infected native aortic aneurysms are rare and associated with high mortality.

CASE PRESENTATION: We report the case of a 95-year-old Caucasian man who was admitted after a domestic fall with abdominal discomfort and chills. Blood cultures repeatedly grew Salmonella enterica (full susceptibility). Computed tomography angiography suggested a distal thoracic aortic aneurysm with suspected vertebral involvement. Fludeoxyglucose F18 positron-emission tomography/computed tomography confirmed intense focal uptake of the distal thoracic aortic wall and synchronous uptake at T9-T10 consistent with an infected aortic aneurysm and spondylodiscitis. Transesophageal echocardiography excluded valvular endocarditis and showed a left atrial appendage thrombus. Because surgery was contraindicated due to prohibitive operative risk, the patient received intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g/day) for 10 days followed by oral amoxicillin 2 g three times daily for 6 weeks. Clinical and biological improvement were observed and there was no recurrence during follow-up.

CONCLUSION: This case highlights the diagnostic utility of positron-emission tomography/computed tomography in recurrent Salmonella bacteremia and supports the role of individualized conservative management in frail elderly patients when surgery is not feasible.

Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) has been identified as a cause of invasive bacterial infections in elderly individuals and those with compromised immune systems. Complications include endovascular infection, vertebral osteomyelitis, and albeit infrequently, mycotic aneurysms. Infected native aortic aneurysms (INAA) are associated with elevated morbidity and mortality rates, frequently necessitating a combination of medical and surgical interventions [ 1 , 2 ]. We present a case of recurrent Salmonella enterica bacteremia in a 95-year-old male patient complicated by both INAA and T9–T10 spondylodiscitis, managed conservatively due to prohibitive surgical risk.

A 95-year-old Caucasian man with a history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation with pacemaker, ischemic heart disease, hypothyroidism, and dyslipidemia was transferred to our Infectious Diseases Unit after experiencing three episodes of recurrent Salmonella enterica bacteremia. The chronological microbiological results associated investigations are summarized in Table 1. He was admitted following a domestic fall, reporting abdominal discomfort and chills, without fever or diarrhea.

On admission, he was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. Physical examination revealed mild abdominal tenderness. Laboratory studies showed elevated C-reactive protein, normocytic anemia, hyponatremia, and hypoalbuminemia. Blood cultures yielded Salmonella enterica, fully susceptible to ceftriaxone, amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.

CT angiography demonstrated a distal thoracic dissecting aneurysm with suspected vertebral involvement (Fig. 1A). Because the patient presented with recurrent bacteremia and CT-angiography findings suggestive of a dissecting thoracic aneurysm with possible adjacent vertebral involvement, we performed fludeoxyglucose F18 positron-emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) to localize metabolically active infectious foci and to search for synchronous lesions. PET/CT is particularly useful when persistent or recurrent bacteremia raises strong suspicion of endovascular infection and when conventional imaging may underestimate the extent of disease. In our case, PET/CT demonstrated intense focal FDG uptake along the distal thoracic aortic wall with periaortic soft-tissue involvement, and concomitant uptake at the T9–T10 endplates and disc (Fig. 1B—pattern highly suggestive of an infected aneurysm and spondylodiscitis, respectively). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was subsequently performed and suggested a possible mitral vegetation; given the superior sensitivity of transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), a transesophageal study was performed and excluded valvular endocarditis, identifying instead a left atrial appendage thrombus. The main diagnostic challenge was to differentiate degenerative aneurysmal changes from infectious involvement. Given the patient’s advanced age, frailty, and the presence of a thoracic infected native aortic aneurysm with synchronous spondylodiscitis, the overall prognosis was considered poor. However, individualized conservative management with close monitoring offered a reasonable chance of stabilization and infection control.

The patient was judged unfit for surgery because of prohibitive operative risk. He received intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g/day) for 10 days, followed by oral amoxicillin (2 g three times daily) for 6 weeks. Amoxicillin was chosen for its excellent bioavailability, documented susceptibility, and favorable tolerance in frail elderly patients compared with fluoroquinolones.

The patient was followed clinically and biologically with serial C-reactive protein (CRP) measurements. Recommended follow-up plan: clinical review and CRP at 3, 6, and 12 weeks; blood cultures if fever or clinical deterioration; repeat CT angiography at 12 weeks to assess aneurysm size and periaortic changes; consider repeat 18F-FDG PET/CT if persistent symptoms or inflammatory markers. At the last follow-up (3 weeks), the patient remained afebrile with normal CRP and negative blood cultures.

No adverse or unanticipated events occurred during treatment. The antibiotic regimen was well tolerated, with no reported gastrointestinal, hematologic, or hepatic side effects.

This case illustrates the multifaceted challenges associated with invasive Salmonella infections in elderly patients. Recurrent bacteremia warrants thorough investigation to identify deep-seated foci, particularly vascular and vertebral sites [ 1 , 2 ].

Imaging is central in such cases. CT angiography remains the standard for vascular infection, while 18F-FDG PET/CT has demonstrated high sensitivity for detecting metabolically active infection, synchronous lesions, and monitoring therapeutic response [ 3– 5 ]. In our case, PET/CT confirmed both aneurysm and vertebral involvement, findings that may have been missed by conventional imaging alone. Transthoracic echocardiography suggested possible mitral vegetation, given the superior sensitivity of TEE, a transesophageal study was performed and excluded valvular endocarditis, identifying instead a left atrial appendage thrombus [ 6 , 7 ].

Surgical excision with in situ or extra-anatomic reconstruction combined with prolonged antimicrobial therapy remains the standard approach for infected aortic aneurysms because it permits source control and reduces relapse risk [ 4 ]. However, operative mortality and morbidity are substantial in elderly and frail patients, and outcomes may be poor in this population [ 8 ]. Reported series indicate that selected patients unfit for surgery may achieve short-term stabilization with prolonged targeted antibiotic therapy and close imaging surveillance, although the long-term risk of relapse or late rupture persists and must be weighed against surgical risk [ 9 ]. A multidisciplinary decision (infectious disease, vascular surgery, geriatrics/anesthesia) is therefore essential to individualize care. In this patient, the decision to manage conservatively was based on prohibitive operative risk and was accompanied by an intensive follow-up plan to monitor for relapse or progression.

This case underscores the importance of investigating recurrent Salmonella enterica bacteremia for potential vascular or skeletal foci, particularly in frail elderly patients. PET/CT played a decisive role in identifying both the infected aneurysm and synchronous spondylodiscitis, supporting its value in guiding individualized management [ 10 ].

When surgery is contraindicated, as in our patient, prolonged targeted antimicrobial therapy under close clinical and imaging surveillance can achieve satisfactory short-term outcomes. Nevertheless, the risk of relapse or late aneurysm rupture remains, requiring long-term follow-up. Our case highlights the feasibility and limitations of conservative treatment in this context and reinforces the need for multidisciplinary decision-making.

In elderly patients with recurrent Salmonella bacteremia, vascular and skeletal complications should be actively investigated. PET/CT is a valuable tool for identifying synchronous infective foci. When surgical intervention is not feasible, prolonged targeted antibiotic therapy with close monitoring may achieve stabilization.
