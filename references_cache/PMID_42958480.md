# Marburg Virus Disease (MVD): a rapid systematic review with narrative synthesis of epidemiology, clinical presentation, diagnostics, management, and public-health response.
**Authors:** Asmare DS
**Journal:** BMC Infect Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12879-026-13959-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13959-3)

## Content

Marburg virus (MARV) is responsible for a severe viral hemorrhagic fever characterized by high case-fatality rates and epidemic potential. Recent outbreaks from 2021 to 2025 across various African nations have brought renewed global focus. We conducted a focused search across PubMed/PMC, WHO, CDC, NEJM, Lancet, Frontiers, and key public health sources up to 20 Nov 2025, using consistent search terms. We compiled evidence related to epidemiology, clinical features, diagnostics, management, prevention, and identified knowledge gaps. Priority was given to primary outbreak reports, systematic reviews, and WHO guidance. MARV reservoirs are Egyptian fruit bats; human infections result from zoonotic spillover and subsequent human-to-human transmission, particularly via direct contact with bodily fluids and funeral practices. Case-fatality rates vary widely (roughly 24-88% historically), and average estimates are near 50% when aggregated across outbreaks. Clinical presentation ranges from abrupt febrile illness with gastrointestinal symptoms to coagulopathy, multi-organ failure, and hemorrhage. RT-PCR and antigen detection are the mainstays of early diagnosis; supportive care is the primary treatment. No licensed vaccine or antiviral existed as of November 2025, but several candidates and monoclonal antibody therapies are in development and clinical testing. Outbreak control relies on rapid surveillance, contact tracing, infection prevention and control (IPC) in healthcare settings, safe burial practices, and community engagement. Marburg virus remains a high-consequence pathogen with substantial morbidity and mortality. Recent outbreaks have reinforced the need for rapid diagnostics, robust IPC in health facilities, accelerated clinical trials for therapeutics and vaccines, and improved ecological surveillance of bat populations and human exposures. Strengthening outbreak readiness and integrating MVD preparedness into routine public-health systems in at-risk countries should be a global priority.

Marburg virus (MARV), a member of the Filoviridae family that is genetically similar to Ebola virus, is the causative agent of Marburg Virus Disease (MVD), a virulent viral hemorrhagic fever that was first identified in 1967 during laboratory-associated outbreaks in Germany and the former Yugoslavia [1]. Since then, MVD has continued to appear intermittently, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, where it produces intermittent but often severe outbreaks characterized by fast clinical deterioration, broad systemic involvement, and high case fatality rates that can surpass 80% in resource-constrained environments [1–3]. Although these outbreaks are rare, they have significant public-health implications because of the virus’s ability to spread from person to person, particularly in healthcare settings, its potential for nosocomial amplification, and the lack of licensed antiviral therapies or widely accessible diagnostic platforms [4].

Between 2021 and 2025, MARV activity increased significantly, with confirmed outbreaks in Equatorial Guinea, Ghana, Rwanda, and Tanzania, indicating both greater geographic dispersion and enhanced diagnostic identification [5]. In December 2024, the World Health Organization announced enhanced diagnostic and surveillance advice, emphasizing prompt diagnosis, standardized case definitions, and improved epidemic preparedness [6]. Collectively, these changes underline the critical need for a current, comprehensive synthesis of epidemiological evidence, clinical features, diagnostic techniques, and public-health measures to advise frontline practitioners, outbreak response teams, and health-policy stakeholders [6].

This study was conducted as a rapid systematic review with narrative synthesis, designed to provide a timely and structured summary of available evidence on Marburg Virus Disease (MVD). Due to the rapid review approach, certain components of a full systematic review following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines were simplified. Specifically, screening and data extraction were conducted by a single reviewer, and no formal meta-analysis was performed. Findings are therefore presented using a descriptive, thematic narrative synthesis.

All records identified through database and grey literature searches were exported and screened based on title and abstract. Potentially relevant articles were retrieved for full-text review. After removal of duplicates, a total of 540 records were screened, of which 385 were excluded based on title/abstract. Full texts of 155 articles were assessed for eligibility, and 53 studies were included in the final synthesis. A simplified PRISMA flow diagram is provided (Fig. 1).

A formal risk-of-bias assessment was not conducted, in keeping with the rapid review design and the heterogeneity of included sources (including outbreak reports, surveillance data, and narrative reviews). However, priority was given to high-quality sources, including WHO reports, CDC guidance, peer-reviewed journal articles, and primary outbreak investigations.

For each included work, we extracted: publication year, location, study design, case counts, CFR, clinical features, diagnostic method, interventions, and outcomes. Given heterogeneity in designs and outbreak sizes, a descriptive, thematic synthesis is presented rather than a meta-analysis.

The period spanning 2021 to 2025 is marked by an increased frequency of MVD outbreaks and the emergence of the virus in countries that had not previously recorded cases, signaling a broader, geographically unstable spillover risk across Sub-Saharan Africa [6]. The succession of first-time MVD outbreaks in new countries—such as Guinea in 2021 [7], Equatorial Guinea (EG) in 2023 [8], Tanzania in 2023 [8], and Ethiopia in 2025 [9], suggests that preparedness efforts must evolve from targeting historically endemic zones (e.g., DRC or Uganda) toward a generalized regional risk assessment. This mandate continuous, coordinated cross-border surveillance and robust response mechanisms, as demonstrated by the rapid coordination efforts convened by the Intergovernmental Authority on Development (IGAD) following the 2025 Ethiopia confirmation [10]. In 2025, two additional outbreaks were reported. A 2025 outbreak in Tanzania resulted in 10 deaths [8]. Most critically, in November 2025, Ethiopia confirmed its first MVD outbreak in the Southern Region, with the initial alert shared on November 12, 2025 [9]. As of November 20, 2025, response measures were fully activated, involving the testing of 28 cases, with six confirmed and three resulting in death [9]. Over 129 suspected contacts were traced across Hawassa, Jinka, and Sodo cities [9].

The historical CFR variance (24% to 88%) is stark.1 This disparity is clearly reflected in the 2023–2024 data: the Equatorial Guinea outbreak resulted in an 88% CFR (40 cases, 35 deaths), while the subsequent Rwanda outbreak yielded a substantially lower 23% CFR (66 cases, 15 deaths) [11]. This evidence demonstrates that MVD mortality is not solely a function of viral strain virulence, but is profoundly influenced by the quality, speed, and comprehensiveness of the public health and clinical response [12]. The vast difference in outcomes, a reduction of over 60% points in mortality provides operational proof that early, robust supportive care and effective public health containment (isolation, contact tracing, and community engagement) are the most impactful current interventions, even in the absence of approved vaccines or specific antivirals. This drives the imperative for continuous investment in Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) and clinical capacity building in at-risk regions [13].

A particularly high-risk scenario for lethal transmission involves healthcare settings. Transmission via contaminated injection equipment or through needle-stick injuries is documented to be associated with more severe disease, rapid deterioration, and potentially a higher fatality rate [1]. This finding emphasizes the critical importance of rigorous IPC protocols for frontline health workers [14]. Furthermore, burial ceremonies that involve direct physical contact with the body of the deceased are recognized as significant drivers of transmission, necessitating the application of specialized safe and dignified burial (SDB) protocols as a core component of outbreak response [15].

The incubation period for MVD ranges from 2 to 21 days following infection.2 Symptoms appear suddenly [16]. The initial presentation is typically non-specific, making clinical distinction from other tropical diseases challenging [16]. Initial symptoms include high fever, chills, severe headache, and debilitating muscle aches [17]. Gastrointestinal manifestations often accompany the onset, including severe nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Chest pain and sore throat are also commonly reported [18].

The clinical course of MVD can be delineated into three chronological phases 3:



Phase 1: Generalised Phase (Days 1–4): This phase is dominated by non-specific systemic symptoms. Patients may develop a non-itchy rash composed of both flat and raised bumps, often appearing on the torso, typically between days 2 and 7 after symptom onset. Early hematological findings are characterized by coagulation defects [19].
Phase 2: Early Organ Phase (Days 5–13): Disease progression accelerates dramatically in this phase. The symptoms become progressively more severe, advancing to liver failure, multi-organ failure, profound hypotension, and shock [20]. Severe cases exhibit neurological symptoms, including agitation, confusion, seizures, and delirium.2 This period sees the onset of severe bleeding or hemorrhage [19].
Phase 3: Late Organ Phase / Convalescence (Days 13+): Patients reach a critical juncture. Death commonly occurs between eight and nine days after the onset of symptoms, typically preceded by severe blood loss and irreversible shock [21]. Patients who survive enter an extended period of recovery and rehabilitation [20].

The hallmark of severe, late-stage MVD is hemorrhagic diathesis, or coagulopathy [19]. This manifest as signs of severe blood loss, ranging from frequent nosebleeds or bleeding gums to spitting, vomiting, or coughing up blood (which may resemble coffee grounds) [19]. Internal bleeding is indicated by black, tarry stool, extensive bruising, and joint swelling. Neurological symptoms such as confusion, severe headaches, or vision loss may signify a brain bleed [19].

The defined timeline of rapid clinical deterioration, peaking in multiorgan failure and terminal events commonly occurring between days 8 and 9, establishes an extremely narrow diagnostic and therapeutic window [21]. Given that initial presentation is non-specific [21], patients often receive diagnosis and treatment only after the therapeutic opportunity for highly effective monotherapies as observed in early animal models has passed. This rapid, lethal trajectory underscores the paramount importance of immediate case identification and aggressive intervention to optimize patient outcomes [19].

Diagnosing MVD based purely on clinical presentation is challenging due to the non-specific nature of early symptoms, which mimic numerous other endemic infectious diseases [22, 23]. These differential diagnoses include malaria, typhoid fever, shigellosis, meningitis, and other viral hemorrhagic fevers [22, 23]. Consequently, clinical suspicion must be elevated and guided by a thorough epidemiological assessment, focusing on known risk factors such as contact with confirmed cases, recent travel to outbreak zones, or prolonged exposure to potential zoonotic habitats like mines or caves [23].

Confirmation that symptoms are caused by Marburg virus infection requires specific laboratory diagnostic methods [23]. According to interim guidance published in December 2024, the goal of diagnostic testing is the timely identification of cases to provide appropriate care and halt transmission [24].

The preferred method for confirmation, which can detect the virus early in the disease course and distinguish between different virus variants, is Nucleic Acid Amplification Testing (NAAT), specifically Reverse Transcriptase Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) assay. NAAT must be used for initial laboratory confirmation of Orthomarburgvirus infections and for further species identification [24].

Antigen-capture ELISA and IgM-capture ELISA: These can also be used to confirm diagnosis within a few days of symptom onset [25].

The recommended sample type for NAAT testing, as stipulated in recent guidance, is whole blood or plasma for living patients, and an oral swab for deceased individuals [25]. All laboratory manipulations of specimens originating from suspected, probable, or confirmed MVD cases must be conducted using appropriate biosafety measures, following a risk-based approach [25].

Rapid outbreak containment necessitates the decentralization of testing capabilities to minimize diagnosis-to-isolation time [26]. The deployment of mobile laboratories to affected areas, such as the operational mobile lab in Jinka, Ethiopia, during the November 2025 outbreak, represents a modern cornerstone of filovirus containment strategy [26]. This strategy expands local testing capacity, reduces the time required for referrals to central facilities, and consequently accelerates the process of case confirmation and isolation [26, 27]. Given the short, lethal clinical course of MVD, delays associated with centralized testing dramatically increase transmission risk. Mobile laboratory deployment collapses the turnaround time for NAAT, enabling the immediate isolation of confirmed cases and enhancing the effectiveness of contact tracing [27].

Beyond case confirmation, robust laboratory capacity supports genomic surveillance. Whole or partial genome sequencing is utilized to characterize the circulating virus strains and complement epidemiologic investigations [28]. This capability is critical for determining the source and connectivity of concurrent outbreaks, such as confirming that there was no evidence of a link between the 2023 outbreaks in Equatorial Guinea and Tanzania.4 Member States are strongly encouraged to share genetic sequence data (GSD) in publicly accessible databases to facilitate global risk assessment and preparedness [29].

MVD is characterized by the absence of currently approved vaccines or specific antiviral treatments. Therefore, the current standard of care is early and intensive supportive management [30]. This regimen is critical, as early supportive care, including aggressive rehydration (intravenous fluids and oral solutions) and symptomatic treatment, demonstrably improves survival rates [31]. Supportive measures focus on managing complications such as hemorrhagic shock, correcting electrolyte and fluid imbalances caused by severe vomiting and diarrhea, and managing symptoms of organ dysfunction.1 If investigational therapeutics are utilized, strict monitoring of patient liver and kidney functions is also essential [31].

The profound variability in CFRs observed globally, particularly the 23% rate in Rwanda 2024 compared to the 88% rate in Equatorial Guinea 2023, underscores the significant life-saving impact of high-quality, early supportive care [32].

A focused research pipeline of antiviral drugs and immunotherapeutic is under development for MVD [33].


Monoclonal Antibodies (mAbs)


Monoclonal antibodies represent a promising class of investigational therapeutics for MVD. Candidates such as MR186 and MR191 have demonstrated high levels of protection in non-human primate (NHP) models, with survival rates ranging from 80% to 100% when administered early in the course of infection [34]. However, human clinical efficacy data remain limited, and no monoclonal antibody therapy has yet been approved for routine clinical use in MVD. Available human experience is largely restricted to compassionate-use settings and outbreak-specific deployments, where controlled efficacy data are lacking [35].

A study evaluating preclinical efficacy highlighted the challenges posed by delayed treatment: while both the antiviral Remdesivir (RDV) and the mAb MR186-YTE protected non-human primates against fatal disease if initiated within five days of infection, initiating treatment at day six post-infection necessitated administering the two treatments in combination to achieve significant protection [36]. This critical finding emphasizes that because patients often present for care late (due to diagnostic challenges), future clinical development must prioritize combination regimens to reverse advanced pathology and maximize human efficacy. Another candidate, MBP-091, is reported to be currently in Phase 1 trials [36].


Antivirals


Several broad-spectrum antivirals are being investigated, including Remdesivir, Favipiravir, and Galidesivir [37]. Based on their use in treating Ebola virus disease (EVD), Remdesivir and Favipiravir may be utilized under compassionate use or expanded access protocols during MVD outbreaks [37]. For instance, Gilead Sciences supplied 5,100 vials of Remdesivir for emergency use during the 2024 Rwanda outbreak. However, clinical trials involving these agents for EVD have generally resulted in limited or inconclusive evidence regarding efficacy. Their deployment in MVD settings mandates strict monitoring of potential adverse effects, including hepatic and renal complications [38].

No MVD vaccines are currently approved by regulatory agencies. However, several candidates are actively progressing through the clinical pipeline [30].

The leading candidate is the single-dose vaccine, rVSV∆G-MARV-GP, developed by IAVI and partners. This vaccine candidate utilizes the Vesicular Stomatitis Virus (VSV) platform, the same technology used for Merck’s licensed Ebola vaccine, Ervebo. Preclinical data for rVSV∆G-MARV-GP are consistently strong, showing that a single intramuscular vaccination completely protected non-human primates against MARV challenge.6 Intranasal administration similarly conferred protection in animal models [39].

Based on these results, a crucial step in the MVD countermeasure pipeline is scheduled for late 2025: a Phase 1 clinical trial (IAVI C104) of the rVSV∆G-MARV-GP vaccine candidate is slated to begin. The use of the established, licensed rVSV platform confers a significant strategic advantage. Since the manufacturing, storage, and logistical requirements (including cold chain) are already standardized through the EVD vaccine experience, rapid deployment through a “Ring Vaccination” strategy, similar to that used in Ebola outbreaks, would be significantly expedited should the safety and immunogenicity data prove successful, as detailed below (Table-2) [39].

The requirement for combination therapy to achieve significant protection when treatment is delayed past the fifth day post-infection underscores a major translational hurdle for specific therapeutics. Since diagnostic challenges inevitably lead to delayed presentation, future efforts must focus on evaluating combination regimens, potentially under robust Managed Emergency Use, Randomized, Controlled Intervention (MEURI) protocols during ongoing outbreaks to efficiently gather definitive human efficacy data [36].

Effective control of MVD outbreaks relies on a comprehensive strategy built upon several interdependent public health pillars [40]. These include: rapid case management, robust surveillance and contact tracing, immediate isolation of confirmed patients, enhanced infection prevention and control (IPC) in healthcare facilities, the provision of good laboratory services, and rigorous social mobilization through risk communication [41].

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are at exceptionally high risk and have been frequently infected when standard IPC precautions were not strictly followed [42]. To mitigate this risk, HCWs must always adhere to standard precautions and apply additional IPC measures for suspected or confirmed MVD patients to prevent contact with infectious body fluids and contaminated materials [43]. Given the documented association between transmission via contaminated injection equipment or needle-stick injuries and more severe disease outcomes and higher fatality rates, strict protocols for safe sharps handling and equipment reprocessing are paramount. Responding agencies prioritize the provision and dispatch of sufficient personal protective equipment (PPEs) and essential supplies to protect frontline staff [44].

Rapid isolation of confirmed MVD patients in designated treatment units is a fundamental measure for interrupting transmission chains.1 Surveillance teams must identify all contacts of infected individuals and monitor their health status for the maximum incubation period of 21 days [45].

Modern outbreak response increasingly relies on integrated technological systems to manage the volume and complexity of tracing. For example, the US CDC has contributed to developing electronic contact tracing modules integrated into the national District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) platform, linked with the national Integrated Diseases Surveillance and Response (IDSR) system in Tanzania [46]. The management of outbreaks, such as the effort to trace 129 suspected cases in the Ethiopia 2025 response, necessitates such robust, integrated surveillance technology to ensure accountability and speed [44].

Community engagement is explicitly recognized as the key to successfully controlling MVD outbreaks. Public health measures like isolation and safe burial protocols are invasive and require high levels of social adherence, making community trust the determining factor in containment success. Failure of RCCE often results in clandestine burials, which accelerate community transmission and undermine surveillance efforts [26].

Burial ceremonies that involve direct physical contact with the deceased are a major documented source of transmission.1 Therefore, the performance of safe and dignified burials (SDBs) by trained teams, utilizing appropriate IPC measures, is consistently recognized as an essential intervention for outbreak control and a specific target for resource allocationc [47].

MVD response requires collaborative, comprehensive, multi-sectoral efforts [27]. International organizations (WHO, Africa CDC, UNICEF, US CDC, INGOs) frequently collaborate with national Ministries of Health [30]. Regional bodies, such as IGAD, also play a vital role by convening emergency meetings to coordinate rapid regional support and preparedness, especially concerning cross-border spillover risk, as demonstrated during the Ethiopia 2025 outbreak [27].

Effective response also relies on continuous capacity building across sectors through training programs, such as the Field Epidemiology Laboratory Training Program (FELTP), covering event-based surveillance, case management, logistics, and psychosocial support [48]. These efforts strongly emphasize the significance of a “One Health” approach, acknowledging that successful outbreak prevention requires integrating activities across human health, animal health, and environmental health, particularly in managing the zoonotic reservoir [27]. Furthermore, the establishment of national electronic surveillance systems (like DHIS2 linked to IDSR) for MVD represents a programmatic advancement, shifting the focus from purely reactive outbreak response to proactive national security preparedness, facilitating earlier detection and faster resource mobilization [30].

This review has several limitations. First, as a rapid systematic review, certain methodological steps—such as duplicate screening and formal risk-of-bias assessment—were not performed. Second, the inclusion of heterogeneous data sources, including surveillance reports and narrative reviews, limits comparability across studies. Third, the absence of meta-analysis prevents quantitative synthesis of outcomes such as case fatality rates. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Marburg Virus Disease remains a highly lethal public health threat, historically demonstrating a pooled case fatality ratio exceeding 60%. The disease is defined by a narrow, critical therapeutic window dictated by its sudden onset and rapid progression to shock and death, typically within 8 to 9 days. The period 2021–2025 has been epidemiologically active, characterized by recurring outbreaks and a concerning trend of geographical expansion, with first-time events documented in Guinea, Equatorial Guinea, Tanzania, and Ethiopia. This regional instability necessitates heightened pan-African preparedness. Clinical outcomes are shown to be highly dependent on the quality of the response. The disparity in CFRs observed (e.g., 23% in Rwanda vs. 88% in Equatorial Guinea) provides compelling evidence that early, intensive supportive care, coupled with rapid isolation achieved through effective surveillance (including mobile laboratories) and robust community engagement, is the single most impactful life-saving intervention currently available. The medical countermeasure pipeline is rapidly advancing. Highly effective monoclonal antibodies (MR186, MR191) have demonstrated near-complete protection in non-human primates, and the leading rVSV vaccine candidate (rVSV∆G-MARV-GP) is scheduled to enter Phase 1 human trials in late 2025.
