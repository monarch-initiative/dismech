and obstructive complications. This study aimed to assess the incidence, 
clinical manifestations, and regression rates of CRs in infants and children 
with TSC, with a focus on genotype-phenotype correlations. METHODS: We conducted 
a retrospective, single-center study of 22 patients with TSC and confirmed CRs, 
admitted to the pediatric unit of Policlinico “Gaspare Rodolico” in Catania 
between 2014 and 2023. Data were collected from medical records, including

CRs are early manifestations of TSC that frequently regress, yet their potential 
for causing hemodynamic and electrical complications necessitates close 
monitoring. Patients with TSC2 mutations appeared to exhibit a trend toward 
faster tumor regression, although this observation requires confirmation in 
larger prospective studies.

Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is a genetic disorder characterized by the formation of hamartomas in multiple organs, including the skin, central nervous system, kidneys, lungs, and heart. It follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern and results from mutations in theTSC1andTSC2genes, located on chromosomes 9q34 and 16p13.3, respectively. These genes encode hamartin and tuberin, key regulators of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, which controls cellular growth and proliferation. Dysregulation of this pathway leads to uncontrolled cell growth, promoting tumor formation in affected tissues. TSC is a rare disorder, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 6,000 live births and a prevalence of 1 in 13,000–30,000 individuals. The disease exhibits variable expressivity, meaning clinical manifestations range from mild to severe, even among individuals carrying the same genetic mutation. Penetrance is nearly complete, but symptoms can differ significantly within families [1,2].

Cardiac involvement is one of the earliest detectable features of TSC, often recognized prenatally or in infancy. The hallmark cardiac abnormality in TSC is rhabdomyoma, a benign tumor of cardiac muscle that can develop in any part of the heart but is most commonly found in the ventricular walls or the interventricular septum. These tumors arise due to dysregulated mTOR signaling, leading to excessive proliferation of myocytes. Cardiac rhabdomyomas occur in up to 80% of individuals with TSC and are often identified during fetal echocardiography, typically between the 20th and 30th weeks of gestation. The number of tumors varies widely, with most patients exhibiting multiple lesions (i.e. ≥ 2), though solitary rhabdomyomas can also occur. The size of these tumors typically ranges from 3 mm to 25 mm. Among neonates with multiple cardiac rhabdomyomas, TSC is present in over 80% of cases, making cardiac imaging a crucial diagnostic tool. The presence of rhabdomyomas in a fetus or newborn strongly suggests TSC, warranting further genetic and clinical evaluation [3].

While many cases of cardiac rhabdomyomas remain asymptomatic, their clinical significance depends on size, location, and number. Small, non-obstructive tumors are often incidental findings and do not require intervention. However, large or strategically positioned tumors can lead to significant cardiac dysfunction through several mechanisms. Obstruction of blood flow can occur when rhabdomyomas are located in the ventricular outflow tracts, leading to valvular stenosis, reduced cardiac output, and, in severe cases, heart failure. Tumors affecting the conduction system, such as those near the atrioventricular node or bundle branches, can result in conduction abnormalities, including atrioventricular block, Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome, or ventricular tachycardia. Large tumors may also impair ventricular contractility, leading to congestive heart failure, particularly in neonates. In rare cases, tumor involvement may contribute to pericardial irritation, resulting in pericardial effusion with the potential for cardiac tamponade [4].

A key characteristic of cardiac rhabdomyomas in TSC is their tendency to regress spontaneously over time. In most cases, tumors shrink within the first years of life, often resolving by early childhood. The exact mechanism of regression remains unclear but is thought to involve myocyte maturation and decreased proliferation signaling. Due to this natural course, surgical intervention is rarely needed unless complications arise. Management is necessary for patients experiencing complications such as arrhythmias, obstruction, or heart failure. Antiarrhythmic therapy is used to manage WPW syndrome or ventricular tachyarrhythmias, while beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers may be indicated in cases of significant conduction disturbances. Surgical resection is rarely required but may be considered in life-threatening obstruction of the ventricular outflow tracts or persistent severe arrhythmias unresponsive to medical therapy. In recent years, mTOR inhibitors such as everolimus and sirolimus have emerged as targeted therapies capable of reducing the size of rhabdomyomas, particularly in symptomatic infants or in patients with severe multisystem involvement. Clinical studies and case reports have demonstrated tumor shrinkage and symptom resolution following treatment, often allowing for the avoidance of surgery or prolonged pharmacologic support [5].

The aim of this retrospective study was to assess the incidence and clinical manifestations, with particular focus on the regression rate, of rhabdomyomas in infants and children with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) and associatedTSC1andTSC2genotypes.

In this retrospective, single-center study, we analyzed a cohort of 22 patients admitted to the pediatric operating unit of the Policlinico “Gaspare Rodolico” in Catania between 2014 and 2023. The cohort comprised 11 males and 11 females, aged between 1 and 28 years (mean age: 11–12 years). This study was conducted in accordance with institutional and national research ethics standards. Ethical approval was obtained from the local ethics committee (ASP of Enna), and written informed consent was acquired from participants or their legal guardians where required. All data were anonymized, and no identifying images or personal details are included.

All patients included in the study had a confirmed diagnosis of TSC, established according to standardized criteria from the Consensus Conference on Tuberous Sclerosis. Cardiac rhabdomyomas were detected in all cases, with diagnosis established via prenatal echocardiography (in 10 patients), neonatal and post-natal ultrasound in all the patients. The patients were selected from a broader cohort of 100 TSC patients followed at our institution, excluding those without cardiac rhabdomyomas to maintain sample homogeneity.

All patients or their legal representatives provided informed consent to participate in the study. Data collection was performed through a detailed review of medical records stored in the hospital archives. Specifically, the following parameters were analyzed: demographic data, findings from fetal and postnatal echocardiography during biannual or annual follow-ups, electrocardiographic findings, genetic analyses of TSC gene mutations, and time to cardiac rhabdomyoma regression. The follow-up period ranged from 1 year to 16 years, with a median duration of 9.9 years. Additional data were obtained via telephone interviews with parents and by requesting relevant clinical documents for research purposes.

Fetal echocardiography was performed during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy using transabdominal ultrasound. The ultrasound systems utilized included GE Vivid 7 and Vivid 9, equipped with multifrequency wide-bandwidth transducers: C2-9 single crystal abdominal convex (2.3–8.4 MHz), 6 S phased-array (2.4–8.0 MHz), 5 S phased-array (3.0–9.0 MHz), and M5Sc active matrix single crystal phased-array (1.5–4.6 MHz). The echocardiographic assessment included 2D imaging, M-mode, anatomical M-mode, color Doppler, pulsed-wave Doppler, and continuous-wave Doppler imaging.

The fetal echocardiographic protocol consisted of standard views: abdominal situs, transverse abdominal view, four-chamber view, left ventricular outflow tract (five-chamber view), right ventricular outflow tract, three-vessel view (3VV), and three-vessel and trachea view (3VT).

High-resolution neonatal and post-neonatal echocardiography was performed using 3.5, 5.0, or 7.5 MHz transducers with a mechanical sector scanner from parasternal, apical, and subcostal windows. Rhabdomyomas were identified as discrete, well-demarcated areas of increased echogenicity within a cardiac chamber or wall, visible in at least two imaging planes. Lesion number, size, and location were recorded. Doppler studies were performed to assess for potential hemodynamic compromise or outflow tract obstruction.

Standard 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) were obtained in all postnatal patients. In addition, 24-hour Holter ECG monitoring was performed in all patients in the study group at least once during the neonatal or post-neonatal period, particularly in cases where arrhythmias were suspected based on clinical signs or standard ECG abnormalities.

Patients underwent serial follow-up including echocardiography and ECG at predefined intervals: during the neonatal period (within the first two weeks of life), and subsequently at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months of age. After the first year, evaluations were conducted annually or more frequently if clinically indicated—for example, in the presence of tumor progression, arrhythmias, or hemodynamic compromise. Holter monitoring was repeated in selected cases, particularly when persistent or new-onset arrhythmias were observed.

Cases showing a reduction of 10–49% in the number or size of cardiac rhabdomyomas, as assessed by serial echocardiographic measurements, were classified as having partial regression. Cases with no appreciable change over time (less than 10% reduction) were considered stable or showing no regression. In contrast, a reduction greater than 50% in the largest tumor diameter or total tumor burden was classified as marked regression. When all previously documented lesions were no longer detectable on follow-up imaging, the case was categorized as having achieved complete regression.

Genetic testing was performed using next-generation sequencing (NGS), combining whole-exome and targeted panel approaches to comprehensively analyze theTSC1andTSC2genes. This included coverage of all coding regions and relevant intronic boundaries, with high-depth sequencing and confirmatory Sanger analysis for key variants. Bioinformatic interpretation followed established guidelines, allowing reliable detection of point mutations, small indels, and copy number variations, thereby optimizing diagnostic accuracy.

Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square (χ²) test, while continuous variables were assessed using the Student’s t-test. Specifically, we compared the distribution of tumor regression patterns (complete, marked, partial, none) between TSC1 and TSC2 mutation groups using the Chi-square test. The mean annual regression velocity was compared between the two groups using the t-test. Due to the limited sample size, most analyses were descriptive, and no statistically significant differences were found. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cardiac rhabdomyomas (CRs) are recognized as the most prevalent cardiac tumors in infants and children, accounting for approximately 0.2% of primary cardiac tumors in this population [6]. These tumors are frequently associated with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), a genetic disorder characterized by the formation of benign tumors in multiple organs due to mutations in theTSC1orTSC2genes. Studies indicate that up to 80% of children with TSC develop CRs, while nearly 50% of children diagnosed with TSC present with CRs [7,8]. Despite their high prevalence in TSC, CRs often follow a benign course, with spontaneous regression being a well-documented phenomenon.

The advent of fetal echocardiography has significantly improved the prenatal detection of CRs, allowing for earlier diagnosis compared to the postnatal period. Almost all fetuses presenting with multiple CRs are diagnosed with TSC [9]. Studies have reported that most CRs are detected after the 24th gestational week, with only 13.7% identified before this period [10]. Our findings align with these observations, as approximately 40% of CRs in our cohort were discovered via fetal echocardiography. These tumors often increase in size during the latter half of gestation, likely due to hormonal changes, but typically do not cause hemodynamic alterations during pregnancy. However, they may become symptomatic after birth or within the first year of life. In our study, only one patient experienced left ventricular outflow tract obstruction necessitating surgical intervention, while the majority remained asymptomatic until later in childhood [11].

Histopathologically, CRs exhibit abnormal myocyte architecture, including pathognomonic “spider cells” and vacuolization. They are generally non-encapsulated, well-circumscribed masses ranging from a few millimeters to several centimeters in size [12]. Approximately 90% of cases present with multiple tumors [13]. In our series, the anterolateral wall of the left ventricle was the most common site for these tumors in bothTSC1andTSC2groups. Additionally, the TSC2 group exhibited a higher number of CRs in the right ventricular myocardium. Other studies have demonstrated a slight predominance of left-sided heart involvement, suggesting that the distribution of CRs may vary based on genetic factors [14].

The clinical impact of CRs largely depends on their location within the heart. They can impair ventricular function, disrupt valve operation, or cause outflow obstruction [15]. In our cohort, two patients exhibited mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation due to CRs located on the papillary muscles. Echocardiography remains the imaging modality of choice for assessing cardiac involvement in TSC, typically revealing CRs as nodular lesions within myocardial walls or protruding into the ventricular cavity [16]. Doppler imaging further aids in identifying any associated hemodynamic disturbances. The use of advanced imaging modalities such as cardiac MRI has also been suggested as a valuable tool for detecting small or deeply embedded CRs that may be missed on standard echocardiographic examination [17].

The natural history of CRs often involves spontaneous regression. Approximately 85% of children under two years of age with TSC have CRs, but this prevalence decreases significantly beyond two years, dropping to 20–25%, which suggests tumor regression during this period [18]. Jóźwiak et al. reported partial resolution of CRs in 50% of cases and complete regression in 18%, noting that these tumors have been reported to grow or appear de novo in 4% of TSC patients [19]. Similarly, Smith et al. observed regression rates of 60% in preadolescent TSC patients and 18% in adults [20], while Muhler et al. did not report any patients with complete regression [21] (Table4). The mechanism behind this spontaneous regression remains unclear, though it has been postulated that maturation and apoptosis of tumor cells contribute to this phenomenon.

In our study, complete regression occurred in three patients (13% of cases), two with TSC2 mutations and one with a TSC1 mutation, at 1, 4, and 10 years of age, respectively. Among patients with TSC1 mutations (n= 8), 50% showed a mild reduction in the number and size of CRs, 25% exhibited a significant size reduction, and 12.5% had no regression during follow-up. In contrast, among patients with TSC2 mutations (n= 14), 28.6% demonstrated a mild reduction in number and size, 42.9% showed a substantial size reduction, 7.1% had no regression, and 7.1% required surgical excision. Interestingly, when evaluating tumor evolution quantitatively, we found a mean annual regression velocity of 12.4% in patients with TSC1 mutations and 18.7% in those with TSC2 mutations, suggesting a faster involution trend in the latter group. These findings support a possible genotype-phenotype correlation influencing CR behavior, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts.

Arrhythmias are among the most common cardiac complications associated with CRs, likely due to disruption of the cardiac conduction system leading to ectopic electrical foci or accessory pathways. Nir et al. reported that Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome is found in approximately 9% of patients with TSC and CRs [22]. In our study, one patient presented with WPW syndrome, a frequency higher than that observed in the general population (0.56% vs. 0.15%) [23]. Other reported arrhythmias include premature ventricular contractions, supraventricular tachycardia, and atrioventricular block, emphasizing the need for regular electrocardiographic monitoring in affected individuals. Three patients in our cohort (13.6%) required antiarrhythmic therapy: two were treated with beta-blockers for 10 and 12 months, respectively, while one received sotalol for 6 months, with therapy discontinued in all cases following clinical improvement.

This study underscores the importance of rigorous and continuous cardiac follow-up in patients with TSC, as CRs can pose life-threatening risks when associated with arrhythmias or other severe cardiac anomalies. In our cohort, three patients (13.6%) required anti-arrhythmic therapy, and two (9.1%) underwent surgical correction of concurrent heart defects, percentages notably higher than those in the general population [24].

However, our study has limitations, including its retrospective design and small cohort size, which may introduce selection bias. Despite these constraints, our findings regarding CR regression rates are consistent with existing literature, though we observed a higher incidence of partial regression. This discrepancy may result from more stringent clinical follow-up in our sample, as many patients were born after 2000, a period during which cardiac ultrasounds became more accessible and affordable. Due to the limited sample size and retrospective nature of the study, predicting which patients will experience complete, partial, or no regression of CRs remains challenging. Notably, our analysis suggests that patients withTSC2mutations may exhibit a more pronounced reduction in tumor size compared to those withTSC1mutations, and a higher localization of CRs in the right ventricle. This observation contrasts with some literature reports that describe a more severe disorder in patients harboringTSC2mutations.

From a clinical standpoint, our findings highlight the need for individualized follow-up strategies based on both genetic background and early imaging features. Regular echocardiographic and electrocardiographic monitoring remains essential, particularly during the first years of life, when most tumor-related complications tend to manifest. Although spontaneous regression is common, early use of mTOR inhibitors—such as everolimus or sirolimus—may be considered in selected cases, particularly in symptomatic infants with hemodynamically significant or growing tumors, or when arrhythmias are difficult to control with standard therapies [25,26], or, in carefully selected cases, even during the prenatal period [27]. These agents, by targeting the dysregulated mTOR pathway in TSC, have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing tumor size and improving cardiac function in both fetal and postnatal settings [26]. However, further controlled studies are needed to define precise indications, timing, and duration of mTOR inhibitor therapy in the context of cardiac rhabdomyomas.
