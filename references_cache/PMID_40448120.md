The CCC complex, composed of CCDC22, CCDC93, and ten proteins of the COMMD 
family, coordinates several critical steps required to recycle internalized 
plasma membrane proteins from endosomes to the cell surface. CCC interacts with 
Retriever, a trimeric cargo recognition complex comprising VPS35L, VPS26C, and 
VPS29, and works closely with the WASH complex, a crucial regulator of branched 
actin polymerization at endosomal membranes. Mutations in genes encoding 
subunits of these three complexes, CCDC22, VPS35L, and WASHC5, have been linked 
with a developmental syndrome known as 3 C (cranio-cerebello-cardiac) or 
Ritscher-Schinzel syndrome. Here, we report a new CCDC22 missense mutation, 
p.E208K, that results in attenuated 3 C syndrome, without cardiac or 
neuroanatomical abnormalities. We show that this mutation impairs CCC complex 
assembly by disrupting a conserved interaction surface required for 
CCDC22-COMMD4 binding. We also review previously described cases and identify 
that CCDC22 p.P172R has a similar attenuated phenotype and impairs complex 
assembly in a similar fashion as p.E208K. The characterization of these 
mutations adds to our understanding of the clinical and molecular spectrum of 
these disorders.

Ritscher-Schinzel syndrome, also known as cranio-cerebello-cardiac (3 C) syndrome, is a rare genetic disorder that affects the development of the skeleton, brain, heart, and other organs. First described clinically in 1987 [1], its genetic causes were not deciphered until 2013 when mutations in WASHC5 were first reported in a Canadian, first-nations kindred [2]. Over the past decade, mutations in additional genes, CCDC22 and VPS35L, that also result in 3 C/Ritscher-Schinzel syndrome have been reported [3–7]. WASHC5, CCDC22, and VPS35L encode subunits within the WASH, CCC, and Retriever complexes, respectively, three functionally linked molecular assemblies involved in endosomal protein recycling [8–10].

Endosomal protein recycling is essential for cellular homeostasis. Under physiologic conditions, cells must balance the process of endocytosis, which consumes membranes and proteins from the cell surface, with active recycling of such membranes and receptors back to the plasma membrane. Therefore, endosomal recycling is essential and highly regulated by multiple protein assemblies, including WASH, CCC, and Retriever complexes. The WASH (Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome Protein and Scar Homolog) complex [11–13] is responsible for promoting actin polymerization at the cytosolic side of the endosomal membrane, a critical step for endosomal recycling. WASHC5 encodes an essential component of the WASH complex, a 134 kDa protein named Strumpellin. The WASH complex is regulated by the COMMD-CCDC22-CCDC93 or CCC complex [8], a 12-protein assembly that limits the endosomal localization and actin polymerization activity of the WASH complex [14]. CCC works closely with the Retriever complex, a trimeric assembly consisting of VPS35L, VPS29, and VPS26C [10]. Retriever allows the system to recognize specific cargo proteins in the endosomal compartment that are to be recycled. CCC and Retriever can assemble into a larger complex known as Commander [15], and in this assembly, the dimer formed by CCDC22 and CCDC93 serves as a scaffold, connecting Retriever on one end to COMMD proteins on the other end [16–18]. The ten members of the COMMD protein family assemble into a ring structure following a specific, conserved order of subunit organization. CCDC22 and CCDC93 stabilizes the assembly by individually interacting with two opposite rims of the ring. Conversely, the COMMD ring stabilizes the dimerization of CCDC22 and CCDC93, which can subsequently influence the interaction between CCDC22-CCDC93 and Retriever. The overall function of the COMMD ring and the reason for the strict conservation of each of the ten COMMD proteins remain unknown.

In this report, we describe a family with attenuated 3 C syndrome associated with a novel CCDC22 mutation, p.E208K, and provide biochemical evidence that the mutation results in partial loss of function by disrupting the binding of CCDC22 to the COMMD ring. We also evaluate previously reported variants and identify another mutation in CCDC22, p.P172R, with a similar molecular and clinical phenotype.

Two male siblings diagnosed with moderate intellectual disability were evaluated. They are the only affected individuals in a large extended pedigree (Fig. 1A). Proband #1 was also diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) Level 3, while proband #2 was diagnosed with cerebral palsy during childhood. Both siblings experienced the onset of seizures at ~ 18 months of age. Proband #2 achieved control by age six through anti-epileptic medication, while proband #1 faced challenges in seizure management until the age of 25, undergoing trials of multiple anti-epileptic medications. Brain MRIs from both patients revealed large internal auditory meatus and foci of hypomyelination in the posterior occipital-parietal white matter. Both brothers experienced disrupted sleep patterns until puberty, characterized by difficulty in falling asleep and maintaining sleep. Other features noted during infancy included congenital cryptorchidism in both probands. Proband #2 faced challenges in feeding, exhibiting generalized hypotonia and failure to thrive. As adults, both siblings share a stature in the 10th percentile (Fig. 1B), although their head circumferences are within the normal range. Proband #2 exhibited a grade 2/6 systolic murmur at the left sternal border during childhood, but subsequent echocardiograms revealed normal cardiac function and no congenital anomalies. In terms of facial features (Fig. 1C), both individuals exhibit straight eyebrows and high, narrow palates with broad alveoli margins and thick palatal shelves. Dental anomalies include crowding and notably large maxillary incisors (Fig. 1D). Additionally, no micrognathia was observed (Fig. 1E), but mild dysmorphism of the external ear was noted in both siblings (Fig. 1F). Digital anomalies were apparent in both brothers including spatulate fingertips, fetal finger pads, flat nail beds, and thin, ridged nails. Proband #2 displayed more severe finger abnormalities, including prominent joints, poor intrinsic hand musculature, and bilateral absence of DIP joint creases on the fourth finger (Fig. 1G). Mild skin syndactyly (2/3 and 3/4) is present in proband #2, along with reduced stability and abduction of the thumbs due to excessive metacarpophalangeal joint flexion. In terms of metabolic or biochemical abnormalities, both individuals have modestly elevated fasting total cholesterol (5.7 mmol/L or 220 mg/dL, and 6.1 mmol/L or 236 mg/dL) and normal plasma copper concentration (16.3 and 16.8 µmol/L).

Exome sequencing revealed that the probands shared a previously unreported variant in the X chromosome, X-49,243,370 G > A, resulting in a missense E208K substitution in the CCDC22 gene (ClinVar SCV005315736). Concurrent sequencing of the parents confirmed that their mother, and not the father, was a carrier of the mutant allele (genotype GA). Repeat trio sequencing 10 years later (both parent and one of the probands) confirmed the same findings. Uni-directional targeted DNA sequencing was performed on 3 maternal uncles and 2 maternal aunts; none of these individuals carried the mutant allele (Fig. 1A). No variants at this position are reported in gnomAD, suggesting an important role of glutamate at position 208 in protein function. Previous studies implicated CCDC22 mutations in X-linked intellectual disability within a large family in Australia [19], later recognized as a variant of 3 C syndrome [20]. The mutation in this family affects a splice site, leading to missplicing and severely reduced protein expression, as well as a missense change (p.T17A). The phenotypic presentation in these patients included cardiac and neuroanatomical abnormalities like those seen in patients with WASHC5 mutations (Table 1) and also included ectodermal dysplasia with scalp defects, as well as altered NF-kB signaling [3]. Three families with missense mutations in CCDC22, p.Y557C, p.P172R, and p.R234C, were subsequently reported [4, 6, 21]. The first mutation, Y557C, produced a severe phenotype with neurological malformations in the posterior fossa, ventricular septal defects, and multiple craniofacial abnormalities, similar to the T17A mutation (Table 1). In contrast, the P172R and R234C mutations, as well as the mutation described here, E208K, were associated with intellectual disability and seizures, but did not result in severe neuroanatomical abnormalities, which were seen only in the T17A and Y557C mutations (Table 1). Cranial and skeletal abnormalities were noted in all CCDC22 mutations reported (Table 1).

An analysis of the recently described model of CCC complex assembly indicates that P172, E208, and R234 reside in the COMMD binding domain (CBD) of CCDC22, a series of helices that make intimate contact with half of the COMMD ring, extending from COMMD4 to COMMD10 (Fig. 2A). In contrast, Y557 is located in the VPS35L binding domain (VBD), a region required for binding to Retriever (Fig. 2A). All the mutations in the CBD affect highly conserved residues (Fig. 2B).

The points of contact between CCDC22 CBD and COMMD4 and COMMD10 appear to be particularly critical in COMMD ring binding, as CCDC22 is most closely co-evolved with these 2 COMMD family members, compared to all other components of the CCC and Retriever complexes (Fig. 2C). Overlaying these mutations onto the recently derived models of the CCC complex [16–18], we identified that E208 is specifically located in the interface between CCDC22 and COMMD4, where it establishes a critical point of contact with K180 in COMMD4 (Fig. 3A). Consistent with this role in protein-protein binding, we observed that CCDC22 E208K had a significantly reduced ability to bind to COMMD4 and all other COMMD proteins that form the COMMD ring (Fig. 3B). In addition, this mutation also decreased the ability of CCDC22 to dimerize with CCDC93, bind to DENND10 (a CCC-associated protein), and bind to the Retriever subunit, VPS35L; this constellation of changes have been recently shown for other mutations that disturb CCDC22-COMMD ring binding [16].

Using the same modeling approach, we observed that P172 is predicted to interact directly with COMMD3 (Fig. 3A). However, the structural model does not identify critical protein-protein interactions mediated by R234, which is in an extended surface loop that is unresolved in recent cryo-EM models but predicted not to contact the COMMD ring according to recent highly concordant AlphaFold2-based models (Fig. 3A) [16–18]. In contrast, Y557 is located at the sharp tip of the VBD fork (Fig. 3C). Based on the resolution of the cryo-EM structure and the inherent flexibility of this region, it is impossible to determine the impact of the Y557C mutation. There is however enough flexibility in the loop for Y557 to contact the Retriever subassembly. (Fig. 3C). Using immunoprecipitation and immunoblot analysis, we tested whether these previously reported patient-associated mutations alter any of these key protein-protein interactions. In agreement with the molecular models, we found that P172R phenocopies the E208K mutations and results in reduced binding between CCDC22 and COMMD proteins, as well as reduced dimerization with CCDC93, reduced binding to DENND10, and decreased binding to the Retriever subunit VPS35L (Fig. 3D). On the other hand, neither R234C nor Y557C caused appreciable changes in any of these interactions. To quantify these changes in complex assembly, we examined these interactions further using immunoprecipitation and western blot analysis with fluorescent secondary antibodies. The aggregate of 4 independent experiments demonstrated once again that E208K and P172R significantly reduced the binding of CCDC22 with CCC complex components (CCDC93, COMMD1, COMMD4, COMMD5) and with the Retriever subunit VPS35L (Fig. 3E). In contrast, R234C and Y557C had no statistically significant effects on CCDC22 binding with CCC or Retriever suggesting that the mechanism by which these mutations may cause disease does not involve CCC complex assembly or Retriever binding but probably is the result of altering interactions with other regulatory factors. Altogether, the genetic, structural, and biochemical evidence confirmed the damaging nature of the E208K and P172R mutations and map their mechanism as disrupting the binding between CCDC22 and the COMMD ring.

The clinical features of 3 C syndrome were first described by Ritscher and colleagues in 1987. Substantial phenotypic variability was found in subsequent reports, and consensus clinical criteria were first suggested by Leonardi et al. to include congenital cardiac anomalies other than patent ductus arteriosus, cerebellum or posterior fossa brain malformations, and craniofacial anomalies [22]. As genetic causes emerged, the syndrome was subcategorized into four types, corresponding to each culprit gene: WASHC5 (type 1), CCDC22 (type 2), VPS35L (type 3), and DPYSL5 (type 4). Interestingly, the first 3 genes identified encode proteins that interact with each other and are linked to endosomal protein sorting, indicating that perturbation of a common functional role for these genes results in a stereotypical phenotype in humans.

WASHC5 was the first gene to be identified in an autosomal recessive form of 3 C syndrome among first nation patients in northern Manitoba previously described with Marles, as well as additional cases in the same community [2, 23] (Table 1). Using a combination of genetic linkage and targeted sequencing, all patients reported were either homozygous or compound heterozygous for any of three variants near the same exon-intron boundary in the WASHC5 gene, resulting in abnormal splicing. Interestingly, one of the variants (c.3335 + 8 A > G) displayed a strong founder effect in the community, with 1 in 9 being a carrier for this mutation, which is absent in individuals of European ancestry. Autosomal dominant mutations in WASHC5 have been reported in a different syndrome, hereditary spastic paraplegia [24–26], which results in progressive lower extremity weakness usually starting at age 20. Most often, the syndrome is caused by missense mutations (V676F, L619F, N471D, and G696A) but splice site mutations have also been described [27], suggesting a haploinsufficiency mechanism. At least one of these missense variants, N471D, results in locomotor defects in homozygous knock-in mice, phenocopying some aspects seen in patients with this mutation [28]. Most recently, several patients with loss of function mutations in VPS35L have been reported [5, 7], and their clinical characteristics resemble many features seen with mutations in WASHC5 and in patients with severe CCDC22 loss of function mutations, such as the p.T17A (Table 1). Interestingly, while the Y557C mutation has the same severe clinical phenotype, the molecular mechanism by which it disrupts CCDC22 remains unclear and the only reported effect of the mutation is mild reduction in CCDC22 protein expression and subtle increase in WASH1 protein expression [29]. In contrast to this, the mutations impairing CCDC22 binding to the COMMD ring, E208K and P172R, cause an attenuated phenotype without structural brain or cardiovascular anomalies. This could be due to the mutant proteins retaining some complex formation, as Fig. 3 shows that there is lower but persistent binding to CCC subunits. Alternatively, the attenuated phenotype may be due to normal CCC-independent functions of CCDC22 E208K and P172R, which are probably lost in patients with the CCDC22 p.T17A mutation, which is associated with very little protein expression due to missplicing. A similar phenotype was noted for R234C, although the molecular mechanism for this mutation is lacking. It is worth noting that mutations in WASHC5, CCDC22 and VPS35L all result in hypercholesterolemia in humans thought to be due to abnormal LDL clearance secondary to defects in LDL receptor recycling, a phenotype also seen in mouse models of liver deficiency of Ccdc22, Vps35l and Commd1 [5, 7, 30–32].

From an evolutionary perspective, the encoding gene for a WASH complex subunit most closely co-evolved with Retriever and CCC is WASHC5 (Strumpellin), followed by WASHC4 (SWIP, Fig. 2C). The latter gene, although not considered a cause of 3 C syndrome, has been linked to autosomal recessive intellectual disability with additional overlapping phenotypes seen in WASHC5 mutations [33–35]. In contrast, no human mutations in WASHC1, WASHC2, or WASHC3 have been reported to date. This striking correlation between co-evolution and phenotypic expression of mutations in humans suggests that specific genes encoding WASH, CCC, and Retriever subunits play converging roles and may be associated with specific alterations of normal development and physiology, as in the case of 3 C syndrome, and probably other clinical manifestations yet to be described.

Exome sequencing was performed as part of clinical evaluation of the patients reported here. The initial sequencing analysis was carried out by Victorian Clinical Genetics Services (VCGS, Victoria, Australia) and later trio sequencing was performed by Blueprint Genetics (Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA). Plasma cholesterol and plasma copper reported here were also evaluated as part of clinical care.

Gene co-evolution among CCC, Retriever and WASH complex encoding genes was assessed using the machine-learning based phylogenetic profiling online portal (https://mlpp.cs.huji.ac.il/), previously described in detail [36].

Residues of interest in CCDC22 were highlighted on a recently reported cryo-EM model of the CCC complex [18], and where experimental models were incomplete, this was complemented by an experimentally validated AlphaFold2 model of the CCC complex [16].
