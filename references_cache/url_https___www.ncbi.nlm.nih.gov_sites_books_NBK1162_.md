            <div class="main-content lit-style" itemscope="itemscope" itemtype="http://schema.org/CreativeWork"><div class="meta-content fm-sec"><h1 id="_NBK1162_"><span class="title" itemprop="name">Mucopolysaccharidosis Type I</span></h1><div itemprop="alternativeHeadline" class="subtitle whole_rhythm">Synonyms: Alpha-L-Iduronidase Deficiency, IDUA Deficiency, MPS I</div><p class="contrib-group"><span itemprop="author">Lorne A Clarke</span>, MD.</p><a data-jig="ncbitoggler" href="#__NBK1162_ai__" style="border:0;text-decoration:none">Author Information and Affiliations</a><div style="display:none" class="ui-widget" id="__NBK1162_ai__"><div class="contrib half_rhythm"><span itemprop="author">Lorne A Clarke</span>, MD<div class="affiliation small">Professor, Medical Genetics<br />University of British Columbia<br />Vancouver, BC, Canada<div><span class="email-label">Email: </span><a href="mailto:dev@null" data-email="ac.cbu@ekralc.enrol" class="oemail">ac.cbu@ekralc.enrol</a></div></div></div></div><p class="small">Initial Posting: <span itemprop="datePublished">October 31, 2002</span>; Last Revision: <span itemprop="dateModified">December 4, 2025</span>.</p><p><em>Estimated reading time: 43 minutes</em></p></div><div class="jig-ncbiinpagenav body-content whole_rhythm" data-jigconfig="allHeadingLevels: ['h2'],smoothScroll: false" itemprop="text"><div id="mps1.Summary" itemprop="description"><h2 id="_mps1_Summary_">Summary</h2><div><h4 class="inline">Clinical characteristics.</h4><p>Mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I) is a progressive multisystem disorder with features ranging over a continuum of severity. While affected individuals have traditionally been classified as having one of three MPS I syndromes (Hurler syndrome, Hurler-Scheie syndrome, or Scheie syndrome), no easily measurable biochemical differences have been identified and the clinical findings overlap. Affected individuals are best described as having either a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> consistent with either severe (Hurler syndrome) or attenuated MPS I, a distinction that influences therapeutic options.</p><p><i>Severe MPS I:</i> Infants appear normal at birth. Typical early manifestations are nonspecific (e.g., umbilical or inguinal hernia, frequent upper respiratory tract infections before age 1 year). Coarsening of the facial features may not become apparent until after age one year. Gibbus deformity of the lower spine is common and often noted within the first year. Progressive skeletal dysplasia (dysostosis multiplex) involving all bones is universal, as is progressive arthropathy involving most joints. By age three years, linear growth decreases. Intellectual disability is progressive and profound but may not be readily apparent in the first year of life. Progressive cardiorespiratory involvement, hearing loss, and corneal clouding are common. Without treatment, death (typically from cardiorespiratory failure) usually occurs within the first ten years of life.</p><p><i>Attenuated MPS I:</i> Clinical onset is usually between ages three and ten years. The severity and rate of disease progression range from serious life-threatening complications leading to death in the second to third decade, to a normal life span complicated by significant disability from progressive joint manifestations and cardiorespiratory disease. While some individuals have no neurologic involvement and psychomotor development may be normal in early childhood, learning disabilities and psychiatric manifestations can be present later in life. Hearing loss, cardiac valvular disease, respiratory involvement, and corneal clouding are common.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Diagnosis/testing.</h4><p>The diagnosis of MPS I is established in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with suggestive clinical and laboratory findings by: detection of deficient activity of the lysosomal enzyme &#x003b1;-L-iduronidase (IDUA) in combination with elevation of glycosaminoglycan levels; and/or identification of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>IDUA</i> on <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a>. Identification of the causative <i>IDUA</i> variants plays an important role in the determination of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Management.</h4><p><i>Treatment of manifestations:</i> An essential component of management is the determination of whether the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> has severe or attenuated MPS I. This requires detailed clinical and laboratory assessment and can be challenging in very young individuals.</p><p><i>Targeted therapies:</i> Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is considered the standard of care for children with severe MPS I. Outcome is significantly influenced by disease burden at the time of diagnosis (and thus, by the age of the individual). HSCT can improve cognitive outcomes, increase survival, improve growth, reduce facial coarseness and hepatosplenomegaly, improve hearing, prevent hydrocephalus, and alter the natural history of cardiac and respiratory symptomatology. HSCT has lesser effects on the skeletal and joint manifestations, corneal clouding, and cardiac involvement. HSCT alters the course of cognitive decline in children with severe MPS I; cognitive outcome is greatly influenced by the degree of cognitive impairment at the time of transplantation. Due to the morbidity and mortality associated with HSCT, it is currently recommended primarily for children with severe MPS I.</p><p>Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with laronidase (Aldurazyme<sup>&#x000ae;</sup>), licensed for treatment of the non-CNS manifestations of MPS I, improves liver size, linear growth, and mobility and joint range of motion; slows progression of respiratory disease; and improves sleep apnea in persons with attenuated disease. The age of initiation of ERT influences the outcome.</p><p><i>Supportive care</i>: Infant learning programs/special education for developmental delay; physical therapy, orthopedic surgery as needed, joint replacement for progressive arthropathy, atlanto-occipital stabilization; spinal cord decompression for cervical myelopathy; cerebrospinal fluid shunting for hydrocephalus; early median nerve decompression for carpal tunnel syndrome based on nerve conduction studies before clinical manifestations develop; special attention to anesthetic risks; hats with visors/sunglasses to reduce glare, corneal transplantation for ophthalmologic involvement; cardiac valve replacement as needed and bacterial endocarditis prophylaxis for those with cardiac involvement; tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy for eustachian tube dysfunction and/or upper airway obstruction; ventilating tubes; hearing aids as needed; CPAP for sleep apnea; gastrointestinal management for diarrhea and constipation.</p><p><i>Surveillance:</i> Annual assessment by a team of physicians with knowledge of the multisystem nature of MPS I. Specialists and assessments: orthopedic surgery including annual assessment of median nerve conduction velocity; ophthalmology, cardiology (including echocardiography), respiratory with assessment of pulmonary function and sleep studies, audiology, and otolaryngology. Assessment for constipation and/or hernias as needed. Early and continuous monitoring of head growth in infants and children with imaging as needed; assessment for evidence of spinal cord compression by neurologic examination; developmental assessment annually; and psycho-educational assessment of children with attenuated disease prior to primary school entry.</p><p><i>Evaluation of relatives at risk:</i> Early diagnosis prior to significant disease manifestations is warranted in relatives at risk in order to initiate therapy as early in the course of disease as possible.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Genetic counseling.</h4><p>MPS I is inherited in an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> manner. At conception, each child of a couple in which both parents are <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for a <i>IDUA</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> has a 25% chance of being affected, a 50% chance of being an asymptomatic <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a>, and a 25% chance of being unaffected and not a carrier. Carrier testing for at-risk relatives and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-testing/">prenatal testing</a> for pregnancies at increased risk are possible if both disease-causing <i>IDUA</i> variants have been identified in the family.</p></div></div><div id="mps1.GeneReview_Scope"><h2 id="_mps1_GeneReview_Scope_"><i>GeneReview</i> Scope</h2><div id="mps1.Tc" class="table"><p class="large-table-link" style="display:none"><span class="right"><a href="/sites/books/NBK1162/table/mps1.Tc/?report=objectonly" target="object">View in own window</a></span></p><div class="large_tbl" id="__mps1.Tc_lrgtbl__"><table class="no_bottom_margin"><thead><tr><th id="hd_h_mps1.Tc_1_1_1_1" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Mucopolysaccharidosis Type I: Included Phenotypes</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td headers="hd_h_mps1.Tc_1_1_1_1" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;"><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Severe MPS I (Hurler syndrome)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Attenuated MPS I (Hurler-Scheie syndrome / Scheie syndrome)</div></li></ul>

Although single-tier &#x003b1;-L-iduronidase (IDUA) enzyme activity measurement was the original and remains the most common approach taken in the United States, the potential adoption of a second tier involving measurement of dried blood spot glycosaminoglycans is currently under way by some centers. The addition of the second tier greatly increases the positive predictive value of NBS for MPS I [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.clarke.2020.91">Clarke et al 2020</a>].</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Single-tier NBS for mucopolysaccharidosis type I (MPS I) is primarily based on quantification of IDUA enzyme activity on dried blood spots.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>IDUA enzyme activity values below the cutoff reported by the screening laboratory are considered positive and require follow-up biochemical testing and clinical evaluation. Follow-up biochemical testing includes confirmation of deficiency of IDUA enzyme activity in blood as well as demonstration of elevation in urinary glycosaminoglycan levels.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Positive NBS results should be reviewed by a clinical specialist with experience in MPS I to ensure that the appropriate additional laboratory testing and clinical assessment is performed and correctly interpreted to determine whether this a true positive NBS result and to definitively establish the diagnosis of MPS I.</div></li></ul></div><div id="mps1.Scenario_2__Symptomatic_Individual"><h4>Scenario 2 &#x02013; Symptomatic Individual</h4><p>A symptomatic individual who has findings associated with either attenuated MPS I or severe untreated MPS I (Hurler syndrome) may present because of any of the following: NBS not performed, false negative NBS result, and/or caregivers not adherent with the recommended assessment plan following a positive NBS result.</p><p>Supportive (particularly when multiple findings are present) but nonspecific clinical findings and preliminary laboratory findings can include the following.</p><p>
<b>Clinical findings</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Coarse facial features</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Early frequent upper respiratory infections including otitis media</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Inguinal or umbilical hernia</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Hepatosplenomegaly</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Characteristic skeletal findings (gibbus deformity, limitation of joint range of motion)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Noninflammatory arthropathy</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Characteristic ocular findings (corneal clouding)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Hydrocephalus</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Developmental delay</div></li></ul><p>Note: Clinical findings vary by disease severity. Clinical findings alone are not diagnostic.</p><p><b>Preliminary laboratory findings.</b> Analysis of urine glycosaminoglycans (GAG) (i.e., heparan and dermatan sulfate) may be quantitative (measurement of total urinary GAGs or specific GAG disaccharides) or qualitative (GAG electrophoresis to analyze the specific GAGs excreted).</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Neither the quantitative nor the qualitative method can diagnose a specific lysosomal enzyme deficiency, including MPS I; however, an abnormality detected by either or both methods indicates the likely presence of an MPS disorder.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>GAG electrophoresis can exclude and include certain MPS disorders; however, definitive diagnosis requires additional testing (see <a href="#mps1.Establishing_the_Diagnosis">Establishing the Diagnosis</a>).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Both methods have reduced <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sensitivity/">sensitivity</a>, particularly when urine is dilute.</div></li></ul><p><b>Family history</b> is consistent with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> inheritance (e.g., affected sibs and/or parental <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/consanguinity/">consanguinity</a>). Absence of a known family history does not preclude the diagnosis.</p></div></div><div id="mps1.Establishing_the_Diagnosis"><h3>Establishing the Diagnosis</h3><p>The diagnosis of MPS I <b>is established</b> in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with the suggestive clinical AND laboratory findings <a href="#mps1.Suggestive_Findings">above</a> and detection of deficient IDUA enzyme activity or identification of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic (or <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a>) variants in <i>IDUA</i> on <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK1162/table/mps1.T.mucopolysaccharidosis_type_i_mole/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmps1Tmucopolysaccharidosistypeimole">Table 1</a>).</p><p>Note: (1) Due to the presence of IDUA pseudodeficiency, the establishment of the diagnosis of MPS I requires the demonstration of BOTH deficiency of IDUA enzyme activity AND elevation of urine GAGs. Note: Depending on the sequencing method used, single-<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exon/">exon</a>, multiexon, or whole-gene deletions/duplications may not be detected. While affected individuals have traditionally been classified as having one of three MPS I syndromes (Hurler syndrome, Hurler-Scheie syndrome, or Scheie syndrome), no easily measurable biochemical differences have been identified [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.muenzer.2004.s27">Muenzer 2004</a>] and the clinical findings overlap; thus, affected individuals are best described as having either severe or attenuated MPS I, a distinction that influences therapeutic options. The greatest variability is observed in individuals with attenuated MPS I.</p><p>An accurate determination of the proportion of individuals with severe or attenuated MPS I has not been published. Data from the international MPS I Registry available in 2011 showed that of the 891 individuals included in the registry, 57% were classified as having Hurler syndrome, 23.5% as having Hurler-Scheie syndrome, and 10% as having Scheie syndrome; 8.6% were classified as either unknown or indeterminate. The potential ascertainment bias of registry data and the lack of a clear definition of phenotypic features for each of the subcategories should be considered in interpretation of the data [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.daco.2012.911">D'Aco et al 2012</a>].</p><div id="mps1.T.mucopolysaccharidosis_type_i_comp" class="table"><h3><span class="label">Table 2. </span></h3><div id="mps1.Severe_MPS_I_Hurler_Syndrome"><h4>Severe MPS I (Hurler Syndrome)</h4><p>Severe MPS I is characterized by a chronic and progressive disease course involving multiple organs and tissues [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.neufeld.2001">Neufeld &#x00026; Muenzer 2001</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.muenzer.2009.19">Muenzer et al 2009</a>]. Infants with severe MPS I appear normal at birth but may have inguinal or umbilical hernias. The mean age of diagnosis for severe MPS I is approximately ten months; most affected children are diagnosed before age 18 months [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.giugliani.2021.674">Giugliani et al 2021</a>]. In untreated individuals, death due to cardiorespiratory failure usually occurs within the first ten years of life.</p><p><b>Craniofacial and physical appearance.</b> Coarsening of the facial features, caused by storage of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in the soft tissues of the orofacial region and facial bone dysostosis, becomes apparent within the first two years. Thickening of the alae nasi, lips, ear lobules, and tongue becomes progressively more evident. Thickening of the calvarium results in macrocephaly. Scaphocephaly is common. Facial and body hypertrichosis are often seen by age 24 months, at which time the scalp hair is coarse, straight, and thatch-like.</p><p><b>Hepatosplenomegaly.</b> Protuberance of the abdomen caused by progressive hepatosplenomegaly is common. Although organ size may be massive, storage of GAGs in the liver and spleen does not lead to organ dysfunction.</p><p><b>Skeletal.</b> Progressive skeletal dysplasia (dysostosis multiplex) involving all bones is seen in all individuals with severe MPS I. Children have significant early bone involvement. Mild dysostosis, particularly of the hip, as well as thickening of the ribs, can be detected on radiographs soon after birth but findings may be interpreted as within normal limits at this early age. Gibbus deformity (dorsolumbar kyphosis) often becomes clinically apparent within the first ten months; it has been reported as early as age six months [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.mundada.2009.930">Mundada &#x00026; D'Souza 2009</a>].</p><p>MPS I registry data show that by age six months the median growth for individuals with severe MPS I begins to deviate from normal, falling below the third percentile of normal by age four years [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.viskochil.2019.2425">Viskochil et al 2019</a>]. Defective ossification centers of the vertebral bodies lead to flattened and beaked vertebrae and subsequent spinal deformity. Complications may include spinal nerve entrapment, acute spinal injury, and atlanto-occipital instability.</p><p>The clavicles are short, thickened, and irregular. Long bones are short with wide shafts; the knees are prone to valgus and varus deformities. Endochondral growth plates are thickened and disordered. Typically, the pelvis is poorly formed. The femoral heads are small and coxa valga is common. Involvement of the femoral heads and acetabula leads to progressive and debilitating hip deformity. Progressive arthropathy leading to severe joint deformity is universal; significant and functionally impactful joint stiffness is common by age two years.</p><p>Phalangeal dysostosis and synovial thickening lead to a characteristic claw hand deformity. Carpal tunnel syndrome and interphalangeal joint involvement commonly lead to poor hand function. Carpal tunnel syndrome is often missed because of its insidious onset; it often presents with few symptoms or signs other than thenar atrophy.</p><p><b>Ophthalmologic.</b> Corneal clouding occurs in all individuals with MPS I. Progression can lead to severe visual impairment. Open-angle glaucoma may occur. Retinal degeneration resulting in decreased peripheral vision and night blindness is common. Blindness can result from a combination of retinal degeneration, optic nerve compression and atrophy, and cortical damage from hydrocephalus.</p><p><b>Cardiovascular.</b> Cardiac involvement is seen in all individuals with severe MPS I. Cardiac involvement is evident by echocardiography much earlier than observed clinically. Progressive thickening and stiffening of the valve leaflets can lead to mitral and aortic regurgitation and stenosis, which may become hemodynamically significant in the later stages of disease. Mitral valve regurgitation is the more common valvular disease in individuals with severe MPS I [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.neufeld.2001">Neufeld &#x00026; Muenzer 2001</a>]. As lysosomal storage continues in the heart, cardiomyopathy, sudden death from arrhythmia, coronary artery disease, and cardiovascular collapse may occur. A small subset of individuals with severe MPS I have an early-onset fatal endocardiofibroelastosis.</p><p><b>Hearing loss.</b> Hearing loss, which is common in severe MPS I, is correlated to the severity of somatic disease. Hearing loss results from frequent middle-ear infection, from eustachian tube dysfunction caused by storage of GAGs within the oropharynx, dysostosis of the ossicles of the middle ear, scarring of the tympanic membrane, and damage to the eighth nerve.</p><p><b>ENT (otolaryngologic).</b> Chronic recurrent rhinitis and persistent copious nasal discharge without obvious infection are common. Storage of GAGs within the oropharynx with associated enlargement of the tonsils and adenoids can contribute to upper airway complications, along with narrowed trachea, thickened vocal cords, redundant tissue in the upper airway, and an enlarged tongue. This upper airway involvement leads to noisy breathing (particularly at night) and a deep and gravelly voice, and is a main component of obstructive sleep apnea, a common complication of MPS I. CNS involvement can also contribute to sleep apnea.</p><p><b>Gastrointestinal system.</b> Inguinal hernias should be repaired surgically with the expectation that they may recur. Umbilical hernias are generally not treated unless they are exceedingly large.</p><p>For unknown reasons, many children with severe MPS I periodically experience loose stools and diarrhea, sometimes alternating with periods of severe constipation. These problems may or may not diminish with age; they are exacerbated by muscle weakness and physical inactivity, as well as antibiotic use for other medical complications.</p><p><b>Hydrocephaly.</b> Communicating high-pressure hydrocephalus is common in individuals with severe MPS I. Impaired resorption of cerebrospinal fluid causes an increase in intracranial pressure, leading to brain compression. Increase in intracranial pressure can cause rapid cognitive decline in some individuals. Symptoms may be difficult to assess and progression insidious. The degree to which hydrocephalus contributes to the neurologic deterioration in children with severe MPS I is unknown.</p><p><b>Intellect.</b> Although early psychomotor development may be normal, developmental delay is usually obvious by age 18 months. A measurable decrease in intellectual capacity occurs monthly thereafter (as graded by the Bayley Mental Development Index) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.shapiro.2018.76">Shapiro et al 2018</a>]. Subsequently, most children do not progress developmentally but plateau for a number of years, followed by a slow decline in intellectual capabilities. By the time of death at age eight to ten years, most children are severely intellectually disabled.</p><p>Children with severe MPS I develop only limited language skills, likely related to the triad of developmental delay, chronic hearing loss, and enlarged tongue.</p><p>In contrast to <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/hunter/">MPS II</a> and <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/mps3/">MPS III</a>, the severe developmental effects in children with MPS I are associated with placid rather than aggressive behavior. Seizures appear to be uncommon even at the end stages of disease.</p><p><b>Pathophysiology.</b> The metabolic and physiologic bases of the various symptoms in MPS I are complex and involve the direct cellular effect of lysosomal storage of GAGs, inflammatory pathway activation, alteration of extracellular matrix composition and function, interference of other lysosomal and endosomal pathways, cell signaling, and alteration of autophagy.</p></div><div id="mps1.Attenuated_MPS_I_HurlerScheie_Syndr"><h4>Attenuated MPS I (Hurler-Scheie Syndrome / Scheie Syndrome)</h4><p>If development is normal by age 24 months and if moderate somatic involvement is evident (e.g., mild hepatomegaly, relatively normal joint range of motion, mild dysostosis on skeletal radiographs, mild corneal clouding), an individual should be classified as having attenuated MPS I.</p><p><b>Onset</b> of disease in children with attenuated MPS I is variable, usually occurring between ages three and ten years, but recognition of the diagnosis may be delayed [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.giugliani.2021.674">Giugliani et al 2021</a>].</p><p><b>Craniofacial and physical appearance.</b> The physical appearance of individuals with attenuated MPS I varies. Coarseness of facial features is less obvious than in individuals with severe MPS I. Findings can include a short neck, wide mouth, and square jaw.</p><p>Children with attenuated MPS I have variable growth restriction that may not be apparent until later childhood [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.viskochil.2019.2425">Viskochil et al 2019</a>].</p><p><b>Hepatosplenomegaly</b> is variable in individuals with attenuated MPS I.</p><p><b>Skeletal.</b> Skeletal and joint manifestations are the most significant source of disability and discomfort for individuals with attenuated disease, who may have severe bone involvement but no cognitive impairment [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.vijay.2005.872">Vijay &#x00026; Wraith 2005</a>]. The MPS I Registry showed that more than 85% of persons with attenuated MPS I have dysostosis, primarily in the vertebrae and femur [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.thomas.2010.421">Thomas et al 2010</a>]. Kyphosis, scoliosis, and severe back pain are common. Spondylolisthesis of the lower spine leading to spinal cord compression can occur.</p><p>Progressive arthropathy affecting all joints and eventually leading to loss of or severe restriction in range of motion is universal. Carpal tunnel syndrome was present at a median age of nine years 11 months in 138 individuals with attenuated MPS I included in the MPS I Registry [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.viskochil.2017.1269">Viskochil et al 2017</a>]. Poor hand function resulting from the characteristic claw hand deformity, carpal tunnel syndrome, and interphalangeal joint stiffness is often observed. Most individuals do not have the characteristic early symptoms of carpal tunnel syndrome (see <a href="#mps1.Management">Management</a>).</p><p><b>Ophthalmologic.</b> Corneal clouding, exhibited by approximately 82% of children with attenuated MPS I, was identified at a median age of 9.1 years [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.thomas.2010.421">Thomas et al 2010</a>]. Corneal clouding can lead to significant visual disability. Glaucoma, retinal degeneration, and optic atrophy can occur.</p><p><b>Cardiovascular.</b> Significant cardiac involvement is estimated to occur in approximately 88% of children with attenuated MPS I at a median age of 11.7 years [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.thomas.2010.421">Thomas et al 2010</a>]. Virtually all individuals with MPS I will have evidence of cardiac valvular thickening. Cardiac involvement can present as progressive disease of the mitral and aortic valves with regurgitation and/or stenosis, for which valve replacement may be necessary. Aortic valvular disease is more likely to occur in children with attenuated MPS I than in those with severe MPS I [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.neufeld.2001">Neufeld &#x00026; Muenzer 2001</a>]; however, in some individuals, all valves are affected. In a group of 78 persons with attenuated MPS I, 40% had involvement of one valve and 60% had involvement of two or more valves [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.thomas.2010.421">Thomas et al 2010</a>].</p><p>Coronary disease may also be a feature of attenuated MPS I.</p><p><b>Hearing loss.</b> Moderate-to-severe hearing loss develops in many individuals with attenuated MPS I, particularly children with significant somatic disease. Hearing impairment, most commonly in the high frequency range, is likely caused by a combination of eustachian tube dysfunction, dysostosis of the ossicles of the middle ear, and eighth nerve involvement.</p><p><b>ENT (otolaryngologic).</b> Rhinorrhea is common. Sleep apnea as a result of obstructive airway disease and possibly central nervous system involvement occurs in individuals with attenuated MPS I.</p><p><b>Gastrointestinal system.</b> Hernias were present in approximately 65% of persons with attenuated MPS I included in the MPS I Registry [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.thomas.2010.421">Thomas et al 2010</a>]. Many have also had inguinal hernias during infancy, often requiring repeated surgical correction.</p><p><b>Respiratory system.</b> Progressive pulmonary disease may manifest as abnormalities of forced vital capacity. Respiratory complications (and cardiac involvement) are among the leading causes of premature death.</p><p><b>Hydrocephaly.</b> The risk of communicating hydrocephalus and its complications are lower in attenuated MPS I than severe MPS I. However, hydrocephalus may occur with insidious onset.</p><p><b>Other neurologic findings.</b> Arachnoid cysts may develop. The predictive power of changes noted on MRI does not appear to be significant in individuals with attenuated MPS I [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.neufeld.2001">Neufeld &#x00026; Muenzer 2001</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.valayannopoulos.2010.20">Valayannopoulos et al 2010</a>].</p><p>Progressive compression of the spinal cord with resulting cervical myelopathy caused by thickening of the dura (hypertrophic pachymeningitis cericalis) is common in individuals with attenuated MPS I. Cervical myelopathy may present initially as reduced activity or exercise intolerance and may not be recognized until the injury is irreversible.</p><p><b>Intellect.</b> Although development may be normal in early childhood, children and adults with attenuated MPS I may have detectable learning disabilities. No correlation between the degree of multiorgan disease and intellectual deficits in attenuated MPS I has been observed [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.shapiro.2015.61">Shapiro et al 2015</a>]. If intellectual abilities decline, the course is more protracted than in individuals with severe disease.</p><p><b>Prognosis.</b> The rate of disease progression can range from serious life-threatening complications leading to death in the second to third decade, to a normal life span (albeit with significant disease morbidity).</p></div></div><div id="mps1.GenotypePhenotype_Correlations"><h3>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</h3><p>There is a close correlation of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genotype/">genotype</a> to <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> in MPS I based on data from 538 individuals within the international MPS I registry [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.clarke.2019.281">Clarke et al 2019</a>]. Complete loss of IDUA enzyme activity, often due to homozygosity or compound heterozygosity of the common <a href="/sites/books/NBK1162/table/mps1.T.notable_idua_variants/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmps1Tnotableiduavariants">p.Gln70Ter</a> or <a href="/sites/books/NBK1162/table/mps1.T.notable_idua_variants/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmps1Tnotableiduavariants">p.Trp402Ter</a> pathogenic variants, is associated with severe MPS I. Any combination of two "severe" variants leads to severe MPS I. In individuals with severe MPS I, 68% (257/380) had two variants that would be predicted to severely disrupt <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> transcription or translation, 76 of the remaining 123 individuals (20%, 76/380) had recurrent variants, and 47 (12.4%, 47/380) had at least one unique variant.</p><p>Attenuated MPS I is usually associated with at least one <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a> variant; registry data showed that 95.6% (151/158) of individuals with attenuated MPS I had at least one missense variant. It is postulated that the missense variant permits some residual enzyme activity.</p><p>Exceptions include the following variants associated with attenuated MPS I:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><a href="/sites/books/NBK1162/table/mps1.T.notable_idua_variants/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmps1Tnotableiduavariants">p.Tyr343Ter</a>. A premature stop codon that is used as an acceptor <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/splice-site/">splice site</a>, thereby generating an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/in-frame/">in-frame</a> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/deletion/">deletion</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.leechen.1997.939">Lee-Chen &#x00026; Range of motion exercises appear to offer some benefits in preserving joint function and should be started early. Once significant joint limitation has occurred, increased range of motion may not be achieved without HSCT.</p><p>Various orthopedic approaches can be undertaken, particularly in individuals with attenuated disease. Joint replacement and atlanto-occipital stabilization may be necessary. These procedures must be performed at appropriate times in the individual's clinical course and must take into account the presence of other disease complications.</p><p>Carpal tunnel syndrome should be treated especially in individuals with attenuated MPS I and individuals with severe MPS I who have had HSCT. Most individuals lack typical symptoms (pain, tingling, or numbness) until severe compression occurs [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.van_heest.1998.236">Van Heest et al 1998</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.bahadir.2009.402">Bahadir et al 2009</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.viskochil.2017.1269">Viskochil et al 2017</a>]; thus, nerve conduction studies should be used early in the course of disease to identify persons with carpal tunnel syndrome at a time when surgical release may be most beneficial. Surgical decompression of the median nerve results in variable restoration of motor hand activity [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.van_heest.1998.236">Van Heest et al 1998</a>]. Intervention at an early stage, prior to severe nerve damage, optimizes outcome; repeated surgery may be required.</p><p><b>Surgical management.</b> Individuals with MPS I present major anesthetic risks, including death [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.moores.1996.459">Moores et al 1996</a>]. It is appropriate for affected individuals to undergo general anesthesia in centers staffed by anesthesiologists experienced in managing individuals with a mucopolysaccharidosis [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.neufeld.2001">Neufeld &#x00026; Muenzer 2001</a>]. Important considerations:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Dysostosis multiplex can lead to instability of the spine, including the atlanto-axial joint. Careful positioning and avoidance of hyperextension of the neck are necessary.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Induction of anesthesia for any purpose can be difficult because of the difficulty of maintaining an adequate airway. Smaller-than-anticipated endotracheal tubes may be required for endotracheal intubation because the trachea may be narrowed and the vocal cords thickened.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Intubation may require fiberoptic laryngoscopy.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Recovery from anesthesia may be slow and postoperative airway obstruction is a common problem.</div></li></ul><p><b>Ophthalmologic.</b> Wearing peaked caps or eye shades can help reduce glare resulting from corneal clouding. Corneal transplantation is successful for individuals with attenuated disease, although donor grafts eventually become cloudy. Individuals with clear grafts may still experience poor vision because of involvement of the retina and/or optic nerve [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.neufeld.2001">Neufeld &#x00026; Muenzer 2001</a>].</p><p><b>Cardiovascular.</b> Cardiac valve replacement should be considered early. Bacterial endocarditis prophylaxis is advised for individuals with cardiac abnormalities [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.neufeld.2001">Neufeld &#x00026; Muenzer 2001</a>].</p><p><b>Hearing loss.</b> Tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy correct eustachian tube dysfunction and decrease upper airway obstruction. Early placement of ventilating tubes is recommended in severely affected individuals. Hearing aids should also be considered.</p><p><b>ENT (otolaryngologic).</b> Sleep apnea may require tracheotomy or high-pressure continuous positive airway pressure with supplemented oxygen. Tracheostomy is often required to maintain the airway and control pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure.</p><p><b>Gastrointestinal system.</b> Some gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea and constipation) can be controlled by diet, including control of the amount of roughage. Increased roughage and the conservative use of laxatives may ease constipation.</p><p><b>Hydrocephaly.</b> Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure and progressive ventricular enlargement indicate need for a shunting procedure. Ventriculoperitoneal shunting in individuals with MPS I who have moderate-to-severe hydrocephalus is generally palliative and improves quality of life.</p><p><b>Other.</b> Progressive compression of the spinal cord with resulting cervical myelopathy should be aggressively and quickly evaluated in individuals with attenuated disease or those who have had HSCT. Early surgical intervention may prevent severe complications.</p></div></div><div id="mps1.Surveillance"><h3>Surveillance</h3><p>The recommended minimal schedule of assessments is highlighted in <a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.muenzer.2009.19">Muenzer et al [2009]</a>.</p><p>Persons with MPS I, regardless of disease severity and mode of treatment, should be actively followed at a center that is experienced with the care of individuals with MPS disease.</p><div id="mps1.T.mucopolysaccharidosis_type_i_reco_2" class="table"><h3><span class="label">Table 7. </span></h3><div class="caption"><p>Mucopolysaccharidosis Type I: Recommended Surveillance</p></div><p class="large-table-link" style="display:none"><span class="right"><a href="/sites/books/NBK1162/table/mps1.T.mucopolysaccharidosis_type_i_reco_2/?report=objectonly" target="object">View in own window</a></span></p><div class="large_tbl" id="__mps1.T.mucopolysaccharidosis_type_i_reco_2_lrgtbl__"><table class="no_bottom_margin"><thead><tr><th id="hd_h_mps1.T.mucopolysaccharidosis_type_i_reco_2_1_1_1_1" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">System/Concern</th><th id="hd_h_mps1.T.mucopolysaccharidosis_type_i_reco_2_1_1_1_2" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Evaluation</th><th id="hd_h_mps1.T.mucopolysaccharidosis_type_i_reco_2_1_1_1_3" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Frequency</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td headers="hd_h_mps1.T.mucopolysaccharidosis_type_i_reco_2_1_1_1_1" rowspan="2" scope="rowgroup" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">

For at-risk newborn sibs when <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-testing/">prenatal testing</a> was not performed: in parallel with newborn screening, either test for the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/familial/">familial</a> <i>IDUA</i> pathogenic variants or measure IDUA enzyme activity and urinary GAG excretion.</p><p>See <a href="#mps1.Related_Genetic_Counseling_Issues">Genetic Counseling</a> for issues related to testing of at-risk relatives for <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> purposes.</p></div><div id="mps1.Pregnancy_Management"><h3>Pregnancy Management</h3><p>Women with MPS I who become pregnant require assessment and frequent monitoring of cardiorespiratory and spinal cord involvement.</p></div><div id="mps1.Therapies_Under_Investigation"><h3>Therapies Under Investigation</h3><p>With the success of ERT for MPS I demonstrated by clinical trials, an increased effort is under way to improve responsiveness to ERT and to develop other forms of therapy directed at areas/organs that may not be responsive to ERT, such as skeletal and neurologic involvement.</p><p><b>Combined ERT and HSCT.</b> There have been limited observational data on the outcome of individuals with severe MPS I treated with long-term combined ERT and HSCT. <a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.polgreen.2020.104">Polgreen et al [2020]</a> report on ten individuals who received long-term ERT post HSCT; growth may have been improved in the younger individuals.</p><p><b>Delivery of enzyme to the CNS.</b> Intravenous infusion of recombinant proteins does not lead to transfer of proteins across the blood-brain barrier. Various means to provide enzyme to the CNS are currently being researched. These approaches include CSF instillation of enzyme via direct injection, continuous pumps, microcapsule implants, and production of chimeric recombinant proteins, enabling passage across the blood-brain barrier.</p><p>A clinical trial of intrathecal ERT is currently under way in individuals who have evidence of spinal cord involvement. To date, this method has reduced CSF GAG levels and CSF pressure and has been found to be safe. The efficacy of intrathecal ERT is unclear [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.munozrojas.2008.2538">Munoz-Rojas et al 2008</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.dickson.2015a.71">Dickson et al 2015a</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.dickson.2015b.69">Dickson et al 2015b</a>].</p><p><b>Stabilization of mutated enzyme with substrate analogs.</b> It is now generally accepted that lysosomal enzymes must be processed through a complex intracellular sorting mechanism prior to transport to the lysosome. Many single-nucleotide variants underlying lysosome enzyme deficiencies lead to disease by altering the folding of the protein after translation, such that the misfolded protein cannot be transported to the lysosome. Small-molecule substrate analogs have been shown to stabilize mutated lysosomal proteins in tissue culture and thus enable transport of these enzymes to the lysosome. Once in the lysosome, these mutated enzymes are likely able to metabolize enough substrate to alter the disease course. As most individuals with attenuated MPS I have at least one <i>IDUA</i> pathogenic <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a> variant, the development of substrate analogs for &#x003b1;-L-iduronidase may lead to new forms of therapy for this disorder.</p><p><b>Substrate deprivation.</b> Decreasing the quantity of stored substrate in lysosomal disorders is currently being investigated for the treatment of <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/gaucher/">Gaucher disease</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.cox.2003.513">Cox et al 2003</a>]. Potential use of similar molecules that may decrease the production of GAGs or other substances that are stored in MPS disease may have a future role in treatment [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.piotrowska.2006.846">Piotrowska et al 2006</a>]. These approaches are still in the animal model phase, with attempts including silencing of key GAG synthetic enzymes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.kaidonis.2010.194">Kaidonis et al 2010</a>] to certain GAGs.</p><p><b>Gene- and cell-based therapy.</b> Advances in both <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a>- and stem cell-based therapies for genetic diseases could potentially influence treatment of MPS I [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.di_domenico.2005.81">Di Domenico et al 2005</a>].</p><p>Search <a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">ClinicalTrials.gov</a> in the US and <a href="https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/ctr-search/search" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">EU Clinical Trials Register</a> in Europe for access to information on clinical studies for a wide range of diseases and conditions.</p></div></div><div id="mps1.Genetic_Counseling"><h2 id="_mps1_Genetic_Counseling_">Genetic Counseling</h2><p>
<i>Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with
information on the nature, mode(s) of inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them
make informed medical and personal decisions. The following section deals with genetic
risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic
status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or
ethical issues that may arise or to substitute for consultation with a genetics
professional</i>. &#x02014;ED.</p><div id="mps1.Mode_of_Inheritance"><h3>Mode of Inheritance</h3><p>Mucopolysaccharidosis type 1 (MPS I) is inherited in an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> manner.</p></div><div id="mps1.Risk_to_Family_Members"><h3>Risk to Family Members</h3><p>
<b>Parents of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The parents of an affected child are obligate heterozygotes (i.e., presumed to be carriers of one <i>IDUA</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> disease-causing variant based on family history).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Molecular genetic testing is recommended for the parents of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> to confirm that both parents are <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for an <i>IDUA</i> disease-causing variant and to allow reliable <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/recurrence-risk/">recurrence risk</a> assessment. If a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> is detected in only one parent, the following possibilities should be considered:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>One of the pathogenic variants identified in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> occurred as a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> event in the proband [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.j_nsson.2017.519">J&#x000f3;nsson et al 2017</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Uniparental isodisomy for the parental <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosome/">chromosome</a> with the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> resulted in homozygosity for the pathogenic variant in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Heterozygotes are asymptomatic and are not at risk of developing the disorder.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Sibs of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If both parents are known to be <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for an IDUA disease-causing variant, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of being affected, a 50% chance of being an asymptomatic <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a>, and a 25% chance of being unaffected and not a carrier.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Although MPS I is a heterogeneous disorder, affected sibs will have a clinical course similar to the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Heterozygotes (carriers) are asymptomatic and are not at risk of developing the disorder.</div></li></ul><p><b>Offspring of a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> The offspring of an individual with MPS I are obligate heterozygotes (carriers) for an <i>IDUA</i> disease-causing variant. Individuals with severe MPS I generally do not reproduce, but fertility is likely unaffected by the disease; thus, advances to treatments and improved outcomes may lead to pregnancies in the future.</p><p><b>Other family members.</b> Each sib of the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>&#x02019;s parents is at a 50% risk of being a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a> of an <i>IDUA</i> disease-causing variant.</p></div><div id="mps1.Carrier_Detection"><h3>Carrier Detection</h3><p>
<b>Molecular genetic testing</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If both <i>IDUA</i> disease-causing variants have been identified in an affected family member, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> can be used to identify carriers among at-risk family members.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Molecular genetic testing of <i>IDUA</i> to determine <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a> status can be offered to both parents of an affected deceased child with MPS I in whom no molecular testing of <i>IDUA</i> was performed and for whom no DNA samples are available. If both parents are found to be carriers, the diagnosis of MPS I in the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> is confirmed and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier-testing/">carrier testing</a> can be offered to family members. If only one parent has an identifiable <i>IDUA</i> disease-causing variant, carrier testing using molecular genetic techniques would be available to the carrier parent's family members.</div></li></ul><p><b>Enzyme analysis.</b> Measurement of &#x003b1;-L-iduronidase enzyme activity in leukocytes is not a reliable method of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a> determination.</p></div><div id="mps1.Related_Genetic_Counseling_Issues"><h3>Related Genetic Counseling Issues</h3><p>See Management, <a href="#mps1.Evaluation_of_Relatives_at_Risk">Evaluation of Relatives at Risk</a> for information on evaluating at-risk relatives for the purpose of early diagnosis and treatment.</p><p>
<b>Family planning</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The optimal time for determination of genetic risk and discussion of the availability of prenatal/<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> is before pregnancy.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is appropriate to offer <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> (including discussion of potential risks to offspring and reproductive options) to young adults who are affected, are carriers, or are at risk of being carriers.</div></li></ul><p><b>DNA banking.</b> Because it is likely that testing methodology and our understanding of genes, pathogenic mechanisms, and diseases will improve in the future, consideration should be given to banking DNA from probands in whom a molecular diagnosis has not been confirmed (i.e., the causative pathogenic mechanism is unknown). For more information, see <a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.huang.2022.389">Huang et al [2022]</a>.</p></div><div id="mps1.Prenatal_Testing_and_Preimplantatio"><h3>Prenatal Testing and Preimplantation Genetic Testing</h3><p>In families in which the molecular basis of MPS I is known, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-testing/">prenatal testing</a> should be performed by <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a>, as enzyme activity measurements (particularly those performed by laboratories with limited experience) have potential inherent difficulties.</p><p>
<b>Molecular genetic testing</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Both <i>IDUA</i> disease-causing variants identified in an affected family member.</b> Once both <i>IDUA</i> pathogenic variants have been identified in an affected family member, prenatal and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> for MPS I are possible.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Carrier status documented in only one parent.</b> When one parent is a known <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygote/">heterozygote</a> and the other parent has inconclusive enzymatic activity and no <i>IDUA</i> disease-causing variant on <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a>, or when the mother is a known heterozygote and the father is unknown and/or unavailable for testing, options for <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-testing/">prenatal testing</a> can be explored in the context of formal <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a>.</div></li></ul><p><b>Biochemical genetic testing.</b> Measurement of &#x003b1;-L-iduronidase enzyme activity in cultured cells obtained by amniocentesis or CVS was formerly used for <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-diagnosis/">prenatal diagnosis</a>; however, the presence of IDUA pseudodeficiency as well as a lack of laboratories available to accurately perform this assay has led to the current use of molecular methods [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.clarke.2021">Clarke 2021</a>].</p><p>Differences in perspective may exist among medical professionals and within families regarding the use of prenatal and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a>. While most health care professionals would consider use of prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing to be a personal decision, discussion of these issues may be helpful.</p></div></div><div id="mps1.Resources"><h2 id="_mps1_Resources_">Resources</h2><p>

The protein sequence is thought to contain six N-glycosylation sites [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.zhao.1997.22758">Zhao et al 1997</a>].</p><p><b>Mechanism of disease causation.</b> Loss of function</p><p>
<b><i>IDUA</i>-specific laboratory technical considerations</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The presence of rare pseudodeficiency alleles renders interpretation of &#x003b1;-L-iduronidase enzyme activity difficult. Pseudodeficiency relates to the finding of reduced or undetectable &#x003b1;-L-iduronidase enzymatic activity with the use of artificial substrates, but no evidence of altered glycosaminoglycan metabolism with the use of radiolabeled (35S) GAG [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.aronovich.1996.75">Aronovich et al 1996</a>]. Pilot studies of newborn screening for MPS I have identified three additional <i>IDUA</i> pseudodeficiency variants: p.Ala79Thr, p.His82Gln, and p.Val322Glu [<a class="bk_pop" href="#mps1.REF.pollard.2013">Pollard et al 2013</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It has been observed that <i>IDUA</i> contains an overlapping transcript coding for a putative sulfate transporter termed Sat-1 (<i>SLC26A6</i>). No pathogenic variants of <i>SLC26A6</i> have been reported in humans to date. It is conceivable that pathogenic variants could affect the function of both <i>IDUA</i> and <i>SLC26A6</i> and thus lead to a complex <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>For ClinGen variant interpretation guidelines, see <a href="https://cspec.genome.network/cspec/ui/svi/doc/GN091" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">ClinGen Lysosomal Diseases Expert Panel Specifications to the ACMG/AMP Variant Interpretation Guidelines for IDUA Version 1.1.0</a>.</div></li></ul><div id="mps1.T.notable_idua_variants" class="table"><h3><span class="label">Table 8. </span></h3><div class="caption"><p>Notable <i>IDUA</i> Variants</p></div><p class="large-table-link" style="display:none"><span class="right"><a href="/sites/books/NBK1162/table/mps1.T.notable_idua_variants/?report=objectonly" target="object">View in own window</a></span></p><div class="large_tbl" id="__mps1.T.notable_idua_variants_lrgtbl__"><table class="no_bottom_margin"><thead><tr><th id="hd_h_mps1.T.notable_idua_variants_1_1_1_1" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Reference Sequences</th><th id="hd_h_mps1.T.notable_idua_variants_1_1_1_2" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">DNA Nucleotide Change (Alias&#x000a0;<sup>1</sup>)</th><th id="hd_h_mps1.T.notable_idua_variants_1_1_1_3" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Predicted Protein Change</th><th id="hd_h_mps1.T.notable_idua_variants_1_1_1_4" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Comment [Reference]&#x000a0;<sup>2</sup></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td headers="hd_h_mps1.T.notable_idua_variants_1_1_1_1" rowspan="18" scope="rowgroup" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">
