# Clinical characteristic and prognosis of adult inpatients with high altitude pulmonary edema: a multi-central, retrospective study in plateau of China from 2012 to 2022.
**Authors:** Feng X, Hua M, Yang C, Zhu S, Ma X, Yang X, La Z, Sun Z, Shi X
**Journal:** BMC Pulm Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12890-025-03798-4](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-025-03798-4)

## Content

BACKGROUND: High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a common consequence of inadequate acclimatization to high altitude. However, there is currently limited evidence regarding the clinical characteristics and risk factors contributing to adverse outcomes in patients with HAPE. METHODS: This study enrolled 411 patients with HAPE from four hospitals in the plateau, between 2012 and 2022, and described their clinical characteristics. The relationship between the altitude of onset and the duration of stay in plateau and adverse outcomes was analyzed using restricted cubic splines. Stepwise logistic regression was conducted to investigate the risk factors for adverse outcomes in HAPE patients. RESULTS: The risk of pulmonary hypertension in HAPE patients was positively associated with onset altitude, while the risk of pulmonary hypertension, thrombosis, high-altitude cerebral edema, and death was negatively related to duration of stay in plateau (P for overall association =0.029, P for nonlinear association =0.071). The multivariate logistic model showed that HAPE patients with onset altitude of ≥ 3,500 m, duration of stay in plateau ≤ 1-day, elevated lactic dehydrogenase and prolonged prothrombin time had a higher risk of pulmonary hypertension (OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.23–0.74). Patients with a history of hypertension and longer hospital stays had an increased risk of thromboembolism (OR = 6.71, 95% CI: 1.77–25.46). Fever (OR = 3.16, 95% CI: 1.17–8.55), headache (OR = 2.79, 95% CI: 1.37–5.65), and elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels (OR = 3.94, 95% CI: 1.93–8.04) were risk factors for high-altitude cerebral edema. Additionally, mortality risk was higher in patients with headache (OR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.01–4.39), pulmonary hypertension (OR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.08–8.13), high-altitude cerebral edema, and elevated BUN (OR = 5.26, 95% CI: 2.39–11.59) and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide levels (OR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.20–5.78). CONCLUSIONS: Our findings indicate the importance of careful assessment and management of clinical indicators and risk factors in patients with HAPE, further to take effective early interventions.

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is characterized by loss of stamina, dyspnea, and dry cough during exertion, followed by dyspnea at rest, rales, cyanosis, cough, and pink, frothy sputum [1]. HAPE is a severe form of high-altitude illness that typically occurs in individuals who rapidly ascend to 2500 m or higher within 1–5 days of arrival [2].

In general, the incidence of HAPE is approximately 1 in 10,000 visitors [3]. However, up to 7% of travelers experience HAPE on their first ascent to high altitudes above 4,000 meters [4]. Additionally, individuals with a history of HAPE have a 60% likelihood of developing symptoms again [5]. Although the incidence is not high, HAPE is a high-risk condition that can result in mortality rates of up to 50% if not diagnosed and treated promptly [5]. Fortunately, previous studies demonstrated that HAPE is entirely reversible if recognized promptly and treated appropriately [6]. However, it has been reported that some patients who manage to descend from high altitude or receive professional medical care on-site still experience worsening symptoms, and in some cases, death can occur after descend, particularly in those with both HAPE and high-altitude cerebral edema. Moreover, most high-altitude regions are extremely remote, making it challenging to implement preventative measures in advance or accurately predict the risk of HAPE [7]. Thus, early identification of high risk HAPE patients is crucial.

Although some case series on HAPE have been published, no prior studies have focused on inpatients to examine clinical characteristics and prognosis. Consequently, the risk factor estimates for prognosis in these case series are not robust, and the clinical characteristics and prognosis of remain poorly described. In this study, we present data of all patients admitted with confirmed HAPE and a definite clinical outcome at the four hospitals in Qinghai—Qinghai Provincial People’s Hospital, Qinghai University Affiliated Hospital, Qinghai Red Cross Hospital, and Qinghai Cardio-Cerebrovascular Specialty Hospital from 2012 to 2022. We aim to identify risk factors for in-hospital mortality for patients and describe the clinical characteristics observed during hospitalization.

This study retrospectively included 416 patients diagnosed with HAPE in 4 hospitals at different altitudes in Qinghai Province (Qinghai Provincial People’s Hospital, Qinghai Red Cross Hospital, Qinghai Cardio-Cerebrovascular Specialty Hospital, and Qinghai University Affiliated Hospital) from January 2012 to December 2022. Then we excluded 5 patients with invalid ID number, and finally included 411 HAPE patients.

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board at Qinghai Provincial People’s Hospital, Qinghai University (Ethical number: 2022 − 301). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived as this was a retrospective study.

HAPE clinical diagnostic criteria were: (1) recent arrival at altitudes>2500–3000 m within 1–5 days; (2) Early symptoms include excessive exertional dyspnoea in relation to the patient’s companions, mild cough, chest tightness and reduced exercise performance. As oedema progresses, cough and dyspnoea worsen and orthopnoea develops. Gurgling in the chest and pink frothy sputum indicate advanced cases; physical examination: central cyanosis, shortness of breath, tachycardia, decreased breath sounds, and audible wet rales in the lungs during the stage of alveolar edema; (3) Chest CT shows early thickening of lung texture, scattered flocculent, nodular or reticular shadow, scattered flaky ground-glass density shadow in both lungs and/or Chest X-ray reveals that unilateral or bilateral infiltration shadows centered on the pulmonary hilum, often diffuse and irregularly distributed, or fused into a large patchy shadow. The heart shadow is mostly normal, and there are also signs of pulmonary hypertension and enlargement of the right heart; (4) exclusion of other diseases, especially acute respiratory distress syndrome, cardiogenic pulmonary edema, severe pneumonia; (5) symptoms remission by transfer to plains or low altitude [8].

Standards for clinical diagnosis of high altitude pulmonary arterial hypertension were: mean pulmonary artery pressure > 30mmHg or pulmonary artery systolic pressure > 50mmg measured by echocardiography [9]. Acute high-altitude cerebral edema was a clinical diagnosis with the patient typically presenting with signs of encephalopathy, preceded by signs and symptoms of Acute Mountain Sickness [10]. Thromboembolism’s what happens when arteries or veins blood flow were obstructed by using Vascular ultrasound or reconstruction Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) scanning.

Patient demographic data, including age, sex, nationality, ID number, birthplace, permanent residential addresses, and onset address were collected from electronic medical records. The onset address was defined as the location where a patient first experienced HAPE symptoms. To determine the specific elevation, we first cleaned and anonymized the addresses. These addresses were then uploaded to the Gaode Data Open Platform (https://lbs.amap.com/), where we retrieved the corresponding longitude and latitude for each address. Since Gaode uses the GCJ-02 coordinate system, we converted these coordinates to the WGS coordinate system for accurate altitude matching. Finally, we employed ArcGIS software to determine the corresponding elevation based on the longitude and latitude data. We also collected clinical data including medical history, symptoms, imaging examinations, anthropometric measurements, laboratory tests, treatment, and prognosis. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m).

We present baseline characteristics of patients with HAPE based on the elevation of onset. Normally distributed continuous variables were described using the mean ± standard deviation and the differences between groups were compared based on the student’s t-test. The continuous variables of the skewed distribution were described using the median (upper quartile, lower quartile), and the comparison between groups was based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were described as the number of cases (composition ratios) in the group, and compared differences between groups based on chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability test. We used restrictive cubic splines to show the association between altitude onset and duration of altitude entry and adverse outcomes (pulmonary hypertension, thrombosis, high-altitude cerebral edema, death) in patients with HAPE, and we also showed the association between high-altitude polycythemia and duration of plateau access. We used stepwise logistic regression to explore the influencing factors for each of the four adverse outcomes (pulmonary hypertension, thrombosis, high-altitude cerebral edema, death), and plotted forest plots to show odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc. Cary, NC) and R (version 3.5.3, https://www.r-project.org/). We used multiple imputation method to impute missing data. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Among the 411 HAPE patients, the altitude of onset was 3787 m (3300–4200 m). Those with an onset altitude of ≥ 3500 m had a higher altitude of birthplace [2200 (1100, 2261) vs. 1607 (97.5, 2261) m] or permanent residence [2200 (1000, 2261) vs. 500 (54, 2200) m] and a lower BMI (24.69 ± 2.67 vs. 25.31 ± 3.08 kg/m2) compared to those with an onset altitude of < 3500 m. In addition, the proportion of heart failure comorbidities was lower in HAPE patients whose onset altitude was above 3500 m (11.5 vs. 20.7%), but the proportion of different clinical symptoms was not significantly different from that in HAPE patients whose onset altitude was < 3500 m (Table 1).

As shown in Fig. 1, we only found that the risk of combining pulmonary hypertension in HAPE patients was positively associated with onset altitude (Fig. 1A, P for overall association =0.029, P for nonlinear association =0.071), but no significant association between thrombosis, high-altitude cerebral edema, death, and altitude of onset (Figure B-D). In addition, we found that the risk of pulmonary hypertension, thrombosis, high-altitude cerebral edema, and death in patients with HAPE was inverted L-shaped with the duration of plateau entry (Fig. 1E-H), i.e., the risk decreased with the time of plateau entry in the first two days and then stabilized, while the risk of plateau polycythemia was positively correlated with the duration of plateau entry (Fig. 1I, P for overall association =0.010, P for nonlinear association =0.347).

The results of the multivariate logistic model showed that the risk of pulmonary hypertension was increased by 79% in patients with HAPE at an altitude of ≥ 3500 m (OR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.02–1.34), and the risk was reduced by 58% in patients with HAPE who entered the plateau for more than 1 day compared with those who entered the plateau within 1 day (OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.23–0.74). Lactic dehydrogenase (LDH) > 250 IU/L (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.12–3.42) and prothrombin time (PT) > 14 s (OR = 3.50, 95% CI: 1.27–9.63) are risk factors for the development of pulmonary hypertension, while systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, lymphocyte count > 4 × 109, PT < 12 s, dyspnea, and peripheral distribution of lungs on imaging indicate a lower risk of developing the pulmonary artery (Fig. 2A).

We found that patients with HAPE with a history of hypertension had an increased risk of thrombosis (OR = 6.71, 95% CI: 1.77–25.46), and that the risk of developing thrombosis increased by 2.59-fold for each five-day increase in hospital stay (OR = 3.59, 95% CI: 2.12–6.08). The presence of cough indicates a lower risk of thrombosis (OR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.04–0.80) (Fig. 2B).

We also found an increased risk of high-altitude cerebral edema in patients with a temperature of > 38 degrees Celsius (OR = 3.16, 95% CI: 1.17–8.55), headache symptoms (OR = 2.79, 95% CI: 1.37–5.65), a blood urea nitrogen (BUN) > of 7.1 mmol/L (OR = 3.94, 95% CI: 1.93–8.04), and without co-infection (OR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.36–6.03). Conversely, patients with HAPE who had cough (OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.17–0.74) and dyspnea (OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.18–0.97) were at lower risk of developing high-altitude cerebral edema (Fig. 2C).

For death, we found that patients with HAPE who developed headache (OR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.01–4.39), pulmonary hypertension (OR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.08–8.13), BUN > 7.1 mmol/L (OR = 5.26, 95% CI: 2.39–11.59), and NT-proBNP ≥ 100 pg/mL (OR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.20–5.78) had a 1.11-fold, 1.96-fold, 4.26-fold, and 1.63-fold increased risk of death, respectively. Similarly, patients with HAPE who present with symptoms of cough (OR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.15–0.93) and dyspnea (OR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.14–0.80) have a lower risk of death(Fig. 2D).

Up-to-date, this is the first multi-central study conducted on plateau to investigate the prognosis of HAPE. We examined patients with confirmed HAPE and found a high prevalence of pulmonary hypertension, thrombotic events, and high-altitude cerebral edema and we investigated the associated risk factors. In addition, this study explored the mortality rate and factors related to in-hospital death for HAPE patients during their hospital stays. A total of 411 patients with confirmed HAPE underwent Doppler echocardiograph, vascular ultrasound, and CT scan based on their clinical condition. The prevalence of pulmonary hypertension, thrombotic events, and high-altitude cerebral edema in our study population was 55.2% (227/411), 5.4% (22/411), and 12.2% (50/411). The total fatality rate of all the HAPE inpatients was 11.7% (48/411).

HAPE rarely occurs at altitudes lower than 2,440 meters [11]. The incidence of HAPE influenced by the elevation, altitude gain, speed of ascent, and individual susceptibility [12]. As it expected, altitude plays an essential role in the onset of HAPE, with prevalence increasing as elevation rises. In our study, 295 patients (72.8%, 295/411) made their first rapid ascent to altitudes above 3,500 m. These findings are consistent with previous investigations [2, 13].

Several mechanisms contribute to prevalence of pulmonary hypertension, thrombotic events, and high-altitude cerebral edema in HAPE patients. First, individuals susceptible to HAPE often fail to respond adequately to acute hypoxia resulting in more severe pulmonary artery hypertension. This response is mediated by sympathetic overactivation, damage to the endothelium and alveolar epithelium, which are exacerbated by inflammatory responses under hypoxic conditions [14, 15]. Key pathways and mediators involved endothelin-1, vascular endothelial growth factor, hypoxia-inducible factor, and inducible nitric oxide synthase. Furthermore, sodium channels that regulate water transport, Na-K-ATPase, and aquaporin, along with changes in pulmonary hemodynamics and elevated hydrostatic pressure, play a vital role in the acute and rapid progression of HAPE [16–18]. Second, certain aspects of altitude excursions can increase blood viscosity, such as dehydration leading to hemoconcentration and polycythemia, as well as the compensatory rise in hematocrit during acclimatization, which likely elevates the risk of thrombotic events. Furthermore, studies have shown hypoxia and low temperature at high altitudes can induce hypercoagulable states, thereby increasing the incidence of cardio-cerebrovascular events [19].

Notably, further analysis in our research found as the duration of time spent at high altitude increased, the proportion of HAPE patients with pulmonary hypertension, thrombotic events, and high-altitude cerebral edema, as well as the mortality rate, gradually decreased, which is highly consistent with the acclimatization. Previous studies have shown that good acclimatization leads to a reduced incidence and severity of acute mountain sickness during slow, staged ascents [20, 21]. Compared individuals who are acutely exposed to high-altitude hypoxia, the proportion of subjects with confirmed pulmonary hypertension was significantly lower in those who were chronically exposed to high altitude hypoxia [22]. In most patients, symptoms of pulmonary hypertension resolved within 1 to 21 months after returning to sea level [23]. Importantly, pulmonary hypertension and signs of right ventricular failure in patients with subacute adult mountain sickness gradually reverse after spending several weeks at low altitude [24]. Occasionally, the disease may further progress, even if moving to low altitude, and lead to death [23]. These findings suggested that in-hospital patients with inadequate acclimatization should be diagnosed and treated promptly to improve the prognosis and reduce mortality.

One of the unique responses of pulmonary circulation to alveolar hypoxia is hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV). When HPV becomes sustained, it activates molecular pathways that lead to pulmonary artery remodeling, ultimately resulting in high-altitude pulmonary hypertension (HAPH) [25, 26]. We found that those with altitude of onset ≥ 3500 m, LDH > 250IU/L, PT > 14 s, had a higher risk of HAPE, while those with SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, lymphocyte count > 4 × 109, PT < 12 s, dyspnea, and peripheral distribution of lungs on imaging showed a lower risk of HAPE. Altitudes between 3,500 and 5,500 m are considered very high, where extreme hypoxia can cause SaO2 to drop to 75–85% and PaO2 to 40–60 mmHg [27]. Acute hypoxia disrupts energy metabolism in cardiomyocytes, reducing membrane protein synthesis and damaging cell membrane integrity. This increases membrane permeability, leading to the release of large amounts of intracellular LDH into the circulation [28]. Abnormal coagulation function is considered one of the important physiological processes in pulmonary hypertension, providing mechanical support for the association between PT and HAPH [29]. Previous studies have shown that SBP tends to increase with altitude during plateau acclimatization [30], making low SBP a potential indicator of inadequate adaptation. This study suggests that patients with pulmonary edema with SBP < 120 mmHg should pay attention to the possibility of subsequent HAPH. Additionally, previous studies have found that patients with pulmonary hypertension have significantly lower lymphocyte counts compared to those without the condition [31], which may support the findings of this study despite the lack of mechanistic evidence.

We found that a history of hypertension and a prolonged hospital stay may be associated with an increased risk of thrombotic events in patients with HAPE. Inflammatory responses under hypoxic conditions might contribute to the pathogenesis of HAPE in susceptible individuals, potentially leading to damage of endothelium and alveolar epithelium [16, 17, 32]. Moreover, certain factors associated with altitude exposure—such as dehydration leading to hemoconcentration, low temperatures, bed rest, reduced physical activity, and polycythemia—can increase blood viscosity and the risk of thromboembolism [19]. For these reasons, this paper suggests an elevated risk of venous stasis, vascular injury, and hypercoagulability in patients with HAPE.

Additionally, we found that HAPE patients with body temperature ≥ 38 °C, headache symptoms, BUN > 7.1mmol/L and no concurrent infection were more likely to develop high-altitude cerebral edema. Headache is the most common and prominent symptom of acute mountain sickness. Some researchers proposed that high-altitude headache is multifactorial, involving various chemical and mechanical factors activating a final common pain pathway [33]. Fever and dehydration can elevate serum creatinine levels in patients with high-altitude cerebral edema.

Those with headache, BUN > 7.1mmol/L, NT-proBNP ≥ 100pg/mL, and pulmonary hypertension had higher risk of mortality. There is reasonable concern that arterial hypoxemia during prolonged high-altitude exposure, along with its neurohumoral responses, could exacerbate kidney damage and increase the risk of its progression [34]. NT-proBNP release may be stimulated by hypoxia and is elevated in association with pulmonary artery hypertension [35–38]. Therefore, headache, elevated BUN and NT-proBNP, pulmonary hypertension could more accurately reflect body hypoxic damage and the severity of HAPE. It is important to highlight that the mortality rate among patients with HAPE and high-altitude cerebral edema was 87.5% (42/48), whereas the mortality rate for HAPE patients without cerebral edema was only 2.2% (8/363). This factor were excluded from the final multivariate regression model due to insufficient overlap, which prevented the model from converging. This finding underscores the need to closely monitor the mortality risk for HAPE patients exhibiting symptoms such as headache, BUN > 7.1 mmol/L, NT-proBNP ≥ 100 pg/mL, pulmonary hypertension, and high-altitude cerebral edema.

Moreover, we also observed that patients with HAPE who developed respiratory symptoms (cough, dyspnea) in the absence of other clinical manifestations had a lower risk of adverse outcomes. It is widely known that hypoxia can induce inflammation and activate hypoxia signaling pathway which led to levels of circulating proinflammatory cytokines increase and leakage of fluid [14, 39, 40], then causes only the lung or systemic inflammation reaction. The findings suggested the patient with HAPE complicating systemic inflammatory reaction syndrome has poor adverse outcomes compared with the single HAPE patient. In essence, the results may reflect the severity of acute hypoxia-induced inflammation response. Our data raised the question of screening for the degree of inflammatory response and developed effective early interventions to improve outcomes in hospitalized patients with HAPE.

Our study has some limitations. First, all HAPE patients in this study were inpatients from four hospitals in Qinghai Province, which may limit the generalizability of the study findings, especially among patients with mild HAPE. Second, some patients had ultrasound scans only in the early stage and did not have continuous dynamic monitoring, which may cause the clinical characteristics to be underestimated. Third, due to the critical condition of the patients, CT scans were restricted, which significantly underestimated the incidence of thrombotic events and high-altitude cerebral edema. Fourth, we haven’t collected the ways in which HAPE patients enter the plateau. Therefore, for the people who arrive at the plateau by flight, there may be a certain lag in the collection of the altitude of incidence, and further research and confirmation are needed in the follow-up. Finally, this study is retrospective. We hope to conduct a prospective larger cohort to further clarify the clinical characteristics in HAPE patients, determine the corresponding risk factors, and explore optimized individualized preventive measures to reduce HAPE-related adverse prognoses.

An increased altitude of onset was associated with a heightened risk of pulmonary hypertension in patients with HAPE, while a prolonged stay at plateau altitude is linked to a reduced risk of adverse outcomes. In HAPE patients, elevated LDH levels, prolonged PT, and decreased SBP correlated with a higher risk of pulmonary hypertension. Additionally, a history of hypertension and extended hospitalization were associated with an increased risk of thrombotic events. Symptoms such as fever, headache, elevated BUM, and the absence of infection indicated a higher risk of high-altitude cerebral edema. Furthermore, the presence of headache, elevated BUN elevated NT-proBNP levels, pulmonary hypertension, and high-altitude cerebral edema signified an increased risk of mortality. Finally, screening for the degree of inflammatory response and developing effective early interventions should play an extremely important role in improving outcomes in hospitalized patients with HAPE.
