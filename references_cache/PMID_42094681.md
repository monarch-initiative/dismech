# Evaluating the potential carcinogenic hazard of diisononyl phthalate in humans via systematic integration of human, animal cancer studies, and mechanistic data.
**Authors:** Lea IA, Buerger AN, Vincent MJ, Rivera BN, Choksi NY, Schaefer H, Britt J, Fitch S, Haws L, Borghoff SJ
**Journal:** Curr Res Toxicol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.crtox.2026.100295](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.crtox.2026.100295)

## Content

Diisononyl phthalate (DINP) is a high molecular weight phthalate used in commercial products and polyvinyl chloride production. Herein, a systematic evaluation of DINP evidence streams (i.e., human cancer, animal cancer, and mechanistic data) was carried out to inform carcinogenic hazard in humans. Relevant data from peer-reviewed literature and publicly available laboratory reports were extracted and critically appraised. Mechanistic data were organized according to the Key Characteristics of Carcinogens (KCCs) and integrated into key events in rodent cancer modes of action (MoAs). Evidence from epidemiological studies is limited, but does not indicate an association between DINP exposure and cancer, with three studies reporting no association with breast cancer, and one reporting an imprecise increase in prostate cancer risk. Four chronic bioassays demonstrated DINP causes cancer in rodents, with increases in liver tumors in mice and rats, kidney tumors in male F344 rats, and mononuclear cell leukemia (MNCL) in F344 rats. Mechanistic data strongly support that DINP is non-genotoxic (KCC2), and that in rodents DINP induces oxidative stress (KCC5) and alters cell proliferation (KCC10). Multiple evidence stream integration and interpretation support that DINP elicits rodent-specific liver tumors through the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha, a MoA widely considered to lack human relevance. Likewise, the weak kidney tumor response in male rats was attributed to α2u-globulin nephropathy, a male rat-specific response. MNCL, a common lesion in aging F344 rats, was not considered relevant for predicting human cancer. Together, these data indicate that DINP is unlikely to pose a carcinogenic hazard to humans.

Diisononyl phthalate (DINP) is a high molecular weight phthalate and complex mixture of diesters available as two types: 1,2-benzenedicarboxylic acid esterified with branched alcohols consisting of C8-C10 (C9 rich) alkyl side chains (primarily C7 carbon backbone) or esterified with isononyl alcohols (CASRNs 68515–48-0 and 28553–12-0). DINP is mainly used as a plasticizer in polyvinyl chloride (PVC) manufacturing, although it is also incorporated into a variety of other commercial and consumer products. Human exposure to DINP occurs via oral, inhalation, and dermal exposure routes. Biomonitoring studies report widespread detection of DINP metabolites in human urine, indicating exposure in the general population, although significant declines in urinary metabolite levels have recently been reported in young German adults (Kasper-Sonnenberg et al., 2012). In indoor environments, DINP partitions among air, dust, and surfaces, producing potential inhalation, dermal, and incidental oral (dust-ingestion) exposures in the general population (Wei et al., 2025). However, the United States (US) Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)’s recent Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA) DINP risk evaluation (US EPA, 2025a) identified inhalation exposure in workers as the primary route driving risk assessment, and indicated dermal, ingestion and ambient inhalation air exposure as pathways not of concern for the general population.

Several authoritative and regulatory agencies have published human health DINP assessments. In early 2025, US EPA published its TSCA risk evaluation, finding that high dose oral exposures in experimental animals produced liver and developmental toxicity, which were identified as the most sensitive non-cancer endpoints (US EPA, 2025a, US EPA, 2025b). These effects were used to derive points of departure of 12 mg/kg-day (acute and intermediate exposure) based on developmental toxicity and 3.5 mg/kg-day (chronic exposure) on liver toxicity. DINP was also observed to induce liver tumors in experimental animals (rats and mice), but only at doses higher than those causing non-cancer effects. The European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) Risk Assessment Committee reviewed the reproductive and developmental toxicity database in 2018 and concluded that DINP does not warrant reproductive toxicity classification under the Classification, Labelling and Packaging (CLP) Regulation (ECHA, 2018). The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) Food Contact Materials, Enzymes and Processing Aids (CEP) Panel (EFSA, 2019) evaluated the toxicological database for DINP and identified a no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) of 15 mg/kg-bw/day based on spongiosis hepatis, increased liver enzyme levels, and elevated liver and kidney weights in F344 rats (Lington, 1997, EFSA, 2019). For reproductive and developmental endpoints, EFSA identified a no-observed-effect level (NOEL) of 50 mg/kg-bw/day based on reduced fetal testosterone production and testicular histopathology (e.g., multinucleated germ cells) (Clewell, 2013, EFSA, 2019). EFSA concluded that DINP affects both reproductive and hepatic systems, with the liver being the more sensitive target organ. The California Environmental Protection Agency’s Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA) listed DINP as a carcinogen based on evidence from chronic dietary bioassays in rats and mice. Findings included significant increases in liver tumors (three studies), mononuclear cell leukemia (MNCL), a common lesion in F344 rats, and kidney tumors (one study). Non-significant increases in pancreatic islet cell tumors, Leydig cell tumors, and uterine tumors were reported in another study (OEHHA, 2013).

To date, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has not evaluated the possible human cancer hazard associated with DINP exposure; however, DINP is scheduled for review in June 2026, along with other phthalates (butyl benzyl phthalate and dibutyl phthalate). A substantial body of literature now evaluates DINP’s toxicity and potential carcinogenicity in epidemiological studies, experimental animal models, and mechanistic investigations. Currently, IARC uses the key characteristics of carcinogens (KCCs) framework to organize and evaluate mechanistic data as a stream of evidence for evaluating substances and their potential to cause cancer in humans (IARC, 2019). The KCC framework was originally defined by Smith et al. (2016) and represents a series of ten characteristics or biological properties observed for substances classified as carcinogenic in humans (Group 1) according to IARC. This identification and sorting of mechanistic data within the KCCs have been adopted in hazard assessments not only by IARC, but also by the National Toxicology Program (NTP) Report on Carcinogens (RoC) (2018). The framework has also been aligned with key events (KEs) in carcinogenic adverse outcome pathways (AOP) and modes of action (MoA) frameworks for selected carcinogens (Meek, 2014, Meek and Wikoff, 2023). IARC’s approach to mechanistic data has been considered by some to be overly broad, such that general mechanistic events (e.g., genotoxicity) are considered as strong evidence without establishing whether a causal or quantitative link to cancer in humans exists (Becker, 2017, Goodman, 2020). While helpful to organize mechanistic evidence, by themselves, the KCCs do not predict carcinogenicity or distinguish carcinogens from non‑carcinogens. Integration across data streams and consideration of dose–response, potency, exposure metrics, and time order of events are elements that are central to MoA and AOP frameworks and needed to establish carcinogenic potential.

The purpose of this assessment is to conduct a systematic evaluation of the evidence base concerning DINP’s carcinogenic potential in humans. The assessment encompasses human and animal cancer data, along with mechanistic evidence organized across KCCs. The mechanistic data were evaluated to support KEs in animal tumor MoAs and within biological pathways relevant to human tumors, if identified. This structured organization of mechanistic evidence, combined with the human and animal cancer evidence streams, supports a transparent and scientifically robust approach to evaluating DINP’s carcinogenic potential in humans. This work constitutes a comprehensive and systematic DINP carcinogenicity evaluation that includes rigorous evidence identification, critical study appraisal across all three evidence streams, and data integration to reach an overall conclusion for the potential cancer hazard associated with DINP exposure.

The approach for assessing DINP’s carcinogenic potential includes systematic identification of literature, screening and categorization of the data by evidence stream, data extraction and critical appraisal, followed by synthesis and integration across evidence streams (Fig. 1). The systematic evaluation framework employed herein was constructed from the principles described in the IARC Preamble (IARC, 2019). The evidence considered included cancer data in humans and experimental animal models, as well as mechanistic data from exposed humans (epidemiological investigations), in vivo animal studies, in vitro experimental studies, and in silico models. The mechanistic evidence includes a summary of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) of DINP in humans and experimental animal models. It also evaluates data mapped to KCCs, including data regarding emerging issues of cancer. Finally, it examines MoAs of tumors in experimental animal models that do not, or are unlikely to, operate in humans (IARC, 2019). The human and animal cancer data were integrated according to the principles of the World Health Organization (WHO)’s International Programme on Chemical Safety (IPCS) (Meek, 2014, Meek and Wikoff, 2023). The mechanistic data were organized by KCC (Smith et al., 2016) and then mapped to KEs across biological pathways for proposed MoAs and assessed according Wikoff et al. (2019).

A systematic literature search was conducted to identify relevant human cancer, animal cancer, and mechanistic data for DINP. Two search strategies were designed to maximize identifying potentially relevant publications. The first search was developed to broadly target any DINP study and was based on syntax developed for DINP by US EPA, consisting of chemical name, synonyms, abbreviations, and CASRNs. The second search was developed to capture epidemiological DINP and cancer investigations and consisted of DINP-specific metabolites and cancer-related syntax. For this review, only metabolites that are specific to DINP were considered (i.e., monoisononyl phthalate [MINP], mono(hydroxyisononyl) phthalate [MHINP, 7OH-MMeOP], and mono(oxoisononyl) phthalate [MOINP, 7oxo-MMeOP]). Metabolites that were not specific to DINP but were shared between DINP and similar chain-length phthalates, such as diisodecyl phthalate (DIDP), were largely excluded. The only exception was inclusion of mono-carboxy-(iso)octyl phthalate (MC[I]OP, 7cx-MMeHP) which is a metabolite of both DINP and DIDP. This metabolite is commonly used in the literature as a biomarker of DINP exposure, and has been used in recent US EPA assessments as a DINP biomarker in epidemiological studies (US EPA, 2025b). Both searches were conducted in the PubMed and Embase databases on October 10, 2024; they were subsequently re-run on August 1, September 2, and October 1, 2025. The literature search syntax is detailed in Appendix A. Literature searches were supported by hand searching the identified peer-reviewed studies, as well as authoritative documents to identify publicly available laboratory reports.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed based on Population, Exposure, Comparator, and Outcome (PECO) statements and used to identify relevant literature and laboratory reports with human cancer, animal cancer, or mechanistic data relevant to assessing DINP’s carcinogenic potential. These are described in Appendix A.

Peer-reviewed literature identified from these searches was deduplicated and uploaded to SysRev, an artificial intelligence (AI)-focused platform, for categorization via auto-labeling. Auto-labels were developed according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Based on the title-abstract (TiAb), SysRev was then used to predict the exposure and categorize the measured endpoints. A subset of 20 papers was assessed to evaluate the accuracy of the SysRev responses; this review indicated that the AI approach did not systematically misclassify or exclude relevant studies. Peer-reviewed literature with the AI-labels was then uploaded into DistillerSR for manual TiAb review by a team of four scientific reviewers who performed an unblinded second review (one reviewer per paper) as quality control of the SysRev predictions for all papers. No papers were excluded based solely on the SysRev responses. When the reviewer response conflicted with the predicted response from SysRev, a consensus answer was achieved through discussion with a senior reviewer. This process provided support for the reliability of the initial AI-based relevance screen indicating that SysRev provided accurate but slightly over-inclusive responses. Studies (except ADME studies) determined to meet or potentially meet the inclusion/exclusion criteria at the TiAb level were advanced for full-text review, where their relevance was confirmed before advancing to data extraction. All studies underwent full-text review by two reviewers. When responses between the two reviewers conflicted, a consensus answer was achieved through discussion with a senior reviewer.

In vitro data were also downloaded from the US EPA Forecaster (ToxCast) program (Kavlock, 2012, Richard, 2021). Assay data were retrieved from invitroDB v4.2 using an open-source R package (ToxCast pipeline or tcpl) (Filer et al., 2017) and are made available in Appendix B.

Data were extracted from all studies determined to be relevant at full-text review. Study quality and reliability were appraised for each extracted study using the tool appropriate to the evidence stream.

Human epidemiological studies that evaluated an association between cancer incidence and/or mortality and DINP exposure underwent data extraction, including tabulation of study design details (e.g., study population and selection details, participation rates, follow-up and latency information, exposure characterization methods, outcome ascertainment method, statistical methods, and included covariates) and cancer-related outcomes (including reported risk estimates and confidence intervals). These studies were then evaluated for risk of bias (RoB) based on the NTP Office of Heath and Translation considerations (OHAT, 2019). Studies were classified as Tier I (low RoB), Tier II (neither high nor low RoB), or Tier III (high RoB).

Chronic 2-year cancer bioassays in animal models exposed to DINP were evaluated for tumor outcomes. Neoplastic and non-neoplastic tumor incidence per study were tabulated, along with relevant study design details (e.g., species, strain, sex, age, exposure route and duration, and number of animals and mortality). An authoritative DINP assessment was reviewed for additional context (CPSC, 2001, US EPA, 2025a). Animal cancer bioassays (laboratory reports and peer-reviewed literature) underwent critical appraisal using the SciRAP quality appraisal tool (Molander et al., 2015) and subject matter expert (SME) evaluation. These studies were not included as part of the mechanistic assessment.

Mechanistic data comprising endpoints mapping to the ten KCCs (Smith et al., 2016) along with emerging issues of cancer, summarization of ADME in humans and experimental animal models, and tumor response MoA not considered to operate in humans were reviewed. Mechanistic data for each KCC, as well as emerging issues of cancer, were extracted, along with relevant study design information. Each extracted mechanistic endpoint was evaluated for evidential strength, biological activity, and reliability. Laboratory reports and peer-reviewed publications for the same study were considered together.

Human and animal primary literature and review articles identified (at TiAb level) as containing ADME information were used to summarize DINP ADME across multiple exposure routes and species. The key metabolites identified during this process were qualitatively considered when assessing DINP’s carcinogenic potential.

Two sources for mechanistic data were identified: peer-reviewed publications and study reports and in vitro data downloaded from the US EPA Forecaster (ToxCast) program (Kavlock, 2012, Richard, 2021).

Study details were extracted from relevant mechanistic studies, including chemical characterization (CAS RN, purity, supplier), strain, sex, route of exposure, exposure duration, doses/concentrations, tissue of analysis, and corresponding cytotoxicity/ overt toxicity. Each endpoint measured in an assay was mapped to an individual KCC, and details were extracted, including the KCC evaluated (i.e., KCCs 1–10), endpoint name (e.g., chromosome damage), assay/method (e.g., chromosome aberration assay), and, for exposed human assays, biomarker/outcome (e.g., percent aberrant cells). The activity score (1 = active; 0 = inactive; −1 = activity in a direction considered non-adverse) was determined, consistent with guidance from Wikoff et al. (2019), and a summary narrative of the findings was generated. Findings that occurred at or above the concentrations/doses that elicited cytotoxicity/overt toxicity were not considered reliable indicators of effects.

Assay data from ToxCast were retrieved from invitroDB v4.2 using an open-source R package (ToxCast pipeline or tcpl) (Filer et al., 2017). An R script was used to extract curve-fit parameters, quality flags, hit calls, z-scores, and AC50 values. DINP was deemed to be “active” when the continuous hit call was ≥0.90. Data quality for active assays was determined using a step-wise process. First, the Integrated Chemical Environment (ICE) database criteria for invitroDB v4.2 were applied (NTP, 2025). If any of the ICE criteria were met, then the hit call was changed from active to inactive. Next, AC50 scores (if available) for an assay endpoint were compared to the AC50 values for the matched viability assay (if available), and if the AC50 for the assay endpoint was greater than the AC50 for the matched viability assay, the hit call was changed to inactive. When no matched viability assay was available, the z-score was considered. If the z-score was less than 3, the assay was considered inactive (Judson et al., 2016). Finally, if the AC50 was higher than the DINP global cytotoxicity AC50 value, the hit call was changed to inactive.

Several sources were considered to guide mapping mechanistic endpoints to individual KCCs (Guyton, 2018, Madia, 2021, Rigutto, 2025. 2025.). Endpoint mapping was confirmed on release of the IARC technical report on KCCs during this assessment (IARC, 2025, IARC, 2025). US EPA ToxCast assays were mapped to KCCs using a hierarchical framework based on KCC-assay mapping information from IARC’s kc-hits (Reisfeld et al., 2022), and ICE database (NTP, 2025), as well as manual mapping based on the US EPA assay descriptions and the IARC KCC Guidance Document (IARC, 2025, IARC, 2025).

For each endpoint extracted, a study quality and reliability assessment was conducted. For endpoints from in vivo animal, in vitro, and in silico models, the Klimisch et al. (1997) criteria were used, and for endpoints from epidemiological studies, the Money et al. (2013) approach was used. The Money approach mirrors the Klimisch criteria and scoring but with specific adaptations for epidemiological study designs (confounding, bias, and uncertainty). For all study types, the reliability scores were: 1 = reliable without restriction; 2 = reliable with restriction; 3 = not reliable. Because of the large number of high-throughput (HT) assays for DINP, reliability scoring was not conducted on a per assay basis. Instead, all assays were assigned a Klimisch score of 2 (reliable with restriction) based on US EPA’s provision of assay details and protocols, cytotoxicity information availability, dose–response analyses, and chemical purity information. Reliability scores were incorporated into the quantitative mechanistic assessment, informing the internal validity.

Mechanistic data were mapped to KCCs according to the framework described in the IARC Preamble (IARC, 2019), with the data categorized according to the model type (i.e., human observational, in vitro human primary cells and tissues, in vivo mammalian, in vitro immortalized human cells, in vitro mammalian, in vitro non-mammalian [Ames assays only], and in silico). Model strength was assigned based on the weighting structure outlined by Wikoff et al. (2019), with modifications for Ames assays (in vitro: non-mammalian), which is a validated assay to predict mutagenicity. As described by Borghoff et al. (2025), the following scoring structure for model strength was used:•8 – human: controlled exposure•6 – human: epidemiological•4 – in vitro: primary human cells and tissues; in vivo: mammalian•2 – in vitro: immortalized cell lines, human; in vitro: mammalian; in vitro: non-mammalian Ames assay•1 – in silico; cell-free systems

The model strength scores were incorporated into the quantitative integration of mechanistic data for KCCs 1–10, informing the evidence strength as a measure of external validity. The strength of these models for predicting activity in humans was considered based on review of the IARC preamble.

For each KCC, the evidence was assembled for each line of evidence: (1) exposed humans; (2) primary human cells or tissues; (3) in vivo mammalian models; and (4) immortalized human cells lines, in vitro mammalian evidence, and in vitro non-mammalian (KCC2 only). This step provided an assessment of the robustness and sufficiency of the data within each line of evidence for each KCC and for the overall KCCs. An activity call was made for each KCC-evidence stream combination for which there were sufficient data. These evidence stream-specific activity calls were later considered when making an overall activity call for each KCC (see Section: Overall strength of mechanistic data). For any KCC-evidence stream combination for which there were insufficient data (approx. ≤2 studies or measured endpoints), no activity call was made. Only data considered reliable (Klimisch or Money score of 1 or 2) were included in this evaluation. Studies with a reliability score of 3 were excluded. All data are available in Appendix B.

Consistent with the IARC Preamble (IARC, 2019), data related to obesity and diabetes across all lines of evidence were considered relevant as emerging issues of cancer. Such data were extracted according to the protocol used for mapping mechanistic data to KCCs 1–10. Examples of relevant endpoints included body mass index (BMI), insulin resistance, serum and urine glucose, serum triglycerides, and gastrointestinal microbiome alterations.

For the overall evaluation of DINP’s carcinogenic potential in exposed humans, tumors identified in animal studies were evaluated for consideration of a MoA and whether they operated in humans in the context of reported examples identified by IARC (Capen, 1999, IARC, 2003). If a MoA for a tumor response was demonstrated not to operate in humans, then this tumor response was not used as supporting evidence for carcinogenic potential in humans in the overall evaluation for DINP’s human cancer hazard.

For each KCC, the overall strength of the mechanistic data was determined by considering data sufficiency and activity calls across all evidence streams, consistency of findings, and relevance of the model (i.e., in vivo studies, in vitro assays, etc.) used to assess cancer outcome. Notably, the overall strength of the evidence considers all data for each KCC (i.e., across all evidence streams), with KCC-specific considerations of model strength and relevancy. For each KCC, the strength of the evidence was assigned as strong, limited, or inadequate, with consideration of the IARC Preamble guidance (IARC, 2019), as described below.

Strong evidence: Overall evidence base was coherent with consistent activity in reliable studies/endpoints. This included: a) strong evidence in exposed humans; b) strong evidence in human primary cells or tissues (human receptors and enzymes, etc.); or c) strong evidence in experimental in vivo animal models supported by evidence in several studies in human primary cells or tissues.

Limited evidence: Overall evidence base was suggestive of activity, with studies covering a narrow range of experiments, endpoints, and species. Also assigned because of inconsistencies in similar studies, and/or incoherence across studies of different endpoints or different experimental systems.

Inadequate evidence: Few or no data available; unresolved questions about study design, adequacy, conduct, or interpretation; or results were negative.

An overall determination regarding DINP’s carcinogenic potential in humans was made by integrating all three streams of evidence (human cancer, animal cancer, and mechanistic data) to consider the biological plausibility of a carcinogenic response in humans, considering principles of WHO IPCS MoA and human relevance framework (Meek, 2014, Meek and Wikoff, 2023). ADME data were used to evaluate the translational relevance of animal data to humans. According to Halliwell and Whiteman (2004) and Murphy et al. (2022), oxidative stress methods/endpoints were critically evaluated for their reliability in measuring oxidative damage, and assays with significant limitations were excluded from the quantitative mechanistic integration. All other (reliable) mechanistic data, organized by KCC, were integrated into biological pathways via MoA analysis where appropriate (as no human cancer AOPs were available). Integration also considered the evidence consistency, dose–response, and data relevance and reliability across all evidence streams to inform the DINP’s overall carcinogenic hazard in humans.

The initial literature search conducted on October 10, 2024 yielded 1,012 references that underwent AI auto-labeling in SysRev and screening at the TiAb level for potential relevance. The updated literature searches (most recently on October 1, 2025) identified an additional 137 peer-reviewed papers, and hand searching identified 14 peer-reviewed studies and 23 publicly available laboratory reports. Following deduplication, 1,094 peer-reviewed studies and laboratory reports underwent review at the TiAb level (Fig. 1). Of the references reviewed at the TiAb level, 273 were advanced for review at the full-text level. Four epidemiological investigations were identified that examined the association between DINP exposure and cancer, three laboratory reports and two peer-reviewed publications (published using data from a single laboratory report) described chronic cancer bioassays, and 179 studies were identified as containing mechanistic data, of which 62 contained mechanistic data in exposed humans and 117 in experimental models.

Four epidemiological studies were identified that evaluated cancer outcomes associated with DINP or relevant metabolites, including three studies evaluating breast cancer mortality and/or incidence (Parada, 2018, Reeves, 2019, Zhang, 2022), and one study evaluating prostate cancer incidence (Chang et al., 2018). The determined RoB for each of these studies is summarized in Table 2. Each of the identified studies had a potential bias in at least one key domain (confounding, exposure characterization, or outcome ascertainment). Overall, the three breast cancer studies were reliable, with some possibility for bias (i.e., Tier II − neither high nor low RoB). Reeves et al. (2019) relied on self-reported cancer diagnoses to identify cases, allowing for the possibility of inaccurate reporting, and may bias toward or away from “no effect”, depending on the analysis conducted. The unique design of the LIBCSP studies (Parada, 2018, Zhang, 2022), in which the authors added a follow-up phase to a case-control study, resulted in mixed reliability in key RoB domains (exposure and outcome characterization) that were dependent on study phase. The mixed design of these studies supports triangulation-based synthesis, where the unique strengths of each study design provide context for the design-specific limitations in the alternate phase of the study. For example, the case-control evaluations in these studies ascertained exposure at the time of enrollment, which cannot establish temporality of the exposure–response association or the etiological relevance of exposures. Although there is no clearly defined minimally sufficient latency period for breast cancers, the additional follow-up period (≥18 years) was considered adequately long to establish the etiological relevance of the findings. Additionally, mortality-based evaluations for cancers with relatively low mortality rates (including breast cancer) may underreport the true number of cases, and underpredict risk for studies based on cancer mortality. In the earliest two-year standard cancer bioassay, male and female F344 rats were chronically exposed via the diet to 0, 0.03%, 0.3%, or 0.6% (w/w%) DINP for approximately 104 weeks (Table 3) (Lington, 1997, Biodynamics, 1986). Although a specific test guideline was not identified, the study was reported to have been conducted in accordance with GLP requirements. Calculated mean dose levels in male and female rats were reported: 0, 15–18, 152–184, and 307–375 mg/kg-bw/day based on amount of DINP in the diet, food consumption, and body weight. Changes in food consumption, body weight, and animal survival in the male and female rats at the highest dose group (307–375 mg/kg-bw/day) were consistently lower than concurrent controls. At study termination, body weights of exposed rats were 22% (female) and 25% (male) lower than controls. Animal survival was greater than 60% in all the dose groups, but decreased survival was noted in both male and female rats at the two highest dose levels (152–184 and 307–375 mg/kg-bw/day), with a statistically significant decrease only noted in females at both dose levels (Table 3). There was no dose-related increase in liver or kidney tumors in this study, with a significant MNCL increase reported in both male and female rats at the two highest dose levels (152–184 and 307–375 mg/kg-bw/day) (Table 3). There were both transitional cell carcinomas in 3/80 male rats at the highest dose, with 1/80 and 2/80 tubular cell carcinomas at the lowest and highest DINP dose, respectively. As referenced in Lington et al. (1997), these tumor types should not be combined for evaluating tumor incidence. MNCL was noted by the study authors to increase over time and to be the most common cause of death for the study animals. MNCL is a common neoplastic lesion observed in untreated male and female F344 rats. Overall, this study was identified as reliable for the purposes of evaluating DINP-related tumor responses in exposed F344 rats (Appendix C, Figs. S1, S2).

In second two-year cancer bioassay conducted at BioDynamics (Biodynamics, 1987), male and female Sprague-Dawley rats were exposed chronically (up to 104 weeks) via diet to 0, 500, 5,000, or 10,000 ppm DINP, corresponding to calculated mean dose levels ranging from 0, 27–33, 271–331, and 553–672 mg/kg-bw/day in male and female rats (Table 3). Although no specific test guideline was identified in this report, the study was noted to have been conducted in accordance with GLP requirements. Throughout the study, mean body weights of high-dose females were consistently lower compared to the control group, despite higher food consumption from approximately week 10 through study termination. DINP-related effects on survival were not observed in either male or female rats when compared to their concurrent control group. Comprehensive histopathological evaluation was conducted for all surviving animals at study termination (∼104 weeks); however, while the liver was examined in all dose groups, other tissues were only evaluated in the control and high-dose groups. In female rats, an increased hepatocellular carcinoma incidence was observed at the highest dietary DINP concentration (672 mg/kg-bw/day). Although statistical analyses were not provided in the BioDynamics study report for tumor incidence comparison between groups, statistical analyses results were reported by the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) (CPSC, 2001) or US EPA (2025b). A significant increase in hepatocellular carcinomas at the highest dose (553–672 mg/kg-bw/day) compared to the control group was identified, along with a significant dose–related increase in these hepatocellular carcinomas (Table 3). While apparent increases in tumor incidences were noted in the testes, uterus, and pancreas of high-dose animals relative to controls, these findings were found not to be statistically significant, and fell within historical control variability range. Given that these tissues were only evaluated in the control and high-dose groups, these observations were not considered informative for assessing dose–response relationships or carcinogenic potential. Overall, this study was considered reliable for evaluating DINP-related tumor responses in exposed Sprague Dawley rats (Appendix C, Figs. S1, S2).

Two cancer bioassays were conducted at Covance Laboratories and were designed in accordance with applicable US EPA guidelines under TSCA (40 CFR) and in compliance with US EPA GLP requirements (TSCA; 40 CFR Part 792) (Covance, 1998b, Covance, 1998a).

In one study, male and female F344 rats were exposed chronically to DINP via diet at concentrations of 0, 500, 1,500, 6,000, or 12,000 ppm (Covance, 1998b) (Table 3). A separate group of animals were exposed to 12,000 ppm DINP for a shorter duration (weeks 1–78). The calculated dietary dose levels ranged from 0 to approximately 29.2–36.4, 88.3–108.6, 358.7–442.2, 733.2–885.4, and 637.3–773.6 mg/kg-bw/day in male and female rats. Food consumption, survival, and body weights were consistently lower in high-dose males and females compared with controls, with food consumption exhibiting sporadic intergroup differences lacking any type of pattern. At study termination, mean body weights at the highest dose level (637.3–885.4 mg/kg-bw/day, exposed for either 78 or 104 weeks), were statistically significantly lower than concurrent controls, beginning at weeks 5–105 in males and 2–105 in females. Survival was significantly decreased in male rats exposed to the highest DINP dose (733.2–885.4 mg/kg-bw/day) for up to 104 weeks, with no difference in survival noted in exposed female rats. No statistically significant increases in hepatocellular adenomas were observed in either male or female rats. An increased incidence of hepatocellular carcinomas was observed at ∼104 weeks only in males at the highest DINP exposure concentration. When liver adenomas and carcinomas were combined, both male and female rats exhibited a statistically significant increase at the highest dose group following 104 weeks of exposure. Renal tubular carcinomas, but not transitional cell carcinomas, were increased in male rats exposed at the highest DINP concentration for 78 weeks followed by 26 weeks of no exposure. In addition, MNCL incidence was significantly increased in both male and female rats exposed to the highest DINP concentration (637.3–885.4 mg/kg-bw/day) for both 78 and 104 weeks. Overall, this study was considered reliable for evaluating DINP-related tumor responses in F344 rats (Appendix C, Figs. S1, S2).

The only mouse two-year standard cancer bioassay was conducted in male and female B6C3F1 mice chronically exposed to DINP for up to 104 weeks at dietary exposure concentrations of 0, 500, 1,500, 4,000, and 8,000 ppm, with a group exposed to 8,000 ppm for 78 weeks followed by 26 weeks of no exposure (Table 3) (Covance, 1998b). The dose levels calculated based on body weight, food consumption, and dietary DINP concentrations resulted in estimated dose levels of 0, 90.3–112.0, 275.6–335.6, 741.8–910.3, 1560.2–1887.6, and 1,377.4–1581.0 mg/kg-bw/day in male mice and female mice. There was a statistically significant decrease in mean body weight at the two highest DINP dose levels in both male and female mice compared to their concurrent control group with exposure up to 104 weeks. Total mean food consumption was generally comparable between dose groups, except for an increased consumption that was statistically significant between weeks 78–104 in the highest dose group (∼1581.0–1,377.4 mg/kg-bw/day) in male mice. In these mice, a statistically significant increase was observed in hepatocellular adenomas at the highest exposure concentration of 8000 ppm (∼1581.0–1887.6 mg/kg-bw/day) in female mice, with a significant increase in hepatocellular carcinomas at both 4,000 (∼910.3 mg/kg-bw/day) and 8,000 ppm in female mice and at 8000 ppm in male mice. Overall, this study was considered reliable for evaluating DINP-related tumor responses in B6C3F1 mice (Appendix C, Figs. S1, S2).

Examination of ADME data for DINP is critical for identifying and contextualizing relevant mechanistic data, particularly considering the use of DINP metabolites as exposure biomarkers in humans along with the bioactivity of DINP metabolites in assays that report mechanistic data used to inform potential carcinogenicity in humans. The ADME of DINP in humans and rodent models has been previously described by authoritative bodies, largely focusing on metabolite similarities (US EPA, 2025b, EC, 2003, ECHA, 2013, Health Canada, 2015). Overall, following oral exposure of humans and rodents, DINP is rapidly absorbed and hydrolyzed by esterases in the gastrointestinal tract to MINP, and distributed systemically to both liver and kidneys (US EPA, 2025b, ECHA, 2013, Health Canada, 2015). MINP is then rapidly metabolized into stable oxidative secondary metabolites, primarily MHINP, MCIOP, and MOINP, which are excreted in the urine or feces (Koch and Angerer, 2007, Saravanabhavan and Murray, 2012, Silva, 2006).

A summary of the available ADME studies in humans and rats is provided to demonstrate the overall toxicokinetic profile similarity between the two species. In one study, a male volunteer was provided a single dose of 1.27 mg/kg-bw of deuterium-labeled DINP and urine collected over 48 h. Rapid DINP distribution and elimination occurred, with 43.6% recovered in urine within 48 h as MHINP (20.2%), MCIOP (10.7%), MOINP (10.6%), and MINP (2.2%) (Koch and Angerer, 2007). In a study of ten male and ten female volunteers given an oral dose of 0.78 or 7.3 mg DINP, there was 32.9 ± 6.4% recovery as MHINP (12.2 ± 3.20%), MCIOP (10.9 ± 3.07%), MOINP (6.6 ± 1.77%), and MINP (3.1 ± 1.01%) in the urine within 48 h (≥90% of which occurred in the first 24 h); the half-lives of all metabolites were estimated between four to eight hours (Anderson et al., 2011). Sprague-Dawley rats administered a single oral gavage DINP dose (300 mg/kg) showed a similar urinary profile, with MHINP, MCIOP, and MOINP excreted as the major urinary metabolites (Silva et al., 2006). Overall, DINP’s metabolic profile in exposed humans and rodents is qualitatively similar, facilitating interpreting and comparing cancer and mechanistic data in animal models to assess potential cancer outcomes in humans.

In experimental animal studies, no micronuclei were reported in the bone marrow following oral gavage administration of mice up to 2,000 mg/kg/day DINP for two days (McKee et al., 2000). DINP was negative in standard Organisation for Economic Coordination and Development (OECD) genotoxicity tests in vitro. DINP was evaluated and found negative for mutagenic activity in the bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames assay) in three studies using up to five bacterial strains, as recommended by OECD 471 (S. typhimurium TA 98, TA100, TA1535, TA1537, and TA1538), and DINP levels up to 10,000 µg/plate in the absence or presence of S9 (McKee, 2000, Zeiger, 1985, Mason, 1980). Similarly, two studies showed no mutation frequency increase in the mouse lymphoma assay (with and without S9 activation) at 8 μL/mL DINP (Barber, 2000, Hazleton, 1986). No chromosomal aberrations were reported in vitro in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells exposed to 160 μg/mL DINP with and without metabolic activation with S9 (McKee et al., 2000). A statistically significant but marginal increase in γ-H2AX expression was observed in the nuclei of human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) that were differentiated into neurons. This finding was not associated with changes in expression of early response genes (e.g., c-fos, egr1, and npas4) that can be stimulated by double strand DNA breaks (Xu et al., 2020). No activity was detected in any of the six HT assays (five p53 beta-lactamase assays and one ELG1 luciferase assay) mapped to this KCC. Data are provided in full in Appendix B.

Overall, there were insufficient data to evaluate genotoxicity in humans and mammalian in vivo models, but there was sufficient in vitro evidence. Therefore, the data across all evidence streams was considered sufficient to evaluate KCC2. Based on the results of the in vitro assays and aligned with the conclusion reached by US EPA, OEHHA, ECHA, and EFSA (OEHHA, 2013, US EPA, 2025b, EC, 2003, EFSA, 2005), strong evidence exists that DINP is non-genotoxic, supporting non-genotoxic MoAs for selected tumor responses.

One of two epidemiological studies found an association between maternal urinary DINP metabolite concentration (MCIOP, MHINP, MOINP) and differential methylation patterns in DNA from placental tissue (Jedynak et al., 2024). The study reported sex-specific placental DNA methylation changes in several genes (e.g., Adenomatous polyposis coli [APC]), though functional consequences could not be determined because of lack of gene expression analysis (Jedynak et al., 2024). In one of two experimental animal studies, female-specific increases in hepatic Fasn promoter methylation correlated with a marginal increase in Fasn expression following perinatal DINP exposure, persisting from PND21 to 10 months of age, with no changes in other peroxisome proliferator-actived receptor (PPAR) target genes or global methylation (Neier, 2019, Neier, 2020). In a single in vitro assay conducted in the thyroid human cell line, Nthy-ori 3–1, exposure to 1 μM DINP did not alter chromatin accessibility that might suggest altered gene expression (Nazzari et al., 2023). No activity was reported in HT assays that measure epigenetic regulation. Data are provided in full in Appendix B.

Overall, the available KCC4 data across all evidence streams (humans, mammalian models, in vitro models) were insufficient and deemed inadequate to support integration into the assessment (Fig. 2).

Because measures of oxidative stress analyzed in blood or urine have multiple possible origins, the IARC mechanistic framework guidance for interpreting these endpoints was applied in this analysis (IARC, 2025, IARC, 2025). No reliable measures of oxidative stress were reported in epidemiological studies. Two in vivo studies reported increased oxidative stress [elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reduced glutathione (GSH)] in male ICR mice following oral gavage exposure to DINP (≤4.8 mg/kg-bw/day for 5 weeks) and in male BALB/c mice after dermal exposure (≤200 mg/kg-bw/day for 28 days). These findings occurred in kidneys (Gu et al., 2021) and liver and kidneys (Liang and Yan, 2020). Data are provided in full in Appendix B.

Overall, the data from the experimental animal models was sufficient to integrate into the assessment (Fig. 2). The evidence for oxidative stress (KCC5) showed consistent activity (strong) in experimental animal models (e.g., rodent liver and kidney), but there was inadequate data to evaluate KCC5 in exposed humans or primary human cell lines or tissues so overall the evidence was deemed limited (Fig. 2).

No reliable measures of immunosuppression were reported in epidemiological studies. One immunophenotyping study conducted using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells showed no changes in T lymphocyte populations after DINP exposure (6-hr, ≤2536.65 μ M) (Krause et al., 2023).

Two of ten studies in experimental animals reported decreased leukocyte counts following DINP exposure. One study conducted in non-human primates reported a statistically significant lymphocyte count decrease following daily DINP administration (500 mg/kg-bw/day) via nasogastric intubation for 14 days (44% vs. 69% in the control group) (Pugh et al., 2000). While statistically significant, lymphocyte levels were still within the normal reference range for male cynomolgus macaque (20–79% for 2–3 yr old monkeys) (Li, 2022, Yan, 2025). In the other study, male F344 rats exposed to DINP in diet for 28 days exhibited reduced leukocyte counts at the highest tested dose (2% DINP), with no change in neutrophil numbers. No dose dependence was observed in this study, reducing the likelihood that this response was directly attributable to DINP exposure (Midwest Research Institute, 1981). Inconsistent changes were reported in hematological endpoints (e.g., reduced red blood cells, hemoglobin, and hematocrit) in three studies at 4, 13, 26, and 52 weeks (Hazleton, 1991, Covance, 1998b, Midwest Research Institute, 1981).

No HT assays relevant to KCC7 were identified; one in vitro study reported a dose-dependent decrease in phagocytosis in THP-1 cells exposed to DINP at concentrations ≥0.2 µM (Bennasroune et al., 2012). Data are provided in full in Appendix B.

Overall, the data from the experimental animal models were sufficient to integrate into the assessment (Fig. 2). Experimental animals are not generally considered good models for immunosuppression-driven cancer formation in humans (Engels, 2019, McLaughlin-Drubin and Munger, 2008). Immunosuppression-driven tumors in humans are often associated with a viral infection, which is not the case in experimental animals. There was inadequate data to integrate into the assessment for exposed humans or primary human cell lines or tissues (Fig. 2). Since there was no consistent activity reported in animal models, there is limited evidence to support KCC7 activity.

Measurements of receptor mediated effects were reported in many studies. The nuclear receptors considered in the evaluation were aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), androgen receptor (AR), constitutive androstane receptor (CAR), pregnane X receptor (PXR), estrogen receptor (ER), PPARs (PPARα, PPARβ and PPARγ), progesterone receptor (PR), thyroid receptor (TR), glucocorticoid receptor (GCR), and prolactin receptor (PRLR). The following endpoints were considered: functional measurements of receptor activation (e.g., cell proliferation in response to receptor activation), levels of receptor binding hormones, receptor expression levels, expression of genes regulated by the receptor, and receptor activity as measured in reporter gene assays. In addition, the aromatase (CYP19) involved in steroid hormone synthesis was also assessed. Data are provided in full in Appendix B and summarized for AhR, GCR, PRLR, PR, TR, ER, PXR, and CAR in Appendix C.

One study was identified that reported PPARα activation by DINP in vitro via microarray (De Abrew et al., 2015). However, there is ample additional evidence to support PPARα activation by DINP and/or its metabolite MINP, including associative events such as peroxisome proliferation, peroxisomal β-oxidation and other KEs (e.g., KE 3: Alteration of cell growth pathways) that are integral to the PPARα MoA for liver tumors in rodents. These data are reviewed in Buerger et al. (Buerger et al., in press), summarized in Appendix C, and discussed in the Section:: “Tumors Not Relevant to Humans Based on MoA and Species-Specificity”.

One of 12 epidemiological studies reported a positive association between serum testosterone levels and serum MOINP (a DINP metabolite) concentrations. Individuals with measurable MOINP concentrations (0.03–2.82 ng/mL) had higher adjusted mean testosterone levels than those with MOINP below the limit of detection (15.5 vs. 13.6 nmol/L testosterone respectively). The other DINP metabolites reported (MHINP and MCIOP) showed decreasing trends in serum testosterone levels, with increasing metabolite levels. The testosterone to luteinizing hormone (LH) ratio was significantly lower for individuals with MOINP concentrations of 0.03–2.82 ng/mL and those with metabolites below the limit of detection (3.3 vs. 3.6 testosterone/LH ratio respectively) (Specht et al., 2014). Five of 12 epidemiological studies reported negative associations between DINP metabolite concentrations and serum or hair testosterone levels. In vitro assays conducted to assess DINP (vs. metabolites) activity reported hAR antagonist activity in two (of four) assays in yeast, LLC-MK2, CHO-K1, or MDA-kb2 cells (Czernych, 2017, Ma, 2019).

Among ten experimental animal studies, DINP had no effect on circulating testosterone levels, but was reported to reduce fetal testicular testosterone in three rodent studies (Borch, 2004, Gray, 2024, Hannas, 2011). However, as suggested by AOPs published by Li and Spade (Li and Spade, 2021), testosterone reduction in rodent fetal testis may not be relevant to cancer outcomes in humans. Two studies in immortalized human cell lines reported no agonist or antagonist effects. One H295R steroidogenesis assay conducted according to OECD test guideline 456 reported an increase in testosterone levels following exposure of up to 30 μM DINP for 48 h (Moche et al., 2021); however, a second H295R study did not, at concentrations up to 20 mg/L (48 μM) (Lee et al., 2019). DINP was inactive in 15 AR (agonist, antagonist, or co-factor recruitment assays) HT assays conducted in immortalized human cell lines. Two molecular docking assays (in silico) showed DINP was able to bind to AR, but with lower affinity than 5-α-dihydrotestosterone. As these in silico findings do not add weight to the assessment, they were not included in the overall interpretation of the identified data. Data are provided in full in Appendix B.

Findings for other receptors, not included in the overall interpretation of this KCC, are provided in Appendix C.

Overall, the AR activity from the epidemiological and experimental animal studies as well as in vitro assays were sufficient to integrate into the assessment (Fig. 2). In animal models and immortalized cells, the data lacked concordance, and provided limited evidence to support activity for DINP within KCC8. The data from epidemiological studies and in vitro assays using the human receptor were inconsistent, lacked concordance, and provided limited evidence to support activity for DINP within KCC8 (Fig. 2).

There were no epidemiological investigations or studies conducted in primary human cell lines for KCC10. Among six animal studies, four (of five studies evaluating liver and kidney) reported increases in biomarkers or ultrastructural indicators of cell proliferation. In the kidney, male F344 rats exhibited proliferative changes following repeated oral exposure, including foci of excessive cell proliferation in the renal cortex (12 months, 307 mg/kg-bw/day), and increased severity of regenerative/basophilic tubules (13 weeks, calculated dose ≥275.8 mg/kg-bw/day). In both studies, no comparable effects were observed in female rats (Caldwell, 1999, Hazleton, 1991). In the liver, increased cell proliferation was reported in several studies. Increased hepatocellular proliferation was observed in male and female rats after one week of exposure to 12,000 ppm DINP via diet (calculated doses: 733.2 mg/kg-bw/day for males and 885.4 mg/kg-bw/day for females); this effect was not present at later timepoints, such as 2, 13, or 104 weeks (Covance, 1998b). In mice, Kaufmann et al. (Kaufmann et al., 2002) reported increased hepatocellular proliferation at all dietary DINP levels in males (≥500 ppm, calculated dose 116 mg/kg-bw/day) and at the highest dose level in females (≥4000 ppm, calculated dose 1,278 mg/kg-bw/day) after one week of dietary exposure. After four weeks of dietary exposure, cell proliferation was still elevated in exposed male mice, but effects were observed only in the 1500 ppm exposure group in female mice (calculated dose 546 mg/kg-bw/day). Concerning cell proliferation and cell death, no tubule dysplasia (cell proliferation) and no hypoplasia, necrosis, or mineralization (indirect measure of cell death) were observed at any dose level in the testes of adult rats following dietary DINP exposure to 0, 760, 3800, 11400 ppm (target dose levels: 0, 50, 250, 750 mg/kg-bw/day) from gestation day (GD) 12 to postnatal day (PND) 14 (Clewell et al., 2013).

Studies in immortalized cell lines report mixed findings, with increased colony formation in human cell lines (A549, BEAS-2B, and H292) after four weeks of exposure to ≤10 µM DINP (Taş et al., 2019). As a surrogate or indirect measure of cell viability, two studies report opposing effects on mitochondrial function, including either reduction in basal oxygen consumption, reserve capacity and ATP production, or increases in total or active mitochondrial numbers (Rajkumar, 2022, Poitou, 2022). Additional studies show gap junction intercellular communication inhibition in immortalized cells lines and increased cell division and growth in cultured porcine granulosa cells (Basini, 2024, Ctverackova, 2020). No activity was reported in 28 HT assays measuring cell growth and proliferation in human primary or immortalized cells. Data are provided in full in Appendix B.

Overall, the data from experimental animal studies were sufficient to integrate into the assessment. With consistent activity reported, strong evidence exists for increased proliferative effects (KCC10) in rodent liver and kidney. There was inadequate data to integrate into the assessment for exposed humans or primary human cell lines or tissues therefore the overall evidence is limited.

Consistent with the principles of the IARC Preamble (IARC, 2019), mechanistic data relevant to obesity, diabetes, and insulin resistance were evaluated as pathways that may link metabolic dysfunction to increased cancer risk in humans. In 20 epidemiological studies, ten reported an association between specific DINP metabolite concentrations and obesity or blood lipid endpoints, such as increased weight, metabolic syndrome, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), elevated body mass index, or decreased high density lipoprotein (HDL) (Appendix C). There were three epidemiological studies that evaluated the association of DINP metabolite concentrations with insulin resistance and/or blood glucose. Only one of these identified an association between DINP and the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) (Appendix C). Study design limitations were significant in these epidemiological studies (Appendix B) and, therefore, clear evidence that exposure to DINP is associated with obesity or changes in blood lipid, glucose, or insulin resistance could not be established.

In experimental animal studies, clinical chemistry and urinalysis showed variable results, with inconsistent changes in blood lipid parameters and/or blood or urinary glucose (Appendix B). Four in vitro assays showed changes adipogenesis markers, including one assay conducted in human SGBS cells (Appendix C).

Overall, the data from the human and experimental animal studies, as well as in vitro assays, did not provide strong and consistent evidence to integrate these data into an assessment of the potential cancer hazard associated with DINP exposure.

Dietary DINP resulted in statistically significant increases in liver tumors in rodents in three of four chronic carcinogenicity bioassays (Biodynamics, 1987, Covance, 1998b, Covance, 1998a). Mechanistic evidence indicates that DINP-induced liver tumors in rodents operate through the PPARα–mediated MoA, which includes KE 1: PPARα activation; KE2: Alteration of cell growth pathways; KE3: Perturbation of cell growth and survival; KE4: Clonal expansion of preneoplastic foci; and the adverse outcome (AO): liver tumors (Corton, 2014, Corton et al., 2018). This pathway is well-established for rodent hepatocarcinogenesis and not considered relevant to humans because of fundamental differences in PPARα transcriptional networks between rodents and humans and non-human primates (Corton, 2014, Corton et al., 2018, Felter, 2018, Yamada et al., 2025). The evidence that DINP acts through the PPARα MoA for liver tumors in rodents was evaluated in detail by Buerger et al. (in press) according to the PPARα MoA framework described by Corton, 2014, Corton et al., 2018 and the guidance from US EPA and IPCS, including alternative MoA assessment and evidence evaluation in context of the Modified Bradford Hill Criteria (Boobis, 2006, Meek, 2014, US EPA, 2005). The MoA analysis by Buerger et al. (in press) is briefly described herein.

Evidence for PPARα activation (KE1) by MINP, a metabolite of DINP, in mice and rats is strong, and data likewise indicate human PPARα activation by MINP in vitro (Buerger et al., in press). Within the KCC framework, this KE corresponds to KCC8 (Receptor-mediated effects, specifically PPARα activation) (Guyton, 2018, Smith, 2016). However, although in vitro PPARα bioactivity data were limited for DINP (Appendix C) and direct evidence for KE1 (PPARα activation) is not available for DINP (Appendix B), this KE is supported based on its rapid metabolism in vivo to MINP (Section: ADME in Humans and Rodent Models), which has been demonstrated to be a PPARα agonist in vitro. Specifically, MINP activated both mouse and human PPARα in luciferase reporter assays conducted in mouse 3T3-L1 cells, with the mouse receptor exhibiting greater sensitivity than the human receptor (Bility et al., 2004). In COS-1 cells transfected with hPPARα, MINP produced a modest increase in reporter activity (approximately 1.75-fold above control) (Laurenzana et al., 2016). The direct evidence of rodent PPARα activation in vitro is further supported by the evidence for associative events (e.g., peroxisome beta-oxidation, peroxisome proliferation, hepatocellular hypertrophy) supporting KE1 in rodents exposed to DINP, which are not observed in vitro or in vivo in non-human primates or primary human hepatocytes (Buerger et al., in press). Evidence for KE3 (Perturbation of cell growth and survival) in rodents exposed to DINP supports short-term increases in hepatocellular proliferation (Buerger et al., in press), which is consistent with evidence from other weak PPARα activators acting through this MoA in rodents (Corton, 2014, Corton et al., 2018). Within the KCC framework, evidence for this KE is identified within KCC10 (Altered cell proliferation and cell death) (Guyton, 2018, Smith, 2016). This increase in hepatocellular proliferation was observed in vivo and in vitro across rat and mice studies (Hasmall, 1999, Kaufmann, 2002, Shaw et al., 2002, Smith, 2000, Valles, 2003, Covance, 1998b, Covance, 1998a), but not in non-human primates or primary human hepatocytes (Hasmall, 1999, Pugh, 2000, Shaw et al., 2002). Together, activity in KE1 and KE3 (corresponding to evidence from KCC8 and KCC10, respectively) have been observed in rodents or in vitro assay using DINP or its metabolites. Although data directly informing KE2 (Alteration of cell growth pathways) and KE4 (Clonal expansion of preneoplastic foci) in this MoA are limited, Buerger et al. (in press) noted these events can be reasonably inferred based on the strong evidence supporting KE1, KE3, and the AO.

Evidence does not support DINP acting through alternative MoAs for liver tumors (Buerger et al., in press). Biological differences between rodents and humans support the lack of human relevance of this MoA, along with DINP-specific evidence that further supports the specificity to rodents. Therefore, the evidence demonstrates that DINP-induced rodent liver tumors develop through a PPARα-mediated MoA that does not operate in humans, and, as such, these tumors are not relevant for evaluating DINP’s potential carcinogenic hazard to humans.

Chronic dietary exposure to DINP resulted in a low kidney tumor incidence in male F344 rats across two chronic cancer bioassays (Table 3) (Lington, 1997, Biodynamics, 1986, Covance, 1998b). Renal transitional cell carcinomas, a rare neoplastic lesion in rodent cancer bioassays (summarized in US EPA, 2025b), occurred sporadically, without statistical significance, and showed no consistent dose–response relationship in male rats (Lington, 1997, Biodynamics, 1986, Covance, 1998b). Similarly, renal tubular cell carcinomas were observed at low incidence, with limited reproducibility across these two rat cancer bioassays and exposure durations. Importantly, kidney tumors were observed exclusively in male rats; no renal tumors were reported in DINP-exposed female rats or in male or female mice (Covance, 1998a). As discussed below, the renal tubular carcinomas were concluded to arise from a male rodent-specific MoA, and were considered not relevant for use in human health risk assessment (CPSC, 2001, ECHA, 2013, NICNAS, 2012, US EPA, 2025b).

The male-specific nature of the kidney tumors, together with the absence of genotoxicity, supports interpretation within a male rat–specific α2u-globulin (α2u) – associated nephropathy MoA, which is not considered relevant to humans. Criteria for this MoA have been established by US EPA (1991) and IARC (IARC, 1999) for nongenotoxic chemicals that induce renal tubular tumors exclusively in male rats through an α2u-nephropathy MoA. Evaluation of the available data against these criteria to determine human relevance is presented in Appendix C, Table S1.

Although these criteria were developed based on chemicals that produce a strong α2u-nephropathy response with a higher tumor incidence than observed for DINP, regulatory guidance (US EPA) recognizes that weaker inducers may not exhibit all the hallmark histopathological features, consistent with observations for DINP. When evaluated within the IARC and US EPA frameworks, most criteria are supported or partially supported by the DINP dataset, despite some data gaps. Notably, direct evidence for reversible binding to α2u is lacking and was therefore categorized as “not supported”.

As summarized in Table S1 (Appendix C), the weight of evidence across mechanistic, histopathological, and apical endpoints, supports the conclusion that α2u-mediated nephropathy provides a biological plausible explanation for kidney tumors identified in DINP exposed male rats. The overall non–genotoxic profile for DINP, along with the sex- and species-specific histopathological changes in DINP studies (e.g., α2u–positive droplets, tubular injury/regeneration, and papillary mineralization at interim time points), track with the canonical progression of α2u-nephropathy as outlined by both US EPA (1991) and IARC (1999). While other nongenotoxic MoAs for rodent kidney tumors exist (e.g., sustained cytotoxicity with compensatory cell proliferation, chronic oxidative stress leading to cytotoxicity etc.), these MoAs are generally not male rat–specific, in contrast to α2u-mediated nephropathy (US EPA, 1991).

Although chronic progressive nephropathy (CPN) was present in male F344 rats exposed to DINP, this lesion is a common spontaneous finding in rats and occurred at comparable incidence and severity in control animals (Caldwell, 1999, Lington, 1997). CPN is recognized as a rodent-specific process without human relevance and, while it can exacerbate renal tumor responses in male rats under certain exposure conditions (Hard, 2011, Hard, 2019, Hard et al., 2009), there is no evidence that CPN contributed meaningfully to the renal tumor findings observed following DINP exposure.

Overall, then, the weight of evidence supports that kidney tumors observed in male rats following chronic DINP exposure are attributable to a male rat specific MoA, α2u-globulin–associated nephropathy, a mechanism not operative in humans (Appendix C, Table S1). Accordingly, these tumor findings are not relevant to human carcinogenic hazard assessment.

Although the MoA for MNCL is not known, it is a common age-related finding in rats with species- and strain-specificity (Maronpot et al., 2016). MNCL arises in large granular lymphocytes and is identified in both sexes of untreated F344 rats at high incidence. MNCL occurred at a statistically significant increased incidence in male and female F344 rats in two chronic bioassays of DINP, but not in SD rats or in B6C3F1 mice. These effects were observed after 104 weeks of exposure and were coincident with significant loss of body weight in one of the two studies. The highest historical incidence reported for untreated male rats was 72% (Maronpot et al., 2016), which is above the highest incidence (64%) that occurred with DINP exposure. The highest historical rate reported in untreated female rats was 52%, which is similar to or higher than incidence observed in mid- and high-DINP- exposed female rats, respectively. Given the historical control range, occurrence of the tumors in one strain of rodents (F344 rats), and lack of a well-defined MoA underlying MNCL tumorigenesis, the lack of biological relevance of MNCL neoplasms to humans and their toxicological significance remain uncertain, in agreement with interpretations described in regulatory opinions (CPSC, 2001, ECHA, 2013, NICNAS, 2012, US EPA, 2025b). Thus, MNCL tumor findings are not relevant to human carcinogenic hazard assessment.

There were no human studies considered reliable without risk of bias (Tier I) to evaluate the potential carcinogenic activity of DINP. Four standard two-year cancer bioassays (three in rats and one in mice), all with DINP exposure via diet, resulted in liver tumors in three of the four studies, very low kidney tumor incidence in male rats at the highest dose levels, and a significant MNCL increase in both F344 rat studies. The MoAs driving tumorigenesis in the liver and kidney are not considered relevant to humans, while MNCL is a common tumor response in this strain of rats, and, as such, not relevant for integrating for human cancer hazard assessment. Mechanistic data integration from epidemiological studies provides no evidence of activity across any of the KCCs (Appendix C, Fig. S3). Mechanistic data from experimental animal studies shows strong evidence of oxidative stress (KCC5) and increased proliferative effects (KCC10) in rodent liver and kidney (Appendix C, Fig. S4). Taken together, then, the findings from reliable cancer bioassays and the following factors support the conclusion that DINP is unlikely to pose a carcinogenic hazard to humans (Fig. 3): 1.) the absence of genotoxic activity; 2.) proposed MoAs not considered relevant to humans; 3.) limited or no mechanistic activity in exposed humans and primary human cells or tissues across most KCCs.Fig. 3Integration of the totality of data informing human carcinogenicity hazard from exposure to DINP.

Integration of the totality of data informing human carcinogenicity hazard from exposure to DINP.

This systematic evaluation of DINP’s carcinogenic potential in humans integrated evidence from four epidemiological investigations, four chronic rodent carcinogenicity bioassays, and mechanistic studies conducted in humans, animal models, primary cells, and immortalized cell lines. The mechanistic data provide support for KEs within rodent-specific tumor MoAs. Based on the totality of the integrated evidence, DINP is unlikely to pose a carcinogenic hazard to humans.

The available human evidence regarding DINP exposure and cancer mortality or incidence is limited, with only four studies identified (Chang, 2018, Parada, 2018, Reeves, 2019, Zhang, 2022). Three studies evaluated breast cancer mortality and/or incidence and found no evidence of an elevated risk or exposure–response associated with DINP exposure (Parada, 2018, Reeves, 2019, Zhang, 2022). These studies were generally considered reliable with limitations (Tier II), with some identified potential biases attributable to confounding (Parada, 2018, Zhang, 2022), limited exposure characterization due to use of spot samples (which cannot capture inter-individual variability or temporal changes in exposure), inadequate etiological relevance in the case-control evaluations of the LIBCSP studies (Parada, 2018, Zhang, 2022), and possible underreporting of effects due to outcome misclassification (Reeves et al., 2019) or using mortality for the follow-up evaluations in the LIBCSP studies (Parada, 2018, Zhang, 2022). These judgments are consistent with US EPA (2025c) determinations of “medium quality” for Parada et al. (2018) and Reeves et al. (2019); US EPA did not identify Zhang et al. (2022)) or Chang et al. (2018) in its evaluation. Collectively, the currently available epidemiological evidence is limited and does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding the presence or absence of association. However, the varying study designs and identified areas of bias across the epidemiological evidence for breast cancer outcomes, integrated with the toxicological and in vivo evidence, supports evidence triangulation (Lawlor et al., 2016) and increases confidence that the epidemiological observations are not driven by bias alone.

One additional study was identified that reported a large, statistically significant association, with a wide confidence interval between urinary MINP concentrations and prostate cancer incidence (Chang et al., 2018). This study was determined to have a high RoB and a high degree of uncertainty attributable to the very small sample size and lack of precision. Causal determinations regarding DINP and cancer are not supportable from the epidemiological evidence, alone, due to the limited evaluations. Due to a lack of prostate cancer in experimental animal models, only mechanistic evidence in exposed humans or human tissues or primary cells would provide insight to contextualize the findings from this observational epidemiological study to shed light on whether it is biologically plausible for an increase in the incidence of prostate tumors to be associated with DINP exposure.

The role of the AR is well established in human prostate tumor carcinogenesis, with AR and AR pathway-related gene dysregulation playing an important role in the disease pathogenesis (Bai, 2021, Maekawa et al., 2024). Prostate cancer has been known to respond to androgen deprivation therapy for over 80 years (Huggins and Hodges, 1972). However, no mechanistic evidence supports a role for DINP in prostate carcinogenesis in humans. In vitro studies of the human AR show that DINP, at concentrations up to 1000 μM, did not activate this receptor (Czernych, 2017, Engel, 2017, Kang, 2025, Kruger et al., 2008, Ma, 2019, Moche, 2021). Although, receptor antagonism was reported in two (out of six) studies (Czernych, 2017, Ma, 2019), AR antagonism is not associated with tumor initiation or progression, and, instead, underlies the therapeutic benefit of androgen (primarily testosterone) deprivation prostate cancer therapy (Chen, 2022, Cole, 2025). While urinary DINP metabolites have been negatively associated with circulating testosterone levels, endogenous testosterone concentrations are not correlated with prostate cancer risk or progression in humans (Endogenous Hormones Prostate Cancer Collaborative Group and et al , 2008, Lopez, 2017, Michaud et al., 2015). Accordingly, these observations do not support a causal role for DINP in AR-mediated prostate cancer. Other mechanistic pathways relevant to prostate carcinogenesis, including chronic inflammation and localized immunosuppression, are also not supported by the DINP evidence base; no studies reported localized immunosuppression in the prostate, and a single study evaluating inflammatory cytokines in humans found no association with DINP metabolites (Berman et al., 2022).

In experimental animal studies, DINP induced liver tumors in rodents in three of four standard chronic carcinogenicity bioassays (Lington, 1997, Biodynamics, 1987, Biodynamics, 1986, Covance, 1998b, Covance, 1998a) and increased kidney tumor and MNCL incidences were reported in both F344 rat bioassays (Biodynamics, 1987, Covance, 1998b). DINP is consistently non-genotoxic based on in vitro and in vivo evidence, a conclusion supported by multiple regulatory and scientific reviews (EC, 2003, EFSA, 2005, OEHHA, 2013, US EPA, 2025b). Mechanistic DINP data were derived predominantly from animal in vivo studies and exposed animal cells or immortalized cell lines, with limited data from exposed humans or human primary cells. The most robust mechanistic evidence in non-human models informs KCC2 (genotoxicity), KCC8 (receptor-mediated effects), and KCC10 (altered cell proliferation, cell death, or nutrient supply). Organizing the mechanistic evidence across the KCC framework enabled evidence strength integration and tumor-specific MoA evaluation. Because of limited or absent data across several other KCCs, mapping to KEs within human-relevant carcinogenic pathways was not feasible.

Given the lack of genotoxicity, the rodent tumor findings were evaluated under non-genotoxic MoA frameworks in accordance with IARC Preamble (IARC, 2019) guidance, which emphasizes human relevance of tumor mechanisms. Chronic dietary DINP exposure produced liver tumors in rodents through a PPARα-mediated MoA characteristic of rodent hepatocarcinogenesis, as summarized in this assessment, with detail review provided by Buerger et al. (in press). This pathway involves PPARα activation and downstream perturbations in cell growth and survival, leading to hepatocellular proliferation and tumor formation. Fundamental species differences in PPARα signaling, along with the absence of comparable responses in non-human primates and primary human hepatocytes, support the conclusion that this MoA does not operate in humans. DINP-specific evidence further indicates that PPARα activation depends on metabolism to MINP, and that the parent compound lacks direct activity in human PPARα assays.

Kidney tumors occurred at low incidence exclusively in male F344 rats. Except for a statistically significant increase observed in one study at the highest exposure concentration (12,000 ppm) (Covance, 1998a), no clear dose–response relationship with DINP exposure was found, with many regulatory agencies questioning the significance of this tumor response for evaluating human cancer hazard (CPSC, 2001, EC, 2003, ECHA, 2013, US EPA, 2025b). The fact the tumors occur only in male rats, together with the lack of genotoxicity, supports a male rat–specific renal MoA. This MoA involves α2u-globulin nephropathy and exacerbation of CPN, both of which are recognized as rodent-specific processes that do not occur in humans (Hard, 2011, Hard, 2019, Hard et al., 2009). US EPA and IARC provide criteria for evaluating this type of male rat specific MoA (US EPA, 1991, IARC, 2019). Based on these criteria, DINP would be considered a weak inducer of kidney tumors in male rats in both studies. This presents a challenge when evaluating the MoA, because many of the KEs can only be identified in short-term studies. Based on the sporadic increased incidence of these tumors occurring via a non-genotoxic mechanism, combined with evidence for increased kidney cell proliferation in exposed male, but not female, rats, along with the presence of CPN, these tumors are not significant for human cancer evaluation.

MNCL occurred at a statistically significant increased incidence, but within historical control ranges, in F344 rats, but not in SD rats or in B6C3F1 mice. Given that MNCL is a common tumor in control F344 rats, bioassay interpretations can be improved by increasing the statistical stringency from the cut-off of p < 0.05 for rare tumors to p < 0.01 for common tumors (Haseman, 1983) as a possible carcinogenic effect indicator. At this level, the MNCL finding following DINP exposure would not be deemed statistically significant. Moreover, several published reviews have described the morphological differences between rat MNCL and the rare large granular lymphocyte leukemias that occur in humans, concluding that rat MNCL is not a direct corollary of human leukemia, but largely histologically and mechanistically distinct (Maronpot, 2016, Thomas, 2007). Consistent with various regulatory agency findings that MNCL lacks human relevance (e.g., Health Canada, ECHA, NICNAS), as such the MNCL findings reported in this study should not be considered when determining DINP’s carcinogenic potential in humans.

DINP demonstrates a consistent lack of genotoxicity across a substantial body of evidence. Limited human cancer studies do not show a reliable association between DINP exposure and increased tumor incidence. A low incidence of liver and kidney tumors was observed in chronically exposed rodents; however, MoA analysis indicates that these arise through pathways not relevant in humans, and the data is characteristic of a weak inducer of both pathways. An extensive body of mechanistic data supports these findings, with evidence of increased cell proliferation in rodent liver and kidney only. Taken together, then, the weight of evidence supports the conclusion that DINP is unlikely to pose a carcinogenic hazard to humans.
