# Autoimmune mechanisms in Guillain-Barré syndrome subtypes: a systematic review
**Authors:** O. I. Oshomoji, J. O. Ajiroba, S. O. Semudara, M. A. Olayemi, S. O. Adeoye
**Journal:** Bulletin of Faculty of Physical Therapy (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s43161-024-00258-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s43161-024-00258-8)

## Content

Abstract
Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is a complex autoimmune disorder characterized by acute onset of motor weakness, often following an infectious illness. The pathophysiology of GBS involves a multifaceted interplay between immune mechanisms and environmental factors, leading to demyelination or axonal degeneration. This systematic review aims to elucidate the autoimmune mechanisms underlying the various subtypes of GBS, including acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP), acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN), acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN), and Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS). A thorough literature search identified 71 studies published between 2010 and 2024 that provided insights into the immunopathological features, clinical implications, and future directions for research. Key findings indicate that specific autoantibodies, such as anti-GM1 and anti-GQ1b, are associated with distinct subtypes of GBS, contributing to the disease’s heterogeneity. Understanding these autoimmune mechanisms is crucial for improving diagnostic accuracy, therapeutic strategies, and prognostic indicators in GBS. This review highlights significant gaps in current research, emphasizing the need for further studies to explore the genetic and environmental factors that influence GBS susceptibility and the role of vaccinations in triggering autoimmune responses.

Introduction
Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is a group of acute 
immune-mediated polyneuropathies characterized by 
the immune system’s attack on peripheral nerves, often 
triggered by infections such as Campylobacter jejuni, 
cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), and 
Zika virus [1, 2]. The syndrome includes several sub -
types, primarily acute inflammatory demyelinating 
polyneuropathy (AIDP), Miller Fisher syndrome (MFS), 
acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN), and acute 
motor and sensory axonal neuropathy (AMSAN). Each 
subtype presents with distinct clinical characteristics, 
electrophysiological profiles, and underlying immune 
mechanisms [3].
There is a rising focus on identifying specific auto -
immune pathways and understanding their clinical 
implications for each GBS subtype, which is critical for 
advancing diagnosis and treatment. Studies have revealed 
subtype-specific autoantibodies, such as anti-GM1 in 
AMAN and anti-GQ1b in MFS, offering valuable insights 
into GBS pathogenesis [3]. However, a comprehensive 
understanding of how these antibodies and immune 
responses contribute to nerve damage across the differ -
ent subtypes remains incomplete [2].
Recent research emphasizes the importance of elu -
cidating the distinct autoimmune mechanisms in each 
GBS subtype. This study seeks to provide evidence on 
specific autoimmune pathways that contribute to GBS

Literature review
Epidermiology
GBS remains a leading cause of acute neuromuscular 
paralysis worldwide, with an annual incidence of 1–2 per 
100,000 people [4]. Epidemiological studies have shown 
that the incidence of GBS can be influenced by geo -
graphical factors, seasonality, and the presence of spe -
cific infectious agents [2]. For instance, infections with 
Campylobacter jejuni are a well-known antecedent to 
GBS, particularly in endemic regions. Recent data also 
suggest an association between viral infections, including 
Zika virus and COVID-19, and the onset of GBS [4].
The predominant feature of GBS is immune dysfunc -
tion, where antibodies, complement activation, and 
immune cells target peripheral nerves. AIDP , the most 
common subtype in Western countries, is marked by 
demyelination of nerves, while AMAN and AMSAN pri -
marily affect the axonal components of nerves [5]. MFS, 
a variant of GBS, is characterized by ophthalmoplegia, 
ataxia, and areflexia, and is associated with anti-GQ1b 
antibodies [6].
Despite ongoing research, many gaps remain in under -
standing the precise autoimmune mechanisms involved 
in each subtype of GBS, with variations in pathogenesis 
depending on geographic and epidemiological factors [2, 
7]. A systematic synthesis of current literature is essential 
to bridge these knowledge gaps and offer direction for 
future research.
Clinical implications
GBS diagnosis, treatment, and patient outcomes depend 
on a deep understanding of the autoimmune mecha -
nisms that are involved. According to Yuki et  al., accu -
mulating knowledge about the specific autoantibodies, 
such as anti-GQ1b in MFS, could lead to better diagnos -
tic accuracy and might also help the clinician in choos -
ing particular immunotherapies that best apply to the 
patient’s subtype [6]. According to Willison et  al., there 
is an appreciation for complement activation, as well as 
immune cells infiltrating with the demyelination/axonal 
degeneration process, which may provide new avenues 
for therapeutic intervention, including the use of comple-
ment inhibitors [2].
In terms of treatment, current therapies for GBS (IVIG 
and plasmapheresis) are directed at modulating the 
immune response but are not subtype-specific [8]. A bet -
ter understanding of the specific autoimmune processes 
in each of these GBS variants may translate into more 
focused therapies targeted at the basic mechanisms of 
nerve damage, for better patient outcomes, particularly 
in more severe forms like AMSAN [9].
Research gap and justification
Despite significant advancements in understanding 
the autoimmune mechanisms involved in GBS, several 
critical gaps remain in the literature. Notably, the het -
erogeneity of immune responses and variability in clini -
cal presentations across GBS subtypes complicate both 
diagnosis and treatment. There is a particular need for 
further investigation into the role of T-cells and other 
less-studied immune components in disease progression, 
as these elements may significantly influence the clinical 
outcomes of GBS.
Additionally, while many studies have explored the role 
of specific autoantibodies, there is a lack of comprehen -
sive analyses examining the interplay between various 
immune markers and their cumulative impact on disease 
progression. Understanding these interactions could pro-
vide insights into individual variations in GBS and con -
tribute to more personalized treatment approaches.
Moreover, the influence of genetic and environmen -
tal factors on individual susceptibility to GBS remains 
underexplored. Investigating these factors is crucial, as 
they may help identify at-risk populations and inform 
preventive strategies.
Addressing these gaps is essential for refining diagnos -
tic criteria and developing targeted therapeutic strategies. 
A deeper understanding of the autoimmune processes in 
GBS will not only enhance clinical management but also 
improve patient outcomes, ultimately leading to more 
effective and individualized care.
Research methodology
Search strategy
The literature search aimed to identify relevant studies on 
the autoimmune mechanisms underlying GBS subtypes, 
including AIDP , MFS, AMAN, and AMSAN, published 
between the years 2010 and 2024. The search strategy 
used a combination of thesaurus keywords and Medical 
Subject Headings (MeSH) terms. Specifically, the search 
terms included keywords related to “GBS” , “autoimmune 
mechanisms” , “subtypes” , “ AIDP” , “MFS” , “ AMAN” , and 
“ AMSAN” , combined with Boolean operators (AND, OR) 
to capture a broad spectrum of studies.

The review followed a structured, eight-step process 
adapted from Okoli and Schabram’s (2015) guidelines 
[10]. This began with defining the purpose of the lit -
erature review establishing a protocol, and conducting 
necessary training. The literature search was then car -
ried out across three major databases, with the distri -
bution of studies as follows: PubMed contributed 30 
studies (42.3%), Scopus contributed 18 studies (25.4%), 
and Web of Science contributed 23 studies (32.3%), total-
ing 71 studies (100%). The next steps involved a practical 
screening of the studies, followed by a quality appraisal 
to assess relevance and rigor. Data extraction was then 
performed, and the selected studies were synthesized. 
Finally, the findings were documented and compiled in 
the systematic review.
The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews 
and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) flowchart was used to 
illustrate the flow of information through the different 
phases of the systematic review (Fig. 1).
Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Studies eligible for inclusion were those that investi -
gated the autoimmune mechanisms associated with GBS 
subtypes and provided data on autoantibodies, immune 
markers, and clinical features. Exclusion criteria included 
non-peer-reviewed articles, studies not published in Eng-
lish, and those lacking relevant data (Table 1).
Data extraction
Data extraction was done in line with a predefined form 
for extraction [11]. It was performed by two independent 
reviewers, O.I. and J.O., who were not affiliated with each 
other. This systematic approach ensured accuracy and 
consistency. The extracted data was later tabulated and 
used for the analysis.
Quality assessment
The relevant evaluation tools were utilized to assess the 
quality of the studies that were included. For randomized 
controlled trials (RCTs), the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 
was used, while the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale was applied 
for observational studies. The checklist developed by the 
Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) was used to 
evaluate qualitative research.
When evaluating each study, several criteria were con -
sidered, including study design, sample size, methods

Synthesis of studies
A narrative synthesis was employed to describe the 
findings of the review, with a focus on identifying com -
mon themes, trends, and gaps in the current literature. 
The studies were grouped by GBS subtype (AIDP , MFS, 
AMAN, AMSAN) to allow for a comparative analysis of 
the autoimmune mechanisms involved.
Data analysis
The extracted data were analyzed both qualitatively and 
quantitatively. A narrative synthesis summarized findings 
related to autoimmune mechanisms in GBS, highlighting 
key themes and patterns across subtypes.
Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 
20.0. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize 
the data. Inferential statistical tests, including independ -
ent t-tests and chi-square tests, were used to compare 
differences between groups. The I 2 statistic was used to 
assess heterogeneity among studies. Random-effect mod-
els were applied to account for heterogeneity and ensure 
robust results. Findings are presented in tables and fig -
ures to illustrate the relationships and differences in auto-
immune processes across GBS subtypes.
Results and discussion
Overview of included studies
This systematic review encompassed 71 studies published 
between 2010 and 2024, focusing on the autoimmune 
mechanisms associated with various GBS subtypes. 
These studies explored a range of research designs to 
examine the immunopathological processes, autoanti -
bodies, and clinical correlations associated with GBS var -
iants, such as AIDP , AMAN, AMSAN, and MFS.
The research designs of the reviewed studies varied, 
encompassing both observational and interventional 
approaches. Figure  2 graphically represents the distribu -
tion of study designs, while Table  2 provides a detailed 
summary. Indeed, the most common design adopted 
among the included studies is the case–control study. 
This may be understandable since case–control studies 
are efficient at comparing immune responses between 
GBS patients and matched controls an important 
approach to the elucidation of autoimmune diseases.
In analyzing the dataset, case–control studies 
accounted for 35.2%, playing a pivotal role in examin -
ing immune responses by comparing GBS patients with 
healthy controls. These studies identified key immu -
nological markers, such as anti-GM1 antibodies and 
complement activation, crucial for understanding the 
pathogenesis of GBS subtypes, particularly AMAN [12, 
13]. Case studies comprised 19.7%, providing detailed 
explorations of rare presentations of GBS, such as MFS. 
These studies linked anti-GQ1b antibodies to MFS, offer-
ing insights into the subtype’s clinical and immunologi -
cal characteristics [6, 14]. Cohort studies, representing 
16.9%, focused on long-term outcomes and progression 
of GBS. Cohort studies on Zika virus-related GBS high -
lighted the persistent impact of viral-induced autoim -
munity on GBS pathogenesis [15].
Clinical trials, at 15.5%, emphasized treatments like 
intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and plasmapher -
esis, which advanced immunomodulatory therapies for 
severe subtypes such as AMAN and AMSAN [2]. Lastly, 
cross-sectional studies accounted for 12.7%, contribut -
ing to understanding the prevalence and clinical features 
of GBS across diverse populations [5] (Supplementary 
material 1).

the publication trends, with a notable surge in 2020–
2024, where 43.7% of the studies were published.
There was progressive growth in research from 2015 
to 2019, reflecting global concern about GBS and its

AIDP
The predominant mechanism in AIDP is demyelina -
tion due to T-cell activation and elevated cytokines like 
TNF-α and IL-6, which drive inflammatory responses 
leading to the destruction of myelin sheaths. Soltani 
et al. and Li et al. highlighted the correlation between 
cytokine profiles and demyelination, while Chiang et al.

AMAN
 Anti-GM1 antibodies are a key player in AMAN, 
where they target gangliosides on axonal membranes, 
causing axonal degeneration. Shang et  al. and Kim 
et  al. demonstrated the destructive role of these anti -
bodies, particularly in Campylobacter jejuni-triggered 
GBS [12, 13]. Dimachkie and Barohn also identified 
anti-glycolipid antibodies as a hallmark of AMAN, cor -
roborated by Koga et al. who linked molecular mimicry 
between infectious agents and gangliosides to the auto -
immune response [20, 24].

AMSAN
 Anti-GM1 antibodies also feature prominently in 
AMSAN, but here the immune response involves more 
severe axonal damage, compounded by complement 
activation, which exacerbates nerve injury. Hagen and 
Ousman, and Willison et  al. discussed the complement

MFS (Miller Fisher syndrome)
Anti-GQ1b antibodies are the defining marker for 
MFS, causing cranial nerve dysfunction and leading to 
ophthalmoplegia and ataxia. Yuki et  al. and Chi et  al. 
described the role of molecular mimicry in triggering 
these antibodies underscoring the complexity of immune 
responses involved in GBS [6, 14].
Mechanistic pathways and clinical implications
The autoimmune mechanisms identified in this review 
have important clinical implications, particularly for 
diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis.
Diagnostic precision
Autoantibodies, such as anti-GM1 (in AMAN and 
AMSAN) and anti-GQ1b (in MFS), enhance diagnostic 
precision by enabling earlier detection and subtype clas -
sification [26]. These markers facilitate timely, targeted 
treatments and improve patient stratification for tailored 
therapeutic approaches.
Prognostic indicators
High levels of anti-GM1 antibodies in AMAN and 
AMSAN are associated with more severe axonal damage 
and poorer recovery [12]. These markers can help predict 
prognosis and guide therapeutic decisions.
Post‑infection autoimmunity
Infections like COVID-19 and the Zika virus have been 
linked to the development of GBS through molecular 
mimicry, where viral antigens cross-react with periph -
eral nerves [4, 15]. SARS-CoV-2 can trigger immune 
responses that lead to nerve damage, while the Zika 
virus induces similar autoimmune reactions [12, 15, 16]. 
Understanding these mechanisms is essential for devel -
oping effective preventive measures and early interven -
tion strategies.
Therapeutic interventions
Current therapeutic strategies for GBS, such as IVIG and 
plasmapheresis, are widely applied across all subtypes 
despite notable variations in effectiveness and safety 
among different subtypes. Research indicates that while 
IVIG and plasmapheresis are generally effective, their 
benefits are particularly pronounced in cases of AMAN 
and AMSAN [8]. In these subtypes, immune-modulatory 
mechanisms play a crucial role in mitigating axonal dam -
age triggered by anti-ganglioside antibodies and com -
plement activation, highlighting the need for a more 
targeted immune response [2, 12].
A meta-analysis of clinical trials examining IVIG’s effi -
cacy across GBS subtypes underscores its substantial 
benefits for AIDP , the most prevalent form in Western 
countries, where it effectively addresses immune-driven 
demyelination. However, outcomes for MFS patients 
treated with IVIG have been inconsistent, suggesting that 
more targeted immunomodulatory therapies may be nec-
essary to address the unique pathophysiology associated 
with anti-GQ1b antibodies in MFS [3].
Complement inhibitors, such as eculizumab, offer 
promising therapeutic strategies, particularly for axonal 
forms of GBS [7, 27]. These emerging treatments, which 
target complement activation, have shown significant 
potential in AMAN and AMSAN patients by prevent -
ing the formation of the membrane attack complex and 
thereby reducing axonal damage [2, 7]. Studies have 
demonstrated that complement-targeted therapies can 
improve outcomes in these patients by limiting immune-
mediated damage. Incorporating such therapies into 
treatment protocols may yield improved outcomes, espe -
cially for patients with severe axonal involvement [2, 7].
In addition, future research should critically evaluate 
therapies that specifically target autoimmune pathways 
associated with each GBS subtype. Tailored immu -
nomodulatory approaches may improve treatment effi -
cacy and outcomes, particularly in subtypes with more 
complex pathophysiologies, such as MFS.
Impact of vaccination on GBS
The review also explored the relationship between vac -
cinations, particularly against COVID-19, and the inci -
dence of GBS. Keh et  al. investigated vaccine-induced 
autoimmunity, specifically how molecular mimicry can 
result in GBS [28]. While the incidence of GBS post-vac -
cination remains rare, the immune response in certain 
individuals can lead to antibody production that cross-
reacts with gangliosides on nerve cells.
The clinical implications of these findings are signifi -
cant; early detection of immune responses in vaccinated 
individuals could prevent the onset of GBS through 
prompt immunomodulation treatments [29]. Chang 
et  al. recommend monitoring at-risk populations and 
implementing rapid therapeutic interventions such as

Challenges in GBS research and future directions
Despite advancements in understanding the autoimmune 
mechanisms of GBS, significant challenges persist. Key 
issues include the heterogeneity of immune responses 
among patients, which complicates diagnosis and treat -
ment. The variability in autoantibody profiles, cytokine 
responses, and clinical features suggest that GBS sub -
types are likely influenced by a combination of genetic, 
environmental, and infectious factors, making a stand -
ardized treatment approach challenging (Table 8).
Future research should focus on identifying more reli -
able biomarkers for early detection of GBS and further 
exploring the genetic and environmental factors that pre -
dispose individuals to the syndrome. Understanding the 
regional differences in GBS subtypes and their respective 
triggers—such as AMAN  being more common in Asia

Comprehensive summary of key findings
This systematic review provides essential insights into the 
pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment of GBS subtypes:
AIDP is characterized by T-cell activation and 
cytokine elevation, leading to demyelination and 
slower nerve conduction. Identifying elevated 
cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6 can improve diagno -
sis and treatment. AMAN and AMSAN are driven 
by anti-GM1 antibodies and complement activa -
tion, causing axonal degeneration. Early detection 
of anti-ganglioside antibodies can predict motor 
deficit severity and improve recovery with targeted 
immunotherapies like complement inhibitors. MFS 
involves anti-GQ1b antibodies, affecting cranial 
nerves and causing ophthalmoplegia. Molecular 
mimicry plays a key role, making anti-GQ1b anti -
body testing critical for timely diagnosis and inter -
vention.
Emerging infections, such as COVID-19 and Zika 
virus, are recognized as GBS triggers via molecular 
mimicry. Monitoring and early intervention in post-
infection and post-vaccination cases can prevent the 
full onset of GBS in vulnerable populations. Challenges 
include the heterogeneity of immune responses and the 
lack of early biomarkers. Further research is needed, par -
ticularly in developing geographic-specific strategies to 
address regional differences in GBS prevalence.
Contributions of the study
This study contributes to the field by providing a com -
prehensive review of the immunological mechanisms 
involved in GBS, highlighting the importance of both 
humoral (antibody-mediated) and cellular (cell-medi -
ated) responses. Specifically, it underscores the role 
of molecular mimicry and cross-reactivity between 
pathogen antigens and peripheral nerve gangliosides. 
The research has also drawn attention to the variations 
in immune responses among different GBS subtypes, 
which may have implications for personalized treat -
ment approaches. Furthermore, by focusing on the link 
between infectious agents and GBS onset, this study adds 
to the growing body of evidence that supports the devel -
opment of vaccines as a preventive measure.
Limitations of the study
This study does not capture the full complexity of 
immune responses in GBS, and reliance on animal mod -
els limits its applicability to humans. The heterogeneity of 
GBS subtypes also poses challenges for generalizing find -
ings across all patient populations. The lack of large-scale 
controlled clinical trials restricts the practical application 
of new therapies, and while this study focuses on infec -
tious triggers, other environmental or genetic factors 
have not been fully explored.
Conclusion and recommendation
This study has explored the autoimmune mechanisms 
underlying GBS, emphasizing the immune-mediated 
attack on the peripheral nervous system. Key findings 
highlight the roles of molecular mimicry, autoanti -
bodies, and the activation of both innate and adaptive 
immune responses. These insights advance our under -
standing of GBS pathogenesis, particularly the impact
