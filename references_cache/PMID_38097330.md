# Genetic Spectrum of Polycystic Kidney and Liver Diseases and the Resulting Phenotypes.
**Authors:** Yang H, Sieben CJ, Schauer RS, Harris PC
**Journal:** Adv Kidney Dis Health (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1053/j.akdh.2023.04.004](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.akdh.2023.04.004)

## Content

Polycystic kidney diseases are a group of monogenically inherited disorders characterized by cyst development in the kidney with defects in primary cilia function central to pathogenesis. Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) has progressive cystogenesis and accounts for 5-10% of kidney failure (KF) patients. There are two major ADPKD genes, PKD1 and PKD2, and seven minor loci. PKD1 accounts for ∼80% of patients and is associated with the most severe disease (KF is typically at 55-65 years); PKD2 accounts for ∼15% of families, with KF typically in the mid-70s. The minor genes are generally associated with milder kidney disease, but for DNAJB11 and ALG5, the age at KF is similar to PKD2. PKD1 and PKD2 have a high level of allelic heterogeneity, with no single pathogenic variant accounting for >2% of patients. Additional genetic complexity includes biallelic disease, sometimes causing very early-onset ADPKD, and mosaicism. Autosomal dominant polycystic liver disease is characterized by severe PLD but limited PKD. The two major genes are PRKCSH and SEC63, while GANAB, ALG8, and PKHD1 can present as ADPKD or autosomal dominant polycystic liver disease. Autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease typically has an infantile onset, with PKHD1 being the major locus and DZIP1L and CYS1 being minor genes. In addition, there are a range of mainly recessive syndromic ciliopathies with PKD as part of the phenotype. Because of the phenotypic and genic overlap between the diseases, employing a next-generation sequencing panel containing all known PKD and ciliopathy genes is recommended for clinical testing.

Polycystic kidney diseases (PKD) are a group of inherited disorders characterized by cyst development in the kidney.1 They can be subdivided into those where kidney and liver disease are the major phenotypes (nonsyndromic) and syndromic diseases where PKD is part of a wider disorder involving multiple organ systems. A common organelle associated with kidney cyst development is thought to be the primary cilium, a single sensor antenna found on most cell types and extending from the apical surface of epithelial cells into the lumen of the tubule.2 PKD proteins might, therefore, localize to the primary cilium, be involved in cilia generation or regulate its protein composition, or more generally associated with trafficking of cilia localized proteins. The wide range of signaling functions of the cilium, especially during development, plus the variety of cell types with cilia, explain the pleotropic phenotypes associated with these ciliopathies.

The focus of this review is on nonsyndromic forms of PKD that can be divided into those caused by a single pathogenic variant, monoallelic, that are grouped under the heading of autosomal dominant polycystic kidney diseases (ADPKD).1 The second grouping are those caused by biallelic variants inherited from the two parents, classed as autosomal recessive polycystic kidney diseases (ARPKD). A separate group of monoallelic diseases where liver cysts are more prevalent and clinically significant than kidney cysts, are autosomal dominant polycystic liver diseases (ADPLD). The syndromic PKD group have a wide variety of phenotypes and many genes involved; more than 100 genes have been associated with syndromic ciliopathies with kidney cysts and/or tubulointerstitial nephritis.2 These diseases are usually recessively inherited and include: nephronophthisis (NPHP), Joubert syndrome (JBTS), Meckel syndrome (MKS), short-rib thoracic dysplasia (SRTD), and Bardet-Biedl syndrome (BBS), with orofaciodigital syndrome type 1 (OFD1) an X-linked dominant disorder. Phenotypes in NPHP include tubulointerstitial nephritis, kidney fibrosis and cysts, with the addition of cerebellar vermis hypoplasia, retinal degeneration, polydactyly, and congenital hepatic fibrosis (CHF) in JBTS. Non kidney phenotypes in MKS include encephalocele or other central nervous system (CNS) defects, CHF, and polydactyly, in SRTD retinal disease, skeletal and craniofacial abnormalities, and polydactyly (SRTD), in BBS cognitive impairment, retinal disease and obesity, and facial, oral cavity, digital, and CNS problems in OFD1.23 The reader is referred to recent reviews for more details of syndromic ciliopathies.2,3

ADPKD is by far the most common form of PKD with ~1/1000 individuals affected. The disease is characterized by kidney cyst development, starting in the fetus and continuing through the lifetime of the patient. Cyst development and growth compress the remaining tubules resulting in inflammation and fibrosis and in many cases a reduction and ultimately loss of kidney function. The rate at which the disease develops varies but ~50% of patients have kidney failure (KF) by ~60 years of age;4 worldwide 5–10% of individuals with KF have ADPKD. The majority of ADPKD patients have a positive family history but as many as 20% of families can be traced back to a likely de novo mutation.5

ADPKD is a genetically heterogeneous disorder. The major genes are PKD1 and PKD2, that first were identified nearly 30 years ago using positional cloning approaches. More recently, with the advent of next generation sequencing (NGS), at least seven minor ADPKD genes have been identified. Although they only account for a small proportion of ADPKD patients, their identification has been of diagnostic value and provided insights into pathogenesis.

PKD1 is a large gene in terms of the transcript size and coding region (Table 1). The 5’ three quarters of the gene lie in a segmentally duplicated region of the genome, with 6 pseudogenes with homology of >97% located more proximally on 16p. This genomic complication has led to long-range PCR approaches specifically amplifying PKD1 traditionally employed for genetic screening, although exon capture and NGS screening of PKD1, along with other PKD genes, is now widely employed.6,7 The second major gene is PKD2 (Table 1). PKD1 encodes polycystin 1 (PC1) a 4303aa membrane associated protein (11 transmembrane; TM regions) with a large extracellular region (>3000aa) that is cleaved but remains associated with the membrane portion of the protein. PKD2, encodes PC2, a 6 TM containing TRP family calcium channel subunit. PC2 has significant homology with the last 6 TM regions of PC1, and a 3:1 PC2:PC1 complex has been resolved by cryo-EM which has different channel properties from the homotetrameric PC2 complex.8,9 The PC1/PC2 complex is thought to be localized to primary cilia (as well as other locations) but its precise function is still hotly debated.

Kidney clinic identified ADPKD populations screened in the research setting show ~80% of patients are PKD1, ~15% PKD2, with ~5% due to other genes or unresolved (Figure 1).10,11 In population studies including ADPKD patients with a lower risk of KF, PKD1 represents a lower proportion of this population with the other groups increased.12 Also, in the clinical testing environment, where many missense PKD1 variants are classed as of uncertain significance (VUS), the proportion of patients genetically diagnosed as PKD1 is lower and the unresolved group larger (see Clinical Genetic Testing for more details).

Gene type is a major factor associated with the severity of kidney disease. The average age at KF is ~58y for PKD1 and ~75y for PKD2.4 The severity of kidney disease can also be determined by MRI or CT measured total kidney volume (TKV), with larger kidneys, especially at younger ages, associated with poorer outcomes.13 PKD1 patients have larger kidneys than PKD2, although the rate of increase does not differ; rather, PKD1 kidneys grow rapidly at an early stage probably due to more cyst development.14

There is a high level of allelic heterogeneity for both PKD1 and PKD2 with more that 1500 PKD1 and 200 PKD2 pathogenic variants identified (ADPKD Variant Database; pkdb.mayo.edu). These variants are of all possible mutation types, including ones predicted to truncate the protein (frameshifting deletions/duplications/insertions, nonsense, canonical splicing, and >1 exon deletions/duplications /insertions; copy number variants; CNV) and nontruncating (inframe deletions/duplications/insertions, missense, and noncanonical splicing) and found throughout both genes (Figure 2). No single variant accounts for more than 2% of the whole ADPKD population. From the data in the ADPKD Variant Database, the proportion of families with truncating and nontruncating mutations is ~63% and ~37% for PKD1 and ~86% and ~14% for PKD2, respectively (Figure 2).

The type of pathogenic variant to PKD1 is associated with the severity of kidney disease. Truncating (T) variants result in an average age at KF of ~55y compared to ~67y for nontruncating (NT).15 Consistent with PKD1-T causing more severe disease, these patients typically have lower eGFR and larger height adjusted TKV (htTKV) in age matched patients.10 A small proportion of ADPKD patients, ~80% of which are female, have clinically significant PLD. However in this case, the occurrence in PKD1-T, PKD1-NT, and PKD2 groups is similar to their prevalence in ADPKD populations.16 This indicates that genetic modifiers beyond the disease-causing change and environmental factors are likely influencing the development of severe PLD.

The PKD1-NT variants are heterogeneous consisting of ones fully inactivating the protein and hypomorphic variants, that still generate functional protein (see also Unusual Presentations and Complex Genetics). There have been attempts to identify fully penetrant and hypomorphic alleles by in silico methods, giving rise to PKD1-NT groups, NT1 (also called Mutation Strength Group; MSG2) and NT2 (MSG3), with the age at KF differed by ~5.5 years between the two groups (PKD1-NT1 = 60.8y and PKD1-NT2 = 66.2y).4 These NT groupings also differed in eGFR and htTKV values in age adjusted data. Therefore, knowing the genotype of the patient can be of prognostic value.

Positional cloning analysis of large ADPLD families identified two major genes, PRKCSH and SEC63 (Table 1).17–19 These genes account for ~20% and ~15% ADPLD families, respectively, although likely a much higher proportion of severe ADPLD individuals requiring surgical resection or liver transplantation.20
PRKCSH and SEC63 encode proteins involved in the glycosylation, trafficking, and quality control of cell surface and secreted proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER; Table 1). Analysis of cells null for Prkcsh or Sec63 showed inefficient trafficking of PC1, suggesting that these proteins may cause cysts because of insufficient PC1 in cilia.21 A genetic relationship between Pkd1 and these genes was also shown by enhanced cystogenesis when Prkcsh or Sec63 were conditionally inactivated in the kidney in the Pkd1+/− background compared to wildtype.21

Global screening by whole exome sequencing (WES) of unresolved ADPLD patients showed an enrichment of single loss of function (LOF) mutations in several genes in patients compared to controls, suggesting ADPLD candidate genes. These genes were GANAB, ALG8, and PKHD1.22
GANAB and ALG8 are involved in similar trafficking processes of membrane/secreted proteins (Table 1), with PC1 again inefficiently trafficked in cells null for these genes. Biallelic PKHD1 mutations cause ARPKD (see below); monoallelic variants associated with liver cysts is consistent with a previous study of carrier parents of ARPKD patients.23 These three genes have also been implicated in cystic kidney phenotypes (see below).

WES and sequencing of candidate PKD genes (targeted next generation sequencing; tNGS) of genetically unresolved ADPKD patients and those naïve to testing has identified several minor loci (Table 1). The first to be found was GANAB, with so far only a handful of families identified with a phenotype of mild cyst development in the kidney without a clear reduction in function, and often PLD, including ADPLD presentations.24 The next identified gene was DNAJB11, encoding a co-chaperone protein that aids folding of proteins in the ER.25 It this case, 27 families have been described (77 patients) that have a consistent phenotype of non-enlarged kidneys with small kidney cysts but develop fibrosis and KF later in life.26 Therefore, DNAJB11 is associated with a significant risk of KF, with the average at KF of ~75y, similar to PKD2. WES analysis of genetically unresolved ADPKD/ADPLD patients and analysis of a large population of WES sequenced subjects identified 16 individuals with single pathogenic variants to ALG9.27 The ALG9 protein also aids protein trafficking in the ER via glycosylation editing (Table 1). The phenotype is generally quite mild PKD, with or without liver cysts. Recently, two other ALG genes have been associated with an ADPKD phenotype. WES screening of ADPKD subjects and analysis of a large WES population identified 25 individuals in 5 family with an ALG5 variant of interest.28 In this case, the phenotype is of non-enlarged kidneys and fibrosis in older patients, often leading to KF. Analysis of a very large population with WES data (n=~174,000) identified 236 individuals with LOF variants to the previously identified ADPLD gene, ALG8.29 These individuals were at a significantly increased risk of having a kidney/liver cyst diagnosis; 69.2% of 52 with CT or MRI imaging had bilateral kidney cysts. In functional studies, loss of these genes in cellular systems showed loss/reduced trafficking of the mature form of PC1 to the cell surface and in some cases to cilia, again suggesting a link of cysts and defective PC1 trafficking.22,24,25,27 A final gene associated with ADPKD encodes the cilia localized intraflagellar transport (IFT) protein, IFT140 (Table 1).30 The monoallelic phenotype characterized in 59 patients from 38 families was of a limited number of larger cysts often causing kidney enlargement but not usually reduced GFR, and rarely liver cysts. Conditional loss of Ift140 in the kidney results in a rapidly progressive PKD phenotype,31 but the mechanism of the monoallelic disease is unknown. Interestingly, IFT140 lies just ~0.5Mb from PKD1 in 16p13.3 and so families can cosegregate PKD1 variants, some of which may modify the phenotype. Recent population studies have indicated that single PKHD1 variants can be associated with kidney as well as liver cysts.12

Although adult presentations of ADPKD are typical, occasionally (<1% of cases) individuals with much more severe, very early onset (VEO) ADPKD, characterized by in utero or perinatal large cystic kidneys, can occur in families with otherwise typical disease. One reason for VEO ADPKD is co-inheritance in trans of a LOF and a hypomorphic allele (usually at PKD1), termed biallelic inheritance. Since the first descriptions, two moderately sized case series have illustrated how biallelic inheritance can cause VEO ADPKD.32–35 Sometimes, families can have a negative family history because two hypomorphic alleles that have limited phenotypic consequences alone are inherited in trans, causing VEO or more typical ADPKD.33 Animal studies have mimicked biallelic adult and VEO disease, showing that the level of functional and localized PC1 protein correlates with disease severity.36

Although the majority of ADPKD patients have a positive family history, de novo mutational events are quite common.5 While the majority of these occur during the generation of the egg or the sperm, and so every cell in the patient has the mutant allele, mutation later in the embryo so that only some cells are affected can occur. Recently, this somatic mosaicism was documented in 20 PKD1 families; overall the mosaic individuals had milder cystic disease than expected given the pathogenic variant.37 In 5 families the mosaic variant was transmitted to the next generation, and so was incorporated into the mosaic individual’s germ as well as somatic cells. In the situation of a suspected de novo mutation, mosaicism should be considered, especially if the disease is milder than expected from the genetic findings.

Population studies provide information about the relative importance of different ADPKD loci and clues to penetrance of the kidney disease associated with each gene. Analysis of the large population of ADPKD patients recruited to the CRISP observational trial or the HALT PKD clinical trial showed the genetic makeup of that ADPKD population. Both studies required a clinical diagnosis of ADPKD but not a minimal TKV for inclusion. Figure 1 shows that ~80% have PKD1 pathogenic variants, ~67% truncating and ~33% nontruncating, while PKD2 accounts for ~15%, ~83% truncating and 17% nontruncating. Wider genetic screening, WES and tNGS, identified 4 other ADPKD genes, GANAB, ALG9, DNAJB11, and IFT140, together accounted for 0.8% of the population, while patients with mutations in two collagen genes more usually associated with Alport syndrome or hereditary angiopathy with nephropathy, aneurysms, and muscle cramps (HANAC) were also identified (see Table 2); 3.7% of the population remained genetically unresolved.10,24,25,27,30,38,39 Therefore, in the renal clinic population, with comprehensive genetic analysis and evaluation of variants employing research criteria, a genetic diagnosis can be made in most patients. Furthermore, PKD1 and PKD2 account for the vast majority of patients, but other loci are found at a low level.

In the WES UK Biobank population of ~240,000 individuals, 521 were defined from ICD9/10 codes to have ADPKD. Analysis of the genetic data showed, as expected, that truncating variants to PKD1 or PKD2 were the ones most significantly associated with ADPKD.30
IFT140 was the third gene where truncating variants were significantly associated with ADPKD, and overall is likely the third most common gene.30
ALG9 was also nominally associated but this was not significant at a genomewide level. In a broad population of ~174,000 individuals collected by Geisinger, 235 were identified from ICD9/10 codes and confirmed by manual inspection of clinical data to have ADPKD.12 Of these, 180 (76.6%) were likely genetically resolved; 127 PKD1 (70.5%) and 34 PKD2 (18.9%), with 19 (10.6%) accounted for by other genes: IFT140 (7), GANAB (4), PKHD1 (3), HNF1B (2; see Table 2), ALG8 (1) and ALG9 (1). The lower solve rate likely reflects the analysis by just WES that might miss some variants especially in duplicated PKD1,40 and the criteria used to define pathogenic variants. The higher level of alternative genes causing ADPKD likely reflects the milder ADPKD phenotypes in this populations selected by codes rather than in the nephrology clinic. Also of note, for all genes apart from PKD1 and PKD2, LOF variants were found in many individuals not ICD9/10 coded as ADPKD or with kidney or liver cysts. This indicates that the phenotype associated with a single copy of many of these genes is mild, and perhaps in some cases is not associated with a cystic phenotype (low penetrance genes). Further analysis of such populations will be required to get a fuller view of the penetrance of these genes.

ARPKD is the biallelic form of PKD. The presentation is typically in utero or neonatally with large echogenic kidneys the most distinctive feature (see Bergmann et al1) for greater details of the clinical phenotype. The large kidneys can inhibit lung development leading to pulmonary hypoplasia and resulting in neonatal demise in ~25% of affected individuals. Childhood KF occurs in ~50% of surviving children. The liver disease is CHF rather than the PLD seen in ADPKD and can result in portal hypertension and bleeding esophageal and gastric varices. Later presentations, however, are seen where nephromegaly is less evident and complications of the CHF predominate.41

The major gene for ARPKD is PKHD1 which encodes a large protein, fibrocystin (FPC; Table 1).42 FPC is thought to be cilia related, but its precise role is unknown. Like the major ADPKD genes, there is very significant allelic heterogeneity for PKHD1 with more than 200 different pathogenic variants described that are found throughout the gene. In viable cases, ~60% of mutations are nontruncating and ~40% truncating, with the array of different variant types in these categories.43,44 There is no pathogenic variant that accounts for a large proportion of the population, but there is evidence of some more common variants, such as p.Thr36Met, found in particular populations. There is evidence of genotype/phenotype associations with individuals with biallelic truncating variants not usually viable.43,44 Therefore, it is likely that many nontruncating variants generate a fraction of the normal protein amount that is still functional; they are hypomorphic variants. One reason for the later disease presentation in some individuals seems to be allelic effects associated with the penetrance of at least one of the causative alleles.41

Biallelic variants to DZIP1L have been described as a minor gene for ARPKD (Table 1).45 DZIP1l is a cilium associated protein and, in the mouse, loss causes a syndromic ciliopathy phenotype. However in humans, large cystic kidneys but without apparent liver disease is seen. So far, few families have been described with most consanguineous families with homozygous missense variants.46 A third ARPKD gene is CYS1, encoding the ciliary protein cystin (Table 1). The classical ARPKD mouse model cpk is homozygous for a Cys1 mutation, and recently one family with biallelic pathogenic variants and having childhood PKD and CHF was described.47

In the case of ADPKD, the diagnosis in adult, at risk family members is usually made by abdominal imaging detecting numerous bilateral kidney cysts. However, in certain situations, such as individuals presenting with kidney cysts and a negative family history, unusually severe or mild cystic disease, or when a definite negative diagnosis is needed (such as a living related transplant donor), genetic testing can help to define the diagnosis. A genetic diagnosis can also be of prognostic value.4 As indicated, ADPKD, ADPLD, and ARPKD are genetically heterogeneous disorders, and in addition several other loci can be mistaken for these diseases (see Table 2 for details), although usually a wider array of extrarenal phenotypes differentiates them. Plus, many genes cause syndromic ciliopathies with possible phenotypic overlap.2,3 Therefore, genetic testing for PKD is best performed with a panel containing all of these known genes rather than a gene-by-gene Sanger sequencing approach. There is also evidence that WES may not screen all of the duplicated PKD1 locus efficiently,40 and so a purpose-built capture and NGS panel for PKD genes is recommended.6,7

Before screening, individuals should be counselled about the possible downsides of receiving a diagnosis of a clinically significant monogenic disease,48 which is important despite the enactment of anti-discriminatory legislation for those affected by genetic diseases in many countries. Following the screening, a relatively large array of variants that differ from the reference genome are typically identified. Therefore, an array of methodologies and data repositories (Figure 3) are utilized to identify likely causative variants. To prevent the over diagnosis of monogenic diseases following clinical genetic testing, guidelines for the level of proof of pathogenicity of the detected variants have been adopted by the genetic testing community.49 Factors highly supportive of pathogenicity are: variants being truncating, having previously been described as pathogenic, and/or are rare or not present in large repositories of sequence data from normal individuals. In silico predictions of pathogenicity, segregation in a family, de novo occurrence, functional data, and/or the fit with the clinical phenotype also support a disease-causing classification (Pathogenic or Likely Pathogenic). However, for unique variants in diseases like ADPKD, where just one variant is needed to cause the disease, many are classed as a VUS and a genetic diagnosis is not made. In some cases, additional segregation in affected family members or new description of the variant in the literature or in a variant database may promote the variant to a pathogenic status. However, presently fewer ADPKD families receive a clinical genetic diagnosis than from research testing, where a lower level of proof of pathogenicity may be applied. Hopefully improvements to the clinical scoring scheme can be made for the ADPKD genes to increase the yield from clinical testing.
