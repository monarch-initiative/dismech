</div></section></section><section><section><section><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section><h3>Objective</h3>
<p>Neurologic bowel incontinence and dysfunction are common with Cauda Equina Syndrome (CES). The study objective was to evaluate the efficacy of Peristeen Anal Irrigation System (PAIS)<sup>TM</sup> in people with CES.</p></section><section><h3>Design</h3>
<p>Clinical Trial.</p></section><section><h3>Setting</h3>
<p>Spinal Cord Rehabilitation outpatient clinic at the Health Sciences Centre in Winnipeg.</p></section><section><h3>Methods</h3>
<p>Twelve participants with a mean age of 46.2 years (range 34–72 years, 4 females) with CES used PAIS<sup>TM</sup> bowel routine for 10 weeks.</p></section><section><h3>Outcome Measures</h3>
<p>Change in Neurogenic Bowel Dysfunction Score (NBD) over 10 weeks relative to baseline. <strong>Secondary outcomes:</strong> Change in St. Mark's Fecal Incontinence score (SMFI), Cleveland Clinic Constipation score (CCC), and modified Rectal Surgeons Fecal Incontinence Quality of Life Score (QOL) at week 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 compared to baseline, and self-rating of bowel function at baseline and 10 weeks. Additionally, colonic transit times were assessed using the radioactive markers (Sitzmarks) method.</p></section><section><h3>Results</h3>
<p>Ten participants completed the study. Post-intervention primary outcome NBD score improved (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01). Secondary outcomes also improved significantly, including SMFI (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01), CCC (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01), QOL (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01), self-rating of bowel function (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01), and transit time improved by 22% (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05).</p></section><section><h3>Conclusion</h3>
<p>Overall, a significant improvement was observed with the PAIS<sup>TM</sup> for both primary, as well as secondary outcome measures, without any significant adverse effects. As this non-pharmaceutical method of bowel management is effective and has the potential to improve symptoms of bowel dysfunction in people with CES, it should be considered for those in which traditional methods of managing neurogenic bowel fail.</p></section><section><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Fecal incontinence, Transanal irrigation, Constipation, Quality of life, Cauda Equina Syndrome</p></section></section><section><h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>People with spinal cord injury (SCI) often develop neurogenic bowel dysfunction which results in severe problems with management of their bowels.<sup><a>1</a>,<a>2</a></sup> These problems include fecal incontinence, constipation, impaction, pain, lengthy bowel routines, extremely slow transit times, and the potential for toxic mega colon or rupture.<sup><a>3–5</a></sup> A lesion below the conus medullaris of the spinal cord, involving the cauda equina results in Cauda Equina Syndrome (CES), which includes lower motor neuron (LMN) bowel dysfunction.<sup><a>6</a></sup> People with CES can develop severe fecal impaction in the proximal colon due to their prolonged colonic transit time.<sup><a>3</a>,<a>5</a></sup> In addition, the anal sphincter is flaccid which results in incontinence of stool. This may cause additional health problems, such as infection of a pre-existing pressure injury or urinary tract infection (UTI), as well as often having a negative impact on an individual's social life and community participation.<sup><a>7</a></sup></p>
<p>Improving bowel function is consistently rated among the highest priorities for individuals with SCI in numerous studies.<sup><a>8</a>,<a>9</a></sup> An optimal bowel program results in predictable, regular, and thorough evacuation of the bowels, without the occurrence of incontinence and additional complications. However, many individuals with SCI are unable to achieve an effective and regular bowel routine, despite their best efforts.<sup><a>1</a></sup> At present, the options are limited. The gold standard for the management of LMN bowel dysfunction in CES is described in the Clinical Practice Guidelines and consumer guidelines by Paralyzed Veterans of America (PVA).<sup><a>4</a>,<a>10</a></sup> According to these guidelines, individuals with LMN bowel dysfunction (also referred to as “areflexic” bowel) aim to have formed and firm stool, then the rectum is stimulated followed by manual evacuation and Valsalva maneuvers. However, those with CES and LMN bowel often do poorly despite following these guidelines.<sup><a>11</a></sup></p>
<p>Currently, one form of bowel management that does not consistently fall within the scope of standard practice for people with CES is transanal irrigation. The Peristeen Anal Irrigation System<sup>TM</sup> (PAIS; Coloplast Corporation, Oakville, Canada) works by introducing water into the lower bowel through a rectal catheter, which hydrates inspissated stool, and stimulates colonic peristalsis to mechanically mobilize the fecal matter. Christensen and colleagues compared the use of this system in people with SCI to “conservative” bowel management. They found that using transanal irrigation improved fecal incontinence and constipation, as well as the symptom-related quality of life.<sup><a>12</a></sup> This study included participants with CES LMN bowel dysfunction but did not separately analyze them. Many other studies have also shown effectiveness in individuals with SCI,<sup><a>13</a>,<a>14</a></sup> multiple sclerosis (MS),<sup><a>15</a>,<a>16</a></sup> and spina bifida (SB).<sup><a>17–20</a></sup></p>
<p>Given this effectiveness, transanal irrigation is becoming a recognized and cost-effective treatment option for neurogenic bowel.<sup><a>21–23</a></sup> However, at present there are no published reports evaluating transanal irrigation specifically in people that have CES with LMN bowel dysfunction. Since those with CES have a flaccid anal sphincter, the efficacy of the system may be different from those with an intact anal sphincter reflex. Therefore, it is unknown if the specific neurogenic bowel profile in CES is suitable to be treated with irrigation. The objective of this study was to assess the effectiveness of PAIS<sup>TM</sup> in people with CES who had significant bowel dysfunction despite following PVA guidelines.</p></section><section><h2>Materials and methods</h2>
<p>This study was approved by the University of Manitoba Bannatyne Campus Biomedical Research Ethics Board. Potential participants were identified through the Spinal Cord Rehabilitation outpatient clinic at the Health Sciences Centre in Winnipeg. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18 years or older at enrollment; (2) diagnosis of CES for at least 1 year before enrollment; (3) LMN bowel dysfunction with at least one of the following: spends 30 min or more attempting to defecate every day or every second day, episodes of fecal incontinence once or more per month, or abdominal discomfort before or during defecation; (4) trialed and failed standard management according to PVA guidelines. Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of bowel obstruction, (2) inflammatory bowel disease, (3) known anal or colorectal stenosis, (4) colorectal cancer, (5) active diverticulitis, (6) endoscopic polypectomy in the prior 4 weeks, (7) ischemic colitis, (8) long-term corticosteroid therapy, (9) bleeding diathesis or being on anticoagulant therapy, (10) diabetic polyneuropathy, (11) previous major colorectal or perianal surgery, (12) current pregnancy or lactation, (13) immunosuppression medication or (14) implant for sacral nerve stimulation.</p>
<section><h3>Use of transanal irrigation system</h3>
<p>At their baseline visit, participants were taught how to use the PAIS<sup>TM</sup> safely. Briefly, the water bag is filled with potable, room temperature water, and the tubing is then attached to the bag lid, control unit and catheter (<a>Fig. 1</a>). The pre-lubricated catheter is inserted into the rectum and the balloon is inflated until secure. The desired amount of water is slowly pumped into the bowel. The balloon is then deflated, and the catheter is removed while sitting on a toilet, which causes the water and bowel contents to empty. Supplies were provided to each participant for the duration of the trial. Volume of water pumped into the bowel ranged from 500 to 1000 ml as per product recommendations. Participants were instructed to use the system every day for the first 2 weeks, after which they could choose to use the system daily or every second day for the remainder of the trial. Additionally, participants were told not to change medications. Participants were contacted by phone every week to check how use of the system was progressing, to answer any questions, and to report medication changes or adverse events.
</p>
<figure><h4>Figure 1.</h4>
<p><a><img/></a></p>
<div><a>Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>The Peristeen® Anal Irrigation System consists of: (1) top lid, (2) a water bag that is filled with potable, room temperature water; a pre-lubricated rectal catheter with inflatable balloon, (3) a pump, (4) a control unit with tubing and (5) rectal catheter for insertion.</p></figcaption></figure></section><section><h3>Outcome measures</h3>
<p>At baseline (week 0) participants completed multiple outcome measures. Change in Neurogenic Bowel Dysfunction (NBD) score<sup><a>24</a>,<a>25</a></sup> (range, 0–47, with 47 representing severe symptoms) from baseline to 10 weeks was the primary outcome measure. This is a validated symptom score where each symptom of neurogenic bowel dysfunction is weighted concerning its impact on quality of life. The NBD Score was also administered by phone call survey at 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 weeks, and change from baseline was recorded as a secondary outcome measure. Other secondary outcome measures compared at week 10 to baseline included: (1) Cleveland Constipation Score<sup><a>26</a></sup> (range, 0–30, with 30 representing severe symptoms; a score of 15 or greater indicates significant constipation) for those with constipation, (2) St. Mark's Incontinence Questionnaire<sup><a>27</a>,<a>28</a></sup> (range, 0–24, with 24 representing severe symptoms; a score of 9 or greater signifies significant incontinence) for those with incontinence, (3) a modified American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons Fecal Incontinence Quality of Life (QOL) score<sup><a>29</a></sup> (a symptom-related quality-of-life score from which four subscales can be extracted: lifestyle, coping behavior, depression/self-perception and embarrassment, with the questions modified by replacing “symptoms of fecal incontinence” in each question with “symptoms of fecal incontinence or constipation”; the higher the score, the better the QOL), (4) Self rating of bowel function with Likert scales using numeric box scales: overall bowel function, influence on daily activities, and general satisfaction (each range 0–10, with 10 representing perfect function, no influence, and perfect satisfaction respectively), and reapplied at termination, and (5) Colonic transit time, measured with the Metcalf Method,<sup><a>30</a>,<a>31</a></sup> which utilizes Sitzmarks<em>®</em> (Konsyl Pharmaceuticals, Inc., MD, USA). At week –1 (1 week before starting treatment), participants took one Sitzmarks capsule (each containing 24 distinct radiopaque markers) once a day for 3 consecutive days (days 1–3 of week –1) while following their typical dietary and bowel routines. On days 4 and 7 of week –1, participants had a supine abdominal x-ray. The number of markers that were visible in the colon was counted on each x-ray. Colonic transit time was calculated by adding the number of visible markers on both x-rays. This process was repeated starting the last week of the study.</p></section><section><h3>Statistical analysis</h3>
<p>All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v24 (IBM Inc. Chicago, IL). Paired <em>t</em>-test was conducted to evaluate the effect of PAIS<sup>TM</sup> on the primary outcome measure, <em>i.e.</em> pre–post NBD score. The NBD Score as a secondary outcome measure at intervals was analyzed using one-way repeated measure ANOVA with post-hoc Tukeys’ pairwise comparison. All other outcome measures were analyzed using Student's <em>t</em>-test. All significance tests were two-sided, with alpha set at 0.05.</p></section></section><section><h2>Results</h2>
<p>Twelve people were enrolled; two participants dropped out of the trial early due to difficulty in using the system, and ten completed the trial (<a>Table 1</a>). At the start of the trial, all participants were performing digital stimulation and manual evacuation. Most of the participants (70%) were using suppositories, laxatives, or some form of stool softener.
</p>

<p>Primary outcome (the change in NBD score from baseline to 10 weeks) significantly improved (<em>t</em> = 3.6, <em>P</em> = 0.005). Additionally, the result of one-way repeated measure ANOVA showed that PAIS<sup>TM</sup> had a significant effect (<em>F</em><sub>(2.1, 19.5) </sub>= 8.81, <em>p</em>  = 0.002, <em>η</em><sup>2 </sup>=  0.49) on NBD scores. For the secondary outcome of the in-trial NBD scores, two participants were unavailable for one follow-up time-point each. The average of the two time-points on either side of the missing data point was used in place of the missing data points. Compared to the baseline there was a significant improvement in NBD scores at all time points; week 1 (<em>P</em> = 0.003), week 2 (<em>P</em> = 0.023), week 4 (<em>P</em> = 0.005), week 6 (<em>P</em> = 0.002), week 8 (<em>P</em> = 0.002) (<a>Fig. 2</a>a).

<p>At baseline, nine participants had a score above the threshold of 15 and thus were considered to have constipation. At week 10, only three participants had a score of 15 or greater. Of the nine participants with baseline constipation, the average Cleveland Constipation Score, out of 30, improved from 18.2 ± 1.8–12.4 ± 3.1 (t = 5.497, df = 8, <em>P</em> = 0.0006; <a>Fig. 2</a>b).</p>
<p>At baseline, eight participants had a St. Mark's Incontinence Questionnaire Score of ≥ 9 out of 24 (indicating significant incontinence) and in these participants, the average scores improved from 15.1 ± 5.0 at baseline to 11.3 ± 5.3 at 10 weeks (<em>t</em> = 4.651, df = 7, <em>P</em> = 0.002; <a>Fig. 2</a>c).</p></section><section><h3>Quality of life score and rating of bowel function scores</h3>
<p>The average QOL Score, out of 52, improved from 25.1 ± 8.0–35.6 ± 8.7 (<em>t</em> = 3.159, df = 9, <em>P</em> = 0.01). The overall self-rating of bowel function score improved from 2.2/10 ± 1.9 at baseline to 5.2/10 ± 2.3 at 10 weeks (<em>t</em> = 3.558, df = 9, <em>P</em> = 0.006), satisfaction with bowel function improved from 1.6/10 ± 2.1–5.4/10 ± 2.6 (<em>t</em> = 4.593, df = 9, <em>P</em> = 0.001), and the influence of bowel function on daily activities improved from 2.9/10 ± 2.5–6.5/10 ± 1.7 (<em>t</em> = 4.469, df = 9, <em>P</em> = 0.001).</p></section><section><h3>Colonic transit time</h3>
<p>One participant who completed the study had very poor-quality radiologic images due to body habitus thus transit time could not be estimated. The average transit time for the other nine improved from 84.1 ± 41.35 at baseline to 65.4 ± 44 h at week 10 (<em>t</em> = 2.32, df = 9, <em>P</em> <em>&lt; </em>0.05; <a>Fig. 2</a>d). <a>Figure 3</a>(b) shows representative day 4 x-rays, at baseline and week 10.

<p>No serious adverse events occurred during this trial. However, some of our participants encountered some difficulties using this system (<a>Table 2</a>). Two participants withdrew from the study; one found that it seemed the bowel was empty, due to no further stool coming and lack of sensation, but after 10 min, more stool came out. The other participant that withdrew had difficulty using the system as it would fall out of place. Of those that completed the study, some found it difficult to keep the balloon in place, given the nature of CES with no or little sphincter muscle tone. This was solved by holding the catheter in place with one hand while manipulating the dial to allow water in with the other hand. One participant found that using the system was very difficult at first, due to hard, impacted stool in the rectum at baseline, but this resolved after using the system regularly. Some reported cramping and resolved this by slow introduction of water.

<div><a>Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section></section><section><h2>Discussion</h2>
<p>The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of the PAIS<sup>TM</sup> to improve symptoms of neurogenic bowel dysfunction in individuals with CES. Clinically relevant and statistically significant improvements were seen in all outcome measures in this study. Findings from this study revealed that PAIS<sup>TM</sup> shows potential to be an effective method for bowel management for people with CES.</p>
<p>Many of the positive outcomes that we observed with the PAIS<sup>TM</sup> were in agreement with studies on SCI related neurogenic bowel by Christensen <em>et al.</em><sup><a>12</a>,<a>24</a></sup> However, Christensen's group looked at individuals with SCI, which predominantly included individuals with suprasacral lesions, with only one being “S2 and below”. Emmanuel's study on cost-effectiveness also included CES participants but also did not separate the results of this population specifically.<sup><a>21</a></sup> This is the first study to focus solely on individuals with CES.</p>
<p>The primary outcome, NBD score, improved significantly from baseline with the use of PAIS<sup>TM</sup>. Studies have shown that improvements in NDB scores are directly related to improved physical functioning and quality of life in individuals with SCI.<sup><a>7</a>,<a>32</a></sup></p>
<p>The secondary outcome measures, Cleveland Constipation Score and modified American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons Fecal Incontinence Quality of Life Score, also improved substantially over the 10 weeks of using PAIS<sup>TM</sup> in our study. An improvement of over 30% in symptoms of constipation was observed throughout the trial. This is consistent with the findings reported by Christensen <em>et al</em>.<sup><a>12</a>,<a>24</a></sup> in studies on individuals with SCI-related neurogenic bowel. The incontinence scale improved from 15.1/24 ± 5.0 at baseline to 11.3/24 ± 5.3 at 10 weeks. Although this is statistically significant (<em>P</em> = 0.002), and is an improvement of 25% overall, only three out of eight of those with baseline incontinence (≥9) had scores of 8 or less at the end of the study, and thus clinical significance is difficult to determine.</p>
<p>The colonic transit time which was evaluated by Sitz marks<sup>TM</sup> method also improved. For most participants, the post-intervention x-rays showed statistically and clinically significant improvements. Colonic transit time improved significantly from an average transit time of 84.1 h to 65.4 h, a change of over 22%. This is also clinically relevant, as normal transit time is 24–48 (mean 34.72),<sup><a>33</a></sup> and over 72 h is considered abnormal. The baseline average of 84.1 h in our participants is comparable to the literature reports in individuals with CES of 86.64 h.<sup><a>2</a></sup></p>
<p>Each participant served as their control, which is, in part, a strength of the study as it minimizes the effect of inter-subject variability. This type of treatment would not allow blinding of participants to the intervention or a placebo control. Study limitations include small sample size and short study time. However, the importance of our findings is dramatic, given that this is the first study in this population to find a beneficial alternative to the prior gold standard conservative bowel care. Although all study participants had CES, there was a wide variability of the degree of severity and level of neurologic impairment, which likely contributes to the variability of results. To have further impact, a larger multi-center study with a control group would further add to the credibility of these results. Additionally, it is important to recruit a more consistent (homogenous) group or <em>a priori</em> plan subgroup analysis due to the complexity and degree of variability of neurologic impairment with CES.</p>
<p>Despite the duration and complex nature of the protocol, the dropout rate was low. Positive feedback about the PAIS<sup>TM</sup> system was received from participants who completed the study. In regular clinical follow-up, it became apparent that eight of the 10 participants that completed the study continued to use the PAIS<sup>TM</sup> system as part of their regular bowel routine and have reported continued benefits. Longer-term efficacy has been reported in the multiple sclerosis population.<sup><a>16</a></sup> For those that did drop out, they found the catheter difficult to keep in place or expulsion of retained fluids at a later time problematic. In a study with people with spina bifida using enemas experiencing similar problems, it was found that inflating the Foley balloon with a greater volume corrected the problem of leaking during the infusion, and the problem of leaking of the solution within one hour of completing enema administration was successfully treated by re-inserting the catheter into the rectum to drain residual fluid after evacuation.<sup><a>20</a></sup></p>
<p>It is important to note that an improvement in symptoms was observed early in this study and was maintained over the 10 weeks. This could potentially advocate for a period that could be relatively short with insurers and funding agencies, such that if early improvement and tolerability are not seen, then it is unlikely to be of benefit with long-term use. Advocacy is important to make sure that insurers and payers cover the system to allow this population to access this therapy. In studies by Christensen and colleagues, cost-effectiveness of this transanal irrigation treatment in people with SCI was shown;<sup><a>21</a>,<a>22</a></sup> although product-related costs were higher for transanal irrigation, the costs for a caregiver to help with bowel management and changes/washing due to leakage was lower.</p></section><section><h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>This trial explored the effects of the Peristeen Anal Irrigation System<sup>TM</sup> in people with Cauda Equina Syndrome with lower motor neuron type of bowel dysfunction. Overall, this non-pharmacological intervention improved all measures of neurogenic bowel dysfunction over a ten-week trial, with only minor adverse effects. Evidence from this study provides support that this method of bowel management is safe, effective, and has the potential to improve the quality of life for patients with CES and neurogenic bowel dysfunction. Long-term follow-up data are required to confirm the results of this study.</p></section><section><h2>Acknowledgements</h2>
