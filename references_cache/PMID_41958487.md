# Epidemiological Features, Etiopathogenesis, Diagnostic Evaluation, and Management Protocol of Splenic Tuberculosis: A Systematic Review.
**Authors:** Chaudhary P, Kumar R, Alokjee M, Bhalla A, Thakur S, Das P
**Journal:** J Glob Infect Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.4103/jgid.jgid_102_25](https://doi.org/10.4103/jgid.jgid_102_25)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: The splenic tuberculous involvement is common than other intra-abdominal solid organs such as the liver, pancreas, and kidney. The aim of this article is to present and share a review of the English-language literature on splenic tuberculosis (TB) to gain a better understanding of etiopathogenesis, epidemiological features, and diagnostic methods and provide guidelines for its management and to present our experience of six cases.

METHODS: The systematic search of the literature was performed on PubMed and Medline from 1950 to 2019 according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement.

RESULTS: Fifty-two manuscripts were included in this systematic review. All resulting titles, abstract, and full text, whenever available, were read and kept for reference.

CONCLUSION: Direct histopathological demonstration is the best diagnostic modality. Fine-needle aspiration cytology is the study of choice and polymerase chain reaction assay increases its sensitivity. The standard short course antitubercular therapy for 6 months is recommended for isolated splenic TB, and for widespread disease, 12-month therapy is recommended. Surgery is reserved for failure of medical therapy and complications such as abscess formation.

In patients with miliary tuberculosis (TB), splenic tuberculous involvement is common than other intra-abdominal solid organs such as liver, pancreas, and kidney. Isolated splenic TB is rare even in regions with high TB prevalence and is seen more commonly in immunosuppressed patients. Coley, in 1846, was the first clinician to consider the possibility of TB of the spleen and suggested the term “primary TB of spleen.”[ 1 ] In 1912, Winternitz reported the first case of splenic TB.[ 1 ] In 1938, Englebrdh-Holm insisted on using the term tuberculous splenomegaly instead of primary TB.[ 2 ] In 1946, Meredith et al. published the first detailed report on TB of the spleen.[ 3 ] There are several case reports on splenic TB and 1041 cases have been reported in the literature till date; however, a detailed literature on this pathology is not available. Most of the available literature describes only clinical presentation and treatment of splenic TB. Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) is the causative pathogen.

The authors encountered six cases of splenic TB in the last 10 years. This aim of this article is to present a comprehensive review of all the available literature and describe epidemiological features, etiopathogenesis, various diagnostic modalities, and management protocol of splenic TB, thus providing detailed information and an update on this pathology and our experience of six cases.

The search strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and primary and secondary outcomes were defined before the search. The systematic search of the literature was performed on PubMed and Medline according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement [Figure 1]. Electronic searches were undertaken in PubMed and MEDLINE using the MeSH terms “spleen” in combination with “tuberculosis,” “tuberculous,” “tubercular,” “miliary tuberculosis,” “disseminated tuberculosis,” “tuberculosis in immunocompromised patients,” “complications,” “management,” and “surgical intervention.”

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flowchart

Observation prospective and retrospective studies, case series, and case reports were included for the full review. Exclusion criteria included patients with a history of any form of surgical intervention on spleen and malignancy of spleen. Each pertinent article was assigned to a table including author, tear of publication, age, gender, presenting complaints, site of tumor, treatment modality, and follow-up.

The outcome measures were chosen to assess effectiveness of various diagnostic methods and treatment modalities.

The primary outcome measures were:

For diagnostic evaluation: Clinical manifestations, pathological findings including macroscopic as well as microscopic features, endoscopic findings and biopsy technique, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay and staining methods, role of various imaging studies.

Management: Lacunae in management guidelines, role of surgical intervention and choice of surgery, role of medical management and surgical intervention.

The secondary outcome measures were evaluated to assess other parameters: Assessment of clinical parameters, prognosis, spread and complications.

Data were extracted by one author independently and then compared. The study author provided additional data if incomplete data were noted. Titles/abstracts considered potentially relevant were retrieved for review of the full manuscript. The list of full manuscript meeting inclusion criteria were compared, and any disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus.

The institution research ethics committee/review board has confirmed that no ethical approval is required for a systematic review and that the study has been performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments

Fifty-two manuscripts were included in this systematic review. All resulting titles, abstract, and full text, whenever available, were read and kept for reference, and the findings were summarized.

Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) accounts for approximately 15%–20% of all TB cases; of these, 3%–11% are abdominal.[ 4 5 ] Approximately 20% of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-noninfected patients of TB have an extrapulmonary disease.[ 6 ] Spleen is the third most commonly involved organ in miliary TB.[ 7 ] The five most commonly involved organs in order of frequency are lung (100%), liver (82%), spleen (72%), lymph node (55%), and bone marrow (45%).[ 7 8 ] In a retrospective study by Valencia et al., of 137 patients of TB with HIV positive status, 23 had splenic involvement.[ 9 ] Out of these 25 cases, only the spleen was involved in 18 cases, and 7 cases had associated hepatic involvement.[ 9 ] All of these 18 patients were found to have a splenic abscess. In a retrospective study, the rate of splenic TB has been reported to be 8%.[ 5 ] Pottakkat et al. reported that the rate of TB is 12% in patients subjected to splenectomy,[ 10 ] while Simu et al. reported it to be 4%.[ 11 ] A splenic abscess incidence has been reported to be 0.14%–0.7% in an autopsy study.[ 12 ] Dixit et al. concluded that in patients with splenic TB, pulmonary TB is associated in 62% and HIV coinfection in 50% of cases.[ 13 ]

Splenic TB is more common in males than females in most of the case series and reported cases.[ 12 13 ] Young adult males are most commonly involved. The youngest reported case is that of a neonate[ 14 ] with disseminated disease and splenic involvement, while the oldest reported patient is 79 years old.[ 15 ] In a retrospective study, the male-to-female ratio has been reported as 3:1, and most of the patients were in the age range of 25–50 years.[ 13 ]

Patients who develop splenic TB have some risk factors such as splenic trauma, pyogenic infection, hemoglobinopathy, sickle cell disease, leukemias, polycythemia, and other splenic abnormalities.[ 16 17 ] Patients infected with HIV or who are immunocompromised are more likely to develop splenic TB.[ 17 18 ] Many reported cases of splenic tuberculous abscess are found to have underlying HIV infection.[ 9 19 ] Lienas-Garcia et al. concluded that for all types of splenic abscesses, immunosuppressed states are the predisposing factors in two-thirds of the patients.[ 20 ] A study done by Alavi et al. showed a high prevalence of splenic TB in diabetics compared to nondiabetics.[ 21 ] Alcoholics also have a high risk of splenic abscess formation.[ 17 ]

Splenic TB can be classified as primary or secondary. In primary cases, there is no evidence of TB elsewhere. Primary splenic TB is rare and usually develops as a result of either dissemination of pulmonary or miliary TB, following either ingestion of contaminated food or infected sputum. In a study on eight cases of splenic TB, seven patients had extrasplenic involvement.[ 10 ]

There are five pathomorphological types of splenic TB-nodular TB, tuberculous splenic abscess, calcific TB, mixed type TB, and multiple microabscesses as a part of miliary TB.[ 22 ] Splenic TB involvement usually follows hematogenous dissemination or contiguous spread of infection from mesenteric lymph nodes, intestine, pancreatic, subphrenic, and perinephric area. Hepatic involvement in association with the spleen has been reported in many cases. Hematogenous dissemination frequently occurs in the HIV infected individuals.[ 23 ] In immunocompetent cases, spleen involvement is usually by contiguous spread, or it may occur as a part of severe disseminated disease. Tuberculous abscess formation in the spleen is usually attributed to the exaggerated immune response with the formation of caseation necrosis.

Diagnosis of isolated TB is difficult. Misdiagnosis is common. Definition/criteria of isolated splenic TB are given in Table 1. Clinical features and laboratory parameters support the diagnosis. Imaging studies are important for diagnosis and also to rule out or confirm the presence of any other tuberculous focus in the abdominal cavity. The best diagnostic modality is direct demonstration of bacilli on Histopathological examination.

There are no specific signs and symptoms of splenic TB.[ 20 ] Patients may remain asymptomatic initially or may present with abdominal discomfort, heaviness, or nonspecific pain abdomen. The splenic abscess has been reported to be the most common manifestation of splenic TB and presents with high-grade fever with or without chills and left upper quadrant pain.[ 24 ] All of our patients presented with fever. Forty-nine cases of splenic abscess have been reported in the literature—two of our patients presented with splenic abscess. One of them was an isolated TB case, while the other one had disseminated disease. When a patient presents with nonspecific pain abdomen, pain is usually dull, often associated with vomiting and constitutional features of TB such as anorexia, weakness, weight loss, and night sweats.

Patients may also present with pyrexia of unknown origin; many cases have been reported in the literature.[ 10 25 ] Another common presentation is left upper quadrant mass. Splenic enlargement has been reported to be the most common presenting sign.[ 10 24 ] Al-Naami et al. reported a case in which a patient presented with left upper quadrant mass, and further evaluation revealed the mass as a subcutaneous abscess originating from and communicating with a splenic abscess.[ 26 ] Lin et al. reported that the symptoms of splenic TB in order of frequency are fever (82.3%), weight loss and weakness (44.12%), and splenic enlargement (13.2%–100%).[ 5 ]

A rare presentation of splenic TB reported in the literature is difficulty in breathing in association with constitutional features. This could be due to reactionary pleural effusion or pyothorax resulting from pleural rupture of splenic abscess. Wangai et al. reported a case of splenic TB complicated by massive right pleural effusion, with right and midlower zone consolidation.[ 27 ] Other presentations of splenic TB described in the literature include fatigue, malaise, anorexia, and weight loss. Yellowish discoloration of the skin, high colored urine, vomiting, chronic diarrhea, melena, and hematemesis are uncommon presenting complaints.[ 28 ]

Out of six patients, two of our patients had isolated splenic TB, one was found to have associated hepatic, and peritoneal TB, and the remaining three cases were a part of disseminated disease.

Spleen is mild to moderately enlarged in most cases; however, massive splenic enlargement can also be found. The splenomegaly is secondary to proliferation of the reticulum cells of the pulp and to the presence of tuberculous foci. The splenomegaly can also be due to hypersplenism because of late detection in the late stages of disease.[ 29 ] The majority are usually huge, weighing from 1 kg to 3 kg. The most common gross pathological finding is the presence of multiple white caseating nodules in the spleen, coalescing to form a sizeable yellowish mass of solid consistency [Figure 2a and b]. Another common gross finding is calcification within the splenic parenchyma. The large solitary abscess has also been reported.[ 30 ] Other findings are subcapsular infarcts, enlarged splenic hilar lymph nodes.

(a) Multiple splenic abscesses and ruptured abscess cavity and (b) multiple white caseating nodules in the spleen, coalescing to form a large yellowish mass of solid consistency

Microscopically, the splenectomy specimen shows very cellular pulp with numerous confluencing miliary tubercles. The hallmark of TB caused by MTB is epitheloid granulomas composed of macrophages aggregates, epitheloid cells and langhans giant cells with variable degree of central caseous necrosis involving both the red and white pulps. Circumscribing the macrophages is a cuff of both T- and B-lymphocytes within the rim of fibroblasts. These lesions vary in size from 1 mm to >2 cm. The large size granulomas are visible macroscopically. The hilar lymph nodes may also show caseating granulomas. Perisinusoidal inflammation and fibrosis may develop in relation to tubercular granulomas. In some granulomas, fibroblasts predominate with little or no necrosis and few macrophages.

Routine laboratory tests are nonspecific. Hemogram shows low hemoglobin, gross granulations in neutrophils, raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and varying degrees of pancytopenia. These hematologic effects are because of splenic involvement, and these effects are severe when the spleen gets primarily involved. Hypersplenism with splenomegaly results in pancytopenia. Neutropenia is the most common hematological abnormality as reported in various studies.[ 31 32 ] In a patient with splenic abscess, TB should be considered as a pathogen in the presence of prolonged severe neutropenia. Polycythemia has also been reported.[33] Pathological hematopoiesis can also be seen. Peripheral smear may show increased activity of granulocyte series with an increase in immature granulocytes. Megakaryocytic series may also show increased activity.

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is an important method of diagnosing splenic TB. It is done from a large abscess, microabscesses, or from nodules under ultrasound guidance. The sensitivity and specificity of FNAC is up to 88% and 100%, respectively.[ 34 35 ] In a study by Wangai et al., FNAC failed to demonstrate Mycobacteria and revealed only scanty leucocytes and erythrocytes.[ 20 ] Basa et al. concluded that FNAC should be done whenever splenomegaly presents with fever of unknown origin, and it can reveal caseating granulomas also.[ 36 ] Azzam et al. reported that the yield of FNAC is poor.[ 32 ] FNAC is contraindicated when there is a risk of splenic rupture. However, no such complication of FNAC has been reported in the literature.

Core needle biopsy (CNB) has a high diagnostic yield in splenic pathologies. It is considered a superior diagnostic modality to FNAC in characterizing splenic lesions as it can differentiate granulomatous lesions and other radiographically comparable lesions.[ 37 38 ] CNB is associated with high rates of complications such as bleeding and a high risk of splenic rupture. Wangai et al. reported a case in which ultrasound guided CNB was performed and demonstrated classical granulomatous inflammation and acid fast bacilli.[ 27 ]

Analysis of pus culture may help in diagnosing TB. Adenosine deaminase analysis (ADA) of cyst fluid also aids in diagnosis. ADA is an essential enzyme in T-lymphocytes, and its increased concentration may be found in fluids present in the zones of TB serositis.

Bone marrow biopsy has no role in diagnosing splenic TB. It is indicated only when it is challenging to differentiate tuberculous splenic mass lesions from malignant spleen lesions such as lymphoma or when some other hematological malignancy is suspected. Azzam et al. reported a case in which a patient presented with pyrexia of unknown origin with severe neutropenia, negative blood cultures, and computed tomography (CT) scan was inconclusive. Bone marrow biopsy was also normal.[ 32 ] Histopathological examination of spleen then confirmed the diagnosis of splenic TB. In patients with splenic TB, bone marrow biopsy may show pathological hematopoiesis with increased activity of granulocytic and megakaryocytic series.[ 22 ]

In the majority of the cases, chest X-ray fails to demonstrate specific findings suggestive of TB. In cases with miliary TB, it may reveal multiple miliary opacities throughout the lungs. In a retrospective study, 62% of the patients were found to have associated pulmonary TB.[ 13 ]

Ultrasonography (USG) is an essential diagnostic modality for splenic pathologies. It helps assess the number, site and nature of the lesion in the spleen. It can also diagnose the presence of similar lesions in other intra-abdominal organs. It also helps in identifying a suitable window for aspiration, biopsy, and percutaneous drainage.

Typical tuberculous lesions on the splenic capsule are usually too small to be detected easily by USG. USG may show splenic abscesses, calcifications, and nodular lesions. Calcification in the spleen is an important feature to diagnose TB. Mitra et al. reported that hypoechoic lesions, well-demarcated which may be solitary or multiple, represent tuberculomas and may be associated with necrotic or hemorrhagic areas.[ 39 ] Irregular anechoic lesions with the thick and shaggy wall are suggestive of a tuberculous abscess.[ 39 40 ] Lin et al. reported a case in which USG showed a sizeable hypoechoic mass lesion with regular margins and homogenous internal echoes in the spleen and diagnosed it as lymphoma, which later turned out to be a case of splenic TB.[ 5 ] In a retrospective study on 8 patients with splenic TB, Suri et al. concluded that USG guided FNAC offers a safe and accurate method for diagnosing abdominal TB, especially those involving lymph nodes and spleen.[ 41 ]

The diagnosis of splenic TB is difficult before laparotomy, as reported in the majority of the cases. Since this disease is potentially curable with chemotherapy, it is essential to diagnose it before any surgical intervention to avoid associated morbidity of the surgical procedures. contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) is the imaging study of choice to diagnose splenic TB.[ 39 ]

On CT scan, splenic tuberculous lesions appear as solitary or numerous hypodense areas[ 5 10 ] [Figure 3]. Tuberculous abscesses are usually small in size, ill-defined and exhibit mild contrast enhancement while pyogenic abscesses are unilocular with rim-enhancement.[ 41 ] On the basis of CT scan findings, splenic TB has been classified into two types, micronodular (smaller than 10 mm) and macronodular (larger than 10 mm). Large nodular tuberculous lesions appear as large hypodense lesion with central irregular necrosis and heterogenous enhancement.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography shows hypodense area of size 4 cm × 4.4 cm × 3.3 cm at upper pole of the spleen

The important differential diagnosis of such hypodense lesions on CECT includes pyogenic abscess; fungal infections; neoplastic, parasitic, congenital cystic lesions; infarction, hamartoma, hemangioma, vascular tumors; sickle cell disease; metstatic deposits, most commonly from gastric, colonic and ovarian malignancies; sarcoidosis; lymphomas; and splenic trauma.[ 10 39 ] In such cases, diagnosis is established by biopsy and histopathological examination.

Kao et al. reported that Ga-67 imaging study is useful in patients with fever of unknown origin with a splenic lesion.[ 42 ]

Histopathological examination is still the best diagnostic modality for TB. If all other studies fail to diagnose TB, laparoscopic splenic biopsy should be done and unnecessary splenectomies can thus be avoided. Lymph nodes, if present, can also be taken and biopsied. Zhan et al. recommended laparoscopic splenic biopsy.[ 22 ] Laparoscopic biopsy and/or splenectomy is indicated when other less invasive diagnostic modalities have failed.[ 43 44 ] Table 2 shows the role of surgical intervention in the diagnosis and management of splenic TB. Diagnostic laparoscopic splenectomy was done in one of our patient.

The sensitivity and specificity of XPERT MTB are 77.3% and 98.2%, respectively.[ 45 ] Dhibar et al. reported a case in which diagnosis was supported by XPERT MTB.[ 17 ] Wangai et al. also reported a case confirmed on splenic biopsy specimen by gene XPERT.[ 27 ] The advantages of PCR are its rapidity, high sensitivity and specificity, and its ability to identify the etiological agent up to the species level. Some studies have suggested that the sensitivity of PCR is enhanced on formalin fixed, paraffin embedded tissues as compared to acid-fast staining for MTB.[ 27 44 ]

The management of splenic TB depends on the clinical presentation, its pathological stage, and its occurrence in isolation or as a part of disseminated disease. The treatment principles are timely diagnosis, treatment in combination with optimum doses and duration of antitubercular therapy (ATT), whenever indicated timely surgical intervention with pre- and postoperative ATT, prevention of complications, and regular assessment of disease burden and follow-up.

The initiation of ATT is the mainstay of therapy. Once a diagnosis is established without splenectomy, TB is potentially curable with ATT alone.[ 27 45 ] The ATT results in an improvement of the clinical state and regression of the symptoms. As reported in the majority of studies, patients with splenic TB respond very well with standard ATT involving four drugs: Isoniazid (5 mg/kg BW/day), rifampicin (10 mg/kg BW/day), pyrazinamide (30 mg/kg BW/day), and ethambutol (20 mg/kg Bw/day) for 2–4 months, subsequently isoniazid and rifampicin for 6–12 months.

According to the WHO guidelines, isolated splenic TB with no history of TB should be considered as a new case of EPTB, and ATT should be given for 6 months.[ 46 ] The treatment regimen consists of 2 months of intensive phase with four drugs followed by 4 months continuation phase with two drugs. Azzam et al. recommended that ATT be continued for 12 months for all types and stages of splenic TB.[ 32 ] The best chemotherapy results are seen in patients with solitary splenic TB without abscess formation.[ 26 47 ] EPTB is associated with a lower bacillary burden than pulmonary disease. Therefore, isolated extrapulmonary disease such as isolated splenic TB can generally be treated with standard short-course regimens effective for pulmonary disease. However, a disseminated disease with tuberculous splenic involvement needs to be treated with 12 months of chemotherapy.

Some studies have recommended using glucocorticoids in combination with ATT in patients showing slow response, thus avoiding therapeutic splenectomy.[ 9 23 ] An excellent response to ATT is validated by improvement in symptoms and overall general condition, the return of neutrophil counts to normal range, and the regression of radiological features.

Isoniazid induced hepatic injury is idiosyncratic and potentially lethal. As hepatic involvement has been reported in many cases of splenic TB,[ 9 48 49 ] ATT should be initiated cautiously, and weekly monitoring of liver enzymes and serum bilirubin is strongly recommended. Sometimes, rapid killing of bacilli with ATT results in the release of large amounts of microbial components, which stimulates an exuberant inflammatory response. This is essentially a hypersensitivity reaction to the persistent mycobacterial antigen. This phenomenon is self-limiting and can be managed with supportive treatment and continued ATT with or without steroids.

USG is the investigation of choice for follow-up and also in guiding the duration of therapy. The CT imaging is preferred over USG for confirmation of complete resolution of the pathology and in patients requiring any form of surgical intervention during or after ATT.

Abscess cavities larger than 5 cm needs to be drained. If image-guided simple aspiration of the abscess cavity is planned, then the patient should be explained about the possibility of multiple aspirations at frequent intervals. Image-guided percutaneous tube drainage is an effective procedure and is preferred over simple aspiration. Pre- and postprocedure ATT should be continued.[ 17 ] Thanos et al. reported that percutaneous drainage’s success rate is between 67% and 100%.[ 50 ]

Sometimes patients need invasive and extensive procedures. Splenectomy is to be done in complicated or refractory disease. The indications of surgical intervention, such as laparoscopic or open splenectomy are:

Imaging studies and image-guided FNAC and CNB fail to diagnose splenic TB

Malignancies of the spleen, such as lymphoma or vascular tumors, cannot be ruled out

In the presence of complications such as splenic rupture, splenic vein thrombosis, features of hypersplenism, and portal hypertension develop

Surgery is indicated for the involvement of other parts of the gastrointestinal system

The disease fails to show complete response with ATT

In patients with enlarged spleen with impending rupture.

Splenectomy may be done laparoscopically or via a formal laparotomy. Laparoscopy is preferred over an open procedure. Till 1990, splenectomy was the treatment of choice for splenic TB. However, with evidence supporting therapeutic chemotherapy, ATT has become the first-line treatment for splenic TB. The response is good, and studies have shown complete regression of disease with ATT alone. Though, some authors have reported that without splenectomy, response to ATT is not optimum.[ 10 32 ] In a study by Rhazal et al., out of 6 cases, laparotomy was done in five. The splenectomy was done in two cases for diagnosis, while one underwent splenectomy for splenic abscess refractory to medical treatment.[ 35 ]

Open splenectomy was done in two of our patients for failed medical therapy in one and associated involvement of small intestine in the other patient. The different types of surgical interventions done with diagnostic and/or curative intent are given in Table 2.

Delay in diagnosis is expected because of the rarity of this pathology. A group of patients may present when the disease gets complicated. Delayed diagnosis and missed diagnosis often results in complications. These complications include splenic rupture, hypersplenism, portal hypertension with or without gastrointestinal bleed, splenic pseudocyst, splenic vein infarction, perforation of abscess cavity into peritoneum or rupture into adjacent organs with resulting fistula formation into gastrointestinal tract, pleural space, and lung parenchyma. These complications can be life-threatening. The complications, mechanism, and effect of splenic TB are given in Table 4.

In a study by Llenas-Garcia et al., the mortality was 25% in patients with splenic TB, and no other study has reported mortality.[ 20 ]

Splenic TB is not uncommon, but it is an unusual site for TB, and the lesions are nodular. The diagnosis is difficult, and misdiagnosis is expected because of the lack of specific clinical features. It occurs in various forms: nodular, calcific, solitary abscess, multiple microabscesses due to miliary TB, and mixed type. The author recommends:

In patients presenting with pyrexia of unknown origin with splenomegaly and a history of pulmonary or abdominal TB and in the presence of splenic lesions on USG, splenic TB should always be considered as one of the differentials

In a patient with a large solitary splenic abscess, TB should be considered as a pathogen in the presence of prolonged severe neutropenia when the disease is endemic

Tuberculous involvement of the spleen in miliary TB is more common in immunocompromised than in immunocompetent patients
