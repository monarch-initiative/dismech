Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is the most common soft tissue sarcoma of childhood (Skapek et al., 2019). This malignancy is defined by its skeletal muscle histogenesis, and was originally described by light microscopy features as embryonal (ERMS) or alveolar (ARMS) morphology. Over the last decade, molecular findings have become more important than histology in predicting clinical behavior, and tumors are now classified based on the presence or absence of a driving fusion oncogene (Missiaglia et al., 2012;Skapek et al., 2013). Those tumors without the fusion oncogene (fusion negative, FN-RMS) often have embryonal morphology and gain-of-function mutations in the RAS signaling pathway, including in RTKs, PI3K, and/or the MEK/ERK pathways. In contrast, tumors with the fusion oncogene (fusion positive, FP-RMS) usually have an alveolar morphology, and stable reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 2 and 13 or 1 and 13, yielding the signaturePAX3::FOXO1orPAX7::FOXO1fusion genes, respectively. Survival for high-risk RMS, whether FN-RMS or FP-RMS, is less than 30 %. The subgroup of children with metastatic FP-RMS represents a uniquely vulnerable population, with fewer than 10 % of patients surviving. A constant disappointment in the pediatric sarcoma community is that despite decades of basic, translational and clinical research, survival has not improved for this group of children. There is much work to be done to improve their outcome.

This review will summarize the history, contemporary application, clinical evaluation, and future of FP-RMS systemic therapy. Excellent reviews of the use of surgery and radiation on local control of FP-RMS are available elsewhere (Frankart et al., 2021;Rogers & Dasgupta, 2021;Terwisscha van Scheltinga et al., 2023). It is also worth noting that many rare variant fusions are being discovered in FP-RMS that involve eitherPAX3/7orFOXO1fused to a novel partner (or fusion of two completely different genes) (Sankhe et al., 2025). However, it is too early to know whether these variant fusions portend a similar FP-RMS biology, outcome and targetability as PAX3/7::FOXO1; this will be important to investigate in the future.

The combination of vincristine, actinomycin D, and cyclophosphamide (VAC) was first employed in 1969 by Pratt et al. (Pratt, 1969), albeit in different doses and schedules compared to contemporary trials (Arndt et al., 2009;Gupta et al., 2024;Hawkins et al., 2018). They demonstrated the superiority of the VAC drug combination over single-agent therapy for treating children with RMS (Pratt et al., 1968). Over the years, the doses and schedules of the VAC regimen have evolved to treat ARMS through trials conducted by the Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study Group (IRSG, which conducted IRS trials) and the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) (Arndt et al., 2009;Crist et al., 1995;Crist et al., 2001;Hawkins et al., 2018;Maurer et al., 1988;Maurer et al., 1993).Fig. 1briefly summarizes the mechanism of action of current standard of care chemotherapies (including VAC) for RMS.

VAC has been the backbone of RMS therapy since the 1960s. Vincristine (and vinorelbine) disrupt mitotic spindle formation, which ultimately leads to microtubule crystallization, mitotic arrest, and cell death. Findings from IRS-I, IRS-II, and IRS-III studies indicated no discernible benefit when incorporating another agent, such as the anthracycline doxorubicin, into the VAC combination for patients with group III and IV RMS regardless of histology (Crist et al., 1995;Maurer et al., 1988;Maurer et al., 1993). Histology was integrated into risk-stratified treatment for the first time in the IRS-III trial based on results of the IRS-I and IRS-II studies that showed worse outcomes in patients with ARMS compared to ERMS (Crist et al., 1995;Maurer et al., 1988;Maurer et al., 1993). Furthermore, including doxorubicin and cisplatin, with or without etoposide, into the VAC regimen did not improve outcomes for patients with group III and IV disease in the IRS-III study (Crist et al., 1995). Data from IRS-IV indicated that VAC (cyclophosphamide 2.2 g/m2/day/cycle) had similar outcomes to VAI (vincristine, actinomycin D, and ifosfamide) and VIE (vincristine, ifosfamide, and etoposide) for children with RMS (Crist et al., 2001).

Subsequent COG studies for non-metastatic patients with ARMS were D9803 (Arndt et al., 2009), ARST0531 (Hawkins et al., 2018), and ARST1431 (Gupta et al., 2024). D9803 showed that survival was not improved with the addition of topotecan to VAC chemotherapy (Arndt et al., 2009). Similarly, adding VI (vincristine and irinotecan) to lower-intensity VAC (cyclophosphamide 1.2 g/m2/day/cycle) did not improve event-free survival or overall survival (EFS or OS) for these patients in ARST0531, although toxicity was reduced (Hawkins et al., 2018). Likewise, in the ARST1431 trial, the addition of temsirolimus to the VAC/VI chemotherapy and vinorelbine and cyclophosphamide maintenance backbone did not result in improved EFS or OS of these patients (Gupta et al., 2024).

In attempts to maximize treatment intensity to improve survival, recent COG trials for metastatic ARMS such as ARST0431 and ARST08P1 had condensed and intensified therapy to include all established active agents (vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, etoposide, and VAC) (Malempati et al., 2019;Weigel et al., 2016). Furthermore, they explored additional agents like irinotecan, temozolomide, or cixutumumab. Despite these intensified treatments, patients with metastatic ARMS continued to exhibit the poorest outcomes, with a 3-year EFS ranging from 6 to 16 % (Malempati et al., 2019;Weigel et al., 2016). Overall, these findings suggest that augmenting VAC with other available agents did not improve outcomes for high-risk RMS, and in the COG VAC or VAC/VI remains the standard treatment for patients with FP-RMS.

Based on a recent meta-analysis pooling data from five studies involving patients with relapsed or refractory RMS, individuals with ARMS experienced a 41 % higher response rate compared to those with ERMS when treated with vinorelbine alone or in combination with lower doses or oral cyclophosphamide (Allen-Rhoades et al., 2023). Therefore, the ongoing COG ARST2031 trial tests whether incorporating vinorelbine to the VAC regimen and adding vinorelbine and cyclophosphamide maintenance improves the survival of high-risk RMS patients. Another strategy being tested utilizes an evolution-inspired approach to either incorporate vinorelbine and cyclophosphamide maintenance as soon as a complete response is achieved with VAC, or use adaptive therapy with VAC only based on the disease response (NCT04388839).

Similar to North American studies, European trials of RMS, including ARMS, have shown that intensifying standard induction ifosfamide, vincristine, actinomycin D (IVA) chemotherapy by adding agents such as doxorubicin, carboplatin, epirubicin, or etoposide has not led to improved survival outcomes (Bisogno et al., 2018;Flamant et al., 1998;Oberlin et al., 2012). Likewise, the early introduction of carboplatin and etoposide in poor responders did not demonstrate a benefit (Stevens et al., 2005). The BERNIE trial, which evaluated the addition of the VEGF-targeting antibody bevacizumab in a randomized fashion to IVADo (IVA doxorubicin)/IVA in newly diagnosed metastatic RMS patients, followed by 12 months of vinorelbine and oral cyclophosphamide maintenance therapy, did not show a significant improvement in survival with the addition of bevacizumab (Chisholm et al., 2017).

The EpSSG RMS 2005 trial demonstrated that six months of maintenance chemotherapy with vinorelbine and continuous low-dose cyclophosphamide, administered after completion of IVA- or IVADo-based induction therapy in patients achieving a complete response, significantly improved both EFS and OS in high-risk RMS as defined by European RMS risk stratification. One-third of the study population had localized ARMS with no nodal disease; the positive findings from this group helped establish maintenance therapy as a new standard of care for these patients treated with IVA induction chemotherapy in Europe (Bisogno et al., 2019;Bisogno et al., 2025;Merks et al., 2025). Building on these results, the ongoing FaR-RMS trial (NCT04625907) in Europe is investigating different durations of maintenance therapy post-induction chemotherapy: 6 vs. 12 months for patients with high-risk RMS, which includes localized fusion-positive RMS, and 12 vs. 24 months for those with localized node-positive or metastatic fusion-positive RMS (Chisholm et al., 2024).

Since the addition of doxorubicin to IVA during induction treatment did not improve outcomes in high-risk non-metastatic RMS, IVA remains the standard induction regimen for high-risk localized RMS in Europe (Merks et al., 2025). Nonetheless, European cooperative groups continue to use IVADo as the induction regimen for very high risk (VHR) patients—those with localized node-positive or metastatic fusion-positive ARMS—due to their dismal prognosis and the lack of more effective alternatives currently (Chisholm et al., 2024). To improve systemic therapy further, the FaR-RMS trial will include an upfront randomisation between the novel IRIVA regimen (IVA plus irinotecan) once the recommended phase II dose of irinotecan is established and either IVA for high-risk (HR) patients or IVADo for VHR patients, leveraging the promising activity of VI observed in the upfront and relapsed setting for RMS (Chisholm et al., 2024;Defachelles et al., 2021;Hawkins et al., 2018). Additionally, the FaR-RMS trial is evaluating the role of radiotherapy in enhancing local control and metastatic control in patients with HR and VHR RMS. This trial also incorporates for the first time a revised risk stratification approach based onPAX::FOXO1fusion gene status, replacing traditional histological subtyping.

Since the discovery of the signature fusion oncogenes in FP-RMS in the early 1990s, an understanding of the biology of FP-RMS has continued to mature. Although myogenic histogenesis was understood very early, the molecular derangements supporting FP-RMS oncogenic phenotypes have been identified in phases, usually after a particular cancer pathway has been discovered and found dysregulated in cancer. This section will review efforts to target components of mammalian signaling pathways, including receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), epigenetic modifiers, DNA damage pathways, and developmental networks (Fig. 2).

Cellular signaling pathways that have or are being evaluated as targetable in FP-RMS include those active at the plasma membrane, including RTKs and downstream signaling members, organic cation transporters (OCTs) that facilitate metformin transport, and developmental pathway receptors for TGF-beta, Smoothened, Notch, and Wnt. In principle, PAX3::FOXO1 activity itself can be modified by targeting epigenetic modifiers, transcriptional inhibitors, enzymes that impact PAX3::FOXO1 post-translational modifications, or DNA damage/repair pathways. Finally, PAX3::FOXO1 mRNA or protein could in principle be directly targeted for degradation by siRNAs/ASOs or PROTACs/bioPROTACs, respectively. RTKs such as IGF-1R, MET, PDGFR, VEGFR1, and FGFR4 are known to be downstream transcriptional targets of PAX3::FOXO1 (Crose & Linardic, 2011). Given their role in myoblast proliferation, progression, and metastasis, as well as the availability of small molecule inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies, there has been a strong rationale to target RTKs in patients with recurrent RMS. Unfortunately, this strategy has been disappointing to date. For example, single-agent studies with the multi-RTK inhibitors crizotinib (Schoffski et al., 2018), sorafenib (Kim et al., 2015), cabozantinib (Akshintala et al., 2021), ceritinib (Geoerger et al., 2015), lenvatinib (Gaspar et al., 2021), and pazopanib (Glade Bender et al., 2013) have shown limited activity in RMS patients with measurable disease. Similarly, responses to monoclonal antibodies targeting IGF-1R have also been rare (Pappo et al., 2014;Weigel et al., 2014). Even when additional agents are added to address putative mechanisms of resistance (Akshintala et al., 2023;Wagner et al., 2015), there has been insufficient activity to move these strategies forward. It is possible these drugs may peform better when used in a different clinical context, such as the combination of the multi-RTK inhibitor regorafenib with an established chemotherapy regimen such as vincristine plus irinotecan (Casanova et al., 2023). This approach has shown activity and toxicity, necessitating sequential rather than concomitant administration. Alternatively, the use of cabozantinib as a “maintenance therapy” for patients in high-risk remission with likely minimal residual disease is also being studied (NCT05135975).

While overexpression is the most common molecular change seen with most RTKs in RMS tumors, activating mutations have been identified in FGFR4 in approximately 7–13 % of RMS tumors (Shern et al., 2021), including some in FP-RMS (JGT et al., 2009), suggesting potentially increased sensitivity to targeted inhibition. Erdafitinib is a pan-FGFR inhibitor that has been tested in children, but experience in RMS is so far limited (Lee et al., 2023). Several FGFR4-specific inhibitors are now under development (Fiorito et al., 2022). However, the clinical activity of RTK inhibitors in treating RMS so far has been less than seen with bone sarcomas (Just et al., 2021). Identification of predictive biomarkers of sensitivity and/or resistance and determination of the best clinical context will be necessary to optimize the use of this treatment strategy for patients with FP-RMS.

Histone deacetylases (HDACs) are an important component of epigenetic regulation in RMS (Selim et al., 2023). Use of the selective HDAC inhibitor entinostat has been found to suppress expression of FOXO1 fusion proteins and downstream targets (Gryder et al., 2019). However, clinical translation of encouragingin vivoanimal studies can be challenging, as mice tolerate doses of these agents that may not be clinically relevant for RMS patients (Pacenta et al., 2021). To date, responses have not been reported in early phase RMS trials where patients were treated with single-agent HDAC inhibitors (Wood et al., 2018). As with targeting RTKs, combination with chemotherapy may be helpful, as suggested by interim results of an ongoing study of mocetinostat and vinorelbine for both FP and FN-RMS (Federman et al., 2022). Bromodomain and Extra-Terminal Domain (BET) proteins are another potential therapeutic target given expression of BET protein BRD4 is necessary for the oncogenic effects of FOXO1 fusion proteins (Gryder et al., 2017). BET inhibitors are now in clinical trials for pediatric patients (NCT03936465), though no results are available.

DNA replication is a fundamental process for cellular proliferation and preservation of genome integrity. Endogenous and exogenous factors consistently challenge this process, including but not limited to misincorporated ribonucleotides, abnormal DNA secondary structures, reactive oxygen species, ultraviolet light, ionizing radiation, chemical agents, etc. (Yano & Shiotani, 2023) Cells have evolved to combat replication stress via DNA damage response (DDR) systems. ATR (ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related) and ATM (ataxia telangiectasia mutated) proteins are two important regulators of the DDR.

ATR serves as a master regulator of the DDR and helps coordinate the response to DNA damage and replication problems (Karnitz & Zou, 2015). It is part of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-related kinase (PIKK) family, which also includes ATM, mTOR, DNA-PKcs, SMG1, and PI3K. Inhibition of ATR in tumor cells has been widely found to trigger cell death, which has led to many adult clinical trials investigating ATR inhibitors as monotherapy and in combination with systemic agents and/or radiotherapy (Yano & Shiotani, 2023). These studies have yielded some positive results, although more investigation is needed. In ARMS, fusion protein-induced replication stress and DNA damage creates a dependency on repair processes (e.g. WEE1, ATR activity) that are targetable, and preclinical studies have demonstrated increased sensitivity of ARMS cells to ATR inhibition (Dorado Garcia et al., 2022). Of ongoing phase I/II clinical trials in pediatric patients, one is investigating elimusertib as monotherapy in ARMS (NCT05071209), and another is studying prexasertib in combination with irinotecan (NCT04095221). A recent update from the trial investigating prexasertib with irinotecan reported two patients with RMS enrolled in the trial, of which both had stable disease for six months on the recommended phase II dose (Slotkin et al., 2022). Fusion status was not indicated in the report.

ATM is primarily involved in the response to DNA double-strand breaks (Karnitz & Zou, 2015). ATR inhibitors in tumor cells with low or mutated ATM have shown a similar ability to elicit cell death. Numerous adult clinical trials have investigated ATR inhibition in cancers with ATM mutations as monotherapy or combined with chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and/or radiotherapy (Yano & Shiotani, 2023). Most trials (albeit phase I) have found that cancers harboring ATM mutations are sensitive to ATR inhibitors. ATM mutations in RMS are rare but have been identified, and appear to be more prominent in ERMS (Kim, Light, et al., 2021b;Shern et al., 2014).

While pediatric patients with RMS have been included in some ATR inhibitor trials, given the rarity there is understandably no data on therapy directed specifically towards RMS tumors harboring ATM mutations. The AcSé-ESMART trial (Geoerger et al., 2023) investigated combined PARP and ATR inhibition through combination olaparib plus ceralasertib in children with advanced malignancies (Gatz et al., 2023), though the current reporting from this trial does not indicate any RMS patients were included in this arm. A separate arm of this same trial investigated the use of carboplatin combined with the WEE1 inhibitor adavosertib, which impairs the G2/M DNA damage checkpoint. Eligibility included patients whose tumors had mutations impairing DNA damage repair or homologous recombination; four patients with FP-RMS were treated on this arm, all of whom developed progressive disease within two cycles (Gatz et al., 2024).

The developmental pathways serve a large role in regulating cellular growth and differentiation even after embryo patterning. It is no surprise that cancer cells may attempt to hijack these pathways through overexpression, inhibition, or molecular aberrations to promote tumorigenesis. The Wnt, Notch, Hippo, Hedgehog, and TGF-β pathways are all implicated in varying degrees to the development and progression of FP- and FN-RMS (Belyea et al., 2012;Gustafson et al., 2024;Roma et al., 2012). However, little progress has been made in finding ways to target and disrupt these mechanisms. One reason is that targeted agents against these pathways are still early in development. Another reason is that crosstalk exists between pathways (Roma et al., 2012;Slemmons et al., 2017), which could provide an avenue for cancer cells to escape therapeutic sensitivity. This could potentially be combated through combination therapy, but further investigation is needed.

The Wnt pathway is fundamental to the proliferation and differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells, including normal growth, differentiation, and regeneration of skeletal muscle. The canonical and non-canonical pathways are both implicated in the proliferation of RMS (Chen, 2016;Hayes et al., 2018). Many cancers possess aberrant activation of the Wnt pathway through mutations in β-catenin, however mutations in β-catenin in RMS are uncommon and have thus far been found only in FN-RMS (Shern et al., 2021). The postulated mechanisms for Wnt pathway activation in RMS include autocrine loop activation, fusion proteins, or other genomic alterations. Upregulation of Wnt pathway modulators, such as secreted frizzled related protein 3 (SFRP3), have been found in FP-RMS (Kephart et al., 2015). This is of particular importance when considering viable options for targeted therapy. Many ongoing clinical trials involve drugs targeting different components of the Wnt pathway (Martinez-Font et al., 2021;Park & Kim, 2023), however no current trials include patients with RMS.

The Notch pathway helps regulate cell fate determination and stem cell proliferation during tissue development and maintenance. Abnormal Notch upregulation has been found in many types of cancers, and it is widely and consistently activated in both FP- and FN-RMS (Conti et al., 2015;Roma et al., 2012). Numerous gamma-secretase/Notch signaling pathway inhibitors have been tested clinically and have yielded mixed results (Azaro et al., 2021;Massard et al., 2022;McCaw et al., 2021). Perhaps the most exciting of these agents is nirogacestat, which was FDA approved in 2023 for adults with desmoid tumors who require systemic treatment (Gounder et al., 2023). A clinical trial in pediatric patients with unresectable desmoid tumors is underway (NCT04195399). Based on the results and tolerability, use in other pediatric tumors with Notch upregulation such as FP-RMS could be considered.

The Hippo pathway is another critical developmental pathway that helps regulate cell proliferation, survival, and stemness and additionally possesses tumor-suppressive properties. When turned on, the Hippo pathway inhibits the transcriptional coactivators YAP1 and WWTR1 (TAZ) from entering the nucleus and binding with transcription factors, most notably TEAD family members. This, in turn, inhibits processes like cell proliferation, differentiation, self-renewal, and stemness (Deel et al., 2015). PAX3::FOXO1 can turn off the Hippo pathway to promote proliferative and anti-apoptotic properties, thus enhancing tumorigenesis and malignant transformation (Crose et al., 2014;Oristian et al., 2018). FN-RMS has also been found to have downregulation of the Hippo pathway. (Slemmons et al., 2015;Slemmons et al., 2017;Tremblay et al., 2014). The Hippo pathway’s role in cancer is still a relatively new concept and many therapies have been developed but are still being studied preclinically (Cunningham & Hansen, 2022). The mechanism of these drugs exists in the form of direct targeting of the YAP1/TAZ-TEAD complex to impair binding and transcriptional activity and indirect targeting of other proteins associated with the pathway, such as G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) and NF2. One such example is in the drug VT3989, which functions by inhibiting TEAD palmitoylation and thus blocking YAP1 function. A phase I trial is currently underway for adults with malignant mesothelioma and other solid tumors possessing an NF2 mutation (NCT04665206). As more targeted agents are developed, testing these agents in FP-RMS should be explored.

The Hedgehog (Hh) pathway plays a major role during embryogenesis by influencing cell fate, including myogenesis. Examples of this include maintaining survival of primitive cells of the dermomyotome, inducing expression of the myogenic regulatory factors Myf5 and Myod1 to initiate differentiation, assisting in skeletal muscle growth, regulating cell migration of the distal limb muscle cells, and promoting myofiber regeneration during adult tissue repair (Zarzosa et al., 2023). There is a high level of Hh pathway activation in RMS. Its activation appears to be more prominent in FN tumors (Roma et al., 2012), and hyperactivation of the pathway has been correlated with a worse prognosis (Satheesha et al., 2016;Yoon et al., 2020). One study found a correlation between upregulation of the transcriptional regulator GLI1 and vincristine resistance in RMS and Ewing sarcoma (Yoon et al., 2020). Three FDA-approved Hh pathway inhibitors currently exist, all of which are SMO inhibitors that function to inhibit a GPCR responsible for Hh pathway activation. Vismodegib and sonidegib are FDA-approved for the treatment of adult basal cell carcinoma (BCC), and glasdegib is FDA-approved for the treatment of adult AML (Zarzosa et al., 2023). SMO inhibitors have been particularly effective for tumors associated with mutation-driven Hh pathway activation like BCC and medulloblastoma (MB). The same effectiveness has not been met in tumors associated with ligand-dependent Hh pathway activation (Berlin et al., 2013;De Jesus-Acosta et al., 2020;Kaye et al., 2012;Zarzosa et al., 2023). There is conflicting data about the prevalence of Hh mutations in RMS and other sarcomas, but overall, the majority of RMS tumors and other sarcomas appear to elicit Hh pathway hyperactivation in a ligand-dependent manner (Almazan-Moga et al., 2017;Bridge et al., 2002;Calzada-Wack et al., 2002;Pressey et al., 2011;Teot et al., 2018;Tostar et al., 2006). This raises the question of whether SMO inhibitors would be clinically effective in patients with RMS. Preclinical studies with SMO inhibitors have exhibited mixed results (Almazan-Moga et al., 2017;Ridzewski et al., 2015;Tostar et al., 2010), yet there are several clinical trials directing these agents against RMS (Zarzosa et al., 2023). A recent phase Ib/II trial combined vismodegib and a γ-secretase inhibitor in adults with RMS and other sarcomas. The phase II trial closed prematurely due to discontinuation of γ-secretase inhibitor development, but the analyzed results did not show the experimental drugs to significantly improve clinical efficacy (Gounder et al., 2022). A separate phase I/II trial was conducted with sonidegib in adult and pediatric patients with recurrent/refractory MB and other pediatric patients with tumors potentially dependent on Hh signaling (NCT01125800). While four pediatric patients with RMS participated in the study, none showed evidence of activity. Fusion status was not indicated in the study (Kieran et al., 2017). GLI inhibitors are starting to be developed and tested, but no results regarding their clinical impact in RMS are currently available (Zarzosa et al., 2023). Given the correlation between GLI1 upregulation and therapy resistance in RMS, GLI inhibitors could pose an intriguing treatment option for future trials.

TGF-β is a member of the transforming growth factor (TGF) family of proteins and serves many functions involving cell maintenance and differentiation. Under normal expression, it possesses tumor suppressor effects and promotes differentiation. When overexpressed, TGF-β inhibits myogenic differentiation and promotes tumorigenesis through epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Bhushan et al., 2024). Overexpression of TGF-β in RMS has been reported in the literature (Bhushan et al., 2024;Wang et al., 2010). Numerous clinical trials involving TGF-β pathway antagonists in recent years (Huang et al., 2021;Kim, Malek, et al., 2021a) have yielded inconsistent results (Teixeira et al., 2020). There does not appear to be any current clinical data involving TGF-β pathway antagonists in RMS, though additional investigation is warranted given the pathway’s implication in RMS tumors including FP-RMS.

While immunotherapies have revolutionized standard treatment approaches in diverse cancer types, these approaches have not yet found traction in treating FP-RMS. SeeFig. 3for a graphical summary of immunotherapeutic approaches being evaluated or consider for FP-RMS. An attractive starting point to target FP-RMS immunologically is the unique sequence of the fusion sites in thePAX3/7::FOXO1oncoprotein generating a tumor-cell specific antigen (Goletz et al., 1998). Some of the first efforts in this arena include a pilot study at the NIH treating relapsed Ewing sarcoma (EWS) and FP-RMS patients with fusion peptide pulsed irradiated pheresis product as a vaccine with IL-2 infusions (Dagher et al., 2002). No durable responses were seen in these patients with large, aggressive sarcomas. Subsequent efforts at the NIH greatly refined this approach in a clinical trial that enrolled 52 patients with EWS and FP-RMS who underwent pheresis prior to standard treatment, followed by a vaccine including autologous T cells andin vitrogenerated autologous dendritic cells pulsed with the fusion peptide present in their respective tumors (Mackall et al., 2008). This vaccine was given when patients had no detectable disease. Encouraging survival results were observed comparing the 32 patients who completed the vaccines with the 20 patients who were enrolled but unable to complete the vaccines. A fusion peptide directed immune response was present in 39 % of patients, though it was transient. Separate studies by this group did identify a PAX3::FOXO1 fusion peptide epitope presented by HLA*B:07 (van den Broeke et al., 2006), but other investigators could not generate PAX3::FOXO1 specific T cellsin vitrorestricted to HLA*A:01, A:02, or A:03 (Rodeberg et al., 2005). These studies provide foundational proof-of-principle that T cells can recognize epitopes from PAX3/7::FOXO1 fusion proteins, although this discovery has not yet translated into effective immune therapies for patients. It must be highlighted that the vaccine studies (Mackall et al., 2008) were performed in the era prior to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy (anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4, or anti-PD-L1), which is now known to optimize vaccine-induced responses. With the advent of ICI, novel vaccine platforms, and combination treatment approaches, fusion peptide vaccines certainly continue to warrant investigation such as an ongoing Germany-based vaccine clinical trial targeting sarcoma fusion oncoproteins including PAX3/7::FOXO1 (NCT06094101).

Immune checkpoint inhibitors blocking signaling through CTLA-4, and PD-1, and PD-L1 have greatly impacted the treatment of multiple adult malignancies and have become part of standard therapy in various diseases (Vaddepally et al., 2020). Although there have been no dedicated clinical trials restricted to patients with RMS or FP-RMS, pediatric and young adult RMS patients have been included in broader treatment cohorts. Fusion status of the RMS patients included in the trials is not always part of the publications, making it difficult to ascertain the effectiveness in the FP-RMS patients. In the five trials treating RMS patients with ICI (Nivolumab (anti-PD-1) alonen= 11 (Davis et al., 2020), Pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) alonen= 7 (Geoerger, Kang, et al., 2020a), Atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) alonen= 10 (Geoerger, Zwaan, et al., 2020b), Ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) alonen= 2 (Merchant et al., 2016), and Nivolumab + Ipilimumab n = 15 (Davis et al., 2022)), only one objective response was seen in the 45 RMS patients reported. This 25-year-old with FP-RMS experienced a partial response and remained on study therapy for 12 cycles, eventually discontinuing due to grade 3 amylase elevation and grade 4 lipase elevation and continued without disease progression at 36 months (Davis et al., 2022). Apart from this exceptional response, the overall activity of ICI in unselected RMS patients has been discouraging. Possible limiting factors for this strategy include prior therapy leaving limited immune fitness and an overall low burden of tumor mutations and neoantigens (Samstein et al., 2019;Shern et al., 2014). However, recent studies of the immune microenvironment in FP-RMS indicate a level of endogenous anti-tumor immune activation that needs further exploration. A cohort of 22 archived FP-RMS tumor biopsies revealed the presence of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) in 10 of the tumors, with the most common sites of tumor being head and neck (n= 4) and lymph nodes replaced by tumor (Chen et al., 2020). The presence of TLS (organized extra-nodal follicles containing T-, B-, and antigen presenting cells) is increasingly recognized as a positive prognostic sign in general, and portends responsiveness to ICI (Fridman et al., 2022). The PEMBROSARC trial validated this in adult sarcoma patients treated with anti-PD-1 pembrolizumab with immune-modulating low-dose cyclophosphamide and compared their “all-comer” sarcoma cohort versus a cohort of patients selected for the presence of TLS. The 6-month non-progression rate in the TLS+ cohort was 40 % (95 % confidence interval (CI), 22.7–59.4) with an overall response rate of 30 % (95 % CI, 14.7–49.4) compared to a no progression rate of 4.9 % (95 % CI, 0.6–16.5) and overall response rate of 2.4 % (95 % CI, 0.1–12.9) in the “all-comer” cohorts (Italiano et al., 2022). While no RMS patients were enrolled in this trial, it raises the question of potential responses to ICI in FP-RMS patients with TLS and what additional tumor antigens may be targets of the immune response.

Adoptive cellular therapy approaches seek to overcome the endogenous immune barriers discussed above byex vivogeneration and activation of tumor-reactive immune effector cells such as T-cells or NK cells that are then transferred back to the patient in large numbers. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells targeting the tumor-associated antigen HER2 was one of the first attempts in solid tumors and enrolled patients with RMS. While few clinical responses were observed, one patient with metastatic FN-RMS had a prolonged complete remission following CAR-T administration (Hegde et al., 2020), indicating the potential for effectiveness of this strategy. As the PAX3/7::FOXO1 fusion protein is a transcription factor, it cannot be directly targeted by a CAR construct that relies on robust cell surface expression. Thus, identifying cell-surface proteins with expression limited to tumor cells is the holy grail of CAR approaches. Recently, a CAR-T directed against the FGFR4 receptor tyrosine kinase has shown exciting results in pre-clinical models of RMS (Sullivan et al., 2022;Tian et al., 2023) and is being developed for upcoming clinical trials. A dual CAR-T targeting both the surface protein CD276 (B7-H3) (Lavoie et al., 2021) and FGFR4 has also shown efficacy in pre-clinical settings including FP-RMS (Timpanaro et al., 2023). Identification of these high value cell surface proteins for CAR-T therapies will also inform the development of antibody drug conjugates. For example, while B7-H3 is being exploited as described above in CAR-T approaches, it has also been shown to be a viable target in pre-clinical ADC studies of pediatric solid tumors including FP-RMS (Kendsersky et al., 2021).

Natural killer (NK) cells, which have an innate ability to identify and destroy tumor cells, can also be delivered via adoptive transfer or by engineering CAR-NK cells, along with various maneuvers to increase NK cell cytotoxic activity. Preclinical success shows that FP-RMS cells can be killed by NK cells (Heim et al., 2023), and an ongoing clinical trial including FP-RMS patients utilizes TGF-β resistant allo-NK cells given with chemotherapy (NCT05634369). A recent review cited completed or ongoing NK cell trials in pediatric sarcomas (Omer et al., 2021). Additional immune effector cell populations such asin vitro-activated cytokine-induced killer cells further broaden the repertoire that can be targeted against RMS (Moser et al., 2025). While successes with immunotherapy approaches have been few in FP-RMS patients to this point, further understanding of the mechanisms of immune cell interactions with FP-RMS remains crucial to inform future combination therapy trials with the potential for improved responses.

While there are many efforts to identify novel therapies based on mechanism, several groups have evaluated existing agents for repurposing, usually in small numbers of FP-RMS cell lines or ARMS/FP-RMS patient populations, and have nominated agents targeting the FGF7-FGFR2 axis (Milton et al., 2022), PI3K-AKT pathway (Piazzi et al., 2022), and general anti-cancer pathways such as metformin (Metts et al., 2023). Metformin was studied in a phase I trial in combination with vincristine, irinotecan and temozolamide (Metts et al., 2023), and had one partial response in an ARMS patient, but thePAX::FOXO1fusion status of this ARMS was not reported in this study. Going forward, it will be critical to obtain the fusion status of the RMS cell/tumor type being evaluated in order to assess therapeutic efficacy of any agent against FP-RMS.

Much of the focus over the past decades has been on developing therapies that interfere with targetable proteins that support FP-RMS biology, such as kinases and more recently epigenetic proteins with catalytic activity (Olanich & Barr, 2013;Wachtel & Schafer, 2018). However, there are now efforts to target the oncofusion itself, both at the transcript and the protein levels (Fig. 2).

At the transcript level, RNA-directed methods involving siRNA or anti-sense oligonucleotides (Watts & Corey, 2012) may induce the degradation of thePAX3::FOXO1transcript. RNA interference moved from a basic biology tool to the world of FDA-approved therapeutics in 2018 with the approval of patisiran (ONPATTRO™), an injectable lipid-RNAi complex for treatment of adult hereditary transthyretin-mediated (hATTR) amyloidosis (Adams et al., 2018). Since then, advances in design, delivery, and manufacturing have improved specificity, potency, and durability (Jadhav et al., 2024). Proof-of-concept for targeted delivery of siRNA to RMS cells includes a recent study involving siRNA silencing of the genesKRT17andCRYAB/HSPB2 (Duan & Townley, 2022). The team took advantage of high GSH (glutathione) levels in RMS cell lines, compared to fibroblasts, to develop nanocarriers involving mesoporous silica linked to siRNA via disulfide bonds. The delivered siRNA significantly suppressed cell viability. A similar delivery path may enable using siRNA-based agents against thePAX3:: FOXO1fusion transcript. Anti-sense oligonucleotides (ASOs) bind to sequences specific to the target RNA, resulting in perturbation of protein expression through a variety of molecular mechanisms (Collotta et al., 2023;Dhuri et al., 2020). Several ASOs have been approved by the FDA for therapeutic use, including various molecular targets and indications. A recent example of targeting an ‘undruggable’ transcription factor relevant to oncology via ASOs involves the prolific proto-oncogeneMYC (Gill et al., 2021). Ott and colleagues developed conformationally stabilized ASOs designed to targetMYCmRNA using a synthetic chemical library approach. Called MYCASOs, the team incorporated locked nucleic acid (LNA) bases at the 5′- and 3′-ends along with a phosphorothioate backbone and internal DNA bases to prepare 23 library members. Most of the MYCASOs decreased MYC protein expression at 10 nM after 24 h when delivered with a lipid-based formulation. The mechanism of MYC downregulation involved RNase H1-mediated cleavage ofMYCmRNA. The MYCASO approach led to inhibition of cell proliferation, perturbation of MYC-driven gene signatures, and a decrease in tumor burden for a MYC-induced murine model of hepatocellular carcinoma. Indeed, ASOs have been developed against bothFOXO1 (Samuel et al., 2006)to improve hepatic and peripheral insulin action andPAX3 (Zhang & Wang, 2007)to impact the expression of thePAX3:: FOXO1fusion. To develop clinically relevant ASOs for RMS, next-generation modified ASOs should be designed to optimize against nuclease degradation or immunogenicity and increase passive diffusion into cells (Dhuri et al., 2020). Selectivity for the fusion transcript overPAX3andFOXO1transcripts must be considered in the design process and downstream functional assays should be used to assess phenotypes relevant to both PAX3 and FOXO1. It should also be noted that both siRNA and ASO approaches will benefit from advances in therapeutic delivery (Guo et al., 2024;Kohashi et al., 2024).

At the protein level, the goal would be to degrade or otherwise inactivate the PAX3::FOXO1 protein. Since the PAX3::FOXO1 protein, like other oncofusion transcription factors, has no catalytic activity and is highly disordered, it has been a recalcitrant target (Henley & Koehler, 2021). However, with the development of chemical tools to design and modify bivalent chemical degraders such as proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), it may be possible to identify molecules that bind PAX3::FOXO1 protein that can be used as chemical handles to send them to the proteasome (Bekes et al., 2022), making use of ubiquitous E3 ligases or potentially with as-yet-to-be-discovered E3 ligases with appropriate tissue enhancement or specificity towards the fusion. Newer approaches such as covalent chemistry methods may similarly render inactive fusion oncoproteins if critical ligandable sites can be identified and modified (Boike et al., 2022).

Another exciting opportunity blends the advantages of peptide-based agents and targeted protein degradation to develop highly specific biodegraders (i.e., bioPROTACs or ubiquibodies) that consist of peptides that specifically engage a target fused to a minimal E3 ligase domain to induce degradation of the target (Stephens et al., 2021). Coupling peptide-design or discovery for PAX3::FOXO1 with delivery of the corresponding mRNA in lipid nanoparticles may offer a platform to deliver highly selective degraders towards the fusion. Finally, artificial intelligence tools such as protein language models can be used to generate PAX3::FOXO1-specific binders and develop more efficient bio-degraders against the target (Brixi et al., 2023).

While much is now understood about the molecular underpinnings of FP-RMS, this sarcoma remains one of the deadliest cancers of childhood and adolescence. Since the discovery of its driving fusion oncogenesPAX3::FOXO1andPAX7::FOXO1in the 1990s, basic, translational and clinical researchers have attempted to develop novel therapies based on the dysregulated signaling pathways and molecular biology of this cancer. This document reviews the history of these dogged but unsuccessful efforts, yet also offers the possibility that agents can be developed to specifically treat FP-RMS using novel approaches, including immunotherapy, RNA-directed therapies, and degrader therapies directed towards the oncofusions themselves.

There are several points that deserve discussion. First, there is the question of whether a narrow focus on FP-RMS is worthwhile, rather than a broader attack on RMS (FP-RMS and FN-RMS) that could treat all children with RMS based on their skeletal muscle histogenesis. Given that fusion oncogenes are the demonstrated drivers of FP-RMS, it is reasonable and logical to attack these entities, but it is also reasonable to continue the broad focus on RMS in general. Although FP-RMS patients are more likely to relapse, FN-RMS tumors are much more common, and trials for relapsed RMS often contain similar numbers of each group. The need for better therapies is too high to abandon either path. Second, even if novel approaches such as fusion protein-informed immunotherapies, fusion gene-directed RNA therapies, and/or fusion protein-directed degraders are successful, how to approach treatment of those children whose tumors express rare variant fusions beyond the typicalPAX3::FOXO1is important. Will specific agents need to be developed for each individual oncofusion, whose numbers are increasing monthly as new fusions are being detected? Or can therapeutic maneuvers be identified that attack the core biology of all FP-RMS, regardless of which oncofusion they bear? Third, there is evidence fromin vitromodeling that although FP-RMS are addicted to their oncogene activity, even those FP-RMS tumor cells who have the fusion oncofusion eradicated can recur and become oncofusion-independent (Pandey et al., 2017). Similarly, heterogeneity in the level of expression of the fusions may have different phenotypic consequences (Regina et al., 2021). Hence ASOs and degraders that are not fully delivered or not effective at inducing cell death could lead to recurrence. Whether or not this occurs, or could occur, in human patients has yet to be determined.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge that we don’t know what we don’t know. For example, while targeting RNA via anti-sense technologies is accelerating our understanding and implementation, the field of small molecule interference between RNA-protein interactions is still nascent. Similarly, our understanding of the role of the noncoding genome in FP-RMS is very much lagging compared to what is understood about the proteins encoded by the exome. There are technologies that have not even been discovered yet that may hold the key to treating FP-RMS. This underscores the importance of ascertaining fusion status in any basic science, translational, pre-clinical or clinical study evaluating novel anti-RMS agents.

Progress has been painfully slow in improving outcomes for FP-RMS, and to date, the use of additional cytotoxic chemotherapy agents has not changed the poor outcome for patients with metastatic disease. It is hoped that a better understanding of underlying biology and the effective leverage of new strategies for targeting RNA, proteins, and the immune system will finally result in meaningful advances for treating this aggressive childhood cancer.
