Fabry disease is a rare but life-threatening, X-linked, inherited lysosomal 
storage disorder in which globotriaosylceramide is insufficiently metabolized 
because of reduced α-galactosidase A activity. Cellular globotriaosylceramide 
accumulation causes a multisystemic disease, which, if left untreated, reduces 
life expectancy in female and male individuals by around 10 and 20 years, 
respectively, leading to progressive renal failure, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, 
cardiac arrhythmia, and premature cerebral infarction. The method of choice for 
confirming the diagnosis is the determination of reduced α-galactosidase A 
activity in leukocytes in male individuals and the molecular genetic detection 
of a disease-causing mutation in female individuals. Current approved treatment 
includes enzyme replacement therapy (agalsidase alfa [0.2 mg/kg body weight], 
agalsidase beta or pegunigalsidase alfa [both 1.0 mg/kg body weight]) every 
other week intravenously or, if a responding ('amenable') α-galactosidase A 
mutation is present, oral pharmacological chaperone therapy (migalastat 123 mg, 
every other day). Future therapeutic options may include substrate reduction 
therapy, gene therapy, messenger RNA therapy, and/or vesicle-packaged enzyme 
replacement therapy. This review presents current and future treatment options 
with advantages and disadvantages of the different treatment options.

Fabry disease is a lysosomal storage disease that is based on a deficiency of α-galactosidase A and, if left untreated as a multisystemic disease, reduces life expectancy in female and male individuals by around 10 and 20 years, respectively.The typical ‘red flags’ of the disease include neuropathic pain of the extremities and gastrointestinal symptoms as well as hypo-/hyperhidrosis, grouped angiokeratoma, and a cornea verticillata, which already occur in childhood. As the disease progresses, Fabry nephropathy, cardiomyopathy, and strokes can develop.The current therapy consists of the use of enzyme replacement therapy (agalsidase alfa, agalsidase beta, pegunigalsidase alfa, every 2 weeks intravenously) or chaperone therapy (migalastat, one capsule orally every second day; if the mutation is amenable).Current research focuses on potential future therapeutic options, which may include substrate reduction therapy, gene therapy, messenger RNA therapy, and/or vesicle-packaged enzyme replacement therapy.

Fabry disease (FD) is an X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by a deficiency in α-galactosidase A (AGAL/GLA) activity resulting in a progressive life-threatening multisystemic disease [1]. As a lysosomal storage disease, FD belongs to the sphingolipidoses. The lysosomal enzymatic AGAL deficiency leads to insufficient degradation of the substrate globotriaosylceramide (Gb3). Progressive cellular (mainly lysosomal) Gb3 accumulation causes a multisystem disease affecting vital organs by activation of proinflammatory and profibrotic signaling pathways, among others. Fabry disease affects male and female individuals of all ethnic groups with an incidence of 1:40,000 to 1:117,000, with newborn screenings revealing a higher incidence of 1:3100 [2]. While the lower reported historic incidence is mainly based on classic FD-causing GLA mutations, the newborn screenings also identified late-onset variants with an attenuated clinical phenotype. A higher frequency of late-onset variants (1/5732) was also demonstrated by a recent study in an unselected population in the UK [3].

Almost all cells of the human body can show a pathogenic Gb3 accumulation. Typical signs and symptoms of FD (“red flags”) include angiokeratoma (periumbilical, flank, back, and genital area), cornea verticillata, tinnitus, FD-specific neuropathic pain (burning pain in the palm of the hands and the soles of the feet and a severe FD pain crisis), hypohidrosis or hyperhidrosis, and gastrointestinal symptoms (including bloating, diarrhea, and/or abdominal pain) [1]. Multiple organs or single organ systems such as the kidneys, heart, and blood vessels as well as the central and peripheral nervous system can be affected and develop progressive functional disorders over time (Fig. 1). The number and dimension of affected organ systems and the disease burden increase with age (Fig. 1) [1, 4]. The extent of AGAL deficiency caused by the mutation determines the severity of the clinical phenotype. Thus, patients can be separated into classic and late-onset phenotypes. The classic phenotype is characterized by absent or very low AGAL activity (< 2% of the normal) [6] with significantly elevated globotriaosylsphingosine (lyso-Gb3) levels, symptoms, and organ manifestations already occurring in childhood and a severe disease burden. The late-onset phenotype shows a moderately decreased AGAL activity (2–20% of normal) [6], moderately elevated lyso-Gb3 levels, and symptoms and manifestations in the third to fourth decade of life with predominant cardiac involvement.

The presence of FD is suspected on the basis of a family history and/or evidence of the typical manifestations described above (Fig. 1). In male patients, the determination of AGAL activity in leukocytes (blood) is the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis. Pathologically deficient enzymatic AGAL activity indicates the presence of FD [7]. Because female patients with FD often present with normal enzymatic AGAL activity, a molecular genetic analysis with detection of a disease-causing mutation within the GLA gene is mandatory to confirm the diagnosis in female individuals (EDTA blood or dried blood spot card). The detection of genetic variants allows a classification of their pathogenic significance into five categories (“pathogenic,” “probably pathogenic,” “unclear significance,” “probably benign,” and “benign” [8]). In addition, as GLA genotyping is important to assess the possible response to pharmacological chaperone therapy [7], genotyping should also be performed in male patients. As a specific biomarker (marker of disease burden), a pathologically elevated lyso-Gb3 can contribute to improved diagnosis and monitoring. In the case of kidney or heart biopsies, multi-lamellar myelin bodies (‘zebra bodies,’ ‘paper roll phenomenon’) detectable by electron microscopy can help to confirm the diagnosis.

As part of multidisciplinary care, the following treatment goals should be achieved: (1) a reduction in symptoms (especially pain reduction); (2) delaying/preventing the progression of organ manifestations (especially in the kidneys, heart, and central nervous system); (3) improving quality of life; and (4) normalization of life expectancy [7, 9]. The initiation of FD-specific therapy is based on the patient’s individual risk in the sense of precision medicine, i.e., medical treatment is adapted to the individual characteristics of each patient. Furthermore, precision medicine is based on the classification of patients with different risk profiles. For example, affected male individuals have a higher risk of a more severe disease course than female individuals. Thus, the current treatment recommendations suggest that affected male individuals should be treated prophylactically and female individuals only if there are signs of incipient organ damage typical for FD [7, 10]. However, this approach is controversial, particularly for female individuals, as early initiation of therapy also appears to make clinical sense in female individuals with a classic phenotype before irreversible end organ damage has occurred [11]. Hence, an early initiation of therapy is particularly necessary in male individuals with significantly reduced enzymatic AGAL activity, elevated lyso-Gb3, and FD-typical organ manifestations. Female individuals with late-onset mutations can manage for many years without FD-specific therapy if the course of the disease is stable, thus avoiding possible side effects and saving therapy costs. Patients with benign GLA variants should not be treated with FD-specific therapies.

Since 2001, ERT is available as a causal treatment for FD [1]. The recombinant AGAL enzyme is infused every other week (Fig. 2). Currently, three preparations in different dosages are approved for a lifelong therapy every other week. Agalsidase alfa (Takeda, Tokyo, Japan) is produced in a human cell line (human fibrosarcoma cells HT-1080) and infused in an approved dosage of 0.2 mg/kg body weight in ~ 40 min. Agalsidase beta (Sanofi Genzyme, Cambridge, MA, USA) is produced in Chinese hamster ovary cells and infused in a recommended dose of 1.0 mg/kg body weight in ~ 280 min (at approximately 70 kg body weight). According to the current literature, the infusion time can usually be gradually reduced to 90 min if well tolerated [12]. The efficacies of agalsidase alfa and beta were repeatedly demonstrated and are reviewed elsewhere [13–15]. If therapy is initiated early before irreversible end-organ damage occurs, the following therapeutic effects can be achieved: stabilization of renal function, delay of progression to end-stage renal failure, stabilization of cardiac wall thickness, a reduction in left ventricular hypertrophy, relief of neuropathic pain, a reduction in gastrointestinal symptoms, and an improvement of the ability to sweat and of quality of life [16–18]. However, affected female individuals often remain untreated despite organ manifestations [19–21], although early initiation of therapy is particularly important for the (long-term) success of treatment [22]. Of note, especially in children with Fabry nephropathy and significant Gb3 accumulation in podocytes, a dose-dependent “clearance” effect was demonstrated [23–28].

Furthermore, there is currently ongoing research to establish biosimilars to agalsidase beta. An agalsidase beta biosimilar (JR-051; JCR Pharmaceuticals and Sumitomo Dainippon Pharma, Osaka, Japan) is approved in Japan since 2018. Preclinical studies in cell culture, rats, non-human primates, and a mouse model of FD showed that JR-051 and agalsidase beta had comparable pharmacokinetic properties [29, 30]. Both products are distributed in the body, metabolized, and excreted in a comparable manner [29, 30]. In addition, the substrate reduction (Gb3) was similar. The clinical phase I and phase II/III (JapicCTI-152854) studies demonstrated the safety and bioequivalence of JR-051 to be comparable to agalsidase beta in terms of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (both 1 mg/kg body weight, intravenously every other week) [31].

Another biosimilar is currently being developed in Argentina (AGABIO; Biosidus, Buenos Aires, Argentina, NCT05843916). Cultured Fabry fibroblasts showed comparable dose–response curves for AGABIO and agalsidase beta and in AGAL-knockout mice and rats, both enzymes were rapidly cleared from the plasma and showed equal reductions in tissue and plasma sphingolipids [32]. In a subsequent phase I study, no differences were detected in adverse effects or antibody induction, indicating that AGABIO meets the criteria for being considered similar to the reference formulation agalsidase beta [33].

As a third and newest drug, pegunigalsidase alfa (Chiesi, Parma, Italy) is a recently approved PEGylated (PEG: polyethylene glycol) covalently cross-linked form of AGAL produced in Tobacco protoplasts. The PEGylation is expected to increase the circulating plasma half-life (80 h vs 1–2 h) and reduce immunogenicity, which could improve efficacy compared with previously available agents and might allow an extension of the infusion interval (monthly intravenous administration) [34–37]. Pegunigalsidase alfa is approved since 2023 (US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency) for adult patients (≥ 18 years of age) at a dosage of 1.0 mg/kg body weight every other week with an initial infusion time of at least 3 h for the treatment of FD. If well tolerated, the infusion time can be gradually reduced to 90 min.

The therapeutic efficacy of pegunigalsidase alfa was analyzed in three different phase III studies to date (BALANCE, BRIDGE, BRIGHT). In the BALANCE study (head-to-head study), the non-inferiority of pegunigalsidase alfa compared to agalsidase beta after 24 months of treatment was reported (both 1.0 mg/kg body weight, every other week) [38]. Of 127 patients screened, 78 met the inclusion criteria and were randomly (2:1) assigned to pegunigalsidase alfa (n = 52 patients) or agalsidase beta (n = 25) [38]. In detail, median (95% confidence interval [CI]) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) slopes after 24 months of treatment were − 2.51 (− 3.79 to − 1.24) mL/min/1.73 m2/year with pegunigalsidase alfa and − 2.16 (− 3.81 to − 0.51) mL/min/1.73 m2/year with agalsidase beta. The difference in median eGFR slope for the intent-to-treat population between arms was − 0.36 mL/min/1.73 m2/year (95% CI − 2.44 to 1.73). As the lower limit of the CI was above the prespecified non-inferiority margin of − 3.0 mL/min/1.73 m2/year difference between the pegunigalsidase alfa and agalsidase beta arm, the non-inferiority was achieved and pegunigalsidase alfa was approved [38]. However, because of the X-linked inheritance, affected female and male individuals with FD should be analyzed separately. In the BALANCE study, the male individuals in particular presented with different outcomes dependent on the treatment [38]. While male individuals receiving agalsidase beta presented with a slight loss of eGFR (− 2.01 mL/min/1.73 m2/year [95% CI − 3.98 to − 0.04]), male individuals treated with pegunigalsidase alfa presented with a more pronounced loss of eGFR by − 3.44 mL/min/1.73 m2/year [95% CI − 5.38 to − 1.50]), resulting in a difference of − 1.43 mL/min/1.73 m2/year [95% CI − 3.96 to 1.10] [38]. In the overall population, median plasma lyso-Gb3 remained relatively stable in each arm with a < 2 nM change from baseline to 24 months (1.15 nM for pegunigalsidase alfa and − 1.50 nM for agalsidase beta). At 24 months, the median (range) plasma lyso-Gb3 change from baseline in male individuals was 5.30 nM (− 32.2 to 32.7) with pegunigalsidase alfa and − 2.40 nM (− 102.3 to 2.4) with agalsidase beta (p = 0.0001), demonstrating a more pronounced plasma lyso-Gb3 decrease in male individuals treated with agalsidase beta [38]. In female individuals, the change was minimal: 0.10 (− 4.0 to 5.8) with pegunigalsidase alfa and − 0.30 nM (− 0.7 to 0.9) with agalsidase beta (p = 0.54) [38].

Future studies are now warranted to compare the treatment effects of pegunigalsidase alfa with agalsidase beta in male patients in more detail. In the BRIDGE study (switch-over study), the safety and efficacy in patients with FD receiving pegunigalsidase alfa (1.0 mg/kg body weight, every other week), previously treated with agalsidase alfa (0.2 mg/kg body weight, every other week) were reported [39]. Of 27 screened patients, 22 met the eligibility criteria and 20 patients (13 male, 7 female) completed the study. Pegunigalsidase alfa was well tolerated and only five patients (23.0%) reported infusion-related reactions. Overall, the annualized slope of the eGFR changed from − 5.90 mL/min/1.73 m2/year with agalsidase alfa to − 1.19 mL/min/1.73 m2/year with pegunigalsidase alfa. As ‘historical’ eGFR measurements, which were collected about 2 years before the start of the study, were used to calculate the annualized eGFR slope with agalsidase alfa, the treatment effect of switching from agalsidase alfa to pegunigalsidase alfa has to be interpreted with caution. As a biochemical response, treatment with pegunigalsidase alfa led to a mean plasma lyso-Gb3 reduction of 31.5%. This effect could be due to the dose increase from 0.2 to 1.0 mg/kg body weight, as the dose change from agalsidase alfa to agalsidase beta leads to comparable lyso-Gb3 results [27]. BRIGHT was an open-label switch-over study in adults with FD, previously treated with agalsidase alfa or beta every other week for ≥ 3 years, who switched to pegunigalsidase alfa 2 mg/kg every 4 weeks for 52 weeks [36]. This (double) dosage with an extended infusion interval is not yet approved, but is of interest to patients to improve their quality of life. The primary objective assessed the safety, including the frequencies of treatment-emergent adverse events (AEs). In total, 30 patients were enrolled (24 male) and 23 previously received agalsidase beta. Thirty-three of 182 treatment-emergent AEs (in nine patients) were considered treatment related, but all were rated as mild/moderate. Overall, the median eGFR slope was − 1.9 (− 8.3; 1.9) mL/min/1.73 m2/year. Interestingly, a significant influence of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) on eGFR was observed (ADA negative [n = 20]: − 1.2 [− 6.4; 2.6] mL/min/1.73 m2/year; ADA positive [n = 9]: − 8.4 [− 11.6; − 1.0] mL/min/1.73 m2/year) [36].

In general, it can be assumed that ADA-positive patients experience a more rapid disease progression because of limited treatment efficacy caused by ADAs and usually severe phenotypes based on nonsense AGAL mutations. Estimated GFR values at the switch were better in ADA-positive patients, suggesting that at least in the BRIGHT study, the ADA-positive male patients were not clinically more severely affected than ADA-negative male patients [36]. In addition, the pre-switch data demonstrated comparable eGFR slopes with agalsidase beta for ADA-negative and ADA-positive patients [36]. Furthermore, in patients with pre-existing ADAs, the pharmacokinetics (including circulating half-life, maximum concentration, and area under the curve) of pegunigalsidase alfa after infusion was significantly reduced and did not improve over 52 weeks of treatment [36].

Thus, it can be concluded that the different eGFR slopes between ADA-negative and ADA-positive patients within the BRIGHT study are not only based on the phenotype, but at least to some extend due to the therapy-reducing effect of pre-existing ADAs against the infused enzyme [40]. No de novo ADA formation against pegunigalsidase alfa was observed and for three ADA-positive patients, a seroconversion at the end of the study without detectable (free) antibodies was reported. However, these data should be interpreted carefully. Because of the long circulating half-life of pegunigalsidase alfa, the circulating ADAs might be bound and thus no longer measurable by standard techniques such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays [41, 42]. Despite these technical problems, future studies are now warranted to analyze the effect of ADAs on the treatment efficacy of pegunigalsidase alfa in affected patients.

Since May 2016, the pharmacological chaperone migalastat (Galafold; 123-mg hard capsules, every other day; Amicus Therapeutics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) is approved as the first oral therapy for FD (Fig. 2). For incorrectly folded AGAL protein variants, the addition of migalastat under cell culture conditions (HEK293T cells) can increase endogenous enzymatic activity [43]. This effect is based on a reversible binding of migalastat to the active center of the enzyme, followed by a proper translocation to the lysosomes due to the resulting stabilization. After uptake of the pharmacological chaperone-enzyme complex into the lysosomes, the small molecule dissociates owing to the lower pH. Because of the more effective folding and transport, a higher lysosomal enzymatic AGAL activity is achieved, resulting in an improved lysosomal Gb3 degradation. The AGAL variants showing increased enzyme activity after administration of migalastat are termed “amenable” variants [43]. In good laboratory practice (GLP) HEK assay, HEK293T cells are transfected with the AGAL mutation of interest and after incubation with migalastat, the enzymatic AGAL activities are measured. Mutations are termed “amenable” if a ≥ 1.2-fold increase over baseline and an absolute increase of ≥ 3% wild-type activity is achieved [43]. The small molecule is intended for the long-term treatment of adults and adolescents aged ≥ 12 years and a ≥ 45-kg body weight with a confirmed diagnosis of FD and an amenable mutation. α-Galactosidase A mutations that respond to migalastat (1388 ‘amenable’ mutations) are listed at www.galafoldamenabilitytable.com (last update: 29 May, 2024).

Recent long-term data (≤ 8.6 years) demonstrated that the renal function of patients with FD was maintained during treatment with migalastat regardless of the previous treatment status (ERT naïve or ERT pretreated) and sex [44]. In addition, the overall incidence of FD-associated clinical events in patients receiving long-term treatment (up to 8.6 years) with migalastat compared favorably with historical reports on ERT [45]. As described above, because of its mode of action, migalastat is only suitable for patients with amenable mutations. These are usually missense mutations with a certain residual enzymatic activity. Naturally, these patients are not as severely affected as patients with nonsense/null mutations without any endogenous AGAL activity, for whom only ERT is thus a current treatment option. Comparisons of treatment efficacies between treatment options (i.e., ERT vs pharmacological chaperone therapy) must therefore be interpreted with caution, as the treated groups are often clinically different.

As migalastat is mainly cleared by the kidneys, migalastat is not recommended in patients who have an eGFR < 30 mL/min per 1.73 m2 to avoid an accumulation. In this respect, a recent study evaluated the pharmacokinetics, dialyzability, and tolerability of migalastat 123 mg in non-FD subjects with end-stage renal disease on stable hemodialysis/hemodiafiltration [46]. According to their investigations, the authors conclude that migalastat 123 mg every other week might be the proposed dose regimen for further evaluation in patients with FD with end-stage renal disease [46]. However, appropriate studies need to demonstrate that this treatment interval is really effective and increases endogenous AGAL activities significantly over time.

While ERT replaces the missing or defective enzyme by infusing a genetically modified enzyme, the aim of substrate reduction therapy is a reduction in the AGAL substrate with a consecutive reduction in cellular Gb3 accumulation (Fig. 2; Table 1). Three different preparations are currently in the approval process. Lucerastat (oral, 2 times 1 g/day; Idorsia, Allschwil, Switzerland) is a low-molecular-weight iminosugar inhibiting glucosylceramide synthase (GCS) and the biosynthesis of glycosphingolipids, including downstream Gb3, offering a new oral treatment alternative [47, 48]. A pivotal phase III clinical trial (MODIFY [NCT03425539]) is ongoing since 2018 [49]. Even though lucerastat did not achieve the primary endpoint (reduction in neuropathic pain) during 6 months of treatment compared to placebo, a significant reduction in plasma lyso-Gb3 concentrations was shown. Venglustat (ceramide analog, oral, 15 mg/day; Sanofi Genzyme) is another GCS inhibitor being investigated as a central nervous system-penetrating small molecule as a potential therapy for several lysosomal storage diseases (FD, Gaucher disease, Tay–Sachs disease, and Sandhoff disease). The authors concluded that a favorable safety and tolerability profile was demonstrated in healthy volunteers and patients with FD [50, 51]. However, two patients experienced severe psychiatric treatment-emergent AEs leading to permanent treatment discontinuation [51]. Consequently, two phase III trials are currently being conducted: CARAT (NCT05280548; primary endpoint: left ventricular mass index by magnetic resonance imaging) and PERIDOT (NCT05206773; neuropathic and abdominal pain).

The third compound AL01211 (AceLink Therapeutics, Newar, CA, USA) is also a small molecule and currently within an active phase II trial (NCT06114329). To the best of our knowledge, the chemical structure has not yet been published. AL01211 is a novel, proprietary selective GCS inhibitor with high potency against GCS with limited off-target activity. An open-label study evaluated the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and preliminary efficacy of AL01211 in male subjects with classic FD naïve to any FD-specific treatment (ERT or pharmacological chaperone therapy). AL01211 was generally well tolerated without AL01211-associated serious AEs, thus supporting its further clinical development [52, 53]. The phase II study is located in China only.

Gene therapy is a promising future therapeutic option. By introducing DNA or RNA with the genetic code for the GLA gene into patients’ cells, the causal chain would be reached earlier compared with other treatment options (Fig. 2). For the transduction, currently two different approaches are used including lentiviral and adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based vectors. Lentiviral vectors offer both in vivo and ex vivo application. Common target cells are non-dividing T cells or hematopoietic stem cells (e.g., CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells). In the ex vivo approach, these cells are first mobilized and then extracted from the patient, cultured in vitro, and lentivirally transduced before being re-implanted into the patient. The DNA synthesized by reverse transcriptase from the viral genome can integrate into the host genome, followed by the transgenic protein expression in target cells [54].

The first and so far only lentiviral-based approach for FD was based on ex vivo transduction of hematopoietic stem cells and subsequent re-implantation of transduced cells (AVR-RD-01; AvroBio, Cambridge, MA, USA, Table 2). In the first phase I study (NCT02800070), the safety and tolerability in male adult patients (aged 18–50 years) with classic FD (no or very low AGAL activity) were assessed. Further inclusion criteria were receiving ERT prior to enrollment, no AGAL antibodies above a threshold (five-fold above normal values), and an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. For stem cell mobilization, patients either received filgrastim alone (16 µg/kg body weight) [n = 2] or in combination with plerixafor (240 µg/kg body weight) [n = 3]. One day after conditioning with a non-myeloablative reduced dose of melphalan (100 mg/m2), re-implantation of autologous transduced cells was performed [55]. The authors decided not to choose a more myelosuppressive conditioning protocol, which might result in a better engraftment of transduced cells, as such protocols are more invasive, are accompanied by more AEs, and have the potential for future oncogenic events [56]. Another pitfall is that lentiviral gene therapy can trigger T-cell leukemia and leukemogenesis, depending on the integration site of the transgene [57–59]. However, for the first trial of lentiviral-mediated gene therapy for FD, Saleh et al. [60] demonstrated that the treatment did not lead to hematopoietic clonal dominance and the authors conclude that the risk of leukemogenic transformation was not increased. Three from five patients were able to discontinue ERT during the study period [55]. The enzymatic AGAL activity in plasma and leukocytes increased strongly after re-implantation but decreased within the following. However, after 5 years, the values were still above the individual baseline values (before gene therapy), but plasma AGAL activities in 4/5 patients and leukocyte activity in 3/5 patients were below the reference values, and thus too low [55]. In the following phase I/II study (NCT03454893), male patients over 16 years of age with a classic FD phenotype (< 1% normal wild-type AGAL activity), ERT naïve and migalastat naïve (3 years prior to treatment initiation), and an eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 were included. Nine patients were treated and no AEs related to the drug were reported [61]. Four of 9 patients tested positive for anti-AGAL antibodies after an AVR-RD-01 infusion. No replication competent lentivirus or aberrant clonal expansion was detected. An increase in AGAL activity in plasma and leukocytes and a decrease in Gb3 in plasma and urine after infusion were detected (mean values). In two patients with available renal biopsies, an 87% and 100% reduction of peritubular capillary Gb3 inclusions in the kidney was observed [61]. Although promising at the beginning, the phase I/II trial was terminated prematurely by Avrobio because of increasing challenges in the market and regulatory environment for FD accompanied by recent clinical trial data showing variable responses among patients.

Adeno-associated virus-based vectors are considered one of the most promising approaches in gene therapy. They are particularly suitable for the transfer of small genes, typically less than 5 kb (including the flanking Inverted Terminal Repeat [ITR]) [62]. This ITR-flanked DNA is packaged into an expression cassette, which then enters the cell nucleus where it might be present as episomal DNA. Various target organs can be selected, with the liver often being a preferred target organ [62]. Compared with other vectors, such as adenoviruses, AAVs exhibit lower immunogenicity, although an immune response against the capsid protein or the delivered DNA is possible and needs to be controlled [62].

As AAV persist in the infected individual for life after infection, it was previously assumed that a gene transfer with AAV vectors is well tolerated in humans without causing immune reactions or severe AEs. However, first clinical trials for hemophilia B with AAV vectors demonstrated AAV capsid-specific T-cell responses resulting in transaminitis and the loss of transgene expression [63, 64]. The T-cell-mediated liver damage can be reduced with appropriate immunosuppression [65]. In addition, the risk of an immune response has also been shown to be related to the vector dosages [65]. Even under immunosuppression, high vector dosages can lead to serious and even fatal AEs, including hepatitis, myocarditis, and thrombotic microangiopathy [65]. Furthermore, neurological damage including the loss of dorsal root ganglia and cerebral T2 hyperintensities was reported [65]. In addition to the direct T-cell-mediated immune response, AAV transduction (either due to gene therapy or a natural AAV infection) can lead to the formation of virus-specific antibodies, which can trigger further inflammatory processes and/or prevent a successful therapy [65]. Thus, patients need to be tested for pre-existing AAV neutralizing antibodies before receiving AAV-based gene therapies. Current research addresses the effectivity of plasmapheresis (with or without immune adsorption) [66, 67], in vivo IgG destruction (imlifidase) [68], proteasome inhibition (bortezomib) [69, 70], and B-cell depletion (rituximab) [71] on initial or repeated AAV transduction. Additional approaches include the manufacturing of highly efficient viruses and more efficient packaging, requiring lower virus dosages, or the use of other AAV variants for sequential transduction [72].

In recent years, four different approaches were conducted using AAV-based vectors for FD (Table 2).

(1) The STAAR study (isaralgagene civaparvovec [ST-920-201]; Sangamo; NCT04046224) utilized AAV2/6-based vectors, targeting the liver (Table 2). The study was conducted in two parts (dose escalation and subsequent dose expansion with five groups [group 1: anti-AGAL antibody positive, group 2: anti-AGAL antibody negative, group 3: female, group 4: renal cohort and group 5: cardiac cohort]) with treatment-emergent AEs and severe AEs as primary endpoints. Adult patients with FD and at least one of the following symptoms: cornea verticillata, acroparesthesia, anhidrosis, or angiokeratoma were included. Patients with pre-existing neutralizing antibodies against AAV6 and/or an eGFR < 40 mL/min/1.73 m2 were excluded. Additional inclusion criteria for the renal cohort were an eGFR between 40 and 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 and a linear negative eGFR slope (≥ 2 mL/min/1.73 m2/year), but no dialysis and transplantation or acute renal failure 6 months prior to enrollment. For the cardiac cohort, inclusion criteria were left ventricular hypertrophy or cardiac changes indicating disease progression. In the initial dose-escalation phase, nine patients were treated with four different dose levels (0.5e13 vg/kg; 1.0e13 vg/kg; 3.0e13 vg/kg; 5.0e13 vg/kg) [73]. In the subsequent dose expansion phase, 11 patients were treated with the highest dose (5.0e13 vg/kg) [NCT04046224] [74]. Supraphysiologic AGAL activities and a decrease of plasma lyso-Gb3 were measured in almost all patients with a follow-up period of at least 6 months. 10/10 patients were able to discontinue ERT. Anti-AGAL antibodies decreased significantly in seven previously positive patients, in five to the undetectable limit. Of note, comparable to patients receiving pegunigalsidase alfa, the continuous expression of AGAL might bind the free antibodies, leading to false-negative measured antibody titers [41]. Thus, without appropriate assays excluding the presence of AGAL/ADA-complexes in patients’ sera, these data also need to be interpreted with caution. An improvement of SF-36 and FOS-MSSI scores was also observed [74]. After one year of follow-up, a long-term observational study (NCT05039866) follows.

(2) Freeline therapeutics also started a dose-ranging phase I/II study, MARVEL1, to test the safety and tolerability of FLT190 (AAV, target organ liver) in adult men with classic FD (Table 1). FLT190 consists of a synthetic capsid (AAVS3) containing an expression cassette with codon-optimized human GLA complementary DNA under the control of a liver-specific promoter FRE1 (AAV2/S3-FRE1-GLAco) [75]. Three patients were included but the study was terminated prematurely in May 2023 (NCT04040049) to focus on a gene therapy for Gaucher disease.

(3) Another phase I/II study uses a specific AAV to target especially the heart (INGLAXA [4D-310]; 4D Molecular Therapeutics, Emeryville, CA, USA; Table 2). Six adult patients with FD (classic or late onset) were recruited and after 12 months, an improvement in the global longitudinal strain and peak peak oxygen consumption was demonstrated in two of three patients. Unfortunately, three of the six patients developed atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome after 3–7 days after treatment and the study was paused [76]. Nevertheless, the US Food and Drug Administration lifted the clinical hold on the phase I/II study in August 2024.

(4) UniQure’s phase I/II study of AMT191 (AAV5) is testing the safety and tolerance of the drug with treatment-emergent AEs and the duration of DNA production from the vector in body fluids as primary endpoints. Adult male individuals with classic FD (AGAL activity <1% and pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant), an eGFR of ≥ 40 mL/min/1.73 m2, a plasma lyso-Gb3 of ≥ 2.3 ng/mL, and additionally neuropathic pain and/or gastrointestinal symptoms are included. Participants are currently being recruited (NCT06270316). Thus, isaralgagene civaparvovec (Sangamo, Brisbane, CA, USA) is currently the most promising and advanced gene therapy and could be the first to be approved for FD.

Two new considerations for the potential treatment of FD include messenger RNA (mRNA) therapy and vesicle-packaged ERT. In systemic mRNA therapy, an mRNA encoding the protein of interest is packaged in lipid nanoparticles. In the target organism, all endogenous post-translational modifications such as protein folding, glycosylation, and trafficking are taking place.

DeRosa et al. [77] demonstrated that treatment with lipid nanoparticle-formulated mRNA led to successful expression of human AGAL in mice. In both wild type and AGAL-deficient mice, a maximum serum AGAL protein level was measured 6 h post-treatment (1 mg/kg), which is over 1300-fold above normal values (4 µg of AGAL/mL serum compared with approximately 3 ng of AGAL/mL serum). The protein could be detected in AGAL-deficient mice 1 week after treatment in the liver, spleen, heart, and kidney (by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) and showed increased levels compared with ERT-treated mice (e.g., kidney: 2.42 ng of hAGAL/mg total protein vs 0.37 ng hAGAL/mg of total protein; heart: 11.5 ng hAGAL/mg of total protein vs 1 ng of hAGAL/mg total protein). A (lyso-)Gb3 reduction was also measured. In non-human primates, a significant increase in serum AGAL was detected 24 h after treatment [77].

Zhu et al. [78] also conducted a study in wild-type- and AGAL-deficient mice and non-human primates. When wild-type mice were treated with hAGAL mRNA 0.5 mg/kg, an eight-fold higher activity was measured in the liver, spleen, and plasma and a two- to three-fold higher activity in the kidney and heart compared with the control group. In AGAL-deficient mice, AGAL activities increased dose dependently in the liver, heart, plasma, and kidney. Globotriaosylsphingosine levels in AGAL-deficient mice decreased by 50% (heart, kidney) to 80% (liver, spleen) 3 days after treatment with 0.5 mg/kg compared with untreated animals. After 35–42 days, a re-accumulation in the plasma and organs was observed [56].

Another independent group also showed a significant reduction in lyso-Gb3 in the plasma, heart, kidney, and liver and additionally a reduction in proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6, nuclear factor kappa-B, and tumor necrosis factor-α in AGAL-deficient mice [79]. A third approach focuses on the administration of ERT, aiming to increase the circulating half-life in plasma and improve the cellular uptake. The use of nanoliposomes as shuttles showed a reduction in Gb3 deposition in a cell model [80] and in AGAL-deficient mice (also in the brain) [81]. Furthermore, using ionically cross-linked polyelectrolyte complexes consisting of trimethylchitosan and AGAL, an increased uptake and efficacy was demonstrated [82]. Polystyrene capsules were also used but showed lower enzyme activity when encapsulated compared with the free enzyme under the selected conditions [83].

The use of natural extracellular vesicles (EVs) is another potential treatment approach. It is possible to isolate these vesicles from cells previously transfected with DNA and a rapid effective uptake and direct transfer to the lysosomes was shown in cell models [84]. In Chinese hamster ovary cells, a significantly increased enzyme activity was measured in cells treated with EVs compared with agalsidase alfa administration. α-Galactosidase A-deficient mice injected seven times with EVs showed no side effects. After systemic intravenous administration, a higher accumulation of EVs was observed in the liver and spleen and less in the kidney and lung. No accumulation was observed in the heart, brain, skin, and muscles. Intra-arterial administration led to detectability in the brain. After a single dose of EV-AGAL (1 mg/kg body weight), levels comparable to wild type were achieved in kidneys, which were approximately twice as much compared with agalsidase alfa [85].

Enzyme replacement therapy is based on the intravenous infusion of a recombinant protein. Consequently, there is a certain risk that the infused enzyme will be recognized as foreign and a corresponding immune response will be induced, resulting in the formation of antibodies against the enzyme [86]. Several studies in patients with lysosomal storage disorders including FD and Gaucher disease but also Pompe disease demonstrated that patients without any endogenous enzyme (termed “cross-reactive immunologic material” negative) have a high risk of developing an immune reaction after starting ERT [40, 87–89]. Because of the different effects on both the affected patient and the success of therapy, it is very important to distinguish between two different reactions caused by continuous exposure to ERT.

Infusion-associated reactions (IARs) occur most frequently in ERT-naïve patients with FD immediately after initialization of ERT. The affected patients are often CRIM-negative male patients without any endogenous AGAL activity and enzyme [90–92]. The reported symptoms are usually limited to fever and/or chills and require premedication with antihistamines and corticosteroids as well as a reduced infusion rate [91] (Fig. 3A). In addition to milder symptoms including urticarial skin lesions, facial flushing or swelling, headache and aching limbs, more severe reactions with chest and throat tightness, dyspnea, hypotension, and life-threatening anaphylactic reactions were described (although less frequently) [93–96] (Fig. 3A). According to the manufacturers, 24% of patients treated with agalsidase alfa and 67% of patients treated with agalsidase beta may experience these adverse reactions; in patients treated with pegunigalsidase alfa, the frequency is reported as 22%. According to the literature, IARs are mainly anaphylactoid (non-IgE-mediated) reactions, as only a few affected patients have been tested as IgE positive (anaphylactic reaction) to recombinant AGAL [95, 97–101]. The IgE-independent mechanisms leading to hypersensitivity reactions (anaphylactoid reactions) to the infused enzyme are not yet fully understood [102]. However, it is assumed that, for example, changes in osmotic pressure during the infusion could lead to a destabilization of mast cells and thus to a histamine release [103]. In addition, abnormal activation of the complement system may lead to type II hypersensitivity [95]. However, in a recent study, reduced C4 levels were found in only 25% of patients with IARs, suggesting that the primary cause of IARs are non-complement-mediated cytotoxic hypersensitivity reactions [95]. In this study, the authors also demonstrated that circulating dendritic cells were drastically reduced during IARs, suggesting that the cells may be deposited at the sites of inflammation [95]. An increase in natural killer cells and a decrease in T cells were also observed [95]. In addition, there was a significant increase in the cytokines interleukin-4, interleukin-8, and tumor necrosis factor-α, suggesting non-specific degranulation of mast cells and indicating an interaction of immune cells leading to IgE-independent, mast cell-specific allergic inflammation [95].

In addition to the general recommendations for the prevention of IARs [91], more specific protocols for the treatment of severe reactions were established in recent years. In patients with IgEs to agalsidase beta, a graded dosing and individualized infusion rate regimen over a period of 54 weeks (27 infusions) resulted in successful re-institution of agalsidase beta in five out of six patients [99]. In a more recent study, the tolerability of agalsidase beta was demonstrated in eight patients (only one IgE-positive patient and six patients with IgGs against AGAL) with a combination of premedications including corticosteroids, mast cell stabilizers, and H1 and H2 blockers [95].

For the successful re-institution of agalsidase alfa, an adapted three-bag protocol [104] based on a consecutive 1:10 dilution of agalsidase alfa 14 mg in 250 mL of normal saline (solution 3: agalsidase alfa 14 mg in 250 mL of saline; solution 2: 225 mL of saline and 25 mL of solution 3; solution 1: 225 mL of saline and 25 mL of solution 2) was reported as successful in two IgE-negative brothers [94]. Another successful protocol was published recently, following a 1-bag/4-step protocol with oral premedication (cetirizine 10 mg and famotidine 40 mg) administered 30 min before the procedure [105]. Within the four steps, the infusion rates were doubled every 15 min starting with 10 mL/hour (after step 1: 0.36 mg, step 2: 0.72 mg, step 3: 1.44 mg, step 4: 11.88 mg) so that a cumulative target dose of galsidase alfa 14.4 mg was reached at the end of an infusion time of 107 min [105]. In comparison to the protocol reported by Aydin and colleagues [94], the latter resulted in a much lower total infusion time of 107 min compared with 507 min. By contrast, another case study revealed that a three-bag protocol alone or in combination with pretreatment with glucocorticoids, H1/H2 antagonists, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and even B-cell depletion by rituximab was not able to reintroduce agalsidase beta in a patient with IgEs against AGAL, but moreover needed a specific anti-IgE inhibition by omalizumab [96]. This indicates that there does not seem to be a general solution for the treatment of IARs, but that individually tailored approaches are required for the affected patients.

About 40–70% of all male patients with FD (especially in the absence of endogenous AGAL activity) receiving ERT develop IgG antibodies against the infused enzymes. The antibodies show a high cross-reactivity towards agalsidase alfa and beta, leading to enzyme inhibition, decreasing the therapeutic efficacy [40, 87]. In detail, neutralizing ADAs already inhibit the infused enzyme during an infusion [106] as well as endothelial enzyme uptake and intracellular enzymatic activity [107] (Fig. 3A). To date, only higher doses of ERT were able to compensate the neutralizing antibodies against agalsidase alfa or agalsidase beta [106, 108, 109]. However, a dose escalation (i.e., switch from agalsidase alfa to agalsidase beta) appears to have heterogeneous long-term effects in patients, as it can also trigger an increase of ADA titers after the switch [109].

Pre-existing ADAs against agalsidase alfa or agalsidase beta show a lower affinity and a lower inhibitory capacity against pegunigalsidase alfa in vitro [110]. This is probably due to the PEGylation of the enzyme, which shields at least some of the epitopes from the antibodies [110, 111]. By contrast, PEGylation itself can also induce immune reactions (Fig. 3B). The overall frequency for anti-PEG antibodies is reported as ~ 40% [112]. Sources of PEG potentially leading to anti-PEG antibodies include oral drugs, cosmetics, and alimentation (Fig. 3B). In addition, a recent study demonstrated that the vaccination against severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 with mRNA-1273 (Moderna) and BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech) can trigger the de novo formation of anti-PEG antibodies and increase pre-existing anti-PEG antibodies [113, 114]. As demonstrated, the vaccination-induced de novo formation of anti-PEG antibodies can result in high anti-PEG titers, showing inhibitory pegunigalsidase alfa function in vitro [111]. However, with an absent stimulus (i.e., vaccination booster), these titers decreased over time and although pre-existing anti-PEG antibodies were detected in also up to ~ 40% in a study cohort of patients with FD, the anti-PEG titers were relative low and had no significant impact on pegunigalsidase alfa function in vitro [111]. Of note, by contrast to anti-AGAL antibodies, recognizing the amino acid backbone, anti-PEG antibodies recognize the PEG residues and are thus relevant in all patients (male and female). Future studies are now warranted to analyze the effect of anti-PEG antibodies on the pharmacokinetics and efficacy of pegunigalsidase alfa treatment.

In addition to the direct neutralization of the infused enzyme, ADAs might also form ADA/AGAL immune complexes (“planted antigens,” type 3 reaction), mediating complement activation and resulting in membranous nephritis (MN), as reported for other ERT-treated rare diseases [115–117]. The underlying mechanisms are not yet fully understood. However, it is conceivable that according to the Heidelberg–Kendall curve, the respective concentrations of the antigen and the free antibodies could influence the precipitation and formation of larger immune complexes (Fig. 3C). To the best of our knowledge, the formation of immune complexes and associated MN in patients with FD taking agalsidase alfa or beta has not yet been described in the literature, so far. This could be partly due to the short circulating half-life of the two enzymes (agalsidase alfa and beta) in plasma and their relatively low infusion dosages (compared with Pompe disease), decreasing an appropriate risk. A long circulating half-life, in contrast, might thus be counterproductive, as the occurrence of immune complex-associated MN was reported in one patient from the BALANCE study [38]. Treatment with pegunigalsidase alfa was discontinued in this patient [38]. Thus, patients with an unexplained rapid drop in eGFR receiving pegunigalsidase alfa should undergo a kidney biopsy to exclude immune complex-mediated MN.

Comparable to other lysosomal storage diseases (Gaucher disease, Pompe disease), a possible therapeutic strategy to prevent or reduce ADAs in FD could also include immunosuppressive treatment of affected patients [13, 118, 119]. Most successful approaches included the inhibition of folic acid metabolism, DNA alkylation, and antibody-mediated B-cell depletion in yet ERT-naïve patients [119–128]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no specific studies were reported for FD, so far. At least in a collective of transplanted male patients with FD, it was shown that immunosuppressive therapy (to prevent transplant rejection) can suppress antibody formation [129]. Higher immunosuppressive doses were associated with lower antibody titers and less ERT inhibition [129].

The long circulating half-life of pegunigalsidase alfa could contribute to the fact that at least ERT-naïve patients without pre-existing antibodies against AGAL do not form antibodies during the course of therapy, or that at least the risk is reduced. However, this needs first to be demonstrated in appropriate studies. Currently, the frequency for the de novo formation in pegunigalsidase alfa-treated patients is reported as 16% (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/product-information/elfabrio-epar-product-information_en.pdf; date of last access: 21 January, 2025). Serum conversion in patients with pre-existing antibodies against agalsidase alfa or beta after switching to pegunigalsidase alfa has not yet been sufficiently clarified, as the antibodies could be masked because of the long circulating half-life of pegunigalsidase alfa [41].

In addition to immunosuppression, oral tolerance might also be functional for FD. Oral tolerance is an active immunological process mediated in a dose-dependent manner. The orally administered antigens, like food antigens, can be internalized by the small intestine’s immune system, resulting in an immune regulatory response. For a detailed review, refer to Rana et al. [130]. The oral tolerance was applied to auto-immunity and inflammatory diseases including peanut allergy [131, 132], allergic asthma [133, 134], and pollen allergy [135], but also ERT-treatable diseases including Pompe disease [136] and hemophilia [137–140] in preclinical as well as clinical studies. However, it seems that in food allergies, stopping oral therapy seems to result in a loss of desensitization, although it is not known if the same is true for oral tolerance to ADAs [130]. In addition, oral tolerance seems to be most efficient in yet untreated (ERT-naïve) patients. So far, it has not been demonstrated whether oral administration of AGAL might support immune tolerance, resulting in the absence or low ADA titers in an AGAL-deficient background. The induction of oral tolerance would require high daily dosages of recombinant AGAL. However, as a dietary supplement, production costs using expression platforms including plants or yeasts should be much lower compared with current ERT costs. Of note, patients treated orally with human recombinant AGAL might benefit twice, in that the risk for ADAs could be reduced, in addition to a reduction in gastrointestinal symptoms [129].

However, ADA-positive patients with an AGAL excess during infusions presented with better renal and cardiac outcomes during infusion compared with ADA-positive patients with an antibody excess (Fig. 3C) [106, 108]. This might indicate that in patients with ADAs, a clinical effect of infused enzyme doses depends on ADA titers and their saturation during infusion, leading to the assumption that not every ADA-positive patient necessarily needs an intervention to benefit from ERT.

For many patients, oral therapy with migalastat appears to be a good alternative to ERT. However, patients respond differently to the therapy, which may be because of the severity of the disease, possible comorbidities, adherence to therapy, and their individual mutation, making appropriate studies complex. Recent studies demonstrated that in vitro amenability does not necessarily correspond to clinical responsiveness [141, 142]. Some amenable AGAL mutations with low residual activity in the good laboratory practice HEK assay showed no biochemical response with respect to the marker lyso-Gb3 in corresponding patients treated with migalastat [141, 142]. This raises the question of which endogenous AGAL activity needs to be achieved in the migalastat-treated patient in order to stop or even reverse disease progression. To the best of our knowledge, this question was not addressed adequately in the literature so far and requires appropriate studies.

To assess the therapy efficacy, it is recommended to measure the AGAL activity of migalastat-treated patients, which should increase significantly in male patients if the individual mutation is amenable and the patient shows an appropriate compliance [143, 144]. Because of the presence of a healthy allele, the efficacy of migalastat in female patients cannot be clearly demonstrated because AGAL activity is initially within the normal range. However, studies including clinical trials, real-world data, but also individual experiences from treating physicians revealed inconsistencies for measured AGAL activities. While some studies showed AGAL activity increasing over time in peripheral blood mononuclear cells [145, 146], other studies based on measurements from dried blood spots did not [147]. This observation might be explained by the mechanistic action of migalastat, which is a competitive inhibitor of AGAL and is cleared by the kidneys [148, 149]. A recent study demonstrated that AGAL activities in patients should be measured 24 h after migalastat intake at the earliest and that measurements from peripheral blood mononuclear cells seem to be more valid than from dried blood spots [150]. In samples from the latter, the unbound migalastat molecules in plasma might interfere with AGAL and inhibit enzymatic activities during measurements [150]. We would like to emphasize at this point that the measured low AGAL activity in these patients is based solely on an incorrect measurement method and that the patients benefit from the treatment. Hence, studies analyzing the effect of migalastat on endogenous AGAL activities in treated patients should aim for blood samplings drawn 24 h after migalastat intake at the earliest.

Symptomatic concomitant medication is therapeutically necessary for the symptoms and organ manifestations typical of FD. As for other renal diseases, nephroprotection with inhibitors of the renin-angiotensin system is recommended for FD nephropathy, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers [151, 152]. As a high-sodium diet can reduce the efficacy of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers [153] and is associated with an increased risk of end-stage renal disease progression in patients with proteinuria [154], a low-sodium diet (< 5 g NaCl/day) is recommended for patients with FD with proteinuria [155]. According to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO), sodium-dependent glucose transporter 2 inhibitors are currently generally recommended for nephroprotection and cardioprotection [156]. Sodium-dependent glucose transporter 2 inhibitors are also administered to patients with FD, although initial study data in this population are still pending [157].

Pain of high intensity and frequency dominates the daily lives of many patients with FD and impairs their quality of life. There are scant data on the symptomatic treatment of neuropathic pain in FD. According to current knowledge, the combination of ERT and symptomatic analgesics leads to the most effective pain relief. According to current Delphi consensus recommendations, pregabalin and duloxetine can be used as a first-line analgesic medication for the treatment of neuropathic pain in FD [158]. Topical capsaicin and lidocaine patches can be used as a second-line analgesic medication [158]. Amitriptyline can also be used as a first-line analgesic medication [158], but with caution, as tricyclic antidepressants can have a negative impact on the cardiac conduction system, which is frequently impaired in FD. The analgesic effect of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in neuropathic pain seems to be low, thus they should be administered with caution because of their nephrotoxicity.

Fabry disease is a lysosomal storage disease based on an enzymatic AGAL deficiency leading to a multisystem disease. Because of X-linked inheritance, male individuals are affected more severely and earlier than female individuals. Clinically, the disease can be divided into classic and late-onset phenotypes. The typical “red flags” of FD include neuropathic pain of the extremities and gastrointestinal symptoms as well as hypo-/hyperhidrosis, grouped angiokeratomas, and cornea verticillata. In the course of the disease, affected patients experience progressive renal dysfunction, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, heart failure, cardiac arrhythmia, and early-onset cerebral events. If left untreated, life expectancy in female and male individuals is reduced by around 10 and 20 years, respectively. The currently approved FD-specific therapy consists of the use of ERT (agalsidase alfa, agalsidase beta, pegunigalsidase alfa) or pharmacological chaperone therapy (migalastat). Other potential therapeutic options, including substrate reduction therapy and gene therapy, are still under development.
