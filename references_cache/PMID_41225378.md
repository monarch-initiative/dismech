# A case report: pediatric rhabdomyosarcoma presenting with bone marrow metastasis.
**Authors:** Xiong T, Wan Q, Yuan C, Huang H, Zhong L, Xu Z, Wu C
**Journal:** BMC Pediatr (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12887-025-06310-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-025-06310-3)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is the most common soft tissue sarcoma in children and adolescents, yet bone marrow metastasis is relatively rare. The clinical manifestations and bone marrow features of RMS with bone marrow metastasis are atypical, leading to frequent misdiagnosis as hematologic malignancies or other solid tumors at initial presentation, which consequently delays treatment initiation.

CASE PRESENTATION: An 11-year-old male patient presented with hemorrhagic rash and arthralgia as the initial symptoms. Early bone marrow cytology was suggestive of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Further immunophenotyping could not rule out neuroblastoma (NB). Subsequent contrast-enhanced MRI, PET-CT scans, and pathological biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (ARMS).

CONCLUSION: This case highlights that RMS can initially present with bone marrow metastasis, mimicking hematologic malignancies. When primitive immature cells are observed in bone marrow aspirates, RMS should be considered in the differential diagnosis. A definitive diagnosis relies on a comprehensive approach including immunohistochemical markers, molecular genetic testing, and advanced imaging to identify the primary lesion.

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is the most common soft tissue sarcoma in children and adolescents [1]. Over 35% of affected children present with metastasis at initial diagnosis, yet bone marrow metastasis is relatively rare [2], occurring in only 6% of all RMS cases [3]. Due to its atypical clinical manifestations and bone marrow pathological features, most patients are initially misdiagnosed with hematologic malignancies or other solid tumors, resulting in delayed treatment initiation [2]. To date, few case reports have documented such patients, contributing to diagnostic errors and inappropriate therapeutic interventions [4].

This report describes a case of alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (ARMS) with bone marrow metastasis. The patient presented with hemorrhagic rash and arthralgia as initial manifestations. Initial bone marrow cytology was suspicious for acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Subsequent immunophenotyping could not exclude neuroblastoma (NB). The diagnosis was ultimately confirmed by contrast-enhanced MRI, PET-CT imaging, and pathological biopsy. By integrating literature evidence, this article analyzes the clinical characteristics, pathological morphology, molecular genetic alterations, and differential diagnosis of this particular case of RMS with bone marrow metastasis. It is important to note that the insights derived from a single case report are inherently limited in their generalizability. The aim of this report is to enhance clinical awareness of this tumor entity in pediatric populations.

An 11-year-old male was admitted with a one-week history of hemorrhagic rash and arthralgia. Physical examination shows that the pain mainly affects the left knee and the right hip joint. The affected joints showed no redness or swelling, and there were no signs of limited mobility. The rash is mainly distributed on the upper and lower limbs. A 1 cm lymph node could be palpated in the left popliteal fossa. No obvious enlargement was observed in the other superficial lymph nodes. The abdomen was soft, and no obvious abnormalities were found on palpation of the liver and spleen. Based on these clinical features, he was hospitalized with a preliminary diagnosis of Henoch-Schönlein purpura (HSP).

Laboratory studies revealed leukocytosis (WBC 15.7 × 10⁹/L) with neutrophilic predominance (67.2%), hemoglobin level of 124 g/L, and mild thrombocytopenia (platelet count 76 × 10⁹/L)-an atypical feature for HSP. Urinalysis showed no abnormalities. Color Doppler ultrasound of the hip, knee, and ankle joints indicated no significant effusion. However, ultrasound of the left popliteal fossa identified a well-defined cystic mass measuring approximately 12 × 7 mm, exhibiting homogeneous internal echogenicity and prominent vascular flow signals. Given the clinical presentation of thrombocytopenia, limb pain, and hemorrhagic rash, further imaging studies (CT and ultrasonography) were performed with parental consent to evaluate for possible visceral hemorrhage or mass-occupying lesions. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated splenomegaly and mildly enlarged abdominal lymph nodes; the liver, pancreas, kidneys, and retroperitoneal structures appeared normal. Non-contrast CT scans of the head and chest revealed no acute abnormalities. The concurrent presence of thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly, and persistent pain despite unremarkable initial imaging studies presented a diagnostically challenging scenario on admission, prompting further hematologic evaluation.

Driven by the clinical triad of thrombocytopenia, limb pain, and splenomegaly, bone marrow aspiration was performed on the second day of admission. Cytomorphological assessment demonstrated monoblast-like or promonocyte-like cells dominating 68% of nucleated cells, characterized by abnormally large immature cells with irregular contours. These cells exhibited abundant cytoplasm staining opaque grayish-blue with prominent pseudopodia formation and bubbles, alongside enlarged nuclei displaying pathological folding, indentation, and lobulation. The chromatin appeared finely reticulated with discernible nucleoli. POX staining showed negative. Critically, trilineage hematopoiesis (granulocytic/erythroid/megakaryocytic) was profoundly suppressed, with lymphocytes constituting only 4.5% of cellularity. This constellation of findings-dominant monocytoid proliferation, aberrant cytological features, and marrow failure-strongly suggested AML-M5 (Fig. 1).

Notably, the abnormally large cell size, the abundant opaque grayish-blue cytoplasm with vacuoles and pseudopods, and the large nuclei with finely reticulated chromatin were critical morphological clues that, although initially suggestive of acute monocytic leukemia, are also known to be associated with metastatic solid tumors. This morphological overlap underscores the necessity of further immunohistochemical or flow cytometric analysis for definitive distinction.


To refine the diagnostic workup, flow cytometric immunophenotyping was performed on bone marrow samples on day 3 of admission. A detailed analysis was conducted to characterize the aberrant cell population. Gating on the CD45/SSC dot plot identified a distinct, dominant aberrant population (accounting for 14% of nucleated cells) with intermediate forward/side scatter (FSC/SSC)-indicating a size slightly larger than lymphocytes. Critically, this population was completely negative for CD45 (leukocyte common antigen), effectively ruling out a primary hematologic malignancy. Further immunophenotyping revealed that this CD45-negative population was positive for CD9, CD56, and CD81(Fig. 2), a profile that is highly atypical for leukemia but has been reported in certain solid tumors, notably NB and RMS. Concurrently, the residual lymphocytes constituted 42% of nucleated cells (elevated proportion), with expanded B-cell subsets showing no monotypic light chain expression, ruling out a lymphoproliferative disorder. The composite immunophenotype of CD45−/CD56+/CD9+/CD81 + was pivotal in shifting the diagnostic suspicion away from leukemia and towards a metastatic small blue round cell tumor, such as NB or RMS [5–7]. Complementary cytogenetic analysis revealed a complex karyotype: 47,XY,+1,add(2)(q33),psu dic(8;1)(q24;p13),+13,?del(13)(q14)×2 [11]/46,XY [9](Supplementary Material 1).

Given that the flow cytometry analysis of the bone marrow could not exclude metastatic carcinoma or NB, and considering the high incidence of NB in children [8], further testing was performed for Neuron-Specific Enolase (NSE), Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA), Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG), and S-100 Protein. The results showed a markedly elevated NSE level of 21.80 ng/mL (reference range: 0–6 ng/mL). Other values were as follows: AFP 1.47 ng/mL (reference range: 0–20 ng/mL), CEA 0.54 ng/mL (reference range: ≤5 ng/mL), hCG 1.64 mIU/mL (reference range: 0–10 mIU/mL), and S-100 3.06 ng/mL (reference range: 0.021–0.5.021.5 ng/mL). With the aim of further locating the primary tumor lesion, PET-CT imaging was performed on hospital day 5. The scan demonstrated a soft tissue mass and nodules posterior to the left popliteal fossa, which exhibited markedly elevated FDG uptake. Additionally, multiple sites of bone destruction and enlarged pelvic lymph nodes showed abnormally increased FDG metabolism. These findings are highly suggestive of a malignant process (Supplementary Material 2). The findings are most consistent with a primary malignancy in the left calf with metastatic involvement of the aforementioned sites. A biopsy of the hypermetabolic focus in the left calf is therefore recommended.

To further characterize the surgical target lesion, we performed a contrast-enhanced MRI of the calf. On day 6 of admission, contrast-enhanced MRI of the calf revealed patchy areas of high signal intensity on T2-weighted fat-suppressed sequences and low signal intensity on T1-weighted images (T1WI) within the bilateral distal femurs, proximal tibiae, and fibular bones, demonstrating mild post-contrast enhancement; the bone cortices remained smooth and intact without significant abnormalities or destruction. Posterior to the distal left femur, an irregular mass-like lesion displayed high T2 signal intensity, isointense-to-hypointense T1 signal intensity, and mild post-contrast enhancement with relatively well-defined margins, adjacent to which two nodular lesions exhibited slightly high T2 signal intensity. Adjacent to the musculature posterior to the middle and lower segments of the left tibia, a mass-like lesion showed isointense T1 signal, high T2-fat-suppressed signal, high diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) signal, and heterogeneous mild enhancement with scattered non-enhancing areas post-contrast, with relatively well-defined margins and a craniocaudal length of approximately 202 mm; no other significant abnormal signals were observed in the remaining structures. (Fig. 3). A detailed physical examination of the left lower leg was performed at the time of the biopsy, which confirmed the presence of the mass.

On day 10 of admission, the biopsy of the lesion revealed diffuse infiltration of small round tumor cells into skeletal muscle tissue. These cells displayed indistinct nucleoli, scant eosinophilic cytoplasm, and frequent mitotic figures, with scattered rosette-like multinucleated giant cells. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong positivity for Desmin (+++), CD56 (+++), ALK (+++), Myogenin (+++), and MyoD1 (+++), while showing negative results for CD99, NSE, CgA, LCA, Pan-CK, GATA-3, NKX2.2, MPO, and NUT. The tumor exhibited a high proliferation index (Ki-67 + 95%), retained INI1 expression (+++), wild-type P53 pattern, and nuclear/cytoplasmic S-100 staining. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) confirmed FKHR (FOXO1) gene rearrangement. These findings collectively support a definitive diagnosis of ARMS (Fig. 4).

Based on a definitive diagnosis of ARMS with widespread metastasis, the patient was classified as Clinical Group IV under the Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study (IRS) system. According to the TNM pretreatment staging criteria, the disease was categorized as T2 (tumor > 5 cm), N1 (regional lymph node involvement, confirmed by PET-CT), M1 (distant metastasis present), corresponding to Stage 4. Under the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) risk stratification, the combination of alveolar histology and metastatic disease placed the patient in the high-risk category, which carries a poor prognosis and necessitates intensive multimodal therapy. Following the establishment of the diagnosis, the family discontinued treatment at our institution, and no further follow-up information is available.

RMS represents the most common soft tissue sarcoma in children, exhibiting considerable heterogeneity in age of onset, primary site, metastatic patterns, driver mutations, and histological subtypes [9]. Epidemiological studies have revealed significant geographical variations in RMS incidence across different countries and regions [10]. Bone marrow metastasis occurs in approximately 6% of all newly diagnosed RMS cases [3], and these patients are identified as a subgroup with a particularly poor prognosis [11, 12]. While predominantly affecting children, RMS with bone marrow metastasis also occurs in adults and elderly patients [13, 14]. Patients with bone marrow involvement often present with atypical primary tumor locations that are difficult to detect. Initial manifestations frequently mimic hematological malignancies or other primary bone marrow neoplasms, characterized by hematologic abnormalities [2, 14, 15]. Consistent with literature reports, this pediatric case clinically presented with thrombocytopenia, limb pain, and splenomegaly-findings initially suggestive of acute leukemia.

Notably, this child’s initial symptoms were primarily rash and joint pain, leading to a preliminary diagnosis of allergic purpura-a clinical presentation not previously documented in published case reports. Although mild thrombocytopenia was observed (platelet count 76 × 10⁹/L), which can occasionally occur in HSP, it further complicated the clinical picture [16]. Moreover, the pain was predominantly localized to the lower limb joints and accompanied by a scattered rash, features that could easily contribute to misdiagnosis. This case represents a rare instance of ARMS presenting with hemorrhagic rash and joint pain, mimicking both HSP and AML. It also documents the concurrent presence of bone and bone marrow metastases, as evidenced by PET-CT revealing osteolytic lesions and marrow infiltration-a phenomenon that has been scarcely reported in the literature.

RMS with bone marrow metastasis may be misdiagnosed as a hematological malignancy [17–19]. Several case reports of ARMS exist where extensive lymph node metastasis, the presence of immature and atypical marrow-infiltrating cells on bone marrow cytology smears, and the absence of an identifiable primary tumor often led to misdiagnosis as acute leukemia or lymphoma, presenting significant diagnostic challenges [20, 21]. In this patient, the bone marrow exhibited extreme hypercellularity. The cells were large, with abundant grayish-blue cytoplasm frequently containing vacuoles and pseudopods. The nuclei were large, with loose chromatin, and occasional nucleoli were noted. The abnormal proliferation of the mononuclear series accounted for 68%, while lymphocytes constituted 4.5%, leading us to initially suspect acute leukemia. Flow cytometric immunophenotyping aided further diagnosis. The cells were positive for CD9, CD56, and CD81, allowing us to rule out leukemia. While RMS cells can express the immunophenotype CD45−/CD56+, this pattern alone is insufficient to identify RMS cells as it is present in many types of non-hematopoietic small blue round cell tumors, including NB, among others [18].

Flow cytometry is widely employed in the diagnosis of hematologic malignancies and lymphomas, with numerous studies demonstrating its role in enhancing overall diagnostic accuracy [22–24]. While flow cytometry enables rapid diagnosis, classification, and treatment monitoring for most hematologic malignancies, its application in solid cancers remains limited and challenging [25]. Few studies have specifically evaluated the utility of flow cytometry for diagnostic screening and subtyping in pediatric solid tumors [26]. This limited adoption may stem from the association of distinct pediatric tumor subtypes with unique and specific immunophenotypes. Current applications of flow cytometry in solid tumors primarily focus on detecting bone marrow involvement or circulating NB cells in NB [27], identifying leptomeningeal metastases in retinoblastoma [28], and monitoring treatment response in RMS patients [29]. Among the markers evaluated in pediatric solid tumors to date, CD56 has been the most extensively studied. CD56 is expressed in the majority of pediatric solid tumors [26], a finding corroborated in our case. However, CD56 expression levels vary considerably across different tumor subtypes. Although flow cytometry offers rapid, quantitative detection of metastatic cells in bone marrow, its reliance on antigen expression patterns can impose limitations on definitive tumor subclassification. Consequently, multi-parametric immunophenotypic analysis necessitates integration with molecular validation for accurate diagnosis. Future research should prioritize exploring the regulatory mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment that drive phenotypic heterogeneity and optimizing detection strategies to improve specificity [30].

NB can be diagnosed when bone marrow biopsy or aspiration demonstrates characteristic NB cells or positive anti-GD2 antibody staining, accompanied by elevated urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) and increased serum NSE levels [31]. However, in this pediatric case, GD2 staining was negative (Supplementary material 3), NSE elevation was not significant, and contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen revealed no significant lesions; therefore, PET-CT and contrast-enhanced MRI were further performed. For RMS, non-contrast MRI typically shows isointense or hypointense signal on T1WI, hyperintense signal on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) with homogeneous or heterogeneous intensity, and diffusion restriction on DWI; post-contrast enhancement demonstrates moderate to marked heterogeneous enhancement of the mass [32]. Our results demonstrate patchy areas of high signal intensity on T2-weighted fat-suppressed sequences and low signal intensity on T1WI within the bilateral distal femurs, proximal tibiae, and fibular bones, showing mild post-contrast enhancement. An irregular mass-like lesion in the soft tissue posterior to the distal left femur exhibits high T2 signal intensity, isointense-to-hypointense T1 signal intensity, and mild post-contrast enhancement with relatively well-defined margins, accompanied by two adjacent nodular lesions demonstrating slightly high T2 signal intensity. Adjacent to the musculature posterior to the middle and lower left tibia, a mass-like lesion displays isointense T1 signal, high T2-fat-suppressed signal, high DWI signal, and heterogeneous mild enhancement with scattered non-enhancing areas post-contrast, with relatively well-defined margins and a craniocaudal dimension of approximately 202 mm; these imaging characteristics are consistent with RMS.

Most studies on metastatic RMS do not subdivide outcomes based on metastatic sites or combine bone and bone marrow metastases in their analyses, yet the coexistence between osseous metastasis and bone marrow metastasis remains unclear [9]. Although some studies report a high correlation between bone marrow and skeletal metastases [11, 33], others indicate limited simultaneous involvement of both at initial diagnosis [3]. In this case, PET-CT revealed multiple osteolytic lesions throughout the body with markedly increased FDG uptake, suggesting concurrent bone marrow metastasis and osseous metastasis.

RMS expresses specific skeletal muscle phenotypic markers, including desmin, myogenin, MyoD1 and myogenic regulatory factors. Immunohistochemical staining and FISH or NGS enhances the reliability and accuracy of RMS diagnosis. This technique plays a critical role in distinguishing RMS from other tumor types and further subclassifying RMS variants. Importantly, the expression of MyoD1 protein and myogenin serves as essential diagnostic criteria for RMS and represents an evidence-based gold standard for differential diagnosis against other neoplasms [34]. In this pediatric case, the final pathological diagnosis confirmed ARMS.

Bone marrow cytogenetic analysis revealed complex abnormalities. Analysis of 20 metaphase cells from cultured marrow specimens showed that 11 cells exhibited karyotypes involving multiple chromosomal aberrations. FISH confirmed a positive FKHR (FOXO1) gene break in this pediatric case, evidenced by split red/green signals and patterns of one red, one green, and one yellow signal. Approximately 80% of ARMS cases harbor recurrent chromosomal translocations-specifically t(2;13)(q35;q14) and t(1;13)(p36;q14)-which generate PAX3-FOXO1 and PAX7-FOXO1 fusion genes, respectively. The PAX3-FOXO1 fusion is more prevalent than PAX7-FOXO1 and associates with a higher-grade malignancy [35].

Based on our experience, when bone marrow morphology shows atypical features (e.g., cytoplasmic vacuoles or cell clustering) and/or flow cytometry reveals a CD45⁻/CD56⁺population, PET-CT or MRI should be promptly performed to detect occult solid tumors and guide targeted biopsy, thereby avoiding misdiagnosis and enabling rapid definitive diagnosis.

In summary, this case illustrates that RMS can present with bone marrow metastasis as its initial manifestation, closely mimicking primary hematologic malignancies and leading to potential misdiagnosis. Cytomorphologically, the presence of primitive immature cells in bone marrow smears should raise suspicion for RMS infiltration, warranting its inclusion in the differential diagnosis. However, a definitive diagnosis cannot rely on morphology alone. It requires a multimodal approach where immunohistochemical markers and molecular genetic testing are critical for confirmation, while advanced imaging such as PET-CT is indispensable for detecting occult primary lesions and guiding accurate staging. Finally, the insights drawn from this single case are valuable for raising awareness but are limited in their generalizability, underscoring the need for further collaborative studies to better understand this disease presentation.
