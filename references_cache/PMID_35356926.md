# Predictive ability of inflammatory markers and laboratory parameters in Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease: A single-center retrospective comparative study.
**Authors:** Mishima K, Kamiya Y, Matsushita M, Imagama S, Kitoh H
**Journal:** Medicine (Baltimore) (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1097/MD.0000000000029061](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000029061)

## Content

Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease (LCPD) presents with chronic nature of inflammation, characterized by prolonged synovitis. So far, no single blood marker has been identified to guide clinicians in estimating the severity and prognosis. Blood neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) or systemic immune inflammation index (SII) is a simple indicator of subclinical inflammation. This study aims to examine the predictive ability of NLR, SII, and common laboratory parameters for estimating the severity of LCPD. The pre-operative laboratory findings at the time of osteotomy and implant removal in patients with unilateral LCPD who had been treated with the Salter innominate osteotomy and followed up until skeletal maturity as well as those of age-matched control patients with idiopathic noninflammatory conditions were analyzed. The datasets of 26 or 38 LCPD patients at the time of osteotomy or implant removal, respectively, and those of 20 control patients were available for analysis. At the time of osteotomy, compared to the control group, a significantly higher mean NLR or SII and a significantly lower mean alkaline phosphatase value were observed in the LCPD group. The alkaline phosphatase levels of patients with the modified lateral pillar (LP) group-A hips were significantly lower than those with the non-LP-A hips, whereas no significant differences were observed in any of the parameters between patients with favorable LP-A or -B hips and those with unfavorable LP-B|C border or -C hips. In agreement with the conventional opinion, it may be difficult to predict a meaningful prognosis of LCPD with the use of inflammatory markers or common laboratory parameters obtained in the initial stage of the disease.

Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease (LCPD) is juvenile osteonecrosis of the femoral head presumably following a single or repeated ischemic insult to the capital femoral epiphysis.[1] Although the fundamental etiology underlining ischemic event remains poorly understood, potential implications of subclinical coagulation or lipid abnormalities have been documented in previous studies.[2,3] In contrast to coxitis simplex, a self-limited condition causing hip arthritis, LCPD is characterized by prolonged synovitis and associated intracapsular effusions, suggesting the aspect of chronic inflammatory diseases. Despite the illness chronicity following the onset of osteonecrosis, no single blood marker has been identified to guide clinicians in estimating the severity and prognosis as well as making an early diagnosis of LCPD.[4] Although acute local inflammation sharply increases systemic levels of acute-phase reactants including serum C-reactive protein and plasma fibrinogen, no single blood marker sensitive to disease chronicity has been identified.[5] Alternatively, the strong association between the neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) or the systemic immune inflammation index (SII), which are calculated from complete blood count with differential, and prognosis of chronic inflammatory diseases, including autoimmune diseases, metabolic syndrome, ischemic diseases, and malignancies, has been recently recognized and utilized as an independent predictor of their outcomes.[6-9] In the present study, we aimed to examine the clinical availability of the NLR and SII as well as common laboratory parameters for the prediction of the prognosis in LCPD patients undergoing surgical containment therapy in the active stage of the disease.

This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board (IRB 2018-0011). We retrospectively investigated clinical charts and radiographic images of 83 patients who had been diagnosed with unilateral LCPD from 1987 to 2013, undergone the Salter innominate osteotomy between 6.0 and 9.0 years of age, and followed up until skeletal maturity at our tertiary institution. The values of routine pre-operative laboratory testing, which was performed at the time of osteotomy and implant removal, were obtained from each patient’s chart. The NLR was calculated by neutrocyte count divided by lymphocyte count. The SII was calculated by the NLR multiplied by platelet count.[10] Considering age-specific reference intervals of laboratory parameters, 36 patients who had undergone common laboratory testing between 6.0 and 9.0 years of age from 2001 to 2018 for pre-operative checkup or exclusion of inflammatory conditions and been conclusively diagnosed with idiopathic non-inflammatory conditions were extracted from our computerized intradepartmental database and served as a control group. Exclusion criteria of laboratory datasets for analysis were those of patients who had fever >37.5°C, cold symptoms, white blood cell count >10,000/μL, or CRP >0.3mg/dL at the timing of blood test, considering the possibility that they had acute inflammatory reactions induced at sites other than the affected hip at that moment, as well as those lacking complete blood count with differential, leaving pre-operative datasets of 26 or 38 patients (LCPD group) at the time of osteotomy or implant removal, respectively, and those of 20 patients of the control group for analysis. The final diagnosis of the control group included is shown in Table 1. Serial radiographs of the hip during the disease course were evaluated to determine disease severity at the time of mid-fragmentation stage according to the modified lateral pillar (LP) classification, with the group-A and -B hips categorized as favorable prognosis and the group-B|C border and -C hips classified as unfavorable prognosis.[11] The assigned group was used to determine the association between each of the preoperative laboratory parameters at the time of osteotomy and the LP groups. The modified Waldenström classification system was utilized for the staging of the disease at the time of blood test.[12] Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS statistics 25 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). An unpaired t test was utilized for the comparison of between-group data. A one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Dunnett test was used to determine whether there were any statistically significant differences among the means of 3 or more independent groups. The Fisher exact test was used to analyze the association between the fragmentation pattern and the LP group. A P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant. Values are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation.

The radiographic outcomes of the 2 subgroups of the LPCD group are listed in Tables 2 and 3. The mean NLR and SII values significantly differed between the LCPD group and control group at the time of osteotomy, but not at the time of implant removal (Table 4). Across all parameters of the laboratory testing, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) level alone was significantly lower in the LCPD group than the control group at the time of osteotomy, whereas no significant differences were observed in any of the parameters between the 2 groups at the time of implant removal (Table 4). When examining differences of NLR, SII, and ALP values at the time of osteotomy among the LP groups, none of them significantly differed between patients with favorable prognosis and those with unfavorable prognosis, whereas ALP levels of patients with the LP-A hips were significantly lower than those with the non-LP-A hips (Fig. 1). A longitudinal comparison of NLR, SII, and ALP values at the time of osteotomy and implant removal in the same patient population of the LCPD group (n = 20) showed a significant difference regarding ALP levels (Fig. 2).

The importance of blood-cell ratios such as NLR and a novel index SII has been increasingly recognized as inflammation markers that have considerable ability to predict the severity and/or prognosis of a wide range of chronic inflammatory conditions, including malignancies, chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, ischemic heart and brain diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic syndrome, osteoporosis, and autoimmune diseases.[6,8,9,13-16] Chronicity of local inflammation in LCPD has been clinically revealed in a previous investigation showing persistence of synovitis in the affected hip for a mean of 18 months after the onset of the disease.[17] The results of the present study that the NLR and SII values in the LCPD group were significantly higher at the earlier stage of the disease seems to support the chronic nature of LCPD. From a mechanistic standpoint, induction of ischemic osteonecrosis in an animal model gave rise to sustained accumulation of activated neutrophils and macrophages at the affected tissues for up to 6 weeks after the induction, when the re-ossification phase had already occurred in that model,[18] indicating chronic local inflammation in this etiology. In contrast to our results that no association was observed between the NLR values obtained mostly at the initial or sclerotic stage and the disease severity assessed with the LP classification, a previous study demonstrated that the NLR values at the fragmentation stage were significantly higher in the unfavorable prognosis group with LP-B|C and -C hips than in the favorable prognosis group with LP-A and -B hips.[19] Accordingly, asymptomatic systemic inflammatory reaction is likely to be induced and sustained due to prolonged local inflammation of the hip joint at the earlier stage of LCPD. Higher NLR and SII values may be associated with worse disease severity, benefitting prognostic assessment and subsequent decision making in containment therapy, depending on the disease stage in which blood testing is performed.

Our results that a mean of ALP levels was significantly lower in the LCPD group at the earlier stage of the disease, falling below a mean of the age-specific reference values were suggestive of underling a patients’ characteristic of LCPD.[20] Serum ALP is a biochemical marker that represents bone turnover, thereby increasing in children during the growth period compared to adults.[20] Thus, lower-than-average ALP levels in the LCPD group may reflect delayed skeletal maturation, universally accepted as an innate characteristic of LCPD.[21] Our results regarding serum ALP at the initial or sclerotic stage may provide a limited predictive value for prognosis of LCPD, whereas a significant correlation has been shown between serum concentrations of osteocalcin, one of the well-known osteogenic markers, and the disease severity determined with the LP classification system. Although temporal relation between the timing of blood tests and the disease stages was not described in that literature, pre-operative concentrations of serum osteocalcin were significantly lower in the LP-B group than in the LP-B|C and -C groups.[22] Since osteocalcin is secreted exclusively by mature osteoblasts, circulating osteocalcin levels may become sharply elevated in response to enhanced reparative reactions probably reflecting the extent of osteonecrosis. A significant increase in ALP levels at the time of implant removal (Fig. 2), which was mostly obtained at the re-ossification stage, may also represent the reparative bone formation. Therefore, some osteoblasticspecific molecules can be encouraging markers at the earlier stage of the disease with good sensitivity and specificity for predicting the disease severity.

There are several limitations to this study. First, we chose hospital-based patients, not age and sex-matched healthy volunteers as the control group. Therefore, if a different cohort of patients were selected as the control group, the results would be altered. Second, there were only a limited numbers of patients in the LCPD group. It should be noted that we were unable to examine the clinical availability of NLR, SII, or common laboratory parameters in predicting postoperative prognosis by the LP classification. Finally, we only included Japanese patients in this study. It is still unclear whether races differ regarding common laboratory parameters.

In conclusion, the NLR, SII, or ALP values at the earlier stage of the disease, which can be obtained from common laboratory testing, seemed to reflect the clinical features of LCPD such as prolonged local inflammation and delayed skeletal maturation. It may be difficult to predict a meaningful prognosis of LCPD with the use of inflammatory markers or common laboratory parameters obtained in the initial stage of the disease.
