Dear Editor,

Monkeypox is caused by the monkeypox virus, a zoonotic orthopox DNA virus related to the virus that causes smallpox, which was first recorded in humans in the Democratic Republic of Congo in 1970 [1,2]. Although monkeypox has previously been endemic in Africa and periodically caused outbreaks, it appears to have shifted from primarily animal-to-human transmission to more frequent human-to-human transmission [3]. According to the World Health Organization, the 2022 monkeypox outbreak is expanding globally and infecting many individuals [1,4]. The monkeypox outbreak has exacerbated the already massive impact of the 2019 coronavirus disease pandemic on healthcare facilities worldwide [5].

Monkeypox virus is transmitted by large respiratory droplets, close or direct contact with skin lesions, and possibly by contaminated mixtures [1]. The classic clinical presentation of human monkeypox includes fever and lymphatic lymphadenopathy, followed by a generalized rash [3]. However, the present global human monkeypox virus pandemic indicates that the biological features of the virus have changed [1]. In the 2022 monkeypox outbreak, the clinical features of the disease differ from its historical presentation. Recent human monkeypox cases have been described as atypical, with the rash affecting fewer regions of the body, sometimes limited to a single region, and lesions manifesting at various phases of the progression of the disease [3]. Monkeypox patients sometimes pose a diagnostic dilemma for physicians due to the absence of the typical rash [4].

Monkeypox can potentially cause symptoms and disease in otolaryngology. I conducted a rapid literature search of the Pubmed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase databases using a combination of keywords (“monkeypox” AND “pharyn* OR throat OR nasal congestion OR cough OR tonsil* OR epiglott* OR peritonsillar abscess”) in order to examine the body of evidence on monkeypox-related otolaryngological manifestations. The initial symptoms in patients with monkeypox can manifest as oropharyngeal symptoms, which include pharyngitis, epiglottitis, odynophagia, and oral or tonsillar lesions [1]. In a cohort of 528 patients with monkeypox from 16 countries, 26 (4.9%) had oropharyngeal symptoms as their initial manifestation [1]. Of these patients, 113 (21.4%) had a clinical presentation of pharyngitis. Patel et al. found that of 197 patients, 27 (13.7%) had oropharyngeal lesions, and nine (4.6%) had tonsillar erythema, pustules, edema or abscess [2]. Patients with severe tonsillar ulcers may also experience dysphagia because of edema of the tissue around the tonsils, which can obstruct the airway and increase the risk of severe consequences [3].

The study by Patel et al. observed sore throat in 33 of 197 (16.8%) patients with monkeypox [2]. Similarly, a study from Spain found that 34 of 185 (18.4%) patients with monkeypox experienced throat pain [3]. Additionally, when patients with monkeypox went to the clinic, they had respiratory symptoms such as nasal congestion, cough and dyspnea [6]. In an observational study of 48 patients with monkeypox from Spain, eight (16.7%) had cough symptoms and four (8.3%) had nasal congestion [6].

Tonsillar lesions may be typical of human monkeypox virus infection [2]. A prospective observational cohort study in Spain revealed that 19 of 181 (10.5%) monkeypox patients suffered tonsillitis [7]. Of the 19 monkeypox patients with tonsillitis, all of whom had white ulcerative lesions on their tonsils and tested negative for group A streptococcal antigen, 18 (94.7%) reported receptive oral sex [7]. Tonsillar signs in monkeypox patients may be associated with receptive oral sex. Another case series from London, the United Kingdom, found that nine of 197 (4.6%) monkeypox patients had tonsillar signs [2]. Two monkeypox patients in this case series experienced tonsillar abscesses [2]. Davido et al. described a monkeypox patient who presented with a peritonsillar abscess and unilateral cervical lymphadenopathy [4]. This patient underwent a cervical lymphadenopathy puncture, and the cervical lymphadenopathy and throat swab specimens were confirmed positive for the monkeypox virus using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [4].

Otolaryngologists should be aware of monkeypox due to the specific presentation of the disease in the throat in order to prevent misdiagnosis. Patel et al. reported that tonsillar abscesses were observed in a patient referred to the otolaryngology center who had previously presented with clinical manifestations of neck pain, submandibular swelling, dysphagia and dyspnea [2]. The patient was confirmed as a monkeypox patient after testing positive for the monkeypox virus by PCR of tonsillar and skin swabs. In 23% of patients, the mucosal lesions were present only in the oropharynx [1]. Otolaryngological clinical signs alone without the characteristic rash may lead to misdiagnosis [4]. Therefore, in the context of the recent monkeypox outbreak, clinicians should obtain a thorough medical history and undertake a comprehensive examination to rule out monkeypox in patients with oral and pharyngeal lesions. If necessary, the patient may be given a multidisciplinary consultation to clarify the diagnosis.

In conclusion, the clinical presentation of patients with monkeypox occurring worldwide remains poorly defined. According to the existing evidence, patients with monkeypox may not present the characteristic rash and exhibit only pharyngeal lesions. Therefore, clinicians should better understand these new clinical characteristics exhibited by monkeypox patients in order to identify and diagnose the disease. Furthermore, it is suggested that the health authorities expand the definition of monkeypox, which will help clinicians to identify monkeypox virus infections more easily.
