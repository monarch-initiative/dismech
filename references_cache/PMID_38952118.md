INTRODUCTION: The clinical features of bi-allelic IGF1 defects are well 
established, i.e., severe growth failure and microcephaly, delayed psychomotor 
development, and sensorineural deafness. However, information on clinical and 
endocrine consequences of heterozygous IGF1 variants and treatment options is 
scarce. We aimed at extending the knowledge base of the clinical presentation 
and growth response to recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) of patients 
carrying such variants.
METHODS: Retrospective case series of patients with pathogenic heterozygous IGF1 
variants.
RESULTS: Nine patients from six families were included, harbouring five whole or 
partial gene deletions and one frameshift variant resulting in a premature stop 
codon (three de novo, one unknown inheritance). In the other two families, 
variants segregated with short stature. Mean (SD) birth length was -1.9 (1.3) 
SDS (n = 7), height -3.8 (0.6) SDS, head circumference -2.5 (0.6) SDS, serum 
IGF-I -1.9 (0.7) SDS, serum IGFBP-3 1.1 (0.4) SDS (n = 7), and GH peak range 
5-31 μg/L (n = 4). Five patients showed feeding problems in infancy. Average 
height increased after 1 and 2 years of rhGH treatment by 0.8 SDS (range 0.3-1.3 
SDS) and 1.3 SDS (range 0.5-2.0 SDS), respectively. Adult height in 2 patients 
was -2.8 and -1.3 SDS, which was, respectively, 1.3 and 2.9 SDS taller than 
predicted before start of treatment.
CONCLUSION: Haploinsufficiency of IGF1 causes a variable phenotype of prenatal 
and postnatal growth failure, microcephaly, feeding difficulties, low/low-normal 
serum IGF-I values in contrast to serum IGFBP-3 in the upper-normal range. 
Treatment with rhGH increased growth in the first 2 years of treatment, and in 2 
patients adult height after treatment was higher than predicted at treatment 
initiation.

The insulin-like growth factor (IGF) system plays a key role in mammalian foetal growth, postnatal growth, brain development, and metabolism [1–4]. IGF-I signals through the type 1 IGF receptor (IGF-IR). It circulates in a large complex with IGF-binding protein 3 (IGFBP-3) and acid-labile subunit (ALS), which increases its half-life and bio-availability. In foetal life, IGF-I levels are mainly influenced by nutrition. After birth, the production of IGF-I, IGFBP-3, and ALS additionally becomes dependent on growth hormone (GH) [5], although nutritional status still influences IGF-I production. Circulating IGF-I is produced primarily by the liver [6], while most tissues also produce IGF-I locally [7, 8]. IGF-I therefore exerts both endocrine and autocrine/paracrine effects, and their respective roles may vary in different tissues and periods of life [9–12]. In rodents, growth is mostly regulated by the local production of IGF-I, as evidenced by mice devoid of autocrine/paracrine IGF-I showing a dramatic decrease in bone accretion [13, 14], and hepatic-Igf1 null mice having normal body size despite a 75–80% decrease in serum IGF-I [15]. However, later experiments in mice expressing Igf1 exclusively in the liver demonstrated that endocrine IGF-I still contributes to approximately 30% of adult body size [9]. In addition, circulating IGF-I is involved in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism, blood pressure, and provides negative feedback to pituitary GH secretion [6, 16–19].

In line with these important functions of IGF-I in pre- and postnatal life, bi-allelic loss-of-function of IGF1 causes extreme pre- and postnatal growth failure, low placental weight, microcephaly, delayed psychomotor development, sensorineural deafness, and insulin resistance and adiposity in adolescence (OMIM #608747) [4, 20, 21]. Other reported clinical features included mild frontal bossing, a triangular face and retrognathia and a normal or exaggerated GH response in stimulation tests. In most cases, serum IGF-I was low, although normal to high levels have also been described [22, 23]. In contrast, serum ALS and IGFBP-3 were usually normal, resulting in a lower IGF-I/IGFBP-3 molar ratio. This has been suggested to reflect lower IGF-I bioavailability [24], as the ratio correlates with free as well as with bioactive IGF-I [25]. Short-term treatment with recombinant human IGF-I (rhIGF-I) or recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) showed a modest effect on growth [22, 26–29].

The observation of relatively short stature and microcephaly in family members with heterozygous variants raised the question whether IGF1 haploinsufficiency may also cause growth failure and whether 1 should screen patients with short stature for this probably more prevalent condition [22]. So far, only a few studies have reported a total of eight families with heterozygous frameshift variants or gene deletions [30–33]. We aimed to further explore the phenotype of IGF1 haploinsufficiency in 9 patients from six families, and document the growth response to rhGH in eight, including adult height data for 2 patients.

In this descriptive case series, patients with (likely) pathogenic (class 4 and 5) [34, 35] heterozygous variants in IGF1 were collected from university hospitals in the Netherlands. Nine patients from six families were included, harbouring whole (n = 3) or partial (n = 2) gene deletions and one frameshift variant resulting in a premature stop codon. Five subjects carried a deletion encompassing more genes than IGF1 alone; however, none of these genes were associated with the patients’ phenotypes (details in online suppl. Information; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000540053). Patients 2, 3, and 4 (family 2) were previously described by our group [30]. At that time, patients 2 and 3 were 8 and 6 years old, respectively. In the current report, we describe their growth response to rhGH treatment until attainment of adult height.

Data were collected from patient records. Reference data were used to calculate birth weight and head circumference SDS [36], height SDS [37], target height SDS [38], predicted adult height (PAH) [39], sitting height/height index SDS [40], and body mass index (BMI) SDS [41]. Standardized protocols were used for hormone measurements by local hospitals. Serum IGF-I was measured in the laboratories of the local hospitals. For most patients, IGF-I SDS was calculated by these laboratories. When IGF-I was only reported in mass units (nmol/L), we used a reported reference for children [42] and an in-house reference for adults to calculate SDS. In 2 patients, an IGF-I generation test was performed [43]. Free IGF-I was measured in 1 patient manually using the AnshLabs Free IGF-I kit (AL-122, Ansh-Labs, Houston, TX, USA). The assay employs a two-site antibody principle to measure the dissociable fraction of IGF-I. In house reference, values were used to calculate free IGF-I SDS.

Genomic DNA was isolated using the Chemagen automated DNA isolation work station (Janus Chemagic 360, Perkin Elmer). Single nucleotide variation and copy number variation (CNV) analysis of the IGF1 gene (NM_000618.5) was performed using Sanger sequencing and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (SALSA MLPA Probemix P262 GHI, MRC Holland) according to standard procedures (details available upon request). Single nucleotide variation and CNV classification was performed using ACMG/AMP criteria [34, 35, 44]. Genome-wide CNV analysis was performed using Affymetrix CytoScan HD array, Illumina Infinium CytoSNP-850K BeadChip or Illumina Infinium GSA+MD-24 v1.0 Beadchip according to standard procedures (details available upon request).

Patient 1 is a 5-year-old boy. He received phototherapy for unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia after birth, and nasogastric tube feeding for 2 weeks because of insufficient drinking technique. He showed mild frontal bossing and a small chin. The de novo heterozygous interstitial deletion of at least 5.2 Mb includes 38 protein-coding genes. He started rhGH treatment at 1.5 years and height SDS increased by 2.5 SDS after 3 years. IGF-I levels during treatment varied between 1.0 and 2.4 SDS. At 2 years of age, he showed functional gastroparesis and received another 4 months of nasogastric tube feeding followed by an unexplained sharp rise in weight (BMI 3.5 SDS).

Patient 2 is a 25-year-old woman who had suffered from perinatal asphyxia. She showed insufficient drinking techniques improving after logopaedic support and received physical therapy for motor developmental delay. The IGF-I generation test showed partial GH resistance, with an insufficient IGF-I rise (<1 SDS) on a dose of 0.7 mg and 1.4 mg/m2 day, but a 2.0 SDS rise on 2.8 mg/m2 day. A maternal heterozygous duplication of four nucleotides in IGF1 was found, causing a frameshift resulting in a premature stop codon. We previously reported that the mutated IGF-I in this family does not bind to the IGF-I receptor [45]. She was treated with rhGH from 7.5 to 15.4 years, with IGF-I levels varying between −0.8 and −0.2 SDS. She had menarche at 13.8 years. Adult height was −2.8 SDS, 1.3 SDS taller than predicted at start.

Her brother (patient 3) presents with the same IGF1 variant and has a 47,XYY karyotype. He was treated with rhGH from 6.8 to 17.4 years, with IGF-I levels ranging between −1.3 and −0.9 SDS. His adult height was −1.3 SDS, 2.9 SDS taller than predicted. Their mother (height −3.7 SDS), patient 4, had feeding problems during infancy and had her menarche at 11 years.

The maternal grandfather also carried the IGF1 variant, had short stature (−2.7 SDS), and a history of failure to thrive. He became overweight and suffered of early dementia and chronic diarrhoea [30]. The father of patient 2 and 3 does not carry the variant (height −1.2 SDS). In the family of the mother (patient 4), her sister does not carry the variant (maternal aunt of index case, height −2.0 SDS, IGF-I −0.4 SDS), and neither does their mother (maternal grandmother of index case, height −2.7 SDS). Therefore, the variant segregates with a decrease in height SDS (height SDS range −4.4 to −2.7 compared to a range of −2.7 to −2.0).

Patient 5 is a 14-year-old boy. He showed feeding difficulties at 3 months of age, and remained dependent on dietary interventions throughout childhood. He had a broad forehead with slight midface hypoplasia, a full nose tip and a wide mouth. A heterozygous interstitial deletion of at least 1.2 Mb was found, covering 11 protein-coding genes. In the first 1.3 years on rhGH treatment (started at age 12.2 years), he remained prepubertal, IGF-I increased to −0.2 and 1.1 SDS and height velocity and height SDS increased considerably (Table 3).

The deletion was inherited from his untreated father (height −4.2 SDS). The short stature of his mother (−3.2 SDS) has remained unexplained. One of his brothers was short (height −2.7 SDS) and also carries the variant (patient 6). He had mild feeding difficulties in infancy and presented with a broad forehead and slightly flat midface. After rhGH was started at 4.3 years, height SDS increased by 0.4 SDS after 1 year, while serum IGF-I increased from −0.6 to 0.4 SDS. Two additional brothers of patient 5 and 6 have normal stature and were not tested.

Patient 7 is a 15-year-old short boy presenting with a proximal insertion of thumbs. An IGF-I generation test showed partial GH resistance with an insufficient rise of IGF-I on 0.7 mg/m2 day and 2.0 SDS increase on 1.4 mg/m2 day. A pathogenic heterozygous interstitial deletion of at least 217 kb was found, containing exons 1 and 2 of IGF1. The variant was not present in his mother (height −1.8 SDS), and his father (height −1.6 SDS) was not tested. The patient was also diagnosed with Klinefelter syndrome (karyotype 47,XXY), has a normal IQ, received speech therapy, and experienced issues with concentration, memory and behaviour at school. He started rhGH at 3.2 years old and at 13.4 years of age an aromatase inhibitor was added. Serum IGF-I values during treatment varied between −2.1 and −0.0 SDS. At 15.2 years, all medications were discontinued by the patient. Current height is −2.2 SDS and bone age is delayed by 1.8 years, resulting in a PAH of −1.6 SDS.

Patient 8, an 11-year-old boy of Iraqi descent, carries a de novo heterozygous interstitial deletion of at least 487.8 kb, including exons 3–4 of IGF1 and four other genes. At 10.9 years, rhGH and a GnRH analogue were started, resulting in a clear growth acceleration in the first year, while IGF-I levels during treatment were around −1.0 SDS.

The clinical presentation of patient 9, a 7.5-year-old girl, included a unilateral duplicated collecting system of the kidney, mild bilateral clinodactyly of her fifth digits, and mild developmental delay. A de novo heterozygous interstitial deletion of at least 2.2 Mb encompasses 38 genes including IGF1 was detected prenatally. In the first month after birth, IGF-I was −1.9 SDS. However, at 3.4 years old, IGF-I was 1.0 SDS, and she exhibited high IGF-I values during rhGH treatment, which was started at 4.8 years (online suppl. Fig. 1). The cause of the high IGF-I values is not known. No variants were detected in IGF1R or PAPPA2, nor in IGF1 in the wild-type allele. Her free IGF-I during treatment was −0.23 SDS, measured during a concomitant total IGF-I of +2.9 SDS. The total height gain during 2 years of treatment was 1.1 SDS.

We analysed 9 patients with heterozygous IGF1 variants from six families and observed severe proportionate short stature (mean −3.8 SDS), moderate microcephaly (mean −2.5 SDS), early feeding difficulties, low serum IGF-I values in combination with IGFBP-3 in the upper half of the reference range, sufficient stimulated GH peaks and moderately low birth weight (−1.7 SDS) and length (−1.9 SDS).

All IGF1 variants were classified as pathogenic. Three were de novo, and inheritance was unknown in 1 case. There was clear segregation with short stature in families 2, 3 and 7. In three of the five deletions, the whole IGF1 gene was deleted, in the other two only two exons (1–2 or 3–4). Four IGF1 deletions were part of a relatively large deletion containing 4–38 genes. Most of these genes are only associated with autosomal recessive diseases and besides IGF1, none of these were associated with the patients’ phenotypes.

Eight patients were treated with rhGH, initiated at an age between 1.5 and 12.2 years. We believe that in prepubertal children, particularly in the very young, delta height SDS is a better parameter of the growth response to treatment than the change in height velocity because of the rapidly decreasing height velocity in healthy children in that age period [46]. In contrast, in adolescents who remain prepubertal during treatment, the delta height SDS tends to underestimate the growth response. This is due to an increase of height gain and spread of the distribution in the reference population, associated with increasing percentages of adolescents entering puberty. In such individuals, the change in height velocity is more informative. In the 6 patients who started rhGH before the age of 10 years, height SDS increased by 1.0 in the first year of treatment, while average height velocity increased from 6.4 to 9.3 cm/year. In the two adolescents (patients 5 and 8) who remained prepubertal, height SDS increased by 0.3 and 0.5, while height velocity increased significantly (+5.3 and +3.4 cm/year, respectively). In the 6 patients in whom 2 year growth data were available, average height velocity remained stable during treatment (9.1 cm/year) and the mean total height SDS gain over 2 years was 1.3. Bone age delay tended to decrease during treatment, so that the effect on adult height may be less than suggested by the 1–2 year results.

Adult height was attained by 2 patients. Patient 2 is short (−2.8 SDS), but 1.0 SDS above height SDS at start, 1.3 SDS above PAH at start of treatment, and 0.9 SD taller than her mother. In patient 3, adult height is −1.3 SDS, 3.3 SDS above height SDS at start, 2.9 SDS higher than PAH at start, and 2.4 SD taller than his mother. Since this patient also carries an XYY karyotype, it is impossible to state whether this remarkably positive adult height gain is fully due to rhGH treatment, or partly due to his karyotype. XYY individuals are known to show macrocephaly, obesity, and some increase in height SDS with age reaching an average adult height of 1.3 SDS [47, 48]. However, we believe that the excellent first year growth response (+1.4 SDS) and the large total height gain are mostly due to rhGH treatment. The same principle may apply to patient 7 with a XXY karyotype, as XXY patients show an average adult height of 0.9 SDS [47]. Collectively, our data suggest that the effect on adult height of rhGH treatment in these patients is comparable to the reported treatment effect of +1.25 SDS in short patients born SGA [49], although substantial inter-patient variability exists.

Although all patients showed an increment in IGF-I SDS of at least 1.0 SDS during treatment, serum IGF-I remained below 0 SDS in 50% of treated children, in spite of supraphysiologic rhGH dosages. This is in line with the results of the IGF-I generation tests in 2 patients, in whom only high GH dosages led to a sufficient response of serum IGF-I. We speculate that the partial GH insensitivity in these patients is due to an already near maximally stimulated expression of the unaffected IGF1 gene by the supposedly high GH secretion due to insufficient negative feedback. No apparent correlation between height SDS gain and rhGH dose, first-year IGF-I increment or serum IGF-I SDS during treatment was observed. Statistical power was insufficient to draw strong conclusions, but in future patients, one may consider to titrate the rhGH dosage on normalisation of serum IGF-I. In addition, the relatively high serum IGFBP-3 in combination with low to low-normal IGF-I is suggestive for a low-free IGF-I concentration and low IGF-I bioavailability. In future studies, we recommend measurement of free IGF-I in untreated patients and during rhGH treatment, to further investigate the pathophysiology of short stature in these patients, and to assess whether the apparent growth response is mediated by an increase of IGF-I bioavailability or a direct effect of rhGH.

Remarkably, patient 9 exhibited IGF-I levels above +2.0 SDS during treatment. Her IGF-I before treatment was −1.8 SDS, but later was +1.0 SDS and thus already relatively high. Her treatment response was good, but not significantly better than the other patients. No variants in IGF1R or missense variants in IGF1 on the wild-type allele were found. There was a discrepancy between her total IGF-I during treatment (+2.8 SDS) and her free IGF-I (−0.2 SDS), pointing towards decreased bioavailability of IGF-I. No variants in PAPPA2 were found. A similar phenomenon has been described in Silver Russell syndrome in those with 11p15 hypomethylation [50], but the Netchine-Harbison score was three and methylation defects were not studied.

It is tempting to speculate that treatment with rhIGF-I or a combination of rhGH and rhIGF-I [51, 52] might yield a larger effect on growth, compared with rhGH alone, in view of the observation that rhGH does not achieve a sufficient amount of circulating IGF-I in several patients. However, so far data regarding long-term effects of rhIGF-I have been mostly limited to patients with Laron syndrome [53]. To our knowledge, there is only one report on the effect of rhIGF-I treatment in a patient with IGF1 haploinsufficiency, showing a poor growth response after 8 months [31]. We believe that arguments against treatment with rhIGF-I in patients with IGF1 haploinsufficiency include: (1) risk of hypoglycaemia (reported in 8% of patients with Laron syndrome) [51, 54]; (2) a suppressive effect on endogenous GH production, potentially leading to a decrease of the direct growth promoting effects of GH; and (3) an expectedly modest effect on growth since paracrine effects of IGF-I may be stronger than endocrine effects.

A remarkable observation in our study is that in two out of five boys aneuploidy was observed. We have no explanation for this finding. To our knowledge, there is only 1 reported case of Klinefelter syndrome and GH resistance, but IGF1 was not sequenced in that patient [55].

Our observations are largely in line with previous reports (summarized in the online suppl. Information). Fuqua et al. [31] reported a boy with short stature (−4.2 SDS) carrying a heterozygous splice-site IGF1 variant with a predicted frameshift resulting in early protein termination. He was born AGA, had normal head circumference, low IGF-I (−2.2 SDS) with normal IGFBP-3, and showed a modest response to rhGH as well as to rhIGF-I (both treatments given for less than a year). The variant segregated with short stature in the family. Batey et al. [32] reported a short boy (−3.1 SDS) with a heterozygous deletion of the entire IGF1 gene. He was born AGA, showed microcephaly, mild micrognathia and epicanthus, bilateral fifth finger clinodactyly, mild developmental delay, and low-normal IGF-I values with normal IGFBP-3. Finally, Staels et al. [33] described 5 patients with short stature and heterozygous deletions of the entire IGF1 gene. Four were born SGA, four had microcephaly, two failure to thrive, three mild learning difficulties, four clinodactyly, and serum IGF-I levels were −2.2 to −0.4 SDS with normal IGFBP-3. Variants segregated with short stature. Three were treated with rhGH, showing an increase in height of 1.3 SDS during 2.7 years of treatment, 1.1 SDS in 3.5 years, and 0.7 SDS in 4 years, respectively. No adult heights were reported. In our 9 patients, we observed less patients with clinodactyly (1 patient) and developmental issues (1 patient), but more feeding problems (5 out of 9) and a better growth response to rhGH treatment.

We additionally found a tenth patient in the Netherlands with a missense variant in IGF1, a class 3 variant of uncertain significance (c.206G>T, p.Arg69Met) and therefore not included in the current study. This 12-year-old girl had severe feeding difficulties in early life, low BMI, low-normal head circumference, and low-normal IGF-I (−1.8 SDS). The variant was inherited from her mother (height −1.6 SDS), and her part of the family included several family members with severe short stature. For further details, we refer to the online supplementary Information.

The here presented expanded number of reported patients with IGF1 haploinsufficiency, in combination with previous observations in patients with bi-allelic IGF1 defects or mono- or bi-allelic IGF1R defects, can provide a view on the role of IGF-I in the presence of normal GH secretion in human physiology. There is no doubt that a normal IGF-I secretion and sensitivity is needed for normal linear and cranial growth in pre-and postnatal life. In contrast, an impaired function of IGF-II as in most patients with Silver-Russell syndrome, is associated with relative macrocephaly [56]. Since sensorineural deafness has only been observed in patients with absence of any IGF-I function [21], sufficient compensatory mechanisms in inner ear development can apparently prevent hearing loss in patients with some remaining IGF-I functionality. Based on observations in patients with bi- or mono-allelic IGF1 defects or mono-allelic IGF1R defects, a normal exposure to IGF-I appears to be important for the regulation of appetite and feeding. This may be related to the expression of Igf1r in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus in rats as well as humans [57], critical for the regulation of feeding and metabolism [58, 59]. The well-known association between early feeding problems and Silver-Russell syndrome (OMIM #180860), which is in approximately 60% of cases associated with a downregulated expression of the IGF2 gene or other genes on chromosome 11p15.5, suggests that the same applies to IGF-II [60].

The current study is the largest detailed description of patients with IGF1 haploinsufficiency to date and doubles the number of reported cases. It is also the first report to describe the effect of rhGH with inclusion of adult height data. A possible limitation of our study is the risk of selection bias, as short stature was the reason for genetic investigation in all index cases. Nevertheless, all of our patients harboured pathogenic gene variants, the phenotype was remarkably consistent with published cases of IGF1 or IGF1R haploinsufficiency, and there was clear segregation with short stature within families. Lastly, the retrospective nature of the study, which is inherent to studying rare diseases, may have led to variable ascertainment of specific clinical features.

In conclusion, the current study shows that IGF1 haploinsufficiency due to pathogenic IGF1 variants cause pre- and postnatal impaired linear growth, microcephaly, early feeding difficulties and decreased serum IGF-I, in contrast to a serum IGFBP-3 in the upper half of the reference range and normal stimulated GH peaks. We advise to screen for IGF1 variants when short stature is accompanied by either of these symptoms. Treatment with rhGH increases linear growth and may increase adult height. The results offer clinicians and geneticists guidance on whom to screen for IGF1 defects, on the clinical phenotype that can be used to mirror patients carrying IGF1 variants of unknown significance, and provides useful information on the results of rhGH treatment in this condition.
