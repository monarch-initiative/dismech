# Spatial control of parathyroid hormone receptor-1 signaling dictates ligand-specific biological outcomes.
**Authors:** Höppner J, Jüppner H, Reyes M, Yadav PS, Wang D, Dean T, Hanna P, Civitelli R, Inoue A, Schipani E, Portales-Castillo I, Gardella TJ
**Journal:** Signal Transduct Target Ther (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41392-026-02795-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41392-026-02795-1)

## Content

Parathyroid hormone receptor-1 (PTH1R) is a class B GPCR that regulates mineral ion homeostasis via parathyroid hormone (PTH) and skeletal development via parathyroid hormone-related peptide (PTHrP). While PTHrP signaling is thought to occur predominantly at the plasma membrane, PTH is considered to also signal from endosomes; however, the mechanisms that encode subcellular PTH1R actions, as well as the biological relevance of plasma membrane vs internalized PTH1R activation, remain unclear. Here, by examining the Eiken syndrome mutant R485X-PTH1R, in which C-tail truncation removes key βarrestin docking sites, we identified βarrestins as critical modulators of compartmentalized PTH1R signaling in response to endogenous ligands. Patients with Eiken syndrome display delayed bone mineralization, consistent with enhanced PTHrP actions, as well as hypocalcemia along with elevated PTH, which paradoxically indicates impaired PTH function. Mechanistic studies in cell-based assays and humanized R485X-PTH1R knock-in mice revealed that βarrestins dampen PTHrP-driven Gαs signaling at the plasma membrane but promote PTH-induced endosomal PTH1R signaling. βarrestins thus appear to impose bimodal, compartment-specific modulation of PTH1R signaling, thereby resolving the paradoxical Eiken phenotype. The findings support the notion that differences in subcellular PTH1R localization determine ligand-specific signaling outcomes in skeletal physiology and mineral ion homeostasis.

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) comprise the largest family of membrane receptors and regulate diverse physiological processes.1,2 The parathyroid hormone (PTH) receptor-1 (PTH1R) is a class B1 GPCR3,4 that is expressed in bone, kidney, and growth plate cartilage5 and is a key pharmacological target for disorders of bone and mineral metabolism, such as osteoporosis and hypoparathyroidism.6 A distinctive feature of PTH1R is that it responds to two endogenous peptide ligands, PTH and parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP), to control two distinct biological outcomes.7–10 PTH is secreted by the parathyroid glands and acts in bone and kidney to maintain calcium and phosphate homeostasis (Fig. 1a),11 whereas PTHrP is produced locally in growth plate cartilage and acts to control bone development (Fig. 1b).11–14 Both ligands stimulate receptor coupling to Gαs to result in rapid increases in intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) as the principal second messenger signaling molecule.7 Recent studies suggest that whereas PTHrP signals transiently mainly from the cell surface, PTH can additionally promote sustained cAMP signaling from endosomes.15–20 This difference in spatiotemporal signaling for PTH vs PTHrP peptides was initially revealed in cell-based assays; For PTH1R, as for many GPCRs, the C-terminal tail plays a critical role in trafficking the receptor to distinct subcellular compartments22,23 and thereby acts to shape the overall spatiotemporal signaling response that occurs in the different target cells. The importance of the PTH1R C-terminal tail in human physiology is underscored by the identification of a homozygous PTH1R mutation, R485X, which removes most of the receptor’s C-terminal tail, in patients with Eiken syndrome.24,25 Patients with this ultrarare disease present with a paradoxical phenotype, i.e. delayed bone mineralization—consistent with enhanced PTHrP-mediated PTH1R signaling during skeletal development13,26 and elevated PTH serum levels without corresponding increases in calcium levels24,25,27–30—suggesting impaired PTH-mediated PTH1R signaling in bone and kidney cells.31

The C-tail truncation in R485X-PTH1R removes serine and threonine phosphorylation sites that are critical for efficient recruitment of βarrestins.32–34 βarrestins are multifunctional coupling proteins originally identified as being involved in terminating GPCR signaling.35 More recently, however, βarrestins have also been shown to facilitate endocytosis as well as to promote the assembly of signaling complexes, including with mitogen-activated protein kinases36–38 or G proteins at endosomes, to thereby promote and extend the signaling response.39–44 βarrestins are thus now perceived to have more complex roles in shaping the overall spatiotemporal signaling profile attained by a given GPCR upon agonist activation.45–47 Such considerations raise the possibility that altered βarrestin binding to the R485X Eiken syndrome PTH1R mutant results in a shift in the balance of signaling that occurs at the plasma membrane vs at endosomes and thus differentially impacts the responses to endogenous PTH and PTHrP in target cells. This scenario, if confirmed, could provide an explanation for the paradoxical phenotype seen in Eiken syndrome patients and further improve understanding of basic PTH1R biology.

Here, we utilize cell-based signaling assays together with genetically engineered mice to dissect the spatial signaling properties and physiological consequences of the R485X-PTH1R mutant of Eiken syndrome. We find that impaired βarrestin recruitment by R485X-PTH1R leads to amplified Gαs signaling at the plasma membrane in response to PTHrP, which in turn correlates with delayed bone mineralization in mice. In addition, impaired βarrestin binding to R485X-PTH1R leads to diminished PTH-driven cAMP signaling at endosomes, which in turn correlates with blunted PTH-induced elevations in calcium and 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D (1,25(OH)2D) levels in mice. These findings not only help resolve the paradoxical phenotype observed in Eiken syndrome patients but also support the notion that βarrestins serve as pivotal modulators of compartmentalized PTH1R signaling and thus differentially dictate responsiveness to PTH vs PTHrP. The findings thus support a physiological relevance of spatially encoded signaling for a GPCR involved in skeletal development and calcium homeostasis. The results may also have relevance for the development of spatially biased PTH1R-targeted therapeutics.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms by which bone development and calcium homeostasis are impacted in Eiken syndrome and thus explain the paradoxical phenotype toward PTH vs PTHrP signaling seen in patients, we generated humanized hPTH1R mice expressing the R485X allele using oviductal nucleic acid delivery (i-GONAD)48–50 (Supplementary Fig. S1a–c). Homozygous R485X-hPTH1R mice exhibited shortened tails compared to their WT-hPTH1R littermates (hereafter WT mice) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. S1d). Heterozygous R485X-hPTH1R mice were phenotypically indistinguishable from WT mice (Supplementary Fig. S1d, e); therefore, subsequent investigations focused on homozygous R485X-hPTH1R mice (hereafter: R485X mice).

We examined the bone morphology of dissected skeletons obtained from WT and R485X mice at different times during development. Whole-mount skeletal staining in R485X mice revealed markedly reduced mineralization at postnatal day (P) 3, which was most pronounced in carpal/metacarpal and tarsal/metatarsal bones as well as tail vertebrae (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. S2). The mineralization defects were largely resolved by P21–P60, while abnormal bone shape persisted (Supplementary Fig. S2). Such a normalization in bone mineralization is also observed in Eiken patients.25,28

Histology and spatial transcriptomics analyses showed that at embryonic day (E) 15.5, tibiae of WT mice contained well-organized growth plates with defined zones of Col2-positive proliferative, Ihh-positive prehypertrophic, and Col10-positive hypertrophic chondrocytes, adjacent to a zone of mineralized spongiosa (Fig. 1e; Supplementary Fig. S3). The R485X tibial growth plates lacked such well-defined zones and instead showed only a few dispersed Col10-positive hypertrophic chondrocytes (Fig. 1e), little or no mineralized spongiosa and were composed predominantly of Col2-positive chondrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S3). At birth (P0), the mutant growth plates appeared greatly widened and showed reduced Ihh expression (Supplementary Fig. S3a) as well as reduced mineralization (Fig. 1f). At P21, the secondary ossification center (SOC) was underdeveloped, but by P60, the mutant growth plates were indistinguishable from WT (Supplementary Fig. S4a). This normalization in growth plate morphology was paralleled by a partial catch-up in long bone length, which was markedly reduced in R485X mice at P21 but almost comparable to WT mice by P90 (Supplementary Fig. S4b). The R485X mice thus exhibit impaired growth plate chondrocyte differentiation and delayed bone mineralization, which parallels the skeletal phenotype observed in Eiken syndrome patients. As PTHrP acts in developing growth plates to prevent premature mineralization (Supplementary Fig. 1a),13,51 the growth plate phenotype of R485X mice suggests a gain-of-function effect toward PTHrP actions in this target tissue.26,49,52

We then examined the impact of the R485X mutation on calcium homeostasis, as mediated by PTH. Three-month-old R485X mice were hypocalcemic and showed reduced serum 1,25(OH)2D, with significantly elevated levels of endogenous PTH (Fig. 1g; Supplementary Fig. S5). Concomitantly, urinary cAMP levels were reduced (Fig. 1g; Supplementary Fig. S5). The combined observation of elevated PTH, hypocalcemia, low serum 1,25(OH)2D, and reduced urinary cAMP is consistent with PTH resistance and hence a loss-of-function effect of the R485X mutation toward actions of this ligand in the kidney. Levels of circulating FGF23 and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) measured at several postnatal time points (P3-P90) were not different between WT and R485X mice (Supplementary Fig. S5), suggesting that the R485X mutation did not cause a general defect in kidney function. Together, the findings demonstrate that R485X mice recapitulate the delayed bone mineralization phenotype of Eiken syndrome patients, consistent with a gain-of-function effect towards PTHrP in growth plates, along with PTH resistance, suggesting a paradoxical loss-of-function effect toward PTH.

To dissect the specific mechanisms underlying the paradoxical phenotype observed in R485X Eiken syndrome patients and mice, we utilized cell-based assays to first analyze the effect of the R485X-PTH1R mutation on PTHrP-induced signaling. We previously showed that the R485X mutation results in reduced βarrestin recruitment as well as increased basal and PTHrP-dependent cAMP signaling in HEK293 cells.32 We therefore hypothesized that diminished βarrestin binding to R485X-PTH1R results in enhanced coupling of the mutant receptor to Gαs at the plasma membrane and hence elevated cAMP signaling responses to PTHrP. To further explore this model, we utilized a phosphorylation-deficient (PD)-PTH1R in which 9 phosphorylatable serine and threonine residues in the C-tail are replaced by alanines (Supplementary Fig. S6a).33 In line with previous data, both the R485X- and PD-PTH1R mutants showed impaired βarrestin recruitment to the plasma membrane as measured in a NanoBiT bystander assay in which smBiT-tagged βarrestin2 was paired with LgBiT-CAAX (Supplementary Fig. S6b) and likewise exhibited reduced colocalization with yellow fluorescent protein (YFP)-tagged βarrestin2 upon binding TMRPTH(1-34) visualized by fluorescence microscopy (Supplementary Fig. S6c).

To specifically investigate PTH1R-mediated G protein activation, we used the GsOneGo BRET reporter, consisting of the Gαs subunit tagged with YFP as a BRET acceptor and a membrane-anchored (MAS; membrane anchoring sequence) nanoluciferase tagged with a detector module that recognizes active-state Gαs at the plasma membrane as the BRET donor.53 This MAS-GsOneGo biosensor was stably expressed in HEK293-derived cells (HGO24 cells), which were supertransfected with either WT-, R485X- or PD-PTH1R. Both R485X- and PD-PTH1R showed, as compared to WT-PTH1R, elevated basal levels of Gαs activation (Supplementary Fig. S6d), left-shifted PTHrP(1-36) peptide-dose response profiles (Supplementary Fig. S6e), and persistent Gαs activation after PTHrP(1-36) washout (Fig. 2a). The R485X- and PD-PTH1R mutants also exhibited an enhanced cAMP response to PTHrP(1-36) stimulation, as compared to WT-PTH1R and measured after ligand washout in HEK293 cells stably expressing the glosensor cAMP reporter (Supplementary Fig. S6f; empty vector). To further investigate the relationship between βarrestins and PTH1R/Gαs mediated signaling, we used small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) to deplete βarrestin1 and 2 expression in HEK293-derived cells. Quantification of βarrestin2, the predominant βarrestin isoform in HEK293 cells,54 confirmed a nearly complete siRNA-mediated knockdown of the target protein (Supplementary Fig. S7a). Cotransfection of HGO24 cells with WT-PTH1R and βarrestin1/2 siRNAs resulted in moderately increased basal levels of activated Gαs at the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. S7b) and increased the potency and maximal response of PTHrP(1-36) stimulation (Supplementary Fig. S7c), including following ligand washout (Fig. 2b). In HEK293-derived GP2.3 cells stably expressing WT-PTH1R and the glosensor cAMP reporter, siRNA-mediated βarrestin1/2 knockdown resulted in increased basal cAMP generation (Supplementary Fig. S7d) as well as enhanced cAMP generation following PTHrP stimulation measured after ligand washout (Supplementary Fig. S7e).

These data overall are consistent with the model that with the intact WT-PTH1R, βarrestins bind initially to the phosphorylated receptor C-tail and then subsequently engage the receptor’s TMD core to thus sterically hinder G protein binding and terminate cAMP signaling.34 This bipartite mode of βarrestin binding further predicts that the C-tail component of the interaction stabilizes but is not essential for the TMD core component of the interaction, which by itself is sufficient to hinder G protein coupling (Supplementary Fig. S7f). To test this model further, we overexpressed βarrestin2 in HEK293 cells transiently expressing WT-, R485X- or PD-PTH1R and measured the effects on signaling. We thus observed that overexpression of βarrestin2 suppressed basal Gαs activation at the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. S6d) and right-shifted the PTHrP(1-36) dose-response curve for each PTH1R variant (Fig. 2c–e). Overexpression of βarrestin2 further increased the maximal Gαs activation response attained by R485X- and PD-PTH1R, which was not apparent with WT-PTH1R. A possible explanation for the increase in response maxima is that βarrestins enhance the folding and/or trafficking of the C-tail mutant receptors to thereby increase the number of receptors at the cell surface, but the specific underlying mechanisms remain to be determined.

Similar to the results in HGO24 cells, βarrestin2 overexpression blunted the otherwise enhanced basal cAMP responses by R485X- and PD-PTH1R (Supplementary Fig. S7g). This suppressive effect was, however, not observed for βarrestin2dFLR, a construct deleted for the finger-loop region which mediates the contacts with the GPCRs TMD core (Supplementary Fig. S7g).55 These results indicate that deficient engagement of R485X-PTH1R with βarrestins due to loss of the PTH1R C-tail interaction leads to impaired binding of βarrestins to the PTH1R TMD core and thus to enhanced Gαs signaling from the plasma membrane, both basally and in response to PTHrP ligand stimulation.

Our in vitro data demonstrate that the R485X mutation reduces βarrestin interaction and thus leads to hypersensitivity to PTHrP stimulation. To test the relevance of this model in vivo, we ablated endogenous PTHrP in growth plate chondrocytes of R485X mice, as we hypothesized that this would confer a rescue effect on the proposed PTHrP hypersensitivity in bone mineralization. Monoallelic ablation of PTHrP in proliferative chondrocytes (R485X-PTH1R; Col2Cre; PTHrP+/f) increased the tail length relative to that in R485X mice (R485X-PTH1R; Col2Cre; PTHrP+/+) (Fig. 3a) and enhanced bone mineralization at P3 and P7 (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. S8a–c). Histology of the tail vertebrae revealed that monoallelic PTHrP ablation in R485X mice resulted in the formation of a nascent hypertrophic zone in the center of the vertebrae, which in R485X mice consisted only of proliferative cells (Fig. 3c). Tibial histology of newborn (P0) R485X mice showed that PTHrP ablation resulted in a gene-dose-dependent shortening of the growth plate (Supplementary Fig. S8d). In addition, heterozygous PTHrP ablation restored the mineralization in the secondary ossification center observed at P21 in R485X mice (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Fig. S8b). To further study the effect of the R485X mutation on growth plate chondrocytes, we used an ex vivo system of primary chondrocytes isolated from the costal arches of 3-day-old R485X and WT mice. After 10 days in culture, the expression of Col10 (collagen 10), a marker of chondrocyte differentiation, was reduced in chondrocytes obtained from R485X mice, as compared to WT mice (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Fig. S10a), thus parallelling the histologic findings in embryonic mice (Fig. 1e). The finding of reduced expression of Col10 as well as reduced Ihh and PTHrP, which is positively regulated by Ihh, in chondrocytes of R485X mice is consistent with increased signaling by the mutant PTH1R, leading to suppressed chondrocyte differentiation.13,57,58 Treatment of WT and R485X chondrocytes with a PTHrP(7-36) antagonist peptide59 increased Col10 expression (Fig. 3h, i; Supplementary Fig. S10a), further supporting the notion that the R485X mutation confers a gain-of-function effect on chondrocyte differentiation ex vivo that is dependent on locally produced PTHrP.

Next, we tested whether pharmacological inhibition of PTH1R signaling could rescue the growth plate defect in R485X mice in vivo. As this defect was detectable histologically as early as E15.5, we treated the mice in utero by subcutaneously implanting an osmotic infusion pump containing either vehicle or a PTHrP(7-36) antagonist peptide59 into a pregnant female on day 5 post coitus and analyzed the offspring at birth (P0). Histological examination of isolated tibias from R485X pups revealed that PTHrP(7-36) treatment resulted in longer bones and increased mineralization, as compared to tibial sections of vehicle-treated R485X pups (Fig. 3j; Supplementary Fig. S10b). These findings provide in vivo evidence that the growth plate phenotype caused by the R485X mutation arises from excess PTH1R signaling induced by endogenous PTHrP. Moreover, they establish that a pharmacological intervention targeted to the PTH1R can act directly within the growth plate and thus restore skeletal development in this preclinical disease model mouse.

As noted above, previous studies suggest that PTHrP primarily drives cAMP production from the plasma membrane, whereas PTH additionally signals from endosomes.16,17,60 This spatial bias in PTH1R signaling presumably derives from structural differences of the two ligands10 but may additionally involve compartment-specific actions of βarrestins, by which βarrestin either terminates PTH1R signaling at the plasma membrane, as supported by our findings, or promotes signaling at endosomes, as shown for other GPCRs and suggested for the PTH1R.16,40,41,47,61 We therefore hypothesized that impaired βarrestin engagement by R485X- or PD-PTH1R results in blunted PTH-driven endosomal signaling. To examine this, we first quantified PTH1R internalization using pH-sensitive GFP (pHlourin2), which was inserted into the extracellular domain (ECD) of each PTH1R variant. This modified pH-GFP shows enhanced fluorescence at the acidic pH (<6) of mature endosomes relative to the fluorescence at neutral pH at the plasma membrane.62 In cells expressing WT-pHlourin2-PTH1R, PTH(1-34) elicited a robust increase in GFP fluorescence, consistent with receptor accumulation in acidic intracellular compartments, whereas both R485X- and PD-pHlourin2-PTH1R exhibited reduced GFP fluorescence upon PTH(1-34) addition (Fig. 5b; Supplementary Fig. S11a). Thus, the loss of C-tail phosphorylation impaired PTH1R trafficking into acidic endosomes. Some increase in GFP fluorescence was nevertheless detected for each mutant, suggesting that both C-tail mutants can traffic to endosomes. Consistent with this, analysis of HEK293 cells transiently transfected to express WT-, R485X- or PD-PTH1R along with an early endosomal marker (FYVE-GFP) revealed ligand-induced TMRPTH/GFP colocalized clusters for the WT and mutant receptors (Supplementary Fig. S11b). These findings support the notion that both C-tail mutants retain a partial capacity to internalize into early endosomes upon PTH(1-34) stimulation.

We then investigated whether the R485X- and PD-PTH1R mutations impair endosomal Gαs signaling. First, we used derivatives of mini-Gs, which is an engineered Gαs probe that preferentially binds active-state receptors and enables visualization/quantification of receptor-G protein engagement in living cells.63 By fluorescence microscopy, we confirmed mini-Gs recruitment to WT-PTH1R by observing colocalization of NES-mini-Gs-venus with TMRPTH(1-34) (Supplementary Fig. S11c). We next quantified receptor-G protein engagement at endosomes using FYVE-LgBit paired with mini-Gs-smBiT in a NanoBiT assay. We found that both R485X- and PD-PTH1R exhibited diminished G protein engagement at endosomes following PTH(1-34) stimulation, as compared to WT-PTH1R (Fig. 5c). We then generated an HA-tagged 2xFYVE-GsOneGo biosensor that specifically localizes to endosomes (Supplementary Fig. S11d) and found that endosomal Gαs activation for both the R485X- and PD-PTH1R mutants was attenuated, as compared to WT-PTH1R following PTH(1-34) stimulation (Fig. 5d). This finding contrasts with the enhanced Gαs activation response for the R485X and PD mutants at the plasma membrane (Fig. 5a).

Next, we investigated the role of βarrestins in modulating PTH-induced Gαs activation by the WT-PTH1R at either the plasma membrane or endosomes. To this end, we transiently cotransfected HEK293 cells stably expressing WT-PTH1R (GP2.3 cells) with either the plasma membrane (MAS)- or endosome (2xFYVE)-anchored GsOneGo sensor together with either control siRNA or siRNAs targeting βarrestin1 and 2. Depletion of βarrestins increased the potency of the PTH(1-34)-induced Gαs activation by WT-PTH1R at the plasma membrane (Fig. 5e), consistent with our observations for PTHrP(1-36) (Fig. 2b). In contrast, βarrestin depletion blunted the PTH(1-34)-induced Gαs activation response by WT-PTH1R at endosomes (Fig. 5f). These results imply compartment-specific differences in the effect of βarrestin on PTH1R signaling, in that βarrestins impair signaling at the plasma membrane but promote signaling at endosomes. Notably, βarrestin knockdown did not significantly impair WT-PTH1R internalization to endosomes (Supplementary Fig. S11e, f), consistent with βarrestin-independent modes of PTH1R internalization, as previously proposed for the PTH1R and other GPCRs,54,64–67 and hence a more direct role of βarrestins in promoting Gαs coupling to PTH1R localized at endosomes.

Indeed, recent studies by others suggest that β arrestinscan facilitate the assembly of GPCR-G protein-βarrestin signaling complexes at endosomes and thereby enable intracellular cAMP generation.40,44,47,61,68 As both R485X- and PD-PTH1R retained some capacity for endosomal trafficking, we hypothesized that loss of C-tail phosphorylation in these mutants impairs their capacity to form such signaling complexes and thus to stimulate intracellular cAMP signals. To test this hypothesis, we first examined the recruitment of βarrestin2 and Gβ1 to endosomes after PTH(1-34) stimulation. In agreement with our prior BRET analyses,32 both mutants exhibited significantly reduced βarrestin2 recruitment to endosomes, as compared to WT-PTH1R (Fig. 5g). Moreover, in cells expressing C-tail mutant PTH1Rs, endosomal Gβ1-smBiT recruitment after PTH(1-34) treatment was impaired, as compared to WT-PTH1R (Supplementary Fig. S11g). To more directly assess whether PTH1R can assemble an endosomal signaling complex containing βarrestin and Gαs, we employed a tripartite BRET-based assay using 2xFYVE-LgBiT together with βarrestin2-smBiT as the donor and NES-mini-Gs-venus as the acceptor. Stimulation with PTH(1-34) induced a robust dose-dependent BRET signal in cells expressing WT-PTH1R, consistent with the assembly of an endosomal PTH1R-Gαs-βarrestin signaling complex. In contrast, the signal was significantly blunted in cells expressing either R485X- or PD-PTH1R (Fig. 5h).

Here, we investigated the significance of spatiotemporal control of PTH1R signaling in mediating the responses to the two distinct endogenous peptide ligands for this GPCR, PTHrP and PTH. Our findings support (1) a key role for spatial compartmentalization of PTH1R signaling in mediating the responses to PTH vs PTHrP and (2) that βarrestins are critical mediators of this compartmentalization effect and thereby limit PTHrP signaling to the plasma membrane while promoting PTH signaling in endosomes.

For PTHrP actions in growth plate chondrocytes, our findings are consistent with a model by which locally produced PTHrP acts to regulate the precise timing of cell differentiation70 by activating PTH1R signaling transiently at the cell surface.12,13,60 Our findings imply that this signaling is terminated by βarrestin binding to the receptor’s TMD core and thereby outcompeting G proteins. βarrestin binding would thus limit the duration and amplitude of the signaling produced upon PTHrP stimulation at the plasma membrane and desensitize the receptor to prevent additional rounds of activation by new PTHrP binding events. A deficiency in βarrestin recruitment to the R485X-PTH1R removes this constraint and hence amplifies the PTHrP-induced signaling output, which leads to the delayed chondrocyte differentiation and bone mineralization observed in Eiken syndrome patients and model mice. Consistent with this interpretation, pharmacological inhibition of PTH1R signaling using a PTH antagonist peptide restored growth plate architecture in R485X mice, providing in vivo proof-of-principle support for the rationale of using therapeutic intervention to correct bone as well as mineral ion pathologies associated with conditions of excessive PTH1R signaling.

Notably, patients with brachydactyly type E (BDE) were recently reported, that carry either a E465K or E469K mutation in PTH1R.71 BDE is characterized by premature mineralization of metacarpal and metatarsal bones, i.e., a phenotype opposite to that observed in Eiken syndrome. Remarkably, we found that the E469K mutation increases βarrestin recruitment to the PTH1R in vitro and thereby limits G protein coupling to the receptor at the plasma membrane.72 These findings further point to a key role for βarrestins in regulating and terminating PTH1R signaling during skeletal development. Collectively, these observations are consistent with the notion that PTHrP is distributed in developing tissue along a concentration gradient70 that acts in concert with intracellular βarrestin to control the extent and timing of cell signaling responses that underlie the proper formation of the mature tissue.

For PTH, our findings suggest that βarrestins act to promote PTH1R signaling in endosomes to maintain normal calcium homeostasis, which is physiologically achieved by low picomolar concentrations of the hormone in the serum. The data thus imply that this mode of internalized signaling by PTH is needed to induce an appropriate increase in the transcriptional regulation of 1,25(OH)2D synthesis, consistent with previous findings in Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells and mice.20 In this context of PTH signaling, impaired βarrestin recruitment to the PTH1R, as observed with R485X-PTH1R or with the biased analog dLeu7-PTH(1-34),20 results in a loss-of-function effect manifested as inadequately low blood calcium as well as low 1,25(OH)2D levels despite substantially elevated serum PTH. Notably, while our in vitro data show that ablation of βarrestins enhances both PTHrP- and PTH-mediated signaling at the plasma membrane, the findings in PRT-βarrDKO mice, revealing a loss-of-function phenotype for calcium homeostasis, support a critical role for βarrestins in the renal response to PTH, which presumably involves facilitating endosomal signaling. These results further support the notion that βarrestin-mediated endosomal signaling is not simply a means to extend the duration of G protein signaling but is a mechanism to provide a qualitatively distinct signaling output that serves to achieve a specific physiological effect, which, for PTH, is involved in calcium homeostasis, e.g., the stimulation of 1,25(OH)2D production.20 As this effect of PTH involves the transcriptional activation of the 1α-hydroxylase (CYP27B1) gene, it is tempting to speculate that signaling from endosomes may facilitate this response due to the proximity of the signaling complex to the nucleus, as suggested also for other GPCR systems,42,73–77 but this notion remains to be experimentally validated.

While prior studies of compartmentalized GPCR signaling have largely relied on in vitro and cell-based systems,16,42,78 our findings provide in vivo genetic evidence for the biological relevance of endosomal signaling by a class B GPCR. Moreover, our findings suggest that differential effects on signaling geometry—i.e., the balance between signaling output at the plasma membrane versus endosomes—could underlie differences in therapeutic responses attained by various PTH and PTHrP analogs. The identification of βarrestin-promoted endosomal Gαs signaling as a key driver of the renal response to PTH provides a rationale for developing PTH1R-targeted therapies that promote endosomal signaling and thereby enhance certain transcriptional outputs, such as CYP27B1 induction, that are beneficial in the treatment of hypoparathyroidism or other disorders of bone and mineral ion physiology.20,79–81

Although our study supports a model in which endosomal PTH1R signaling and βarrestins play central roles in the bimodal regulation of PTH- vs PTHrP-mediated functions, we cannot rule out other possible roles of βarrestin signaling not related to G proteins, as implicated in previous studies.36,37 Our findings, however, that (a) Hdac4 knockout, which is a well-characterized downstream target of cAMP-mediated PTH1R signaling in chondrocytes,26,56 rescued the growth plate phenotype and (b) R485X mice showed reduced 1,25(OH)2D serum levels as well as cAMP urinary excretion, both of which are downstream of Gαs signaling in PRT cells,69,82 support a disease mechanism that predominantly involves effects on downstream signaling through Gαs. We also note that our analyses of signal compartmentalization rely primarily on subcellularly targeted (at the plasma membrane vs endosomes) biosensors that report on local levels of receptor-G protein engagement or activation rather than the direct quantification of cAMP pools at compartment-restricted locations. Thus, while our conclusions are generally consistent with prior work that measured PTH- and PTHrP-induced changes in bulk cAMP15,16 and are supported by our current in vivo data on physiological phenotypes, we cannot yet formally define how differences in local concentrations of cAMP, including a potential role for other signal regulators, such as phosphodiesterases,83,84 may shape the overall spatial dynamics of downstream PTH1R signaling in target cells.

We further note that R485X- and PD-PTH1R likely have an intrinsic defect, relative to WT-PTH1R, in their capacities to traffic through the endocytic network, which limits their use in fully separating the specific contributions of the plasma membrane versus endosomal signaling. We find, however, that βarrestin deficiency does not produce an apparent defect in WT-PTH1R internalization yet causes a selective impairment of endosome-associated Gαs activation. We further acknowledge that mechanistic insights derived from in vitro assays in overexpression-based systems may not fully translate to in vivo physiology, for which cell type-specific expression patterns and regulatory networks likely also shape signaling outcomes. This limitation underscores a broader challenge for the GPCR field and highlights the value of in vivo genome editing techniques for generating experimental models that permit direct correlations of receptor signaling and effector interactions in vitro with physiological outcomes in vivo or in ex vivo cultures of primary cells, as utilized here for the PTH1R.
