# Epidermodysplasia Verruciformis in CADINS Disease: Expanding the Phenotype.
**Authors:** Aggarwal R, Spratt AR, PID PGI Consortium, Snow AL, Vignesh P
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-024-01663-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-024-01663-3)

## Content

CARD11-associated atopy with dominant interference of NF-κB signaling (CADINS) disease occurs in humans carrying a dominant negative (DN), loss of function (LOF) mutation in CARD11. Despite variable penetrance and expressivity, this disease is typically characterized by severe atopy with aspects of combined immunodeficiency, including recurrent respiratory and cutaneous viral infections, hypogammaglobulinemia, and other symptoms(1).Here we report the first case of CADINS disease with epidermodysplasia verruciformis (EV) as a clinical feature.

A 34-year-old man presented with dry red skin patches involving the lower extremities, starting at 5 years of age.The lesions later spread to involve scalp, ears, and upper limbs, exacerbated during winter periods and accompanied by severe itching. He had history of recurrent ear discharge and 3 episodes of pneumonia requiring hospital admission in early childhood. Up to age 32, he only sought complementary alternative medicine, and reported no improvement. He then developed vesiculopustular lesions over the face, chest and extremities and was diagnosed with molluscum contagiosum. He required multiple sessions of surgical curettage for molluscum lesions over the next 2 years, with worsening eczema and episodes of cutaneous fungal infections.

On examination, lichenoid papules were seen diffusely on his bilateral lower limbs, suggestive of a chronic atopiform rash (lichen simplex chronicus). Onychomycosis and onycholysis were present (Figure 1A). Deep seated sago-grain-like vesiculopustular lesions were noted over his palms and volar surfaces of bilateral forearms, suggestive of pompholyx.Scars of healed molluscum contagiosum infection were noted over the face and upper chest. He had multiple hypopigmented macules distributed over the forehead along the hair margin, suggestive of acquired EV. Other sun-exposed areas were examined and were not involved(Figure 1B).

On laboratory testing, complete blood count was grossly normal (Haemoglobin: 150 g/L, total leucocyte count: 5.1×109/L, polymorphonuclear cells 34%, lymphocytes 58%, monocytes 8%, eosinophils 0%, platelets: 230×109/L). Lymphocyte subset analysis revealed normal T, B and NK proportions, with a mildly elevated percentage of CD8+ T cells and a reduced CD4:CD8 ratio. (CD4/CD8 = 0.98; Normal = 1–3.6)). Immunoglobulin (Ig) profiling revealed elevated IgE (3281 kUIgE/L; Normal = <100 kU/L), with normal IgG, IgA and IgM levels. Th17 cells and interleukin-6 (IL-6)-dependent STAT1 phosphorylation in lymphocytes were normal. Lymphocyte proliferation in response to stimulation with phytohemagglutinin was normal, but severely reduced with anti-CD3/CD28 agonistic antibodies (Supplementary Figure 1). Based on these findings, hyper-IgE syndrome was considered as a possible diagnosis.

A skin biopsy from a hypopigmented macule over the forehead revealed marked acanthosis and hyperkeratosis in the epidermis, with atypical keratinocytes evident having vesicular chromatin, prominent nucleoli, blue/grey granular cytoplasm and peri-nuclear halos.These morphological features were consistent with EV (Figure 1C), with no evidence of malignancy noted. Targeted next generation sequencing (InvitaeInborn Errors of Immunity panel) revealed a novel, privateheterozygous variant in CARD11 (Caspase recruitment domain family member 11, c.137T>C, p.Leu46Pro). This variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. His mother and asymptomatic elder brother did not harbor the same genetic variant; his reportedly asymptomaticfather died in a road traffic accident and could not be tested.

Several lines of evidence suggested Leu46Pro (L46P) is a deleterious CARD11 variant: (a) Leu46 is highly conserved across species and located in the CARD domain, a hotspot for DN mutations, (b) most in silico algorithms predict pathogenicity (e.g. CADD=29.4), and (c) L46P behaved as a LOF variant in a recent saturation genome editing screen performed in a human diploid B cell lymphoma line(2). To empirically test for DN activity, we transfected CARD11-deficient JPM50.6cells with wild-type (WT) and/or mutant CARD11 expression plasmids as previously described (1). Unlike WT CARD11, L46P failed to restore NF-κB activity upon stimulation, comparable to empty vector (EV) or two LOF control variants (E57D, R891*) (Figure 1D). When co-transfected in a ~50:50 ratio, L46P also dampened NF-κB activation via WT CARD11, indicating dominant interference (Figure 1E). This effect was less pronounced relative to the DN variant E57D (perhaps reflecting reduced expression), but significant relative to co-transfection with EV, WT, or the R891* variant that showed no DN effect. The patient was thus diagnosed with CADINS disease. He was treated with topicaland oral antifungals for onychomycosis,topical steroidsand mycophenolate mofetil (1g twice a day) for recalcitrant atopic dermatitis.Antibacterial and antifungal prophylaxis was initiated to protect against further dermatophyte infections (trimethoprim/sulphamethoxazole 180/800 mg plus itraconazole 200 mg once a day). He remains well on follow-up,with no recurrence of molluscum contagiosum, non-progressive EV lesions, and intermittent exacerbation of eczema.

CARD11 is a scaffold protein expressed in lymphocytes, required for signaling downstream of both T and B cell receptors (TCR/ BCR). Receptor activation opens CARD11 into an active conformationcapable ofrecruiting various signaling partners including B cell lymphoma 10 (BCL10) and MALT1 paracaspase (MALT1) that form the “CBM” signalosome complex. Active CBM complexestrigger downstream signaling to the NF-κB, mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathways (3).

The CBM-opathies are a spectrum of inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) caused by mutations in the CARD11-BCL10-MALT1 complex. Biallelic CARD11 and BCL10 null/LOF defects are reported to cause combined immunodeficiency, with absence of atopy and cutaneous viral infections (4). Biallelic MATL1 LOF mutations also cause combined immunodeficiency, accompanied by immune dysregulation characterized by early onset atopy and dermatitis in 67% cases (4). Heterozygous gain of function (GOF) CARD11 mutations result in a lymphoproliferative IEI known as B cell expansion with NF-κB and T cell anergy (BENTA) disease, characterized by splenomegaly, polyclonal B-cell lymphocytosis, and recurrent sinopulmonary infections (4). However, disseminated molluscum infection has been reported in 36% cases. Conversely, CADINS disease occurs in patients carrying a heterozygous LOF/DN CARD11 variant that disrupts canonical NF-κB and mTORC1 signaling in T cells, resulting inTh2 skewing, elevated IgE production and atopy (5). Early onset, severe atopic disease can be seen in 89% of these patients, with cutaneous viral infections reported in 68% of cases (4). To date, reported viral skin infections in CADINS patients primarily include molluscum and herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1), with no reports of HPV-related disease in CADINS or other CBM-opathies.

Consistent with most CADINS cases reported to date, our patient presented with refractory atopic dermatitis, elevated serum IgE, recurrent sinopulmonary infections and difficult to treat cutaneous viral/fungal infections (4). Severe atopic dermatitis in these patients is oftendifficult to treat. Systemic/topical steroids and/or cyclosporine have been used with varied results(6,7). Dupilumab, a humanized anti-IL4Rαantibody that blocksTh2-associated IL-4 and IL-13 signaling, has been used successfully to treat one CADINS patient with severe atopic dermatitis (7). Other biologic drugs targeting Th2 cytokine signaling (e.g. mepolizumab) or IgE(e.g. omalizumab) may be useful but remain untested (4).We initiated our patient on mycophenolate mofetil for treatment of recalcitrant atopic dermatitis and observed an improvement with no progression of lesions. However, complete resolution has not yet been noted.

Acquired EV was suspected and confirmed in our patient, with a clinical history and immunological investigations consistent with an IEI affecting the T-cell compartment. He had multiple pityriasis versicolor-like lesions along the entire hairline on the forehead (sun-exposed area). Moreover, a skin biopsy from the described forehead lesions revealed the histologic finding of “blue cells” which are pathognomonic for HPV-infected keratinocytes. Unfortunately, confirmatory HPV serotype testing by polymerase chain reaction could not be performed in this case. However, the absence of EVER1/EVER2 mutations (included in the targeted next generation sequencing panel performed for this patient) ruled out primary forms of EV. EV occurs when persistent human papillomavirus (HPV) infection leads to epidermal dysplasia, resulting in formation of diffuse verruca plana-like lesions. Secondary or acquired EV occurs in patients with T-cell mediated immune defects (8). Considering abnormal T and B cell function that is well described in CADINS disease, the occurrence of EV in our patient is plausible despite no reports of EV in CADINS patients to date. Hence our case further expands the phenotypic spectrum observed in CADINS, including definitive clinical and experimental data that validate the pathogenicity of this novel CARD11 variant (L46P).
