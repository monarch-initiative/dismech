# X and Y gene dosage effects are primary contributors to human sexual dimorphism: The case of height.
**Authors:** Berry ASF, Finucane BM, Myers SM, Martin CL, Ledbetter DH, Willard HF, Oetjens MT
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2503039122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2503039122)

## Content

Many human phenotypic traits vary between the sexes, including adult height for which males are, on average, 13 cm taller than females. The biological mechanisms for this sexual dimorphism are not entirely understood. One hypothesis to explain the sexual dimorphism in height relates to differential expression in males and females of SHOX, a height-related gene in the pseudoautosomal region 1 (PAR1) on the X and Y sex chromosomes. SHOX expression is reduced on the inactive X chromosome (Xi), compared to the active X in females. Sex chromosome aneuploidies (SCAs), characterized by an atypical number of X and/or Y chromosomes, serve as informative models for investigating PAR1-related gene dosage effects. Here, we leveraged three large biobanks to study 928,605 individuals, including 1,225 adults with SCAs: 45,X (n = 95), 47,XXY (n = 505), 47,XYY (n = 290), and 47,XXX (n = 335). By modeling height across individuals with various sex chromosome complements, we quantified the contributions of five sex-related genomic contributors to height, including Xi chromosome dosage, Y chromosome dosage, male sex hormones, and effects of Klinefelter and Turner syndromes. We found that a unit increase in Y chromosome dosage confers 3.1cm (95% CI, 1.9 to 4.3) more to height than a unit increase in Xi chromosome dosage, independent of hormonal variables. The larger increase in height conferred by the Y chromosome explained 22.6% of the observed difference in height between 46,XY males and 46,XX females. This finding is consistent with the hypothesis that reduced SHOX expression in females results in a net difference in height between the sexes.

Sex-related differences are observed for many medical phenotypes, from autoimmune conditions which disproportionately affect females to the strong male predominance in autism spectrum disorder (1–4). The complexity of the association between these phenotypes and biological sex necessitates a framework to elucidate their underlying causes (5). Height provides an ideal model for investigating genomic contributors to sex differences, as it is a normally distributed trait in the general population that is routinely measured as part of clinical care or research, and there is a large and reproducible discrepancy between the sexes (6). The mean height of adult males is ~13 cm taller than that of females (7, 8); however, the biological mechanisms for this sexual dimorphism have not been fully elucidated.

The human sex chromosomes are known to harbor genetic contributors to height (9, 10), most importantly the SHOX gene which is located in the pseudoautosomal region 1 (PAR1) on the short arms of the X and Y (11–15). This region escapes the normal mammalian process of X inactivation in females, allowing both sexes to retain two functioning copies of PAR1 genes. However, there is evidence that the transcription of SHOX and other PAR1 genes is reduced on the inactive X chromosomes (Xi) compared to the active X, indicating that these genes are only partially spared from silencing (7, 16). This observation would result in higher SHOX expression in males (46,XY) than in females (46,XX) (17), a finding that was recently confirmed in musculoskeletal tissue, a relevant tissue type for genes involved in height determination (17, 18). These data strongly support the hypothesis that reduced SHOX expression on the Xi chromosome versus the Y chromosome could be an important contributor to sex-related height differences (7).

Additionally, case studies of patients with gonadal dysgenesis, who lack sex hormones and most secondary sex characteristics, have revealed that adults with a 46,XY sex chromosome complement are 7 cm taller than those with 46,XX (19). Similarly, individuals with 46,XX testicular disorder, characterized as having a 46,XX sex chromosome complement with a translocated copy of the sex-determination gene, SRY, located on the Y chromosome, have significantly shorter stature than typical 46,XY males (20). These findings suggest that a sex chromosome dosage effect may largely explain the observed height discrepancy between males and females, independent of the influence of sex hormones (7, 21–23).

In this study, we analyze a large population-based dataset to further explore human height dimorphism among adults with typical and atypical sex chromosome complements. Individuals with sex chromosome aneuploidies (SCAs), characterized by having either more or fewer than the expected number (two) of X and/or Y chromosomes, serve as important models for investigating endocrine and genetic contributors to sex-related height differences (19, 24). SCAs are relatively common, affecting approximately 1 in 450 individuals (25), and are associated with a range of phenotypes, including infertility, brain structure and function, hormonal imbalances, and differences in adult height (26–33). The loss of a single copy of SHOX, as in 45,X [Turner syndrome (TS)], is associated with short stature, while having an additional copy of SHOX, as in 47,XYY, 47,XXX, and 47,XXY [Klinefelter syndrome (KS)], results in tall stature (11). However, these gene dosage effects may differ depending on which sex chromosome (X or Y) is gained or lost. For example, two studies, one on 109 clinically diagnosed boys from Copenhagen and another on SCA data collected from case reports, each observed that height was increased in 47,XYY syndrome compared to 47,XXY (8, 34). By contrast, a recent study in 305 adolescents and adults with SCAs did not find a significant height difference between 47,XYY and 47,XXY (35).

To model the independent contributions of five sex-related genetic contributors to height—the Xi chromosome, Y chromosome, male sex hormones, and syndromic features specific to KS and TS—we compared the heights of individuals with a range of sex chromosome complements across three large biobanks. We used this model to determine whether there is evidence of a sex chromosome dosage effect that increases height among biological males relative to biological females, independent of male sex hormones. We then quantified the relative contributions of male sex hormones and sex chromosome dosage to the observed sex difference in height. This study has important distinctions relative to prior research, as our large population-based cohort (N = 928,605) is composed entirely of adults who have achieved their final stature. The subcohort of 1,225 adults with SCAs was identified using a genome-first approach from unselected healthcare populations and an epidemiological research study. This recruitment strategy is less likely to be influenced by the biases present in clinical cohorts, such as the overrepresentation of individuals with severe phenotypic presentations, including extreme deviations from average height (36).

Our dataset included 928,605 adult participants across three population biobanks with genetically determined sex chromosome complements, after excluding samples with missing data or that failed quality control (SI Appendix, Table S1). Of those, 1,225 individuals (0.13%) were identified with one of the SCAs included in this study: 45,X (n = 95), 47,XXY (n = 505), 47,XYY (n = 290), and 47,XXX (n = 335) (Table 1). The heights of individuals with each sex chromosome complement were normally distributed in each cohort (Fig. 1), with some heterogeneity across ancestry groups (SI Appendix, Table S2).

To construct a model of the relationship between sex-related genomic variables and height, we performed multivariate linear regression models in each cohort to quantify the effects of five sex chromosome-related variables among individuals of EUR and AFR ancestry (SI Appendix, Table S5). These analyses were performed to derive the coefficients to be included as independent variables in the model including: 1) the Xi chromosome effect (Xi), 2) the Y chromosome effect, independent of male sex hormones (Y), 3) male sex hormones, independent of the Y chromosome (MSH), and syndromic features (SF) specific to 4) KS (SFKS) and 5) TS (SFTS) (Table 2). The binary MSH coefficient captures the presence or absence of the typical complement of sex hormone associated with male gonadal sex. SFKS and SFTS are included as covariates in statistical models to account for height differences caused by abnormal sex hormone levels associated with KS and TS, respectively. SFTS also accounts for the possibility that sex chromosome deletions have a larger phenotypic effect than duplications, as observed with other copy number variants. 47,XYY- and 47,XXX-specific coefficients were not included in the model because neither syndrome is associated with hormonal differences or other syndromic features that affect height. The active X chromosome was not included as a coefficient in the model because every sex chromosome complement has exactly one active X chromosome. The difference in effect between the Xi and Y chromosomes (ΔXiY effect) was derived from the model.

The height contribution of the Y chromosome was larger than that of the Xi chromosome after accounting for male sex hormones and the syndromic features of SCAs (SI Appendix, Table S6). There was some cross-cohort heterogeneity in the meta-analyses (I2 < 65.4%); however, no Q tests had P < 0.05. In the meta-analysis of the EUR subset, we calculated a ΔXiY effect of 3.1 cm (95% CI, 1.9 to 4.3) of height that is attributable to genetic differences between the sex chromosomes, independent of hormonal differences (Fig. 4A). In proportion to the sex difference in height between 46,XY males and 46,XX females, the estimated ΔXiY effect is 22.6% (13.9 to 31.4%) in size. The height contributions of each individual component can be used to predict the difference in height between pairs of sex chromosome complements (Fig. 4B). For example, the difference in height between 46,XX and 47,XYY can be estimated by adding the effects of the MSH, ΔXiY, and an additional Y chromosome.

We used our estimates of the Xi, Y, MSH, and SFKS coefficients derived from the meta-analysis of the EUR ancestry subset to predict height differences between pairs of sex chromosome complements. We then compared predicted height differences between pairs of sex chromosome complements to height differences observed in The MVP (Fig. 4C). MVP contains height data for 1,670 adults with 47,XXY, 47,XYY, or 47,XXX (37, 38) (SI Appendix, Table S2). We calculated the mean difference in height between pairs of sex chromosome complement groups in MVP and compared them to the estimated height difference using our model of sex chromosome components. Mean height differences between sex chromosome complement defined groups in the MVP cohort were within the 95% CI of the calculated estimates of the differences between components. This shows that sex chromosome dosage is able to predict height differences between SCA groups in an external dataset. Our model of sex chromosome dosage and height could not be applied to comparisons that include 45,X due to a lack of individuals with 45,X in MVP.

Pathogenic variants in SHOX are the cause of short stature in a condition known as Léri-Weill dyschondrosteosis. We hypothesized that sex differences might influence the effect size of pathogenic SHOX variants on height due to X-inactivation affecting the PAR1 region on the female X chromosome. In males, a pathogenic SHOX variant would occur on the PAR1 of either the Y chromosome or an active X chromosome, potentially leading to a larger effect when mutated. To test this hypothesis, we examined the Million Exome Variant Browser and identified NM_000451.4(SHOX):c.583C>T (p.Arg195Ter) as the most common (MAF = 7.9 × 10−6) loss-of-function variant classified as pathogenic in ClinVar (39). In the MyCode cohort, the presence of a heterozygous genotype was associated with an 18.6 cm (95% CI, 10.5 to 26.7) and 8.9 cm (95% CI, 4.1 to 13.9) reduction in height among males and females, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The effect on height was significantly greater in males compared to females (P < 0.05).

In the present study, we examined the contributions of X and Y chromosomes to the sex difference in human height. As expected, the widely reported ~13.7 cm sex difference was constant across three population biobanks and genetic ancestry. By examining 1,225 individuals with SCAs identified among 928,605 biobank participants, we found that increased Y chromosome dosage conferred a larger effect on height than increased X chromosome dosage, independent of the effect of male sex hormones. Our multivariate model of sex chromosome dosage and height showed that the Y chromosome contributes an additional 3.1 cm to adult height relative to the Xi chromosome. When applying this estimate to the height difference between 46,XY males and 46,XX females, under the assumption of a linear model, the ΔXiY effect would confer 22.6% of the observed sex difference in height. A significant ΔXiY effect, independent of sex hormones, indicates that a genetic factor specific to the Y chromosome leads to a greater contribution to height compared to the X chromosome. We also observed that a pathogenic SHOX variant has a significantly larger effect on height in males relative to females in a small sample of variant-positive individuals. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that elevated SHOX expression on the Y chromosome relative to the Xi chromosome results in taller stature among males than females, largely explaining human sexual dimorphism for height.

In our meta-analysis of the European subset, both the SFKS and SFTS coefficients were significantly negative, suggesting that syndromic features associated with KS and TS were significantly associated with a reduction in height. An extra Xi on a female background had a larger effect on height relative to an extra Xi on a male background, which may be explained by the negative height contribution from syndromic features of KS. KS is associated with reduced testosterone relative to 46,XY males (40), which could contribute to a small but significant decrease in height relative to expectation from X and Y chromosome dosage alone. Similarly, sex chromosome haploinsufficiency is associated with a larger effect on height relative to triplosensitivity, which suggests a nonlinear association between SHOX dosage and height. Alternatively, syndromic features (e.g. reduced androgens) of TS could be a contributor to the significant decrease in height relative to expectation from sex chromosome dosage alone (41).

Prior work has shown that height increases until the number of sex chromosomes reaches four in females and five in males, at which point height decreases (35, 42, 43). Our observation that sex chromosome haploinsufficiency has a greater impact on height compared to triplosensitivity supports the nonlinear relationship between sex chromosome dosage and height. Irrespective of a correlation between sex chromosome dosage and height, the additive relationship observed in our results supports the hypothesis that Y chromosome gene dosage, independent of sex hormones, contributes to the height difference males and females. A nonlinear relationship between sex chromosome dosage and height may suggest that the relationship is not sufficiently explained by SHOX expression alone. In the future, gene expression studies of individuals with more than one supernumerary sex chromosome could be used to elucidate how sex chromosome dosage contributes to the observed nonlinearity of height change relative to the number of sex chromosomes.

SHOX is the primary determinant of the height differences that result from changes in sex chromosome complement. This is evidenced by the observation that a single protein-truncating variant in SHOX is equivalent to two-thirds of the height reduction associated with the loss of the entire X chromosome in Turner syndrome (44–46). However, it is unknown whether other PAR1, PAR2, or X-Y homologs that escape X-inactivation contribute to the remaining difference in stature. This possibility remains largely unexplored, as previous studies of the relative contributions of genetic variants to anthropometric traits have excluded the sex chromosomes (47). Biochemical and functional differences between X-Y homologs, an evolutionarily conserved group of genes, may affect the relative height contributions of each sex chromosome (48). Future studies could evaluate the relative height contributions of such X-Y homologs by comparing the heights of sex-matched individuals with deletions or duplications in such genes.

Our study population was diverse with respect to genetic ancestry, and yet the results were largely consistent across ancestry groups. The only significant exception was the finding that 47,XXY was associated with a larger height increase relative to 46,XY among individuals of African ancestry compared to the other ancestry groups studied here (Fig. 3). For those groups, the SFKS coefficient was associated with a small but significant height decrease, while the SFKS coefficient was not associated with height among individuals of African ancestry. This discrepancy is potentially spurious due to the smaller sample size of the African ancestry group in All of Us and the UK Biobank. Still, as few studies have investigated clinical phenotypes associated with KS in African ancestry populations, this difference may be biologically meaningful and potentially related to differential patterns of X chromosome inactivation across ancestry groups. More work on ancestrally diverse SCA cohorts will be necessary to validate the findings reported here.

Recently, gene expression studies of individuals with SCAs have sought to understand the relationship between sex chromosome dosage and autosomal gene expression. These studies have uncovered global changes in the transcriptome of lymphoblasts, fibroblasts, and other cell lines that are correlated with variation in X and Y chromosome dosage (49–54). A deeper understanding of the widespread effects observed within the transcriptome may provide insight into the abundance of sex differences observed in humans. Incorporating phenotypic data from biospecimen donors and expanding the range of tissues examined may enhance the insights gained from these research efforts. For example, future studies could investigate musculoskeletal SHOX expression and height in individuals with atypical chromosome complements to better determine their relationships.

Sex differences are observed in many clinically significant phenotypes but are poorly understood (5). Disentangling the effects of sex-related hormones from genomic variation could provide new insights into the pathogenesis of medical conditions with observed sex discrepancies, including autoimmune, cardiovascular, and neurodevelopmental psychiatric disorders. Our study and others demonstrate how SCA research can facilitate the modeling of phenotypic variability in response to changes in sex chromosome dosage. This approach has been recently applied to other traits with sex differences, including autism spectrum disorder, where we demonstrated that increased Y chromosome dosage is associated with higher risk compared to X chromosome dosage (1). By studying the relationship of human sexual dimorphism to X and Y chromosome dosage, future SCA research may shed new light on the mechanisms that contribute to sex-influenced medical disorders and other clinically meaningful differences between males and females.

The study population included adult participants from three large biobanks: Geisinger’s MyCode Community Health Initiative, a large U.S. health care cohort (55), NIH’s All of Us (version 7) cohort (56), and the UK Biobank (57). Genetic ancestry was determined using a principal component analysis of the genotype data. Individuals were classified into EUR, AFR, AMR, EAS, or SAS based on 1,000 Genome Project classification (58). The study population was filtered to include only individuals 18 y or older with recorded height data and with genotype arrays that passed quality control. The MyCode study was approved by the Geisinger Institutional Review Board (IRB), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The All of Us protocol was reviewed by the All of Us IRB, and informed consent for All of Us participants was obtained either in person or via an eConsent platform. This platform encompasses primary consent, HIPAA Authorization for research use of electronic health records (EHRs) and other external health data, as well as consent for the return of genomic results. Access to the UK Biobank was granted under project 49945, with ethical approval from the North West Multi-Centre Ethics Committee.

The log R ratios (LRR) of genotype probes across the X and Y chromosomes were used to identify each participant’s sex chromosome complement as previously described (1, 59, 60). Briefly, for each genotype array platform, the median (MyCode) or mean (All of Us, UK Biobank) LRR of the X chromosome (LRRx) was plotted against the median (MyCode) or mean (All of Us, UK Biobank) LRR of the Y chromosome (LRRy). Sex chromosome complement was determined by comparing the LRRy:LRRx ratio to specific thresholds established for each cohort and genotype platform (SI Appendix, Table S7). For individuals with 45,X, the proportion of cells with the 45,X complement (percent mosaicism) was estimated by dividing the median (MyCode) or mean (All of Us, UK Biobank) LRRx by the lowest observed female value within each sequencing platform(1, 32, 59). Participants were classified into the 45,X SCA group if at least 60% of their cells were estimated to be 45,X (1, 32, 59). Those with a lower percentage of 45,X cells were excluded from further analyses. The LRR and B allele frequency distributions across the sex chromosomes were manually reviewed to confirm the classification of SCAs. Only individuals with 46,XX, 46,XY, 47,XXX, 47,XXY, 47,XYY, or 45,X were included in downstream analyses. Chromosomal male and female sex were determined based on the presence or absence of a Y chromosome, respectively. Participants whose chromosomal sex did not match their self-reported sex were excluded (SI Appendix, Table S1). Additionally, analyses performed using the All of Us cohort excluded individuals with 45,X due to restrictions on reporting groups with fewer than 20 participants.

To compare height across sex chromosome complements, EHR-documented height measurements were gathered for MyCode and All of Us participants. Only measurements taken before 01-01-2021 in MyCode and 07-02-2022 in All of Us were included. Measurements taken before the age of 18 y, and outlier measurements more than 3 SD from each individual’s median height were excluded. The median height in centimeters across all included measurements was used for analyses in the MyCode and All of Us cohorts. The height of each adult UK Biobank participant at the time of initial assessment was used for analyses in the UK Biobank cohort.

Each of the six sex chromosome complements included in this study was broken into sex-related genomic components, each of which has an independent contribution to height (Fig. 4). The components include 1) an inactive X chromosome (Xi), 2) a Y chromosome independent of male sex hormones (Y), 3) male sex hormones independent of Y chromosome dosage (MSH), 4) syndromic features specific to Klinefelter Syndrome (SFKS), and 5) syndromic features specific to Turner Syndrome (SFTS) (Table 2). Xi and Y are considered quantitative contributors, while MSH, SFKS, and SFTS are considered binary contributors. The combined effects of the active X chromosome and the autosomes are considered baseline, and all sex chromosome complements are assumed to have equal contributions from these baseline factors. The height of each sex chromosome complement was calculated by adding together the baseline factors and the appropriate component(s) as follows: 46,XX = Xi; 46,XY = Y + MSH; 47,XXX = 2Xi; 47,XXY = Xi+Y + MSH+SFKS; 47,XYY = 2Y+MSH; 45,X = SFTS. For example, in our model, the height above baseline for individuals with 47,XXY is equal to the additive effects Xi, Y, MSH, and SFKS.

Variant NM_000451.4(SHOX):c.583C>T (p.Arg195Ter) was identified across MyCode, All of Us, and UK Biobank participants. Analyses were restricted to a single pathogenic variant and cohorts with at least 10 variant-positive individuals to minimize the effects of allelic heterogeneity and population effects. Only the MyCode cohort met these inclusion criteria. The effect on height among males and females was determined using a linear model controlling for age and the first ten principal components of ancestry. A t test was used to determine whether the effect size in males was significantly different from the effect size in females. Our criteria for pathogenic variants included a classification as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in ClinVar, a minimum of two review stars, and at least one submission from clinical testing. ClinVar and the Million Exome Variant Browser were accessed on March 26, 2025.

A linear model controlling for age and ten principal components of ancestry was performed to model the association between sex chromosome complement and height for each pair of sex chromosome complements. The data were stratified by continental ancestry group and performed independently within each population cohort. Coefficients for comparisons with fewer than 20 total samples were not computed. The results for each sex chromosome complement comparison for each ancestry group were then meta-analyzed across cohorts using a fixed-effects model in metafor (61). Cross-cohort meta-analyses were only performed for comparisons that could be performed in at least two cohorts.

A multivariate linear model controlling for age and ten principal components of ancestry was performed to model the association between the five sex chromosome components (Xi, Y, MSH, SFKS, and SFTS) and height. The model was performed among individuals of European ancestry in each cohort. The results were then meta-analyzed across cohorts using a fixed-effects model in metafor.
