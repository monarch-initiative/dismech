</div></section></section><section><section><section><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>CaBPs are a family of Ca<sup>2+</sup>-binding proteins related to calmodulin and are localized in the brain and sensory organs, including the retina and cochlea. Although their physiological roles are not yet fully elucidated, CaBPs modulate Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling through effectors such as voltage-gated Ca<sub>v</sub> Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels. In this study, we identified a splice-site mutation (c.637+1G&gt;T) in Ca<sup>2+</sup>-binding protein 2 (<em>CABP2</em>) in three consanguineous Iranian families affected by moderate-to-severe hearing loss. This mutation, most likely a founder mutation, probably leads to skipping of exon 6 and premature truncation of the protein (p.Phe164Serfs<sup>∗</sup>4). Compared with wild-type CaBP2, the truncated CaBP2 showed altered Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding in isothermal titration calorimetry and less potent regulation of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels. We show that genetic defects in <em>CABP2</em> cause moderate-to-severe sensorineural hearing impairment. The mutation might cause a hypofunctional CaBP2 defective in Ca<sup>2+</sup> sensing and effector regulation in the inner ear.</p></section><section><h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>Hearing loss is a common sensory disorder that can significantly impact quality of life. The estimated incidence of congenital hearing loss in developed countries is 1 in 650–1,000, and the majority of cases are attributable to genetic factors.<a><sup>1</sup></a> Genetic hearing loss can be part of a syndrome or the only detectable disease manifestation (nonsyndromic). Autosomal-recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (arNSHL) is the most common type of inherited hearing loss and is genetically heterogeneous; 61 genes and &gt;100 loci have been reported (Hereditary Hearing Loss Homepage). The fact that genetic hearing loss is exceptionally heterogeneous can be explained by the complexity of the auditory system, which involves coordination of multiple processes involving different parts of the ear and nervous system. A defect in any part of this complex chain of events can lead to hearing impairment or deafness.</p>
<p>Most families segregating arNSHL typically have a prelingual, bilateral, severe-to-profound hearing loss. An exception is found with mutations in <em>TECTA</em> (MIM <a>602574</a>) and <em>STRC</em> (MIM <a>606440</a>); these mutations cause prelingual moderate-to-severe hearing loss that is usually most pronounced in the mid-frequency range.<a><sup>2–4</sup></a> Recently, we identified a locus associated with arNSHL on 11q12.3-11q13.3 (DFNB93) in an Iranian family that also presents a similar moderate-to-severe hearing-loss phenotype.<a><sup>5</sup></a> This is the third arNSHL-associated locus associated with a milder phenotype.</p>
<p>CaBP2 is a member of a subfamily of Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding proteins with high similarity to calmodulin (CALM1).<a><sup>6</sup></a> Like calmodulin, CaBP family members interact with and regulate effectors such as voltage-gated Ca<sub>v</sub> channels in a Ca<sup>2+</sup>-dependent manner.<a><sup>7,8</sup></a> In the cochlea, Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx through Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 (L-type) Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels into hair cells triggers synaptic transmission to spiral ganglion neurons that convey the auditory information to the brain.<a><sup>9,10</sup></a> Ca<sup>2+</sup>-binding proteins (CABPs) localized in hair cells might modulate presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx, e.g., by antagonizing calmodulin-mediated Ca<sup>2+</sup>-dependent inactivation (CDI) of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 channels.<a><sup>11,12</sup></a> Here, we report that a mutation in <em>CABP2</em> (MIM <a>607314</a>) is the cause of DFNB93 moderate-to-severe hearing loss and reveal a role for CaBP2 in the mammalian auditory system.</p></section><section><h2>Material and Methods</h2>
<section><h3>Clinical Investigation</h3>
<p>Pure-tone audiograms and speech-reception thresholds were obtained from all individuals. Pure-tone audiometry was performed with air conduction at 250, 500, 1,000, 2,000, 4,000 and 8,000 Hz, and bone conduction was performed at 250, 500, 1,000, 2,000, and 4,000 Hz. In addition, transient-evoked otoacoustic emissions (TEOAEs) were recorded in all affected individuals and click-evoked broadband (100–3,000 Hz) auditory brainstem response (ABR) measurements were done on a subset of affected individuals. All affected members underwent comprehensive ophthalmological examinations, including a visual acuity test, a color vision test, cover test, retinoscopy, autorefractors, slit-lamp examination, pupil dilation, and a visual field test. To investigate cardiovascular problems, we recorded electrocardiograms and echocardiographs on most of the affected individuals.</p>
<p>All Iranian families and 100 Iranian controls were ascertained by the Cellular and Molecular Research Center, Shahrekord, Iran. The three families affected by mutations in <em>CABP2</em> are of Iranian ethnicity. Families Sh10 and Sh11 live in the Fars province in the south of Iran, and the He family is from the Chaharmahal va Bakhtiari province in the southwest of Iran. All medical examinations of the Sh10, Sh11, and He families were performed in a local clinical center.</p>
<p>An additional 24 unrelated Belgian and Dutch individuals who show a specific moderate-to-severe hearing-loss profile were collected through the Centre of Medical Genetics, Antwerp and Radboud University Nijmegen Medical Centre.</p>
<p>Fourteen unrelated families affected by Usher syndrome were collected through the Radboud University Medical Centre. Five families were diagnosed with Usher syndrome type I (USH1 [MIM <a>276900</a>]), five families were diagnosed with Usher syndrome type II (USH2 [MIM <a>276901</a>]), and four families were diagnosed with atypical Usher syndrome. Per family, DNA was available from the parents, one to three affected individuals, and one to three unaffected individuals. These families could so far not be explained by two mutated alleles of the nine known genes in which mutations cause Usher syndrome; these genes are <em>MYO7A</em> (MIM <a>276903</a>), <em>USH1C</em> (MIM <a>605242</a>), <em>CDH23</em> (MIM <a>605516</a>), <em>PCDH15</em> (MIM <a>605514</a>), <em>USH1G</em> (MIM <a>607696</a>), <em>USH2A</em> (MIM <a>608400</a>), <em>GPR98</em> (MIM <a>602851</a>), <em>DFNB31</em> (MIM <a>607928</a>), and <em>CLRN1</em> (MIM <a>606397</a>).</p>
<p>This study was carried out according to the guidelines of the ethical committees of the University of Antwerp and Shahrekord University of Medical Sciences. Informed consent was obtained from all individuals.</p></section><section><h3>Target Enrichment and Next-Generation Sequencing</h3>
<p>We developed a custom SureSelect DNA capture array (1M) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) that contains all gene exons and noncoding RNAs in the complete 9.6 Mb DFNB93 region. Gene exons and noncoding RNA coordinates were selected on the basis of the Ensembl genome database (hg19/GRCh37 draft).<a><sup>5</sup></a> In addition, we added 1,000 bp to the 5′ UTR of each gene and 50 bp to the 5′ end and 20 bp to the 3′ end of each exon to include the promoter region and to detect possible splice-site mutations. We then filtered the final selected region (1.3 Mb) for repeats (UCSC RepeatMasker) to exclude the development of probes that capture nonspecific regions in the genome. The custom DNA capture array was designed with a 20× probe tiling.</p>
<p>DNA from affected member V:14 of the DFNB93-affected family (Sh10; <a>Figure 1</a>) was fragmented with a Covaris S220 instrument (Covaris, Woburn, USA). Subsequently, the sample was prepared for sequencing according to the SureSelect DNA Capture Array protocol (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). In short, universal adaptor nucleotides were ligated, and the library was amplified before hybridization to the array (65°C for 65 hr). Hybridized molecules were eluted, amplified, and quantified with quantitative PCR (qPCR) and diluted to a final concentration of 10 nM. Enriched DNA fragments were then sequenced on an Illumina Genome Analyzer, GAIIx (Illumina, San Diego, USA) with the use of 1× 72 bp reads.</p>

<p>Using genomic DNA from an affected and control individual, we used PCR to amplify a fragment containing 105 bp of <em>CABP2</em> intron 5, 148 bp of <em>CABP2</em> exon 6, and 622 bp of <em>CABP2</em> intron 6 (with wild-type [WT] and mutant [MT] 5′ donor sites), and we cloned it into the pSPL3 exon trapping vector (Life technologies, Carlsbad, USA) by using BamHI and EcoRI restriction enzymes (Fermentas, St. Leon-Rot, Germany). <p>Before amplifying CaBP2 from the cDNA clone IRAKp961K1413Q (ImaGenes GmbH, Berlin, Germany) with oligonucleotides CABP2O1 and CABP2O2, we removed a 4 nt insertion after exon 2 of the original cDNA to obtain the sequence as reported in RefSeq NM_016366.2 by means of overlap PCR. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing.</p>
<p>Protein purification was performed as described earlier.<a><sup>15</sup></a> In brief, the <em>E. coli</em> strain Rosetta 2 (DE3) (Agilent Technologies) was transformed with pET28a containing cDNA for His6-CaBP2-wt or His6-CaBP2-p.Phe164<sup>∗</sup>, and 100 ml of bacterial overnight culture was inoculated in 5 l of 2YT medium (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and grown at 30°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended, and opened up with a Branson 250 sonicator (Branson, Danbury, CT). After centrifugation at 21,280 g, the His<sub>6</sub>-tagged proteins were affinity purified from the supernatant with a HisTrap FF crude column (GE Healthcare, Munich, Germany). The proteins were eluted from the column, and the His6-tag was cleaved by the incubation of the protein solution with 1 U thrombin per 1 ml protein solution overnight at 4°C. This protein fraction was loaded on a gel-filtration column (Superdex 200 10/300, GE Healthcare) and run at 0.5 ml/min in buffer (pH 7.4) pretreated with Chelex100 (Bio-Rad, Munich, Germany). Only the fractions corresponding to the monomers of CaBP2-wt or CaBP2-p.Phe164<sup>∗</sup> were collected for Ca<sup>2+</sup>-binding assays, and the volume was further reduced in a Vivaspin 20 concentrator (Sartorius Stedim Biotech). Both proteins were readily purified in the absence and presence of Ca<sup>2+</sup>. However, Ca<sup>2+</sup> enhanced the fraction of monomeric CaBP2 and was therefore present until the gel filtration for the experiments shown in the manuscript. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was performed with a VP-ITC isothermal titration calorimeter (MicroCal/GE Healthcare, Munich, Germany) at 20°C with 210–250 s of spacing time between injections for allowing complete temperature equilibration with the reference cell. CaBP2-wt or CaBP2-p.Phe164<sup>∗</sup> proteins (110–120 μM) were titrated with a buffer solution containing 5 mM Ca<sup>2+</sup> with the following parameters: one injection with 2 μl, 50 more injections with 5 μl, and a stirring speed of 300 rpm. A blank titration was performed with the same parameters but without protein in the sample cell, i.e., Ca<sup>2+</sup>-containing buffer was injected into Ca<sup>2+</sup>-free buffer.</p></section><section><h3>Analysis of CaBP2 Regulation of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 in Transfected Cells</h3>
<p>Human embryonic kidney (HEK) cells transformed with SV40 T-antigen (HEK 293T cells) were maintained in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen, CA), 50 U/ml penicillin, and 50 μg/ml streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO<sub>2</sub>. Cells were transfected with cDNAs encoding rat Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 α<sub>1</sub> subunit (1 μg from D. The human WT and MT CaBP2 proteins were cloned into NheI and BamHI sites of the pEGFP-N1 vector (Clontech, CA) and verified by DNA sequencing before use.</p>
<p>Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed at room temperature 36–60 hr after transfection. Extracellular recording solutions contained 150 mM Tris, 1 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, and 10 mM CaCl<sub>2</sub>. Intracellular recording solutions contained 140 mM N-methyl-d-glucamine, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 2 mM Mg-ATP, and 5 mM EGTA. The pH of extracellular and intracellular recording solutions was adjusted to 7.3 with methanesulfonic acid. <section><h3>Next-Generation Sequencing of the DFNB93 Region Identifies a Splice-Site Mutation in <em>CABP2</em>
</h3>
<p>The DFNB93 region contains more than 300 annotated and hypothetical genes, and several genes are expressed in the mouse and human inner ear. Because there are many strong candidate genes in the region,<a><sup>5</sup></a> we sequenced all genes and noncoding genes in this region by using a custom DNA capture array to identify the disease-causing mutation in one affected individual from the family. The final target region on the array included 1.1Mb for a total of 449,007 probes, and contained 85% of the original target area. After sequencing, reads were aligned to the human genome, and 27.7% of the reads were on target. Mean coverage in the target area was 97.6×.</p>
<p>After the identified homozygous variants were filtered through the 1000 Genomes Project November 2010 release and dbSNP131, 47 previously unreported variants remained and included two exonic mutations, one splicing mutation, six nontranslated mutations, 16 intergenic (downstream or upstream) mutations, and 22 intronic mutations. The two exonic variants included one nonsynonymous variant, c.1379A&gt;G (p.Glu460Gly) (RefSeq NM_003626.2 [<a>NP_003617.1</a>]), in <em>PPFIA1</em> (MIM <a>611054</a>) and synonymous variant c.174G&gt;A (p.Pro58Pro) (RefSeq NM_033036.2 [<a>NP_149025.1</a>]) in <em>GAL3ST3</em> (MIM <a>608234</a>). The splice-site variant, c.637+1G&gt;T (RefSeq NM_016366.2), was located at the 5′ donor site of intron 6 of <em>CABP2</em> (<a>Figure 1</a> and <a>Figure S1</a>, available online).</p>
<p>The variants in <em>PPFIA1</em> and <em>CABP2</em> were subsequently validated by Sanger DNA sequencing, which only confirmed the splicing variant in <em>CABP2</em>. Both variants were present in 100% of the reads, although the variant in <em>PPFIA1</em> only had 5× coverage compared to the 34× coverage of <em>CABP2</em>—this might explain the lack of confirmation of the first variant. Next, we checked the inheritance of the <em>CABP2</em> variant in the entire Sh10 family (<a>Figure 1</a>) and screened an additional 100 random Iranian controls to ensure that the variant is not a frequent polymorphism. The mutation was not detected in any of the controls, and inheritance was consistent with hearing loss in the family.</p>
<p>Several prediction programs (Human splicing finder v.2.4.1, NNSPLICE 0.9, and Netgene 2)<a><sup>17–19</sup></a> indicate that the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation in <em>CABP2</em> causes a loss of the functional donor site at this position. Loss of the 5′ donor site will most likely result in exon skipping of exon 6 or the use of cryptic splice sites.</p></section><section><h3>
<em>CABP2</em> c.637+1G&gt;T Leads to Exon Skipping</h3>
<p>Because we could not detect <em>CABP2</em> mRNA expression in human blood or saliva, we evaluated the effect of the c.637+1G&gt;T <em>CABP2</em> mutation on splicing by an exon-trapping system. We cloned exon 6 of <em>CABP2</em> and the surrounding introns (with WT and MT c.637+1G&gt;T 5′ donor sites) into the exon-trapping vector pSPL3 (<a>Figure 2</a>A). The multiple cloning site of pSPL3 is located within the intron of a minigene and is flanked by functional splice donor (sD) and splice acceptor (sA) sites. If the cloned DNA contains an exon with functional sD and sA sites, splicing will occur between the vector and insert sequences. If regular minigene splicing occurs, a secondary 177 bp PCR product is seen with primers against the minigene exon sequences. If <em>CABP2</em> exon 6 splice sites are used, a 325 bp product is amplified; this was the case with WT cDNA (<a>Figure 2</a>B). Analysis of cDNA prepared from COS-7 cells transfected with a MT c.637+1G&gt;T 5′ donor site only revealed a PCR product of 177 bp, indicating that the mutation at c.637+1G&gt;T leads to a complete skipping of exon 6 (<a>Figure 2</a>B). The correct sequence of all secondary PCR products was confirmed by Sanger DNA sequencing. Skipping of exon 6 is expected to lead to a shifted reading frame and a premature truncation of the protein (p.Phe164Serfs<sup>∗</sup>4 [RefSeq <a>NP_057450.2</a>]).</p>

<em>CABP2</em> c.637+1G&gt;T Is a Founder Mutation</h3>
<p>To identify additional individuals with mutations in <em>CABP2</em>, we screened 39 consanguineous Iranian families affected by variable degrees of prelingual hearing loss by genotyping three microsatellite markers (D11S913, D11S1889, and D11S1337) near <em>CABP2</em>. We identified two families (Sh11 and He) with affected individuals who were homozygous in this region (<a>Figure 1</a>). Affected family members presented with an audiogram similar to the affected individuals in the Sh10 family (<a>Figure 3</a>). Sanger DNA sequencing of all seven exons and intron-exon boundaries of <em>CABP2</em> revealed the same c.637+1G&gt;T mutation in these families. To investigate whether the c.637+1G&gt;T splice-site mutation in Iranian families Sh10, Sh11, and He is a founder mutation, we analyzed an additional set of microsatellite markers (n = 5) and SNPs (n = 13) in the region (<a>Figures S2–S4</a>). This showed that affected individuals from the three families shared the same haplotype in a region of 0.52 Mb (<a>Figure S5</a>), which suggests that the mutation found in these three families arose on the same ancestral haplotype.</p>

<p>All affected individuals with the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation in <em>CABP2</em> present with a prelingual, stable, and bilateral form of hearing loss with a similar audiometric profile. The hearing loss is moderate to severe and is more pronounced in the mid frequencies, resulting in a typical U-shaped audiogram (<a>Figure 3</a>). Routine clinical examinations did not reveal any special ophthalmological findings. Upon cardiovascular investigation in the Iranian families, no sign of bradycardia was noted, although this had been previously reported for alterations in Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 (encoded by <em>CACNA1D</em> [MIM <a>114206</a>]).<a><sup>20</sup></a> However, physical characteristics of the affected individuals showed ectomorphic and Marfanoid features. <p>The frame shift and premature stop are expected to truncate CaBP2 such that it lacks the Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding EF hands 3 and 4. Because the remaining portion of the MT protein would possess only one functional EF hand, we hypothesized that the truncation should alter Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding. To test this hypothesis, we performed ITC by using His6-tagged WT and p.Phe164<sup>∗</sup> (a premature stop predicted to result from exon skipping) CaBP2 purified from <em>E. coli</em>. Both proteins were readily purified and remained soluble in the absence and presence of Ca<sup>2+</sup> (<a>Figure S6</a>). Ca<sup>2+</sup> favored the monomeric form and, hence, was only removed prior to the ITC in the experiments shown. By ITC, we found an endothermic Ca<sup>2+</sup>-binding reaction (probably mediated by EF hands 1, 3, and 4; <a>Figure 4</a>A) for WT CaBP2 in three independent experiments. In contrast, the truncated protein displayed a weaker exothermic reaction (probably mediated by EF hand 1, <a>Figure 4</a>B) in three independent experiments. These results confirm that the truncation predicted to arise from the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation via exon skipping alters Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding and thereby might interfere with its role as a Ca<sup>2+</sup> sensor.</p>

<p>To gain further insights into the functional consequences of the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation, we compared the abilities of the WT and truncated CaBP2 to modulate Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels. These channels mediate presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx and subsequent exocytosis of glutamate in auditory inner hair cells (IHCs).<a><sup>22,23</sup></a> CaBP2 is also localized in IHCs<a><sup>11</sup></a> and so might play a role in regulating Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 and IHC synaptic transmission. In transfected HEK 293T cells, CaBP family members, including CaBP1 (encoded by <em>CABP1</em> [MIM <a>605563</a>]) and CaBP4, strongly suppress CDI of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3.<a><sup>11,12</sup></a> CDI is a negative feedback regulation of Ca<sub>v</sub> channels and is mediated by incoming Ca<sup>2+</sup> ions and calmodulin binding to the pore-forming Ca<sub>v</sub> α<sub>1</sub> subunit.<a><sup>8</sup></a> CaBPs inhibit Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 CDI in part by competing with calmodulin for binding to the channel.<a><sup>11</sup></a> Thus, we compared the effects of WT and MT CaBP2 on the inactivation of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 Ca<sup>2+</sup> currents in transfected HEK 293T cells.</p>
<p>In initial immunoblot experiments to confirm the protein levels of WT and MT CaBP2, we noticed that the MT CaBP2 exhibited ∼10-fold higher protein levels than did the WT CaBP2 (<a>Figure 5</a>A). To account for this difference, we compared CDI in cells expressing WT or MT CaBP2 after transfection with 10-fold more cDNA for WT CaBP2 than for MT CaBP2. Ca<sup>2+</sup> currents were evoked by 500 ms step depolarizations, and CDI strength was measured as the fraction of residual current at the end of the pulse relative to the peak current amplitude (r500). CDI was significantly weaker in cells transfected with maximal levels of WT CaBP2 (r500 = 0.92 ± 0.01, n = 6; p &lt; 0.001) than in cells transfected with Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 alone (r500 = 0.19 ± 0.02, n = 7) (<a>Figure 5</a>B). At protein levels comparable to those of the WT CaBP2 at maximal transfection, the MT CaBP2 showed weaker CDI suppression than did the WT (r500 = 0.62 ± 0.06, n = 9; p &lt; 0.02). At this protein level, the MT CaBP2 was still effective in suppressing CDI because r500 was significantly greater than for Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 alone (p = 0.001). This difference in WT and MT CaBP2 was negated at lower protein levels of both (r500 = 0.32 ± 0.11 and n = 6 for WT versus r500 = 0.36 ± 0.03 and n = 6 for MT CaBP2; p = 0.09).</p>

Taken together, these results show that c.637+1G&gt;T impairs the function of CaBP2 regulating Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 channels and potentially other targets important for hearing.</p></section><section><h3>Evaluation of <em>CABP2</em> Mutations in Affected Individuals with Other Ethnicities and Individuals with Usher Syndrome</h3>
<p>We screened 24 unrelated affected Belgian and Dutch individuals with a moderate-to-severe hearing loss for mutations in <em>CABP2</em> by sequence analysis of the exons and exon-intron boundaries, but we could not identify a clear damaging mutation in any of them. In addition, given that CaBP2 is localized in both the retina and inner ear, we considered it as a candidate gene in which mutations could cause Usher syndrome. We performed genotype analysis for three flanking microsatellite markers—D11S1883, D11S1889, and D11S4113—in 14 small Usher-syndrome-affected families with one to three affected individuals per family. For one USH1-affected family and one USH2-affected family, genotypes were compatible with linkage. However, sequence analysis of exons and exon-intron boundaries did not reveal any putatively pathogenic sequence variant.</p></section></section><section><h2>Discussion</h2>
<p>Like other CaBP family members, <em>CABP2</em> is closely related to calmodulin. Different from calmodulin, CaBP2 has only three functional Ca<sup>2+</sup>-binding EF-hand domains (EF hands 1, 3, and 4) because EF hand 2 contains a 3 amino acid deletion that would inhibit Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding.<a><sup>6</sup></a> Although calmodulin is localized in nearly all eukaryotic cells, CaBP2 has thus far been detected in the retina<a><sup>6</sup></a> and the inner ear.<a><sup>11</sup></a> In this study, we identified <em>CABP2</em> mutations in affected individuals who are from the DFNB93-affected family and who present with moderate-to-severe hearing loss.</p>
<p>We identified three Iranian families (Sh10, Sh11, and He) affected by a splice-site <em>CABP2</em> mutation, c.637+1G&gt;T, that segregates with hearing loss (<a>Figure 1</a> and <a>Figure S1</a>). To evaluate the effect of the splice-site mutation on splicing, we developed an exon-trapping system (<a>Figure 2</a>). This indicated that the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation most likely leads to skipping of exon 6. Skipping of exon 6, if RNA is not degraded by nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, is predicted to lead to a shifted reading frame, a premature truncation of the protein (p.Phe164Serfs<sup>∗</sup>4), and a complete loss of EF hands 3 and 4. To check whether the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation in three Iranian families arose on the same ancestral haplotype, we genotyped several microsatellite markers and SNPs near <em>CABP2</em> (<a>Figure S5</a>). We found that affected individuals from the three families shared a haplotype of 0.52 Mb, suggesting that the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation is a founder mutation inherited from an ancient common ancestor. The small size of the shared region suggests that this mutation might be hundreds or even thousands of years old.</p>
<p>Structural analyses of Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding to CaBP1, which, like CaBP2, has a nonfunctional EF hand 2, indicate that high-affinity Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding to EF hands 3 and 4 induces conformational protein changes that might be required for Ca<sup>2+</sup>-dependent regulation of effectors. In contrast, Ca<sup>2+</sup> binds with relatively low affinity to EF hand 1.<a><sup>24</sup></a> Indeed, the CaBP2 truncation predicted by c.637+1G&gt;T altered Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding in our ITC experiments (<a>Figure 4</a>), which is consistent with the expectation that EF hands 3 and 4 are required for high-affinity Ca<sup>2+</sup> binding in WT CaBP2.</p>
<p>We showed previously that although CaBP1, CaBP2, CaBP4, and CaBP5 are present in mouse IHCs, CaBP1 most strongly suppressed CDI of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 channels.<a><sup>11</sup></a> Using higher levels of CaBP2 cDNA than in our previous study, we now report that CaBP2, like CaBP1, can nearly abolish CDI. It is important to note that factors governing the protein levels and localization of CaBPs in IHCs might differ from those in transfected HEK 293T cells. For example, IHCs might possess sorting and trafficking mechanisms that favor the abundance of CaBP2 at presynaptic Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 channel clusters, such that CaBP2, indeed, could play an important role in modulating IHC Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx.</p>
<p>Our electrophysiological results show that the truncation due to the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation impairs but does not abolish the ability of CaBP2 to modulate Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3. By analogy with CaBP1/Ca<sub>v</sub>1.2 interactions,<a><sup>25</sup></a> the CaBP2 C-terminal, which is absent in the truncated mutant, might be necessary for anchoring CaBP2 to Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 and permitting efficient competition with calmodulin. However, the major determinants of CaBP1 regulation of Ca<sub>v</sub>1 channels are the N-terminal lobe and linker region.<a><sup>25</sup></a> The corresponding regions are spared in the truncated MT CaBP2, which likely permits residual modulation of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 CDI.</p>
<p>The observed reduction of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 current density by CaBP2 is consistent with the effects of CaBP1 and CaBP4 on Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 channels in transfected HEK 293T cells.<a><sup>11,12</sup></a> CaBPs can inhibit Ca<sub>v</sub>1 channel current density by antagonizing the acceleration of forward trafficking of Ca<sub>v</sub>1 channels by calmodulin.<a><sup>26,27</sup></a> The CaBP2 truncation was more potent in preventing this effect on current density than in impairing the inhibition of CDI (<a>Figure 5</a>C). This suggests that the CaBP2 C-terminal lobe, which is absent in the c.637+1G&gt;T mutant, might be more important in regulating plasma membrane levels of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 than in regulating CDI.</p>
<p>On the basis of our present findings and the colocalization of CaBP2 and Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 in IHCs,<a><sup>11</sup></a> dysregulation of IHC synaptic transmission could be one pathogenic mechanism underlying hearing impairment in DFNB93. Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 channels are the primary voltage-gated Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels in IHCs and outer hair cells and are required for hearing in mice and humans.<a><sup>20,22,23</sup></a> In IHCs, the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation in <em>CABP2</em> would most likely enhance inactivation of synaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx. This, in turn, could reduce rates of transmitter release and consequently diminish spiral ganglion neuron firing and ascending auditory-pathway activation. If, like in HEK 293T cells, CaBP2 normally reduces Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 current density in IHCs, the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation in <em>CABP2</em> could also have deleterious effects via comparatively higher Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 current density. Excessive Ca<sup>2+</sup> entry and the ensuing increased glutamate release could ultimately be toxic and lead to degeneration of hair cells, afferent synapses, and spiral ganglion neurons. The presence of CaBPs other than CaBP2 in IHCs could partially compensate for the effects of the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation in <em>CABP2</em>, which might explain the moderate hearing loss associated with DFNB93.</p>
<p>It is noteworthy that the structural CaBP2 defect caused by the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation in DFNB93 is analogous to that in CaBP4 of individuals who have congenital cone-rod synaptic disorder and who carry a <em>CABP4</em> mutation (c.646C&gt;T [p.Arg216<sup>∗</sup>]).<a><sup>28</sup></a> Individuals with a c.646C&gt;T (p.Arg216<sup>∗</sup>) mutation exhibit reduced visual acuity and abnormal color vision, which could result from disruption of CaBP4 interactions with Ca<sub>v</sub>1.4 Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels.<a><sup>28</sup></a> Like Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3 in IHCs and hearing, Ca<sub>v</sub>1.4 in photoreceptors regulates glutamate release and is required for vision.<a><sup>29,30</sup></a> Moreover, CaBP4 is localized in photoreceptor presynaptic terminals and enhances Ca<sub>v</sub>1.4 function, although via a different mechanism than CaBP2 regulation of Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3.<a><sup>16</sup></a> In affected individuals with the c.646C&gt;T (p.Arg216<sup>∗</sup>) mutation, mRNA encoding the truncated CaBP4 is not subject to nonsense-mediated decay and is still detected at levels comparable to those of the mRNA encoding WT CaBP4.<a><sup>28</sup></a> If the same is true for the c.637+1G&gt;T mutation in <em>CABP2</em>, our electrophysiological results most likely pinpoint one disease mechanism in DFNB93-affected individuals. The fact that similar alterations in the Ca<sub>v</sub>1 regulators, CaBP2 and CaBP4, lead to analogous defects in hearing and vision, respectively, supports the physiological significance of CaBP-Ca<sub>v</sub>1 interactions for sensory transmission.</p>
<p>However, CaBP2 might be required for normal hearing through roles in other cell types besides IHCs. CaBP1 and CaBP5 are localized in multiple neuronal cell types in the retina and likewise,<a><sup>6</sup></a> CaBP2 could be localized in spiral ganglion neurons, as well as in hair cells. Moreover, CaBP2 could also interact with targets in addition to Ca<sub>v</sub>1.3.<a><sup>31–33</sup></a></p>
<p>In conclusion, we identified mutations in <em>CABP2</em> in individuals with moderate-to-severe hearing loss. Mutations in <em>CABP2</em> cause an audiometric phenotype that is similar to those related to <em>TECTA</em> mutations but differs from the typical profound deafness phenotype that is seen in most families segregating arNSHL. Our results suggest the importance of screening for mutations in <em>CABP2</em>, as well as in <em>TECTA</em>, in families with this milder audiometric phenotype.</p></section><section><h2>Acknowledgments</h2>
