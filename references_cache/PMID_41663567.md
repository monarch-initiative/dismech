Mediator complex (MED) is an important auxiliary factor in the RNA polymerase II transcription apparatus, and MED13 is a subunit in the CDK8-kinase module of the complex. Currently, extensive clinical evidence has implicated its involvement in the pathogenesis of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). However, the mechanism by which dysfunction of MED13 contributes to NDDs remains poorly understood. Here, we specifically knocked down Med13 expression in cortical neurons usingin-uteroelectroporation to examine its function in cortical development and utilized mass spectrum to explore the downstream molecules involved in cortical development. We found that silencingMed13in cortical neurons impaired its radial migration and contralateral projection as well as dendritic complexity in mice. Differential protein analysis of human neuroblastoma cells (SH-SY5Y) with MED13 deletion revealed a large number of dysregulated proteins, including PLXNA4. Notably, the impaired radial migration and callosal projection, but not dendritic complexity, were largely restored by overexpression of PlxnA4 in Med13 knock-down neurons. Collectively, our findings establish that Med13 regulates cortical neuronal radial migration and callosal projection at least in part through PlxnA4, shedding light on the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of MED13-associated NDDs.

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are a category of complex disorders including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), intellectual disability (ID), attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and others1,2. Large-scale genomic studies have identified hundreds of gene loci conferring risk to NDDs, and accumulated studies revealed functional convergence of risk genes on many biological processes in the brain, one of which is the development of the neocortex3,4. During cortical development, six morphologically distinct layers form in an “inside to outside” pattern through multiple steps, including neural stem cell proliferation, neuronal differentiation, and migration5. The newly-born cortical neurons migrate along radial fibers to reach their proper destination and form functional circuitry, which is tightly regulated by both extracellular and intracellular signaling to ensure proper positioning and projections6,7. Disruption of this process leads to cortical malfunction that is believed to be implicated in the pathogenesis of diverse NDDs4,8.

The Mediator complex, consisting of distinct modules including the head, middle, tail, and CDK8 kinase module, serves as a transducing developmental and environmental signaling onto the basal transcriptional machinery9,10. Its primary function is to recruit Pol II and general transcription factors to gene promoter regions, thereby facilitating transcription initiation. During neurodevelopment, the Mediator complex coordinates various critical cellular processes, including proliferation, homeostasis, and differentiation, and emerging evidence links its dysregulation to the etiology of NDDs. Med13 (Mediator complex 13) has also been accepted as a risk gene for NDDs11. In 2014, a loss-of-function mutation in theMED13gene was first identified in patients with ASD12. Subsequent Genome-wide association study (GWAS) points out its strong correlation with NDDs, including ASD, and recent data have shown that patients carryingMED13mutations manifest as a spectrum of phenotypes that overlap with various NDDs13,14,15. A previous study has also shown that the genetic inactivation ofMed13caused embryonic lethality as early as embryonic day (E) 8.5 in mouse16. However, the biological functions of MED13 underlying these NDDs remain elusive.

In this study, we sought to explore the biological role of Med13 in cortical development byin uteroelectroporation (IUE) combined with RNAi in mouse. The results showed thatMed13silencing in the neocortex impaired the radial migration and callosal projection of cortical neurons, and decreased their dendritic complexity during embryonic development. Differential protein analysis of human MED13-deficient SH-SY5Y cells revealed dysregulated expressions of a certain number of proteins converged towards neurodevelopmental pathways. Notably, PLXNA4 was dramatically downregulated, and interestingly, overexpression of PlxnA4 could largely restore the defective migration and contralateral projection. Our results demonstrate that Med13 is required for normal migration of cortical neurons and the formation of their callosal projection, in which PlxnA4 is a key downstream molecule, thus providing insights into the mechanistic underpinnings of MED13-associated pathogenesis of NDDs.

The shMed13 and shMED13-#2 (Mus musculus) targeting sequence is 5′-ATCTTGATCCTGATATCATTA-3′ and 5′-TCGCATGATGAAGTAACTAAT-3′ (nucleotides 6076-6099 and 6213-6233 of NM_001080931.2), respectively. shMED13 (Homo sapiens) were constructed against 5′-GAACTAACACCTGGATCTAAA-3′ (nucleotides 2450-2470 of NM_005121.3). The complementary oligonucleotides were annealed and inserted into the pSUPER–GFP vector (pSUPER for short), which itself alone was also used as a control. The cDNA sequences used for constructing overexpression plasmids for Med13 and PlxnA4 were obtained from NCBI. ShMed13-resistant Med13 (Med13R) plasmid was generated by mutating 7 nucleotides within the shRNA target sequence without changing translated amino acids. All expression plasmids for Med13, PlxnA4-Flag, and Med13Rwere constructed into the pCAGGS vector using ClonExpress MultiS One Step Cloning Kit (C113, Vazyme). The MED13 guide RNAs were designed using the Broad Institute genetic perturbation platform (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/gpp/public/analysis-tools/sgrna-design) and were inserted into vector lentiCRISPRv2 with the Bsbm1 sites. The promoter sequence ofPLXNA4was obtained from NCBI, and the first 2000 bp before the start codon was considered as the promoter region and then inserted into the vector PGL3. All constructs were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

IUE was performed according to previous procedures17. Briefly, pregnant mice at E14.5 were deeply anaesthetized by sodium pentobarbital (70 mg/kg body weight). A plasmid mix (1 mg/ml for pSUPER, shMed13, pCAGGS-Med13R, or pCAGGS-PlxnA4 together with 1 mg/ml of pCAG–GFP) was injected into the lateral ventricles through glass capillaries. Five square electric pulses (36 V) were then delivered through the uterus at 1 s interval with forcep-type electrodes connected to an electric pulse generator (ECM830, BTX). After the uterine horns were repositioned into the abdominal cavity, the abdominal wall and skin were sutured. Pups were reared to different postnatal stages. Mice used in this study were kept in specific pathogen-free conditions, and all experiments, as well as animal care practices, have been reviewed and approved by the Animal Committee of Fudan University, Shanghai, China.

HEK293T and SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in DMEM and DMEM-F12 containing 10% fetal bovine serum, respectively. For transfection, HEK293T cells of 70%-80% confluence were transfected using Lipofectamine 8000 (C0533, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To examine the efficiency of shRNA knockdown, we replaced full-length Med13 cDNA (1 μg) with Med13R cDNA (1 μg) and co-transfected it with shMed13 (2 μg) for 48 h before RT-qPCR analysis.

For western blot, the cells were lysed by RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysate was boiled at 100°C for 5 min after being mixed with loading buffer. After cooling to room temperature, the lysate was loaded on SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST at room temperature for 2 h and underwent primary antibody incubation overnight at 4 °C. After incubating with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000; LF102, Epizyme) for 2 h at room temperature, the samples were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (Epizyme). The primary antibodies were used as follows: anti-MED13 (1:500; A301-278A, Bethyl Laboratories), anti-GAPDH (1:2000; LF205, Epizyme).

To generate lentivirus, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with lentiCRISPRv2-LacZ or lentiCRISPRv2-GFP-MED13 sgRNA along with packaging plasmids PSPAX2 and PMD2.G using Lipofectamine 8000 (C0533, Beyotime). Viral particles released into the cell culture supernatant were filtered with 0.45 µm filters (SLHVR33RB, Millipore) to remove cellular debris. SH-SY5Y cells were then transduced by culturing with viral supernatants, and stable cell lines were selected by culturing in complete medium containing blasticidin. After 7 days of selection, there were no viable cells in mock well,s and discrete blasticidin-resistance colonies were visible in wells transfected with lentivirus (ST018, Beyotime).

The cultured cell samples were lysed in SDS lysis buffer (1% SDS, 10 mM TCEP, 40 mM CAA, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5). The protein was extracted by sonication, denatured, and alkylated at 95 °C for 10 min. The protein concentration was determined using fluorescence emission at 350 nm using an excitation wavelength of 295 nm. The samples were then subjected to SP4 digestion by trypsin.

For LC-MS/MS, 1 μg peptides were analyzed by DIA-based method for relative proteome quantification using Vanquish Neo UHPLC coupled with an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The peptides were separated using a commercial analytical column (Acclaim™ PepMap™ 100 C18 HPLC column, 75 μm × 250 mm, C18 2 μm resin), with a 60 min LC gradient. Buffer A consisted of 0.1% (v/v) FA in H2O, and Buffer B consisted of 0.1% (v/v) FA in 80% acetonitrile (ACN).

The MS parameters were set as follows. The spray voltage was set at 2400 V in positive ion mode, and the ion transfer tube temperature was set at 320 °C. Data-independent acquisition was performed using Xcalibur software in profile spectrum data type. The MS1 full scan was set at a resolution of 120,000 @ m/z 200, normalized AGC target 300%, maximum IT 50 m,s and RF lens 50% by Orbitrap mass analyzer (350–1200 m/z). The cycle time of 3 s was used with 100 DIA isolation windows with 6 Da window size width (400–1000 m/z). The N (number of spectra) was set as 34. The MS2 scans generated by HCD fragmentation at a resolution of 30,000 @ m/z 200, normalized AGC target 2000%, and maximum IT 54 ms. The fixed first mass of the MS2 spectrum was set as 200 m/z. The normalized collision energy (NCE) was set at NCE 32%.

To gain insights into the function of Med13, we first examined the temporal and spatial expression pattern ofMed13in the mouse brain. In situ hybridization showed that abundant hybridization signals were detected in the ventricular zone (VZ), subventricular zone (SVZ) and cortical plate (CP) at various embryonic stages (Fig.1A), and notably, Med13 transcripts were extensively distributed in all cortical layers at postnatal stage (Fig.1A). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) revealed that Med13 was detectable in the brain from E12.5, peaks at E14.5 and markedly deceased after birth (Fig.1B). Meanwhile, RT-qPCR also revealed higher expression levels in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus compared with those in other brain regions (Fig.1C). To map the cell-type specificity of Med13 during corticogenesis, we dissociated cortices, cultured neurospheres, and performed co-immunostaining for Med13 together with the neural-progenitor markers Pax6, Nestin and the intermediate progenitor marker Tbr2. Med13 immunoreactivity was localized in Nestin⁺, Pax6⁺, and Tbr2⁺ cells (Fig.1D). In differentiated neuronal cultures, Med13 was co-localized with Map2 in differentiating cortical neurons (Fig.1D). Collectively, these data indicate that Med13 is broadly expressed across the major cell lineages that drive cortical development.

To decipher the function of Med13 during cortical development, we designed shRNA against Med13 (shMed13), which was co-electroporated with GFP construct into the developing mouse cortex at E14.5 using IUE as previously described18. To examine the specificity of shRNA, we further constructed shMed13-resistant Med13 plasmid (Med13R) by introducing 7-point mutations within the targeting locus without alteration of the amino acid sequence (Supplementary Fig.1). We first tested the knockdown efficiency by co-transfection of shMed13 and Med13 expression plasmids with and without Med13Rplasmid in HEK293T cells. RT-qPCR results showed that the shMed13 indeed reduced expression ofMed13, but was not affected in the presence of Med13R(Fig.2A, Supplementary Fig.1). After IUE was performed at E14.5, the distribution of GFP-labeled cells in the neocortex was examined at E17.5 (Fig.2B, C) or postnatal day (P) 7 (Fig.2D, E). As illustrated by the quantification, about 43% of the GFP-labeled cells successfully migrated to the cortical plate (CP) in the control group, whereas only 26% of Med13-knockdown neurons reached the CP, with 19% of them stalled in the VZ/SVZ at E17.5. At P7, when almost all neurons completed the migration process and stayed in layers II-III in control mice, approximately half of the Med13-knockdown neurons remained in the migratory path as well as white matter, indicating thatMed13silencing leads to impaired migration of cortical neurons. As expected, when shMed13 and Med13Rwere co-electroporated, the migration defect was not observed, as shown by the fact that almost all GFP-labeled cells were located in the upper layers at P7 (Fig.2D, E). To further confirm this phenotype, we generated an independent shRNA construct (shMed13-#2), targeting a distinct sequence with ~20% knockdown efficiency. Consistent with the shMed13-caused phenotype, a subset of GFP-positive neurons remained dispersed along the migratory path and failed to reach their correct laminar positions (Supplementary Fig.2). Overall, these results indicated that Med13 is required for the radial migration of cortical neurons.

To examine if Med13 knockdown affects neuronal identity, immunostaining of NeuN, Cux1, Satb2, Ctip2, Tle4, and Blbp was performed. In the control brain, the GFP-tagged neurons in the upper layers were immunopositive for NeuN, a marker for postmitotic neurons20, or Cux1 and Satb2 (Supplementary Fig.3A–C), markers for upper cortical neurons21, while none of them were stained with Blbp, a marker for astrocytes22, or Ctip2 and Tle4, markers for lower cortical neurons23. This was also the case for the cortical neurons that were located in layers II-IV in Med13-knockdown brains (Supplementary Fig.3A–C). However, it was evident that only a portion of the neurons located in the lower layers were stained with NeuN (about 46.5%), Cux1 (about 43.8%), and Satb2 (about 24.1%) in the Med13-knockdown group (Fig.2G), showing defective differentiation of these neurons. Notably, none of them were stained with Blbp, Ctip2, or Tle4 (Fig.2F), showing that they do not take on glia or lower-layer neuron fate. Furthermore, those located in white matter were not labeled with any markers mentioned above (Fig.2F). Collectively, these results suggest that the differentiation process of Med13-knockdown neurons is impaired.

In the cerebral cortex, most pyramidal neurons in upper layers extend their axons crossing the midline and project to the contralateral cerebral cortex, forming the corpus callosum24. As expected, the GFP-labeled callosal axons were observed to penetrate into the contralateral somatosensory cortex, mainly at the border between the primary and secondary cortex (S1/S2) at P7 (Fig.3A). However, in the brains transfected with the shMed13, the axon terminals at the border region were significantly reduced (Fig.3A, B). The impaired callosal projection was largely recovered in the shMed13 and Med13Rco-transfected brains (Fig.3C, D). In addition, the reduction of callosal axons at the border region was more evident at P10, at which more callosal axons invaded into the border region in control brains (Fig.3E, F).

In the observation of Med13-knockdown neurons, we found that the dendritic tree was simplified, based on the morphological reconstruction of the cortical neurons by Z-stack confocal scanning on both groups. The mediator complex plays a vital role in transcriptional initiation and might act as a hub gene in regulatory networks. Given that proteins execute biological processes, we employed mass spectrometry to profile gene expression changes in MED13-deficient SH-SY5Y cells (Fig.5A). This analysis identified 144 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 86 upregulated and 62 downregulated. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs in the biological processes category involved regulation of cell morphogenesis, neuron projection development, and neuronal migration as well as cerebral cortex development (Fig.5B, C).

To further establish the link between MED13 and NDDs, we compared the DEGs that have a non-ambiguous human ortholog with the gene databases for intellectual disability25, autism spectrum disorder26, and fragile X syndrome27, and revealed a total of 20 overlapped genes (Fig.5D, F). The expression level of some highly NDDs-relevant genes was further verified by RT-qPCR (Fig.5E). Altogether, our data point to the fact that MED13 deficiency alters the expression of genes implicated in NDDs pathogenesis.

Among the proteins with altered expression, PlxnA4 was selected for further analysis. As a co-receptor for the Semaphorins signaling pathway, PlxnA4 is indispensable for the proper radial migration of cortical neurons and axon guidance during cortical development28,29. More importantly, the migration defects caused by Med13 knockdown phenocopy what is observed in PlxnA4-knockdown cortices29, raising the possibility that PlxnA4 is involved in mediating the migration defects caused byMed13silencing. RT-qPCR confirmed that MED13 depletion in SH-SY5Y cells downregulated PLXNA4 mRNA (Fig.5E). We extended this observation in primary neurons by performing IUE of shMed13 plus GFP at E14.5, dissociating the cortices, and culturing the neurons for 72 h. Immunofluorescence staining for PlxnA4 showed that GFP-positive (shMed13-expressing) neurons exhibited a 24.7 ± 5% reduction in PlxnA4 fluorescence intensity compared with adjacent GFP-negative (non-electroporated) neurons (Supplementary Fig.4), demonstrating that Med13 is required for sustaining PlxnA4 protein levels in individual cortical neurons. Together, these complementary findings imply that PlxnA4 might be a downstream target of Med13 involved in neuronal migration.

To further test this, we generated a PlxnA4-overexpression plasmid and co-electroporated it with GFP into E14.5 cortices. Immunostaining revealed a robust increase in PlxnA4 fluorescence and a strong FLAG signal exclusively in GFP-labeled neurons at P7, confirming specific overexpression of PlxnA4 in the targeted cells (Fig.6A). It should be noted that PlxnA4 overexpression alone did not perturb radial migration, as evidenced by the normal distribution of electroporated neurons in the upper layers. Strikingly, co-expression of PlxnA4 with shMed13 substantially reversed the migration delay caused by Med13 knockdown (Fig.6B, D), but failed to restore dendritic complexity, which remained severely simplified (Supplementary Fig.5). Meanwhile, we found that all GFP-positive neurons successfully migrated to the layer II-IV expressed both Cux1 and NeuN in the PlxnA4 and shMed13 co-electroporation group. In contrast, a very small fraction of GFP-positive neurons that remained arrested along the migratory route still lacked detectable Cux1 expression (Supplementary Fig.3D). These data collectively suggest that PlxnA4 partially rescues shMed13-induced defects in both radial migration and neuronal differentiation.

We next investigated whether defective callosal projection could be rescued after PlxnA4 overexpression. Results showed that compared to cortices electroporated with shMed13 alone, those co-electroporated with a PlxnA4 overexpression construct and shMed13 plasmid exhibited significantly more axons invading the S1/S2 border region (Fig.6C, E). In conclusion, our findings suggest that PlxnA4 overexpression could partially restore the perturbed migration and callosal projection of cortical neurons caused byMed13silencing.

To verify whether MED13 regulates the transcription ofPLXNA4in vitro, we performed a dual luciferase assay, in which certain DNA motifs were inserted into the promoter region of the luciferase gene to measure its transcription activity. As expected, the absence of MED13 markedly suppressed the transcription ofPLXNA4(Fig.6F, G), showing that Med13 participates in the cortical neuron development, presumably by modulating the transcription of PlxnA4.

Genetic studies have provided strong evidence supporting an etiological role for genes encoding components of the Mediator complex in NDDs. Med13 belongs to the CDK8-kinase module that can reversibly bind the mediator complex core region11. Compelling clinical evidence shows that members of this module, including Med12/12 L (a paralog of MED12), Med13/Med13L (a paralog of MED13), are strongly correlated with the pathogenesis of NDDs11,30,31,32. However, biological functions and molecular mechanisms underlying mediator complex-related NDDs remain poorly understood. Here, we provided the first body of evidence that Med13 is closely involved in corticogenesis, with reduced Med13 expression perturbing neuronal migration, dendrite morphology, and axon projection in the developing cortex.

Recent findings have shown that Med13L exhibits a tissue-dependent expression profile with high levels in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, and its expression in the brain appear an embryonic stage-dependent manner33. However, there has been no information available about the spatiotemporal expression pattern of Med13 in embryonic and postnatal brains. Our study reveals that Med13 is highly expressed during embryonic stages, especially at E14.5, a stage of robust neurogenesis. Meanwhile, Med13 is relatively enriched in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus compared to other brain regions and broadly expressed across various cell types during corticogenesis, like neural progenitors and post-mitotic neurons, suggesting its involvement in cortical development. Knockdown of Med13 in a subset of upper-layer cortical neurons at E14.5, we observed migration defects in postnatal brains. Neuronal migration is a well-studied process, and defects in the migration of developing neurons give rise to multiple cortical malformations (e.g., lissencephaly, heterotopia, polymicrogyria), which are frequently observed in individuals with NDDs, including ASD/ID and ADHD34,35,36. Additionally, Med13 downregulation in the developing cortex impairs dendritic outgrowth and branching, and callosal axon projections to the contralateral cortex. It is important to note that abnormalities in dendritic arborization and axon projection are linked to the pathology of many mental disorders, including ASD/ID37,38. Problems in establishing dendritic complexity are commonly observed in individuals with psychiatric disorders39. These results indicate that Med13 deficiency disrupts normal neuronal connectivity, potentially contributing to NDD-associated symptoms.

To uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying Med13’s role in cortical development, we detected the genes with altered expression in MED13-deficient SH-SY5Y cells. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were enriched in pathways related to intermediate filament organization, neuron projection, and cerebral cortex development, consistent with our in vivo observations. Notably, 22 DEGs overlapped with NDDs-related gene databases, and Med13 expression was strongly correlated with high-confidence ASD/ID genes, supporting its role as a regulatory hub in cortical networks linked to NDDs pathogenesis. As transcriptional regulation in cortical development is dynamically and multidimensionally modulated40, it is also conceivable that Med13 modulates precursor proliferation, differentiation, and mitosis at distinct stages of cortical development.

Among the identified DEGs, PlxnA4 acts as a co-receptor within the conserved Sema3/6 signaling pathway, a cascade well-established to govern key neurodevelopmental processes including radial migration, axon guidance, and synaptic connectivity in the developing cortex28,41. More importantly, the radial migration defects induced by Med13 knockdown closely recapitulate the migratory deficits reported in PlxnA4-downregulated cortical neurons28,42. This phenotypic similarity prompted us to hypothesize a functional link between Med13 and PlxnA4 in regulating neuronal migration. Consistent with this model, PlxnA4 mRNA and protein levels were significantly reduced in Med13-depleted SH-SY5Y cells and in cortical neurons.

Functional validation experiments showed that overexpression of PlxnA4 partially rescued both the radial migration and callosal projection defects in Med13-silencing neurons, establishing PlxnA4 as a critical downstream effector of Med13 for these two processes. The cellular lineage in which Med13 is required to maintain physiological PlxnA4 expression remains to be defined through lineage-specific deletion. As a conserved subunit of the CDK module of Mediator complex, Med13 is thought to act as a signal relay between cell-type-specific transcription factors and RNA polymerase II10. The mechanism by which it is targeted to the PlxnA4 locus, whether through direct TF contact, enhancer looping, or CDK8-dependent phosphorylation of chromatin regulators, awaits future investigation,n and indirect modes of regulation cannot be excluded.

The partial rescue of migration and callosal projection defects by PlxnA4 overexpression strongly indicates that additional downstream effectors of Med13 are involved. Our proteomics data revealed that Med13 silencing alters the expression of multiple genes with well-established roles in cortical development and neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including Cux2 and DCX. Specifically, Cux2 functions as a pivotal transcription factor that modulates the proliferation of neuronal intermediate progenitors and the specification of cortical layer identities43. While DCX encodes a microtubule-associated protein essential for radial neuronal migration and the establishment of proper laminar architecture in the developing cortex44,45. As PlxnA4 overexpression failed to restore dendritic complexity in Med13-knockdown neurons, additional downstream mediators must be involved. Previous studies have established that the homeobox factors Cux1 and Cux2 act cell-autonomously and complementarily to drive dendrite branching, spine maturation, and synapse formation in layer II–IV cortical neurons46,47, suggesting that the decrease in Cux2 expression could likewise underlie the dendritic simplification after Med13 knockdown. Further studies are needed to clarify their contribution to Med13-dependent cortical defects. Collectively, our findings position Med13 as a master transcriptional hub to orchestrate cortical development and NDDs susceptibility. Mapping its downstream network will provide insights to seek therapeutic intervention in MED13-related NDDs.

Our study establishes that Med13 is essential for dendritic/axonal development and radial migration in the cortex, partially through PLXNA4-dependent mechanisms. These results highlight Med13’s critical role in cortical circuit assembly and provide mechanistic insights into its involvement in NDDs pathogenesis.
