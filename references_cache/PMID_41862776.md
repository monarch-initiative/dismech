BACKGROUND: The optimal treatment strategy for epidermal growth factor receptor
(EGFR)-mutated advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)-sequential tyrosine
kinase inhibitor (TKI) monotherapies versus upfront combination
therapies-remains debated.
OBJECTIVE: The Giotrif In Advanced NSCLC Taiwan (GIANT) study evaluated the
real-world outcomes of sequential afatinib-based strategies.
PATIENTS AND METHODS: This multicenter retrospective cohort study included 733
treatment-naïve patients with American Joint Committee on Cancer stage IIIB-IV
NSCLC harboring Del19 or L858R EGFR mutations who received first-line afatinib
across seven major Taiwanese medical centers between 2016 and 2024. Patients
were stratified by second-line therapy into two groups: afatinib-osimertinib (n
= 303) and afatinib-chemotherapy/other lines (n = 430). The primary outcome was
overall survival (OS).
RESULTS: Sequential afatinib-osimertinib therapy achieved a median OS of 55
months (95% confidence interval [CI] 53.2-66.4) compared to 32.3 months (95% CI
30.3-34.5) with alternative strategies (adjusted hazard ratio 0.43, p < 0.001).
Survival benefits were consistent across EGFR mutations and brain metastasis
statuses.
CONCLUSION: Sequential afatinib-to-osimertinib therapy achieved remarkable OS
exceeding 55 months in real-world practice, with outcomes appearing competitive
with contemporary combination strategies. These findings support sequential TKI
therapy as a durable and effective alternative that warrants prospective
validation.

Sequential afatinib-to-osimertinib therapy is a durable, effective, and economically viable alternative for patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated advanced non-small cell lung cancer.Biomarker-guided sequential therapy provides valuable real-world evidence and extensive clinical benefits across various patient groups and healthcare systems.Prospective randomized trials that directly compare sequential monotherapy to upfront combination strategies are needed to establish optimal treatment approaches that take into account quality of life and cost-effectiveness.

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations representing the most prevalent actionable oncogenic drivers in Asian populations, present in 40–60% of cases compared to 10–15% in Western populations [1, 2]. Among EGFR mutations, deletions in exon 19 (Del19) and the c.2573T>G (p.L858R) point mutation in exon 21 account for approximately 85–90% of all EGFR-mutated NSCLC cases and are associated with robust responses to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [3]. The therapeutic landscape for advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past two decades, evolving from cytotoxic chemotherapy to targeted molecular therapy, and more recently, to combination therapies that have fundamentally reshaped treatment paradigms and patient outcomes.

The evolution of EGFR-TKI therapy can be traced through three distinct generations of agents, each offering unique mechanisms of action and clinical benefits. First-generation reversible EGFR-TKIs (gefitinib and erlotinib) and second-generation irreversible pan–human epidermal growth factor receptor (pan-HER) inhibitors (afatinib and dacomitinib) dominated the initial era of targeted therapy, demonstrating significant improvements in progression-free survival (PFS) and quality of life compared to platinum-based chemotherapy [4, 5]. However, the near-universal emergence of acquired resistance, most commonly through the EGFR T790M mutation in approximately 50–60% of cases, necessitated the development of third-generation TKIs [6]. Osimertinib, a third-generation irreversible EGFR-TKI with selective activity against both EGFR-sensitizing mutations and the T790M resistance mutation, emerged as a transformative agent in this therapeutic evolution [7, 8].

The landmark FLAURA trial (NCT02296125) fundamentally altered the first-line treatment landscape by demonstrating that osimertinib monotherapy achieved superior PFS (18.9 months vs. 10.2 months) and overall survival (OS) (38.6 months vs. 31.8 months) compared to first-generation TKIs (gefitinib or erlotinib), establishing osimertinib as the preferred first-line standard of care for EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC [9, 10]. This paradigm shift raised important questions about optimal treatment sequencing: should the most potent TKI be reserved for first-line therapy, or could sequential strategies using earlier-generation TKIs followed by osimertinib at progression yield comparable or superior outcomes? The FLAURA-2 trial (NCT04035486) subsequently challenged the monotherapy approach by demonstrating that combining osimertinib with platinum-pemetrexed chemotherapy as first-line therapy significantly prolonged PFS compared to osimertinib monotherapy (25.5 months vs. 16.7 months; hazard ratio [HR] 0.62, p < 0.001), suggesting that combination therapies could overcome limitations of sequential monotherapies [11]. Most recently, the MARIPOSA trial (NCT04487080) introduced another paradigm-shifting combination strategy, demonstrating that amivantamab (a bispecific antibody targeting EGFR and MET proto-oncogene, receptor tyrosine kinase [MET]) plus lazertinib (a third-generation EGFR-TKI) achieved an unprecedented median PFS of 23.7 months compared to 16.6 months with osimertinib monotherapy (HR 0.70, p < 0.001), further solidifying combination therapy as an emerging standard [12].

Despite these impressive trial outcomes, several critical knowledge gaps remain in translating these findings into real-world clinical practice, particularly in Asian healthcare systems. First, while the sequential use of second-generation EGFR-TKIs (particularly afatinib) followed by osimertinib upon progression has been widely adopted in clinical practice based on biological rationale, retrospective analyses, and pharmacoeconomic considerations, its real-world effectiveness remains inadequately characterized [13, 14]. Second, patient populations enrolled in randomized controlled trials typically represent selected cohorts with favorable performance status, minimal comorbidities, and strict inclusion criteria, which may limit their generalizability to broader patient populations encountered in routine practice. Third, treatment decisions in real-world settings are influenced by factors including drug availability, reimbursement policies, physician preferences, patient comorbidities, and financial considerations that are not captured in clinical trials.

The effectiveness of the sequential afatinib-to-osimertinib strategy is supported by mechanistic and clinical observations. As an irreversible pan-HER inhibitor, afatinib achieves broader target inhibition than first-generation TKIs and has demonstrated superior OS in certain patient subgroups [15]. The GioTag study (NCT03370770) previously demonstrated that patients receiving sequential afatinib followed by osimertinib achieved a median time-to-treatment failure of 27.6 months and a median OS of 37.6 months, suggesting that this sequential approach could yield outcomes comparable to first-line osimertinib monotherapy [16]. However, whether this sequential strategy remains competitive in the era of combination therapies, as demonstrated in the FLAURA-2 and MARIPOSA trials, requires validation through contemporary real-world data.

The Giotrif In Advanced NSCLC Taiwan (GIANT) study was designed to address these critical evidence gaps by comprehensively evaluating real-world treatment outcomes and sequential therapy patterns in Taiwanese patients with NSCLC. Conducted as a multicenter study across seven major medical centers in Taiwan, GIANT compared sequential afatinib–osimertinib to sequential afatinib–chemotherapy/other therapy among patients with advanced NSCLC harboring EGFR Del19 or L858R mutations. Therefore, this analysis has two primary objectives: (1) to characterize real-world treatment outcomes of sequential afatinib-based strategies in a contemporary Taiwanese cohort; and (2) to inform evidence-based treatment decision-making regarding optimal positioning of sequential TKI strategies versus upfront combination strategies in the rapidly evolving therapeutic landscape of EGFR-mutated NSCLC.

This multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted across seven major medical centers in Taiwan. The study period spanned from January 20, 2016, corresponding to the initiation of afatinib (Giotrif) treatment for the first enrolled patient, to October 8, 2024, corresponding to the end of follow-up. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board (IRB) at each participating center (Chang Gung Memorial Hospital IRB No.: 202401791B0; Taipei Veterans General Hospital IRB No.: 2020-07-046CC; National Taiwan University Hospital IRB No.: 202311051RINA; Taichung Veterans General Hospital IRB No.: CE23507A; National Cheng Kung University Hospital IRB No.: --/A-ER-112-425), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [17]. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the ethics committee waived the requirement for informed consent.

The inclusion criteria were (1) a histologically or cytologically confirmed diagnosis of NSCLC classified as stage IIIB, IIIC, or IV according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Cancer Staging Manual, seventh edition [18]; (2) presence of activating EGFR Del19 or L858R mutations confirmed by validated molecular testing methods; (3) treatment-naïve status with no prior systemic therapy for advanced disease; (4) receipt of first-line afatinib (Giotrif) as initial treatment; and (5) availability of adequate clinical and follow-up data. The exclusion criteria were (1) uncommon or compound EGFR mutations, (2) prior EGFR-TKI exposure, or (3) incomplete treatment records.

Following first-line afatinib therapy, patients were systematically classified into two distinct cohorts based on their second-line treatment strategy: (1) the afatinib–osimertinib sequential group, comprising patients who received osimertinib as second-line therapy upon disease progression on afatinib; and (2) the afatinib–chemotherapy/other therapy group, comprising patients who received platinum-based chemotherapy, alternative targeted agents, or other systemic therapies as second-line therapy. This classification enabled direct comparison of the sequential third-generation TKI strategy versus alternative therapeutic strategies following first-line afatinib.

Comprehensive clinical data were systematically extracted from the patients’ electronic medical records at each participating center. The collected variables included patients’ demographics (age, sex, and smoking history), disease characteristics (histology, stage, and EGFR mutation), performance status (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group [ECOG] score), clinical characteristics (presence of brain metastases at baseline, response assessments, and progression dates), treatment details (start and discontinuation dates, dose modifications, treatment duration, and subsequent lines of therapy), and survival outcomes. The tumor response was evaluated according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors criteria (version 1.1) through routine clinical imaging assessments [19].

The primary outcome measure was OS, defined as the time from the initiation of first-line afatinib therapy until death from any cause or last follow-up (censored). The secondary outcome measures were (1) time on treatment (ToT), calculated from initiation of afatinib therapy until discontinuation of second-line therapy; (2) first-line PFS (PFS-1), defined as the time from initiation of afatinib therapy to disease progression; (3) second-line PFS (PFS-2), defined as the time from the start of second-line therapy (osimertinib, chemotherapy, or other agents) to subsequent disease progression or death; and (4) combined PFS (PFS-C), computed as the sum of PFS-1 and PFS-2, representing the total duration of disease control across sequential treatment lines.

All data were recorded using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed using RStudio (version 2022.02.3; Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA), with a two-tailed p < 0.05 considered statistically significant for all analyses. Normality of data distribution was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Patients’ characteristics and treatment patterns are summarized using descriptive statistics. As they were non-normally distributed, continuous variables are presented as the median with the interquartile range (IQR) or range and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and compared between groups using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were less than five.

Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival curves were generated for each treatment group. Differences in survival distributions between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Factors associated with mortality were identified using multivariate Cox regression, with the strength of associations presented as adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Data quality was ensured through systematic verification procedures at each participating center, including cross-checking against source electronic medical records and resolution of discrepancies through consensus among investigators. Regular data monitoring meetings were held throughout the study period to ensure consistency in data collection and classification across centers.

Between January 20, 2016, and October 8, 2024, 1125 patients with advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC received first-line afatinib across the seven participating major medical centers in Taiwan. Of these, 733 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The patients were stratified into two treatment groups based on their second-line therapy: 430 (59%) received chemotherapy or other therapies (afatinib–chemotherapy group), and 303 (41%) received osimertinib (afatinib–osimertinib group). The treatment outcomes of patients in both treatment groups are summarized in Table 2. The PFS with first-line afatinib therapy was comparable between the study cohort (13.5 months [95% CI 12.6–14.7], n = 733; Fig. S1A in the electronic supplementary material) and those who did not receive a second-line therapy (12.9 months [95% CI 11.7–15.9], n = 295; Fig. S1B). The afatinib–osimertinib group demonstrated significantly superior response to first-line afatinib therapy compared to the chemotherapy group (partial response 79% vs. 64%, p < 0.001). The median PFS with first-line afatinib therapy was significantly longer in the afatinib–osimertinib group (20.4 months [95% CI 18.4–22.2] vs. 10.3 months [95% CI 9.5–11.3], p < 0.001). Second-line treatment response rates were also higher in the afatinib–osimertinib group (partial response 33% vs. 23%, p < 0.001). The median OS was significantly longer in the afatinib–osimertinib group compared to the afatinib–chemotherapy group (55 months [95% CI 53.2–66.4] vs. 32.3 months [95% CI 30.3–34.5], p < 0.001; Fig. 2). The median ToT was 39.6 months (95% CI 35.2–45.2) in the afatinib–osimertinib group compared to 18.3 months (95% CI 17.7–20.0) in the afatinib–chemotherapy group (p < 0.001, Fig. 3).

PFS-2 reached 11 months (95% CI 9.8–14.0) with osimertinib compared to 5.3 months (95% CI 4.7–5.8) with chemotherapy (Fig. S1C). The PFS-C was, in turn, significantly better in the afatinib–osimertinib group compared to the afatinib–chemotherapy group (32.8 months [95% CI 29.8–36.8] vs. 17.2 months [95% CI 16.6–18.6], p < 0.001; Fig. S1D). Within the afatinib–chemotherapy group, TKI ± chemotherapy achieved the highest median PFS-2 (9.8 months [95% CI 5.1–15.7], p = 0.01), with other chemotherapeutic regimens achieving comparable PFS-2 (Fig. S3).

T790M mutation testing was performed in 487 patients (66%) following disease progression on first-line afatinib (Table S2, in the electronic supplementary material, and Fig. 4). Of the tested patients, 289 (59%) harbored the T790M mutation. The T790M positivity rate was significantly higher in the afatinib–osimertinib group compared to the afatinib–chemotherapy group (96% vs. 17%, p < 0.001), reflecting the fact that T790M-positive status was the primary criterion for osimertinib selection. Sequential afatinib–osimertinib therapy provided substantial survival benefit regardless of brain metastasis status or EGFR mutation compared to sequential afatinib–chemotherapy. Among patients with EGFR Del19 mutations and brain metastases, the median OS was 53.2 months (95% CI 49.8–67) with afatinib–osimertinib compared to 25.6 months (95% CI 21.3–34.1) with afatinib–chemotherapy (p < 0.001, Table S4 in the electronic supplementary material). Similar benefits were observed in patients with EGFR L858R mutations and brain metastases (51.0 months [95% CI 41.4–NA] vs. 27.7 months [95% CI 22.1–34.4], p < 0.001) and in patients without brain metastases across both mutations.

Cross-trial comparisons were conducted to contextualize the findings of the GIANT study within the contemporary therapeutic landscape (Table S5 in the electronic supplementary material). Important differences existed in study periods and follow-up duration. The GIANT study enrolled patients from 2016 to 2023, with a median follow-up of 44 months (IQR 29–60) in the afatinib–osimertinib group. In contrast, the MARIPOSA-ASIAN (2020–2022) and FLAURA-2 (2020–2021) trials had more recent enrollment periods with shorter initial follow-up (22.5 and 19.5 months [range 0–33.3], respectively). Recently released updated data from the FLAURA-2 trial extended the median follow-up to approximately 51 months (range 0.2–60.4).

Baseline characteristics were generally comparable across studies, including the median age (61–63 years) and female predominance (56–63%). However, the distribution of EGFR mutations showed variation, with similar numbers of patients having the Del19 and L858R mutations in the GIANT study (48–52% each), while the Del19 mutation was more common than the L858R mutation in the MARIPOSA-ASIAN subset (55% vs. 45%) and FLAURA-2 trial (60–61% vs. 38–39%). Nonetheless, baseline brain metastasis rates were comparable across the GIANT study (32–34%), the MARIPOSA-ASIAN subset (43–44%), and the FLAURA-2 trial (40–42%).

Comparisons of OS revealed compelling findings. The sequential afatinib–osimertinib strategy in the GIANT study achieved a median OS of 55.0 months (95% CI 53.2–66.4), representing a 70% improvement over afatinib–chemotherapy sequencing (32.3 months [95% CI 30.2–35.6]; aHR 0.43 [95% CI 0.35–0.52], p < 0.001). This sequential approach demonstrated outcomes that appeared competitive with contemporary combination strategies. Updated data from the FLAURA-2 trial showed median OS of 47.5 months (95% CI 41–NA) with osimertinib-chemotherapy compared to 37.6 months (95% CI 33.2–43.2) with osimertinib monotherapy (HR 0.77 [95% CI 0.61–0.96], p = 0.02). The MARIPOSA-ASIAN subset demonstrated immature OS data, with the median not reached in either arm (HR 0.84 [95% CI 0.58–1.23], p = 0.38) due to shorter follow-up.

Subgroup analyses by EGFR mutation revealed consistent benefits between Del19 and L858R. The median OS was significantly longer with sequential afatinib–osimertinib compared to sequential afatinib–chemotherapy among both patients with the EGFR Del19 mutation (54.2 months [95% CI 53.5–71.2] vs. 31.6 months [95% CI 28.6–38.1], p = 0.001; Figure S2A) and patients with the EGFR L858R mutation (55.2 months [95% CI 43.3–69.4] vs. 32.7 months [95% CI 30.2–35.6], p = 0.001; Figure S2B). These mutation-specific outcomes provide granularity not yet reported in the MARIPOSA-ASIAN or FLAURA-2 trials.

After excluding patients enrolled in the GIANT study with an ECOG performance status > 1 to match the eligibility criteria in the MARIPOSA and FLAURA-2 trials, visual alignment of Kaplan–Meier survival curves (Figures S4 and S5) enabled qualitative comparisons. The overlaid survival curves suggested that sequential afatinib–osimertinib therapy demonstrated trajectories that tracked closely with, and potentially exceeded, those of the osimertinib–chemotherapy arm in the FLAURA-2 trial. Comparison with the MARIPOSA-ASIAN subset showed similar early trajectories; however, longer follow-up will be required for a definitive mature assessment of OS.

The principal finding of our study is that sequential afatinib-to-osimertinib therapy achieved a median OS of 55 months (95% CI 53.2–66.4), substantially exceeding outcomes with alternative second-line strategies (32.3 months [95% CI 30.2–35.6]; aHR 0.43 [95% CI 0.35–0.52], p < 0.001) and demonstrating remarkable durability that appeared competitive with, or potentially superior to, contemporary first-line combination strategies. This sequential strategy leverages the complementary mechanisms of second-generation irreversible pan-HER inhibitors followed by selective third-generation EGFR-TKI therapy targeting both sensitizing and T790M resistance mutations [15, 16]. The biological rationale underlying this strategy is supported by the high T790M positivity rate (96%) among patients who progressed on afatinib and subsequently received osimertinib therapy, consistent with afatinib’s potent and irreversible EGFR inhibition that preferentially selects for T790M-mediated resistance over alternative bypass mechanisms [20]. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that prolonged first-line EGFR-TKI therapy promotes the emergence of T790M-mediated resistance. Huang et al. reported that patients treated with first-generation EGFR-TKIs for more than 11 months were significantly more likely to develop T790M mutations, reflecting the sustained selective pressure that drives this resistance mechanism [21]. Our data corroborate these observations in the context of afatinib, where potent and irreversible EGFR inhibition appears to preferentially select for T790M rather than alternative bypass pathways.

The survival advantage observed with sequential second- to third-generation EGFR-TKI therapy in our study is further supported by the Korean RESET real-world cohort, in which patients receiving sequential TKIs achieved approximately twofold longer survival than those who did not receive such sequencing [22]. This consistency across independent Asian real-world cohorts—the GIANT study in Taiwan and the RESET study in Korea—strengthens the evidence base for sequential TKI strategies and underscores the biological and clinical validity of this treatment paradigm in routine practice.

From a pragmatic healthcare perspective, sequential afatinib–osimertinib therapy offers several practical advantages that merit consideration, particularly in resource-limited settings and healthcare systems with reimbursement constraints. Sequential monotherapy strategies avoid the cumulative toxicities associated with combination chemotherapy–TKI regimens, potentially improving treatment tolerability and patients’ quality of life [23]. The substantial cost difference between sequential monotherapies and upfront combination strategies represents a meaningful consideration for healthcare systems and patients facing financial toxicity. Furthermore, reserving osimertinib therapy for T790M-positive progression enables biomarker-guided precision therapy, ensuring that the most advanced TKI reaches patients who are most likely to benefit while maintaining therapeutic options for subsequent lines. Under the constraints of Taiwan’s National Health Insurance reimbursement policies during the study period, this sequential strategy maximized therapeutic benefit and promoted more efficient resource utilization in clinical practice.

Critical evaluation of our study cohort reveals important differences in patient eligibility and baseline characteristics compared to landmark clinical trials. The FLAURA-2 and MARIPOSA trials enrolled highly selected patient populations with stringent inclusion criteria, including a mandatory ECOG performance status of 0–1, adequate organ function, and specific exclusion criteria that eliminated many patients encountered in routine practice. In contrast, 5.6% of the patients enrolled in the GIANT study had an ECOG performance status of ≥ 2, reflecting real-world practice patterns where treatment decisions must accommodate patients with compromised performance status. When we restricted our analysis to patients enrolled in the GIANT study who met the trial eligibility criteria for performance status (ECOG 0–1), survival outcomes remained robust and closely aligned with those in the trials, suggesting that the effectiveness of the sequential strategy translates across performance status strata.

The baseline prevalence of central nervous system (CNS) disease also differed across studies, with brain metastases present in 32–34% of patients enrolled in the GIANT study compared to 40–44% in the FLAURA-2 and MARIPOSA trials. This difference may reflect varying screening practices, with contemporary trials mandating baseline brain imaging while real-world practice employs symptom-driven assessment. Importantly, our subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent survival benefits with sequential afatinib–osimertinib therapy regardless of baseline brain metastasis status, with the median OS exceeding 51 months in patients with CNS involvement—a remarkable outcome given the historically poor prognosis of this population. These findings support the effectiveness of sequential TKI strategies in managing intracranial disease, likely attributable to osimertinib’s superior CNS penetration and activity against brain metastases [24].

Supportive care, imaging technologies, molecular diagnostics, and clinical management strategies underwent substantial evolution during the GIANT study period, spanning 2016 to 2024. In our study cohort, earlier patients received treatment in the era before the approval of first-line osimertinib therapy, when sequential strategies represented standard practice, while later patients were treated in an era with expanding awareness of combination approaches. This temporal heterogeneity introduces complexity in outcome interpretation but simultaneously provides valuable real-world evidence that spans the evolution of clinical practice.

The GIANT study has several important limitations that warrant acknowledgment. To begin with, its retrospective design introduces inherent selection bias, confounding by indication, and incomplete data capture that limit causal inference [25, 26]. In addition, treatment allocation was non-randomized and influenced by clinical factors, T790M testing availability, physician preferences, and reimbursement considerations. For instance, the prolonged PFS with first-line afatinib therapy of 20.4 months in the osimertinib-sequenced group substantially exceeded the 10.3 months observed in the chemotherapy group, suggesting that patients who ultimately received osimertinib therapy may have had more indolent disease biology or superior treatment adherence. Indeed, the significantly higher response rate to first-line afatinib therapy in this group (79% vs. 64%, p < 0.001) supports this interpretation.

Furthermore, T790M testing was not performed uniformly across the study cohort, with 34% of patients lacking post-progression molecular assessment. The T790M rate in this study (59%) is higher than that previously reported [27]. A possible explanation is that we did not systematically track biopsy adequacy or failure rates, which could have inflated our T790M positivity rate. That said, we did not exclude non-evaluable or failed samples from the denominator; instead, cases without confirmed T790M status were categorized as “unknown/not done.” Another limitation is that detailed procedural-level biopsy data were not consistently available for all centers, reflecting the retrospective and multicenter nature of this study. In addition, the GIANT dataset spans multiple years (2016–2024), during which changes in Taiwan’s National Health Insurance reimbursement policies and improvements in diagnostic testing accessibility could have influenced treatment continuation patterns and clinical outcomes. For example, patients treated in the earlier years of the study received care before the widespread availability of liquid biopsy technologies and before osimertinib was accessible as a first-line option, whereas patients treated later benefited from expanded molecular testing capabilities and evolving treatment paradigms.

Lastly, cross-trial comparisons represent indirect analyses subject to substantial confounding from differences in patient populations, treatment eras, supportive care practices, and outcome assessment methodologies [28]. Individual patient-level data from the FLAURA-2 and MARIPOSA trials were unavailable, precluding robust statistical adjustment for baseline differences. Finally, the predominantly Taiwanese patients in our cohort population may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic populations with different EGFR mutation distributions and treatment responses.

Despite these limitations, the GIANT study provides valuable real-world evidence that complements RCT findings by reflecting clinical practice beyond controlled settings. Real-world data capture authentic treatment decisions, drug accessibility constraints, patient heterogeneity, and management approaches that characterize routine oncology care. In this context, the study’s limitations can be reframed as strengths—demonstrating how sequential TKI therapy performs in populations with comorbidities, variable performance status, financial constraints, and treatment discontinuations that would typically exclude them from clinical trials. The concordance between our real-world survival outcomes and those reported in highly selected RCT populations further suggests that the sequential afatinib–osimertinib strategy delivers robust clinical benefits across diverse patient groups and practice settings.

Our findings suggest that sequential afatinib-to-osimertinib therapy represents a durable, effective, and economically viable treatment strategy for patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC, achieving survival outcomes that appear competitive with contemporary combination strategies. For healthcare systems and patients where upfront combination strategies pose financial, toxicity, or access barriers, sequential TKI therapy offers a rational alternative supported by robust real-world evidence. However, prospective randomized trials directly comparing sequential monotherapy strategies to upfront combination strategies are needed to definitively establish optimal treatment paradigms. Future studies should incorporate comprehensive quality-of-life assessments, cost-effectiveness analyses, and patient-reported outcomes to guide personalized treatment selection beyond survival endpoints alone.

The GIANT study provides robust real-world evidence demonstrating that sequential afatinib-to-osimertinib therapy achieves a remarkable median OS of 55 months in Taiwanese patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC, representing outcomes competitive with or exceeding contemporary first-line combination strategies in the FLAURA-2 and MARIPOSA trials. The consistent survival benefits observed across EGFR mutations (Del19 and L858R) and regardless of baseline brain metastasis status highlight the broad applicability of this sequential strategy. The high T790M positivity rate (96%) among patients progressing on afatinib, along with the extended PFS with second-line osimertinib therapy of 32.8 months, highlight the biological rationale and clinical effectiveness of biomarker-guided sequential therapy.

While acknowledging the inherent limitations of the retrospective design and cross-trial comparisons, these findings challenge the paradigm that upfront combination therapy invariably produces superior outcomes and support sequential TKI monotherapy as a viable, evidence-based treatment strategy. Prospective randomized trials directly comparing sequential to combination strategies are warranted to definitively establish optimal treatment paradigms incorporating quality-of-life and cost-effectiveness endpoints.
