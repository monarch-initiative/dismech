BACKGROUND: Smith Magenis Syndrome (SMS) is a rare genetic disorder caused by 
RAI1 haploinsufficiency. Obesity in people with SMS is believed partially due to 
dysfunction of the proximal melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) pathway. We therefore 
studied effects of treatment with the MC4R agonist setmelanotide on obesity and 
hunger, as well as metabolic, cardiac and safety, in individuals with SMS.
METHODS: People with SMS received once-daily setmelanotide injections, with the 
dose titrated bi-weekly to a maximum of 3 mg over ∼1 month; and a full-dose 
treatment duration of 3mo. The primary outcome was percent change in body 
weight. Secondary outcomes included hunger, waist circumference, body 
composition, and safety.
RESULTS: 12 individuals, ages 11-39 y, enrolled and 10 completed the full-dose 
treatment phase. Mean percent change in body weight at end-treatment was 
- 0.28 % [(95 % CI, -2.1 % to 1.5 %; n = 12; P = 0.66]. Participants experienced 
a significant decrease in total cholesterol associated with a significant 
decrease in HDL-cholesterol and a trend for lower LDL-cholesterol. Self-reported 
hunger was reduced at end-treatment (p = 0.011). All participants reported 
adverse events (AEs), most commonly injection-site reactions and skin 
hyperpigmentation. No AEs led to withdrawal or death.
CONCLUSIONS: In this trial, setmelanotide did not significantly reduce body 
weight in participants with SMS. Participants reported significant differences 
in hunger, but such self-reports are difficult to interpret without a 
placebo-treated group. The changes in lipid profiles require further 
investigation. Results of this study do not suggest that dysfunction of the 
proximal MC4R pathway is the main etiology for obesity in people with SMS.

Smith-Magenis Syndrome (SMS) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by either an interstitial 17p11.2 deletion that encompasses the retinoic acid-induced 1 gene (RAI1) in ~90% of cases, or a pathogenic variant of RAI1 (~10% of cases) causing haploinsufficiency [1–3]. Incidence of SMS is estimated to be 1:15,000 to 1:25,000 [4]. Individuals with SMS exhibit distinctive craniofacial features, infantile hypotonia with feeding difficulties and failure to thrive, neurobehavioral issues, short stature, otolaryngologic symptoms, and childhood onset obesity [1,4]. Most people with SMS have overweight or obesity (80% with body mass index [BMI]≥85th percentile) and often exhibit overeating and foraging behaviors by the time they reach adolescence [5] requiring constant vigilance by their caregivers [6]. No specific pharmacotherapies exist to treat obesity and hyperphagia in people with SMS.

RAI1 haploinsufficiency has been shown to affect feeding, satiety and fat deposition patterns in mice and humans [5]. Rai1+/− mice exhibit high circulating leptin and peptide YY, indicating high peripheral satiety signals. The obesity seen in these mice appears due to hyperphagia caused by disruption to hypothalamic satiety signaling pathways [5]. RAI1 directly regulates the expression of brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), whose deficiency is associated with hyperphagia, early-onset obesity, hyperactivity, and cognitive impairment in mice and humans [5,7,8]. Rai1+/− mice also are described to have reduced proopiomelanocortin (Pomc) expression, which is correlated to increased food intake and body weight [5,9], suggesting proximal melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) signaling might be disrupted. It has thus been hypothesized that obesity in individuals with SMS is at least in part caused by hypothalamic dysfunction, specifically within the MC4R pathway that regulates energy balance and body weight (Figure 1).

If deficient signaling upstream of the MC4R is primarily responsible for the obesity of SMS, a possible treatment for obesity in people with SMS would be augmentation of MC4R signaling by an MC4R agonist. Setmelanotide is an MC4R agonist that retains specificity and functionality of α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH), the endogenous ligand of MC4R. Setmelanotide preferentially activates MC4R which may compensate for the upstream deficiencies in the pathway [10,11]. Previously, setmelanotide was effective in reducing obesity in individuals with POMC and LEPR deficiency obesity, as well as those with Bardet-Biedl Syndrome (BBS) [10–13]. To investigate the importance of proximal MC4R signaling in the obesity and hyperphagia of individuals with SMS, we investigated the impact of open-label setmelanotide treatment in individuals with SMS.

The data in this investigation were collected in a phase 2, open-label, multi-institutional “basket-design” pilot study that included a number of distinct cohorts of individuals aged 6y and older with rare genetic disorders of obesity that included POMC/PCSK1/LEPR heterozygous deficiency obesity, POMC/PCSK1/LEPR compound heterozygous or homozygous deficiency obesity, Smith-Magenis Syndrome (SMS), Bardet-Biedl Syndrome (BBS), SH2B1 deficiency obesity, chromosomal rearrangement of the 16p11.2 locus causing obesity, carboxypeptidase E (CPE) compound heterozygous or homozygous deficiency obesity, leptin deficiency obesity with loss of response to metreleptin, SRC1 deficiency obesity and MC4R deficiency obesity. This analysis reports data from participants with a genetic diagnosis of SMS. All participants (or guardians/legally authorized representatives) signed written informed consent forms, and child participants provided assent before any study-related procedures were performed. This study was conducted in accordance with the International Council on Harmonisation for Good Clinical Practice, Declaration of Helsinki, and the appropriate regulation requirements. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards for each site and signed consent and assent as appropriate were obtained before the start of any study procedures. This protocol was approved by each of the Investigator’s IRBs (Rhythm reference #: RM-493-014) and was registered by Rhythm Pharmaceuticals as ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03013543 on 2017-01-04.

Eligible participants for this analysis included individuals with genetically confirmed diagnosis of SMS who had obesity, defined for those ages 16y and older as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and for children ages 6y to <16y as BMI≥ 95th percentile for age and sex. Individuals were excluded from participation in this study if they had recently (within 2mo) participated in an intensive diet and/or exercise regimen with or without the use of weight loss agents that resulted in >2% weight loss. Other exclusion criteria included: use of medication approved to treat obesity within 3mo of first dose of study drug; gastric bypass surgery within the previous 6mo or any prior gastric bypass surgery resulting in >10% weight loss durably maintained; diagnosis of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, personality disorder, major depressive disorder, or other psychiatric disorder(s); suicidal ideation, attempt or behavior; clinically significant pulmonary, cardiac, or oncologic disease; HbA1c > 9.0% at screening; history of significant liver disease; glomerular filtration rate (GFR) < 30 mL/min at screening; history or close family history of melanoma or participant history of oculocutaneous albinism; significant dermatologic findings relating to melanoma or pre-melanoma skin lesions; participation in any clinical study with an investigational drug/device within 3mo prior to the first day of dosing; prior exposure to setmelanotide; inability to comply with a once-daily injection regimen; and females who were breastfeeding or nursing.

Adults and children who were found eligible started taking subcutaneous injections of 2.0 mg/day and 1.0 mg/day, respectively, with a dose titration increase of 1.0 mg every 2 weeks until a maximum dose of 3.0 mg/day was reached. The dose-titration phase varied in duration from 2–4 weeks depending on number of titrations needed to establish the top tolerated dose. Participants continued to receive daily setmelanotide for a total of 12 more weeks (3mo). Due to variable lengths of titration phases, each participant’s end-treatment time point occurred at different weeks of follow-up. Body weight, physical examinations, and safety assessments, including blood pressure and heart rate, were recorded at every visit. Body composition assessments (Tanita Scale SC-240 [TANITA Corporation, Tokyo, Japan] at Marshfield clinic or iDXA [Lunar Corporation, Madison, WI, USA] at the National Institutes of Health, Baylor College of Medicine, Washington University in St. Louis, Division of Pediatric Endocrinology and Diabetes, University Medical Center Ulm, and Seattle Children’s Research Institute) along with metabolic, endocrine, hematologic, and pharmacokinetic testing were conducted at regular intervals. Change in patients’ hunger was assessed throughout the course of treatment using a Global Hunger Questionnaire which asked 1 question at all visits: “Overall, how would you rate the hunger you experience now? (1 = ‘No hunger’, 2 = ‘Mild hunger’, 3 = ‘Moderate Hunger’, 4= ‘Severe Hunger’).” At follow-up visits, a second question was also asked: “Overall, how does the hunger you experience now compare to your hunger before you started the study? (1 = ‘Much less hungry’, 2 = ‘Somewhat less hungry’, 3 = ‘No change in hunger’, 4 = Somewhat hungrier’, 5 = ‘Much hungrier’)”. Dermatological assessments were also conducted throughout the progression of the study. Depression and suicidality were regularly assessed and monitored throughout the entire course of the study using the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale and Patient Health Questionnaire-9.

The primary outcome of this study was percent change in body weight after 3mo of treatment at the top dose (total treatment duration including dose titration 4mo). A key secondary outcome was safety and tolerability of setmelanotide injections, assessed by the frequency and severity of AEs, vital signs, and laboratory evaluations. Other secondary and exploratory outcomes included change from baseline in Global Hunger scores, waist circumference, BMI, body fat mass, lean mass, fasting lipids, glucose-related variables, changes in physical examinations, clinical laboratory evaluations and injection-site reactions over the 4mo of setmelanotide administration.

Due to the rarity of SMS in the general population and the exploratory nature of this phase 2, proof-of-concept study in people with rare genetic diseases of obesity [10], the size of the SMS sample was a priori planned to be no more than 15 individuals; this sample size was not selected to meet specific statistical testing considerations. For all outcomes, baseline was defined as the last value measured prior to the first dose of study drug. Outcomes were summarized using a last observation carried forward (LOCF) analysis. Unless stated otherwise, P values (via two-tailed, paired T-tests at a 0.05 significance level) and corresponding 95% two-sided confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For hunger scores, the nonparametric Related-Samples Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test was used. Results should be considered exploratory for effect size estimation purposes rather than for formal statistical hypothesis testing, and no statistical corrections for multiple comparisons were used.

Twelve participants with SMS were screened for eligibility from October 2018 through June 2021. All 12 were found eligible and initiated setmelanotide injections (Table 1). Six had a heterozygous deletion in chromosome 17p11.2 that included the RAI1 gene and six had a heterozygous intragenic RAI1 pathogenic variant that was a frameshift, missense, or nonsense mutation. The average age of participants was 19.5±8.2y, with 4 participants between the ages of 11–14y and 8 participants age ≥16y. The average baseline weight and BMI of participants were 92.5±24.6kg and 38.8±8.7kg/m2 (Table 2). Participants <16y all had BMI≥110% of the 95th percentile [14]. The median overall current hunger, as measured by the global hunger questionnaire, was 4 at baseline, which was equivalent to “extreme hunger” on a scale of 1–4.

All 12 participants reached the full dose of setmelanotide, and ten participants (83%) completed the 3-month full-dose treatment period. Two participants discontinued treatment by 2mo of study drug, with one withdrawn by the caregiver because of no weight change and one withdrawn by the investigator because of noncompliance with taking study drug. Despite early termination, all participants are represented in baseline and final data sets (and figures) through the LOCF method of analysis. Among participants, compliance with the injection regimen (percentage of prescribed injections taken) was 72.3%. At the end-treatment visit, four participants were missing data for HbA1c; three people were missing data for weight, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and heart rate; two participants were missing data for current hunger, waist circumference, lipid profiles, fasting glucose and leptin; one participant was missing body composition data. To account for these missing data at the end-treatment visit, we used the last observation carried forward (LOCF) analysis method. Baseline values, end-treatment (LOCF) values, and changes in body composition and laboratory data during the study are shown in Table 2. Individual weight and BMI trajectories are shown in Fig. 2A–D.

The LOCF analysis (Table 2) showed that participants did not achieve a significant mean change in body weight from baseline at end-treatment (−0.28% [95% CI, −2.1% to 1.5%]; n=12; P=0.66). Only 1 participant lost more than 5% of initial body weight. There was also no significant mean change in BMI from baseline at end-treatment (−0.27kg/m2 [95% CI, −0.98kg/m2 to 0.45kg/m2]; n=12; P=0.43). Similarly, there were non-significant changes from baseline in body fat mass (−0.88kg [95% CI, −3.65kg to 1.88kg]; n=12; P=0.50; Fig. 3A), in lean body mass (1.64kg [95% CI, −0.69kg to 3.96kg]; n=12; P=0.15; Fig. 3B), and in waist circumference (−3.28cm [95% CI, −8.07cm to 1.50cm]; n=12; P=0.27, Fig. 3C). The one participant who lost more than 5% in body weight, age 39y, lost 14 kg of fat mass while gaining 9 kg in lean mass, suggesting, since there was such a substantial gain in lean mass, that the participant may have engaged in a very vigorous lifestyle program that included a marked increase in physical activity. There were no significant differences in weight change for patients with gene deletions or pathogenic variants (−1.3±3.0 vs. 0.6±1.0; F=2.1, p=0.176).

Neither mean systolic nor diastolic blood pressure changed significantly after treatment (Table 2). Mean change in systolic and diastolic blood pressure values from baseline at end-treatment were −5.17mmHg (95% CI, −10.77mmHg to 0.43mmHg); n=12; P=0.067 and 0.61mmHg (95% CI, −4.15mmHg to 5.37mmHg); n=12; P=0.78, respectively. Similarly, mean heart rate did not change significantly; change in heart rate from baseline at end-treatment was 1.97 BPM (95% CI, −5.51 BPM to 9.46 BPM); n=12; P=0.57.

Interestingly, nominally significant changes in lipid profiles were observed after treatment with setmelanotide (Table 2). Participants experienced a decrease in total cholesterol (−16.83mg/dL [95% CI, −31.62mg/dL to −2.04mg/dL]; n=12; P=0.029) that was at least in part due to a decrease in HDL-cholesterol (−4.67mg/dL [95% CI, −8.54mg/dL to −0.79mg/dL]; n=12; P=0.022) as well as a trend towards a reduced LDL-cholesterol (−13.0mg/dL [95% CI, −26.73mg/dL to 0.73mg/dL]; n=12; P=0.061). However, serum triglycerides did not change significantly from baseline, with a mean percent change from baseline of 0.42mg/dL [95% CI, −25.64mg/dL to 26.47mg/dL]; n=12; P=0.97). After treatment with setmelanotide there was a statistically significant relationship between change in weight during the study and change in total cholesterol (Fig. 3D. slope=5.704, P=0.049). There were no statistically significant relationships between change in weight and change in LDL cholesterol (slope=3.251, p=0.258) or change in weight and change in HDL cholesterol (slope=0.929, p=0.251).

Neither serum glucose (P=0.44) nor HbA1c (P=0.46) changed significantly during the study (Table 2). As expected, participants had elevated mean (±SD) circulating leptin concentrations at baseline (63.6±24.5 ng/mL) but participants did not experience a significant change in leptin from baseline at end-treatment (1.2ng/mL [95% CI, −9.69ng/mL to 12.09ng/mL]; n=10; P=0.81).

Using the last observation carried forward analysis, participants reported significant reduction in the median overall hunger they currently experienced from baseline at end-treatment (Fig. 3E, p=0.011) on a scale of 1–4. On a scale of 1–3 (1= “much less hungry”, 2= “somewhat less hungry”, 3= “no change in hunger”), participants reported a median score of 1 (“much less hungry”) after treatment with Setmelanotide.

Per protocol analyses, using only the data for those who took setmelanotide for the entire study period and had both baseline and final results, showed the same pattern for both significance and direction of findings and are therefore not separately presented.

According to the C-SSRS questionnaire there were no treatment emergent suicidal ideations or suicidal behavior among participants after treatment with setmelanotide. At baseline, participants scored a mean (±SD) score of 6.4 ± 4.0 on the PHQ-9. The percent change in PHQ-9 score from baseline at end-study was −48.9% and the absolute change was statistically significant at −2.9 (95% CI, −5.63 to −0.17); n=11; P=0.05.

Setmelanotide was generally well tolerated even though each participant reported at least one treatment-emergent AE and at least one drug-related AE (Table 3). All participants experienced at least one form of injection site reaction (100%). The next most common treatment-emergent adverse event was skin hyperpigmentation (75%). Participants also experienced treatment-emergent diarrhea (33.3%), nausea (16.7%), new nevi (16.7%), new macules/papules/plaques (25%) and hyperlipidemia (16.7%). One participant experienced a treatment-emergent incomplete right bundle branch block which investigators considered mild and possibly related to setmelanotide treatment. This participant was considered recovered 3mo after discontinuing treatment. One female participant experienced mild treatment-emergent polymenorrhea (10%). One male participant experienced a mild treatment-emergent increase in erection frequency (50%) as well as mild erectile dysfunction (50%). One participant experienced mild insomnia (8.3%) which subsequently remitted. One participant experienced mild eosinophilia from which they recovered during the course of treatment. There were no serious treatment-emergent AEs, nor were there any treatment-emergent AEs that led to study withdrawal or death.

We present data investigating the effects of an MC4R agonist on body weight in people with SMS, an autosomal dominant condition caused by loss of function of RAI1 that has been proposed potentially to be caused by impaired proximal MC4R pathway signaling. This open-label, phase 2 trial investigated the safety and efficacy of setmelanotide for the treatment of obesity and hyperphagia related to SMS as well as its effects on obesity-associated laboratory values. After 3 months of full-dose (3mg) treatment, setmelanotide was not associated with substantial reduction of body weight in study participants. There is a potential that weight loss was underestimated since outcomes were assessed using a last observation carried forward analysis, but similar results were found for a per protocol analysis, suggesting that few individuals with SMS are likely to experience the robust weight losses observed for individuals with POMC deficiency, LEPR deficiency, or the Bardet Biedl syndrome [10–13], where large total weight reductions are frequently observed early in treatment.

RAI1 haploinsufficiency in mice leads to downregulated POMC which in people is associated with severe early-onset obesity [9]. Therefore, we hypothesized that setmelanotide would restore signaling activity in the MC4R pathway by compensating for defects upstream of the receptor and directly activating MC4R neurons in the hypothalamus of patients with SMS. The results of the current report suggest that SMS-related obesity may in part be mediated by mechanisms outside of the proximal MC4R signaling pathway. MC4R pathway activity also influences Bdnf expression downstream of MC4R [5], and the pathway’s effects on food intake are shown to be regulated through activation of Bdnf’s TrkB receptor [15]. Chip-chip and reporter studies support direct binding of RAI1 to an intronic Bdnf enhancer as the primary means of Bdnf regulation [5]. The importance of RAI1 regulation of BDNF, which occurs downstream of the MC4R may provide insight regarding why setmelanotide was relatively ineffective for weight loss in people who have SMS. Central infusion of BDNF in MC4R-deficient mice was shown to suppress their hyperphagia and reverse their obesity [15], and it is possible BDNF might provide a possibly more effective treatment for people with SMS and obesity. The link between RAI1, BDNF and SMS symptoms is not fully understood, and it is important to explore the role of BDNF in obesity more critically in genetic disorders like SMS that cause obesity. To our knowledge, there are no other pharmacotherapy studies of obesity treatment for patients with SMS, and data for the benefits of the medications currently approved for nonsyndromic obesity treatment are thus also lacking.

The safety profile of setmelanotide was consistent with that observed in previous clinical studies in individuals with POMC, LEPR deficiency obesity or a clinical diagnoses of BBS [10–13]. In participants with SMS, setmelanotide was well tolerated with no discontinuations due to AEs, and the most common treatment emergent AEs were injection site reactions and skin hyperpigmentation. There were no associations between setmelanotide treatment and statistically significant changes in heart rate or blood pressure.

Participants in the current study experienced a significant decrease in total cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, and a trending decrease in LDL-cholesterol. Previous setmelanotide studies observed lowered total cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, triglycerides, and an increase in HDL- cholesterol which was consistent with the weight loss seen [13]. It is possible that even the small weight changes observed in the present study, combined with any dietary interventions families potentially initiated, were enough to slightly change cholesterol values. However, these HDL driven changes in cholesterol do not necessarily reflect lipid improvements, and do not constitute noteworthy consequences, particularly in this small sample. Future investigations of setmelanotide should include careful monitoring of lipid panels.

Significant changes were also seen in participant’s perceived current hunger scores. It is important to note that all participants had neurocognitive impairments that might have impacted how they responded, even though the severity of impairment prevented only one from completing the self-reported hunger questionnaires. Since these changes are subjective and independent of any statistically significant weight loss or change in body composition, it is unclear if the perceived decrease in hunger was due to a possible placebo effect or indeed occurred but was insufficient, given the complexities of syndromic obesity in people with SMS, to alter body weight. Perhaps changes in hunger affected macronutrient selection and thus contributed to the change in lipids. It is also possible that self-reported global hunger in SMS is not tightly linked to actual behaviors that affect body weight. If confirmed in placebo-controlled trials, reductions in hyperphagia might be beneficial to families managing individuals with SMS as a part of a multi-pronged strategy. For instance, given that disrupted circadian rhythms impact weight, tasimelton, recently FDA approved to improve sleep dysfunction in SMS could be studied in conjunction with setmelanotide.

There are currently no approved targeted treatments for obesity and hyperphagia in patients with SMS, or for most other rare genetic disorders of obesity [12]. Available therapies are focused on symptom management, and use approaches developed for the general population. Obesity that is left untreated can lead to many conditions including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, and certain types of cancers [16]. We suggest attention directed at Bdnf and its related pathways (Figure 1) may be needed to elucidate its role in obesity and hyperphagia phenotypes of individuals with SMS and inform future exploration of targeted therapies for SMS and perhaps the WAGR syndrome [17].

This study has certain limitations. First, the study was designed to be open-label, uncontrolled and non-comparative. This design was supported by the positive clinical responses to setmelanotide in previous open-label studies of individuals who have other monogenic MC4R pathway deficiencies [10–13]. Randomized trials would be needed to define setmelanotide’s effects for SMS, but given the limited efficacy observed in the current trial, may not be warranted. Due to cognitive impairments of individuals with SMS, the utility of the participant-completed questionnaires is in question. The overall compliance of only 72.3% is another limitation, since greater efficacy might have been observed if all doses of the medication had been administered. Another limitation, given the youngest participant in this study was 11y, is that these findings have limited generalizability for younger populations.

Setmelanotide did not significantly reduce mean body weight in participants with SMS over the course of 3mo of full-dose treatment. These results suggest that the proximal MC4R pathway does not play a leading role in the disease pathophysiology of SMS-related obesity, and that obesity characteristics in SMS are mediated by other mechanisms, perhaps involving downstream mediators such as BDNF.
