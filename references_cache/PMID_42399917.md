# Efficacy of leflunomide in active Takayasu arteritis: a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial : Takayasu arteritis clinical trial in China (TACTIC).
**Authors:** Sun Y, Kong X, Dai X, Wu R, Pan L, Wu L, Chen X, Yang P, Chen H, Ma L, Jin X, Zhang W, Jiang L
**Journal:** BMC Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12916-026-05009-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05009-1)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Leflunomide (LEF) shows promising effect in Takayasu arteritis (TAK), but evidence from randomized controlled trials is lacking. This study aims to investigate the efficacy and safety of LEF versus placebo combined with prednisone for the treatment of TAK.

METHODS: This is a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial at six sites across China, conducting from December 22, 2016, to November 4, 2022. A total of 116 eligible patients were recruited and randomized 1:1 to receive LEF (20 mg/d, p.o.) or matched placebo for 24 weeks, with all patients having initial prednisone of 0.6 mg/kg/d and following a taper starting at week 4. By week 24, patients in the LEF group who did not achieve clinical remission discontinued the study; all other patients (both LEF and placebo groups) received LEF (20 mg/d) from week 25 to week 52. The primary outcome was clinical remission at week 24. Secondary outcomes were time-to-clinical remission, mean prednisone dose at week 24, clinical remission in those who switched to LEF from week 25, disease recurrence and time-to-recurrence, imaging changes, and safety.

RESULTS: Fifty-four and 57, 45 and 48 patients were included in LEF and placebo group of modified intention-to-treat (mITT) and per-protocol set (PPS). In mITT set, clinical remission was achieved in 44/54 (81.5%) LEF- and 45/57 (78.9%) placebo-treated patients (risk difference: 2.6, 95%CI: -12.5, 17.2) at week 24. The same trend was observed in the sensitivity analyses. Post hoc analyses indicated greater risk differences (LEF minus placebo) in the female subgroup, ≤ 40 years subgroup, refractory subgroup, systemic symptoms subgroup, imaging type II subgroup and in those achieving the composite endpoint of both clinical remission and stable/improved imaging. At week 24, LEF group had a lower mean prednisone dose (mean difference: -2.1, [-4.3, 0.1] mg/d). Adverse events were observed in 13 (24.5%) LEF- and 22 (39.3%) placebo-treated patients. Serious adverse events were reported in six LEF- and four placebo-treated patients. One death was reported in placebo group.

CONCLUSIONS: Although the primary endpoint was not met, our results still provide evidence supporting LEF as a potential alternative treatment option for TAK.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02981979.

Takayasu arteritis (TAK) is a rare, large-vessel vasculitis that predominantly affects young Asian females [ 1– 3 ]. Vascular stenosis and occlusion can lead to severe ischemic events involving multiple organs (e.g., acute myocardial infarction, stroke) and even death, resulting in a significantly higher mortality risk (2.7 to 17.3 folds) in TAK patients compared to age- and sex-matched individuals in the general population [ 4– 7 ]. Timely and effective treatment is therefore crucial to improve outcomes in this young patient population.

Glucocorticoids (GCs) are the first-line therapy for active TAK [ 8 , 9 ]. High-dose GCs are initially efficacious, but disease recurrence can occur in ~ 60% of patients during GCs tapering [ 10 ]. Prolonged use of GCs is associated with substantial toxicity [ 11 , 12 ]. Consequently, immunosuppressive therapies are recommended to minimize the dose and duration of GC exposure, among which, conventional immunosuppressants are indicated for active TAK, while biologic agents are reserved for refractory cases [ 8 , 9 ]. However, evidence supporting the efficacy of these immunosuppressive therapies is largely derived from clinical experience, small open-label trials, and retrospective studies, with few phase 3 placebo-controlled trials available. To date, only six randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have been conducted in this area [ 13– 19 ], two of which evaluated the conventional immunosuppressant mycophenolate mofetil. There remains an unmet need for high-quality evidence to guide optimal treatment strategies.

Leflunomide (LEF) is a widely used conventional immunosuppressant that inhibits cell proliferation, suppresses tyrosine kinase signaling, and blocks the production of proinflammatory cytokines [ 20 ]. It has demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety in the treatment of autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis [ 21– 23 ]. In 2012, the first open-label study of 14 TAK patients demonstrated that 70% patients could achieve at least partial clinical remission, and GC dose could be reduced by 50% during LEF treatment [ 24 ]. Subsequent cohort and case-control studies have further reported the efficacy of LEF for active TAK [ 25– 30 ]. The most recent study reported a comparable complete response rate between LEF (78%) and adalimumab (88%) at 15-month follow-up [ 31 ]. Despite these promising findings, evidence from RCTs remains lacking.

We therefore conducted an investigator-initiated, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to investigate the efficacy and safety of LEF versus placebo combined with prednisone for active TAK: “Takayasu arteritis clinical trial in China” (TACTIC; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02981979).

This study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials reporting guideline for RCTs. The study protocol and statistical analysis plan are provided in Additional file 1.

TACTIC was an investigator-initiated, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, which was conducted in six centers across China in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki 1964. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital Fudan University (Shanghai, China) and each locally authorized ethics committees of the other clinical centers. Patients provided written informed consent. Research started on 22 December 2016 and completed on 4 November 2022.

A total of 116 participants, with active disease within the past 3 months, fulfilled 1990 American College of Rheumatology criteria for TAK [ 32 ] and met the eligible criteria according to the study protocol, were recruited (shown in Additional file 1).

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either LEF or placebo. The allocation sequence was computer-generated using a blocked randomization design (block sizes of 4), stratified by study center. This sequence was generated and maintained by an independent randomization center via a centralized interactive web-response system, ensuring allocation concealment. Assignment was thus fully concealed from the investigators enrolling participants. The treatment was masked to patients, investigators, clinical-outcome assessors, study site personnel, and statisticians throughout the trial. The packaging of LEF and placebo was identical in appearance, labeled only with a unique randomization number. For emergency use, each study site was provided with a set of sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes corresponding to the randomization numbers. If opened, the time, date, and reason were to be documented on the envelope and signed by the investigator. At trial end, all unopened envelopes were returned to the principal investigator to confirm that masking had been maintained.

Patients received oral LEF 10 mg twice daily or matched placebo through the first 24 weeks. Concurrently, all patients received oral prednisone at an initial dose of 0.6 mg/kg/d. The initial prednisone dose was maintained for 4 weeks and then tapered by 5.0 mg every 2 weeks. Tapering could be delayed if disease activity persisted, as assessed by investigators. Two independent investigators formulated a tapering plan for the same time. If there were inconsistence between the two investigators’ perspectives, a third experienced senior rheumatologist (Lindi Jiang) made the final decision. Once the prednisone dose was reduced to 10 mg/d, this dose was maintained until week 52 (Additional file 1 and Additional file 2: Table S1). Patients in the LEF group who did not achieve clinical remission by week 24 discontinued the study; all other patients (both LEF and placebo groups) received LEF (20 mg/d) from week 25 to week 52.

Patients were assessed on scheduled follow-up visits (baseline and weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24 28, 32, 40, 48, 52) (Additional file 1). The primary outcome was clinical remission at week 24, defined by meeting all of the following criteria: (i) no systemic symptoms; (ii) no new onset of ischemic symptoms or signs; (iii) a normal erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), defined as below of the upper limit of the reference values for each central laboratory.The determination of primary outcome will be reviewed and confirmed by an expert panel to exclude potential bias (e.g., infection, organ damage-related manifestations, or elevated ESR unrelated to disease activity).

The secondary outcomes included: (i) time-to-clinical remission: from the date of randomization to the date of first documented clinical remission, assessed up to 24 weeks; (ii) the mean prednisone dose at week 24; (iii) clinical remission at week 52 in those switching from placebo to LEF from week 25; (iv) disease recurrence through week 25 to week 52, which is denoted as reactivation of disease activity (NIH criteria ≥ 2) [ 33 ] for patients who had achieved clinical remission; (v) time-to-recurrence from the beginning of week 25 to the date of first documented recurrence, assessed up to 52 weeks; (vi) imaging changes at week 24 and week 52 compared with baseline as described in Protocol. Safety was assessed as the prevalence and severity of AEs, adverse drug reactions, and laboratory values according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

The sample size was calculated based on based on estimated clinical remission rates for the primary endpoint: 40% in the placebo group and 70% in the LEF group derived from literature [ 10 , 26– 31 ]. A superiority test with a margin of 10% was used. It was calculated that a sample size of 96 (48 per group) was needed, giving the trial 80% power to detect a significant difference between treatment groups with a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. With an estimated dropout rate of 10% after randomization, we planned to recruit 116 patients (58 per group).

Modified intention-to-treat (mITT) set included all patients who undergo randomization and received at least one dose of the intervention. Per protocol set (PPS) included those who completed ≥ 24 weeks of treatment, had no major protocol deviations, and maintained ≥ 80% drug compliance. Patients lost to follow-up before week 24 were classified as “non-responders” for primary outcome analyses.

The primary outcome analysis was conducted in the mITT set. The cumulative incidence of clinical remission at week 24 and its 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was estimated by the Wald test. The risk difference (LEF minus placebo group) and 95%CI was estimated using the Newcombe–Wilson test. Statistical significance was defined as a lower 95% CI bound > 10%. Missing values of ESR for the primary outcome definition were imputed using the observed value from the closest scheduled evaluation visit. There was no multiplicity adjustment in the analyses of primary outcomes. Sensitivity analyses included: (1) Newcombe–Wilson test in the PPS set; (2) imputed missing outcomes in best sceneries based on ITT set; (3) imputed missing outcome in worst sceneries based on ITT set.

For secondary outcomes analyses, the difference in mean prednisone dose between LEF and placebo groups at week 24 was estimated by covariance analyses after adjusting baseline levels. The least squares mean (LSM) for the changes of prednisone dose at week 24 was calculated using a mixed-effect model for repeated measures with treatment group, visit, treatment-by-visit interaction as fixed effect, baseline as the covariate and subject as a random effect. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to summarize time to clinical remission and time to disease recurrence and log-rank test was used to compare group differences. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95%CIs were estimated using Cox regression analyses with baseline calibration following the proportional hazards assumption test. No multiplicity adjustment was applied to secondary outcome analyses. Safety was assessed in the safety set as defined in Protocol, and adverse events were reported with p values without adjustment for multiple testing.

Post hoc subgroup analyses based on mITT set were conducted according to sex, age (> 40 years, ≤ 40 years), disease status (newly diagnosed, refractory patients referring to those relapsed or failed to get clinical remission to previous treatment), presence of systemic symptoms (yes or no), presence of ischemic symptoms (yes or no), ESR levels (normal or elevated), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels (normal or elevated), and imaging types (type I, type II, type III, type IV, type V) [ 34 ]. Post hoc analysis of a composite endpoint defined as achievement of both clinical remission and stable/improved imaging was also conducted.

The detailed statistical analysis plan was shown in Protocol. Statistical analyses were undertaken using SAS 9.2 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Prism 10.1 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical tests were two-sided and conducted at a significance level of p = 0.05.

A total of 116 patients were recruited and assigned randomly to the LEF group (n = 57) and placebo group (n = 59). As summarized in Figs. 1, 48, 54 and 57 patients were included in the LEF group and placebo group of mITT and mPPS sets, respectively. Baseline demographics and disease characteristics based on the mITT set were well balanced between LEF and placebo groups (Table 1). The mean age of patients at the time of informed consent was 35.7 ± 11.5 in the LEF group and 33.4 ± 10.9 in placebo group. The mean prednisone dose at baseline was 34.0 ± 6.9 mg/d and 32.8 ± 4.4 mg/d in the two groups, respectively.

In the mITT set, clinical remission at week 24 was achieved in 44/54 (81.5%) LEF-treated and 45/57 (78.9%) placebo-treated patients (risk difference: 2.6, 95%CI [-12.5, 17.2]). In the sensitivity analyses, the same trend was observed in PPS set (risk difference: 10.1 [-2.6, 23.1]), best sceneries based on ITT set (2.8 [-12.4, 18.2]), and worst sceneries based on ITT set (-0.9 [-7.8, 5.7]) (Table 2).

No significant difference in time to clinical remission was observed in the mITT set (HR: 1.11, 95%CI [0.7, 1.63]) or in the sensitivity analyses (Fig. 2).

At week 24, LEF group had a lower mean prednisone dose (11.6 ± 3.9 mg/d versus. 13.7 ± 6.7 mg/d; mean difference: -2.1, 95%CI [-4.3, 0.1] mg/d) and a greater reduction in dose (mean difference: 3.7 [-2.5, 12.4] mg/d) (Fig. 3A). Additionally, both groups showed improvements in ESR and hs-CRP levels at week 24 (mean difference in ESR: 6.4 [1.5, 12.4 mm/H; hs-CRP: 2.6 [-1.2, 6.7] mg/L), with a trend toward earlier improvement in the LEF group (Fig. 3B and C). By week 24, two patients in the LEF group, who did not achieve clinical remission, discontinued the study. Seven placebo-treated patients did not achieve clinical remission by week 24, among whom, three discontinued due to personal willingness, whereas the other four patients switched to LEF treatment (Fig. 1). These four switch patients (100%) achieved clinical remission by week 52.

Disease recurrence was assessed in 80 patients by week 52 as shown in Fig. 1. Overall, 12/80 (15.0%) patients experienced disease recurrence: seven in the initial LEF group and five in the initial placebo group. The estimated recurrence rate was 16.3% (95%CI [5.1, 26.7]) in the initial LEF group and 12.2% (2.1, 21.7) in the initial placebo group (HR: 1.36 [0.43, 4.31]) (Additional file 2: Figure S1).

At week 24, 5/54 (9.3%) LEF-treated and 4/57 (7.0%) placebo-treated patients indicated improved imaging compared with baseline (mean difference 2.3 [-1.2, 6.4]). Worsen imaging was observed in 3/54 (5.6%) LEF-treated and 5/57 (8.8%) placebo-treated patients (mean difference − 3.2 [-6.8, 0.9]). Worsen imaging included new stenosis (three, one in LEF group and two in placebo group), and progression of wall thickness (five, two in LEF group and three in placebo group) (Additional file 2: Table S2).

Imaging at week 52 demonstrated that 3/80 (3.8%) had improved compared with baseline. Progression of wall thickness was observed in 2/80 (2.5%) patients compared with baseline.

AEs occurrence by week 24 is summarized in Table 3. Infections were the most common AEs, presenting in 12/53 LEF-and 20/56 placebo treated patients (22.6% versus. 35.7%, p = 0.34). Alopecia (6/53 [11.3%] versus. 2/56 [3.6%], p = 0.05) and menstrual disorder (4/53 [7.5%] versus. 1/56 [1.8%], p = 0.04) were more common in the LEF group compared with placebo group. A significant difference in other AE items between groups was not observed. Six severe adverse events (SAEs) were reported in the LEF group, with three leading to study discontinuation (one perivalvular leakage; one cryptococcal pneumonia; one vision loss). In the placebo group, four SAEs were recorded and resulted in study discontinuation (one liver dysfunction; one hypertension; one ileus; one death caused by acute myocardial infarction).

During week 25 to week 52, the most common AEs were infection (28/87, 32.2%), followed by gastrointestinal reaction (23/87, 26.4%), liver dysfunction (13/87, 14.9%), and alopecia (11/87, 12.6%). Three SAEs were reported and caused study discontinuation (one bone fracture; one vulvar ulcer; one perianal abscess).

As shown in Table 4, greater risk differences (favoring LEF) were indicated in the following subgroups: female patients (5.2 [-2.3, 13.7]), aged ≤ 40 years subgroup (4.6 [-3.8, 22.1]), refractory patients (8.5 [-1.7, 20.5]), systemic symptoms subgroup (9.0 [-1.5, 19.8]), and imaging type II subgroup (30.0 [20.5, 44.5]). The composite endpoint (clinical remission plus stable/improved imaging) also showed a favorable risk difference for LEF 7.6 (-1.2, 15.9).

In this trial, we did not find sufficient evidence to demonstrate the superiority of LEF over placebo in inducing clinical remission at week 24. However, the study still provides three potential contributions in the following aspects. First, this study presents as the first multi-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to evaluate LEF efficacy in TAK treatment. Second, LEF showed a trend toward facilitating GC tapering and improving inflammatory markers. Third, post hoc analyses suggested numerical improvements of LEF over placebo in specific subgroups (female patients, aged ≤ 40 years, refractory disease, systemic symptoms, and imaging type II), though these still need to be confirmed in the future research. These findings support LEF as a potential alternative treatment for TAK.

The primary endpoint was not met likely due to the unexpectedly high remission rate in the placebo group (78.9% versus. the projected 40%). TAK is a rare disease for which few RCTs have been conducted, and sample size calculation is challenging because of limited existing knowledge for assuming the treatment effect or the performance of the placebo group. Furthermore, the high placebo-group remission rate may be attributed to the study’s GC tapering strategy, which was adjusted based on disease activity rather than a fixed schedule to minimize the risks of ischemic events. Additionally, maintaining prednisone at 10 mg/d until week 52, which was consistent with a recent RCT for TAK [ 19 ], may have also contributed to the high placebo-group remission rate. Indeed, the unexpectedly high remission rate in the placebo group is likely explained by this strategy rather than a true placebo effect.

In this study, LEF showed a favor trend in reducing GC doses and accelerating improvements in inflammatory markers, which is clinically meaningful for more rapid disease control and minimizing GC-related toxicity. Notably, a numerical higher cumulative incidence of clinical remission was observed in LEF treated refractory subgroup compared with matched placebo treated subgroups, which further supports its utility in patients with more severe or treatment-resistant disease. In addition, the recurrence rate in our study is comparable to that reported for other conventional immunosuppressants and biologic agent: methotrexate (16–44%) [ 27 , 35 , 36 ], tocilizumab (11–50%) [ 13 , 14 , 37– 39 ], tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors (10–53%) [ 27 , 31 , 39 ], and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (10–22%) [ 30 , 36 ]. This suggests that LEF may be effective for maintaining long-term remission. However, dedicated subgroup trials are needed to confirm these findings in the future.

Controlling vascular inflammation and the subsequent fibrosis is a big challenge in TAK [ 40 ]. However, GCs cannot improve vascular fibrosis; instead, long-term GCs treatment may aggravate vascular injury and increase the risk of cardiovascular events [ 12 ]. Recent studies have shown that LEF exerts antifibrotic effects in TAK by regulating macrophage differentiation [ 41 ], interfering with macrophage-derived glycoprotein metastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB) [ 42 ], and disrupting the binding of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 5 (PCSK5) to pro-transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) in aortic adventitial fibroblasts [ 43 ]. The lower rate of imaging progression in the LEF group compared with that in the placebo group (5.6% versus. 8.8%) support the notion that LEF might provide superior control of vascular inflammation and anti-vascular-fibrosis effects.

Though LEF did not increase the incidence of SAEs, it is still worth noting that one opportunistic infection in the LEF group was reported, leading to treatment discontinuation. The risk of infection associated with immunosuppressants must always be considered. In additionally, the incidence of alopecia and menstrual disorder was significantly higher in the LEF group compared to the placebo group. Considering that TAK predominantly affects young women, these side effects might negatively impact patients’ quality of life and potentially lead to long-term treatment non-adherence. Therefore, the risk-benefit balance should be considered in clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, our study was conducted exclusively in Asian patients, so we do not know if our findings could be generalized to other ethnic groups. Second, maintaining all patients at 10 mg/day prednisone until week 52 may likely mask signs and symptoms of disease activity, confounding efficacy assessments. Third, the post hoc findings in refractory subgroup patients may be weakened by the marked heterogeneity of these subgroups, which includes individuals with prior failure to diverse treatments (e.g. tocilizumab, MTX, etc.) and varying disease durations. The interpretation and application of these results should be in caution. Thus, the generalizability and practical implications of our findings need to be confirmed in further study.

This is the first multi-centers, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to evaluate LEF efficacy in TAK. Though the primary endpoint was not met, the results provide evidence supporting LEF as a potential alternative treatment option for TAK, particularly in specific subgroups and for facilitating GC tapering.
