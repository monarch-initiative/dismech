# Epigenetic age acceleration in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis revealed by DNA methylation clocks.
**Authors:** Kurbanov DB, Ahangari F, Adams T, De Man R, Tang J, Carlon M, Abu Hussein N, Cortesi E, Zapata M, De Sadelaar L, Wuyts W, Vanaudenaerde B, Kaminski N, McDonough JE
**Journal:** Am J Physiol Lung Cell Mol Physiol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1152/ajplung.00171.2024](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajplung.00171.2024)

## Content

In this research, we delve into the association between epigenetic aging and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), a debilitating lung disease that progresses over time. Utilizing the Illumina MethylationEPIC array, we assessed DNA methylation levels in donated human lung tissue from patients with IPF, categorizing the disease into mild, moderate, and severe stages based on clinical assessments. We used seven epigenetic clocks to determine age acceleration, which is the discrepancy between biological (epigenetic) and chronological age. Our findings revealed a notable acceleration of biological aging in IPF tissues compared with healthy controls, with four clocks-Horvath's, Hannum's, PhenoAge, and DunedinPACE-showing significant correlations. DunedinPACE, in particular, indicated a more rapid aging process in the more severe regions within the lungs of IPF cases. These results suggest that the biological aging process in IPF is expedited and closely tied to the severity of the disease. The study underscores the potential of DNA methylation as a biomarker for IPF, providing valuable insights into the underlying methylation patterns and the dynamics of epigenetic aging in affected lung tissue. This research supports the broader application of epigenetic clocks in clinical prognosis and highlights the critical role of biological age in the context of medical research and healthcare.NEW & NOTEWORTHY Using epigenetic clocks, we found a notable acceleration of biological aging in IPF tissues, particularly in DunedinPACE, suggesting that the biological aging process in IPF is accelerated and closely related to the severity of the disease. The study also underscores DNA methylation's potential as a biomarker for IPF, as well as the dynamics of epigenetic aging and the need to consider biological age in medical research and healthcare.

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a fatal interstitial lung disease with a median survival of 3-3.5 years, though survival can range up to 10 years.(1) It results in severe changes to normal lung biology, including alveolar epithelial cell hyperplasia, fibroblast proliferation and myofibroblast activity, and extracellular matrix deposition. However, while various agents of lung injury have been associated with IPF (e.g. smoking, dust, wood, livestock),(2) repeated injury is not sufficient to explain the persistent transformation of lung physiology and continued disease progression.

The disease course of IPF varies widely between individuals, including with respect to the speed of disease progression, and disease phenotypes have been associated with several factors including immune responses to bacteria,(3) the regulation of cigarette-smoke-induced inflammation,(4) and multiple pathways of alveolar epithelial and mesenchymal cell remodeling.(5)

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have yielded further insights into genetic factors contributing to IPF, and genetic associations with disease course and mortality outcomes. A recent GWAS study of idiopathic interstitial pneumonia analyzed 876 cases and replicated previously associated genes (e.g. MUC5B, involved in lung mucociliary clearance) and gene mutations involved in telomere length regulation, telomerase, and telomerase reverse transcriptase.(6) The results suggest that within IPF disease, there is a group of patients with genetic polymorphisms resulting in shortened telomeres, abnormal lung epithelial cell integrity and mucociliary clearance, all associated with increased mortality and fulminant disease.(7) Thus, the distinct pathological phenotypes reflect the intersection of all of the genetic and environmental factors involved in this complex disease.

Telomere shortening, in which DNA chromosome ends are increasingly exposed during cell division, resulting in DNA damage(8, 9) and metabolic dysfunction(10-12), is one of the molecular “hallmarks” of aging that has been demonstrated in IPF lung tissue. Age is thus the greatest risk factor associated with IPF, with age at onset of disease typically being greater than 50 years with an average of 70 years.(13) Therefore, it is yet unclear whether these changes reflect accelerated aging or the dysregulation of aging-associated pathways.

Epigenetic changes, such as DNA methylation (DNAm) are influenced by one’s environment, lifestyle, and genetics.(14) DNAm functions via the covalent addition of a methyl group to a cytosine nucleotide, largely at CpG dinucleotide sites.(15) DNAm itself has been implicated in IPF pathobiology. For example, increased expression levels of DNA methyltransferase enzymes have been identified in specific areas of IPF lung tissue, including hyperplastic epithelium at fibroblast foci, and changes in DNAm have been associated with changes in gene expression.(16)

DNAm is also associated with aging and the methylation status of specific CpG sites can be characterized as an “epigenetic clock”, including Horvath’s clock and Hannum’s clock,(17, 18) both of which have been shown to be highly associated with chronological age, and may even have a stronger correlation with chronological age than telomere length.(19) Epigenetic age acceleration is defined as the difference between the epigenetic clock’s prediction of biological age and one’s chronological age, and it functions as a biomarker of biological aging processes.(18) Second-generation epigenetic clocks, such as PhenoAge(20) and GrimAge,(21) have been developed that incorporate clinical biomarkers that emphasize the divergence of chronological age and biological age in morbidity and mortality to provide a measure of cumulative health effects of age-related damage. Most recently, a third generation clock DunedinPACE,(22) was developed with repeated measurements of a single cohort which focused on the aging process by reflecting the pace or rate of aging dynamics. More so than chronological age, these sorts of biological aging measures are strongly associated with all-cause and cause-specific mortality, as well as cognitive and physical function.(23-25) Epigenetic clocks, therefore, function as biomarkers of aging and age-related disease as a result of both internal and external forces, with the variance between individuals reflecting the integrated effects of time, environmental exposures, genetics, and disease.

Aging and aging-related processes (e.g. shortening of telomeres) have been shown to be accelerated in IPF lungs, but this occurs at differential paces within a single lung.(26) As a deeply heterogeneous disease, IPF disease severity can vary across a single lung, primarily affecting the peripheral and basal regions.(27) These epigenetic changes as identified by various epigenetic clocks, however, have not yet been examined through the scope of IPF. From these observations, the question of how disease severity in the lungs is associated with this differentially accelerated aging and epigenetic clocks arises.

Thus far, it has been unclear whether the biological markers used by the epigenetic clocks to determine aging are specifically associated with disease severity and regional variation. The clocks may predict mortality and morbidity in a patient, but in this paper, we examined the regional variation of disease severity and epigenetic clocks to identify how these clocks are associated with IPF severity and regional variation of aging progression. It was hypothesized that more severe regions of disease would have more accelerated aging based on the results of the epigenetic clocks. Nevertheless, investigating this relationship using epigenetic clocks and biological markers may reveal whether disease severity is related to accelerated aging in IPF.

Human lungs were collected following local hospital ethical committee approval (ML6385) and informed patient consent. Donor lungs were collected for research purposes in accordance with Belgian law. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of UZ Hospital in Leuven, Belgium. Samples were obtained as previously described.(28) Patients diagnosed with IPF who underwent lung transplantation at UZ Hospital in Leuven, Belgium were selected (n=10) and donor lungs not suitable for transplantation (n=6) were used as controls (Supplement Table S1). Two lung cores were randomly selected from upper, mid, and lower regions, with six samples taken per lung. One sample was discarded due to mislabeling. Subjects were males and matched for age.

Alveolar surface density measurements were previously obtained and described.(8) In brief, microCT scanning was used to obtain images of lung tissue and radiographic disease severity, quantified across samples as alveolar surface density (i.e. surface to volume ratio). IPF samples were then categorized into IPF1 (early-stage, similar to control), IPF2 (progressive), and IPF3 (advanced fibrosis) as previously described,(28) based on principal component and expectation-maximization clustering using alveolar surface density, and collagen 1 and collagen 3 volume fractions from histology.

DNAm levels were obtained using the Illumina Infinium Human MethylationEPIC BeadChip by the InfiniumHD methylation protocol, which measured bisulfite-conversion-based single-CpG resolution DNAm levels in the genome at 866,836 different sites. Raw IDAT files containing this DNAm data were generated using Illumina’s GenomeStudio software and then analyzed using R Statistical Software v4.1.2, except for AltumAge which was run with Python (3.11.0). All code is available in the supplement. Covariates, including technical and biological factors, were correlated with principal components to explore the effects of independent variables.

Seven epigenetic clocks were calculated: Horvath’s clock, Hannum’s clock, AltumAge, GrimAge, GrimAge2, Levine's PhenoAge, and DunedinPACE (see the supplement for details on their calculations). Correlations between chronological age and each DNAm age measure were evaluated, and scatter plots were generated. Age acceleration was defined as the residual from linear regression of epigenetic age estimate on chronological age, producing measures of DNAm age acceleration, where positive values indicate that a sample is biologically older than its chronological age suggests.

To analyze the relationship between epigenetic age acceleration and IPF (including IPF severity groups: IPF1, IPF2, IPF3), linear mixed-effects modeling was performed using disease state or disease severity group as fixed effects, and random intercepts for subject to account for the repeated measures design. The significance of the fixed effect was assessed using the Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT), which compares the goodness-of-fit of the full model (including the fixed effect) to a null model (excluding the fixed effect), yielding a Chi-square statistic and p-value. Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons of means.

Methylation data can be found in the gene expression omnibus (GSE268362).

Results reported are for the principal component versions of the Horvath, Hannum, PhenoAge, and GrimAge epigenetic clocks, as well as for non-principal component measures AltumAge, GrimAge2 and DunedinPACE. A correlation between chronological age and epigenetic age was measured by Horvath’s clock (r = 0.66), Hannum’s clock (r = 0.62), AltumAge (r = 0.79), PhenoAge (r = 0.6), GrimAge (r = 0.93), and GrimAge2 (r = 0.93, not shown), while DunedinPACE was not correlated with age (Figure 1). Measures were obtained by subject, with n=10 for IPF and n=6 for control.

Epigenetic age acceleration measures were obtained for association with IPF as well as IPF disease severity (Figure 2). IPF was associated with significant epigenetic age acceleration measured by Horvath’s Clock (χ2 (degrees of freedom: 1) = 4.71, p = 0.03) by about 4.1 years ± 2 (SE) in IPF lungs compared to controls, as well as a trend towards epigenetic age acceleration measured by Hannum’s Clock (χ2 (1) = 3.784, p = 0.052) by about 3.8 years ± 2 (SE). PhenoAge also measured significant epigenetic age acceleration in IPF (χ2 (1) = 5.72, p=0.017) by about 4.6 years ± 1.9 (SE), as did DunedinPACE (χ2 (1) = 7.82, p = 0.005). There was no association between IPF and epigenetic age acceleration measured by AltumAge (χ2 (1) = 1.68, p = 0.19), GrimAge (χ2 (1) = 0.79, p = 0.38), or GrimAge2 (χ2 (1) = 0.08, p = 0.77). Measures were obtained across all samples, with n=60 for IPF and n=35 for control.

When comparing IPF severity groups and control subjects (Figure 3), there was a significant difference in epigenetic age acceleration between the disease severity groups (IPF1, IPF2, IPF3) and the control group in DunedinPACE (χ2 (3) = 15.19, p=0.0017). Further post-hoc analysis using pairwise comparisons revealed that the differences in epigenetic age acceleration between control and IPF2 (estimate = −0.112, SE = 0.0388, p = 0.0384) and between control and IPF3 (estimate = −0.141, SE = 0.0395, p = 0.00718) were statistically significant. Additionally, there was a significant difference between IPF1 and IPF3 (estimate = −0.0751, SE = 0.0279, p = 0.0419), while the differences between control and IPF1 (estimate = −0.0657, SE = 0.0393, p = 0.358), IPF1 and IPF2 (estimate = −0.0459, SE = 0.0277, p = 0.352), and IPF2 and IPF3 (estimate = −0.0292, SE = 0.0274, p = 0.711) were not statistically significant.

There was no significant epigenetic age acceleration between the disease severity groups (IPF1, IPF2, IPF3) and the control group as measured by Horvath's clock (6.29, p = 0.098), Hannum’s Clock (4.60, p = 0.20), AltumAge (1.70, p = 0.64), PhenoAge (6.07, p = 0.11), GrimAge (1.1, p = 0.78), or GrimAge2 (1.80, p = 0.62). Measures were obtained across all samples and included IPF categories, with n=20 for IPF1, n=22 for IPF2, n=18 for IPF3, and n=35 for control.

In this study, we hypothesized that more severe regions of disease would have more accelerated aging based on the results of the epigenetic clocks and have, therefore, investigated epigenetic age acceleration in IPF lung tissue. A correlation between chronological age and epigenetic age as measured by various epigenetic clocks was identified. The clocks that demonstrated divergence with chronological age, including Horvath’s clock, Hannum’s Clock, PhenoAge, and DunedinPACE, were also associated with epigenetic age acceleration in IPF lungs compared to control lungs. In particular, the results from DunedinPACE were particularly intriguing with a significant difference in epigenetic age acceleration found between the disease severity groups and control subjects, particularly between IPF1 and IPF3 severity groups.

Epigenetic clocks of three different “generations” were used. DunedinPACE is a third-generation epigenetic clock not trained on chronological age, unlike first-generation and second-generation clocks, the latter of which is also trained to predict measures of dysregulated physiology. An ideal epigenetic clock has no residual and no acceleration. Indeed, improved prediction of chronological age from DNA methylation is associated with reduced association with mortality,(29) suggesting that association with mortality arises from biological age-associated “confounder” CpGs. DunedinPACE revealed differences in biological age between IPF severity groups because it measures the rate of aging, likely making it sensitive to dynamic, localized changes in disease progression such as inflammation and fibrosis that are characteristic of IPF. In contrast, second-generation clocks like PhenoAge focus on cumulative systemic aging, which may not capture the acute or regional variations seen in IPF.

Epigenetic age measures, especially sensitive biomarkers of biological aging, may provide insight into the mechanisms that link individual genetic risk factors with environmental exposures that accumulate over the course of life and result in morbidity and mortality. They can also serve as predictive biomarkers for therapy response. Third-generation clocks like DunedinPACE focusing on the rate of aging makes them useful for evaluating the impact of interventions or lifestyle changes on the aging process in real-time. DunedinPACE has demonstrated its potential as such a biomarker and is already functioning as one: a recent randomized control trial of long-term calorie restriction reported reduced “pace of aging” with a decrease in DunedinPACE age acceleration over two years,(30) while PhenoAge or GrimAge showed no change. Just as epigenetic age measures may allow for faster evaluation of geroscience interventions for aging, they may prove to be an important component in the development of candidate therapeutics for complex diseases such as IPF.

While this study provides insights into the relationship between epigenetic age acceleration and IPF severity, several potential pitfalls should be considered. Firstly, this study is cross-sectional in design, limiting our ability to establish causality or temporal relationships between epigenetic age acceleration and disease progression. This is particularly relevant as epigenetic clocks are typically developed from cross-sectional data, though DunedinPACE does benefit from a longitudinal cohort design. Additional longitudinal studies would help confirm the directionality of these associations. The use of whole-lung samples may obscure cellular heterogeneity in aging patterns, and future studies targeting specific cell populations, such as alveolar epithelial cells or fibroblasts, could provide deeper insights into cell-type-specific aging in IPF. Additionally, the inclusion of both a current and an ex-smoker in the control cohort may introduce confounding effects, as smoking influences DNA methylation patterns.(31) Larger cohorts with better control for smoking history are needed to assess its specific impact on epigenetic aging. The study's reliance on male samples, due to the limited availability of female biobank samples, also restricts the generalizability of findings across sexes. Future work should aim to include more balanced cohorts to evaluate potential sex-based differences. Furthermore, the epigenetic clocks used were trained on blood-based datasets and may not fully capture lung-specific aging dynamics. The observation of negative age acceleration in control lung samples suggests an organ-specific effect, highlighting the need for future development of lung-specific epigenetic clocks to better understand tissue-specific aging and disease processes. The findings were limited by the study's specific cohort, emphasizing the need for replication in larger, more diverse populations with integrated multi-omics data. External validation of epigenetic age acceleration in IPF samples is also needed, as a previous study on alveolar macrophages isolated from IPF patients’ bronchoalveolar lavage fluid did not identify epigenetic age acceleration in the epigenetic clocks used.(32) However, potential differences may have been obscured by the large variability of non-PC derived epigenetic clocks and the presence of unknown or unmeasured confounding variables. The use of PC versions of epigenetic clocks, as well as the DunedinPACE measure, which excluded probes with poor reliability, helped address this issue, but residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Lastly, this study is focused on a specific cohort which limits the generalizability of its results. The next step would be to replicate the study with larger and more diverse populations, as well as further integration of multi-omics data.

Overall, our findings show a link between epigenetic age acceleration and IPF progression which may help to further understand aging-associated dysregulation related to IPF. These results also support the significance of assessing biological aging as changes in biomarkers that are not accounted for by chronologic age but instead reflect more relevant decline in overall system function. Further investigation into the relationship between differential methylation, age acceleration, and gene expression may provide insights into IPF's underlying mechanisms, potential therapeutic targets, and the use of DNA methylation as a biomarker.
