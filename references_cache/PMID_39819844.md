# GEMIN5 and neurodevelopmental diseases: From functional insights to disease perception.
**Authors:** Martinez-Salas E, Francisco-Velilla R
**Journal:** Neural Regen Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.4103/NRR.NRR-D-24-01010](https://doi.org/10.4103/NRR.NRR-D-24-01010)

## Content

GEMIN5 is a predominantly cytoplasmic multifunctional protein, known to be involved in recognizing snRNAs through its WD40 repeats domain placed at the N-terminus. A dimerization domain in the middle region acts as a hub for protein-protein interaction, while a non-canonical RNA-binding site is placed towards the C-terminus. The singular organization of structural domains present in GEMIN5 enables this protein to perform multiple functions through its ability to interact with distinct partners, both RNAs and proteins. This protein exerts a different role in translation regulation depending on its physiological state, such that while GEMIN5 down-regulates global RNA translation, the C-terminal half of the protein promotes translation of its mRNA. Additionally, GEMIN5 is responsible for the preferential partitioning of mRNAs into polysomes. Besides selective translation, GEMIN5 forms part of distinct ribonucleoprotein complexes, reflecting the dynamic organization of macromolecular complexes in response to internal and external signals. In accordance with its contribution to fundamental cellular processes, recent reports described clinical loss of function mutants suggesting that GEMIN5 deficiency is detrimental to cell growth and survival. Remarkably, patients carrying GEMIN5 biallelic variants suffer from neurodevelopmental delay, hypotonia, and cerebellar ataxia. Molecular analyses of individual variants, which are defective in protein dimerization, display decreased levels of ribosome association, reinforcing the involvement of the protein in translation regulation. Importantly, the number of clinical variants and the phenotypic spectrum associated with GEMIN5 disorders is increasing as the knowledge of the protein functions and the pathways linked to its activity augments. Here we discuss relevant advances concerning the functional and structural features of GEMIN5 and its separate domains in RNA-binding, protein interactome, and translation regulation, and how these data can help to understand the involvement of protein malfunction in clinical variants found in patients developing neurodevelopmental disorders.

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play crucial roles in fundamental cellular processes, frequently assembled into dynamic macromolecular complexes. Typically, RBPs engaged in nucleic acid metabolism are organized in multi-subunit networks (Agarwal and Bahdur, 2023), such that each component performs a dedicated function. Therefore, the dynamic nature of these complexes requires the identification of their components to elucidate their role under specific cellular conditions (He et al., 2023). RBPs often comprise several RNA-binding domains (RBDs), which determine the interaction with their partners, either RNAs or other proteins (Lunde et al., 2007; Van Nostrand et al., 2020a). Many RBPs contain functionally separable domains that determine their post-transcriptional control of gene expression, including splicing, RNA stability, and translation (Luo et al., 2020; Schmok et al., 2024). In the particular case of understudied proteins, as in the case of GEMIN5, investigating the functional implications of the separable domains provides significant advantages to understand the activities of the entire protein. In turn, this adds to the fact that the presence of distinct domains bestows versatile features on many RBPs. Despite the critical role of known RBDs in well-characterized RBPs, studies done over the last years have evidenced the existence of numerous RBPs lacking conventional RBDs (Castello et al., 2012; Hentze et al., 2018). This observation reinforces the need for a comprehensive analysis of poorly studied RBPs to understand their involvement in RNA-dependent processes.

The protein GEMIN5 was identified as the 170-kDa protein of the survival of motor neurons (SMN) complex in human cells (Gubitz et al., 2002; Figure 1), and later in Drosophila melanogaster (Kroiss et al., 2008). The SMN complex is responsible for the biogenesis of small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (snRNP) complexes, the major constituents of the spliceosome (Pellizonni et al., 2002; Fischer et al., 2011). The SMN oligomeric complex includes 9 members, SMN, GEMINs 2–8, and unr-interacting protein (UNRIP, also known as Serine-Threonine Kinase Receptor-Associated protein, STRAP) (Otter et al., 2007). Each of the SMN members performs a non-redundant role (Faravelli et al., 2023). Assembly of most snRNPs takes place in the cytoplasm through the coordinated action of protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) and SMN complex, facilitated by the assembly chaperone pICln (Neuenkirchen et al., 2015). The SMN complex brings pre-small nuclear RNAs (pre-snRNAs) to the seven Sm core rings (E, F, G, D1, D2, D3, and B/B′) (Urlaub et al., 2001), through recognition of the snRNA code sequence (5′-AAUUUUUG-3′) by GEMIN5 (Battle et al., 2006). In turn, Sm core assembly depends on Sm proteins and snRNA recognition by the SMN/GEMIN2 complex via an intermediate step, consisting of GEMIN2 bound to the SmD1/D2/F/E/G pentamer, and the SMN-GEMIN2 binding domain (Zhang et al., 2011). SMN and snRNPs are also present in the nuclear compartment accumulating in Cajal bodies prior to targeting to splicing factor compartments (Raimer et al., 2017). This process is regulated by the target of rapamycin kinase-dependent phosphorylation of SMN serine residues (Schilling et al., 2021).

Reduction in SMN protein levels results in spinal muscular atrophy, a neurodegenerative disease caused by alterations of the survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene, characterized by loss of spinal motor neurons (Lefevbre et al., 1995; Burghes and Beattie, 2009). While the SMN complex works as a functional entity, recent evidence has shown that at least part of its members perform critical functions in diverse ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes (Matera et al., 2024). For instance, both SMN and GEMIN5 are ribosome-binding proteins (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2016; Lauria et al., 2020). GEMIN3, a DEAD-box RNA helicase (DDX20) also known as DP103, interacts with SMN (Charroux et al., 1999) and regulates the transcription of neuron-specific genes (Campbell et al., 2000). In mice, GEMIN3 is highly expressed in testis and is involved in the translation control of specific mRNA targets encoding regulators of the cell cycle by interacting with the translational machinery in prospermatogonia (Zou et al., 2024). In Drosophila melanogaster, Gemin3 is an essential gene required for larval motor function and pupation (Shpargel et al., 2009). Gene expression activities of GEMIN3 include regulation of transcription, mRNA transport, and atypical signaling pathways (Curmi and Cauchi, 2018). GEMIN4 forms part of a protein complex associated with calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase 2 (CAMKK2) and coat complex protein I (COPI), involved in Golgi-associated vesicle trafficking (Stewart et al., 2021). Besides, both GEMIN3 and GEMIN4 are involved in the biogenesis of miRNA machinery and the associated RNA-induced silencing complex (Mourelatos et al., 2002; Saliminejad et al., 2019), while SMN, GEMIN3, and GEMIN5 have been implicated in reprogramming regeneration of hair cells, liver, and caudal fin in zebrafish (Pei et al., 2020).

In recent years, genetic variants detected in genes encoding some of the proteins mentioned above have been connected to neurodevelopmental diseases, although the mechanisms directly related to the functions performed by the SMN proteins individually remain largely unknown. It is worth noting that while functional validation represents a powerful way to assess the pathogenicity of genetic variants in poorly characterized proteins, functional testing is not feasible in many cases. In these cases, gaining knowledge about the structure-function relationship in protein variants and the effects of their deleterious mutations can help to understand how abnormal protein function is connected to disease.

This review analyzed studies found on the PubMed, Uniprot, and PDB databases using the following keywords: Gemin5, SMN, RNA-binding, translation, RNA disease, and developmental disorders. Selected articles were published between 2002 and 2024 and represent the most relevant advances in the field. Information from relevant articles was used to obtain key information on the discovery of the function of GEMIN5 in humans and other relevant animal models.

Research performed in different laboratories has been instrumental in characterizing additional properties of the protein GEMIN5, despite the early discovery as a member of the SMN complex in metazoan (Gubitz et al., 2002). Briefly, the human GEMIN5 protein (1508 amino acids) comprises distinctive domains performing different functions, the WD40 repeats at the N-terminus, a tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domain, and a non-canonical RNA binding site (RBS1/2) at the C-terminus (Figure 1A). Initially, work done by the Dreyfuss’s team identified GEMIN5 as the protein responsible for snRNA recognition through its WD40 repeats domain and delivering of snRNAs to the Sm complex (Battle et al., 2006; Lau et al., 2009; Yong et al., 2010; Figure 1B). However, understanding the molecular mechanism of GEMIN5 action in snRNA recognition remained obscure until the three-dimensional structure of the WD40 repeats domain was solved in the apo-form, and in complex with the Sm site or m7G cap of pre-snRNAs (Jin et al., 2016; Tang et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2016). Typically, crystal structures provide snapshots of dynamic events such that the extent of the dynamic conformation of a given molecule may be broader than that seen in the crystal structure. Nonetheless, as in the case of other proteins, the comparison between the structure of the apo-form and the ligand-bound molecule provides a molecular view of the interaction between GEMIN5 and the snRNA ligand. Collectively, the three-dimensional structure of the WD40 repeats domain in complex with the Sm site or m7G cap of pre-snRNAs revealed that this domain consists of two seven-bladed β propellers, named WD1-WD2, which recognize the Sm site and the m7G cap of pre-snRNAs via distinct binding sites by respective base-specific interactions (Jin et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2016).

Subsequent studies solved the three-dimensional structure of the central region of human GEMIN5, unveiling the presence of a TPR domain (Moreno-Morcillo et al., 2020). Notwithstanding, the TPR-like motif of GEMIN5 folds into a canoe-shaped dimer, which does not conform to the typical length of 34 amino acids (D’Andrea and Regan, 2003). Instead, helical pairs of 32–33 and 27–29 amino acids alternate, showing a conserved TPR pattern of small and large hydrophobic residues. The experimental demonstration of this robust dimerization domain underscored novel clues into the biochemical properties of this protein, besides clearing the way for the functional analysis of the remaining regions of the protein (Martinez-Salas et al., 2020; Nelson and Pandey, 2024). Importantly, the dimerization module is functional in living cells, driving the interaction between the half C-terminal region (G5C) and the full-length protein (p170), which anchors members of the splicing and translation machineries. Disruption of the dimerization ability by a point mutation (A951E) at the dial axis of the TPR domain hinders the interaction with many of these factors (Moreno-Morcillo et al., 2020). Besides, conformational changes induced by point mutations within residues of the helices that determine the stability of the dimer interfere with the ability of the protein to interact with protein partners (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2022). Consequently, the dimerization domain of GEMIN5 serves as a hub for protein-protein interaction.

Beyond the WD40 repeats and the TPR domains, the human GEMIN5 protein also encloses a non-canonical RNA-binding site towards the C-terminus (Fernandez-Chamorro et al., 2014; Figure 1B). The presence of distinct canonical and non-canonical domains has been recognized as a frequent feature of human RBPs (Agarwal and Bahadur, 2023). Robust computational analyses have shown that the amino acid composition of the RBS1 moiety and Gemin5 RNA sequence coevolved across the evolutionary scale, such that the coevolving pairs are centered on the conserved PNSS motif of RBS1 and a stem-loop structure of Gemin5 mRNA. Further studies using nuclear magnetic resonance of the RBS1 domain unveiled the impact of R, S, W, and H amino acids flanking the PNSS motif for RNA recognition specificity. This motif (RPCPNSSWWRAGHR) promotes RNA-binding via π–π interactions, highlighting the relevance of aromatic and arginine residues for RNA interaction (Embarc-Buh et al., 2021). Thus, both the net charge and the π-amino acid density of the RBS1 region are key factors for RNA binding, in agreement with earlier data reported in the literature (Wilson et al., 2016). Indeed, protein-RNA complexes can be formed by π–π stacking interactions between nitrogenous base rings and π-containing amino acids (W, H, F, and Y), as well as the charged residues R, E, and D.

Obtaining structural insights into a critical region of understudied proteins often allows a rapid progress in the discovery of new properties of the entire protein. Accordingly, solving the crystal structure of the TPR moiety that encompasses the dimerization domain, facilitated disentangling the structure of G5C by cryo-EM (Guo et al., 2022). The G5C three-dimensional structure adopts a homodecamer architecture consisting of a dimer of pentamers. Assembly of the pentamer/decamer is critical for G5C to bind cognate RNA ligands, and also to regulate mRNA translation, by forming a cavity that includes positively charged residues of the TPR moiety in front of RBS1 residues promoting RNA binding. Substitutions of basic residues for alanine within the TPR domain near the RBS1 moiety do not alter the decamer architecture, but weaken the binding to SL1 RNA. Given that the TPR module alone does not bind RNA, two protomers should likely be coordinated for RNA binding (Moreno-Morcillo et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2022). It is worth noting that G5C binds preferentially RNA ligands depending on RNA secondary structure rather than sequence, in contrast to the N-terminal region (G5N) that exhibits snRNAs sequence specificity (Xu et al., 2016; Francisco-Velilla et al., 2018). The G5C three-dimensional structure together with previous works identifying GEMIN5 targets allows proposing a theoretical model to sort out the opposite roles of GEMIN5 in translation. In this model, the full-length protein may form a homodecamer via G5C connected with the N-terminal region via a disordered linker (residues 739–844).

The AlphaFold model predicts the structure of the GEMIN5 monomer composed of accurately predicted regions (the WD40 repeats and the TPR domains) and others of unpredicted structure, corresponding to the spacer between the WD40 and the TPR domain, as well as some stretches of the linker between the TPR and the RBS domains (Figure 2). The organization of GEMIN5 conforms with other RBPs, which are functionally modular and comprise specific regions that endow regulatory activity through interactions with cellular factors (He et al., 2023). Nonetheless, since the three-dimensional structure of the full-length GEMIN5 protein remains unknown, there are open questions that need to be addressed in future studies. For instance, it is not known if the full-length protein oligomerizes by dimerization or pentamerization, and whether there are intermediate states between the monomer, the dimer, and/or the pentamer in the cell environment. Also, it will be important to know if only the monomer or the oligomer protein present in the cytoplasm is involved in RNA-binding, snRNA recognition, and/or translation regulation, and whether GEMIN5 oligomerization can elicit assembly of granules, as observed for other protein-RNA complexes (Hallegger et al., 2021). Besides, since several residues of the protein are post-translationally modified by phosphorylation, it will be of great interest to discover the signaling pathways that activate these modifications and how these modifications contribute to protein activity (Husedzinovic et al., 2014). All these questions need to be answered in the near future to discern the multiple functions of this versatile protein.

The unique combination of all the structural domains juxtaposed in GEMIN5 is not found in currently known RBPs, strongly suggesting that this particular organization may be behind its functional versatility. However, the WD40 repeats and the TPR domain are contiguous in proteins such as the yeast vesicle coating proteins β’-coat complex protein (COP) and α-COP of the COPI complex and the protein transport protein Sec31 of the COPII complex (Lee and Goldberg, 2010). Furthermore, although the TPR motif is found in numerous RBPs, such as interferon-induced proteins with tetratricopeptide repeats 1 (IFIT1) (Ray et al., 2023), the presence of a dimerization motif near the region folding as a pentamer is rarely found in RBPs with known structure (Guo et al., 2022). None of these proteins share functional similarity with GEMIN5, suggesting that the acquisition of distinct structural domains may have opened the path for the development of novel functions.

Beyond snRNAs, compelling results obtained over the years confirm the ability of GEMIN5 to interact with multiple viral and cellular mRNAs under different situations (Figure 1B). Early on, the search for proteins interacting with RNA elements conferring cap-independent translation identified a p170 doublet, consisting of eIF3A and GEMIN5 (Pacheco et al., 2008). Then, a proteomic study of UV-crosslinked complex identified GEMIN5 bound to specific RNA structural elements of two viral internal ribosome entry site (IRES) elements, domain 5 of the picornavirus foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) and domain 3 of hepatitis C virus (Pacheco et al., 2009). Additionally, GEMIN5 is associated with viral factories in cells infected with Sindbis virus, reminiscent of the association of GEMIN5 with the viral IRES in FMDV-infected cells (Piñeiro et al., 2012; Garcia-Moreno et al., 2019). Apart from viral RNAs, a prominent role of GEMIN5 in RNA-binding is supported by its presence in the list of RBPs bound to cellular mRNAs using unbiased RNA interactome capture procedures in HeLa cells (Castello et al., 2012). More recently, it has been reported that GEMIN5 binds to flunarizine-modulated mRNAs (Salman et al., 2024). Treatment of cells with flunarizine (a calcium blocker used to treat neurological illnesses) results in a temporal expression of GEMIN5 and the transcriptional repressor lysine demethylase 6B (KDM6B) in the spinal cord of control and SMN-deficient mice, implying the existence of a concerted mechanism to regulate gene expression. Remarkably, the involvement of GEMIN5 in various RNA-driven cytoplasmic processes is fully compatible with its abundant localization outside of the SMN complex (Battle et al., 2007).

The mRNA targets of the full-length GEMIN5 identified in the RNA-binding protein resource ENCODE (https://www.encodeproject.org) by enhanced crosslinking and immunoprecipitation in two human cells lines, HEPG2 and K562, are specifically enriched at the beginning of 5′-untranslated region and sequences around the start codon (Van Nostrand et al., 2020b). This study also identified GEMIN5 heavily enriched in the spliceosome U1 RNA, and the 28S ribosomal RNA. Additionally, a previous study reported that GEMIN5 binds to sequences located in the 3′-untranslated region of SMN mRNA resembling the snRNP code of snRNAs (Workman et al., 2015). Collectively, these data illustrate various RNA sequences being recognized by this multifaceted protein.

Structural analysis of nuclear magnetic resonance aimed to understand the involvement of GEMIN5 in RNA-binding revealed that the RBS1 polypeptide adopts a flexible conformation (Embarc-Buh et al., 2021), indicating a dynamic conformation suitable to interact with different partners. Numerous works suggest that intrinsically disordered regions in RBPs may be involved in recruiting multiple targets, either RNA or proteins (Okuda et al., 2022; Basu et al., 2023; Babu and Freeman, 2024). In agreement with the flexibility of the RBS1 region, cross-linking and immunoprecipitation-based sequencing studies revealed the interaction with RNA targets enriched in G:C content, predicted to fold as stem-loops. Beyond other cellular RNAs, an abundant target of the RBS1 domain was Gemin5 mRNA, including two stem loops located within the coding region (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2018). The cohort of targets identified using the complete protein or the RBS1 moiety is in accordance with the observation that the ordered RBDs and the intrinsically disordered regions of a given RBP can bind their partners independently (Zeke et al., 2021), as it seems to be the case of GEMIN5.

Research done over time has identified distinct partners of GEMIN5 compatible with its implication in translation regulatory events (Fierro-Monti et al., 2006; Bradrick et al., 2009; Pacheco et al., 2009; Workman et al., 2015; Francisco-Velilla et al., 2016; Van Nostrand et al., 2020b). In eukaryotic cells, the process of translation is regulated by cis-acting RNA elements and trans-acting factors, including eukaryotic translation initiation factors (eIFs) (Hinnebusch et al., 2016). A cap-dependent mechanism determines the initiation of translation in most mRNAs. However, mRNAs that overcome cap-dependent inhibition, exemplified by IRES elements, use cap-independent mechanisms (Martinez-Salas et al., 2018). IRES elements are structured RNA entities that can function independently in heterologous contexts recruiting ribosomal subunits to an internal position in the mRNA (Lozano and Martinez-Salas, 2015; Barrera et al., 2020). Functional analysis conducted in Gemin5 shRNA-depleted cells uncovered the involvement of GEMIN5 as a down-regulator of both, cap-dependent and IRES-driven translation initiation (Pacheco et al., 2009). Nonetheless, the activity of GEMIN5 in translation regulation depends upon physiological conditions. GEMIN5 is proteolyzed at the (R)(R/K)(L/A)(R) sequence, placed immediately upstream of helix1 of the TPR moiety, in FMDV infected cells by the action of the viral protease Lpro yielding an 85 kDa C-terminal cleavage product (Piñeiro et al., 2012). Proteolytic cleavage also occurs in other host proteins involved in translation control and antiviral immunity including eIF4GI–II, DAXX1–2, ISG15, TBK1, LGP2, and MDA5, strongly altering the normal activity of the protein (Saiz and Martinez-Salas, 2021). In the case of GEMIN5, the C-terminal fragment stimulates IRES-dependent translation. Moreover, GEMIN5 binding induces a local conformation of the RNA structure of a hairpin flanked by A/U/C-rich sequences and competes with the binding of the polypyrimidin-binding protein (PTB) (Piñeiro et al., 2013). This result is reminiscent of the PTB/hnRNPA1 competition observed for the sm site of U7 snRNA, illustrating common features of distinct GEMIN5 targets (Yang and Dominski, 2023).

Therefore, GEMIN5 appears to perform two different roles in translation control. The full-length protein down-regulates global RNA translation, while the half C-terminal fragment enhances IRES-dependent translation and the RBS1 region within the C-terminus promotes translation of its own mRNA (Pacheco et al., 2009; Piñeiro et al., 2013; Francisco-Velilla et al., 2018). Interestingly, RBS1 stimulates the translation of reporters carrying the Gemin5 RNA targeting sequence, while destabilization of the stem-loop structure abrogates the stimulatory effect on translation.

The function of any given RBP is linked to its cognate RNA targets. RNA motifs provide a signature for the binding of specific proteins (Gerstberger et al., 2014; Wassmer et al., 2024), which in turn determines the fate of the mRNA target. In particular, selective translation allows orchestrating the synthesis of specific proteins in response to different signals through the concerted action of individual RBPs and cis-acting elements of mRNAs (Zhulyn et al., 2023; Tidu and Martin, 2024). The function of GEMIN5 in selective translation was recently shown through the identification of the mRNAs associated with polysomes in a GEMIN5-dependent manner (Embarc-Buh et al., 2022; Figure 1B). The mRNAs under-represented in polysomes in Gemin5 depleted cells uncovered factors involved in protein synthesis and nucleic acid metabolism, while overrepresented mRNAs were mostly linked to signaling processes. However, a comparison of cross-linking and immunoprecipitation targets with polysome-associated RNAs revealed that only a fraction of the GEMIN5 targets were enriched in polysomes. Among the polysome-enriched mRNAs, two groups bear distinctive cis-acting sequences, the 5′terminal oligopyrimidine tracts present in mRNAs encoding ribosomal proteins, and the 3′stem-loop structure of histone mRNAs (Meyuhas and Kahan, 2015; Marzluff and Koreski, 2017). Interestingly, these mRNAs encode essential factors for protein synthesis and nuclear metabolism, two fundamental cellular processes that are required to sustain cell growth.

The observation that only a fraction of the mRNA targets is differentially associated with polysomes in growing cells, supports the view that the GEMIN5-ribosome subpopulation favors the recognition of mRNAs harboring specific cis-acting elements (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2016; Embarc-Buh et al., 2022). Consistent with the role of GEMIN5 in translation regulation, the protein is detected in native ribosomal fractions. Additionally, the interactome of GEMIN5 identified by mass spectrometry reveals that several ribosomal proteins remained associated with GEMIN5 after RNase treatment, although other partners copurified with the N-terminal region via RNA bridges (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2016). The association of various RBPs with native ribosomes, and their potential involvement in the regulation of translation, have been reported in previous studies (Simsek et al., 2017; Li and Wang, 2020). These observations are in line with the notion that heterogeneous ribosomes can selectively regulate protein synthesis, and also with the spatial localization and translation of mRNAs in subcellular compartments enriched in ribosomes associated with RACK1 (Shi et al., 2017; Chouaib et al., 2020; Bourke et al., 2023).

Likely due to its dual function as RNA-binding and protein-protein interactor (Figure 3), the GEMIN5 interactome encompasses RBPs that form part of RNP complexes participating in multiple aspects of cell physiology (Wollen et al., 2021; Delle Vedove et al., 2022). Both the WD40 repeats and the TPR domains are well-known platforms for protein-protein interactions involved in a plethora of cellular processes in all organisms (D´Andrea and Regan, 2003; Xu and Min, 2011). Beyond SMN members, previous work showed the interaction of GEMIN5 with components of the translation machinery (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2016). Additional protein targets have been identified using individual domains, which belong to RNA-dependent machineries. Remarkably, the interaction with specific networks depends upon the capacity of the individual region to form oligomers with the endogenous protein (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2024). While the oligomerization-proficient regions of the GEMIN5 protein interact with RNA processing and translation machineries, the oligomerization-deficient regions of the protein are enriched in protein folding, stress response, immune response, and retina homeostasis.

The composition of the interactome analyzed under specific conditions reflects the dynamic organization of cellular macromolecular complexes in response to internal and external signals. During a stress response, GEMIN5 is recruited into cytoplasmic particles along with stress granules (SG) proteins, mRNAs, and 40S subunits, likely contributing to the storage of mRNAs (Vu et al., 2021; Berchtold et al., 2018). A paradigmatic example of cellular stress is the formation of SGs, a membrane-less accumulation of RNPs assembled in response to translation initiation inhibition during stress (Nicchita, 2024). Given that RBPs play a central role in determining the cellular proteome, their abundance, availability, and functions are regulated in response to stress, such that their malfunction is known to produce various developmental disorders (Park et al., 2021). The core components of SGs, such as the Ras GTPase-activating protein-binding protein (G3BP), the cytotoxic granule-associated RNA binding protein (TIA1), the cell cycle associated protein 1 (CAPRIN1), and the ubiquitin associated protein 2-like (UBAP2L), provide scaffolds through their low-complexity domains to facilitate the recruitment of additional SG components. This is also observed in neurodegenerative diseases (Jia et al., 2022; Mori et al., 2024), which gather pathological proteins into insoluble aggregates alongside SG proteins and become resistant against disassembly. In this regard, a recent study of the interactors of CAPRIN1 in arsenite-induced SGs identified GEMIN5, Small Nuclear Ribonucleoprotein U5 Subunit 200 (SNRNP200), ankyrin repeat and KH domain containing 1 (ANKHD1), and TALIN-1 proteins, among other proteins (Vu et al., 2021). CAPRIN1 is a ubiquitously expressed protein that regulates the transport and translation of mRNAs of genes involved in synaptic plasticity. Remarkably, a CAPRIN1 missense variant identified in children who developed early-onset ataxia, cognitive decline, and muscle weakness, forms an aggregate that also includes Ataxin2 (ATXN2), GEMIN5, SNRNP200, and alpha-synuclein, presumably reducing the availability of these proteins for their normal activities (Delle Vedove et al., 2022).

Data derived from functional analysis of GEMIN5 in conjunction with whole exome sequencing of patients suffering from neurodevelopmental disorders support the idea that mutations in the Gemin5 gene could be detrimental. Notwithstanding, the limited knowledge of this protein combined with the clinical and genetic heterogeneity observed in patients render many individuals undiagnosed, as it also occurs with variants in other understudied genes. In humans, GEMIN5 is encoded by a single gene located in the chromosome 5 (154,887,411–154,938,211). The primary transcript, about 50 kb long, comprises 28 exons encoding a protein of 1508 amino acids which is expressed in all human tissues (http://www.humanproteomemap.org) (Kim et al., 2014; Uhlen et al., 2015). The high expression level of GEMIN5 is observed in the cerebral cortex and cerebellum, but also in many highly proliferating tissues such as the colon, kidney, testis, placenta, breast, lymph node, and bone marrow (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000082516-GEMIN5/tissue).

Patients carrying Gemin5 biallelic variants display various degrees of neurodevelopmental delay, hypotonia, and cerebellar ataxia (Kour et al., 2021; Rajan et al., 2022; Saida et al., 2022). Remarkably, most of these Gemin5 variants are predicted to generate defective dimerization proteins through amino acid changes on the TPR domain (Table 1). In contrast, single-point substitutions present in the normal human population are spread all along the gene. Notably, 57% of the clinical variants reported to date are located in the dimerization domain. Amino acid replacements induce conformational changes in the protein structure in those mutants which have been experimentally analyzed, such as R1016C and D1019E (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2022). In the particular case of the recurrent variant R1016C, the recessive mutation is always accompanied by either a truncated version, a frameshift or an intron variant in the other allele, or a pathogenic mutant on the WD40 repeats, the TPR or the RBS domain (Table 1), in part explaining the observation of single R1016C heterozygotic mutants in the normal population. The three-dimensional structure of the TPR domain shows a salt bridge between R1016 and E1018, and a H-bond with D1019 (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2022). Loss of the salt bridge results in increased hydrophobicity of the mutant R1016C, thereby a distortion of the solvent-exposed loop. This observation highlights the relevance of maintaining the TPR structure for protein function.

Attempts to generate KO mouse models revealed that GEMIN5 is essential for survival, in full agreement with the involvement of this protein in fundamental cellular processes (Rajan et al., 2022). Molecular analysis of individual clinical variants defective in dimerization exhibits decreased levels of GEMIN5-ribosome association, compatible with defects in translation regulation (Francisco-Velilla et al., 2022). Besides, pathological mutations in GEMIN5 perturb the expression, subcellular distribution, stability, and its interacting partners, disrupting snRNP complex assembly formation, thereby suggesting a loss-of-function mechanism (Kour et al., 2021). Earlier work performing genetic screening in Drosophila demonstrated that the orthologue of GEMIN5, termed RIG, encodes a nuclear receptor interacting protein involved in ecdysone signaling during larval development (Gates et al., 2004). Subsequently, it was found that neuronal levels of GEMIN5 are required for viability (Borg and Cauchi, 2013). Therefore, the detrimental activity of Gemin5 variants in model systems, such as the Drosophila rigor mortis or the zebrafish hematopoietic stem cells, strongly supports the essential role of this protein (Liu et al., 2021; Fortuna et al., 2023).

The current reports of neurological disorders with cerebellar atrophy and motor dysfunction (OMIM # 619333) prompted the interest of biomedical researchers in neurology and genetics in Gemin5 variants (Nelson and Pandey, 2024). As a result, the number of clinical variants associated with GEMIN5 deficiencies increased in the last years (Ibrahim et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Cascajo-Almenara et al., 2024; Table 1), and it is expected that the list of variants will grow as the knowledge of the different functions of the protein and the pathways involved augments. For instance, functional studies conducted in fibroblasts generated from patients carrying compound heterozygosity at the Gemin5 gene revealed a decrease in CoQ10 biosynthesis compared to controls. GEMIN5-deficient patients also exhibit repression of genes involved in CoQ10 biosynthesis, as observed in rigor mortis flies. Furthermore, the recent finding of Gemin5 biallelic variants predominantly affecting the TPR and the RBS1 domains in patients with infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathies expands the phenotype of disorders associated with mutations in this gene (Zhang et al., 2024). Therefore, a deeper knowledge of the protein activities is also expected to increase the phenotypic spectrum of Gemin5 variants.

Here, we have presented a comprehensive overview of the hallmarks of GEMIN5 protein. The implication in snRNAs binding, and as a result in snRNPs assembly, was the initial property identified for this protein, while its unforeseen involvement in translation control was found by serendipity during an unbiassed search for RBPs interacting with IRES elements. The definition of the WD40 repeats domain as the interacting region with snRNAs provided the basis for a molecular function of GEMIN5, and as a consequence, it was considered the main role of this protein. The discovery of novel properties of this protein from structural and functional studies was a key issue in identifying Gemin5 variants in patients developing neurodevelopmental disorders. Importantly, GEMIN5 clinical variants are defective in oligomerization and translation regulation, and also disturb a different set of transcripts compared to SMN variants found in spinal muscular atrophy patients, suggesting the involvement of distinct molecular mechanisms related to defects or altered levels in each of these individual proteins, SMN or GEMIN5.

The singular organization of the structural domains juxtaposed on GEMIN5 allows this protein to interact with multiple targets, and therefore to perform various functions, as illustrated by spliceosome assembly and translation regulation (Martinez-Salas et al., 2020; Nelson and Pandey, 2024). However, while regulation of translation by GEMIN5 has emerged as a biologically relevant function of this versatile protein, the detailed molecular mechanisms that enable selective control of translation constitute a challenge to be elucidated in future studies. Due to its multiple activities, inhibition of GEMIN5 activity either by silencing or expression of defective variants, and also by substitutions in residues subjected to post-translation modifications, is expected to have pleiotropic detrimental effects, making it difficult to identify specific pathways involved in selective translation. Advances in RNA-protein interactions and single molecule methodologies for detecting mRNAs and their translation may help to characterize in further detail the individual mRNAs subjected to selective translation under the control of GEMIN5. Moreover, we anticipate that progress in the structure determination of large macromolecules may elucidate the conformations adopted by GEMIN5 in the cellular scenario, allowing discriminating its diverse functions in living cells.

Integrating functional and structural knowledge with clinical and functional genomics can accelerate the diagnosis of Gemin5-deficient patients, although uncovering the structure, mechanisms, and functions of this protein within the context of human disease is challenging. Thus, structural and functional studies of Gemin5 variants identified in patients should open new avenues to explore the molecular pathways involved and eventually help in the development of potential therapeutic targets.
