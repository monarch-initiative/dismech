Introduction
Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a rare but aggressive soft 
tissue sarcoma that arises from skeletal muscle progenitor 
cells. While it mainly occurs in children and adolescents, it 
can also develop in adults, though such cases are uncommon. 
RMS accounts for 50% of all childhood soft tissue sarcomas, 
which, in turn, represent 7% of all pediatric malignancies1. 
In one of the most recent epidemiological studies published 
in the USA, covering the period from 2003 to 2017, the 
age-adjusted RMS incidence rate was 4.58 cases per million 
children, with an average annual percent change of 0.3%. 
The five-year relative survival rate was 68%2.
Abstract
Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a highly aggressive type of soft tissue sarcoma, primarily affecting children and 
adolescents, with rare cases in adults. It accounts for 50% of pediatric soft tissue sarcomas, which constitute 7% 
of all malignant tumors in children. Prognosis depends on the TNM stage, clinical stage and risk stratification as 
well as from the presence of the PAX/FOXO1 fusion gene, with a 5-year survival rate of 70% for children and much 
lower for adults. The standard care for patients with RMS requires a multimodal approach, including local control 
of the tumor through surgical resection and/or radiotherapy, alongside multiagent chemotherapy. In recent years, 
there is an ongoing effort to develop innovative and effective targeted therapeutic methods tailored for patients 
that enhance survival and reduce long-term adverse effects. Advancements include 1) Updated recommendations 
for tumor resection surgery 2) Systemic chemotherapy administered pre- and post-operatively 3) Precision 
radiotherapy, particularly brachytherapy 4) Molecular targeted therapies, and 5) Immunotherapy combined with 
nanomedicine as frontline treatment options rather than last-resort measures. The limited number of focused 
clinical studies on RMS has impeded the thorough assessment of the efficacy and safety of these interventions; 
however, an anticipated increase in high-quality clinical research is expected to facilitate progress in this field.

In the past, RMS was classified into two main subtypes: 
Alveolar RMS (ARMS) and Embryonal RMS (ERMS). However, 
the 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) updated its 
classification to include four main subtypes: Embryonal RMS, 
Alveolar RMS, Pleomorphic RMS (PRMS), and Spindle Cell/
Sclerosing RMS (ssRMS)3. More recently, additional subtypes 
have identified, such as: 1) a subtype characterized by 
EWSR1/FUS–TFCP2 fusions and 2) a subtype characterized 
by the MEIS1–NCOA2 fusion 4. Among all the subtypes, 
ERMS is the most common, accounting for 57% of cases, 
especially in patients aged 0 to 4. The ARMS subtype 
accounts for 23%, ssRMS for 3-10%, and the pleomorphic 
RMS is the rarest subtype3.
Due to its mesenchymal origin, RMS can develop in nearly 
any part of the human body; however, its highest incidence is 
recorded in the head and neck regions (approximately 40%, 
with 24% specifically in the parameningeal area) followed by 
the genitourinary tract (31%) and the extremities (20%)5. 
Tumors in the hands and feet are more likely to develop in 
older patients and are more often of the alveolar subtype. 
Much lower rates of RMS are recorded in the chest and 
abdomen including the biliary tract and retroperitoneum6.
The treatment of RMS is characterized by its complexity 
and multidisciplinary nature, necessitates a multimodal 
approach. There are several modalities in the clinical arsenal 
that can combine surgery, chemotherapy (pre-operative 
and/or post-operative), radiotherapy, molecular-targeted 
therapies, immunotherapy, precision medicine, and novel 
therapeutic drugs, such as the ferroptotic agent erastin 7. 
The ultimate goal is the complete tumor resection, as it 
is critical for improving patients’ survival. Chemotherapy 
is an important part of treatment, administered both 
preoperatively and postoperatively to target cancer cells that 
may have disseminated and to reduce the risk of metastasis 
or recurrence. Radiation therapy is also used, especially in 
localized disease or as palliative in more advanced cases. 
The exact treatment plan depends on tumor’s location, 
histological subtype, and stage at diagnosis, which is why 
care is tailored to each individual.
Prognosis
The prognosis for patients with RMS can vary and 
depends on several factors. Risk stratification plays a 
main role in guiding treatment and estimating survival 
rates. Patients are categorized into low-, intermediate-, or 
high-risk groups based on a) the TNM (Tumor, Nodes and 
Metastasis) staging system before treatment, b) the clinical 
group classification and c) the presence or absence of the 
PAX/FOXO1 fusion8. The TNM Staging system, which is used 
to evaluate the extent and severity of the cancer is based on 
three key factors: a) T (Tumor): The size and location of the 
main tumor, b) N (Nodes): Whether the cancer has spread 
to nearby lymph nodes and c) M (Metastasis): Whether the 
cancer has spread to other parts of the body. Further on, the 
Clinical Group Classification (CCC) is based on the extent of 
the cancer that could be resected in the initial surgery and 
how far it had spread at the time. The CCC groups include 
a) Group I: This group includes patients with no lymph node 
involvement and complete tumor excision with no evidence 
of remaining cancer cells, b) Group II: Patients in this group

Finally, some key prognostic factors include:
•  Age: Children aged 1–9 tend to have the most favorable 
outcomes, while infants and adolescents experience worse 
survival rates, likely due to lower doses of chemotherapy 
and radiation and a higher incidence of complications.
•  Tumor Site: Tumors located in favorable areas, such as 
the orbit, non-parameningeal head and neck, and certain 
genitourinary regions, generally have better prognoses 
compared to tumors in less favorable locations.
•  Tumor Size: Larger tumors, especially those over 5 cm 
in diameter, are associated with poorer outcomes. Tumor 
shrinkage following induction chemotherapy is linked to 
improved survival.
•  Metastatic Disease: The presence of metastases at 
diagnosis indicates a worse prognosis, particularly when 
multiple sites or bones and bone marrow are involved.
•  Histology: ERMS has a better prognosis than ARMS, with 
a 5-year survival rate of 82% for embryonal and 65% 
for alveolar. Nodal involvement affects outcomes more 
negatively in alveolar cases.
•  Resectability: Patients with complete tumor resection 
have higher survival rates, with 5-year survival reaching 
90% in fully resected cases.
•  Biological Factors: Molecular and genetic factors, such as 
the presence of specific chromosomal aberrations, have 
emerged as significant predictors of treatment response 
and overall survival. For instance, translocations involving 
PAX3 or PAX7 with FOXO1, are commonly associated 
with ARMS and correlate with poorer prognosis due 
to a increased risk of treatment failure and mortality. 
These genetic alterations can lead to dysregulation 
of key signaling pathways involved in cell growth and 
differentiation, ultimately influencing tumor behavior and 
response to therapy.
Surgical treatment 
The treatment of RMS is multimodal, involving 
preoperative (neoadjuvant) chemotherapy long with efforts 
for radical tumor resection, and may incorporate adjuvant 
radiotherapy or chemotherapy following surgical intervention. 
The surgical approach depends on several factors, the most 
important of which include the tumor’s location, size, type, 
and biological characteristics as well as the patient’s age, 
the tumor’s response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, its 
relation to critical anatomical structures, and, of course, the 
surgeon’s expertise. Recent recommendations regarding the 
surgical management of patient with RMS include10:
1.  Surgical resection should only be considered prior to 
administering preoperative chemotherapy (upfront 
surgical resection) if a radical resection (R0) is anticipated 
and it will not result in significant disability for the young 
patient, regardless of the tumor’s location.
2.  When preoperative (neoadjuvant) chemotherapy is the 
initial treatment option, the objective of the subsequent 
primary excision is to achieve an R0 resection of the 
tumor. However, an R1 resection is also acceptable, 
followed by low-dose targeted radiotherapy.
3.  R2 excision (debulking procedure) is no longer 
recommended, as the final outcomes for these patients 
appear to be similar to those who did not undergo 
surgery11.
4.  The site of biopsy, whether it is an incisional or core needle 
biopsy, should be excised en bloc.
5.  After tumor resection, surgical margins should be marked 
with clips to guide possible adjuvant radiotherapy.
6.  Approximately 33% of tumors that initially receive 
radiotherapy end up with a residual mass at the end 
of treatment. This does not require further surgical 
intervention, and the final outcome is comparable to those 
of surgical patients, with significantly lower morbidity12.
7.  Efforts should be made to preserve fertility in all young 
patients, both pre- and post-pubertal, regardless of sex.
8.  A sentinel lymph node biopsy should be performed for all 
tumors affecting the trunk and extremities.
9.  In boys aged ≥10 years with paratesticular RMS, biopsy of 
the ipsilateral lymph nodes is necessary.
In 2023, an international panel of experts established 
specific guidelines for the surgical management of RMS in the 
extremities. These guidelines, in addition to those previously 
mentioned, include the following recommendations13:
1.  In extremely rare cases, an aggressive surgical approach 
should be considered to achieve radical tumor resection 
(R0 or R1 resection), despite the likelihood of significant 
functional impairment, including potential amputation.
2.  Postoperative reconstruction of the affected limb is a 
highly complex process that requires a careful planning 
and multidisciplinary collaboration of various medical 
specialists, including orthopedic, plastic, and vascular 
surgeons.
3.  Local relapse of the tumor generally has a very poor 
prognosis. It should be treated at a specialized tertiary 
center where radical resection of both the tumor and the 
regional lymph nodes can be performed, regardless of the 
patient’s age.
Two years earlier, the same international expert panel 
released guidelines regarding the surgical management of 
paratesticular RMS. These guidelines included the following 
recommendations14:
1.  The preferred surgical approach for resecting RMS located 
in the spermatic cord/ paratesticular region is the inguinal. 
A radical orchidectomy is the optimal surgical procedure, 
ensuring that the tunica vaginalis remains intact. The 
transcrotal approach, however, should be avoided.

Reconstructive surgical proceduresReconstructive surgical procedures
The strategy for potential reconstructive surgery should 
be developed early, in conjunction with the initial surgical 
treatment. This procedure can either occur simultaneously 
with the initial operation or at a later stage and may be 
performed before or after radiotherapy, depending on the 
treatment plan. If radiotherapy is anticipated, the methods 
for reconstructive surgery should be modified accordingly16. 
In most cases, after surgery for genitourinary RMS, 
reconstructive intervention is conducted simultaneously—
specifically during pelvic exenteration—utilizing the 
technique of constructing an incontinent ileal conduit. After 
oncologists have completed the treatment and the patient 
has been disease-free for a minimum of three years, various 
continent reconstructive methods can then be planned and 
performed17.
Combination of surgery and brachytherapyCombination of surgery and brachytherapy
Combining surgery with brachytherapy (BT) has shown 
promising results in patients with RMS. This approach helps 
control the tumor more effectively while minimizing the 
damage to surrounding healthy tissues. Due to the fact that 
BT delivers radiation directly to the tumor site, it is especially 
useful in cases where complete surgical removal is not 
possible. Using MRI or CT scans, physicians can tailor the 
radiation dose to the specific characteristics of the treating 
area18. Recent studies show that this method can control 
the tumor locally and preserve organs, which is particularly 
important when RMS affects the vagina, bladder, or 
prostate19. A 2024 systematic review20, which included 13 
clinical studies on RMS, reported that 5-year progression-
free survival rates ranged from 57% to 87.5%, while overall 
survival rates ranged from 42% to 82%. About 25% of 
the 55 patients had a relapse, most of which occurred at 
the initial tumor site rather than at distal locations. These 
findings highlight that while combining surgery and BT offers 
strong local control, a well-planned treatment strategy is still 
needed to address the risk of recurrence.
Chemotherapy 
Various chemotherapy regimens have proven 
effective for local tumor control, although their efficacy 
in addressing distal metastases remains less clear21. The 
chemotherapy regimens used in Europe and North America 
differ significantly. In Europe, the IVA regimen (Ifosfamide, 
Vincristine, Actinomycin D) predominates, whereas the VAC 
regimen (Vincristine, Actinomycin D, Cyclophosphamide) is 
more commonly used in the United States. Notably, one of the 
first randomized controlled clinical trials published by Crist et 
al. (2001)22 found no statistically significant differences in 
outcomes for patients with non-metastatic disease treated 
with either the IVA or VAC regimen. The primary objective 
of systemic chemotherapy in all cases is to eradicate the 
disease as effectively as possible and to achieve complete 
control in conjunction with local surgery; radiation therapy 
may also be incorporated into this treatment strategy.
Although the optimal timing for initiating chemotherapy 
has yet to be established, preoperative (neoadjuvant) 
chemotherapy regimens are preferred in North America. 
High-dose chemotherapeutic regimens were traditionally 
used for metastatic disease; however, the final outcomes 
did not prove to be better compared to standard regimens, 
while the side effects were notably severe both in frequency 
and clinical intensity. Similarly, no statistically significant 
improvement in the outcomes of metastatic disease was 
observed when combining high-dose chemotherapy with the 
stem cell rescue technique7.

Radiotherapy 
The radiotherapy (RT) treatment modalities for RMS 
include: 1) Brachytherapy (BT) and 2) External beam 
radiation therapy (EBRT). BT is an advanced radio-
oncological treatment technique that aims deliver targeted 
radiation directly to the tumor while minimizing exposure 
to the surrounding healthy tissues. In BT, 125Ior 192Ir seeds 
are implanted directly into the tumor, delivering high 
doses of radiation with remarkable precision. This precise 
localization is achieved using CT or ultrasound scan. Since 
the surrounding healthy tissues receive minimal radiation 
exposure, recovery after treatment is generally swift. 
Davila Fajardo et al. (2024) 24 published a summary of 
international clinical practice guidelines for the application of 
brachytherapy in treating RMS, which include the following:
1.  Treatment should be applied in fully specialized 
centers where clinicians have received training in both 
brachytherapy and the management of RMS.
2.  The optimal size for the application of brachytherapy 
is tumors smaller than 5 cm in their largest diameter; 
however, there are no absolute contraindications for its 
use on larger tumors.
3.  The decision to initiate BT should be made after 
three cycles of chemotherapy (around the 9 th week of 
treatment), although in selected cases it can be started 
after six cycles (around the 16th week).
4.  192Ir is the most commonly used radioactive source, 
which can be applied either intracavitary or interstitially, 
depending on the tumor’s location.
5.  The organs at risk of radiation damage are those located 
within a radius of 5 -10 cm from the irradiated area.
6.  In selected patients with relapsed RMS, BT may be applied 
either after a previous EBRT or even after an initial BT 
treatment.
Finally, the authors concluded that BT, whether used alone 
or in conjunction with surgery, can be an effective treatment 
option for RMS when performed by qualified physicians in 
selected cases.
In contrast, RT involves delivering ionizing radiation 
through a device located away from the patient’s body. 
Like brachytherapy, it is used in combination with surgery 
either post-operatively (adjuvant therapy) or pre-
operatively (neoadjuvant therapy). An absolute indication 
for radiotherapy is for all clinical group III patients after the 
administration of preoperative chemotherapy, including 
those who achieve radiological remission of the disease25.
Among the innovative radiotherapy methods developed 
in recent years is proton beam therapy (PBT), which has the 
potential to deliver a much more effective and localized dose 
of radiation compared to traditional photon therapy. Dong 
et al. (2023)26 conducted a meta-analysis evaluating the 
efficacy of PBT for treating RMS, incorporating 11 studies 
with a total of 544 patients. The results showed that 1) the 
5-year local control rate was 84%, 2) the 5-year event-
free rate was 76% and, 3) the 5-year overall survival rate 
was 82%. Both the acute and late side effects of PBT were 
mild, rated as grade 1 or 2. This suggests that PBT can be 
an effective treatment with minimal side effects for patients 
with RMS.
Molecular targeted therapies
Targeted therapies have introduced new possibilities for 
treating RMS, particularly by addressing specific molecular 
pathways involved in the disease. However, their progress 
in treating RMS has been slower than in other cancers. 
Molecular targeted therapies achieve anticancer effects 
through various mechanisms, including the inhibition of 
cell proliferation, metastasis, and angiogenesis. In RMS, 
important targets include insulin-like growth factor 1 
receptor (IGF-1R), platelet-derived growth factor receptor 
(PDGFR), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), 
all of which are critical for tumor growth. Other targets 
are anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), mesenchymal-
epithelial transition factor (MET), and mammalian target of 
rapamycin (mTOR), which a role in cell signaling and growth 
regulation27.
Among the targeted therapies studied, Insulin-like growth 
factor 1 receptor (IGF1R)-directed therapy has shown some 
clinical promise, yet its efficacy remains limited, benefiting 
only a small group of patients, often for a short duration due 
to development of treatment resistance 28,29. Overcoming 
these challenges requires a combination of targeted 
therapies and conventional chemotherapy, aiming to disrupt 
multiple cancer mechanisms simultaneously.

with conventional chemotherapy offers potential for improved

Unlike anticancer drugs, which indiscriminately targets 
both cancerous and healthy cells, molecular targeted 
agents selectively are designed to specifically attack cells 
containing specific molecules involved in cancer growth and 
proliferation. Several agents are currently in clinical trials for 
RMS, including7:
•  Pazopanib: is an oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets 
c-kit and VEGFR. Pazopanib is an approved drug for the 
treatment of soft tissue sarcomas after chemotherapy 
failure. While its efficacy in RMS remains inconclusive, 
clinical trials report a median overall survival of 12.5 
months for patients treated with pazopanib, compared to 
10.7 months in control group30. 
•  Sorafenib: is an oral multikinase inhibitor that targets 
several signaling pathways, including c-KIT, B-RAF, FLT3, 
PDGFR-β and VEGF-2,3. In a study including only 10 RMS 
patients, the administration of Sorafenib, at an oral dose of 
200 mg/m2, showed no objective response31. However, a 
higher dose of 400 mg twice daily for 28 days in patients 
with soft tissue sarcomas, lead to a partial response in 
14%, stable disease in 33%, progressive disease in 
49%, with 8% unevaluable cases. The progression-free 
survival was four months, while the overall survival was 
around 12 months, indicating modest efficacy only in 
disease stabilization and not at achieving significant tumor 
regression32.
•  Crizotinib: is a tyrosine kinase receptor inhibitor that 
targets RON, ROS, MET and ALK receptors. In a study with 
advanced or metastatic ARMS patients, Crizotinib showed 
a limited efficacy with both objective response and disease 
control rate at 14%31.
•  Temsirolimus: is an mTOR inhibitor. In a study with RMS 
patients who were treated with Temsirolimus, the disease 
control rate was at a low 6%, indicating limited efficacy in 
managing the disease34.
•  Cixutumumab: is a monoclonal antibody that targets 
IGF-1R.
   In a study examining Cixutumumab’s effects on patients 
with relapsed or refractory solid tumors, including RMS, 
the partial response rate was 5% for RMS. This limited 
response suggests that Cixutumumab may offer some 
benefits for RMS patients however its overall effectiveness 
appears low35.
•  Entinostat: is an oral histone deacetylase inhibitor 
that targets the PAX3-FOXO1 fusion protein in RMS 
cells30. Specifically, Entinostat sensitized RMS cells to 
chemotherapy by destabilizing PAX3-FOXO1 mRNA.
All these molecular targeted therapeutic agents, along 
with others currently under study, are expected to be 
promising additions to the clinical arsenal for RMS in the 
coming years31.
Immunotherapy and nanomedicineImmunotherapy and nanomedicine
In recent years, the search for new RMS treatments that 
are both effective and less toxic has brought growing hope, 
especially in the areas of immunotherapy and nanomedicine. 
Immunotherapy works by helping the body’s own immune 
system recognize and attack cancer cells. Several strategies 
are currently being studied. One of the most promising 
involves immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), a type of drug 
that enhance the immune response. These drugs block 
certain proteins that normally keep the immune system in 
check, allowing T-cells to find and destroy cancer cells more 
effectively.
Key checkpoints targeted by ICIs include:
1.  Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD-1) : PD-1 is a 
receptor found on T-cells that, when it binds to PD-L1 or 
PD-L2, prevents T-cell activation. ICIs like nivolumab and 
pembrolizumab block PD-1, reactivating T-cells so they 
can recognize and destroy cancer cells.
2.  Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1): Some ICIs target 
PD-L1 directly, preventing it from binding to PD-1, which 
also enhances T-cell responses.
3.  Cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte-Associated Protein 4 
(CTLA-4): CTLA-4 is another inhibitory receptor that 
suppresses immune responses. Ipilimumab is an example 
of an ICI that targets CTLA-4, promoting T-cell activation.
Immune checkpoint inhibitors, particularly those targeting 
the B7-H3 protein encoded by the CD276 gene, have shown 
promising results— though still in the experimental stage—
for treating pediatric solid tumors, including RMS32. Similarly, 
the humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody Enobilituzumab, 
which also targets the B7-H3 protein, has demonstrated 
encouraging experimental results33. While ICIs are used for 
the treatment of various malignancies, their clinical efficacy 
in sarcomas remains a matter of ongoing research. The ICIs 
have shown positive results in treating solid tumors, the 
potential benefits of ICIs in RMS are still being explored.

approaches that are still in the experimental - trial stage, 
including the following33:
•  Natural killer (NK) cells therapies with six clinical trials 
currently underway.
•  CAR-T cells therapies, which are still characterized by 
significant toxicity,
•  CAR-NK cells therapies, which have recently shown positive 
indications for the treatment of glioblastoma34 and,
•  CAR -CIK cells therapies, focusing on T- lymphocytes and 
cytokine-induced killer cells (CIK), which also offer the

advantage of modulating the patients’ innate immunity. 
Nanomedicine is an innovative and promising field 
in cancer treatment; utilizing the unique properties of 
nanoparticles (ranging from 1 to 100 nm) to enhance drug 
delivery and efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects. 
This innovative approach is still in the early stages but holds 
the potential to improve the therapeutic index of existing 
treatments by providing targeted and localized delivery 
of chemotherapeutic agents. Moreover, positive findings 
observed in experimental mouse models must be replicated 
in human clinical studies35. Finally, combining nanomedicine 
with immunotherapy may offer significant therapeutic 
advances, as it has the potential to serve as a first-line 
therapy to eradicate most of RMS cells, with adjuvant 
therapies aimed at complete eliminating the tumor33.
Conclusions
Over the past few years, there has been an increasing 
focus on the clinical application of innovative therapeutic 
techniques for treating RMS, particularly for patients who do 
not respond to initial treatments, experience rapid disease 
relapse, or face high toxicity that forces them to discontinue 
therapy. These innovative strategies include: updated 
and more precise guidelines for the surgical resection of 
tumors, neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy, locally 
administered and highly precise radiotherapy, especially in 
the form of brachytherapy, molecular targeted therapies, 
and immunotherapy along with nanomedicine as a first-
line treatment option rather than a last resort. The limited 
number of clinical studies focusing exclusively on RMS 
prevents an accurate assessment of the efficacy and safety 
of these innovative therapies, highlighting the urgent need 
for an increase in high-quality clinical studies.
