# Dynamic S-acylation controls CMG2 maturation extracellular matrix regulation and anthrax toxin susceptibility in vivo.
**Authors:** Abrami L, Joliot O, Blaskovic S, Valentin G, Hevia CF, Mercier V, Kunz B, Mesquita FS, van der Goot FG
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-026-75028-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-75028-2)

## Content

CMG2/ANTXR2 functions as a Collagen VI receptor required for extracellular matrix homeostasis and as the primary portal for anthrax toxin entry. Mutations in CMG2 cause Hyaline Fibromatosis Syndrome (HFS), a rare and often fatal genetic disorder characterized by excessive extracellular matrix accumulation, yet the molecular mechanisms regulating CMG2 function remain poorly understood. We show that CMG2 is controlled by ordered cycles of S-acylation and deacylation that regulate its folding, trafficking, and signalling competence. S-acylation by ZDHHC7 on two juxtamembrane cysteines protects CMG2 from ER-associated degradation by stabilizing folding intermediates, leading to a ~ 5-fold increase in folded receptors competent for ER exit. In the Golgi, ZDHHC3-dependent acylation of a third cysteine promotes Arf6-mediated CMG2 transport to the plasma membrane, where it exerts its functions. Ligand binding triggers recruitment of the thioesterase APT2, which enables release of CMG2 from the actin cytoskeleton and endocytosis, linking extracellular recognition to intracellular signalling and uptake. Inhibition of APT2 reduces Collagen VI turnover and strongly attenuates anthrax toxin toxicity in a zebrafish model, showing lipidation cycles as regulators of receptor function and potential therapeutic targets.

Capillary Morphogenesis Gene 2 (CMG2/ANTXR2) is a ubiquitously expressed type I membrane protein that plays a central role in extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis1–4. Loss-of-function mutations in CMG2 cause Hyaline Fibromatosis Syndrome (HFS), a severe genetic disease characterized by excessive accumulation of collagen type VI (COL6) and other ECM components4–7. CMG2 also serves as the major mammalian receptor for the anthrax toxins8. Despite its central roles in both physiology and infection, how CMG2 biogenesis and signalling are regulated at the molecular level remains poorly understood.

We previously reported that CMG2, and its paralog TEM8 (ANTXR1), which is also an anthrax toxin receptor9 and associated with genetic disease10, GAPO syndrome, undergo S-acylation and that this is required for productive anthrax toxin internalization11. S-acylation refers to the covalent attachment of medium-chain fatty acids, typically palmitate, to cytosolic cysteine residues via thioester bonds12–14. This process is catalysed by the transmembrane ZDHHC family of acyltransferases and reversed by cytosolic acyl-protein thioesterases such as APT1/LYPLA1, APT2/LYPLA2, and ABHD17 family members13–15. This dynamic modification can regulate protein conformation, subcellular trafficking, protein-protein interactions, turnover, and function16. While S-acylation delays anthrax toxin endocytosis11, the molecular consequences of CMG2 acylation, its dynamics, and the enzymes involved have remained unknown.

CMG2 is synthesized with a signal sequence leading to its co-translational insertion into the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)17. There, it undergoes folding, with the assistance of glycan-binding chaperones17. However, the CMG2 ectodomain18 (Fig. 1a) folds inefficiently, which reduces tolerance to missense mutations19. A variety of HFS missense mutations impair CMG2 folding, resulting in its degradation via the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) and protein-loss dependent loss-of-function3,19,20. Once folded, CMG2 exits the ER and transits through the Golgi to reach the plasma membrane19. At the cell surface, ligand-free CMG2 associates with the cytoskeleton by interacting with a Talin-Vinculin-Actin (TVA) containing complex21. This TVA interaction is released when a ligand, such anthrax toxin or Collagen VI4 binds to the ectodomain of CMG2, so that its cytoplasmic domain can then engage proteins recruited for CMG2 signalling and endocytosis such as RhoA, β-arrestin, and Cbl11,21,22. This transition is essential for both toxin uptake and CMG2-mediated COL6 internalization21, yet whether and how S-acylation participates in this signalling switch has not been addressed.

Here we show that CMG2 function is controlled by temporally ordered S-acylation-deacylation cycles that coordinate receptor maturation, trafficking, and signalling. We show distinct acyltransferases and thioesterases acting at successive stages of the CMG2 life cycle, linking lipid modification to ER quality control, secretory transport, and ligand-induced receptor activation. Our findings reveal that reversible lipidation modulates CMG2 abundance, controls its ability to undergo ligand-induced conformational switching, and governs downstream endocytic signalling. Consistent with this regulatory role, genetic or pharmacological modulation of deacylation alters extracellular matrix turnover and influences susceptibility to anthrax toxin in vivo. These results show dynamic S-acylation as a central regulator of CMG2 biology and highlight deacylation enzymes as potential therapeutic targets in both HFS and anthrax intoxication.

CMG2 and its paralogue TEM8 have been reported to undergo S-acylation11 (Fig. 1a). We confirmed this finding in cultured cells using [³H]-palmitate metabolic labelling, and in mouse tissues using the Acyl-RAC assay, which captures S-acylated proteins in cell extracts (Fig. 1bc).

CMG2 isoform 4 contains three cytosolic cysteines (Cys344, Cys345, and Cys479; Fig. 1a). We generated a panel of single, double, and triple cysteine-to-alanine mutants, with the wild-type (WT) corresponding to CCC and the triple C344A-C345A-C479A mutant denoted as AAA. The ACC, AAC and AAA mutants were expressed at a slightly reduced level (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Loss of all three cysteines abolished [³H]-palmitate incorporation (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1b). Among the double mutants, the AAC variant (where Cys344 and Cys345 are mutated) showed the strongest reduction, only partially explained by its lower expression level, suggesting that S-acylation of Cys344 and 345 might facilitate modification of the most distal cysteine. Kinetic labelling experiments showed that efficient S-acylation of CMG2 requires the sequential modification of cysteines from the transmembrane domain to the C-terminal cytosolic tail (Fig. 1e).

We next assessed site occupancy using PEGylation, in which hydroxylamine cleaves the thioester bond, enabling labelling of the liberated cysteine with PEG-maleimide of 5 kDa. Because CMG2 is glycosylated on two asparagine residues, it migrates as a smear in SDS-PAGE17, complicating interpretation of PEGylation-induced mobility shifts. We therefore treated all samples with N-glycosidase F to remove the N-linked sugars prior to SDS-PAGE. The AAA mutant did not show any band shift upon PEGylation, indicating the specificity of the assay (Fig. 1f). For the AAC mutant, a slightly shifted band could be observed, in addition to the unshifted band, indicating that some CMG2 molecules had undergone acylation on Cys479 (Fig. 1f). For the CCA mutant, the initial (unshifted) band essentially disappeared, indicating that the entire CCA CMG2 population underwent acylation. Two shifted bands were observed, the upper, and major, band thus corresponding to dual acylated CCA (Fig. 1f). The ACC mutant showed a similar pattern, but at greatly reduced intensity, suggesting that acylation of the cysteine can occur, but only does so on a limited number of molecules withing the CMG2 ACC population. Finally, the WT protein also showed a pattern similar to CCA, but with an upper band migrating slightly higher that the CCA upper band, consistent with S-acylation of all three cysteines (Fig. 1f).

Altogether, this analysis indicates that CMG2 undergoes S-acylation sequentially, first on the two juxtamembranous cysteines, which facilitates the modification of the most distant cysteine. At steady state, the entire WT CMG2 population is S-acylated, mostly on all three cysteines.

Only pool M1 led to a reduction in CMG2 S-acylation (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 2a). Subsequent individual knockdowns within this pool revealed that ZDHHC7 and ZDHHC3 are responsible for mediating CMG2 S-acylation (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 2b,c). Recomplementation of ZDHHC3 or 7 silenced cells with the corresponding acyltransferase led to recovery of the S-acylation level (Supplementary Fig. 2d). We next investigated whether the two enzymes act on different cysteines, and possibly in different intracellular compartments. ZDHHC7 localizes to the early secretory pathway (ER and Golgi) and ZDHHC3 to the Golgi23. To investigate whether S-acylation occurs in the ER when CMG2 is newly synthesized, we inhibited protein synthesis with cycloheximide. We monitored ZDHHC7-mediated S-acylation by silencing ZDHHC3, and conversely ZDHHC3-mediated S-acylation by silencing ZDHHC7. ZDHHC7-mediated S-acylation was essentially abolished by cycloheximide (CHX) (Fig. 2c), indicating that ZDHHC7 modifies primarily newly synthesised proteins. In contrast, ZDHHC3-mediated S-acylation was unaffected by cycloheximide (Fig. 2c), consistent with ZDHHC3 acting in the Golgi on CMG2 molecules synthesized prior to drug addition. Next, we tested the effect of disrupting the Golgi with brefeldin A, and observed the opposite pattern: ZDHHC7-mediated S-acylation was unaffected by brefeldin A, whereas ZDHHC3-mediated S-acylation was essentially abolished (Fig. 2d). Finally, we tested whether the CCA (i.e. C479A only) mutation would phenocopy ZDHHC3 silencing. Cycloheximide treatment strongly reduced CCA S-acylation while brefeldin A treatment had no effect (Fig. 2e). Thus, CMG2 undergoes sequential S-acylation, first in the ER on Cys344 and Cys345 by ZDHHC7, and subsequently on Cys479 by ZDHHC3 in the Golgi (Fig. 2f).

Because CMG2 is S-acylated during or shortly after synthesis, we asked whether this modification influences its biogenesis and thus the amount of fully folded protein that exits the ER. We performed [³⁵S]-Cys/Met pulse-chase labelling with a short 20 min pulse to focus on newly synthesized molecules. CMG2 decayed with a t₁/₂ of ~6.5 h, both for endogenous (Ctrl) and ectopically expressed (WT) protein (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 3a,b). Inhibition of the proteasome with MG132 increased the signal at t = 0 and markedly delayed and reduced degradation, indicating that in untreated cells, a major fraction of newly synthesized CMG2 is destroyed during the first 5–6h (maturation phase, Fig. 3a) by ERAD, reminiscent of the behaviour of wild type cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator (CFTR)24 and its most common ΔF508 disease mutant25,26. Silencing ZDHHC7 and 3 completely recapitulated the effect of mutating the three cysteines (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). The difference in the [³⁵S]-Cys/Met signal at t = 0 for WT and AAA CMG2 indicates that some WT molecules undergo S-acylation during the 20 min pulse. However S-acylation of the pool of newly synthesized CMG2 molecules may take longer than 20 min. To test this, we compared the effects of 20 min and 2 h [³⁵S]-Cys/Met labeling durations. Extending the pulse period resulted in an apparent reduction and delay in the degradation of the pool of newly synthesized WT CMG2 (Fig. 3c). The effect of the pulse length was lost when silencing ZDHHC7 and ZDHHC3 (Fig. 3c), confirming that reduced degradation observed with longer pulse duration reflects enrichment of stabilised molecules rather than altered degradation kinetics. We have made similar observations for two other S-transmembrane proteins undergoing S-acylation in the ER, calnexin and LRP6 and for both decay kinetics were strongly influenced by the [³⁵S]-Cys/Met labeling time27,28. Supporting the importance of S-acylation during biogenesis, silencing ZDHHC7 and ZDHHC3 in primary human fibroblasts caused a marked reduction in total CMG2 protein levels (Fig. 3d).

Since S-acylation is reversible, we next examined whether during biogenesis in the ER, CMG2 also undergoes deacylation. To address this, we first assessed whether CMG2 can be deacylated at all. [³H]-palmitate pulse-chase analysis, using a 2 h pulse, showed a slow and progressive loss of radiolabel from CMG2 (Fig. 3e,f). Loss of [³H]-palmitate signal can occur by deacylation as well as by protein degradation. To determine the contribution of deacylation, we tested the effects of acyl-protein thioesterase inhibitors. The pan-thioesterase inhibitor Palmostatin B and the APT2-specific inhibitor ML349, but not the APT1 inhibitor ML348 or the ABHD17 inhibitor ABD957, prevented the loss of [³H]-palmitate during the chase period (Supplementary Fig. 3c, Fig. 3g). Kinetic measurements showed that APT2 silencing indeed substantially delayed and reduced the loss of [³H]-palmitate from CMG2 (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 3d).

To more specifically determine whether APT2, which is a cytosolically expressed enzyme, can act on CMG2 during biogenesis, we again performed [³⁵S]-Cys/Met pulse-chase experiments. Knockdown of APT2 protected newly synthesized CMG2 from ERAD, with stabilization evident already during the pulse (Fig. 3f). The effect was even more striking when cells were treated with Palmostatin B, for reasons that remain to be clarified (Supplementary Fig. 3e), which was here used for practical reasons rather than the APT2 inhibitor ML349. Importantly, Palmostatin B did not affect the decay kinetics of the AAA mutant, indicating that the observed effects are related to S-acylation of WT CMG2 (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

These results show that during the folding of a newly synthesised population of CMG2, APT2 can remove the protective S-acylations, thereby promoting the premature targeting of these molecules to ERAD. Quantitatively, comparison of CMG2 levels 5 h after the pulse reveal an approximately 4 to 6-fold difference between ZDHHC7/3 silencing, APT2 silencing and PalmB (p = 0.001, Fig. 3c,h, Extended Fig. 3f), highlighting the major contribution of S-acylation to CMG2 folding efficiency.

This mechanism has implications for Hyaline Fibromatosis Syndrome (HFS) missense mutations that affect folding3,19,20. We previously showed that several of these mutations, which map to the extracellular von Willebrand A domain, fold properly when produced in E. coli19, indicating that in human cells it is the slower folding kinetics—rather than an intrinsic inability to fold—that triggers ERAD recognition. Our current findings suggest that inhibiting deacylation, to protect folding intermediates from ERAD targeting, should lead to an increase in the amount of successfully folded receptors. We tested this hypothesis on the L45P HFS mutation19. Indeed, Palmostatin B prevented the premature degradation of CMG2 L45P and led to an increase of CMG2 levels, reaching similar levels as observed for WT CMG2 (Fig. 3i).

Because L45P is retained in the ER through recognition by the ER quality control machinery, these observations suggest that CMG2 deacylation can occur at the cytosolic surface of the ER. To test whether APT2 can access CMG2 in this compartment, we immunoprecipitated the ER-retained L45P mutant and assessed its interaction with APT2. We observed robust co-immunoprecipitation between L45P CMG2 and APT2 (Extended Fig. 3g). We extended this analysis to three additional HFS-associated mutations that cause ER retention of CMG2 (G105D, I189T, and L349R), all of which also interacted with APT2 (Extended Fig. 3g).

As a further proof of concept for the therapeutic potential of APT2 inhibition to increase the CMG2 protein levels, we treated mice by intraperitoneal injection with the APT2 inhibitor ML349 for several days, sacrificed the animals at defined time points, and collected multiple organs for analysis. ML349 treatment led to a time-dependent increase in CMG2 abundance across all tissues tested, as most apparent in the colon, (Fig. 3j, Supplementary Fig. 3h), without significant changes in mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 3i). Altogether, the present findings show that newly synthesized CMG2 is robustly protected from ERAD through the S-acylation of juxtamembrane cysteines and indicate that inhibition of APT2 could be a potential strategy for improving CMG2 expression in contexts where receptor folding is impaired.

Because ZDHHC3 S-acylates Cys479, we tested whether the CCA mutant would match the behaviour of WT CMG2 in ZDHHC3 silenced cells. This mutant exhibited reduced plasma membrane localization and was insensitive to ZDHHC3 knockdown (Fig. 4e), confirming that S-acylation of Cys479 promotes CMG2 surface delivery. Since Golgi-to-plasma membrane transport of S-acylated proteins has been proposed to depend on Arf629, we tested its potential role. Silencing Arf6 led to decreased CMG2 surface levels (Fig. 4f), and the interaction between CMG2 and Arf6 was significantly reduced when Cys479 was mutated, or ZDHHC3 was depleted (Fig. 4g,h).

Together, these findings show that ZDHHC3-mediated S-acylation of Cys479 allows CMG2 to engage Arf6 and traffic efficiently from the Golgi to the plasma membrane.

We next investigated whether the S-acylation status of CMG2 influences its functions, first focusing on ligand-induced signalling at the plasma membrane21. In the absence of ligand, surface CMG2 interacts with Talin and thereby Vinculin and Actin (TVA), anchoring it to the actin cytoskeleton21. Ligand binding however triggers a switch from TVA binding to recruitment of the actin regulators RhoA and mDia, as well as with proteins required for signalling and endocytosis such as the scaffold protein β-arrestin or the kinase src, which then phosphorylates CMG211,21,22,30,31, (Fig. 5a). This switch is readily visible when analysing CMG2 immunoprecipitates under conditions where cells are treated or not with the receptor binding component of the anthrax toxin, the protective antigen (PA), either in its 83 or 63 kDa form at 4 °C followed by a 30 min incubation at 37 °C: in control cells, vinculin and actin co-IP with CMG2, while in toxin treated cells, the receptor is associated with RhoA (Fig. 5b). The TVA release is not observed when cells are kept at 4 °C21. The AAA CMG2 mutant failed to release TVA components in response to PA binding, and consistently did not recruit RhoA (Fig. 5b,c). Similarly, silencing ZDHHC7 and ZDHHC3 prevented PA-induced TVA release, despite PA binding, and RhoA recruitment (Fig. 5c). We have previously shown that non-acylated anthrax toxin receptors lead to premature unproductive toxin uptake, i.e. where monomeric PA is internalized but the enzymatic subunits of the toxin remaining outside of the cell11. This greater endocytic ability of non-acylated CMG2 led us to ask whether ligand binding could induce deacylation of WT CMG2. To test this, cells were labelled with [³H]-palmitate for 2 h, chased to allow CMG2 to reach the plasma membrane, and then stimulated with toxin. PA induced a time-dependent loss of [³H]-palmitate (Fig. 5d). This decay could not be prevented by treating cells with the vacuolar ATPase inhibitor Bafilomycin A, excluding the possibility that the observed loss of [³H]-palmitate occurred by lysosomal degradation of CMG2 (Fig. 5d). Similarly, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 had no effect (Fig. 5d,e, Extended Fig. 4c). Thus, extracellular ligand binding triggers intracellular CMG2 deacylation, a regulatory step that we had previously hypothesized but could not directly demonstrate without the mechanistic insight into CMG2 S-acylation provided by the present study.

APT2 is a soluble globular protein that can interact with membranes following a three-step process involving electrostatic interactions, insertion of a hydrophobic loop (the ß-tongue) and S-acylation on Cys223. The S-acylation-deficient C2S mutant was unable to bind CMG2 (Fig. 6e,f), confirming that APT2 must itself be S-acylated to deacylate its substrates23. Similarly, two other APT2 mutants with impaired membrane binding capacity, the APT2PosPatch—in which a positively charged patch is mutated and which is unable to approach membranes through electrostatic interactions23, and APT2 M68E, which affects the hydrophobicity of the membrane-inserting ß-tongue23, also failed to be recruited to CMG2 upon ligand binding (Supplementary Fig. 5d). The catalytically inactive S121A APT2 mutant in contrast was able to bind CMG2, but failed to trigger TVA release (Fig. 6e,f), confirming the requirement for the thioesterase activity. Interestingly, inhibition of APT2 with ML349 prevented its recruitment to CMG2 (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Fig. 5e,f), while the ABHD17 inhibitor ABD957 had no effect (Fig. 6f). This was not due to an effect of ML349 on the ability of APT2 to bind membranes, since APT2 was still found in the membrane fraction upon fraction of a post nuclear supernatant generated from ML349 treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 5g), and was able to bind to liposomes in vitro in the presence of ML349 (Supplementary Fig. 5h). Since ML349 inserts into the hydrophobic acyl-chain binding pocket of APT234, these results indicate that for APT2-CMG2 interactions to be detected by immunoprecipitation, a CMG2-bound acyl chain must insert into the hydrophobic pocket of APT2. APT2 recruitment always coincided with loss of binding of the actin cytoskeleton to CMG2 (Fig. 6g).

Thus, recruitment of membrane-anchored APT2 constitutes the first cytosolic response to ligand binding, and deacylation acts as the execution step of the CMG2 signalling switch, converting a cytoskeleton-anchored receptor into an internalization-competent complex (Fig. 6h).

We therefore monitored the different steps of lethal toxin’s mode of action biochemically. Treatment with the APT2 inhibitor ML349, but not the APT1 inhibitor ML348, drastically inhibited PA pore formation (Fig. 7c,d). This effect was not due to impaired PA oligomerization, as acidification of cell lysates prior to SDS–PAGE22 revealed normal oligomer assembly (Fig. 7c, Supplementary Fig 6a). Consistent with impaired PA pore formation, MEK2 cleavage was not detected in ML349-treated cells (Fig. 7c). In further agreement, APT2 silencing (Supplementary Fig 6b) and APT2 knockout (Supplementary Fig 6c) strongly reduced PA pore formation and MAPK kinase cleavage, and both phenotypes were rescued by re-expression of APT2 (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Importantly, APT2 knockout did not cause a general defect in endocytosis or in the endo-lysosomal trafficking pathway, as assessed by the normal EGF internalization and degradation observed in APT2 KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 7abc). APT2-mediated deacylation was also required for the physiological function of CMG2, as ML349 treatment impaired collagen VI degradation (Fig. 7f).

Together, these findings demonstrate that the activity of APT2 is essential for CMG2 to transition into an internalization-competent state, and is therefore essential for anthrax toxin entry into cells and collagen VI homeostasis (Fig. 7g).

Injection of PBS had no effect on the morphology of the vasculature or blood flow (Fig. 8a-f”, movies S1 and S5). Injection of LeTx severely impaired red blood cell circulation within 24 h post-injection, without affecting gross morphology or cardiac contractility (Fig.7 b,b”, movies S2 and S6), as previously reported38 and consistent with the observation that LeTx does not induce cell death in embryonic tissues or blood vessels38. Whole-embryo imaging revealed a complete or near-complete arrest of red blood cell circulation through the axial vessels and intersegmental vessels (ISVs) (Fig. 8b”, movie S2 and S6). Confocal imaging of the trunk vasculature further revealed a marked reduction in ISV diameter in LeTx-injected embryos (Fig. 8 f,f” vs. e,e”).

To enable systematic and reproducible phenotypic scoring, LeTx-induced circulatory defects were classified into three severity degree based on the perfusion of the vasculature by red blood cell, as illustrated by representative confocal images (Fig. 8i-k). Severity grade 1 corresponds to embryos retaining axial blood flow in the dorsal aorta and caudal vein, with partial red blood cell perfusion of the ISVs. Grade 2 describes embryos with preserved axial circulation but a complete absence of ISV perfusion. Grade 3, the most severe phenotype, is characterized by the absence or markedly reduced red blood cell flow in the axial vessels.

We next co-injected LeTx together with Palmostatin B. We observed a concentration-dependent rescue of red blood cell circulation (Fig. 8l, movie S3 and S7), with a decrease in grade 3 to 77, 72 and 50% at 1, 10 and 100 µM respectively, with a concomitant increase in grade 1 and grade 2 phenotypes. At 100 µM, 33% of Palmostatin B-treated embryos achieved grade 1 rescue. Confocal imaging confirmed that partial restoration of red blood cell circulation in the axial vessels was accompanied by a recovery of ISV diameter and partial re-establishment of ISV perfusion (Fig. 8g–g”).

ML349 was significant more potent in rescuing the embryo phenotype (Fig. 8l, movie S4 and S8). At 1 µM and 5 µM, the proportion of severely affected embryos remained elevated at 74% and 70%, respectively, with limited grade 1 rescue (3% and 8%). At 50 µM, however, a striking phenotypic redistribution was observed: the proportion of grade 3 embryos collapsed to 10%, while 74% of embryos achieved grade 1 rescue and 10% grade 2, representing near-complete restoration of red blood cell circulation at this concentration (Fig. 8d”). Confocal imaging corroborated these findings, demonstrating substantial recovery of ISV diameter and red blood cell perfusion in ML349-treated embryos at 50 µM, approaching values observed in PBS-injected controls (Fig. 8h–h”).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that both Palmostatin B and ML349 rescue LeTx-induced impairment of red blood cell circulation in a concentration-dependent manner in vivo, with ML349 exhibiting markedly greater effect (Fig. 8l).

Notably, in this co-injection paradigm, inhibitors must enter cells and inhibit deacylation while the toxin is simultaneously available for internalization to exert its effects. The observed rescue is therefore particularly striking. These findings support the idea that inhibition of deacylation can counteract toxin activity in vivo and suggest that this approach may have therapeutic potential in the context of infection, where toxin exposure occurs progressively rather than as a single bolus.

CMG2 is a multifunctional receptor that links the extracellular matrix to cytoskeletal signalling and endocytosis, and is also hijacked as the primary host receptor by anthrax toxin. Here we show that CMG2 biogenesis and function are controlled by sequential S-acylation–deacylation cycles acting across the secretory and endocytic pathways. These cycles enable CMG2 to adopt distinct conformational or interaction states that correspond to discrete stages of its lifecycle: folding in the ER, transport to the plasma membrane, and ligand-induced signalling and internalization. The lipidation events we describe therefore couple CMG2 biogenesis with its function as a signalling and endocytic receptor at the plasma membrane.

Our findings demonstrate that S-acylation of the juxtamembrane cysteines by ZDHHC7 in the ER promotes productive folding by preventing premature degradation of newly synthesized CMG2 by ERAD. Conversely, APT2-mediated removal of these acyl chains renders folding intermediates vulnerable to ERAD. Thus, acute APT2 inhibition increases CMG2 levels in cells and in vivo. These observations establish S-acylation as a key positive determinant of CMG2 protein homeostasis. Since a subset of Hyaline Fibromatosis Syndrome-associated missense mutations result in impaired CMG2 folding kinetics, chronic partial APT2 inhibition represents a rational therapeutic strategy to increase CMG2 expression in these patients. This mechanism is conceptually related to pharmacological strategies aimed at stabilizing CFTR and other folding-compromised mutated transmembrane proteins in the ER.

After ER exit, a second S-acylation event, mediated by ZDHHC3 in the Golgi on Cys479, allows CMG2 to interact with Arf6 and traffic to the plasma membrane. This indicates that CMG2 biogenesis is regulated not only at the folding stage but also during transport through the secretory pathway, with distinct S-acylation events conferring sequential benefits.

At the cell surface, we find that CMG2 must be S-acylated for the ligand-induced release from Talin-Vinculin-Actin and for the recruitment of RhoA, and other proteins required for signalling and endocytosis such as β-arrestin, mDia and myosin light chain11,21,22. These data support a model in which S-acylation constrains the cytoplasmic tail in a conformation that is competent to propagate the ligand-induced conformational change across the transmembrane helix, allowing CMG2 to reposition its cytosolic interactions. In the absence of S-acylation, ligand binding still occurs but the conformational signal fails to cross the membrane, preventing receptor activation.

Finally, we show that ligand binding triggers the recruitment of APT2 to CMG2, involving the sequential insertion of the CMG2-bound acyl chains into the hydrophobic acyl-chain binding pocket of APT2. APT2 recruitment precedes TVA release, and mediates CMG2 deacylation, which converts the receptor from a cytoskeleton-anchored to actin regulatory and internalization-competent state. While regulated endocytosis of full-length CMG2 requires this S-acylation dependent release from cytoskeletal interactions, such regulatory constraints can be bypassed by truncation mutants that lack the cytosolic tail39,40.

Consistent with our observations, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of APT2 blocks anthrax lethal toxin internalization, pore formation in endosomes, and downstream MAPK cleavage in the cytosol. Similarly, in the physiological setting, APT2 inhibition prevents CMG2-mediated collagen VI degradation. Finally, APT2 inhibition has a protective effect in zebrafish towards Lethal Toxin induced vascular damage. These results demonstrate that deacylation is not simply the reversal of a modification that favours biogenesis, but a signalling execution step.

The opposing effects of APT2 on CMG2 biogenesis and ligand-induced internalization highlight the stage-specific, temporally separated, nature of S-acylation-deacylation cycles and therefore that APT2 inhibition may have different outcomes depending on timing and duration. Post-exposure prophylaxis toward Bacillus anthracis infection would require an acute and maximal inhibition of APT2 activity, allowing the immune system to clear the infection, without long-term consequences due to receptor inactivation. In contrast, HFS treatment would require a chronic low concentration or intermittent treatment, allowing an increase in CMG2 protein expression, while at least intermittently retaining CMG2’s signalling ability and ECM regulation. More generally, these findings underscore the need to consider temporal dynamics when targeting lipid-modifying enzymes therapeutically.

In summary, this work identifies coordinated and temporally ordered S-acylation-deacylation cycles as a central regulatory principle controlling CMG2 folding, trafficking, and function, both physiological and pathological. Hence, this study demonstrates that S-acylation-deacylation cycles can control sequential trafficking steps along the biosynthetic and endocytic pathway. Beyond clarifying the molecular logic of CMG2 S-acylation, our findings establish APT2 as a druggable regulator of receptor function, with implications for both HFS therapy and anthrax infection, as well as other genetic diseases affecting the folding of membrane proteins, 50 to 70% of which undergo S-acylation41–44.

Micro-injections were performed into the systemic circulation via the duct of Cuvier using a gas-driven microinjector (PV820, World Precision Instruments) coupled to a micromanipulator (IM-300, Narishige), with pulled borosilicate glass microcapillary pipettes (World Precision Instruments), and visualized under a SZ61 stereomicroscope (Olympus).

Anthrax lethal toxin (LeTx) was prepared by combining 9.25 μM lethal factor (LF) and 6.25 μM protective antigen (PA), supplemented with 0.1% phenol red and kept on ice until use. For deacylation inhibition treatments, LeTx was mixed with either Palmostatin B or ML349 at three different concentrations (1, 10 and 100 μM for Palmostatin B, and 1, 5, and 50 μM for ML349). Each embryo received two consecutive pressure pulses of approximately 2 nL each, for a total injected volume of 4 nL, resulting in a final quantity of 37 fmol of LF and 25 fmol of PA per embryo as described in Bolcome et al., 200838. Control embryos received an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Timing for downstream analyses was initiated immediately following the last pulse.

Following injection, embryos were transferred to fresh system water containing 0.002% PTU and incubated at 28.5 °C. Phenotypic scoring was performed 24 h post-injection. For imaging, embryos were embedded in 0.8% low-melting-point agarose in glass-bottom dishes (MatTek Corporation, USA) and anesthetized with 0.008% tricaine. Fluorescence images and time-lapse movies were acquired using an MVX10 MacroView microscope (Olympus) equipped with an MVPLAPO 1x objective and a SOLA solid-state light engine (Lumencor), operated with cellSens Standard 2.3 software (Olympus).

To visualize perfusion and blood flow in the intersegmental vessels, aorta and caudal vein, same embryos were imaged with an inverted LEICA confocal SP8 microscope equipped with a HC FLUOTAR 25×0.95NA water objective, using hybrid photon counting detectors (HyD). For each embryo a z-stack was captured with a 1 μm step size. Each image was acquired using the same settings across all samples. Images were further processed with ImageJ v1.54p. Figures show maximum intensity projections of 40-50 μm depth corresponding to approximately half of the embryo volume, including the aorta, caudal vein, and ISVs. Crops were performed using a constant ROI size.

Data were expressed as the percentage of affected embryos relative to total embryos per treatment group. A minimum of 20 embryos were analysed in each experimental condition, across 4 independent repetitions.

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses; The experiments were not randomized; The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
