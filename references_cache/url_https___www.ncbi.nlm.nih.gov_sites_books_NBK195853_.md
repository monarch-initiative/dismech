MEGD(H)EL syndrome is characterized by neonatal hypoglycemia, a sepsis-like clinical picture for which no infectious agent can be found, feeding problems, poor weight gain, muscular hypotonia, developmental delay, and liver involvement. By age two years, spasticity, dystonia, epilepsy, and progressive deafness prevent further psychomotor development and/or result in loss of acquired skills. Affected children are completely dependent on care for all activities of daily living; speech is absent. Individuals with <i>SERAC1</i>-related cHSP may have cognitive deficits that are generally mild, juvenile-onset spastic paraplegia, and dystonia. <i>SERAC1</i>-related juvenile- or adult-onset generalized dystonia is characterized by dystonia, cognitive issues, bradykinesia, and spasticity. In all forms the course is progressive over time.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Diagnosis/testing.</h4><p>The diagnosis of SERAC1 deficiency is established in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with suggestive clinical and metabolic (3-methylglutaconic aciduria) findings and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>SERAC1</i> identified by <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a>.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Management.</h4><p><i>Treatment of manifestations:</i> Treatment of MEGD(H)EL syndrome is supportive. Care is best provided by a multidisciplinary team including a metabolic pediatrician, pediatric neurologist, dietician, and physical therapist when possible. Management of neonatal hypoglycemia; feeding therapy and gastrostomy tube as needed; respiratory problems resulting from excessive drooling improve with botulinum toxin injection in the salivary glands, extirpation of salivary glands, and/or rerouting of glandular ducts. Supportive management of spasticity; some individuals have experienced (temporary) improvement of spasticity with oral or intrathecal baclofen treatment. Consideration of deep brain stimulation for dystonia and/or parkinsonism. Developmental and educational support; management of seizures per experienced neurologist; treatment of liver disease per hepatologist; treatment of hearing loss should be guided by expected benefit; treatment of scoliosis per orthopedist; management of vision issues per ophthalmologist; low vision services as needed; social work and family support.</p><p>Treatment of <i>SERAC1</i>-related juvenile and adult phenotypes includes developmental and educational support as needed; supportive treatment of spasticity; baclofen may provide temporary improvement in spasticity; treatment of dystonia and movement disorder per neurologist; deep brain stimulation may be beneficial; feeding therapy and gastrostomy tube as needed. Respiratory problems resulting from excessive drooling improve with botulinum toxin injection in the salivary glands, extirpation of salivary glands, and/or rerouting of glandular ducts. Standard treatments of seizures and hearing loss; social work and family support.</p><p><i>Surveillance:</i> Monitor blood glucose in those with MEGD(H)EL syndrome. At each visit and at least annually assess growth, nutrition, safety of oral intake, neurologic manifestations, developmental progress, cognition, educational needs, hearing, musculoskeletal manifestations, ophthalmology evaluation, and family needs; laboratory assessment of liver dysfunction per hepatologist.</p><p><i>Agents and circumstances to avoid:</i> Medications known to impair mitochondrial function (e.g., valproic acid).</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Genetic counseling.</h4><p>SERAC1 deficiency is inherited in an <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> manner. If both parents are known to be <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for a <i>SERAC1</i> <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of inheriting <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic variants and being affected, a 50% chance of inheriting one pathogenic variant and being an asymptomatic <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a>, and a 25% chance of inheriting neither of the <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/familial/">familial</a> pathogenic variants and being unaffected and not a carrier. Once the <i>SERAC1</i> pathogenic variants have been identified in an affected family member, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier-testing/">carrier testing</a> for at-risk relatives and prenatal/<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> are possible.</p></div></div><div id="megdel.GeneReview_Scope"><h2 id="_megdel_GeneReview_Scope_"><i>GeneReview</i> Scope</h2><div id="megdel.Tc" class="table"><p class="large-table-link" style="display:none"><span class="right"><a href="/sites/books/NBK195853/table/megdel.Tc/?report=objectonly" target="object">View in own window</a></span></p><div class="large_tbl" id="__megdel.Tc_lrgtbl__"><table class="no_bottom_margin"><thead><tr><th id="hd_h_megdel.Tc_1_1_1_1" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">SERAC1 Deficiency: Phenotypic Spectrum&#x000a0;<sup>1</sup></th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td headers="hd_h_megdel.Tc_1_1_1_1" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;"><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>MEGD(H)EL syndrome (3-<i>me</i>thyl<i>g</i>lutaconic aciduria with <i>d</i>eafness-dystonia, [<i>h</i>epatopathy], <i>e</i>ncephalopathy, and <i>L</i>eigh-like syndrome)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Juvenile-onset complicated hereditary spastic paraplegia (cHSP) with mild non-progressive intellectual disability</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Juvenile- or adult-onset generalized dystonia-parkinsonism</div></li></ul>

(comparable to <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/narp/">Leigh syndrome</a>) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.wortmann.2015.98">Wortmann et al 2015</a>]. All affected individuals with MEGD(H)EL syndrome had a distinctive brain MRI pattern with five characteristic disease stages affecting the basal ganglia, especially the putamen.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Stage 0. Normal MRI</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Stage 1. T<sub>2</sub>-weighted signal changes present in the pallidum</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Stage 2. Swelling of the putamen and caudate nucleus. The dorsal putamen contains an "eye" that shows no signal alteration and (thus) seems to be spared during this stage of the disease.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Stage 3. The putaminal eye increases, reflecting progressive putaminal involvement. This "eye" was found in all individuals with MEGD(H)EL syndrome during a specific age range (&#x0003e;1-4 years), and has not been reported in other disorders, making it pathognomonic for MEGD(H)EL syndrome and allowing diagnosis based on MRI findings.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Stage 4. Basal ganglia degeneration until near loss; cortical and cerebellar atrophy</div></li></ul><p>Note: The basal ganglia alterations are also seen in adults with SERAC1 deficiency. There is no study available in which the longitudinal course of the lesions is investigated.</p></div><div id="megdel.Family_History"><h4>Family History</h4><p>Family history is consistent with <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> inheritance (e.g., affected sibs and/or parental <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/consanguinity/">consanguinity</a>). Absence of a known family history of affected individuals does not preclude the diagnosis.</p></div></div><div id="megdel.Establishing_the_Diagnosis"><h3>Establishing the Diagnosis</h3><p>The diagnosis of SERAC1 deficiency is established in a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with suggestive clinical and metabolic (3-methylglutaconic aciduria) findings and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic (or <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a>) variants in <i>SERAC1</i> identified by <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK195853/table/megdel.T.serac1_deficiency_molecular_gen/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmegdelTserac1deficiencymoleculargen">Table 2</a>).</p><p>Note: (1) Per American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG)&#x000a0;/ Association for Molecular Pathology variant interpretation guidelines, the terms "<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>" and "<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a> variant" are synonymous in a clinical setting, meaning that both are considered diagnostic and can be used for clinical decision making [<a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.richards.2015.405">Richards et al 2015</a>]. Reference to "pathogenic variants" in this <i>GeneReview</i> is understood to include likely pathogenic variants. (2) Identification of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> <i>SERAC1</i> variants of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/uncertain-significance/">uncertain significance</a> (or identification of one known <i>SERAC1</i> pathogenic variant and one <i>SERAC1</i> variant of uncertain significance) does not establish or rule out the diagnosis.</p><p>Molecular genetic testing approaches can include a combination of <b><a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a>-targeted testing</b> (single gene testing, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multigene-panel/">multigene panel</a>) and <b>comprehensive</b>
<b><a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing</b> (<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exome-sequencing/">exome sequencing</a>, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genome-sequencing/">genome sequencing</a>). Gene-targeted testing requires that the clinician determine which gene(s) are likely involved (see <a href="#megdel.Option_1">Option 1</a>), whereas comprehensive genomic testing does not (see <a href="#megdel.Option_2">Option 2</a>).</p><div id="megdel.Option_1"><h4>Option 1</h4><p><b>Single-<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> testing.</b> Sequence analysis of <i>SERAC1</i> is performed first to detect <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a>, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/nonsense-variant/">nonsense</a>, and <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/splice-site/">splice site</a> variants and small intragenic deletions/insertions. Note: Depending on the sequencing method used, single-<a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exon/">exon</a>, multiexon, or whole-gene deletions/duplications may not be detected. If only one or no variant is detected by the sequencing method used, the next step is to perform gene-targeted <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/deletion-duplication-analysis/">deletion/duplication analysis</a> to detect exon and whole-gene deletions or duplications.</p><p><b>A spasticity, dystonia, deafness, nuclear mitochondrial, or intellectual disability</b>
<b><a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multigene-panel/">multigene panel</a></b> that includes <i>SERAC1</i> and other genes of interest (see <a href="#megdel.Differential_Diagnosis">Differential Diagnosis</a>) is most likely to identify the genetic cause of the condition while limiting identification of pathogenic variants and variants of <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/uncertain-significance/">uncertain significance</a> in genes that do not explain the underlying <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>. Note: (1) The genes included in the panel and the diagnostic <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sensitivity/">sensitivity</a> of the testing used for each <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> vary by laboratory and are likely to change over time. (2) Some multigene panels may include genes not associated with the condition discussed in this <i>GeneReview</i>. (3) In some laboratories, panel options may include a custom laboratory-designed panel and/or custom phenotype-focused <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exome/">exome</a> analysis that includes genes specified by the clinician. (4) Methods used in a panel may include <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sequence-analysis/">sequence analysis</a>, <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/deletion-duplication-analysis/">deletion/duplication analysis</a>, and/or other non-sequencing-based tests.</p><p>For an introduction to multigene panels click <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/app5/#app5.Multigene_Panels">here</a>. Feeding is complicated by the movement disorder and often also by excessive drooling, often requiring tube feeding.</p><p><b>Neurologic manifestations.</b> During the first year of life affected infants often present with truncal hypotonia. Some do not survive the neonatal period due to multiorgan failure, some succumb to liver failure in infancy, and others to (pulmonary) infections later in life. The oldest living affected individual is older than age 24 years.</p></div><div id="megdel.JuvenileOnset_cHSP"><h4>Juvenile-Onset cHSP</h4><p>Two families with juvenile-onset cHSP have been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.roeben.2018.39">Roeben et al 2018</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.yan.2021.816265">Yan et al 2021</a>].</p><p>In one family, juvenile-onset paraspasticity, complicated by non-progressive mild cognitive deficits, was observed in which five of six affected individuals from a large <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/consanguineous/">consanguineous</a> family were <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/homozygous/">homozygous</a> for a <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/splice-site/">splice site</a> variant [<a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.roeben.2018.39">Roeben et al 2018</a>]. id="megdel.Differential_Diagnosis"><h2 id="_megdel_Differential_Diagnosis_">Differential Diagnosis</h2><p><b>MEGD(H)EL syndrome.</b> Increased urinary excretion of the branched-chain organic acid 3-methylglutaconic aciduria (3-MGA-uria) is a relatively common finding in children investigated for suspected inborn errors of metabolism [<a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.wortmann.2013b.913">Wortmann et al 2013b</a>]. Inborn errors of metabolism with 3-MGA-uria as a discriminative feature (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK195853/table/megdel.T.megd_h_el_syndrome_genetic_diff/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmegdelTmegdhelsyndromegeneticdiff">Table 4</a>) show a characteristic "syndromal" pattern of signs and symptoms [<a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.wortmann.2013a.923">Wortmann et al 2013a</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.wortmann.2013b.913">Wortmann et al 2013b</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.kovacsnagy.2018.373">Kovacs-Nagy et al 2018</a>].</p><p>Mitochondrial disorders should also be considered in the differential diagnosis of MEGD(H)EL syndrome. Mitochondrial disorders are caused by pathogenic variants in mitochondrial DNA or nuclear DNA and can present with any sign or symptom. More than 370 genes associated with mitochondrial disorders have been identified [<a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.heath.2025.75">Heath et al 2025</a>]. Tissues with higher requirements for oxidative metabolism, such as the central nervous system and cardiac and skeletal muscle, are predominantly affected. (See <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/mt-overview/">Primary Mitochondrial Disorders Overview</a>.)</p><p>3-MGA-uria is common in mitochondrial disorders [<a class="bk_pop" href="#megdel.REF.wortmann.2012a.13">Wortmann et al 2012a</a>], although 3-methylglutaconic acid (3-MGA) excretion is lower than in MEGD(H)EL syndrome, and clinical features observed in MEGD(H)EL syndrome are frequently found in mitochondrial disorders. For example, progressive deafness is often reported with the mitochondrial DNA <a class="def" href="/sites/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> m.3243A&#x0003e;G, and Leigh syndrome and dystonia are a typical neuro(radio)logic finding in mitochondrial disorders in relation to deficiency of complex I or IV of the respiratory chain. In the US, early intervention is a federally funded program available in all states that provides in-home services to target individual therapy needs.</p><p><b>Ages 3-5 years.</b> In the US, developmental preschool through the local public school district is recommended. Before placement, an evaluation is made to determine needed services and therapies and an individualized education plan (IEP) is developed for those who qualify based on established motor, language, social, or cognitive delay. The early intervention program typically assists with this transition. Developmental preschool is center based; for children too medically unstable to attend, home-based services are provided.</p><p><b>All ages.</b> Consultation with a developmental pediatrician is recommended to ensure the involvement of appropriate community, state, and educational agencies (US) and to support parents in maximizing quality of life. Some issues to consider:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>IEP services:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>An IEP provides specially designed instruction and related services to children who qualify.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>IEP services will be reviewed annually to determine whether any changes are needed.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Special education law requires that children participating in an IEP be in the least restrictive environment feasible at school and included in general education as much as possible, when and where appropriate.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Vision and hearing consultants should be a part of the child's IEP team to support access to academic material.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>PT, OT, and speech services will be provided in the IEP to the extent that the need affects the child's access to academic material. Beyond that, private supportive therapies based on the affected individual's needs may be considered. Specific recommendations regarding type of therapy can be made by a developmental pediatrician.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>As a child enters the teen years, a transition plan should be discussed and incorporated in the IEP. For those receiving IEP services, the public school district is required to provide services until age 21.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>A 504 plan (Section 504: a US federal statute that prohibits discrimination based on disability) can be considered for those who require accommodations or modifications such as front-of-class seating, assistive technology devices, classroom scribes, extra time between classes, modified assignments, and enlarged text.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Developmental Disabilities Administration (DDA) enrollment is recommended. DDA is a US public agency that provides services and support to qualified individuals. Eligibility differs by state but is typically determined by diagnosis and/or associated cognitive/adaptive disabilities.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Families with limited income and resources may also qualify for supplemental security income (SSI) for their child with a disability.</div></li></ul></div><div id="megdel.Motor_Dysfunction"><h4>Motor Dysfunction</h4><p>
<b>Gross motor dysfunction</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Physical therapy is recommended to maximize mobility and to reduce the risk for later-onset orthopedic complications (e.g., contractures, scoliosis, hip dislocation).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Consider use of durable medical equipment and positioning devices as needed (e.g., wheelchairs, walkers, bath chairs, orthotics, adaptive strollers).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>For muscle tone abnormalities including hypertonia or dystonia, consider involving appropriate specialists to aid in management of baclofen, tizanidine, botulinum toxin, anti-parkinsonian medications, or orthopedic procedures.</div></li></ul><p><b>Fine motor dysfunction.</b> Occupational therapy is recommended for difficulty with fine motor skills that affect adaptive function such as feeding, grooming, dressing, and writing.</p><p><b>Oral motor dysfunction</b> should be assessed at each visit and clinical feeding evaluations and/or radiographic swallowing studies should be obtained for choking/gagging during feeds, poor weight gain, frequent respiratory illnesses, or feeding refusal that is not otherwise explained. Assuming that the child is safe to eat by mouth, feeding therapy (typically from an occupational or speech therapist) is recommended to help improve coordination or sensory-related feeding issues. Feeds can be thickened or chilled for safety. When feeding dysfunction is severe, an NG-tube or G-tube may be necessary.</p><p><b>Speech, language, and communication issues.</b> Speech-language evaluation should be considered early in development for children who have delayed communication milestones or who are not yet talking. Evaluation for alternative means of communication (e.g., <a href="https://www.asha.org/NJC/AAC/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">augmentative and alternative communication</a> [AAC]) is appropriate for individuals who have speech or receptive and expressive language difficulties. An AAC evaluation should be completed by a speech-language pathologist who has expertise in the area. This evaluation typically takes into account cognitive abilities, sensory impairments, and motor skills to determine the most appropriate form of communication. AAC devices can range from low-tech, such as picture exchange communication, to high-tech, such as voice-generating devices. Contrary to popular belief, AAC devices do not hinder verbal development of speech, but rather support optimal speech and language development. <a href="https://www.radboudumc.nl/EN/Pages/default.aspx" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">Radboud UMC</a>
