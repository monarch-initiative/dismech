# Development of oxidative stress-associated disease models using feto-maternal interface organ-on-a-chip.
**Authors:** Richardson LS, Kammala AK, Kim S, Lam PY, Truong N, Radnaa E, Urrabaz-Garza R, Han A, Menon R
**Journal:** FASEB J (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1096/fj.202300531R](https://doi.org/10.1096/fj.202300531R)

## Content

Oxidative stress (OS) and inflammation arising from cellular derangements at the fetal membrane-decidual interface (feto-maternal interface [FMi]) is a major antecedent to preterm birth (PTB). However, it is impractical to study OS-associated FMi disease state during human pregnancy, and thus it is difficult to develop strategies to reduce the incidences of PTB. A microfluidic organ-on-chip model (FMi-OOC) that mimics the in vivo structure and functions of FMi in vitro was developed to address this challenge. The FMi-OOC contained fetal (amnion epithelial, mesenchymal, and chorion) and maternal (decidua) cells cultured in four compartments interconnected by arrays of microchannels to allow independent but interconnected co-cultivation. Using this model, we tested the effects of OS and inflammation on both fetal (fetal → maternal) and maternal (maternal → fetal) sides of the FMi and determined their differential impact on PTB-associated pathways. OS was induced using cigarette smoke extract (CSE) exposure. The impacts of OS were assessed by measuring cell viability, disruption of immune homeostasis, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), development of senescence, and inflammation. CSE propagated (LC/MS-MS analysis for nicotine) over a 72-hour period from the maternal to fetal side, or vice versa. However, they caused two distinct pathological effects on the maternal and fetal cells. Specifically, fetal OS induced cellular pathologies and inflammation, whereas maternal OS caused immune intolerance. The pronounced impact produced by the fetus supports the hypothesis that fetal inflammatory response is a mechanistic trigger for parturition. The FMi disease-associated changes identified in the FMi-OOC suggest the unique capability of this in vitro model in testing in utero conditions.

Preterm birth (PTB) before 37 weeks of gestation is a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality(1). It affects approximately 575,000 pregnancies (10.5%) in the United States each year(1, 2). The exact cause of preterm labor resulting in PTB is unclear, but it is most likely a final common outcome from activating one or more biochemical pathways3–9. Hence, it is now considered a “multifactorial syndrome with heterogeneity in pathophysiology and biomarkers.” A better understanding of risk factors, risk-associated pathophysiology, biochemical markers, and mechanistic factors that activate the premature labor process are required to better predict PTB and develop interventions to prevent PTB(3, 4).

A balanced inflammation and redox state are required for feto-placental growth at the two feto-maternal interfaced, placenta-decidua basalis and fetal membrane-decidua parietalis, development during normal pregnancy. A premature shift towards a pro-inflammatory environment and increased redox radicals due to various risk exposures by the mother and/or the fetus can cause premature activation of the labor cascade of events, prelabor membrane rupture, premature cervical ripening, preterm labor, and delivery(5–7). These shifts in inflammatory and redox environments have been shown to play. Thus, inflammation and oxidative stress functions are inseparable components during normal pregnancies as well as pathologic adverse events. In relation to intrauterine tissue, increased fetal metabolic demands, reduced maternal supply of metabolic substrates, and depleted antioxidants contribute to oxidative stress induction around the fetal membrane feto-maternal interface(8). However, most of what we know about inflammation and oxidative stress-associated PTB is modeled on ascending infections(9–11). One difficulty with this model is that infections account for only a subset of PTBs(12). In many cases, no apparent pathogens can be identified based on cultures or molecular aproaches. Likely, other factors such as bleeding disorders, obesity, poor nutrition, maternal stress, or environmental toxins may generate, or combined with infection, contribute to the oxidative stress and inflammatory responses that result in PTB(13–16). All of these non-infectious risk factors can produce sterile or noninfectious inflammation, which can contribute to similar pathophysiologic signatures as ascending infection, leading to PTB.

Recent studies have explored sterile inflammation theories and the mechanistic contributors to this process. For example, environmental pollutants (e.g., cigarette smoke, flame retardant chemicals, and other chemicals like Bisphenol A [BP]A and BPS) can cause an intrauterine oxidative radical increase, leading to tissue and cellular damage that results in inflammation similar to that seen with intrauterine infection(17–19). Using cells from the fetal membranes, we have previously reported an oxidative stress and sterile inflammation model produced by cigarette smoke extract(20–28). Cigarette smoke causes reactive oxygen species production from fetal cells, induces DNA damage and telomere shortening, p38MAPK activation, and cellular senescence(28). In addition, epithelial cells undergo a transition to pro-inflammatory mesenchymal cells(29–35). Both of these processes are expected to generate cellular-level inflammation. Tissue-level inflammatory mediators are indicative of cellular and nuclear damage and also are capable of chemotaxis of immune cells that can further enhance inflammation.

Current in vitro and in vivo research models to examine risk factors and risk-induced pathophysiologic pathways of pregnancy have contributed immensely to our current knowledge base. However, several limitations exist that prevent us from better understanding the intercellular interactions between the feto-maternal units. Widely used in vivo models such as mice are hampered by anatomical and endocrine differences between such animal models and human pregnancies. Most in vitro models do not sufficiently mimic the structure and functions of human feto-maternal units. Importantly, intercellular interactions between fetal and maternal cells can also modify feto-maternal responses in both normal and pathologic pregnancies, further creating challenges for most in vitro models. Therefore, humanized models that can better recapitulate the interactive nature of the fetal membrane-decidua feto-maternal interface (FMi) are needed. Using a microfluidic-based organ-on-a-chip (OOC) technology platform that uses interconnected cell culture chambers containing fetal (fetal membrane or amniochorion) and maternal (decidua) cells, we have previously recreated an ascending infection model of the FMi(36). Using this model (FMi-OOC), we have shown the propagation of an infectious agent from the maternal to fetal side and the development of inflammatory responses by the fetal and maternal cells(36–38). The results from this infection-associated inflammation model was compared to that from mouse models of ascending infection, where similar responses at the feto-maternal tissues led to PTB(36–38).

This successful development of the healthy and infection-associated inflammation FMi-OOC models led us to test here the hypothesis that oxidative stress (OS) experienced on the maternal side due to various risk factors, or fetal side due to intra-amniotic buildup of reactive oxygen radicals, can cause differential cellular and sterile inflammatory responses. The responses at the FMi that depend on the side where OS arises could be indicative of predisposing properties by either the maternal or fetal side to PTB. Using the baseline parameters of cell viability, expression of various cytoskeletal markers, production of endocrine and paracrine (extracellular vesicles) mediators, as well as inflammatory mediators obtained using our previously developed healthy (normal/control) FMi-OOC model that uses primary and primary-derived cell lines from fetal membrane/decidua(36–39), here we developed an OS-trigged disease model of the FMi. This model was used to assess the effects of OS on the maternal side and its impact on fetal response, as well as the effects of OS on the fetal side and its effects on the maternal side.

We used established immortalized cell lines from term, not in labor, human fetal membranes and decidua parietalis, as reported in our prior publications(39–41), to be used in our FMi-OOC model (Figure 1A/B). Human maternal decidual cells (hFM_DEC) were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Mediatech Inc., Manassas, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 10% penicillin/streptomycin (Mediatech), and 10% amphotericin B (Sigma-Aldrich, Inc. St. Louis, MO). Human amnion epithelial cells (hFM_AEC) were cultured in KSFM supplemented with bovine pituitary extract (30 μg/mL), epidermal growth factor (0.1 ng/mL), CaCl2 (0.4 mM), and primocin (0.5 mg/mL). Human amnion mesenchymal cells (hFM_AMC) were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 5% FBS, 10% penicillin/streptomycin, and 10% amphotericin B. Human chorion trophoblast cells (hFM_CTC) were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 0.20% FBS, 0.01 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 0.5% penicillin/streptomycin, 0.3% BSA, 1× ITS-X, 2 μM CHIR99021, 0.05 μM A83–01, 1.5 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid, 50 ng/mL epithelial growth factor, 0.08 mM VPA, and 1× Revitacell (Rock inhibitor/Y27632). All cells were grown at 37°C and 5% CO2 until they reached 80–90% confluency. Immortalized cells have been validated against primary cells previoulsy(41). Cells under passage 30 were used for experiments.

To model the fetal membrane-decidua FMi, a microfluidic in vitro device was designed and fabricated to mimic the in utero tissue. The FMi-OOC used in the current study has been previously developed, tested, and validated(36, 40, 42–45). Briefly, the device is composed of four concentric-circle-shaped culture compartments (one for maternal cells and three for fetal cells) interconnected through arrays of microfluidic channels to allow co-cultivation communication among the four different cell types (Figure 1B). Chamber one, the center chamber, contains maternal hFM_DECs, chamber two has fetal hFM_CTCs, chamber three has fetal hFM_AMCs, and the outermost chamber four is for fetal hFM_AECs. Each cell culture chamber is 250 μm in height, and the width of each chamber (maternal decidua – 3,000 μm; fetal chorion – 2,000 μm, fetal mesenchyme – 2,000 μm, and fetal epithelium – 600 μm) (Figure 1B) was designed to mimic the thickness of each maternal and fetal layer as seen in utero(46, 47). Thus, this design allows the four different cell types to be cultured in four separate microenvironments (e.g., different culture media). The chambers are interconnected through an array of 24 microchannels (width: 35 μm, length: 600 μm for CTCs to DECs and AMCs to CTCs, 300 μm for AECs to AMCs; all height: 5 μm) that perform several functions, such as preventing the movement of cells between compartments during the initial cell loading process, allowing independent localized biochemical treatments to each compartment, enabling independent elution of supernatant from each cell compartment, and allowing biochemicals to diffuse among the chambers to enable cell-cell communication(31, 46, 48). The number of microchannels, along with the height and width, remained the same throughout all experiments as to not affect the diffusion rate of drugs and products. The device also contains an on-chip reservoir block, where each reservoir is aligned on top of the inlets and outlets of each chamber (Figure 1B). The designed platform was fabricated in polydimethyl siloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184, 1:10 mixture, DowDuPont, Midland, MI, USA) using a two-step photolithography master mold fabrication process, followed by a soft lithography process of replica-molding the final PDMS device from the master mold(49). To improve the bonding of the PDMS layer onto the glass substrate, the PDMS layers were treated with oxygen plasma (Harrick Plasma, Ithaca, NY, USA) for 90 s, followed by bonding the layer onto a glass slide. This process was repeated to bond the PDMS reservoir layer on top of the device. The assembled device was stored dry (for up to 1 month).

Before cells were added on-chip, the OOC devices were coated with collagen to 1) help with cell attachment to the glass slide and 2) mimic the basement membrane of the amnion and chorion. Before using the FMi-OOC, the devices were washed with 70% ethanol for 10 min for sterilization, washed three times with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then the microchannels were coated with type IV basement membrane collagen Matrigel to model the basement membrane (Corning Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix, DEV-free; 1 : 25 in media). The Matrigel filled the microchannels connecting the AEC compartment to the AMC compartment as well as the microchannels between the AMC compartment and the CTC compartment, mimicking the amnion and chorion basement membranes in utero, respectively, within the FMi-OOC. The device was then incubated overnight at 37°C in a 5% CO2 environment. To confirm the presence of collagen within the microchannels, the PDMS device was delaminated and imaged using a scanning electron microscope (TESCAN MIRA3; SEM MAG 1.48 kx and SEM HV 10.0 kV) after sputter-coating the device with a thin layer of Au (Figure 1C).

Methods for obtaining and preparing cell-free collagen from amniotic membrane extracellular matrix (ECM) follows our previously reported procedure(36, 37, 39, 40).

One day after the Matrigel coating, the devices were washed two times with complete DMEM-F12 medium before cell seeding. Immortalized cells (Figure 1D) were trypsinized and loaded into the FMi-OOC device, starting from the center chamber to the outside chamber (60,000 hFM_DEC cells for chamber one, 200,000 hFM_CTCs + 5% primary collagen(50) + 25% Matrigel for chamber two, 62,500 hFM_AMCs + 20% primary collagen + 25% Matrigel for chamber three, and 120,000 hFM_AECs for chamber four), similar to the concentration ratios seen in utero. Following cell seeding, the FMi-OOC devices were placed in wells of a 6-well plate and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 environment overnight before treatment.

To induce OS at the FMi, cigarette smoke extract (CSE) was used as previously described(21, 30, 51, 52), with some modifications. Cigarette smoke from a single commercial cigarette (unfiltered Camel; R.J. Reynolds Tobacco, Winston Salem, NC, USA) was bubbled into 25 ml of hFM_AEC or hFM_DEC medium to obtain the CSE. The stock CSE was then sterilized using a 0.25 mm Steriflip filter unit (Millipore Sigma) and diluted to 1:50 in a cell-specific medium before use.

To create an OS disease model (i.e., maternal or fetal sterile inflammation), as expected in subjects with preterm labor, cells in the FMi-OOC were treated with one of the following conditions for up to 72 hours (h): 1) normal cell culture conditions with media specific for each type of cell (referred to in the rest of the manuscript as “control”), 2) OS treatment (1:25 ratio) on the maternal hFM_DEC cells, or 3) OS treatment (1:25) on the fetal hFM_AEC cells. This dose of OS is within the range reported in women with PTBs(23, 29, 53, 54). CSE is a validated and reproducible OS inducer in our system(21, 28–32, 51, 52, 54, 55) as well as it is major risk factor of pregnancy and PTB. After the cells have reached 70–80% confluency in the FMi-OOCs (12–24 h after cell seeding), media from the reservoirs were removed and the hFM_DEC or hFM_AEC chamber-reservoir was treated with the respective conditions, followed by incubating the device at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity environment for up to 72 h. To maintain low levels of perfusion between the cell culture chambers, the reservoirs on top of the device were filled with the following volumes of culture media at time zero (decidua – 90 μl, chorion – 60 μl, mesenchyme – 60 μl, and epithelium – 50 μl). At 48 h time point, 1/3 volume of cell-specific media or treatment was added to each reservoir on top of the resident media in the same gradient. This was to allow the cells to have extra nutrients to withstand a 3-day culture and to minimalize the potential for the device to dry up due to evaporation.

To assess the cytotoxic effects of CSE on the cells cultured in the FMi-OOC, a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity detection kit (11644793001, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) was used. Cell culture media from each chamber of the FMi-OOC was collected after 24, 48, or 72 h of treatment. Approximately 10 μL of cell supernatants were used to perform the cytotoxicity assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 90 μL of LDH solution was added to 10 μL of cell supernatants in a 96-well plate. The assay plate was incubated at room temperature in dark for 20 minutes. Media was collected 12–24 h after cell seeding and used to remove LDH background. Fresh cell culture media (10 μL) was used as a blank. Cell supernatants from cells in the FMi-OOC were treated with 15% Triton X-100 and mixed thoroughly to ensure lysis of cell membranes was used as a positive control (100% cytotoxicity). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

To assess CSE propagation in the FMi-OOC, frozen media samples were thawed at room temperature and 200 μL of ice-cold methanol was added to 50 μL of media. The mixture was vortexed for 5 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was passed through a 0.2-μm pore membrane filter, and 20 μL of the filtrate was injected for targeted Nicotine LC-MS/MS analysis.

Targeted liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-QQQ) analysis was performed on a TSQ Altis mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) coupled to a binary pump UHPLC (Vanquish, Thermo Scientific). Scan parameters for target ions can be found in Table 1. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Kinetex 5 μm, 150 mm x 2.1 mm C18 column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) maintained at 30°C using a solvent gradient method. Sample acquisition and data analysis were performed using Trace Finder 3.3 (Thermo Scientific).

Immunocytochemical staining for Vimentin (Abcam; ab92547; 1:300), Cytokeratin (CK)-18 (Abcam; ab668; 1:800), Histocompatibility Antigen (HLA)-G (Abcam; ab52455; 1:200), and HLA-DR (Abcam; ab929511; 1:50) were used to monitor cell types and document their immune regulatory status. Antibodies were titrated to determine appropriate dilutions to ensure specific and uniform staining. After 72 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton-X, and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin in 1× PBS, before incubation with primary antibodies overnight. Cells were washed three times in 1× PBS and then incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies (Abcam; Alexa Fluor 488-rabbit; ab150073) (Invitrogen; Alexa Fluor 594-mouse; A11005) (1:1000) for 1 h. The FMi-OOCs were washed with 1× PBS and then treated with NucBlue® Fixed ReadyProbes Reagent (R37606; ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) (2 drops per ml) to stain the nucleus. Primary and secondary concentrations were validated based on previous FMi-OOC-based studies.

To assess for OS-induced cellular senescence, senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining was conducted using a commercial histochemical staining assay following the manufacturer’s instructions (CS0030–1KT; Sigma-Aldrich, Inc.; St. Louis, MO). Briefly, cells cultured in the FMi-OOC were washed twice with 1× PBS, fixed for 15 minutes with the provided Fixation Buffer, washed again with 1× PBS, and incubated for 3 h at 37°C with fresh β-Gal staining solution. Following incubation, cells were evaluated using a standard light microscope.

After 24 h, 48 h, or 72 h of culture/treatment, bright field microscopy was performed (Nikon Eclipse TS100 microscope; ×4 and ×10 magnification, or Keyence all-in-one fluorescence BZ-X810 microscope, ×4, ×10, and ×40 magnification) to determine cell morphology, nascent collagen production by cells, intermediate filament expression, immune regulatory markers, cell migration, and senescence.

Two random regions of interest per device (N=3) and per chamber were used to determine overall CK-18, vimentin, HLA-DR, and HLA-G fluorescence expression. Laser settings, brightness, contrast, and collection settings were uniform for all images collected. Images were not modified (brightness, contrast, and smoothing) for intensity analysis. ImageJ software was used to measure staining intensity.

The cell shape index was determined for AECs and AMCs by evaluating one frame from each N (total of 3 images) per treatment for cell circularity using the ImageJ software. The cell shape index was calculated using the following formula: SI=4π*Area/Perimeter2, which is an established method that was originally reported to determine vascular cell shape(56). A circle would have a shape index of 1; a straight line would have an index of 0.

Two random regions of interest per device (N=3) and per chamber were used to determine the percent population of senescent cells (teal-colored cells). Laser settings, brightness, contrast, and collection settings were uniform for all images collected. ImageJ software was used to measure the number of SA-β-Gal+ cells manually with the cell counter tool. These values were divided by the average number of cells per image to generate the percent population of senescent cells after 72 h.

To analyze changes in inflammatory mediators, interleukin (IL)-8, IL-6, IL-10, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) were analyzed from the cell supernatants in the FMi-OOC after 24, 48, or 72 h of treatments. Supernatants were collected from the reservoirs of the devices using a pipette. Standard curves were developed with duplicate samples of known quantities of recombinant proteins that were provided by the manufacturer. Sample concentrations were determined by relating the fluorescence values that were obtained to the standard curve by linear regression analysis. IL-6:IL-10 Ratio was calculated by converting the pg/mL results into molar concentrations of each cytokine and dividing IL-6 by IL-10 concentrations.

Table 2 shows the comparison between the values of cellular and inflammatory endpoints measured (reported as fold changes) in this manuscript. The comparative control vs. CSE treatment fold change values for cellular transition or immune status, cellular senescence, and inflammation were calculated in the following manner. The overall cellular transition fold change in the fetal-to-maternal or maternal-to-fetal OS trigger conditions were calculated by combining the fold change of the following 72 h results: 1) hFM_AEC cell shape index, 2) hFM_AEC vimentin/CK-18 ratio, 3) hFM_AMC cell shape index, and 4) hFM_AMC vimentin/CK-18 ratio. The overall immune status fold change in the fetal-to-maternal or maternal-to-fetal OS trigger conditions were calculated by the fold change of HLA-DR expression in the hFM_DECs. The overall cellular senescence fold change in the fetal-to-maternal or maternal-to-fetal OS trigger conditions were calculated by the fold change of control vs. CSE SA-β-Gal results in the hFM_DECs and hFM_AECs. The overall inflammation fold change in the fetal-to-maternal OS trigger conditions were calculated by combining the fold change of the following results: amnion values included 24 h IL-6, IL-8, and GM-CSF fold change in hFM_AEC and 48 h IL-6, IL-8, and GM-CSF fold change in hFM_AMC, while Decidua values included 72 h IL-6, IL-8, and GM-CSF fold change in hFM_DECs. The overall inflammation fold change in the maternal-to-fetal OS trigger conditions were calculated by combining the fold change of the following results: Decidua values included 24 h IL-6, IL-8, and GM-CSF fold change in hFM_DECs, while amnion values included 72 h IL-6, IL-8, and GM-CSF fold change in hFM_AEC and 48 h IL-6, IL-8, and GM-CSF fold change in hFM_AMC. If any of these results showed controls containing higher values than CSE treatments, the number added to the total fold change was set to zero. The exception to this was the cell shape index results, where the expected numbers are smaller values for CSE treatment.

All data were analyzed using Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Student’s t-test was used to compare results with two means. Ordinary one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to compare normally distributed data with at least three means. Data is shown as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Asterisks denote p values as follows: * p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001.

The fetal membrane, made up of multiple cellular and collagen layers, lines the intrauterine cavity providing mechanical, endocrine, and immune support to the growing fetus, along with forming one of the FMis (fetal membrane [amniochorion]-decidua parietalis) throughout gestation (Figure 1A). The previously established FMi-OOC(36, 40, 42, 43, 57), comprised of four concentric circular cell culture chambers containing fetal-derived amnion epithelium (human fetal membrane [hFM] AECs), mesenchyme (hFM_AMC), and chorion (hFM_CTC), along with maternal decidua (hFM_DEC), connected by microchannel arrays, can be utilized to recreate the in utero fetal membrane-decidua parietalis FMi in vitro (Figure 1B). The cellular layers located inside the fetal membrane stroma in utero (i.e., hFM_AMC and hFM_CTC) are cultured in vitro in a 3D suspension of Matrigel and primary amnion-derived collagen (Figure 1C). Microchannel arrays that separated the hFM_AEC/hFM_AMC and hFM_AMC/hFM_CTC chambers were coated in Type IV collagen to recreate the amniochorion basement membrane (Figure 1C).

To further validate and establish a “healthy” FMi-OOC model, baseline characteristics such as cell viability, cell morphology, cellular transition, immune status of FMi cells, and inflammatory status of FMi cells were documented for 72 h. This time point was established based on the previous FMi-OOC findings that labor-associated changes can be documented within 72 h due to infection(36), exosome-derived damage associated molecular patterns(37), and environmental toxicants(43). After 72 h of culture, FMi cells maintained 95–90% viability due to the presence of the on-chip media reservoir that provides sufficient nutrients throughout the culture (Figure 2A). Amnion cells (hFM_AEC & hFM_AMC) were assessed for changes in cellular transition status (i.e., epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [EMT]) after 72 h, as changes of these cells to a mesenchymal phenotype are associated with labor signaling(30, 32). Amnion cells cultured within the FMi-OOC maintained their epithelial or mesenchymal morphology as indicated by no change in the CSI values (Figure 2B & 2C) and no change in the intermediate filament expression of epithelial cytokeratin-18 (CK-18; red) or mesenchymal vimentin (green) (Figure 2B & 2D). Choriodecidua interface cells (hFM_CTC & hFM_DEC) were assessed for changes in immune regulation status (i.e., expression of histocompatibility antigen-G [HLA-G] in the chorion and HLA-DR in the decidua) after 72 h, as changes in immune cell regulation are associated with immune tolerance at the FMi during pregnancy(58, 59). Immune regulatory receptors HLA-G (red) and HLA-DR (green) in the chorion and decidua, respectively, did not change in expression after 72 h of culture within the FMi-OOC (Figure 2B & 2D). Additionally, homeostatic levels of both anti- and pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and IL-6 normalized after 72 h of culture, indicated by an IL-10:IL-6 molar ratio below 0.5 (Figure 2E). These results demonstrate that the FMi-OOC platform does not innately induce cellular dysfunction or inflammatory changes after three days of culture (Figure 2). Therefore, this model of “healthy (normal/control) FMi” will be used to establish two different oxidative stress (OS)-associated disease models of the FMi during pregnancy.

Due to activation of physiological pathways (e.g., increased fetal metabolic demand & decrease of innate antioxidant supplies) at term and pathological pathways at preterm, such as reactive oxygen radicals build up in the intra-amniotic cavity, EMT, cellular aging (i.e., senescence), and inflammation in the fetal membrane cells contributes to labor onset(21, 31, 32, 60). Similarly, fetal vascular pathologies, intra-amniotic colonization and infection of microbes, metabolic dysfunctions, and accumulation of pollutants and toxicants in the amniotic fluid can cause the buildup of intra-amniotic OS that can cause damage to fetal membrane cells and propagate toward the mother. To determine the effect of in utero fetal OS exposure on maternal cells, a well-documented OS-inducer CSE(21–23, 27–32, 48, 51) was used to treat the fetal cells (hFM_AECs) within the FMi-OOC (Figure 3A). LC-MS/MS documented the propagation of nicotine, an indication of CSE trafficking, from the fetal hFM_AEC chamber to the fetal hFM_CTC chamber within 24 h and to the maternal hFM_DEC chamber within 48 h (Figure 3B). Before determining OS-induced cell dysfunction or inflammation across the FMi, it was confirmed that CSE treatment did not impact cell viability over 72 h (Figure 3C).

Fetal OS exposure induced changes in the cellular transition status of both amnion cells (hFM_AEC & hFM_AMC) as indicated by a significant increase in mesenchymal morphology (i.e., lower CSI value; hFM_AEC: 0.75±0.01 CSI vs. 0.70±0.01 CSI, p=0.0009; hFM_AMC: 0.36±0.03 CSI vs. 0.25±0.03 CSI, p=0.008) (Figure 3D–E) and a significant increase in the hFM_AEC vimentin/CK-18 ratio (0.85±0.04 relative intensity units [RIU] vs. 0.97±0.02 RIU, p=0.007;) compared to controls (Figure 3D & 3F). The immune regulatory status of the choriodecidua interface was not altered by fetal OS exposure, as shown by no change in HLA-G or HLA-DR expression (Figures 3D & 3F). OS treatment induced significant levels of cellular senescence, as identified by the presence of SA-β-Gal positive teal cells across the FMi within 72 h compared to controls (hFM_AEC: p=0.001; hFM_AMC: p=0.0007; hFM_CTC: p<0.0001; hFM_DEC: p=0.04) (Figure 3G). As reported in the literature, senescence on the maternal cells were not as pronounced as seen in fetal cells(8, 28).

The concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-8, IL-6, GM-CSF) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10) were measured after 24, 48, and 72 h of CSE exposure (Figure 4) to determine if OS-induced sterile inflammation remained localized to the fetal chamber or whether it would cross the FMi and affect the maternal side. OS-treated hFM_AEC significantly increased IL-8 and GM-CSF production within 24 h compared to controls (IL-8: p<0.0001; GM-CSF: p=0.002) (Figure 4A). Pro-inflammatory changes, such as a significant down-regulation of anti-inflammatory IL-10 production in the hFM_AMC (48 h IL-10: p=0.04), an increase in GM-CSF production in the hFM_CTC (48 h GM-CSF: p=0.04), and increased production in GM-CSF by the hFM_DEC by 72 h (GM-CSF: p=0.03) were observed (Figure 4A). These data show the ability of OS-induced sterile inflammation on the fetal side to cause inflammation in the maternal cells, either self-generated by each cell type in response to OS or by the propagation of various metabolites from the fetal side to the maternal side. In response to the increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines, hFM_CTC significantly down-regulates pro-inflammatory IL-6 after 72 h (p=0.04) (Figure 4A). This is likely an act to restore homeostasis at the FMi; however, unsuccessful due to the high degree of sterile inflammation upon OS exposure on the fetal side. OS-induced immune imbalance is indicated by the shift in pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine production in all fetal and maternal cell layers within the FMi-OOC. Collectively, these data are supportive of an association with fetal OS exposure leading to cellular dysfunction (i.e., EMT and senescence) and inflammation (i.e., GM-CSF dominant), promoting labor-associated changes across the FMi (Table 2).

A variety of risk factors, including maternal obesity, poor nutrition, behavioral factor (e.g., smoking, drinking), environmental pollutant exposure, and psycho-social factors, have been documented to increase systemic maternal OS levels, which are associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes(8, 29, 54, 55, 61). To determine the effect of systemic maternal OS exposure on fetal cells, CSE was used to induce OS in hFM_DECs within the FMi-OOC (Figure 5A). LC-MS/MS analyses documented CSE-linked nicotine propagation from the maternal hFM_DEC chamber to the fetal hFM_AMC chamber within 24 h and to the fetal hFM_AEC chamber within 48 h (Figure 5B). OS induced by maternal CSE treatment did not impact FMi cell viability over 72 h (Figure 5C). Maternal OS exposure induced cellular transitions in hFM_AECs as indicated by a significant increase in mesenchymal morphology (hFM_AEC: 0.81±0.01 CSI vs. 0.74±0.01 CSI, p<0.0001) (Figure 5D–E), along with an increase in the vimentin/CK-18 ratio ( 2.1±0.4 RIU vs. 4.8±1 RIU, p=0.01) (Figure 5D & 5F), but did not affect hFM_AMCs compared to controls. The immune regulatory markers of the decidua were affected by maternal OS exposure, as shown by a significant increase in HLA-DR expression compared to controls (0.03±0.01 vs. 0.2±0.1 RIU, p=0.04) (Figure 5D & 5F). However, HLA-G expression in the chorion was not affected (Figure 5D & 5F). OS treatment in the decidua induced significant levels of cellular senescence across the FMi within 72 h compared to controls (hFM_DEC: p=0.01; hFM_CTC: p=0.0002; hFM_AMC: p<0.0001; hFM_AEC: p=0.03) (Figure 5G).

Pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines (i.e., IL-8, GM-CSF, IL-6, and IL-10) were measured for up to 72 h to determine if OS-induced sterile inflammation remained localized to the maternal chamber or whether it would induce fetal inflammation (Figure 6). OS-treated hFM_DECs exhibited increased production of IL-8, GM-CSF, and IL-6 over time, and became significant by 48 h compared to controls (24 h IL-8: p=0.15; 24 h IL-6: p=0.05; 48 h IL-8: p=0.0003) (Figure 6A). IL-8 level was also significantly increased in the hFM_CTC by 48 h (IL-8: p=0.04) and in the hFM_AEC by 72 h (IL-8: p=0.02) (Figure 6A). In response to the increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines, hFM_CTC significantly down-regulated pro-inflammatory GM-CSF after 72 h (p=0.03) (Figure 6A). Additionally, CSE treatment of the decidua significantly increased the production of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in hFM_DECs and hFM_AMCs after 24 h (p=0.04 and p=0.01, respectively) (Figure 6A). However, even with an increase in IL-10 (24 h) and a decrease in GM-CSF (72 h), an immune balance was not observed across the FMi, suggesting that the innate response of hFM_DECs and hFM_AMCs in producing IL-10 was insufficient to overcome the pro-inflammatory impact of OS. Together, these results show a connection between maternal OS exposure leading to cellular dysfunction (i.e., increases in HLA-DR, EMT, and senescence) and inflammation (i.e., dominant pro-inflammatory IL-8), promoting labor-associated changes across the FMi (Table 2).

The lack of models that mimic the structural and functional characteristics of the FMi (57, 62, 63) has hindered the advancements in determining mechanobiological properties, determining the development of PTB disease states, and testing interventional molecules as well as understanding their mechanisms of action. Such technological challenges in conducting preclinical studies at the FMi have impacted the field of perinatal medicine in reducing the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. The primary purpose of this study is to establish an in vitro humanized FMi’ healthy and disease state’ model. Here we have developed and characterized the biomimetic and microfluidic in vitro FMi-OOC platform by first defining its maternal and fetal cellular phenotypes under “healthy” conditions. FMi cells within this OOC device maintained their 1) viability, 2) cellular transition status, 3) immune regulatory receptor expression, and 4) inflammatory homeostasis. These findings mimic the healthy state of the membrane decidual interface in utero, validating the relevance of the FMi-OOC platform’s utility in determining the fetal membrane FMi’s physiological state. After establishing the ‘healthy’ FMi-OOC platform, we then determined if OS experienced on the maternal side, due to various risk factors, or fetal side, due to intra-amniotic buildup of reactive oxygen radicals, can cause cellular dysfunction and a sterile inflammatory response. In addition, we compared how such responses differ depending on whether the maternal or fetal sides of the FMi experience OS. Data from these two disease models showed: 1) bi-directional propagation of OS across the FMi, 2) induction of cellular transition in amnion cells, 3) changes in decidual innate immune cell receptor expression, 4) an increase in cellular senescence, and 5) shifts in inflammatory status (i.e., pro-inflammatory cytokine release) that was subsequently observed in the opposite FMi compartment (i.e., Fetal → Maternal or Maternal → Fetal). Fetal OS caused more amnion-based cellular pathologies and inflammation, whereas maternal OS induced localized immune regulatory changes along with low levels of FMi cellular senescence and inflammation. The fetal-to-maternal propagation of OS and OS-induced effects are expected to facilitate a dysfunctional membrane and choriodecidua compared to the changes imposed by the propagation of pathological mediators from the maternal to the fetal side. The overwhelming pathological response observed that are mediated by the fetal side supports the hypothesis that the fetal inflammatory response is a mechanistic trigger for term or preterm labor. In summary, the FMi-OOC platform can be used to test OS and sterile inflammation-associated disease states initiated either on the fetal or maternal side; thus, highlighting its utility in the field of perinatal obstetrics.

The fetal membrane-decidua parietalis interface is one of the two FMis during pregnancy. This FMi is comprised of multiple fetal and maternal cellular-collagen layers (64–67) that have been well characterized at the tissue(22, 55, 68–77) and cellular layer(21, 27, 28, 32, 78–82). This complex tissue contains two fetal-derived epithelial membranes, the amnion and chorion, that are connected by multiple collagen layers (forming a well-organized stroma that contains mesenchymal cells(67). The highly elastic amnion membrane undergoes constant turnover and cell shedding that leads to gap formation (microfractures), which are repaired by cellular transitions (i.e., cyclic EMT and MET)(29, 30, 32, 83, 84). The chorion membrane is comprised of a tight multilayer trophoblast cells that play a key role in membrane barrier function, immune cell regulation and endocrine homeostasis (79, 80, 85). The chorion layer lines the apical side of the maternal decidua and immune component, creating the FMi (81). Decidua produces prolactin and expresses HLA-DR, as well as regulates immune cell maturation and their activation to maintain immune quiescence during pregnancy(86, 87). All of these factors contribute to membrane homeostasis, and dysfunction within one or more aspects of these functions could induce adverse pregnancy outcomes. The FMi-OOC platform presented here recreated the human FMi in vitro as it maintains fetal-maternal cell layers in the proper orientation, accounts for the different thicknesses of each layer seen in utero, and allows cell-cell or cell-collagen cross-talk during experiments. In this study, we characterized normal FMi cell homeostasis as balanced cellular transition status in the amnion, maintenance of immune regulatory receptors at the choriodecidua layers, and overall inflammatory balance and high cell viability.

Intra-amniotic OS, the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and sterile inflammation at term and preterm are well-reported phenomena(6, 10, 22, 88–91). In vitro cultures of amnion and chorion cells and ex vivo fetal membrane explant cultures have documented the following multiple biological processes and signaling pathways related to intra-amniotic oxidative imbalance-associated pathologies (i.e., ROS production, DNA fragmentation, p38MAPK activation, EMT, senescence, and inflammation) (21, 22, 25, 27, 28, 32, 51, 75, 92–98) that shift immune status to a pro-inflammatory environment. Changes in the inflammatory environment at the choriodecidua interface have been associated with the suppression of anti-inflammatory hormone (i.e., progesterone) and immune cell regulators (i.e., HLA’s) that allow for maternal immune cell infiltration into the membranes. Similar biological processes and labor-inducing signaling pathways have been documented in intra-amniotic infection and sterile inflammation animal models (i.e., mouse(52, 99, 100) and non-human primates(101, 102)) of PTB. These results highlight the pathological phenotype present in FMi cell layers during fetal OS exposure; however, it is unclear if these same findings could be recreated using an OOC platform. Here we characterize an intra-amniotic OS-associated disease state on-chip by the following cellular features of dysfunction: 1) amnion layer cellular transitions to a mesenchymal phenotype (i.e., AECs and AMCs), 2) no change to choriodecidua immune receptor expression, 3) cellular senescence, and 4) a pro-inflammatory imbalance across the FMi layers. Though not all pathology-associated endpoints were investigated on-chip, collectively, these data document OS exposure leading to cellular dysfunction (i.e., EMT and senescence) and shifts in the inflammatory environment (i.e., pro-inflammatory cytokine production) that can promote labor-associated changes across the FMi-OOC similar to those seen in other validated model systems described above. High maternal OS due to a variety of risk factors (i.e., toxicant exposure) and maternal complications (i.e., gestational diabetes, genetic predispositions) has been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes(19, 103–105). However, it is not as associated with membrane dysfunction (as characterized above), rupture, or the induction of spontaneous PTB. In this study, we documented maternal sterile inflammation-associated pathology on-chip by the subsequent cellular features of dysfunction: 1) amnion epithelium cellular transitions to a mesenchymal phenotype (i.e., AECs), 2) low levels of cellular senescence, and 3) pro-inflammatory inflammation across the FMi-OOC. Though the exact biological function of increased HLA-DR is unknown, we hypothesize that it could play a homeostatic role at the choriodecidua interface. An increase in HLA-DR ligand interaction with immune cell ligands has been shown to neutralize immune cell activation, suggesting increased HLA-DR expression in the decidua is indicative of a localized response to an altered immune status that is trying to be regulated. Further studies are needed to identify additional immune regulatory receptors that play critical roles within the maternal decidua and elucidate the exact role of HLA-DR. In summary, fetal-derived OS induces amnion-level cellular dysfunction, FMi cellular senescence, and catastrophic levels of inflammation that are clinically associated with pathologic fetal membrane weakening. In contrast, maternal-derived OS induces localized immune regulatory changes along with low levels of FMi cellular senescence and inflammation. We theorize that the steady and slow bombardment of maternal OS on the FMi layers throughout gestation does not induce a pathologic state, but instead creates an underlying persistent level of dysfunction that primes the tissue to collapse due to a second offense (i.e., infection, toxicant exposure, maternal illness). The pronounced responses from fetal stimulation within the FMi-OOC platform support the theory that a robust fetal inflammatory response is critical for the onset of labor signaling(8).

Using OOC platforms to study the FMi allows researchers to overcome multiple limitations of standard in vitro models (i.e., 2D & 3D cultures; transwells), ex vivo models (i.e., placenta perfusion & explants), and in vivo small (i.e., mouse, rat, guinea pig) and large animal models (i.e., sheep, pig, cow) due to their multi-layer multi-cellular biological functions enabled through microfluidics and microfabrication. They are also ideal for modeling and studying physiological and pathological pregnancy conditions as they contain proper cellularity (i.e., collagen & fetal-maternal cells), orientation (i.e., distinct cell culture chambers for each layer), and cell-cell/cell-collagen cross-talks (i.e., paracrine, endocrine, and integrin signaling). Further technological advances such as incorporating biosensors (i.e., electrical or biochemical sensors) and incorporating maternal vascular flow, will help to better understand the dynamic cytokine regulation between cell layers under healthy and disease states, could expand the utility of these platforms. Additionally, a limitation of this current platform is the lack of inclusion of immune cells at the chorio-decidua interfaces, which will be incorporated in future experiments and will be critical in understanding the effects of immune receptor changes due to OS. Here OS is induced by CSE, which is a potent laboratory stimulant that provides reliable, and reproducible ROS data and OS-induced changes as seen in utero. One of the limitations is that CSE contains close to 7,000 chemicals and only water-soluble fractions (~ 5,000) will be captured in our model system. Although this provides a much better model than just testing nicotine, we may still be missing the contributions of other 2,000 chemicals. This is still not a major concern as we are not testing the pathologic properties of CSE (as a risk factor of preterm birth or pPROM), but used as a laboratory reagent; however, it also contains a variety of water-soluble factors that have not been individually characterized to understand their effects on the feto-maternal interface.

In conclusion, here we have established healthy and disease states (i.e., intra-amniotic OS and systemic maternal OS) of the FMi on-chip and characterized their unique cellular phenotypes that validate these platforms. These fully characterized disease organ-on-a-chip models can now be used to identify potential therapeutic targets at the FMi (drug discovery), screen therapeutic compounds to determine safety, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy (preclinical trial platforms), and be used to identify biomarkers of fetal-associated dysfunction in the maternal circulation (clinical diagnostics). Broadly the FMi-OOC developed and presented here is poised to make an impact in the field of obstetrics and basic translational research.
