# Large phenotypic variability with severe respiratory involvement in MEGF10-related myopathies: Description of three cases.
**Authors:** Pennisi A, Caramizaru A, Severa G, Sternberg D, Dumitrescu A, Metay C, Biancalana V, Carlier RY, Quijano-Roy S, Malfatti E, Dobrescu A
**Journal:** J Neuromuscul Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1177/22143602261454427](https://doi.org/10.1177/22143602261454427)

## Content

MEGF10 biallelic variants cause early-onset myopathy, areflexia, respiratory distress and dysphagia (EMARDD), congenital myopathies with cores, and later-onset myopathies with respiratory failure. As MEGF10 plays a key role in satellite cells proliferation and differentiation, these disorders are considered primary Satellite Cell-opathies. We present clinical, genetic, and laboratory findings from three patients with MEGF10-related myopathy from two families: a 6-year-old boy with EMARDD, and two siblings with a milder myopathy with respiratory involvement. Patient 1, male, showed decreased fetal movements and profound neonatal hypotonia with respiratory failure, requiring tracheostomy soon after birth. At 6 years, he had lack of head and trunk control, scoliosis, and upper limbs antigravity movements. Muscle MRI revealed mild-to-moderate, symmetrical fibro-fatty replacement affecting scapular, paravertebral, gluteal and adductor muscles, with bilateral absence of the semimembranosus. Muscle biopsy revealed a reduction of Pax7-positive satellite cells. Genetic studies disclosed the novel homozygous MEGF10 c.337T>C, p.(Cys113Arg) variant. Patient 2 is a 24-year-old woman with myopathic facies, progressive axial and generalized weakness, and thoracolumbar scoliosis at 11 years, leading to progressive respiratory involvement necessitating tracheostomy at 14 years. Her 17-year-old brother, patient 3, experienced a threatened abortion and perinatal complications consisting of neonatal hypoxia, right arm monoplegia, and clavicle fracture, followed by motor delay, scoliosis and progressive respiratory dysfunction, requiring non-invasive ventilation at 16 years. P2 and P3 harbour the novel homozygous c.917G>C, p. (Arg306Pro) MEGF10 pathogenic variant. We describe three new patients with MEGF10-related myopathy, showing severe but variable clinical expression, associated with two novel variants.

MEGF10 (5q23.2) encodes the transmembrane protein multiple EGF-like domains 10, which is expressed in the spinal cord, brain, and skeletal muscle satellite cells. This protein plays a critical role in mediating apoptosis during cell phagocytosis by binding to the phosphatidylserine expressed on the surface of apoptotic cells; it is also involved in cell adhesion, motility, and proliferation. MEGF10 is essential for satellite cells proliferation.
1


Homozygous or compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in MEGF10 have been linked with congenital core myopathy,
2
 also termed Early-onset Myopathy, Areflexia, Respiratory Distress and Dysphagia (EMARDD, MIM 614399) by Logan et al.
3
 The main clinical features included generalized hypotonia, respiratory insufficiency due to diaphragmatic involvement, and feeding difficulties. Additional findings included reduced fetal movements, scoliosis, dysphagia, high-arched palate, distal joint contractures, foot deformities, and diaphragmatic eventration.
4
 Severely affected individuals often require ventilatory support within the first months or years of life, and in some cases, never achieve independent ambulation.
5


Satellite cells have been extensively studied due to their central role in muscle repair, leading to the identification of several markers, including the transcription factor Pax7, in quiescent satellite cells.
6

MEGF10 itself serves as a marker of quiescent satellite cells, with significant expression in skeletal myoblasts. Experimental modulation of Megf10 alters the expression of Pax7 and myogenic regulatory factors, thereby indicating a role in maintaining the balance between satellite cells proliferation and differentiation.
7
 The role of MEGF10 in the satellite cells NOTCH signaling pathway was firstly reported in 2007.
7
 To date, MEGF10-related myopathies are considered primary Satellite Cell-opathies.8,9

Here, we report three patients with MEGF10-related myopathy (MEGF10-RM), harboring two novel pathogenic variants.

Clinical findings, laboratory results, and molecular genetic data were collected from three patients with MEGF10-RM. Patient 1 (P1) was evaluated and is currently followed at the neuromuscular reference center of Raymond Poincaré Hospital in Garches, France. Patients 2 (P2) and 3 (P3) were diagnosed and assessed at the Regional Center of Medical Genetics Dolj, Craiova, Romania.

P1 is a male child born to consanguineous Algerian parents (Figure 1(a)). His mother reported reduced fetal movements during pregnancy. Delivery required vacuum-assisted extraction due to non-progressive labor, and was followed by immediate neonatal resuscitation, including stimulation, suctioning, and several minutes of ambu-bag ventilation. Apgar score was 2/6/8. Birth weight was 3,520 g. Head circumference was normal. At six hours of life, clinical examination showed profound hypotonia and absent sucking reflexes. He was admitted to a neonatal intensive care unit, where he received CPAP for six days, and nasogastric feeding for several weeks. A maternal-foetal group B Streptococcus (GBS) infection was also documented. At four months of age, the patient had not yet achieved head control and exhibited severe hypotonia, areflexia, thoracic scoliosis and pectus excavatum. No craniofacial dysmorphism was observed, except for a high-arched palate. Electromyographic examination revealed a myopathic pattern.

The respiratory course was severe, with high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) required by 2 months due to complete unilateral lung atelectasis, which recurred at 4 months in the context of an RSV (respiratory syncytial virus) infection. This episode was complicated by respiratory arrest and a right-sided pneumothorax, necessitating endotracheal intubation. Following several unsuccessful extubation attempts, the patient was transferred to an ICU. Computed tomography confirmed a complete diaphragmatic paralysis. Extubation was achieved at 6 months of age; however, the patient remained dependent on continuous non-invasive nasal ventilation (24h/day), and subsequently underwent tracheostomy and gastrostomy at nine months of age.

By the age of six years, the patient remained fully dependent on assisted ventilation via tracheostomy, and required a Garches brace due to severe cervico-axial hypotonia. Head and trunk control were absent. Bulbar dysfunction was profound, with complete reliance on gastrostomy for nutrition. He did not develop oral language, and used pictograms to communicate. In contrast to the severe axial weakness, the patient was able to move his arms against gravity.

P2 is a young female born to consanguineous Romanian (Figure 1(b)) parents after an uneventful pregnancy. Family history revealed the presence of two paternal first cousins with scoliosis and pectus carinatum, respectively.

She had a normal psychomotor development, with thoraco-lumbar scoliosis appearing at 11 years of age and contributing to a progressive impairment of the respiratory function. Tracheostomy was performed at 14 years of age (Table 3). Two muscle biopsies were obtained from patient 1, at 6 months and 15 months of age, respectively. Both showed unspecific myopathic findings including important fiber size variability and moderate lipid droplets content augmentation in type 1 fibers. Of note, there was a mild increase of endomysial connective tissue. No muscle biopsies were available for P2 and P3.

P1 karyotype, Prader-Willi and myotonic dystrophy type 1 testing were negative. Whole genome sequencing disclosed a homozygous MEGF10 missense variant of uncertain significance, NM_001256545.2:c.337T>C, p. (Cys113Arg), in exon 6 (Table 1). This novel variant is absent from gnomAD (v4.1.0); it has a high CADD phred score (29.70), and it is consistently predicted as damaging/pathogenic by multiple algorithms (SIFT, PolyPhen-2, AlphaMissense, REVEL, ClinPred).
17
 P1 also harbors a COL6A2 (NM_001849.4:c.1970-9G>A) intronic variant previously reported in patients carrying biallelic pathogenic COL6A2 variants,
18
 not in keeping with the patient’s phenotype. Genetic analyses of P2 and P3 disclosed the presence of the novel NM_001256545.2:c.917G>C, p. (Arg306Pro), homozygous MEGF10 variant. This variant has a very low frequency in gnomAD v4.1.0 (allele count 2, no homozygotes), and a high CADD phred score (35.00); spliceAI predicts a donor site loss, and multiple missense predictors classify it as damaging/pathogenic.
16

MEGF10-related conditions are clinically heterogeneous in terms of severity and onset, with adulthood presenting cases also reported.13,14 For this reason, we suggest the term of MEGF10-RM.

Our patients showed features similar to previously reported MEGF10-RM patients,1,3,12,19 with P1 exhibiting a particularly severe phenotype. Similar phenotypes have been described, including prenatal onsets with reduced fetal movements or cases leading to death in the first year of life.
10
 P1 received antibiotics treatment for a maternal–foetal group B Streptococcus (GBS) infection in the immediate neonatal period; despite clinical improvement of intercurrent issues, profound hypotonia and ventilatory dependence persisted. Therefore, a transient contribution to the initial respiratory distress cannot be entirely excluded. Nevertheless, given the lifelong course and the later demonstration of complete diaphragmatic paralysis, we consider GBS an intercurrent perinatal factor rather than a causal element.

At 5 months, the weakness pattern in P1 was relatively atypical compared to other congenital myopathies,
20
 with better proximal strength (quadriceps and deltoids quoting 4/5 MRC) and distal hands and feet muscular weakness. In regard to the thin corpus callosum, central nervous system structural anomalies are not a recognized feature of MEGF10-RM, which primarily affects satellite cells and skeletal muscles. In the absence of other consistent neurological signs, we interpret this finding as incidental. Nonetheless, because MEGF10 is also expressed in the brain, we cannot entirely exclude the correlation of this finding with MEGF10; longitudinal neurodevelopmental follow-up is warranted.

Patients 2 and 3, siblings, had similar evolutions, with P3 also exhibiting a neurodevelopmental delay. Both developed severe scoliosis and respiratory impairment: P2 required tracheostomy, whereas P3 underwent spinal fusion and uses non-invasive ventilation. These differences likely reflect their ages at diagnosis and the limited care access during their early history.

P1 muscle biopsies showed non-specific changes, with a mild connective tissue increase consistent with earlier reports. Previous studies have described histological alterations including fatty tissue replacement, increased connective tissue, regenerating fibers, fiber-size variation, moth-eaten fibers and minicores.1,10 In some cases, an initially non-specific biopsy was followed by a later biopsy revealing myofibrillar myopathy patterns with cores, inclusions, and whorled fibers.
12
 In patient 1, we did not observe important differences between the two muscle biopsies. Very importantly, we demonstrated a reduction in Pax7 positive satellite cells in P1 muscle (Figure 5), as well as an important activation of these cells, suggesting a dysregulation of myogenesis and supporting the concept of MEGF10-RM as a satellite cell-opathy.
8
 Our satellite cells findings are in-keeping with other data from the literature,
3
 although reports of normal Pax7 expression in MEGF10-RM patients exist.
7


MEGF10-RM muscle MRI data remain scarce in the literature. In one early-onset case with a homozygous exon 7 deletion, a marked involvement of gluteal and quadriceps muscles was observed, with near-complete fatty replacement, while hamstring and gracilis muscles were relatively spared,
5
 similarly to the pattern of involvement found in P1 (Figure 4). The bilateral absence of the SM muscles is a particular aspect, resembling another Primary Satellite Cell-opathy, Rigid Spine Muscular Dystrophy 1, associated with SELENON variants, where a severe atrophy of SM muscles is reported.
21


Importantly, differential diagnosis includes other Primary Satellite Cell-opathies, as well as spinal muscular atrophy with respiratory distress (SMARD1), which may mimic MEGF10-RM through early diaphragmatic weakness and respiratory failure.4,22

The homozygous MEGF10 c.337T>C, p. (Cys113Arg), missense variant found in P1 affects a highly conserved cysteine within an EGF-like domain, where cysteine residues form disulfide bridges. Cysteine substitutions in EGF-like domains are among the most common MEGF10 variants,1,3,11–14 and are thought to interfere with the tyrosine phosphorylation activity.
23
 A pathogenic variant involving a neighboring cysteine, (p.Cys118Arg), has been reported in a patient with milder, adult-onset disease.
13
 The variant has a heterozygous status in each parent, further supporting the pathogenic role of p. Cys113Arg.

The homozygous MEGF10 missense variant identified in patients 2 and 3, NM_001256545.2:c.917G>C, p. (Arg306Pro), although classified as VUS in some platforms (Franklin, InterVar), is considered likely pathogenic in others (VarSome, GeneBe). Its homozygous status in both affected siblings strongly supports a pathogenic effect.
24


Genotype–phenotype correlations remain evolving. Previous studies suggest that missense variants are often associated with a later onset, while null variants usually cause early respiratory dysfunction.13,25 However, early disease onset has also been observed in patients with biallelic missense variants, as well as in P1 (Table 4, MEGF10 cases summary). P1 also carried a heterozygous intronic variant in COL6A2 (NM_001849.4:c.1970-9G>A), previously associated with recessive collagen VI-related muscular dystrophies.15,18,26 No second variant was identified (either SNV or CNV), and the phenotype was not consistent with a collagen VI myopathy. Interestingly, collagen secretion studies in fibroblasts revealed intracellular retention and a reduced collagen VI network compared to healthy controls (Supplementary Figure 1). Although these findings likely reflect an incidental carrier status, we do not entirely exclude the possibility of an interaction between MEGF10 and extracellular matrix or COL6 secretion. COL6A2 is part of a group of genes linked with Secondary Satellite Cell-opathies, with biallelic pathogenic variants affecting both satellite cells and muscle fibres.
8
 However, further evidence would be necessary to determine whether an interaction between MEGF10 and COL6A2 could influence the phenotype.

In our case series, axial involvement led to severe scoliosis with secondary thoracic deformity, further aggravating respiratory function. This disproportionate respiratory impairment, requiring tracheostomy or non-invasive ventilation while patients are still ambulant (Figure 2(a)–(c), patient 2), is a relatively distinctive feature of MEGF10-RM. Few other myopathies show a similar pattern, such as SELENON-related congenital myopathies with dorsal hyperlordosis and trunk shift,
27
 or late-onset Pompe disease, where severe respiratory dysfunction can occur in ambulant patients, usually due to primary diaphragmatic involvement rather than thoracic deformities.
28


MEGF10-RM exhibits a broad phenotypic spectrum. Although many cases follow a moderate course, some individuals, such as patient 1, present with severe forms with prenatal onset. Here we enlarge the genetic spectrum reporting two novel pathogenic variants.

Early and tailored management of complications, including progressive scoliosis requiring surgical intervention, may help reduce the respiratory burden and improve quality of life. Further studies are needed to clarify MEGF10-RM natural history and to identify prognostic markers.
