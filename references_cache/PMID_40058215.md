INTRODUCTION AND IMPORTANCE: Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) hamartomas 
comprise a spectrum of disorders that involve multiple systems and originate 
from a group of allelic disorders from germ line mutations in the PTEN gene. 
PTEN hamartomas involve a spectrum of disorders with diversed clinical 
manifestations and diagnosis can be challenging, particularly when lesions mimic 
other conditions.
CASE PRESENTATION: We present a case of a PTEN hamartoma in an eighteen-year-old 
male, who presented with a history of swelling on the chin with episodic bleeds. 
Initial diagnosis of an arteriovenous malformation was made radiologically but 
was later confirmed by histopathological and immunohistochemistry to be a case 
of PTEN hamartoma.
DISCUSSION: PTEN is a tumor suppressor gene, and patients with germline PTEN 
mutations are more likely to develop malignancies, particularly epithelial, 
mesenchymal, and hematopoietic cancers. PTEN hamartomas cause a variety of 
conditions, including Cowden syndrome, Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome, 
PTEN-related Proteus syndrome, and Proteus-like syndrome. As a result, cancer 
surveillance is critical in managing PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome (PTHS) 
patients. All PTEN mutation carriers should adhere to approved cancer 
surveillance measures.
CONCLUSION: This case highlights the unusual presentation of PTHS. It can 
present as an isolated PTEN hamartoma of Soft Tissue (PHOST) lesion without 
systemic findings and establishing a genetic diagnosis is important to the 
patient's future health. A multidisciplinary approach with the clinical, 
radiologic and pathologic findings of PTHS and PHOST lesions will more rapidly 
lead to an accurate diagnosis. Prompt diagnosis and appropriate treatment are 
important to prevent potentially severe outcomes.

Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) hamartomas comprise a spectrum of disorders that involve multiple systems and originate from a group of allelic disorders from germ line mutations in the phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) gene on chromosome 10q23. [1] The gene inhibits the PI3K-AKT/mTOR pathway, which regulates tumor progression, vascular growth, and cell proliferation. When this tumor suppressor gene is inactivated or altered, it leads to tumor growth due to increased pathway activity, which prevents apoptosis and causes uncontrolled cell proliferation and angiogenesis. [2] Although it's a tumor suppressor gene, it behaves differently from classical ones. Classical tumor suppressors follow Knudson's “two-hit” hypothesis, where a significant reduction in function triggers tumor progression. However, this gene's impact varies by tissue and might require less reduction for tumorogenesis. [1]

Herein, we present a case of PTEN hamartoma in an 18-year-old male with chin swelling and episodic bleeding. Initially diagnosed as an arteriovenous malformation via ultrasound, it was later confirmed as PTEN hamartoma through histopathology and immunohistochemistry. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria. [16]

An 18-year-old male sought consultation for a 3.5 × 4 cm swelling on the left side of his chin, with occasional arterial bleeding. He had no prior treatment or family history of similar issues but reported a trauma to the area 10 years ago. The postoperative period was uneventful, and he could perform lower lip eversion.

A physical examination revealed an irregular swelling on the mental region extending from labiomental crease to 1.5 cm below the mentum, measuring 3.5 × 4 cm in size with an irregular shape with a few nodules on the surface. The skin on the surface of the swelling appeared with a reddish discolouration, glossy in appearance with no ulceration or discharge noticed but periodic bleeding patchy hair growth on the swelling. The swelling was tender and warm on touch, firm in consistency, pulsatile and was compressible on pressure; and regained size upon removal of pressure.

Colour Doppler study reveals high flow nature of the swelling with multiple feeding arteries visualised as shown in Fig. 1. An MRI with contrast revealed multiple abnormal vascular channels clustered in the subcutaneous tissue of the mentum, predominantly on the left side. The lesion measured 2.8 × 3.6 × 4 cm and extended into deeper muscular planes, involving the mentalis, depressor labii inferiors, and digastric muscles. It was also noted to abut the anterior cortex of the mandible without causing cortical erosion. Time Resolved imaging of Contrast Kinetics Angiography (or TRICKS angiography) and a Time-of-Flight angiogram of the face revealed a dilated left facial artery with feeders to the lesion. An early opacification of draining veins into the left internal jugular vein indicated high flow. During surgery, the lesion was firm, lacked a capsule, but was separable from surrounding structures. The muscular layer and subcutaneous fat were resected. The tissue was congested with a large draining vessel and fibrous background. The remaining mentalis muscle was sutured to the periosteum, and the wound was closed in layers, maintaining lower lip symmetry and horizontal alignment. The postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient was able to evert the lower lip.

A singular skin covered soft to firm in consistency soft tissue specimen measuring 4.5x3x2.5 cm with foci of haemorrhage was received.

PTEN typically acts as a tumor suppressor gene, and its loss or inactivation is common in many sporadic malignancies. Patients with germline PTEN mutations are at higher risk for various malignancies, including epithelial, mesenchymal, and hematopoietic. [3] PTEN hamartomas involve a spectrum of disorders such as Cowden syndrome, Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome and PTEN-related Proteus syndrome and Proteus-like syndrome [2,4].

Numerous case reports and series have described intramuscular or soft tissue vascular lesions resembling PHOST. Some of these lesions, previously identified as intramuscular hemangioma, complex vascular malformation, “infiltrating angiolipoma” of muscle and soft tissue, and angiomatosis, may actually be instances of PHOST. [[5], [6], [7], [8]]

PHOST is an overgrowth of mingled soft tissue elements, primarily adipose, dense and myxoid fibrous tissues and abnormal blood vessels of various types. While one or more of these elements are found in other vascular anomalies, it is their combination and unusual morphology that makes PHOST distinct.

The histopathological complexity of PHOST is reflected by the various diagnosis rendered at other institutions of cases submitted for consultation, differential diagnosis of which included arteriovenous, venous, and other vascular anomalies. [9]

In arteriovenous malformations, abnormal arteries, veins, and mixed vessels are less segregated, with arteries being less coiled or narrowed, and muscular hyperplasia confined mainly to the media. Unlike PHOST, nodular venous clusters are absent. Extensive limb involvement in PHOST may suggest Parkes Weber syndrome.

In this rapid-flow disorder, there is congenital skeletal and muscular overgrowth in an entire limb, along with diffuse pink skin patches. Imaging and tissue analysis reveal a widespread arteriovenous malformation. [10] In venous malformation, the most common intramuscular vascular anomaly, abnormal thin- and thick-walled veins, sometimes with a nodular distribution, are present; however, abnormal arteries are absent. [11] Some venous malformations, so-called ossified intramuscular hemangioma, have abundant bone formation, but they lack other characteristics of PHOST. [12] Adipocytic and fibrous components in arteriovenous and venous malformations are usually minor compared to PHOST. Allen and Enzinger's three types of intramuscular “hemangiomas” should be considered in the differential diagnosis of PHOST. The “small-vessel” type, with its characteristic lobules of small capillary-like vessels, does not appear in PHOST. We believe the “large-vessel” and “mixed” types are venous malformations, though some could be PHOST. [5]

Patients with PHTS have an elevated risk of developing cancer, particularly breast and endometrial cancer in women, with lifetime risks of 67–85 % and 19–28 %, respectively. Additionally, both men and women face an increased lifetime risk of epithelial thyroid and renal cell cancers, ranging from 6 to 38 % and 2–24 %, respectively. [13,14]

Historically, germline PTEN hamartoma syndrome is not to be considered in the diagnosis of isolated soft tissue vascular PTEN hamartoma cases without additional physical examination and clinical history of other anomalies. We suggest genetic testing for PTHS and PTEN genes in patients presenting with isolated soft tissue vascular anomalies. Although PTHS can manifest as an isolated PHOST lesion without systemic features, genetic diagnosis is essential for guiding future healthcare decisions. A multidisciplinary team experienced with PTHS and PHOST can expedite diagnosis, leading to better treatment and cancer screening. [9,15]

In conclusion, our study suggests that all patients who present with fast-flow vascular anomalies or who have multiple DVAs on brain MRI should have their head circumference measured, and male patients should also be examined for penile freckling. Those who are macrocephalic or have penile freckling should be considered candidates for PTEN testing. MRI should be used to evaluate symptomatic mass lesions. Typical features include multiple fast-flow vascular anomalies, ectopic fat disrupting muscle architecture, and disproportionate dilation of draining veins. These findings may suggest PTEN testing. Prompt identification of PTEN mutations is essential for individuals at increased risk of developing thyroid, breast, and endometrial cancers, particularly in young patients. Early detection enables proactive surveillance, facilitating the timely diagnosis of potential malignancies. This is especially crucial for young individuals who may be asymptomatic carriers.
