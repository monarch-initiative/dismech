# Novel Fetal Phenotype for Pan-Chung-Bellen Syndrome Including Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia.
**Authors:** Sandhu N, Osman N, Boe N, Hedriana H, Singh K
**Journal:** Prenat Diagn (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/pd.6879](https://doi.org/10.1002/pd.6879)

## Content

What is already known about this topic? ◦. Pan‐Chung‐Bellen syndrome is a newly described pediatric syndrome with known features including developmental delay and intellectual disability, dysmorphic features, and central nervous system, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and skeletal anomalies. What does this study add? ◦. We describe a fetus with Pan‐Chung‐Bellen presenting with multiple anomalies including congenital diaphragmatic hernia, which has not been previously described and may be important for inclusion into the phenotype of this disorder.

We report on a fetus with multiple congenital anomalies on prenatal ultrasound whose exome sequencing with amniocentesis revealed a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the FRYL gene. Ultrasound findings were consistent with prior phenotypic descriptions; however, the fetus also had congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH). This case highlights the importance of comprehensive fetal genetic testing, literature analysis, and prenatal ultrasound for prognostic prenatal counseling.

A 29‐year‐old gravida 3 para 1 presented at 28 weeks' gestation for second‐opinion fetal ultrasound, which identified severe fetal growth restriction and severe polyhydramnios, left‐sided CDH with cardiac dextroposition (Figure 1), mesomelia of the left upper extremity with fixed left wrist, suspected right pelvic kidney, and possible hemivertebrae. The observed/expected lung to head ratio (LHR) was 30%, predicting moderate pulmonary dysplasia. Fetal echocardiogram was significant for hypoplasia of the aortic arch.

(a) Transverse view of fetal chest on ultrasound at 28 weeks' gestation with left‐sided CDH. Fetal stomach is seen adjacent to 4 chamber view of the fetal heart with cardiac dextroposition. The right lung area was 2.2 square centimeters with an LHR of 0.83 and observed‐to‐expected LHR of 30%. (b) Supine x‐ray of neonate with left rib dysplasia, multiple segmentation abnormalities of vertebrae, and hypoplastic left radius with absent left thumb.

Amniocentesis was performed at 28 weeks and 1 day and resulted in a male with an incidental heterozygous pathogenic deletion of 17q21.31 including the BRCA1 (Breast Cancer 1) gene on chromosomal microarray. Exome sequencing identified a heterozygous pathogenic frame‐shift variant in the FRYL (Fry‐like transcription coactivator) gene [variant c.4139del p. (L1380Rfs*21)] consistent with autosomal dominant Pan‐Chung‐Bellen syndrome, previously known as FRYL‐related neurodevelopmental disorder with multiple anomalies (Table 1).

At 35 weeks' gestation, the patient presented with ruptured membranes and required urgent cesarean delivery for non‐reassuring fetal status. The infant weighed 1760 g and was intubated and transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit.

Physical examination of the newborn was notable for left enophthalmia, retrognathia, left upper extremity mesomelia with radial deviation of the left hand and absent left thumb, scoliosis, and hypospadias with cryptorchidism. Postnatal echocardiogram showed cardiac dextroposition, aortic arch hypoplasia, unicuspid aortic valve, and large patent ductus arteriosus. X‐rays noted left CDH, dysplastic left ribs, multiple segmentation abnormalities in the vertebrae, hypoplastic left radius, and absent left thumb (Figure 1b). On abdominal ultrasound, the left CDH was seen involving the stomach and bowel and horseshoe kidney was identified (Table 2).

The infant was not deemed in ideal condition for surgical repair of CDH at less than 2 kg, and there was concern that despite repair, pulmonary function would remain significantly impacted by dysplastic ribs. Repeat echocardiogram showed left atrial thrombosis requiring anticoagulation. Ultimately, on day 4 of life, the family desired a transition to comfort care, and the infant passed away. The couple had genetic counseling for the incidentally identified BRCA1 gene deletion, and parental testing for the deletion was recommended given the potential health implications; however, they declined.

Only one study by Pan et al. [1] consisting of 14 cases was identified in the literature delineating the phenotype associated with pathogenic variants in the FRYL gene, with features including developmental delay, intellectual disability, dysmorphic features, and central nervous system, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and skeletal anomalies. Previously reported skeletal anomalies included one non‐fusion of the sternum and one pectus deformity, but not vertebral/rib anomalies, or limb contracture. The patients had a mean age of 11.3 years and all were living except for one with neonatal death. None had CDH or a pattern of laterality, as seen in our case (left‐sided CDH, left arm mesomelia, and left enophthalmia). The specific pathogenic variant identified in our case was novel. The infant had prenatal and postnatal findings including cardiovascular and genitourinary anomalies which fit with the phenotype of Pan‐Chung‐Bellen Syndrome. CDH, however, was unique to this case, not having been previously reported in association with variants in the FRYL gene.

The FRYL gene is thought to play a role in neuronal development and gene regulation. It has not been previously associated with CDH and is not included in gene panels related to CDH. This case raises the possibility that CDH may be part of the broader phenotypic spectrum of Pan‐Chung‐Bellen syndrome. We propose that genetic evaluation for CDH should include exome sequencing or addition of the FRYL gene to CDH gene panels. The evaluation of a fetus with CDH should include a skeletal survey given the association with both CDH and Pan‐Chung‐Bellen syndrome.
