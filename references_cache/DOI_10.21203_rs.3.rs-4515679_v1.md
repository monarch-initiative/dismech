# The cochlear morphology alteration and hearing loss in Cep250 knockout mice
**Authors:** Benyu Nan, Xi Gu, Xinlei Wu, Keyang Chen, Chuqin Zhang, Qijun Fan, Yingying Chen, Bobei Chen, Xiufeng Huang
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-4515679/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-4515679/v1)

## Content

Abstract
Background: Usher syndrome is a genetic disorder characterized by sensorineural hearing loss, progressive vision loss, and in some cases, vestibular dysfunction. It is the most common cause of combined deafness and blindness. Cep250 is a candidate gene for atypical Usher syndrome. This study explores inner ear morphological and auditory functional changes in atypical Usher syndrome using a Cep250−/− mouse model.
Methods: We constructed the Cep250−/− mice using the CRISPR/Cas9 technology, and analyzed scRNA-seq data derived from studying Cep250 expression in the cochlea of normal mice at different stages. Auditory brainstem responses (ABRs) were applied to wild-type, heterozygous and homozygous Cep250−/− mice at about P30 to P60 to assess the general function of the inner ear. The swimming test was used to examine the vestibular function of the inner ear. Immunofluorescent staining was applied to observe hair cell morphology and count hair cell numbers.
Results: We demonstrate that Cep250−/− mice exhibit impaired hearing function, particularly in high-frequency ranges, whereas their vestibular function remains unaffected. Immunofluorescence staining reveals a significant reduction in the number of cochlear hair cells in Cep250−/− mice, confirming the association between Cep250 gene mutation and hearing function loss. Heterozygous mice show no significant changes in hearing, indicating that a single allele mutation in Cep250 is insufficient to affect normal Cep250 expression levels.
Conclusion: Our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of atypical Usher syndrome and may guide future research and therapeutic strategies for this condition.

Usher syndrome (USH) is clinically characterized by congenital sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), progressive vision loss, with or without vestibular dysfunction. By the age of 65, approximately 50% of USH patients will experience both blindness and deafness. It is an autosomal recessive inherited form of SNHL, with an incidence ranging from 1/6000 to 1/25000[1,2].

USH is classified into typical and atypical forms based on the severity and onset of visual, auditory, and vestibular dysfunction. Typical USH can be further divided into three subtypes based on the age of onset and severity of visual and auditory symptoms: USH1 (USH1) is the most severe subtype, with the onset of retinitis pigmentosa (RP) and profound congenital SNHL occurring before the age of 10. It often presents with congenital vestibular dysfunction. USH2 (USH2) typically manifests with RP and moderate to severe congenital SNHL starting around the age of 20. Vestibular dysfunction is usually absent. USH3 (USH3) is characterized by late-onset RP and progressive SNHL, which may be accompanied by vestibular dysfunction. Typical USH follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. However, there is an increasing number of reported cases of USH that do not fit into any of the classical syndromes and are termed atypical USH[3,4]. Atypical USH lacks a clear definition and shows significant overlap with other conditions such as multiple neuropathies, hearing loss, ataxia, retinitis pigmentosa, and early-onset cataracts. It is described as a phenotype resembling USH but not fully matching the USH1, USH2, or USH3 phenotypes, exhibiting high variability, including the absence of congenital SNHL, instability in the pathology of cone and rod cells, and the presence of macular diseases[3,5,6].

Currently, research on the clinical phenotype of atypical USH is progressing slowly, with only sporadic case reports. Some relevant genes, including CEP78, CEP250, ARSG, and ABHD12, have been reported, but in-depth mechanistic studies are scarce[7–10]. Furthermore, some other pathogenic genes remain unidentified, significantly increasing the difficulty of clinical diagnosis and treatment. Given that the treatment outcomes for different types of SNHL are not ideal, understanding the molecular pathogenesis and mechanisms of atypical USH can provide accurate genetic counseling and effective interventions for patients and their families. This project aims to elucidate the morphological and functional changes in the inner ear auditory and balance organs of Cep250 knockout mice.

Cep250 knockout mice were created on a C57BL/6J background using the CRISPR/Cas9 strategy in our prior research[11]. The mouse Cep250 gene (ENSMUSG00000038241) resides on chromosome 2. Guide RNA sequences targeting intron 2 and intron 12 were employed. The guide RNA sequences used for targeting Cep250 were: GAGTTTCTGTCTGCGGGAGT, CCAGGTAAAGCCAGAAGGGG, GTCACTGTGGGGAGCGCTCA, and GCTTGAACTCTAGCTTCGTG. Ultimately, a deletion spanning 8593 bp from exon 3 to exon 12 was observed in the Cep250 knockout mouse allele. Founders were genotyped via DNA sequencing and T7E1 analysis.

The ABR was measured in anesthetized mice, positioned on a heated pad within a soundproof booth. Three needle electrodes were inserted, with one at the vertex, one over the mastoid of the target ear, and one at the base of the tail. Signals were collected using a low-impedance Medusa digital biological amplifier system (RA4L; TDT; 20-dB gain). Stimulus intensity ranged from 90 to 0 dB in 5 dB steps to identify threshold decibel SPL above the noise floor. A visible and repeatable signal wave was recorded in two averaged runs. ABR waveforms were obtained from responses to 500 tone bursts at each test frequency, amplified 10,000 times with band-pass filters set between 100 Hz and 3 kHz. Wave 1 amplitude was measured from peak to trough. Light tapping on the lower limbs was used to facilitate recovery from anesthesia, and animals were not manipulated until they regained voluntary movement.

A swimming test was performed to detect the blance function of the mice. A container was placed in a quiet, well-lit, and temperature-controlled room to minimize stress and external disturbances. The mouse was allowed to acclimate to the testing environment for at least 10–15 minutes before starting the experiment, which helps reduce stress and ensures more reliable results. Then mice were placed into the container filled with water to a depth of at least 15 centimeters at a temperature of 24–26°C for observation for up to 1 minute to evaluate their swimming ability. After the test, a heated pad (set at 37°C) was placed inside the cage for drying the mice after the swim test. Keep a close eye on the mouse throughout the test to ensure its safety and well-being.If the mouse shows signs of distress, exhaustion, or inability to swim, remove it from the water immediately and provide appropriate care and support. Scoring was done based on assessment criteria.

The adult mice cochleae were dissected from the temporal bones and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 37°C for 60 minutes. After three rinses with PBS, they underwent a 3-day decalcification in 10% EDTA. The entire cochlear explant was divided into three equal parts representing the basal, middle, and apical turns. The primary antibody used was anti-MYOSIN VIIA (1:500 dilution; Proteus Biosciences). Following three PBS washes to remove unbound primary antibodies, samples were incubated for 1 hour at 37°C with the secondary antibody, donkey anti-rabbit 488 (1:500 dilution, Invitrogen). The samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 555 phalloidin (1:1,000 dilution;Thermo Fisher) for 30 minutes in dark for fluorescent visualization of hair cell F-actin. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:800 dilution, Sigma Aldrich) at 37°C for 10 minutes. Finally, the samples were mounted and imaged using a Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Biberach, Germany).

We reported a nonsense CEP250 mutation (c.562C > T, p.R188*) in a consanguineous family with nonsyndromic retinitis pigmentosa[12]. The proband aged 28 yeas old was diagnosed as retinitis pigmentosa according the Optical coherence tomography (OCT) and Electroretinogram (ERG) results. The patient did not report any systemic abnormalities and his family members were unaffected. We have successfully generated transgenic mice with Cep250 knockout. Through assessment of visual acuity, auditory function, and vestibular function in Cep250 knockout mice, we observed alterations in retinal morphology and hair cell counts of the cochlea. We did not detect vestibular function in the mice, nor did we observe abnormalities in skin hair quantity, color, or limb morphology. Based on our observations, the phenotype of Cep250 knockout mice resembles atypical Usher syndrome.

The expression of Cep250 in different age groups and different regions of the inner ear.

Based on the gEAR data, we found that Cep250 is almost not expressed in the inner and outer hair cells of the auditory epithelium region in P8 mice (Fig.1A). At P20, inner hair cells begin to express Cep250 in trace amounts (Fig.1B), with a noticeable increase in expression levels of Cep250 in both inner and outer hair cells of the auditory epithelium region at P25-30 mice (Fig.1C). Cep250 is almost not expressed in the vestibular epithelium region's utricular hair cells of P1,12,100 mice (Fig.2A).

Hearing loss deteriorates in Cep250 knockout mice.

We sought to determine whether loss of cep250 function would lead to hearing loss in Cep250 KO mice. Auditory function in mice was characterized by measuring auditory brainstem response (ABR), which are electrical signals evoked from the brainstem during presentation of an acoustic signal. Figure3shows the ABR thresholds of homozygous KO (Cep250−/−), heterozygous (Cep250 +/−), and wild-type (WT) (Cep250 +/+) mice at 1–2 months of age. ABR thresholds of KO mice were significantly elevated at 20k and 30k frequencies tested compared with that of wild-type and heterozygous mice (Fig.3A). Notably, the ABR thresholds of heterozygous mice remained comparable to that of wild-type mice at 1–2 months of age., indicating that a single functional allele provides sufficient protein for HC function.

We conducted a swimming test on neonatal Cep250 knockout (Cep250−/−) and wild-type (WT) mice to assess their vestibular function. The results indicate that there is no statistically significant difference in vestibular function between Cep250−/−mice in the HO (homozygous) and WT groups (ns, p > 0.05, Fig.3B).

We extracted cochlear tissues from Cep250 knockout (Cep250−/−) and wild-type (WT) mice for immunofluorescence staining of the inner ear. The observation focused on changes in the number and morphology of hair cells. The results revealed a significantly lower quantity of basal turn hair cells in Cep250−/−cochlear tissues compared to WT tissues, with statistical significance (****p < 0.0001). However, the morphology and quantity of hair cells in the middle and apical turns were similar to those in WT tissues (Fig.4).

Analysis of Cep250 expression in the auditory and vestibular epithelia hair cells using the gEAR database showed minimal expression in the vestibular hair cells at P12 and P100. Additionally, Corti's organ auditory epithelium displayed no Cep250 expression at P12 and P20, but started expressing hair cells at P25 and P30. This finding may explain our observation: Cep250−/−mice exhibit hearing loss, while vestibular function remains unaffected.

Our previous researches proved that Cep250−/−mice experience a late-stage retinal degeneration attributed to ciliopathy disorders.[11,13]This study aims to utilize the Cep250−/−mouse model to explore the morphological and auditory functional changes in atypical Usher syndrome. Given the incomplete understanding of the clinical features and molecular mechanisms of atypical Usher syndrome, this research fills a gap in the field and contributes to a deeper understanding of the disease's pathogenesis.

By utilizing CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knock out the Cep250 gene, we simulated the genetic mutation scenario of atypical Usher syndrome in a mouse model. Auditory brainstem response (ABR) threshold measurements and vestibular function tests were conducted to investigate the hearing and balance function of the mice. Additionally, immunofluorescence staining techniques were employed to observe changes in the morphology and quantity of inner ear hair cells. The research findings indicate that the Cep250−/−mouse model exhibited impaired hearing function, while vestibular function remained unaffected. This aligns with the clinical presentation of atypical Usher syndrome, which is characterized by varying degrees of hearing loss without necessarily accompanying balance impairments. This could be attributed to the different expression of Cep250 in auditory and vestibular hair cells, with Cep250 gradually increasing in auditory hair cells from birth to adulthood, while almost absent in utricular hair cells. The expression of three typical USH genes is high in vestibular hair cells and the epithelial cilia of the ampullae (Fig.5–6). Furthermore, Cep250 is not expressed in the vestibular maculae and ampullae of the semicircular canals, which may help explain why Cep250−/−mice do not exhibit vestibular dysfunction.

Immunofluorescence staining results revealed a significant reduction in the number of hair cells in the cochlear basal turn of Cep250−/−mice, further confirming the association between Cep250 gene mutation and hearing function loss. Our research findings indicate that Cep250−/−mice exhibit no significant hearing loss in low-frequency hearing but experience a decline in high-frequency. Although Cep250 is a gene associated with ciliopathies, there is no significant changes were observed in the number of cilia in the hair cells of the cochlear (Fig.4C). Further exploration into the role of Cep250 protein expression in the survival of hair cells is warranted. Previous studies have suggested that dysregulation of arginine metabolism may exacerbate retinal degenerative diseases in Cep250−/−mice[11]. We speculate that there may be reciprocal regulation between Cep250 and arginine in the hair cells. These questions warrant further investigation and validation through RNA-seq sequencing in the next stage of our research.

Our study found that heterozygous mice did not exhibit significant changes in hearing compared to wild-type mice, indicating that a single allele mutation in the Cep250 is insufficient to affect normal levels of Cep250 expression in cells. This is consistent with our previous finding of no visual acuity changes in heterozygous mice. Samer Khateb et al. found that in Iranian Jewish patients, homozygous mutations in Cep250 combined with homozygous mutations in C2orf71 result in severe sensorineural hearing loss and early-onset severe retinal pigment degeneration, while homozygous mutations for Cep250 combined with heterozygous mutations for C2orf71 result in early-onset severe sensorineural hearing loss and mild retinal pigment degeneration[9]. Heterozygous mutations for Cep250 combined with homozygous mutations for C2orf71 only cause age-related sensorineural hearing loss[9]. Daiki Kubota et al. found a case in a Japanese family where heterozygous for Cep250 mutations induced mild hearing loss and hereditary cone-rod dystrophy[14]. This evidence suggests that homozygous for Cep250 mutations induce hearing loss and retinal degeneration, and mutations for C2orf71 are also important pathogenic genes for atypical Usher syndrome. However, whether mutations for C2orf71 exacerbate hearing loss in Cep250 mutant mice remains unknown.

In conclusion, our study utilizing the Cep250−/−mouse model sheds light on the morphological and auditory functional changes associated with atypical Usher syndrome. We demonstrated that Cep250−/−mice exhibit impaired hearing function, particularly in high-frequency ranges, while vestibular function remains unaffected. This mirrors the clinical presentation of atypical Usher syndrome, characterized by varying degrees of hearing loss without concurrent balance impairments.
