# Trichothiodystrophy-causative pathogenic variants impair a cooperative action of TFIIH and DDX1 in R-loop processing.
**Authors:** Ferri D, Branca G, Lanzafame M, Gandolfi E, Riva V, Maga G, Nardo T, Landi C, Bini L, Arseni L, Peverali FA, Compe E, Orioli D
**Journal:** Nucleic Acids Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1093/nar/gkaf745](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkaf745)

## Content

The transcription factor IIH (TFIIH) is a key player in transcription and DNA repair by nucleotide excision repair. It is made of 10 subunits organized in core-TFIIH and CAK sub-complexes bridged by XPD. Pathogenic variants in the ERCC2/XPD gene give rise to xeroderma pigmentosum (XP) or trichothiodystrophy (TTD), two distinct clinical entities with opposite skin cancer proneness. Here, we show that TTD variants cause a partial dissociation of the CAK from the chromatin and from the core-TFIIH. Mass spectrometry analysis reveals that the chromatin-bound CAK, as a component of the entire TFIIH, participates in a protein assembly containing the RNA-binding proteins DDX1, SFPQ, NONO as well as RNA polymerase II (Pol II). Gene silencing experiments demonstrate that the protein assembly is required to process the DNA:RNA hybrids formed during Pol II extension and to protect the cell from transcriptional stress. TTD-specific variants in ERCC2/XPD result in TFIIH instability, altered interaction of the CAK with DDX1-SFPQ-NONO, and R-loop accumulation. Therefore, the limited amount of TFIIH that distinguishes TTD from XP gives rise to transcriptional stress and extensive gene expression deregulations, thus accounting for the wide spectrum of TTD clinical features.

The transcription factor IIH (TFIIH) is a 10-subunit complex involved in basal transcription, gene expression regulation, and nucleotide excision repair (NER), the pathway devoted to the removal of bulky DNA adducts induced by genotoxic agents, such as ultraviolet (UV) light, distinctive environmental mutagens, and chemotherapeutic drugs [1–4]. TFIIH is organized in two sub-complexes, the ternary cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) activating kinase (CAK) sub-complex that harbours the CDK7 kinase activity and the hexameric core-TFIIH sub-complex comprising the ATP-dependent DNA translocase XPB [5]. The 10th subunit is the 5′–3′ ATP-dependent helicase XPD representing the third enzymatic activity of TFIIH that also acts as bridging factor between the core-TFIIH and the CAK [6–9]. Besides being part of the entire TFIIH complex (holo-TFIIH), the CAK sub-complex is found in the cell as free-CAK or even bound to the XPD subunit [10, 11]. As an element of the free-CAK, CDK7 phosphorylates and activates the CDKs required for cell cycle progression [12], whereas, as a subunit of the holo-TFIIH, it triggers transcription initiation by phosphorylating specific transcription factors and the carboxy-terminal domain (CTD) of the large Rpb1 subunit of RNA polymerase II (Pol II).

Pathogenic variants in XPB- or XPD-coding genes give rise to distinct clinical disorders defective in the NER process, including the cancer-prone xeroderma pigmentosum (XP) and the photosensitive form of trichothiodystrophy (PS-TTD) [13]. Despite the common cutaneous photosensitivity, XP and PS-TTD clinical features differ greatly. XP patients display skin pigmentary changes and high incidence of tumour formation in the sun-exposed area of the skin, associated with progressive neurological degeneration [14, 15], whereas PS-TTD patients reveal hair abnormalities, developmental delay, neurological disability, recurrent infections, and signs of premature ageing but no skin cancer development [8, 9].

At the cellular level, all pathogenic XPD variants result in impaired NER functioning and accumulation of unrepaired DNA photolesions, even though they more severely affect XP than PS-TTD cells [16–18]. In addition, variants associated with PS-TTD lead to reduced TFIIH cellular content [19, 20], basal transcription defects [21, 22], and specific transcription deregulations [23–26]. In agreement, we have recently demonstrated that PS-TTD more than XP primary dermal fibroblasts suffer from wide transcriptional impairments [27] that, in some cases, result in altered protein content contributing to PS-TTD clinical features [27–29]. To date, understanding whether the in vivo transcription deregulations are ascribable to the reduced TFIIH amount and/or to TFIIH malfunction caused by TTD-specific variants, it is difficult to distinguish. To address this issue, we investigated the impact of XPD pathogenic variants on holo-TFIIH and its interaction with the chromatin. We demonstrate that holo-TFIIH is part of a large chromatin-associated multiprotein assembly that, besides Pol II, also contains the RNA helicase DEAD box 1 (DDX1), the splicing factor proline and glutamine rich (SFPQ), and non-POU domain-containing octamer-binding protein (NONO). Gene silencing approaches reveal that this multiprotein assembly plays a role in R-loop processing, hybrid RNA–DNA structures originating during transcription. R-loops act as important regulators of gene expression [30, 31] that, if not promptly processed, can be detrimental to transcription and genome stability [32, 33]. Indeed, we find that primary dermal fibroblasts from PS-TTD cases, who are characterized by reduced TFIIH amount, present an excess of unprocessed R-loops in their genome paralleled by an accumulation of DDX1–SFPQ–NONO factors bound to the limited amount of the chromatin-associated CAK. The overexpression of the Ribonuclease H1 (RNase H1), which specifically degrades the RNA strand of R-loop, can recover the reduced transcription observed in PS-TTD. Of note, no R-loop accumulation is observed in primary fibroblasts from XP/XP-D cases. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the reduced TFIIH amount leads to impaired R-loop processing, hence contributing to the wide transcriptional defects underlying PS-TTD pathological condition.

The average fluorescence intensity per nucleus was estimated. For each sample, at least 50 nuclei were analysed from three independent experiments.

The mass spectrometry analysis was performed as previously described [35] and data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [38] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD053839.

In detail, the pattern of proteins co-immunoprecipitated with CDK7 or control IgG was evaluated by staining the SDS–PAGE with Stain-Free Imaging Technology (Bio-Rad). Subsequently, the CDK7 immunoprecipitation was loaded and resolved by monodimensional electrophoresis on 9%–16% homemade polyacrylamide linear gradient gels (18 × 20 cm × 1.5 mm) at 40 mA/gel constant current, at 9°C [39]. MS-compatible silver staining protocol was used to perform gel staining according to Sinha P. et al. [40]. Gels were digitized with Image Scanner III laser densitometer controlled by LabScan 6.0 software (GE Healthcare). Visible bands were manually excised, destained in 2.5 mM ammonium bicarbonate and 50% (v/v) acetonitrile, and then dehydrated with 100% (v/v) acetonitrile. Proteins in bands were reduced with 10 mM DTE in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate (1 h at 56°C) and then alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate at RT (45 min, in darkness). After 10 min of incubation with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, protein bands were dehydrated with 100% (v/v) acetonitrile. Dehydrated protein bands were, then, rehydrated in trypsin solution (Sigma–Aldrich) to perform the in gel protein digestion at 37°C overnight.

Protein identification was carried out by peptide mass fingerprinting (PMF) on an ultrafleXtreme™ MALDI ToF/ToF instrument (Brucker Corporation). 1.25 μl of each digested protein solution, from each excised band, was spotted onto the MALDI target and allowed to dry. Then 0.75 μl of matrix solution (5 mg/ml alpha-ciano-4-hydroxycynnamic acid in 50% (v/v) acetonitrile and 0.5% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid) was applied to the dried sample and dried again. After acquiring the mass of the peptides, a mass fingerprinting search was carried out in Swiss-Prot/TrEMBL and NCBI nr databases using MASCOT search engine available online (Matrix Science Ltd).

Taxonomy was limited to Homo sapiens, mass tolerance was 50 ppm, and the acceptable number of missed cleavage sites was set at one. Alkylation of cysteine by carbamidomethylation was assumed as a fixed modification and oxidation of methionine was considered as a possible modification. The criteria used to accept identifications included the extent of sequence coverage (>10%), the number of matched peptides (>4), and the MASCOT algorithm assigned probabilistic score (>60 or P < .001) [35].

To allow protein–protein interactions, recombinant proteins were incubated O/N at 4°C in interaction buffer (20 mM Hepes, pH 7.8, 100 mM KCl, 10% glycerol, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.05% NP-40, 0.2 mM DTT, and 1× PIC). 2.5 μl of DDX1 primary antibody previously conjugated with Protein G Dynabeads (Invitrogen) were used for each immunoprecipitation reaction. Briefly, 150 μl of cobalt resin pre-washed in lysis buffer supplemented with 10 mM imidazole were added to the supernatant and incubated at 4°C in constant agitation for at least 2 h. After centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 5 min at 4°C, the flow-through was collected and the resin washed three times in wash buffer 1 (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 20% glycerol, 500 mM KCl, 0.1% NP40, 10 mM imidazole, 1× PIC) and three times in wash buffer 2 (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 20% glycerol, 150 mM KCl, 0.1% NP40, 10 mM imidazole, 1× PIC). DDX1 purified protein was dialyzed for 2 h at 4°C in dialysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 20% glycerol, 0.1 mM EDTA, 50 mM KCl, and 0.5 mM DTT). TFIIH functionality in DNA repair and transcription requires a direct interaction of the complex with the chromatin and relies on the presence of both the CAK and core sub-complexes. In view of the strategic position of XPD as bridging factor between the CAK and core-TFIIH, we investigated whether disease-causative alterations in XPD subunit may impact the association/dissociation of the two sub-complexes and their interaction with the chromatin. After establishing the experimental procedure to separate the native chromatin (chromatin-enriched fraction) from the remaining cellular components (soluble fraction), we analysed the distribution of the chromatin-associated TFIIH subunits in primary dermal fibroblasts isolated from healthy donors (CTR) or patients with pathogenic variants in the ERCC2/XPD gene (XP-D) and clinical features of either PS-TTD or XP (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1A). The distribution of XPB and p62 subunits of the core-TFIIH, the CDK7 and CycH subunits of the CAK sub-complex as well as the bridging factor XPD have been analysed by immunoblot, in parallel with the cytoplasmic protein Mek2 and the chromatin-bound marker histone H3. We found that most of the TFIIH subunits are associated with the chromatin of control cells while a limited fraction of the core-TFIIH and <20% of XPD and CAK are found in the soluble fraction, likely as free-CAK playing its role in cell cycle regulation. Of note, a relevant loss of the chromatin-associated XPD and CAK subunits is observed in PS-TTD primary dermal fibroblasts while the core-TFIIH maintains its association with the chromatin. A less pronounced detachment of XPD and CAK is found in XP cells. Similar findings are observed in patients’ immortalized cells, highly pronounced in PS-TTD but partially detectable also in XP cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Overall, these data suggest that PS-TTD causative variants in XPD result in an unbalanced distribution and partial depletion of the CAK sub-complex from the chromatin, likely affecting the functionality of the chromatin-associated TFIIH complex.

To define which molecular mechanisms may be affected by the partial loss of the CAK from the chromatin in PS-TTD, we adopted the strategy of searching for chromatin-associated interactors of the CAK sub-complex in the wild-type (WT) situation. To this aim, the chromatin-enriched fraction of control MRC5 cells was immunoprecipitated with CDK7 antibodies and the co-immunoprecipitated proteins were identified by mass spectrometry analysis. As negative control, the same fraction was immunoprecipitated with IgG antibodies and analysed by Stain-Free Imaging Technology (Supplementary Fig. S2). Several novel CAK-interacting proteins have been identified (Fig. 1B and C), among which is the ATP-dependent RNA helicase DEAD-box 1 (DDX1), which is known to play a role in double-strand break DNA repair (DSBs) by unwinding DNA–RNA hybrid structures and facilitating the repair reaction at transcriptionally active genomic regions [42]. Considering the involvement of DDX1 with both DNA repair and transcription, we decided to investigate deeper its binding to the CAK sub-complex. By immunoprecipitating either CDK7 or DDX1, we found that the interaction between the two proteins occurs more abundantly in the chromatin-enriched fraction (Fig. 1D and E). Furthermore, in addition to the CAK sub-complex, we found that XPD also coimmunoprecipitates with DDX1 (Fig. 1E).

To better characterize the interaction of DDX1 with the CAK and XPD, we next performed in vitro interaction assays using purified recombinant proteins. Pull-down assays showed that recombinant CAK and XPD coimmunoprecipitate with purified DDX1 at low (100 mM KCl) and high (300 mM KCl) salt concentrations, suggestive of strong interactions (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, XPD coimmunoprecipitates with DDX1 both in the presence or absence of recombinant CAK, indicating that their interaction is direct and not mediated by the CAK sub-complex (Fig. 2A). By using a series of XPD protein fragments encompassing amino acids 1–245 (N-terminal portion), 245–443 (ARCH domain), and 444–762 (C-terminal portion), the ARCH domain-devoid XPD (ΔARCH), as well as the full-length XPD, we found that the C-terminal part of XPD is sufficient to interact with DDX1 (Fig. 2B). Remarkably, the in vitro interaction is not impaired by the most frequent XPD alterations related to PS-TTD (XPD/R112H or XPD/R722W) or XP (XPD/R683W) phenotype, indicating that these amino acids might not be directly involved in DDX1 binding even though located in the C-terminal portion of the protein (Fig. 2C).

Next, we investigated the DDX1 interaction with the CAK and XPD in vivo, by performing TIP analysis in which CDK7 was first immunoprecipitated and, after the elution, anti-DDX1 antibodies were used for the second immunoprecipitation (Fig. 2D). In the chromatin-enriched fraction of control MRC5 cells, the bridging factor XPD, the core-TFIIH subunits, and the largest subunit of Pol II (RPB1), all co-immunoprecipitated with the DDX1–CDK7 interacting proteins. Therefore, on the chromatin of living cells, holo-TFIIH, Pol II, and DDX1 are part of a unique large protein assembly.

Knowing the relevance of TFIIH in both basal transcription and NER, we investigated the impact of DDX1 gene silencing (Supplementary Fig. S3) on these mechanisms. As shown in Fig. 3A, NER efficiency evaluated through UDS is not affected by the reduced amount of DDX1 protein in MRC5 cells. Similarly, we did not observe significant differences in the repair of CPDs or 6–4 photoproducts (6–4PPs) between control MRC5 cells and those treated with either control or DDX1-specific siRNA at various time points after UV irradiation (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the incorporation of the uridine analogue 5-ethynyl-2′-uridine (EU) during transcription is strongly impaired upon DDX1 gene silencing (Fig. 3C), suggesting an involvement of DDX1 in transcription. Remarkably, siRNA-mediated DDX1 knockdown resulted in reduced cellular amount of Pol II but not of TFIIE, another essential element of the transcription initiation machinery (Fig. 3D). In addition, in vitro experiments revealed that DDX1 is neither found in the transcription pre-initiation complex (PIC) (Supplementary Fig. S4A) nor influences in vitro reconstituted transcription (Supplementary Fig. S4B) [43], suggesting that DDX1 may not be involved in the recruitment of the proteins associated with transcription initiation.

Previous studies have shown that DDX1 can resolve G-quadruplex structures into R-loops to promote class switch recombination at the immunoglobulin heavy-chain locus [44]. Of note, among the other CDK7-interacting proteins identified by mass spectrometry analysis, we found NONO, SFPQ, and the RNA helicase DHX9 (Fig. 1C), which are all known to be implicated in R-loop processing [45, 46]. Thus, TIP analysis using in sequence the CDK7 and DDX1 antibodies on the chromatin-enriched fraction of MRC5 control cells, revealed that besides holo-TFIIH and Pol II, also NONO and SFPQ, but not DHX9, co-immunoprecipitated with the CDK7-bound DDX1 protein (Fig. 3E). Therefore, these factors are part of a large protein cluster associated with the chromatin, here appointed as Chromatin-associated protein Assembly Containing TFIIH (Chac2H), that is implicated in Pol II-mediated transcription.

Next, we investigated whether the global transcription impairment and the reduced amount of Pol II observed after DDX1 gene silencing (Fig. 3C and D, respectively) might be the consequence of an altered R-loop processing leading to transcriptional stress. Taking advantage of the S9.6 antibodies that specifically recognize the RNA–DNA hybrid component of R-loops [47], slot blot analysis revealed a significant increment of RNA–DNA hybrids in total genomic DNA of MRC5 cells treated with DDX1 siRNA (Fig. 4A), highlighting a contribution of DDX1 in R-loop processing. Similarly, DRIP analysis with S9.6 antibodies followed by real-time RT–PCR revealed a strong and significant accumulation of DNA–RNA hybrids at the 5′ and promoter regions of the reference ACTB gene in DDX1 silenced cells (Fig. 4B). A parallel treatment with RNase H1, which recognizes and degrades the RNA portion of R-loops, severely reduced the S9.6 signals in slot-blot and DRIP assays, indicating the specificity of the antibody under our experimental conditions. Since the R-loop increment is mainly observed at the 5′ region of ACTB gene, we examined the distribution of DDX1 protein in the mouse genome using ChIP-seq data available from the literature (Wang et al., 2021). We found that 50% of all peaks map precisely at the TSS (0 bp) with a symmetrical distribution extending ± 1000 bp around the TSS (Fig. 4C). Overall, these results demonstrate that the chromatin-associated DDX1 protein plays an essential role in R-loop processing, mainly at the 5′ regions of the transcribed genes.

To assess whether the R-loop accumulation caused by DDX1 gene silencing is the primary source of transcriptional impairment, we evaluated the incorporation of EU into RNA upon RNase H1 treatment. As shown in Fig. 5A, RNase H1 overexpression fully rescues the transcriptional impairment caused by DDX1 gene silencing in MRC5 cells, demonstrating the direct link between R-loop accumulation and the transcriptional failure observed upon DDX1 knockdown. Since an excess of unsolved R-loops represents a threat to genome stability as they can collide with the incoming replication forks [48], we evaluated the impact of DDX1 silencing on genome integrity. We found that reduced DDX1 amount results in increased level of H2AX phosphorylation (γH2AX), a marker of DNA DSBs (Fig. 5B). Of note, the expression of RNAse H1 in these cells can fully rescue the γH2AX accumulation (Fig. 5C), demonstrating that the R-loop accumulation caused by reduced DDX1 amount makes a major contribution to transcription impairment paralleled by the loss of Pol II and increased DNA lesions marked by γH2AX accumulation.

Next, we immunoprecipitated the chromatin-associated CDK7 and investigated the co-immunoprecipitation of DDX1, NONO, and SFPQ upon XPD gene silencing. In parallel to the reduced amount of the CAK, we found an increased quantity of DDX1, NONO, and SFPQ associated with the chromatin-bound CDK7 (Fig. 6D). Knowing that R-loops have more chances to form in longer, transcriptionally active genes [49], and that alterations in R-loop processing may affect gene expression, we investigated the length of the down-regulated transcripts in PS-TTD fibroblasts (610 transcripts) compared to healthy controls that we previously identified by RNA-seq analysis [27]. The length of these transcripts was compared to the length of all RNAs expressed in both PS-TTD and healthy controls, which correspond to 37 990 transcripts (reference list) and including genomic protein-coding genes, lncRNAs, pseudogenes, and TECs (polyA EST to be Experimentally Confirmed) annotated on the Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 38 (GRCh38.p14) Assembly. By the Welch’s two-sample t-test, we found that the average length of TTD down-regulated transcripts is significantly (P= 9.252e−07) higher compared to the average length of the reference RNAs (Fig. 8B and Supplementary Fig. S10), further supporting the notion that part of the transcription deregulations in TTD cells may be ascribable to the formation of unprocessed pathological R-loops.

In the present manuscript, we identify novel chromatin-associated protein–protein interactions involving TFIIH, Pol II, DDX1, NONO, and SFPQ. We demonstrate their contribution to R-loop resolution directed to facilitate Pol II-mediated transcription under physiological conditions as well as their partial failure when the TFIIH complex carries XPD alterations leading to PS-TTD but not to XP.

R-loops are physiological transient structures that form in many transcriptionally active regions of eukaryotic genomes. They are made of three nucleic acid strands, including a DNA–RNA hybrid structure and a displaced untranscribed single-stranded DNA. Genome-wide mapping studies indicated that R-loops are abundant at promoters and transcription termination sites, where they regulate gene expression through multiple mechanisms [33]. However, unprocessed and pathological R-loops may represent a source of DNA damage and genome instability since they can hinder transcription elongation and progression of DNA replication forks [50]. The mechanisms through which R-loops are removed from the genome are only partially elucidated; however, it has been shown that other proteins of the transcription-coupled NER (TC-NER) sub-pathway may be implicated. It has been shown that XAB2 can recruit the endonucleases XPG and ERCC1-XPF to the R-loop site and that the latter two enzymes can actively process R-loops into DNA DSBs [46, 48, 50]. CSB contributes to the formation of R-loop-mediated DSBs that will be finally repaired by the transcription-coupled homologous recombination pathway [51, 52]. Our findings that TFIIH, as part of the Chac2H multiprotein assembly, counteracts the formation of persistent R-loops strengthen the involvement of TC-NER factors in the mechanisms of R-loop resolution. By backtracking Pol II progression, these three-stranded nucleic acid structures may stand as obstacles for transcription elongation. To avoid transcriptional stress, cells require the prompt removal of R-loops by activating fast repair mechanisms, including factors of the TC-NER sub-pathway.

The Chac2H protein assembly includes NONO, SFPQ, and DDX1. NONO and SFPQ are RNA-binding proteins that act as heterodimers in multiple nuclear processes, including transcriptional regulation by Pol II, pre-mRNA splicing, and repair of DSBs through non-homologous end-joining ([53] and references therein). More recently it has been shown that NONO and SFPQ collaborate at telomeres as suppressors of RNA:DNA hybrids. Loss of function of both NONO and SFPQ results in increased telomere recombination events and telomere fragility [45]. Similarly, DDX1 is involved in many aspects of RNA metabolism, including the removal of RNAs to promote the repair of DSBs via homologous recombination [42], splicing of tRNA [54], ribosomal RNA processing [55], cytoplasmic mRNA protection in cells exposed to oxidative stress [56], and conversion of RNA G-quadruplex structures into R-loops that, in turn, will promote IgH class switch recombination [44]. Now, we show that NONO, SFPQ, and DDX1 are part of a large chromatin-bound multiprotein assembly that, together with TFIIH, contributes to the physiological resolution of R-loops forming during Pol II-mediated transcription. The relevance of their interaction is attested by the finding that gene silencing experiments targeting either DDX1 or XPD genes in control cells give rise to an accumulation of unresolved R-loops in the ACTB locus (Figs 4 and 6), to a partial loss of Pol II, reduced global transcription rate (Figs 3 and 6), and γH2AX accumulation (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S5), indicative of cellular transcriptional stress conditions. Of note, the finding that the overexpression of RNase H1, an endoribonuclease that specifically degrades the RNA strand of RNA:DNA hybrids, is sufficient to fully recover cell transcription and to avoid genome damage (γH2AX) demonstrates that the accumulation of unprocessed R-loops is the main cause of the transcriptional stress condition and genome instability occurring in response to DDX1 gene silencing (Fig. 5).


DDX1 gene silencing leads to R-loop accumulation at the 5′ of ACTB locus surrounding the TSS (Fig. 4B), suggesting that its RNA:DNA helicase activity may be required in the 5′ portion of the transcribed genes. This is supported by our finding that DDX1 is not part of the PIC complex (Supplementary Fig. S4) but it preferentially associates with the gene TSS (Fig. 4C). Differently, XPD-silencing gives rise to R-loop accumulation along the entire transcriptional unit (Fig. 6C), opening the possibility that additional TFIIH-dependent interactors/mechanisms support Pol II transcription elongation. Of note, the RNA helicase DHX9 is one of the chromatin-bound CDK7 interactors we found by mass spectrometry analysis. Defects in transcription initiation, elongation, and termination caused by DHX9 depletion have also been associated with R-loop accumulation and R-loop-driven genome instability [46, 57]. Nevertheless, the DDX1–CDK7 binding does not seem to involve DHX9 (Fig. 3E), and further investigations are required to dissect the composition and the functional relevance of this additional protein–protein interaction.

The relevance of TFIIH function in R-loop processing is attested by PS-TTD fibroblasts with pathogenic variants in ERCC2/XPD or ERCC3/XPB genes that lead to TFIIH instability (Supplementary Fig. S6 and [20]) and transcription deregulations [27]. Indeed, PS-TTD cells display an accumulation of unresolved R-loops that is not observed in XP fibroblasts with ERCC2/XPD variants not affecting TFIIH stability (Fig. 7). Since both PS-TTD and XP cells are characterized by severe NER impairments, we can conclude that the accumulation of unresolved R-loops in PS-TTD is not the consequence of DNA repair defects but rather of limited TFIIH amount. In agreement, reduced level of WT TFIIH is per se sufficient to mirror the R-loop accumulation observed in PS-TTD cells. Of note, the decreased TFIIH level in XPD knocked down MRC5 cells as well as in immortalized PS-TTD cells results in partial loss of Pol II (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S7, respectively). However, no significant Pol II reduction is observed in PS-TTD primary fibroblasts also characterized by reduced TFIIH level. This discrepancy is likely related to the different proliferation rates of the utilized cells: the probability that R-loops collide with the DNA replication forks and lead to Pol II degradation is greater in highly proliferating cells (MRC5 and immortalized patients’ cells) compared to cells with limited proliferation capacity (primary dermal fibroblasts). Nevertheless, the finding that RNase H1 expression restores to normal levels the transcription of PS-TTD primary fibroblasts (Fig. 8A) is the demonstration that unprocessed R-loops contribute to the wide transcription deregulations characterizing these cells.

While R-loop accumulation is the consequence of reduced TFIIH amount, structural rather than quantitative TFIIH alterations appear responsible for the partial detachment of the CAK from the chromatin in PS-TTD. Indeed, TTD alterations in the bridging factor XPD do not alter the chromatin-binding properties of the core (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1). Knowing the relevance of the CAK kinase activity in Pol II activation at gene promoters, we can conclude that both structural alterations of TFIIH (causing CAK dissociation from the chromatin) and TFIIH instability (followed by R-loop accumulation and Pol II degradation) heavily contribute to the wide gene expression deregulations characterizing PS-TTD cells [27].

Moreover, by in vitro and in vivo analysis (Fig. 2C), we find that DDX1 binding to TFIIH is not affected by the most common pathogenic XPD alterations leading to either PS-TTD or XP clinical features (i.e. the amino acid Arg112 or Arg722 in PS-TTD patients or the residue Arg683 in XP patients, see Supplementary Table S1). This suggests that R112, R683, and R722 do not play a direct role in the formation of the DDX1-docking binding site. Conversely, we find that in PS-TTD fibroblasts as well as in XPD-silenced cells, high levels of DDX1, NONO, and SFPQ are bound to the albeit reduced amount of chromatin-associated CDK7 (Figs 6D, 7A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S8). Therefore, in the presence of unprocessed R-loop accumulation caused by reduced TFIIH amount, DDX1 and other components of the Chac2H multiprotein assembly increase their association to TFIIH, independently of XPD alterations. This finding opens the possibility that the persistence and extension of unresolved R-loops may represent the triggering event for the recruitment of additional Chac2H factors and that their increased interaction with TFIIH may be the result of a better accessibility to the complex caused by the loss of Pol II from the transcriptional machine. We cannot, however, exclude the possibility that other factors associated with TFIIH and with the extended R-loops may become targets for DDX1, NONO, and SFPQ binding.

Overall, the present work widens the types of activities that TFIIH performs in assisting Pol II-mediated transcription, adding the resolution of unresolved and potentially harmful R-loops to the already known TFIIH activities in transcription activation, transcription regulation of specific gene sets, and repair of transcription-blocking lesions. Finally, the central and key role of TFIIH in transcription is attested by the wide spectrum of pathological features displayed by PS-TTD cases.
