# Multiple system atrophy: cure and care.
**Authors:** Buonocore J, Caliò B, Leys F, Hopfner F, Höglinger GU, Quattrone A, Stefanova N, Fanciulli A
**Journal:** J Neural Transm (Vienna) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00702-026-03235-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00702-026-03235-8)

## Content

Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare and rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a variable combination of autonomic failure, parkinsonism, and cerebellar ataxia, with a median survival of 8-10 years from symptom onset. Its aetiology remains poorly understood, as most cases are sporadic and environmental contributors remain unclear. Neuropathologically, MSA is defined by the accumulation of α-synuclein within oligodendroglial cells, forming glial cytoplasmic inclusions that drive widespread neurodegeneration in striatonigral and olivopontocerebellar systems. Recent advances in diagnostic criteria and biomarker development have improved disease recognition; however, early diagnosis remains challenging, particularly during the prodromal phase, when clinical features overlap with other α-synucleinopathies. Advances in fluid biomarkers and multimodal imaging are expected to facilitate earlier detection, improve diagnostic accuracy, and provide more robust tools for monitoring disease progression. Therapeutic development has largely focused on targeting α-synuclein pathology, but these approaches have not yet demonstrated consistent clinical benefit, highlighting the biological complexity of MSA. In the absence of curative treatments, management remains largely supportive, aimed at symptom control, particularly addressing autonomic dysfunction and motor impairment. Emerging care models, including telemedicine and multidisciplinary management, are reshaping clinical practice and may improve access to specialized care. Bridging advances in pathophysiology with patient-centred care will be essential for improving outcomes in MSA.

Introduction
Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare and rapidly pro -
gressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a 
variable combination of autonomic failure, parkinsonism, 
and cerebellar ataxia (Krismer et al. 2024a). Clinically, it is 
classified into two main phenotypes based on the predomi -
nant motor features: the parkinsonian variant (MSA-P), 
characterized mainly by akinetic-rigid symptoms, and the 
cerebellar variant (MSA-C), in which gait and limb ataxia 
predominate.
The estimated incidence is approximately 0.6 per 100,000 
person-years, with a prevalence ranging from 0.5 to 17 per 
100,000, varying significantly by geographic region (Fan -
ciulli and Wenning 2015). The disease carries a poor prog -
nosis, with a median survival of 8–10 years from symptom 
onset (Wenning et al. 2013; Low et al. 2015).

In recent years, growing efforts have focused on eluci -
dating the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying 
MSA, alongside the development of biomarkers and refine-
ment of diagnostic criteria. These advances have improved 
disease characterization and may facilitate earlier diagno -
sis, particularly during the prodromal phase. At the same 
time, emerging insights into α-synuclein biology have high-
lighted the need to distinguish physiological functions from 
the misfolding and aggregation processes that drive disease 
onset and progression, and suggest that MSA arises from 
multiple, converging mechanisms, including pathological 
α-synuclein aggregation, neuroinflammation, and loss of 
neurotrophic support (Sidoroff et al. 2022; Stefanova and 
Wenning 2023).
Despite these advances, major challenges remain, partic-
ularly in achieving early diagnosis and developing effective 
disease-modifying therapies.
This review summarizes recent advances in the under -
standing of MSA, focusing on genetic and pathophysiologi-
cal mechanisms, evolving diagnostic frameworks, emerging 
biomarkers, and novel therapeutic strategies, with particular 
attention to their implications for clinical management and 
patient outcomes.
Aetiology and putative genetic contributors
The aetiology of MSA remains poorly understood. Most 
cases occur sporadically, and consistent environmental risk 
factors have not been clearly identified (Fanciulli and Wen-
ning 2015). Several case–control studies have investigated 
potential environmental contributors. Occupational expo -
sure to organic solvents, pesticides, and heavy metals has 
been examined, with some reports suggesting an increased 
risk among agricultural workers and individuals employed 
in the plastics industry, but these associations have not 
been consistently replicated across populations (Hanna et 
al. 1999; Vidal et al. 2008). Supporting a possible role of 
occupational exposures, a recent analysis of work histories 
from 270 neuropathologically confirmed MSA cases in the 
Mayo Clinic Brain Bank identified an overrepresentation 
of several occupational sectors (i.e. petroleum industry or 
metal industry) compared with population data (Cheshire et 
al. 2025).
More broadly, growing evidence suggests that environ -
mental factors may interact with genetic susceptibility in 
shaping disease risk, with reported associations involving 
alcohol consumption, well-water intake, pesticide expo -
sure, and smoking (Kuo et al. 2022). An inverse association 
between cigarette smoking and MSA has been previously 
reported, consistent with observations in Parkinson’s disease 
(PD), although the link appears less pronounced (Vanacore 
2005). This finding provides a rationale for investigating 
other environmental exposures implicated in PD but that 
remain underexplored in MSA (Krzyzanowski et al. 2025; 
Lin et al. 2026).
Despite the sporadic nature of MSA, the genetic con -
tribution has also been increasingly investigated, with 
epidemiological studies suggesting a relatively modest heri-
tability (approximately 2.09% and 6.65%) (Federoff et al. 
2016). Nonetheless, rare multiplex families showing appar-
ent autosomal dominant or recessive inheritance patterns 
have been reported (Wüllner et al. 2004; Hara et al. 2007; 
Fujioka et al. 2014).
Because the pathological hallmark of MSA is the accu -
mulation of α-synuclein within glial cytoplasmic inclusions 
(GCIs), early genetic studies primarily focused on SNCA, 
the gene encoding this protein (Al-Chalabi et al. 2009; 
Scholz et al. 2009). Rare coding variants have not been 
consistently replicated across studies (Kiely et al. 2013; 
Pasanen et al. 2014), and associations with common single 
nucleotide polymorphisms have produced variable results 
depending on population and study design (Al-Chalabi et 
al. 2009; Scholz et al. 2009; Yun et al. 2010). Copy-number 
gains at the SNCA locus, however, provide indirect evidence 
that increased α-synuclein dosage may contribute to disease 
pathogenesis, including in clinical or pathological pheno -
types suggestive of MSA (Fuchs et al. 2007; Mokretar et al. 
2018; Garcia-Segura et al. 2023; Kon et al. 2024).
Beyond α-synuclein–related mechanisms, candidate-
gene studies have explored additional biological pathways 
potentially involved in MSA pathogenesis. Variants in 
COQ2, implicated in mitochondrial function and oxidative 
stress pathways, were initially associated with MSA in Japa-
nese families, with evidence supporting primarily autoso -
mal recessive inheritance, although heterozygous variants 
have also been proposed as susceptibility factors (MSARC 
2013). This association has not been replicated in European 
or North American populations (Sharma et al. 2014; Ogaki 
et al. 2014).
Research has therefore expanded to genes implicated in 
related neurodegenerative disorders. Among genes associ -
ated with PD, the involvement of LRRK2 in MSA remains 
controversial. Although several studies have found limited 
evidence for a direct association with MSA risk (Ozelius et 
al. 2007; Heckman et al. 2014), other reports have suggested 
that LRRK2 variants may influence disease phenotype and 
progression (Riboldi et al. 2019), including recent data 
associating the p.Gly2385Arg exonic variant with earlier 
onset and longer mean survival than non-carriers (Kouki et 
al. 2026). Associations with GBA variants likewise appear 
to vary across populations (Segarane et al. 2009; Mitsui et 
al. 2015). Additional loci of interest include MAPT sub -
haplotypes (Vilariño-Güell et al. 2011), which have been

More comprehensive genome-wide approaches have 
recently been applied to investigate the genetic basis of 
MSA. Integrative analyses combining genome-wide associ-
ation studies (GWAS), transcriptome-wide approaches, and 
colocalization methods have identified candidate genes such 
as USP38-DT and KCTD7. Variants at these loci colocalize 
with cis-expression quantitative trait loci, linking genetic 
risk to regulatory effects on gene expression and supporting 
a potential role of oligodendroglial biology in MSA patho -
genesis (Chia et al. 2024). A GWAS in autopsy-confirmed 
cases further highlighted suggestive loci, including a sig -
nal near ZIC1/ZIC4, with reduced ZIC4-positive neurons 
observed in olivopontocerebellar atrophy, supporting a role 
in cerebellar vulnerability (Hopfner et al. 2022). Recent 
X-chromosome-wide association analyses have addition -
ally identified sex-specific susceptibility loci, including 
a female-associated signal at Xq28 potentially involving 
F AM3A and PLXNA3, and a male-associated locus at Xp22.2 
within TBL1X, suggesting that sex-related genetic factors 
may also contribute to MSA risk (Ray et al. 2026).
The interpretation of genetic association studies in MSA, 
however, remains complicated by substantial diagnostic 
uncertainty in clinically diagnosed cases. Neuropathologi -
cal examination remains the diagnostic gold standard, as no 
validated biomarker is currently for use in living patients, 
and autopsy studies have shown that up to 38% of clinically 
diagnosed MSA cases are misdiagnosed during life (Koga 
et al. 2015). This limitation likely introduces significant 
heterogeneity into genetic cohorts and may partly account 
for inconsistent findings across studies. Understanding how 
genetic susceptibility, within a likely polygenic architecture, 
interacts with the molecular mechanisms underlying MSA 
will therefore be essential for clarifying its contribution to 
disease development.
Pathophysiological mechanisms
Neuropathologically, MSA is characterized by the abnor -
mal accumulation of α-synuclein within oligodendrocytes, 
forming GCIs (Spillantini et al. 1998). Unlike the twisted, 
single-protofilament structures observed in PD and demen -
tia with Lewy bodies (DLB) (Yang et al. 2022), MSA-
associated aggregates consist of two distinct α-synuclein 
filament types, each composed of paired protofilaments that 
assemble into thicker, largely untwisted structures (Sch -
weighauser et al. 2020).
An early and critical event in MSA pathogenesis is 
the relocalization of the myelin-associated protein p25α, 
also known as tubulin polymerization-promoting protein 
(TPPP). Under physiological conditions, p25α is confined 
to the myelin sheath; however, in MSA it redistributes to the 
oligodendroglial cell body, preceding α-synuclein aggrega -
tion. This shift promotes phosphorylation of α-synuclein 
at serine-129 and facilitates its aggregation into insoluble 
oligomers (Ferreira et al. 2021).
The mechanisms underlying α-synuclein accumulation 
in oligodendrocytes are not yet fully understood. While 
p25α overexpression enhances the recruitment of endog -
enous α-synuclein into pro-degenerative inclusion bodies 
(Song et al. 2007), the relatively low levels of SNCA mRNA 
in oligodendrocytes suggest that local overproduction alone 
is insufficient to account for its accumulation (Asi et al. 
2014; Kon et al. 2024). Consequently, alternative mecha -
nisms have been proposed, including the uptake of neuronal 
α-synuclein aggregates through the processing of dam -
aged axonal segments (De Nuccio et al. 2023), as well as 
impaired clearance due to lysosomal dysfunction or altered 
oligodendroglial secretory pathways (Yu et al. 2020).
Experimental studies suggest that once established, 
α-synuclein aggregates trigger a cascade of oligodendrog -
lial dysfunction, microglial activation, astrogliosis, and neu-
roinflammation (Refolo et al. 2018; Scheiblich et al. 2021; 
Torre-Muruzabal et al. 2023; Heras-Garvin et al. 2025; 
Luque et al. 2026). In parallel, progressive oligodendroglial 
impairment may lead to the release of misfolded α-synuclein 
into the extracellular space, facilitating its uptake by adjacent 
neurons and promoting the formation of neuronal cytoplas-
mic inclusions. Recent neuropathological and experimental 
evidence suggests that neuronal α-synuclein toxicity, rather 
than oligodendroglial cytoplasmic inclusions alone, may 
represent the principal driver of neurodegeneration in MSA. 
In particular, α-synuclein has been shown to accumulate 
early within neuronal nuclei, disrupt the nuclear envelope, 
and induce rapid neuronal loss, while neuronal α-synuclein 
aggregates appear more proteolysis-resistant and neurotoxic 
than the comparatively more soluble and less harmful oligo-
dendroglial aggregates (Wiseman et al. 2025).

The neurodegenerative process is further shaped by 
additional factors, including dysregulated iron metabolism, 
mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired neurotrophic fac -

precursor cells into mature myelinating oligodendrocytes, 
and altered brain insulin signaling (Stefanova and Wenning

heterogeneity of MSA.
Diagnostic criteria and current challenges
A diagnosis of MSA during life still largely relies on clini -
cal criteria and remains challenging, despite substantial 
advances in understanding MSA pathogenesis. Clinicopath-
ological validation studies of the 2008 s consensus criteria 
(Gilman et al. 2008) have shown only moderate diagnos -
tic accuracy, with positive predictive values ranging from 
approximately 62% to 79% (Miki et al. 2019), reflecting 
frequent misclassification with other neurodegenerative dis-
orders, particularly PD, DLB, and progressive supranuclear 
palsy (PSP) (Koga et al. 2015).
In response to these limitations, the 2022 diagnostic 
criteria endorsed by the Movement Disorder Society intro -
duced graded levels of diagnostic certainty (neuropathologi-
cally established, clinically established, clinically probable, 
and possible prodromal MSA) with the aim of improving 
diagnostic precision across different stages of the disease 
(Wenning et al. 2022). Pathological validation studies indi-
cate that these updated criteria improve diagnostic perfor -
mance, particularly by increasing specificity through the 
integration of characteristic clinical features and supportive 
imaging markers (Virameteekul et al. 2023; Sekiya et al. 
2023). Structural MRI findings, including putaminal signal 
abnormalities, middle cerebellar peduncle atrophy, and the 
“hot cross bun” sign, demonstrate high specificity for MSA 
and are therefore incorporated into the criteria for clinically 
established MSA.
Their sensitivity, however, is often limited, especially in 
the early stages of the disease, indicating that the absence of 
MRI abnormalities does not exclude the diagnosis (Sekiya 
et al. 2023; Wilkens et al. 2024). This likely reflects the 
reliance on conventional neuroimaging markers. More 
advanced approaches, such as automated volumetric analy-
ses, are emerging as promising tools with high diagnostic 
accuracy even at early disease stages, although their avail -
ability remains largely limited to specialized centres (Scher-
fler et al. 2016; Vaillancourt et al. 2025).
A need therefore remains for multimodal supportive bio-
markers that integrate complementary approaches captur -
ing different aspects of disease biology (Wan et al. 2023). 
Promising candidates include neurofilament light chain 
(NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), which 
have demonstrated good performance in distinguishing 
MSA from other parkinsonian disorders and healthy con -
trols (Guo et al. 2023; Katzdobler et al. 2024; Kou et al. 
2025). Nevertheless, their diagnostic specificity remains 
limited when considered in isolation, as elevated levels 
are observed across multiple neurodegenerative conditions 
(Parnetti et al. 2019).
To address these limitations, α-synuclein seed amplifi -
cation assays (SAAs) have recently emerged as a valuable 
tool for distinguishing MSA from other α-synucleinopathies 
based on distinct fluorescence amplification patterns. Per -
formance varies across cohorts, likely reflecting differences 
in experimental protocols and substrates (Grossauer et al. 
2023; Ma et al. 2024), and combining SAAs with other bio-
markers may better capture disease heterogeneity (Singer et 
al. 2020).
Diagnostic assessment can be further refined through 
molecular imaging. Some PET tracers show preferential 
binding patterns compatible with MSA compared with other 
α-synucleinopathies (Koga et al. 2017; Smith et al. 2023). 
The α-synuclein PET tracer [18 F]SPAL-T-06 demonstrated 
high-contrast in vivo detection of α-synuclein-pathology 
in patients with both the parkinsonian and cerebellar sub -
types of MSA, showing increased retention in the putamen, 
pons and cerebellar white matter and peduncles in accor -
dance with the distribution of GCI (Matsuoka et al. 2022). 
By contrast, the tracer [18 F]C05-05 successfully detected 
α-synuclein inclusions in the midbrain of patients with PD 
and DLB, whereas in MSA increased signals were observed 
in the basal ganglia and deep cerebellar structures specifi -
cally in patients with the parkinsonian subtype (Endo et al. 
2024).
The development of such multimodal biomarkers is 
particularly relevant for detecting the disease at its earliest 
stages and has stimulated growing interest in the prodromal 
phase of MSA.
Prodromal phase and early biomarkers
Increasing evidence suggests that MSA is preceded by a 
prodromal phase in which neurodegenerative processes are 
already ongoing before the full clinical syndrome becomes 
manifest (Xia and Postuma 2020; Osaki et al. 2021; Jecmen-
ica-Lukic et al. 2021). During this stage, clinical features 
shared across prodromal α-synucleinopathies may help 
inform the underlying disease process. For example, REM 
sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) and subtle motor signs may 
occur both in premotor PD or DLB and in prodromal MSA,

Consistent with this, pure autonomic failure (PAF) has 
emerged as a particularly informative clinical model for 
investigating the mechanisms underlying phenoconversion 
towards central α-synucleinopathies. Although PAF was 
historically considered an α-synucleinopathy restricted to 
the autonomic nervous system (Mendoza-Velásquez et al. 
2019; González-Duarte et al. 2021), longitudinal studies 
have demonstrated that a subset of patients subsequently 
develops a central α-synucleinopathy (Kaufmann et al. 
2017; Singer et al. 2017; Giannini et al. 2018; Coon et al. 
2020; Millar Vernetti et al. 2024; Chiaro et al. 2026). A 
recent meta-analysis including 900 individuals with PAF 
showed that, over a mean follow-up of about six years, 30% 
of patients phenoconverted to a central α-synucleinopathy, 
corresponding to a pooled incidence rate of 5.09 per 100 
person-years. Conversion to MSA occurred in 12% of cases 
and showed the highest phenoconversion rates during the 
early years of follow-up. The study also identified spe -
cific clinical predictors: subtle motor signs were associated 
with near-term phenoconversion to any α-synucleinopathy, 
whereas hyposmia was linked to neuronal types (PD or 
DLB) (Virameteekul et al. 2026). These findings are con -
sistent with those of a recent multicentre longitudinal study, 
which reported relatively early phenoconversion to MSA 
compared with other α-synucleinopathies, suggesting that 
distinct disease mechanisms may already be present during 
the prodromal stage (Millar Vernetti et al. 2024).
Clinical predictors alone, however, may have limited 
diagnostic accuracy in identifying which patients with PAF 
will eventually develop MSA. For this reason, increasing 
attention has been directed towards biofluid biomarkers, 
such as NfL and α-synuclein seeding assays, which have 
shown promising potential in predicting phenoconversion to 
MSA during the prodromal stage (Singer et al. 2021). While 
these biomarkers may help identify individuals at risk of 
developing MSA, additional tools are needed to monitor 
disease progression once it becomes clinically manifest.
Markers of disease progression
The evaluation of disease progression in MSA, as well as 
the assessment of response to disease-modifying therapies, 
has largely relied on clinical measures, most commonly 
the Unified MSA Rating Scale (UMSARS) (Wenning 
et al. 2004). While certain limitations have been identi -
fied, including variable sensitivity to longitudinal change 
across items, with motor features generally showing greater 
responsiveness than autonomic domains, the UMSARS 
remains a widely used instrument for assessing symptom 
burden and its impact on activities of daily living (Palma et 
al. 2021; Foubert-Samier et al. 2022; Krismer et al. 2022). 
Clinical scales may, however, not fully capture subtle bio -
logical changes or treatment-related effects at the individual 
level, prompting increasing interest in integrating objec -
tive biomarkers to complement clinical outcome measures, 
with neuroimaging emerging as a particularly informative 
approach.
Longitudinal MRI studies have shown significant brain 
volume loss in MSA over relatively short timeframes, par -
ticularly affecting the cerebellum, pons, and putamen (Kris-
mer et al. 2024b). In addition, several MRI markers have 
been shown to correlate with disease subtype and symptom 
severity, particularly when imaging findings are integrated 
with clinical or metabolic data (Jin et al. 2025). Beyond 
structural MRI, more advanced functional and molecular 
imaging techniques may further refine the characterization 
of disease progression (Krismer et al. 2025).
Consistent with these findings, the ASPIRE study applied 
a multimodal approach combining clinical measures with 
MRI, DaT-SPECT imaging, and plasma biomarkers in 
patients with early MSA (Fabbri et al. 2026). Measurable 
neuroimaging changes were detectable within six months, 
reflecting the aggressive course of the disease, with cer -
ebellar and pontine atrophy predominating in MSA-C and 
progressive reductions in striatal dopaminergic binding in 
MSA-P. Baseline brainstem and pontine atrophy predicted 
clinical worsening over a six-month period, whereas base -
line DaT-SPECT measures showed stronger predictive 
value over 12 months. Higher baseline plasma NfL levels 
were also associated with faster clinical progression, greater 
disability, and increased mortality, highlighting its value as 
a prognostic biomarker.
A multimodal biomarker approach to disease progression 
may be particularly useful for optimizing patient selection 
and outcome measures in clinical trials, with potential impli-
cations for the development of disease-modifying therapies.
Disease-modifying therapies
Advances in biomarker research and in the understanding 
of disease mechanisms, together with regulatory incentives 
such as orphan disease designation, have stimulated increas-
ing efforts to develop disease-modifying therapies for MSA.
Most therapeutic strategies have focused on different 
stages of the α-synuclein pathway, with a range of phar -
macological and immunotherapeutic approaches devel -
oped to target these processes (Sidoroff et al. 2022). They 
include SNCA-targeting strategies such as intrathecally 
administered antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) designed 
to reduce SNCA mRNA and α-synuclein protein production

of preventing pathological seeding and cell-to-cell propaga-

primary endpoint, amlenetug showed an acceptable safety 
profile and a non-significant trend toward slowing clinical 
progression, supporting further evaluation in Phase 3 stud -

focusing on small molecules that target a-synuclein aggre -

as Anle138b), which is currently being tested in participants 
with MSA [NCT06568237], following encouraging preclin-
ical findings (Heras-Garvin et al. 2019). Another promising 
candidate is the iron-modulating small molecule ATH434. 
Consistent with the preclinical evidence (Heras-Garvin et 
al. 2021; Finkelstein et al. 2022), ATH434 has shown reduc-
tions in brain iron accumulation and slower clinical pro -
gression over 52 weeks, with a safety profile comparable to 
placebo (Alterity Therapeutics 2026).

Recent experimental studies have focused on targeting 
the NLRP3-inflammasome in MSA and have demonstrated 
a very strong rationale for the strategy, including both pre -

clinically safe drug dosing, mechanistic relevance to human 
disease-associated microglia signatures, and a set of putative 
fluid biomarkers for monitoring of treatment effects (Luque 
et al. 2026). Further agents like S1P5 receptor modulators 
[NCT05923866] and compounds modulating inflamma -
some-related signaling (Tseng 2024; NCT06848231), are 
currently under clinical investigation.
Metabolic approaches targeting mitochondrial dysfunc -
tion have also been explored, and recent studies have asso -
ciated high-dose ubiquinol supplementation with a smaller 
decline in motor scores (Mitsui et al. 2023). On the other 
hand, other putative neuroprotective agents, including rilu -
zole and rasagiline, have failed to demonstrate convincing 
clinical efficacy (Seppi et al. 2006; Bensimon et al. 2009).
Recent disease-modifying strategies have increasingly 
focused on restoring neuronal integrity. Cell-based thera -
pies using autologous mesenchymal stem cells from bone 
marrow or adipose tissue have shown potential to modulate 
neuroinflammation and provide neurotrophic support, with 
early clinical studies suggesting a possible slowing of func-
tional decline and preservation of gray matter density (Lee 
et al. 2012; Singer et al. 2019; Chung et al. 2021). Larger 
randomized controlled trials are required to confirm safety 
and efficacy.
A complementary approach uses gene therapy to restore 
neurotrophic signalling within vulnerable neuronal circuits. 
Viral vector–mediated delivery of glial cell line–derived 
neurotrophic factor (GDNF), particularly the AB-1005 plat-
form [P2-3.004], is designed to enhance neuronal survival 
and resilience rather than directly targeting protein aggre -
gation. Although still preliminary, restoration of neuro -
nal function is emerging as a potential research direction 
in α-synucleinopathies, together with strategies aimed at 
reducing the burden of pathogenic molecular alterations.
Clinical management
Current management of MSA remains primarily symp -
tomatic and requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary 
approach focused on preserving functional autonomy (Fan-
ciulli and Wenning 2015). Achieving these goals requires 
coordinated care from neurologists, physiotherapists, 
speech and language therapists, autonomic specialists, and 
palliative care teams, reflecting the complex combination of 
motor and non-motor manifestations that frequently coexist 
and interact in this disorder (Sorrentino et al. 2026).
Motor symptoms generally show a limited or transient 
response to dopaminergic therapy compared with PD, mak-
ing non-pharmacological strategies particularly important. 
Targeted physiotherapy and occupational therapy play a key 
role in maintaining mobility, addressing gait impairment, 
and reducing the high risk of falls associated with the dis -
ease (Krismer et al. 2024a).
Progressive bulbar and respiratory dysfunction further 
complicates the clinical management of MSA. Dysphagia 
may contribute to malnutrition, dehydration, and aspira -
tion pneumonia, making regular swallowing assessment 
and nutritional support essential, while percutaneous endo -
scopic gastrostomy may need to be considered in advanced 
stages (Brown et al. 2020; Ruttle et al. 2026). Laryngeal 
dysfunction, including vocal cord paralysis and stridor, can 
lead to respiratory complications and an increased risk of 
sudden death, occasionally requiring interventions such as 
non-invasive ventilation or tracheostomy (Gandor et al. 
2020). As the complications often emerge during disease 
progression, decisions regarding gastrostomy and tracheos-
tomy should be addressed within a multidisciplinary frame-
work and aligned with the preferences and goals of care of 
patients and their families.
Autonomic dysfunction is another core feature and a 
major therapeutic priority. Orthostatic hypotension, in par -
ticular, can be highly disabling and often requires a combi -
nation of lifestyle measures and pharmacologic treatment 
(Fanciulli et al. 2026). Stabilizing blood pressure is essential 
not only to prevent syncope and injury but also to improve 
fatigue, cognitive fluctuations, and symptoms of orthostatic 
intolerance (Calió et al. 2025).
Both motor and autonomic disturbances contribute not 
only to disability but also to a broader spectrum of non-
motor symptoms, including pain, which is increasingly rec-
ognized as a significant clinical component of MSA. The 
characteristic “coat-hanger” pain is one example, but it cap-
tures only part of a broader clinical spectrum. In a recent 
community-based survey, up to 87% of individuals with 
MSA reported experiencing pain, highlighting its relevance 
as a clinical feature throughout the disease course (Campese 
et al. 2024). These observations suggest that pain in MSA 
is not only common but also multifactorial, reflecting the 
interplay of different pathological mechanisms, including 
rigidity, dystonia, postural abnormalities, autonomic dys -
function, and comorbid conditions. Recognizing this multi-
dimensional nature is important in clinical practice, as pain 
may be underestimated or attributed to a single mechanism, 
while its management often requires a more comprehen -
sive and individualized approach (Campese et al. 2026).

Sex-related differences in the clinical expression of MSA 
may also influence management strategies. Evidence sug -
gests that men more often develop severe cardiovascular 
autonomic dysfunction, including earlier and more pro -

present with greater motor disability and a higher neuropsy-
chiatric burden, particularly depression (Leys et al. 2024). 
Recognizing these differences is important for tailoring both 
pharmacological interventions and multidisciplinary sup -
port to each patient’s specific needs.
Healthcare provision in MSA: challenges and 
arising models
Maintaining regular follow-ups in clinical practice, how -
ever, remains challenging. As the disease progresses and 
mobility declines, many patients experience increasing dif -
ficulty attending visits at tertiary referral centres and may 
gradually lose access to specialized care. These barriers 
became particularly evident during the COVID-19 pan -
demic, when restrictions and reduced hospital access.
further interrupted established care pathways. Temporary 
closures of specialized centres and interruptions in reha -
bilitation services disrupted both diagnostic processes and 
routine follow-up, highlighting how strongly many patients 
depend on in-person support (Fanciulli et al. 2023a). At the 
same time, the rapid expansion of digital health technolo -
gies revealed new opportunities for care delivery. Telemedi-
cine and hybrid models may help reduce geographical and 
mobility barriers, allowing clinicians to monitor symptoms 
longitudinally while observing aspects of daily functioning 
within the patient’s home environment. For individuals with 
progressive autonomic disorders, such approaches may pro-
vide an important bridge between specialized centres and 
the realities of everyday life.
Early studies using these approaches adopted hybrid 
models that combined in-person visits with remote interac -
tions, including programs focused on therapeutic education 
for patients and caregivers and nurse-led remote monitoring 
to enable early detection of complications and improve care 
coordination (Cilia et al. 2025; Fabbri et al. 2025). In this 
context, the Medical Decision Making in Multiple System 
Atrophy (MeDeMSA) Care study, an ongoing 18-month 
monocentric study, is evaluating whether a patient-centred, 
multidisciplinary model that integrates telemedicine with 
individualized treatment plans, including pharmacological 
and non-pharmacological interventions, can improve qual -
ity of life in individuals with MSA (Fanciulli et al. 2025). 
These initiatives suggest that new models may improve 
access to specialized care in MSA, although their impact on 
patients’ quality of life remains to be clarified.
Discussion
MSA remains a highly challenging neurodegenerative dis -
order from both diagnostic and therapeutic perspectives. 
Despite important advances in understanding its molecular 
mechanisms, genetic background, and biomarker develop -
ment, early diagnosis remains difficult, and disease-modify-
ing therapies are still lacking. This gap between biological 
insight and clinical impact is one of the central challenges 
in the field. As a result, improving outcomes for individu -
als with MSA requires not only continued scientific prog -
ress but also the development of clinical care models that 
translate these advances into timely diagnosis, coordinated 
management, and sustained support throughout the disease 
course.
One major barrier to early recognition is the unequal 
availability of expertise in autonomic medicine. Although 
the number of autonomic laboratories in Europe has 
increased in recent years, their distribution remains highly 
heterogeneous, with many services concentrated in North -
ern and Western countries (Habek et al. 2022). Clinical 
expertise also varies substantially across centres, and many 
neurologists report limited confidence in managing hallmark 
autonomic manifestations, such as bladder, cardiovascular, 
or sexual dysfunction. Consequently, patients with MSA 
are often evaluated in clinical settings where autonomic 
symptoms may be underrecognized, despite their diagnos -
tic relevance and significant impact on quality of life (Reis-
Carneiro et al. 2024). Strengthening networks of specialized 
autonomic centres and improving training in autonomic dis-
orders may therefore be important steps towards improving 
diagnostic accuracy and facilitating access to appropriate 
care (Fanciulli et al. 2023b).
Beyond diagnostic challenges, the moment of diagno -
sis is a critical turning point in the disease trajectory. For 
clinicians, the focus is often on symptomatic treatment 
and clinical monitoring. For patients and families, how -
ever, receiving an MSA diagnosis frequently triggers pro -
found uncertainty about independence, daily functioning, 
and future decline. At this stage, a clear clinical roadmap 
is essential. Early, transparent communication, anticipatory 
guidance, and timely discussions of future needs and wishes 
should be considered fundamental components of clinical 
management. Advance care planning, including goals of 
care, resuscitation preferences, and end-of-life decisions,

should be initiated early in the disease course, well before 
cognitive or communication impairments limit the patient’s 
ability to express their preferences (Breitegger et al. 2024). 
Together, these approaches can help reduce uncertainty, 
support shared decision-making, and enable patients and 
caregivers to prepare for the disease’s progressive course 
(Kluger 2025).
Patients and caregivers, consequently, become central 
actors in interpreting symptoms and responding to daily 
challenges. Although limited professional guidance may 
sometimes lead to suboptimal strategies, patients often 
develop practical ways of adapting to symptoms within 
their own environment (Caliò et al. 2026). These experi -
ences offer valuable insight into the realities of living with 
MSA and frequently reveal needs that are difficult to capture 
during routine clinical controls.
Improving outcomes for individuals with MSA will 
require integrated models that bridge advances in under -
standing disease mechanisms and therapeutic development 
with comprehensive care strategies, including symptomatic 
treatment, multidisciplinary collaboration, remote monitor -
ing, and advance care planning across the entire disease 
trajectory (Fig. 1). Such models should not only address the 
biological complexity of the disorder but also place patient 
centricity at the core of clinical management, recognizing 
patients and caregivers as active partners in both the cure 
and care process.
