# Variants in ZZS Complex-Associated Genes TEX11 and M1AP Are Responsible for Male Infertility and Nonobstructive Azoospermia.
**Authors:** Ma A, Wang H, Ke S, Yu Z, Wang X, Li Z, Li Y, Yao L, Zhang F, Kong F, Jiang L, Zhao X, Zhang B
**Journal:** Andrology (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/andr.70291](https://doi.org/10.1111/andr.70291)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA) is the most severe form of male infertility, with genetic factors contributing to approximately 30% of cases. However, only a small fraction of all NOA cases can be explained by the current genetic findings. ZZS complex participates in meiotic recombination and is necessary for spermatogenesis.

OBJECTIVES: To expand the variant spectrum of ZZS complex-associated genes in male infertility and validate the pathogenicity of identified variants in patients with NOA.

MATERIALS/METHODS: A total of 108 Chinese NOA patients were recruited, whole-exome sequencing (WES) and subsequent genetic analysis were performed to identify candidate pathogenic variants. Reverse-transcript PCR (RT-PCR) and quantitative reverse-transcript PCR (RT-qPCR) were performed to detect the mRNA expression of TEX11 and M1AP. Hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunofluorescence staining were performed on testicular sections obtained from patients' biopsies.

RESULTS: Five variants in ZZS complex-associated genes TEX11 and M1AP were identified from five NOA patients. These variants were either rare or absent in public human genetic databases and were predicted to be deleterious. Further functional analysis revealed that the mRNA expression levels of TEX11 and M1AP were significantly reduced in four of the affected patients. Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed the loss of postmeiosis cells in seminiferous tubules of these patients. Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the loss of spermatids and the zygotene arrest of meiosis.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION: Collectively, our findings provide compelling evidence for the pathogenicity of these five variants in the development of NOA. These results not only expand the genetic landscape of NOA but also offer valuable insights for future clinical screening and diagnostic strategies.

Nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA), the most severe form of male infertility, occurs in approximately 1% of all men [1], with gene factors accounting for ∼30% cases including abnormal karyotype, Y‐chromosomal microdeletion, and genetic variants [2]. However, only few NOA cases can be explained by the current genetic findings [2], demonstrating the necessity for further studies to explore new genetic factors involved in NOA.

Meiotic recombination mediates pairing of homologous chromosomes and ultimately ensures their segregation. Impaired meiotic recombination may induce unrepaired double‐strand breaks (DSBs), abnormal segregation of homologous chromosomes and aneuploidy, resulting in NOA. Till now, several genes were reported to be associated with meiotic recombination and NOA in human, including MSH4 [3, 4], MSH5 [5, 6], HFM1 [7, 8], REDIC1 [9, 10].

ZZS complex, initially described in Saccharomyces cerevisiae [11], referred to Zip2–Zip4–Spo16 complex, have been characterized as SHOC1, TEX11, and SPO16 in mammals. ZZS proteins localize to chromosome axes and participate in meiotic recombination during meiotic prophase I. To be specific, SHOC1 binds to the strand invasion site and stabilizes the early recombination intermediates [12]. SPO16 was then recruited to stabilize SHOC1 and forms a XPF‐ERCC1‐like complex together with SHOC1. A recent research found that SPO16‐null meiocytes exhibited milder defects in DNA repair and synapsis than SHOC1‐null meiocytes, indicating that SHOC1 alone is partially functional and SPO16 is required for stabilization of SHOC1 [13]. TEX11 was then recruited to the strand invasion site with the help of M1AP [14] and formed the ZZS complex to promote crossover formation and meiotic progression in males.

In this study, we recruited 108 unrelated NOA patients from multiple centers in China. Whole‐exome sequencing (WES) was conducted to identify potential pathogenic variants responsible for NOA. Remarkably, four hemizygous variants in TEX11 and one homozygous variants in M1AP were identified in five unrelated NOA‐affected individuals. TEX11 or M1AP mRNA were extremely reduced or missing, indicating the loss of TEX11 and M1AP protein. Histopathological analyses exhibited that no spermatids were observed in patients’ testes, and further immunofluorescence staining revealed zygotene arrest of spermatogenesis, consistent with the functional research of ZZS complex reported in human and mice before [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Thus, our study demonstrated that variants in ZZS complex‐associated genes TEX11 and M1AP could result in NOA through disrupting meiotic progression.

A total of 108 Chinese men with NOA were enrolled. Peripheral venous blood was collected from every subject. Conventional diagnostic work‐ups—including G‐banded karyotyping, endocrine profiling, scrotal palpation, ultrasonography, and/or testicular biopsy—were conducted in the referring clinical laboratories. Semen examinations for subjects NOA‐0003, NOA‐0010, and NOA‐0091 followed the WHO sixth guidelines [21]. During the consultation, NOA‐0005 and A5233 were confirmed to be diagnosed with NOA by other centers. The studies were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committees of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval no. 2022‐726‐01). All methods were performed in accordance with the approved guidelines and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the protocol. The Clinical Study Registration Number is not applicable for this study since it is not an intervention study.

WES was performed for all participants. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral‐blood leukocytes with the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN, cat. 51106). Whole‐exomes were captured using AlExome Enrichment Kit V1 (iGeneTech, Beijing, China), and high‐throughput sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). FASTQ files were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19/GRCh37) by Burrows–Wheeler Aligner [22].

In the subsequent analysis of WES data, we conducted variant calling to identify single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (indels). Notably, copy number variant calling based on exome data was not executed. The identification of SNVs and indels was achieved utilizing Genome Analysis Toolkit version 4.0 [23]. Subsequently, these variants were annotated via ANNOVAR [24], referencing a comprehensive array of databases. These databases encompassed HGVS variant description, population frequency, associations with disease or phenotypes, and functional predictions of variants. In accordance with the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics [25], variants were categorized into five distinct classes: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of unknown significance, likely benign, and benign. Meanwhile, copy number variants were detected employing the DNAcopy R package [26].

Variants filtration was performed as we have shown in Supporting Information Figure 1. Briefly, all variants identified after alignment and joint calling were firstly filtered by quality control including callable genotype, adequate read depth and genotype quality. After this, variants that alter the protein sequence or cause a deletion in UTR/regulatory of spermatogenesis‐related genes were retained. Variants with maximum population AF < 0.001 in gnomAD v4.1 genome/exome and Kaviar, or absent from all queried population database were retained. Variants in genes potentially involved in spermatogenesis were given priority for consideration (Supporting Information Figure 1 and Table S1). Finally, the potential variants were confirmed through Sanger sequencing. The sequences of the primers used for Sanger sequencing are detailed in Table S2.

The RNeasy FFPE Kit (Qiagen, 73504) was utilized to extract total RNA from paraffin‐embedded testicular sections of NOA‐0003/NOA‐0010/P5233, and RNAiso Plus reagents (TaKaRa, 9109) was used to extract total RNA from peripheral venous blood of NOA‐0091. Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was carried out employing the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, RR047A). Quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Tli RNaseH Plus; Takara, RR420A) in conjunction with a CFX Real‐Time PCR System (BIO‐RAD, 1855204). The relative expression levels were normalized to the ACTB gene and the control sample. The raw data and calculated values were shown in Table S3. Primers used were listed in Table S4.

Testicular biopsies were obtained and fixed in either Bouin's solution or formalin. Following fixation, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 5 µm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed as we previously reported [27]. Microscopic imaging was performed using an Olympus BX43F microscope equipped with an SC180 camera. The captured images were subsequently processed utilizing the OLYMPUS cellSens Entry software (Tokyo, Japan).

Testicular tissues were fixed in formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin for sectioning at a thickness of 5 µm. For immunofluorescence analysis, the sections were first permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X‐100 in PBS. They were then blocked with DBT solution (comprising 3% BSA and 10% normal donkey serum in Tris‐buffered saline) for 30 min. Following this, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. After three washes in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X‐100 (PBST), secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at 37°C. The slides were then washed three times in PBST, air‐dried, and mounted with VECTASHIELD mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, H‐1000) supplemented with Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, H21492). Imaging was performed using a ZEISS LSM 980 microscope equipped with Airyscan 2, and the images were processed using ZEISS ZEN software (Germany). A list of the antibodies utilized in this study is provided in Table S5.

In order to screen the genetic causes of NOA, we recruited 108 genetically unrelated Chinese NOA individuals from multiple centers. WES were conducted in genomic DNA from all the patients with a normal karyotype (46, XY) and no Y chromosome microdeletions. Five variants in ZZS complex‐associated genes, including four TEX11 variants and one M1AP variants, were identified in five unrelated patients after variant filtration with a series of criteria (Figure 1A), while no SPO16 or SHOC1 variants were identified in these patients. To be specific, NOA‐0003 (Family 1, II:1, 34‐year old) carried a hemizygous TEX11 deletion in 5′UTR (M1: c.‐156_‐41del), NOA‐0005 (Family 2, II:1, 30‐year old) carried a hemizygous TEX11 missense variant (M2: c.511A > G, p.M171V), NOA‐0010 (Family 3, II:1, 34‐year old) carried a hemizygous TEX11 double‐missense variant (M3: c.1534C > A, 1543A > G; p.Q512K, I515V), A5233 (Family 4, II:1, 42‐year old) carried a hemizygous TEX11 frameshift variant (M4: c.1728dup, p.T577Yfs*16), NOA‐0091 (Family 5, II:1, 31‐year old) carried a homozygous M1AP missense variant (M5: c.1196C > T, p.A399V; Figure 1A–E). These variants were verified by Sanger sequencing in all five patients (Figure 1A). All these variants were rare or absent from public human databases, including 1000 Genomes, ESP6500, ExAc, GnomAD, and GnomAD‐EAS (Table 1). It is worth noting that M2 variant was identified by several groups in NOA patients, indicating its potential pathogenicity [16, 20], while the other variants identified by us were novel. In addition, all the missense variants were predicted to be deleterious by at least one of the functional prediction software except M3. Thus, we supposed that the five variants in ZZS complex‐associated genes could be pathogenic for NOA in the five patients.

Patients NOA‐0003, NOA‐0010, and NOA‐0091 are native to Zhejiang Province; A5523 was recruited from Jiangsu Province, and NOA‐0005 from Shandong Province, China. All five subjects were evaluated according to standard clinical practice guidelines [28, 29]. Physical examination and testicular histology revealed normal stature, body‐mass index, and external virilization (appropriate pubertal development, adult male hair distribution, no cryptorchidism, varicocele, or hypospadias). Ultrasonography showed normal prostate and vesicula seminalis. Testicular volume was within the normal range in NOA‐0005, slightly decreased in NOA‐0003 and NOA‐0091, and extremely reduced in NOA‐0010 (Table 2), consistent with the typical testicular atrophy observed in NOA. Routine semen analysis revealed normal semen volume with no sperm detected in ejaculate pellet after high‐speed centrifugation in NOA‐0003, NOA‐0010, and NOA‐0091. Serum reproductive hormones were within the reference intervals for NOA‐0003 and NOA‐0091, whereas follicle‐stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, and prolactin were marginally elevated in NOA‐0010 (Table 2).

In order to confirm the functional effects of the identified variants in TEX11 and M1AP, we first performed RT‐PCR and RT‐qPCR to evaluate the relative expression of TEX11 and M1AP mutant transcript with testes from patients and control. It revealed that the expression of TEX11 was extremely reduced in NOA‐0003, NOA‐0010, and A5233, who carried TEX11 variants, and the expression of M1AP was extremely reduced in NOA‐0091, who carried M1AP variants (Figure 2A,B). It suggested that the variants found in these patients decreased the mRNA expression or introduced the mRNA degradation of TEX11 and M1AP. Given that both M1AP and TEX11 participate in meiotic crossover formation [14, 16, 18, 19], these M1AP and TEX11 variants were considered to be pathogenic for infertility in all these NOA patients.

Testicular biopsies were obtained from NOA‐0003, NOA‐0010, and A5523. H&E staining of testicular sections revealed full spermatogenesis. However, in seminiferous tubules of NOA‐0003, NOA‐0010, and A5233, spermatids were absent (Figure 2C), indicating meiotic arrest.

To corroborate this arrest, paraffin sections were colabeled with the acrosomal marker PNA. In seminiferous tubules of human control, several PNA positive cells were observed, whereas no PNA positive cells were identified in seminiferous tubules from the NOA patients, revealing the absence of spermatids (Figure 3). It is worth noting that the spermatids‐like cells with condensed hematoxylin signals observed in H&E had no PNA signals, ruling out the possibility that they were spermatids. These results were consistent with the H&E staining results, demonstrating the meiotic arrest in the NOA patients carrying TEX11 variants.

Postmeiotic germ cells were absent in the patients carrying ZZS complex‐associated gene variants. A man who was diagnosed with obstructive azoospermia served as the control. Scale bars indicate 20 µm.

We next defined the precise stage of arrest using SYCP3 and γ‐H2AX immunofluorescence. SYCP3 is a lateral element of synaptonemal complexes, which could be used as a marker of spermatocytes. γ‐H2AX is a marker of DNA DSB, which could be used to differ the substage of spermatocytes. To be specific, γ‐H2AX presents a diffused signal in leptotene and zygotene, while in pachytene and diplotene, γ‐H2AX signal converged in the sex body. In seminiferous tubules of human control, SYCP3 positive cells were widely observed and most of them had a converged γ‐H2AX signal at sex body, which is typical for pachytene or diplotene spermatocytes. However, in seminiferous tubules of NOA patients, although SYCP3 signals were still observed, diffused γ‐H2AX signals were observed in all SYCP3 positive cells, indicating the loss of postzygotene spermatocytes (Figure 4 and Supporting Information Figures 2–4). These results are consistent with the findings in previous results related to the function of TEX11 and ZZS complex proteins [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Thus, we concluded that these variants in ZZS protein‐associated genes identified are indeed pathogenic for NOA and male infertility.

In this study, we recruited 108 Chinese NOA patients. Five NOA patients were identified to carry ZZS complex‐associated genes, including four hemizygous variants in TEX11 and one homozygous variant in M1AP. Four of the five variants were novel and the other one was reported in NOA or infertile patients by two groups before [16, 20]. RT‐PCR and RT‐qPCR were performed to confirm that TEX11 and M1AP mRNA expression were extremely reduced in these NOA patients. Moreover, we found that the NOA patients carrying TEX11 variants exhibited zygotene arrest of spermatogenesis, consistent with the previous functional research of TEX11 and other ZZS complex proteins [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Thus, our study discovered new variants in ZZS complex‐associated genes, which can result in NOA and male infertility.

In previous researches, variants in ZZS complex protein encoding genes were reported to cause meiotic arrest and NOA [16, 17, 18, 19, 30, 31, 32], consistent with the functional research of ZZS complex in Saccharomyces cerevisiae [11, 12]. In addition, variants in M1AP, a copartner of the ZZS complex, were reported to cause azoospermia or cryptozoospermia [14, 32, 33, 34]. A recent research has reported the effects of M1AP variants and concluded it with previous findings. To be specific, the loss‐of‐function variants in M1AP cause meiotic recombination failure, but not totally meiotic arrest. Pachytene cells and even spermatids could be observed in seminiferous tubules of patients carrying M1AP variants, but no postzygotene cells could be observed in patients carrying variants in other ZZS complex protein encoding genes. In addition, TEX11, MSH5, and MLH1 signals were reduced but still existed in spermatocytes of patients with M1AP variants. Thus, M1AP was indicated to be an important functional enhancer for promoting meiotic resolution in the ZZS network, but not mandatory [32]. In our study, all patients carrying variants in TEX11 and M1AP were diagnosed with NOA, and testicular sections of patients carrying TEX11 variants exhibited zygotene arrest, consistent with previous reports. Notably, every seminiferous tubule examined in the TEX11‐mutant patients was devoid of spermatids, underscoring the complete penetrance and uniformity of the meiotic arrest phenotype. Regrettably, we did not get the biopsy samples from patients carrying M1AP variants, which could be additional evidence for the function of M1AP and other ZZS complex proteins.

It is worth noting that the FSH, LH, and PRL levels of NOA‐0010 were above the normal range, and the testicular size of NOA‐0010 was extremely reduced. However, the H&E and immunofluorescence staining results of NOA‐0010 were similar with NOA‐0003 and A5233, exhibiting zygotene arrest in spermatogenesis. Similar phenomenon was also observed in previous studies of NOA patients carrying variants in ZZS genes or M1AP [14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 32, 33, 34]. To be specific, most of the patients had normal or slightly reduced testicular size and normal reproductive hormone levels, while few patients had extremely reduced testicular size. Thus, it reminds us the importance to check the histology of the testicular biopsy from patients, which is the golden standard to clarify the abnormalities in spermatogenesis.

Above all, we identified four hemizygous variants in TEX11 and one homozygous variant in M1AP in five of 108 Chinese NOA patients. TEX11 and M1AP mRNA expression were extremely reduced in these patients. Four of these variants were novel and the other one was confirmed to be pathogenic for NOA by other groups. H&E and immunofluorescence staining results suggested that these patients exhibited zygotene arrest of spermatogenesis. Collectively, our data expand the mutational spectrum of ZZS complex‐associated genes in NOA and reaffirm their indispensable role in human meiosis.
