# GPx6 downregulation drives ferroptosis in Kashin-Beck disease chondrocytes via the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis.
**Authors:** Liu Y, Li J, Zhang M, Zhang Y, Wang M, Wang R, Zhao X, Yang P, Chen J, Sun J
**Journal:** Apoptosis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10495-026-02389-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10495-026-02389-w)

## Content

Kashin-Beck disease (KBD) is an endemic osteoarthropathy characterized by chondrocyte death and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation; however, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aimed to explore whether glutathione peroxidase 6 (GPx6) is involved in regulating ferroptosis and matrix degradation in KBD cartilage injury. In articular cartilage from children with KBD, GPx6 expression was markedly reduced, particularly in the deep zone where chondrocyte death and matrix loss were most severe. Similarly, downregulation of GPx6 was observed in a T-2 toxin‑induced rat model and in C28/I2 chondrocytes, accompanied by decreased COL2A1 and ACAN levels and increased expression of MMP13 and ADAMTS4. Molecular docking indicates a potential interaction between T-2 toxin and GPx6. Gpx6 knockout mice exhibited signs of ECM degradation in articular cartilage, along with an enhanced susceptibility to T-2 toxin exposure. Mechanistically, Gpx6 deficiency led to elevated MDA levels, reduced SOD activity and GSH/GSSG ratio, as well as mitochondrial shrinkage, cristae loss, and lipid peroxidation. Knockdown of GPX6 suppressed the expression of SLC7A11, SLC3A2, and GPx4, while Ferrostatin‑1 partially reversed these ferroptosis‑related alterations but failed to restore GPx6 expression. Conversely, overexpression of GPX6 partially restored the function of the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis, improved redox balance, attenuated mitochondrial lipid peroxidation, and mitigated matrix degradation. Collectively, these findings suggest that downregulation of GPx6 may be closely associated with ferroptosis in KBD chondrocytes, and that GPx6 may serve as a potential upstream regulator of this process. Furthermore, dysregulation of the GPx6‑SLC7A11/GPx4 pathway may also be involved in the pathological process of cartilage injury in KBD.

Kashin-Beck disease (KBD) is a chronic endemic osteoarthropathy that primarily occurs in specific regions of China and severely affects children and adolescents [1]. Clinically, KBD is characterized by joint pain, morning stiffness, restricted mobility, enlarged or shortened digits, and progressive joint deformity [2]. Pathologically, it is characterized mainly by chondrocyte death, extracellular matrix (ECM) loss, and growth plate damage [3]. Although the etiology of KBD is considered multifactorial, substantial evidence indicates that environmental factors, particularly T-2 toxin exposure, play an important role in the initiation and progression of cartilage injury [4]. In affected cartilage, disruption of ECM homeostasis is a central pathological event. Type II collagen (COL2A1) and aggrecan (ACAN) are the principal structural components that maintain cartilage integrity [5, 6], whereas matrix-degrading enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 4 (ADAMTS4) drive ECM breakdown [7, 8]. Previous studies have shown that T-2 toxin suppresses COL2A1 and ACAN expression while increasing MMP13 and ADAMTS4, thereby promoting cartilage ECM degradation [9–12]. However, the molecular mechanisms linking chondrocyte death to ECM destruction in KBD remain incompletely understood.

Programmed cell death is increasingly recognized as a major driver of cartilage pathology. In addition to apoptosis, recent studies have highlighted ferroptosis as an important form of regulated cell death in degenerative and toxin-related cartilage injury. Ferroptosis is characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial damage, and collapse of antioxidant defenses, particularly the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis. Within this pathway, SLC7A11 mediates cystine uptake for glutathione synthesis [13, 14], whereas GPx4 detoxifies phospholipid hydroperoxides and suppresses membrane lipid peroxidation [15, 16]. It has been reported that T-2 toxin increases lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), and ferrous iron levels in chondrocytes while suppressing the system Xc⁻/GSH/GPx4 pathway [17], suggesting that ferroptosis contributes to cartilage injury in KBD. Nevertheless, the upstream molecular events that trigger ferroptosis in KBD chondrocytes remain unclear.

Oxidative stress is a well-recognized feature of KBD cartilage injury [18]. Under oxidative challenge, antioxidant enzyme systems are often compensatorily activated to limit free-radical accumulation. In our previous studies, serum superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities were increased in children with KBD, whereas the activity of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) was significantly decreased, indicating that GPxs dysfunction plays a critical role in KBD cartilage injury [19]. The GPxs family consists of three major subgroups, GPx1/2, GPx3/5/6, and GPx4/7/8, among which the function of GPx6 has not been fully investigated. Human GPx6 is a selenoprotein containing selenocysteine, whereas rodent GPx6 is a non-selenoprotein with cysteine at the active site [20]. Recent studies suggest that GPx6 participates in oxidative stress regulation. Its expression is suppressed in zearalenone-induced oxidative injury in porcine intestinal epithelial cells [21], low GPx6 expression is associated with impaired proliferation in mouse liver cancer cells [22], and GPx6 overexpression alleviates neurotoxicity in a Huntington’s disease model [23]. In addition, bioinformatic studies have linked GPx6 genetic variation to susceptibility to several diseases [24, 25]. However, whether GPx6 contributes to chondrocyte death and cartilage degeneration has not yet been reported.

Given the central role of redox imbalance in ferroptosis, we hypothesized that GPx6 may act as an upstream regulator of ferroptotic injury in KBD cartilage. In the present study, we used human KBD cartilage specimens, a T-2 toxin-induced rat model, Gpx6 knockout mice, and cultured chondrocytes to examine whether GPx6 downregulation promotes ferroptosis by suppressing the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis, thereby inducing mitochondrial lipid peroxidation and accelerating cartilage ECM degradation. This study aimed to clarify the role of GPx6 in ferroptotic cartilage injury and to provide new mechanistic evidence for the pathogenesis of KBD cartilage damage.

Paraffin sections of articular cartilage from children with KBD and age-matched controls were obtained from precious samples collected earlier by our laboratory. KBD samples were derived from finger joints of children aged 3–5 years who died from accidents or other diseases, and the diagnosis was based on the National Diagnostic Standard for Kashin-Beck Disease (WS/T 207–2010). Control samples were obtained from children aged 3–5 years from non‑endemic areas who died from traffic accidents or underwent amputation due to trauma. All sample collections followed the principle of informed consent. In this study, three KBD cases and three control children were included. Detailed sample information (including ID, age, sex, cartilage source, disease severity, and cause of death/surgery) is provided in Table S1.

Protein 3D structures were obtained from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). The SDF structure of the ligand was from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock 1.5.6. The protein-ligand complexes were visualized with PyMOL and Discovery Studio software.

The C28/I2 human chondrocyte cell line was generously provided by Dr. Mary B. Goldring (Hospital for Special Surgery, Cornell University, USA). Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (SH30023.01, Hyclone, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were treated with 24 ng/mL T-2 toxin (T002980, TRC, Canada) for 24 h or with Ferrostatin-1 (MCE, USA) at a concentration of 0–50 μm for 24 h. For combination experiments, cells were pretreated with 20 µM Ferrostatin-1 for 4 h and then exposed to 24 ng/mL T-2 toxin for 24 h; additionally, following GPX6 overexpression for 24 h, cells were treated with 10 µM Erastin or 5 µM RSL3 for 24 h, respectively.

C28/I2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 10³ cells/well). After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1–2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek). Cell viability was calculated relative to controls.

Four-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (60–80 g) were randomly divided into control and T-2 toxin groups (n = 8 per group). T-2 toxin was dissolved in a small volume of absolute ethanol and then diluted with 0.9% saline to the working concentration. Rat in the T-2 toxin group received T-2 toxin by gavage at 200 µg/kg weight/day for 4 weeks (this dose was selected on the basis of our previous rat model of cartilage injury [26]). Control rats received vehicle only. At the end of the experiment, rats were euthanized and knee tissues were collected for histological analysis. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines, and were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Health Science Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTUAE2024-1058).

Knee joint sections (4 μm) were prepared after fixation, decalcification, and paraffin embedding. Sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), Safranin O-Fast Green, or Toluidine Blue using standard protocols. Stained sections were imaged using a digital slide scanner (Axioscan 7, Zeiss, Germany) or a microscope (Olympus, Japan). For quantitative analysis, six high-power fields were randomly selected per section. The average optical density (AOD) of proteoglycan staining in each field was calculated using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, USA), AOD = integrated optical density/area of positive staining. The mean value of the six fields was taken as the representative value for that sample. Finally, individual patients (or animals) were used as biological replicates, with at least three biological replicates per group (n ≥ 3 per group).

After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigens were retrieved with 0.25% trypsin (or streptokinase/hyaluronidase for COL2A1). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against GPx6 (1:100, CABT-BL1673, Creative Diagnostics), COL2A1 (1:200, ab34712, Abcam), ACAN (1:200, ab315486, Abcam), MMP13 (1:200, 18165-1-AP, Proteintech), ADAMTS4 (1:200, 11865-1-AP, Proteintech), then HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. DAB was used for visualization, with hematoxylin counterstaining. For quantitative analysis, six high-power fields were randomly selected per section. The percentage of positive cells (positive cells/total cells × 100%) or the average optical density (AOD = integrated optical density/area of positive staining) of positively stained areas in each field was calculated using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. The mean value of the six fields was taken as the representative value for that sample. Finally, individual patients (or animals) were used as biological replicates, with at least three biological replicates per group (n ≥ 3 per group).

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using Trizol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622, Invitrogen, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green PCR premix (RR820A, Takara, Japan) and specific primers on an iQ5 Cycler (Bio-Rad, Germany). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with GAPDH as internal control. The primer sequences for the GPX6 gene are as follows: forward GTGTCCAGGTAGTGGCTTTGTCC, reverse GAGGTCGGAGGGCAGGAGTTC.

Tissues/cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was measured by BCA assay (P0010S, Beyotime). 20 µg protein/lane was separated by SDS-PAGE (PG212, Epizyme), transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore), blocked with 5% skim milk. Primary antibodies (overnight, 4 °C): GPx4 (1:1000, Immunoway), SLC7A11 (1:1000, Immunoway), SLC3A2 (1:1000, Immunoway), TFR1 (1:2000, Immunoway), ACSL4 (1:2000, Immunoway), GPx6 (1:1000, CABT-BL1673, Creative Diagnostics; 1:1000, PAD995Mu01, CLOUD-CLONE). Followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000, Immunoway). Bands were visualized by ECL (Millipore) and quantified with Quantity One (Bio-Rad) and ImageJ (NIH), normalized to GAPDH.

The second exon region of the GPx6-201 transcript (ENSMUST00000004453.8) was knocked out using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology, and this region contains a 154 bp coding sequence. Knockout mice were constructed by the company of Gempharmatech Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse tail tissues for genotyping to distinguish homozygous (Gpx6−/−), heterozygous (Gpx6+/−)), and wild-type (Gpx6⁺/⁺) mice. The genotyping results of Gpx6−/− knockout mice were shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Heterozygous mice were intercrossed to produce homozygous Gpx6−/− and wild-type Gpx6⁺/⁺ littermates. Male mice at three weeks of age were stratified by genotype and then assigned to either the control or T-2 toxin treatment group using a random number sequence, with 12 mice per genotype per group. The mice received daily gavage of T-2 toxin (200 µg/kg) or an equal volume of saline for 4 weeks, after which rib cartilage and knee tissues were collected.

PPI network was generated using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) and visualized with Cytoscape (Cytoscape 3.7.2, https://cytoscape.org/release_notes_3_7_2.html). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID online tool (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/), and results were displayed as bubble charts.

After treatment, cell pellets were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed in osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and observed under transmission electron microscopy.

For GPX6 gene knockdown, small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting GPX6 was designed and synthesized (Heyuan Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd), and then transfected into C28/I2 chondrocytes using the Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher, USA). Knockdown efficiency was assessed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting. For overexpression, the GPX6 coding sequence was cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) and transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000. Overexpression was confirmed by measuring the mRNA and protein levels.

Lipid peroxidation was assessed using BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe (Beyotime, S0043S, China). Cells were incubated with the probe for 30 min at 37 °C, washed, and imaged for red (reduction state) and green (oxidation state) fluorescence. Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using JC-1 staining (Beyotime, C2003S, China) under the same conditions, with red (aggregates) and green (monomers) fluorescence imaged.

MDA levels were measured using a lipid oxidation detection kit (S0131S, Beyotime, China) at 532 nm. SOD activity was assessed using WST-8 (S0101S, Beyotime, China) at 450 nm. The concentrations of glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) were measured using a glutathione assay kit (S0053, Beyotime, China), and the GSH/GSSG ratio was calculated.

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 23.0 statistical software (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Graphical representations were created using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Tukey test to correct for type I errors in multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. All experiments were independently repeated at least three biological replicates.

H&E staining showed that in normal children, articular cartilage exhibited clear stratification and morphologically normal chondrocytes (Fig. 1a). In KBD children, the superficial layer of articular cartilage remained relatively smooth, but the number of chondrocytes in the middle and deep layers was significantly reduced. In the deep layer, coagulative necrosis of chondrocytes was observed, characterized by pyknosis, fragmentation, and karyolysis, eventually leaving only red‑stained cell outlines (“ghost cells”). Local matrix staining loss resulted in focal or band‑like acellular areas. Around the necrotic foci, cell clusters were observed. IHC staining results showed that the GPx6-positive cell rates in all layers of KBD articular cartilage were lower than those in the control group, with the most significant downregulation of GPx6 observed in deep layer chondrocytes (Fig. 1b, c). These findings indicate that downregulation of GPx6 expression is involved in deep layer chondrocyte death and matrix degradation in KBD.

To investigate the mechanism of cartilage damage in KBD, a rat model of cartilage injury was established by exposure to T-2 toxin, a suspected pathogenic factor of KBD. H&E and Safranin O‑Fast Green staining showed that T-2 toxin exposure led to reduced matrix secretion, uneven staining, and focal loss in rat articular cartilage (Fig. 2a, black arrows), indicating cartilage ECM degradation. Immunohistochemical staining revealed that T-2 toxin downregulated GPx6 protein expression in articular cartilage (Fig. 2b). In vitro cell experiments further confirmed that T-2 toxin reduced GPx6 protein expression in C28/I2 chondrocytes (Fig. 2c, d). These results suggest that downregulation of GPx6 is closely associated with T-2 toxin‑induced cartilage damage. To explore whether there is a potential interaction between GPx6 and T-2 toxin, we performed molecular docking analysis using AutoDock software. The results showed that all subtypes of ( GPxs(Homo sapiens) family exhibited possible interactions with T-2 toxin (Figure S2). Binding affinity analysis indicated that GPx6 and GPx8 had relatively strong predicted binding energies (ΔG=−6.7 kcal/mol) (Fig. 2d). Further analysis revealed that GPx6 formed carbon‑hydrogen bonds with TRP130, THR47, and PHE129, among which TRP130 generated a phosphorus‑alkyl interaction with T-2 toxin, while LEU116, LEU119, LYS120, and ARG125 mediated alkyl interactions. These results suggest that some form of interaction may exist between GPx6 and T-2 toxin. Based on these in silico findings, it is hypothesized that T-2 toxin may interact with GPx6, thereby potentially affecting its expression level and possibly participating in cartilage matrix degradation. Therefore, GPx6 may be a potential target involved in T-2 toxin‑induced cartilage damage in Kashin‑Beck disease.

To investigate whether Gpx6 deficiency directly causes cartilage damage, we generated Gpx6 knockout (Gpx6−/−) mice and exposed them to T-2 toxin, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. Western blotting results showed that GPx6 protein levels in Gpx6⁻/⁻ mice were reduced by approximately 95% compared with WT mice (Fig. 3b, c). Safranin O-Fast Green and Toluidine Blue staining were used to evaluate the extracellular matrix of knee joint cartilage. The results showed that, compared with WT mice, Gpx6⁻/⁻ mice exhibited significantly decreased staining intensity in the superficial and middle zones of the articular cartilage, with complete loss in some areas (black arrows), indicating marked ECM degradation. Furthermore, T-2 toxin exposure further aggravated extracellular matrix degradation in Gpx6⁻/⁻ mice, as evidenced by a significant reduction in proteoglycan secretion compared with unexposed Gpx6⁻/⁻ mice. Immunohistochemical staining results showed that, compared with the WT control group, Gpx6⁻/⁻ mice exhibited reduced positive staining for COL2A1 and ACAN, and significantly increased expression of MMP13 and ADAMTS4 in the knee joint cartilage (Fig. 3d–g). After T-2 toxin exposure, the expression of COL2A1 and ACAN in Gpx6⁻/⁻mice was further decreased. These results suggest that Gpx6 deficiency may promote ECM degradation in mouse articular cartilage and exacerbate T-2 toxin‑induced cartilage damage.

To gain deeper insights into the biological function of GPx6, we performed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using the STRING database. Based on the interaction confidence score, GPx6 was found to interact with multiple proteins, including glutathione-disulfide reductase (GSR), peroxiredoxins 6 (PRDX6), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1α (PPARGC1A), glutathione synthetase (GSS), GPx4, and several members of the arachidonate 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX) family (Fig. 4a). GO biological process enrichment analysis indicated that GPx6 and its interacting proteins are primarily involved in processes related to lipoxin biosynthesis, long-chain fatty acid biosynthesis, linoleic acid metabolism, PPAR signaling pathway regulation, arachidonic acid metabolism, glutathione metabolism, lipid oxidation, and response to oxidative stress (Fig. 4b, c). KEGG pathway enrichment further linked GPx6 to glutathione metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, thyroid hormone synthesis, and ferroptosis pathway (Fig. 4d). Experimental validation showed that, compared with WT mice, the content of MDA was significantly elevated in the cartilage tissue of Gpx6−/− mice, while the activity of the antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase (SOD) and the GSH/GSSG ratio were markedly decreased (Fig. 4e-g). These results confirm that GPx6 deficiency disrupts the redox homeostasis of chondrocytes and promotes lipid peroxidation. Although GPx4 is a well-established key regulator of ferroptosis acting through glutathione metabolism, the relationship between GPx6 and ferroptosis still requires in-depth exploration.

To investigate whether GPX6 knockdown induces cartilage ECM degradation through the ferroptosis pathway, we employed siRNA technology to knockdown GPX6 expression in C28/I2 chondrocytes and treated the cells with the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1). The results showed that siGPX6 transfection reduced GPx6 protein expression by 76% (Fig. 5a). Based on CCK-8 assay results, 20 µM Fer-1 was selected for subsequent interventions (Fig. 5b). Transmission electron microscopy revealed that chondrocytes in the siGPX6 group exhibited characteristic ferroptosis-related mitochondrial damage, including increased mitochondrial density, reduced or absent cristae, and matrix granulation/vacuolization, which were significantly ameliorated by Fer-1 treatment (Fig. 5c). BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 staining showed that GPX6 knockdown increased mitochondrial lipid peroxidation (enhanced green fluorescence and diminished red fluorescence), an effect reversed by Fer-1 (Fig. 5d, f). JC-1 staining further demonstrated that GPX6 knockdown decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) (enhanced green fluorescence and diminished red fluorescence), which was partially restored by Fer-1 treatment (Fig. 5e, g). Western blot analysis showed that GPX6 knockdown downregulated the protein expression of GPx4, SLC7A11, and SLC3A2, and upregulated TFR1 and ACSL4 expression, while Fer-1 intervention partially restored these abnormal expressions but had no effect on GPx6 itself (Fig. 5h-n). These results suggest that GPX6 deficiency may induce ferroptosis in chondrocytes by inhibiting the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis.

To investigate whether KBD cartilage injury is mediated by GPx6 deficiency, we performed gain‑of‑function experiments in vitro. The pcDNA‑GPX6 plasmid was transfected into C28/I2 chondrocytes for 30 to 48 h. The results showed that GPX6 mRNA expression peaked at 42 h post‑transfection (Fig. 6a), whereas its protein expression increased in a time‑dependent manner (Fig. 6b, c). Subsequent functional assays were performed at the 48‑hour time point. Compared with the T‑2 toxin alone group, GPX6 overexpression not only significantly increased GPX6 mRNA levels (Fig. 6d), but also upregulated the mRNA and protein expression of COL2A1 and ACAN (Fig. 6e-i), effectively reversing the inhibitory effect of T‑2 toxin on ECM synthesis. These findings suggest that GPx6 may be a promising and effective target for the treatment of KBD cartilage injury.

To verify whether GPx6 deficiency induces chondrocytes injury through ferroptosis mediated by the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis, we performed GPX6 overexpression rescue experiments. Transmission electron microscopy showed that GPX6 overexpression significantly alleviated T-2 toxin‑induced mitochondrial damage in chondrocytes (Fig. 7a). Meanwhile, GPX6 overexpression effectively reduced T-2 toxin‑induced lipid peroxidation levels (measured by BODIPY and MDA assays) and ROS production, restored mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), and enhanced cellular antioxidant capacity (evidenced by increased SOD activity and elevated GSH/GSSG ratio) (Fig. 7b-e, Figure S3). Western blot analysis showed that GPX6 overexpression significantly reversed the inhibitory effect of T-2 toxin on the expression of GPx4, SLC7A11, and SLC3A2, and reduced the T-2 toxin‑induced upregulation of TFR1 and ACSL4 expression (Fig. 7f–l). These findings suggest that GPX6 overexpression may alleviate T-2 toxin‑induced chondrocyte injury by inhibiting ferroptosis mediated by the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis.

To further verify whether this protective effect depends on this signaling axis, we treated GPX6‑overexpressing cells with the ferroptosis activators Erastin or RSL3 to inhibit the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis. Western blot analysis showed that, compared with the control group, Erastin significantly downregulated the expression of SLC7A11, SLC3A2, and GPx4, and upregulated TFR1 and ACSL4; RSL3 downregulated GPx4 expression and upregulated TFR1 and ACSL4. Moreover, both activators markedly suppressed the expression of COL2A1 and ACAN. Compared with Erastin or RSL3 treatment alone, GPX6 overexpression partially reduced the levels of ACSL4 or TFR1, but failed to reverse the downregulation of SLC7A11 and GPx4, nor did it ameliorate the suppression of COL2A1 and ACAN (Fig. 8). Collectively, these results consistently indicate that the protective effect of GPX6 overexpression is markedly attenuated upon inhibition of the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis. Thus, the chondroprotective effect of GPX6 overexpression may depend on the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis.

Kashin-Beck disease is a chronic endemic osteoarthropathy in which chondrocyte death, cartilage matrix loss, and growth plate injury ultimately lead to irreversible skeletal deformity. Although T-2 toxin exposure has long been regarded as an important environmental risk factor, the molecular events connecting this exposure to chondrocyte death and matrix destruction have remained unclear.

In the present study, we preliminarily identified that GPx6 may represent a previously unrecognized potential upstream regulator of ferroptotic cartilage injury in KBD. GPx6 expression was markedly reduced in articular cartilage from children with KBD, especially in the deep zone where chondrocyte death and matrix loss were most evident. Meanwhile, T-2 toxin reduced GPx6 expression in rat cartilage and cultured chondrocytes, whereas Gpx6 deficiency led to marked matrix destruction in articular cartilage and enhanced susceptibility to T‑2 toxin exposure. These findings suggest that GPx6 deficiency may play a relatively important role in KBD‑like cartilage injury, rather than being merely a secondary bystander change.

At the cellular level, chondrocytes subjected to GPX6 knockdown exhibited typical ferroptotic characteristics, including abnormal mitochondrial morphology, accumulation of lipid peroxides, reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, and decreased protein expression of GPx4 and SLC7A11. The ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 partially alleviated these phenotypic damages but failed to restore GPx6 expression. In contrast, overexpression of GPX6 partially improved redox homeostasis, enhanced mitochondrial integrity, upregulated the SLC7A11/GPx4 signaling axis, and attenuated ECM degradation. Conversely, when the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis was inhibited by Erastin or RSL3, the protective effect conferred by GPX6 overexpression was markedly attenuated. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that GPx6 may act as a potential upstream regulator of the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis. A possible mechanism is that impaired GPx6 function may be associated with lipid peroxide accumulation, thereby disrupting intracellular redox homeostasis [27]. This imbalance may interfere with system Xc⁻ activity, possibly through oxidative modification or depletion of reducing equivalents, which might then hinder cystine uptake and deplete intracellular GSH [28]. The resulting GSH deficiency may in turn affect the activity of GPx4, which requires GSH as a cofactor, and ultimately may fail to effectively suppress the chain reaction of lipid peroxidation, thereby contributing to ferroptosis [15]. As a radical-trapping antioxidant, Ferrostatin-1 interrupts the chain reaction of lipid peroxidation by providing hydrogen atoms, acting at a downstream stage of ferroptosis execution [29–31]. This explains its ability to ameliorate the phenotypic damage without affecting upstream GPx6 expression, which indirectly supports the hypothesis that GPx6 may serve as a potential upstream regulator of this pathway. Therefore, enhancing GPx6 function may represent a potential strategy against exogenous toxin‑induced chondrotoxicity and provide some scientific clues for the prevention and treatment of cartilage injury in KBD.

Several aspects of this study strengthen the potential relevance of our findings to the disease. First, the reduction in GPx6 was demonstrated directly in articular cartilage from children with KBD, linking the experimental observations to human disease. Second, the combined use of T-2 toxin exposure and genetic loss-of-function models supports both environmental and intrinsic contributions to cartilage injury. Third, ECM changes were evaluated together with ferroptotic phenotypes, allowing chondrocyte death and matrix degradation to be interpreted within the same pathogenic framework. Notably, cartilage damage in Gpx6 knockout mice was relatively pronounced but not complete, and T‑2 toxin further aggravated the injury, suggesting that GPx6 deficiency may be an important but not exclusive contributing factor to KBD cartilage injury. Previous studies have demonstrated that apoptosis [32], pyroptosis [3], endoplasmic reticulum stress [11], and necroptosis [33] are all involved in KBD cartilage injury, indicating that multiple cell death modalities (i.e., PANoptosis) may coexist in this disease. In future studies, we will systematically apply inhibitors of apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis to dissect the respective contributions of different cell death pathways to KBD and T‑2 toxin‑induced cartilage injury, as well as the potential protective effects of GPx6 in these processes.

This study also has several limitations. First, because there are currently no new cases of KBD and the disease predominantly affects children and adolescents, obtaining human articular cartilage is extremely difficult. Only three age‑ and sex‑matched cases and controls were included in this study, leading to limited statistical power; the conclusions need to be validated in larger cohort studies. Second, although molecular docking suggested a possible interaction between T‑2 toxin and GPx6, the results are limited to in silico simulation and lack direct biophysical evidence (e.g., surface plasmon resonance, cellular thermal shift assay, or co‑immunoprecipitation). Future studies will attempt these experimental methods to further verify the actual interaction between T‑2 toxin and GPx6. Third, current data only suggest that GPx6 may be involved in regulating the SLC7A11/GPx4 pathway, but the precise mechanism remains unclear. For example, whether GPx6 regulates SLC7A11/GPx4 through redox‑sensitive signaling pathways such as NRF2, ATF4, p53, or NF‑κB requires further investigation. Finally, regarding species differences of GPx6, human GPx6 is a selenoprotein (with selenocysteine at its active site), whereas murine GPx6 is a cysteine‑containing homolog. There may be potential differences between the two in enzymatic activity, substrate affinity, antioxidant function, and susceptibility to selenium deficiency. Therefore, the mouse model used in this study may not fully recapitulate the function of human GPx6, and caution is needed when extrapolating the mouse data to humans. Future studies will consider using GPX6 humanized mice or primate models to more accurately evaluate the function of human GPx6 as a selenoprotein. Despite these limitations, our results preliminarily suggest that GPx6 downregulation may be associated with ferroptosis in KBD‑related chondrocytes and that maintaining GPx6 function or inhibiting ferroptosis may represent one potential strategy for preventing cartilage injury in KBD.

In summary, GPx6 may serve as a potential upstream regulator of cartilage injury in Kashin‑Beck disease. Downregulation of GPx6 promotes chondrocyte death and matrix degradation via SLC7A11/GPx4‑mediated ferroptosis, while GPX6 overexpression attenuates cartilage injury by restoring this axis and suppressing ferroptosis. These findings provide initial insights into the pathogenesis of KBD and the development of targeted intervention strategies.
