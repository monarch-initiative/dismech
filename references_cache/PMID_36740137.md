# Early identification of a 12-bp tandem duplication in TNFRSF11A encoding receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B (RANK): Clinical characterization and response to bisphosphonate therapy.
**Authors:** Craven M, Vajravelu ME, Shekdar KV, Levine MA, Mumm S, Whyte MP, Mancilla EE
**Journal:** Bone (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.bone.2023.116698](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bone.2023.116698)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Ultra-rare mendelian osteolytic disorders caused by different length in-frame activating duplications within exon 1 of TNFRSF11A encoding receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B (RANK) comprise familial expansile osteolysis (FEO), expansile skeletal hyperphosphatasia (ESH), early-onset familial Paget's disease of bone (PDB2), juvenile Paget's disease 2 (JPD2), and panostotic expansile bone disease (PEBD). FEO typically presents with childhood-onset deafness followed by resorption of permanent dentition, and then appendicular bone pain, fractures, and deformities from progressive focal expansile osteolytic lesions emerging from a background of generalized high bone turnover. An 18-bp duplication in TNFRSF11A has been reported in all kindreds with FEO, whereas a 12-bp duplication was found in the young man with PEBD complicated by a massive jaw tumor. We report the clinical course and successful treatment with bisphosphonates of a girl with the 12-bp duplication yet a skeletal phenotype seemingly milder than PEBD.

CASE PRESENTATION AND DISCUSSION: This 10-year-old girl presented for dental and orthodontic treatment and was found to have progressive external tooth root resorption. Speech delay was identified at age 18 months, and audiological evaluation showed both conductive and sensorineural hearing loss subsequently treated with a cochlear implant at age 3 years. Biochemical studies indicated increased bone turnover with elevated urinary N-telopeptide levels and serum alkaline phosphatase in the upper normal range. Low lumbar spine bone mineral density (BMD) was revealed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, but whole-body Technetium-99 m bone scintigraphy was normal. Genetic testing identified the identical de novo 12-bp duplication within exon 1 of TNFRSF11A harbored by the young man with PEBD and massive jaw tumor. Bisphosphonate treatment, initiated with one dose of intravenous zoledronic acid that caused prolonged hypocalcemia, then comprised weekly oral alendronate that decreased bone turnover markers and normalized her BMD.

CONCLUSION: Constitutive activation of RANK signaling should be considered a possible cause in any young person with rapid bone turnover, particularly in the context of early-onset deafness and/or root resorption of permanent teeth. Early diagnosis and anti-resorptive treatment, given judiciously to avoid sudden and prolonged hypocalcemia, may prevent further skeletal disease.

Development and function of osteoclasts (OCs) requires signaling through receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B (RANK), a cell-surface receptor for RANK ligand (RANKL) [1]. RANK is encoded by TNFRSF11A (chromosome 18q21.33) and germline, heterozygous, in-frame, activating, tandem duplications of differing lengths within the first exon of TNFRSF11A are associated with several ultrarare osteolytic disorders: familial expansile osteolysis (FEO: OMIM #174810), expansile skeletal hyperphosphatasia (ESH), early-onset familial Paget’s disease of bone (EoPDB or PDB2: OMIM #602080), juvenile Paget’s disease 2 (JPD2), and panostotic expansile bone disease (PEBD) [2]. The specific duplications within its encoded signal peptide constitutively activate RANK and lead to excessive OC formation and action independent of RANKL [2–4]. In contrast, biallelic loss-of-function mutations in TNFSRF11A cause an “osteoclast-poor” osteopetrosis (OPTB7: OMIM #612301), and an especially rare osteopetrosis variant called dysosteosclerosis (OMIM #224300) [5].

These dento-osseous disorders from TNFRSF11A duplications measuring 12, 15, 18, or 27 bps feature variable degrees of conductive hearing loss, resorption of permanent teeth, and high-turnover skeletal disease with severity apparently diminishing with longer duplication of the RANK signal peptide. However, some clinical variation has been reported among affected families and between different affected kindreds harboring the identical RANK duplication. FEO, reflecting the 18-bp duplication, is developmental with its earliest clinical feature, deafness, often diagnosed before 10 years-of-age, followed by permanent tooth resorption, and starting in the second decade of life appendicular bone pain, fractures, and deformities from focal expansile osteolytic lesions resembling the initial osteolytic phase of classic Paget’s disease of bone (PDB) [3,4,6–11]. Expansile osteolytic lesions occur typically in the appendicular skeleton and feature progressive osteoclastic resorption with medullary expansion, pain, fractures, and deformities wherein the affected bone eventually becomes fat-filled [9]. Bone turnover markers (BTMs) are elevated usually with normal serum calcium levels. The absence of evolution to sclerotic bone and lack of axial osteolytic lesions differentiates FEO from classic PDB (OMIM #167250). ESH, from a 15-bp duplication (also found in a girl with JPD2), features hyperostotic widening of long bones, osteolytic finger lesions, and episodic hypercalcemia [12–14]. The other mutations include 27-bp duplications identified in kindreds with PDB2, and the 12-bp duplication in the young man with early-onset deafness and tooth resorption who then developed remarkably severe expansile skeletal disease and a massive jaw tumor (PEBD) [15–17].

Here, we describe a second person with the 12-bp duplication in TNFRSF11A, a girl with early-onset deafness and tooth resorption but otherwise mild skeletal disease responsive to bisphosphonate therapy, suggesting that early diagnosis and treatment may prevent expansile bone lesions and their complications.

Parents and patient agreed to publication; consent for this was waived by our institutional review board.

This 10-year-old girl was referred when found during evaluation for orthodontic treatment to have external tooth root resorption. She had pain with eating and early resorption of her primary teeth and cervical external root resorption (ERR) of permanent teeth. Panoramic dental radiographs had disclosed root resorption of the permanent maxillary and mandibular central incisors with ongoing concerns for early loss of her permanent teeth (Fig. 1) as reported in 2020 by Macaraeg et al. [18] Three years later, cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan images showed continued root resorption of the permanent maxillary and mandibular central incisors (Fig. 2).

Speech delay, noted at 18 months-of-age, was attributed to combined conductive and sensorineural hearing loss. High resolution computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the temporal bones showed the handle of the malleus was present bilaterally (Fig. 3), whereas the remainder of the malleus appeared hypoplastic/dysplastic. Bilaterally, the incus was very hypoplastic and dysmorphic, and the stapes was not well visualized. The cochlea had a dysmorphic appearance with presence of a projection or small extra turn beyond the apical turn (Fig. 3). Bilateral cochlear implant surgery was successful at 34 months-of-age.

Growth along the 60th to 70th percentile was consistent with mid-parental height prediction. Weight was at the 50th to 75th percentile. She had no history of fractures or skeletal pain, and no family history of deafness, dental, or bone problems.

Biochemical studies of skeletal and mineral metabolism were consistent with increased bone turnover with elevated urine N-telopeptide (NTX ARUP Laboratories) and high-normal or mildly increased serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP: CHOP clinical laboratory and UPMC Central PA laboratories), but serum levels of calcium, phosphorus, parathyroid hormone, and magnesium were normal (Table 1). Vitamin D deficiency was identified by a serum 25(OH)D level of 15 ng/mL. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (Hologic QDR Discovery A S/N 70683) showed L1-L4 spine bone mineral density (BMD) of 0.62 g/cm2 (Z-score – 2.02) and normal total body (less head) BMD (Z-score 0.16). Skeletal scintigraphy with technetium-99 m was considered normal including symmetric tracer distribution (Fig. 4). Genetic testing (Blue-print Genetics, Seattle, WA, USA) using their “Skeletal Dysplasia with Abnormal Mineralization Panel Plus”, which provides both sequencing and deletion/duplication analysis, revealed the same heterozygous 12-bp tandem duplication of TNFRSF11A (c.52_63dup, p. Cys18_Leu21dup) that had been reported in 2014 in the young man with PEBD [17]. Parental genetic testing at the same laboratory for the duplication was negative, indicating that her duplication occurred de novo.

She was then treated with cholecalciferol to correct her vitamin D deficiency and given a single intravenous infusion of zoledronic acid, 0.0125 mg/kg. Asymptomatic hypocalcemia of 7.6 mg/dL (8.8–10.7 normal) occurred within 24 h post-infusion despite prophylactic pre-medication with oral calcium 700 mg three times per day and oral calcitriol 0.5 μg twice per day (Fig. 5). Her serum calcium level reached a nadir of 6.8 mg/dL 2.5 days post-infusion, improving after an increase in the calcium and calcitriol dosing to 1500 mg calcium four times per day and 1.5 μg calcitriol per day. Serum calcium decreased to 7.7 mg/dL twelve days post-infusion during weaning of her supplemental calcitriol and calcium doses. Calcitriol therapy ended 2.5 months post-infusion, and calcium was decreased to 500 mg orally once daily so that she met the recommended daily allowance (RDA). Her serum ALP and urinary NTX were normal 3 months post-infusion (Table 1, Fig. 6). She subsequently began low-dose oral alendronate (10 mg PO weekly) 6 months post-infusion, when her urine NTX had rebounded to approximately twice the upper normal limit for age. Bone turnover markers (BTMs) decreased to high-normal values after 6 months of alendronate therapy, with normal levels thereafter with continued alendronate treatment (Fig. 6). Follow-up DXA showed her L-spine BMD Z-score had improved from −2.02 (BMD 0.62 g/cm2) to a Z-score + 0.63 (BMD 0.82 g/cm2) and was then maintained at Z-score + 0.34 (BMD 0.92 g/cm2) after 22 months of weekly oral alendronate therapy (Table 2). Currently, she is active in sports and has had no fractures, bone pain, or bone deformity. There is no evidence of the jaw tumor that occurred in PEBD [17]. However, tooth resorption has progressed.

To verify that the 12-bp duplication in TNFRSF11A reported for the current patient was identical to that of the man reported with PEBD and jaw tumor, we amplified by PCR and Sanger-sequenced DNA from both subjects in our research laboratory at Washington University School of Medicine [12,13,17] to analyze exon 1 of TNFRSF11A encoding the signal peptide of RANK. The PCR amplicon was sequenced in both directions, showing the identical 12-bp duplication (Fig. 7) [17].

This 10-year-old girl manifested early-onset hearing loss, resorption of permanent dentition, low BMD, and increased BTMs. Despite unremarkable skeletal scintigraphy, her phenotype was suggestive of FEO (vide supra) [2–4,6–11]. Remarkably, however, sequence analysis of her TNFRSF11A gene showed that she did not carry the 18-bp duplication found in FEO, but instead harbored the 12-bp duplication in TNFRSF11A that we reported in the man with severe panostotic osteolytic disease designated PEBD [17]. Her mutation had occurred de novo, and because her siblings did not report features of FEO, no additional testing was deemed necessary.

As mentioned, in frame tandem duplications of different lengths in exon 1 of TNFRSF11A have been reported in seemingly different osteolytic disorders featuring RANK activation. FEO is caused by an 18-bp duplication in TNFRSF11A in all published cases, yet there is some variability in severity among kindreds and within families [2,3]. The patient reported here is heterozygous for the 12-bp duplication in TNFRSF11A (c.52_63dup, p.Cys18_Leu21dup) previously associated with the most severe among these disorders, PEBD [17].

The PEBD patient had a history of congenital deafness, shedding of permanent teeth, and bowing deformities of the limbs since age 4 years leading to an abnormal gait by age 11 years and multiple fractures by age 18 years [17]. Remarkably, at age 27 years he developed a large, hemorrhagic mandibular tumor with histopathology that differed from skeletal tumors (e.g., osteosarcoma) typically associated with RANK activation [17]. The basis for his jaw tumor remains uncertain, although possibly an additional, somatic, mutation may have arisen within his jaw.

The patient herein before bisphosphonate treatment seems to have manifested milder skeletal disease than the man with PEBD when he was her age. This may reflect phenotypic variability associated with this 12-bp duplication, as occurs in FEO carrying the 18-bp duplication in TNFRSF11A. The differing phenotypes with the 12-bp duplication may reflect unbalanced expression of the two TNFRSF11A alleles. Alternatively, it may reflect low level mosaicism for our current patient’s 12-bp duplication, recognizing neither parent carried her mutation. Hopefully, her early treatment with bisphosphonates will prevent the severe manifestations of PEBD.

RANK is an osteoclast cell surface receptor, which is dependent on signal peptide cleavage and a transmembrane domain to pass through the endoplasmic reticulum and golgi and then anchor in the plasma membrane. In vitro cell studies of the 15, 18, and 27 bp duplications showed the mutated RANK signal peptide was not cleaved [3,19]. Further cell studies showed that RANK proteins from the 15, 18, and 27 bp mutations stalled in the endoplasmic reticulum and were not detected at the plasma membrane [19]. The heterozygous 27 bp duplication mouse model recapitulated the early-onset Paget disease-like phenotype including osteolytic lesions [20] whereas the homozygous 27 bp duplication mouse developed osteopetrosis with complete absence of osteoclasts [20]. It seems that the wild type allele when combined with a tandem duplication allele accounts for the excess osteoclast activity characteristic of these disorders.

Anti-resorptive treatment options for disorders of accelerated RANKL/OPG/RANK/NF-κB signaling include bisphosphonates and denosumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against RANKL required for osteoclast formation and activity. To our knowledge, denosumab has not been tested in disorders of constitutive RANK activation, wherein the mutated RANK does not reach the cell membrane and is therefore not stimulated by RANKL [2]. Therefore, management of these RANK disorders has relied upon drugs with a post-RANK receptor mechanism of action, such as the bisphosphonates. In a young girl with JPD2 due to a 15-bp TNFRSF11A tandem duplication, pamidronate reduced BTMs but did not correct her bone deformity, or prevent further tooth loss or hearing loss [13]. Aminobisphosphonates may be needed to decrease heritable RANK activation, however, neither etidronate nor intravenous pamidronate corrected the advanced skeletal changes of the woman with ESH, although they helped manage her post-partum hypercalcemia [21]. One dose of intravenous zoledronic acid can produce rapid and sustained responses for classic Paget disease of bone, a disorder manifesting focal high bone turnover, and often associated with germline mutations of other genes affecting osteoclastogenesis and osteoclast action [22]. However, gradual reduction of bone turnover may be needed to avoid hypocalcemia in disorders of RANK activation discussed herein.

Our patient had prolonged hypocalcemia after receiving a single iv dose of zoledronic acid consistent with a marked pharmacologic reduction in osteoclastic bone resorption as well as a subsequent “hungry bones” phenomenon. There was no further hypocalcemia during low-dose oral alendronate therapy.

While treatment with bisphosphonates (oral alendronate or iv pamidronate) reduced BTMs in FEO, clinical effectiveness has varied [4,9]. Alendronate therapy improved BTMs and healed lytic bone lesions in some members of the 5-generation FEO kindred reported in 2002 by Whyte et al. [9], and a decrease in bone pain in response to alendronate and pamidronate treatment has been observed in some case reports [4,9]. Although the literature is unclear whether bisphosphonate treatment for these disorders slows resorption of the permanent dentition, our unpublished experience indicates that it does. In the girl we report here, early bisphosphonate therapy was associated with improved BTMs, increases in BMD, and prevention of osteolytic bone lesions, suggesting that we can improve the natural history of her RANK-associated disorder. Nevertheless, her dental resorption has persisted. Our current findings indicate that long-term study will be necessary to determine the long-term effectiveness of bisphosphonate therapy for these conditions.

We describe a young girl with a relatively mild skeletal phenotype despite her harboring the 12-bp in-frame tandem duplication in TNFRSF11A reported in the extraordinary patient with panostotic expansile bone disease and massive jaw tumor. A genetic basis for RANK activation should be considered in any individual with early-onset deafness from dysplastic ossicles or idiopathic resorption (external lysis) of permanent teeth. Treatment may prevent complication from, or heal, the skeletal disease. Low-dose oral bisphosphonates, currently seemingly necessary life-long, are effective in safely decreasing bone turnover and possibly bone pain and healing lytic lesions. Additional reports of these rare but highly informative mendelian disorders, together with the underlying mutation in TNFRSF11A, will reveal if the phenotypes are distinctive, or if instead there is a broad ranging continuum of disease expressivity.
