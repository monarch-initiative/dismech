# Morphologic and Histologic Assessment of Porcine Model of Legg-Calvé-Perthes Disease at a Clinically Relevant Time Point.
**Authors:** Taboada P, Trautmann D, Croley G, Lee J, Deng Z, Kim HKW
**Journal:** J Orthop Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jor.26093](https://doi.org/10.1002/jor.26093)

## Content

Previous studies using the porcine model of Legg-Calvé-Perthes Disease (LCPD) focused on treatments initiated 1 week following osteonecrosis (ON) induction, which represents the early avascular necrosis (AVN) stage (Stage Ia). Most patients, however, present to the clinic during later stages, like the late AVN (Stage Ib) or the early fragmentation stage (Stage IIa), when development of deformity and revascularization is initiated. This study's purpose was to determine the histologic and morphologic changes in the porcine model at 3 weeks after the ON induction. We hypothesized that the 3-week time point better represents a clinically relevant stage of LCPD when patients present to clinic. Eighteen piglets underwent ON induction surgery. Femoral heads were assessed using X-ray, histology, and micro-CT at 3 weeks post-ON. X-ray and histologic assessments revealed subchondral fracture, mild femoral head deformity, variable degree of revascularization (33 ± 30%, range 0%-88%), and early necrotic bone remodeling changes with significantly decreased osteoblast (p < 0.0001) and osteoclast numbers (p < 0.0001) compared to the normal side. Taken together, the results of this study provide comprehensive morphologic and histologic evidence of the late AVN stage (Stage 1b) in the porcine model, which is a clinically relevant time point to develop and test new treatment strategies. A wide variability of revascularization was seen at the 3-week time point, which stresses the importance of evaluating femoral head perfusion and individualizing treatment even in the early stage of AVN.

Legg−Calvé−Perthes Disease (LCPD) is an idiopathic childhood hip disorder, characterized by loss of blood supply to the femoral head leading to femoral head osteonecrosis (FHO). Over a course of 2−5 years, X‐ray changes progress from an intact sclerotic femoral epiphysis to mild deformity with subchondral fracture, to fragmentation and resorption of the necrotic epiphysis, to re‐ossification and healing over time. LCPD follows four distinct chronological stages as first reported by Waldenstrom: avascular necrosis (AVN) (Stage I), fragmentation (Stage II), re‐ossification (Stage III), and residual (Stage IV) [1]. Each of the first three stages is further divided into early (denoted by “a”) or late (denoted by “b”) based on specific X‐ray changes [2] (Figure 1). A recent study of early‐stage LCPD presentation shows that over 95% of patients present to clinic at the late AVN stage (Stage Ib, 56%) or early fragmentation stage (Stage IIa, 41%) and only 3% at the early AVN stage (Stage Ia) [3]. The reason for this is that Stage Ia represents a clinically silent, early stage when there is no femoral head deformity or shape change in the femoral epiphysis. Development of femoral head deformity, as in Stage Ib and Stage II, is associated with various symptoms and signs, including pain, limping, decreased hip motion, and hip dysfunction. Previous studies have found that early intervention at Stage Ib by non‐weightbearing of the affected leg or a hip containment surgery can significantly reduce the progression of femoral head deformation and even bypass the fragmentation stage, which stresses the clinical importance of developing and timing a model that mimics this stage [4, 5, 6].

The porcine osteonecrosis (ON) model is a well‐established model of LCPD which displays histopathologic and radiographic features closely resembling human disease [7]. It is the most commonly used large animal model of LCPD. FHO is induced in piglets via tying a ligature tightly around the femoral neck and transecting the ligamentum teres [7], inducing the ischemic necrosis of the whole femoral head. Previous studies using this model have focused on the efficacy of various treatments in the early AVN stage, that is, initiation of treatment at 1‐week post‐ON induction before the development of femoral head deformity. Bisphosphonate therapy, whether administered systemically or locally 1 week after ON induction, demonstrated significantly better preservation of trabecular bone volume and trabecular number 7 weeks later [8]. Similarly, administration of anti‐resorptive agent, osteoprotegerin, at 1 week post‐ON induction showed significant efficacy in preserving the femoral head structure [9]. Further, local intraosseous administration of bone anabolic agent, bone morphogenic protein‐2 (BMP‐2), resulted in significantly increased bone formation [10]. Additionally, saline washing of the necrotic femoral head at 1‐week post‐ON induction removed necrotic debris and inflammatory factors, leading to decreased macrophages and osteoclasts and increased new bone formation [11].

The findings from previous studies using the porcine ON model have limited potential for clinical translation as the 1‐week time point for intervention does not address the predominant stage of the femoral head at the first clinic visit. Many patients often present in the late AVN stage (Stage 1b) or early fragmentation stage (stage IIa) when there is the presence of subchondral fracture, initiation of mild femoral head deformity, and pain [12]. Currently, there is a lack of studies in the porcine model that represent this critical time point of disease progression. The purpose of this study was to determine the histologic and morphologic changes in the porcine model at 3 weeks after the ON induction. We hypothesized that the 3‐week time point better represents the clinically relevant Stage Ib of LCPD, characterized by the presence of subchondral fractures and mild femoral head deformity which are commonly observed in patients at initial presentation.

Approval for the study was granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center. Yorkshire and Duroc piglets, aged 6−8 weeks and weighing 20−25 kg, were sourced from a commercial breeder. Animal care and management were overseen by certified laboratory animal technicians and veterinarians. Each pig was housed in a cage measuring 12 square feet (1.1 square meters) and received feeding twice daily.

Eighteen piglets underwent right hip surgery with femoral neck ligation following transection of the ligamentum teres, as previously described [7]. Two thick resorbable sutures (#1 Vicryl Coated [Polyglactin 910] Suture, Ethicon) were used for ligation. Left, unoperated femoral heads served as normal controls. Three weeks after the ON induction surgery, the piglets were killed, and their femoral heads were retrieved for gross and radiographic evaluations and histologic preparation.

Anteroposterior radiographs were obtained and used to determine the epiphyseal quotient (EQ), which indicates the degree of femoral head deformity. EQ is calculated by dividing the maximum height by the maximum width of the femoral head, as previously described (Figure 3A) [13]. The presence of subchondral fracture was assessed using X‐ray images of both intact and transected femoral heads. Following X‐ray imaging, the femoral heads were sectioned coronally into 5 to 6‐mm‐thick slices using a bandsaw and radiographed again for further evaluation. The femoral head slices were then fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin before being transferred to a 70% ethanol solution for micro‐CT scanning.

We used a micro‐CT scanner (Skyscan 1172, Bruker‐micro‐CT, Kontich, Belgium) set at 50 kV and 200 mA, with a resolution of 13.3 μm/pixel, as previously described [14]. Image reconstruction was performed with NRecon (version 1.7.0.4; Bruker‐micro‐CT). The reconstructed images were binarized to a uniform threshold using CTAn (version 1.13.5.1; Bruker‐micro‐CT), focusing on the necrotic epiphysis while excluding the primary and secondary spongiosa beneath the epiphyseal cartilage. The CTAn software was utilized to compute three‐dimensional morphometric parameters, including total tissue volume, bone volume, empty void volume, trabecular thickness, number, and separation. The images of the whole and sectioned femoral heads were acquired using CTvox software (version 3.3.0.0; Bruker‐micro‐CT).

Following micro‐CT scanning, the femoral heads were placed in a series of graded ethanol solutions. Anterior segment of the femoral head was embedded in methyl methacrylate, while posterior segment was decalcified using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and embedded in paraffin. Both segments were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The methyl methacrylate sections were stained with MacNeal tetrachrome or for tartrate‐resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) as previously detailed [11, 15].

Femoral head sections were divided into quadrants after digital scanning to allow for precise anatomical localization of histological changes. The femoral head image was divided along its longest diameter into two 25% length regions on each end, labeled as the “medial peripheral” (MP) and “lateral peripheral” (LP) regions. The central 50% length region was subdivided into “upper central” (UC) and “lower central” (LC) regions (Figure 2A).

Tissue sections were stained with H&E and MacNeal tetrachrome for histologic confirmation of subchondral fracture and assessment of revascularization and healing. Necrotic area was defined by the presence of cells with pyknosis and karyolysis of nuclei, abundance of empty osteocyte lacunae, necrotic cell debris filling the marrow space, and the absence of osteoblasts or osteoclasts on trabecular surfaces. For the purpose of quantifying revascularization following histologic features were used; areas with the presence of cells with normal nuclei, capillaries, and sinusoids in the marrow space, fibrovascular tissue, increased cellular density, hematopoietic cells, or the presence of osteoblasts and osteoclasts on trabecular bone surfaces.

To quantify the percentage of revascularization, ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA; https://imagej.net/) was used. Regions of interest (ROI) were drawn on every x4 image from the entire femoral head obtained from the anterior and posterior sections of the transected femoral head. The percentage of revascularization was calculated by dividing the area of revascularized tissue by the total area of the femoral head. The final percentages of revascularization were averaged between the anterior (MacNeal) and posterior (H&E) sections, ensuring a more comprehensive assessment.

The MacNeal tetrachrome and TRAP‐stained sections were imaged at x10 magnification using the OSTEOIMAGER Scanning Microscope System (BIOQUANT Image Analysis, Nashville, TN). These metrics were analyzed using BIOQUANT OSTEO software (version 16.1.60, BIOQUANT Image Analysis, Nashville, TN). Sampling was performed on a tissue area of at least 40 mm² within each femoral epiphysis, with a minimum distance of 500 μm from the chondro‐osseous junction to exclude primary and secondary spongiosa. The MacNeal tetrachrome sections were used to assess bone surface, osteoblast number, and confirm the revascularized area. The TRAP‐stained sections were used to quantify osteoclast number. The numbers of osteoblasts and osteoclasts were normalized by bone surface. All metrics were calculated per the guidelines set by the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research [16].

Paired t‐tests were used to compare the values between the left side (control) and right side (ON). All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10, GraphPad Software) and SPSS software (version 26; IBM). A p < 0.05 was considered significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Subchondral fractures were identified by gross visualization, X‐ray, histology, and µCT imaging (Figure 2A,B). We found subchondral fractures in 8 out of the 18 ON femoral heads. The subchondral fractures were predominantly located within the LP (6/8, 75%), reflecting the area's role as the primary weight‐bearing zone of the femoral head. Both the central (UC) and MP regions exhibited subchondral fractures in (4/8, 50%) and (5/8, 62.5%) of the samples, respectively (Figure 2C).

At 3 weeks post‐ON induction, the femoral heads exhibited mild to no clinically observable deformity (Figure 3A). Among the 18 ON samples, 8 (44.5%) had no deformity (< 3% femoral head height loss), while 10 (55.5%) had mild deformity (3%–25% femoral head height loss). The mean EQ of the ON heads was significantly decreased compared to the control heads (control 0.452 ± 0.02 vs. ON 0.426 ± 0.04, p = 0.01) (Figure 3B).

The revascularization of the ON heads varied among different animals (Figure 4A), with 16 of 18 samples showing revascularized tissue. In ON samples, the mean revascularized area was 36 ± 34%, with values ranging from 0% to 98%. Five samples exhibited less than 5% revascularization, while four samples had revascularization > 70% (Figure 4B). The MP region had the highest percentage of samples showing revascularization (16/16 samples; 100%), while the remaining regions had less percentage of samples showing revascularization: LP (10/16, 63%), UC (8/16, 50%), and LC (10/16, 63%) (Figure 4C).

The osteoblast count normalized to the bone surface (Ob/BS) (mm) was significantly lower in the ON side compared to the control side (control: 5.39 ± 2.47 vs. ON 1.63 ± 2.62, p < 0.0001) (Figure 5A,B). The osteoclast count per bone surface (Oc/BS) (mm) was also significantly lower in the ON side compared to the control side (control: 2.33 ± 1.01 vs. ON: 0.23 ± 0.47, p < 0.0001) (Figure 5A,C).

We performed a comprehensive assessment of histological and morphological changes in a porcine model of LCPD at the 3‐week time point following the induction of FHO. This study was undertaken to address the gap in knowledge of specific histopathological and radiographic changes that occur in a later stage of FHO in the piglet model, which is the most commonly used large animal model of LCPD. Of the 18 piglets studied, eight demonstrated subchondral fracture within the femoral head and ten demonstrated mild femoral head deformity, important radiographic features that mirrors radiographic observations in the late AVN or early fragmentation stage of LCPD. X‐ray and micro‐CT evaluation, however, did not show a presence of vertical bone fissure or epiphyseal fragmentation, indicating that the 3‐week post‐ON induction time point is more consistent with the late AVN stage (Stage Ib) than the early fragmentation stage (Stage IIa). Furthermore, the absence of significant difference of micro‐CT trabecular parameters between the normal and ON sides supports the conclusion of this time point represents the late AVN stage. A significant increase in the osteoclast number associated with the fragmentation stage was also not observed [17, 18].

It is interesting to note that approximately 30% of patients with LCPD develop subchondral fracture at the AVN stage [19] which is slightly lower than 44% of ON femoral heads (8 out of 18) that developed subchondral fracture in this study. Our observation of these fractures at 3 weeks post‐ON induction supports the results from previous porcine studies [13] demonstrating that femoral head mechanical strength begins declining as early as 2 weeks post‐ON induction, suggesting that some fractures may have started forming before our study's time point.

Further studies showed significantly increased calcium content of the immature trabecular bone and increased nanoindentation modulus in the subchondral region, making the subchondral bone more brittle and susceptible to microcrack formation and subsequent subchondral fracture with continued mechanical loading [17, 18]. The observed subchondral fractures in our study were notably concentrated in the weight‐bearing LP region, a location likely associated with greater mechanical stress as outlined by Wen et al.'s biomechanical analysis of the lateral pillar [20], underscoring the significant biomechanical factors implicated in the pathogenesis of femoral head deformity in FHO. A recent MRI study also demonstrated dynamic deformity of the femoral head in the early‐stage LCPD with weight bearing [21].

Quantification of femoral head deformity through the EQ revealed a mild but statistically significant reduction in mean EQ values in the ON heads compared to control heads, indicative of an early femoral head deforming process. These findings support the clinical relevance of the porcine model at 3 weeks post‐ON induction in recapitulating key pathophysiological aspects of the late AVN stage of LCPD, particularly in elucidating the initiation and progression of femoral head deformity characteristic of the disease [22, 23]. Histologic features during this stage included subchondral fractures with separation from subchondral bone at the deep layer of the articular cartilage—observed in 44% of ON femoral heads—along with early signs of trabecular compaction fracture and disorganization. Significantly decreased osteoblast and osteoclast numbers on the bone surface and the lack of trabecular changes seen on micro‐CT evaluation of non‐subchondral region of the femoral head are consistent with the late AVN stage. As the necrotic femoral heads enter the fragmentation stage, significantly increased osteoclastic resorption of the necrotic bone is expected, occurring at a time when femoral head deformity progresses alongside further mechanical weakening. While previous preclinical studies which initiated therapeutic intervention at 1 week post‐ON induction provide a proof of concept for various treatments discussed in the introduction section, initiating therapeutic intervention at 3 weeks post‐ON may provide better outlook of treatment effect at a more clinically relevant time point.

Evaluation of revascularization patterns within necrotic femoral heads revealed substantial variability and heterogeneity. In our study, assessment of revascularization was performed histologically, and it ranged from 0% to 98% with the mean of 36 ± 34%. This variability aligns with findings from early‐stage LCPD, where a gadolinium‐enhanced perfusion MRI study showed a wide range of femoral head perfusion (5%−70% with the mean of 35 ± 16%) [24]. One important consideration when interpreting revascularization findings is that our assessment was limited to histologic sections of the femoral head rather than whole‐head perfusion imaging, as used in clinical perfusion MRI studies. While prior studies suggest that the anterior region is slower to revascularize, our approach of averaging revascularization between anterior and posterior sections provides a more balanced measure of femoral head perfusion. While some variability of revascularization was expected, it was surprising to see four samples with > 70% revascularization at the 3‐week post‐ON timepoint. This raised the question of whether there was a failure of suture ligature to produce FHO. Two observers carefully examined histologic sections of these samples and found clear evidence of cell necrosis in the marrow space and empty lacunae in trabecular bone confirming ischemic ON. A previous study of the piglet model (n = 10) using gadolinium‐enhanced perfusion MRI also showed a complete absence of blood flow in all femoral heads at 48 h [25]. Interestingly, early evidence of revascularization starting from the periphery of the femoral head was seen as early as 1 week post‐ON induction in the study. It is possible that some animals have increased angiogenic response while some have delayed angiogenic response after FHO, revealing an opportunity to investigate the factors associated with individual variability in angiogenesis. There is a clear need for further research to identify factors influencing revascularization and to develop individualized treatment strategies based on the level of revascularization present in the femoral head. It is notable that while revascularization was increased in some samples, the numbers of osteoblasts and osteoclasts were still significantly decreased in the ON samples. These findings suggest that the femoral heads were in a relatively early phase of the revascularization process, where recruitment of immune and repair cells was taking place without differentiation of progenitor cells into osteoblasts and osteoclasts for necrotic bone remodeling.

In summary, our findings underscore the clinical relevance and translational potential of the piglet ON model in recapitulating key pathophysiological features of late AVN stage of LCPD. By providing a nuanced understanding of histological and morphological changes at the 3‐week time point post‐ON induction, our study serves as a foundational platform for elucidating the intricate mechanisms underpinning ON progression. The availability of a large animal model at a clinically relevant stage of FHO will advance the development of targeted therapeutic interventions tailored to address the evolving pathophysiology of LCPD.

This study has several limitations. The piglet model of LCPD, while useful, does not fully replicate the human condition of LCPD. However, the model does provide the convenience of rapid transition from the early AVN stage to the late AVN stage which may take several months or more in humans. Due to tissue damage during histologic processing, the osteoblast count for two samples could not be manually reviewed, reducing the cohort size from n = 18 to n = 16. Another limitation of our study is that revascularization assessment was based on histologic sections rather than whole‐head imaging.
