# Analysis of compound heterozygous and homozygous mutations found in peripheral subunits of human respiratory Complex I, NDUFS1, NDUFS2, NDUFS8 and NDUFV1, by modeling in the E. coli enzyme.
**Authors:** Alkhaldi HA, Vik SB
**Journal:** Mitochondrion (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.mito.2022.11.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mito.2022.11.007)

## Content

Respiratory Complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) is composed of 45 subunits, seven mitochondrially-encoded and 38 imported. Mutations in the nuclearly-encoded subunits have been regularly discovered in humans in recent years, and many lead to cardiomyopathy, Leigh Syndrome, and early death. From the literature, we have identified mutations at 17 different sites and constructed 31 mutants in a bacterial model system. Many of these mutations, found in NDUFS1, NDUFS2, NDUFS8, and NDUFV1, map to subunit interfaces, and we hypothesized that they would disrupt assembly of Complex I. The mutations were constructed in the homologous E. coli genes, nuoG, nuoCD, nuoI and nuoF, respectively, and expressed from a plasmid containing all Complex I genes. Membrane vesicles were prepared and rates of deamino-NADH oxidase activity measured, which indicated a range of reduced activity. Some mutants were also analyzed using recently developed assays of assembly, time-delayed expression, and co-immunoprecipitation, which showed that assembly was disrupted. With compound heterozygotes, we determined which mutation was more deleterious. Construction of alanine mutations allowed us to distinguish between phenotypes that were caused by loss of the original amino acid or introduction of the mutant residue.

Mitochondrial Complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) of the mammalian electron transport chain comprises 45 protein subunits, including one found in two copies. Thirty-eight of these subunits are encoded by 37 nuclear genes, and the proteins are imported into the mitochondrion. The seven remaining protein subunits are encoded by mitochondrial genes. The three essential activities of Complex I are carried out by fourteen core subunits: NADH oxidation, ubiquinone reduction, and proton translocation. The core subunits appear to be found in all examples of Complex I, including bacteria. The seven mitochondrially-encoded subunits constitute the membrane-embedded core subunits. The seven nuclearly-encoded core subunits are found in the peripheral arm of Complex I, which extends into the mitochondrial matrix, or the cytoplasm in the case of bacteria (for recent reviews see (Agip et al., 2019; Dang et al., 2020; Kampjut and Sazanov, 2022; Padavannil et al., 2021; Parey et al., 2020)).

The core subunits of the matrix arm in humans include NDUFS1, NDUFS2, NDUFS3, NDUFS7, NDUFS8, NDUFV1, and NDUFV2. These subunits contain a flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and all iron–sulfur (FeS) clusters necessary for electron transfer to ubiquinone. In some bacteria, including E. coli, genes for two subunits are fused, resulting in the homologous subunits NuoG, NuoCD, NuoB, NuoI, NuoF, and NuoE, respectively. NDUFV1 (NuoF) contains the flavin, which is reduced directly by NADH. It also contains FeS cluster N3 which accepts electrons from the flavin. The next three FeS clusters in the electron transport chain are found in NDUFS1 (NuoG): N1b, N4 and N5. In E. coli, NuoG also contains a fourth FeS cluster, N7, which is not involved in the electron transport to ubiquinone. The next steps in electron transport are FeS clusters N6a and N6b, found in NDUFS8 (NuoI). The final FeS cluster is N2, found in NDUFS7 (NuoB). Subunits NDUFS2 and NDUFS3 (NuoCD) contain no FeS clusters, but they have extensive interactions with other subunits. An additional FeS cluster N1a, found in NDUFV2 (NuoE), has a role in preventing superoxide formation at the flavin site. In addition to the core subunits, mammalian Complex I contains 31 so-called supernumerary (or accessory) subunits (Elurbe and Huynen, 2016; Vinothkumar et al., 2014). The structure and pathogenicity of human mutations in the supernumerary subunits has been recently reviewed (Dang et al., 2020; Padavannil et al., 2021). The structures of mitochondrial Complex I from several species, including human (Gu et al., 2016), ovine (Fiedorczuk et al., 2016), bovine (Blaza et al., 2018), mouse (Agip et al., 2018) and yeast (Grba and Hirst, 2020; Parey et al., 2018) have been determined.

The membrane arm contains the subunits that translocate protons from the matrix to the inner membrane space. These core subunits are all encoded by mtDNA: ND1, ND2, ND3, ND4, ND4L, ND5, and ND6. Two subunits interact extensively with the peripheral arm, ND1 and ND3, and together with NDUFS2, NDUFS3, NDUFS7 and NDUFS8, form the core of the Q-module. In addition to three FeS clusters, the Q-module forms the ubiquinone binding site. The other five subunits primarily contribute to proton translocation. They include the three large, antiporter-like subunits ND2, ND4, and ND5 that are functionally linked through a series of interacting charged residues.

Complex I from E. coli has been a useful model system for the mitochondrial enzyme. Recently, nearly full structures have been obtained by cryo-EM (Kolata and Efremov, 2021; Schimpf et al., 2022), and the bacterial enzyme appears very similar to the core subunits of the mitochondrial enzyme. The structures of Complex I from human and from E. coli are compared in Fig. 1. The enzyme from E. coli contains seven membrane subunits that are homologous to the seven mitochondrially encoded ones in human Complex I. All its subunits are encoded by the nuo operon (Weidner et al., 1993). These genes can be expressed from an inducible plasmid in a strain deleted for the nuo operon (Amarneh et al., 2006), allowing for simple comparison of mutants (Zhang et al., 2022).

In this project, seventeen human clinical mutations were selected that were found in patients with various conditions thought to be associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, including cardiomyopathy (Forte et al., 2019) and Leigh syndrome (Bakare et al., 2021; Chang et al., 2020; Loeffen et al., 1998). They occur at subunit interfaces between core subunits, and therefore, seemed likely to disrupt assembly. Evaluations of the pathogenicity of these mutations are described at the ClinVar (Henrie et al., 2018) site (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), and ranged from “pathogenic” to “conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity” to “uncertain significance”. All were modeled at corresponding positions in the E. coli proteins and analyzed. In general, a second mutation was constructed, in which the residue was changed to alanine. This helped determine whether loss of function is associated with the loss of the original amino acid, or by the introduction of the mutant amino acid. Furthermore, many of these mutations are compound heterozygous, and the bacterial system allows the mutations to be analyzed separately to determine the phenotype of each. In addition, we applied methods developed recently in this lab to determine whether assembly of Complex I was disrupted.

The activity assays were modified for the Epoch 2 plate reader (BioTek, Burlington, VT) from methods described previously (Amarneh et al., 2006; Michel et al., 2011). To avoid oxidation by the alternative NADH oxidase in E. coli membranes we used deamino-NADH (dNADH) (Matsushita et al., 1987). The dNADH oxidase activity was assayed by using oxygen as a terminal electron acceptor. NADH oxidase activity assays were started with 0.25 mM dNADH (extinction coefficient 6.22 mM−1 cm−1). The dNADH oxidase activity assay was performed in 50 mM MOPS, 10 mM MgCl2, pH 7.3 at 20°C, and the absorbance was monitored at 340 nm for 2 min. The uncoupler FCCP was added to a final concentration 1 μM from 1 mM ethanol stock. The rate of dNADH oxidase from membranes of BA14/pBAD33(A-N) was about 1.0 μmol dNADH per min per mg protein. Proton translocation assays were conducted by measuring the fluorescence quenching of the acridine dye ACMA (9-amino 6-chloro 2-methoxy acridine) as a ΔpH indicator using excitation and emission wave lengths of 410 and 490 nm, respectively. Proton translocation assays were performed in 50 mM MOPS, 5 mM MgCl2, 50 mM KCl pH7.3) at 20°C. 1 μM valinomycin, was included to eliminate the buildup of a membrane potential during NADH oxidation, and ACMA was 1 μM. The reactions were initiated with dNADH (0.25 mM) and after about 2 min the proton gradient was collapsed by the addition of FCCP (1 μM) from 1 mM ethanol stock.

Subunit NDUFS2 in humans has 457 amino acids and a molecular mass of 51.8 kDa. It bears about 40% sequence identity to the C-terminal 364 amino acids of the NuoCD subunit of E. coli Complex I. The N-terminal 232 amino acids of NuoCD are structurally similar to NDUFS3 in humans, while the sequence identity is low. These subunits are found near the junction of the peripheral and membrane arms of Complex I. They contain no FeS clusters, but NDUFS2 forms part of the ubiquinone binding site. Three clinical mutations in NDUFS2, R138Q, Y141C, and R228Q were modeled in the nuoCD gene as R270Q, Y273C, and R360Q. These residues are conserved among E. coli, T. thermophilus, ovine, and human species. The locations of the mutations for the human and the E. coli proteins are shown in Fig. 2. Residue R138 is near NDUFS8 (NuoI) and the N2 FeS cluster of NDUFS7 (NuoB). Residue Y141 is also at an interface, near the N2 FeS cluster. Residue R228 is nearby at an interface with NDUFS8 (NuoI). A female child, born of healthy non-consanguineous parents, with a healthy older brother, was diagnosed within two hours after birth with elevated plasma lactate, difficulty in swallowing and feeding, and respiratory arrest. They found a mutation human R138Q in NDUFS2 (NuoCD_R270Q), and the patient was compound heterozygous with R333Q, also in NDUFS2. Her fibroblasts showed an increased level of oxidative stress, Complex I activity was low, and assembly appeared to be about 50% of normal. The patient died at 3.5 months (Tuppen et al., 2010). The mutation Y141C (NuoCD _Y273C) was first identified in an individual during a screen of candidates for mitochondrial disease, but the patient did not have any Complex I phenotype (Tuppen et al., 2010). It is classified as a mutation of uncertain significance by ClinVar (Henrie et al., 2018). The human mutation R228Q (NuoCD _R360Q) was first identified as homozygous in two siblings with consanguineous parents. They suffered from optic atrophy and cardiomyopathy, and both died within 2 years. Another sibling was reported healthy (Loeffen et al., 2001).

In E. coli, R270A,Q, Y273A,C, and R360A,Q, were constructed and moved to the expression vector pBAD33(A-N). Membrane vesicles were prepared, and enzyme activity assays were measured. With R270A,Q and Y273A,C membranes had very low levels of dNADH oxidase activity, measuring less than 10% of the wild type rate. R360Q had a reduced level of dNADH oxidase activity, 82 ± 3%, as a compared to wild type, while the R360A variant had a higher activity of 90 ± 2%. In proton translocation assays, all mutants showed a rate corresponding to the dNADH oxidase activity (Fig. 3), as would be expected for mutations in the peripheral arm. The presence of NuoCD in membrane vesicles was confirmed by SDS gel electrophoresis, and immunoblotting with anti-CD antibody (Fig. 4A). It shows that NuoCD_Y273A in membranes was lower than all of the other substitutions. In native gels, a complex corresponding to the size of Complex I (540kDa) for all samples was detected using anti-CD and anti-HA antibodies, with the exception of Y273A, in which Complex I level was greatly decreased (Fig. 4B,C). When stained for the membrane subunit N using the HA antibody, a faster moving band was prominent, probably indicating a complex lacking the peripheral arm (Fig. 4C). These results suggest that NuoCD_Y273A might have limited ability to assemble into Complex I, or that the resulting complex is unstable to the native gel procedure. When a native gel was used for an in-gel assay, similar bands of activity corresponding to full-size of Complex I could be seen in each lane. This indicates that a low level of Complex I formed in the Y273A mutant, and the color developed over a long incubation time (Fig. 4D).

R360Q showed only a modest effect on activity when expressed from vector pBAD33(A-N), and no effect on the level of Complex I assembled, so a different test of its ability to assemble was applied by expressing pBAD33(A-I). For the wild type genes, a complex containing all peripheral subunits B-CD-E-F-G-I can be immunoprecipitated after expression of this pla. Membrane and supernatant fractions were prepared and solubilized by dodecyl maltoside. Samples were immunoprecipitated with the HA antibody, targeting NuoG. pBAD33(A-I) was used in the positive control and IgG was used as negative control. Samples were separated into membrane and supernatant fractions as shown in Fig. 5 for subunits B, CD, E, F, and I. Results show that NuoCD and NuoI are both predominantly found in the membrane fraction for both wild type and R360Q. NuoE and F are both found in both fractions for both wild type and R360Q. Wild type NuoB is found predominantly in the membrane fraction, but with the NuoCD_R360Q, an increased amount of NuoB is in the supernatant fraction. These results indicate that this mutation disrupted formation of the Q-module subcomplex. The results of co-immunoprecipitation (Fig. 5) showed NuoCD and I were not co-immunoprecipitated with NuoG, in the presence of NuoCD_R360Q, as compared with wild type. NuoB was weakly precipitated from both soluble and membrane fractions. NuoE and F were precipitated from both fractions, similar to the wild type lane. This indicates the possibility of a severe assembly defect with NDUFS2_ R228Q under normal expression in humans. In addition, E. coli modeled NDUFS2_ R138Q and Y141C appear to have greatly diminished activity, even when assembled normally.

Subunit NDUFV1 has a bound flavin (FMN) which is a two electron acceptor from NADH, and which transfers electrons one at a time to the FeS clusters, starting with N3, found in the same subunit. Four clinical mutations, R128L, R199P, E377K, and R386H, were modeled in the nuoF gene in E. coli as R102L, T173L, E349K, and R358H, respectively. R128, E377, and R386 were conserved among E. coli, T. thermophilus, ovine, and human species, but R199 was conserved only among the other three, and not E. coli. The locations of the residues for the human, and E. coli proteins are shown in Fig. 6. Residues NuoF_R102 and NuoF_T173 are at an interface with NuoE, the residue NuoF_E349 is at an interface with NuoE, and G, and residue NuoF_R358 is at an interface with NuoG. The corresponding interfaces in the human enzyme are shown in Fig. 6.

The mutant NDUFV1_R128L was discovered in a 12-year-old male, who was diagnosed with Leigh syndrome and Complex I deficiency (Pronicka et al., 2016). He was compound heterozygous with a second mutation in the same gene: V245M (NuoF_V219 in E. coli), which was also predicted to be pathogenic. The mutation encoding NDUFV1_R199P was first identified in a male, diagnosed at birth with lactic acidosis, respiratory compensation, hypoglycemia, decreased spontaneous movements, hyperreflexia, and Leigh syndrome. He died at 4 months. This individual was also compound heterozygous with NDUFV1_R88G (NuoF_R62G in E. coli), and this allele was considered more likely to be pathogenic (Marin et al., 2013). The mutation encoding NDUFV1_E377K was first identified in a screen of patients with Complex I deficiency. The patient was an infant female, homozygous, and who was diagnosed with fatal infantile lactic acidosis, feeding difficulties, increased respiratory effort, vomiting episodes, failure to thrive, and development delay. Death was reported to be caused by lethal infantile mitochondrial disease (Calvo et al., 2010). The mutation encoding NDUFV1_R386H was discovered in two siblings with Leigh syndrome, born of first cousin parents. The boy developed vomiting, and dysphagia, and Complex I deficiency was discovered at three and half months. He died one month after diagnosis. His sister developed rotatory nystagmus and mild peripheral hypotonia, and she died at seven and half months (Vilain et al., 2012). This mutation was also discovered in a screen of patients with Complex I deficiency (Calvo et al., 2010).

In E. coli, mutations encoding variants R102L,A, T173P,A, E349K,A, and R358H,A, were constructed in nuoF and moved to the expression vector pBAD33(A-N). Membrane vesicles were prepared, and dNADH oxidase activity assays were measured. NuoF_R102L had 86 ± 1% of the wild type rate, while R102A had a higher activity of 93 ± 1%. The T173P variant had a reduced level of activity 80 ± 5% as a compared to wild type, while T173A had higher activity of 92 ± 4%. E349K essentially lacked dNADH oxidase activity (less than 1%) as compared to wild type, while E349A had a much higher activity of 91 ± 1%. R358H had activity of 77 ± 3% as compared to wild type, while R358A had a slightly higher activity of 87 ± 3% (Fig. 7A). In proton translocation assays, the rates generally corresponded to the level of oxidase activity (Fig. 7B), as would be expected for mutations in the peripheral arm. The presence of NuoF in membranes was confirmed by SDS gel electrophoresis, and immunoblotting with anti-F antibody (Fig. 8). The level of NuoF with E349K in membranes was extremely low as compared to wild type. In a native gel, a complex corresponding to the size of Complex I (540kDa) was detected using anti-HA antibodies, except in the case of E349K, in which the level of Complex I was strongly decreased. Moreover, when a native gel was used for an in-gel assay only a weak band was seen with E349K, similar to results modeling this residue in Y. lipolytica (Varghese et al., 2015).

NDUFS1 is the largest subunit in complex I with 727 amino acids, and a molecular mass of 79.5 kDa. It has three FeS clusters that accept electrons from FeS cluster N3 in NDUFV1, in sequence: the binuclear N1b, followed by the tetranuclear clusters N4 and N5. Three clinical mutations in NDUFS1, I177T, R241W, and ΔE211 were modeled in the nuoG gene as I152T, R217W, and ΔD186, respectively. Residues I177 and R241 are conserved among E. coli, T. thermophilus, ovine, and human species, but E211 is found in a less conserved region, and the corresponding residues in E. coli and T. thermophilus are D. The locations of the residues in the human, and E. coli proteins are shown in Fig. 9. Residue NDUFS1_I177 is found adjacent to FeS cluster N4, and near an interface with NDUFV1 (NuoF). R241 is near an interface with NDUFS8 (NuoI), between FeS clusters N5 and N6a (NDUFS8). E211 is found at the surface of NDUFS1, near subunit NDUFV2 (NuoE), and in E. coli it is also near the extended C-terminus of NuoI.

The mutation encoding NDUFS1_I177T (NuoG_I152T) was first reported in two siblings, as compound heterozygous. The second allele had a premature stop codon, and mRNA was not detected. They were diagnosed with an abnormal level of lactic acidosis, feeding difficulties, organ failure, respiratory failure, and both died at five-six weeks. Analysis of skeletal muscle indicated low levels of Complex I activity (Bjorkman et al., 2015). The mutation encoding NDUFS1_ΔE211 (NuoG_ΔD186) was first reported in a female child, who died at the age of 2 years. At five months, she was diagnosed with nystagmus, and her later development stagnated motorically as well as mentally, indicating a progressive neurological disorder. She was compound heterozygous with a second mutation in NDUFS1 encoding V228A (NuoG_T203). Native gel analysis indicated disrupted assembly of Complex I (Hoefs et al., 2010). The mutation encoding NDUFS1_R241W (NuoG_R217W) was first discovered in a brother and sister, who were normal until the age of 2 months and then presented with macrocytic anemia and dystonia or metabolic acidosis. They were compound heterozygous with a premature stop codon in the other allele. They died at ages 3 and 5 months, respectively (Benit et al., 2001).

I152A,T, R217A,W, D186A, and ΔD186 were constructed in E. coli nuoG and moved to the expression vector pBAD33(A-N). Membrane vesicles were prepared, and dNADH oxidase activity assays were measured. NuoG_I152T had activity of 91 ± 2% as a compared to wild type, while a much lower level of activity for I152A of 54 ± 7% was seen. NuoG_R217W had essentially no activity (<1%), while R217A had a much higher activity of 91 ± 8%, relative to wild type. The NuoG_ΔD186 also had a high activity of 91 ± 2%, while D186A had a very low level of dNADH oxidase activity of 2 ± 1%, relative to wild type (Fig. 10). In proton translocation assays, rates were generally consistent with dNADH oxidase activities, as would be expected.

The presence of NuoG- I152A,T, R217A,W, D186A, and ΔD186 variants in membrane preparations was determined by SDS gel electrophoresis followed by immunoblotting with the NuoG antibody. D186A was not detected by the NuoG antibody, but NuoF was seen to be weakly expressed, using NuoF antibody (Fig. 11A), indicating a low level of Complex I in the membrane fraction. A native gel was used to analyze assembly of Complex I (Fig. 11B). Complex I was detected in all cases using an HA-antibody against the HA-tagged NuoN, except variant D186A, where Complex I was not visible. Using an in-gel assay in a native gel, all mutations showed bands of activity corresponding to full-size Complex I, but bands for NuoG_R271W and D186A were weak (Fig. 11C). In summary, when modeled in E. coli, NDUFS1_I177T and ΔE211 appeared to assemble Complex I normally, with slightly diminished activity. The alanine substitutions had reduced activity, suggesting that those regions could be sensitive to certain mutations. In contrast while the modeled NDUFS1_ R241W assembled normally, it had near zero activity. In this case the alanine substitution had normal activity, suggesting that the tryptophan was particularly deleterious.

In a previous study by this lab it was found that expressing peripheral arm subunits B-CD-E-F-G together in a time-delayed expression system showed over 90% of the Complex I activity achieved when the entire operon was expressed together (Alkhaldi and Vik, 2022; Zhang and Vik, 2021). In that experiment, genes in pBAD33(A+H-N) were expressed first, and genes in pET22b(B-G) were expressed last. Here we tested NuoF_R358H and NuoG_I152T in the same time-delayed expression system, because each of these residues have interactions with the other subunit. Mutations for R358H and I152T were moved to expression vector pET22b(B-G). Three pairs of plasmids (1) pBAD33(A+H-N), pET22b(B-G WT), (2) pBAD33(A+H-N), pET22b(B-G R358H), and (3) pBAD33(A+H-N), pET22b(B-G I152T) were grown in chemical medium and were initially induced with arabinose at A600 = 0.3, after 30 min followed by addition of 0.5% (w/v) glucose and 0.045% (w/v) D-fucose for repression at the pBAD promoter. After 30 min, the second plasmid was induced by IPTG, and harvested after 30 min at A600 = 1.0. Membrane vesicles were prepared, and enzyme assays were measured. The time-delayed expression of nuoB-G with NuoF-R358H, had a reduced level of dNADH oxidase activity of 64 ± 7% as compared to the wild type, pET22b(B-G) which was 93 ± 4% (Fig. 12A). That suggested diminished assembly due to the mutation. For the time-delayed expression of nuoB-G with NuoG_I152T, an activity of 81 ± 1%, indicated a higher efficiency in assembly but still less than the wild type 93%. Activities seen in the proton translocation assay generally followed the rates of dNADH oxidase activity (Fig. 12B), as expected. In the native gel, a complex corresponding to the size of Complex I (540kDa) was detected for both mutants using anti-NuoM antibodies, as shown in Fig. 12C. Moreover, a second native gel was used for an in-gel assay which showed similar bands of activity that corresponding to full-size Complex I (Fig. 12D).

The NDUFV1_R128L (NuoF_R102) was found in a compound heterozygous patient, with NDUFV1_V245M. This residue is conserved among E. coli, T. thermophilus, ovine, and human species. To test the significance of the V245M substitution, NuoF_V219M was constructed and analyzed. Likewise, NDUFV1_R199P is compound heterozygous with NDUFV1_R88G, which is also conserved among E. coli, T. thermophilus, ovine, and human species. NuoF_R62G was also constructed and analyzed. The locations of these mutations for the human Complex I and E. coli Complex I are shown in Fig. 13. Both contact the flavin through their backbone NH groups. The level of dNADH oxidase activity of NuoF_V219M was found to be 51 ± 1% as compared to wild type, and the activity of NuoF_R62G was only 23 ± 4% of wild type. Proton translocation rates correspond to the rates of dNADH oxidase activity, as expected (Fig. 13C–F). The rates are displayed along with those of the partner mutants in the compound heterozygous states.

In humans, the mutant NDUFS1_ΔE211 was found to be compound heterozygous with NDUFS1_V228A, which might appear to be less deleterious. Residue V228 is conserved among human, ovine, and T. thermophilus species, but not in E. coli. The corresponding residue in E. coli is NuoG_T203. Therefore, to compare the effects of these two mutations, T203A was constructed in NuoG of E. coli. The locations of these mutations are shown for the human subunit NDUFS1 (Fig. 14A) and the E. coli subunit NuoG (Fig. 14B). The level of dNADH oxidase activity of T203A was found to be 19 ± 3% as compared to wild type (Fig. 14C), with a corresponding level of proton translocation (Fig. 14D), as expected. The results show that these three mutations, producing NDUFV1_V245M (NuoF_V219M), NDUFV1_R88G (NuoF_R62G), and NDUFS1_V228A (NuoG_T203), are more deleterious than the originally analyzed heterozygous partners.

The variant NuoF_R358H was constructed with a second substitution in an interacting residue of NuoG, H176A, and the variant NuoG_I152T was constructed with a second substitution in an interacting residue of NuoF, R358A. Both secondary substitutions reduce the possible interaction surface, and if this contact region is important, they should increase the deleterious effect of the original substitution. The locations of these residues are shown for the human Complex I and the E. coli Complex I in (Fig. 15).

The level of dNADH oxidase activity for the variant NuoG_H176A was 85 ± 4% of the wild type rate, for NuoF_R358H was 77 ± 3%, and that of the double substitution was reduced to 66 ± 3% (Fig. 16AB). Proton translocation rates were found to correspond to the levels of dNADH oxidase activity, as expected. These results showed that reducing the interaction surface of the NuoF_R358H by introducing NuoG_H176A further reduced the activity of Complex I. The level of dNADH oxidase activity of the double substitution NuoF_R358A + NuoG_I152T was 72 ± 3% as compared to the wild type rate, where the single substitutions, as previously measured, were 91 ± 2% for NuoG_I152T (Fig. 10A) and 87 ± 3% for NuoF_R358A (Fig. 7A). The proton translocation rate corresponded to the level of dNADH oxidase activity, as expected (Fig. 14CD). These results indicated that reducing the contact area between the NuoF and NuoG subunits further reduces the Complex I activity, suggesting that other mutations in this region would also be deleterious.

The presence of the variants of NuoF and NuoG was confirmed by SDS gel electrophoresis and immunoblotting. Native gels were used to analyze assembly of Complex I by immunoblotting with the HA antibody to detect NuoN. Complex I was detected in all the mutants, except variant NuoG_T203A was poorly assembled. A native gel was also used for an in-gel assay, and all mutants showed similar bands activity corresponding to full-size Complex I (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Four clinical mutations in NDUFS8: E36Q, P85L, R138H, and A159D were modeled in the nuoI gene of E. coli (Fig. 17AB). Residues P85 and A159 are conserved in E. coli, T. thermophilus, ovine and human species. Residue R138 is found in a less conserved region, but an R is present in all four species. Residue E36 is in a poorly conserved region, but there appears to be secondary structural conservation, and the residue is conserved in E. coli, ovine, and human species. The corresponding residues in E. coli are E5, P34, R86, and A108. Since the E. coli PDB file 7NYR lacks the first 22 residues of NuoI, the structure of Thermus thermophilus is shown instead to illustrate the location of the corresponding residue A5 (Fig. 17CD). The locations of the other residues are shown for the E. coli subunit (Fig. 17EF). Residue E63 is at an interface with NDUFS2 (NuoCD) near the membrane surface, residue P85 is at an interface with NDUFS7 (NuoB) near the membrane surface, and residue R138 is in a loop at an interface with NDUFS1 (NuoG). Residue A159 is near both FeS clusters in NDUFS8 (NuoI), N6a and N6b, and near an interface with NDUFS2 (NuoCD).

The NDUFS8_E63Q (NuoI_E5) mutation was first identified in a 3-year-old child, who was homozygous and diagnosed with Leigh syndrome. The activity of Complex I was reduced, about 54% in fibroblasts and 8% in muscle, and the level of assembled Complex I in a native gel was only 30–40% of normal (Haack et al., 2012). The NDUFS8_R138H and P85L (NuoI_R86, P34L) mutations were first seen in a compound heterozygous patient (Procaccio and Wallace, 2004). She appeared normal up until the age of seven years, when she gradually lost motor skills, and walking became difficult. She was diagnosed with Leigh syndrome. Each parent was heterozygous for one of the two missense mutations. The activity of Complex I was decreased to 31–43% of normal. Residue P85 (NuoI_P34) is upstream from the two cysteine motifs that coordinate the two FeS clusters N6a and N6b, and it is found to be distant from any FeS cluster (>20 Å). Residue R138 is located about 10 Å from both FeS clusters N6a and N5 (in NDUFS1). The A159D (nuoI_A108D) variant was reported in a child that was compound heterozygous with a second mutation in NDUFS8_R77W. She was diagnosed with mitochondrial encephalopathy and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Complex I activity was low in both muscle (38%) and in fibroblasts (52%). Assembly of Complex I was about 40% by native gel electrophoresis (Haack et al., 2012). This residue is between two residues that coordinate the N6b FeS cluster, Cys156 and Cys160.

Eight substitutions, E5A,Q, P34A,L, R86A,H, and A108D,L were modeled in E. coli and moved to the expression vector pBAD33(A-N). Membrane vesicles were prepared, and enzyme assays were measured, and the results are shown in Fig. 18A. NuoI_E5A had a high level of dNADH oxidase activity 95 ± 6 %, while NuoI_E5Q had a decreased level of 78 ± 9%, as compared to wild type, consistent with the mild phenotype in human. NuoI_P34A had a diminished level of dNADH oxidase activity 55 ± 3%, and NuoI_P34L had 38 ± 5%, as compared to wild type. NuoI_R86A showed a slightly decreased level of dNADH oxidase activity of 94 ± 4%, while NuoI_R86H had a decreased level 83 ± 6%, as compared to wild type. The results suggest that the human phenotype results primarily from the P85L mutation. NuoI_A108D had 88 ± 4% of dNADH oxidase activity, while NuoI_A108L showed a very low level of dNADH oxidase activity of 8 ± 1%, as compared to wild type. The heterozygous mutation, NDUFS8_R77W, could not be modeled in E. coli, due to sequence divergence in that region. Therefore, it remains possible that the human phenotype might be influenced by that substitution. In all cases the proton translocation assays closely corresponded to the dNADH oxidase activity rates, as expected (Fig. 18B).

The presence of subunit I in membranes from E5A,Q, P34A,L, R86A,H and A108D,L, was confirmed by SDS gel electrophoresis. Native gels were used to analyze assembly of Complex I. Complex I was detected in all the samples using an HA-antibody. A native gel was also used for an in-gel assay, and all mutations showed similar bands of activity corresponding to full-size Complex I (Supplementary Fig. S3). This suggests that the mutations affect the activity directly, rather than disrupting assembly.

Mutations in nuclear genes are increasingly implicated in mitochondrial dysfunction and disease. Most of the mutations analyzed in this study were compound heterozygous, in which a different mutant allele was inherited from each parent. In some cases, those mutations produced no symptoms in a heterozygous situation. In general, it was not known which allele was more deleterious. That question was addressed here in the bacterial expression system. Four of the mutants in this study were homozygous in the affected individuals, while their parents were heterozygous, and generally unaffected. The results are summarized in Supplementary Table S5.

Nine of the mutations initially selected for this study occur in compound heterozygotes. A feature of examining the mutations in E. coli is that the mutant alleles can be analyzed individually. All three mutations found in NDUFS1 appeared as compound heterozygotes. The single amino acid deletion, ΔE211, was found withV228A. While it might be predicted that the deletion would be more deleterious than the valine-to-alanine substitution, this was not the case in the E. coli enzyme using the similarly positioned residues: NuoG_ΔD186 yielded an activity of 91%, while NuoG_T203A was 19% of wild type. This patient lived only about 2 years, but the human mutations were not found in the ClinVar database (Henrie et al., 2018). The location of these residues can suggest why the mutations have such different outcomes. The E211/D186 residues are near the junction of FS1-FV1-FS8/G-F-I, but without much direct contact. The V228/T203 residues are found between the N4 and N5 FeS clusters, and so the small cavity generated by the substitution might alter local structure and impede electron transfer without disrupting assembly, as was suggested earlier (Fiedorczuk and Sazanov, 2018). In E. coli the alanine substitution of D186 had very low activity, apparently due to poor assembly. Therefore, it seems possible that the ΔE211 deletion in humans might be more deleterious than in E. coli.

The remaining two mutations in NDUFS1 were compound heterozygous with stop codon mutations, and so it is likely that those alleles were not expressed due to nonsense mediated decay. FS1_I177T was paired with FS1_464x. Two siblings with this mutation died after 5–6 months, but the mutation was not found in ClinVar. In E. coli, when the homologous residue was mutated to I152T the activity was 91%, but I152A had only 54% activity, suggesting that surface contact was important. This residue is in contact with NDUFV1/NuoF, and it is adjacent to Cys176/Cys151 which coordinates FeS cluster N4 in NDUFS1. The consequences of the I152T mutation were further probed by analysis of the double substitution with R358A in NuoF, which showed 72% activity, when an additive effect predicted 79% (0.91 × 0.87). In addition, when tested in a time-delayed expression of nuoA-I the activity was 81%, while an additive effect predicted 85% (0.91 × 0.93). Therefore, both the location near to FeS cluster N4 and to subunit NDUFV1 likely contribute to the loss of activity.

The mutation NDUFS1_R241W was found with NDUFS1_R557x, and this individual died after about 5 months. It is classified by ClinVar as of uncertain significance. In E. coli, the homologous mutation in nuoG, R217W had less than 1% activity. It appeared that the level of NuoG was only somewhat reduced in membranes compared to the wild type, suggesting that loss of function rather than assembly was the primary factor. The alanine substitution R217A had an activity of 91% indicating that the presence of the tryptophan was more deleterious than simply the absence of the arginine. Although the amino acid conservation is not especially high in the region of this arginine, in both human and E. coli that residue contacts a cysteine coordinating the FeS cluster N6a of subunit NDUFS8/NuoI, indicating a conserved position in the enzyme. As suggested earlier, a mutation in this location might disrupt electron transfer from N5 to N6a (Fiedorczuk and Sazanov, 2018).

Two of the mutations found in NDUFV1 appeared as compound heterozygotes. FV1_R128L was found in a patient living at the age of 12 years, along with FV1_V245M. In ClinVar it was described as “Conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity”. In E. coli, NuoF_R102L had activity of 86%, while the alanine substitution R102A had a higher activity of 93%, indicating that the leucine was somewhat more disruptive. In modeling of the second allele, NuoF_V219M, had a lower activity of 51%. This might be unexpected, since valine and methionine are considered more similar than are arginine and leucine. However, the V245/V219 residues are closer to the FMN than the R128/R102 residues, and V219M is the more deleterious mutation in E. coli.

The second compound heterozygote in NDUFV1 encodes R199P and R88G. This individual died at 4 months. The substitution analogous to R199P in E. coli is T173P, which had an activity of 80%. ClinVar described it as having conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity. The alanine substitution in E. coli, T173A, had an activity of 92% indicating that introduction of proline was more deleterious than the loss of the original amino acid. The substitution analogous to R88G in E. coli, NuoF_R62G, had an activity of 23%. This is consistent with an earlier study of human mutations in NDUFV1 modeled in the homologous subunit of Complex I from Yarrowia lipolytica (Varghese et al., 2015). They purified Complex I with a variety of mutations and found that the mutation corresponding to R199P produced a normal enzyme, while the substitution corresponding to R88G resulted in the loss of Complex I. As shown in Fig. 13, R88/R62 contacts the phosphate of FMN through the backbone NH, and so the R88G mutation is likely to contribute significantly to the human phenotype. In contrast, the R199/T173 residues are close to NDUFV2/nuoE and its N1a FeS cluster, and those mutations might not affect Complex I activity directly. The R88G variant appears to be the more deleterious one in this compound heterozygote.

Three of the mutations found in NDUFS8 appeared as compound heterozygotes. FS8_ A159D was found in a patient with muscular hypotonia, along with FS8_ R77W. The former substitution is in a highly conserved region, A108 in E. coli, but the latter is in a less conserved region, and could not be modeled in E. coli. FS8_A159D is considered pathogenic by ClinVar. In E. coli, NuoI_A108D had 88% activity, but A108L had only 8%, suggesting an inability to accommodate the extra bulk. A159/A108 is found adjacent to C160/C109 which coordinates the FeS cluster N6a, and mutations could alter local structure and disrupt electron transfer as previously suggested (Fiedorczuk and Sazanov, 2018). FS8_R77 appears in an ion pair with FS2_E257, and additional contacts are with NDUFA7. In addition, one cannot rule out the contribution of the second mutation, FS8_R77W, to the pathology.

The second compound heterozygote in NDUFS8 is P85L plus R138H, which were modeled in E. coli as P34L and R86H. These mutations were identified in a 9-year-old girl with late onset Leigh Syndrome. The mutations are classified as pathogenic by ClinVar. In E. coli the P34L substitution had an activity of 38%, while NuoI _R86H was 83%, suggesting that the FS8_P85L mutation was more deleterious. In both cases, the alanine substitutions were less deleterious, 55% and 94%, respectively. FS8_R138 is part of a β-hairpin near NDUFS1, close to the FeS clusters of NDUFS8. NDUFS8_P85 is found in a long irregular connection near NDUFS7 and NDUFA12. It was previously suggested that this mutation might disrupt assembly due to its location (Fiedorczuk and Sazanov, 2018). The P34A substitution was previously analyzed in E. coli and when expressed from the chromosome, it yielded an activity in membranes of 65% (Sinha et al., 2012).

The last example of a compound heterozygote was found in NDUFS2, with R138Q and R333Q. This individual had Leigh Syndrome and died at 3.5 months. This mutation was not classified by ClinVar. FS2_R138Q is found in a conserved region at the interface of NDUFS8 and NDUFS7 near the FeS cluster N2, and it was modeled in E. coli as NuoCD_R270Q. FS2_R333 is found in a less conserved region, and not near other subunits. It was not analyzed in E. coli in this study, and no further information about it is available. The NuoCD_R270Q substitution had an activity of 9%, while the alanine mutant was 3% of wild type. In other reports, the membrane activity of R270A was found in one study to be 5% (Belevich et al., 2007), or 4% in another (Sinha et al., 2015), while the R270K mutant was 57% (Sinha et al., 2015), indicating the importance of positive charge. It appears very likely that the FS2_R138Q variant is highly deleterious, while it seems less likely, that FS2_R333Q is similarly deleterious. R138Q was previously predicted to affect electron transfer to FeS cluster N2 (Fiedorczuk and Sazanov, 2018).

Only one mutation in this study appeared to be heterozygous, NDUFS2_Y141C, which is located very close to R138, and FeS cluster N2 in NDUFS7. While this patient had decreased Complex I activity, both parents did not, suggesting that this mutation is not sufficient to be pathogenic in a heterozygous situation. It was modeled in E. coli as Y273C, and it had <1% of the wild type activity. The alanine substitution was similar. In a previous study of the E. coli enzyme, the alanine substitution was found to have 3% activity, tryptophan 5%, and phenylalanine 17% (Sinha et al., 2015). Similar results were found in the Yarrowia lipolytica system with Y144H, and several additional substitutions (Kashani-Poor et al., 2001; Tocilescu et al., 2010). Here it was found that the alanine substitution showed reduced levels of NuoCD in membranes, and low levels of assembled Complex I in native gels. The CD antibody revealed no large complexes, while the N antibody showed a band around 260 kDa, which suggests that this complex lacks the peripheral arm. It appears that the NDUFS2_Y141C substitution would be deleterious, but in a heterozygous situation, the degree of pathogenicity might be influenced by other factors.

Four of the mutations were homozygous in human patients, but with various outcomes in the individual life spans. Two were found in NDUFV1: E377K and R386H. In both cases the parents were heterozygous, and unaffected. The individual with FV1_E377K died at 4 months. This mutation was not classified by ClinVar, and it was modeled in E. coli as NuoF_E349K. The activity was only 1% of wild type, but the alanine mutant had 91% activity, suggesting that the introduction of lysine, rather than the loss of glutamic acid, was key. In membranes NuoF_E349K was found at a very low level, and levels of assembled Complex I in native gels was similarly low. Examination of the protein structure revealed that this glutamic acid was near R200 in NDUFS1, which could explain the deleterious effect of the lysine substitution. In a previous study, when E377K was modeled in Complex I from Y. lipolytica, the FMN was lost, and the purified enzyme had no activity.

The second homozygous mutation in NDUFV1 was R386H. Two siblings died at 4.5 and 7.5 months. At ClinVar it was classified as “conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity”. It was modeled in E. coli as NuoF_R358H, with an activity of 77%. The alanine substitution had a slightly higher activity of 87%, suggesting that the histidine was disruptive. FV1_R386H was also modeled in Y. lipolytica, with similar results: somewhat reduced activity and nearly normal assembly (Varghese et al., 2015). This residue just precedes C388, which coordinates the FeS cluster N3. When NuoF_R358H was tested in pET22b(B-G) in time-delayed expression, the activity was 66% while the expected rate was 72% (0.77 × 0.93), suggesting that the variant assembles less efficiently than the wild type. When the neighboring residue NuoG_H176 was also substituted with alanine, the activity dropped to 66%, compared to the expected 65% (0.77 × 0.85), indicating an additive effect of subunit interactions on assembly.

One mutation in NDUFS2 was homozygous in two siblings, whose parents were asymptomatic, R228Q. The siblings died at 1.5 and 2 years of age. At ClinVar this mutation was classified as pathogenic. This mutation was modeled in E. coli as NuoCD_R360, and an activity of 82% was found. The alanine substitution was found to have 90% activity, indicating a slightly deleterious effect of introducing the glutamine. Assembly appeared normal when all genes were expressed from pBAD33(A-N), but assembly was severely compromised when only subunits A-I were expressed. This residue contacts FS8/NuoI, and during co-immunoprecipitation with HA-tagged NuoG, subunits NuoI and NuoCD were not precipitated. Subunits NuoE (FV2) and NuoF (FV1) were precipitated normally, and NuoB (FS7) showed low levels of precipitation. The results indicate that under optimal bacterial expression this mutation can assemble normally, but under other different conditions of expression, assembly was negatively impacted. From structural analysis it was previously suggested that this mutation might affect assembly (Fiedorczuk and Sazanov, 2018).

The last homozygous mutation occurs in NDUFS8 in a patient that was living after age 3 years. At ClinVar the FS8_E63Q mutant was classified as pathogenic. This mutation was modeled in E. coli as NuoI_E5Q. Although the sequence conservation is not high in this region, both human and E. coli glutamate residues are found in α-helices that contact subunit NDUFS2/NuoCD near the membrane (see Fig. 17). The activity of E5Q in E. coli membranes was 78%, while E5A was 95%, suggesting a negative impact of the glutamine replacement. It was previously suggested that the glutamine substitution might cause misfolding of the α-helix in which it resides (Fiedorczuk and Sazanov, 2018).

In summary, human mutations in nuclearly-encoded Complex I subunits can be modeled successfully in the E. coli enzyme, resulting in a range of phenotypes. In some cases, disruption of assembly of Complex I was clear, either from analysis of expression of all genes, or by expression of subsets of genes. In other cases, when assembly was normal, the results provided insights into the mechanism of Complex I. For compound heterozygous mutations, bacterial expression allows them to be analyzed individually, which may have utility in prognosis of newly discovered human mutations.
