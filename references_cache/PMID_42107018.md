# Safety and Effectiveness of Adalimumab for the Treatment of Pyoderma Gangrenosum: A 52-Week Real-World Prospective Observational Study.
**Authors:** Yamamoto T, Tanizaki H, Yamasaki K, Matsubara N, Nakayama M, Iwashita E, Yamanaka K
**Journal:** Dermatol Ther (Heidelb) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s13555-026-01772-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-026-01772-4)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare, autoinflammatory skin disorder characterized by painful ulcers, with scarce treatment options. Adalimumab is a recently approved tumor necrosis factor-alpha inhibitor for PG, for which real-world data remain limited. This study evaluated the long-term safety and effectiveness of adalimumab in patients with PG.

METHODS: This multicenter, prospective, open-label, single-arm, postmarketing observational study enrolled patients with PG who were prescribed adalimumab and were monitored for 52 weeks. The primary outcome was safety, assessed as the incidence proportion of infections reported as adverse drug reactions (ADRs). Secondary outcomes included other safety measures and effectiveness, estimated using the Physician Global Assessment (PGA), Investigator Inflammation Assessment, and Verbal Rating Scale (VRS) for pain. Relapse rates after remission and changes in PG subtype during the observation period were also examined.

RESULTS: Sixty-seven patients with PG were enrolled in the study. The mean age was 61.9 years, and 77.6% of the patients had comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus (19.4%), ulcerative colitis (16.4%), and hypertension (14.9%); 59.7% were concomitantly receiving systemic steroids. The PG subtypes among the enrolled patients were ulcerative (n = 62), pustular (n = 2), vegetative (n = 2), and bullous (n = 1). The incidence proportions of infections reported as ADRs and serious ADRs were 14.9% and 9.0%, respectively. The proportions of patients with a PGA score (total lesions) of 0/1 at weeks 12, 26, and 52 were 36.0%, 46.2%, and 57.7%, respectively, and with a VRS score of 0 at weeks 26 and 52 were 45.7% and 52.4%, respectively. No relapses occurred among patients who discontinued treatment due to symptom improvement.

CONCLUSIONS: This real-world study demonstrated no new safety concerns with adalimumab and demonstrated its effectiveness, including pain relief, across a heterogeneous PG population. These findings support its use as a standard treatment for PG, including in patients receiving concomitant systemic steroid therapy.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT04750213.



Why carry out this study?
Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare, painful, autoinflammatory skin disease with a significant impact on quality of life and limited treatment options; adalimumab is the only approved therapy for PG in Japan.There is a lack of long-term, real-world data on the safety and effectiveness of adalimumab across diverse PG subtypes and patient profiles.This study aimed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of adalimumab over 52 weeks in Japanese patients with PG, including those with prior tumor necrosis factor-α inhibitor exposure and concomitant steroid use.
What was learned from the study?
Adalimumab was well tolerated, and no new safety concerns were identified, with infections reported as adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and serious ADRs in 14.9% and 9.0% of patients, respectively; 57.7% of patients achieved a Physician Global Assessment (total lesions) score of 0/1.The findings support the use of adalimumab in broader clinical settings, including in patients with large lesion sizes, concomitant systemic steroid use, and nonulcerative PG subtypes.


Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare, rapidly progressive, autoinflammatory neutrophilic dermatosis characterized by painful ulcers, predominantly affecting the lower extremities [1, 2]. The disease is classified into five clinical subtypes—ulcerative, pustular, bullous, proliferative, and peristomal—of which ulcerative is the most common [3]. PG is often associated with systemic comorbidities such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and hematologic disorders [1]. The presence of these comorbidities has been reported to be associated with a threefold higher mortality rate in patients with PG than in the general population [4, 5]. Furthermore, in patients with PG, quality of life (QoL) may be severely impacted by persistent impairments in physical, emotional, and social functions associated with intense pain and cutaneous lesions with a disfiguring appearance [1].

In Japan, the annual incidence of PG is reported to be approximately 3–10 per million people, with a prevalence of 5.8 per 100,000 people [3], which is comparable to or slightly higher than the estimates reported in Western countries [5, 6]. Although the pathogenesis of PG remains unclear, neutrophil dysfunction, abnormal immune response, genetic alterations, and dysregulation of the innate immune system are considered to be key factors in its development [2, 7]. Severe pain is a hallmark symptom of PG, making effective pain management a critical component of its treatment. PG presents significant therapeutic challenges, particularly in patients with extensive lesions, comorbidities, or refractory disease. Current treatment strategies are largely guided by the severity and extent of the disease [3, 8]. Topical therapy is the preferred initial treatment for patients with mild localized lesions but is insufficient for progressive symptoms. Although cyclosporine is not currently approved for the treatment of PG, both systemic cyclosporine and prednisolone are widely used for its management. The STOP GAP trial was a randomized controlled study that compared the efficacy of cyclosporine and prednisolone in the treatment of PG. In the STOP GAP trial, the therapeutic effects of these two agents were generally equivalent, and while each has different high-risk adverse effects, their overall safety profiles were considered relatively comparable. However, both drugs accounted for approximately half of the cases that did not achieve healing within 6 months, and approximately 30% of cured cases relapsed [9]; these results are consistent with those of other reports [10–12]. Biologic agents that modulate immune function have also shown efficacy in PG [13], with tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors (TNFi) such as infliximab and adalimumab being commonly used as treatment options, but no biologics have been approved for PG treatment [14]. Thus, there remains a need for newer therapeutic agents with confirmed efficacy and safety for the treatment of PG [15, 16].

Adalimumab is a fully human recombinant immunoglobulin G (IgG)1 monoclonal antibody that specifically binds to TNF-α, neutralizing its biological function by blocking its interaction with the p55 and p75 cell surface tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptors [17]. Based on the results of an open-label, single-arm, multicenter, phase 3 study (NCT03311464), adalimumab was approved in Japan in November 2020 for the treatment of PG [18, 19]. However, the pivotal trial had several limitations—it was conducted in a small cohort (n = 22), included only patients with ulcerative PG with lesions < 100 mm in diameter, excluded patients with prior TNFi exposure, and restricted concomitant steroid use to < 10 mg/day of prednisolone.

In 2022, the first clinical practice guidelines for PG in Japan were published by the Japanese Dermatological Association [3]. Globally, adalimumab is the first drug indicated for PG; however, given the absence of a standardized global treatment framework, there is a critical need to generate real-world evidence across a broader spectrum of disease presentations.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the long-term safety and effectiveness of adalimumab for the treatment of Japanese patients with PG in real-world practice.

This multicenter, single-country, prospective, open-label, single-arm, postmarketing observational study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04750213) was conducted in accordance with the Good Postmarketing Study Practice (GPSP) and Ministerial Ordinance No. 171 of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan, dated 20 December 2004. As a prerequisite for the approval of adalimumab to treat PG, the study protocol was reviewed and authorized by the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency of Japan. Although not strictly required by the GPSP, ethical approval from institutional review boards or ethics committees of the participating medical centers was obtained when required. The details of the study design have been previously described in the interim report of this study [20].

The target sample size was set at 60 patients. Patients who had previously received adalimumab for PG were excluded, whereas those with prior use of adalimumab for non-PG indications were included. Enrollment was completed through a central registration system within 14 days of initiating adalimumab therapy. Patients or their legal guardians provided informed consent to participate in the study prior to initiating adalimumab treatment.

Adalimumab was administered in accordance with the package insert [21]. Treatment was initiated with a starting dose of 160 mg, followed by 80 mg administered 2 weeks later and then 40 mg repeatedly administered every week from week 4 onward after the first dose. Participants were observed for a period of 52 weeks after initiating therapy. If treatment was discontinued due to healing, relapse was assessed at week 52 after the initiation of therapy. Changes in PG subtype during the observation period were also monitored.

Case report forms were submitted by the investigators via an internet-based electronic data capture system. Data were collected from the medical records of each participating institution. Adverse events (AEs) were documented along with details on their severity, time of onset, assessment of drug causality with and without adalimumab, and outcomes. Disease severity was assessed using the Physician Global Assessment (PGA; 0 [complete resolution] to 6 [maximal deterioration]) [22]. Inflammation (erythema and border elevation) was assessed using the Investigator Inflammation Assessment (IIA; 0 [no inflammation] to 4 [very severe]) [22]. Pain level was measured using the Verbal Rating Scale (VRS; 0 [no pain] to 3 [severe pain]) [23, 24].

The primary outcome was the incidence proportion of patients who experienced any infection considered an adverse drug reaction (ADR) over the 52-week follow-up period. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of serious infections categorized as ADRs, as well as the occurrence of ADRs other than infections during the study. Effectiveness measures included PGA scores (total lesions and target lesions) at weeks 12, 26, and 52; IIA scores for target lesions at baseline and weeks 12, 26, and 52; and VRS scores at baseline, week 26, and week 52. Additionally, relapse rates and PG subtypes at week 52, time to relapse, and change in pain intensity assessed using VRS at weeks 26 and 52 were evaluated. Factors potentially influencing safety and effectiveness were considered as exploratory outcomes.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize patients and PG disease attributes. AEs and ADRs were classified according to system organ classes and preferred terms using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities/Japanese edition (MedDRA/J, version 27.0). The percentages of patients who achieved a PGA score of 0/1 or 0 (both total lesions and target lesions) at weeks 12, 26, and 52 and at discontinuation; an IIA score of 0 (evaluating erythema and border elevation at the target lesions) at baseline, at weeks 12, 26, and 52, and at discontinuation; and a pain VRS score of 0 at baseline, week 26, week 52, and discontinuation are presented using 95% Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals (CIs). For pain VRS scores of 0 at these time points, the results are expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD). Missing data were not imputed in the primary analysis; however, for some endpoints, analyses were also performed using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Patients were enrolled in the study between November 2020 and January 2023. A total of 67 patients from 54 sites in Japan were included in the safety analysis set. A total of 34 AEs occurred in 24 patients and led to treatment discontinuation in 16 patients, 12 of which were reported as ADRs. Three ADRs—pneumonia, bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, and eczema—were reported as not resolved and nonserious.

Two deaths were reported during the study—one patient developed an aortic dissection on day 70 and died on day 71. This patient had complications of ulcerative colitis and arteriosclerosis, and the cause of death was reported to be complications other than PG. The other patient developed acute pneumonia on day 12 and cytomegalovirus enterocolitis and cerebral infarction on day 13 and died on day 19. The event was reported as not related to adalimumab by the investigator but was considered possibly related by the sponsor.

Among the 12 patients who discontinued treatment due to improvements in symptoms, none experienced relapses by week 52. One patient reported a change in PG subtype during the observation period, from ulcerative at the start of the study to vegetative at the time of treatment discontinuation.

This final analysis of a 52-week, real-world, postmarketing observational study provides evidence supporting the long-term safety and effectiveness of adalimumab in the treatment of PG in Japanese clinical practice. Compared with previous reports, no new safety signals were observed, including the nonulcer subtypes. The profile of serious events was consistent with that described in the phase 3 study on PG [18, 19]. Two deaths were reported during the study, both deemed unrelated to treatment. However, the sponsor considered the case of cytomegalovirus enterocolitis, pneumonia, and cerebral infarction to be possibly related to treatment. This 80-year-old patient had received corticosteroids prior to starting adalimumab but did not use them concomitantly after starting adalimumab and had comorbid RA and nephrotic syndrome. In patients with immunologic comorbidities, their physical condition and medication use may decrease resistance to infection and increase mortality [25]. Therefore, special caution is necessary as usual when administering adalimumab for PG in such patients.

Oral corticosteroids, widely used as monotherapy or in combination for the treatment of PG, have also been noted to be a risk factor for AEs [9, 11, 13]. In the present study, 40 patients concomitantly received steroids, of whom 33 received doses ≥ 10 mg/day. There was no difference in the occurrence of ADRs (17.5% [7/40] versus 25.9% [7/27]) and AEs (32.5% [13/40] versus 40.7% [11/27]) with or without concomitant corticosteroids, and no characteristic trend depending on the steroid dose was observed in terms of the effectiveness indices (data not shown). However, since the total number of patients was limited and the analyses included various populations including patients who initiated, adjusted, or discontinued concomitant steroids during treatment, the present results are limited, and further detailed analyses are needed. The serious ADRs and AEs reported in this study were consistent with findings from previous real-world studies using adalimumab concomitantly with corticosteroids for conditions such as Crohn’s disease [26] and ulcerative colitis [27]. Notably, among the seven patients in the steroid combination group who experienced ADRs, five had been administered doses ≥ 10 mg/day. Compared with baseline data, the number of patients receiving concomitant corticosteroids and the dose of concomitant corticosteroids tended to decrease slightly at each time point. This trend is consistent with the findings from a case-based review of patients with PG and RA that reported improvement in both conditions after adalimumab treatment and corticosteroid dose reduction [28]. Given the recommendation for individualized steroid tapering to maintain disease control, prevent flares, and reduce side effects in PG [11, 25], patients on concomitant steroids and adalimumab for PG require more careful monitoring.

Generally, wound healing requires time, and some patients required a year to achieve a PGA score of 0/1 in this study, yet no increased safety concerns were observed, as noted from the incidence proportions of AEs ≥ 4 weeks to < 12 weeks, ≥ 12 weeks to < 26 weeks, and ≥ 26 weeks to < 52 weeks of 9.8%, 13.2%, and 10.9%, respectively (data not shown). Furthermore, among patients who achieved cure, no cases of relapse were noted up to week 52. In the STOP GAP study [9], which compared prednisolone and cyclosporine, the 6-month efficacy for target lesions was 47.0%. Although the results of this study are considered comparable, they should be carefully interpreted considering the risk of an observer bias, as this was a postmarketing observational study in which evaluations were performed only by the investigator. Relapses occurred in approximately 30.0% of remission cases, with two-thirds of the patients experiencing at least one AE. Although the present study was a single-arm, observational study conducted in a limited number of patients, precluding direct comparisons to other reported agents evaluated in different patient populations, the findings provide real-world evidence supporting the efficacy and safety profile of adalimumab in the management of PG. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than indicative of equivalence or superiority relative to alternative treatments.

The median target lesion area in this study was approximately 23.3 cm2, which is notably larger than that observed in the prednisolone group in the STOP GAP study (8.1 cm2) [9]. In the phase 3 trial, where the longest diameter of the target lesion site was < 100 mm, the proportion of patients who achieved a PGA score of 0/1 was 54.5% at week 26 and 66.7% at week 52 [18, 19], which is comparable to the findings in the < 100 mm (major axis) group in the present study, 45.0% at week 26 and 64.3% at week 52. In the ≥ 100 mm group, the proportion of patients with a PGA score of 0/1 was 47.1% at week 26 and 50.0% at week 52, a slightly lower rate than in the < 100 mm group. The incidence of ADRs was 37.5% in the ≥ 100 mm group, which tended to be higher than 12.5% in the < 100 mm group. Since the continuation rate was similar (45.8% versus 42.5%), these results suggest that the benefits of adalimumab may outweigh its risks even when the target lesion size is large. However, as subgroup analyses—particularly those for large lesion sizes and nonulcerative PG subtypes—were based on a limited number of cases, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory. Adalimumab should be used with particular attention to the possible occurrence of ADRs in patients with large PG lesion sizes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the safety and effectiveness of adalimumab in all PG subtypes. Although the number of patients enrolled in the study was limited, adalimumab showed comparable outcomes across the nonulcerative PG subtypes evaluated; nevertheless, the results of these subgroups should be considered exploratory due to the small sample sizes. Changes in subtype were also investigated in this study, with one patient experiencing a change from the ulcerative to vegetative subtype during the observation period. This patient had comorbidities of ulcerative colitis and IgG4-related disease and discontinued treatment due to an ADR of hepatic function disorder on day 18; the subtype change was confirmed at the time of discontinuation. Although subtype changes are rare and the cause is not clear, it is possible that some changes in immune status may have occurred in this case because changes in immune status are known to be closely associated with PG [29].

Consistent with previous reports, the majority (77.6%) of the patients in this study had comorbidities, including autoimmune diseases. Among them, seven had a history of TNFi treatment. Effectiveness outcomes were evaluated in six of seven patients, of whom four achieved a PGA total score of 0/1 including prior adalimumab use, while one discontinued treatment due to lack of efficacy. Of the six patients, one experienced eczema, which was reported as an ADR. These results indicate that adalimumab could show effectiveness in patients with a history of TNFi use, including adalimumab for indications other than PG.

PG is typically characterized by severe stabbing pain, which impacts patients’ QoL [1, 30]. Steroids and opioids are often prescribed for severe pain, and in cases where systemic analgesics are ineffective, amputation might be considered [30, 31]. In this study, 45.7% of the patients achieved a VRS score of 0 at week 26 of treatment, demonstrating the effectiveness of adalimumab in relieving PG pain in the early stages of treatment, potentially reducing the need for strong analgesics such as opioids or the consideration of amputation for severe pain. Considering that the phase 3 trial for PG reported improvements in the Dermatology Life Quality Index [18, 19], adalimumab may also be expected to improve the QoL of patients in real-world settings.

However, as approximately 40% of patients do not respond to adalimumab, alternative treatment options are required for these patients, and novel targeted agents and biologics are currently being explored for the treatment of PG, which may improve patients’ QoL and reduce PG-related deaths in the future [2].

This study has some limitations. First, the small number of cases—particularly the limited number of nonulcerative subtypes—imposes constraints on the statistical analyses. Second, this was a single-arm study, and no comparator was used; therefore, it is challenging to determine a definitive risk–benefit profile compared with the standard of care such as steroids or immunosuppressive agents for PG. Third, as there was no central review committee, efficacy results were assessed by investigators only, with a potential risk of observer bias. Lastly, as this study included only Japanese patients, potential racial and/or ethnic differences affecting the study outcomes cannot be excluded.

This final analysis of a postmarketing observational study confirmed the safety and effectiveness of adalimumab in a real-world population of Japanese patients with PG, including those with diverse subtypes, prior TNFi exposure, and concomitant systemic steroid use. No new safety signals were identified; adalimumab was effective and well tolerated across all PG subtypes and provided rapid pain relief, and no relapses were reported following symptom remission. These results reinforce the risk–benefit profile of adalimumab from previous clinical trials for PG, supporting its use as a standard treatment for a broad range of PG presentations; however, conclusions for nonulcerative subtypes should be interpreted cautiously due to small subgroup sizes. Given the limited number of studies in the PG population exploring all subtypes, these results provide useful information on future prescriptions of adalimumab for PG.
