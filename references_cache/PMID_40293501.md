# Restoration of Shal/K(V)4 proteostasis and motor function in a Drosophila model of spinocerebellar ataxia type 19/22.
**Authors:** Hsiao CT, Fu SJ, Cheng KM, Lo H, Tang CY, Chan CC, Jeng CJ
**Journal:** Cell Mol Life Sci (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00018-025-05711-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-025-05711-y)

## Content

Loss-of-function mutations in the human KCND3 gene encoding KV4.3 K+ channels are linked to the autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disease spinocerebellar ataxia type 19/22 (SCA19/22). Previous biophysical and biochemical analyses in vitro support the notion that the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of SCA19/22 is associated with the dominant-negative effects of disease-causing KV4.3 mutants on proteostasis of their wild-type (WT) counterpart. Herein we aimed to explore whether the disease-causing mutants might perturb protein expression of endogenous KV4.3 channel in human cells, as well as contributing to in vivo pathomechanisms underlying motor impairments and neurodegeneration in an animal model of SCA19/22. Substantial reduction in human KV4.3 protein level was validated in skin fibroblasts derived from heterozygous SCA19/22 patients. Genetic knockdown of endogenous Shal, the fly ortholog of human KV4.3, in Drosophila led to locomotor impairment, ommatidia degeneration, and reduced brain cortex thickness, all of which was effectively ameliorated by transgenic expression of human KV4.3, but not KV1.1 K+ channel. Transgenic expression of SCA19/22-causing human KV4.3 mutants resulted in notable disruption of endogenous Shal proteostasis, locomotor function, and ommatidia morphology in Drosophila. Enhanced expression of the Drosophila molecular chaperones HSC70 and HSP83 in our fly model of SCA19/22 corrected Shal protein deficit, locomotor dysfunction, and neurodegeneration. Overexpression of Hsp90β also upregulated endogenous human KV4.3 protein level in patient-derived skin fibroblasts. Our findings highlight Drosophila as a suitable animal model for studying KV4.3 channelopathy in vivo, and accentuate a critical role of defective KV4.3 proteostasis in the pathogenesis of motor dysfunction and neurodegeneration in SCA19/22.

Spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA), a heterogeneous group of inherited (autosomal dominant) neurodegenerative disorders, is characterized by progressive cerebellar ataxia, as well as variable degrees of pyramidal, extrapyramidal, cerebral, or spinal cord symptoms [ 1– 2 ]. Clinical manifestation of SCA includes loss of voluntary muscle contraction and movement control, resulting in disruption of eye movement, gait stability, speech, or swallowing. Based on genetic loci and chronological order of discovery, more than 40 distinct genetic types of SCA have been classified to date [ 3– 4 ].

SCA type 19/22 (SCA19/22) (OMIM# 607346) is linked to loss-of-function mutations in the human KCND3 gene that encodes the voltage-gated potassium (K+) channel KV4.3 [ 5– 6 ], which plays a critical role in determining membrane excitability and action potential firing in neurons and cardiac myocytes [ 7– 10 ]. Over 20 SCA19/22-related KCND3 mutations have been identified in patients with cerebellar ataxia [ 11– 12 ]. In vitro functional assays using heterologous expression systems revealed that many disease-causing mutant human KV4.3 channels are associated with significantly reduced K+ current amplitude [ 5– 6 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ]. Further biophysical and biochemical analyses suggested that the observed reduction in functional expression of mutant KV4.3 channels can be ascribed to aberrant voltage-dependent gating, as well as defective protein homeostasis (proteostasis) such as enhanced protein degradation and impaired membrane trafficking [ 6 , 13– 14 , 16 , 18 ]. Moreover, mutant KV4.3 channels may instigate notable dominant-negative effect on channel gating and proteostasis of their wild-type (WT) counterpart [ 13– 14 ], consistent with the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of SCA19/22.

Postmortem examination of SCA19/22 patients revealed significant loss of cerebellar Purkinje cells, along with reduced KV4.3 protein level [ 6 , 19 ]. Nonetheless, it is still an open question whether disease-causing mutants may indeed perturb protein expression and/or membrane trafficking of endogenous KV4.3 channels in native tissues. Additionally, in light of the lack of a feasible animal model for SCA19/22, it remains unclear whether the foregoing in vitro biophysical/biochemical anomaly associated with mutant KV4.3 channels may contribute to in vivo pathomechanisms underlying motor impairments and neurodegeneration in human patients. In this study, we set out to corroborate the proteostatic impacts of SCA19/22-related mutants on endogenous human KV4.3 and Drosophila Shal (the fly homolog of human KV4 channels) in human cells and transgenic flies, respectively. We presented several lines of evidence confirming the dominant-negative effect of disease-causing mutants on endogenous KV4.3/Shal proteostasis. Outstandingly, our analyses of multiple lines of transgenic Drosophila support the notion that disruption of endogenous Shal proteostasis by disease-associated human KV4.3 mutants is sufficient to induce prominent locomotor dysfunction and neurodegeneration, and that correction of defective Shal protein expression considerably rescues the neural defects. These findings underscore the pathogenic significance of defective KV4.3 proteostasis in SCA19/22.

Two-electrode voltage-clamp recordings were performed using Warner OC-725 C oocyte clamp (Harvard Apparatus) to record K+ currents through Shal1, Shal2, and KV4.3 channels. The recording bath contained Ringer solution [(in mM): 3 KCl, 115 NaCl, 1.8 CaCl2, 10 HEPES, pH 7.4 with methanesulfonic acid]. With an agarose bridge, the bath solution was connected to a ground chamber (containing 3 M KCl), into which two ground electrodes were inserted. Borosilicate electrodes (0.1-1 MΩ) filled with 3 M KCl were used for voltage recording and current injection. Data were acquired and digitized via Digidata 1440 A using pCLAMP 10.2 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were filtered at 1 kHz and digitized at 10 kHz, with all recordings conducted at room temperature (20–22 °C). To mitigate potential biases from channel expression variations among different batches of oocytes, we employed a previously described data normalization procedure [ 20 ].

For voltage clamp experiments, oocytes were held at − 90 mV and leak currents arising from passive membrane properties were subtracted by using the–P/4 method provided in the pCLAMP system. To investigate voltage-dependent activation properties of the K+ channels, oocytes were subject to 500-ms test pulses ranging from − 60 mV to + 60 mV in 10-mV increments to generate peak K+ current-voltage (I-V) curves. From peak I-V curves, relative K+ channel conductance (G/Gmax) at a given test potential was calculated as G/Gmax=∆IK/∆IK,+60, where ∆IK is current increment determined from peak K+ current difference between adjacent test pulses, and ∆IK,+60 is the ∆IK at the test potential + 60 mV. Steady-state voltage-dependent activation of the K+ channels was then analyzed by fitting G/Gmax-voltage (G-V) curve with a Boltzmann equation: G/Gmax=1/{1 + exp[(V0.5a–V)/ka]}, where V0.5a is the half-activation voltage, and ka is the activation slope factor. To assess voltage-dependent inactivation properties of the K+ channels, oocytes were subject to 1-second prepulses ranging from − 120 to + 10 mV with 10-mV increment, followed by a 500-ms test pulse at + 60 mV. Normalized peak K+ currents at + 60 mV (I/Imax) were then plotted against corresponding prepulse potentials. Steady-state voltage-dependent inactivation of the K+ channels was then analyzed by fitting I/Imax-voltage curve with a Boltzmann function: I/Imax=1/{1 + exp[(V0.5i–V)/ki]}, where V0.5i is the half-inactivation voltage, and ki is the inactivation slope factor.

In the postmortem cerebellum of a SCA19/22 patient, KV4.3 channel and the Purkinje cell marker calbindin displayed comparable decrease in protein level [ 6 ], which may simply reflect significant loss of cerebellar Purkinje cells, but may also imply a potential disruption of KV4.3 proteostasis. We therefore attempted to determine whether endogenous KV4.3 in SCA19/22 patients might exhibit defective proteostasis. Since patient-derived skin fibroblasts have been widely used for assessing protein expression patterns in neurons and muscles for various neuromuscular diseases [ 21– 23 ], we collected skin fibroblasts from two Taiwanese SCA19/22 patients with heterozygous KV4.3 C317Y or P375S mutations. As indicated by our previous in vitro biochemical analyses in the heterologous expression system, these two KV4.3 mutant channels exhibited enhanced protein degradation and impaired membrane trafficking [ 14 ]. Figures 1A-B and Supplementary Figure S1 illustrate that, compared to the control sample from a normal subject, endogenous KV4.3 protein levels in skin fibroblasts from the heterozygous SCA19/22 patients were notably decreased by about 50% or more. In addition, confocal microscopic analyses revealed that endogenous KV4.3 in patient-derived skin fibroblasts showed substantially reduced immunofluorescent signal, as well as impaired plasma membrane localization (Fig. 1C). Together, these observations are consistent with the idea that the two SCA19/22 patients are indeed associated with defective KV4.3 proteostasis.

Next, we evaluated the feasibility of using Drosophila as an animal model to study human KV4.3 channels in vivo. Drosophila Shal K+ channels and mammalian KV4 channels (i.e., KV4.1, KV4.2, and KV4.3) share numerous features in protein structure and biophysical properties [ 24– 25 ]. The neurophysiological function of Shal has been thoroughly investigated in flies [ 26– 31 ]. Importantly, transgenic elimination of neuronal Shal K+ currents with a dominant-negative Shal mutant construct resulted in altered membrane excitability and defective locomotion in larval and adult flies, suggesting that Shal channels may be essential for regulating repetitive neuronal firing and rhythmic motor behaviors in Drosophila [ 29 ]. Moreover, age-dependent changes in Shal expression were previously demonstrated in adult flies [ 26 , 30– 31 ]. To further evaluate the functional impact of compromised Shal proteostasis in flies, we employed the GAL4-UAS system with the pan-neuronal elav-GAL4 driving the expression of two independent UAS transgenes of RNA interference (RNAi) constructs targeting Drosophila Shal: elav> Shal-RNAi KK and elav> Shal-RNAi TRiP [ 32– 34 ]. Two additional fly lines, elav-GAL4 and Shal-RNAi, were also used as controls. As depicted in Figs. 2A-B, in the presence of elav-GAL4-driven Shal-RNAi, endogenous Shal protein level in the brain was considerably diminished in 3 days post-eclosion (hereafter referred to as 3-day-old) adult flies. In contrast, without elav-GAL4-driven activation, introducing Shal-RNAi per se failed to measurably affect endogenous Shal protein level (Fig. 2A-B). Furthermore, by accessing the locomotor activity of the transgenic flies in a climbing assay (also known as negative geotaxis assay) (Fig. 2C), we observed a significant association of Shal deficiency with severe locomotor dysfunction in 3- and 7-day-old adult transgenic knockdown flies (Fig. 2D-E). For example, over 90% of the tested control transgenic flies climbed above the 6-cm target line within 10 s, whereas less than 2% of the tested elav> Shal-RNAi KK flies managed to accomplish the same motor task. In the absence of elav-GAL4-driven activation, however, the application of Shal-RNAi alone did not appear to detectably interrupt locomotor function (Fig. 2D-E). Our findings are in agreement with a previous report showing that pan-neuronal, but not motoneuron-specific, Shal knockdown dramatically altered the climbing behaviors of 4-day-old adult flies [ 35 ].

There are two alternatively spliced Shal isoforms in Drosophila, Shal1 and Shal2 [ 36 ], that were both coimmunoprecipitated with human KV4.3 (Fig. 2F-G), consistent with the notion that human KV4.3 may coassemble with Shal1/2 to form functional tetrameric voltage-gated K+ channels [ 24 ]. We then asked whether human KV4.3 might restore the neural dysfunction associated with the forgoing Shal-deficient flies. To address this question, we applied pan-neuronal (elav-GAL4-driven) Shal knockdown in combination with overexpression of human KV4.3 in Drosophila (Fig. 2H). In addition, we examined another Shal-knockdown fly overexpressing a distinct type human K+ channel, KV1.1 (Fig. 2H), which is homologous with Drosophila Shaker and thus not expected to form heteromultimeric channels with Shal/KV4 [ 24 ]. Figure 2I illustrates that, in the climbing assay, overexpression of human KV4.3, but not KV1.1, dramatically corrected Shal-deficiency-associated locomotor impairment. This lack of measurable effect by KV1.1 overexpression implies that the restoration of overall membrane K+ conductance per se may not be sufficient for rescuing locomotor dysfunction in Shal-deficient flies. Instead, it may emphasize the significance of Shal/KV4-specific proteostasis, such as proper subcellular localization in neurons.

To investigate whether Shal knockdown may cause neurodegeneration, we studied the structural integrity of Drosophila light-sensing neurons (photoreceptors), which has been widely used to inspect neurodegeneration-related phenotypes [ 37 ]. We employed the eye-specific GMR-GAL4 to knock down endogenous Shal and overexpress human KV4.3 in Drosophila. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was applied to examine the morphology of the fly compound eye, which is composed of hundreds of hexagon-shaped ommatidia, each containing eight photoreceptors [ 38– 39 ]. A small fraction of young (3-day-old) adult flies with Shal knockdown displayed deformed ommatidia and/or significant loss of bristles (Fig. 3A-C). In 7- and 10-day-old adult flies, Shal deficiency was associated with much more prominent deformity in the exterior ommatidia morphology (Fig. 3B-C). Significantly, severe morphological defects were observed in Shal-deficient, aged (35-day-old) adult flies (Fig. 3B-C). This apparently age-dependent, progressive aggravation of ommatidia morphology in Shal-deficient adult flies is consistent with the presence of substantial Shal deficiency-associated neurodegeneration. Notably, Shal deficiency-related morphological defects in the ommatidia of adult flies were considerably ameliorated by overexpression of human KV4.3 (Fig. 3D).

We further assessed whether Shal deficiency may cause neurodegeneration in the Drosophila brain by examining the effect of pan-neuronal Shal knockdown on the thickness of the brain cortex in 3 days post-eclosion adult elav> Shal RNAi KK flies. By performing whole-brain DAPI staining of central brain neurons, followed by confocal microscopic imaging analyses, we observed that Shal knockdown resulted in a substantial reduction in the thickness of the brain cortex by about 17 μm (Suppl. Fig. S2). Importantly, overexpression of human KV4.3, but not KV1.1, effectively rescued the brain cortex deficit in Shal RNAi flies.

Taken together, the observed functional and morphological compensations of Shal deficiency by human KV4.3 highlight a potential exchangeability, which likely represents an evolutionarily conserved function, between Shal and human KV4.3 in Drosophila. Moreover, these findings indicate that Drosophila is indeed a suitable animal model for studying disease-related KV4.3 proteostatic defects in vivo.

In the heterologous expression system, SCA19/22-causing KV4.3 mutants are associated with enhanced protein degradation and may substantially suppress the protein expression of their WT counterpart [ 14 ]. Hence, the abovementioned reduction in endogenous KV4.3 protein level in skin fibroblasts from heterozygous SCA19/22 patients (Fig. 1B), which entails both WT and mutant KV4.3, may be interpreted as the effect of haploinsufficiency due to reduced mutant KV4.3 protein level. Alternatively, it may also result from the dominant-negative effect of the mutants on WT KV4.3 protein level. We sought to test the latter hypothesis by asking whether two SCA19/22-causing human KV4.3 mutants, V338E and P375S, may disrupt Drosophila Shal proteostasis in vitro and in vivo.

Previous biophysical and biochemical analyses suggested that KV4.3 V388E and P375S mutants displayed profoundly diminished protein levels and failed to generate measurable K+ current [ 14 ]. In HEK293T cells, the mRNA level of the disease-causing human KV4.3 mutants appeared to be comparable to that of the WT (Suppl. Fig. S3). Figures 4A-B demonstrate that, upon equimolar coexpression with the human KV4.3 mutants, but not their WT counterpart, the total protein level of Drosophila Shal1 and Shal2 was significantly decreased in HEK293T cells. Electrophysiological analyses in Xenopus oocytes further revealed that coexpression with the two KV4.3 mutants, but not KV4.3 WT, resulted in about 50% or more reduction of Shal1/2 K+ current amplitude (Fig. 4C-F; Suppl. Fig. S4). In addition, upon coexpressing with the mutants, cell-surface localization of KV4.3/Shal proteins was substantially diminished (Suppl. Fig. S5). These observations strongly support the idea that human KV4.3 V338E and P375S mutants exert notable dominant-negative effects on Drosophila Shal1/2 proteostasis in vitro. To further test the hypothesis that the two SCA19/22-causing human KV4.3 mutants may interfere with endogenous Shal proteostasis in vivo, we generated transgenic UAS flies of human KV4.3 WT, V338E, or P375S, followed by examining how they affect the nervous system upon pan-neuronal expression of individual transgenes driven by elav-GAL4. As demonstrated in Figs. 4G-H, expression of the disease-causing human KV4.3 mutants, but not their WT counterpart, led to significant attenuation of endogenous Drosophila Shal protein level, consistent with the presence of substantial dominant-negative effect of the human mutants on Shal proteostasis in vivo.

As demonstrated in Fig. 2, compromised Shal proteostasis (elav> Shal-RNAi) resulted in a prominent locomotor defect in Shal-deficient Drosophila. We therefore asked whether the dominant-negative effect of SCA19/22 KV4.3 mutants on endogenous Shal proteostasis may also disrupt locomotor function in adult flies. Figure 5A depicts that, for 7-day-old adult flies subject to the climbing assay, about 75% of the human KV4.3 WT-expressing transgenic flies climbed above the 6-cm target line within 10 s. In comparison, only about 59% and 44% of the transgenic flies expressing the KV4.3 V338E and P375S mutants, respectively, accomplished the same motor task. By 20 s, about 87% of WT-expressing flies had climbed above the target line, whereas only about 82% and 63% of V338E and P375S-expressing flies, respectively, had achieved the same goal. Furthermore, for 14-day-old adult flies tested with the same motor function assay, about 41% of the WT-expressing flies climbed above the 6-cm target line within 10 s, significantly higher than transgenic flies expressing the V338E (23%) or P375S (17%) mutants (Fig. 5B). By 20 s, more than 74% of the WT-expressing flies climbed above the target line, while less than 47% of the V338E and P375S-expressing flies accomplished the same motor task, consistent with the presence of age-dependent, progressive aggravation of locomotor dysfunction in the transgenic SCA19/22 Drosophila. The neurotoxic human amyloid-β42 (Aβ42) fragment, carrying the familial Alzheirmer’s ‘Arctic’ mutation [ 40– 41 ], is known to cause selective degradation of endogenous Shal protein that critically contributes to age-dependent locomotor deficit, learning/memory defect, and neurodegeneration in Drosophila [ 31 , 42 ]. Accordingly, transgenic expression of human Aβ42 Arctic peptide dramatically exacerbated locomotor function in 7-day-old adult flies, and virtually obliterated locomotor function in 14-day-old adult flies (Fig. 5A-B).

By performing yeast two-hybrid screening of a rat brain library, we have identified several KV4.3-interacting proteins that might contribute to proteostasis of the K+ channel. Among these potential binding partners of KV4.3 are the molecular chaperones Hsc70 and Hsp90β. Figures 7A-B illustrate that, in both human fibroblasts and rat brain lysates, endogenous KV4.3 was effectively coimmunoprecipitated with Hsc70/Hsp90β. Biochemical investigation using the heterologous HEK293T expression system also confirmed that, like human KV4.3, Drosophila Shal1/2 coexisted in the same protein complex with Hsc70/Hsp90β (Fig. 7C-D). We further demonstrated that, in the presence of Hsc70 or Hsp90β, the dominant-negative effect of the SCA19/22-causing human KV4.3 mutants V338E and P375S on Drosophila Shal1/2 protein expression was virtually abolished in HEK293T cells (Fig. 8). Drosophila has long been used as an animal model for elucidating the pathomechanisms of various human neurodegenerative diseases [ 43– 46 ]. For instance, several transgenic fly models were established for specific types of SCA, including SCA1 and SCA3, which involve CAG repeat expansions in the genes encoding ataxin-1 and ataxin-3, respectively [ 47– 48 ]. Molecular characterizations based on transgenic expression of human SCA1 or SCA3 mutants in Drosophila further underlined the significance of protein folding and stability in SCA-associated neurodegeneration [ 47 , 49– 51 ].

In the present report, we aimed to establish a viable Drosophila model of SCA19/22 to validate the pathophysiological significance of defective KV4.3 proteostasis in locomotor dysfunction and neurodegeneration. In agreement with previous heterologous expression studies and postmortem brain tissue analyses [ 6 , 13– 14 , 16 , 18 ], we observed a significant reduction of endogenous human KV4.3 protein level in skin fibroblasts derived from SCA19/22 patients with heterozygous KCND3 mutations. By knocking down endogenous Shal, the fly ortholog of human KV4 channels, in Drosophila, we further demonstrated that overexpression of human KV4.3, but not KV1.1, effectively ameliorated locomotor impairment and neurodegeneration caused by Shal protein deficiency. Transgenic expression of SCA19/22-causing human KV4.3 variants in Drosophila resulted in notable dominant-negative effects on endogenous Shal proteostasis, locomotor function, and ommatidia morphology. Moreover, enhanced expression of the molecular chaperones HSC70 and HSP83 (Drosophila orthologs of Hsc70 and Hsp90β, respectively) in our fly model of SCA19/22 led to substantial correction of endogenous Shal protein expression, as well as considerable rescue of locomotor dysfunction and neurodegeneration. Importantly, the overproduction of Hsp90β also upregulated endogenous human KV4.3 protein levels in patient-derived skin fibroblasts. To the best of our knowledge, we have presented the first direct evidence supporting Drosophila as a suitable animal model for studying KV4.3 channelopathy in vivo, as well as emphasizing a critical role of defective KV4.3 proteostasis in the pathogenesis of motor dysfunction and neurodegeneration in SCA19/22.

Shal proteostasis has been suggested to serve as a potential set point for regulating homeostatic signaling in Drosophila neurons [ 35 ]. Furthermore, in adult flies, endogenous Shal protein levels display progressive age-dependent decline starting from 3 days post-eclosion, which appears to be linked to their age-related descent in locomotor performance [ 30 ]. In line with previous studies showing that suppression of endogenous Shal channels led to impaired locomotion in larval and adult flies [ 29 , 35 ], herein pan-neuronal Shal knockdown and transgenic human KV4.3 V338E/P375S-expressing flies were associated with age-related deterioration in locomotor function. Our targeted knockdown of Shal or expression of human SCA19/22 mutants in the eye further revealed slowly progressive degeneration of photoreceptor neurons. Together, these observations emphasize that, at least in Drosophila, proper expression of endogenous Shal K+ channels is required for maintaining locomotor function and perhaps structural integrity of neurons, the detailed molecular mechanism of which, however, is currently unclear. One potential explanation could be that Shal plays an essential neurodevelopmental role in Drosophila. Unlike the well-known neurotoxicity of SCA1/3-causing ataxin-1/3 polyglutamine mutants, the majority of SCA-associated mutant proteins harboring conventional mutations, such as SCA19/22-related KV4.3 mutants, did not appear to be associated with significant cellular toxic effects [ 1– 2 , 52 ]. Consistent with this idea, in the climbing assay, the magnitude and progression of locomotor dysfunction imposed by KV4.3 V338E/P375S were dwarfed by those inflicted by the highly toxic Aβ42 Arctic peptide (Fig. 5). Therefore, transgenic expression of human KV4.3 V338E and P375S mutants might not directly exert a dominant toxic or cell death effect in flies. Rather, as demonstrated in our SCA19/22 Drosophila model, the disease-causing human KV4.3 mutants profoundly perturbed endogenous Shal proteostasis, which could substantially impair critical neurodevelopmental components early in the larval stage and thereby manifest as age-dependent, slowly progressive aggravation of locomotor dysfunction and neurodegeneration in adult flies. Future investigation will be required to assess the potential neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative roles of Shal and KV4.3 in flies and mammals, respectively.

Drosophila Shal and human KV4.3 belong to the same family (KV4) of voltage-gated K+ channels and may therefore form heteromultimeric channels [ 24 , 53 ], as supported by our coimmunoprecipitation data shown in Fig. 2G. Moreover, in both Drosophila and mammalian neurons, Shal/KV4 channels are strictly expressed in the axonal initial segment and somatodendritic compartment [ 25 , 54– 55 ]. In contrast, Drosophila Shaker and human KV1.1 are members of the KV1 family of voltage-gated K+ channels [ 24 , 53 ], and predominantly localized to the axon and nerve terminal [ 55– 57 ]. Interestingly, in the transgenic Shal knockdown Drosophila, Shal deficiency-associated locomotor impairment was markedly corrected by overexpression of human KV4.3, but not KV1.1. This failure of discernible rescue effect by KV1.1 implies that the integrity of Drosophila locomotor function probably depends on the precise localization of Shal/KV4 in the specific neuronal compartment, rather than the overall membrane K+ conductance of the neuron. A previous report focusing on synaptic transmission in larval flies demonstrated that endogenous Shal and Shaker K+ channels displayed distinct subcellular localization patterns in motoneurons and differentially regulated synaptic homeostasis at the Drosophila neuromuscular junction [ 55 ]. Functional segregation of KV4.3 and KV1.1 appears to take place in the human nervous system as well, since aberrant proteostasis and voltage-dependent gating of human KV1.1 channel is linked to a distinct type of cerebellar ataxia (episodic ataxia type 1) that does not involve neurodegeneration [ 58– 60 ]. Consequently, we speculate that the lack of apparent exchangeability between Shal and the human KV1.1 in the Shal knockdown Drosophila could be ascribed to differential membrane trafficking properties between Shal and the Shaker-like human KV1.1. In contrast, human KV4.3 might retain a Shal-like subcellular localization pattern within specific fly neuron compartments, thereby supporting synaptic transmission events critical for neurodevelopmental processes. Detailed experimental analyses in larval and adult flies will be necessary to validate this conjecture.

In the heterologous expression system, SCA19/22-causing KV4.3 mutants substantially disrupt the proteostasis of human KV4.3 WT and Drosophila Shal1/2, manifesting as reduced protein stability and aberrant membrane trafficking (Fig. 4A-F; Suppl. Fig. S5) [ 13– 14 ]. Similarly, in our SCA19/22 fly model, the human KV4.3 mutants markedly suppressed endogenous Shal protein expression (Fig. 4G-H). However, in light of our lack of electrophysiological recordings of Shal K+ currents in Drosophila, we failed to directly address the impact of the human KV4.3 mutants on cell-surface expression of endogenous Shal channels in fly neurons. Overproduction of the molecular chaperones HSC70 or HSP83 effectively reversed the dominant-negative effects of KV4.3 mutants on endogenous Shal protein level (Fig. 9B-C), but only led to partial rescue of the locomotor dysfunction and neurodegeneration of the SCA19/22 Drosophila (Fig. 9D; Suppl. Fig. S6). Since we did not examine the effect of the molecular chaperones on functional expression of endogenous Shal K+ channels in fly neurons, it remains an open question whether HSC70 or HSP83 overexpression might be able to mitigate the disruption of the membrane trafficking/subcellular localization of endogenous Shal channels by the human KV4.3 mutants. In addition, given that the disease-causing KV4.3 mutant proteins are significantly less stable and subject to enhanced proteasomal and lysosomal degradation [ 6 , 13– 14 , 16 , 18 ], it is unclear whether the molecular chaperones could be effective in correcting the folding defect of the mutants per se. Further investigation into the molecular nature of the folding defects of the human KV4.3 mutants, as well as how they interact with molecular chaperones compared to their WT counterpart, may offer deeper insights into the mechanisms underpinning the regulation of Shal/KV4.3 proteostasis by molecular chaperones.

Another important issue that requires further clarification concerns whether the pathogenic significance of defective KV4.3 proteostasis, as deduced from our analyses of human KV4.3 C317Y, V338E, and P375S in patient-derived skin fibroblasts and transgenic flies, could apply to other as yet uncharacterized SCA19/22-causing mutations. Postmortem analyses of brain tissue from a Dutch SCA19/22 patient carrying the loss-of-function KV4.3 T532P mutation revealed the presence of a notably reduced KV4.3 protein level [ 6 ]. Similar to the phenotype of T532P in the heterologous expression system [ 6 , 13 ], another SCA19/22-causing KV4.3 mutant, T377M, also exhibited enhanced protein degradation and impaired membrane trafficking, as well as exerting a considerable dominant-negative effect on proteostasis and thus a functional expression of its WT counterpart [ 5 , 14 ]. A recent study of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived from the skin fibroblasts of two Chinese SCA19/22 patients carrying the T377M mutation further indicated that, compared to the matched healthy control iPSCs, T377M iPSCs were associated with a significant decrease in KV4.3 protein abundance [ 11 ]. Taken together, it is likely that defective KV4.3 proteostasis in vivo may also apply to other SCA19/22-causing mutations.

The current study has several limitations. First, we have yet to identify the defective neural circuit(s) associated with locomotor dysfunction and neurodegeneration in Shal knockdown and SCA19/22 Drosophila. Loss of Shal protein expression in motoneurons per se did not appear to significantly affect climbing behavior in adult flies [ 35 ]. It remains to be determined whether locomotor function in flies may depend on Shal proteostasis in other neural circuits, such as sensory neurons in the peripheral nervous system and cortical neurons in the central brain. Second, as mentioned above, the precise subcellular localization of human KV4.3 and KV1.1 channels was not determined in Drosophila neurons. Third, we failed to provide functional insight into Shal-knockdown and SCA19/22-associated neurodegeneration in Drosophila. Intracellular recordings of Shal K+ currents and action potential firing, as have been implemented previously [ 29 ], should be employed in the future to directly evaluate the impact of aberrant Shal proteostasis on the functional features of Drosophila neurons. Fourth, a significant fraction of Shal knockdown flies were associated with developmental defects affecting non-neuronal tissues, such as reduced pupation rate, low eclosion success, and unexpanded wings (data not shown). The pathomechanism underlying these Shal deficiency-related developmental abnormalities is unclear and requires future clarification.

In some animal models of cerebellar ataxia, motor deficits were effectively mitigated by modulation of ion channel functions. For example, 4-aminopyridine (4-AP), a specific K+ channel blocker, corrected motor dysfunction and neurodegeneration in mouse models of cerebellar ataxias unrelated to K+ channelopathy (e.g., episodic ataxia type 2 and SCA1) [ 61– 62 ]. In addition, upregulation of the molecular chaperones Hsp40 and Hsp70 powerfully suppressed neurodegeneration in Drosophila models of SCA1 and SCA3, respectively [ 47 , 51 ]. Of note, the molecular chaperones Hsc70 and Hsp90β are known to play critical roles in regulating proteostasis of disease-associated misfolded K+ and Cl− channels [ 63– 67 ]. As a result, promotion of K+ channel proteostasis by molecular chaperones could be helpful for patients suffering from cerebellar ataxias entailing loss-of-function mutations in K+ channel genes, including episodic ataxia type 1, SCA13, and SCA19/22. Our demonstration of the beneficial effect of Hsc70/Hsp90β overproduction in correcting Shal protein deficit, locomotor dysfunction, and neurodegeneration in the fly model of SCA19/22, as well as increasing human KV4.3 protein level in patient-derived skin fibroblasts, highlights the therapeutic potential of chemicals or small molecules targeting KV4.3 proteostasis in the treatment for SCA19/22.
