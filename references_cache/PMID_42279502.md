Relapsing polychondritis (RP) is a rare autoimmune disease of cartilaginous 
structures, often diagnosed late due to nonspecific presentations. Both RP and 
granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) can cause diffuse tracheobronchial wall 
thickening on computed tomography (CT) and may be seronegative for 
anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), creating a diagnostic impasse. We 
report a 46-year-old man with two months of fever, productive cough, and sternal 
pain. A saddle nose deformity was the only cartilaginous sign; serum ANCA was 
repeatedly negative. Neck CT showed diffuse tracheal and bilateral main 
bronchial wall thickening; the report listed amyloidosis and GPA as differential 
diagnoses, omitting RP. Despite laboratory, microbiological, and imaging workup, 
the fever fulfilled criteria for fever of unknown origin (FUO), prompting 
18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET)/CT. PET/CT 
demonstrated intense FDG uptake in the cartilaginous wall of the 
tracheobronchial tree, forming the classic inverted-Y sign, with bilateral 
costal cartilage hypermetabolism (a site not involved in GPA) and no uptake in 
the kidneys, sinuses, or orbits, collectively establishing a diagnosis of RP. 
Corticosteroid therapy elicited prompt clinical and biochemical response. This 
case demonstrates that 18F-FDG PET/CT can differentiate RP from GPA when CT and 
serology are uninformative.

Enhanced CT of the neck and chest in a 46-year-old man presenting with a 2-month history of persistent fever, productive cough, and anterior chest pain. Physical examination revealed a recently developed saddle nose deformity; no auricular inflammation or peripheral arthritis was evident. Laboratory investigations demonstrated markedly elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein 16.09 mg/dL; erythrocyte sedimentation rate 120 mm/h; ferritin peak 898 ng/mL). Serum anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) was repeatedly negative on screening assay, and both proteinase 3 (PR3)-ANCA and myeloperoxidase (MPO)-ANCA were negative on enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Renal function was normal (blood urea nitrogen 10.8 mg/dL; creatinine 0.61 mg/dL), and urinalysis showed no hematuria or proteinuria. Extensive microbiological workup, including blood cultures, tuberculosis assays, and viral panels, was unrevealing. Two months prior, the patient had been hospitalized at an outside hospital where broad-spectrum antibiotics had failed to control fever; he remained continuously febrile throughout the two-month interval, up to admission to our hospital. (A) Coronal CT image demonstrating diffuse smooth wall thickening and enhancement (red arrows) along the trachea and bilateral main bronchi. (B) Axial CT image at the level of the trachea showing circumferential wall thickening (red arrows). Chest CT showed no parenchymal nodules, cavities, or alveolar hemorrhage to suggest pulmonary granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) involvement. CT of the paranasal sinuses revealed only a retention cyst in the left sphenoid sinus, with no destructive sinonasal lesions to suggest GPA. Dynamic airway CT was not performed. The radiological report listed amyloidosis and GPA as differential diagnoses; relapsing polychondritis (RP) was not included. In light of the CT findings suspicious for GPA, further workup for IgG4-related disease and atypical infections was not pursued at that stage. GPA can cause indistinguishable tracheobronchial CT changes [1] and is ANCA-negative in approximately 10–20% of confirmed cases [2], rendering seronegative status uninformative. Meeting criteria for fever of unknown origin, whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT was subsequently performed as part of the systematic evaluation [3].

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET)/CT demonstrating tracheobronchial involvement in the same patient. (A) Maximum intensity projection (MIP) image demonstrates intense FDG uptake along the trachea and both main bronchi in a continuous, linear pattern, forming the classic inverted-Y sign, a pattern characteristic of tracheobronchial chondritis in RP [4,5]. Notably, no abnormal uptake is identified in the renal parenchyma, paranasal sinuses, or orbits, which are sites typically involved in GPA, further arguing against a vasculitic etiology [6]. (B,C) Coronal PET and PET/CT fusion images show diffuse, intense hypermetabolism (maximum standardized uptake value; SUVmax 8.8) confined to the cartilaginous wall of the trachea and bilateral main bronchi. (D,E) Axial PET and PET/CT fusion images at the level of the trachea show FDG uptake predominantly in the anterior and lateral walls corresponding to the C-shaped cartilaginous rings, with relative sparing of the posterior membranous wall. Although posterior wall involvement can be seen in a subset of RP patients, this predominantly cartilage-selective distribution is consistent with RP [7].

18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrating bilateral costal cartilage involvement in the same patient. (A,B) Coronal PET and PET/CT fusion images through the costal cartilages reveal bilaterally symmetric focal hypermetabolic lesions (SUVmax 3.8) in the costal cartilages. Costal chondritis is a hallmark of RP and is characteristically absent in GPA [8]. The combination of tracheobronchial and bilateral costal cartilage involvement, with no renal, orbital, or paranasal sinus uptake, constituted a PET distribution diagnostic of RP and incompatible with GPA. The combination of saddle nose deformity (indicating nasal chondritis) and PET-confirmed airway and costal chondritis satisfied two major criteria of the Michet classification for RP [9]. High-dose corticosteroid therapy was initiated shortly after diagnosis with intravenous methylprednisolone (125 mg/day) for approximately 2 weeks at a local hospital, followed by oral prednisolone with gradual tapering at a tertiary care center, to which the patient was transferred for ongoing management. Methotrexate was subsequently added as a steroid-sparing agent. The patient demonstrated prompt clinical and biochemical response within one month, with normalization of inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein from 16.09 to 0.14 mg/dL; erythrocyte sedimentation rate from 120 to 5 mm/h), and has been maintained on chronic immunosuppression with periodic dose adjustments over approximately nine months of follow-up, consistent with the relapsing–remitting course of RP. This response further satisfied the Damiani and Levine criteria of multi-site chondritis with corticosteroid responsiveness [10] and was supportive of the RP diagnosis. RP is a rare disease, with an estimated prevalence of 9.0 per million [11], and is frequently overlooked in the differential diagnosis of tracheobronchial disease, as illustrated in this case, where RP was absent from the radiological report entirely. Unlike CT, which demonstrated only the morphological abnormality of wall thickening, PET/CT revealed three complementary features that collectively established the diagnosis: predominantly cartilage-directed uptake within the airway walls, simultaneous involvement of the costal cartilages, a site not apparent on CT, and the absence of uptake at sites characteristic of GPA such as the kidneys, orbits, and sinuses. No single feature alone is pathognomonic; however, their combination constitutes a distinctive pattern characteristic of RP. 18F-FDG PET/CT has been shown to have high sensitivity for cartilaginous inflammation in RP, capable of revealing involvement that may be overlooked on conventional imaging or by clinical criteria alone [4,5,12]. In the present case, the cartilage-selective pattern on PET/CT redirected the diagnostic trajectory toward RP, despite its absence from the initial radiological differential. This case highlights the importance of clinical context in radiological interpretation. Although saddle nose deformity was clinically apparent, it was not weighted in the initial differential diagnosis, which instead prioritized vasculitic etiologies based on the CT pattern. Such reliance on imaging features without sufficient clinical contextualization may contribute to diagnostic delay in rare conditions such as RP. For clinicians encountering unexplained tracheobronchial wall thickening with negative or inconclusive serology, awareness of this rare diagnosis and early 18F-FDG PET/CT should be considered to guide further management.
