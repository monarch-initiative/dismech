# HPV18-Positive Cervical Large Cell Neuroendocrine Carcinoma With Rapid Progression Despite Pembrolizumab Combined With Chemotherapy: A Case Report With Immunogenomic Insights.
**Authors:** Ogura J, Sato S, Koshida Y, Kasuga M, Yokoe T, Kanamoto N
**Journal:** J Obstet Gynaecol Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/jog.70426](https://doi.org/10.1111/jog.70426)

## Content

HPV18-positive cervical large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) is exceptionally rare. We report a 40-years-old woman with FIGO 2018 stage IVB LCNEC who progressed rapidly despite paclitaxel-carboplatin-bevacizumab plus pembrolizumab, followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy and later cytotoxic regimens. Biopsy showed chromogranin A and synaptophysin positivity, diffuse p16, and Ki-67%~80%. Comprehensive profiling revealed very low tumor mutational burden (TMB, 1.21/Mb), microsatellite stability, no homologous recombination deficiency, and minimal PD-L1 (~1%), consistent with a poorly immunogenic tumor microenvironment (TME). This profile may explain the limited effect of immune checkpoint inhibition in this histology. The case suggests that HPV or p16 positivity alone did not predict clinical benefit from pembrolizumab-based therapy in advanced cervical LCNEC, and supports careful biological assessment before treatment selection. These observations provide practical guidance for clinicians and support evaluation of alternative or investigational strategies for this aggressive tumor.

Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) of the uterine cervix is an extremely rare and aggressive tumor, accounting for less than 0.1% of all cervical cancers [1]. Most cervical LCNECs are associated with high‐risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, particularly types 16 and 18, and are characterized by p16 overexpression. LCNEC typically progresses rapidly with high metastatic potential, and there is no universally accepted standard of care [2]. In the absence of high‐level evidence, international guidance commonly recommends platinum–etoposide (EP) as first‐line therapy for cervical neuroendocrine carcinoma. By contrast, in Japan, no single first‐line regimen has been formally specified for cervical LCNEC.

Recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly pembrolizumab, have shown efficacy in advanced HPV‐associated cervical cancers [3]. By contrast, evidence for ICI use in cervical LCNEC remains very limited. Although pulmonary LCNEC has shown responses to ICIs in some reports [4], the relevance of this analogy to cervical LCNEC is uncertain. The overlap between pulmonary and cervical LCNEC lies mainly in their high‐grade neuroendocrine morphology and classification, whereas their etiologic and molecular backgrounds differ substantially, with pulmonary LCNEC typically linked to smoking‐related genomic damage and cervical LCNEC usually linked to HPV‐associated carcinogenesis. Therefore, extrapolation of treatment strategies from pulmonary LCNEC to cervical LCNEC should be regarded as exploratory rather than evidence‐based. At the same time, recent studies suggest that neuroendocrine differentiation is associated with an immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment (TME), with reduced antigen presentation and limited immune‐cell infiltration, which may diminish immunotherapy effectiveness [5].

Here, we report a case of HPV18‐positive cervical LCNEC that showed rapid disease progression despite chemo‐immunotherapy and subsequent treatments. To our knowledge, this is one of the earliest reports of ICI use in cervical LCNEC. Through analysis of the clinical course and immune‐evasion pathways—compared with pulmonary LCNEC—we highlight how biological differences may influence treatment responsiveness in this tumor type.

A 40‐years‐old woman, gravida 4 para 2, presented with persistent abdominal pain and a 3 cm cervical mass that was identified on pelvic examination. She was referred to our hospital for further evaluation in mid‐May of Year X.

Pelvic MRI showed a cervical mass with suspected parametrial invasion and right pelvic lymphadenopathy (Figure1a,b). PET‐CT revealed multiple osteolytic lesions involving the pubic bone, bilateral iliac bones, and the left ischium, indicating metastases (Figure1c–f). No visceral metastases were identified.

Cervical biopsy showed a poorly differentiated carcinoma with neuroendocrine morphology.

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed strong positivity for chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and p16, with a Ki‐67 labeling index of approximately 80% in hot spot regions. Somatostatin receptor (SSTR2) staining was negative (Figure2a–g).

Based on these morphological and immunohistochemical findings, histopathological evaluation by a gynecologic oncology pathologist confirmed the diagnosis of LCNEC according to WHO 2020 criteria, characterized by large nuclei with coarse chromatin, abundant cytoplasm, and high mitotic activity.

The tumor was thus diagnosed as HPV‐related LCNEC of the uterine cervix, corresponding to FIGO 2018 Stage IVB (T2bN1M1).

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) was initially considered; however, due to the extensive bone metastases, systemic chemotherapy was initiated. From mid‐June, the patient started a combination regimen of paclitaxel, carboplatin, bevacizumab, and pembrolizumab.

After the first cycle, she developed a severe drug eruption suspected to be an immune‐related adverse event (irAE). Intravenous corticosteroid therapy was administered and tapered. Despite continuation of therapy through three cycles, reassessment demonstrated progressive disease per iRECIST, with the appearance of new lesions despite a transient decline in serum NSE and SCC, indicating a lack of overall clinical benefit. The regimen was discontinued and definitive CCRT was initiated in mid‐August.

Radiation therapy was administered at a total dose of 59.4 Gy in 33 fractions, along with weekly cisplatin chemotherapy. During CCRT, the patient experienced grade 3 neutropenia and thrombocytopenia, requiring administration of granulocyte‐colony stimulating factor (G‐CSF) and platelet transfusion.

Despite these interventions, CT in early November showed disease progression with new pulmonary nodules (Figure3). As UGT1A1 polymorphisms were negative, second‐line irinotecan plus nedaplatin (CPT‐11 + NDP) was initiated.

After two cycles, her general condition deteriorated, and she was hospitalized with anorexia and fatigue. Fluid replacement was initiated; however, subsequent CT showed rapid exacerbation of pulmonary metastases (Figure3), and the CPT‐11 + NDP regimen was discontinued. Given the markedly impaired pulmonary condition and the limited expected benefit, we discussed the poor prognosis and the option of best supportive care with the patient and family before initiating further treatment. After this discussion, carboplatin and etoposide combination therapy (CBDCA+ETP) was started, recognizing the limited likelihood of benefit because few other regimens with precedent in high‐grade cervical neuroendocrine carcinoma remained.

In early February, the patient presented again with worsening cough and chest pain, which were attributed to rib fractures and pneumonia, and was rehospitalized for symptom management. Steroids, antibiotics, and oxygen therapy were administered, and continuous opioid analgesics were started. Symptomatic care continued until the patient's death in late February of Year X + 1.

Comprehensive genomic profiling using FoundationOne CDx revealed a tumor mutational burden (TMB) of 1.21 mutations/Mb, microsatellite stability (MS‐stable), and a negative homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) signature. In addition, HPV18 DNA was detected.

No actionable genetic alterations were identified. Immunohistochemistry for PD‐L1 revealed a tumor proportion score of approximately 1% (Figure2h). No autopsy was performed. The clinical timeline is shown in Figure3.

This case presented HPV18‐positive cervical LCNEC, a rare and aggressive subtype, characterized by rapid progression despite pembrolizumab combination chemotherapy and strong p16 expression. Although pembrolizumab has demonstrated efficacy in advanced or recurrent cervical cancer in KEYNOTE‐826, our case suggests that such benefits may not be generalized to cervical LCNEC.

Regarding treatment selection, published evidence for cervical LCNEC remains limited to case reports, small series, and broader reviews of neuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix [4,5]. In these reports, platinum‐based chemotherapy, particularly platinum–etoposide, is the most commonly described systemic approach, but LCNEC‐specific prospective data are lacking and outcomes remain poor [4,5]. In the present case, paclitaxel–carboplatin–bevacizumab plus pembrolizumab was selected because no standard first‐line regimen for cervical LCNEC has been established in Japan, pembrolizumab‐based therapy had become available in routine practice for advanced or recurrent cervical cancer, and limited reports of immune checkpoint blockade in neuroendocrine cervical cancer and LCNEC of other organs suggested a possible, although unproven, benefit [6,7,8]. After discussing with the patient regarding the limited evidence and available treatment options, including platinum–etoposide, we selected this regimen. We acknowledge that this regimen was not an established first‐line standard for cervical LCNEC and that platinum–etoposide would also have been a reasonable alternative.

SCNEC and LCNEC should also be distinguished. In general, SCNEC is composed of smaller cells with scant cytoplasm, whereas LCNEC shows larger cells with more abundant cytoplasm and more conspicuous nucleoli. However, because cervical LCNEC is far less common than SCNEC, most available therapeutic data are derived from mixed neuroendocrine cervical carcinoma cohorts or SCNEC‐dominant series. Recent population‐level data suggest that LCNEC may present with somewhat less advanced local tumor features than SCNEC, yet overall survival remains similarly poor [9]. Accordingly, in this manuscript we use “LCNEC” when referring specifically to the present histology and “neuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix” only when discussing evidence derived from mixed cohorts.

To our knowledge, this is among the first reports describing ICI use specifically in cervical LCNEC, offering insights into its immune characteristics. HPV‐associated tumors can exhibit “immune hot” features driven by HPV viral antigens (E6/E7) that stimulate cytotoxic T‐cell responses [10]. By contrast, neuroendocrine tumors, including LCNEC, frequently exhibit an “immune cold” TME, with minimal immune cell infiltration and abundant immunosuppressive cytokines [11,12]. The limited immune activity observed in our case is consistent with these features, suggesting distinct immune resistance mechanisms intrinsic to cervical LCNEC.

Three immunobiological mechanisms may explain the lack of benefit from ICI in this patient.

First, impaired antigen presentation. Despite HPV18 and p16 positivity, impaired antigen presentation might have restricted cytotoxic T‐cell recognition, resulting in a state of “immune ignorance” [11]. Although HPV‐derived antigens may have dominated the tumor immunopeptidome, their presence alone is unlikely to sustain antitumor immunity when antigen presentation is defective or immune‐escape programs are active [11,12].

Second, an immunosuppressive TME. Factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), interleukin‐10 (IL‐10), and transforming growth factor‐beta (TGF‐β), which suppress dendritic cell maturation, restrict T‐cell infiltration, and expand myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). These factors may counteract the immunostimulatory effects of viral neoantigens like HPV‐derived oncoproteins (E6/E7), creating an “immune desert” phenotype [11,12].

Third, low tumor mutational burden (TMB). In this case, comprehensive genomic profiling showed a TMB of only 1.21 mutations/Mb, which is very low by current benchmarks [13]. This may also reduce neoantigen diversity and impair sustained immune responses.

Comparison with pulmonary LCNEC is informative but should be interpreted cautiously. The similarity lies mainly in their shared high‐grade neuroendocrine morphology and in the fact that both are classified as neuroendocrine carcinomas. However, pulmonary LCNEC typically arises in older patients with a strong smoking history and often shows smoking‐related genomic damage, including molecular subsets characterized by TP53/RB1 or TP53/STK11/KEAP1 alterations [14]. Cervical LCNEC, in contrast, is usually HPV‐associated and develops in a distinct tissue and etiologic context. Pulmonary LCNEC therefore provides a biologic reference point, but not a direct therapeutic surrogate, for cervical LCNEC. In our case, the combination of very low TMB, minimal PD‐L1 expression, and rapid progression despite pembrolizumab is more consistent with an immune‐cold cervical LCNEC phenotype than with the more immunotherapy‐responsive subset reported in pulmonary LCNEC [8,14,15]. Available molecular profiling data in cervical neuroendocrine carcinoma also suggests that PD‐L1 positivity and TMB‐high status are uncommon overall, but these data are not LCNEC‐specific and should be interpreted with caution [16].

From a gynecologic oncology perspective, this case offers several important clinical implications:

First, HPV infection and p16 overexpression alone did not predict benefit from pembrolizumab‐based therapy in LCNEC. Histologic subtype and immune context must be considered in treatment selection.

Second, early response assessment and prompt discontinuation of ineffective therapy are critical in this rapidly progressive subtype.

Third, the absence of actionable alterations on next‐generation sequencing reveals the current limitations of biomarker‐guided therapy in LCNEC.

Fourth, in localized disease, early aggressive surgery should be considered when complete resection is feasible, given the limited effectiveness of systemic options.

Accordingly, comprehensive immunogenomic evaluation—encompassing HLA status, MHC class I expression, the density and spatial distribution of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), checkpoint expression, and the cytokine context may better stratify candidates for immunotherapy.

Future strategies to overcome immune evasion could include epigenetic modulators (DNMT/HDAC inhibitors), STING/TLR agonists, adoptive T‐cell approaches (including TIL therapy), and HPV‐targeted therapeutic vaccines in rational combinations with ICIs.

In conclusion, this case represents one of the earliest reported applications of immune checkpoint inhibition in HPV18‐positive cervical LCNEC. Despite biomarkers often associated with ICI responsiveness, such as p16 overexpression and viral antigen presence, the tumor exhibited rapid progression under immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy. These findings underscore the limitations of relying solely on HPV status when selecting immunotherapy, and emphasize the need for immunogenomic profiling and individualized treatment strategies for this rare malignancy.
