# Insights Into Congenital Lymphatic Anomalies Underlying Fetal Effusions.
**Authors:** Vargo SG, Rogerson D, Devine P, Van Ziffle J, Sparks TN
**Journal:** Prenat Diagn (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/pd.70016](https://doi.org/10.1002/pd.70016)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: We describe a series of pregnancies with autosomal dominant lymphedema and generalized lymphatic dysplasia in the fetus diagnosed with prenatal exome or genome sequencing. We focus on specific syndromes, fetal features, and parental symptoms to deepen our understanding of congenital lymphatic anomalies.

METHODS: Pregnancies with one or more fetal effusions were prospectively enrolled from 2017 to 2024 and underwent exome or genome sequencing. Fetal effusions included increased nuchal translucency ≥ 3.5 mm, cystic hygroma, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, ascites, and/or skin edema. Records were reviewed to extract personal and family history, ultrasound findings, and pregnancy outcomes.

RESULTS: Among 303 pregnancies with one or more fetal effusions, eight (3%) had a molecular diagnosis of autosomal dominant lymphedema or generalized lymphatic dysplasia. Gestational age at detection of fetal effusion(s) ranged from the first through third trimesters. Four fetuses inherited the genetic variant(s) from a biological parent. Of these, one parent was asymptomatic, and three had largely milder symptoms than their fetus. Perinatal outcomes were more favorable for fetuses with heterozygous PIEZO1 variants.

CONCLUSION: Autosomal dominant lymphedema and generalized lymphatic dysplasia can present with a breadth of fetal effusions from the first through third trimesters and are frequently inherited from a biological parent with less severe symptoms. These data provide a deeper understanding of how congenital lymphatic anomalies manifest in utero and inform expectations about recurrence risk in future pregnancies.

Congenital lymphatic anomalies are characterized by the accumulation of lymphatic fluid in extracellular spaces as a result of abnormal formation or dysfunction of the lymphovascular system [1]. Many genes are essential for the development, maintenance, and function of the lymphovascular system, and disease‐causing variants in these genes can lead to clinical manifestations of lymphatic anomalies [1]. For example, FOXC2 is reported to have a role in the normal development and function of lymphatic valves, and FLT4 is involved in lymphangiogenic sprouting and the proliferation, survival, and migration of endothelial cells [1, 2, 3]. Additional genes important for lymphatic development include CCBE1, CELSR1, EPHB4, GATA2, and PIEZO1, among many others [1, 4, 5].

Lymphovascular dysfunction and accumulation of lymphatic fluid can lead to edema in various parts of the body, and disease manifestations can be observed to differing degrees across the lifespan from in utero to childhood or adult years [1]. Individuals with disease‐causing variants in genes associated with lymphatic anomalies may present with no aberrant features, with edema alone, or with additional findings such as vascular malformations, upturned toenails, distichiasis, or cardiac anomalies due to expression of abnormal protein products in blood vessels, skin, eyes, and other tissues [6]. Although these presentations have been well‐classified in the pediatric and adult populations, details of the fetal manifestations of congenital lymphatic anomalies are more limited and their classification in utero is more challenging [7]. Overall, congenital lymphatic anomalies are thought to underlie 2%–5% of euploid pregnancies with fetal effusions, and in utero features may include increased nuchal translucency (NT), cystic hygroma, pleural effusion, ascites, pericardial effusion, skin edema, polyhydramnios, and/or more isolated lymphovascular malformations [4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

Congenital lymphatic anomalies can occur de novo or be inherited in autosomal dominant, recessive, or X‐linked manners. When inherited, variable expressivity and reduced penetrance have been reported within affected families [1, 4]. There is limited data to suggest that an unaffected or minimally affected biological parent can pass on an autosomal dominant lymphatic anomaly syndrome to their fetus that manifests severely with fetal effusions or non‐immune hydrops fetalis (NIHF) [4, 15, 16, 17]. In order to better counsel expectant families, further data are needed to illustrate how often variants causing lymphatic anomalies are inherited when a fetus is found to have effusions, what the outcomes of such pregnancies might be, and what can be expected for future pregnancies.

We report a series of autosomal dominant lymphedema and generalized lymphatic dysplasia syndromes that were diagnosed through prenatal genomic sequencing after presenting with a variety of fetal effusions. We examine the associated genes and variants, fetal presentations, and relevant symptoms in the biological parents when inherited. These data contribute to a deeper understanding of how congenital lymphatic anomalies manifest in utero and inform the approach to clinical counseling about the potential severity of disease and recurrence risk.

This is a case series of prospectively enrolled pregnancies from 2017 to 2024. We recruited pregnancies with one or more of the following fetal effusions: increased NT ≥ 3.5 mm, cystic hygroma, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, ascites, and/or skin edema. We focus on those pregnancies diagnosed with an autosomal dominant lymphedema or generalized lymphatic dysplasia, as X‐linked conditions were not identified in our cohort and parental symptoms are generally not expected with parental carrier status for autosomal recessive forms of these diseases. Molecular diagnoses were made through either exome or genome sequencing as described in further detail below, with a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant identified in a gene known to be associated with abnormal lymphovascular development. Three of the genetic variants we report were previously published by our group [11, 18] but with very minimal phenotypic data previously and without the fine level of detail we now provide including evolution of the fetal phenotype across gestation and parental symptoms. Pregnancies with a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) in a gene associated with abnormal lymphovascular development are not reported in this series, given the uncertainty about diagnosis in such situations. Regulatory approval was obtained from our institutional review board and all participants provided written informed consent.

Genomic sequencing was performed through the University of California, San Francisco Genomic Medicine Laboratory (GML), with exome sequencing performed beginning in 2017 and genome sequencing beginning in 2023. The UCSF GML is certified by the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) program and results of exome and genome sequencing were returned to patients and providers in real time. The GML performed exome sequencing using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer in rapid‐run mode prior to 2020 and the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer from 2020 onward. Genome sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer. Variant call format files were uploaded into Ingenuity Variant Analysis (Qiagen) before 2020 and into Moon (Diploid) from 2020 onward. Detailed fetal phenotypic features were provided to the GML using Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms to support the most accurate identification of relevant genetic variants [19]. A multidisciplinary board convened on a weekly basis to review genetic variants identified through this sequencing pipeline and classify reportable variants according to criteria outlined by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics [20].

Detailed clinical data were extracted for each pregnancy, including personal and family history, obstetric history, pregnancy course and complications, prenatal imaging findings, delivery information, and infant outcomes. All prenatal ultrasounds for each pregnancy were obtained and reviewed to ascertain the full fetal phenotype and contribute the most accurate HPO terms for bioinformatic analyses as described above. For each live birth, follow‐up was performed through at least the birth hospitalization, whereas for each fetal or infant demise, additional pathology records were obtained when available to capture the full phenotype.

Among 303 pregnancies with one or more fetal effusions, eight (3%) had a molecular diagnosis of autosomal dominant lymphedema or generalized lymphatic dysplasia. Overall, the gestational age at initial detection of fetal effusion(s) ranged from the first through third trimesters. Four fetuses inherited a lymphatic anomaly from a biological parent (with potentially a fifth that remained uncertain due to low‐level mosaicism). Three of these biological parents manifested symptoms consistent with a lymphatic anomaly that were largely milder than those observed in their fetus, and one was asymptomatic. A 36 year old G3P1011 had an anatomical ultrasound at 23 weeks 6 days showing a right pleural effusion, bilateral lower extremity edema to the knees, and normal middle cerebral artery (MCA) Doppler measurements. Amniocentesis yielded normal male chromosomal analysis and chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) had a 2.85 Mb paternally inherited interstitial deletion at 7p12.1p11.2 containing one OMIM gene (HPVC1) unrelated to the fetal presentation. Exome sequencing identified a paternally inherited heterozygous missense pathogenic variant in FLT4 (c.3121C > T, p.Arg1041Trp) associated with lymphatic malformation 1 (Milroy disease), a congenital lymphatic anomaly that can present with fetal effusions and postnatal lower extremity edema often to the knees [1, 21, 22]. Femur lengths were noted to be 3 weeks less than dates. Bilateral thoracenteses at 27 weeks 2 days removed 60 and 70 mL of fluid from the left and right pleural spaces, respectively, and a right thoracoamniotic shunt was placed. Fluid analyses revealed a large number of lymphocytes consistent with chylothorax and culture was negative. Induction of labor was pursued at 31 weeks 6 days due to shunt failure and worsening NIHF. The infant was confirmed to have bilateral pleural effusions and lower extremity edema. For that child, now 5 years old, additional manifestations include hydrocele and mild unilateral pedal edema. The biological father reported a history of unexplained, intermittent bilateral lower extremity edema throughout his life.

A 30 year old G4P2012 with a previous pregnancy affected by NIHF and oligohydramnios presented for first trimester ultrasound at 12 weeks 0 days. The NT was elevated at 4.2 mm. Chorionic villus sampling (CVS) was performed, yielding a normal male chromosomal analysis and CMA. At 19 weeks 0 days, a small pericardial effusion, bilateral echogenic kidneys, and right pelviectasis were seen. Exome sequencing uncovered a maternally inherited likely pathogenic heterozygous nonsense variant in FOXC2 (c.1027G > T, p.Glu343Ter) associated with lymphedema‐distichiasis syndrome, a congenital lymphatic anomaly that can present with fetal effusions and lymphedema, distichiasis, and yellow nails [1, 6, 23]. The pregnancy was terminated at 19 weeks 3 days, and pathology of the products of conception was unremarkable. Unfortunately, there was no remaining DNA from the prior pregnancy to test for the FOXC2 variant. The biological mother did not have distichiasis and reported a history of unexplained bilateral lower extremity edema for the prior 14 months, although with potential contribution of May‐Thurner syndrome that arose the same year.

A 41 year old G5P0402 had a history of two preterm births, an unexplained intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD), and a neonatal demise after preterm premature rupture of membranes with shoulder dystocia at delivery. In the current pregnancy, ultrasound at 14 weeks 5 days identified a 16‐mm cystic hygroma, skin edema, and bilateral pleural effusions. Fetal ascites was seen at 18 weeks 4 days, followed by anasarca at 20 weeks 0 days. Additional findings at 22–23 weeks were placentomegaly, oligohydramnios, small to absent stomach, small bladder, and worsening NIHF. MCA Doppler measurements were normal. At 24 weeks 1 day, the patient underwent a repeat cesarean delivery due to worsening NIHF, fetal distress, and desire for full intervention. The infant had profound anasarca, cystic hygroma, and pleural effusions, and unfortunately demised quickly. Autopsy revealed a multiloculated cystic hygroma, anasarca, pleural effusions, and absent corpus callosum, and did not identify distichiasis. Postnatal chromosomal analysis and CMA returned normal female results. Exome sequencing identified a heterozygous frameshift pathogenic variant in FOXC2 (c.914_921del, p.Tyr305Cysfs*155) associated with lymphedema‐distichiasis syndrome [6, 23]. The biological parents did not submit samples, leaving the inheritance uncertain, but neither reported a history consistent with lymphedema‐distichiasis syndrome.

A 29 year old G1P0 had normal first and second trimester obstetric ultrasounds. At 28 weeks 3 days, she reported spots in her vision, right upper quadrant pain, and headache. Evaluations did not identify evidence of preeclampsia, but ultrasound at 32 weeks 2 days revealed large bilateral pleural effusions, ascites, massive skin edema, polyhydramnios, unilateral mild ventriculomegaly, and normal MCA Doppler measurements. Amniocentesis and right thoracentesis were performed, and initial genetic tests included normal male chromosomal analysis and CMA. At 32 weeks 3 days, the right pleural effusion had reaccumulated and NIHF had worsened, at which time a primary cesarean delivery was performed. Unfortunately, neonatal death rapidly ensued. Autopsy showed a small pericardial effusion, significant pleural effusions, ascites, and skin edema. Genome sequencing identified a paternally inherited likely pathogenic heterozygous stop gain variant in CELSR1 (c.5512C > T, p.Arg1838*) associated with lymphatic malformation 9 syndrome, which has incomplete penetrance and can manifest with NIHF and/or lymphedema of the lower extremities beginning in childhood [24, 25, 26, 27]. The biological father reported no symptoms of lymphatic malformation 9 syndrome.

A 21 year old G1P0 had an anatomical survey at 21 weeks 5 days showing fetal ascites and left pleural effusion. Amniocentesis was performed; chromosomal analysis showed normal male results and CMA had a maternally inherited 296 kb gain of Xq13.2, containing one gene associated with human disease (NAP1L2) unrelated to the fetal effusions. Exome sequencing found a likely pathogenic heterozygous missense variant in PIEZO1 (c.2610G > A, p.Met870Ile), associated with dehydrated hereditary stomatocytosis with or without pseudohyperkalemia and/or perinatal edema (DHS). Low‐level mosaicism (7%) of the PIEZO1 variant was detected in the maternal sample, representing true inheritance versus technical artifact. Heterozygous PIEZO1 variants can lead to variable features including perinatal edema and/or anemia, dehydrated erythrocytes with hemolytic anemia, and adult‐onset edema [28, 29, 30, 31]. The biological mother reported no symptoms associated with DHS. The fetus developed worsening ascites, bilateral effusions, scalp edema, abdominal circumference measurements 5–10 weeks ahead of dates, and polyhydramnios. MCA Doppler measurements were normal. At 31 weeks 6 days, the patient developed elevated blood pressures, proteinuria, right upper quadrant pain, headaches, and blurry vision and was ultimately diagnosed with preeclampsia with severe features. At 32 weeks 5 days, fetal paracentesis was performed followed by cesarean delivery of a live male infant. The infant had only mild anemia at birth with a hematocrit of 34% and had not received an in utero blood transfusion.

A 39 year old G3P0020 was found to have a cystic hygroma measuring 4.6 mm at 14 weeks 6 days. Ascites was then seen at 15 weeks 6 days and amniocentesis was performed. Initial genetic testing included normal female chromosomal analysis, CMA, and Noonan syndrome gene panel. At 19–20 weeks, the cystic hygroma persisted, MCA Doppler measurements were normal to borderline elevated (1.4–1.5 MoM), and there were bilateral pleural effusions, trace pericardial effusion, skin and pedal edema, and hypoplastic nasal bone. Percutaneous umbilical blood sampling (PUBS) showed fetal anemia (Hgb 5.5 g/dL) with spherocytes and intrauterine transfusion (IUT) was performed. Exome sequencing revealed a de novo heterozygous likely pathogenic missense variant in PIEZO1 (c.1792G > A, p.Val598Met) associated with DHS [28, 29, 30, 31]. Multiple PUBS/IUT procedures were performed over subsequent weeks with intermittent fetal anemia (hemoglobin 8.4–12.5 g/dL). While some fetal effusions resolved, ascites and skin edema persisted, and the abdominal circumference measured 7–10 weeks ahead of dates. Cesarean delivery was performed at 37 weeks 4 days. The infant had facial and lower extremity edema, underwent paracentesis at 1 day of life, and required blood transfusion at 3 weeks (hemoglobin 7.2 g/dL). At 4 years old, she has mild compensated anemia without lymphedema.

A 30‐year old G1P0 patient with a clinical diagnosis of DHS had a history of chronic hemolytic anemia and cholelithiasis but no overt lymphedema. First trimester ultrasound was normal, but at 19 weeks 6 days, there was ascites with normal MCA Doppler measurements. Amniocentesis was performed and CMA showed a 35 kb deletion of 12p13.33, containing one gene associated with human disease (CACNA2D4) that indicated carrier status for retinal cone dystrophy 4. Genome sequencing revealed a maternally inherited heterozygous pathogenic missense variant in PIEZO1 (c.7367G > A, p.Arg2456His) associated with DHS [28, 29, 30, 31]. PUBS was recommended given the PIEZO1 variant and maternal history, which showed borderline fetal anemia (hemoglobin 10.7 g/dL), and a small volume IUT was performed. The fetal ascites was stable to increase over 21–29 weeks and then decreased to resolution by week 35. The fetal abdominal circumference measured 5–6 weeks ahead of dates, MCA Doppler measurements remained normal, and polyhydramnios was seen during week 35. Bilateral pleural effusions and a pericardial effusion were transiently noted, but these findings did not persist. The patient underwent induction of labor at 39 weeks 1 day followed by cesarean delivery for fetal intolerance. The infant was discharged at 3 days of life and at 4 months old he remains asymptomatic without anemia, hemolysis, or lymphedema.

A 35 year old G1P0 had normal first and second trimester ultrasounds. Ultrasound performed at 29 weeks 2 days for maternal age then identified skin edema, bilateral pleural effusions, polyhydramnios, and single umbilical artery. Over the next 4 weeks, additional findings included pericardial effusion, intermittent ascites, small stomach, hepatomegaly, abdominal circumference measuring 3–7 weeks ahead of dates, and femur lengths measuring 3–4 weeks less than dates. MCA Doppler measurements were largely normal except two measurements at approximately 32 weeks (1.55 and 1.66 MoM), and ductus venosus Doppler waveforms consistently showed reversed a‐wave. Unfortunately, IUFD occurred at 33 weeks 6 days and the patient delivered a demised male infant. After delivery, genetic testing was initiated. CMA showed normal male results and genome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous likely pathogenic missense variant (c.2063C > T, p.Pro688Leu) in EPHB4 associated with lymphatic malformation 7 and capillary malformation‐arteriovenous malformation 2 syndromes [32, 33]. These conditions have variable expressivity and may include features such as fetal effusions and postnatal lymphedema, anemia, and arteriovenous malformations [32]. Neither a detailed postnatal examination nor autopsy was performed.

We present a series of pregnancies complicated by autosomal dominant lymphedema and generalized lymphatic dysplasia syndromes, including a deep phenotypic look at each fetal presentation, the inheritance patterns, and relevant symptoms in biological parents. These cases highlight the spectrum of fetal congenital lymphatic anomaly presentations, with fetal effusions in various compartments developing throughout the first through third trimesters. They further illustrate the variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance seen with congenital lymphatic anomalies, with at least half of the variants inherited from biological parents who were either asymptomatic or had largely milder symptoms than their fetus.

Characterizing the fetal phenotypes of genetic diseases is a crucial endeavor to improve the accuracy of prenatal diagnosis and inform expectations for prognosis. Congenital lymphatic anomalies more often manifest in utero with effusions in isolation, although they can demonstrate additional congenital anomalies [4, 8, 9]. The prenatal findings of these diseases may or may not parallel known postnatal features. For example, the case of Milroy disease in our cohort with an FTL4 variant presented with lower extremity edema as well as pleural effusions, mirroring the predilection for lower extremity edema reported among postnatal individuals with this diagnosis [1]. In contrast, the two cases we report with lymphedema‐distichiasis syndrome and FOXC2 variants presented with various fetal effusions ranging from cystic hygroma to ascites and diffuse anasarca. Such fetal presentations are consistent with limited data previously published about in utero manifestations of FOXC2‐related disease, but they differ from the described postnatal lower extremity edema, and other features such as accessory eyelashes will be missed in utero [1, 6].

Differentiating patterns in the perinatal phenotypes and prognoses of specific congenital lymphatic anomalies remains challenging due to the limited number of well‐described cases, but some observations deserve further study. For example, prenatal presentations of congenital lymphatic anomalies associated with CELSR1 and EPHB4 variants can include severe effusions and poor outcomes with fetal or neonatal demise, consistent with the cases we present [4, 24, 32]. In contrast, all three cases in our cohort with autosomal dominant variants in PIEZO1 associated with DHS resulted in living children. Prenatal manifestations of DHS are reported to range from none to severe fetal hydrops, while postnatal presentations include hemolytic anemia, lymphedema, and long‐term outcomes that are generally more favorable [29, 31]. PIEZO1 disorders represent a spectrum of disease features; homozygous cases can cause generalized lymphatic dysplasia with or without erythrocyte abnormalities and heterozygous cases can cause DHS with or without lymphedema [4, 29]. Though DHS can cause fetal and postnatal anemia, this alone may not be the cause of NIHF, evidenced by effusions being chylous in nature when tested and the onset of postnatal lymphedema [29, 31]. In our series, the fetal features of cases with DHS were similarly mixed, with two having fetal anemia not predicted to be severe enough to precipitate NIHF in isolation [34], and one presenting with a cystic hygroma that would not be attributable to anemia. The complex pathophysiologic development and communication between the lymphatic and vascular systems likely contribute to variable fetal presentations of congenital lymphatic anomalies, although further research is needed to better understand specific gene functions, their impact on lymphovascular development, and their association with the unique fetal manifestations of disease [4, 29].

Our finding that autosomal dominant congenital lymphatic anomaly variants are often inherited provides useful data about the incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity of these conditions. In inherited cases, the biological parent was either asymptomatic or had less severe symptoms, and it is possible that some seemingly unaffected parents have symptoms so mild that they go unnoticed and unreported [35]. The existing literature includes reports of variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance among many congenital lymphatic anomalies [4, 17, 22, 23, 24, 25, 27, 35, 36, 37]. Fetal phenotypes more severe than parental phenotypes have been documented in some families affected by FOXC2 and PIEZO1 mutations, where parents with minimal symptoms of lymphedema or hematologic findings have had offspring with NIHF leading to fetal or neonatal demise [17, 23]. Sex differences have also been hypothesized with congenital lymphatic anomalies and lymphedema, with familial studies suggesting more prominent manifestations of CELSR1 variants in females [25, 38]. Targeted testing of the biological parents is an important consideration when a fetal congenital lymphatic anomaly is identified to inform recurrence risk and expectations about potential severity of disease in future pregnancies. Further research is needed to clarify the long‐term outcomes of affected fetuses who survive the tenuous perinatal period, as well as to investigate genotype‐phenotype correlations and the potential roles of modifier genes, epigenetics, and environmental factors.

This study has several strengths. Given the rarity of congenital lymphatic anomalies, this series adds depth to the small pool of described prenatal cases in the literature, reflects a similar estimate for the frequency of lymphatic anomalies underlying fetal effusions as reported in prior studies, and offers detailed prenatal genotype‐phenotype correlations which are invaluable for counseling and prognostic expectations. We further contribute unique data about the parental phenotypes seen among inherited congenital lymphatic anomalies, which are often missing from published cases, and add valuable data points to understand the breadth of phenotypes observed and inform recurrence risk counseling. Limitations of our work include unknown inheritance for one case and unknown postnatal phenotype for another. Additionally, parental symptoms were determined through careful history taking; not all biological parents underwent a formal assessment for lymphedema and it is possible that additional symptoms could have been identified with further follow up or assessment of their own fetal phenotypes.

In conclusion, we present a series of prenatally diagnosed lymphedema and generalized lymphatic dysplasia syndromes and characterize not only their unique perinatal presentations but also their variable expressivity. Autosomal dominant congenital lymphatic anomalies appear to be commonly inherited from a biological parent, who may have no recognizable congenital lymphatic anomaly symptoms or milder symptoms relative to those seen in their fetus. These data provide a deeper understanding of how congenital lymphatic anomalies manifest in utero, inform expectations about the potential severity of fetal disease and recurrence risk, and highlight the potential role of targeted interventions in the prenatal period. Testing strategies such as exome or genome sequencing that detect a breadth of congenital lymphatic anomalies among fetuses with effusions will be important for contributing further data and improving the accuracy of counseling for families suspected to have a congenital lymphatic anomaly syndrome.
