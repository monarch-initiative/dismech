# Webb-Dattani syndrome in a 17-year-old girl.
**Authors:** Alyami JS, Almistehi WM, Alkanhal KI
**Journal:** Endocrinol Diabetes Metab Case Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1530/EDM-25-0114](https://doi.org/10.1530/EDM-25-0114)

## Content

SUMMARY: Webb-Dattani syndrome (WEDAS) is an extremely rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the ARNT2 gene. It is characterized by a triad of congenital hypopituitarism, structural brain abnormalities, and multisystem developmental defects. We report the case of a 17-year-old girl with WEDAS who presented with global developmental delay, panhypopituitarism, and arginine vasopressin (AVP) deficiency, formerly known as central diabetes insipidus with adipsia, visual impairment, renal anomalies, and spastic quadriplegia. Her endocrine profile revealed deficiencies in ACTH, TSH, and ADH, and gonadotropins, with a possible growth hormone deficiency. Management included hormone replacement with hydrocortisone, levothyroxine, and desmopressin, as well as fluid regulation and supportive care. Despite multiple hospitalizations due to complications including hypernatremia and infections, the patient survived into adolescence - the longest reported survival in this condition to date - before passing away at age 17. This case expands the known clinical phenotype of WEDAS, emphasizing the importance of early recognition, genetic testing, and a multidisciplinary approach to care for affected individuals, particularly in consanguineous populations where the syndrome may be underdiagnosed.

LEARNING POINTS: Webb-Dattani syndrome (WEDAS) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by an ARNT2 pathogenic variant, presenting with a triad of congenital hypopituitarism, structural brain anomalies, and developmental delay. Adipsic diabetes insipidus is a hallmark complication in WEDAS and requires vigilant fluid management due to the absence of thirst sensation and persistent hypernatremia. Multisystem involvement - including renal anomalies and neurogenic bladder - may occur, expanding the known phenotypic spectrum of ARNT2-related disorders. Early recognition and genetic testing are essential for accurate diagnosis, especially in patients from consanguineous backgrounds presenting with multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies. Long-term survival, although rare, is possible with coordinated multidisciplinary care addressing endocrine, neurological, renal, and infectious complications. This case underscores the importance of individualized endocrine replacement therapy and caregiver education in managing complex syndromic conditions such as WEDAS.

Webb–Dattani syndrome (WEDAS) is an uncommon autosomal recessive disorder described in 2013 by Webb et al. (1). This rare condition results from a pathogenic variant in the ARNT2 gene and is characterized by a triad of congenital hypopituitarism, structural brain abnormalities, and various developmental defects. The syndrome’s hallmark features include multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies, postnatal microcephaly, and visual and renal anomalies.

The exact prevalence of WEDAS remains unknown, as only a limited number of cases have been documented in the medical literature since it was first described by Webb et al. Subsequent reports have also predominantly involved patients from consanguineous families of Saudi Arabian descent, suggesting a possible founder effect in this population (2).

ARNT2 (aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator 2) plays a crucial role in the development of the hypothalamo-pituitary axis and is essential for regulating several transcriptional pathways during brain and endocrine organ development (3). Animal models have shown that ARNT2 is vital for the expression of vasopressin and corticotropin-releasing hormone, which further explains the multisystem involvement observed in WEDAS (4). In humans, pathogenic variants in ARNT2 result in a distinctive constellation of clinical features that overlap with, but are distinguishable from, other syndromes involving hypopituitarism, such as septo-optic dysplasia or SOX gene-related disorders (3, 5).

Given the rarity and complexity of this condition, reporting new cases is essential to enhance clinical recognition, guide genetic counseling, and refine multidisciplinary management strategies. This case report aims to contribute and enhance the understanding of this rare condition and improve recognition and care for affected individuals.

A 14-year-old girl was referred to the endocrinology clinic with a diagnosis of Webb–Dattani syndrome. Her past medical history was notable for global developmental delay, blindness, seizure disorder, a small left kidney with neurogenic bladder, and spastic quadriplegia.

Family history revealed that the patient’s parents are first-degree cousins, and multiple relatives were affected by the same genetic condition. Her brother died at the age of 2 years, her sister at 2 months, and several cousins were known to carry the same ARNT2 pathogenic variant – one of whom died at age 11 from severe hospital-acquired pneumonia.

At her initial endocrinology clinic visit on March 16, 2021, she was 14 years and 10 months. Physical examination revealed low blood pressure (88/56 mmHg), a heart rate of 101 bpm, and underdeveloped secondary sexual characteristics (Tanner stage 1 for breast and pubic hair development, and absence of axillary hair).

At her initial assessment, the patient’s height was 121 cm and weight 24 kg, corresponding to a BMI of 16.3 kg/m2 (≈3rd percentile for age). Her growth parameters consistently tracked below the expected range for age and sex, in keeping with chronic pituitary hormone deficiencies.

Genetic testing confirmed a homozygous pathogenic variant in the ARNT2 gene, consistent with Webb–Dattani syndrome. Due to the recent passing of the patient and the family’s request for privacy, further genetic data from relatives could not be obtained.

Laboratory results indicated elevated sodium (154 mmol/L) and chloride (119 mmol/L) levels. An X-ray for bone age assessment showed a delay, with a bone age of 8 years against her chronological age of 14 years and 9 months. The patient was diagnosed with Webb–Dattani syndrome, panhypopituitarism (ACTH, TSH, and ADH deficiency), central adrenal insufficiency, central hypothyroidism, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, possible growth hormone deficiency, and arginine vasopressin (AVP) deficiency, formerly known as central diabetes insipidus (Table 1).

Management of the patient’s endocrinopathy included hydrocortisone (5 mg in the morning and 2.5 mg in the evening; total daily dose 7.5 mg, equivalent to 7.5 mg/m2 based on a body surface area of approximately 1.0 m2), levothyroxine (37.5 μg orally once daily), and intermittent sublingual desmopressin (15 μg) as needed for breakthrough symptoms.

The care plan included medication management, increased fluid intake, and consideration of growth hormone and gonadal hormone replacement therapy. Puberty-related issues and the risk of osteoporosis were discussed with the family, who decided to temporarily defer pubertal hormone replacement.

Over the following months, the patient’s clinical course was marked by persistent endocrine abnormalities and challenges in management. She missed several appointments, but laboratory results consistently showed elevated sodium levels and other hormonal imbalances. Particular concern was the gradual worsening of her fluid balance, with sodium levels and plasma osmolality continuing to rise despite treatment.

On January 10, 2023, although the patient could not attend, laboratory results were available. Sodium levels remained elevated (150 mmol/L), as did plasma osmolality (309 mOsm/kg). Persistent adipsic diabetes insipidus, with recurrent episodes of hypernatremia, was noted throughout multiple follow-up visits despite ongoing management. Hormone levels, including ACTH, cortisol, IGF-1, LH, and FSH, were persistently abnormal during follow-up. The persistence of these abnormalities underscored the ongoing challenges in managing the patient’s complex endocrine disorders.

Electrolyte imbalance was intermittently observed during intercurrent illnesses but improved with consistent desmopressin use and supervised fluid management.

A critical point in her care occurred on January 3, 2024, when she had severe hypernatremia. Persistent hypernatremia occurred primarily during intervals of desmopressin non-adherence and limited oral water intake, rather than due to desmopressin resistance. Once adherence improved and fluid intake was supervised, serum sodium gradually normalized.

Despite the critical lab values, she presented with stable vital signs and a normal Glasgow Coma Scale score. This episode led to adjustments in her treatment plan, including increased fluid intake and the addition of vitamin D and calcium supplementation. The patient’s condition was further complicated by recurrent infections and respiratory issues, with multiple admissions. In August 2024, she passed away at home.

This case report describes a 17-year-old girl with Webb–Dattani syndrome (WEDAS) caused by a homozygous frameshift pathogenic variant in the ARNT2 gene. The patient’s clinical presentation aligns closely with the phenotype originally described by Webb et al. in a large consanguineous Saudi Arabian family (1). Key features include congenital hypopituitarism with multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies, arginine vasopressin (AVP) deficiency, severe developmental delay, visual impairment, and renal anomalies.

Our patient exhibited a wide spectrum of hormonal deficiencies, including adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), gonadotropins, and antidiuretic hormone (ADH), along with suspected growth hormone deficiency, collectively referred to as panhypopituitarism. This broad endocrine dysfunction highlights the crucial role of ARNT2 in both pituitary organogenesis and hypothalamic signaling pathways (1, 2). Animal studies have shown that ARNT2 is a transcriptional partner of SIM1, which is critical for the development of the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus, an area involved in producing hormones such as corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and vasopressin (5). These molecular mechanisms help explain the clinical manifestation of ACTH deficiency and adipsic diabetes insipidus in WEDAS.

The persistence of hypernatremia and adipsic diabetes insipidus despite treatment remains a significant challenge in the management of WEDAS. Unlike other causes of arginine vasopressin (AVP) deficiency, adipsic DI in WEDAS patients results from disrupted hypothalamic osmoregulation due to ARNT2 dysfunction, making fluid regulation particularly difficult (1, 5). The absence of thirst sensation increases the risk for life-threatening hypernatremia and requires strict caregiver vigilance and scheduled fluid intake.

Interestingly, our patient also presented with a small left kidney and neurogenic bladder, features not consistently reported in previously published cases. However, this may reflect phenotypic variability, as renal anomalies have been described in earlier cohorts (1, 3). Wines et al. (4) documented ARNT2 expression in the developing kidney in murine models, suggesting a potential developmental role in renal morphogenesis (5). The neurogenic bladder observed may point toward broader hypothalamic or autonomic nervous system involvement.

While the severe neurological impairment observed in our patient is consistent with previous reports, her prolonged survival until age 17 is unique among published cases (Table 2). Other individuals with WEDAS have typically succumbed to complications in infancy or early childhood, primarily due to infections, adrenal crises, or fluid-electrolyte imbalances (1, 2). Her extended survival may indicate phenotypic heterogeneity or more effective multidisciplinary management. It is also plausible that her relatively prolonged survival was related to early recognition and management, facilitated by the strong family history and known genetic diagnosis. This underscores the importance of family screening, anticipatory management, and genetic counseling in families with identified ARNT2 pathogenic variants, which may improve outcomes and survival in affected individuals.

The overlapping clinical features between WEDAS and other disorders involving pituitary development (e.g. SOX2/3 pathogenic variants and septo-optic dysplasia) can complicate diagnosis. However, the consistent presence of arginine vasopressin (AVP) deficiency and severe developmental anomalies in WEDAS cases suggests that genetic testing for ARNT2 pathogenic variants should be prioritized in patients presenting with this constellation of findings (2, 4).

This case underscores the critical need for early diagnosis, genetic confirmation, and coordinated multidisciplinary care involving endocrinology, neurology, nephrology, urology, and infectious disease teams. The complexity of endocrine replacement therapy – particularly in patients with adipsic DI – necessitates close biochemical monitoring and robust caregiver education to prevent serious complications.

This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of Webb–Dattani syndrome and reinforces the critical role of ARNT2 in hypothalamo-pituitary development and function. Early recognition of this syndrome is crucial for appropriate management of hormone deficiencies and associated complications. Genetic testing for ARNT2 pathogenic variants should be considered in patients presenting with this constellation of features, particularly those from consanguineous backgrounds. Our case also underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in managing patients with Webb–Dattani syndrome. The complex endocrine dysfunction requires careful hormone replacement and monitoring, while the neurological, visual, and gastrointestinal issues necessitate input from various specialties.
