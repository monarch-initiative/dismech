ABSTRACT
TSPEAR (chr. 21q22.3) encodes a protein involved in tooth development and is predominantly expressed in the enamel knot.
Biallelic loss of function variants in TSPEAR  cause ectodermal dysplasia, tooth agenesis and sensorineural hearing loss.
However, the role of TSPEAR in auditory processes is unclear. This study aimed at better delineating the allelic and clinical spec-
trum of TSPEAR - associated disorders. We identified homozygous and compound heterozygous causative variants in TSPEAR  
[NM_144991.3] in 11 patients from seven families. Abnormalities in tooth number and shape (conical teeth and tooth agen -
esis with a variable number of missing teeth) were found in all affected individuals. Maxillary retrusion was present in 6/11.
Manifestations in other ectodermal-  derived organs were seen in a minority of patients. None of the individuals had hearing loss.
We identified a total of 10 variants, of which seven have not been previously published, and analyzed the effect of missense var -
iants to support their pathogenicity. Our results demonstrate that individuals with biallelic variants in TSPEAR  show complete
penetrance for dental manifestations, but not for other ectodermal abnormalities. TSPEAR - related disorder is more common
than previously thought, while hearing loss is not a feature of the disease.
1   |   Introduction
Odontogenesis refers to the process of tooth development,
leading to the formation of 20 and 32 teeth in the primary and
permanent dentition, respectively (Nieminen  2009). Different
signaling pathways regulate odontogenesis by antagonistic or
synergistic interactions. They include the bone morphogenetic
protein (BMP), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), tumor necrosis
factor (TNF), sonic hedgehog (SHH), and WNT pathways (Lan
et al. 2014).

Differences in tooth number are relatively common anomalies
and include supernumerary teeth and tooth agenesis. Based on
the number of missing congenital teeth, tooth agenesis is further
classified as follows (Ravi et al. 2023 ):
 i. Hypodontia: Absence of up to six teeth;
 ii. Oligodontia: Absence of more than six teeth;
 iii. Anodontia: Complete absence of teeth.
Hereditary, environmental, and evolutionary factors influence
tooth agenesis, which can either occur in association with vari -
ous systemic manifestations and syndromes or be isolated (Yin
and Bian 2015).
Pathogenic variants in several genes expressed during den -
tal development are involved in the etiology of tooth agenesis
(Modafferi et al.  2025). Thrombospondin-  type laminin G (TSP)
domain and epilepsy-  associated repeats ( TSPEAR) (OMIM
*612920) encodes two alternative transcripts: the main tran -
script of 3967 bp in length is composed of an N-  terminal signal
sequence, followed by a thrombospondin TSP domain and seven
tandem EAR domains that are 44 residues long. According to
phylogenetic analysis and AlphaFold structural analysis, the
laminin-  G- like domain interacts with heparin sulfate proteo -
glycans, participating in extracellular matrix-  dependent signal-
ing and allowing spatial and temporal sequestration of growth
factors involved in the development of ectodermal placodes
(Jackson et  al.  2023). The protein product is predominantly
expressed in the enamel knot of the developing tooth (Jackson
et al. 2023). Using siRNA- mediated down- regulation of TSPEAR
expression, Peled et al. (2016) studied the role of TSPEAR in the
regulation of Notch signaling, identifying several differentially
expressed genes of the NOTCH1  regulatory network, known to
be involved in hair and tooth development.
Biallelic rare variants in TSPEAR  were first associated with iso -
lated sensorineural hearing loss, but large replication studies are
not available (Delmaghani et al.  2012; Shi et al.  2024) and the
role of the gene in hearing function is still unclear and conflict -
ing. Recent studies described pathogenic variants in association
with ectodermal dysplasia and with isolated tooth agenesis, sug-
gesting that TSPEAR - associated disorders may be a spectrum
with varying severity (Du et al.  2018; Song et al. 2020). To date,
only about 30 individuals have been reported in the scientific
literature (Jackson et  al.  2023; Peled et  al.  2016; Delmaghani
et  al.  2012; Shi et  al.  2024; Du et  al.  2018; Song et  al.  2020;
Bowles et  al.  2021; Rabie et  al.  2022; Pavlenkova et  al.  2021;
Wredenhagen et al. 2023; Sirica et al. 2025; Kars et al. 2021).
Here we describe the phenotypes and genotypes of a new cohort
of 11 patients with TSPEAR - associated disorder, expand the re -
port with novel variants, demonstrate the primary association
of this gene with tooth agenesis, and refute the correlation with
hearing loss.
2   |   Material and Methods
We evaluated a cohort of 93 individuals from 80 families with
ectodermal manifestations and/or tooth agenesis who were
referred to the molecular genetics laboratory of Meyer Children's
Hospital IRCCS in Florence (Italy) from Italian clinical genetics
and/or dermatology divisions from 2019 to 2024, and selected
those with variants in TSPEAR .
2.1   |   Phenotypic Analysis
We performed a retrospective in- depth phenotypic analysis of all
individuals found to carry biallelic TSPEAR variants, by medical
chart review and analysis of standardized proforma provided by
the referring physicians. Clinical manifestations were recorded
using Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) ( http:// www. human
-  pheno type-  ontol ogy. org). Specific attention was paid to signs
and symptoms related to ectodermal dysplasia and hearing loss.
Facial features of available photographs were evaluated inde -
pendently by the referring clinical geneticists and by the senior
author and were annotated using The Elements of Morphology:
Standard Terminology nomenclature (Carey et  al.  2012). The
frequencies of clinical manifestations were calculated using the
number of individuals for whom evidence of the presence/ab -
sence of each feature was available.
2.2   |   Editorial Policies and Ethical Considerations
All the individuals involved in this study and/or their legal
guardians provided informed consent to the review of med -
ical records and publication of photographs. The study was
conducted in accordance with the principles stated in the
Declaration of Helsinki.
2.3   |   Molecular Analysis
Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using the
QIAamp Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and was used
to construct libraries (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA) for
singleton or trio exome sequencing (ES) analysis. Exonic se -
quences from DNA were enriched with KAPA HyperExome
probes (Roche Nimblegen, Basel, Switzerland) and sequenced
with NextSeq500/550 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) using
150 bp paired end reads.
An automated in-  house bioinformatic pipeline enabled read
alignment to the reference genome Grch37 (hg19) (Burrows–
Wheeler Aligner), mapping and analysis with the Integrative
Genome Viewer (IGV) software (McKenna et  al.  2010;
Thorvaldsdottir et  al.  2013), variant calling with GATK v.3.7
(McKenna et  al.  2010), and variant annotation with Annovar
(Wang et al.  2010) as previously described (Landini et al.  2020;
Rocca et al. 2022).
Off- target variants (> 20 bp splice acceptor and splice donor)
and variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) > 1% in the
gnomAD v.4.0.0 Database and in our in-  house exome control
cohort (4000 exomes) of unrelated subjects analyzed for non-
ectodermal disorders were filtered out. Variants were filtered
by applying an in silico panel of genes associated with ectoder -
mal dysplasia and tooth agenesis reported in Online Mendelian
Inheritance in Man, Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD),

Variants prioritization was performed according to the inheri -

prediction tools (REVEL, MetaRNN, CADD, Polyphen-  2, SIFT,

interpretation according to the American College of Medical
Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) was followed to classify the pri-

our in- house pipeline with a normalized read count approach to
determine the presence of copy number variations considering
only selected genes as previously described (Landini et al. 2020).
Only variants classified as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic
(LP), and variants of uncertain significance (VUSs) were con -
sidered. Variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.
Singleton TES was conducted in P1 (Family I), P2 (Family II), P4
(Family III), and P9 (Family V). Segregation analysis by Sanger
sequencing was conducted in siblings (P3 for Family II and P5
for Family III) and on the parents. Trio TES was performed in
Family VI and Family VII, while TES on three siblings (P6, P7,
and P8) and both parents was done for Family IV.
2.4   |   Protein Modeling of Missense Variants
Missense variants were analyzed with in silico modeling. The
structure of TSPEAR as predicted by AlphaFold was obtained
from the Uniprot database (Q8WU66) (Jumper et  al.  2021).
Besides the primary structure, the normal function of the pro -
tein depends on folding and stability. The potential impact of
missense variants on the protein structure and function was
predicted using in silico tools, based on machine-  learning al -
gorithms that exploit the three-  dimensional protein structure,
to quantify the effect of the missense variants on protein sta -
bility in terms of the Gibb free energy of unfolding (Δ G). The
difference in the corresponding unfolding free energy of the mu-
tated and wild-  type protein is described by the average folding
energy change (ΔΔG) measured in kcal/mol. Decreasing values
(ΔΔG < 0) indicate a destabilizing variant, while increasing val -
ues (ΔΔG > 0) define a stabilizing variant (Pancotti et al.  2022).
ΔΔG can be influenced by the chemical nature of amino acid
variants and the functional category of proteins involved
(Pandey and Alexov  2024). Five different predictors were used,
selected from Pancotti et al. ( 2022), according to their study of
prediction performance and the ability of the tools to satisfy the
antisymmetry properties.
3   |   Results
Of the 93 patients from 80 families referred for ectodermal dys -
plasia/tooth agenesis molecular testing, we obtained a diagno -
sis in 62 (data not shown), thus achieving a diagnostic yield of
67% in this highly selected population. Eleven individuals from
seven families (11/93 patients, 12%; 7/80 families, 9%) had bial -
lelic variants in TSPEAR [NM_144991.3] that were deemed to be
disease-  causing.
3.1   |   Clinical Manifestations
We evaluated a total of 11 patients (six males and five females).
Median age at testing was 16 years (range 5–41 years), with six
children and five adults. The demographic and clinical data of
the present cohort are shown in Table 1.
Abnormalities in teeth number and shape were consistent in
all the patients (Figure  1): all individuals had conical teeth and
presented with either oligodontia or hypodontia. Dental enamel
hypoplasia was noted in four (Figure 1 ).
Although all individuals were referred to testing mainly for
tooth agenesis, the initial assessment and a re-  evaluation of
their phenotypes after obtaining the molecular results showed
that abnormal sweating was observed in 4/11 (36.4%), and thin
or sparse hair in 4/11 (36.4%). Follow- up into adulthood showed
that thin hair was limited to childhood, with adult patients hav -
ing normal hair. Dystrophic nails were found in only one patient.
Six out of 11 individuals (55%) did not meet the criteria for ecto -
dermal dysplasia at the time of evaluation, as they did not exhibit
involvement of at least two of the ectodermal- derived structures
(sweat glands, skin, nails, hair, and teeth). Facial analysis did
not recognize distinctive facial features, but maxillary retrusion
was present in 7/11 (64%). At least four patients exhibited a high
anterior hairline, and one had a flat facial profile. All the pa -
tients had normal cognitive functioning, except for P9, who pre -
sented with borderline intellectual developmental disorder and
features of Poland syndrome. Since P9 exhibited a more complex
clinical picture, the investigations were extended to other genes
compatible with the phenotype to look for a possible second di -
agnosis, but no other pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants
were found. Previous testing for fragile X syndrome and chro -
mosomal microarray were normal.
Audiometric testing was performed in 4/11 patients with normal
results in all. None of the patients presented concerns for hear -
ing loss at the clinical evaluation.
3.2   |   Molecular Data
TES allowed the identification of 10 different variants in TSPEAR
[NM_144991.3], of which three are novel and four have not been
previously published in the literature but have been submitted
to a publicly available database (ClinVar, last assessed July 21,
2025). The variants detected in this cohort are summarized in
Table 2 and their schematic representation in the TSPEAR pro -
tein is shown in Figure 2 .
Most patients were compound heterozygotes, except for
P9 and P11, who were homozygotes. Their parents were
non- consanguineous.
All the variants but two were classified as pathogenic ( n = 5)
or hot VUSs ( n = 3), and their combination was deemed to be
disease-  causing in all the patients.

3.3   |   Protein Modeling of Missense Variants
The pathogenicity of missense variants can be difficult to es -
tablish. Three patients in the present cohort (P6–P8) were
compound heterozygotes for a VUS and a benign variant, both
previously published (Jackson et  al.  2023; Peled et  al.  2016).
Another missense variant was classified as a hot VUS (P10).
To provide further evidence of their pathogenicity, we applied
protein modeling. The results from in silico predictors and the
comparison of the 3D structure prediction between wild type
and mutated residues are shown in Figure  3. A destabilizing ef -
fect is confirmed by all the investigated tools for p.Phe626Ser,
similarly to p.Gly498Val, except for DDGun. Predictions for
p.Asp639Asn are conflicting, with PremPs and INPS3D sup -
porting a destabilizing effect. DDmut was therefore applied to
study the combined effect of p.Phe626Ser and p.Asp639Asn
and showed a ΔΔ G value of −1.7, which is compatible with a
synergistic destabilizing effect on the protein caused by the co-
presence of the two variants.
4   |   Discussion
Biallelic loss of function variants in TSPEAR  (truncating
and missense changes that destabilize the beta-  propeller of
the protein) have been associated with ectodermal dyspla -
sia, tooth agenesis and sensorineural hearing loss (Jackson
et al.  2023; Rabie et al.  2022). Our study aimed at better de -
lineating the allelic and clinical spectrum of TSPEAR - related
conditions.
By TES, we identified biallelic disease-  causing variants in 12%
of individuals referred for tooth agenesis/ectodermal dyspla -
sia. This result suggests that TSPEAR is an emerging and pre -
dominant gene involved in these phenotypes unlike previously
thought (Modafferi et al.  2025), and is in line with recent find -
ings from Jackson et al. (2023), who found a carrier rate of 1/140

Besides ectodermal dysplasia and tooth agenesis, an associa -

noted. A recent article reported on a patient with congenital

and to provide the results of hearing testing where available.
None of the individuals had signs of hearing loss and 4/4 had
normal audiometric exams. These pieces of evidence taken to -

ing loss.

NMD, based on the rule that only premature termination codons
(PTCs) upstream of the final exon-  exon junction by more than
50–55 nucleotides trigger efficient NMD (Dyle et al.  2020). The
frameshift deletion p.Phe413Tyrfs*144 produces a PTC located
50 nucleotides upstream from the last exon and is predicted to
undergo efficient NMD. Therefore, the identified nonsense and
frameshift variants are likely loss of function (pLOF), as well
as the start-  loss variant c.2T>C occurring in the translation
initiation codon. However, it is not known whether the loss of
Met1 disrupts all protein translation, or an abnormal protein is
produced using an alternate start codon. Since a potential alter -
native downstream start codon is predicted at residue 69, func -
tional studies will be useful to predict NMD for the start-  loss
variant. The in-  frame delins c.1493_1494delinsTG leads to the
substitution of a Glycine with a Valine at amino acid residue 498.
All the in silico tools we used, except for DDGun, returned ΔΔ G
values greater than +0.5 kcal/mol for this variant, supporting a
destabilizing effect. Also, the mutant Val498 likely determines a
hydrophobic interaction with His449 that alters the protein con-
formation. Although this variant is formally classified as a hot
VUS, we provided additional pieces of evidence that support its
pathogenicity.
We also applied in silico tools to further evaluate the two mis -
sense variants identified in P6, P7, and P8 of Family IV and pre -
viously described in other individuals (Jackson et al. 2023; Peled
et  al. 2016; Du et  al.  2018; Kars et  al.  2021): p.Phe626Ser and
p.Asp639Asn. All the prediction tools supported a destabilizing
effect on the protein for p.Phe626Ser, highlighting a loss of hy -
drophobic interactions with residues Tyr618, Typ620, Leu600,
Phe532, and Phe591. Predictions are less concordant for p.As -
p639Asn, with 2/5 predictors supporting a destabilizing effect.
PremPS modeling showed loss of van der Waals interactions
with Asp588, establishing van der Waals interactions with Ile330
instead of Gly329. We then studied the prediction of the two
variants combined, to mimic the compound heterozygous state
seen in our patients: of note, DDMut showed a negative ΔΔ G
value, supporting their destabilizing combined effect. Although
the results of the prediction tools would not change the ACMG
classification of these variants per se and functional studies are
needed to evaluate the variants' impact, taken together with
the segregation in three affected siblings and a highly specific
phenotype consistent with the gene, they strongly support vari -
ant pathogenicity. p.Asp639Asn is seen in 19 homozygotes in
GnomAD and is a founder variant that may have arisen around
the end of the last ice age (Jackson et  al.  2023). We therefore
hypothesize that it may be a hypomorphic variant, which has a
mild impact on protein function and causes the phenotype when
combined with another more deleterious variant. Overall, the
highly specific morphological patterns observed in individuals
with TSPEAR- related disorder provide valuable diagnostic clues
and warrant further functional studies to explore how these
variants disrupt the developmental pathways that determine
dental shape.
In conclusion, we demonstrated that individuals with biallelic
variants in TSPEAR show complete penetrance for dental prob -
lems (tooth agenesis and conical teeth), but not for other ecto -
dermal manifestations. We showed that the TSPEAR- related
disorder is more common than previously thought and is likely
underdiagnosed, and suggest that TSPEAR  should be included
in first-  line genetic panels for patients with tooth agenesis, re -
gardless of whether they meet the strict criteria for ectodermal
dysplasia. We provided evidence that questions the previously
suggested association of TSPEAR  with hearing loss. Finally,
we expanded the genotype and characterization of variants in
TSPEAR.
