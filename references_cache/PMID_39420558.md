# LSM7 variants involving key amino acids for LSM complex function cause a neurodevelopmental disorder with leukodystrophy and cerebellar atrophy.
**Authors:** Crespin M, Siquier-Pernet K, Marzin P, Bole-Feysot C, Malan V, Nitschké P, Hully M, Roux CJ, Lemoine M, Rio M, Boddaert N, Courtin T, Cantagrel V
**Journal:** HGG Adv (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xhgg.2024.100372](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xhgg.2024.100372)

## Content

Cerebellar atrophy and hypoplasia are usually identified on MRI performed on children presenting signs of cerebellar ataxias, developmental delay, and intellectual disability. These signs can be associated with hypo- or de-myelinating leukodystrophies. A recent study reported two cases: one child diagnosed with leukodystrophy and cerebellar atrophy, harboring a homozygous variant in LSM7, and another who died in utero, presumed to have another homozygous variant in LSM7, based on the parents' genotype. LSM7 encodes a subunit of the LSM complex, involved in pre-RNA maturation and mRNA degradation. Consequently, it has been suggested as a strong candidate disease gene. This hypothesis was supported by functional investigations of the variants. Here, we report a patient with neurodevelopmental defects, leukodystrophy, and cerebellar atrophy, harboring compound heterozygous missense variants in the LSM7 gene. One of these variants is the same as the one carried by the first case reported previously. The other one is at the same position as the variant potentially carried by the second case reported previously. Based on comparable neuroimaging, clinical features, and the involvement of the same amino acids previously demonstrated as key for LSM complex function, we confirm that LSM7 disruption causes a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by leukodystrophy and cerebellar atrophy.

Cerebellar hypoplasia or atrophy is a key neuroradiological finding in children with cerebellar ataxia. These patients often present an associated imbalance, poor coordination, developmental delay, and intellectual disability.1 More specifically, children with cerebellar atrophy exhibit deficiencies in attention, processing speed, visuospatial functions, and language.2 Identification of cerebellar atrophy is usually based on the enlargement of cerebellar interfolial spaces. It can either be isolated or associated with other neuroimaging signs such as hypomyelination, abnormal signals in the dentate nucleus, progressive white matter defects, cerebellar cortex T2 hyperintensities, and basal ganglia involvement.3 Combination of cerebellar atrophy with signal abnormalities of cerebral white matter is further suggestive of progressive defect, which can occur in leukodystrophies such as POLR3-related leukodystrophy (MIM: 607694). Mutations in multiple genes have already been identified as causative of cerebellar atrophy, but molecular diagnosis remains unsolved in many patients.4,5

Definition of leukodystrophies evolved over time. Initially, it strictly included oligodendrocytes and myelin-associated diseases.6 However, this term now encompasses all genetically determined diseases affecting the white matter in general, regardless of the cell type or the mechanism involved.6 Consequently, they are highly heterogeneous regarding clinical manifestations, symptoms onset, and prognosis. For instance, some cases can acquire walking abilities and have a life expectancy similar to the general population, whereas others will never walk, some may even die in utero. The natural evolution of leukodystrophies varies, with degenerative, static, episodic, and improving conditions.6

Currently, diagnoses are increasingly based on molecular testing, hence the necessity to identify new genes that could be involved in cerebellar atrophies5,7 and leukodystrophies with potential treatment implications.6

SM-like (LSM) proteins belong to SM family of proteins characterized by the presence of Sm domain or “Sm fold.” This family is highly conserved from bacteria to humans and plays pleiotropic roles in RNA metabolism.8,9 LSM proteins assemble in characteristic complexes with a central pore that interacts directly with short uracil-rich stretches of RNA.10 LSM7 (MIM: 607287) is involved in two different heptameric circular protein complexes that can be formed, LSM1-7 and LSM2-8.9,11 These complexes play distinct roles in two different cell compartments. Notably, LSM1-7 is an important cytoplasmic factor for mRNA decay, consisting of two important processes: 3′ to 5′ and 5′ to 3′ degradation. It acts jointly with the protein PATL1 (MIM: 614660) as an activator of the decapping process needed for 5′ to 3′ degradation.11,12 This process appears to depend on the deadenylation step12 as these factors bind preferentially to oligoadenylate mRNAs compared with polyadenylated mRNA. LSM 2–8 act as a chaperone protein complex in the nucleus for U6 small nuclear RNA (U6 snRNA), a spliceosome component and contributes to its nuclear retention.8,9,11 U6 snRNA is the most conserved U snRNA and participates in the spliceosome by recognizing a 5′ sequence of introns, GUAUGU.13,14 Therefore, it has a crucial role at the active site of the spliceosome. Overall, LSM7 participates in pre-RNA splicing regulation and mRNA degradation. Surprisingly, among the eight main LSM genes, none of them has been clearly associated with a Mendelian disease.

A recently published study by Derksen et al.15 reported two unrelated cases. One of them (individual 1) exhibited spastic quadriparesis, developmental delay, progressive sensorineural hearing loss, hypospadias, and feeding difficulties. MRI showed a cerebellar hypoplasia associated with white matter abnormalities indicative of a leukodystrophy. He underwent exome sequencing which revealed a homozygous variant in LSM7 (GenBank: NM_016199.2; GRCh37/hg19) at position c.121 G>A (p.Asp41Asn) (chr19: 2324172C>T). The other case (individual 2) died in utero at 32 weeks of gestation and was found to have thick nuchal translucency and encephalocele. The parents had a history of recurrent fetal deaths. Three of the fetuses were karyotyped and no chromosomal abnormalities were found. Duo exome sequencing was then conducted only for the consanguineous parents as the DNA was not available for any of the fetuses, including individual 2. This analysis revealed that both parents are heterozygous for the same variant in LSM7: c.206G>C (p.Arg69Pro) (chr19: 2321785C>G) but are not carriers of a variant associated with known recessive diseases. Therefore, this LSM7 variant was hypothesized to be responsible for the multiple fetal deaths. Notably, this couple had three healthy children who were not analyzed by exome sequencing. Then, authors investigated the pathogenicity of the homozygous p.Asp41Asn and the presumed homozygous p.Arg69Pro variants. To do so, they used patient cells, pull-down experiments, and mutagenesis in zebrafish. Based on results consistent with the pathogenicity of both variants, the authors considered LSM7 as a strong candidate gene for leukodystrophies with cerebellar atrophy. Since the genetic status of the previously reported fetus (individual 2) is unknown, there is only one reported case with a likely pathogenic variant in LSM7 (c.121 G>A; p.Asp41Asn, individual 1) and the identification of new cases with LSM7 variants is necessary to confirm and define the involvement of this gene in human disease.

Our case was referred to a clinical geneticist at Hôpital Necker-Enfants Malades for developmental delay, spastic quadriparesis, and cerebellar ataxia. Both of his parents have Algerian origins. Family history revealed one early spontaneous miscarriage, without any genetic testing or pathological examination. For the proband, pregnancy was spontaneous. Notable events included maternal thrombocytopenia and a placental abruption at 17 weeks of amenorrhea causing metrorrhagia, followed by normal echographic controls. He was born prematurely at 29 weeks of gestation with height, weight, and head circumference between the 50th and 75th percentile. Neonatal period was marked by a hospitalization in intensive care for managing respiratory distress and simple icterus.

At 6 years old, his psychomotor development is significantly delayed. He is able to walk a few steps with a walking frame assistance, speaks only five words, points with his finger to ask for things, and responds to simple requests. There have been no signs of any regression in his development to date. Neurological features found during his clinical examination included axial hypotonia, postural instability, spasticity of the four limbs, extrapyramidal dyskinesia, and cerebellar features such as nystagmus, oculomotor apraxia, tremors of the limbs, and ataxia. The first signs of cerebellar dysfunction were observed at 4 years old. He presented anxiety and oppositional behavior. No signs of sleep disturbances or epilepsy were reported, and an EEG conducted at 1 month of age showed no abnormalities. Additional clinical findings include a right eye strabismus. No other organs are affected—notably, a normal cardiac ultrasound was observed at the age of 7 years. He has no facial dysmorphism. His growth in terms of weight and height, as well as his head circumference, are within the normal range for a 6-year-old. Brain MRI conducted at the age of 6 years showed bilateral, asymmetrical, and multifocal T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in the deep white matter involving the semioval centers and periventricular regions, particularly in the front of the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles. It also displayed cerebellar atrophy (Figure 1) in the context of a normal MRI at 2 months (Figure S1).

To establish the disease etiology, we performed an array CGH analysis16 that showed no abnormality, and then a trio genome analysis (GS) at the Imagine Institute Genomic Core Facility (Paris, France; see supplemental information for details). Written informed consent for publication of clinical details and biological data was obtained from the parents of the case described in this study. GS analysis did not identify variants in known disease genes for cerebellar atrophy and leukodystrophy such as POLR3A (MIM: 614258), POLR3B (MIM: 614366), UFM1 (MIM: 610553), TUBB4A (MIM: 602662), or AARS2 (MIM: 612035). However, this analysis identified a variant reported in ClinVar as pathogenic (i.e., RCV001543592.1).15 This LSM7 variant is at position c.121G>A (p.Asp41Asn; Chr19:g.2324172C>T), heterozygous and inherited from the father. On the other allele, the proband is heterozygous for an LSM7 c.205C>T variant (p.Arg69Trp; chr19: 2321786G>A), transmitted by the mother, and that is predicted to be deleterious by SIFT and Mutation Taster. Sanger sequencing consistently detected the variants in the parents, the affected case, but not in the unaffected sibling (Figures 2B and 2C). The variant p.Arg69Trp has not been characterized functionally so we took advantage of the structure of the human LSM2-8 complex included in the pre-B spliceosome complex17 to investigate its consequence at the structural level. The amino acid Arg69 of LSM7 establishes polar contacts with LSM5, at the level of Glu35 (Figure 2D) as detected in yeast, in both Lsm1-7 and Lsm2-8 complexes.15 These polar contacts are disrupted by the replacement of Arg69 with a nonpolar amino acid (i.e., Trp) (Figure 2D) with potential consequences on the assembly of both LSM complexes. These results suggest that the compound heterozygous variants detected in our patient are pathogenic and underlie his neurological condition.

Significantly, the case reported here presents a similar clinical presentation as individual 1 reported by Derksen et al. Indeed, they both have spastic quadriparesis forcing them to use a wheelchair to move around, associated with a global developmental delay. Individual 1 presented with hypospadias and progressive sensorineural hearing loss which was not found in our case. Furthermore, they exhibited similar white substance abnormalities indicative of a leukodystrophy. In addition, they also presented cerebellar defects. MRI scans of our patient revealed cerebellar atrophy, while individual 1’s MRI scans conducted at 17 and 35 months were annotated as cerebellar hypoplasia. However, in the context of the developing cerebellum, atrophy and hypoplasia are often not distinguishable.18 Importantly, both patients’ MRIs show a reduced size of the cerebellar vermis compared with an age-matched control and an enlargement of cerebellar interfolial spaces.

Surprisingly, LSM7 variant p.Arg69Trp affects the amino acid residue presumed to be involved in disease etiology of individual 2. Although the substitution with tryptophan could have less pronounced effect on the structure compared with proline, the impact is similar at the amino acid biochemical level (i.e., polar vs. non-polar) with comparable consequences on the loss of polar contacts.15

On top of the redundant identification of these variants in comparable clinical presentations, Derksen et al. collected multiple functional arguments in favor of pathogenicity. They detected reduced levels of LSM7 mRNA and protein in fibroblasts extracts from individual 1. Subsequently, pull-down assays revealed reduced association of the two mutated LSM7 proteins with the other LSM proteins of both LSM1-7 and LSM2-8 complexes. In line with this observation, most of the known interactors of LSM complexes were not associated with LSM7 mutants. These results suggested a reduced activity of LSM complexes. Finally, a LSM7-Crispant zebrafish model lacking functional LSM7 protein was developed. This model showed an impaired nervous system development, a reduced motor response to light stimuli and an increased apoptosis of Olig2+ oligodendrocytes. All these defects were rescued by injection of the wt LSM7 transcript in zebrafish larvae, but not with the mutant versions except for the p.Arg69Pro variant that allowed at least some partial recovery of the eye diameter phenotype.15 Overall, these experiments provide solid in vitro and in vivo evidence of the pathogenicity of the p.Arg69Pro and p.Asp41Asn variants.

Based on this functional evidence, redundant genetic and comparable clinical observations, we concluded that our patient provides definitive evidence of the pathogenicity of LSM7 variants. Consequently, genetic counseling could be provided for this family who requested a medical termination of pregnancy for a fetus carrying both LSM7 variants (p.Asp41Asn and p.Arg69Trp) described here, and approved by the local prenatal committee.

It could be speculated that the p.Arg69Pro is more detrimental at a homozygous state than the p.Asp41Asn, since it has presumably led to fetal death. Nevertheless, the individual carrying both the p.Arg69Trp and p.Asp41Asn variants appears to be similarly affected to the individual carrying the p.Asp41Asn variant at a homozygous state.

Interestingly, among the known genetic causes of leukodystrophies or cerebellar atrophy, multiple defects in RNA processing have been identified causing neurodegeneration and white matter disorders,19 which is consistent with the involvement of LSM7 in a central nervous system pathology. For example, variants in EXOSC3 (MIM: 606489), a gene implicated in mRNA decay,20 are responsible for hereditary spastic paraplegia and pontocerebellar hypoplasia (MIM: 614678).21,22 Another important gene for mRNA decay is DCPS (MIM: 610534), which can be responsible for Al-Raqad syndrome (MIM: 616459), an intellectual disability syndrome with leukoencephalopathy and cerebellar atrophy.23,24 Furthermore, defects in the splicing process can also lead to neurodevelopmental disease. Indeed, bi-allelic variants in SNAPC4 (MIM: 602777) induce decreased snRNAs levels associated to neurodevelopment regression, cerebellar atrophy, and progressive spastic paraparesis (MIM: 620515).25 Moreover, variants in a U2 snRNA gene in mice cause global neurodegeneration, more severe in the cerebellum.26 This suggests an important role for snRNAs in cerebellar development and cerebellar cell survival. Further investigation in patient-derived cells would be of high interest to better understand which aspects of RNA processing are mostly impaired and which genes are affected. RNA sequencing analyses after applying differentiation protocols able to produce neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes27 could be used to identify the molecular and cellular bases that underlie neurodegeneration and the neurodevelopmental defects in this condition.

Overall, LSM7 is the first gene in the LSM protein family to be functionally validated for a human Mendelian disease. Only LSM1 (MIM: 607281) is a candidate gene for neurodevelopmental disorder with dysmorphic and malformation features. Indeed, four patients are reported with variants in LSM1, displaying a global developmental delay, dysmorphic features, genitourinary malformations including hypospadias, variable cardiovascular malformations, vertebral abnormalities, and white matter hyperintensity in T2 FLAIR on cerebral MRI.28,29 However, these variants have yet to be functionally validated. Moreover, the other LSM genes have never been reported in human pathology. Identification of more individuals with bi-allelic variants in LSM genes should lead to a better understanding of the physiopathology of impaired mRNA splicing and decay in leukodystrophies and neurodevelopmental diseases.
