EDITORIAL
FDA Approval of the Congenital OTOF Gene Therapy Otarmeni
Reshapes the Future of Hearing Care
Ellen Reisinger1,2 · Christopher R. Cederroth3,4,5

© The Author(s) under exclusive licence to Association for Research in Otolaryngology 2026
Following Regeneron’s recent publication on the efficacy
and safety of the OTOF  gene therapy for DFNB9 patients
in the New England Journal of Medicine [ 1], the US Food
and Drug Administration announced on April 23rd the
approval of Otarmeni, the first gene therapy for a genetic
form of hearing loss,  under the Commissioner’s National
Priority Voucher pilot program (CNPV), 2.5 years after
the first patient in the CHORD study was treated. This
is a historical moment in the auditory field and a turning
point for translational otology, bringing gene therapy for
congenital deafness into clinical reality.
While the FDA’s decision is solely based on Regen‑
eron’s results, additional programs worldwide are pro‑
viding convergent evidence on safety and hearing levels
after treatment [2 ]. A recent report published in Nature  
from Yilai Shu’s team in China shows persistent bene ‑
fits 2.5 years after gene therapy, with many participants
achieving pure‑tone thresholds comparable to or better
than typical outcomes with cochlear implantation, and
experiencing low‑grade adverse effects [3 ]. The suc‑
cess of the dual‑vector delivery, two suitable surgical
delivery routes, and the hearing recovery observed will
pave the way for future therapies on hearing loss. While
much remains to be understood on vector selection, pro‑
moter design, dose, age of treatment, duration of treat‑
ment effects, and potential limits of auditory perception
and cortical plasticity, the increasing number of children
treated with the approved therapy as well as in ongoing
OTOF trials by other pharmacompanies will allow us to
address these questions. The translational validity of the
animal models in this context is in strong support of such
investigations.
Importantly, the joint indication of a gene‑specific and
time‑sensitive therapy makes genetic diagnosis of infants
with bilateral hearing loss a necessity. Gene panels and
auditory electrophysiology (by means of auditory brain‑
stem response [ABR] screening) offer a solution for diag‑
nosing DFNB9 within a reasonable time for the newly
approved gene therapy, but the incremental yet highly sig‑
nificant discovery of new deafness genes justifies a shift
in the mentality of otolaryngology clinics toward broader
genomic approaches (e.g., exome or genome sequencing)
and ABR screens in parallel to cochlear implant programs.
However, because not all centers have access to such diag‑
nostics, there will be a need for international strategies to
avoid a form of inequity. Current guidelines will be chal‑
lenged and will require revisiting the decision sequence
optimal for clinics and families, i.e., children diagnosed
with profound hearing loss but intact otoacoustic emissions
should first receive a genetic diagnosis before receiving a
cochlear implant.
From the patient perspective, children treated with OTOF
gene therapy and their families will require tailored sup ‑
port, taking into account their delayed language acquisi‑
tion and speech understanding due to the late maturation of
the auditory pathway. One‑fourth of the CHORD children
reached normal hearing sensitivity on pure ‑tone audiom ‑
etry; however, normal pure‑tone sensitivity does not neces‑
sarily equate to fully normal hearing in complex listening
environments. In addition, the remaining children may have

is possibly a consequence of not all inner hair cells being
transduced with the two gene therapy vectors. Studies in
otoferlin knock‑out animals reveal a lack of 40–50% of syn‑

impairing speech comprehension [7 , 8]. These considera‑
tions emphasize the need to systematically assess speech‑in‑
noise performance, listening effort and fatigue, and to opti‑
mize hearing‑aid fitting accordingly in this patient group.
Regeneron announced the therapy will be available for
free to patients in the US. For Europe, this benefit remains
uncertain. Even though Otarmeni is not yet approved in
Europe, the high prevalence of clinically significant OTOF  
mutations in Europe, even if somewhat lower than in the
US or parts of Asia, strongly argues for an EU‑level access
strategy (Table  1). This is an opportunity for clinics to
redesign the rehabilitation pipeline and set a multidiscipli‑
nary follow ‑up with language acquisition measures, spa ‑
tial hearing, and speech‑in‑noise performance (still very
variable across countries) together with long‑term efficacy
monitoring. These measures appear particularly important
to consider in light of the likely persistence of subclini‑
cal synaptopathy and central disorganization potentially
persisting after treatment.
There are existing rare‑disease frameworks (Orphanet,
EURORDIS, historic EU working groups, and hereditary ‑
hearing centers of expertise) that would certainly form a
strong backbone to a new European Rare Disorders Net‑
work for Hereditary Hearing Loss. In the meantime, a more
explicit and coordinated network focused on genetic deaf‑
ness is needed. NIH’s HearGene Connect, presented at the
ARO 49th MidWinter Meeting in Puerto Rico on February
6th 2026, is a major milestone in this direction, providing
a model for an integrated registry and patient‑support net‑
work. A European counterpart, linked through shared core
outcomes, could greatly accelerate learning. Central from
a patient perspective will be the need to establish core out‑
come measures that assess speech intelligibility, listening
efforts, fatigue, social integration, and mental health. Opti‑
mally, these would be highly reliable and validated across
continents and languages. Finally, from an ethics point of
view, it will be critical to progress in the foreground of the
Deaf community, being attentive to their perspectives and
expectations, and support families that choose or decline
gene therapy.
