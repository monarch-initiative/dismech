# Preclinical pharmacology and safety studies to support an AAV9 NGLY1 gene therapy clinical trial for the treatment of NGLY1 deficiency.
**Authors:** Zhu L, Cook JW, Newton A, Dwight SS, Beahm B, Wilsey M, Mueller WF, Schweighardt B
**Journal:** Mol Ther Methods Clin Dev (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.omtm.2025.101524](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.omtm.2025.101524)

## Content

GS-100 is an AAV9 gene replacement therapy for the treatment of N-glycanase 1 (NGLY1) deficiency, an ultra-rare, recessive disorder characterized by developmental delay, intellectual disability, hyperkinetic movement disorder, elevated liver enzymes, (hypo)alacrima, and peripheral neuropathy. In pharmacology studies using Ngly1 -/- rats, intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) administration of GS-100 showed dose-dependent biodistribution, increased hNGLY1 mRNA expression, reduced disease biomarker N-acetylglucosamine-asparagine (GNA), improved motor function, and prevented phenotype progression. Reductions in GNA within central nervous system (CNS) tissues and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) correlated significantly with improvements, suggesting that CSF GNA may serve as a clinical biomarker of therapeutic activity. Safety studies conducted in wild-type rats and non-human primates (NHPs) indicated no adverse clinical signs, mortality, body weight changes, or negative neurobehavioral/nerve conduction effects at or near the clinical starting dose of 4e14 vector genome (vg)/individual. In addition, GS-100 biodistribution was widespread in CNS and peripheral tissues. These nonclinical data supported the Food and Drug Administration approval of an Investigational New Drug application for a first-in-human phase 1/2/3 dose-escalation clinical trial. The trial's co-primary endpoints include changes in CSF GNA biomarker levels and improvements or stabilization of motor function to assess the safety and efficacy of GS-100 administered i.c.v. in patients with NGLY1 deficiency.

N-glycanase 1 (NGLY1) deficiency (OMIM# 615273) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by the loss of NGLY1 enzymatic activity with no approved treatment. NGLY1 deficiency is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the NGLY1 gene encoding the NGLY1 protein, a ubiquitous cytosolic N-glycanase that specifically cleaves off the N-glycan between the GlcNAc residue and the asparagine residue on proteins targeted for degradation and is implicated in the endoplasmic-reticulum-associated protein degradation pathway that recognizes and eliminates unfolded or incorrectly processed glycoproteins. In the United States, the estimated incidence of NGLY1 deficiency is approximately 1 in 4 million live births. There are currently 42 patients diagnosed with NGLY1 deficiency in the US and ∼150 patients who have been diagnosed with the disease worldwide1 (unpublished data). Clinically, the disease is characterized by global developmental delay and/or intellectual disability, a hyperkinetic movement disorder,2 transient elevation of liver transaminases, (hypo)alacrima (insufficient or absent tear production), and a chronic diffuse sensorimotor neuropathy that affects the nerves in a length-dependent manner.3,4,5 Most patients are unable to walk and do not develop verbal communication skills, requiring significant caregiver support for daily management.

Grace Science, LLC has developed an NGLY1 gene replacement therapy, referred to as GS-100, to deliver a functional copy of the NGLY1 gene to patients with NGLY1 deficiency.6 A biomarker for NGLY1 deficiency, N-acetylglucosamine-asparagine (GNA) (GlcNAc-Asn),7,8 and a rat model (Ngly1−/− rat) of the disease9 have been critical to the development of GS-100. GNA has been shown to be elevated in patient blood spots,7 urine, and plasma,8 as well as in the plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of the Ngly1−/− rat disease model. The Ngly1−/− rat disease model, created using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing on the Sprague Dawley background, is homozygous for a deletion of exons 11 and 12 and the 3′ polyA region of the Ngly1 gene.9
Ngly1−/− rats exhibit phenotypes that closely mirror those observed in patients,6,9 including impaired spatial learning, neurological impairment, gait and motor function deficits, peripheral neuron/axon loss, and elevated disease biomarker GNA levels.6,8,10,11 Based on published clinical data,1,2 the average age of symptom onset in patients with NGLY1 deficiency is approximately 4 months. Behavioral phenotypes in Ngly1−/− rats have been observed as early as 5 weeks of age,9 although earlier onset is difficult to assess due to the challenges of behavioral testing and mortality in pre-weaning rodents (3 weeks of age and younger). Post-natal day (P) 28 (P28), approximately equivalent to a 6-year-old human patient, was selected as the dosing age in nonclinical pharmacology and toxicology studies of GS-100 to support the potential for both safety and therapeutic benefit in young pediatric patients who are already symptomatic.

Intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) delivery of GS-100 to the NGLY1 deficiency rat model (Ngly1−/− rat) significantly reduced levels of GNA in CSF6 and was shown to serve as a critical biomarker for the pharmacodynamic response to GS-100 in Ngly1−/− rats.6

In total, three non-GLP (Good Laboratory Practice) pharmacology studies (Table S1) were conducted in Ngly1−/− rats to assess the route of administration, safety, and efficacy of GS-100 over a wide range of dose levels. A route of administration study (Study rROA), reported previously,6 established i.c.v. delivery as the preferred delivery method for GS-100 with respect to pharmacological effect. Following this, and reported here, a dose range-finding study (Study rDOS) and a pharmacology study using clinical-grade material (Study rPHRMTX) were conducted to collectively assess vector biodistribution, NGLY1 mRNA and protein expression, CSF GNA levels, functional outcomes, and toxicology profiles following i.c.v. administration of GS-100 over a wide range of dose levels under non-GLP conditions. With respect to safety, two GLP toxicology studies were conducted in wild-type animals, one in rats (Study rGLPTX) and one in cynomolgus macaques (Study nhpGLPTX), to assess the biodistribution and toxicology of GS-100 in rats as well as a larger animal species.

The preclinical studies outlined in this paper established a safety profile for GS-100 and demonstrated that GS-100 is able to reduce CSF biomarker GNA levels and improve locomotor function to near wild-type levels in a rat model of the disease. Furthermore, these preclinical studies allowed the identification of a starting clinical dose for administration of GS-100 to patients with NGLY1 deficiency via the i.c.v. route of administration. The safety and efficacy of GS-100 in patients with NGLY1 deficiency are currently being assessed in an ongoing phase 1/2/3 (NCT06199531) clinical trial.

To support a first-in-human clinical study in patients with NGLY1 deficiency, multiple preclinical studies were conducted to evaluate GS-100 pharmacodynamics, biodistribution, and toxicology (Table S1). In chronological order, these studies include a previously reported non-GLP study in Ngly1−/− rats to determine the optimal route of administration (Study rROA6), a non-GLP dose range-finding study in Ngly1−/− rats (Study rDOS), a non-GLP pharmacology study using clinical-grade drug product (Study rPHRMTX, Table S1), and two GLP toxicology and biodistribution studies, one in wild-type rats (Study rGLPTX) and one in cynomolgus macaques (Study nhpGLPTX). In the non-GLP dose range-finding (Study rDOS) and pharmacology (Study rPHRMTX) studies using Ngly1−/− rats, GS-100 toxicology, vector biodistribution, hNGLY1 mRNA and protein expression, GNA levels, and functional assessments were evaluated across doses ranging from 9.5e8 to 1.1e13 vector genome (vg)/rat. In the two GLP toxicology studies, vector biodistribution, hNGLY1 mRNA expression, toxicity, and safety pharmacology endpoints, including CNS functional observational battery (FOB) testing and nerve conduction velocity (NCV), were assessed in wild-type rats across dose ranges from 9.4e11 to 1.7e13 vg/rat and cynomolgus macaques across dose ranges from 2.0e12 to 1.7e14 vg/non-human primates (NHP) (equivalent to 5.8e10 to 4.8e12 vg/rat).

To investigate vector biodistribution, juvenile wild-type and Ngly1−/− rats (∼PND 28) received i.c.v. administration of either vehicle or GS-100 at three different dose levels in Study rPHRMTX (Table S1) based on the effective dose range identified in Study rDOS. Six weeks after dosing, frozen hemi-brain and thoracic spinal cord samples were collected for genomic DNA isolation and subsequent qPCR analysis to quantify the biodistribution of GS-100 vg DNA at termination in the CNS tissues of Ngly1−/− rats. Administration of GS-100 via i.c.v. to Ngly1−/− rats resulted in dose-dependent vector DNA biodistribution in brain and spinal cord (thoracic) tissues (Figures 1A and 1B). At the high dose (1.1e13 vg/rat), vector biodistribution levels (2−dCt = 3.10 ± 0.53) in the brain were significantly higher than those measured at the low dose (6.2e11 vg/rat; 2−dCt = 0.91 ± 0.70, p = 0.02, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons). Vector biodistribution levels in the brain (2−dCt = 2.00 ± 0.23) at the mid dose (6.7e12 vg/rat) trended higher than those at the low dose (Figure 1A). In the spinal cord, vector biodistribution levels at the high dose (2−dCt = 0.78 ± 0.15) were significantly higher compared to those at the low dose (2−dCt = 0.04 ± 0.02; p = 0.0006) (Figure 1B). GS-100-mediated hNGLY1 mRNA expression was also evaluated in the brain and thoracic spinal cord tissues of Ngly1−/− rats treated at the three different dose levels of GS-100 in Study rPHRMTX at 6 weeks post i.c.v. dosing. In the brain, significant differences were observed in hNGLY1 mRNA levels between the low-dose group (6.2e11 vg/rat; 2−dCt = 22.3 ± 5.9) and both the mid-dose (6.7e12 vg/rat; 2−dCt = 108.4 ± 9.8, p = 0.002, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons) and high-dose (1.1e13 vg/rat; 2−dCt = 176.6 ± 18, p < 0.0001, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons) groups (Figure 1C). As was observed for brain vector DNA biodistribution, there was no significant difference in hNGLY1 mRNA expression levels between the mid- and high-dose groups (p = 0.08, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons). In the thoracic spinal cord, a significant difference (p = 0.0003, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons) in hNGLY1 mRNA expression was detected between the low- (2−dCt = 1.37 ± 0.36) and high-dose (2−dCt = 25.1 ± 4.2) groups but not between the low- and mid-dose (2−dCt = 8.57 ± 0.89) groups (p = 0.2, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons) or mid- and high-dose groups (p = 0.44, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons, Figure 1D).

These results are consistent with previous studies (rROA,6 Study rDOS data not shown) and indicate that i.c.v. delivery of GS-100 results in dose-dependent vector biodistribution and hNGLY1 mRNA expression in the brain and spinal cord of Ngly1−/− rats.

With respect to GNA levels in CNS tissues 6 weeks following i.c.v. administration of GS-100, GNA levels in Ngly1−/− rats in the low-dose group (6.2e11 vg/rat) did not show a trend toward reduction in median GNA levels in whole brain tissue (6,660 ± 224 ng/mg, 6.8% median reduction, p > 0.99, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons) or spinal cord tissue (3,306 ± 123 ng/mg, 5.3% median reduction, p > 0.99, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons) when compared to those of Ngly1−/− rats in the vehicle-treated group (7,020 ± 365 ng/mg in the brain and 3,295 ± 154 ng/mg in the spinal cord, respectively). Significant reductions in median GNA levels compared to vehicle-treated controls were observed in both the mid- (6.7e12 vg/rat) and high-dose (1.1e13 vg/rat) groups. In brain tissue, median GNA decreased by 42.5% (4,241 ± 177 ng/mg for mid dose vs. 7,020 ± 365 ng/mg for vehicle-treated Ngly1−/− rats, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons) and 45.9% (3,865 ± 188 ng/mg for high dose vs. 7,020 ± 365 ng/mg for vehicle-treated Ngly1−/− rats, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons), while in spinal cord tissue, it decreased by 21.7% (2,493 ± 106 ng/mg for mid dose vs. 3,295 ± 154 ng/mg for vehicle-treated Ngly1−/− rats, p = 0.0002, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons) and 36.9% (2,195 ± 110 ng/mg high dose vs. 3,295 ± 154 ng/mg for vehicle-treated Ngly1−/− rats, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons) (Figures 3A and 3B). No significant difference in median GNA reduction was detected between the mid- and high-dose groups in brain or spinal cord tissue, suggesting a possible plateau in effect.

With respect to CSF GNA levels 6 weeks following i.c.v. administration of GS-100, Ngly1−/− rats in the low-dose group (6.2e11 vg/rat, 227.6 ± 8.5 ng/mL) showed significant reduction in median CSF GNA levels compared to vehicle-treated Ngly1−/− controls (290.6 ± 28 ng/mL, p = 0.004, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons) with a median reduction of 21%. Significant reductions in CSF GNA levels compared to vehicle-treated Ngly1−/− controls were also observed for the mid-dose (165.0 ± 8.0 ng/mL, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons) and high-dose (141.8 ± 6.5 ng/mL, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons) groups, with median reductions of 46.2% and 53.0%, respectively, compared to vehicle-treated Ngly1−/− animals (Figure 3C). No significant difference in CSF GNA reduction levels was detected between the mid- and high-dose groups.

Importantly, CSF GNA levels significantly correlated with GNA levels in brain (r2 = 0.5042, p < 0.0001, Pearson’s correlation, Figure 3D) and spinal cord tissue (Figure S1). Furthermore, GNA levels in brain and spinal cord tissues showed a significant correlation with one another (Figure S2). These correlations suggest that CSF GNA levels may serve as a pharmacodynamic marker for GS-100 activity in the CNS in the clinic.

i.c.v. administration of GS-100 in NHPs did not result in any observable changes in hematology, coagulation, or urinalysis parameters at any of the 3 dose levels tested. Clinical chemistry analyses revealed no adverse alterations attributable to GS-100 at any dose. A moderate and transient increase in alanine transaminase levels was observed in the high-dose (1.7e14 vg/NHP) group, an effect not observed in the mid- (2.1e13 vg/NHP) or low-dose (2.0e12 vg/NHP) groups.

Furthermore, administration of GS-100 at any dose level in the NHP GLP toxicology study did not lead to mortality, adverse clinical observations, body weight changes, or neurobehavioral abnormalities. In the nerve conduction study, GS-100 treatment did not result in any GS-100-related sensory (sural nerve, peroneal sensory nerve, saphenous sensory nerve, radial sensory nerve, and median sensory nerve) or motor (tibial motor nerve) neuropathies in any animals for any of the 3 dose groups (Table S3). Necropsy and pathological analyses revealed no GS-100-related macroscopic findings.

Microscopic evaluations revealed findings at all dose levels, including lesions of minimal severity in the DRGs, dorsal root nerve roots, and multiple hindlimb nerves (Table S3). The clinical significance of these findings was assessed in the context of in-life evaluations such as clinical signs and nerve conduction velocity measurements. FOB and NCV assessments indicated that GS-100 administration did not result in changes in neurobehavior or sensory nerve conduction at any dose level, suggesting no adverse clinical manifestations of the microscopic findings.

The severity of microscopic findings was generally higher in the high-dose (1.7e14 vg/NHP) group compared to the mid (2.1e13 vg/NHP) and low-dose (2.0e12 vg/NHP) groups. A single animal in the mid-dose group exhibited additional adverse findings that were attributed either to mechanical trauma or an undetermined relationship to the test article. Other histopathologic findings at this dose were detected in the same animal that had inadvertent mechanical trauma during the injection procedure, as exhibited by marked focal parenchyma loss near the injection site. Minimal nerve fiber degeneration in the brainstem was also detected in this same animal and may have been related to the parenchyma loss due to degeneration of axonal projections coming from the injured area. In addition, mild cerebellar necrosis was detected in this animal that could not be definitively related to the test article, as it was not observed in any other treated animals, including all 4 animals at the high dose, and is not a recognized sequela of AAV-based test articles.14,15 No additional adverse findings were observed in the low-dose group. Non-adverse, dose-dependent findings present at all dose levels included gliosis and mononuclear cell infiltration in the liver (Table S3).

Based on preclinical studies in both rats and NHPs described in this paper and in a previous paper,6 a starting clinical dose of 4e14 vg per individual for GS-100, equivalent to 2.1e13 vg/NHP and approximately 6.0e11 vg/rat when scaled by brain weight, was selected. Given that NGLY1 deficiency is an ultra-rare disease with a shortened lifespan and no current treatments and that patients will have only one chance for GS-100 administration, this clinical starting dose was chosen as it was deemed safe while still offering the prospect of therapeutic benefit.

Notably, in the Ngly1−/− rats, GS-100 dose levels close to the clinical starting dose level identified here were associated with trends in improved motor function (Figures 5A and 6A) and dose-dependent reductions in the GNA biomarker (Figure 3C). Moreover, reduced neuronal degeneration and necrosis in the DRGs were observed across all dose levels in Study rPHRMTX (Figure 7) and in Study rDOS (data not shown), suggesting that GS-100 may provide additional benefits by targeting DRG pathology, an NGLY1 deficiency disease phenotype.6,16,17 Improvements in motor function and GNA reduction were minimal at doses below the rat equivalent of 4e14 vg/individual. Therefore, 4e14 vg/individual was selected as the clinical starting dose, as it is expected to be near the minimum dose level at which patients are likely to benefit from i.c.v. administration of GS-100 based on nonclinical pharmacology data in Ngly1−/− rats.

Extensive in-life assessments and histopathology confirmed the safety of the selected 4e14 vg/individual equivalent dose in nonclinical studies. Specifically, animals dosed at levels equivalent to 4e14/individual in the rat and NHP GLP toxicology studies (5.9e11 vg/rat and 2.1e13 vg/NHP, respectively) exhibited no mortalities or adverse effects on body weight, clinical observations, or clinical pathology parameters. The minimal histopathologic findings observed at the equivalent dose in both studies were not accompanied by any functional deficits as measured by FOB assessments or nerve conduction studies (Table S3), supporting a favorable safety profile at the proposed clinical starting dose. These results show that the starting clinical dose is safe while still providing prospect of benefit in this ultra-rare disease population.

While higher doses (10-fold above the selected clinical starting dose equivalent, corresponding to 6.0e12 vg/rat or the NHP high dose of 1.7e14 vg/NHP) yielded greater improvements in motor function and GNA reduction in the Ngly1−/− rats, the equivalent doses administered to NHPs were associated with moderate to marked adverse histopathologic findings. Planned dose-escalation cohorts are designed for the clinical study (NCT06199531) to identify the highest safe and effective dose in the clinical trial. The first escalation cohort will increase the dose by 2.5-fold, to 1e15 vg/individual, followed by a second dose escalation to 3e15 vg/individual in the subsequent cohort.

The toxicology studies in rats and NHPs shed light on the importance of matching dosing procedures to clinical procedure and translatability from a small rodent model to a larger primate model. While the Ngly1−/− rat served as a useful model for efficacy, rats had limitations as a safety model in these studies due to the difference in i.c.v. procedure between rats and NHPs (bolus injection versus infusion in larger animals and patients) and the small ventricle size. In the rat Study rGLPTX, adverse findings such as hippocampal neuronal loss, nerve tissue loss, and ventricular dilatation were observed at doses higher than 1.0e13 vg/rat, predominantly along the needle track, likely due to the constraints of administering a bolus injection into the small rat brain. In contrast, no such adverse findings were present in Study nhpGLPTX at equivalent doses where GS-100 was administered via slow infusion through a cannula. These observations suggest that the adverse effects in rats were attributable to procedural factors rather than the drug itself and are therefore not expected to occur in human patients when using the catheter-based, slow-infusion method similar to the i.c.v. procedure used in NHPs.

Despite the robust design and comprehensive functional endpoints assessed in these nonclinical studies, there are limitations that need to be considered. First, the maximum nonclinical study duration was 12 weeks post dose, which may not fully capture the long-term durability of transgene expression, sustained functional benefit, or any potential late-emerging toxicities. Extended monitoring in the ongoing clinical trial will be critical to evaluate persistence of therapeutic effect and long-term safety. In addition, while no adverse impact of anti-drug immune responses was detected in the nonclinical studies, immune-related safety events or negative impact on treatment effect could occur in the clinical setting. Such risks can be mitigated in clinical trials with close immunological monitoring, as well as prophylactic and reactive immune suppression.

The nonclinical findings reported here informed key aspects of the clinical trial design for GS-100. These include initial dose selection based on safety in rats and NHPs and the minimum efficacious dose observed in Ngly1−/− rats, as well as functional endpoints based on improvement in locomotor function close to wild-type levels in Ngly1−/− rats. Importantly, CSF GNA is being leveraged as a real-time pharmacodynamic biomarker in the clinical trial; the strong correlative results between CSF GNA and functional outcomes in the rat studies support its utility for monitoring CNS transduction and informing dose-response relationships in patients.

In conclusion, the preclinical studies in a rat model of NGLY1 deficiency and wild-type rats and NHPs collectively demonstrate that i.c.v. administration of GS-100 offers both a promising therapeutic effect and an acceptable safety profile. These findings not only validate the selection of a 4e14 vg/individual clinical starting dose but also highlight the potential of GS-100 as a safe and effective gene replacement therapy for this debilitating condition.

To evaluate any potential toxicity of GS-100 on the DRGs and peripheral nerves, nerve conduction studies were performed on cynomolgus macaques before dosing and then at 4 weeks and 3 months post dosing. Sensory nerve conduction was assessed in the lower limbs, focusing on nerves with roots in the lumbosacral plexus, and in the upper limbs, targeting nerves with roots in the brachial plexus. To account for variability in DRG pathology, at least two nerves at each level were examined. Motor nerve conduction velocity was measured from distal muscles innervated by the sciatic or tibial nerve following orthodromic stimulation at two locations along the nerve’s pathway. Additionally, the F-wave was tested in at least one motor pathway of the lower limb. At each time point, the onset latency, amplitude, and duration of each compound muscle action potential or sensory nerve action potential were recorded for each nerve. The distance from the recording site to the stimulation cathode was measured, and conduction velocity was calculated using the onset latency of the response and this distance. This combination of metrics enabled assessments of demyelination and axonopathy. Statistical comparisons were conducted at each individual time point and across different time points.

The accelerating rotarod test is designed to measure balance, coordination, physical condition, and motor learning in rats.12,13 Rats underwent rotarod testing using the Fusion v.6 Rotating Rod System. Conditioning sessions were performed on the morning of or a day prior to the actual testing and involved the animal being placed on the rotarod set to a constant speed of −4 rpm (the minus sign indicates that the rod is moving in a direction that causes the rat to ambulate away from the observer) for 4 min. During the actual test, animals were placed on a rotating rod programmed to accelerate from 4 to 40 rpm at a rate of 0.15 revolutions per second over the course of 4 min. For animals staying on the rotarod for the entire 4-min period, a time of 240 s was recorded as the fall time. The elapsed time from the start of the trial to the animal’s fall was recorded digitally by the instrument. Each animal was evaluated in three trials with at least 15 min between trials. The average of all three trials is reported for each animal.
