# Neuronal hyperactivity becomes mTORC1 independent due to transcriptional changes in tuberous sclerosis complex disease models.
**Authors:** Afshar-Saber W, Ruiz JF, Gisser I, Yang Z, Mehendale L, Teaney NA, Hobson R, Glass MR, Pham TT, Bainbridge E, Sahin M, Winden KD
**Journal:** Cell Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.celrep.2025.116664](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2025.116664)

## Content

Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is caused by variants in either TSC1 or TSC2, which cooperate to inhibit the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1). TSC is associated with neurological disorders that are attributed to disinhibition of mTORC1, but the mechanisms connecting dysregulation of mTORC1 to molecular and physiological changes in neurons remain unclear. In this study, we aim to understand transcriptional changes in TSC and identify downregulation of the immediate-early gene EGR1 in TSC2-deficient excitatory neurons. Furthermore, we find that activity-dependent transcription is impaired in TSC due to abnormalities in maturation-dependent DNA demethylation. Finally, we determine that mTORC1 inhibition started late in neuronal maturation of human neurons is only partially effective in reversing gene expression changes and ineffective in reducing spontaneous neuronal hyperactivity in TSC. These data demonstrate a critical window in early brain development where mTORC1 dysregulation leads to transcriptional changes that contribute to persistent neuronal abnormalities.

Many genes associated with the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway are related to various neurodevelopmental disorders.1 Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), caused by variants in either TSC1 or TSC2, is one of the prototypical disorders within this group. TSC is characterized by various benign tumors and skin changes, and most patients have epilepsy; autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and other neuropsychiatric manifestations are also common.2 The proteins TSC1 and TSC2 bind to each other and inhibit mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1), one of two main complexes that define the specificity and activity of the kinase mTOR.3 Loss of the TSC1/2 complex leads to the inability of cells to regulate mTORC1, and most of the manifestations of TSC are attributed to hyperactivation of mTORC1.4 Precision medicine trials have demonstrated that mTORC1 inhibitors such as sirolimus (rapamycin) and everolimus are highly effective for some manifestations of TSC.5,6

However, mTORC1 inhibitors have been found to be less effective for neurological symptoms associated with TSC compared to benign tumors. Although everolimus was approved for refractory epilepsy in patients with TSC, the treatment group continued to experience near-daily seizures.7 In addition, mTORC1 inhibition does not have any effect on neurocognitive symptoms in patients with TSC.8,9 This is seen throughout disorders associated with mTORC1 hyperactivation, such as phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) tumor hamartoma syndrome, where everolimus has a minimal effect on behavioral and cognitive symptoms.10 Epilepsy has also been shown to correlate with negative developmental sequelae,11 but early treatment of seizures does not alter cognitive outcomes.12 These data suggest that TSC is associated with perturbation of the early development of neural circuits, which becomes resistant to later treatment with mTORC1 inhibitors. This is particularly discouraging because the neurological symptoms are the highest contributors to morbidity in patients with TSC.13 Identifying the pathological mechanisms responsible for these changes is critical for developing more effective therapies targeting neurological symptoms in TSC.

Many targets of mTORC1 are involved in growth and proliferation, and the molecular pathways that are dysregulated due to loss of TSC1/2 have been well characterized in proliferating cells, where they typically contribute to tumor formation.14 However, the molecular pathogenesis of TSC1/2 deficiency in post-mitotic neurons is less clear. Several studies have demonstrated that loss of TSC in neurons leads to increased mTORC1 activity, cytomegaly, and increased neurite outgrowth.15 However, features associated with neuronal activity and synaptic connectivity have shown conflicting results.16 For example, studies have found increased or decreased dendritic spine density depending on the model used.17,18 In addition, differences in spontaneous activity appear to be cell-type dependent.19–21 These data suggest that there might be cell-type-specific effects of mTORC1 hyperactivation throughout the brain. In addition, the best characterized targets of mTORC1 are involved in facilitating translation, and therefore, several studies have examined changes in ribosomal binding and post-transcriptional regulation in TSC.18,22–24 However, studies have also demonstrated substantial transcriptional dysregulation in neurons from models of TSC.22,25,26 Despite these observations, there is a limited understanding of the mechanisms by which mTORC1 alters transcription, especially in neurons. We hypothesized that these cell-type-specific transcriptional changes would be strong candidates for causing early neuronal and circuit dysfunction in TSC.

In this study, we aimed to determine whether certain neuronal cell types might be particularly sensitive to mTORC1 hyperactivation and understand the mechanisms of transcriptional dysregulation within those populations. We used single-nucleus sequencing on an animal model of TSC and observed that layer 5 pyramidal neurons showed the most gene expression changes. Among these changes, we found a significant enrichment for targets of the transcription factor EGR1, which itself was downregulated. We then found that activity-dependent transcription of EGR1 was impaired in TSC2−/− human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived neurons. Epigenetic changes involve DNA alterations that affect gene regulation without altering the underlying code, and we specifically examined DNA methylation in human neurons and identified a lack of demethylation of the EGR1 promoter during neuronal differentiation. Remarkably, we observed an overall reduction in demethylation of distal enhancers during differentiation of TSC2−/− neurons. We also demonstrated that expression changes of EGR1 targets and genes associated with impaired demethylation become rapamycin resistant during later neuronal maturation. Finally, mTORC1 inhibition started later in neuronal maturation does not reduce neuronal hyperactivity observed in TSC2−/− neurons. These data demonstrate that failure of activity-dependent demethylation during neuronal differentiation in TSC2−/− neurons leads to gene expression changes in neurons that become resistant to mTORC1 inhibition, which contributes to spontaneous hyperactivity in TSC.

We first used an animal model of TSC that displays spontaneous seizures and behavioral abnormalities.27 The mutant mice have manipulations of both alleles of Tsc2, leading to a significant reduction in TSC2 protein in neurons but not a complete absence. There is a germline deletion in Tsc2 that causes a complete loss of function of one allele (Tsc2del).28 The second allele has a conditional in-frame deletion in Tsc2 (Tsc2fl) driven by CRE recombinase under a Synapsin-1 promoter that causes a substantial decrease in protein from that transcript but retains its functionality.29 The combination of these two alleles leads to >80% reduction in TSC2 protein in neurons in the mutant mice.27 The control mice have two copies of the unrecombined conditional allele, leading to wild-type levels of functional TSC2 protein. In this model, we have demonstrated that spontaneous seizures begin after postnatal day 50 (P50).30 Therefore, we decided to examine gene expression in this model at P30 to avoid reactive changes due to seizures and focus on the changes that were driving epileptogenesis. We dissected the frontal cortex of these animals, isolated nuclei, and performed single-nucleus sequencing. After quality control, we obtained single-cell transcriptomes from 13,585 Tsc2del/fl SynCre+ mutant and 7,519 Tsc2fl/fl SynCre− control cells. We grouped cells based on their gene expression and identified 10 clusters (Figure 1A). We then annotated these clusters based on known marker genes and observed the expected cell types, although the samples were enriched for cortical neurons (Figure 1B). We then investigated whether the proportions of cell types differed between mutant and control animals, and we observed that there were reductions in cells annotated as oligodendrocytes (Figures 1C and 1D), which is consistent with prior data showing hypomyelination in TSC.31 Interestingly, we also observed that there was a tendency toward an enrichment in neurons in the mutant animals, especially cluster 9, which corresponded to layer 5 pyramidal neurons. However, immunohistochemistry on a separate set of animals showed similar numbers of BCL11B+ layer 5 pyramidal neurons, suggesting that neuronal cell types are unchanged between mutant and control animals (Figure S1). These data demonstrate that, besides known changes in glia, the neuronal cell type complement in Tsc2del/fl SynCre+ animals is intact.

We then investigated cell-type-specific gene expression changes within mutant animals and observed that cluster 9 had the greatest number of differentially expressed genes across all clusters (Figure 1E). To further verify that cluster 9 represents layer 5 pyramidal neurons, we examined known transcriptional markers of various cell types32 and observed high expression of three layer 5 markers (Dkk3, Nefh, and Pcp4) within cluster 9 (Figure 1F). We next examined the differentially expressed genes within cluster 9 and found that they were enriched for proteins localized to synaptic vesicles (p = 1.0e-22; modified Fisher’s exact test33) and the postsynapse (p = 3.4e-16; modified Fisher’s exact test), consistent with alterations in synaptic transmission.

To explore the mechanisms causing these changes in gene expression, we hypothesized that transcriptional regulation would be the critical mechanism because of the changes in nuclear RNA we observed. Therefore, we examined the promoter regions of the differentially expressed genes and identified enriched motifs corresponding to several transcription factors. We evaluated the top five predicted transcription factors based on their change in expression between Tsc2fl/fl SynCre− and Tsc2del/fl SynCre+ animals within cluster 9 (Figure S2A). Of these proteins, we found that EGR1 showed the highest baseline expression in Tsc2fl/fl SynCre− neurons and was reduced in Tsc2del/fl SynCre+ neurons (Figure 2A). We then compared the differentially expressed genes within cluster 9 to a chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) dataset that identified putative EGR1 target genes in vitro.34 Consistent with our prior data, we observed significant enrichment of EGR1 transcriptional targets among the differentially expressed genes from cluster 9 (Figure S2B). These data demonstrate that TSC2-deficient layer 5 pyramidal neurons show expression changes in synapse-related genes, which are potential targets of the transcription factor EGR1.

We then examined EGR1 expression in control and mutant animals. We microdissected lower-layer areas of the cortex from control and mutant animals and performed qPCR for Egr1. Accordingly, we observed a significant reduction in Egr1 expression in mutant samples compared to control (Figure 2B). The RNA sequencing data predict changes in EGR1 in layer 5 pyramidal neurons, so we performed immunohistochemistry for EGR1 with the established cortical layer 5 neuron marker BCL11B. We observed decreased EGR1 intensity within the BCL11B+ cells of the cortex, consistent with a reduction in protein expression of EGR1 in the mutant animals (Figures 2C and 2D). These data confirm that EGR1 is reduced in TSC2-deficient cortical neurons in vivo and support its involvement in the dysregulation of gene expression in mutant animals.

To determine whether EGR1 dysregulation is also observed in human neurons and to interrogate the mechanisms of EGR1 dysfunction in TSC, we used neurons differentiated from hiPSCs from two different patients with TSC due to variants in TSC2 (TSC2+/−). We then used genome engineering to either correct the patient variant (TSC2+/+) or introduce another variant into the second allele (TSC2−/−), generating two isogenic series of hiPSCs.26,35 We generated hiPSC-derived neurons using induced expression of Neurogenin-2 after a brief patterning step.35 We took this approach because these neurons have been shown to express the lower-layer marker TBR1,36 suggesting that we might observe similar changes to the deep-layer neurons in the mouse model. qPCR of EGR1 on day 30 of differentiation showed a significant reduction in EGR1 and decreased expression of two target genes, DLG4 and RELN (Figure S2C). We then measured EGR1 protein levels and found that they were significantly decreased in TSC2−/− neurons compared to TSC2+/+ neurons (Figure 2E). There was also a slight reduction in TSC2+/− neurons, suggesting added post-transcriptional regulation of EGR1 (Figure 2E). Therefore, EGR1 dysregulation in TSC2-deficient neurons is present across species and models.

EGR1 is a well-characterized activity-dependent gene and is frequently used in several systems as a marker of neuronal activity.37–39 Given that we observed decreased expression of EGR1 in TSC2-deficient neurons at both the mRNA and protein levels, we decided to measure the activity of the EGR1 promoter and determine whether its activity dependence is altered in TSC. We cloned 2 kb upstream of the human EGR1 gene, inclusive of the promoter and enhancer, based on histone marks, into a plasmid upstream of luciferase. We then transduced hiPSC-derived neurons with the EGR1-luciferase vector and a vector encoding Renilla luciferase under a constitutive promoter. On day 25 of differentiation, we evaluated luciferase expression and observed a significant reduction in luciferase activity in the TSC2−/− neurons, demonstrating reduced EGR1 promoter activity at baseline (Figure 3A). To determine whether the activity dependence of the promoter was also affected, we examined luciferase induction due to calcium influx in rat primary cortical neurons. Primary rat neurons were transduced with either a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) directed against Tsc2 or a scrambled control as well as the EGR1-luciferase vector. On day 14 in vitro, we added potassium chloride to drive calcium influx and continuously monitored luciferase activity. We observed that the Tsc2 shRNA neurons showed less induction of the EGR1 promoter due to calcium influx compared to the control (Figure S3). We then confirmed these findings in hiPSC-derived neurons and observed that the TSC2+/+ and TSC2+/− neurons demonstrated substantial induction of the EGR1 promoter, while the increase was impaired in TSC2−/− neurons (Figure 3B). These data demonstrate that TSC2 deficiency leads to reduced EGR1 promoter activity at baseline and that the activity dependence of this promoter is diminished in neurons.

Several transcription factors are known to mediate the activity-dependent induction of EGR1, among which CREB is the most widely studied.40,41 Calcium influx leads to phosphorylation of CREB, and phosphorylated CREB translocates to the nucleus to activate transcription. Therefore, we analyzed CREB phosphorylation at serine 133 in hiPSC-derived neurons on day 25 of differentiation in response to calcium influx mediated by potassium chloride. In addition, we used forskolin to increase cyclic AMP and activate protein kinase A (PKA), another important kinase that phosphorylates CREB.42 In TSC2+/+ and TSC2+/− neurons, we observed significant increases in S133 phosphorylation of CREB after treatment with both potassium chloride and forskolin. However, we did not observe any significant increase in S133 phosphorylation of CREB in TSC2−/− neurons after either treatment (Figure S4).

EGR1 regulates many diverse transcriptional targets, but it has been specifically demonstrated to function as a repressor for several proteasome subunits in neurons.43,44 We first examined gene expression changes within cluster 9 using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)45 and observed a significant upregulation of the proteasome gene set in layer 5 pyramidal neurons from mutant animals (Figure S5A). Additionally, we validated these findings in hiPSC-derived neurons and observed a similar upregulation of the proteasomal gene set in TSC2−/− hiPSC-derived neurons compared to TSC2+/+ neurons (Figure S5B).25 We then examined the expression of proteasome subunits and related proteins (TAP1, PSMB8, and PSME1) using western blotting and observed that they were significantly upregulated in TSC2−/− neurons (Figure 3C). Given that EGR1 should be directly modulating their expression at the transcriptional level, we analyzed a larger set of proteasome subunits and related genes using qPCR. We observed that all of these genes were upregulated in TSC2−/− neurons compared to TSC2+/+ neurons and that this change was significant for six of nine of these analyzed genes (Figure 3D). To determine whether the activity of the proteasome was increased, we performed proteolysis assays using Suc-Leu-Leu-Val-Tyr-AMC (Suc-LLVY-AMC) in lysates from hiPSC-derived neurons. We observed a significant increase in AMC fluorescence in lysates from TSC2−/− neurons, consistent with increased proteasome activity in these cells (Figure 3E). These data demonstrate that levels of proteasome subunits and their activity are increased in TSC2−/− neurons, consistent with de-repression due to decreased expression of EGR1.

Given that we had observed decreased activation of the EGR1 promoter, we searched for other mechanisms that could account for the changes in EGR1 promoter activity. In non-neuronal cells, the EGR1 promoter becomes demethylated during cellular maturation, allowing the promoter to become activity dependent.46 We hypothesized that impaired demethylation of the EGR1 promoter during differentiation and maturation of TSC2−/− neurons could lead to the abnormal transcriptional regulation of EGR1 we observed. We chose to focus on the primary enzymes that initiate DNA demethylation (TET1, TET2, and TET3) through oxidation to 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, which is followed by reversion to the unmodified state through the base excision repair pathway.47 We examined the expression of these genes in previously published RNA sequencing data from TSC2−/− neurons,25 and we found that TET1 and TET2 were expressed and downregulated in these neurons. qPCR of TSC2−/− neurons confirmed the significant reduction of TET1 and TET2 mRNA (Figure 4A). We also examined the expression of the DNA methylases (DNMT1, DNMT3a, and DNMT3b), and we did not observe any changes in the prior transcriptional data.25 However, DNMT1 has been shown to be post-transcriptionally regulated by mTOR in non-neuronal cells,48 and therefore we examined the expression of DNMT1 using western blotting. We observed a significant upregulation of DNMT1 at the protein level in TSC2−/− neurons (Figure 4B). These data demonstrate that TSC2−/− neurons show an imbalance in expression of epigenetic regulators that suggest a bias toward impaired demethylation in TSC2−/− neurons.

To determine the functional significance of the changes in DNA methylation regulators, we performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing of hiPSCs and hiPSC-derived neurons on day 25 of differentiation. We first examined the EGR1 region of the genome, and we observed significant demethylation of regions of the EGR1 proximal enhancer and promoter in TSC2+/+ neurons compared to TSC2+/+ stem cells (blue line, Figure 5A). These data are consistent with other studies demonstrating demethylation of the promoter that accompanies differentiation and maturation.46 Remarkably, we did not observe any significant changes in methylation of the EGR1 promoter in TSC2−/− neurons compared to TSC2−/− stem cells (red line, Figure 5A), demonstrating impaired epigenetic regulation of this region with neuronal differentiation. We then identified hypomethylated regions throughout the genome of TSC2+/+ neurons, and we estimated the level of methylation of these regions within stem cells and neurons across genotypes. We calculated differential methylation within each of these regions between neurons and stem cells of the same genotype. Comparing volcano plots of these changes between TSC2+/+ and TSC2−/− cells, we observed that TSC2−/− neurons showed a deficit of demethylated regions compared to TSC2+/+ neurons (Figures 5B and 5C). This was confirmed using a quantile-quantile plot of log10
p values of demethylated regions in TSC2+/+ and TSC2−/− neurons, as there was a deviation from the identity consistent with a bias toward more significant p values in the TSC2+/+ neurons (Figure 5D). These changes were not observed in TSC2+/− neurons, demonstrating that they are specifically due to loss of the second allele of TSC2 (Figures S6A and S6B). We then examined the sequence composition of genomic regions that showed demethylation in TSC2+/+ neurons compared to stem cells but did not show demethylation in TSC2−/− neurons. These intervals showed a highly significant enrichment in a G-rich motif (E = 8.9e-111; Figure 5E). Interestingly, EGR1 also binds to a G-rich motif, and these regions, which show lessened demethylation in TSC2−/− neurons, are also significantly enriched for predicted EGR1 binding sites (E = 3.9e-64). Finally, we examined whether these regions might be enriched for genomic features involved in gene expression regulation and found that they significantly overlapped with distal enhancers but not proximal enhancers or promoters (Figure 5F). Furthermore, genes near distal enhancers that showed altered methylation were enriched in several categories associated with nervous system development (Figure S6C). These data demonstrate that TSC2−/− cortical neurons display altered epigenetic maturation, specifically impairments in demethylation of important transcriptional regulatory features. In addition, EGR1 is implicated in this process not only through changes at its own promoter but also at genome-wide predicted binding sites.

Based on the data demonstrating that distal enhancers show impaired demethylation in TSC2−/− neurons during neuronal maturation, we hypothesized that loss of TSC2 and subsequent mTORC1 disinhibition may impact neuronal function differently depending on the affected stage of development. Therefore, we differentiated cortical neurons from all three genotypes of stem cells and treated these cells with either an mTORC1 inhibitor (rapamycin, 20 nM) or vehicle (DMSO) starting either early, during neuronal maturation on day 10, or late, after circuit development on day 30. We then collected samples on day 40 of differentiation. We first measured phosphorylated S6 as a measure of mTORC1 activity, and, as expected, we observed a significant increase in TSC2−/− neurons and suppression by rapamycin regardless of when treatment was started (Figure S7A). We then performed RNA sequencing on these samples. We observed that vehicle-treated TSC2−/− neurons were distinct from all other conditions (Figure 6A), consistent with prior data demonstrating that loss of both alleles of TSC2 causes a substantial alteration in gene expression.22,25,26 We also found that TSC2−/− neurons treated with rapamycin early in development clustered with rapamycin-treated TSC2+/+ and TSC2+/− neurons, showing that early treatment with rapamycin can ameliorate most gene expression changes in TSC2−/− neurons (Figure 6A). Remarkably, TSC2−/− neurons that were treated with rapamycin later in development clustered separately from the TSC2+/+ and TSC2+/− neurons and showed persistent similarities to the TSC2−/− vehicle-treated neurons (Figure 6B). These data demonstrate a critical window during neuronal development during which mTORC1 hyperactivation leads to gene expression alterations that become resistant to rapamycin treatment over time.

To better understand these changes , we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis across all samples and examined the unbiased expression patterns for genes that were altered in TSC2−/− neurons and only rescued with early but not late rapamycin treatment. The co-expression module ME9 demonstrated the best correspondence with this pattern and was comprised of genes that were downregulated in TSC2−/− neurons (Figures 6C and S7B). Consistent with the incomplete effect of rapamycin on TSC2−/− neurons, these genes were largely normalized by early rapamycin treatment but showed persistent downregulation with late rapamycin treatment (Figure 6C). We then investigated the molecular processes associated with this group of genes using functional annotation and found that they were significantly enriched in processes associated with synaptic function and DNA binding (Figure 6D). Based on these data, we hypothesized that persistently methylated distal enhancers in TSC2−/− neurons might play a role in these gene expression alterations. We identified all genes whose transcription start site was near these distal enhancer regions (5–50 kB), and we used their connectivity to ME9 as a measure of their persistent expression dysregulation despite late rapamycin treatment. We observed that genes near more distal enhancers were more likely to have higher connectivity in ME9 (Figure S7C). Therefore, we used GSEA and determined that genes near five or more distal enhancers that showed impaired demethylation in TSC2−/− neurons were significantly more likely to be associated with ME9 (p = 0.009; Figure 6E). Given that EGR1 was associated with distal enhancers that showed impaired demethylation in TSC2−/− neurons, we examined the connectivity of EGR1 targets in ME9 and found that it was significantly higher than all other protein-coding genes (p = 2.5e-14; t test; Figure 6F). These data demonstrate that failure of epigenetic maturation through demethylation of distal enhancers leads to transcriptional dysregulation in TSC2−/− neurons, which become resistant to rapamycin treatment after development.

We then examined the impact of methylation on gene expression dysregulation in TSC2−/− neurons. We differentiated cortical neurons from TSC2−/− hiPSCs, and we treated them with a DNA methylation inhibitor, 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine, starting on day 10 of differentiation. This compound leads to non-specific genome hypomethylation, and we hypothesized that this would reactivate the EGR1 promoter in TSC2−/− neurons. We performed qPCR on day 25 of differentiation, and we observed significant upregulation of EGR1 compared to the vehicle-treated condition. We then measured expression of several putative transcriptional targets of EGR1 and observed significant upregulation in five of seven tested targets (GRM3, NXPH1, CHRM3, NRXN2, and EPHB1) (Figure S7D). These data demonstrate that inhibition of DNA methylation is sufficient to increase expression of EGR1 and its targets in TSC2−/− neurons, indicating that epigenetic changes contribute to neuronal abnormalities in TSC.

We hypothesized that the gene expression changes resistant to mTORC1 inhibition after neuronal maturation might be relevant for the neurological disorders in TSC because those symptoms are also less sensitive to mTORC1 inhibitors. Therefore, we examined the effect of mTORC1 inhibition on spontaneous neuronal activity at different points during neuronal development. We differentiated one isogenic series of hiPSCs as above, and on day 6 of differentiation, we replated neurons onto multielectrode array plates and recorded spontaneous activity. As in the gene expression experiments, we started treatment with rapamycin either early during maturation, on day 10, or after circuit development, on day 30. Different concentrations of rapamycin were used (0.2, 2, and 20 nM), and DMSO was used as a vehicle control. We observed that rapamycin treatment at any dose started early during maturation prevented spontaneous hyperactivity in the TSC2−/− neurons (Figure 7A) but had no effect on TSC2+/+ or TSC2+/− neurons (Figures S8A and S8B), consistent with our prior findings.25 In contrast, rapamycin had no effect on spontaneous activity when started later, on day 30 (Figure 7B). At the last time point of recording (day 38), treatment with rapamycin started on day 10 led to no significant difference in firing rate between TSC2+/+ neurons and TSC2−/− neurons (Figure 7C). Remarkably, TSC2−/− neurons treated with rapamycin on day 30 showed significantly elevated spontaneous activity compared to TSC2+/+ neurons, and there was no difference between neurons treated with DMSO or rapamycin (Figure 7D). We then examined other indicators of neuronal hyperactivity, and we observed a similar pattern with burst frequency. Early rapamycin treatment completely rescued the increased burst frequency of TSC2−/− neurons (Figure 7E), while late rapamycin treatment did not affect burst frequency in TSC2−/− neurons (Figure 7F). Finally, we examined neuronal synchrony and found that TSC2−/− neurons developed correlated activity sooner than TSC2+/+ or TSC2+/− neurons and that this was rescued by early rapamycin treatment (Figure 7G) but not affected by late rapamycin treatment (Figure S8C). These data demonstrate that gene expression dysregulation in TSC2−/− neurons that persists despite late treatment with rapamycin is sufficient to maintain neuronal hyperactivity and, thus, implicates those genes and molecular pathways in aberrant physiological activity in TSC.

In this study, we evaluated the molecular mechanisms underlying TSC pathogenesis, focusing on transcriptional dysregulation in TSC2-deficient neurons. We found that layer 5 pyramidal neurons displayed the most gene expression changes in an animal model of TSC, which was associated with decreased expression of the transcription factor EGR1. EGR1 was also reduced in hiPSC-derived cortical neurons with biallelic variants in TSC2, modeling somatic pathogenic variants that occur in TSC.49,50 Consistent with the well-characterized role of EGR1 in repressing proteasomal gene expression,43,44 we demonstrated that TSC2−/− neurons show increased expression of proteasome subunits and increased proteasome activity. EGR1 expression is typically regulated by neuronal activity, but we observed that this was blunted in TSC2−/− neurons, which was associated with persistent methylation of its promoter. TSC2−/− neurons also show reduced demethylation of distal enhancers genome-wide during neuronal differentiation, which are significantly enriched for EGR1 binding sites. Finally, we demonstrated that transcriptional dysregulation in TSC2−/− neurons at a subset of synaptic genes becomes rapamycin resistant after differentiation. Taken together, we delineated a pathway by which mTORC1 hyperactivation early in neuronal development leads to failure of epigenetic maturation, resulting in impairment of activity-dependent processes and persistent neuronal abnormalities.

There have been a limited number of transcription factors implicated in neuronal dysfunction in TSC,51–53 but no activity-dependent transcription factors have been identified previously. EGR1 is a transcription factor that has been studied as a key regulator of neuronal plasticity and neurotransmission in an activity-dependent manner.54 In its role as an immediate-early gene, it has been shown to influence the regulation of gene expression in response to growth factors or neuronal activity.55 EGR1 is primarily associated with persistence of long-term potentiation rather than its induction,56,57 and EGR1 deletion causes impairments in learning and memory.58,59 Conversely, EGR1 overexpression has also been shown to facilitate plasticity and enhance the capacity to form stable spatial memories.60 Among the best-characterized synaptic targets of EGR1 is ARC,60 a cytoskeletal protein that has bidirectional roles in synaptic signaling.61 EGR1 can also regulate several synaptic proteins, such as PSD95, Drebrin, and Synapsin-1, as well as several GABA receptors.62–65 Interestingly, hyperexcitability has been observed with deletion of EGR1 in neurons,62 which suggests that its role is relatively complex, not only stabilizing potentiation of synapses but also limiting activity and preventing runaway excitation. Several of these phenotypes have been described in TSC1/2-deficient neurons, supporting a pathogenic role for decreased EGR1 expression in TSC. For instance, studies in animal models have demonstrated that synaptic plasticity is either diminished or completely occluded.66,67 In addition, studies across multiple model systems have demonstrated that TSC1/2-deficient cortical neurons are hyperactive.21,25,35,68 Therefore, these data support EGR1 dysfunction leading to neuronal abnormalities that have been observed in TSC.

Given the role of EGR1 as an immediate-early gene, EGR1 abnormalities in TSC directly implicate activity-dependent processes in the disorder. Signaling derangements in TSC have been demonstrated to impinge on activity-responsive molecular pathways,69 and we observed alterations in CREB phosphorylation. Several signaling pathways converge on CREB regulation. For example, PDK1 shows reduced activity in TSC and facilitates activation of PKA,70,71 suggesting a potential mechanism for impaired activation of CREB due to increased mTORC1 activity. In addition, our data demonstrate that activity-dependent transcription is also impaired. Other genetic causes of neurological symptoms similar to TSC have also demonstrated alterations in activity-dependent processes.72,73 For example, CACNA1C variants lead to Timothy syndrome, which is associated with epilepsy, ASD, and intellectual disability, and human neurons with variants in CACNA1C have been shown to display gene expression abnormalities consistent with activity-dependent alterations.74 Deletion of Tsc1 in mouse hippocampal cultures led to a blunted transcriptional response to picrotoxin,21 and Tsc2+/− hippocampal slices do not show changes in ribosomal binding due to metabotropic glutamate receptor stimulation.24 Although these studies observed basal increases in these processes, these data are consistent with impaired activity-dependent responses in TSC, further supporting abnormal regulation of EGR1 in the disorder.

Dysregulation of EGR1 in TSC leads to abnormalities in multiple processes, including the ubiquitin-proteasome system. EGR1 has been shown to directly repress several proteasome genes at the transcriptional level,43,44 consistent with our data showing increased expression of proteasome subunits and activity. The proteasome is a multiprotein complex that takes proteins tagged for degradation with ubiquitin and degrades these peptides.75 There is substantial evidence of dysregulation of the proteasome being involved in neurodevelopmental disorders, including several pathogenic variants in proteasome subunits.76–78 In addition, functional dysregulation of the proteasome has been demonstrated across multiple models, although the mechanisms connecting proteasome alterations to neural circuit changes are less clear. Interestingly, degradation of the RNA binding protein fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein (FMRP) via the proteasome has been shown to be increased in TSC2-deficient human neurons, leading to dysregulation of its target transcripts in neurons.35 In addition, increased proteasome activity has been shown to likely play a role in audiogenic seizures in the fragile X syndrome animal model.79 These data demonstrate that EGR1 can alter neuronal connectivity and activity through several mechanisms and that increased proteasome activity could contribute to the pathogenesis of TSC.

Acquiring the ability to undergo activity-dependent transcription is a key feature of neuronal maturation,80 and we observed that failure of this process in TSC2−/− neurons was associated with epigenetic abnormalities. Understanding of epigenetic changes in TSC is primarily limited to neuroprogenitors and non-neuronal cells.81,82 Epigenetics is known to play an important role in neuronal differentiation,83 and it has been suggested to be involved in ASD and other brain disorders.84–86 For example, Rett syndrome is caused by variants in MECP2, which binds to methylated DNA and suppresses transcription, and MECP2 expression is responsive to neuronal activity, linking activity dependence to epigenetic changes in neurons.87,88 Neurons have been shown to undergo substantial changes in DNA methylation throughout development, suggesting that these processes are critical for neuronal maturation.89,90 TET1 does not have intrinsic DNA binding capabilities and relies on chaperone proteins for its association with DNA.91 EGR1 and TET1 have been shown to bind to one another, and this complex has been suggested to mediate activity-dependent methylation changes in neurons.92 These data support our findings that regions that do not undergo demethylation in TSC2−/− neurons are enriched for predicted EGR1 binding sites. Modulation of enhancer activity plays an important role in circuit development,93 and therefore impaired EGR1-TET1 mediated demethylation of distal enhancers in TSC2−/− neurons could play an important role in the pathogenesis of neurological symptoms in TSC.

Together, these data demonstrate that loss of TSC1/2 leads to widespread gene expression abnormalities in neurons by preventing the normal developmental activation of EGR1 distal enhancers due to a failure of neuronal maturation-dependent DNA demethylation. These gene expression changes cannot be reversed by mTORC1 inhibition after maturation, implying a critical window during which mTORC1 hyperactivation irretrievably perturbs neuronal development. Furthermore, these changes are sufficient to maintain neuronal hyperactivity in TSC2-deficient neurons. These data are consistent with animal studies delineating sensitive periods during which rapamycin can prevent specific behavioral phenotypes.94 In addition, clinical trials have demonstrated that neurological symptoms in TSC display varying levels of response to mTORC1 inhibitors.7–9 Studies of other neurodevelopmental disorders, such as CDKL5 deficiency disorder, have also found limits of the reversibility of neurological symptoms, consistent with these data.95 Given the synaptic and cellular processes that we found to be insensitive to rapamycin treatment and their association with neuronal activity, these pathways represent strong candidates for the development and persistence of neurobehavioral symptoms in TSC. Further work is necessary to determine how these changes at the transcriptional level impact structural and functional interactions, leading to aberrant neural circuits and maladaptive behaviors. More importantly, this work has implications for the treatment of neurological symptoms in TSC because it suggests that strategies involving mTORC1 inhibition can only be successful if started early in development. There is a clinical trial in progress to determine whether rapamycin treatment started after birth can prevent neurological symptoms in TSC (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05104983). Last, this work implies that targeting the molecular defect after neuronal differentiation is not likely to correct the neuronal circuit abnormalities. This has been observed in other models of epilepsy and ASD,96 and further study is needed of pathological processes that may only be active during specific stages of neuronal differentiation and how this impacts treatment of these disorders.

One limitation of this study is that the proximate mechanism by which mTORC1 hyperactivation causes TET1 and TET2 downregulation remains unclear. Although direct transcriptional mechanisms are possible, another potential explanation is that this is due to increased degradation of their transcripts, as we have previously described increased mRNA degradation in TSC.23 Another possibility is that the upregulation of DNMT1 through the ability of mTORC1 to facilitate translation through 5′ UTR motifs is sufficient to repress these demethylases. The second clear limitation of this study is that the specific effects of the transcriptional and epigenetic dysregulation on synaptic structure and function in TSC2-deficient neurons are not known. While we have identified a promising cohort of genes that are altered in TSC2-deficient neurons and associated with synapses, future studies will be required to tease out the precise interactions and effects on neural circuit development and connectivity.
