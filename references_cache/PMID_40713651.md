# Organism profiles and empirical treatments for periprosthetic joint infections.
**Authors:** Jiang G, Wang W, Yang Y, Zhang M, Yang Y, Jiang Q
**Journal:** J Orthop Surg Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13018-025-06007-4](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-025-06007-4)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Information on pathogens and sensitive antibiotics is crucial for treating periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), one of the most severe complications of joint arthroplasty. Lacking this information is not uncommon, and empirical antibiotic treatment should be adopted as a compromise. This study aimed to develop regional-specific antimicrobial regimens and provide a reference for empirical antibiotic treatment for PJI by retrospectively analyzing the pathogen profiles of PJI patients treated in our center and their antibiotic sensitivities.

METHODS: PJI patients treated at our center from January 2018 to December 2024 were retrospectively recruited. Joint aspiration was performed preoperatively to collect synovial fluid for culture and differential cell counting, and synovial tissue samples were obtained intraoperatively from at least three different sites for culture. Patients were diagnosed according to the guidelines of the 2018 International Consensus Meeting and the European Bone and Joint Infection Society. The culture-positive rate, distribution of gram-positive pathogens, methicillin resistance, mixed infections, and multidrug resistance were analyzed. The effective coverage rates of antibiotics were determined, and appropriate empirical antibiotic regimens were proposed.

RESULTS: A total of 255 PJI patients, comprising 104 males and 151 females, were included; 141 patients had hip PJI, and 114 had knee PJI. Among them, 224 patients (87.8%) had positive culture results. We isolated 335 pathogens, including 218 coagulase-negative staphylococcal infections (65.1%). Staphylococcus epidermidis was the most prevalent pathogen, with 86 isolates (25.7%), followed by Staphylococcus aureus, with 45 isolates (13.4%), and Streptococci, with 20 isolates (6.0%). In hip PJI, the most common pathogens were Staphylococcus epidermidis (50 isolates, 26.6%) and Staphylococcus aureus (30 isolates, 16.0%), and in knee PJI, Staphylococcus epidermidis was predominant (36 isolates, 24.5%). In terms of drug resistance, 48.1% of the staphylococcal strains were methicillin resistant, and 57.6% of the pathogens were multidrug resistant. Staphylococci showed 100% sensitivity to vancomycin and linezolid but were highly resistant to β-lactams and quinolones. In patients with acute postoperative PJI, the combination of vancomycin combined with ceftazidime was 98.4% effective. In patients with chronic PJI, vancomycin combined with imipenem and meropenem achieved effective coverage rates of 94.4% and 95.5%, respectively. The combination of linezolid with meropenem also achieved a 95.5% effective coverage rate.

CONCLUSION: Gram-positive bacteria were the predominant pathogens associated with PJI, with high rates of methicillin resistance and multidrug resistance. The combination of vancomycin and meropenem is an empirical antibiotic regimen for culture-negative chronic PJI patients in this region, with the combination of linezolid and meropenem as an alternative. For patients with culture-negative acute postoperative PJI, vancomycin combined with ceftazidime is suggested as the preferred empirical therapy.

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is one of the most severe complications of joint arthroplasty. The incidence of PJI ranges from 0.8 to 1.9% for total knee arthroplasty and from 0.3 to 1.7% for hip arthroplasty [1, 2]. The five-year mortality rates for patients with PJI in the hip and knee joints are as high as 32.8% and 28.3%, respectively [3].

Antibiotic treatment and surgical intervention are the two main approaches for managing this complication. The debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) procedure has demonstrated a high success rate in infected unicompartmental knee arthroplasties (UKAs) [4]. Identification of the causative pathogens and sensitive antibiotics is crucial for the successful treatment of PJI. However, 25–31.8% of PJI cases yield culture-negative results [5, 6]. The absence of information on pathogenic bacteria in these patients poses significant challenges in the selection of appropriate antibiotics; inappropriate antibiotic usage reduces the efficacy of PJI treatment and increases the risk of developing antibiotic resistance, exacerbating infection and worsening patient outcomes [7]. Additionally, for acute PJI patients, empirical antibiotic therapy should be administered before culture results can be obtained, especially when immediate surgical interventions such as the DAIR are needed. Although patient optimization is ideal before DAIR, in clinical practice, the procedure is often required immediately because of systemic symptoms (e.g., fever, signs of sepsis) or severe local manifestations (e.g., pronounced joint swelling, local warmth and pain, and synovial fluid leakage). Therefore, analyzing the distribution of pathogens and their antibiotic sensitivities in PJI patients can aid in the formulation of more scientific and effective antimicrobial regimens for acute infection and culture-negative patients.

Notably, bacterial distributions may have regional disparities, and resistance patterns can evolve with antibiotic usage. Prior studies have underscored the predominance of Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis as causative pathogens [8]. In particular, MRSA prevalence and nosocomial colonization pose significant challenges in orthopedic settings [9]. Moreover, rare pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis can present as periprosthetic infections, especially in developing regions [10]. Given the lack of relevant data in our region, clarifying the local bacterial distributions and antibiotic sensitivities is vital for promoting precise and standardized treatment of PJI. However, patients with culture-negative PJI may have previously received antibiotic therapy, which may have altered the susceptibility of these microorganisms to these agents. Alternatively, empirical therapy should be tailored on the basis of the patient’s clinical context. Considering these factors, the bacterial distributions and antibiotic sensitivities of PJI patients at our center were retrospectively analyzed to guide the selection of empirical antibiotic therapy.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital affiliated with Nanjing University (approval number 2024-1027-01), and the requirement for informed consent was waived. This retrospective analysis included patients with hip and knee PJI treated at our institution from January 2018 to December 2024. PJIs were diagnosed according to the 2018 International Consensus Meeting (ICM) guidelines [11]. In addition, for patients who did not have a confirmed diagnosis of PJI by the ICM guidelines, the European Bone and Joint Infection Society (EBJIS) [12] would be employed, i.e., those with a single positive culture of methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRS). All patients underwent surgical treatment, and preoperative joint aspiration and intraoperative tissue collection were carried out for culture. The exclusion criteria included a history of septic arthritis (SA) prior to primary joint replacement, surgical site infection (SSI) without deep prosthetic involvement, an inability to identify the causative pathogen using routine culture methods (e.g., tuberculosis or nontuberculous mycobacteria), and the absence of surgical intervention in our center.

Joint aspiration was performed for each patient prior to surgery. In the case of chronic PJI, patients should not be on antibiotics for a minimum of 2 weeks prior to intra-articular culture [13]. The suprapatellar approach was used for knee joint aspiration, whereas the anterior‒lateral approach was used for hip joint aspiration. A minimum of 5 mL of synovial fluid was obtained and injected into aerobic/facultative aerobic culture bottles (BacT/ALERT FA Plus, bioMérieux, USA) for anaerobic bacterial culture and into anaerobic/facultative anaerobic culture bottles (BacT/ALERT FN Plus, bioMérieux, USA) for aerobic bacterial and fungal culture. The samples were sent to the laboratory within 30 min of collection and incubated at 37 °C in an automated incubator for 14 days (BacT/ALERT microbial culture monitoring system, bioMérieux, USA). Additional synovial fluid was used for the leukocyte esterase strip test and synovial fluid analysis (white blood-cell count and polymorphonuclear leukocyte percentage). In cases of “dry tap”, 5–10 mL of sterile physiological saline was injected into the joint and immediately aspirated under ultrasound guidance. This approach was employed in 13 patients and served only as an adjunct when standard aspiration failed. The culture results were compared with those from intraoperative samples. Among these 13 patients, 10 had concordant microbiological results, whereas 3 had discordant results. Following consultations with experts from the clinical microbiology and pharmacy departments, the intraoperative culture results were adopted as the definitive reference in all discordant cases to guide treatment decisions. During the aspiration procedure, direct contact between the needle and the prosthesis was achieved in each case to ensure the collection of samples around the prosthesis. Special care should be taken during this procedure to prevent neurovascular complications.

This study included patients who underwent one-stage revision, two-stage revision, DAIR, or arthrodesis. The surgical procedures were as follows: (1) One-stage revision: Complete prosthesis removal, radical debridement, and immediate reimplantation of a new prosthesis with antibiotic-loaded bone cement. (2) Two-stage revision: Initial prosthesis removal, debridement, and placement of an antibiotic-impregnated cement spacer. After a specified time interval, antibiotic course and confirmed infection control, a new prosthesis was implanted. (3) DAIR: Retention of the prosthesis with thorough debridement of infected periprosthetic tissues, exchange of modular components (e.g., polyethylene liners), and irrigation. (4) Arthrodesis: Resection of the infected joint, debridement, and fusion stabilization using internal fixation or external frames. In all procedures, except for DAIR, tissue samples were collected from the prosthesis–bone interface for microbiological culture to identify any infection. For DAIR, samples were collected from infected tissue only.

Each intraoperative sample was collected prior to the administration of surgical prophylactic antibiotics to ensure the accuracy of the samples. During the surgical procedure, synovial fluid and multiple tissue samples were collected for aerobic and anaerobic culture. The infected tissue samples were collected from the synovium, tissue next to the prosthesis on both the femur and tibial sides of the knee, and from both the femur and acetabulum sides of the hip. The samples were then manually milled in a sterile grinding cup containing 5–10 mL of saline solution. The homogenized liquid (2–5 mL) was transferred into both aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles for culture. In addition, the removed prostheses were placed in a sterile container with approximately 400 ml of sterile saline and sonicated at 40 Hz for five minutes. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for five minutes. By discarding the supernatant, the concentrate was resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline and added to aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles as well [14, 15].

All pathogenic cultures were processed in strict accordance with the “National Clinical Laboratory Procedures” guidelines, and no duplicate samples from the same patient were subjected to strain counting. The quality control strains used included Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 29213), Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC 29212), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853), and Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922). Pathogen identification was performed via matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time‒of‒flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, bioMérieux, France), the VITEK biological identification system, and an automatic fluorescent blood culture system (bioMérieux, France). Antibiotic susceptibility testing was conducted using the VITEK antibiotic sensitivity analysis system and associated susceptibility cards (bioMérieux, France). The results were interpreted according to the 2020 guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [16].

Patient data, including age, sex, diagnosis, surgery type, bacterial culture results, and antibiotic susceptibility results, were retrieved from our hospital’s electronic medical records system. All the data were initially imported into Microsoft Excel for preprocessing and statistical organization.

To evaluate the efficacy of antimicrobial agents, a new concept, the “effective coverage rate”, was developed to assess the efficacy of antibiotic regimens. The effective coverage rate for a specific antibiotic was defined as the proportion of pathogens sensitive to that antibiotic relative to the total number of pathogens identified, i.e., the number of pathogens sensitive to the index antibiotic)/(total number of pathogens identified in the study. When a combination of antibiotics was used, the effective coverage rate was defined as the number of pathogens sensitive to either antibiotic/the total number of pathogens identified in the study.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0.0.0; IBM, USA). Quantitative variables, such as age, are presented as variances. Qualitative variables, such as sex and bacterial species, are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Analysis of variance was employed to compare quantitative data and assess statistical significance. Chi-square tests, continuity-corrected chi-square tests, and Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare bacterial distributions and antibiotic susceptibilities across different groups. A p value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance to ensure the validity of the results.

The data are summarized in Table 1. In this study, 255 PJI patients, including 104 males and 151 females, were recruited, with an average age of 67.1 ± 10.1 years. The affected joints included 141 hips and 114 knees, and none of the patients had PJI in the bilateral joints. According to the type of infection, 47 patients (18.4%) were diagnosed with acute postoperative PJI, 1 patient (0.4%) with acute hematogenous PJI, and 207 patients (81.2%) with chronic PJI. The comorbidities included diabetes mellitus in 46 patients, hypertension in 106 patients, and rheumatoid arthritis in 10 patients. In 74 (29.0%) patients, two or more pathogens were identified, indicating polymicrobial infection. Eighteen of 74 (24.3%) patients with polymicrobial, but only 21 of 224 (9.3%) with monomicrobial infections had a sinus tract (p = 0.001). With respect to methods of surgical intervention, 194 patients underwent one-stage revision surgery, 45 patients underwent two-stage revision surgery, 15 patients underwent DAIR, and one patient underwent arthrodesis.

Pathogenesis was identified in 224 of the 255 patients, with a positive culture rate of 87.8% (Additional Figure and Table 2). A total of 335 bacterial strains were identified, and gram-positive bacteria (GPB) accounted for 300 strains (89.6%), with 171 strains identified in hip PJI and 129 strains identified in knee PJI. Gram-negative bacteria (GNB) accounted for 28 strains (8.4%), with 15 strains identified in hip PJI and 13 strains identified in knee PJI. Additionally, 7 fungal strains were identified, 2 from hip PJI and 5 from knee PJI.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that all the gram-positive staphylococcal (GPS) samples presented 100% sensitivity to vancomycin and linezolid. MRS exhibited sensitivity rates of 77.6% and 55.3% to gentamicin and clindamycin, respectively, whereas methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus (MSS) had sensitivity rates of 94.1% and 74.5%, respectively (Table 4). Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CNS) demonstrated sensitivity rates of 64.7%, 70.6%, and 95.0% to clindamycin, levofloxacin, and rifampicin, respectively, whereas S. aureus had corresponding sensitivity rates of 53.3%, 84.4%, and 88.9%, respectively. Furthermore, we identified one case of S. epidermidis resistant to teicoplanin and one case of glycolytic staphylococci resistant to tigecycline. Additionally, Enterobacteriaceae were 100% sensitive to imipenem and meropenem (Table 5), and no resistance to common antifungals was detected in the 7 fungal strains.

Excluding fungi, bacteria with no sensitivity results and patients with acute hematogenous PJI, we analyzed the remaining bacterial antibiotic sensitivity data to determine the effective coverage rates of specific antibiotic combinations for PJI patients in this region (Table 6). These antibiotic combinations were selected on the basis of international expert consensus, previous literature, and clinical practice experience and have been validated as effective antimicrobial regimens [17–21]. For patients with known microorganisms before surgery, targeted therapy with sensitive and narrow-spectrum antibiotics was used, and if sufficient pathogens were identified from intraoperative cultures, the postoperative regimen was further adjusted to the most appropriate antibiotic on the basis of susceptibility results. Empirical antibiotic therapy was reserved for patients without a confirmed microbiological diagnosis prior to revision surgery, such as those with multiple negative preoperative synovial fluid and blood cultures, or those requiring urgent surgical intervention for acute infection due to systemic symptoms or severe local inflammation, in whom culture results were not yet available. In patients with acute postoperative PJI, the combination of imipenem or meropenem with vancomycin achieved 100% effective coverage rates, whereas vancomycin combined with ceftazidime achieved a 98.4% rate. In patients with chronic PJI, vancomycin combined with ceftazidime, imipenem, or meropenem achieved effective coverage rates of 94.0%, 94.4%, and 95.5%, respectively. The combination of linezolid with meropenem also achieved a 95.5% effective coverage rate.

One of the pivotal steps in the successful treatment of PJI is the use of effective antibiotic therapy, which relies on the identification of pathogen species and their sensitive antibiotics. In cases of culture-negative cases or acute PJI when culture results are not available, empirical antibiotics should be used. In the present study, pathogen information was missing for 12.2% of 255 PJI patients, although the percentage was relatively low compared with that reported in previous studies [22–25]. Owing to the variation in pathogen distribution and antibiotic sensitivities in different regions and populations, an understanding of pathogen characteristics and antibiotic sensitivities in local PJI patients is critical. Our study revealed that GPB, particularly S. epidermidis and S. aureus, were the predominant pathogens in our PJI patients. Additionally, 48.1% of the isolates were MRS, and 57.6% were multidrug resistant, suggesting increased challenges in infection management. On the basis of these findings, empirical treatment regimen protocols were established with reasonable effective coverage rates to provide more precise clinical guidance.

Previous studies have indicated that GPB are the primary pathogens involved in PJI after hip and knee arthroplasty. In this study, GPB accounted for 89.6%, which was greater than the proportions reported in other studies (Table 6) [23–29]. The high prevalence of CNS (especially S. epidermidis) and virulent GPB (notably S. aureus) aligns with their well-documented capacity for prosthetic surface colonization and biofilm formation [27, 30]. However, it should be noted that biofilm formation is not limited to staphylococci; other PJI-associated pathogens, including GNB and anaerobes, also possess this capability. Therefore, special attention must be given to the management of biofilm-associated infections caused by a broad range of microorganisms in PJI.

The main type of pathogen involved in GPB in PJI is controversial, and the data are summarized in Table 7. In patients from Europe, Frank et al. [31] conducted a retrospective analysis of 493 German PJI patients and reported that CNS was the most prevalent pathogen at 44.9%, whereas in the United States, CNS remained the predominant bacteria [28]. In contrast, some reports [32, 33] have indicated that S. aureus is significantly more prevalent among PJI patients and that its prevalence is higher than that in European PJI patients, and Rupp et al. [34] also identified S. aureus as the primary pathogen (27.9%) by analyzing 81 German PJI patients. In our study, CNS pathogens were the most common, accounting for 65.1% of the 335 pathogens, followed by S. aureus at 13.4%. These controversies suggest that there are significant regional and population-based differences in the pathogen distribution of PJI, necessitating region-specific empirical treatment regimens.

In this study, the proportion of Streptococcus was significantly greater in knee PJI patients than in hip PJI patients (12.2% vs. 1.1%, P = 0.000), which is consistent with findings from a central China PJI survey [25]. Additionally, Froschen et al. [38] reported a greater incidence of streptococci in knee PJI patients than in hip PJI patients from Germany, with an overall proportion of streptococcal infections in PJI patients of approximately 15%. In contrast, several other studies [8, 20, 25] did not report a discrepancy in the proportion of streptococci between hip and knee PJI patients. One study reported that streptococci were more common in hip PJI [35]. However, patients treated with DAIR or long-term antibiotic therapy had significantly greater cure rates for streptococcal PJI than for MSS PJI [39]. Thus, we hypothesize that in acute PJI patients with streptococcal infections, better treatment outcomes may be achieved following DAIR or long-term antibiotic therapy.

On the other hand, the MRSA detection rate was 5.7% in this study, which was lower than that reported by other centers during the same period [22, 24, 40] but significantly greater than the rates reported in 1999 (0.9%) and 2001 (1.8%) [25]. The proportions of multidrug-resistant bacteria and MRS bacteria were 57.6% and 48.1%, respectively, which were greater than those reported by most other Chinese centers [23–25]. These results highlighted the increase in antibiotic resistance in PJI pathogens, emphasizing the need for continued monitoring and targeted therapy.

In this study, penicillin antibiotics exhibited limited efficacy against most GPSs, with an overall resistance rate as high as 62.7%. Notably, 42.2% of the Staphylococcus aureus isolates were resistant to oxacillin, a prevalence significantly higher than previously reported rates of 7–24%2 [38]. These findings suggest that penicillin antibiotics are less effective in treating PJI caused by GPS.

Although our study revealed that the sensitivity rate of MRS strains to gentamicin was only 77.6%, antibiotic resistance is dose dependent, and local delivery of antibiotics, such as gentamicin in bone cement, may still provide therapeutic efficacy even if pathogens show in vitro resistance [41]. In our center, the majority of patients underwent one-stage revision, and topical antibiotics (e.g., vancomycin) were administered postoperatively for 10–14 days. In these cases, the intra-articular concentration of vancomycin can be 1,000 mg/L in the synovial fluid, which significantly exceeds the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) [42]. However, considering that two-stage revision remains the standard procedure in most centers, we recommend that vancomycin be incorporated into the bone cement alongside gentamicin to enhance the antimicrobial spectrum and local drug efficacy. Furthermore, clindamycin demonstrated an average sensitivity of only 53.3% against GPS in our study, which is consistent with the findings reported by Yu et al. [25]. This highlights its limitations as a prophylactic agent during the perioperative period of total joint arthroplasty (TJA).

Vancomycin has been recommended for perioperative prophylaxis in patients with a high risk of infection who underwent arthroplasty, i.e., those with previous surgery, RA, and revision arthroplasty. It also serves as the most common antibiotic used to treat PJI. Our data revealed that no vancomycin- or linezolid-resistant strains were involved in pathogenesis, which is consistent with the findings of a previous study [43]. Teicoplanin and tigecycline are generally used as last-resort treatments for multidrug-resistant GPB infections [44]. Although the rarity of resistant pathogens indicates that resistance to these drugs has not yet become widespread, their emergence may signal a trend toward the development of more complex resistance mechanisms within bacterial populations.

Quinolones exhibited an overall sensitivity rate exceeding 70% for treating PJI caused by GPS. However, owing to their relatively high resistance rates, they are not recommended as first-line treatment options for PJI. Rifampicin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole are capable of penetrating bacterial biofilms, thereby enhancing the efficacy of other antibiotics [45]. Despite numerous studies reporting resistant strains [46], the sensitivity of GPS strains to rifampicin was greater than 88.9% in this study, whereas the sensitivity to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole was slightly greater. Thus, these drugs play crucial auxiliary roles in managing biofilm-forming strains.

Additionally, GNB strains resistant to meropenem and imipenem were detected in our PJI samples. The sensitivity rates of GNB for cephalosporins and aminoglycosides were 75.8% and 71.1%, respectively, whereas the sensitivity for fluoroquinolones was lower, at 57.4%, which is below the previously reported > 60%24. Although GNB are relatively rare, their treatment remains highly challenging.

Antibiotic sensitivity refers to the proportion of pathogens sensitive to a specific antibiotic, and the antibiotic coverage rate refers to the proportion of pathogens with available sensitivity results among all identified pathogens. To more accurately reflect the efficacy of antimicrobial agents across the entire PJI patient population, we introduced the “effective coverage rate” metric to evaluate the sensitivity proportion of a given antibiotic among all pathogens identified in this study.

Vancomycin and teicoplanin are both glycopeptide antibiotics. The efficacy of vancomycin in the treatment of PJI is well documented [32, 33, 47, 48]. In this study, vancomycin alone achieved effective coverage rates of 93.8% and 89.1% in the acute postoperative PJI group and chronic PJI group, respectively. On the basis of these findings, vancomycin is recommended as the first-line empirical antimicrobial therapy for culture-negative PJI patients in this region, provided that there are no contraindications. Although vancomycin requires slow infusion, therapeutic drug monitoring, and carries a risk of nephrotoxicity, it remains widely used because of its proven efficacy in treating PJI. Notably, intra-articular administration of vancomycin can significantly increase drug concentrations in synovial and bone tissues, potentially enhancing treatment outcomes [49]. Therefore, we advocate vancomycin as the preferred agent in most cases. In contrast, teicoplanin offers several advantages, such as the possibility of bolus administration, ease of empirical use, and a favorable safety profile. While its sensitivity was slightly lower than that of vancomycin in our cohort, the difference was not statistically significant. Thus, teicoplanin remains a valuable alternative, especially for patients who are at risk of vancomycin-related adverse events or when simplified administration is needed. However, the narrow spectrum of vancomycin does not cover GNB, necessitating combination with other antibiotics to ensure broad antimicrobial coverage and high sensitivity for culture-negative PJI patients in this region.

Previous studies have indicated that carbapenems can be effective components of empirical PJI regimens [17, 32, 38]. Our results demonstrated that vancomycin combined with imipenem or meropenem achieved effective coverage rates of 94.4% (with imipenem) and 95.5% (with meropenem) in chronic PJI patients, respectively. Although imipenem is effective against cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacteriaceae [20], its potential for organ toxicity makes meropenem a more favorable option. Moreover, given the increasing prevalence of MRSA and multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria (MDR-GNB), the vancomycin‒meropenem combination has demonstrated excellent efficacy, further supporting its role as a preferred empirical therapy for chronic PJI. While the empirical use of carbapenems, such as meropenem, raises concerns owing to their classification as last-resort antibiotics, their broad-spectrum efficacy makes them a valuable option in select high-risk clinical scenarios, particularly in cases of severe infection, a history of multidrug-resistant pathogens, or failure of initial empirical regimens.

Linezolid offers advantages in oral bioavailability, tissue penetration, and tolerability to certain toxic reactions, providing greater flexibility and safety in clinical use. Studies have shown that the combination of linezolid with carbapenems has superior therapeutic effects on complex infections with a lower risk of resistance development [50]. Hence, linezolid combined with meropenem may serve as a short-term empirical alternative in select cases, such as patients intolerant to vancomycin or other first-line agents. However, its use should be limited to short durations and requires close monitoring for hematologic and neurologic toxicity.

The combination of vancomycin with third-generation cephalosporins, such as ceftazidime and ceftriaxone, represents a widely adopted empirical regimen in the management of PJI. Ceftriaxone offers broad-spectrum coverage against most GNB, whereas ceftazidime provides enhanced activity against difficult-to-treat GNB such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Both agents also retain partial activity against GPB. When used in combination with vancomycin, which reliably targets GPB, including MRS, this regimen ensures expanded antimicrobial coverage across a broad spectrum of pathogens [36]. In our study, the combination of vancomycin with ceftazidime achieved effective coverage rates of 98.4% for acute postoperative PJI and 94.0% for chronic PJI, whereas vancomycin with ceftriaxone achieved rates of 93.8% and 90.3%, respectively. On the other hand, for acute PJI cases, infections are predominantly caused by GPB, and biofilm formation is less established. Given its enhanced efficacy against GNB and lower risk of promoting resistance than carbapenems do, vancomycin combined with ceftazidime may be considered the first-line empirical treatment in cases of acute hematogenous PJI.

Rifampicin exhibits excellent bactericidal activity against staphylococcal biofilms and is a key component of combination therapy in PJI. It not only enhances the penetration of coadministered antibiotics into biofilms but also actively targets sessile bacteria through the inhibition of bacterial RNA polymerase [51, 52]. Its clinical efficacy, particularly in combination with fluoroquinolones, has been demonstrated in both randomized trials and observational studies in patients undergoing DAIR or one-stage exchange [21]. However, our study revealed that the combination of vancomycin or linezolid with rifampicin did not significantly improve the effective coverage rate for empirical therapy in PJI patients in this region compared with the use of vancomycin or linezolid alone. Considering the risk of resistance and our results, we do not recommend rifampicin-containing combinations for routine empirical therapy in our PJI patients. Their use should be restricted to confirmed acute staphylococcal PJI treated with DAIR [53, 54] and fulfilling specific criteria, such as short symptom duration, fair soft tissue conditions and incision healing, stable implants, and documented rifampin susceptibility [21].

Several inherent limitations cannot be overlooked. First, although our center treats the majority of PJI patients in the region, the limited sample size and single-center design may not fully represent the pathogen profiles and antibiotic sensitivities of PJI patients across the entire area. Second, factors such as patients’ medical history, prior antibiotic use, and surgical history, which may influence the incidence of mixed infections and pathogen distributions, were not completely documented, and the differences in pathogens and sensitivities between different types of acute and chronic PJI patients could not be analyzed in detail. Additionally, second-generation gene sequencing tests are not routinely applied in most patients, potentially leading to missed diagnoses of pathogens with weak growth capabilities or those not detectable by blood cultures (Mycobacterium tuberculosis). Nevertheless, the clinical outcome of the proposed antibiotic combination cannot be verified due to the retrospective nature of the current study. Further multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes should be performed to assess the effectiveness of different combinations of empirical antibiotic regimens for treating PJI patients without identified pathogens.

This study demonstrated that GPB remains the primary pathogen in PJI patients in our region, predominantly comprising S. epidermidis and S. aureus. These pathogens exhibit high levels of resistance, with 48.1% being MRS and 57.6% being MDROs. On the basis of these findings, we recommend the combination of vancomycin and meropenem as an empirical antibiotic regimen for culture-negative chronic PJI patients in this region, with the combination of linezolid and meropenem as an alternative. For patients with culture-negative acute postoperative PJI, vancomycin combined with ceftazidime is suggested as the preferred empirical therapy. Notably, the combination of vancomycin or linezolid with rifampicin did not have significant advantages and is therefore not recommended for empirical therapy in this setting.
