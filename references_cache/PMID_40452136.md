# Long-Term Outcome of Young Infants With Suspected Neuroblastoma following Observation as Primary Therapy: A Report From the Children's Oncology Group.
**Authors:** Dholaria H, Naranjo A, Zhang FF, London WB, Diller L, Shamberger RC, Barnewolt CE, Schmidt ML, Maris JM, Cohn SL, Bagatell R, Nuchtern JG
**Journal:** Pediatr Blood Cancer (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/pbc.31829](https://doi.org/10.1002/pbc.31829)

## Content

Several studies have established that patients with localized perinatal neuroblastoma can be safely observed; however, long-term outcomes have not been previously reported. We evaluated long-term outcomes of infants with suspected perinatal neuroblastoma enrolled on the Children's Oncology Group (COG) ANBL00P2, which included an expectant observation approach. Among 87 eligible patients, the 10-year event-free survival (EFS) was 93.7%, and overall survival (OS) was 98.0%. Most tumors regressed spontaneously; only three patients reported progression to higher stage disease. This report reinforces the long-term safety of non-interventional management in perinatal neuroblastoma, sparing infants unnecessary surgery and associated risks.

Neuroblastoma (NB) originates from the developing sympathetic nervous system and is the most common extracranial malignant solid tumor in children. Prognosis is influenced by age, stage, and biology1. In many cases, infants with localized tumors require only observation or surgery.

Approximately 90% of perinatally detected neuroblastoma tumors are localized to the adrenal2 and are Stage L1 per the current International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) Staging System4. Unfavorable characteristics such as MYCN amplification or diploid DNA content are seen rarely in perinatal NB5,6. Overall survival (OS) for children with perinatal NB is >90%, with 100% OS for children with limited disease treated with resection alone2,7. However, surgical interventions in young infants are associated with significant risks8.

Several studies have demonstrated that an observation only approach (reserving surgery for specific indications) for children with localized perinatal neuroblastoma is safe and results in excellent outcomes. Spontaneous regression has been reported, supporting a “wait-and-see approach6,9. Based on these data, the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) conducted ANBL00P2 (NCT00445718), a prospective study of expectant observation of infants ≤6 months old with small perinatal adrenal masses. Among 87 eligible infants, 3-year event-free survival (EFS) for a neuroblastoma event was 97.7±2.2% and 3-year OS was 100%. Approximately 80% of observed children were spared resection; 3-year resection-free survival (RFS) was 79.8±6.0%10. These results led to a new standard of care in North America that includes expectant observation for infants <6 months of age with small adrenal tumors. The current study was undertaken to evaluate long term outcomes in this cohort.

ANBL00P2 was open to accrual July 2001-January 2010. Eligibility criteria were described previously10. Briefly, eligible patients were ≤6 months old with localized adrenal masses on one side of midline ≤16 mL if solid and ≤65 ml if ≥25% cystic. Patients with prior abdominal surgery, prior chemotherapy, or bone marrow involvement were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained. Parents/legal guardians were offered expectant observation or immediate surgery. For patients on the observation arm, abdominal sonograms and urinary levels of vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) and homovanillic acid (HVA), were obtained during the 90-week observation period, with cross-sectional imaging at 6 and 42 weeks. Surgical resection was indicated if there was a ≥50% increase in mass volume or if elevated catecholamines did not return to baseline/nadir value within 12 weeks, or the VMA/HVA ratio did not rise above 0.5 within 6 weeks regardless of tumor volume. After the 90-week observation period, resection was recommended for patients with residual masses >2mL or with elevated catecholamines regardless of mass volume.

All eligible infants enrolled were included in the current analytic cohort. EFS, OS, and RFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and survival curves were compared between groups with the log-rank test11. For EFS, time to event was defined as time from enrollment on ANBL00P2 until first occurrence of a neuroblastoma event, any malignant event, or death from any cause, or until time of last contact if no event occurred. Post-surgical identification of adrenal hematomas and extralobar pulmonary sequestrations were not included per this definition of an event. For OS, death was the only event considered. For RFS, occurrence of resection was the only event considered.

Of the 83 eligible infants in the original ANBL00P2 observation arm, 56 completed the 90-week observation period. Twenty-seven discontinued observation early, 13 of whom were lost to follow-up. Median follow-up time for the 82 children in the observation cohort still alive at the time of data analysis was 4.7 years (range: 71 days to 15.2 years); median follow up time for 55 patients still alive after completion of the observation period of 90 weeks was 3.5 years (range: 1 day to 12.9 years). Ten-year EFS, OS and RFS of 83 children in observation arm from time of enrollment were 93.3% (±8.1%), 97.9% (±4.5%), 79.9% (±12.7%) respectively (Figure 1A). While the number of patients who completed 10 years of follow up was relatively small, the survival curves were flat between 5 and 10 years, and the point estimates of survival at the 5- and 10-year timepoints were the same. EFS, OS and RFS of 56 children who completed observation from time of completion of observation were 94.7% (±12.6%), 97.4% (±9.0%), 98.1% (±7.9%) respectively (Figure 1B).

No difference in long-term EFS, OS or RFS based on solid/cystic tumor status was noted (Figure 1C). Ten-year EFS was 90.9% (±12.3%) for solid NB and 100% for cystic NB. Ten-year OS was 97.1% (±6.7%) vs 100% and 10-year RFS was 73.4% (±18.9%) vs 85.4% (±16.3%) for solid vs cystic NB respectively.

Evolution to higher stage disease (n=2) was previously reported10. One infant had INSS Stage 4S disease with liver involvement at time of first follow-up ultrasonography; this child was alive at 10.6 years. The second had >50% increase in mass volume and underwent surgery for Stage 2B disease; this child had no further relapse/progression at 1.7 years but was subsequently lost to follow-up. One additional infant was found to have INSS Stage 4 disease after 4.5 years of follow-up, and was subsequently treated for high-risk disease. Consent for additional data collection was withdrawn. Data were censored at time of discontinuation of follow-up at 5.7 years; vital status is unavailable. One patient who completed the observation period without requiring a resection subsequently died due to medical issues unrelated to neuroblastoma (end-stage renal disease due to a preexisting Joubert syndrome with a Dandy-Walker malformation).

Late effects were limited. One child from the observation arm had complete tumor regression but developed hypertension and renal artery stenosis with an atrophic kidney. One additional child, not included in the previous report, underwent a laparoscopic right adrenalectomy; the reason was not reported. The child was disease-free at 2.6 years when lost to follow-up. No other late effects, surgeries or complications were reported.

A “Wait-and-see” approach to management of perinatal neuroblastoma has been recommended by multiple groups based on short-term follow-up data6,12–15, but there is only a single report (n=5) regarding 10-year outcomes16. This is the first report to assess the long-term outcome of a large, prospectively monitored perinatal NB cohort. Our findings reinforce the safety of an observation-only approach.

The original ANBL00P2 report described 16 infants on the observational arm who eventually had surgery, one of whom had Stage 4S disease. With longer follow up, one additional child developed high-risk disease after 4.5 years. The patient’s initial tumor had regressed completely during the observation period, and it is not clear that upfront resection in the perinatal period would have prevented progression. The second tumor may have been a de novo metachronous NB rather than a recurrence. It is likely that progression to higher stage disease is driven by tumor biology, regardless of surgical or observational approach. Detailed data regarding the biology of this child’s subsequent disease is not available, however future study of tumors from rare children like this one may provide important insights. Analysis of circulating tumor DNA together with serial ultrasonographic and catecholamine monitoring may be investigated in future surveillance studies.

The high rate of spontaneous regression in the ANBL00P2 study cohort and the excellent long-term outcomes documented provide further support for the concept that most infants can be spared surgical intervention and associated risks. Use of non-invasive monitoring may reduce patient burden and provide insights into the biology of perinatal NB that may guide strategies for other low-risk pediatric cancers.
