# Hypoxia Promotes an Adrenergic to Mesenchymal Transcriptional Program Transition in Neuroblastoma.
**Authors:** Chaves GS, Chennakesavalu M, Moore K, Adams H, Chittenden C, Wilkinson E, Vayani O, Kaufman ME, Chlenski A, He C, Cohn SL, Applebaum MA
**Journal:** Clin Transl Sci (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cts.70508](https://doi.org/10.1111/cts.70508)

## Content

Adrenergic (ADRN) neuroblastoma cells have a paradoxical increase in TET1 expression and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5-hmC) deposition in hypoxia. However, the effect of hypoxia on the mesenchymal (MES) lineage remains less well understood. To identify lineage specific hypoxia responses, we evaluated 5-hmC and RNA expression in a panel of ADRN and MES cells cultured in normoxic or hypoxic conditions. In hypoxia, MES cells had only a modestly elevated expression of hypoxia signaling and no phenotypic changes. In contrast, ADRN cells showed increased 5-hmC deposition and decreased mitochondrial function in hypoxia. In hypoxia, only ADRN cells had increased expression of MES signature genes (p < 0.05) and related pathways including TNF-α signaling. Publicly available RNA-Seq data of diagnostic tumors was accessed (GSE62564 and GSE73517). Each tumor was assigned a hypoxia score using a published hypoxia signature. A MES/hypoxia signature comprising 94 MES signature genes that had increased expression in ADRN cells exposed to hypoxia was also generated. This MES/hypoxia signature was significantly correlated with a T cell infiltration signature (r = 0.66, p = 2.3 × 10-12). Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log rank test assessed overall survival (OS) differences between groups. High-risk patients with hypoxic tumors that had high MES/hypoxia scores had significantly improved 3-year OS compared to those with low MES/hypoxia scores (71% vs. 40%; p = 0.0054). Superior survival was also observed in high-risk patients with hypoxic tumors that had high MES/hypoxia scores in the validation cohort (GSE73517; n = 28; 3-year OS 78% vs. 26%, p = 0.0012). Our results suggest that hypoxia changes expression of genes in ADRN cells towards MES cells.

Neuroblastoma is a pediatric cancer of the peripheral nervous system. More effective therapy is needed for children with high‐risk neuroblastoma as 5‐year survival rates remain approximately 50%–60% [1, 2]. While outcomes have improved with modern therapies, many patients continue to have recurrence of their disease, necessitating an enhanced understanding of molecular mechanisms of how tumor govern the growth and survival [3]. Neuroblastoma tumors contain stem‐like mesenchymal‐like (MES) and committed adrenergic (ADRN) cells that are characterized by distinct super‐enhancer landscapes and gene expression profiles [4, 5, 6]. Importantly, neuroblastoma cell lines are capable of interconverting between the MES and ADRN lineage with corresponding changes in phenotype [4, 5, 6, 7]. MES cell lines are resistant to many standard treatments in vitro, and increased MES cells have been detected in relapsed neuroblastoma tumors [4, 8].

The TNF‐alpha (TNF‐α) signaling pathway has also been shown to be active in MES cells. This pathway plays a complex role in regulating tumor growth, and its effects are context dependent. While TNF‐α can have pro‐tumorigenic effects by promoting tumor cell migration, angiogenesis, and immune suppression, this cytokine has also been shown to induce antitumor immune responses by mediating an inflammatory response in the tumor microenvironment [9]. Interestingly, neuroblastoma patients with T cell inflamed tumors have been reported to have superior survival compared to patients with non‐T cell inflamed tumors [10].

Regions of severe hypoxia have been identified in solid tumors, subsequently affecting numerous cellular pathways [11]. Neuroblastoma cells grown under hypoxic conditions have an undifferentiated, aggressive phenotype corresponding to altered gene expression of genes regulated by Hypoxia Inducible Factor (HIF) [12, 13, 14]. In addition, hypoxia has been shown to increase the metastatic potential of neuroblastoma cells [15], increase the stem cell‐like phenotype [16], and alter proliferation [17]. Furthermore, the activation of hypoxia response pathways has also been associated with worse prognosis in patients with neuroblastoma [18, 19].

In neuroblastoma, hypoxia‐induced increases in HIF‐1α have been shown to increase the expression of the 10‐11‐translocation‐5‐methylcytosine dioxygenase‐1 (TET1) enzyme which converts 5‐methylcytosine to 5‐hydroxymethylcytosine (5‐hmC), formyl‐, and carboxyl‐cytosine [13, 14]. This response is paradoxical and specific to neuroblastoma, as the TET enzymes are oxygen‐dependent and are subsequently less active in low oxygen environments [20, 21]. However, this unexpected increase in TET1 and 5‐hmC, a key DNA modification of open chromatin and active gene expression [22], has only been identified in ADRN and not MES type neuroblastoma cell lines. Here, we sought to further identify the biologic and prognostic significance of the response to hypoxia specific to ADRN and MES lineages.

Neuroblastoma cell lines LA1‐55n (ECACC Cat# 06041203, RRID:CVCL_2548), LA1‐5 s (RRID:CVCL_2549), SH‐SY5Y (RRID:CVCL_0019), SHEP (RRID:CVCL_0524), NBL‐W‐N (RRID:CVCL_9900), NBL‐W‐S (RRID:CVCL_9901), SK‐N‐BE2 (RRID:CVCL_0528), and NBL‐S (DSMZ Cat# ACC‐656, RRID:CVCL_2136) were grown at 5% CO2 in RPMI1640 medium (Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated FBS, 2 mmol/L l‐glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were verified with STR testing and were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma. LA1‐55n, LA1‐5 s; SH‐SY5Y, SHEP; and NBL‐W‐N, NBL‐W‐S are isogenic pairs composed of an ADRN (LA1‐55n, SH‐SY5Y and NBL‐W‐N) and a MES (LA1‐5 s, SHEP and NBL‐W‐S) cell line originating from the same parental cell line. For all experiments, cell lines were exposed to normoxia (21% O2) or hypoxia (1% O2) for 48 h unless otherwise specified.

A MTT (3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) solution was prepared in PBS at 5 mg/mL and filtered according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 48 h of treatment, cells were incubated with MTT solution for 4 h at 37°C. Plates were read on a Bioteck Synergy 2 Microplate Reader, using a wavelength of 490 nm and a reference wavelength of 630 nm.

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was hydrolyzed into individual nucleosides for LC–MS detection as described [13, 14]. The mixed nucleoside solution was resolved by liquid chromatography on a Zorbax XDB‐C18 2.1 50 mm column (1.8 mm particle size), attached to an Agilent 1200 Series liquid chromatography instrument and then resolved on a coupled Agilent 6410 Triple Quad Mass Spectrometer for individual nucleoside detection.

Total RNA was isolated from 60 mm cell culture plates using Trizol (ThermoFisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's protocol. SuperScript III First‐Strand Synthesis System (ThermoFisher Scientific) was used for first‐strand cDNA synthesis. Real‐time quantitative PCR was performed using the 7500 Fast Real‐Time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific) instrument, following the manufacturer's protocol. Target‐gene primers were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies for each gene. After RT‐qPCR, results were normalized to 18S ribosomal RNA RPLP1. Calculation of the gene expression levels followed the 2−ΔΔCT method. Representative values from three independent experiments are shown.

Following total RNA extraction using Trizol, DNA was removed with the TURBO DNA‐free kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to manufacturer instructions. One hundred base pair, paired‐end libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000. FASTQC v0.11.5 was used to assess sequence quality [23]. Raw reads were processed with Trimmomatic [24] and aligned to hg38 with Bowtie2 v2.3.0 [25] using default settings. Aligned reads with Mapping Quality Score ≥ 10 were counted using featureCounts [26] of Subread using the gene flag and the gencode.v33.annotation.gtf file from GENCODE [27].

Raw read counts corresponding to 5‐hmC and RNA sequencing (RNA‐Seq) across exons in the genes were loaded into the DESeq2 v1.20.018 package in R [28]. Differential RNA expression between ADRN and MES cells and between normoxia and hypoxia was performed using DESeq2. Genes within the top and bottom 10% of fold change and threshold adjusted p‐value (p
adj) of < 5% were considered significant and used for further analyses. The R package Pheatmap (version 1.0.12) was used to perform unsupervised hierarchical clustering and calculate the distance matrix between samples.

ClusterProfiler (RRID:SCR_016884) version 4.0.5 was used to assess enriched signaling pathways using MSigDB gene sets [29]. Visualization of enriched pathways present in MSigDB collections H, C2, and C5 was performed using msigdbr (RRID:SCR_022870) version 7.5.1 [30]. Signature scores were determined by gene set variation analysis using the GSVA (RRID:SCR_021058) package version 1.44.2 [31].

HIF‐1α ChIP‐Seq data were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) accession number GSE167478, hMe‐Seal data from GSE167475 and GSE167477. RNA‐Seq of the SEQC‐NB cohort (n = 498; 176 high‐risk) was downloaded from R2 (r2.amc.nl) under accession number GSE62564. These datasets were used for the Discovery cohort. Data from the GSE73517 cohort (n = 105; 56 high‐risk) were used as a Validation cohort.

R Project for Statistical Computing (RRID:SCR_001905) version 4.1.1 and GraphPad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798) were used for statistical analysis. T‐tests were used to test for differences in mitochondrial function, quantitative PCR expression, and HPLC‐MS/MS 5‐hmC deposition. The chi‐squared test was used to test independence between the ADRN and MES cellular identities and hypoxia. Differences in survival were assessed with the log‐rank test using 3‐year point estimates of overall survival (OS) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were assessed. p‐values < 0.05 were considered significant.

To establish transcriptional programs in ADRN and MES cells in normoxia, gene expression profiles of five ADRN cell lines were compared to three MES cell lines in normoxia. We identified 1298 genes with significantly increased expression in ADRN cells compared to MES cells (Figure 1A, Tables S1 and S2) that constituted an ADRN signature in subsequent analyses. Pathway analysis demonstrated enrichment of neuronal pathways, as expected (Figure 1B, Table S2). In MES cells, 1469 genes had increased expression compared to ADRN cells that constituted a MES signature in subsequent analyses (Table S1). Collagen containing extracellular matrix, hallmark of epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT), hallmark hypoxia, and hallmark tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) signaling via NF‐κB were among the pathways enriched in the MES cells in normoxia (Figure 1C, Table S3). We compared the ADRN and MES gene sets with those reported by Van Groningen et al. [4] and found significant overlap for both ADRN genes (133/1298, p = 3.9e‐56) and MES genes (139/1469, p = 1.5e‐46).

RT‐qPCR was used to evaluate hypoxia‐induced changes in TET1 mRNA expression in ADRN and MES cells. Consistent with prior reports, TET1 was significantly increased after 48 h exposure to hypoxia compared to normoxia in ADRN cells (log2 fold change 6.43, p = 0.005) (Figure 1D). There was also a smaller but significant increase in TET1 expression in MES cells in hypoxia (log2 fold change 1.48, p = 3.4e‐5). As expected, HPLC‐MS/MS demonstrated hypoxia significantly increased the percent of global cytosines with 5‐hmC in ADRN cells cultured under hypoxic versus normoxic conditions (0.011% versus 0.007%, p = 0.012; Figure 1E). In contrast, hypoxia did not significantly change global 5‐hmC levels in MES cells compared to normoxia (0.005% vs. 0.008%, p = 0.43; Figure 1E).

We next investigated changes in gene expression programs in response to hypoxia across ADRN and MES neuroblastoma cells using RNA‐Seq. ADRN cells cultured under hypoxic conditions for 48 h demonstrated up‐regulation of 554 genes and down‐regulation of 635 genes (Figure 2A; Table S4). As reported in hypoxia [18], there was increased expression of genes of canonical glycolysis (Figure 2B). Pathways identified in MES cells in normoxia, including hypoxia, TNF‐α signaling via NF‐κB, and collagen biosynthesis were also among the most significantly enriched up‐regulated pathways in ADRN cells exposed to hypoxia (Figure 2B; Table S5). Downregulated pathways enriched in ADRN cells exposed to hypoxia included mitosis, DNA metabolism, and cell cycle (Figure 2C; Table S6), also consistent with previous reports [18]. Consistent with the expression changes, mitochondrial function of ADRN cells cultured under hypoxic conditions for 48 h was also decreased (p = 5.4e‐5, Figure 2D).

In MES cells, the expression of 212 genes increased in 48 h of hypoxia exposure while 103 genes had decreased expression (Table S7). We found that genes up‐regulated in hypoxia in MES cells were enriched for hypoxia response and glycolysis pathways but not TNF‐α signaling. Genes that were decreased in hypoxia were enriched for mitochondrial processes (Figure S1). Hypoxia also did not significantly change cell cycle gene expression or mitochondrial function of MES cells (p = 0.15; Figure 2D). Taken together, our results suggest that hypoxia induces a MES‐like gene expression pattern in ADRN cells but not in MES cells.

RT‐qPCR was used to investigate hypoxia‐induced changes in the expression in select MES signature genes [staniocalcin (STC1), procollagen‐proline, 2‐oxoglutarate‐4‐dioxygenase subunit alpha‐1 (P4HA1), and basic helix–loop–helix family member e40 (BHLHE40)] in ADRN cells over time. The MES genes were selected as they were expressed at high levels in MES cells and have been identified in MES signatures in other studies in neuroblastoma [4, 8]. We found that all three of the representative MES signature genes were significantly up‐regulated after 144 h in hypoxia (p < 0.05) in the ADRN cell line NBL‐W‐N (Figure 3A).

In SK‐N‐BE2 cells, increased expression of (B) STC1 and (C) BHLHE40 are accompanied by HIF‐1α binding and increased 5‐hmC deposition. In the NBL‐W‐N/NBL‐W‐S isogenic pair, the (D) STC1 and (E) BHLHE40 MES genes had increased gene expression and modest increases in 5‐hmC in the ADRN NBL‐W‐N (WN) cell line but not NBL‐W‐S (WS) in hypoxia.

Our results thus far have established that under hypoxia, ADRN cells have increased TET1 expression, 5‐hmC deposition levels, and expression of MES signature genes. To examine how HIF‐1α mediates TET deposition of 5‐hmC on MES signature gene expression, HIF‐1α ChIP‐Seq and hmC‐seal data of ADRN cells cultured in normoxic and hypoxic conditions was accessed from GSE167477. In hypoxia, there was increased binding of HIF‐1α and increased 5‐hmC deposition in the gene body and promoter regions of the STC1 and BHLHE40 genes (Figure 3B,E). These results suggest that the increased expression of the MES genes detected in hypoxic ADRN cells is due, at least in part, to HIF‐1α‐driven and TET‐mediated changes in 5‐hmC deposition.

We next sought to evaluate whether hypoxia was associated with ADRN or MES expression signatures in patient tumors. As hypoxia appeared to be an intrinsically activated pathway in MES cell lines, we chose to isolate those genes by intersecting our MES signature genes (n = 1469) with the 554 genes from ADRN cells that were upregulated in hypoxia. MES signature genes were those that had increased expression in MES cells in normoxia compared to ADRN cells in normoxia. There were 94 overlapping genes which we defined as our MES/hypoxia signature (Figure 4A). As expected, these genes were enriched for pathways previously identified in MES cell lines including EMT, hypoxia, TNF‐α signaling via NF‐κB, and inflammatory response (Figure 4B). In a Discovery cohort, GSE62564, each patient with high‐risk disease (N = 176) was assigned a MES/hypoxia score which was dichotomized as either high or low signature expression. Based on prevalences provided by prior studies [32] and our prior experience [33], we assigned those tumors with the highest third of MES/hypoxia score as high and the remainder as low. We similarly assigned a hypoxia signature score to each patient based on a published hypoxia gene set [34] and grouped patients as having tumors with high or low hypoxia tumors relative to median hypoxia scores. We confirmed that MES/hypoxia signature scores correlated with the MES signature (r = 0.92, p < 2.2e‐16; Figure 4C) and with a T cell infiltration signature [35] (r = 0.66, p < 2.2e‐16; Figure 4D), as has been reported [33, 36].

Because the T cell infiltration signature has been correlated with improved survival in high‐risk neuroblastoma patients [10, 33], we next hypothesized that our MES/hypoxia signature would be prognostic for patients bearing hypoxic tumors. Within the subset of 88 patients with high hypoxia signatures, those with elevated MES/hypoxia signature scores (N = 48; 21 MYCN‐amplified and 27 MYCN‐nonamplified) had significantly improved OS compared to those (N = 41; 24 MYCN‐amplified and 15 MYCN‐nonamplified, 1 unknown) with low MES/hypoxia scores (3‐year OS: 71%, 95% CI: 56%–82% vs. 40%, 95% CI: 25%–55%, p = 0.0054; Figure 4E). No significant difference in OS was identified in the subset of 88 high‐risk patients with low hypoxia signatures according to high MES/hypoxia (N = 17; 6 MYCN‐amplified and 11 MYCN‐nonamplified) compared to (N = 71; 41 MYCN‐amplified and 30 MYCN‐nonamplified) low MES/hypoxia scores (3‐year OS: 76%, 95% CI: 49%–90% vs. 53%, 95% CI: 39%–64%, p = 0.84; Figure 4F).

These findings were confirmed in a Validation cohort (GSE73517) consisting of 56 patients with high‐risk neuroblastoma. We again confirmed that MES/hypoxia signature scores correlated with the MES signature (r = 0.95, p < 0.05; Figure S2A) and with the same T cell infiltration signature (r = 80, p < 0.05; Figure S2B). Within the subset of the Validation cohort with high hypoxia scores, those with elevated MES/hypoxia signature scores (N = 15) had significantly improved OS compared to those (N = 13) with low MES/hypoxia scores (3‐year OS 78%, 95% CI: 46%–92 versus 26%, 95% CI: 6.3%–52%, p = 0.0012; Figure S2C). Again, no significant difference in OS was identified in the cohort of 28 high‐risk patients with low hypoxia signatures according to high MES/hypoxia (N = 6) compared to (N = 22) low MES/hypoxia scores (3‐year OS 100%, 95% CI: 100%–100% versus 54%, 95% CI: 32%–72%, p = 0.18; Figure S2D).

In the current study, we investigated hypoxia‐induced changes in gene expression in ADRN and MES neuroblastoma cells and found that hypoxia induced changes in gene expression in both. Specifically, hypoxia induced the expression of MES signature genes in ADRN cells, including the increase in pathways related to cytokine signaling (Figure 1B,C). These changes in expression were potentially due to TET1‐mediated 5‐hmC deposition on these genes, which is known to facilitate transcriptional activation. Finally, to evaluate the role of ADRN to MES transcriptional program activation in tumors, we separated high‐risk tumors with high hypoxia scores from high‐risk tumors with low hypoxia scores in publicly available tumor datasets. We showed that patients with high‐risk neuroblastoma who have tumors with increased hypoxia signature scores and increased expression of our MES/hypoxia signature (MES signature genes with upregulated expression in ADRN cells in hypoxia) had superior OS compared with patients with increased hypoxia signature scores but decreased expression of our MES/hypoxia signature (Figure 4E,F). The MES/hypoxia score was itself correlated with T cell infiltration scores (Figure 4D). These findings inform both our biologic understanding of neuroblastoma and identify potential opportunities for the development of immunotherapies.

Neuroblastoma is characterized by heterogeneity and neuroblastoma cells can spontaneously interconvert between MES and ADRN cell states. ADRN and MES have different responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy [4, 5, 6], and some studies suggest that MES cells contribute to relapse [4, 8]. Consistent with previous reports, we found that in hypoxia, TET1 and 5‐hmC levels increase in ADRN cells (Figure 1D,E) [13, 14]. This response mechanism has been shown to be driven by interactions of both MYCN and HIF‐1α leading to the activation of the hypoxia response program [14]. In contrast, MES cells generally have lower expression of MYCN [37] and we found that hypoxia induces minimal changes in TET1 expression and no differences in 5‐hmC in these cells (Figure 1E). Interestingly, MES cells had evidence of activation of hypoxia transcriptional programs when cultured in normoxic conditions, possibly representing an intrinsic adaptation to hypoxia. This may explain the more minimal changes identified in both gene expression and mitochondrial function when MES cells were exposed to hypoxia. While we have limited data on the biologic consequences of this phenotype switching, future experiments to elucidate this will be important. Specifically, it will be necessary to understand if ADRN cells become more resistant to chemotherapy as a result of their more MES expression patterns in hypoxia and whether this switch can be overcome to reduce sensitivity. Additional functional experiments would be to better understand if the ADRN cells obtain other features such as increased migration and invasion often seen with hypoxia exposed cancer cells [15].

Our results suggest that hypoxia has both pro‐ and antitumor effects on the biology of neuroblastoma cells. The hypoxic shift from the more neuronal and faster growing ADRN cells to a more MES‐like state results in pro‐tumor effects by inducing what is perhaps a more senescent state and decreasing sensitivity to chemotherapy. The MES phenotype has been associated with chemoresistance and a more stem‐like state [4] as well as activation of the RAS‐MAPK pathway and increased prevalence in relapsed tumors. Conversely, we found that MES cells and their associated expression patterns are associated with inflammatory signaling and T cell infiltration. It has been reported that this change makes MES cells more efficiently targeted by cytotoxic T and natural killer cells and more likely to respond to immune checkpoint blockade [36]. While checkpoint blockade has not shown efficacy for children with neuroblastoma, our data suggest that in tumors with hypoxic reprograming, this class of therapy has the potential to be more efficacious.

While the therapeutic implications of lineage plasticity in patients' tumors require further investigation, our results provide intriguing possibilities for the development of refined immunotherapeutic approaches for neuroblastoma tumors affected by hypoxia. There are reports of MES signatures in diagnostic biopsies being associated with improved outcomes [32, 38]. Further suggesting how hypoxia can make some neuroblastoma tumors less aggressive, it was recently shown that elevated HIF‐2α can decrease MYCN expression and push neuroblastoma cells towards a less aggressive phenotype [39]. In agreement with other studies, we showed that MES cells express higher expression of pathways including TNF‐α and NF‐κB compared to ADRN cells (Figure 1C) [33, 36, 40, 41, 42, 43]. In both cohorts of high‐risk patients analyzed, we found increased expression of our 94 gene MES/hypoxia signature to be associated with improved OS specifically in tumors that expressed higher levels of a hypoxia signature, demonstrating the robustness of this signature (Figure 4E). Thus, while hypoxia remains associated with worse survival [18], there may be aspects of the hypoxia response that could be further studied to better understand subsets of patients with high‐risk disease. However, these findings are in retrospective cohorts of patients where key information such as treatment received is unknown. Thus, this signature requires confirmation in prospective studies before it would be considered for use as a clinical prognostic biomarker.

We further identified hypoxia‐induced increased expression of STC1, BHLHE40, and P4HA1 over time (Figure 3A) and demonstrate increased 5‐hmC deposition in the promoter regions of STC1 and BHLHE40 (Figure 3B,E). STC1 has been shown to be coexpressed with TNF‐α in macrophage differentiation [44] and is expressed in the developing neural crest [45]. P4HA1 is involved in collagen biogenesis and fibrosis induced by hypoxia and may mediate aggressive cancer behaviour in hypoxic environments [46, 47]. BHLHE40 is involved in mitochondrial function [48] and involved in tumor‐infiltration of CD8+ T cells as part of the TNF‐α response [49].

Our work has several limitations that should be noted. First, our findings are largely correlative and reliant on transcriptional and epigenetic profiling and additional experiments are needed to confirm additional phenotypic changes of ADRN cells in hypoxia. In previous work, our group showed that HIF‐1α directly binds and activates TET1 which is also controlled by MYCN [14]. While we have used these data to suggest that HIF‐1α binding regulates the shift towards a MES phenotype in hypoxia, additional work is needed to show this is causative. Future directions to demonstrate will be to repeat ChIP and expression analysis under genetic and chemical inhibition of HIF‐1α to show that phenotype switching is blunted with loss of this critical transcription factor. These data are also restricted to in vitro analysis and additional in vivo experiments are warranted. Our findings of the MES/hypoxia signature being correlated with outcomes are also limited by small sample size. Despite this, we used two independent cohorts to validate the association which adds robustness to these findings.

In summary, in ADRN cells, hypoxia induced epigenetic and gene expression programs consistent with an ADRN to MES transition, which may have implications for tumor interaction with surrounding cells. This study adds to both our biologic understanding of neuroblastoma and identifies potential opportunities for further developing immunotherapies for children with high‐risk neuroblastoma. Further investigation into the mechanisms controlling the up regulation of MES genes and their implications should be carried out.
