Angelman syndrome (AS) is a severe neurological disorder characterized by 
intellectual disability, absence of speech, spontaneous seizure, and motor 
dysfunction. The absence of functional maternally derived UBE3A protein is 
considered the primary cause of AS, yet the downstream signaling pathways remain 
elusive. Here, we show the voltage-gated K+ channel Kv4.2 as an 
activity-dependent substrate for UBE3A. We show that UBE3A binding of Kv4.2 at 
its N terminus, ubiquitinating residue K103, induces activity-induced Kv4.2 
protein loss. In a mouse model of AS, we observe elevated Kv4.2 protein level 
and abolished kainic acid-induced Kv4.2 protein loss. Moreover, deficits in 
mEPSC frequency and spike-timing-dependent long-term potentiation, as well as 
certain behaviors including cognitive inflexibility found in AS mice, are 
rescued when bred with Kv4.2 conditional knockout mice. These findings indicate 
a UBE3A downstream pathway regulating plasticity and cognitive behaviors and 
provide potential targets for the treatment of AS.

Angelman syndrome (AS) is a severe debilitating neurodevelopmental disorder with an estimated incidence of 1 in 20,000 that is characterized by intellectual disability, impaired motor coordination, seizures, sleep impairments, increased anxiety, lack of speech, and high comorbidity with autism spectrum disorder.1,2 It is caused by loss of function of imprinted genes on human chromosome 15q11–13 or by deletions or mutations in the ubiquitin ligase E3A (UBE3A, also known as E6-AP) gene, which resides in this region.3–6 Imprinting of this gene results in the exclusive expression of the maternal allele in hippocampal neurons and other nerve cells.7 The UBE3A gene encodes an HECT (homologous to E6AP COOH terminus) type of ubiquitin protein ligase E3A (UBE3A), which primarily acts as an E3 ligase in ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation by the 26S proteasome.8 The identification of the UBE3A gene as the causal gene for AS6 allowed for the generation of mouse models of AS to study its function. Heterozygous mice with a maternally inherited Ube3a deletion (hereafter called ‘‘AS mice’’) show motor and cognitive defects similar to the symptoms shown by AS individuals.9–11 In addition to behavioral phenotypes, it has previously been shown that excitatory transmission within the CA1 region of the hippocampus, and also in the medial prefrontal cortex, is impaired in AS mice.12–14 Synaptic deficits specific to inhibitory synapses targeting layer 5/6 excitatory neurons of the visual cortex have also been reported.15 Evidence from induced pluripotent stem cells from AS patients suggests that these synaptic deficits arise as a result of dysfunctions in the maturation of membrane excitability.16 Together, these studies show that loss of UBE3A function contributes to an imbalance in excitatory/inhibitory transmission through dysregulation of synaptic maturation in multiple brain regions. This imbalance is likely to underlie learning/memory phenotypes characteristic to the disease.

A few neuronal proteins have recently been identified as UBE3A substrates, including Ephexin5,17 small conductance potassium (SK2) channels,18 and calcium- and voltage-dependent big potassium (BK) channels.19 However, novel UBE3A substrates that contribute to AS phenotypes are still to be uncovered. Here, we investigated the role of rapidly activating and inactivating somatodendritic voltage-gated K+ (Kv) A-type currents. A-currents regulate action potential (AP) repolarization and repetitive firing and attenuate back-propagation into the dendrites of CA1 hippocampal pyramidal neurons.20,21 Kv4.2 is the prominent A-type voltage-gated potassium channel expressed in hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neuron dendrites and controls dendritic excitability,20 which impacts neuronal plasticity22,23 and contributes to learning and memory.24,25 Kv4.2 channels function in macromolecular protein complexes with accessory subunits, including the K+-channel interacting proteins (KChIP1–4) and dipeptidyl peptidases 6 and 10 (DPP6 and DPP10).26 Both KChIPs and DPPs work in concert to modify Kv4 surface expression and channel kinetics.27,28 KChIPs are Ca2+-sensing proteins that accelerate Kv4 channel opening, slow inactivation, shift voltage dependence of inactivation toward more depolarized potentials, and accelerate recovery from inactivation.29–31 DPP6 is a type II transmembrane protein that increases Kv4.2 membrane expression and single-channel conductance and accelerates the inactivation and recovery from inactivation of Kv4 subunit-containing channels.32,33

Our previous study defined a Pin1 isomerase-dependent mechanism that regulates the composition of the Kv4.2-DPP6 complex, neuronal excitability, and cognitive flexibility.34 In response to activity, Kv4.2 is phosphorylated at T607 by p38 kinase, which triggers Pin1 binding to and isomerization of Kv4.2, resulting in Kv4.2-DPP6 dissociation.34 Kv4.2 and DPP6 have been implicated in neurological diseases, such as autism spectrum disorder,35–37 temporal lobe epilepsy,38,39 fragile X syndrome,40,41 and neurodegeneration.42 In the present study, we identify Kv4.2 as an activity-dependent substrate of UBE3A via a tandem affinity purification-mass spectrometry (TAP-MS) pull-down assay. Subsequent biochemical studies revealed that UBE3A binds to DPP6-associated Kv4.2, ubiquitinates Kv4.2 at residue K103, and mediates its activity-dependent degradation. These findings are further supported by evidence showing that UBE3A-mediated Kv4.2 protein degradation following kainic acid (KA) treatment requires phosphorylation at the Pin1 binding sites within DPP6-containing complexes. To further study the role of Kv4.2 in AS, we generated a Kv4.2 conditional knockout (Kv4.2cKO, Emx1-cre/KCND2F/F) mouse line. Cross-breeding AS with Kv4.2cKO mice rescued some physiological and behavioral impairments associated with AS, including a learning and memory task and synaptic plasticity. Taken together, we describe Kv4.2 dysregulation as a critical contributor to AS pathology, and our study provides a new perspective on the molecular mechanisms underlying dysfunctions in AS.

We used a TAP-MS assay to identify Kv4.2 interacting proteins in cultured hippocampal neurons, where TAP-Kv4.2 was expressed via lentivirus.34,43 To examine whether neuronal activity alters Kv4.2 protein complexes, hippocampal neurons were treated with either α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA, 50 μM for 15 min) or vehicle control at day in vitro (DIV) 14 before harvest. Kv4.2-interacting proteins were affinity purified, separated on SDS-PAGE, excised, and analyzed by MS (Figure 1A). UBE3A was identified by MS in the AMPA but not control treatment samples (Table S1 and Figure 1B). To confirm the TAP-MS result, we performed western blot analysis, which showed that AMPA treatment increases UBE3A-Kv4.2 association compared to control in cultured hippocampal neurons (Figure 1C). UBE3A-Kv4.2 binding was also confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) when expressed in HEK 293T cells (Figure 1D). In addition, Kv4.2 N terminus deletion (ΔNT) dramatically reduced binding to UBE3A, while Kv4.2 C terminus deletion (ΔCT) did not (Figure 1D), indicating that UBE3A binds to Kv4.2 NT. The MS data (Figures 1A and 1B) and western blot analysis (Figure 1C) suggested that UBE3A binding to Kv4.2 is activity dependent. We then examined whether neuronal excitation induced by KA alters UBE3A and Kv4.2 association using immunostaining. UBE3A and Kv4.2 antibodies used in the immunostaining were verified using AS and Kv4.2KO mice, respectively (Figures S1A–S1C). The result showed that the proportion of co-localized UBE3A and Kv4.2 immunoreactivity is significantly increased in CA1 stratum radiatum in mice after KA administration (25 mg/kg, intraperitoneally [i.p.], 30 min) compared to controls (Figure 1E), while UBE3A immunoreactivity is not altered (Figure S1C). Co-localization of UBE3A and Kv4.2 with post-synaptic marker Homer1 can be observed in CA1 stratum radiatum (Figure S1D). These results indicate that UBE3A and Kv4.2 binding is driven by neuronal activity, consistent with our previous data (Figures 1A–1C).

UBE3A ubiquitinated its substrates on internal lysine residues as previously reported.18,44,45 Both the Kv4.2 NT and CT contain several intracellular lysine residues. We first examined whether Kv4.2 can be ubiquitinated when expressed in HEK 293T cells. The result showed that Kv4.2 is ubiquitinated as mono-ubiquitination, multi-ubiquitination, and poly-ubiquitination in HEK 293T cells (Figure S2A). We then showed that Kv4.2 ubiquitination is reduced when co-transfected with UBE3A C820A (ligase dead mutant) compared to co-transfected with UBE3A wild type (WT) (Figure 2A), suggesting that UBE3A ubiquitinates Kv4.2 in HEK 293T cells. To determine the specific UBE3A ubiquitination site on Kv4.2, we performed an in vitro ubiquitination assay using UBE3A, E1, UbcH5a, and ubiquitin. FLAG- and Myc-tagged Kv4.2CT, Kv4.2NT, and its mutant protein fragments were purified by anti-FLAG M2 affinity gel (Figures S2B and S2C). In vitro ubiquitination was performed with various negative controls to validate UBE3A ubiquitination of Kv4.2NT (Figure S2D). In addition, ubiquitination of UBE3A was also examined as a positive control for the in vitro ubiquitination (Figure S2E), as UBE3A can be self-ubiquitinated as previously reported.46 UBE3A ubiquitinated Kv4.2NT significantly more than Kv4.2CT (Figure 2B), suggesting that Kv4.2NT is the main target of UBE3A, and consistent with the data that UBE3A binds to Kv4.2NT (Figure 1D). We next examined whether UBE3A ubiquitinates Kv4.2NT at lysine residues. As predicted, the in vitro ubiquitination assay showed that lysineless Kv4.2NT (0K) significantly reduced ubiquitination by UBE3A (Figure 2C), consistent with the notion that UBE3A ubiquitinates lysine residues of a substrate. To identify the UBE3A-specific ubiquitin attachment sites, all three lysine residues (K36, K103, and K138) in Kv4.2NT were individually mutated to alanine (A) via site-directed mutagenesis and inspected using the in vitro ubiquitination assay. Kv4.2NT WT, K36A, and K138A were ubiquitinated by UBE3A similarly (Figure 2D). In contrast, Kv4.2NT K103A showed significantly reduced ubiquitination by UBE3A (Figure 2D). As a lysine-to-alanine mutation not only removes lysine but also its positive charge, it might impact protein structure and ubiquitination. We, therefore, verified the result using lysine to arginine at 103 in Kv4.2NT. Ubiquitination of Kv4.2NT K103R by UBE3A is significantly impaired compared to that of Kv4.2NT WT (Figure S2F), consistent with the effect of Kv4.2NT K103A (Figure 2D). Together, these results demonstrate that UBE3A specifically ubiquitinates the K103 residue of Kv4.2.

Kv4 channels function as tetramers in the plasma membrane.28 Residue K103 is located in the T1 domain of Kv4.2, which is a highly conserved, amino-terminal cytoplasmic domain (~130 amino acids) immediately preceding the first putative transmembrane helix S1 and confers specificity for tetramer formation.47,48 We asked whether ubiquitination on K103 affects Kv4.2 tetramerization. Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) is a distance-dependent process that involves the non-radiative transfer of energy from an electronically excited donor to a nearby acceptor fluorophore and is detected only when donor and acceptor are <10 nm apart.49 We introduced Kv4.2-CFP (cyan fluorescent protein) and Kv4.2-YFP (yellow fluorescent protein) into HEK 293FT cells. FRET was observed as Kv4.2 formed tetramers (Figure 2E). The FRET efficiency was significantly reduced when co-transfected with ubiquitin (Figure 2E), indicating that ubiquitin affects Kv4.2 tetramerization. We next examined FRET of the Kv4.2 K103A mutant. The result showed that ubiquitin expression did not alter FRET of the Kv4.2 K103A mutant (Figure 2E). The stoichiometric expression ratios of Kv4.2-CFP and -YFP fusion proteins in the FRET experiments are shown in Figure S3. These data suggest that ubiquitination on K103 may affect Kv4.2 oligomerization.

A number of studies have shown that UBE3A targets substrates for degradation.18,44,45 We next examined whether the Kv4.2K103A mutation affects its degradation in response to AMPA treatment in cultured hippocampal neurons. Kv4.2K103A mutation was expressed on the cell surface similarly to Kv4.2WT when expressed in HEK 293T cells (Figure S2G). Next, we generated lentivirus expressing Kv4.2 WT or K103A and infected primary hippocampal neurons as described previously.43 To reduce the effect of endogenous Kv4.2, we cultured hippocampal neurons from Kv4.2KO mice. AMPA treatment (25 μM) significantly reduced Kv4.2 protein level in both WT and K103A mutant (Figure 2F). However, the reduction of Kv4.2 protein in the K103A mutant was significantly less pronounced relative to Kv4.2 WT protein level (two-way ANOVA, effect of K103A mutant: F(1, 10) = 9.597, p = 0.0113; Figure 2F), suggesting that Kv4.2 K103 contributes to activity-dependent degradation of Kv4.2. To validate whether AMPA-induced Kv4.2 protein loss is via proteasome degradation pathway, we cultured hippocampal neurons from WT mice and examined the effect of proteasome inhibitor MG132 on AMPA treatment. The result showed that AMPA (25 μM, 30 min) induced Kv4.2 protein loss was blocked by pre-treatment of MG132 (30 μM, 30 min) in cultured hippocampal neurons (Figure 2G). Taken together, the biochemical data presented thus far show that UBE3A binds to internalized Kv4.2 at its NT, ubiquitinates at K103, and mediates Kv4.2 degradation in response to neuronal activity.

To further study the role of Kv4.2 in AS, we examined Kv4.2 protein level and function in UBE3A-deficient mice.10 We utilized the first and most widely used AS mouse model, which was generated by knocking out 3 kb of exon 2 in the Ube3a gene.10 Outside-out somatic patch recordings of Kv4.2-mediated IA density was significantly elevated in AS mice compared to WT littermates (Figure 3A), while other parameters were not significantly altered (Table S2). It has been shown previously that alteration in Kv4.2-mediated IA impacts the firing mode of CA1 pyramidal neurons.34,50,51 Specifically, increases in somatodendritic IA density reduce sub- and supra-threshold membrane excitability, which have been shown to control hippocampal network dynamics.51 Thus, the increased somatic IA in AS may contribute to intrinsic excitability irregularities previously observed in the hippocampus.52 To assess this, we used whole-cell patch-clamp (current-clamp) recordings in identified somata of CA1 pyramidal neurons. From a natural resting potential, injected square currents of increasing magnitude revealed that AS mice displayed reduced input resistance and but no difference in firing frequency (Table S3).

To normalize for any potential differences in resting membrane potential across groups, as observed previously,52 this current injection protocol was repeated in each cell with the addition of a bias current to hold resting membrane potential at –70 mV. Remarkably, this revealed a significant reduction in firing frequency in CA1 pyramidal neurons from AS mice (two-way ANOVA, effect of AS: F(1, 37) = 10.50, p = 0.0025; Figure 3B). The reduction in firing frequency is likely due to an increase in the latency to fire (Figure 3C) and inter-spike intervals (Table S3) observed in AS mouse samples under the –70 mV resting condition. Stronger differences across groups under this condition could be due to the increased availability of Kv4.2 activation at this hyperpolarized membrane potential.20,53 Interestingly, firing frequency was only reduced during the first 250 ms of square current injections as measured by instantaneous firing frequencies. Differences between groups seemingly recovered in the last 250 ms, as no difference in firing frequency or inter-spike interval duration was observed (Table S3). Furthermore, input resistance was significantly reduced in AS mouse samples at subthreshold current injections under the –70 mV condition (Figure 3D). This implies that a subthreshold conductance, such as IA, contributes to reduced intrinsic excitability. Taken together, we reveal a significant reduction in intrinsic excitability in CA1 pyramidal neurons of AS mice, which is amplified when neurons are held at more hyperpolarized conditions, consistent with our observation of increased IA in patches recorded from AS hippocampal neurons (Figure 3A).

Next, we examined Kv4.2 protein level in AS mice. Western blot analysis of proteins from different hippocampal subcellular fractions showed that Kv4.2 levels were significantly higher in synaptosomes (SNS), post-synaptic density (PSD), and non-PSD fractions of AS mice than in those from WT mice (Figure 3E). As KA administration stimulates UBE3A binding to Kv4.2 (Figure 1E), we tested the possibility of UBE3A’s role in KA-induced Kv4.2 protein loss.38 KA administration (25 mg/kg, i.p., 3 h) reduced Kv4.2 protein level in the hippocampus of WT mice but not of AS mice as analyzed by western blot (Figure 3F). In addition, immunostaining experiments showed that KA administration decreased Kv4.2 immunoreactivity in CA1 pyramidal neuron apical dendrites of WT mice but not of AS mice (Figure 3G). As UBE3A ubiquitinates Kv4.2 in vitro (Figures 2B–2D), we examined whether Kv4.2 ubiquitination is altered in AS mouse brain. We enriched ubiquitinated proteins using a ubiquitination detection kit, and the samples were then blotted for Kv4.2 (Figure 3H). Kv4.2 ubiquitination was significantly reduced in AS hippocampi (Figure 3H). Notably, KA administration (25 mg/kg, i.p., 30 min) increased Kv4.2 ubiquitination in WT but not in AS mouse hippocampi (Figure 3H). These data further support the notion that UBE3A mediates activity-dependent Kv4.2 degradation (Figure 2G) and contributes to Kv4.2 distribution at the somatodendritic membrane of CA1 pyramidal neurons.

Our previous studies showed that a p38-Pin1-Kv4.2 pathway regulates the Kv4.2-DPP6 complex.34 We hypothesized that activity-dependent degradation of Kv4.2 by UBE3A occurs on DPP6 containing Kv4.2 complexes. Here, we compared the Kv4.2 phosphorylation capability of p38 in different complexes when reconstituting the Kv4.2 complex in HEK 293T cells. The result showed that expression of DPP6 or KChIP2 decreased Kv4.2 phosphorylation at T602 and T607, but all complexes including Kv4.2 alone, Kv4.2-DPP6, Kv4.2-KChIP2, and Kv4.2-DPP6-KChIP2 are capable of being phosphorylated by p38 (Figure 4A). However, when analyzing various complex compositions, p38 induced significantly more Kv4.2 phosphorylation at T602 and T607 when complexed with DPP6 than Kv4.2 complexed with KChIP2 (Figure 4A). Pin1 isomerase functions on Kv4.2 only when it is phosphorylated at T602 and T607.34 These data suggest that DPP6 containing Kv4.2 complexes are susceptible to Pin1 targeting. We then examined Pin1’s effects on Kv4.2-UBE3A association when Kv4.2 is expressed in different complexes in HEK 293T cells. Our coIP experiments showed that co-expression with an isomerase dead Pin1 mutant (Pin1C113S) decreased UBE3A binding to Kv4.2 in both DPP6- and KChIP2-containing complexes compared to Pin1 WT (Figure 4B). However, Pin1C113S induces a higher degree of reduction of UBE3A-Kv4.2 association when co-expressed with Kv4.2-DPP6 complexes compared to expression with Kv4.2-KChIP2 complexes (Figure 4B), suggesting that Kv4.2 phosphorylation and Pin1 isomerization increases UBE3A binding to Kv4.2. These data are consistent with the phosphorylation data (Figure 4A) and indicate that DPP6-containing Kv4.2 undergoes activity-dependent binding to UBE3A and degradation.

Next, to determine whether Kv4.2 phosphorylation at Pin1 binding sites T602/7 and DPP6 are required for KA-induced Kv4.2 protein loss, we employed mice with a Kv4.2 phosphorylation mutation at the Pin1 binding site (Kv4.2T607A, called ‘‘Kv4.2TA mice’’)34 and DPP6KO mice.54 Western blot analysis revealed that KA administration (25 mg/kg, i.p., 3 h) reduced Kv4.2 protein in the hippocampus of WT mice but not of Kv4.2TA mice (Figure 4C), suggesting that Kv4.2 phosphorylation at the T602/7 Pin1 site is required for KA-induced Kv4.2 protein loss. Similar results were observed in DPP6KO mice (Figure 4D), suggesting that DPP6 is also required for KA-induced Kv4.2 protein loss. Next, we examined Kv4.2 ubiquitination in Kv4.2TA mouse brain. As predicted, Kv4.2 ubiquitination was significantly reduced in Kv4.2TA hippocampi (Figure 4E). Interestingly, KA administration (25 mg/kg, i.p., 30 min) increased Kv4.2 ubiquitination in WT but not in Kv4.2TA mouse hippocampi (Figure 4E). Therefore, it appears that neuronal activity induces Kv4.2 phosphorylation at the Pin1 site on Kv4.2-DPP6 complexes, causes Pin1 isomerization of Kv4.2, dissociation from DPP6, and Kv4.2 internalization. This further leads to Kv4.2 ubiquitination and degradation by UBE3A.

To further study Kv4.2’s role in AS, we created a conditional Kv4.2KO mouse by inserting LoxP sites flanking exon 1 of Kv4.2 using CRISPR-Cas9 techniques (Figure S4A). The floxed mice were identified by PCR followed by sequencing and then mated with Emx1-cre mice to generate Kv4.2 conditional KO (Kv4.2cKO, Emx1-cre/KCND2F/F). Kv4.2cKO mice were born with expected Mendelian ratios, with no differences in mortality rate or weight of heterozygous or homozygous Kv4.2cKO mice compared to WT littermates. Western blot analysis validated the deletion of Kv4.2 protein in hippocampus and cortex but not striatum (Figure S4B), which is consistent with the expression pattern of Emx1.55 Outside-out somatic voltage-clamp recordings of IA in Kv4.2cKO mice show a 70% reduction with little impact on the sustained K+ current, confirming loss of functional Kv4.2 in CA1 pyramidal neurons (Figure S4C).

We used single-cell voltage-clamp recordings in CA1 pyramidal cells and identified that inhibitory synaptic transmission onto CA1 pyramidal neurons is preserved in AS mice; miniature inhibitory post-synaptic current (mIPSC) frequency, amplitude, and kinetics were similar to those of WT littermates (Figures S5A–S5C). In contrast, recordings of miniature excitatory post-synaptic currents (mESPCs) revealed significant increases in mEPSC frequency and amplitude in AS mice relative to WT littermates (Figures 5A–5C). This is consistent with recent observations in CA1 pyramidal neurons.12

While the impact of UBE3A loss on mEPSC amplitude alludes to a post-synaptic locus for synaptic alterations in AS mice, the increase in mEPSC frequency in AS recordings may indicate changes in pre-synaptic release probability in excitatory synapses. To investigate the nature of the synaptic differences observed between the lines (AS vs. WT littermates), we measured evoked EPSCs (eEPSCs) in identified CA1 pyramidal neurons in juvenile (post-natal days 35–45 [P35–P45]) mice. Because Kv4.2 is predominant in filtering synaptic inputs along the apical dendrites in a proximal-distal gradient,20 we evoked release from Schaffer collateral inputs (CA3 → CA1) in stratum radiatum approximately 150 μm from the soma of the patched neuron. No difference in paired-pulse ratio was observed between AS mice and WT littermates (Figure S5D), suggesting that release probability from these synapses is preserved. Together with the mEPSC findings of increased amplitude and frequency (Figures 5A and 5B), the collective physiological evidence suggests that a higher number of functional excitatory synapses are present in AS mice.

Kv4.2-mediated IA magnitude has previously been shown to regulate synaptic maturation state in the hippocampus.56 We, therefore, hypothesized that dysregulation of Kv4.2 in AS may contribute to the synaptic disparities observed in these model mice. To investigate the contribution of Kv4.2 in the observed synaptic phenotypes in AS mice, we bred Kv4.2cKO with AS mice to remove Kv4.2 in excitatory neurons in the AS background to produce AS/Kv4.2cKO double-KO (DKO) mice. Outside-out somatic voltage-clamp recordings of IA validated the deletion of IA in hippocampus in DKO mice (Figure S4D). Removal of Kv4.2 in the AS mice fully rescued the increase in mEPSC frequency, but not amplitude, relative to WT littermates (Figures 5A and 5B). This suggests that Kv4.2 contributes to the increased number of excitatory synapses on CA1 pyramidal neurons of the hippocampus in AS mice.

The IA gradient in the apical dendrites of CA1 pyramidal neurons regulates the amplitude of back-propagating action potentials (bAPs) and filtering of incoming synaptic potentials along their length. This underlies Kv4.2’s role in long-term synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus and learning and memory. Specifically, Kv4.2 and its association with DPP6 is key in regulating spike-timing-dependent long-term potentiation (STD LTP).54 We hypothesized that the dysregulation of Kv4.2-DPP6 complexes, increase in Kv4.2 protein levels, and Kv4.2-induced alterations in excitatory synaptic connectivity in SR of the hippocampus of AS mice may contribute to impairments in plasticity and learning and memory observed in the disease. We first used a theta-burst-pairing LTP (TBP) protocol previously described54,57 to assess the capability of inducing STD LTP in AS mice in identified CA1 pyramidal neurons. While TBP stimulation drove a reliable (10/10 cells potentiated) and robust, ~1.75-fold, long-lasting (30 min post pairing) increase in EPSC amplitude in WT mice, we were unable to generate STD LTP in AS mice using this protocol (Figure 5D). Quantification and analysis of both the time-dependent increase in EPSC amplitude and the overall change in amplitude averaged over the entire period post potentiation revealed a significant reduction in potentiation magnitude in AS mice relative to WT littermates (Figure 5D). To test whether loss of Kv4.2 in the AS background could rescue this deficit, we performed TBP STD LTP recordings in DKO and Kv4.2cKO mice. Knocking out Kv4.2 in AS mice fully rescued STD LTP (Figure 5D), suggesting that Kv4.2 dysregulation is a key contributor in loss of STD LTP in the hippocampus of AS mice. Similar observations were made in Kv4.2cKO mice (Figure 5D). Together, the electrophysiological evidence provided here suggests that the dysregulation of the Kv4.2 complex prominently contributes to the synaptic alterations observed in AS.

A number of behavioral deficits in AS mice have been reported, including learning and memory, locomotor activity, and repetitive behaviors.10,18,58,59 Behavioral tests for mouse models of AS have been developed to assess phenotypes in the domains of motor performance, repetitive behavior, and anxiety, and to test drugs and novel UBE3A mutants.59,60 Here we examined four tests—open-field, rotarod, marble-burying, and nest-building tests—in WT littermates, AS mice, Kv4.2cKO mice, and DKO mice (Figures 6A–6D). In the open-field test, AS mice displayed reduced movement while Kv4.2cKO showed increased movement compared to WT littermates (two-way ANOVA; main effect of AS: F(1, 80) = 9.861, p = 0.0024; main effect of Kv4.2: F(1, 80) = 7.716, p = 0.0068; Figure 6A). The DKO mice corrected the movement impairment in AS mice, but there was no interaction between Kv4.2 and AS (Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 80) = 0.2349, p = 0.6293; Figure 6A). In the rotarod test, AS mice showed a clear deficit compared to WT littermates, but the DKO mice showed behavior similar to that of AS mice (three-way ANOVA; main effect of AS: F(1, 370) = 206.8, p < 0.0001; main effect of Kv4.2: F(1, 370) = 3.298, p = 0.0702; Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 370) = 3.713, p = 0.0548; Figure 6B). In the marble-burying test, AS mice also displayed deficits in marble burying compared to WT littermates, and the DKO mice did not rescue the phenotype (two-way ANOVA; main effect of AS: F(1, 64) = 51.66, p < 0.0001; main effect of Kv4.2: F(1, 64) = 0.08382, p = 0.7731; Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 64) = 0.04579, p = 0.8312; Figure 6C). In the nest-building test, AS mice also lost the ability to build nests compared to WT littermates (Figure 6D). Interestingly, the DKO mice gained some ability to build nests compared to AS mice (three-way ANOVA; main effect of AS: F(1, 315) = 176.7, p < 0.0001; main effect of Kv4.2: F(1, 315) = 2.745, p = 0.0985; Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 315) = 11.87, p = 0.0006; Figure 6D). These data are consistent with AS mouse phenotypes reported previously59,60 and suggest that Kv4.2 might contribute to nest-building impairment in AS mice.

AS mice have shown impairment in learning and memory.10,18,59,61 In light of the physiological deficit and the accompanying biochemical changes, we sought to determine whether the conditional disruption of Kv4.2 might rescue cognitive functions in AS mice. WT littermates, AS mice, Kv4.2cKO mice, and DKO mice were examined in a Morris water maze test. AS mice and DKO mice exhibited swimming deficits in opaque water (only able to keep their noses above the water and so removed from testing) although they were unimpaired in clear water. In the fear-conditioning test, AS and Kv4.2cKO mice showed significantly impaired freezing toward the context but not the cue. However, in context fear conditioning, DKO mice did not show improvement of freezing behavior compared to AS mice (three-way ANOVA; context: main effect of AS: F(1, 720) = 18.78, p < 0.0001; main effect of Kv4.2: F(1, 720) = 8.488, p = 0.0037; Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 720) = 1.544, p = 0.2144; cue: main effect of AS: F(1, 1008) = 0.02065, p = 0.8858; main effect of Kv4.2: F(1, 1008) = 2.796, p = 0.0948; Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 1008) = 0.6582, p = 0.4174; Figures 6E–6G).

Our previous study showed that Kv4.2 plays a role in reversal learning.34 We decided to examine the mice in an operant reversal test (Figure 7A).34 AS mice showed impairment in initial learning while Kv4.2cKO mice exhibited normal initial learning compared to WT littermates (two-way ANOVA; main effect of AS: F(1, 75) = 7.213, p = 0.0089; main effect of Kv4.2: F(1, 75) = 0.1441, p = 0.7053; Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 75) = 0.1441, p = 0.7053; Figure 7B), suggesting that DKO mice did not rescue the initial learning deficits in AS mice. All four groups of mice exhibited similar lever-press activity for reward in the random ratio (RR2) session (three-way ANOVA; main effect of AS: F(1, 370) = 0.9399, p = 0.3329; main effect of Kv4.2: F(1, 370) = 0.6843, p = 0.4087; Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 370) = 2.214, p = 0.1376; Figure 7C), indicating their motivation to get food rewards and ability to press the levers is normal once they have learned. In the reversal learning test, all the mice showed reduced inactive lever (previously active) presses over the first 3 days of reversal learning time, but only AS mice showed elevated inactive lever presses compared to WT littermates (three-way ANOVA; main effect of AS: F(1, 225) = 24.54, p < 0.0001; main effect of Kv4.2: F(1, 225) = 0.9839, p = 0.3223; Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 225) = 0.0006072, p = 0.9804; Figure 7D). In addition, AS mice displayed fewer active lever presses than WT littermates during the first 3 days of reversal learning (Figures 7E–7G), suggesting an impairment of reversal learning in AS mice. Kv4.2cKO showed a trend of reversal learning impairment that is not statistically significant. Interestingly, DKO mice showed a significant improvement on the second and third day of reversal learning compared to AS mice (three-way ANOVA; reversal day 1: Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 375) = 5.733, p = 0.0171; reversal day 2: Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 370) = 22.83, p < 0.0001; reversal day 3: Kv4.2 × AS interaction: F(1, 375) = 14.67, p = 0.0001; Figures 7E–7G). Together, these data show that disruption of the Kv4.2 complex contributes to an enhanced rate of reversal learning compared to AS mice.

AS mice present a complex phenotype, not surprisingly given the multitude of UBE3A targets.62 The present study identifies Kv4.2 complexes as additional targets of UBE3A and reveals their contribution to AS pathology. We describe that a mechanism showing activity-dependent degradation of DPP6-containing Kv4.2 complexes by UBE3A regulates neuronal plasticity and cognitive flexibility. In other words, our study provides a possible mechanism of cognitive inflexibility in AS mice.

In the present study, TAP-MS assay suggested Kv4.2 as an activity-dependent UBE3A substrate (Figures 1A and 1B; Table S1). A proteomic analysis of UBE3A in T-REx 293 cells and SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells showed that UBE3A exists in a high-molecular-weight complex, HUN.63 We did not find other components of the HUN complex in our TAP-MS assay (Table S1), perhaps due to complex variations in different cell types. Our follow-up studies confirmed Kv4.2 and UBE3A association (Figures 1C and 1D) and that Kv4.2 K103 ubiquitination by UBE3A mediates activity-dependent degradation of Kv4.2. An in vitro ubiquitination assay of Kv4.2 NT showed only mono- and multi-ubiquitination of Kv4.2 but not poly-ubiquitination. However, we showed that negative controls (no E1, no E2, no E3, or no ATP) did not generate mono- and multi-ubiquitination of Kv4.2, indicating that Kv4.2 NT is mono- and multi-ubiquitinated by UBE3A in vitro. In HEK 293T cells, Kv4.2 mono- and multi-ubiquitination can also be detected along with poly-ubiquitination (Figures 2A and S2A). A similar result was observed in an in vivo ubiquitination assay in the mouse hippocampus (Figures 3H and 5E). These data suggest that besides poly-ubiquitination, mono- and multi-ubiquitination of Kv4.2 are typical forms of Kv4.2 ubiquitination. One possible reason why poly-ubiquitination of Kv4.2 in the in vitro ubiquitination assay was not observed might be that the UBE3A substrates used in the in vitro ubiquitination assay were Kv4.2 fragments, which likely differ in structure compared with the whole protein.

Our cell biological and biochemical analysis showed that Kv4.2 abundance in the somatodendritic domain is increased in AS mice. Electrophysiological and western blot analysis revealed significant increases in protein levels and loss of KA-induced degradation (Figures 3E–3G). The human/mouse brain is continuously active, even when not engaged in learning and memory or other activities. Kv4.2 protein levels were increased in the synaptoneurosomal fraction of AS mice without stimulation of neuronal activity (Figure 3E), suggesting that this spontaneous brain activity also regulates Kv4.2 protein level in a UBE3A-dependent manner.

Consistent with previous observations,12 we also identified an enhancement in excitatory transmission onto CA1 pyramidal neurons within the hippocampus of AS mice (Figures 5A and 5B). In the absence of alterations in inhibitory transmission (Figures S5A and S5B), this suggests an imbalance in excitatory/inhibitory transmission, tilting the equilibrium toward enhanced excitatory synaptic drive within the hippocampal network. We show that Kv4.2 indeed plays a prominent role in setting this imbalance in AS, as AS/Kv4.2cKO DKO mice exhibit a complete rescue of mEPSC frequency (Figure 5B), similar to that found in Ephexin5 KO mice.12 In that study, removing Ephexin5 from AS mice specifically rescued some hippocampal-dependent behaviors. There might be some interplay between Ephexin5 and Kv4.2 in terms of Rho signaling that has been shown to mediate p38-MAPK activity.64 Reduced dendritic intrinsic excitability is also likely in the AS mice. LTP expression induced with a theta-burst-pairing of synaptic inputs and bAPs, a protocol producing substantial membrane depolarization in CA1 pyramidal neuron apical dendrites,54 is abolished in AS mice. We provide evidence that this reduction in STD LTP magnitude is due to an increase in the abundance of Kv4.2 in this domain; its removal in the AS background fully rescues loss of LTP (Figure 5D). Since we observe reliable initiation of APs at the soma in both AS and WT littermates during STP LTP induction, it is likely that the increase in Kv4.2-mediated conductance opposes the depolarization produced by converging bAPs and synaptic potentials within the dendrites, thereby increasing the threshold required for STD LTP expression in AS mice.

The cognitive deficits in AS mice are expected to result from loss of UBE3A in cortical and hippocampal neurons. We expect that Kv4.2cKO might alter learning and memory behaviors in AS mice, as Emx1-cre is highly expressed in excitatory neurons in cortex and hippocampus.55,65 Our previous studies showed that Kv4.2 plays a role in cognitive flexibility,34 the ability to appropriately adjust one’s behavior according to a changing environment. We, therefore, determined whether AS mice showed deficits in reversal learning. Interestingly, AS mice displayed both impaired initial learning and reversal learning (Figure 7). This is consistent with a previous report that showed reversal learning deficits in AS mice when tested in a Morris water maze.61 Only the reversal learning can be rescued by Kv4.2cKO, adding support to the role of Kv4.2 in regulating cognitive flexibility.34 Thus, there is accumulating evidence suggesting that Kv4.2 is involved in this form of learning. It is plausible that activity-dependent turnover of the Kv4.2 complex is a key contributor to a type of plasticity underlying cognitive flexibility.

Taken together, our findings define a mechanism of activity-dependent Kv4.2 degradation that is implicated in AS and provide a foundation to understand UBE3A downstream signaling pathways underlying neuronal excitability and plasticity. Various strategies to reduce Kv4.2 expression or function may present possible therapeutic targets for AS treatment.

Our study provides novel insights into the mechanism of activity-dependent degradation of DPP6-containing Kv4.2 complexes by UBE3A, which regulates neuronal plasticity and cognitive flexibility. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we utilized AMPA treatment in culture or KA administration in mouse to induce neuronal activity. Whether the learning and memory process triggers Kv4.2 degradation by UBE3A has not been tested in this work. Therefore, future studies would be ideal to further investigate Kv4.2 ubiquitination and protein level in WT and AS mice after learning tasks.

Second, we utilize an Emx1-cre-mediated conditional KO of Kv4.2 for the electrophysiological and behavioral rescues. Our data suggested that reducing A-type potassium channels by conditional KO Kv4.2 can improve STD LTP and reversal learning deficits in AS mice. However, we have not tested whether reducing the overabundance of Kv4.2 in AS mice would be sufficient to rescue the phenotypes. It will be interesting to examine whether heterozygous Kv4.2KO mice (returning levels back to near WT) can rescue AS phenotypes. Finally, a limitation of the current study is the use of only male mice for electrophysiological and behavioral experiments. As sex differences in cognitive flexibility were observed, future studies should include female subjects to assess the rescue behaviors.

All data were recorded with a Multiclamp 700b amplifier (Molecular Devices) and a Digidata 1440A digitizer. Signals were low pass filtered at 5 kHz and digitized at 10 kHz using Clampex 10.7 software and were acquired in bridge balance mode to compensate series resistance. Liquid junction potential was not corrected for. Passive membrane properties were measured after initial break-in to avoid alteration in equilibrium potential and dialysis due to solution exchange. Whole-cell capacitance and series resistance were measured from Multiclamp 700B commander. A voltage step of –10 mV was initiated, and the decay tau of the whole-cell capacitive transient was used to calculate these parameters. Resting membrane potential was measured after switching to I = 0 in current-clamp configuration. Recordings where resting membrane potential was more depolarized than –50 mV were omitted from analysis. Action potentials were evoked in current-clamp mode in patched CA1 pyramidal neurons under two recording conditions: resting membrane potential and ‘‘hyperpolarized’’ membrane potential (see below). Series resistance was measured upon initial break-in and cells were discarded if resistance was identified as >20 MU.

For the resting condition, following the recording of passive properties, square 500 ms current pulses were elicited in 50 pA steps with current injections ranging from –200 pA to +300 pA. This train of sweeps was repeated three times for each cell and the elicited responses were averaged at each current injection magnitude. This protocol was immediately repeated in each cell under the ‘‘hyperpolarized’’ condition to control for any differences in resting membrane potential. For this condition, an additional current injection was manually added in current-clamp to bring the cell’s resting membrane potential to –70 mV. This additional injected current varied across cells and subtracted from the injected current applied during the repeated protocol. To normalize for variability in the amount of current needed to bring each cell to –70 mV, all measures are reported relative to rheobase.

Rheobase was measured as the minimum amount of injected current that drove action potentials in a given cell. For the purposes of normalizing measures obtained through the current step protocol (described above), the same protocol was used to estimate rheobase. The minimum current step (50 pA increments) to drive action potentials was considered rheobase for a given cell. For more precise measurements of rheobase and action potential threshold across cells, an additional ramp protocol was utilized immediately following the current step protocol (under both resting and hyperpolarized conditions). Ramp injections consisted of three 1 s sweeps, which consisted of a gradual increase in injected current from 0 to 400 pA. The minimum amount of injected current to drive an action potential, and the membrane potential at that point in the ramp, were considered the rheobase and action potential threshold, respectively.

Following establishment of whole-cell configuration, A-current was recorded in voltage clamp mode from outside-out patches pulled from somata of identified CA1 pyramidal cells in hippocampal slices. Outside-out patch pipettes (3–6 MU) were filled with K+ Gluconate-based intracellular solution consisting of the following (in mM): 20 KCl, 125 K-Gluconate, 1 EGTA, 4 NaCl, 4 Na2ATP, 0.3NaGTP, 10 HEPES, 10 Phosphocreatine. All recordings were performed at room temperature (~25°C). Tetrodotoxin citrate (TTX) (500 nM), Gabazine (4 μM), and CNQX (2 μM) were added to the extracellular bath (ACSF) in order to block voltage-gated Na+ channels, and GABAA and AMPA receptors, respectively. A-current density was calculated using a standard subtraction protocol. A pre-pulse step to –120 mV for 600 ms from a holding potential of –65 mV was initiated to relieve inactivation of A-type K+ channels. Voltage was then stepped to +40 mV for 500 ms to measure total outward K+ current. A subsequent sweep consisted of a pre-pulse step to –30 mV to inactivate the transient A-current followed by a step to +40 mV to measure the non-inactivating, sustained current. Peak amplitude of the sustained current was subtracted from total outward K+ current amplitude offline to isolate transient A-current. Leakage and capacitive currents were digitally subtracted. Peak amplitude was normalized to current density by dividing by patch capacitance, which was measured following formation of the outside-out patch configuration and was analyzed with pClamp Clampfit (Molecular Devices) software. Inactivation kinetics were measured by fitting a single exponential to each isolated IA current trace. Traces are averages of 10 sweeps. Recovery from inactivation was calculated using a double voltage-step (–120 to +30 mV) protocol with increasing inter-step intervals ranging from (in ms): 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 50, 100, 200, 500, and 1000. Recovery from inactivation was calculated as the ratio of the peak amplitude of the second current trace relative to the point where the initial A-current was fully inactivated and was normalized to the peak amplitude of the initial A-current. Single exponentials were fitted to the non-linear fitted curves of normalized recovery from inactivation and the taus from each individual recovery curve were averaged and compared. Voltage-dependent activation was recorded using a voltage step protocol consisting of a 600 ms pre-step to –120 mV, followed by a series of 12 steps (10 mV each from –80 to +30 mV). Leakage and capacitive currents were digitally subtracted. The same protocol but with a 600ms pre-step from –65 mV to-30 mV was used to record non-inactivating currents, which were offline subtracted from the overall K+ currents to obtain inactivating A-type K+ currents. Currents were then converted into conductances, normalized to peak conductance at +40 mV, plotted against the holding voltage and fitted with a Boltzman function to obtain V1/2-activation and kactivation. Voltage-dependent inactivation was recorded using a voltage-step protocol consisting of a series of 14 pre-steps (each 600 ms and 10 mV, from –120 to +10 mV) followed by an activating voltage step to +20 mV. Leakage and capacitive currents were digitally subtracted. Peak currents were measured for each step and the I/V relationship was fitted with a Boltzmann function to obtain V1/2-inactivation and kinactivation. Data were recorded with a Multiclamp 700b amplifier (Molecular Devices) and a Digidata 1440A digitizer, were digitized at 10 kHz and low-pass filtered at 2 kHz using a Bessel filter. Series resistance was measured upon initial break-in and cells were discarded if resistance was identified as >20 MU.

Miniature excitatory synaptic currents (mEPSCs) and miniature inhibitory synaptic currents (mIPSCs) were recorded from identified pyramidal neurons in area CA1 of hippocampal slices. In order to assess the ratio of excitatory and inhibitory drive onto individual neurons within the hippocampus, mEPSCs and mIPSCs were recorded in each patched neuron in whole-cell voltage clamp mode in the presence of TTX (500 nM). After break-in, excitatory events were recorded after a 2-min waiting period to ensure patch stability. mEPSCs were then recorded in 2-min epochs at a holding potential of –70 mV. Following the 2-min epoch, CNQX (2 μM) and APV (50 μM) were washed onto the slice to block glutamatergic ligand-gated channels and isolate mIPSCs. When inward synaptic currents disappeared, the holding potential was changed to +20 mV in order to measure mIPSCs, which were recorded in 2-min epochs. In select recordings, Gabazine (4 μM) was washed onto the slice to verify the outward currents measured were GABAergic inhibitory events. Event amplitudes and kinetics were measured by the ‘‘Template Search’’ event detection procedure in Clampfit. Decay times were measured as time of decay to from 80% to 20% of peak for all events. Data were recorded with a Multiclamp 700b amplifier (Molecular Devices) and a Digidata 1440A digitizer, were digitized at 10 kHz and low pass filtered at 2 kHz using a Bessel filter. Series resistance was measured upon initial break-in and continuously monitored cells were discarded if resistance was identified as >20 MU.

Evoked excitatory synaptic currents (eEPSCs) were recorded in area CA1 from patched pyramidal neurons in voltage clamp mode from a holding potential of –70 mV. Synaptic currents were evoked with a monopolar glass stimulating electrode filled with external ACSF and connected to a A385 Stimulus Isolator (World Precision Instruments). The stimulating electrode (Warner instruments) was placed ~150 μm from the pyramidal cell layer into stratum radiatum to activate Schaeffer collateral axons in the presence of 4 μM Gabazine. The stimulating electrode was positioned ~50 μm lateral to the apical dendrite of the patched neurons. To measure excitatory synaptic strength an input/output curve was generated with increasing magnitudes of stimulation strength. After a 2-min baseline stimulation, stimulation strengths were adjusted ranging from (in milliamps; mA): 0.5, 5, 10, 50 and 100 recordings were repeated three times and averaged. EPSCs were normalized to average EPSC amplitude measured with the minimum 0.5 mA stimulation strength to minimize cell to cell and/or slice to slice variation in initial EPSC amplitude.

Paired-pulse facilitation in CA1 pyramidal neurons was measured by stimulation with a bipolar glass stimulation electrode, placed in the stratum radiatum approximately 150 μm from the pyramidal cell layer and 50 μm lateral from the apical dendrite of the patched neuron. 4mM Gabazine was included in the ACSF during the recordings. eEPSCs were recorded in voltage clamp mode and a double stimulation was given, with varying inter-stimulus intervals (ISIs) ranging from (in milliseconds): 50, 100, 200, 400, 600. Each set of ISI was repeated three times and the average eEPSC peaks measured after baseline-subtraction. The facilitation index (i.e., pairedpulse ratio) was calculated as the average eEPSC elicited by the second stimulus, divided by the average eEPSC elicited by the first stimulus. Series resistance for evoked EPSC recordings was measured upon initial break-in and cells were discarded if resistance was identified as >20 MU. Additionally, every 10 s a hyperpolarizing current pulse (25 pA, 50 ms) was injected into the cell to continue to monitor series resistance throughout the recording.

In order to measure long-term potentiation in patched CA1 pyramidal neurons, a theta-burst pairing protocol was used.57 A monopolar glass electrode was placed in stratum radiatum (~150 μm from pyramidal cell layer) and ~50 μm lateral to the pyramidal cell that was patched (from the apical dendrite) to stimulate Schaffer collateral fibers. Recordings were performed in the presence of Gabazine (4 mM) to block GABAA receptors. Before synaptic plasticity induction, baseline EPSCs were recorded for 5 min in voltage clamp mode at a holding potential of –65 mV with stimulation frequency set to 0.1 Hz. The last 10 stimulations of a 5-min baseline were plotted and used as baseline to ensure stabilization. To induce LTP, presynaptic induced EPSPs and post-synaptic action potentials were paired at a 40 ms time window in current clamp mode. Somatic depolarizing current injection (2 nA, 2 ms) was used to elicit action potentials. Specifically, the theta-burst protocol consisted of 10 bursts of 5 EPSPs elicited at 100 Hz followed by 2 action potentials at 50 Hz (Δ40 ms). The bursts were elicited at 5 Hz to constitute a theta-burst train. The trains were repeated 5 times at 0.2 Hz. Following LTP induction, EPSCs were recorded in voltage clamp mode at a holding potential of –65 mV with stimulations occurring at 0.1 Hz, as in baseline recordings. Additionally, every 10 s a hyperpolarizing current pulse (25 pA, 50 ms) was injected into the cell to monitor series resistance. Cells were discarded is series resistance changed by > 25% during the recording. Amplitudes of EPSCs were measured every third stimulation prior to LTP induction and every ~30 s following LTP induction (total ~30 min post-potentiation). The magnitude of LTP was quantified by averaging EPSC amplitudes over 30 min post-induction and normalizing to baseline responses.

All behavioral experiments were performed during the light period of the light/dark cycle. Aged-matched (3–8 months old) adult male littermate mice were used for all the experiments. All behavioral testing and scoring were performed by experimenters who were blind to genotype.

Locomotor activity was assessed in a novel open-field square arena (50 3 50 cm) constructed of white Plexiglas as previously described.34 Mice were acclimated to the testing room for at least ~10 min and then placed in the arena and left to explore freely for 30 min. The total distance moved by each mouse in the open arena was recorded, and the final outcome is indicated as distance moved in meters. Results were analyzed with ‘‘ANY-maze’’ software (ANY-maze, Wood Dale, IL, USA).

Motor function was tested using the accelerating rotarod (4–40 rpm, in 5 min). Mice were given two trials per day with a 45–60-min inter-trial interval for 5 consecutive days (same hour every day). For each day, the average time spent on the rotarod was calculated, or the time until the mouse made three consecutive wrapping/passive rotations on the rotarod (latency in seconds). Maximum duration of a trial was 5 min.

Open Makrolon (polycarbonate) cages (40 3 22 3 18 cm) were filled with 4 cm of fresh bedding material. On top of the bedding material, 15 blue glass marbles were arranged in an equidistant 5 3 3 grid and the animals were given access to the marbles for 20 min. After the test, the mice were gently removed from the cage. Marbles covered for more than 75% by bedding were scored as buried, and the outcome measured is the number of buried marbles.

To measure nest building, mice were single housed for a period of 5–7 days before the start of the experiment. Subsequently, used nesting material was replaced and 9 pieces of compressed extra-thick blot filter paper (Bio-Rad) were added to the cage. The number of totally unused nest material (filter paper) was counted and noted every day for a consecutive of 5 days, each day at the same hour.

A 3-day fear-conditioning protocol was employed to test contextual fear memory and cue fear memory. On day 1, the mice were placed in clear-walled enclosures that were located in a sound-attenuating chamber that contained a FireWire camera, light, and speaker. On day 1, the enclosure was outfitted as context A which consisted of a steel grid floor and unlabeled walls. The enclosure was cleaned with 70% ethanol between animals. Mice were acclimated for 120 s in the fear-conditioning chamber, and then a tone (30 s, 2 kHz, 85 dB) was played and co-terminated with a mild shock (2 s, 0.6 mA). Mice were trained by the tone/shock for three times with 1 min interval. Mice were removed from the chamber 30 s after the last tone/shock. On day 2, contextual fear memory was assayed by placing the mice back in context A for 300 s. On day 3, the mice were brought to the testing room that was now dimly illuminated with red light. The mice were placed in context B, which consisted of an enclosure with a flat floor and 3 walls with gray-colored or black and white stripes. The enclosure was cleaned between animals with Vimoba. Mice were acclimated for 120 s in the fear-conditioning chamber, and then a tone (30 s, 2 kHz, 85 dB) was played. Mice were in the enclosure with four rounds of the tone in 1 min interval. Percent time spent freezing (defined as the absence of all movement except for respiration) was measured throughout these experiments by FreezeFram4 and served as an index of fear memory.

Operant reversal learning was performed as previously described.34 Mice were food restricted to 85%–90% of their free-feeding weight over several days prior to testing and throughout the experiment. Mice were trained to lever press on an FR1 schedule in daily sessions with an endpoint of 30 rewards or 30 min. When they collected all of the rewards in the allotted time for 3 consecutive days, animals progressed to RR2 sessions lasting 25 min, during which they could earn unlimited rewards. After 5 training days, levers were reversed such that the reinforced lever became non-reinforced and vice versa. Mice were given daily 15-min reversal learning sessions for 5 days.

Biochemistry and behavioral data were analyzed by Origin 2018b with two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test and GraphPad Prism 9.5 with One-Way ANOVA, two-Way ANOVA and Three-Way ANOVA where appropriate. Significant main effects or interactions were followed up with multiple comparison testing with the use of Holm–Sidak or Tukey. Results were considered significant when p < 0.05. Electrophysiological data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 7 (7.0d). Prior to performing inferential statistics, all datasets were tested for normal distribution and the presence of statistical outliers. Outliers were identified, and removed, using ROUT method (Q = 1%). The use of parametric or non-parametric analysis was determined after testing for normal distribution in the data using the D’Agostino & Pearson normality test (alpha level = 0.05). Non-parametric statistics were used if the data failed normality testing. For all voltage-clamp electrophysiological analysis of IA, two-tailed, unpaired t-tests with Welch’s correction were used to assess parameters between WT and AS. For analysis of firing properties, Mann-Whitney u-tests were used to compare group means unless the assumptions of parametric two-tailed t-tests were met (normal distribution, equal variance). To normalize for differences in current injection magnitudes across samples (due to variance in the bias current injected to reach –70 mV for hyperpolarized recordings), all measures obtained from the square current injection protocol were reported for current injection magnitudes that were equivalent to 2x rheobase of a given sample. For analyses of synaptic transmission, all parameters were initially assessed between WT and AS using two-tailed, unpaired t test with Welch’s correction. For assessment of synaptic parameters between multiple groups (n > 2 groups), an Ordinary One-Way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons was used for data that passed normality testing. A Kruskal-Wallis Test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons was used for data that failed normality testing. A two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc for multiple comparisons was used to analyze spike-timing-dependent long-term potentiation over time with additional analysis via One-Way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons. All the data are presented as mean ± SEM. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ns, nonsignificant (p > 0.05).
