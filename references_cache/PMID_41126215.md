# Beckwith-Wiedemann spectrum (BWSp): an update on diagnosis, management, and follow-up from the scientific committee of the Italian BWSp association.
**Authors:** Russo S, Milani D, Meossi C, Marcucci L, Pajno R, Butti N, Cocchi G, Tannorella P, Bertoletti M, Carli D, Meazzini MC, Tortora C, Ferrari M, Zampino G, Massuras S, Ferrero GB, Quarello P, Rossetti G, Montirosso R, De Pellegrin M, Riccio A, Mussa A
**Journal:** Ital J Pediatr (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13052-025-02131-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13052-025-02131-3)

## Content

Beckwith-Wiedemann spectrum (BWSp) is a congenital imprinting disorder characterized by overgrowth, cancer predisposition, and diverse clinical manifestations, resulting from epigenetic and genetic alterations at chromosome 11p15.5. BWSp represents the most common imprinting disorder, with a prevalence exceeding 1:10,000. The disorder is primarily associated with loss or gain of methylation at imprinting control regions IC2 and IC1, paternal uniparental disomy of 11p15, or pathogenic variants in CDKN1C. Advances in molecular diagnostics have refined genotype-phenotype correlations, improving both clinical management and tumor screening protocols. This review, produced by the Scientific Committee of the Italian BWSp Association (AIBWS), builds upon the 2018 international consensus, incorporating updated scientific evidence up to 2024. The committee critically assessed post-2017 literature using PRISMA and Delphi methodologies to revise ten key topics, including diagnosis and criteria, prenatal testing, molecular testing strategies, tumor surveillance, macroglossia surgery, growth monitoring, limb-length discrepancy, cognitive and psychosocial outcomes, and MLID (multi-locus imprinting disturbances). A major focus is optimizing diagnosis in cases with negative methylation tests on DNA from blood, where somatic mosaicism often necessitates alternative tissue testing. The review emphasizes prenatal diagnosis challenges, recommends including ART-related pregnancies in diagnostic criteria, and proposes a prenatal scoring system. Updated tumor surveillance strategies are presented, including universal α-fetoprotein screening for hepatoblastoma up to 3 years and genotype-based protocols for Wilms tumor. CDKN1C-related neuroblastoma surveillance is also addressed. MLID, often co-occurring with IC2-LoM, is discussed regarding clinical relevance, testing strategies, and implications for recurrence risk, particularly involving maternal-effect gene variants. Orthopedic and surgical management of limb-length discrepancy (LLD) and macroglossia is reviewed, alongside growth chart development and their role in personalized interventions. New findings on cognitive, behavioral, and psychosocial aspects highlight the need for routine screening and supportive care. The transition to adult care remains underexplored, though recommendations include attention to residual pediatric complications, fertility, and potential long-term risks. This review reinforces the importance of a multidisciplinary and personalized approach to BWSp across the lifespan, calling for further research to refine diagnostics, long-term outcomes, and transition models.

The Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (BWS, OMIM #130650) is a rare congenital overgrowth disorder characterized by complex pathogenesis due to alterations in the imprinted gene cluster on chromosome 11p15.5. It presents with a wide range of clinical features, including macroglossia, lateralized overgrowth, abdominal wall defects, and an increased risk of embryonal tumors. First described in 1963 by Beckwith and Wiedemann, the syndrome's complexity stems from its heterogeneous presentation and the variety of molecular mechanisms involved [1–4]. The condition has been renamed as a spectrum (BWSp) due to its considerable clinical variability. BWSp is the most common overgrowth syndrome, cancer predisposition syndrome, and imprinting disorder, with a prevalence of over 1 in 10,000 individuals [5]. The molecular basis of this variability continues to be an active area of research [6].

BWS serves as a model for understanding genomic imprinting disorders and their effects on growth regulation and cancer susceptibility. The disorder is primarily associated with dysregulation of genomic imprinting at the 11p15.5 chromosomal region, which contains a cluster of imprinted genes essential for normal growth and development. Key imprinted genes include IGF2 (insulin-like growth factor 2), H19, CDKN1C (cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 C), and KCNQ1OT1 (potassium voltage-gated channel subfamily Q member 1 Opposite Strand/Antisense Transcript 1). Imprinting of these genes is regulated during gametogenesis, with differential methylation patterns leading to monoallelic expression from either the maternal or paternal allele. In BWSp, common molecular alterations are: gain of DNA methylation (GoM) at the H19/IGF2:IG-DMR, also known as IC1, which regulates the expression of IGF2, loss of methylation (LoM) at the KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR, also known as IC2, which regulates the expression of CDKN1C or paternal segmental uniparental disomy of 11p15.5 (UPD(11)pat) [7]. Additionally, 11p15.5 copy-number variants (CNVs) or pathogenic variants in CDKN1C (cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor type 1 C) have been implicated in a minority of cases.

Management of BWSp has progressed significantly, with advancements in molecular diagnostics enhancing the understanding of genotype–phenotype relationships. International guidelines have been developed to optimize diagnosis, clinical surveillance, and therapeutic interventions, aiming to improve the quality of life and patients’ outcomes [6].

Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying BWSp provides valuable insights into its clinical management and personalized follow-up strategies. This review explores the complexities of BWSp, emphasizing recent discoveries and their implications for future research and clinical practice. In 2018, a pivotal consensus was published to standardize the diagnosis and management of BWSp, providing clear diagnostic criteria and specific genotype-based cancer screening strategies, which significantly transformed clinical approaches [6]. Since then, advances in diagnostic techniques and personalized management strategies have further improved follow-up care for patients with BWSp. This Scientific Committee of the Italian BWSp Association (AIBWS, www.aibws.org) endorsed the recommendations given in 2018 [6], which remain firmly established. This review offers an update summarizing key scientific developments from 2018 to the present and contextualizing recommendations within the framework of the Italian and European healthcare systems.

The methodology for this review is grounded in the framework established by the same group in 2016 [7] and by the 2018 consensus [6], with many of the authors actively involved in both works. This review, produced by the Scientific Committee of AIBWS, aims to build upon rather than replace the previous consensus, incorporating new scientific knowledge. As such, we revised critically new publications from 2017 onward, with the goal of updating the current state of the art in the field. To gather relevant literature, four of the authors (SM, PR, AM, LM) used the search string "Beckwith-Wiedemann" on PubMed, screening all articles published from 2017 onward and selecting them according to the PRISMA method [8] (Fig. 1). The articles selected for review were categorized by topic of interest and, based on this selection, a first meeting of the Scientific Committee decided identifiying a working group for each topic (Supplementary Table 1). Each working group then selected the most relevant articles within their area of focus, downloaded, read, and independently summarized them. This allowed the groups to identify new thematic points that were either not mentioned in the 2018 consensus or represented innovations in the field. Following this initial review, the members of the different working groups convened to discuss, using the Delphi method [9], which findings should be included in this review article for each specific topic. After reaching a consensus, each group produced a set of recommendations highlighting the scientific advances in their area of expertise. The guidelines for clinical diagnosis and molecular testing for BWSp, based on cardinal and supportive criteria established in the 2018 consensus statement, have been pivotal in reducing diagnostic variability among clinicians by providing objective elements for diagnosing this heterogeneous disorder. The BWSp scoring system, developed from the 2018 international consensus, has proven effective in identifying patients for molecular testing, with high sensitivity (85%) but lower specificity (43%), meaning it reliably identifies most affected individuals but may lead to false positives [10–13]. These results validate the clinical utility of the criteria while emphasizing areas for further optimization: the criteria capture a wide spectrum of phenotypes, yet variability in clinical presentations across ethnic groups poses challenges suggesting genotype–phenotype variability influenced by ethnicity [11]. Expanding molecular testing to diverse tissues and recognizing ethnic-specific patterns can enhance diagnostic accuracy and management strategies [10–14]. Although effective for diagnosis, the scoring system does not predict cancer risk, underscoring the necessity for subgroup-specific tumor screening protocols [12].

Following the established criteria, approximately 20% of patients remain unsolved, highlighting the need to deepen our understanding of differential diagnoses [10, 15]. These efforts are crucial to guide clinicians toward considering other genetic conditions with overlapping and distinct features. The main challenge lies in recognizing ultra-rare or as-yet unclassified overgrowth conditions that require differentiation from BWS. We therefore suggest performing additional tests (e.g. Exome/Genome Sequencing, Chromosomal Micro-Array) in cases meeting BWSp criteria and with negative methylation tests and CDKN1C sequencing on blood-extracted DNA: in partiular, we suggest considering a wide range of differential diagnoses, in particular in patients with developmental delays without a history of hypoxia or hypoglycemia, and with normal brain imaging. Similarly, molecular investigations in BWSp cases with negative methylation tests on blood-extracted DNA must be particularly thorough, especially when lateralized overgrowth (LO) is present. Extending molecular studies to somatic DNA extracted from other tissue or from the affected regions significantly improves diagnostic yield, potentially enabling to either confirm the diagnosis or allow the identification of conditions with phenotypic overlap but vastly different management implications, as PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum (PROS), mosaic RASopathies, or vascular overgrowth syndromes [16, 17]. Tailored molecular workflows not only refine tumor screening protocols but also pave the way for treatment in other overgrowth disorders [14].

Moreover, even in cases with confirmed molecular diagnoses of BWSp, atypical features may occur potentially in the framework of “blended” (mixed phenotypes with overlapping features) or “composite” (distinct phenotypes that individually explain the patient’s characteristics) conditions. The frequency of dual diagnoses in the literature is estimated to range between 2% and 7.5% of syndromic patients [18, 19].

ART has been associated with an increased prevalence of BWSp, accounting for up to 15% of diagnoses [10, 20–22]. The reasons for this association remain debated [14, 23–25]. Several studies reported an increased incidence of twinning in BWSp, suggesting a potential link with abnormal early embryonic development [26]. However, it is unclear whether this is related to or independent of the mechanisms connecting BWSp to assisted ART.

In 2020, Carli et al. proposed a prenatal scoring system that included ART-induced pregnancy and monozygotic twinning as a supportive criterion for molecular investigations [20]. Building on this framework, we recommend formal integration of ART-related pregnancy and monozygotic twinning into the 2018 international consensus criteria, assigning each 1 supportive point (Table 1). This addition recognizes the increased association of ART and monozygotic twinning with BWSp and ensures these cases are adequately considered for molecular testing. The prenatal phenotype of BWSp includes several features [27–30]. While more than 80% of clinically suspected patients have a molecular defect postnatally [6, 31], the prenatal diagnostic yield is significantly lower (approximately 25%) and depends heavily on the number and type of features observed [28]. Many of these features become evident on ultrasound only between the second and third trimester of gestation. Tissue mosaicism is a major challenge for molecular testing, and the debate about the most suitable tissue and timing for testing remains unresolved. Interestingly, a recent study reported that the average degree of methylation alteration at 11p15 is significantly more pronounced in prenatally diagnosed cases than in postnatally diagnosed patients, suggesting that more severely affected cases are preferentially detected during pregnancy, whereas milder phenotypes may remain unrecognized until later in life [28]

The molecular defects identified prenatally also differ from those seen postnatally. IC2-LoM (~ 70%) and CDKN1C pathogenic variants (~ 13%) are more common among prenatally diagnosed cases due to their association with omphalocele, a frequent indication for prenatal testing [32, 33]. Conversely, UPD(11)pat (12.5%) and IC1-GoM (4.2%) are less prevalent as they are associated with nonspecific features like generalized or lateralized overgrowth, which are harder to detect prenatally [7]. Most prenatal presentations reported in the literature include isolated omphalocele, placentomegaly, placental mesenchymal dysplasia (PMD), or late-pregnancy macroglossia [34].

In familial cases where mothers carry pathogenic variants in CDKN1C, chromosomal microdeletions/duplications, or balanced translocations involving chromosome 11, the recurrence risk for BWSp is 50%. Unlike mosaic cases, these germline anomalies are not tissue-specific and do not impose timing or sampling restrictions for molecular testing, ensuring straightforward diagnostic opportunities [35].

In addition to familial cases, some prenatal features are highly suggestive of BWSp and can serve as stand-alone criteria for molecular testing. These include isolated omphalocele, PMD, and macroglossia [36]. Omphalocele, particularly when isolated, is the most common antenatal anomaly in BWSp and represents a reliable ultrasound finding. Studies estimate that 17–25% of fetuses with isolated omphalocele have BWSp [37, 38]. Over 70% of these cases show IC2-LoM, while ~ 12.5% have CDKN1C pathogenic variants [28, 32]. Similarly, PMD and macroglossia are highly specific findings and should be considered major indications for prenatal testing [34]. PMD is a rare vascular placental anomaly characterized by a normal biparental trophoblast and abnormal mesenchymal growth caused by the overexpression of paternal genes in the stromal component [36]. PMD is uncommon in the general population (0.02% of pregnancies), yet it is observed in around 20% of cases linked to BWSp [34]. The molecular findings associated with PMD include genome-wide UPD, UPD(11)pat, and IC2-LoM, highlighting the importance of considering this anomaly as a key indicator for BWSp molecular testing [39]. PMD is often detected early in pregnancy through ultrasound and should prompt timely and comprehensive molecular investigation. Macroglossia, another significant prenatal finding, is highly suggestive of BWSp but develops later, in the second trimester, and it often worsens as pregnancy progresses [40]. Its specificity warrants its inclusion as a major criterion for prenatal testing [20] but its delayed presentation can make its prenatal assessment challenging [39]. Other anomalies, while less specific or less prevalent, can serve as supportive or suggestive indicators of BWSp when combined with major findings. These include visceromegaly, polyhydramnios, placentomegaly, nephroureteral anomalies, and elevated pregnancy biomarkers (e.g., PAPP-A, free β-hCG at 10–13 weeks, α-FP, uE3, and total hCG at 15–18 weeks). Approximately 17% of cases show abnormal biomarker levels, further strengthening the case for testing prenatally [20]. Prenatal diagnosis of BWSp is challenging. The choice of tissue and timing of DNA sampling are critical as methylation patterns vary between tissues, mainly due to postzygotic mosaicism. It’s advisable to analyse native chorionic villi (CSV) versus cultured CVSs to avoid culture selection biases in the mosaic. Cultured tissues are more reliable in amniotic fluid (AF) because of the late timing of samples, and cultures are sometimes necessary to obtain sufficient DNA. Both AF and CVS are viable options for testing, though CVS, being an extraembryonic tissue, may occasionally yield discrepancies with postnatal methylation patterns. To ensure complete methylation at all CpGs of IC1 and IC2, accurate results and avoid false positives, sampling is recommended after the 13–14th week of gestation, as methylation may not yet be complete in earlier stages and falsely hypomethylated cases were reported with CVS sampled before the 12th week [32, 33]. In cases of negative prenatal results, postnatal evaluation is needed, and repeat testing may be necessary, particularly when clinical features suggest BWSp. Prenatal counseling should address the limitations of testing, such as mosaicism, tissue variability, diagnostic uncertainties, and ethical considerations. Importantly, parents should be informed that a normal result does not exclude a BWSp diagnosis [35]. Whenever the prenatal testing is performed by MLPA, it is mandatory to use DNAs extracted from the same tissue for samples and controls. It is recommended that prenatal BWSp testing is performed in laboratories specialized in imprinting disorders to ensure accuracy and reliability.

Epimutations in the 11p15.5 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) occur post-zygotically originating a somatic mosaicism, typically exhibiting a variable degree of methylation change. Mosaicism — and the associated overgrowth — can sometimes present with widespread or symmetrical involvement, making the segmental nature of the condition less apparent [32], complicating the interpretation of the molecular tests. The methylation levels detected strongly depend on the tissue sampled: specifically, on the degree of tissue-specific mosaicism and the proportion of cells with abnormal methylation. Importantly, these two factors are often intertwined and cannot be assessed separately, making accurate interpretation particularly challenging.

In clinical practice, when methylation levels in blood-extracted DNA are normal, it becomes crucial to analyze additional tissues to achieve a correct diagnostic classification of epigenetic subtypes. This aspect is particularly relevant in low-scoring patients (the so-called “atypical cases”) and in cases with isolated lateralized overgrowth (LO) [41, 42]. Remarkably, at least 70% patients with isolated LO remain unconfirmed at the molecular level when tested on blood alone [14]. Yet, confirming BWSp by molecular analysis and differentiating it from overlapping disorders is crucial [16]. For example, LO in BWSp must be distinguished from lateralized undergrowth in Silver-Russell syndrome [42–45] or LO in other syndromes, with different etiopathogenesis and requiring different approaches to management [14, 46, 47].

When methylation analysis of DNA from blood is negative, the decision to proceed with further genetic tests depends on the patient's clinical evaluation, family history, and the presence of specific signs and symptoms suggesting an underlying genetic condition. In some cases, based on a detailed clinical assessment, a negative methylation test on blood-extracted DNA test may reasonably exclude BWSp, especially in patients with a diagnostic score of ≤ 2 or presenting minor features only. However, it is generally advisable to consult a genetic specialist experienced in this condition to determine the relevance of additional genetic testing and to guide the diagnostic approach [42, 43].

Several tissues can be analyzed as alternatives to blood, such as buccal swabs, urine, or biopsies. From a laboratory perspective, testing alternative tissues requires comparison with suitable and reliable DNA controls extracted from the same tissue type. This may represent a limitation, as only laboratories experienced and equipped with proper internal controls can warrant the reliability of such analyses. DNA from buccal swabs is often the first analyzed after that from blood, especially in cases of macroglossia. Sampling is non-invasive and involves brushing the buccal mucosa on the side of the face exhibiting overgrowth, carefully avoiding blood contamination. Using buccal swabs and additional testing strategies can increase the diagnostic yield by 7–10% [42, 43]. Skin biopsies are particularly valuable in cases with LO and can further increase the diagnostic yield by 10%. Skin biopsies involve taking a punch sample from an overgrown area of the body and offer the advantage of supporting differential diagnoses against several other conditions with LO: most of them can be diagnosed by Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) testing for variants in genes associated with LO syndromes, such as PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum (PROS) or mosaic RASopathies. However, skin biopsy is more invasive and should be carefully considered based on the patient’s age, clinical severity, and the implications of a definitive diagnosis [14, 16, 17]. In cases where surgery is planned, it provides an opportunity to obtain tissue from affected organs or regions of the body without requiring an additional biopsy. On rare occasions, the only tissue showing (epi)genetic alterations may be the healthy peritumoral tissue removed during surgery [48, 49].

There has been considerable debate regarding cancer screening in BWSp following the changes introduced in the 2018 consensus guidelines. Before 2018, the screening protocol was uniform for all patients, consisting of quarterly abdominal ultrasounds (US) up to 8 years of age and blood alpha-fetoprotein (αFP) measurements up to 5 years of age. However, these recommendations received from the same participants to the consensus a low evidence rating, reflecting uncertainty among experts and differences across healthcare systems and have not been universally accepted [50, 51]. As an example, some retained the pre-2018 screening protocol, further highlighting geographic variations in clinical practice [52].

As concerns WT, the recommendation for US screening in BWSp patients with IC1-GoM and UPD(11)pat until the age of 8 is well-supported. Patients with BWSp have a significantly higher incidence of WT diagnoses between 12 and 84 months compared to non-syndromic WT patients. Furthermore, patients with BWSp diagnosed through screening present with significantly lower stages compared to those who were not screened, further proving screening effectiveness. Moreover, screening up to 7 years of age detects close to 95% of WT cases in BWSp patients [53].

As concerns HB, it appears to develop mostly in patients with UPD(11)pat (3.5% of cases [54], and, with a lower incidence, in patients with IC2-LoM [55]. As recently demonstrated, HB oncogenesis in IC2-LoM is much more complex than previously thought and may involve additional epimutational events, such as IC1 hypermethylation in the absence of UPD(11)pat [49, 56–58]. Alterations at 11p15 drive HB oncogenesis by dysregulating cell-cycle regulators and chromatin organizers, and these changes in DNA can differ between blood, normal liver, and tumor tissue, leading to inconsistent epigenotypes. This mosaicism complicates tumor risk assessment based on blood DNA profiling alone, emphasizing the importance of screening in low-risk molecular subgroups as well [48, 49]. Based on epidemiological data, HB occurs earlier in BWSp patients compared to non-syndromic cases, with over 80% of HB cases diagnosed within the first year of life and nearly all cases within 30 months. Thus, screening for HB in BWSp patients by trimestral blood αFP from birth at least to 3 years of age, regardless of genotype, is advisable [53]. This aligns with recent recommendations from other groups [52] and shortens screening duration from 48 to 36 months with respect to what was recommended in the 2018 guidelines.

Historically, more severe or pronounced phenotypic manifestations of BWSp — involving a greater number or extent of clinical features — were thought to correlate with a higher tumor risk However, recent analyses indicate that tumor risk is not proportional to the number of syndrome characteristics. Atypical or mild phenotypes, including those with blood DNA negativity at 11p5.5 methylation test, may still develop tumors, underlining the need for consistent screening protocols irrespective of phenotype severity [12]. Increasing access to genetic testing has revealed that 16–30% of pediatric WT and HB cases exhibit molecular anomalies typical of BWSp, underscoring the importance of genetic evaluation in children presenting with these tumors [59, 60]. Furthermore, to optimize outcomes, patients with WT or HB should undergo a clinical genetic evaluation: this approach not only helps confirm BWSp but also identifies other genetic conditions associated with these tumors [58, 61]. One of the main limitations of the tumor screening schedule proposed in the 2018 international consensus lies in its divergence from the recommendations adopted in the United States—an inconsistency that, in our experience, has also been reflected in the real-world practice of numerous Italian and European centers (personal observation of the authors). A second important limitation emerges from recent evidence highlighting the occurrence of embryonal tumors, particularly hepatoblastoma, in individuals with minimal or absent clinical manifestations of BWSp and with epigenetic alterations that differ between blood and tumor or peritumoral tissue. These findings suggest a shift in the phenotypic spectrum of BWSp cohorts over time, with an increasing number of patients now presenting with mild or subtle features. In this context, a more cautious and inclusive approach to tumor surveillance appears justified. In 2017, Kamihara et al. recommended a comprehensive screening protocol for patients with CDKN1C variants. This included abdominal ultrasounds, urine vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) and homovanillic acid (HVA), and chest X-rays every three months up to 6 years of age, followed by biannual screenings until 10 years [64]. In contrast, Brioude et al. proposed a simpler approach, recommending abdominal US every three months until 7 years of age [6]. In 2020, Cardoso et al. described 21 patients with CDKN1C variants: one developed a mediastinal ganglioneuroma at 21 years of age, suggesting that neural crest tumors may occur beyond childhood [65] emphasizing the need to consider NB not only in pediatric settings but also during adolescence and early adulthood. For patients with CDKN1C variants, we propose a screening strategy involving urinary tumor markers (Homovanillic Acid – HVA, Vanillylmandelic Acid—VMA, or catecholamine-to-creatinine ratios) and abdominal US every three months until the age of 7 years. Routine chest X-rays are not recommended to avoid unnecessary radiation exposure. Further studies are clearly necessary to assess the effectiveness of this screening protocol.

In recent years, several studies have highlighted that the methylation defects observed in patients with imprinting disorders are not isolated but can be accompanied by dysregulation of additional imprinted loci. This phenomenon has been defined as "multi-locus imprinting disturbances" (MLID), because besides the DNA methylation disturbance associated with the clinical disorder, aberrant dysregulation of further imprinted loci occurs. This might have significant implications for clinical diagnostics, as it suggests a broader spectrum of molecular disturbances that need to be accounted for during the diagnostic process [66]. An exact determination of how many and which loci are required to define MLID is still lacking. Very recently, a joint statement [66] for clinical and molecular diagnosis distinguished clinically relevant DMRs (CA-DMRs) from non-clinically relevant ones (NC-DMRs). CA-DMRs are germline DMRs whose deregulation leads to a specific clinical diagnosis and may be investigated in the diagnostic labs (Table 4), while NC-DMRs are imprinted loci with unknown functions and not associated with a clinical diagnosis. Targeted methods and omics analyses respectively enable the investigation of MLID by interrogating a limited number of CpGs in specific DMRs or by extensively covering all the known DMRs and CpGs, respectively. Targeted methods, such as MS-MLPA, are cost-effective and provide quick results for specific loci, making them suitable for routine diagnostics. However, they are limited in scope and may miss abnormalities in non-targeted loci. Among the targeted tests, the commercial MS-MLPA kit ME034 Multilocus kit [MRC-Holland, Amsterdam, Holland], which allows for the simultaneous testing of 11 imprinted loci associated with imprinting disorders, is widely applied in European labs. In contrast, omics techniques, including methylation arrays and whole-genome bisulfite sequencing, offer a comprehensive view of methylation patterns across the genome, allowing the identification of novel or unexpected loci associated with MLID. These methods, while more informative as they investigate all imprinted loci, require advanced infrastructure, are costlier, and necessitate specialized expertise: these factors limit their accessibility in routine clinical settings and make them more suitable for research purposes. Recently, a bisulfite sequencing-based method targeting 63 imprinted DMRs (ImprintSeq) was developed [67].

In BWSp, MLID has been described among patients with IC2-LoM with an incidence ranging between 12.5% and 50% [66, 68–72]. In our national cohort, the prevalence of MLID in BWS-IC2 patients appears to be higher than that reported in other cohorts (SR, personal data). Possibly due to its mosaic distribution and the functional relevance of affected loci in different tissues, the presence of MLID in BWSp is not consistently accompanied by specific phenotypic features [72–76]. However, a few cases with clinical features of more than one imprinting disorder have been reported: patients with BWSp with IC2-LoM and transient neonatal diabetes mellitus (TNDM) derived from deregulation of the TNDM DMR [77] as well as patient with BWSp and short stature, protruding forehead, and macrocephaly consistent with Temple syndrome and MEG3:TSS-DMR methylation anomalies were reported [78]. Besides, patients with BWSp and pseudo-hypoparathyroidism 1b (PHP-1b) derived from methylation defects or patUPD of chromosome 20q13.32 have been described [2, 79–81]. In that light, monitoring clinically and taking into account anomalies in parathormone and calcium/phosphate derangements is warranted in patients with BWSp and IC2-LoM.

Currently, there are no formal recommendations to test for MLID in all cases of BWSp. However, it is essential to evaluate patients showing features suggestive of an additional imprinting disorder. In the future, it may be worthwhile to extend targeted analyses to identify at least all clinically relevant MLIDs in cases of BWSp associated with IC2-LoM.

Further concerns related to MLID are the increased recurrence risk of some MLID and the reproductive problems occurring in the mothers of some affected patients, emphasizing the importance of genetic counseling. Such counseling can provide valuable insights into the potential risks and guide reproductive decision-making, particularly when pathogenic variants in maternal effect genes (MEGs) are suspected [82]. MEGs are transcribed in oocytes and encode proteins that are transferred to the zygote and needed for the early developmental stages of the embryo and include the proteins of the subcortical maternal complex (SCMC) [83–85]. Pathogenic variants in MEGs result in both reproductive difficulties or recurrent miscarriages in the mother and in imprinting disturbances in the embryo, being therefore responsible for recurrent BWSp or other imprinting disorders [86, 87]. Such conditions are mostly recessive [71, 86–89]. It may be therefore advisable that women with a child with IC2-LOM and experiencing reproductive difficulties undergo specific genetic counseling and their children tested for MLID: in case MLID is identified, it would be appropriate to screen the mothers for SCMC gene variants.

BWSp is the most common cause of macroglossia in childhood, with 90% of children diagnosed with BWSp exhibiting such a feature [15, 31]. Macroglossia typically involves an increase in tongue size in terms of length, width, and/or thickness. The enlargement may be symmetric or consistent with lingual LO [90, 91]. Most of the patients with macroglossia do not require surgery: growth of the oral cavity may eventually accommodate the enlarged tongue [15]. This depends on the degree of tongue enlargement and growth velocity, but also on genotype [91–93]. Overall, approximately 40% of patients undergo surgical tongue reduction.

The need and timing for surgery can range from the neonatal period to pediatric age. The most common indications for surgery include respiratory problems, obstructive sleep apnea, feeding difficulties, persistent drooling, speech issues, orthodontic problems, as well as psychosocial and aesthetic concerns [15, 94–97]. Upper airway evaluation and polysomnography can provide further assessment for obstructive sleep apnea. In cases of respiratory problems, surgery might be necessary earlier in the neonatal period. In the absence of respiratory obstruction, surgery is generally delayed until at least 12 months of age. If the indication for surgery is unclear, the child’s progress should be monitored to determine if indications arise later [92, 94–97].

The enlarged tongue is usually increased in size in all three dimensions, and the aim of surgery is to reduce the tongue bulk while preserving its normal shape and improving function. Surgical techniques vary across centers and include anterior, peripheral, or central reduction. Incisions may be V-shaped, W-shaped, stellate, or keyhole, among others. The choice of technique depends on the type of macroglossia, surgeon expertise, functional or aesthetic outcomes, and safety profile, and should be tailored [98–100]. Surgery should be performed in multidisciplinary centers by experienced surgeons, with the aim of concentrating cases in a limited number of expert units: this approach allows for better outcomes and facilitates clinical studies and long-term follow-up [15, 97, 100, 101].

Long-term follow-up studies generally show favorable surgical outcomes, including resolution of breathing and feeding difficulties, reduced drooling, improved speech, aesthetic and psychosocial improvement, adequate tongue mobility, and usually no substantial effect on taste. Surgical tongue reduction has generally shown favorable functional and aesthetic outcomes. However, recent evidence, including the study by Wagner et al. [97], emphasizes the importance of avoiding early intervention, as earlier timing has been associated with a higher rate of postoperative complications. [97, 101, 102].

Many BWS patients present progressive mandibular overgrowth from childhood to adolescence and glossectomy does not seem to modify the rate of its growth. Consequently, in the absence of airway obstruction, swallowing problems with failure to thrive, impaired feeding functions and speech problems, based on our data, glossectomy is not recommended in BWS patients for preventing Class III malocclusion [102].

Recently, two disease-specific growth charts for children with BWSp have been published [103, 104]. The korean study was based on measurements on 51 prepubertal children (males < 10 and females < 7 years of age, respectively): both had growth pattern with growth pattern with statures greater than their peers' at all the measurements. The study by Maas et al. [104] developed specific growth charts for height, weight, BMI, and head circumference for individuals affected by BWSp, analyzing growth data from 581 individuals across the Netherlands, Italy, and the USA. Patients with BWSp exhibit increased linear growth compared to the general population, with a particularly enhanced growth rate during adolescence. The modeled mean height at 18 years of age was 180.6 cm for males (+ 0.6 SDS) and 166.3 cm for females (+ 0.5 SDS), both exceeding the WHO reference values. Weight and BMI also showed a consistent increase relative to the general population, with BMI values remaining above + 1 SDS at all ages. Head circumference was significantly increased in early childhood, but growth data beyond four years of age were limited.

The study further explored genotype–phenotype correlations and found that the IC1-GOM subgroup was associated with slightly greater height, while individuals with the UPD(11)pat molecular subtype had a modestly reduced height at 18 years of age. Differences in height between countries were also observed, with Dutch individuals being significantly taller than their counterparts in the USA and Italy, necessitating a normalization process to ensure comparability across populations.

These newly developed growth charts provide an important tool for clinicians to monitor the growth of children with BWSp, allowing for a more precise assessment of growth trajectories and adult height prediction. In addition to improving individualized medical care, these charts may also aid in decision-making for interventions such as epiphysiodesis in cases of asymmetric lateralized overgrowth. Despite some limitations, including variability in the dataset and the lack of data beyond adolescence, this study represents a significant advancement in understanding the growth patterns of individuals with BWSp and offers a valuable resource for clinical management.

'A clinical sign often observed in BWSp is LO, characterized by excessive growth localized to one side of the body, resulting in noticeable asymmetry beyond normal variation.. This asymmetry may involve only one part of the body or of an entire side (formerly known as hemi-hyperplasia). Overgrowth affects the affected limb in both girth and length. Limb length discrepancy (LLD) is of greater orthopedic interest than upper limb length discrepancy. LLD may be due to a different length of the tibia, femur, or both bones. LLD displays different degrees of severity: it may be present at birth or appear later, and may have or not a progression during childhood [99]. UPD(11)pat is associated with a more severe LO and LLD, as well as to a higher risk of adverse evolution [105]. LLD can be operatively classified into four subgroups: minor (< 1 cm), mild (1–2 cm), severe (2–5 cm), and critical (> 5 cm) [105]. Minor and mild LLD have a poor tendency to worsen over time. Fifty percent of LLD < 1 cm evolve to mild (40%) and severe (12.5%). Mild grade LLD tends to worsen to severe in 35% of cases; severe LLD tends to evolve to critical in 15.4% of cases [105].

Orthopedic treatment in BWS is primarily aimed at equalizing the different lengths of the lower limbs. In patients with BWSp, treatment strategies might differ from those used in non-BWSp cases, especially when LLD worsen during growth: the different approach might be warranted based on the tall stature usually displayed by patients with BWSp [3, 6]. Recently, disease-specific growth charts for children with BWSp have been published based on 51 Korean children [103]. Both sexes showed stature greater than peers at all ages evaluated (males below 10 and females below 7 years of age, respectively): although the study involved only prepubertal patients, this and several other reports suggest that in general, in BWSp the final stature is higher than in general population. More recently, Maas et al. developed specific growth charts for height, analyzing growth data from 581 individuals demonstrating increased linear growth compared to the general population and a mean height at 18 years of age of 180.6 cm for males (+ 0.6 SDS) and 166.3 cm for females (+ 0.5 SDS), both exceeding the WHO reference values [104]. Also based on these two studies, the preferred strategy to manage LLD is blocking the longer bone rather than lengthening the shorter healthy one: this approach has the advantage to negatively impact the final stature without impacting nor interfering with the growth process [106]. On the opposite, limb lengthening using Lizarov's method (an invasive technique involving circular external fixators) is generally not indicated for preschool and school-age patients due to risks of infection, functional impairment, joint stiffness, pain, and nerve injury [106]. The aim of epiphysiodesis is to modulate the growth of the longer limb until limb length equality is achieved at the end of growth process. This is obtained by allowing for a temporary blocking of the growing cartilage by inserting a small metallic device, such as a plate, above and below the growth plate…and screws perpendicularly to the long axis of the bone. The distal femur and proximal tibia are usually targeted, with tibial overgrowth being predominant in most BWS cases [106]. Epiphysiodesis is usually used for treating LLD > 2 cm, while for LLD ≤ 2 cm a conservative treatment using a shoe lift is commonly recommended. Epiphysiodesis of the proximal tibia, performed at an age around 8 years, is usually the first surgical procedure, followed by the epiphysiodesis of the distal femur growth plates two–three years later. After LLD correction is achieved, implants are removed—usually only after the pubertal growth spurt, when further longitudinal bone growth is minimal or absent. If removal occurs too early, residual growth may lead to undercorrection or overcorrection of LLD, which has traditionally been considered one of the most common limitations of this type of surgery. Implant migration and secondary varus or valgus axial deviation during growth have been reported and might need a revision of the plate’s configuration. When axial deviation occurs, a contralateral hemiepiphysiodesis is commonly used to guide the residual bone growth. Finally, although systematic data are lacking on this issue, the earlier skeletal maturation which has been described in patients affected by BWSp represents an indication to assess bone maturation by writs and hand X-ray before planning surgery, and likely for an earlier surgery compared to non-BWSp subjects [6, 106].

In previous recommendations it was suggested that cognitive development and psychosocial adjustment in children with BWSp should be routinely monitored. Although the cognitive and psychosocial sequelae of BWSp remain poorly investigated, recent studies have confirmed and extended earlier findings. BWSp is not associated with intellectual disability, except in rare cases with severe untreated hypoglycemia, complications of prematurity, unbalanced chromosomal rearrangements, or paternal genome-wide UPD [6, 107]. A recent study involving 30 preschool-age children with BWSp reported that a significant portion exhibited difficulties in language and gross motor functions, likely resulting as a complication of macroglossia and LO, respectively [108]. In such cases, monitoring of speech and motor development is critical, providing appropriate treatment as needed. A more recent study on school-age children with BWS found that, while most participants performed adequately on cognitive tests, they exhibited slower reading and mental calculation [108]. Interestingly, in a survey-based study almost 20% of adult patients with BWS reported learning issues [109]. While future studies may shed light into the connection between MLID and a presumed higher incidence of learning disabilities [110, 111], literature on learning challenges associated with orofacial malformations suggests a complex interplay between genetic predisposition, psychosocial factors, and educational outcomes [112]. Consequently, a standardized learning ability screening is recommended for all school-age children with BWSp, aiming to provide tools and mitigate the social and emotional consequences of undetected learning disabilities [113].

Beyond the potential impact of learning difficulties, frequent hospitalizations, limitations in daily activities, and concerns about physical appearance may impact emotional and behavioral functioning [114]. Additionally, key features of BWSp, including macroglossia, abdominal wall defects, and increased cancer risk, have been shown to influence psychological well-being and psychosocial adjustment in both children with BWSp and their families [115–117]. Increasing evidence suggests that children with BWSp may exhibit emotional-behavioral issues, particularly anxiety, low self-esteem, and social withdrawal [108, 118–121]. These challenges appear to intensify with age, possibly due to the impact of social feedback and the experience of "being different", with potential long-term consequences into adulthood [109].

In conclusion, there is a subtle balance between the importance of comprehensive screening and management of the several health issues in children with BWSp and the psychosocial burden that this model of care implies. To address this, parent-report questionnaires could serve as non-intrusive screening tools for identifying emotional-behavioral problems without adding to the caregiving burden. Identifying children and families at-risk would facilitate timely psychological support and intervention, ultimately improving quality of life.

Several areas of improvement in clinical management can be envisaged for future studies.

Cancer surveillance and recommendations are an unmet need of patients with isolated LO. They face an increased susceptibility to develop tumors of the BWSp spectrum [122], with a non-negligible risk (1.1%- 6%) [16, 122–125]. In particular, patients with isolated LO (i.e. with 2 points at the clinical score and negative molecular tests) represent a challenge from a diagnostic point of view and a zone of shadow for medical guidelines. Specific studies on this population are needed. In the meanwhile, we emphasize the importance of critically assessing these patients for other diagnoses and extending genetic testing to conditions with phenotypic overlap. In any case, in the absence of a molecular diagnosis, they should be offered oncological screening similar to that for BWSp [10, 122]. To overcome the limit of the present diagnostic tools in the detection of low percentage of epimutated cells, novel approaches based on NGS protocols combined with specific treatments to distinguish methylated and unmethylated DNA in the DMRs are under study. Hopefully these new methods will increase the diagnostic rate in BWS. Transition from childhood to adulthood care remains an open issue in BWSp management, and its challenges span medical, psychological, and social domains. A major concern is the lack of data regarding the natural history of adults with BWSp. Adult health issues seem to be largely subsequent to complication or unsolved pediatric problems such as macroglossia, scoliosis, LLD, kidney stones, cryptorchidism, and recurrent urinary tract infections. Therefore, physicians caring for adults with BWSp should focus on such key clinical areas. Furthermore, fertility issues should be a further area of clinical research: azoospermia has been described as common in male patients, but it is unclear whether consequent to cryptorchidism or not [6, 109]. Upon these observations, Greer et al. [128] proposed recommendations for follow up of adult patients, as summarized in Table 5. General clinical experience denies a specific increase in tumor risk in adults with BWSp, and it is a common conviction that after the first decade of life, it is similar to that observed in the general population. However, recent studies adults observed a mild increase in tumor risk in adults with BWSp, approaching nearly 25% [126, 127]. While this data likely results from an overestimation secondary to study design, and there is no need for specific screening, a prudential approach is warranted. It is reasonable to recommend periodic consultations with geneticists/general practitioners and keeping a low threshold for seeking care and investigations in case of unusual symptoms. Further studies are needed to clarify if there are differences in adult health issues in the various genotypes, and if any deserves specific medical follow-up. As an example, patients with CDKN1C variants are at risk for aneurysmal arterial dilations and hearing impairment [128] and might benefit from a specific assessment. Transition is a gradual process through which young patients with BWSp are supported in developing awareness of their condition, with the goal of empowering them to take responsibility for its management in adulthood. For this transition to be effective, adolescents must receive dedicated training and counselling to become true experts in their condition. ## Table 6 Key-point summary of the updates and recommendations

| Diagnostic criteria and scoring system |
| 1. Clinical criteria are robust but have limitations in some populations; critical application is recommended (Table 1) |
| 2. In cases with negative molecular tests for BWSp or uncommon presentations consider possible alternative or dual diagnoses: Whole Exome Sequencing and chromosomal microarray (CMA) might be indicated |
| 3. ART-related pregnancy and monozygotic twinning should be considered and added among the supportive features due to their association and relevance for testing |
| Prenatal Diagnosis |
| 4. Prenatal diagnosis is essential but entails greater uncertainty than postnatal testing. A normal prenatal result does not exclude BWSp; postnatal testing may still be indicated. Counseling should address mosaicism, tissue-specificity, and diagnostic limitations |
| 5. Molecular testing recommendations can generally be guided by criteria (Table 2) |
| 6. Timing and tissue selection are critical: cultured amniocytes show better concordance with postnatal results; CVS should be performed after 13–14 weeks to reduce false negatives |
| Somatic mosaicism and testing of DNA from tissues other than blood |
| 7. High-depth NGS on somatic DNA from affected tissue, especially in cases with LO as main feature, increases diagnostic yield and informs differential diagnosis in cases negative on blood DNA |
| 8. Differential diagnosis with other conditions presenting with LO is crucial for appropriate clinical management |
| 9. In cases negative at 11p5.5 methylation tests on blood DNA, further tests and tissue selection should be guided by an experienced clinical geneticist |
| Tumor Screening for Wilms Tumor (WT) and Hepatoblastoma (HB) |
| 10. Indications for screening WT (US every 3 months up the 8th year of age in cases with UPD(11)pat, IC1-GoM or negative) given in the previous consensus are valid and sound |
| 11. For HB screening, serum αFP and abdominal ultrasound every 3 months until age 3 are recommended across all molecular subgroups. In particular, the IC2-LoM group is included in this screening |
| 12. Tumor risk does not correlate with phenotypic severity; even mild cases or those with negative 11p5.5 methylation testing may develop tumors |
| 13. Genetic evaluation is essential in children diagnosed with WT or HB to confirm BWSp or identify related genetic conditions |
| Tumor surveillance in patients with CDKN1C variants |
| 14. Neuroblastoma (NB) is rare but has been reported across all BWSp subgroups, more frequently in those with CDKN1C variants |
| 15. NB screening remains controversial, as improved survival has not been demonstrated |
| 16. We propose abdominal ultrasound and urinary catecholamine metabolites every 3 months until age 7, while avoiding routine chest X-rays |
| Multi-Locus Imprinting Disturbances (MLID) |
| 17. In BWSp with IC2-LoM, MLID is very common (up to 50%) but not associated with a specific phenotype; in rare cases, features of other imprinting disorders may coexist |
| 18. MLID may be caused by the occurrence of pathogenic variants in genes associated with epigenetic regulation or maternal pathogenic variants |
| 19. Clinical management of MLID testing should be limited to those loci associated with clinical imprinting disorders. Tests and counseling must be be performed by professionals experienced in IDs |
| 20. The pragmatic relevance and clinical implications of diagnosing MLID is still debated and testing MLID belongs mostly to the research area: this should be discussed with the patient before testing |
| 21. Awaiting further scientific evidence, MLID testing in IC2-LoM cases can be of clinical utility and recommended when |
| a. MS-MLPA shows deregulation of more than one DMR |
| b. there is maternal history of recurrent miscarriage or infertility, |
| c. monozygotic twinning or recurrent BWSp-IC2-LoM |
| d. inconsistent or adjunctive non-BWSp clinical features, especially if suggestive of other imprinting disorders |
| 22. MLID can be assessed using targeted methods (e.g., MS-MLPA) or genome-wide approaches (e.g., ImprintSeq), although the latter are mostly research-based |
| Management of macroglossia and Tongue Surgery |
| 23. Glossectomy is required in approximately 40% of cases, based on severity and functional or aesthetic concerns.Various surgical techniques exist and should be individualized and performed only in expert centers |
| 24. Indications include obstructive apnea, dysphagia, sialorrhea, respiratory difficulty, speech impairment, and orthodontic issues In the absence of respiratory urgency, surgery is postponed until after 12 months of age |
| 25. Glossectomy does not prevent mandibular prognathism |
| Growth |
| 26. New BWSp-specific growth charts are now available [Choi, 2024; Maas, 2025], supporting clinical monitoring and orthopedic decision-making |
| 27. BWSp patients show increased height, weight, and BMI compared to the general population, particularly during adolescence, with an estimated mean adult height + 0.5 to + 0.6 SDS relative to references |
| 28. IC1-GoM is associated with taller stature, while UPD(11)pat tends to be linked to slightly shorter height |
| Limb-length discrepancy |
| 29. Limb Length Discrepancy (LLD), even slight, may progress over time and requires monitoring; treatment aims to equalize leg length |
| 30. Surgery is indicated for LLD > 2 cm; for ≤ 2 cm, shoe lifts are typically sufficient |
| 31. Epiphysiodesis is preferred over lengthening of the shorter limb |
| 32. Final height in BWSp is above average, which influences both the timing and choice of orthopedic intervention. Assessment of bone age can be recommended before surgery |
| Assessment of Cognitive Development and Psychosocial Adjustment |
| 33. BWSp is not typically associated with intellectual disability, except in rare cases (e.g., severe hypoglycemia, extensive paternal UPD) |
| 34. Difficulties in speech, gross motor skills, and academic performance are common, and mostly related to macroglossia or lateralized overgrowth |
| 35. Approximately 20% of adults with BWSp report learning difficulties; standardized educational screening is recommended |
| 36. Some patients experience anxiety, low self-esteem, and social withdrawal: parent-reported questionnaires may help identify emotional and behavioral issues early without increasing care burden |
