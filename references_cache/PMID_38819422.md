# Mutation in the GRIA4 Gene Presenting as ESES: Expanding the Genetic Landscape of ESES.
**Authors:** Sharawat IK, Gupta D, Mandal S, Elwadhi A, Panda PK
**Journal:** Ann Indian Acad Neurol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.4103/aian.aian_1139_23](https://doi.org/10.4103/aian.aian_1139_23)

## Content

Dear Editor,

Electrical status epilepticus during slow-wave sleep (ESES), also known as continuous spike-wave of slow sleep (CSWS), represents a specific electroencephalographic (EEG) pattern within the broader clinical spectrum of ESES/CSWS/epilepsy aphasia spectrum.[ 1 ] This pattern can occur alone or alongside other epilepsy or neurodevelopmental syndromes. Although the exact cause is uncertain, factors such as brain malformations, autoimmune disorders, structural brain insults, and genetic factors have been implicated.[ 1 2 3 ] This report introduces a newly identified monogenic mutation in the GRIA4 gene found in a 6-year-old boy diagnosed with ESES.

A 6-year-old boy, without any history of consanguinity in parents and with normal perinatal period, presented with a regression of acquired language and social milestones over the past 9 months. This regression was accompanied by autistic features and aggressive behavior. He had a baseline delay in developmental milestones, with a recently noted clumsy, wide-based gait. There were no seizures, abnormal movements, or neurologic complaints. Physical and neurologic examinations were normal, except for the noted gait abnormality. The child was diagnosed with moderate to severe autism spectrum disorder with a Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS)-2 score of 36.

EEG revealed nearly continuous generalized spike–wave epileptiform discharges during slow-wave sleep, consistent with ESES [spike wave index (SWI) 85%; Figure 1]. Awake EEG also showed occasional spike–wave discharges (SWI- 10%), with significant potentiation during sleep. A diagnosis of ESES was made. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain was normal. Whole exome sequencing identified a novel de novo heterozygous pathogenic missense variation in exon 7 of the GRIA4 gene (c.874A>T, p.Thr292Ser), resulting in the substitution of serine for threonine at codon 292. The variant was predicted to be damaging by MutationTaster2. Segregation analysis did not reveal the mutation in the parents. As mutations in the GRIA4 gene have been reported to cause intellectual disability with or without epilepsy and gait abnormalities, the parents were counseled accordingly.

Sleep accentuated (international 10–20 system, bipolar montage, sensitivity- 20 μV/mm, sweep speed- 30 mm/s), near continuous, generalized, synchronous bursts of spike–slow-wave activity of 1–3 Hz, 600–700 mcv, covering over 90%–95% of the sleep record

The child initially received oral clobazam at 1 mg/kg/day in two divided doses (10 mg twice daily), but behavioral abnormalities persisted and ESES continued after 1 month. Subsequently, intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy was administered at 30 mg/kg/day for 5 days, followed by oral prednisolone for 8 weeks in tapering doses. Two weeks after corticosteroid administration, the child showed some improvement in aggression and hyperactivity, and at 12 weeks, significant improvements were noted in eye contact and other behavioral abnormalities. Repeat EEG showed occasional spike–wave discharges, but no ESES. At 24 weeks post-treatment initiation, the social quotient improved to 38 and the CARS score decreased to 31.

Martin et al.[ 4 ] were the first to report cases of five unrelated intellectually disabled patients in 2017, who possessed pathogenic, heterozygous, de novo missense variants in the GRIA4 gene. GRIA4 codes for an α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptor subunit called GluR4 situated on excitatory glutamatergic synapses, playing a crucial role in memory and learning. Previous studies have emphasized alterations in AMPA receptor subunits as contributors to neurodevelopmental disorders.[ 5 ] Among these five individuals, three experienced seizures and one displayed frequent generalized spike–wave discharges but not ESES. All five cases exhibited mild to severe developmental delay, with variable behavioral abnormalities, including hyperactivity, inattention, autistic behaviors, and aggressive behavior. In addition, most presented gait abnormalities, such as a clumsy or wide-based gait, necessitating support for walking, similar to our case. Three individuals also manifested facial dysmorphism, such as short philtrum, large ears, prognathism, and midface retrusion. Two cases displayed abnormal MRI brain findings. Other associated features included squint, simian crease, extrapyramidal features like choreiform movements, gastroesophageal reflux disease, difficulty in feeding, hiatal hernias, multiple respiratory tract infections in infancy, and even apneas, which were noted in previously published cases. Kornilov et al.[ 6 ] and Poot et al.[ 7 ] also identified monogenic mutations and copy number variations encompassing the GRIA4 gene as linked to intellectual disability in genome-wide association and homozygosity mapping studies.

Wang et al.[ 8 ] have also previously reported an infant diagnosed with neurodevelopmental disorder with or without seizures and gait abnormalities caused by a GRIA4 variant, who presented with severe developmental delay, generalized epilepsy, hypertonia of the limbs, chorioretinal hyperpigmentation, and retinal hypoplasia. Our case has little phenotypic overlap with the previously reported cases, as this is the first reported case associated with ESES, with the index case having no facial dysmorphism and normal neuroimaging.

Treatment of ESES is often challenging and unsatisfactory in terms of improvement in cognition and behavior. van den Munckhof et al.[ 9 ] have reported that antiseizure medications (ASMs), benzodiazepines (BZDs), corticosteroids, and epilepsy surgery were associated with improvement (i.e., cognition or EEG) in a pooled analysis combining the treatment of 575 previously published ESES cases. A subgroup analysis revealed that ASMs and BZDs were associated with improvement in only 34% and 59% of cases, respectively, whereas corticosteroids and surgery caused improvement in 75% and 93% of cases, respectively. In our case, clobazam was initially added, followed by corticosteroids, as the treatment approach.

Clinicians should be vigilant of these newly identified monogenic associations with ESES and the broadening phenotypic spectrum of genes linked to epileptic encephalopathy, such as GRIA4. This awareness will help clinicians and researchers better understand the underlying pathogenesis of ESES and facilitate the development of targeted therapies.
