Objective: Autosomal-dominant lateral temporal epilepsy (ADLTE) is a genetic focal epilepsy characterized by audi-
tory symptoms. Two genes, LGI1 and RELN, encoding secreted proteins, are implicated in the etiology of ADLTE,
but half of the affected families remain genetically unsolved, and the underlying molecular mechanisms are yet to be
clarified. We aimed to identify additional genes causing ADLTE to better understand the genetic basis and molecular
pathway underlying this epileptic disorder.
Methods: A cohort of Italian ADLTE families was examined by whole exome sequencing combined with genome-
wide single-nucleotide polymorphism-array linkage analysis.
Results: We identified two ADLTE-causing variants in the MICAL-1 gene: a p.Gly150Ser substitution occurring in the
enzymatically active monooxygenase (MO) domain and a p.Ala1065fs frameshift indel in the C-terminal domain,
which inhibits the oxidoreductase activity of the MO domain. Each variant segregated with ADLTE in a single family.
Examination of candidate variants in additional genes excluded their implication in ADLTE. In cell-based assays, both
variants significantly increased MICAL-1 oxidoreductase activity and induced cell contraction in COS7 cells, which
likely resulted from deregulation of F-actin dynamics.
Interpretation: MICAL-1 oxidoreductase activity induces disassembly of actin filaments, thereby regulating the orga-
nization of the actin cytoskeleton in developing and adult neurons and in other cell types. This suggests that dysre-
gulation of the actin cytoskeleton dynamics is a likely mechanism by which MICAL-1 pathogenic variants lead to
ADLTE.

Patients and Methods
Family Classification
Multiplex families containing 2 or more affected members with
focal epilepsy characterized by auditory (ringing, humming,
sounds, voices, or music) and/or aphasic symptoms and absence
of structural or metabolic brain abnormalities were classified as
ADLTE.
2 Families with multiple affected individuals, 1 of
whom had typical lateral temporal epilepsy whereas in other
affected members epilepsy could not be directly ascertained,
were classified as “possible ADLTE.” A total of 28 Italian fami-
lies (22 with ADLTE and six with possible ADLTE) without
LGI1 or RELN mutations were ascertained and DNAs collected
by the Genetics Commission Collaborative Group of the Italian
League Against Epilepsy. Written informed consent was ob-
tained from all family members participating in the study. The
study was approved by local Ethics Committees.
Genotyping and Linkage Analysis
Affected and unaffected members of 10 ADLTE families (35
patients [5 deceased], 77 DNAs in total) were genotyped using
the HumanOmni1-Quad v1.0 (Illumina) single-nucleotide
polymorphism (SNP) beadchip, and linkage analysis was per-
formed using the program Merlin
22 assuming autosomal-
dominant inheritance with 70% penetrance, disease allele fre-
quency 0.001, and phenocopy rate 0.0, and using the “affected-
only” design, which classifies all individuals with no clinical
manifestations as “diagnosis unknown.” T wo different sets of
SNPs were utilized, which were selected on the basis of differ-
ent linkage disequilibrium (LD) parameters, r
2<0.4 and
r2 < 0.2, using a reference LD block map built on the family
index cases with the PLINK tool, 23 as described previously.16
Whole Exome Sequencing
and Bioinformatic Analysis
The exomes of affected individuals, 2 patients per family, were
sequenced as described previously. 16 Briefly, exome capture was
performed using SureSelect Human All Exon 50Mb Kit v 2.0
(Agilent T echnologies, Santa Clara, CA), and libraries were
sequenced on Illumina HiSeq2000. Variant calling and genotyp-
ing was performed with GATK.
24 Mean coverage was 39-fold.
Nonsynonymous, splice site, and short insertion/deletion (indel)
variants were annotated by Annovar 25 and filtered versus NCBI
1000 Human Genomes Project (http://www.1000genomes.org/)
and Exome Variant Server (EVS; http://evs.gs.washington.edu/
EVS/) catalogs. We selected only heterozygous variants shared by
the 2 affected individuals examined in each family and then fil-
tered out common variants with frequency >1%. In each family,
variants lying in chromosome intervals with logarithm of odds
(LOD) score > 0 were extracted for further analysis. Also, variants
lying in chromosome regions with LOD scores between 0 and –2
were considered as less likely candidates. High priority was
assigned to variants lying in genes with a lower Residual Variation
Intolerance Score (RVIS) percentile, indicating greater intolerance
to functional variation.
26 In particular, genes with RVIS <25%
were considered the most likely candidates, given that the vast
majority of Mendelian disease genes lie within this RVIS percen-
tile. Finally, the genome aggregation database (gnomAD) browser
(http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) was used to estimate the fre-
quency of selected rare variants in the general population.

Bioinformatic analysis of sequencing data were the same as that

MICAL-1 Variants
Among the variants that passed all filtering steps, two were
found in the same gene, MICAL-1 (NM_022765.3), in
families F23 and F24. In family F23, we found a heterozy-
gous frameshift indel, c.3189-3190del, which resulted in a
deletion of the last three amino acids of the MICAL-1 pro-
tein and addition of 59 extra residues at the C-terminus of
the mutant protein (p.Ala1065fs). This variant was vali-
dated by Sanger sequencing and segregated with ADLTE
in family F23 (Fig 1). It was not found in 240 geographi-
cally matched healthy controls and is present at very low
frequency (4 of 122,880 controls) in the gnomAD data-
base. MICAL-1 is expressed ubiquitously, with higher
expression levels in the embryonic, postnatal, and mature
nervous system and in a few other organs such as thymus,
spleen, testis, and kidney. Its protein product, MICAL-1,
consists of four conserved domains: an N-terminal enzy-
matically active monooxygenase (MO) domain, a calponin
homology (CH) domain, a Lin11, Isl-1, and Mec-3 (LIM)
domain, and a C-terminal coiled coil (CC) domain (Fig
2). Despite its position near the C-terminus, the
p.Ala1065fs variant likely has a functional impact, given
that the C-terminal CC domain autoregulates MICAL-1
enzyme activity (see below).
In family F24, we found a heterozygous missense
variant, c.448G >A, resulting in substitution of the gly-
cine at position 150 with a serine (p.Gly150Ser). The
variant was validated by direct sequencing and segregated
with ADLTE in the family (Fig 1). It occurs at a very
low frequency (5 of 131,305 controls) in the gnomAD
database and was not found in 240 geographically
matched controls. PolyPhen-2, SIFT, and Mutation
Taster predicted that this variant is deleterious (scores
1.00, 0.03, and 0.99, respectively) for the protein func-
tion, whereas M-CAP prediction was likely benign (score
0.020). The Gly150 residue, which lies in the enzymati-
cally active MO domain, is highly conserved across verte-
brate species in MICAL-1 and in the corresponding
positions in the paralogs MICAL-2 and MICAL-3 and
in Drosophila MICAL proteins (Fig 2), suggesting that
this residue is essential for the structure or function of
the MO domain in this family of proteins.

ADLTE-Related Variants Increase MICAL-1
Oxidoreductase Activity
MICAL-1 encodes an intracellular protein that catalyses
reduction-oxidation (redox) reactions through its highly
conserved flavoprotein MO domain to regulate F-actin
dynamics. Namely, MICAL-1 oxidoreductase activity
produces H
2O2,27,36,37 which induces oxidation of spe-
cific F-actin amino acid residues, ultimately leading to

Discussion
We report the identification of heterozygous MICAL-1
variants in three families with ADLTE and show that
two of these variants cause a significant increase of
MICAL-1 oxidoreductase activity, through which
MICAL-1 regulates F-actin dynamics. This functional
effect together with the genetic data presented in this
article and the role exerted by MICAL1 in neuronal
development and functioning strongly support a patho-
genic effect of these variants.

38,40 MICAL-1 oxidoreductase activity
is autoinhibited by the C-terminal CC domain, 43 so that
the wild-type protein is nearly inactive. This autoinhibi-
tion is mediated by binding of the CC domain to the
MO and LIM domains. 27 Removal of the whole C-
terminal domain releases autoinhibition, resulting in con-
stitutive oxidoreductase activity. Small alterations of the
CC domain structure may also result in increased
enzymatic activity of the MO domain. The p.Ala1065fs
mutation we identified truncates the C-terminal–most
three amino acid residues, which may be important for
the stability of the human CC domain or may establish
inhibitory contacts with residues in the N-terminal por-
tion of the protein. In either case, their removal very
likely reduces the ability of the CC domain to suppress
the enzymatic activity of the MO domain. The
p.Gly150Ser variant lies in the MO domain and affects a
highly conserved glycine residue, the substitution of
which with a polar serine may result in a local pertur-
bation of domain conformation that partially releases
inhibition by the C-terminal domain. As for the
p.Arg646Gln variant, it lies in the polypeptide linker
between the CH and LIM domains where functional
consequences of amino acid substitutions are hardly pre-
dictable. However, the relatively higher frequency of this
variant, particularly the fact that it was detected in a
healthy individual from a small group of geographically
matched controls, together with the uncertain clinical
classification of the corresponding affected family, and,
most important, the lack of a detectable effect of this
variant on MICAL-1 oxidoreductase activity cast doubt
on the ADLTE-causative role of this variant, which is
classifiable as a “variant of unknown significance.”
RVIS measures the intolerance of genes to sequence
variation. RVIS values are expressed as percentiles, the
most intolerant genes (those with a few sequence varia-
tions in a given population) having the lowest percentile
scores. Most Mendelian disease genes are classified within
the RVIS 25th percentile.
26 In the original RVIS classifi-
cation of genes based on the EVS population data set, 26
the MICAL1 score was 12.64%. Subsequent versions
based on larger population data, ExAC RVIS and ExAC
v2 RVIS (http://genic-intolerance.org/Search?query=MI-
CAL1), assigned to this gene much higher scores,
64.86% and 90.23%, respectively. This variability may
reflect the fact that the Mendelian disease genes classified
within the RVIS 25th percentile harbor mutations that
cause haploinsufficiency or have dominant-negative
effects (see Petrovski et al
26. Genes harboring gain-of-
function variants, like MICAL-1, might have a less
delimited range of RVIS values, which, in some cases,
might vary considerably depending on the reference pop-
ulation data set. In any case, rare variants in genes with
scores oscillating across the 25% threshold in different
RVIS versions should be further examined.
Accurate regulation of actin cytoskeleton is of cru-
cial importance for proper axonal guidance in the devel-
oping nervous system.
44 Semaphorin proteins are the
largest family of guidance cues that, through interaction
with plexin receptors, mediate actin cytoskeleton re-
modeling and, ultimately, axon repulsive signaling.
45
Sema1A/plexinA is a well-studied ligand-receptor com-
plex whose repulsive effect on growing axon is mediated
by F-actin disassembly induced by the monooxygenase
activity of MICAL, which interacts with the cytoplasmic
region of plexinA.
39,46 Within this context, MICAL-1
mutations that increase F-actin disassembly might result
in alteration of axonal pathfinding. Interestingly, the
Reelin pathway has been shown to regulate remodeling
of actin cytoskeleton by controlling the activity of cofilin,
a protein that depolymerize F-actin.
47 It has been shown
that Reelin signaling, through its receptor ApoER2, inac-
tivates cofilin by phosphorylation of serine 3, thereby sta-
bilizing the actin cytoskeleton by preventing F-actin
disassembly.
48 Stabilization of actin cytoskeleton is
important, during development, to provide stop signals
to extending neuronal processes and, in adult life, to
maintain stable neuronal connections and cortical archi-
tecture (reviewed in ref. Frotscher
49. We hypothesize that
both defective Reelin signaling and increased MICAL-1
oxidoreductase activity caused by mutations in the corre-
sponding genes may lead to increased F-actin disassembly
and cytoskeleton destabilization, ultimately resulting in
the same ADLTE phenotype. Understanding whether or
not there is an inter-relation between the Reelin and
MICAL-1 pathways will be a matter of future studies.
MICAL-1 exerts other functions in the nervous sys-
tem that are mediated by regulation of actin cytoskele-
ton. It has recently been shown that MICAL-1, through
its effects on F-actin remodeling, controls trafficking of
the immunoglobulin superfamily cell adhesion molecules
(IgCAMs) to the neuronal growth cone surface in post-
natal hippocampal neurons, thus defining a role for
IgCAMs in the regulation of lamina-restricted neuronal
connections.
50 Moreover, MICAL-1 likely plays a role in
other biological processes, because its C-terminal domain
can bind to several other molecules.
42,51 Nevertheless,
our findings from cell-based assays suggest that genetic
defects increasing MICAL-1 enzymatic activity may have
an impact on the organization of the actin cytoskeleton,

of the molecular mechanisms underlying ADLTE.
Linkage analysis has traditionally been performed
in large families to map statistically significant linkage
regions harboring disease-causative genes. In this work,
we made use of genome-wide linkage data to filter var-
iants resulting from whole exome sequencing of affected
members of individual families of small/medium size.
Namely, potentially deleterious rare variants lying in
chromosome regions with LOD scores lower than –2.0
were filtered out because they were highly unlikely to
segregate with the epileptic disorder in the corresponding
family, leaving a more manageable short list of candidate
variants in each family where to look for the causative
mutation. We found no potentially pathogenic variants
other than that identified in MICAL-1 in family F23
(Supplementary Table 3). Interestingly, in family F24 we
identified a unique variant, p.Ser908Phe, in the GRIN2A
gene, which is mutated in different epileptic phenotypes
ranging from the severe Landau-Kleffner syndrome and
continuous spike and wave during sleep to benign epi-
lepsy with centrotemporal spikes.
31–33 In a previous brief
report, we considered this as a possible pathogenic vari-
ant.
52 However, the fact that p.Ser908Phe lies in the
cytoplasmic region of the GRIN2A-encoded NMDA
channel subunit, where variants identified in epilepsy
families have no effects on NMDA channel functional-
ity,
34 makes a major pathogenic impact of this variant
improbable. It cannot be excluded, however, that this
variant may contribute to the development of ADLTE as
a susceptibility factor.
We found two pathogenic MICAL-1 variants in 28
families (7%) diagnosed with ADLTE. This should be
considered a preliminary estimate, given that a previous
study suggested that in a proportion of families, particu-
larly in those with a few affected individuals, ADLTE
may be inherited with a complex genetic pattern.
30 A
clear distinction between autosomal-dominant and com-
plex forms of familial lateral temporal epilepsy will be
possible when the genetic landscape of ADLTE will be
fully uncovered, which will also allow a precise assess-
ment of the impact of MICAL-1 as well as other major
genes on ADLTE.
In conclusion, we report the identification of var-
iants causing ADLTE in the MICAL-1 gene, encoding an
intracellular protein implicated in regulation of actin
cytoskeleton. Although its functional relationship with
LGI1 and RELN, encoding extracellular proteins, is
unknown, the discovery of this novel ADLTE-causing
gene identifies dysregulation of actin cytoskeleton dyna-
mics as an important molecular mechanism underlying
ADLTE.
