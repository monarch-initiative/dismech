# Classification of schwannomas and the new naming convention for "neurofibromatosis-2": Genetic updates and international consensus recommendation.
**Authors:** Rai P, Bathla G, Soni N, Desai A, Rao D, Vibhute P, Agarwal A
**Journal:** Neuroradiol J (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1177/19714009251313510](https://doi.org/10.1177/19714009251313510)

## Content

Despite their similar nomenclature, Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) and "Neurofibromatosis type 2" are discrete and clinically distinguishable entities. The name of "neurofibromatosis type 2" has been changed to NF2-related schwannomatosis, to reflect the fact that neurofibromas do not occur in this syndrome and therefore the name "Neurofibromatosis" is factually incorrect. Furthermore, multiple schwannomas, a hallmark feature of NF2, can also occur in patients with mutations in genes including SMARCB1 and LZTR1, all exhibiting overlapping clinical features. Current understanding suggests that schwannomatosis (SWN) encompasses a range of clinical presentations consisting of clearly defined, separate subtypes which share a common phenotype of schwannomas. Recognizing these newly emerging subtypes, the International Consensus Group on Neurofibromatosis Diagnostic Criteria (I-NF-DC) proposed a revised nomenclature for NF2 and related disorders in 2022. This review article focuses on this critical update in diagnostic terminology, highlighting the key gene-related SWN subtypes relevant to neuroradiologists. By emphasizing molecular testing alongside clinical features, the revised system facilitates a more precise diagnosis, potentially paving the way for personalized treatment strategies. Additionally, the flexible structure accommodates future discoveries of genes associated with SWN.

Introduction
Schwannomas represent benign tumors of the peripheral nerve sheath, composed of differentiated neoplastic schwann cells. The presence of multiple schwannomas is classified under the broader term schwannomatosis (SWN). Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) and SWN are genetically distinct tumor predisposition syndromes. Despite sharing autosomal dominant (AD) inheritance and a propensity for multiple schwannomas, NF2 exhibits distinct additional clinical features compared to SWN. Notably, NF2 patients characteristically present with bilateral vestibular schwannomas (VS). Additionally, they have a high lifetime risk of developing meningiomas (approaching 80%), and a significant proportion (20–30%) may also develop spinal cord ependymomas. Ophthalmic features are also predominant in NF2 patients with the presence of lenticular opacities, epiretinal membranes, and retinal hamartomas. 1
In NF2, loss-of-function mutations in the NF2 gene on chromosome 22q12.2 are the primary pathogenic event, 2 whereas SWN is seen in patients with germline mutations within the SMARCB1 or LZTR1 genes, also located on the same 22q centromeric to the NF2 gene.3,4 It is also notable that while patients with SWN lack germline mutations in the NF2 gene, tumors frequently exhibit somatic, tumor-specific mutations of NF2 and/or loss of the second NF2 allele. 3
Although a detailed exploration of the genetic underpinnings of NF2 is beyond the scope of this review article, a brief discussion regarding the same is warranted to underscore the recently revised nomenclature in the context of NF2. NF2 is a fully penetrant disorder and any individual with constitutional or germline pathogenic variant (PV) of NF2 will develop the clinical disorder, with an average age of symptom onset being 22 years. 1 NF2 tumorigenesis follows a two-hit model where the initial event involves germline inactivation of one NF2 allele, followed by somatic inactivation of the NF2 gene on the opposite chromosome (the trans allele). Post-zygotic mosaicism, characterized by a somatic mutation in the NF2 gene, is observed in roughly 60% of NF2 patients with unilateral VS and 33% of patients with bilateral VS.5,6 These mosaic cases may present with a later disease onset. 7
In contrast to NF2, SWN exhibits incomplete penetrance. 8 Germline SMARCB1 or LZTR1 mutations account for 70–80% of the familial SWN cases and only 30% of the sporadic cases. A 3-step/4-hit hypothesis is proposed for the tumorigenesis in SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related SWN. This process culminates in the biallelic inactivation of the culprit gene (either SMARCB1 or LZTR1) alongside the biallelic inactivation of NF2 within the tumor itself. 9 The absence of germline mutations in SMARCB1 or LZTR1 in 20–30% of familial SWN cases suggests the potential existence of additional genes yet to be implicated in the disease pathway. There is also a growing emphasis on testing one unaffected tissue (such as blood or saliva) along with at least two tumors for genetic diagnosis whenever possible as it has been found that some of these patients that meet diagnostic criteria for sporadic SWN may actually be cases of mosaic NF2.
The old terminology for NF2 and SWN was misleading since, unlike NF1, neither of these illnesses contained neurofibromas as a primary criterion, but schwannomas instead. Café-au-lait macules are present in just 1%–2% of NF2 patients, and the other NF1 diagnostic criteria do not exhibit higher incidence in NF2. 1 Furthermore, schwannomas or hybrid nerve sheath tumors are misdiagnosed as neurofibromas in some cases of schwannomatosis (SWN) patients, leading to an inaccurate NF1 diagnosis. 10 There is a great deal of phenotypic overlap between NF2 and SWN, which is the primary diagnostic challenge.
Furthermore, although bilateral VS is a hallmark feature of NF2, unilateral VS can also occur in this condition. Intriguingly, there have been documented cases of unilateral VS accompanied by two or more intradermal schwannomas in patients with SWN 11 and harboring germline mutations in LZTR1. 12 Meningioma development has also been reported in association with SMARCB1-related SWN. 13 The significant overlap between mosaic NF2, early NF2, and SWN necessitated a recent update in diagnostic criteria and nomenclature for NF2 which was undertaken by the International Consensus Group on Neurofibromatosis Diagnostic Criteria (I-NF-DC) in 2022. The multistep procedure carried out over the previous few years by this international committee of NF experts resulted in the new criteria. It involved both in-person meetings and the Delphi method, which employed electronic questionnaires to gather information. 8
The continued naming of NF2 as a Neurofibromatosis only causes confusion with NF1 even amongst the medical profession and as such the term “Neurofibromatosis-2” has been retired in the revised nomenclature to improve diagnostic specificity. This review article explores these recent updates in the context of neuroradiology and their clinical implications.
Revised nomenclature for Neurofibromatosis-2
Traditionally, the diagnosis of NF2 and SWN relied primarily on clinical features. However, recent advances have revealed that there are multiple distinct subtypes of disease which share a common phenotype. To acknowledge the observed phenotypic spectrum, the updated diagnostic criteria incorporate these diverse presentations under the umbrella term “Schwannomatosis.” 8
The addition of genetic factors to support clinical criteria was the most important modification to the diagnostic criteria for NF2 and SWN. The new diagnostic criteria now place a strong emphasis on defining the genetic criteria due to the intricacy of the subject. Additional modifications for NF2 include the inclusion of major and minor criteria as well as the definition of important clinical characteristics such as cataracts, neurofibromas, ependymomas, and epiretinal membranes. 8
Furthermore, the revised nomenclature proposes classifying the specific type of SWN based on the gene harboring a pathogenic variant (PV) identified through molecular analysis. Following this system, the specific type would be denoted as “GENE-related schwannomatosis” (gene name in italics). Consequently, under this updated system:
• Neurofibromatosis-2: renamed as “NF2-related schwannomatosis.”
• Schwannomatosis with SMARCB1 PV: classified as “SMARCB1-related schwannomatosis.”
• Schwannomatosis with LZTR1 PV: classified as “LZTR1-related schwannomatosis.”
• Schwannomatosis in patients with multiple schwannomas and common molecular findings on chromosome 22q: classified as “22q-related schwannomatosis.”
• Schwannomatosis in patients with clinical features suggestive of NF2/SWN but lacking molecular analysis: classified as “Schwannomatosis—not otherwise specified (NOS).”
• Schwannomatosis in patients with negative molecular analysis in both blood and tumor tissue: classified as “Schwannomatosis—not elsewhere classified (NEC).”
This refined nomenclature highlights the importance of comprehensive molecular genetic testing in suspected cases of NF2 or SWN. Such testing may necessitate analysis of multiple tissues, including tumor tissue when available and feasible. Retiring the term “NF2” will also avoid misdiagnosis with Neurofibromatosis type 1. Additionally, the nomenclature would also allow additions as new genes related to the SWN spectrum are discovered.
The current update by the I-NF-DC incorporates the genetic criteria to supplement the clinical criteria to help standardize the diagnostic process. The updated diagnostic criteria include the use of major and minor criteria for diagnosing NF2-related SWN, clarification of first-degree relative with “other than sibling,” modification of key clinical features such as the addition of ependymoma, removal of neurofibroma and glioma, clarification of cataracts as juvenile cataract, and lastly, the addition of retinal hamartomas as a criterion. For SWN, the clinical update focuses on incorporating new histological entities such as hybrid nerve sheath tumors into the criteria (Table 1 and Table 2). The 2022 update also provides clarification for diagnostic criteria for mosaic schwannomatosis.

Genetic classification of schwannomatosis
NF2-related schwannomatosis
With an incidence of approximately 1 in 25,000, NF2-related SWN exhibits a distinct clinical presentation compared to sporadic schwannomas. 14 Notably, NF2-related SWN typically manifests in younger patients, with tumor onset occurring in the third decade of life on average. In contrast, sporadic schwannomas are more commonly present in the sixth decade.15,16 The survival period after diagnosis is about 15 years, with a 10-year survival rate being approximately 67%. 14
The most frequent clinical manifestations include hearing loss, tinnitus, and balance dysfunction, likely secondary to the bilateral VS which is a characteristic of this mixed neuro-cutaneous disorder. High-resolution MRI demonstrates multiple discreet tumor nodules along both the superior and inferior vestibular nerve branches, as well as along the cochlear and facial nerves 17 (Figure 1). Schwannomas associated with NF2 are often characterized by intratumoral heterogeneity, manifesting as a coalescence of multiple nodules or distinct schwannomas within a single tumor mass. Each of these nodules may harbor unique somatic NF2 mutations or deletions. 18 This intratumoral heterogeneity is hypothesized to contribute to the lower rates of surgical efficacy and higher rates of surgical complications observed in NF2-related VS compared to sporadic VS. 19 Schwannomas may also arise from the other cranial nerves, spinal roots (especially the lumbar region) (Figure 2), or peripheral nerves and are often of the plexiform subtype. Intracutaneous schwannomas like in NF1 may also be occasionally seen. 20

Meningiomas represent the second most prevalent tumor type observed in NF2-related SWN, surpassing sporadic meningiomas in terms of earlier onset. Notably, meningiomas can even be the presenting feature, particularly in pediatric patients.1,21 They have a predilection for the intracranial compartment (including along the falx cerebri) than the spinal compartments (Figure 1). Spinal meningiomas are characteristically dumbbell-shaped, arising from the dorsal roots, with portions extending through the neural foramina (Figure 2).
Spinal ependymomas, the most commonly diagnosed gliomas in NF2-related SWN, may occur as multiple intramedullary, slow-growing, asymptomatic masses 1 (Figure 2). Neuropathies are gaining recognition as an increasingly frequent manifestation of NF2-related SWN. Notably, mononeuropathy can be the presenting feature in children with progressive polyneuropathy being more commonly observed in adults. 22 Juvenile posterior cataracts are highly characteristic of NF2-related SWN. Other ophthalmological manifestations may include retinal abnormalities such as hamartomas, tufts, and epiretinal membranes. 23
Cortical and intracortical abnormalities are also emerging as a feature of increasing interest within the realm of NF2-related SWN. Meningioangiomatosis is a distinct pathological finding characterized by plaque-like proliferations of perivascular meningothelial cells and fibroblast-like cells within the cortex. In contrast to the sporadic form, its asymptomatic and multifocal presentation observed in NF2-related SWN typically comes to light only during autopsy 24 (Figure 3). The presence of glial hamartias within the neocortex is another pathognomonic feature specific to NF2-related SWN. These circumscribed collections of glial cells are not typically associated with intellectual disability or the development of gliomas. 25

In NF2-related SWN, tumorigenesis is attributed to loss-of-function mutations within the NF2 gene which encodes merlin, a 70-kDa tumor suppressor protein that participates in various cell signaling pathways. A distinct genotype-phenotype correlation exists between the type and severity of NF2-related SWN, ranging from the mildest phenotype (Gardner) to the severest (Wishart). The UK NF2 Genetic Severity Score (GSS) was established to categorize patients into severity groups (severe, moderate, and mild) based on a combination of clinical manifestations and their underlying genotypes 26 (Figure 4).

SMARCB1-related and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis
Both of these are AD disorders with a combined prevalence of one in 126,000. 27 Patients have a predisposition to develop multiple non-intradermal schwannomas in their early adult years (between the second and fourth decade of life), with an average age of symptom onset being 22 years, and the median age of diagnosis being 40 years.13,27 These schwannomas often affect the peripheral or spinal nerves with the lumbar spine being most commonly affected. 28 While cranial nerve involvement is uncommon, the cranial nerve most commonly affected is the trigeminal. 29 Patients exhibit incomplete penetrance, the reason for which is unclear. 8
Epithelioid schwannomas may arise in the setting of SMARBC1-related and LZTR1-related SWN and a mosaic pattern of INI1/SMARCB1 protein expression on immunostaining may help in narrowing down the differential diagnosis in these subtypes of tumors.30,31
The most common presenting complaint in individuals with SMARBC1-related and LZTR1-related SWN is the presence of diffuse or localized pain or an asymptomatic mass. Interestingly, chronic pain can be a prominent feature and may not necessarily correlate with the location of the schwannomas. Furthermore, the total number, size, and specific location of the tumors do not seem to predict the severity of pain experienced by patients. This suggests a potential genetic contribution to the underlying pain mechanisms in this condition. 13 Rarely, focal weakness or muscle atrophy may be a presenting sign. 32
Beyond SWN, mutations in the SMARCB1 gene contribute to a spectrum of distinct genetic disorders, including rhabdoid tumor predisposition syndrome (RTPS) and Coffin-Siris syndrome. Emerging evidence suggests a genotype-phenotype correlation, with the type of SMARCB1 mutation influencing the specific clinical presentation. Patients with RTPS typically harbor truncating germline mutations in the central part of the gene, while familial SWN is associated with predominantly non-truncating mutations, often located at the 5′ or 3′ ends of the gene. Individuals without a family history of SWN are also more likely to harbor truncating mutations. 33 Conversely, Coffin-Siris syndrome manifests with gain-of-function missense mutations clustered specifically within the C-terminal region of the SMARCB1 protein. 34
Meningiomas have been reported in SMARCB1-related SWN with an incidence of 5%. 35 Schwannomas associated with SMARCB1 mutations exhibit an increased propensity for malignant transformation, necessitating close clinical surveillance in these patients. Rapid schwannoma growth and intractable pain can serve as potential indicators of malignant progression 36 (Figure 5).

Individuals with LZTR1-related SWN may present with unilateral vestibular schwannomas (VS). However, the presence of bilateral VS is a definitive exclusion criterion for this diagnosis. This is because patients with bilateral VS meet the diagnostic criteria for NF2-related SWN.37,38 Compared to SMARCB1-related SWN, LZTR1-related SWN exhibits a higher prevalence of spinal schwannomas 39 (Figure 6). Additionally, Farschtschi et al. reported an increased incidence of segmental schwannomatosis (defined by schwannomas confined to a single limb or a few adjacent spinal segments) within the LZTR1-associated population 40 (Figure 7). Pain and pain-associated quality of life also tend to be worse in these patients. 39

Conclusion
This review examines the recently revised schwannomatosis classification system, which now categorizes patients based on the identified germline mutation (NF2, SMARCB1, or LZTR1). This shift from a subtype-based approach highlights the importance of comprehensive molecular testing, including analysis of both tumor tissue and unaffected tissue (e.g., blood and saliva).
The revised system acknowledges the significant clinical overlap observed between different SWN subtypes. It facilitates a more precise diagnosis, potentially paving the way for tailored treatment strategies. This collaborative approach, integrating clinical and genetic criteria, standardizes the diagnostic process and allows for future expansion as additional SWN-associated genes are identified.
