# Toxicological assessment of E-cigarette flavored E-liquids aerosols using Calu-3 cells: A 3D lung model approach.
**Authors:** Effah F, Adragna J, Luglio D, Bailey A, Marczylo T, Gordon T
**Journal:** Toxicology (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.tox.2023.153683](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tox.2023.153683)

## Content

Scientific progress and ethical considerations are increasingly shifting the toxicological focus from in vivo animal models to in vitro studies utilizing physiologically relevant cell cultures. Consequently, we evaluated and validated a three-dimensional (3D) model of the human lung using Calu-3 cells cultured at an air-liquid interface (ALI) for 28 days. Assessment of seven essential genes of differentiation and transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements, in conjunction with mucin (MUC5AC) staining, validated the model. We observed a time-dependent increase in TEER, genetic markers of mucus-producing cells (muc5ac, muc5b), basal cells (trp63), ciliated cells (foxj1), and tight junctions (tjp1). A decrease in basal cell marker krt5 levels was observed. Subsequently, we utilized this validated ALI-cultured Calu-3 model to investigate the adversity of the aerosols generated from three flavored electronic cigarette (EC) e-liquids: cinnamon, vanilla tobacco, and hazelnut. These aerosols were compared against traditional cigarette smoke (3R4F) to assess their relative toxicity. The aerosols generated from PG/VG vehicle control, hazelnut and cinnamon e-liquids, but not vanilla tobacco, significantly decreased TEER and increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release compared to the incubator and air-only controls. Compared to 3R4F, there were no significant differences in TEER or LDH with the tested flavored EC aerosols other than vanilla tobacco. This starkly contrasted our expectations, given the common perception of e-liquids as a safer alternative to cigarettes. Our study suggests that these results depend on flavor type. Therefore, we strongly advocate for further research, increased user awareness regarding flavors in ECs, and rigorous regulatory scrutiny to protect public health.

Tobacco smoking is the leading cause of preventable deaths worldwide, according to a 2019 World Health Organization report. Cigarette smoke contains around 7,000 chemicals, as noted by the National Center for Chronic Disease Prevention and Health Promotion (US) Office on Smoking and Health, (2014) , and 93 of these substances, including nicotine, have been identified as potentially harmful ( FDA, 2012 ). The addictive nature of nicotine results in tobacco dependency, and nicotine replacement therapies (NRTs) have been employed to aid in smoking cessation. Traditional NRTs including patches and gum can take a while to reach their effective concentration in the bloodstream, limiting their popularity and effectiveness. Electronic cigarettes (ECs) or electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) offer the most successful smoking cessation aid in the UK ( ASH, 2022b ). These battery-operated devices vaporize a solution containing nicotine, providing a faster and more efficient delivery system to the brain, akin to traditional cigarettes. The e-liquids used in EC contain propylene glycol (PG) and/or vegetable glycerine (VG), possibly with nicotine and flavoring agents. Besides delivering nicotine, ECs provide sensory stimulation and behavioral cues, which can help alleviate withdrawal symptoms, reduce cravings, and decrease the likelihood of a relapse, according to a study by Wadgave and Nagesh, (2016) .

While ECs are generally seen as less harmful than traditional cigarettes ( McNeill et al., 2022 , 2018 ), it is unclear how much harm EC use causes compared to non-use, especially among long-term former smokers. There are also concerns about ECs use among young people, and the potential for progression to traditional tobacco smoking. These concerns have led to regulatory restrictions in many countries, including parts of the US, Australia, Ukraine, and Finland. Notably, there was a 3% increase in EC use among 11 to 17-year-olds in the UK between 2020 and 2022 ( ASH, 2022a ). The flavors in ECs play a crucial role in attracting both young people and former smokers to EC use. A complete ban on flavors is therefore undesirable from a harm reduction perspective ( McNeill et al., 2022 ).

While the flavoring compounds in e-liquids are generally regarded safe for consumption and are widely used in the food industry, there are concerns about their potential toxicity when inhaled through ECs ( Farsalinos et al., 2013 ; Khlystov and Samburova, 2016 ; Leigh et al., 2016 ; Sundar et al., 2016 ). Safety evidence concerning inhalation is not extensive. Several studies have reported potential health risks from exposure to flavored e-liquids, such as inhibited cell growth, and increased cytotoxicity, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction ( Effah et al., 2022b ). Nonetheless, we have previously reported that these studies frequently; 1) lack appropriate controls that enable identification of hazards due specifically to flavor components, 2) ambiguity over physiologically relevant doses of flavor/e-liquid used, and 3) no demonstration of a dose-dependent relationship (for an extensive review see ( Effah et al., 2022b ) and references therein). In a recent study, we adopted a high-throughput experimental approach to screening cytotoxicity in a wide range of flavored, nicotine-free e-liquids (n=53) ( Effah et al., 2022a ). In that study, we identified that out of 53 nicotine-free e-liquids, only 3 (i.e. cinnamon, hazelnut and vanilla tobacco) revealed dose-dependent toxicity independent of the PG/VG component in human bronchial epithelial cells (HBEC-3KT) in submerged cultures. Although submerged cultures are widely used, they have several limitations. In this configuration, cells were covered entirely by diluted e-liquids in culture medium, offering a non-physiological environment for lung cells that, under normal conditions, are exposed to air on the apical side. This discrepancy can affect cellular behavior, morphological characteristics, and responses to e-liquids, rendering the model less predictive for in vivo situations ( Barrila et al., 2010 ). Also, heating e-liquids during vaping may release additional toxicants such as volatile organic compounds, breakdown products, and condensation products that could increase adversity. Here, we used Calu-3 cells grown at air-liquid interface (ALI) to assess the toxicological effects of the three flavors we identified in the previous high throughput submerged culture study. The Calu-3 cell line, derived from human bronchial submucosal glands, is a highly relevant model for pulmonary toxicology when grown in ALI culture systems ( Jeong et al., 2019 ; Ji et al., 2016 ; Pasman et al., 2021 ; Sakagami, 2020 ) because they can form tight junctions with robust barrier integrity, essential for creating a relevant lung model (Figure 1), they are widely used in the pharmaceutical industries to study pulmonary drug permeability profiles, significantly impacting the understanding and optimization of drug formulations ( Ong et al., 2013 ; Sibinovska et al., 2020 ). Although several studies have validated Calu-3 cells grown at ALI for 21 days as an appropriate 3D lung model by measuring the transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER), the expression of tight junction proteins, and mucus production as markers for differentiation, none of the studies have investigated them beyond this time-frame or investigated the changes that occur within the genes that encode these markers over the weeks the cells are grown at ALI. Therefore, this study had two main aims. The first aim was to evaluate Calu-3 cells beyond the typical 21 days at ALI by (1) measuring TEER, (2) measuring the expression of genetic markers of differentiation for goblet cell (muc5ac and muc5b), ciliated cell markers (foxj1), and tight junction (tjp1/zo-1), and basal markers (trp63 and itga6) ( Hewitt and Lloyd, 2021 ; Rock et al., 2009 ) using qPCR and how these change over 28 days at ALI, (3) immunofluorescence for mucins production with digital confocal microscopy. The second aim was to assess the effects of the three flavored e-liquids (cinnamon, hazelnut, and vanilla tobacco) identified in ( Effah et al., 2022a ) on the ALI-validated Calu-3 cells by assessing their effects on 1) reactive oxygen species (ROS) in submerged exposures, 2) cytotoxicity and TEER measurements in both suspension exposures and aerosol exposures.

The human bronchial epithelial cell line Calu-3 (ATCC) was cultured in advanced Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, GIBCO), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA, GIBCO), 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (GIBCO) and 10 mM Hepes. Cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. 0.25% Trypsin EDTA (GIBCO) was added during passaging, and cells incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 3-5 minutes. The agitation of the flask detached the cells, which could be observed using the light microscope. Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (GIBCO) containing 5% FBS (GIBCO) was added to neutralise the Trypsin EDTA before the cell suspension was entrifuged at 1000rpm for 5 minutes. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in the appropriate growth media. A small sample was added to trypan blue (Sigma-Aldrich) in a 1:1 ratio and placed into a haemocytometer for a viable cell count. Cells were seeded at 2 x 106 per T175 flask for the next passage.

6 well transwells were used for ALI (ThermoFischer Scientific, US) and transwell inserts were coated in 10μg/cm3 collagen (Advanced BioMatric PureCol bovine collagen solution, Type 1). Calu-3 cells were seeded at passage 3 into PET transwells with 0.4 μm pores and 24 mm thickness (Corning) at 5x105 cells/well. The media used to culture all cells during expansion was previously described (section 2.1). Confluence was observed using a light microscope after 48h. All media was removed from cells once confluence was reached. Once at an ALI, the DMEM in the basal compartment was replaced every 2-3 days whilst the transwells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. ALI cultures used for validation were maintained for up to 28 days, and cells were harvested with Qiazol (stored at −80°C until RNA extraction) every seven days. The ALI cultures that were used to assess the toxicity of cinnamon, vanilla tobacco and hazelnut flavored e-liquids were exposed to either 1% (V/V) of the e-liquid diluted with ALI maintenance media for 1.5h or to the aerosols of these for 0.5h. Aerosol was generated using an in-house electronic cigarette aerosol generator (ECAG) following the CORESTA protocol i.e., a 5s puff with a 30s interval for 0.5h totalling 60 puffs. The EC aerosol generator was set to puff every 30 s at 12W, and the coil resistance of the cartridges used for studies varied between 1.8 and 2.0Ω. Cigarette smoke was generated with the cigarette smoke generator. ALI cultures were placed in the Thermolyne incubator (37°C), where aerosol from the ECAG driven by the air pump (4mL/min) was dispersed in the atmosphere of the incubator allowing the particles to settle down on the apical region (Figure 2). The amount of aerosol reaching the apical region of ALI cultures was characterized by measuring the mass difference between pre-exposure and post-exposure of a glass slide placed in an empty transwell at the same level as cells. The masses of e-liquids deposited were: PG/VG (60/40, 1.21 mg), cinnamon (0.11 mg), hazelnut (0.16 mg), and vanilla tobacco (0.8 mg). One entire cigarette was used to generate cigarette smoke (~12 puffs) and exposed to cells for 0.5h.

Transepithelial Electrical Resistance (TEER) barrier integrity measures tight junction formation between cells ( Srinivasan, et al., 2015 ). Basal media was removed every 2-3 days, and PBS was placed in both the apical and basal compartments, allowing TEER measurements to be recorded using an Evo meter (World Precision Instruments). Adhering to the manufacturer's protocol, the PBS was warmed to equilibrate in the incubator before measurements were recorded. Values were corrected by subtracting the measurement of the blank transwell (PBS without cells) and multiplying by the effective membrane area (4.67 cm2), resulting in measurements related to Ωcm2 ( Srinivasan et al., 2015 ). PBS was removed after TEER measurements, and ALI culture conditions were restored with ALI Medium in the basal compartment. For exposures, TEERs were taken before and after either 1.5 h exposure to e-liquid in suspension culture or 0.5h of aerosol exposure at ALI.

The genetic expression of differentiation markers is paramount to validate 3D models of the microenvironment of the human lung. Therefore, every 7 days, 700μl QIAzol lysis reagent was added to Calu-3 cells in the transwell chamber for 3 min, the lysate was collected and homogenized using a 5mL syringe, and 25gauge needle by aspiration, repeated 10 times. The homogenate was incubated at room temperature for 5 min to promote cell dissociation of nucleoprotein complexes. Then, 140μl chloroform was added, vortexed, and incubated for a further 2 min at room temperature. The cell suspension was then centrifuged at 12,000g, for 15 min at 4°C. The upper colourless aqueous phase was transferred to a 2 mL Qiagen collection tube. The following steps were performed by the QIAcube, (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands). 525μl of 100% ethanol was added and subsequently vortexed. 700μl of the sample was transferred into an RNeasy mini spin column and centrifuged at 8,000g for 15 min at 20°C. This step was repeated. Subsequently, 500μl RPE buffer was added to the RNeasy mini spin column and was centrifuged for 2 min at 8,000g. The RNeasy mini spin column was transferred to a new 1.5 ml collection tube, and 50 μl RNase-free water was added and subsequently centrifuged for 1 min at 8,000g to elute the RNA. RNA purity was calculated using the nanodrop 2000/c spectrophotometer (Thermo-Scientific) from the 260/280 nm and 260/230 nm absorbance ratios.

Up to 1μg of cDNA was synthesized using a deoxyribose-containing nucleoside triphosphate (dNTP) solution-based protocol using the cDNA synthesis kit, from Applied Biosystems (Thermo-Scientific). Initially, serial cDNA dilutions were used to validate the primers of marker genes and genes of interest. Marker genes were used to indicate the differentiation of Calu-3 cells to specific cell types in the ALI cultures. Expression was analyzed in triplicate 10μl reactions containing 1μl cDNA, 5μl Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), 3μl nuclease-free water and 0.5μl of 10μM forward and reverse primers of the appropriate endogenous control (gapdh), tight junctions (tjp1), basal cell (itga6 and trp63) goblet cell (muc5ac and muc5b) or ciliated cell markers (foxj1). All primers were purchased from Thermo-Scientific. The ΔΔCt method ( Haimes and Kelley, 2014 ) was used in the calculations. Some primers used in the in vitro study were validated before use. The primers of differentiation are seen in Table 1.

On day 28 of Calu-3 grown at ALI, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 0.5h. The cells were permeabilized by incubating with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 0.25h. Cells were washed 3 times with PBS. Cells were blocked with 2% BSA (diluted in PBS) for 1h at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were washed 3x with PBS. Cells were incubated with primary antibody (mouse monoclonal antibody, ThermoScientific, UK) at 2 μg/mL concentration/dilution in 0.5% BSA (diluted in PBS), for 3h at room temperature. Cells were washed 3x with PBS. Cells were incubated with a secondary antibody (rabbit polyclonal antibody, Alexa Fluor 488, ThermoScientific, UK) at 1:1000 concentration/dilution in 5% BSA (diluted in PBS), for 1h at room temperature. The antibody solution was aspirated, and cells were counter-stained with 6 μM Hoechst 33342 (DAPI) (ThermoScientific, UK). The cells were washed 3x with PBS, and images were taken with excitation/emission of 499/520 nm with the Z-stacking option on ImageXpress Pico digital confocal microscopy (Molecular Devices, UK) aided by CellReporterXpress software (Molecular Devices, UK).

In this assay, we aimed to characterize whether cinnamon, hazelnut and vanilla tobacco-flavored e-liquids stimulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in Calu-3 cells in submerged cultures. Calu-3 cells were seeded at 6x104 cells in black-walled 96 well plates with clear bottoms. Once cells reached confluence, they were treated with 1.5 mM of cell-permeant 2',7'-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 98% humidity for 0.5h. Subsequently, cells were washed x2 with Dulbecco’s phosphate buffer saline (DPBS) and challenged with diluted e-liquids (diluted with DMEM/F-12, HEPES, no phenol red (Gibco, UK)) or 2% hydrogen peroxide (positive control) for 3h. Fluorescence intensity was measured using the Spectramax M2e microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, California) at 495 nm excitation and 530 nm emission wavelengths.

After 1.5h of flavored e-liquid direct exposure or 0.5h of aerosol exposure to ALI cultures, maintenance media was replaced, and cells were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 98% humidity for 24h. Subsequently, spent media was removed from the basal region of the transwells and retained for lactase dehydrogenase (LDH) assay using the LDH cytotoxicity kit (Roche, UK) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Plate absorbance was measured at 490 nm and 680nm using the BIO-TEK synergy HT absorbance reader (Bio-Tek, USA).

As detailed here, a sampling train was set up to characterise the particle size distribution of the e-cig aerosols. The exhaust from an e-cigarette aerosol generator (eAerosols, Central Valley NY) was attached to an inlet of an airtight, plastic exposure chamber. A Mercer-style impactor (Model SMEIMA7, CH Technologies, Westwood, NJ, USA), in-line with a Gast pump (Model 1023-101Q-6Q608X, Benton Harbor, MI, USA), was attached to another open port of the container. The pump's flow rate with the impactor was set to 4.0 L/min utilizing an external valve and checked with a DryCal air flow calibrator (Mesa Laboratories, Lakewood, CO, USA). The EC cartridges were loaded with each flavor [i.e. PG/VG (60/40), cinnamon, hazelnut and vanilla tobacco]. The EC aerosol generator was set to puff every 30 s at 12W, and the coil resistance of the cartridges used for studies varied between 1.8 and 2.0Ω. The machine puffed into the container/chamber for 2 minutes before the Gast pump was turned on for 2 minutes. Each stage of the Mercer impactor was weighed before and after each sample in a humidity- and temperature-controlled weighing chamber (MTL, Minneapolis, MN, USA) with a microbalance (Model MT5, 1 μg readability; Mettler-Toledo, Columbus, OH). Each aerosol's size distribution was calculated from the mass distribution collected across each stage.

Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA for each experiment. Where one-way ANOVA revealed significance, a Levenes’ variance test was performed to test for differences in variance and to inform the choice of Tukey HSD post-hoc test corrected for multiple comparisons. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9. Cinnamon, vanilla tobacco, and hazelnut assays were repeated at least twice with six technical replicates on different days. Data are expressed as mean ± SD, and the p-value was set at p < 0.05.

The genes of differentiation that were characterized were for mucus-production (muc5ac, muc5b Figure 3A), cilia formation (foxj1, Figure 3B), a tight junction marker (tjp1/zo-1, Figure 3C), and basal cell marker (itga6 and trp63 Figure 3D). We observed a time-dependent increase in muc5b, muc5ac, foxj1, and tpj1/zo-1 gene expression, with the highest expression on day 28 at ALI. In contrast, we observed a time-dependent decrease in itga6 till day 28. Interestingly, for trp63, there was a decline in gene expression from day 0 till day 21, followed by an increase in expression till day 28, returning to day 0 level expression. TEER measurements (Figure 3E) increased significantly from less than 1000 Ωcm2 to about 2500 Ωcm2 over the first 6 days at ALI. Between days 6 and 24, no further increase in TEER was observed until between days 24 and 28, when there was a second rapid increase in TEER, from 1800 Ωcm2 to 3000 Ωcm2. The presence of muc5ac-producing mucins was observed on day 28 at ALI (Figure 3F).

TEER measurement in Figure 4A is the relative TEER value obtained from the difference between post-exposure TEER and pre-exposure TEER after ALI-cultured Calu-3 cells were exposed directly to dilutions of e-liquids for 1.5h on the apical region. Interestingly, the relative TEER for PG/VG (p<0.01), cinnamon (p<0.001), and vanilla tobacco (p<0.05), all at 1%, were significantly different from the relative TEER of media-only control. Hazelnut, however, showed no difference compared to media-only control but was significantly different from PG/VG control. Although LDH release in all groups was slightly higher than in media-only control, there was no significant difference between groups for LDH release. The ROS analysis (Figure 4C) showed no significant difference between PG/VG, vanilla tobacco, and hazelnut at 0.25, 0.5, and 1% vs media-only control. In contrast, cinnamon significantly dose-dependently decreased ROS generation at 0.25% (p<0.05), 0.5% (p<0.001), and 1% (p<0.001) vs media-only and at 0.5% (p<0.05) and 1% (p<0.01) vs PG/VG controls.

This study investigated the relative toxicity of cinnamon, hazelnut, and vanilla tobacco-flavored e-liquids on 21-day ALI-cultured Calu-3 cells compared to a 3R4F Kentucky cigarette positive control. Moreover, a PG/VG vehicle and an air-only control were included for a comprehensive assessment. As expected, we noted that the air-only control had no significant impact on the TEER measurements, maintaining the cell barrier integrity, thus underlining the appropriateness of the exposure period (Figure 5A). TEER measurements for the vanilla tobacco-flavored e-liquid exposure group were unaffected. However, it was found that the PG/VG vehicle, cinnamon, and hazelnut-flavoured e-liquids each exhibited a significant decrease in TEER measurements (p<0.0001) compared to the incubator and air-only controls. These results were not statistically distinguishable from the TEER reduction induced by the 3R4F Kentucky cigarette control. LDH release, a marker of cellular damage and cytotoxicity, was used to ascertain further the effects of e-liquid exposure. It was noted that all groups except for the vanilla tobacco exhibited a significant increase in LDH release (Figure 5B) compared to incubator control and compared to air-only controls, thus corroborating the TEER measurements.

We hypothesized that particle size distribution might play a key role in explaning why vanilla tobacco e-liquid but not aerosol adversely affect TEER. Specifically, it was postulated that vanilla tobacco might have a distinct particle size distribution that limits deposition when compared to the cytotoxic e-liquids (cinnamon and hazelnut). We analysed particle size distribution across the tested e-liquids to test this hypothesis. Vanilla tobacco (Figure 5E) demonstrated a particle size distribution profile similar to that of the PG/VG (60/40) vehicle (Figure 5C), with the highest percentage of particle size being particles with PM <0.99 μm at 74.3% and 75.9% respectively. The particle size distribution for hazelnut (Figure 5D) and cinnamon-flavored (Figure 5F) e-liquids was to larger particles with the majority of PM being >1μm for hazelneu (79.9%) and cinnamon (84.6%), respectively. This suggests that e-liquid flavours affect the size distribution of the generated aerosol and that adversity of certain flavours may be related to larger aerosol size and increased deposition.

This study aimed to evaluate the utility of the Calu-3 human bronchial epithelial cell culture model as a pre-screening mechanism for the inhalation toxicity of EC aerosols. The airway epithelial cells situated on the respiratory tract surface often serve as the initial protective barrier against the respiratory xenobiotics ( LeMessurier et al., 2020 ). Although several lung cell culture models are available, none are considered perfect. The Calu-3 cell line, derived from human bronchial adenocarcinoma and akin to respiratory epithelia, is a widely used in vitro model in academic and industry research for examining inhalation drugs’ pharmaco-toxicology ( Ong et al., 2013 ). This cell line has been extensively employed to study the transport, and metabolism of numerous therapeutic compounds, especially those for cystic fibrosis, due to its relatively high expression of the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) protein ( Haws et al., 1994 ), and the interaction between particles and cells ( Foster et al., 2000 ). A key advantage of using Calu-3 cells, as demonstrated in this study, is their ability to replicate the biological behavior of the human respiratory tract upon exposure to airborne chemicals. Thus, Calu-3 cells are apt for screening respiratory irritation and toxicity ( Ihekwereme et al., 2014 ).

Our study aimed at developing an in vitro model to predict the acute inhalation toxicity of three flavoring chemicals using Calu-3 epithelium. The study began with validating the cell culture model to confirm that the air-liquid interface (ALI) cultured cell layers display the expected morphological and bioelectrical characteristics, as previously reported. Under ALI culture conditions, mucus secretion and tight junction formation were confirmed. We observed a time-dependent increase in TEER values. The optimum window of bioelectric properties in several studies has differed; for example, Ji et al., (2016) , after culturing Calu-3 cells at ALI for 22 days, observed this window between days 8 to 16, Jeong et al., (2019) only cultured the Calu-3 cells for 8 days. They observed the highest TEER values around day 8, whereas Foster et al., (2000) who cultured Calu-3 at ALI for 14 days and observed an increase in TEER from day 6 till 14. Our study agrees with Ji et al., (2016) as we observed an increase in TEER measurement, which initially peaked at day 6 and then plateud until day 24. Interestingly, we observed a rapid increase in TEER values between day 24 and 28. Lee et al., (2021) also cultured Calu-3 cells at ALI for 28 days and observed the highest TEER at day 28, although the increment in TEER value was strictly time-dependent in their study. It is unclear why the discrepancy between these studies; however, factors that may influence these results include but are not limited to the passage number of the cells used at ALI, culture medium, and seeding density.

TEER values offer insight into the physical structure and quality of the cells on the porous membrane by gauging the transcellular and paracellular resistance across the membrane, which amplifies with cell-substrate and cell-cell interaction ( Chen et al., 2015 ). Therefore, the expression of high TEER values we observed in this study may be a result of the time-dependent increase in the tjp1/zo-1, muc5ac, muc5b and foxj1 genes and the time-dependent decrease in trp63 and itaga6. However, the expression trp63 followed an interesting pattern. It declined from day 0 till day 21, followed by an increase in expression till day 28, returning to day 0 level expression. trp63 encodes for the protein p63, pivotal in basal cells generating and maintaining the pseudostratified bronchial epithelium in vitro ( Arason et al., 2014 ). Moreover, in human bronchial epithelial, where trp63 gene was knocked down, failed to differentiate into goblet cells and showed non-phenotypical bronchial basal cells when grown at ALI ( Arason et al., 2014 ). As such, the initial decrease in the basal cell trp63 gene we observed in this study might have occurred during differentiation from basal cells into more specialized bronchial epithelial cells. However, once a full barrier has been achieved, trp63 levels may have been restored to baseline levels to regulate and maintain homeostasis. This may corroborate the second rapid increase in TEER values from day 24 to 28. Indeed, our findings agree with Felix et al., (2021) , who showed an increase in TEER values in caco-2 cells cultured at a liquid-liquid interface, followed by a decrease during differentiation and an increase after a full barrier. Taken together, the results from these differentiation genes and the TEER values indicate that the longer Calu-3 cells are grown at ALI, the more they differentiate into several other bronchial epithelial cells (i.e submucosal glands, goblet cells, ciliated cells, basal cells) to enable the recapitulation of the in vivo microenvironment of human lung epithelium. No study has reported beating cilia in Calu-3 cells grown at ALI. Although we observed a 1.5-fold increase in the foxj1 gene on day 28 compared to day 0, no visible cilia were observed under light microscopy. Conversely, functional cilia have been shown in primary human bronchial epithelial cells grown at ALI for 28 days ( Leung et al., 2020 ). This may indicate that functional cilia are not expressed in Calu-3 cells or a better visualization technique is required to view them. Moreover, the high expression of muc5b compared to muc5ac over the 28 days agrees with the literature as Calu-3 cells derive from human bronchial submucosal glands, which are the main source of MUC5B ( Liegeois and Fahy, 2022 ). In contrast, goblet cells primarily secrete MUC5AC proteins ( Liegeois and Fahy, 2022 ). While several studies have reported on the expression of ZO-1, MUC5AC, and E-cadherins proteins in calu-3 cells grown at ALI ( Jeong et al., 2019 ; Ji et al., 2022 ; Lee et al., 2021 ; Pasman et al., 2021 ), this is the first time a time-dependent variation in the genes encoding these proteins has been investigated.

Currently, more than 8000 flavored e-liquids from various brands are on the UK market ( Effah et al., 2022b ). Although carcinogens appear to be reduced or eliminated in EC ( Amatngalim et al., 2016 ), the inhalation toxicity of EC aerosols with flavoring chemicals, and flavour enhancers, is poorly understood based on toxicological effects on the lungs. Many studies have shown the link between EC and e-liquids to lung tissue damage due to inflammation and immunogenic effects ( Balharry et al., 2008 ). Although the flavoring chemicals used are generally regarded as safe (GRAS), their GRAS status post-inhalation remains elusive, and the impact of specific flavoring chemicals on airway epithelial cells remains unclear ( Neilson et al., 2015 ). In vitro assessment of inhalation toxicology has also been applied to the EC liquids ( Khlystov and Samburova, 2016 ; Leigh et al., 2016 ). Nevertheless, information on flavoring-specific inhalation toxicity and the relative toxicity of flavored-e-liquids vs tobacco smoking remains unclear. In this study, we tested the acute inhalation toxicity of 3 previously identified flavored-e-liquids that demonstrated toxicity in submerged bronchioepithelial cells and compared their toxicity to conventional cigarettes.

We used TEER as the main indicator of tissue stress by measuring the integrity of cellular tight junctions and, thus, epithelial permeability. Apart from hazelnut, PG/VG (60/40), vanilla tobacco, and cinnamon at 1% (V/V) significantly decreased TEER values in suspension culture. In contrast, no effect was observed on LDH release. Interestingly, with aerosol exposures, vanilla tobacco was the only flavored-e-liquid which did not impact on TEER value or LDH release. There were no significant differences in TEER and LDH with the remaining eliquids aerosols compared to 3R4F cigarette controls. , This starkly contrasted our expectations, given the common perception of e-liquids as a safer alternative. EC aerosol contains fewer chemicals and significantly lower concentrations than conventional cigarettes ( Dusautoir et al., 2021 ; Margham et al., 2016 ). However, there is only a few studies assessing the toxicity of EC flavors compared to conventional cigarette. Czekala et al., (2019) have shown that aerosol from blueberry flavored-eliquids induced less toxicity in comparison to conventional cigarette in a 3D in vitro human respiratory model developed from primary cells. However, they only screened one flavor and it provided no justification for choosing that particular flavor. In contrast, Glynos et al., (2018) showed that EC vapors, especially containing flavor, increased bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cellularity, Muc5ac production, as well as BALF and lung oxidative stress markers at least comparably and in many cases more than conventional cigarette. Taken together, our study suggest that potentially adverse health effects associated with EC use may depend on several factors, including device and flavor type. Therefore, we strongly advocate for increased user awareness and rigorous regulatory scrutiny to protect public health.

The discrepancy in toxicity observed for hazelnut and vanilla tobacco when applied as an e-liquid or as an aerosol we observed agrees with Behar et al. (2018) , who carried out a pattern of cytotoxicity study on 35 e-liquids. The patterns were characterized as follows: (1) both the refill fluid and its aerosol were non-cytotoxic (7 of 35=20%) (2) both the refill fluid and its aerosol were cytotoxic (19 of 35=54%) (3) the refill fluid was cytotoxic, but the aerosol was not (1 of 35=3%) (4) the aerosol was cytotoxic, but the refill fluid was not (8 of 35=23%) Collectively when combining patterns (1) and (2), 74% of the 35 unheated refill fluids correctly predicted the cytotoxicity of their corresponding aerosol. The remaining 26% did not predict cytotoxicity because (1) certain flavors (i.e., menthol artic) were cytotoxic in submerged cultures but not in their aerosol exposures in the ALI model (2) other flavors are cytotoxic in their aerosolized form (i.e., butterfinger and caramel) but not in their liquid form ( Behar et al. 2018 ).

In an attempt to justify why the aerosolized vanilla tobacco elicited no toxic effects even though it was the flavor with the highest most deposited mass (0.8 mg) compared to cinnamon (0.11 mg), and hazelnut (0.16 mg), we hypothesized that it might have different particle size distribution compared to cinnamon and vanilla tobacco. Therefore, we characterized the particulate matter (PM) < 2.5 microns produced from the EC aerosol. Particulate matter (PM) is typically categorised by size, with PM10 referring to particles 10 microns in size or smaller and PM2.5 referring to particles 2.5 microns in size or smaller. The human respiratory system is designed to filter out larger particles, such as those in the PM10 range, through coughing and sneezing ( Lippmann et al., 1980 ; Oberdörster et al., 2005 ). However, smaller particles, such as those in the PM2.5 range, can evade these defences and penetrate deeper into the lungs and deposit in the alveolar region, where gas exchange occurs ( Xing et al., 2016 ). Depositing PM2.5 particles in the alveolar region can have significant adverse health effects. For example, long-term exposure to PM2.5 has been linked to an increased risk of respiratory infections, asthma, lung cancer, and heart disease ( Xing et al., 2016 ). Whiles hazelnut and cinnamon showed similar patterns in particle distribution size, which reinforces the previous findings regarding TEER reduction and increased LDH release in the cells exposed to these e-liquids. In contrast, vanilla tobacco and PG/VG showed a similar pattern with the highest particle distribution size below 0.99 microns. Smaller particles tend to have a larger surface area relative to their mass, which can increase their reactivity and ability to interact with biological systems; however, vanilla tobacco showed no cytotoxicity in its aerosolized form. Therefore, this suggests that factors beyond particle size distribution may contribute to the toxicity of cytotoxic PG/VG vehicle, cinnamon, and hazelnut. Although more studies are warranted to delineate why vanilla tobacco aerosol showed no toxicity in calu-3 cells grown at ALI, ( Rowell et al., 2017 ) showed that vanilla tobacco reduced cell proliferation and decreased cell viability dose-dependently. In addition, we showed in our previous study that vanilla tobacco and hazelnut may elicit their toxic effect by decreasing mitochondrial membrane potential and inducing ROS production in immortalized human bronchial epithelial (HBEC-3KT) cells ( Effah et al., 2022a ). Nonetheless, hazelnut and vanilla tobacco e-liquid did not affect calu-3 cells. Interestingly, while cinnamon e-liquid showed no effect on ROS production in HBEC-3KTs, it induced a dose-dependent decrease in ROS production compared to media-only and PG/VG vehicle controls in calu-3 cells. We hypothesize that the differential responses in HBEC-3KT and Calu-3 cells for ROS generation may be rooted in the differential expression of antioxidative stress enzyme in cancerous and immortalized normal cells. However, further research is warranted to test this hypothesis. The evidence provided in this study suggests that not only is the mode of exposure of flavored-e-liquids to pulmonary cells important, but also the cell line used for screening may interfere with the model's predictive power.

We found that PG/VG e-liquid vehicle alone exerts adverse effects in calu-3 cells in both suspension and ALI cultures. In suspension cultures, hazelnut elicited toxic effects; however in aerosol exposures, its toxicity was comparable to that of conventional cigarettes. In contrast, while vanilla tobacco elicited adverse effects in suspension culture, it was non-toxic in its’ aerosol form. While flavored e-liquids are believed to attract long-time tobacco cigarette smokers to switch to EC use and tobacco naïve non-smokers to take up e-cigarettes, they also potentially have adverse effects on airways. These adverse effects will be flavour-dependent. Identifying harmful flavors will help inform public health authorities in the UK and elsewhere about which EC flavors may need monitoring and/or restricting.
