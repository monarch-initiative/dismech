# Coordinated expression and assembly of BiP, p58(IPK), and ER chaperone complexes maximize proinsulin folding in pancreatic β cells.
**Authors:** Jang I, Duffey A, Itkin-Ansari P, Arvan P, Kaufman RJ
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2533617123](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2533617123)

## Content

Proper proinsulin folding in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is prerequisite to producing bioactive insulin, and proinsulin misfolding causing β cell ER stress accompanies pancreatic β cell dysfunction in type 2 diabetes (T2D). How (and which) ER chaperones coordinate to prevent proinsulin misfolding is largely unknown other than an unspecified dependence on the hsp70 member, BiP. A genetically engineered mouse enables efficient, specific pulldown of endogenous islet β cell BiP (GRP78, the major HSP70 ER chaperone) in complexes with client proteins. We demonstrate that BiP assembles in various protein complexes (including cochaperones p58IPK, GRP170, ERdj3, and oxidoreductases PDIA1 and PDIA6) that specifically bind to nonnative proinsulin. BiP requires p58IPK for productive proinsulin folding, whereas nonstoichiometric BiP excess actually hinders proinsulin folding. Coordinated and dyscoordinated BiP/cochaperone assembly in response to demand for proinsulin highlights physiologic and pathophysiologic conditions, respectively, offering a potential check point for therapeutic intervention.

American adults (and adolescents) with prediabetes progress at a high rate to frank type 2 diabetes (T2D)(1, 2); to prevent this progression, early therapeutic interventions are critical(1). In prediabetes, insulin resistance increases pressure on pancreatic β cells to produce more insulin; however, the accumulated burden of β cells to maintain excess insulin production leads to β cell failure in susceptible individuals(3, 4). Insulin production begins in the ER with proinsulin (PI) folding to form three native disulfide bonds — required for PI trafficking to the Golgi/immature granules where C-peptide is excised, forming insulin for storage in mature granules(5). β cells can produce ~6000 PI molecules/second(6) with high glucose further augmenting PI biosynthesis(7). PI misfolding drives diabetes onset in Mutant INS gene-induced Diabetes of Youth (MIDY)(8). Such patients are heterozygotes: misfolded MIDY mutant proinsulin engages co-expressed WT-PI that also becomes entrapped in the ER(9), adding to ER stress(8) that may limit β cell proliferation and induce dedifferentiation(10–12), and may be linked to senescence(13) and β cell death(14). Perturbation on β cell ER homeostasis in T2D(15) may further impair PI folding and trafficking(16). Therefore, the machinery that facilitates PI folding is a crucial checkpoint to limit β cell dysfunction in T2D.

Multiple ER resident proteins are thought to help maintain β cell ER homeostasis, and their perturbation can cause PI misfolding(16). We recently identified the ER chaperone BiP/GRP78 as well as p58IPK, ERdj3, PDIA1, and Prdx4 as PI interacting partners in normal human islets(17). The extent to which these ER resident proteins coordinate in their interactions with PI remains unknown. Here, we have sought to determine how BiP(18) and its partner proteins coordinate the PI folding mechanism – a process essential to β cell health.

A portion of proinsulin (PI) exists as aberrant disulfide-linked complexes even in healthy human and murine islets, with further exacerbation upon exposure to oxidants or perturbants of ER-to-Golgi trafficking, or in islets from T2D patients or db/db mice (even before development of hyperglycemia)(17, 19, 20). Here, we challenged isolated murine islets with high (16.5 mM) glucose media for 24 h. This condition boosted PI levels but increased misfolded PI (Fig. 1a).

Similarly, mice fed high fat diet (HFD) also exhibited increased PI misfolding in islets (Fig. 1b). Moreover, PI misfolding increased in Min6 β cells under ER stress [thapsigargin (Tg)] (Fig. S1a) and in mouse islets with mitochondrial dysfunction [2-deoxyglucose (2DG) and NaN3 (Fig. S1b)]. Indeed, NaN3 alone was sufficient to induce PI misfolding (21). Altogether, PI misfolding increased under conditions associated with β cell failure in T2D [high glucose (Fig. 1a), inhibition of ER to Golgi trafficking (Fig. S1c), metabolic stress (Fig. 1b), ER stress (Fig. S1a), mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. S1b), and oxidative stress (Fig. S1d)]. Under the latter conditions in mouse islets, even as total or immunoprecipitated PI was decreased, co-precipitated BiP was strongly increased (Fig. 1c). The data indicate that BiP association with PI is greatly stimulated under conditions of PI misfolding.

We employed sucrose gradient fractionation to resolve disulfide-linked PI complexes based on the size. We first analyzed Min6 cell homogenates under both reducing (R) and non-reducing (NR) conditions and immunoblotted for PI from each sucrose gradient fraction. PI ran mainly as a monomer in fractions #1–3 with a gradual increase in size of intermolecular disulfide-linked complexes in later fractions (Fig. 1d
nonreducing gel). BiP was distributed from fractions 3–10, quantified in Fig. 1e. We immunoprecipitated PI from even-numbered fractions and observed BiP co-precipitation in fractions 4, 6, and 8 (Fig. 1f), where most PI exists as intermolecular disulfide-linked oligomers and HMW complexes (Fig. 1d).

We similarly fractionated and analyzed mouse and human islets. The PI distribution in normal mouse islets (Fig. 1g) was comparable to that in Min6 cells (Fig. 1d), with PI monomers located in fractions 1–3, with a peak in fraction #2. Mature insulin was also limited to the uppermost fractions (Fig. 1g). In fraction 3, disulfide-linked PI oligomers (dimers, trimers, tetramers, etc.) overlapped with monomers, with progressively larger intermolecular disulfide-linked PI complexes appearing in middle and lower fractions (Fig. 1g
nonreducing gel). BiP was distributed across gradient fractions 3–8, quantified in Fig 1h. The overall PI distribution pattern in non-diabetic human islets (Fig. 1i) showed more PI in fractions 1–3 running as monomers. Disulfide-linked PI dimers, oligomers, and HMW complexes appeared in fraction 3 and middle fractions where BiP was distributed (Fig. 1i). PI associated with BiP in these fractions (Fig. 1j). These results establish that in β cell lines, murine islets, and human islets, a fraction of BiP not only co-sediments with misfolded PI but also co-precipitates with misfolded PI.

We examined islets from 5-week-old db/db mice (an early stage of T2D progression when islet insulin levels were markedly diminished compared to db/+ controls but PI levels were undiminished, Fig. 2a
“Input” blots). The db/db islets exhibited increased levels of the ER (co)chaperones GRP170, p58IPK, and ERdj3, and oxidoreductases PDIA1 and PDIA6 (suggestive of ER stress response, Fig. 2a
“Input” blots), and PI immunoprecipitation co-precipitated a larger amount of these ER proteins (Fig. 2a
IP blots). These data suggest that in early stages of diabetes in db/db mice, misfolded PI is increased (Fig. 2a
nonreducing gel), and ER chaperone/oxidoreductases — as well as PI interactions with these chaperones/oxidoreductases — are also increased.

Most BiP antibodies are unsuitable for co-precipitation of BiP-client protein complexes. To circumvent this difficulty, we generated a mouse model with tissue-specific knock-in of Flag-tagged endogenous BiP in a β cell-specific manner (BiP-FlagKI/KI;Ins1Cre/+, aka “β-BiP-Flag” mice). Ins1Cre -mediated recombination in β cells (Fig. S2a) modifies the knock-in at the Hspa5 locus to insert a Flag epitope at the C-terminus of BiP immediately preceding the final KDEL ER retention signal(22). In heterozygous β-BiP-Flag mice, by immunofluorescence, BiP-Flag was detected in pancreatic islet cells expressing insulin (Fig. S2b). BiP-Flag strongly co-immunostained with PDIA6 (Fig. S2c), which localizes to the ER(23). PI immunoprecipitation from heterozygous β-BiP-Flag mice islets co-precipitated a similar fraction of both endogenous untagged BiP and BiP-Flag (Fig. S2d). Moreover, ER stress induced by tunicamycin upregulated both endogenous untagged BiP and BiP-Flag (Fig. S2e). Thus, in all respects, BiP-Flag functions as endogenous BiP (Fig. S2), as previously suggested(24, 25).

Homozygous β-BiP-Flag females exhibited normal body weight gain(26) (Fig. S3a). While male β-BiP-Flag mice tended to be slightly smaller than controls (Fig. S3b), there was no evidence of diabetes or impaired glucose tolerance in either sex (Fig. S3c–h). Overall, the data indicate a normal phenotype of β-BiP-Flag mice. In β-BiP-Flag mouse islets, BiP (Flag) IP pulled down PI oligomers and HMW complexes, which were further augmented by brief BFA treatment inducing PI retention and misfolding in the ER(20) (Fig. 2b). In addition, BiP co-precipitated ER co-chaperones (GRP170, p58IPK, ERdj3) and the oxidoreductase PDIA6 (Fig. 2b), showing that BiP interacts with numerous partner proteins in the ER. Unbiased BiP-Flag IP-MS analysis with mouse islets from six β-BiP-Flag mice compared to islets from three Ins1Cre/+ mice also identified GRP170, p58IPK, Pdia6, PDIA1 as BiP-interactors among >20 secretory pathway proteins (Table S1).

By sucrose gradient fractionation, BiP-Flag distributed similarly to that of endogenous BiP of control mouse islets; other ER resident proteins and aberrant disulfide-linked PI complexes also were distributed as in control islets (Fig. 2c). Immunoprecipitating BiP (Flag) co-precipitated PI from fractions 3–7 (Fig. 2d), corresponding to disulfide-linked PI complexes (Fig. 2c). Notably, BiP immunoprecipitation disproportionately pulled down more PI from fractions 5 & 7 (Fig. 2e, with total PI in each tested fraction shown as a line graph and BiP pull-down of PI as a bar graph) — supporting that BiP preferentially recognizes and binds misfolded PI disulfide-linked complexes, which are present even in normal healthy mouse islets(19).

BiP pulldown (Fig. 2d) also co-precipitated p58IPK and PDIA6 (peak in fraction 3) as well as ERdj3 and GRP170 (peak in fraction 5). Given that PI pulldown from db/db islets also co-precipitated p58IPK, PDIA6, ERdj3, and GRP170 (Fig. 2a), Fig. 2d suggests the presence of heteromeric BiP-partner proteins co-associated with PI, with their composition likely varying across the sucrose gradient containing different sized misfolded PI complexes.

The BiP-client binding cycle includes strong peptide binding to BiP-ADP, with client release from BiP-ATP(27). The DnaJ family of co-chaperones (including p58IPK and ERdj3, etc.) recruit peptides to BiP-ATP while stimulating the BiP ATPase to create high affinity client binding to BiP-ADP. Here, we combined sucrose gradient fractions 5–7 from Fig. 2c and preincubated ± 2 mM ATP before BiP IP. As expected, after ATP preincubation, PI no longer co-precipitated with BiP (Fig. 2f) and BiP no longer co-precipitated with PI (Fig. 2g). p58IPK co-precipitation with BiP was also ATP-sensitive (Fig. 2f) — although here we were unable to detect p58IPK co-precipitation with PI due to immunoblot interference by IgG heavy chain. GRP170 co-precipitation with BiP or PI was only slightly reduced by ATP preincubation (Figs. 2f, g). Interestingly, PDIA6 and ERdj3 interactions with BiP were also ATP-sensitive, although after ATP preincubation, both ER chaperone partners remained associated with PI (Fig. 2g) even when they were no longer associated with BiP (Fig. 2f).

To distinguish BiP-dependent and -independent PI complexes in β-BiP-Flag islets, we first immunoprecipitated BiP (and its associated proteins, Fig. 3a). BiP was recovered exclusively in this first IP and was associated with PI, GRP170, p58IPK, PDIA1, PDIA6, and Erdj3 (Fig. 3a
“1st
IP”). The supernatant of the first IP was then used to immunoprecipitate the remaining PI (i.e., unbound to BiP). Importantly, p58IPK, PDIA1, PDIA6, and ERdj3 proteins were recovered 17-fold, 5.5-fold, 3.9-fold, and 2.7-fold more in BiP-partner heteromeric units than in BiP-independent PI-containing complexes respectively (Fig. 3a, quantified in the table). Interestingly, GRP170 interaction with PI (see Fig. 2) appeared entirely dependent upon the PI-BiP interaction (Fig. 3a).

Notably, BiP bound disulfide-linked PI dimers, oligomers, and HMW complexes (Fig. 3a
blue arrows) while the majority of BiP-free PI (Fig. 3a
green arrows) was recovered as monomers. Altogether, these data demonstrate that distinct protein complexes interact with unfolded/misfolded PI in the ER, whereas well-folded PI recovered in gradient fraction #1 entirely lacks GRP170, BiP, p58IPK, PDIA6, ERdj3, or PDIA1 (Fig. 2c
bottom). These data indicate that proper PI folding is associated with release from all BiP-dependent and independent ER resident proteins.

In islets of HFD-fed mice BiP-interaction with ER resident partner proteins, and with PI, was even greater (Fig. 3b). A 3 h treatment of islets with high glucose to boost PI synthesis, similarly stimulated the fraction of PI, GRP170, and p58IPK associated with BiP (Fig. 3c). Moreover, the oxidant menadione treatment (up to 3 h) did not increase total BiP or PI levels (reducing gel input, Fig. 3d) but gradually increased the formation of aberrant disulfide-linked PI complexes (Fig. 3d
far left panel). BiP IP recovered an increasing fraction of PI under these conditions and concurrently recovered an increased fraction of its associated partner proteins GRP170, p58IPK, PDIA6, and ERdj3 (Fig. 3d
right panels). Together, these results strongly suggest that various islet stress conditions stimulate the formation of misfolded PI complexes that recruit BiP with its heteromeric co-chaperone/oxidoreductase partners.

As PI associates with p58IPK (ERdj6) and/or ERdj3 (Figs. 2a, 2f, 3a), we expressed recombinant (myc-tagged) human PI in 8 distinct CHO cell lines, each bearing CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of one ERdj member (from Dr. D. Ron, University of Cambridge). CHO cells do not make secretory granules or express insulin processing enzymes; thus, anterograde ER-to-Golgi trafficking of properly folded PI results in constitutive secretion of PI to the medium. We noted a significant decrease in PI secretion concurrent with intracellular retention only in p58IPK/ERdj6-KO cells (Fig. 4a, quantified in
4b). Additionally, control CHO cells with siRNA-mediated p58IPK knockdown were found to exhibit increased PI misfolding and retention intracellularly, with blockade of PI secretion (Fig. S4). Moreover, Min6 cells with siRNA-mediated p58IPK knockdown also significantly increased misfolded PI intracellularly (Fig. 4c–e) while several other ER resident proteins were largely unaffected (Fig. 4c). Also in support of a specific role of p58IPK as an ER co-chaperone in PI folding rather than a general ER stress inducer, we found that intentional acute ER stress induction with high-dose tunicamycin (Tm, 10 μg/ml) did not significantly impair PI trafficking/secretion (Fig. S5; bearing in mind that PI is not an N-linked glycoprotein).

p58IPK-KO mouse islets contain less PI and insulin compared with WT (or Het) islets(28) (Fig. 4f
lower panels), yet high glucose incubation notably increased disulfide-linked PI complexes (Fig. 4f
nonreducing gel). Moreover, in p58IPK-KO islets, PI was entrapped in the ER (marked by PDIA1 staining; Fig. 4g
quantified in
4h). Thus, p58IPK — whose association with PI is tightly linked to BiP function (Fig. 2) — is needed for proper PI folding and export from the ER.

GRP170 is known as a nucleotide exchange factor for BiP but can also interact directly with misfolded secretory proteins(29, 30). Min6 cells with siRNA-mediated GRP170 knockdown exhibited increased PI misfolding (Fig. 5a–c). Knockdown of either BiP or GRP170 in CHO cells increased the steady-state level of PI intracellularly while decreasing PI export to the medium (Fig. 5d lanes #1–4). These effects were even more severe in p58IPK-KO CHO cells (Fig. 5d lanes #5–8, quantified in
5e). Defective secretory trafficking of PI was accompanied in all cases by increased intracellular accumulation of misfolded disulfide-linked PI (Fig. 5d
nonreducing gel).

Reintroduction of p58IPK into p58IPK-KO cells substantially rescued PI secretion and reduced intracellular PI levels, validating that the defect in these CHO cells is due to p58IPK deletion. These phenotypes require continued BiP expression (Fig. S6). Moreover, BiP overexpression alone only modestly increased PI folding and secretion (Fig. 5f lanes 5,6; 15,16), whereas co-expression of BiP and p58IPK together, markedly improved PI folding and trafficking (Fig. 5f lanes 7,8; 17,18). Thus, the BiP/p58IPK partnership can expand PI folding capacity and favor PI anterograde trafficking (Fig. 5f, quantified in
5g). This beneficial effect on PI folding requires BiP-PI binding, as it is not replicated by overexpressing a client binding-defective BiP mutant (BiP-V461F, Fig. 5h). Moreover, the synergistic effect provided by BiP/p58IPK requires a functional DnaJ-domain(27) as it cannot be phenocopied by the p58IPK-DJ1 mutant lacking the J-domain(31) (Fig. 5i). The expressed J-domain mutant (Fig. 5j) still associated with BiP (Fig. 5k) but engaged PI nonproductively on BiP (Fig. 5l), and by sequential IP, PI was also engaged nonproductively on the J-domain mutant molecules that were associated with BiP (Figs. 5m, n). Further, even a p58IPK point mutation in histidine-proline-aspartate (HPD) motif of the J-domain (H422Q) dramatically decreased PI folding and trafficking; thus, neither BiP-V461F nor p58IPK-DJ1 nor H422Q mutants can support PI folding and export (Fig. 5o, quantified in
5p). Altogether, PI folding capacity is linked to the coordinated expression of active BiP in partnership with active p58IPK.

Optimal enhancement of PI folding capacity requires PDIA1(20, 32). Indeed, even short (yet time-dependent) exposure to the PDIA1-specific inhibitor, KSC-34(33) inhibited PI folding as well as the beneficial effects of p58IPK and BiP on PI folding (Fig. S7) — suggesting that BiP/p58IPK-coordinated PI folding still requires ongoing PDIA1 activity.

To explore the importance of BiP–p58IPK stoichiometry, we transfected BiP at different concentrations with fixed amounts of p58IPK in p58IPK-KO CHO cells expressing PI. Consistently, p58IPK expression with the smallest amount of BiP (at, or just above, endogenous levels) increased PI secretion while BiP expression without additional p58IPK did not (Fig. 6a, b). Importantly, PI secretion was not further improved when BiP expression alone was increased; rather, PI secretion declined at the highest BiP expression level (Fig. 6a, b). This result, which is quite similar to that observed in cells expressing the J-domain mutant of p58IPK (Fig. 5l and 5n), suggests that in the absence of sufficient active p58IPK, high BiP availability does not favor PI folding and export and instead shifts the cells towards a condition in which PI folding is blocked in a more stable, pseudo-permanent BiP-bound state. Interestingly, increasing GRP170 expression, alone or alongside BiP and p58IPK, did not improve PI secretion (Fig. 6c, d), suggesting that endogenous GRP170 expression is not limiting, at least in CHO cells, and based on the negligible change of phosphorylated eIF2α in Figs. 4f, 5, and 6, the effects of genetic manipulations of ER chaperones on the PI client exhibited limited impact on global ER homeostasis.

In a parallel approach, we transduced adenoviruses into Min6 pancreatic β cells for different expression levels of BiP and/or p58IPK. Again, a disproportionate increase of BiP alone actually increased PI misfolding (Fig. 6e
nonreducing gel, quantified in
6f). However, PI misfolding induced by BiP overexpression was diminished by adenoviral-mediated expression of p58IPK (Fig. 6e, f). In the setting of a fixed amount of BiP overexpression, a progressive increase of p58IPK expression steadily reduced PI misfolding (Fig. 6g, h). Moreover, in contrast with the detrimental impact on PI of discoordinate BiP overexpression, there was no indication of PI misfolding upon discoordinate overexpression of p58IPK (Fig. 6g, h). Thus, the activity of p58IPK appears to prevent BiP-PI complexes from becoming entrapped and retained in a pseudo-permanent BiP-bound state.

Finally, in β-BiP-Flag, p58IPK-KO mice, when considering that the islets have less than half of total PI compared to p58IPK-WT or Het islets(28) (Fig. 6i
input, below), BiP co-precipitated an abnormally large fraction of β cell PI (Fig. 6i
upper, 6j), consistent with the inability of PI — in the absence of active p58IPK — to escape from pseudo-permanent BiP-bound entrapment within the ER (Fig. 4g, h). Altogether, the data in cell lines and in islets suggest that in the presence of active p58IPK, BiP association favors PI folding and export, whereas in its absence, PI folding is blocked in a pseudo-permanent BiP-bound state.

Numerous events contribute to β cell failure in T2D, including pathological effects of high glucose, genetic and acquired defects in ER-to-Golgi trafficking of PI(16, 34–37), metabolic stress(38–40), ER stress(41), mitochondrial dysfunction(42, 43), and oxidative stress(44) — each have the potential to affect PI folding (Fig. 1, Fig. S1). Resilience to these stresses, essential to prevent diabetes, requires ER chaperones and oxidoreductases(17, 45) [with BiP being most prominent(18)] to limit formation/accumulation of aberrant disulfide-linked PI complexes. Whereas a viable β cell-specific BiP knockout model has not been achieved, the genes encoding BiP and its partner proteins show linkage to human glycemic phenotypes(46) including fasting or 2 h insulin and HOMA-B (BiP), 2 h glucose and random glucose (ERdj6), T2D (GRP170), HbA1c (PDIA1), and even T1D (ERdj3).

Here, not only did we find that PI is predisposed to form aberrant disulfide-linked complexes(19, 20), but in the steady-state we resolved a range of different-sized disulfide-linked PI complexes apparent in β cell lines, rodent islets, and human islets under unstressed healthy conditions (Fig. 1, Fig. S1) — which further increase under diabetogenic conditions (Fig. 2a). As it turns out, the distribution of BiP and other PI interacting proteins in a sucrose gradient coincide with disulfide-linked PI complexes that co-precipitate BiP (including in human islets, Fig. 1i–j) — reciprocally, β cell BiP in these fractions co-precipitates PI complexes (Fig. 2d). These studies were enabled by the novel β-BiP-Flag mice that do not develop glucose intolerance (Fig. S3e–h) and provide quantitative β cell BiP immunoprecipitation that allows probing for both client proteins (Fig. 2) and chaperone partners (Table S1).

Importantly, while islet PI co-precipitates p58IPK with BiP, immunoprecipitating BiP co-precipitates p58IPK with PI (Fig. 2), suggesting the formation of ternary (PI, BiP, p58IPK) rather than (or in addition to) binary complexes. The PI-BiP-p58IPK association is markedly increased under metabolic, and hyperglycemic conditions as well as in diabetic islets, all of which tend to be linked to upregulated PI protein expression (Figs. 2, 3). Upon ATP preincubation, BiP concurrently loses association with both PI and p58IPK (Fig. 2f). Moreover, as demonstrated through loss of function studies in heterologous cells, BiP and active p58IPK form a ternary complex with PI (Fig. 5j, 5n) that facilitates PI folding and trafficking (Figs. 4a–b, 5d–e, Fig. S6), as is the case for endogenous PI in β cell culture (Fig. 4c–e) or primary islets (Fig. 4f–h). These data help explain why p58IPK-null mice(47) and human patients with p58IPK gene mutations(48–51) develop diabetes.

Augmentation of the BiP-p58IPK partnership enables expansion of PI folding capacity, as increased BiP/p58IPK co-expression improves PI folding and anterograde trafficking, which strictly requires an active functional BiP peptide binding domain, an active p58IPK J-domain that supports BiP function, and an appropriate stoichiometry that increases p58IPK levels to match increased BiP (Figs. 5, 6). Thus, with regards to PI folding, BiP cannot “go it alone”.

Other ER resident proteins also co-precipitate with BiP or PI (Figs. 2, 3), but we are still learning their roles in insulin biogenesis. GRP170 effects appear more modest than those of p58IPK (Figs. 5, 6, and Fig. S6). Others have reported that PDIA6 and ERdj3 co-precipitate with BiP and PI (52) — and we find that these BiP partners can remain associated with PI even after ATP-induced BiP dissociation from PI (Fig. 2f, g). Interactions with these BiP partners are already observed in islets of HFD-fed mice that do not have diabetes as well as in diabetic db/db animals, suggesting a change in PI folding demand that begins in prediabetes and continues in frank diabetes.

Two non-mutually exclusive possibilities are that BiP and its heteromeric partners are recruited for disposal of PI complexes, or that they contribute to productive PI folding. Interestingly, PDIA1 seems to function as a PI reductase(20), and GRP170 was reported to guide misfolded PI to degradation(53). Plausibly, distinct secretory or degradative fates may await the distinct chaperone-bound folding intermediates.

Our studies highlight that PI folding is a step vulnerable to many of the same cellular stresses that cause β cell failure in T2D. Our studies include the first demonstration that coordinated upregulation of BiP with p58IPK is required to optimize PI folding and anterograde trafficking — whereas dyscoordinated BiP upregulation actually interferes with PI folding and leads to ER entrapment of BiP-PI complexes (as seen in early β cell dysfunction). Thus, in conclusion, β cell resilience to PI folding challenges will require a coordinated ER chaperone/oxidoreductase response to limit the formation/accumulation of non-native PI that leads to deficient insulin biogenesis. As coordinated activity of BiP appears to be a key regulator of PI folding for insulin synthesis, targeting this transcriptomic/proteomic coordination offers therapeutic potential to ameliorate β cell failure associated with T2D.

Cells were rinsed in TBS and lysed in HG lysis buffer supplemented with 100 U/ml hexokinase and proteinase/phosphatase inhibitors on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation, lysates were incubated with 10 μl anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma) (total volume 200 μl) at 4°C overnight. After washing in TBS, beads were eluted with 500 μg/ml of 3xFlag peptide (Sigma) in TBS buffer (100 μl × twice at 4’C). The eluates were used for 2nd IP with p58IPK Ab (CST-2940) bound protein G Sepharose (Cytiva) at 4’C overnight. Beads were washed thrice with TBS, eluted with 30 μl of 2x Laemmli buffer (with β-ME) and heated to 95°C for 5 min before immunoblotting.

Islets were lysed in sucrose gradient lysis buffer (154 mM NaCl, 20 mM Hepes, 0.3% Tx-100) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors on ice for 30 min, briefly sonicated, centrifuged at 12,000 g × 10 min, layered onto 5–20% sucrose gradients, and ultracentrifuged at 39,000 rpm for 16 h at 4°C. Ten fractions were collected from top to bottom and analyzed by immunoprecipitation or immunoblotting.

Mouse pancreata were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at room temperature. Paraffin embedding, sectioning, and slide preparations were performed by the SBP Histology Core Facility. Following xylene and EtOH treatments, antigen retrieval and blocking, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies and DAPI (Fisher Scientific) for 2 h at room temperature. Confocal images were acquired (Zeiss LSM 710) with a 40x objective.
