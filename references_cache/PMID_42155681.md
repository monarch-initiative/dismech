# Long-acting IL-7 restores T-cell reconstitution in a humanized mouse model of lymphopenia.
**Authors:** Pérez-Díez A, Liu X, Bennett A, Barrios M, Anderson M, Wolfarth AA, Choi D, Lisco A, Sereti I
**Journal:** J Allergy Clin Immunol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaci.2026.04.027](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2026.04.027)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Idiopathic CD4 lymphocytopenia (ICL) is a clinical syndrome characterized by low CD4 T-cell counts (<300 cells/μL), occasionally accompanied by CD8 lymphopenia. ICL, which is associated with serious opportunistic infections, cancers, and autoimmune diseases, has no established treatment.

OBJECTIVE: We tested NT-I7 (efineptakin alfa), a long-acting form of recombinant human (rh) IL-7, as a potential immunotherapy for ICL in a preclinical mouse model.

METHODS: Mice received peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 15 individuals: 5 healthy donors and 10 persons with ICL. Mice receiving ICL cells were either untreated or treated with a single NT-I7 dose. Blood was sampled at different times, and spleens were collected on day 28 to compare lymphocyte reconstitution, IL-7Rα expression, and TCR clonality, while weight was monitored to detect potential graft-versus-host disease. In a single patient study, NT-I7 was administered, and blood lymphocytes were enumerated up to 98 days.

RESULTS: In mice that phenocopied their donors' lymphopenia, NT-I7 treatment restored CD4 T-cell counts to the levels observed in mice receiving lymphocytes from healthy donors. A single NT-I7 dose downregulated IL-7Rα on CD4-T cells for 2 to 3 weeks and increased T-cell counts with preserved polyclonality in 3 of 4 patients tested, without causing graft-versus-host disease. The lymphopenic NT-I7-treated patient experienced a 2- and 3-fold rise in CD4 and CD8-T cell numbers, respectively, 2 months after a single dose of NT-I7.

CONCLUSION: A single dose of NT-I7 restored T-cell numbers in a mouse model of ICL and improved lymphocyte counts in a single-patient study.

Idiopathic CD4 lymphocytopenia (ICL) is a rare syndrome defined as persistent low CD4 T-cell number in blood (<300 cells/μL) in the absence of known monogenic disease, medications, or infections that could explain it; thus, it has no familial linkage, nor evidence of a transmissible agent ( 1 ). In approximately half of the patients, the CD4 lymphopenia is accompanied by CD8 T-cell lymphopenia and, less commonly, low NK and B cell numbers ( 2 , 3 ). The degree of CD4 lymphopenia is clinically relevant, as patients with ICL and CD4 counts below 100 cells/μL are at higher risk of developing opportunistic infections, most commonly caused by human papillomaviruses or Cryptococcus spp., and cancers ( 4 ). Therefore, while improving any other lymphopenic population may also be beneficial, restoration of CD4 T-cell counts and/or CD4-cells’ functional capacity is an important objective of any therapeutic intervention.

Using a humanized mouse model, we have previously identified a subgroup of people with ICL (pICL) who, unlike healthy donors (HD), harbor T-cells incapable of reconstituting an immunocompromised mouse host through homeostatic expansion ( 5 ). This suggests a potential T-cell intrinsic factor/s as the cause, or contributor, of lymphopenia in these patients, since a robust impediment to T-cell proliferation and/or survival persists even in a T-cell empty and xenogenic host. As such, the non-reconstituted (NR) ICL-mice represent an optimal pre-clinical model to assess therapeutic interventions aimed to increase T-cell proliferation and survival capacity that could restore T-cell reconstitution to the levels observed in HD-mice.

Recombinant human IL-7 (rhIL-7) is a candidate therapy to test in this ICL pre-clinical model because of its well-known effects on T-cell proliferation and survival ( 6 , 7 ). In an encouraging, albeit small, clinical trial in pICL, we previously tested repeated doses of rhIL-7 and found them to be safe and effective in increasing CD4 and CD8 T-cell numbers ( 8 ). However, rhIL-7’s short half-life required numerous and frequent injections. A long-acting version of rhIL-7, NT-I7, has been developed to overcome these limitations. NT-I7 is a homodimer of rhIL-7 fused to a hybridized IgD/IgG4 immunoglobulin constant region, allowing its binding to the neonatal Fc receptor, which extends its half-life ( 9 ), thereby reducing the need of multiple dosing. A single administration of NT-I7 has been previously shown to safely increase CD4 and CD8 T-cell numbers in healthy individuals ( 10 , 11 ) and in patients with chemoradiation-related lymphopenia ( 12 ). In a preclinical model of solid tumors, NT-I7 also improved CAR-T cell mediated anti-tumor effects ( 13 ).

We used our humanized mouse model to demonstrate that after a single dose of NT-I7, both CD4 and CD8 T-cells from NR patients recovered their proliferative and survival capabilities while maintaining TCR polyclonality. Finally, we show these results may have relevant clinical implications, as a single dose of NT-I7 given to an ICL patient did result in a robust CD4 and CD8 T-cell expansion.

Study participants provided peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) through enrollment in the clinical study “Etiology, Pathogenesis, and Natural History of Idiopathic CD4+ Lymphocytopenia” (EPIC, NCT00867269) or through the NIH blood bank for healthy donors (HDs). Eligible participants with ICL were adults with confirmed ICL as per ICL criteria, along with evaluations including genetic testing to exclude other diseases. Table I contains the characteristics of the patients included in this study.

A 50-year-old male with history of ICL and long-standing skin and mucosal HPV-related diseases (patient #81 in the preclinical model) underwent treatment with NT-I7 under a single-patient expanded access protocol for compassionate use with a dose of 480 μg/kg of NT-I7 administered intramuscularly (Investigational New Drug 146505). Blood counts for CD4 T-cells, CD8 T-cells, B cells and NK cells were measured at several time points before and after NT-I7 treatment.

Six to 10 weeks old male or female NOD-RAGKO-γcKO (NRG) mice from The Jackson Laboratory received human PBMC i.v. to establish the previously described humanized PBMC (hPBMC) mouse model ( 5 ). Briefly, PBMCs were thawed, counted, and stained to analyze proportions of B, CD4 and CD8 T lymphocytes by flow cytometry. Based on the flow data, we calculated the numbers of PBMCs (ranging from six to 73 million) to inject per donor and per mouse, with the goal of transferring similar numbers of CD3+ and/or CD4+ T-cells from both HDs and pICL within the same experiment (Supp. Table I). In experiments including pICL, we injected five mice with HD PBMC and up to 10 mice per ICL donor, from which half received NT-I7 (200 μg; NeoImmuneTech, Inc.) s.c. and the other half received NT-I7 diluent alone as control. Both NT-I7 and its diluent were further and equally diluted in PBS. Mice were weighed at baseline and then twice a week and euthanized if they lost more than 20% of their initial body weight. Mice were housed in specific pathogen–free conditions. Unless otherwise noted, mice were bled on days three, seven, 14, 21 and 28, when they were euthanized and their spleens collected. Splenocytes were counted and both blood and spleen cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. The NIH is accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care.

Cells were blocked with anti-human CD16/32 antibody (BD Bioscience) for 10 minutes (min) before staining with LIVE/DEAD (L/D) fixable Dead Cell Stain kits for near-IR (Invitrogen) to exclude dead cells and adding fluorochrome conjugated antibodies (panel shown in Suppl. Table II). Cells were then incubated for 30 min at room temperature and washed once with 1% BSA-PBS before acquisition using a Fortessa (BD Biosciences). Analysis was done with FlowJo (Tree Star) software (version 10.7 and above). To identify the different lymphocyte populations in mouse samples, we first made a live lymphocyte gate based on FSS-A and LIVE/DEAD marker, then gated the human CD45+ cells and then gated on CD3negCD19+ for B cells, CD3+CD4+ for CD4 T cells, and CD3+CD8+ for CD8 T cells (Suppl. Fig. 1).

Plasma was obtained at different time points after NT-I7 treatment and after spinning mouse EDTA treated blood in small Eppendorf tubes. We diluted the plasma appropriately before quantifying hIL-7 using the IL-7 ELISA test ELH-IL7 (RayBiotech, Inc.); each sample was run in duplicates.

We sequenced the complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) of the TCR-β chain from day 28 hPBMC splenocytes using immunoSEQ Assay (Adaptive Biotechnologies) and analyzed the data using immunoSEQ Analyzer software (Adaptive Biotechnologies). When sufficient numbers of cells were available, we analyzed individual mouse splenocytes. Otherwise, we pooled splenocytes from the same patient and treatment group. The number of productive CDR3 templates analyzed per sample was at least 29,000.

The 2-tailed Mann-Whitney test was used for 2-group comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was used for other group comparisons. A P value < 0.05 was considered significant. In graphs where data from individual mice are shown, a bar depicted the median of the group. In graphs where no individual data are shown, the median and the interquartile range are depicted unless otherwise noted.

The human studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID), and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to any study procedures in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the NIAID.

We first determined the T cell reconstitution level achieved by different numbers of HD PBMC transferred into both male and female NRG mice by quantifying CD4 and CD8 T-cells in their spleens four weeks after the transfer. Host sex was not a major determining factor, as the numbers of T-cells recovered were not significantly different between the male and female mice (Suppl Fig. 2). We used female mice for future experiments to take advantage of their slight incremental dose-response observed at higher PBMC doses. Once we identified the dose of HD PBMC unable to reconstitute NRG mice, we tested if a single dose of NT-I7 could improve the reconstitution capacity of such suboptimal PBMC transfer (Suppl Fig. 3). As expected, four weeks after PBMC transfer, untreated mice showed poor reconstitution of CD4 and CD8 T-cells (Figure 1A). A single subcutaneous (s.c.), but not intra-peritoneal (i,p.), injection of NT-I7 at the time of PBMC transfer significantly increased the reconstitution of all lymphocyte subsets (CD4, CD8, TCRγδ and B cells) (Figure 1A and Suppl Fig. 4). We collected blood on days seven, 14, and 28 after treatment to compare proliferation, as measured by Ki-67 expression. The biggest differences among the groups were observed at day seven, when both treated groups showed increased T cell proliferation compared to the untreated mice, with the i.p. route being significantly higher (Figure 1B). However, i.p. injected mice showed lower T-cell proliferation by day 28, when the other two groups showed similar levels (Figure 1B). Plasma pharmacokinetics of human hIL-7 between the two routes of administration showed slightly higher levels of hIL-7 in i.p. treated mice during the first nine days post injection (Suppl Fig. 5). Plasma hIL-7 levels were not different between the two routes after that, and still detectable by the end of the experiment. Therefore, i.p. treated mice showed diminished reconstitution compared to s.c. treated mice, despite their high hIL-7 levels in plasma and increased proliferation within the first week after T cell transfer. Despite the increased CD4 and CD8 T-cell reconstitution obtained by s.c. NT-I7 treatment, we did not observe GvHD in these mice (Figure 1C).

We conclude that, in the hPBMC model, a single subcutaneous dose of NT-I7 restores the reconstitution capability of a suboptimal number of HD T cells without signs of overt toxicity.

We have previously shown that CD3 T-cells from a subset of pICL do not reconstitute NRG mice as efficiently as HD T-cells ( 5 ), establishing a good ICL preclinical model to test potential therapeutics. Using this model, we tested whether a single s.c. injection of NT-I7, at the time of cell transfer, could revert the lymphopenia observed by the subset of non-reconstituting (NR) pICL. We found that NT-I7 increased peripheral CD4 T-cells by day 21, which was statistically significant by day 28 (Fig 2A). The effect of NT-I7 was more striking when quantifying the absolute number of lymphocytes in the spleen on day 28 (Fig 2B). NT-I7 treatment significantly increased both CD4 and CD8 T-cell numbers by a 100 to 1,000 times in mice receiving NR-ICL PBMC, to levels comparable to mice receiving similar numbers of HD CD4 T-cells (Figure 2B). In addition, TCRγδ and, at a lower extent, B cells, were also increased in NT-I7 treated ICL mice (Figure 2B). We extended this observation to all six NR patients with ICL tested (Fig. 2C), and the increase in absolute number of splenic T cells were statistically significant in all except ICL-50, who showed a remarkable increase in T cell numbers but a lower number of mice per group was tested due to low PBMC availability. We also tested NT-I7 treatment in mice receiving PBMC from the group of pICL who were able to reconstitute NRG mice, called reconstituting (R)-ICL (45R, 84R, 24R and 79R). In these mice, NT-I7 treatment did not significantly alter CD4 or CD8 T-cell numbers (Fig. 2C), suggesting that the homeostatic equilibrium achieved during normal reconstitution was neither enhanced nor disrupted by NT-I7 treatment. Despite NT-I7 normalizing the capacity of NR-ICL T-cells to reconstitute to similar levels as HD T-cells, NT-I7 treated mice did not develop GvHD during the experiment (Fig. 2D). In contrast, untreated mice receiving T-cells from one HD donor and one R-pICL did suffer GvHD, defined as the decrease of more than 10% in body weight (Fig. 2D).

We conclude that a single dose of NT-I7 rescued the reconstitution capability of pICL’s T-cells without toxicity, demonstrating that in this preclinical model, NR-ICL T-cells are capable to respond to the homeostatic cytokine and achieve similar levels of reconstitution as HD T-cells.

At the molecular level, the IL-7 biologic effects are mediated by the binding to and then downregulating its receptor ( 14 , 15 ), followed by intracellular signal transduction resulting in increased homeostatic ( 16 ) and antigen-driven ( 17 ) T-cell proliferation, which can be measured by Ki-67 expression. We next explored whether NT-I7 caused these molecular changes on treated ICL CD4 T-cells. NT-I7 treatment resulted in downregulation of IL-7Rα on ICL CD4 T-cells by day three which persisted during three weeks of the study (Fig. 3A), while untreated HD and ICL CD4 T-cells maintained similar IL-7Rα levels to their baseline observed before transfer into NRG mice. We observed parallel effects on CD4 T-cell cycling, measured by Ki-67, with NT-I7 treatment strongly increasing and maintaining cycling of ICL CD4 T-cells from days seven to 14, compared to their untreated counterparts, and reaching similar levels to untreated HD CD4 T-cells (Fig. 3B). We next tested if NT-I7 induced an oligoclonal expansion of antigen triggered T-cells, in this case specific for the mouse xenoantigens or if, in contrast, it induced a broad polyclonal proliferation. Overall, we found no increased TCR clonality after NT-I7 treatment (Fig. 3C), suggesting that NT-I7 induced an unbiased proliferation of T-cells, and not uniquely the T cells recognizing the mouse xenoantigens. This finding is supported by the lack of increased GvHD observed in NT-I7 treated mice (Fig. 2D).

We, thus, found that a single dose of NT-I7 induced a robust and sustained response on ICL CD4 T-cells resulting in polyclonal proliferation.

Based on the successful treatment observed in the preclinical ICL model in the absence of toxicity, we enrolled patient ICL-81 in a single-patient expanded access protocol for compassionate use. At the time of treatment, patient ICL-81 was lymphopenic for both CD4 and CD8 T-cells (Table I). After a single dose of NT-I7, as described in methods, his lymphocyte counts in blood were quantified up to 100 days after treatment. The numbers of CD4 and CD8 T-cells increased significantly by day 53, with CD4 T cells remaining high up to day 98 (Fig. 4A). We also found increased number of B lymphocytes, but not NK, cells at day 98 (Fig. 4B).

For comparison, similar kinetics of CD4 and CD8 T-cell expansion were observed after NT-I7 treatment in the preclinical model of the same patient, with both lymphocyte population percentages increasing with respect to their untreated controls and reaching similar levels to those obtained by HD CD4 and CD8 T-cells (Figure 4C). In conclusion, treatment with a single dose of NT-I7 expanded CD4 and CD8 T-cells in an ICL patient with lymphopenia, supporting the translational value of the preclinical model.

Using a preclinical lymphopenic mouse model, we describe here the efficacy of a single dose of a long-acting IL-7 molecule (NT-I7) to induce lymphocyte proliferation and recover T lymphocyte numbers to similar levels achieved by healthy donor T-cells. The long-acting effect of NT-I7 was evident by the long-lasting levels of hIL-7 detected in mouse plasma and sustained in vivo downregulation of IL-7Rα on the treated lymphocytes. NT-I7 treatment did not cause toxicity or increased GvHD, even in mice receiving cells from reconstituting pICL that proliferate similarly to HD. Encouraged by these results, we treated an ICL patient with a single dose of NT-I7, achieving T-cell reconstitution as seen in the preclinical model.

The etiology of ICL remains unknown and thought to be polygenic and possibly heterogeneous ( 4 ). Because IL-7 has a non-redundant role on human T-cell development ( 18 ) and homeostatic survival of both naïve and memory T-cells ( 19 , 20 ), a potential driver or contributor of ICL might be a limited effect of IL-7 on CD4 T-cells. This might be caused by either low serum levels of IL-7 and/or defective IL-7R signaling on T cells. Similarly to what it has been described in other lymphopenic conditions ( 21 ), pICL were found to have increased IL-7 serum levels, which inversely correlated with the degree of lymphopenia ( 22 ). This would suggest a defective and/or desensitized IL-7R signaling as a potential driver of the lymphopenia, which could be the result of decreased IL-7R-mediated clearance, as previously described in patients with HIV ( 23 ), and/or a defective T cell receptor signaling. In agreement with this, our group and others have found blunted response of ICL T-cells to IL-7 ( 24 , 25 ), which is supported by the limitations of some patients’ T-cells to follow lymphopenia and antigen-driven proliferation when injected into immunocompromised mice ( 5 ). Our current work suggests that in some pICL, subpar IL-7R signaling could be bypassed by exogenous addition of IL-7. The preclinical model could be potentially useful to identify these ICL patients who may respond and might benefit from exogenous IL-7 supplementation. In our model, NT-I7 treatment did not enhance GvHD, in contrast to what others have found in the setting of T-cell-replete allogeneic bone marrow transplantation treated with IL-7 ( 26 ). This discrepancy may be explained by the lack of whole-body irradiation and/or organized lymph node structure in our model, since both these factors might be involved in GvHD.

The mouse model was a good predictor of the patient’s response, as NT-I7 treatment increased lymphocyte numbers in both the patient and the patient’s preclinical model. The increase of T and B cells observed in our patient 98 days after treatment is comparable to what was previously observed in HDs receiving intramuscular NT-I7 at 60 μg/kg ( 11 ). Our patient’s recovery was slower, as by day 25 we had not observed an increase in lymphocyte counts, while the HDs described by Kim et al, displayed a significant increase by day 14 despite receiving eight times lower dose of NT-I7 than our patient. Although we cannot draw conclusions based on a single patient, the slower T cell recovery kinetics displayed by the ICL patient might be explained by the patient’s specific ICL etiology, his initial lower number of T-cells, the chemotherapy he completed three months before NT-I7 treatment to treat lung cancer, and/or the possible presence of anti-lymphocyte autoantibodies detected in a large proportion of pICL ( 3 ). It will be interesting to see if the presence of anti-lymphocyte Ab might influence the in vivo efficacy of NT-I7 in future patients.

The route of NT-I7 administration in the mouse model was critical for full NT-I7 effect. While the s.c. route significantly increased T-cell reconstitution, the i.p. route barely improved it. This was despite both routes having similar overall hIL-7 plasma pharmacokinetics and concentrations. We did observe a minor difference with the i.p. route having slightly higher levels of hIL-7 within four days of NT-I7 administration. This finding perhaps drives the significantly higher T cell proliferation observed at day seven in the same group of i.p. treated mice. This might suggest that a strong and fast proliferation driven by early higher concentration of hIL-7 might not be the best way to obtain optimal long term T cell reconstitution. In addition, different tissue distribution might further explain the observed route effects.

Even though mature B cells do not express IL-7Rα, we observed an expansion of the patient’s B-cells in both the clinical and pre-clinical ICL setting after NT-I7 treatment. The B cell expansion observed in the patient might be explained by a combination of IL-7 direct effect on B cell progenitors ( 27 , 28 ) and IL-7 indirect effect on B-cells through the increased expression of BAFF and CD70 on T-cells ( 29 ). In the pre-clinical model, since no hematopoiesis is taking place, only the indirect effect through T-cell activation could explain the observed B cell expansion.

Even if the NT-I7 mediated CD4 and CD8 T-cell increase did not reach the threshold necessary to achieve normal numbers, it might provide a clinical benefit, as both the response to vaccines ( 30 ) and the capacity to fight opportunistic infections ( 4 ) are more preserved in pICL with CD4 counts above 100 cells/μL. In addition, TCR remained polyclonal after NT-I7 treatment, suggesting that NT-I7 might improve ICL patients’ response to pathogens not only by increasing T-cell numbers but also by maintaining a polyclonal TCR repertoire capable to respond to both pathogens and vaccines.

In summary, based on our mouse preclinical model, NT-I7 appears to be a suitable candidate to further investigate for ICL treatment, since there is evidence of robust biologic activity with polyclonal lymphocyte expansion. Clinical studies are ongoing to confirm these findings and assess the potential benefit for this patient population (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05600920).
