# Endovascular profiles linked to neutrophil activation in children and young adults with long COVID.
**Authors:** Steifman CB, Alvarez-Carcamo B, Verma S, McCarthy R, Guthrie LB, Gill KK, Swank Z, Walt DR, Grabowski EF, Fasano A, VanElzakker MB, Irimia D, Yonker LM
**Journal:** Pediatr Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41390-026-05024-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41390-026-05024-1)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Endovascular symptoms are among the most debilitating long COVID symptoms; however, underlying mechanisms are unclear. Children and young adults with long COVID, an understudied population, offer key insight into long COVID pathology.

METHODS: Eighty-four children and young adults ≤25 years from the U.S. and Canada were enrolled; 61 with long COVID and 23 healthy pediatric controls. We assessed symptom burden, quantified fibrin amyloid microclots, endovascular cytokines, cell-free DNA, and conducted in vitro assays to assess Spike-related neutrophil-mediated endothelial cell injury.

RESULTS: Cardiovascular symptoms were prevalent among participants with long COVID. Microclot burden was increased (p = 0.0003), as were markers of angiogenesis and endothelial remodeling, including FGF-2, which correlated with microclots (p = 0.04). Cytokines involved in leukocyte trafficking (sVCAM-1, L-selectin, α-2-macroglobulin) were reduced while cell-free DNA, a marker of intravascular neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, was increased (p = 0.003) and positively correlated with microclot component serum amyloid A (p = 0.004). Co-culture assays revealed that NETosis, triggered by Spike immune complexes, contributes to endothelial injury in long COVID.

CONCLUSIONS: Children and young adults with long COVID with cardiovascular symptoms display increased microclots, endothelial injury, and neutrophil inflammation, which warrant further evaluation and suggest intravascular NETosis as a key driver of endovascular pathology in long COVID.

IMPACT: Children and young adults with long COVID display elevated endothelial biomarkers, underscoring disease-related rather than age-related endovascular profiles following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Children and young adults with long COVID exhibit increased microclot burden in blood. Neutrophil activation may contribute to ongoing endovascular injury in long COVID. A combination of microclots, neutrophil markers, and endothelial cytokines could serve as biomarkers for Long COVID.

Long COVID is an infection-associated chronic condition characterized by continued or new onset symptoms following SARS-CoV-2 infection, affecting millions of children and adults worldwide.1,2However, multifactorial diagnostic challenges exist,3including a lack of objective biomarkers. Although over 200 symptoms have been associated with this condition, cardiovascular symptoms, including orthostasis, tachycardia, and exercise intolerance,4are amongst the most common features. In addition, in adult cohorts, endothelial5,6,7and platelet activation,8hypercoagulability,9and cardiometabolic risk signatures10have been reported following acute COVID, and fibrinogen amyloid deposits, known as microclots,9have been shown to persist after acute infection.11However, these findings are non-specific and often associated with numerous age-related and inflammatory illnesses such as diabetes, sepsis, and Alzheimer’s disease.12,13,14,15

Studies in adults with long COVID have also reported altered immune profiles,16including activated circulating neutrophil phenotypes with elevated levels of neutrophil-associated plasma proteins IL-17C, S100A12, and increases in cytokine pathways signaling for neutrophil chemotaxis.17Additionally, increases in low-density granulocytes with enhanced ability to form neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) have been reported in adults with long COVID,18along with evidence of persistent NET production lasting 6 months or more.19,20One group studied children with long COVID and similarly revealed neutrophil dysfunction with decreased superoxide-producing activity and decreased phagocytosis as compared to convalescent controls.21The impact of neutrophilic inflammation in long COVID and the connection to endovascular findings is unclear.

Persistent SARS-CoV-2 reservoirs22and zonulin-mediated trafficking of Spike proteins into circulation23,24,25are felt to be driving contributors to long COVID pathology. Spike-immune complexes have been shown to activate neutrophils in post-acute syndromes,26and Spike has been shown to alter fibrin formation and impair fibronolysis.27Defining underlying pathology is essential for designing diagnostic tests, prognosticating risk, and informing tailored treatment strategies. Biospecimens from children and young adults with persistent symptoms following acute COVID-19 can offer key insight into disease pathology distinct from confounding age-related inflammatory diseases.

Here, we analyzed symptoms and biospecimens collected from a cohort of 84 children and young adults with the goal of characterizing endovascular pathology in long COVID. We assessed cardiovascular symptoms, quantified endovascular cytokines and markers of neutrophil activation, and examined microclots, stratifying key features driving long COVID phenotypes. Using an in vitro co-culture model, we tested mechanistic hypotheses connecting neutrophil activation and endothelial cell dysfunction to elucidate mechanisms driving endovascular findings in long COVID.

Participants with long COVID from across the United States and Canada were enrolled in the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved MGH COVID-19 Pediatric Biorepository (IRB #2020P000955). Healthy plasma donors were children who enrolled during the early stage of the COVID pandemic (prior to 2022) and were never SARS-CoV-2 infected as confirmed by self-report and negative nucleocapsid antibody testing. Healthy neutrophil donors were individuals who denied signs or symptoms of long COVID and did not have any inflammatory disease, infection, or treatment with immunomodulator therapy. Recruitment occurred through physician referral or IRB-approved social media posting. All participants signed consent form, plus an assent if 7 through 17 years of age, prior to study participation. All procedures were completed in accordance with IRB-approved protocols.

Demographic information was obtained by self-report or medical chart data extraction. Long COVID symptoms were self-reported as defined by World Health Organization (WHO) criteria.28The Long COVID Symptom Burden Questionnaire (SBQ™-LC)29was administered to long COVID patients. Blood samples were used for Spike detection via Simoa,30microclot characterization, cytokine analysis, cell-free DNA quantification, and in vitro mechanistic assays (Fig.1a; Supplementary TableS1).

Blood was collected by venipuncture into EDTA phlebotomy tubes for plasma and heparin or EDTA phlebotomy tubes for microfluidic NETosis assays. All samples were processed within 24 hours. Samples that were collected at local phlebotomy labs were processed immediately. For samples that were collected remotely, two EDTA tubes were drawn, each tube was placed in a sealed bag, and one was placed on ice, and the other at room temperature in a shipping container. These samples were shipped overnight and processed immediately upon arrival at the lab. Of the shipped samples, iced samples were used for cytokine analysis, and room temperature samples were used for microclots and cell-free DNA. Once received in the lab, blood was centrifuged at 1000gfor 10 minutes, then plasma was collected and stored at −80°C.

Simoa assays were performed to test for the presence of SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein in plasma, as previously described.30Participants with Spike detected in their blood are referred to as “Spike-positive” individuals, whereas long COVID participants without Spike detected are “Spike-negative”.

To measure pre-formed circulating microclots, frozen plasma was thawed, then stained with Thioflavin T (ThT) for 30 minutes, as previously described.14ThT-stained plasma was then imaged using fluorescent microscopy: using a 20x objective on a Nikon Ti2 Eclipse with Perfect Focus, the upper right and lower left corners of the square coverslip were set to establish the imaging area. Images were captured with 4×4 binning, then analyzed in ImageJ with calculations performed in Excel. Image dimensions (in µm) were noted, and the total area in µm2was calculated. The count, total area, average size, and percent area (Microclot Burden) were set as measurements. Then, images were thresholded, and the particle analyzer was used with the following settings: size range of 10-Infinity µm, circularity 0-1, and only “summarize” checked. The number of microclots per square centimeter was calculated by dividing the count by the total area.

Plasma was analyzed by multiplex cytokine panels HDAGP17 Human Angiogenesis & Growth Factor 17-Plex Discovery Assay®, HDSAA6 Human Cardiovascular Disease Panel 2-6-Plex Discovery Assay®, and HDCVD9 Human Cardiovascular Disease Panel 3-9-Plex Discovery Assay® (Eve Technologies).

Plasma cfDNA was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer as previously described.26All samples were normalized to healthy controls.

Spontaneous NETosis was quantified using a previously validated microfluidic device that was designed and fabricated as previously described.26Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) was used to prime the device. Using whole blood collected in a heparin or EDTA tube, neutrophils were isolated using EasySep™ magnet and Direct Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (StemCell Technologies #18000 and #19666) and resuspended at a concentration of 1×107cells/mL in IMDM, with 1:1000 Hoechst 3342 dye. 10 µL of the resuspended neutrophil solution was mixed with 10 µL of IMDM and 10 µL of 6 µM SYTOX green, then 5 µL of this mixture was loaded into the microfluidic device. Devices were imaged on DAPI, GFP, and bright-field channels on a Nikon TiE inverted wide-field fluorescent microscope with a bio chamber heated to 37°C with 5% CO2. Images were acquired every 3 minutes for 6 hours. NETosis was quantified as previously described;26percent NETosis was calculated by dividing the number of diffused SYTOX green neutrophils by the total number of neutrophils at time t = 0.

Spike immune complexes were generated as previously described.26Briefly, Spike protein (Sino Biological 40591-V02H3-100) was reconstituted in sterile DI water at a concentration of 142.86 µg/mL, biotinylated (Life Technologies A35538), then passed through Zeba desalting columns. Biotinylated Spike was then incubated with NeutrAvidin nonfluorescent Fluospheres (Life Technologies F8777), spun down, resuspended in plasma diluted 1:10 in 1x PBS from healthy, vaccinated individuals, and incubated. Unbound plasma components were washed, and then Spike immune complexes were resuspended in experimental media and used immediately. Spike alone (without antibodies/immune complexes) was prepared in an identical manner but was resuspended in PBS rather than plasma.

Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs, ATCC PCS-100-010 were grown in Vascular Endothelial Cell Growth Media (ATCC catalog number PCS-100-030) supplemented with bovine brain extract growth kit (ATCC PCS-100-040), passaged at approximately 85% confluency, and seeded on 24-well transwell plates. Experiments were performed once cells reached confluency. Neutrophils from a healthy donor were isolated as described above, resuspended at a concentration of 5 × 106neutrophils/mL. 600 µL of media was added to the basolateral chambe,r and 100 µL of treatment or media was added to the apical chamber. Neutrophil conditions/controls were added to the inner chamber and incubated for 4 hours. Supernatant was sampled in duplicate into a 96-well plate, plus 50 µL of media into two wells as a photometric reference.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was quantified using CyQUANT™ lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay kit (Invitrogen #C20300) per protocol instructions, read at 490 and 680 (background) nm on a plate reader.

After the supernatant was collected for LDH, transwells were washed, and 100 µL of 0.5 mg/mL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP) was added to the apical chamber. After four hours, supernatant was sampled in duplicate from basolateral chambers into a 96-well plate, and HRP was quantified using assay solution (2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS), citrate buffer, water, and H2O2, read at 405 nm on a plate reader. Concentrations were interpolated using the standard curve.

Statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism and RStudio. The correlation matrix was designed with the ‘psych,’ ‘corrplot,’ and ‘Hmisc’ packages. Pearson’s or Spearman correlation coefficientrwas calculated, and the significance of each correlation was assessed using two-tailed t-tests.P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) logistic regression was used to identify features predictive of long COVID using the ‘glmnet’ and ‘dplyr’ packages. Variables with non-zero coefficients were considered relevant predictors and used in downstream analyses. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to visualize variability between groups, and it was performed using the ‘tidyverse’ and ‘factoextra’ packages. K-means clustering was used to explore subgroups within long COVID. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the silhouette method. For individual comparisons between groups, unpaired t-test boxplots were generated using the ‘ggplot2,’ ‘ggpubr,’ and ‘plotrix’ packages. Unless otherwise stated, statistical significance is represented as ∗ =p< 0.05, ∗∗ =p< 0.01, ∗∗∗ =p< 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ =p< 0.0001. The package ‘svglite’ was used to export analyses. Additional analyses were completed using a t-test or Kruskal-Wallis, as noted.

Sixty-one children and young adults with long COVID and 23 healthy pediatric controls from across the United States and Canada were enrolled in the study. The median age for participants with long COVID was 15 years (range 4–25), and 10 years (range 6–14 years) for healthy controls; Demographics are highlighted in Table1. Fifteen percent of individuals with long COVID (n= 9) had Spike protein detected in their blood (“Spike-positive”).

The most frequently reported cardiopulmonary symptoms were dizziness on standing, dyspnea when climbing stairs, and palpitations (77%, 67%, and 63% reporting symptoms, respectively). Other common symptoms included feeling faint (57%), chest tightness (43%), cold hands or feet that lasted longer than normal (43%), or chest pain (43%). A quarter of patients (27%) reported some degree of swelling in their legs or feet (Fig.1b). Because cardiovascular symptoms in this pediatric adult cohort were prominent and consistent with symptoms reported in larger observational studies in adults, we sought to perform an in-depth analysis of endothelial factors, including microclot and endothelial cytokine analysis.

Microclots have not previously been assessed in children with long COVID. We sought to quantify microclots in our cohort using a standardized microclot screen built upon previously described fluorescent microscopic techniques.31Fig.2aprovides representative images of the various shapes and sizes of fibrin amyloid microclots detected in individuals with long COVID. Overall, microclot burden, as quantified by percent of total imaged area occupied by microclots, was significantly increased in long COVID as compared to healthy controls (p= 0.0003; Fig.2b). The total number of microclots per square centimeter and the average microclot size were also significantly increased in long COVID (p= 0.0073,p= 0.0051, respectively; Fig.2b). The majority of microclots in long COVID ranged from 10-50 µm2in size, although some extended over 500 µm2. Fig.2cdisplays the size distribution of microclots in long COVID. Healthy controls have fewer microclots on the whole, with long COVID plasma displaying significant increases in the number of microclots ranging from 200-300 µm and 300-400 µm in size (Supplementary Fig.S1). While the Spike protein may drive microclot formation in vitro,32we found that Spike-positive individuals showed similar distribution of microclot burden, number, and size as compared to Spike-negative individuals. This suggests that either alternative factors may also contribute to microclot formation, or Spike may be masked from detection in some individuals, potentially sequestered within microclot clusters.

Next, we sought to characterize endovascular cytokine responses in children and young adults with long COVID. Using multivariate analyses to globally assess cytokine responses in long COVID, we detected strong interactions between endothelial cytokines, even when accounting for multiple comparisons. Consistent with reports in adult cohorts of increased angiogenesis and vascular remodeling in long COVID,33,34,35this pediatric cohort displayed significant correlations in platelet activation, markers of the coagulation cascade, and endothelial inflammation (Fig.3a). To further analyze these interactions, we directly compared endothelial cytokine responses in long COVID with healthy children. We found that factors associated with leukocyte honing and adhesion, such as soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (sVCAM-1), L-selectin, and α-2 macroglobulin were significantly decreased in long COVID (Fig.3b). Additionally, serum amyloid P (SAP), which stabilizes amyloid fibrils, activates the complement system, and modulates Fcγ receptors (FcgR),36was significantly increased in Spike-negative long COVID (p = 0.02; Fig.3c). Interestingly, FGF-2, a cytokine involved in angiogenesis and wound healing, was significantly increased in Spike-positive individuals but not Spike-negative individuals (Fig.3c). Leptin, which is also important in angiogenesis, vascular permeability, and interaction with endothelial growth factors,37was also significantly increased in both Spike-negative and Spike-positive long COVID (p= 0.02 and 0.005, respectively; Fig.3c). Together, these data suggest that children and young adults with long COVID have increased angiogenesis and endothelial injury with containment of inflammatory cells within circulation.

With evidence of reduced inflammatory extravasation, we then sought to test for markers of increased intravascular inflammation. Knowing that circulating neutrophils undergo intravascular neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation when activated by antibody-bound Spike immune complexes in post-COVID syndromes,26we tested for the presence of circulating neutrophil activation in plasma. We quantified cell-free DNA (cfDNA), an indirect measure of NETosis.38Indeed, we detected a significant increase in cfDNA in long COVID participants as compared to healthy controls (p= 0.0033; Fig.4a). To validate that long COVID neutrophils had higher levels of NETosis than healthy controls, we used established microfluidic assays with immunofluorescent imaging to directly visualize spontaneous, unstimulated NETosis ex vivo.26We compared NETosis in individuals with long COVID (n= 23) with healthy controls (n= 11). Indeed, long COVID neutrophils displayed significantly increased NETosis (p< 0.0001; Fig.4b). Interestingly, cfDNA correlated significantly with serum amyloid A (SAA) (p = 0.0037; Fig.4b), which has been previously described as a prominent component of microclots.8We also found a significant association between cfDNA and SAP (p= 0.02; Fig.4b), further supporting a strong association between circulating amyloid fibrils and neutrophil activation, potentially due to amyloid fibril-mediated activation of neutrophil extracellular traps39or intravascular retention and activation of neutrophils, which in turn triggers amyloid deposition.40

We then sought to assess whether microclots were associated with markers of endothelial injury in our pediatric cohort. Increased quantity of circulating microclots significantly correlated with increases in P-selectin (p= 0.028; Fig.4c), a key regulator of inflammatory thrombotic microangiopathy, which has previously shown to be increased in long COVID.31FGF-2 and HB-EGF were also significantly correlated with increased microclots (p= 0.04 and 0.02, respectively, Fig.4c), providing further evidence that markers of angiogenesis, endothelial cell proliferation, and repair mechanisms are increased in the presence of microclots in children and young adults affected by long COVID.

Because we detected an association between cfDNA (marker of NETosis) and elevations in endothelial biomarkers, we developed an in vitro co-culture model to further interrogate the interaction between circulating neutrophils and endothelium. Here, we allowed human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) to grow to confluence on a transwell plate. Neutrophils from healthy donors were added, NETosis was elicited, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), a key marker of endothelial cell injury and distress, and HRP flux, indicating barrier integrity, were quantified. We have previously shown that Spike, when bound to anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, forms immune complexes that are potent activators of NETosis.26In this model, we confirm that healthy neutrophils, when exposed to Spike immune complexes, undergo robust levels of NETosis (Fig.5b). Because Spike protein can be detected in circulation in long COVID,24,25and neutrophil activation is a feature of long COVID,41we specifically sought to test the impact of Spike and NETosis induced by Spike immune complexes on endothelium.

We first sought to test whether NETosis in general elicits endothelial injury. We added healthy neutrophils atop a mature endothelial barrier, then stimulated the neutrophils with PMA, a potent nonspecific activator of NETosis, or buffer. While exposure to unstimulated neutrophils or PMA alone (without neutrophils) had no impact on the endothelial barrier, endothelial cells exposed to PMA-induced NETosis displayed high levels of LDH release (Fig.5c; Supplementary Fig.S2), greater than LDH released from stimulated neutrophils alone (Supplementary Fig.S2).

Next, we sought to determine whether NETs induced by Spike-immune complexes would also affect endothelial injury. We added healthy neutrophils atop endothelial cells, then added Spike immune complexes or buffer. Again, we detected significant endothelial LDH release following Spike immune complex-induced NETosis, as compared to exposure to unstimulated neutrophils (Fig.5d). In our model, we found that neither Spike nor Spike-immune complexes alone, when added directly to the endothelium (in the absence of neutrophils), elicited LDH release (Fig.5d; Supplementary Fig.S2). These findings suggest that spike-immune complex-activated neutrophils contribute to endothelial injury in long COVID.

We also sought to determine whether Spike immune complex-activated NETosis impacted barrier integrity. Indeed, we found that following exposure to Spike-immune complex-activated neutrophils, endothelial cells displayed a loss of tight junctions, permitting increased HRP flux (Fig.5e). Together, these findings suggest that the endothelium is injured by Spike-immune complex activation of neutrophils in circulation.

Finally, given the elevated presence of cell-free DNA and microclot burden in long COVID, we sought to determine whether endovascular biomarkers would distinguish subgroups of long COVID patients with cardiovascular injury. We found that self-reported cardiovascular symptoms were key factors impacting overall symptom burden and quality of life. However, symptom-severity stratification did not significantly correlate with levels of cell-free DNA or microclot burden. Based on the cytokines, biomarkers, and clinical features analyzed in this study, a LASSO model was used to select eight key predictors of long COVID. The strongest contributors were microclot burden (coefficient = 3.9), followed by cfDNA (2.9 × 10−3) and P-Selectin (4.4 × 10−4) (Fig.6b). These eight features were subsequently used in a principal component analysis (PCA) to evaluate the separation between long COVID and healthy controls. The PCA captured 47.7% of the total variance. Although there was substantial overlap between the two groups, long COVID exhibited a larger 95% confidence ellipse, suggesting greater heterogeneity in cytokine and biomarker profiles (Fig.6c). To further explore this variability, we next aimed to characterize this heterogeneity within long COVID.

K-means clustering was applied to the long COVID cohort to investigate potential subgroups. Using the silhouette method, two clusters were identified as the optimal number of groups (Supplementary Fig.S3), accounting for 32.5% of the variance (Fig.5D). Next, an additional LASSO regression was performed to identify features predictive of the two long COVID clusters (Supplementary Fig.S3). HB-EGF and cell-free DNA were the most influential features in distinguishing clusters, with a significant difference observed between groups 1 and 2 (p< 0.0001 andp< 0.01, respectively, Fig.6e). Similarly, P-selectin (p< 0.0001), VEGF-C (p< 0.0001), and PF4 (p< 0.0001) levels were elevated in group 2 relative to group 1, suggesting potential vascular injury in patients with higher levels of circulating cfDNA (Fig.6e). Elevated P-selectin may also indicate increased endothelial-leukocyte or platelet-leukocyte interactions with a greater capacity for P-selectin-PSGL-1 interactions and amplified leukocyte recruitment. Overall, these findings highlight the potential utility of biomarker-based classification and underscore the importance of understanding how persistent immune dysfunction contributes to endothelial and vascular pathology in long COVID.

Here, we queried cardiovascular symptoms and tested for markers of endothelial injury in a cohort of children and young adults affected by long COVID. While cardiovascular symptoms are non-specific, these symptoms were prominent in individuals with long COVID, and we found that even in children and young adults, long COVID was associated with objective markers of endovascular changes, with increased quantities of microclots, endothelial cytokine dysregulation, and activated neutrophils. Additionally, we provide evidence that activated neutrophils contribute to endothelial injury in long COVID.

Microclots have previously been described in long COVID; however, most studies have primarily focused on adult cohorts ranging from a mean age of 48–52 years.8,31,42Children and young adults are less likely to have confounding age-related cardiometabolic inflammatory profiles, thus increases in both size and frequency of microclots suggest they are directly related to long COVID rather than alternative co-morbidities. The cause of these microclots and the clinical impact, however, are unclear. While fibrinogen activating sites have been described on the Spike protein,32endothelial injury can also elicit fibrin activation.43Here, we provide mechanistic evidence of Spike-induced neutrophil activation resulting in endothelial injury in vitro, and show that a combination of microclots, neutrophil biomarkers, and endothelial cytokines could be used as biomarkers to identify long COVID.

The clinical consequence of increased microclots and altered endothelial phenotypes is not yet known. It is possible that fibrin and amyloid sequestration of circulating Spike limits spike-induced injury; this needs to be further studied. Regardless, our study supports evidence that microclots and endothelial injury are key components of long COVID, and intravascular NETosis, triggered by Spike-immune complexes, may play an important role in eliciting endothelial injury in long COVID. Long-term monitoring of cardiovascular risk in the setting of these abnormal endothelial profiles is warranted. It is not yet established, however, whether treatment targeting these microclots is indicated; anticoagulation therapy should not be used without supporting evidence from well-designed clinical trials.

We implemented biomarker profiling to identify subgroups for vascular responses in long COVID. Importantly, we show that microclots are a strong predictor of long COVID and therefore may be an important biomarker for identifying patients with long COVID in the right clinical context, or for assessing response to therapy. Other key markers, such as cfDNA and P-selectin, were also key features of long COVID, suggesting that a combination of markers of angiogenesis and neutrophil activation may also serve as biomarkers for long COVID. Within long COVID, we identified two subgroups distinguished by levels of cfDNA and endothelial cytokine markers. Larger studies are needed to define the utility of these biomarkers in long COVID and to determine whether these can be used to distinguish long COVID from other autoimmune or inflammatory conditions.

Our study had limitations: first, this was a relatively small cohort of individuals with long COVID. However, we specifically sought to enrich pediatric long COVID, which is an understudied group, and we were able to collect samples from a wide geographic area across the US and Canada. Second, clinical factors may have impacted our findings. Although children and young adults generally do not have age-related co-morbidities that can drive inflammation and endovascular disease, we did not routinely obtain biometric data, medical history, or medication use. More females were included in our group as well, which may suggest sex-related vulnerabilities for long COVID. Additionally, we did not include post-COVID controls. While this would have provided an important comparison, long COVID is often under-recognized in children despite many children having lingering post-infectious symptoms.1Ultimately, it is possible that confounding factors existed, and these should be explored in the future. Third, while we reveal correlations between microclots, angiogenic factors, and neutrophilic inflammation, additional mechanistic studies are needed to define causality. Lastly, our in vitro studies are limited because they cannot capture in vivo interactions; thus, animal models are needed to further test these hypotheses.

In summary, we provide evidence that both children and young adults with long COVID with cardiovascular symptoms display evidence of microclots, with increased biomarkers for endothelial inflammation and vascular remodeling. We also highlight the role of neutrophilic inflammation in eliciting endothelial injury. These data establish a foundation for establishing biomarkers and further elucidating mechanisms driving long COVID, and reveal objective evidence that children and young adults can experience chronic neutrophil activation and elevation of endovascular biomarkers following SARS-CoV-2 infection.
