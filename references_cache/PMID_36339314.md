# Dystonia-Deafness Syndrome Response to Subthalamic Nucleus Stimulation.
**Authors:** Zhang Y, Huang Y, Wang L, Wan X, Yang Y, Zhang M, Guo Y

Keywords:dystonia, subthalamic nucleus, stimulation

Dystonia‐deafness syndrome (DDS) is a distinct clinical entity with an unclear pathophysiological mechanism. Sensory‐neural deafness and dystonia are the dominant characteristics of DDS, with the former always preceding the latter.1Several genetic causes of DDS have been identified, such as Mohr‐Tranebjaerg syndrome; Woodhouse‐Sakati syndrome; large deletion of chromosome 7q21, including epsilon‐sarcoglycan (SGCE); and β‐actin (ACTB) gene mutations.1,2We reported a case of DDS with a mutation in theACTBgene and the substantial improvement from subthalamic nucleus (STN) stimulation.

A 31‐year‐old man of Han nationality was born from nonconsanguineous parents. He had congenital cleft lip and bilateral sensorineural deafness. His height and weight were normal for his age. Infant‐onset hearing loss limited his development of speech capabilities, and he could only speak some single syllables. At the age of 20, dystonia started with clumsy gait and persistent great tremor in both the upper and lower limbs except sleep. The patient complained of pain in the waist and legs. He progressed to a generalized dystonia in 1 year, with the head and neck persistently tilted to the left while the trunk was reversed. This dystonic posture could be alleviated while walking backward and bearing load, as sensory tricks. The patient was unable to walk without crutches.

The patient's mother, who died at age 38, retrospectively presented with deafness, truncal dystonia, and tremor as well. The patient's father had bilateral congenital cataracts. His half‐sister suffered from epilepsy before an accidental death.

Physical exam was remarkable with laterocollis, dystonic tremor in both upper limbs, dystonic gait, and scoliosis. Muscle strength was scored as grade 5/5, and tone was almost normal except for mild hypertonia of both legs. There was no other facial abnormality apart from an upper lip scar after surgical repair. Routine laboratory tests revealed hyperhomocysteinemia, and a radiograph showed severe scoliosis. Results of brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were normal, except the septum pellucida was absent. A suspected diagnosis of DDS was finally confirmed by whole‐exome sequencing (WES), which revealed the pathogenic missense mutation inACTBc.547C > T (p.Arg183Trp) (Fig.1).

The patient received trihexyphenidyl 4 mg daily, but during the course of up to 3 years of treatment, the efficacy was unstable, and his symptoms fluctuated. Subsequent medication with clonazepam, baclofen, and pramipexole were of limited effect. Therefore, deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the bilateral STN was conducted. Significant improvement of motor function (Burke‐Fahn‐Marsden's Dystonia Rating Scales–Dystonia Movement Scale [BFMDRS‐M] 44 vs. 20) was observed at the 12‐month follow‐up (see Video1; stimulation parameters: left/right STN, 2−/7‐; amplitude, 2.8 V/ 3.2 V; pulse width, 80 μsec/90 μsec; frequency, 150 Hz). The greatest BFMDRS‐M improvements were observed for the arm (66.7%) and leg (53.8%) dystonia, with less clinical impact on the neck and trunk. At the follow‐up, pramipexole was suspended while clonazepam and baclofen were tapering. Adverse effects such as paraesthesia and emotional changes were not observed at follow‐up. Our patient returned with great satisfaction as he was able to walk independently (Videos2,3,4).

Before surgery, dystonia in the lower limbs was asymmetric, and he was struggling to walk without assistance. The improvement of walking balance, speed, and mobility was obvious, although there was residual symptoms affecting the neck and lower limbs.

Detailed conditions of the patient with dystonia‐deafness syndrome 5 days before bilateral subthalamic nucleus stimulation. Obvious laterocollis and trunk bending was persisting. Mild, obvious dystonia and tremor in both upper limbs were observed on many actions, and certain action of legs could provoke symptoms in hands.

Detailed conditions of the patient with dystonia‐deafness syndrome 7 months after bilateral subthalamic nucleus stimulation. There was hardly any relief of dystonia in the neck and trunk. Slight dystonia in the upper limbs remained on many actions, but was not observed on action of the distant part of the body or intermittently at rest.

Detailed conditions of the patient with dystonia‐deafness syndrome 12 months after bilateral subthalamic nucleus stimulation. Laterocollis and bending of the trunk was alleviated to some degree. The dystonic symptom in the upper limbs was similar to 5 months ago.

The combination of hearing impairment and dystonia is rare. In light of the prelingual deafness and early‐adulthood‐onset dystonia in this case, combined with the family history and negative results of laboratory and imaging tests, the diagnosis of DDS was suspected. To make a differential diagnosis and predict DBS outcome as recommended,3WES was conducted, and theACTBp.Arg183Trp variant was revealed. TheACTBgene encodes cytoplasmic β‐actin, which participates in many basic cellular processes. It was first identified in monozygotic twin brothers with DDS, followed by 7 similar patients.4Some of them suffered from comorbidities such as epilepsy, and some had positive family histories.2,5An overview of the previous cases is shown in TableS1.

DBS has been widely used for treating dystonia, and therapeutic targets of the global pallidus internus (GPi) and the STN seem to have similar efficacy.6Kwong et al3concluded that GPi‐DBS responses varied among different monogenic forms of dystonia, and it did benefit a majority of patients. Among the 9 patients with DDS, the 4 who received GPi‐DBS were observed with lasting improvement.7,8,9,10STN‐DBS has also been proven to enduringly benefit patients with different types of dystonia, with the potential advantage of a lower economic burden.11,12It was because STN‐DBS was reported to require lower parameter settings and less electrical energy, leading to a longer battery life.12Also, for our patient with limited economic resources, it was more affordable than rechargeable batteries. According to our center's clinical experience, preoperative MRI‐based localization and the final‐target location of STN was related to a higher level of accuracy than the GPi. In view of the this, we treated our patient with STN‐DBS, although there was no precedent of treating DDS with it. For 12 months of follow‐up, the efficacy remained satisfying.

Given the previous evidence and satisfying outcome of our case, STN might be a potential selection of stimulation target for patients suffering from DDS with anACTBmutation. Further high‐quality studies are needed.
