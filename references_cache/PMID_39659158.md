# Cognitive Behavioral Therapy and Lisdexamfetamine, Alone and Combined, for Binge-Eating Disorder With Obesity: A Randomized Controlled Trial.

OBJECTIVE: Binge-eating disorder (BED) is a prevalent, costly public health 
problem associated with serious functional impairments and heightened rates of 
psychiatric and medical comorbidities. Few evidence-based treatments are 
currently available for BED. We tested the effectiveness of cognitive-behavioral 
therapy (CBT), lisdexamfetamine (LDX), and combined CBT+LDX, for BED comorbid 
with obesity.
METHODS: Randomized controlled trial was conducted March 2019 to September 2023 
at a single site. N=141 patients with BED (83.7% women, mean age 43.6, mean BMI 
38.6 kg/m2) were randomized to one of three 12-week treatments: CBT (N=47), LDX 
(N=47), or CBT+LDX (N=47); 87.2% completed independent posttreatment 
assessments.
RESULTS: Mixed models revealed binge-eating frequency decreased significantly in 
all treatments, with CBT+LDX having the largest reduction and significantly 
outperforming CBT and LDX, which did not differ. Intention-to-treat binge-eating 
remission rates differed significantly between treatments, with CBT+LDX having 
the highest remission rate (70.2%) followed by CBT (44.7%) and LDX (40.4%). 
Mixed models revealed percent weight loss increased significantly throughout 
treatment with LDX and CBT+LDX but remained unchanged in CBT. LDX and CBT+LDX 
had significantly greater percent weight loss than CBT starting after one month 
and through posttreatment. Intention-to-treat rates of attaining ≥5% weight loss 
differed across treatments, with LDX having the highest (53.2%), followed by 
CBT+LDX (42.6%) and CBT (4.3%). Analyses revealed significant reductions in 
eating-disorder psychopathology; CBT+LDX had largest reductions and 
significantly outperformed CBT and LDX.
CONCLUSIONS: CBT, LDX, and CBT+LDX showed significant improvements in BED, with 
a consistent pattern of the combined CBT+LDX being superior to the two 
individual treatments, which differed little.

Binge-eating disorder (BED), a prevalent psychiatric disorder (1,2), is a costly public health problem (3) associated with elevated rates of additional psychiatric/medical disorders (4) and serious psychosocial/functional impairments including suicidality (1,2,5). BED is defined by recurrent binge eating (eating unusually large quantities while experiencing loss-of-control), marked distress about binge eating, presence of several behavioral indicators reflecting loss-of-control, and absence of extreme weight-compensatory behaviors (e.g., purging) that characterize bulimia nervosa (6). BED is associated strongly with obesity–with odds ratios 4.6-to-6.6 reported in major epidemiologic studies (1,2)–but has distinct psychopathological and neurobiological features from obesity and other manifestations of disordered eating (7). Left untreated, BED is chronic; for example, 12-month persistence of BED amongst those with lifetime BED diagnosis in a national sample of US adults was estimated at 63.5% (1). Despite the morbidity associated with BED, people with BED are infrequently identified or diagnosed, and those who do seek help rarely receive the very few evidence-based treatments (8).

Research has identified specific efficacious psychological and pharmacological treatments for BED (9,10). Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) is viewed as the “leading” treatment for BED in critical reviews (9,10) and several treatment guidelines (11). Controlled trials have found that CBT for BED reliably results in roughly half of patients attaining binge-eating remission along with significant improvements in associated eating-disorder psychopathology (12-14). CBT for BED has demonstrated superiority for reducing binge eating compared to other active treatments, including behavioral weight-loss therapy (13,14) and antidepressants (fluoxetine, fluvoxamine) (12,15). CBT, however, does not produce weight loss. Although many people with BED do not have obesity (1,2), obesity and weight-loss are frequent concerns/goals of most treatment-seeking patients with BED (10,16).

The sole pharmacological treatment for BED approved by US Food Drug Administration (FDA) is lisdexamfetamine dimesylate (LDX). LDX is a prodrug d-amphetamine thought to increase catecholamine availability. Neurobiological findings suggest binge eating is related to dysfunctions in dopamine and norepinephrine systems which play important roles in regulating eating and reward (7,17). LDX was approved for “severe-to-moderate” BED following three manufacturer-supported RCTs reporting LDX was superior to placebo. One 11-week RCT found LDX (50mg and 70mg, not 30mg) was significantly superior to placebo for reducing frequency of binge-eating days and secondary measures of associated eating-disorder psychopathology (18). Two identical 11-week RCTs confirmed LDX (dose optimization of 50-to-70mg) was significantly superior to placebo for reducing binge-eating days and secondary measures of eating-related psychopathology (19). LDX was associated with significantly higher binge-eating remission rates than placebo: 40% versus 14% (15) and 36-40% versus 13-14% (16).

In prior LDX trials (15,16), weight-loss was observed but was considered a safety measure and reported as a treatment-emergent adverse event rather than a specified outcome measure. Post-hoc analysis suggested LDX might be associated with greater weight-loss than placebo (16) indicating need for RCTs to provide weight-change data in addition to binge-eating outcomes. Current LDX product labelling includes “Limitation of Use” (not for weight-loss and uncertain safety/efficacy for obesity). Uncertainty regarding obesity/weight-loss, alongside restrictive eligibility criteria excluding patients without “moderate-to-severe” BED diagnostic specifier (15,16), limit generalizability and highlight need for further study.

A logical question is whether combining CBT and LDX might enhance outcomes. These two treatments, while individually effective, leave roughly half of patients without deriving sufficient benefit. To date, the strategy of combining pharmacotherapy and psychological treatments for BED has not enhanced outcomes (20,21), except for topiramate-plus-CBT (22); topiramate, however, has limited tolerability. Combining CBT and LDX seems more logical than previous combination trials given the complementary and potentially synergistic nature of CBT and LDX (7). Furthermore, because CBT, the “leading treatment” for BED fails to produce weight loss, testing LDX alone and combined with CBT is logical given the challenge in attaining weight loss in BED (16,21). This RCT tested effectiveness of CBT, LDX, and combined CBT+LDX for BED with comorbid obesity.

Single-site RCT protocol was approved by Yale Institutional Review Board and followed a data safety and monitoring plan with physician safety-officer. Participants provided written informed consent.

Participants (N=141), recruited via advertisements, were eligible if they met DSM-5 (6) BED criteria, were 18-64 years old, and had a body mass index (BMI) ≥30.0 and ≤50.0 (or ≥27.0 with obesity-related comorbidity). Exclusion criteria comprised clinical issues that, regardless of setting, would dictate need for alternative treatments or represent contraindications to lisdexamfetamine. Exclusionary criteria included: concurrent evidence-based treatment for eating/weight disorders, taking contraindicated medications (e.g., opiates, MAOIs, SSRIs, NDRIs, strong inhibitors of CYP2D6, stimulants), participation in another clinical trial/study, uncontrolled medical conditions, contraindications to LDX (e.g., alcohol and substance use disorders, psychosis/bipolar disorder, seizure history, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, history renal, hepatic, or chronic pulmonary disease, systolic blood pressure>160mmHg, diastolic blood pressure>100mmHg, or heart rate>100 beats/minute), and pregnancy/breastfeeding.

The 141 participants had mean age of 43.6 (SD=11.5) years and mean BMI of 38.6 (SD=5.2); 83.7% (N=118) were female, 90.8% (N=128) attended and/or finished college, and 75.9% (N=107) were White. Table 1 summarizes participants’ sociodemographic/psychiatric characteristics, Table 2 summarizes clinical characteristics, and Supplemental Table 1 summarizes somatic/medical conditions.

Assessments were performed by doctoral-level research-clinicians who were monitored throughout the study. Primary outcomes (binge-eating, measured weight) and secondary outcomes were assessed using well-established interviews and self-report measures.

Eating Disorder Examination-Interview (EDE; 16th-edition; 23) was administered to diagnose BED and assess binge-eating frequency and eating-disorder psychopathology (24) at baseline and post-treatment (immediately following 12-week treatments). The EDE, a frequently used primary measure in eating-disorder RCTs (e.g., 10,14,21), has good inter-rater/test-retest reliability (25). In this study, inter-rater (N=66) reliability of EDE was excellent; intra-class correlation coefficients were 0.92 (95% CI 0.87-0.95) for binge-eating frequency and 0.99 (95% CI 0.98-0.99) for global score (which had internal consistency coefficient alpha=0.86).

Weight and height were measured at baseline and weight was measured monthly and post-treatment.

Self-report measures were completed at baseline, monthly, and posttreatment. Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire (26), which has good test-retest reliability (27), obtained binge-eating frequency data during the past 28 days. Beck Depression Inventory-II (28) (alpha=0.92) is well-established measure of depression symptoms/levels (29).

Participants were randomized to one of three treatments for 12 weeks: CBT, LDX, or CBT+LDX. Randomization assigned participants to treatments in blocks of eight (to obviate secular trends and ensure approximately equal proportions); randomization was stratified by two baseline variables (overvaluation of shape/weight ≥4 on EDE and depression score >16 on BDI-II) reported to have prognostic significance for CBT versus pharmacotherapy (30). A biostatistician developed the randomization schedule, which was concealed prior to each randomization and kept blinded from participants until treatment started. Medication was provided open label by Yale Investigational Drug Service. Assessors of posttreatment outcomes were blinded to participants’ treatment conditions.

CBT, delivered in individual 60-minute sessions over 12 weeks. CBT followed manualized protocol developed originally at Oxford (31) that has been refined and empirically-supported for BED with generally comparable outcomes across trials 12 (32), 16 (13), and 24 (12) weeks. CBT is a focal treatment delivered in a collaborative interactive manner to patients who are viewed as colleagues in the therapeutic process. The first phase involves establishing collaborative therapeutic relationship, educating patients about the nature of binge eating and factors thought to maintain the problem (e.g., unhealthy restraint and unstructured eating, body-image disturbance), and initiating behavioral strategies (e.g., self-monitoring) to help identify maladaptive eating behaviors and work towards a normalized eating pattern. Second phase integrates cognitive restructuring, and patients learn to identify and challenge maladaptive cognitions regarding their eating and shape/weight concerns, and how to cope and overcome binge-eating triggers. Third phase focuses on consolidation of skills, maintenance of progress, and relapse prevention.

CBT was delivered by ten research-clinicians with programmatic interests and longstanding experiences in treating eating/weight disorders; nine were postdoctoral clinical psychologists and one was a fifth-year graduate student in clinical psychology with clinical/research experience with both CBT and BED/obesity. After receiving intensive training in the manualized CBT protocols, clinicians were supervised weekly by investigators (including use of recorded sessions) to monitor quality and adherence. Supervision included review of the structure, process, and content of sessions to ensure fidelity and to prevent drift; these same training/monitoring procedures were used in our previous trials testing CBT (12), comparing CBT and behavioral therapy (13), and behavioral therapy (21).

LDX was administered following the dose-optimization (targeting 50-70mg/day) protocol found superior to placebo in large-scale phase 3 RCTs (19). During week 1, LDX was 30 mg/day for initial titration. During week 2, LDX was titrated to 50mg/day. During weeks 3-4, LDX was increased to 70mg based on acceptable tolerability and clinical need. If 70mg was not tolerated reasonably by participants, downward titration to 50mg/day could occur. For the remaining treatment (weeks 4-12), the optimized LDX dose (50-70 mg/day) was maintained. If patients developed intolerable side-effects, the study physician reduced dosing to achieve tolerability and when patients experienced medically-concerning adverse events and/or could not tolerate the medication, they were withdrawn from the medication.

A faculty-level study physician delivered the pharmacotherapy focused on medication management (addressing compliance, safety, and side-effects). Additional psychotherapeutic interventions (behavioral, psychological, nutritional/dietary) were not allowed. Medication adherence and detailed side-effect and safety checklists were performed monthly. Monthly medication refills were accompanied by re-review of medication compliance and dosing schedules.

Sample size was based on power calculations using RCT data for CBT (13) and lisdexamfetamine (19). Trial was conservatively powered to detect medium effects (despite some medium-to-large effects in the literature, e.g., 14) after considering clinically-meaningful effect sizes. Sample of N=140, randomized to three treatments, yielded 80% power for medium effect sizes (f=0.32) for main effects at two-sided alpha level of 0.025 for the two co-primary dimensional outcomes (after assuming 20% attrition).

Analyses were intention-to-treat, performed for all randomized patients who attended first treatment session. We did not adjust p-levels for multiple comparisons; rather, unadjusted p-values are reported for all tests. The two co-primary outcome variables were binge eating and weight loss. Binge eating was analyzed as a continuous variable (i.e., monthly frequency of episodes) and, secondarily as a categorical outcome (i.e., “remission” from binge eating, defined as zero binge-eating episodes during previous 28 days (Eating Disorder Examination-Interview) with missing data imputed as failure (i.e., non-remission). Weight-loss (measured) was analyzed as a continuous variable (i.e., percent weight-loss). We further examined weight-loss as a categorical outcome (i.e., attaining ≥5% weight-loss, based on measured weight-loss from baseline with missing data imputed as failure) as a “complementary” analysis to the primary percent weight-loss variable. In addition to intended “convergence” with the percent weight-loss outcome, 5% weight-loss category–a standard outcome in obesity-related trials (33) and increasingly in BED trials (21)–has been found to be associated with clinical/metabolic benefits in those with BED with obesity (34). Additional secondary outcomes were eating-disorder psychopathology (Eating Disorder Examination Global) and depression (Beck Depression Inventory-II) scores.

Categorical outcomes (e.g., binge-eating remission, 5% weight loss) at post were compared between treatments using chi-square tests. For continuous measures, intention-to-treat analyses used all available data in mixed models without imputation. Variables were checked for normality using normal probability plots and appropriate transformations were applied as needed prior to statistical analysis. Mixed-effects models (35) were used to compare treatments for repeatedly measured quantitative outcomes. In each mixed model we had fixed effects of treatment group (CBT, LDX, CBT+LDX), time as a categorical variable (all available time points for particular outcomes), and their interaction. We considered different error structures, including unstructured, compound symmetry, compound symmetry heterogeneous and when possible (i.e. sufficient number of time-points) autoregressive of first-order/first-order with heterogeneous variances. Best structure for each outcome was selected using Schwarz’ Bayesian Criterion. Focused comparisons of least square means were used to explain significant effects in models.

We explored whether time of measurement (before or during the COVID pandemic; in-person versus remote visit) affected the results by including an indicator for timing of measurement as a covariate. The results did not change substantively; thus, the final models are not adjusted for COVID.

Figure 1 (CONSORT) summarizes participant flow throughout the study. Of the 4534 respondents screened, 386 consented and were evaluated for eligibility, and 141 were randomized and started treatment. Of the 141 participants, N=47 received CBT, N=47 received LDX, and N=47 received CBT+LDX. Post-treatment assessments were obtained for 87.2% (N=123/141) of participants.

Table 2 shows descriptive statistics for co-primary outcomes (binge-eating frequency and percent weight-loss). Figures 2-3 summarize analyses for continuous/categorical outcomes. Supplemental Table 2 lists adverse events.

Mixed models analyses of binge-eating frequency (episodes during the past month) at post-treatment (EDE Interview) revealed a significant interaction between treatment group and time (F(2,130)=3.53, p=0.03) and a significant main effect of time (F(1,130)=530.08, p<0.0001). Frequency of binge-eating episodes decreased significantly in all treatments from baseline to posttreatment (p-values <0.0001). CBT+LDX showed the largest reduction and significantly outperformed LDX (t(127)=−2.60, p=0.01) but not CBT (t(131)=−1.71, p=0.09); CBT and LDX did not differ (t(132)=0.81, p=0.42).

Figure 2-A summarizes frequency of binge-eating (during the past month) assessed monthly throughout the course of treatment (EDE-Questionnaire); this analysis was intended to confirm the primary EDE interview-based analysis (reported above) and to provide additional time-course findings. Mixed models revealed a significant interaction between treatment group and time (F(6,329)=4.69, p=0.0001), a significant main effect of treatment group (F(2,157)=6.84, p=0.001), and a significant main effect of time (F(3,330)=121.02, p<0.0001). Frequency of binge eating assessed monthly decreased significantly in all treatments from baseline to posttreatment (p-values <.0001). By month one, treatments differed significantly (although not at month two): LDX had the lowest binge-eating frequency and significantly outperformed CBT+LDX (t(377)=−2.77, p<0.006) and CBT (t(389)=5.22, p<0.0001) and CBT+LDX significantly outperformed CBT (t(383)=2.56, p=0.01). At posttreatment, CBT+LDX had the lowest binge-eating frequency and significantly outperformed CBT (t(371)=3.24, p=0.001) and LDX (t(371)=2.05, p=0.04); CBT and LDX did not differ significantly (t(378)=1.18, p=0.24).

Figure 2-B illustrates intention-to-treat binge-eating remission rates at post-treatment. Remission rates differed significantly between treatments (Chi-sq(2)=9.77, p=0.008) with CBT+LDX having the highest remission rate (70.2%; N=33/47) followed by CBT (44.7%; N=21/47) and LDX (40.4%; N=19/47).

Figure 3-A summarizes percent weight loss throughout the course of the treatments; Table 2 shows weight values at baseline and post-treatment and changes. Mixed models of percent weight loss revealed a significant interaction between treatment group and time (F(4,111)=5.50, p=.0004), a significant main effect of treatment group (F(2,128)=31.31, p<0.0001), and a significant main effect of time (F(2,111)=17.45, p<0.0001). Percent weight loss increased significantly throughout treatment in CBT+LDX and LDX (ps<.0001) but remained unchanged in CBT (p=0.82). LDX and CBT+LDX had significantly greater percent weight loss than CBT starting with month one and continuing throughout treatment (p-values <0.0001). At posttreatment, LDX and CBT+LDX showed the largest reduction and both significantly outperformed CBT ([t(127)=6.32, p<0.0001] and [t(127)=5.77, p<0.0001], respectively); LDX and CBT+LDX did not differ (t(126)=0.65, p=0.52).

Figure 3-B shows intention-to-treat rates of participants attaining 5% or greater weight loss at post-treatment by treatment condition. The treatments differed significantly in the proportion of participants who achieved ≥5% weight loss (Chi-sq(2)=28.02, p<.0001). LDX had the highest percentage who attained ≥5% weight loss (53.2%; N=25/47), followed by CBT+LDX (42.6%; N=20/47) and CBT (4.3%; N=2/47).

Mixed models analysis of eating-disorder psychopathology (log-transformed Global score of EDE Interview) at posttreatment revealed a significant interaction between treatment group and time (F(2,123)=13.41, p<0.0001), a significant main effect of treatment group (F(2,140)=3.51, p=0.03), and a significant main effect of time (F(1,123)=227.48, p<0.0001). EDE Global scores decreased significantly in all treatments from baseline to posttreatment (p-values <0.0001). CBT+LDX showed the largest reduction and significantly outperformed both LDX (t(119)=−3.86, p=0.0002) and CBT (t(124)=−4.86, p<0.0001); CBT and LDX did not differ (t(125)=−1.14, p=0.26).

Mixed models analysis of depression (square-root transformed BDI scores monthly) revealed a significant interaction between treatment group and time (F(6,334)=2.26, p=0.04) and a significant main effect of time (F(3,334)=79.18, p<0.0001). BDI scores decreased significantly in all treatments to posttreatment (p-values <0.0001); treatments differed (LDX and CBT+LDX >CBT) at month one (F(2,229)=4.58, p=0.01) but did not differ significantly from each other thereafter at either month two (F(2,259)=0.90, p=0.41) or at posttreatment (F(2,239)=2.65, p=0.07).

In this controlled study of adults with BED with co-occurring obesity, CBT, LDX, and combined CBT+LDX were associated with significant improvements in BED, with a consistent pattern of combined CBT+LDX approach being superior to individual treatments. CBT and LDX differed little in binge-eating outcomes, but LDX resulted in significantly greater weight-loss than CBT. For binge-eating, CBT+LDX had the largest reduction (96.1% reduction in frequency of episodes with 70.2% achieving remission) and significantly outperformed CBT (89.5% reduction in frequency with 44.7% achieving remission) and LDX (79.7% reduction in frequency with 40.4% achieving remission). For weight-loss, LDX (5.5% reduction with 53.2% achieving ≥5%) and CBT+LDX (4.8% reduction with 42.6% achieving ≥ 5%) had the largest and significant reductions whereas weight remained essentially stable in CBT (0.5% reduction with 4.3% achieving ≥5%).

The outcomes observed for CBT and LDX are consistent with previous trials testing the interventions against other conditions. The remission rate (zero binge-eating episodes/past month) for CBT of 44.7% is nearly identical to the 44.4% remission rate reported by Grilo and colleagues (13) in a 24-week RCT comparing CBT to other behavioral interventions for BED and approximates modal findings in literature (9,10). The remission rate for LDX of 40.4% in this RCT is nearly identical to the remission rates reported by McElroy (19) for two Shire-funded phase-III trials (36.2%−40.0%).

The significantly superior outcomes for the combined CBT+LDX–the first such comparison in the literature–are particularly noteworthy. This is only the second trial finding significant advantage for combining pharmacotherapy with CBT for enhancing both binge-eating and weight-loss outcomes in BED (20). Only topiramate has been found to enhance outcomes attained with CBT (22); topiramate, however, has limited tolerability with high discontinuation rates because of adverse events. The superior outcomes observed for the combined CBT+LDX intervention (70.2% binge-eating remission with 42.6% attaining 5% weight loss) provide clinicians with a robust approach for patients with BED. The superiority of combined CBT+LDX approach might reflect, in part, complementary and synergistic effect of the two treatments (7). CBT and LDX likely operate via distinct mechanisms (the former via reducing unhealthy restraint and unstructured eating and addressing the core body-image disturbance and the later via eating regulation and reward effects and reduced impulsivity) and one can speculate that some of the LDX effects on improving executive functioning might enhance ability to derive greater benefit from CBT (7). Future research should examine moderators and mediators, and probe for mechanisms of change for these treatments.

For weight-loss, LDX (mean 5.5% reduction with 53.2% achieving ≥5%) and CBT+LDX (mean 4.8% reduction with 42.6% achieving ≥ 5%) are noteworthy particularly given the difficulty attaining weight loss in BED (16,20,21). A systematic review and meta-analysis (16) indicated weight-loss interventions produce less weight-loss in patients with versus without BED. A RCT testing naltrexone/bupropion combination (a current FDA-approved anti-obesity medication) found a mean of only 2.1 percent weight-loss with only 18.8% of patients attaining ≥5% weight-loss (21). The observed weight losses with LDX and CBT+LDX are encouraging given findings that modest amounts (i.e., ≥5%) are associated with significant reductions in cardiovascular disease risk factors in patients with BED with obesity (34).

We note methodological strengths/limitations. Study strengths include manualized CBT and pharmacotherapy delivered by trained/monitored psychologists and physicians, independent assessments using well-validated measures, limited exclusionary criteria to broaden generalizability including full range of BED (not just “moderate-to-severe”) cases, and good retention. Pharmacotherapy involved minimal clinical management without psychotherapeutic/nutritional interventions to reflect “clinical reality” in many clinical settings.

Several limitations are noteworthy. We did not include a therapy-control nor a placebo-medication, which—while not needed for primary aims—could have yielded additional context. LDX and CBT+LDX both had 21.3% participants medically withdrawn, in addition to 12.5% and 4.9% dropouts, respectively, while CBT had 21.3% dropouts. The treatment non-completion rates are consistent with relevant literatures, although 21.3% withdrawal rate for LDX might be viewed as a treatment limitation. Generalizability of findings to different clinical settings and healthcare providers or to persons with different sociodemographic/clinical characteristics is uncertain. Participant group lacked robust diversity: 83.7% were female, 75.5% White, 89.4% heterosexual, and 90.2% attended at least some college; the group, however, comprised some degree of racial/ethnic representation (13.5% Black and 15.6% Latinx). As context, White women account for vast majority of participants in RCTs across eating disorders (36) and epidemiological studies indicate men and minority groups seek BED treatment at low rates (8). To date, neither sex (37) nor race (38) have moderated BED outcomes in aggregated analyses of psychological, pharmacological, and multimodal interventions.

Generalizability of findings to patients with BED without obesity is uncertain given our eligibility requirement. McElroy et al (19) RCT testing LDX included a minority of participants without obesity, but no sensitivity analyses were reported. One RCT testing naltrexone/bupropion for BED—which recruited across weight continuum and stratified randomization by obesity status—reported no predictive/moderating effects of obesity status (39). Generalizability to other manifestations of binge-eating or eating disorders (e.g., bulimia nervosa, anorexia nervosa-binge/purge-subtype) are unknown, and LDX seems contraindicated in patients where weight-loss would be inappropriate. Longer-term outcomes of these acute findings are uncertain. CBT outcomes are well-maintained following treatment (13,14,15) but durability of LDX outcomes is uncertain. Maintenance LDX reduced relapse risk amongst acute LDX responders (40).
