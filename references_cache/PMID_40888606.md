OBJECTIVE: To estimate the risk of cholesteatoma in patients with alpha-1
antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) compared to the general population using
time-to-event analysis.
METHODS: We conducted a nationwide nested cohort study identifying all patients
with AATD in Denmark and subsequently matching each case with up to 10 controls
based on age, sex, and municipality. Hazard ratios (HR) of cholesteatoma by AATD
were calculated using Cox regression analysis adjusted for age and sex.
RESULTS: The study identified 2702 individuals with AATD and 26,750 control
subjects. Individuals with AATD versus controls had a HR for cholesteatoma
surgery of 3.62 (95% CI: 1.93-6.80) and a HR for non-cholesteatoma surgery of
1.40 (1.04-1.89).
CONCLUSION: Individuals with AATD had a 3.6-fold increased risk of cholesteatoma
surgery compared with matched controls without AATD. The data support that AATD
contributes to pathophysiological processes underlying the development of
cholesteatoma.

Alpha‐1 antitrypsin (AAT) is a serine protease inhibitor and an acute phase reactant, which inhibits neutrophil elastase and other proteases thereby protecting mainly elastic tissues of the body [1, 2].

The coding gene, SERPINA1, is localized on chromosome 14, and the Z and S variants of the gene cause about 95% of the cases with severe alpha‐1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) (ZZ and SZ genotypes). AATD is mainly known to cause early onset lung and liver diseases, but AAT and its function have also been investigated in the middle ear in a few studies. A previous study by Carlsson et al. [2] found that individuals with AATD had an increased risk of severe complications in otitis media compared to the general population. Furthermore, effusion from the middle ear in patients with acute and secretory otitis media contains proteolytic enzymes with AAT identified as the main protease inhibitor in the middle ear [2].

Cholesteatoma is an abnormal, noncancerous growth that can develop in the middle ear and mastoid process. A commonly accepted theory for developing cholesteatoma is the invagination theory. According to this notion, a retraction pocket develops because of negative middle ear pressure. If keratin from the squamous epithelium at some point starts to accumulate in the retraction pocket, a cholesteatoma may have been formed [3]. AATD could result in degradation of elastic fibers in the tympanic membrane and Eustachian tube similar to what is seen for elastic tissue of the lungs. Because of this degradation, there may be a greater risk of retraction pocket or Eustachian tube dysfunction, which could predispose to cholesteatoma.

We hypothesized that individuals with AATD have an increased risk of cholesteatoma compared to control subjects in the general population. To test this hypothesis, we determined the risks of cholesteatoma surgery and middle ear surgery in patients with AATD compared to matched control subjects in a nationwide cohort study design.

The study was designed as a nationwide nested cohort study collecting data on patients with AATD from three different registers: the Copenhagen General Population Study, The Danish AAT Deficiency Registry, and the Danish National Patient Registry (LPR) [4, 5, 6, 7]. All patients identified were matched based on age, sex, and municipality with up to 10 controls from the general Danish population without AATD.

Patients diagnosed with AATD were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) code DE88.0 and genotype/phenotype information from the Copenhagen General Population Study and Danish Alpha‐1 Antitrypsin Deficiency Registry.

Cholesteatoma surgery was defined as a cholesteatoma diagnosis in Danish National Patient Registry (ICD‐8: 387.09; ICD‐10: H71.9, H95.0) combined with relevant surgical codes, which was done using the “Surgical and Treatment Classification” (OBK) from the Danish Board of Health from 1977 to 1995 and the “Classification of Surgical Procedures” (NCSP) from the Nordic Medico‐Statistical Committee hereafter. The surgical codes used from NCSP: KDC, KDD, KDE, KDFD30, except for KDCA10, KDCA20, KDCW00, and KDEE00. The surgical codes used from OBK: (20380, 20420, 20440, 20460, 20480, 20500, 20520, 20540, 20560, 20561, 20569, 20580, 20600, 20620, 20640, 20660, 20700, 20990). The surgical codes cover all kind of surgery in the middle ear and eardrum (Table S1). The outcome cholesteatoma surgery was defined as the presence of both a cholesteatoma diagnosis code from the Danish National Patient Registry and a relevant surgical code from OBK/NCSP.

The same diagnostic and surgical codes were used for non‐cholesteatoma surgery, with the exception that individuals with surgical codes linked to a cholesteatoma diagnosis were excluded.

For the outcome of any middle ear surgery, only surgical codes were used. The surgical codes were the same as for cholesteatoma surgery.

Diagnosis codes for hearing loss (both conductive and sensorineural), otorrhea, and middle ear infections were collected from the Danish National Patient Registry as either primary or secondary discharge diagnoses: Hearing loss (ICD‐8: 38800, 38909, 38919, 38929, 38999, 78139; ICD‐10: DH900, DH901, DH902, DH903, DH904, DH905, DH905C, DH905D, DH906, DH907, DH908), otorrhea, and middle ear infections (ICD‐8: 38100, 38101, 38102, 38108, 38109, 38119, 38199, 38209, 38219, 38299; ICD‐10: DH650, DH650A, DH652, DH653, DH660, DH660A, DH661, DH662, DH664, DH669, DH920, DH921).

Diagnosis codes for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), liver cirrhosis, cleft palate, and cleft lip and palate were retrieved from the Danish National Patient Registry as either primary or secondary discharge diagnoses: COPD (ICD‐8: 491‐492; ICD‐10: J41‐J44), liver cirrhosis (ICD‐8: 571.91–571.92, 571.9; ICD‐10: K70.3, K74), cleft palate and cleft lip and palate (ICD8: 74909, 74920, 74921, 74929; ICD10: DQ353, DQ355, DQ357, DQ359, DQ372, DQ373, DQ374, DQ375, DQ378, DQ379).

Pearson's χ
2 test was used for categorical data, whereas Student's t‐test was used for continuous data. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered significant. Kaplan–Meier graphs were applied to illustrate cumulative incidences of outcomes according to AATD status. Hazard ratios (HR) were estimated using Cox regression analyses with age as the underlying time variable. Individuals were followed from 1st January 1977 until the time of event and censored at emigration, death, or end of follow‐up (December 31, 2018), whichever came first. We performed a stratified analysis by sex, age, COPD, liver cirrhosis, middle ear infections, and otorrhea. The possibility of interaction between AATD and these variables was checked by including them as an interaction term in the analyses. All data management and statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 18 (StataCorp. College Station, TX).

The study was approved by Herlev and Gentofte Hospital and Danish ethics committees (identification number: H‐KF‐01‐144/01) and was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants in the Danish AAT Deficiency Registry provided written informed consent in connection with their participation in the Copenhagen General Population Study. No written consent was needed for the nationwide study according to Danish law. Region Zealand's registration number: p‐2024‐17266.

A total of 2702 individuals with AATD were included in the study. The sex, age, and municipality matched control group consisted of 26,750 individuals. Characteristics of the AATD and control groups are shown in Table 1. Individuals with AATD more often had COPD, liver cirrhosis, middle ear infections, otorrhea, any middle ear surgery, and cholesteatoma surgery compared with individuals without AATD (p value < 0.002, Table 1). The prevalence of cleft palate/cleft lip and palate was the same in the study as in the control group (p value 0.727, Table 1). This also applies to hearing loss (p value 0.347, Table 1).

When looking at cholesteatoma surgery only, the cumulative incidence of cholesteatoma surgery remained elevated in individuals with AATD compared with the control group (Figure 3). After adjustment for sex and age, the HR for cholesteatoma surgery in individuals with AATD compared to controls was 3.62 (95% CI: 1.93–6.80) (Figure 2).

When looking at non‐cholesteatoma surgery only, the results showed only a slightly higher cumulative incidence of non‐cholesteatoma surgery in individuals with AATD compared with the control group (Figure 4). This analysis demonstrated that individuals with AATD compared with the matched controls had a 3.6‐fold higher risk for cholesteatoma surgery, a 1.4‐fold higher risk for non‐cholesteatoma surgery, and a 1.6‐fold higher risk of any middle ear surgery. The result for cholesteatoma surgery was consistent when stratifying by possible confounders, such as age, sex, COPD, liver cirrhosis, middle ear infections, and otorrhea. We also investigated whether AATD constitutes a risk factor for congenital cleft palate or cleft lip and palate. Given that these craniofacial anomalies are associated with an increased risk of cholesteatoma development, it was crucial to exclude them as potential confounders [3]. This was ensured through statistical analysis, with a p value of 0.727 confirming the absence of a significant association between AATD and these craniofacial anomalies. Furthermore, we investigated whether AATD constitutes a risk factor for hearing loss, but this was not supported by the data and was excluded as a potential confounder, p value 0.347.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies examining the association between AATD and cholesteatoma surgery specifically. Nakanishi and colleagues analyzed data from the UK Biobank and found an odds ratio of 1.4 (95% CI 1.0–2.0) for cholesteatoma in individuals with MZ heterozygosity, while the odds ratio for individuals with SZ compound heterozygosity was even higher at 2.5 (95% CI 0.8–7.7) [8], supporting our findings of a HR of 3.62 in individuals with severe ZZ and SZ AATD genotypes.

Previous studies seem to support an important role for AAT in the middle ear. El‐saied et al. [9] showed that AAT plays a beneficial role in the healing of the tympanic membrane in mice. AAT also seems to protect middle ear mucosa and reduce proteolytic damage in chinchillas suffering from secretory otitis media and purulent otitis media [10]. One study found that mice in which human AAT was overexpressed regained the functionality of the inner ear faster after an induced trauma, suggesting a healing role of AAT [11]. Finally, Antonelli et al. [12] found that neutrophil elastase activity was especially elevated in middle ear effusions in individuals with cholesteatoma.

Our findings demonstrated an increased risk of cholesteatoma surgery in patients with AATD. This may theoretically be related to the lack of inhibition of neutrophil elastase leading to degradation of elastic fibers in the tympanic membrane similar to what is seen for elastic tissues in the lungs. Chole et al. [13] identified elastic fibers and elastin throughout the human tympanic membrane. Thus, it is possible that elastin degradation in people with AATD may lead to the tympanic membrane being more vulnerable to negative middle ear pressure, increasing the risk of retraction pocket development and cholesteatoma.

The cartilaginous part of the Eustachian tube also contains elastin [14]. Another possible mechanism could be an increased degradation of elastin in the Eustachian tube in individuals with AATD resulting in Eustachian tube dysfunction and hence negative middle ear pressure, retraction pockets, and cholesteatoma [3]. A hampered clearance of keratin could also be speculated to contribute to the development of cholesteatoma in AATD patients.

As AATD is a rare disease, conducting register research using a nationwide nested study design was advantageous. The chosen method allowed us to study a large group, thereby enabling the examination of a sufficient number of cases for statistical analysis and validity. The matching of the study population with the controls regarding age, sex, and municipality was done to minimize the possibility of confounding. Additionally, the control subject sample was randomly selected in a homogenous general population. When using national registers in research, there may be a risk of misclassification. The AATD diagnosis was confirmed by genotyping analysis in 51% of the subjects, whereas the rest were identified by the ICD10 code E88.0 [6, 7, 15]. Although the study population was one of the largest so far, the number of ear surgeries in our cohort was relatively small in the stratified analysis, and results from here should be interpreted with care.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies and largest studies demonstrating an increased risk of cholesteatoma surgery in AATD patients. Such findings could expand the current understanding of potential middle ear pathophysiology.

The cholesteatoma cohort was defined based on the presence of both a diagnostic code and a surgical procedure code. When these codes are combined, the positive predictive value for the presence of cholesteatoma is 89% [16]. Cholesteatoma is often not diagnosed definitively until surgery, when the middle ear can be directly visualized. By requiring both a diagnostic and a surgical code, false positive cases are effectively excluded. Nearly all patients with cholesteatoma undergo surgery. In support of this, Kemppainen et al. [17] found that 95.6% of patients with cholesteatoma were treated surgically, with an average waiting time of 6 months. This combined coding strategy therefore enhances the validity of our cohort and findings.

This nationwide nested study demonstrated a 3.6‐fold increased risk of cholesteatoma surgery in individuals with AATD compared with a random sample of individuals without AATD. The study also found that individuals with AATD had a 1.4‐fold higher risk for non‐cholesteatoma surgery and a 1.6‐fold higher risk of any middle ear surgery. The data support that AATD contributes to pathophysiological processes underlying the development of cholesteatoma in humans.
