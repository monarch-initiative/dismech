# Molecular Changes Implicate Angiogenesis and Arterial Remodeling in Systemic Sclerosis-Associated and Idiopathic Pulmonary Hypertension.
**Authors:** Zhou Y, Tabib T, Huang M, Yuan K, Kim Y, Morse C, Sembrat J, Valenzi E, Lafyatis R
**Journal:** Arterioscler Thromb Vasc Biol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1161/ATVBAHA.123.320005](https://doi.org/10.1161/ATVBAHA.123.320005)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a common complication of systemic sclerosis (SSc) and a leading cause of mortality among patients with this disease. PH can also occur as an idiopathic condition (idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension). Investigation of transcriptomic alterations in vascular populations is critical to elucidating cellular mechanisms underlying pathobiology of SSc-associated and idiopathic PH.

METHODS: We analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing profiles of endothelial and perivascular mesenchymal populations from explanted lung tissue of patients with SSc-associated PH (n=16), idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (n=3), and healthy controls (n=15). Findings were validated by immunofluorescence staining of explanted human lung tissue.

RESULTS: Three disease-associated endothelial populations emerged. Two angiogenic endothelial cell (EC) subtypes markedly expanded in SSc-associated PH lungs: tip ECs expressing canonical tip markers PGF and APLN and phalanx ECs expressing genes associated with vascular development, endothelial barrier integrity, and Notch signaling. Gene regulatory network analysis suggested enrichment of Smad1 (SMAD family member 1) and PPAR-γ (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ) regulon activities in these 2 populations, respectively. Mapping of potential ligand-receptor interactions highlighted Notch, apelin-APJ (apelin receptor), and angiopoietin-Tie (tyrosine kinase with immunoglobulin-like and EGF-like domains 1) signaling pathways between angiogenic ECs and perivascular cells. Transitional cells, expressing both endothelial and pericyte/smooth muscle cell markers, provided evidence for the presence of endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition. Transcriptional programs associated with arterial endothelial dysfunction implicated VEGF-A (vascular endothelial growth factor-A), TGF-β1 (transforming growth factor beta-1), angiotensin, and TNFSF12 (tumor necrosis factor ligand superfamily member 12)/TWEAK (TNF-related weak inducer of apoptosis) in the injury/remodeling phenotype of PH arterial ECs.

CONCLUSIONS: These data provide high-resolution insights into the complexity and plasticity of the pulmonary endothelium in SSc-associated PH and idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension and provide direct molecular insights into soluble mediators and transcription factors driving PH vasculopathy.

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) affects 8–12% of patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc)1–3 and is a major cause of mortality4. SSc associated PH (SSc-PH) can occur under several clinical settings, particularly in the presence of interstitial lung disease (ILD), or as isolated pulmonary arterial hypertension. It can also accompany cardiac involvement or pulmonary veno-occlusive disease (PVOD)5. Compared to patients with other forms of PH, such as idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (iPAH), SSc-PH patients show a less favorable outcome and a lower survival rate6–8.

Vascular disease is recognized as a potential initiating factor in SSc9, supported by the early manifestation of Raynaud’s phenomenon in almost all SSc patients. Pulmonary vasculopathy associated with SSc predominantly develops in arterioles and small arteries, presenting as structural alterations in all layers of the vascular wall: intimal hyperplasia, medial thickening and adventitial fibrosis10. While the exact mechanisms of SSc-PH pathogenesis are incompletely understood, current paradigms support that endothelial injury and inflammation lead to deregulation of growth factors, cytokines and chemokines, which subsequently activate vasoactive and proliferative signaling pathways causing abnormalities in arterial endothelial cells (ECs), smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and fibroblasts11. The cellular origin of obliterative neointimal lesions found in SSc-PH and other types of PH is still a matter of debate. Proliferative expansion of vascular SMCs has been proposed as a major contributor12. An alternative explanation is endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), in which ECs acquire a mesenchymal-like phenotype and participate in neointimal formation13–17. Co-localization of endothelial markers and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) was observed in SSc-PH17,18. Dysregulated angiogenesis is another key feature of SSc-associated vasculopathy. Some investigators have emphasized the loss of angiogenesis, especially in advanced disease19,20, while others the angiogenic features of SSc21, both attributed to an altered profile of pro- and anti-angiogenic factors and a reduced EC response to angiogenic signals.

Recent advances in single-cell technologies allow investigation of EC phenotypic heterogeneity in health and disease at unprecedented resolution. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies of the human lung revealed gene signatures associated with arterial, venous and capillary ECs, as well as circulation-specific (COL15A1− pulmonary venous ECs and COL15A1+ systemic venous ECs) and specialized subpopulations (EDNRB+
SOSTDC1+ aerocytes and FCN3+
IL7R+ general capillary ECs)22. Moreover, angiogenic EC populations have been described in different disease models. In sprouting angiogenesis, tip cells migrate towards the angiogenic stimulus, followed by proliferative stalk cells, which elongate the vessel sprout. Quiescent phalanx cells line the nascent vessels and ensures blood perfusion23. scRNA-seq studies of tumor and laser-induced choroidal neovascularization identified tip cells and proliferating ECs24–26. Conserved tip cell markers include previously described genes APLN, ANGPT2 and CXCR427,28, as well as novel markers PGF, LXN, ADM, ANGPTL2, and INSR. In addition, novel angiogenic cell types including intermediate ECs transitioning towards tip cells, maturing neophalanx cells, and other specialized EC subpopulations such as breach cells, hypothesized to be involved in sprouting initiation, have been discovered25,26.

Here we analyzed scRNA-seq profiles of endothelial and perivascular cells from SSc-PH, iPAH and healthy lungs. We identified EC subpopulations involved in angiogenesis and EndoMT, characterized pathological arterial EC cell states, and investigated perturbed intercellular communication underlying transcriptional alterations of ECs. These studies provide a strong molecular framework for the arterial EC injury, the obliterative vasculopathy, and increased angiogenesis in SSc-PH, and suggest similar molecular processes drive iPAH.

All data and materials have been made publicly available at the GEO and can be accessed at GSE244781. The analytic code will be made available upon request.

This study aims to identify molecular signatures associated with pulmonary vasculopathy in SSc-PH and iPAH. For this purpose, we studied explanted lung tissue samples from SSc-PH patients (n = 16) and iPAH patients (n = 3). Healthy control lung samples (n = 15) were obtained from organ donors whose lungs were unable to be transplanted. Due to the rarity of SSc-PH and iPAH patients undergoing transplant, sample size was determined by availability. No randomization, blinding, or prior power calculations were conducted.

The University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board approved procedures involving human samples. Procurement of explanted lung tissue, sample processing, and single-cell sequencing were carried out as previously described29–34. Samples were processed with 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3’ v1, 3’v2, 3’ v3, 5’ v1 or 5’ v2 reagents (Table S1). Raw data was demultiplexed using Cell Ranger mkfastq function and aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome. Downstream data processing was performed with the R package Seurat35 v4.1.1. Potential doublets were predicted by the R package DoubletFinder36 v2.0.3 and removed. We excluded cells expressing fewer than 200 genes and cells with > 15% mitochondrial counts.

Gene expression matrix was normalized and scaled using NormalizeData and ScaleData functions in Seurat, followed by dimensionality reduction using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The R package Harmony37 v0.1.0 was used to correct batch effects due to the usage of different chemistries. A shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph was constructed using FindNeighbors function in Seurat. Cells were clustered with the Louvain algorithm, as implemented in FindClusters function in Seurat. We used Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to visualize cell clusters. Cell type identities were assigned based on cluster-specific markers generated using the FindMarkers function in Seurat. Clusters with low UMI counts and no known cell-type specific markers were considered low quality and removed. For re-clustering of endothelial, pericyte, smooth muscle and fibroblast populations, we subsetted clusters 3-Vascular Endothelial, 7-Fibroblast, 15-Pericyte/Smooth Muscle and 16-Lymphatic Endothelial, and performed scaling, dimensionality reduction, SNN graph construction and clustering as described above35. We used the AddModuleScores function to calculate average expression levels of gene sets associated with proliferation (MKI67, TOP2A, BIRC5, CENPU, and PCLAF) and the tip cell phenotype (top 50 most highly expressed genes in tip cells previously characterized in human lung tumor25). ECs with a proliferation module score > 0.3 were annotated as proliferating ECs, whereas phalanx cell with a tip module score > 1 were annotated as tip cells. Identities of fibroblast subpopulations, smooth muscle cells, pericytes and other endothelial subpopulations were assigned based on cluster-specific markers generated using the FindMarkers function in Seurat.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed to identify gene markers for phalanx cells using the FindMarkers function in Seurat packages using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum test. Top 100 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) ranked by fold change were used as input for Gene Ontology biological processes enrichment analysis38–40 (http://geneontology.org). A custom reference list consisting of all 36601 genes investigated in the single-cell experiment was used as the background dataset. Differential gene expression analysis comparing SSc-PH or iPAH to control cells was performed for endothelial or mesenchymal subpopulations with the FindMarkers function as well. DEGs with average fold change > 1.5, adjusted p-value < 0.05 and expression in ≥ 10% of cells were used as input for Ingenuity Pathway Analysis41 (IPA, Qiagen). Genes expressed in ≥ 10% of cells were pre-ranked based on fold change and used as input for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). GSEA was performed with the R package fgsea42 v1.20.0 using the “hallmark” gene sets or C2 curated gene sets.

Pseudobulk differential expression analysis was performed to validate results generated using single-cell-based approaches. Raw sequencing counts for each gene and cell were extracted from the Seurat object using the Seurat function GetAssayData, and subsequently summed per gene and cell type for each sample, resulting in a new count matrix. For each cell type, samples containing 10 or fewer cells were excluded from downstream analysis. Analysis of the aggregated count matrix was performed using the edgeR (R package v3.36.0) quasi-likelihood pipeline43. The genes from the differential expression analysis output were pre-ranked based on fold change and used as input for GSEA. GSEA was performed with the R package fgsea42 v1.20.0 using the C2 curated gene sets. DEGs with average log2 fold change > 0.585 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 were used as input for IPA41 (Qiagen).

The R packages Connectome44 v1.0.1 and nichenetr45 v1.1.0 were used for cell-cell interaction analysis. For analysis using Connectome, DEGs showing expression in ≥ 10% of cells with adjusted p-value < 0.05 were used as input for generating differential connectome. Ligand-receptor pairs were filtered to focus on perturbed interactions between arterial endothelial cells and mesenchymal/immune cell populations, and visualized with circus plots.

For NicheNet analysis, arterial endothelial cells were defined as receivers, whereas pericytes, smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts and immune cell populations were defined as senders. DEGs showing expression in ≥ 10% of cells with adjusted p-value < 0.05 were used as potential target genes for ligand prediction.

To identify cell-type specific gene regulatory networks, we performed single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) analysis using the python implementation (pySCENIC) as described by Van de Sande et al46. Briefly, candidate modules consisting of TFs and their predicted target genes were inferred from co-expression patterns by GRNBoost2. Only direct target genes identified by cisTarget was retained. Regulon activities in a particular cell were calculated via AUCell and visualized using Seurat. For SCENIC analyses we subsetted vascular endothelial, pericyte and smooth muscle clusters, and filtered out genes with < 156 UMI counts in all cells or detected in < 1% of cells.

For formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded human lung tissue, immunofluorescence staining was performed with Tyramide SuperBoost kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, sections underwent standard deparaffination and rehydration procedures and were subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval by steaming in citrate buffer (pH 6) for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity of the sample was quenched by incubation in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 1 h, followed by blocking with 10% normal goat serum (Life technologies) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 10% goat serum overnight at 4°C, and secondary antibodies (SuperBoost goat anti-rabbit and goat anti-mouse poly-HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies) for 1 h at room temperature. Tyramide working solution was prepared following the manufacturer’s protocol, incubated for 5 min and neutralized with stop solution. Slides were then counter-stained with Hoechst 33258 (Invitrogen) and mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen). The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit-anti-human COL15A1 (0.8 μg/mL, PA5–53667, Invitrogen), rabbit-anti-human ACKR1 (2 μg/mL, PA5–82549, Invitrogen) and rabbit-anti-human CA4 (1 μg/mL, 10472-R039, Sino Biological), mouse-anti-human SMA (0.0355 μg/mL, M0851, Dako), mouse-anti-human vWF (2.64 μg/mL, M0616, Dako). Lung sections were imaged with FV3000 (Olympus) confocal microscope. Quantitative analysis of the images was performed with ImageJ (v2.14.0).

For immunofluorescence staining of OCT-embedded frozen human lung tissues, 10–15 μm cryosections were thawed from −80°C to room temperature followed by washing in 1X PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocked with normal goat/donkey serum for 1 hour, followed by primary antibody incubation overnight at 4°C. The next day, sections were rewashed with 1X PBS and incubated with 488, 594, or 647 fluorophores containing secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Slides were mounted with Prolong Gold Antifade Solution containing DAPI (Life Technologies). The following primary antibodies were used: sheep-anti-human CD31 (2 μg/mL, AF806, R&D Systems), mouse-anti-human/rat 3G5 (1:100, Ke Yuan lab), mouse-anti-mouse/human SMA-Alexa 647 (2 μg/mL, sc-32251, Santa Cruz).

For cell proportion analysis and quantification of IHC staining, data was assessed for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and equal variances (F test) using the “shapiro.test” and “var.test” functions from the R package stats v4.3.0, respectively. None of the datasets passed the normality test. For comparison between two groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was performed using the “wilcox.test” function from the R package stats v4.3.0. For comparison between three or more groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was performed using the “kruskal.test” function from the R package stats v4.3.0. For single-cell based differential gene expression analysis using Seurat, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed, and the p-values were adjusted based on the Bonferroni correction. For pseudobulk-based differential gene expression analysis and GSEA, the p-values were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. P<0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

We used scRNA-seq to profile explanted lung tissue samples of patients diagnosed with SSc-PH (n = 16) or iPAH (n = 3), as well as healthy lungs declined for transplantation (n = 15; Fig. S1A and Table S1). Some of these samples were described previously29–34. Two of the controls and three of the SSc-PH samples were depleted of epithelial and/or immune cell population in ½ of the cells analyzed (Table S1). For the remaining samples, all cells (745–5738/sample retained after quality control) were analyzed after tissue digestion. The scRNA-seq dataset from 34 lung samples was pre-processed using Cell Ranger (barcode rank plots shown in Fig. S2), and integrated with the R package Harmony37 to correct for observed batch effects associated with the usage of different chemistries (Fig. S1B). After removal of potential doublets predicted by the R package DoubletFinder36, cells were clustered and visualized using UMAP (Figs. S1C and D). Clusters with low UMI counts and no known cell-type specific markers were considered low quality and removed. A total of 111,305 cells were retained for downstream analysis (Fig S3 and Table S2). We identified 23 distinct cell types using literature-based gene markers (Figs. S1E, S1F, and S4, Table S3). Comparing the percentage of each cell type in SSc-PH, iPAH and control samples showed several alterations in cell proportions (Figs. S5A and S5B, Table S4). Notably, the percentage of vascular endothelial cells was higher in all the PH subtypes compared to control lungs. On the other hand, the percent of club/goblet (p = 0.004) and basal cells (p < 0.001), likely markers of honeycomb cysts, were increased only in the SSc-PH samples. The SSc-PH samples, but not the iPAH, also showed a decreased percentage of Alveolar Type 1 (AT1) cells (p = 0.002; Fig. S5B). To assess potential batch effects introduced during sample preparation, we compared cell type abundance among samples processed using different chemistries. No statistically significant differences were observed for most cell types, with the exception of minor cell populations: plasma cells (p = 0.023, Kruskal-Wallis test) and B lymphocytes (p = 0.019, Kruskal-Wallis test, Figure S5C).

To better understand the shared and distinct disease-associated transcription profiles underlying SSc-PH and iPAH vasculopathies, we re-clustered vascular endothelial cells (VECs; expressing VWF, PECAM1), lymphatic endothelial cells (expressing LYVE1 and CCL21), pericytes/smooth muscle cells (expressing ACTA2, DES, MYH11, and TAGLN) and fibroblasts (expressing LUM and DCN, Fig. S1G) to interrogate cell type- and health status-specific gene expression patterns (Supplemental Dataset 1).

We identified a total of 9 vascular endothelial subpopulations, 6 of which showed canonical gene signatures of the classical arterial, venous, capillary EC subtypes populating mature vasculature (Fig. 1A and Table S5). Arterial ECs were characterized by expression of genes involved in Wnt signaling regulation (DKK2) and gap junctions (GJA5; Figs. 1B and C). Compared to healthy lungs, arterial ECs decreased significantly in SSc-PH lungs (p < 0.001) and trended towards decreased frequency in iPAH lungs (Figs. 1D–F). Venous ECs shared a signature of genes associated with chemokine transcytosis (ACKR1), and were further divided into pulmonary venous ECs and systemic venous ECs by differential expression of previously reported subtype-specific markers CPE and COL15A122 (Fig. 1C). The percentage of systemic venous ECs was significantly increased in SSc-PH lungs (p < 0.001), but was comparable between iPAH and control lungs (Figs. 1D–F). Consistent with previous literature, we identified VWF−SOSDC1+ aerocytes and VWF+FCN3+ general capillary ECs47, both of which expressed pan-capillary markers including the gas exchange enzyme CA4 (Fig. 1C). Aerocytes were significantly decreased in SSc-PH (p = 0.017, Fig. 1F), whereas general capillary ECs were comparable in proportion between SSc-PH and control lungs (Fig. S6). Proliferating ECs expressing MKI67 were detected, although comprising only 0.89% of VECs (Figs. 1C and E).

A distinct lymphatic endothelial population was identified by high expression of canonical markers LYVE1 and CCL12, and showed decreased cell numbers in SSc-PH (p < 0.001) and iPAH lungs (Figs. 1C–F).

We identified three angiogenic endothelial subpopulation, namely tip, phalanx and proliferating ECs (Figs. 1A–C). Proliferating ECs and tip cells were identified using module scores, calculated as average expression of a core set of genes associated with proliferation (MKI67, TOP2A, BIRC5, CENPU, and PCLAF) and the tip cell phenotype (top 50 most highly expressed genes in tip cells previously characterized in human lung tumor25). Phalanx cells were marked by enrichment of fatty acid-binding proteins RBP7, FABP4 and taurine transporter SLC6A6, and absence of canonical tip markers (PGF and APLN, Fig 2A). Phalanx cells shared a similar gene expression profile with tip cells, and exhibited high expression level of genes associated with vascular development (CXCL1248,49, HSPG250, SOX1851), endothelial barrier integrity (PLVAP52 and COL15A153) and Notch signaling (NOTCH4, HEY1, Figs. 1B, C and 2A). Although some of these gene markers were shared by arterial and venous populations, phalanx cells were distinguished from these populations by absence of other markers for arterial (DKK2, GJA5) and venous ECs (NR2F254, ACKR1; Fig. 2A). Gene ontology (GO) analysis suggested that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of phalanx cells were significantly enriched in biological processes associated with angiogenesis (Fig. 2B, Tables S6 and S7). Of note, the majority of tip and phalanx cells originated from SSc-PH and iPAH samples (Figs. 1D–F). Proliferating ECs, on the other hand, showed no significant difference in proportion between PH and control lungs (Fig. S6).

We localized nascent vessels comprised of phalanx and tip cells by immunofluorescence microscopy using COL15A1 as marker. ACKR1, a marker for venous ECs, was used to distinguish phalanx and tip cells from systemic venous ECs, which also express COL15A1 (Figs. 2A and S7). Consistent with our scRNA-seq analysis, COL15A1+ ECs were significantly increased in SSc-PH lungs (p = 0.006, Figs 3C, S8A and S8B). All of our SSc-PH patients had ILD. COL15A1+ACKR1−VWF+ ECs were found primarily around the fibrotic foci of these lungs (Fig S8C). Notably, we observed marked variability in phalanx cell proportion and localization within the iPAH patient samples. scRNA-seq clustering identified less than 7% of VECs as phalanx cells in two of the three iPAH lung samples (iPAH 1 and iPAH 2), and these were localized by immunostaining to the recanalized obstructed arteries (Figs. 2C, S8D and S8E). By contrast, scRNA-seq clustering identified a higher percentage of phalanx cells in the other iPAH (iPAH 3) lung sample (69.3%, Fig. S8F). Immunofluorescent staining supported the scRNA-seq observations, suggesting large amounts of COL15A1+ vasculature in the alveoli (Figs. S8G and I), which is normally populated only by CA4+ pulmonary capillaries (Fig. S8H).

In order to better characterize the transcription regulation program for tip cells and phalanx cells, we performed GRN analysis using pySCENIC46 to predict transcription factors driving the unique gene expression profile of endothelial subpopulations (Figs. 2D and S9). pySCENIC analysis revealed that peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) regulon activities and expression were enriched in phalanx cells, whereas Smad1 regulon activities and expression were enriched in tip cells (Figs. 2E and F). Regulon gene expression predicted transcription factors regulating other EC populations as well (Fig. 2D).

Next, to identify intercellular signals potentially regulating the angiogenic activities of phalanx and tip cells, we mapped ligand-receptor interactions between these two populations and other cell types using Connectome44. Ligand-receptor analysis highlighted NOTCH signaling between tip, phalanx and perivascular cells (Figs. 2G and H). Tip cells expressed NOTCH ligands DLL4 and JAG1, whereas phalanx cells showed higher JAG2 expression. Both cell types expressed high levels of NOTCH4. Congruent tip markers PGF and APLN bind to VEGFR1 (FLT1) and APLNR, respectively. Both receptors were enriched in angiogenic EC subpopulations (Fig 2I). Compared to mature EC subtypes (e.g., arterial and general capillary ECs), phalanx and tip cells presented a TIE1hi
TEKlo phenotype (Fig. 2I). Altered expression of TIE1 versus TEK might be affecting Tie1-Tie2 heterodimers, which have been implicated in coordinating angiogenesis and angiopoietin-1 signaling55. PDGFB was enriched in tip cells and predicted to interact primarily with PDGFRB, most highly expressed by pericytes but downregulated in PH (Figs. 2H, I and S10). ROBO4, previously found in stalk cells56, showed enrichment in tip cells (Fig 2I). ROBO4 ligand, SLIT2, was expressed by fibroblast subpopulations (Fig. S10).Additionally, phalanx cells showed upregulation of CXCL12 (Figs. 2G and H), an angiogenic factor57 as well as chemoattractant for lymphocytes expressing its receptor CXCR4 (Fig. S10).

We observed a distinct population of cells expressing canonical markers for both endothelial populations (PECAM1, VWF and CDH5) and pericytes (RGS5, PDGFRB and ACTA2; Fig. 1C), corresponding to previously described gene signatures of cells undergoing endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT). This cluster of cells was excluded as doublets in our previous studies of explanted lung tissue31. However, further examination of DEGs showed that the actin-associated protein synaptopodin (encoded by SYNPO) and the long non-coding RNA, H19, were specifically enriched in this cluster (Figs. 3A–D and Table S8). In addition, these transitional cells were found almost exclusively in SSc-PH and iPAH samples (Figs. 1D–F).

To validate the existence of transitional cells, we performed double immunofluorescence staining for Von Willebrand factor (vWF) and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) in tissue sections from SSc-PH, iPAH and control lungs. vWF+ α-SMA+ cells were present in SSc-PH and iPAH samples, albeit observed in only a minority of vessels (Fig. 3E). The majority of endothelial cells did not express α-SMA (Fig. S11A). Consistent with the scRNA-seq data, transitional cells were not observed in control lungs (Fig. 3E).

To investigate the presence of transitional cells in microvasculature, we further stained lung tissue sections with the 3G5 antibody, which selectively binds to pericytes. Co-localization of CD31 and the 3G5 antigen was observed in ~5% of vessels with diameter < 30 μm in SSc-PH and iPAH lung tissue sections (Fig. S11B).

Pulmonary vascular injury and remodeling occur primarily in small arteries and arterioles in PH58. IPA highlighted significantly increased activity of pathways associated with fibrosis (pulmonary fibrosis idiopathic signaling pathway) and angiogenesis (HIF1α signaling and IL-8 signaling pathway, Fig. 4A and Table S9) in SSc-PH arterial ECs compared to control. This was supported by upregulation of genes encoding connective tissue growth factor (CCN2), type IV collagen (COL4A1), and regulators of ECM accumulation (MMP2, SERPINE1; Fig. 4B and Table S10A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was further performed with the R package fgsea42 and showed consistent results. SSc-PH arterial ECs showed significant enrichment of genes from the matrisome pathway (NES = 1.38, adjusted p-value = 0.043, Fig. 4C and Table S11). In addition, SSc-PH arterial ECs showed upregulations of genes associated with cell proliferation (CCND1) and vascular dysfunction (EDN1). Notably, we observed an overlap of 10 genes between upregulated genes in SSc-PH and iPAH. These included genes encoding ECM protein COL4A1, and HYAL2, which is associated with ECM remodeling (Fig. 4D and Tables S10A and B). This suggests shared pathological processes in end-stage SSc-PH and iPAH pulmonary arterial ECs.

To account for within-sample correlations, we confirmed differential expression analysis by pseudobulk analysis. Pseudobulk analysis confirmed significant upregulation of COL4A1, MMP2, CCND1 and EDN1 (Fig. S12A and Table S12). Pathway enrichment analysis suggested similar perturbations in pathways related to ECM remodeling and angiogenesis (Supplemental Figure S12B and C).

We proceeded to investigate perturbed cell-cell communications between arterial ECs and their adjacent cell types using differential connectome analysis. First, we examined dysregulated incoming signals from other cell types towards arterial ECs (Figs. 5A–B). Differential connectome analysis comparing SSc-PH to control highlighted upregulation of several interactions with vasoactive and angiogenic effects (Fig. 5A). SSc-PH macrophages expressed increased ACE (Fig. 5C), which inhibits bradykinin receptor B2 (BDKRB2)-mediated vasodilation by degrading bradykinin and interacting with B2 receptors59,60. VEGFA and TNFSF12 (TWEAK), potent pro-angiogenic mediators61, were significantly upregulated in SSc-PH adventitial fibroblasts. Their respective receptors, KDR and TNFRSF12A, were expressed more highly in SSc-PH arterial ECs (Fig. 5C).

We also examined the ligands impacting PH arterial ECs using NicheNet, a method based on prior knowledge of ligand-receptor regulated genes45. Notably, VEGFA was also identified as a ligand by NicheNet, as were TGFB1 and AGT comparing SSc-PH to controls (Figs. 6A–C). VEGFA mRNA was upregulated in multiple fibroblast populations. TGFB1, which is regulated largely by its activation, was expressed most highly by myeloid populations, and only modestly upregulated by fibroblast subpopulations (Figs. 6A and B).

Differential connectome analysis comparing iPAH to control samples demonstrated upregulated APOE/LDLR and FGF7/FGFR1 signaling (Fig. 5B). APOE was primarily expressed in macrophages and upregulated in iPAH. FGF7 was expressed in the major fibroblast subpopulations but showed significant upregulation selectively in alveolar fibroblasts (Fig. S13).

Next, we identified dysregulated outgoing signals from arterial ECs (Figs. 7A–B). Arterial ECs showed significantly increased expression of END1, BMP6, FGF2 and FGF18 in SSc-PH (Figs. 7A and C). Partial depletion of epithelial or immune cell populations had a minimal impact on the ligand-receptor analysis. Analysis performed with and without partially depleted samples demonstrated substantial overlap (Fig. S14).

In addition, we explored upregulated cell-cell interactions between venous and capillary endothelial populations and other cell populations. CXCL12, upregulated in SSc-PH venous ECs (Fig. S15A), is a ligand for CXCR4 and contributes to the migration of monocytes in pulmonary vasculature62. EDN1 was upregulated in both SSc-PH and iPAH pulmonary venous and general capillary ECs. Its receptor, EDNRB, was upregulated in fibroblast subpopulations (Fig. S15B).

Pericytes were identified by the previously described marker, RGS529. Smooth muscle cells (SMCs) showed high expression of calponin 1 (CNN1). Both cell types exhibited enrichment of α-smooth muscle actin (ACTA2) expression and were significantly increased in SSc-PH lungs (p = 0.002 for both cell types, Figs. 1C–F). To investigate cellular heterogeneity with higher resolution, we extracted and re-clustered the SMCs and pericytes (Fig. S16A). Consistent with the recent scRNA-seq identification of contractile and synthetic SMCs by Crnkovic et al.63, we identified two SMC subclusters, discriminated by differential expression of DES and COL1A1. DEShi SMCs showed higher expression of genes associated with smooth muscle contraction (MYH11, DES, ACTG2). COL1A1hi SMCs presented enrichment of genes encoding collagens (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1 and COL4A1) and fibroblast markers (LUM and DCN). We also identified a mixed cluster which highly expressed markers for both pericytes (RGS5, CSPG4, COX4I2, NDUF4L2 and PDGFRB) and SMCs (CNN1 and TAGLN; Fig. S16B and Table S13). All clusters were present in SSc-PH, iPAH and control lungs, showing a marked expansion in PH lungs compared to controls (Fig. S16C). However, we were not able to identify oxygen-sensing and fibroblast-like SMCs using module scores for the top 100 markers for each of these subtypes63 (Fig. S16D).

Increased medial thickness caused by smooth muscle cell hyperplasia is an important feature of both SSc-PH and iPAH. Despite the marked expansion of mural cell clusters in PH lungs, proliferating cells made up less than 1% of mural cells (Figs. S16E–F). However, CCND1, which encodes cyclin D1, a key regulator of cell cycle progression, was among the most highly upregulated genes in SSc-PH SMC/pericytes (Fig. S16G and Table S14). In addition, GSEA analysis of differential gene expression in SMCs and pericytes highlighted significant downregulation of apoptotic hallmark genes in SSc-PH (NES = −2.1, adjusted p-value = 0.008) and iPAH (NES = −2.07, adjusted p-value = 0.006; Fig. S16H and Tables S15A and B).

We identified a total of 4 transcriptionally distinct fibroblast phenotypes. Two major subsets shared by healthy and PH lungs, adventitial and alveolar fibroblasts, were enriched with literature-based markers MFAP5 and SPINT2, respectively (Fig. 1C)64. Myofibroblasts, predominantly found in SSc-PH samples (Figs. 1D–F), were characterized by high expression of ACTA2 and collagen-encoding genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1 and other matrix genes; Fig. 1B). A minor fibroblast subpopulation expressing WIF1 was identified (Fig. 1C), consistent with our previous scRNA-seq analysis on SSc and healthy lung fibroblast subsets29. Notably, the percentage of alveolar and WIF1+ fibroblasts was similar between PH and control lungs, whereas adventitial fibroblasts showed decreased percentage in SSc-PH (p = 0.034, Figs. S6 and 1F).

Previous molecular understanding of vascular disease in SSc-PH has been modest and derived primarily from studies of SSc vascular disease in other organs, studies of serum biomarkers, and studies of iPAH. Here, we directly analyze SSc lungs, showing altered transcriptional profiles of vascular cells, and emergent cell populations associated with SSc-PH. Given that SSc vasculopathy mainly affects arterioles and small arteries, arterial EC dysfunction is highly relevant to pathogenesis including increased vascular tone and vascular remodeling. SSc is traditionally considered a disease associated with loss of vessels, especially at the late stage20,21. Indeed, we saw a striking decrease in the percent of arterial ECs associated with all PH pathologies. Perhaps this occurs as arterial ECs transition into mesenchymal cells and obliterate vessels. Transitional cells expressing both endothelial and pericyte/SMC markers supported the role of EndoMT in SSc-PH vasculopathy, as has been previously shown in iPAH. Intimal hyperplasia is a feature of vasculopathy in SSc lungs, kidneys and skin, suggesting this may be a common mechanism driving the obliterative vasculopathy of SSc9. In silico analysis predicted that VEGF-A, TGF-β1, TNFSF12(TWEAK), Angiotensin and bradykinin stimulate altered arterial EC function in SSc-PH. TGF-β1 strongly promotes EndoMT and can synergize or antagonize signals from VEGF65. We additionally identified a marked expansion of tip and phalanx cells, cells involved in sprouting angiogenesis, in SSc-PH. Thus, our data are consistent with a model of arterial EC injury/loss/transition and compensatory disordered vascular regeneration, both of which may play important roles in the decreasing pulmonary cross-sectional area and the clinical manifestation of pulmonary hypertension. SSc-PH and iPAH arterial ECs may share similar pathological cell states, as evidenced by a noticeable overlap of upregulated genes.

Apart from arterial EC dysfunction, we noticed a remarkable expansion of angiogenic tip and phalanx cells, which are seen rarely in control lungs, indicating active vascular regeneration in SSc-PH. The location of nascent blood vessels lined by COL15A1+ACKR1−VWF+ phalanx cells varied in PH lungs. In SSc-PH lungs, neovascular cells were primarily found around fibrotic foci. Notably, phalanx cells in PH lungs share remarkably similar gene signatures with fibrosis-associated ECs identified in other diseases, including PLVAP+ ECs in liver cirrhosis66 and COL15A1+ ECs in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis67. This implies that common pathways may exist for neovascularization of fibrotic tissues. On the other hand, COL15A1+ACKR1−VWF+ cells were localized to either the alveoli or recanalized in iPAH lungs. While this may be related to variability of tissue sampling, it is also possible that angiogenic processes vary dramatically among PH patients. The current study discusses transcriptomic and histopathologic characteristics of these cell types. Functional studies are challenging due to the remarkable plasticity demonstrated by ECs in vitro and the absence of a mouse model which fully recapitulates human SSc. Further investigations are required to fully elucidate the nature of these angiogenic ECs and the relevance of sprouting angiogenesis in SSc-PH.

Cell-cell communication is crucial to specification and function of angiogenic EC subtypes. The roles of Notch and VEGF signaling in tip cell formation are widely appreciated68. Consistent with previous studies69, DLL4 was highly expressed by tip cells. However, JAG1, another Notch ligand previously identified as a stalk marker70, was also upregulated in tip cells. Jagged1 and Dll4 have antagonizing effects on angiogenesis70. Previous mechanistic studies have indicated that Dll4 suppresses pathologic VEGF-induced hypervascularization71,72. Thus, co-expression of these two ligands in tip cells raises the hypothesis that Jagged1 might oppose Dll4/Notch1 signaling and promote hypersprouting73. Further in-depth investigations are required to elucidate the precise consequences and significance of this observed molecular interplay.

We identified PPAR-γ and Smad1 as potential key transcription factors in phalanx and tip cells. PPAR-γ regulates lipid metabolism74 and induces the expression of fatty acid binding proteins FABP475 and RBP776, which were specifically enriched in phalanx cells. These results are in line with previous studies showing upregulation of fatty acid uptake in quiescent ECs77 and further highlight PPAR-γ as a potential regulator. Smad1 mediates BMP signaling and has a recognized role in regulating tip/stalk phenotype and EC migration78. Thus, PPAR-γ and Smad1 could potentially serve as novel targets for manipulation of angiogenesis.

Accumulation of α-SMA+ neointima is a hallmark for PH. In line with the pathology, we observed a significant increase of SMCs and pericytes in SSc-PH. Identification of α-SMA+ CD31+ cells in our and previous studies15,17 supports the contribution of EndoMT to neointima formation. Furthermore, we identified the long non-coding RNA H19 as one of the markers for transitional ECs. H19 is also enriched in fibroblast-like ECs in mouse atherosclerotic plaques79 and has reported roles in EndoMT across different vascular beds80,81.

In addition to endothelial transdifferentiation, SMC proliferation has been proposed as the source of α-SMA+ neointima and could also contribute to arterial muscularization. While we did not observe significantly more proliferating SMCs/pericytes, upregulated CCND1, encoding a positive regulator of cell cycle, and downregulation of apoptosis hallmark genes suggest an anti-apoptotic phenotype. Thus, expanded mural cells may be more a function of increased longevity than increased proliferation. Alternatively, our inability to identify increased proliferating mural cells might be due to their rarity in our dataset.

PDGFB-PDGFRβ and Jagged-Notch3 signaling play central roles in the recruitment and maturation of perivascular mural cells82–84. Despite the expansion of pericytes, our data indicated that PDGFRB and NOTCH3 were downregulated in SSc-PH and iPAH pericytes. This might reflect receptor downregulation upon engagement by ligand. Alternatively, this might point to insufficient pericyte recruitment and impaired stability of nascent vessels. Reduced pericyte recruitment has been demonstrated in iPAH using in vitro co-culture systems with Wnt5a identified as a key mediator85,86. Here we propose altered PDGF/NOTCH signals might also contribute to the paradoxical inadequacy of vascular repair in spite of active angiogenesis in SSc-PH.

This study is subjected to several limitations. Because iPAH patients rarely come to transplant, our study is limited by small numbers of these patient subsets. As a result, we were statistically underpowered to provide robust and conclusive results to address the differences between iPAH and SSc-PH EC phenotypes. Nonetheless, we wish to present distinct transcriptomic and histopathologic characteristics of the iPAH patients enrolled in this study, which may reflect distinct pathogenic pathways. Future research with larger sample sizes is warranted to generalize our observations. Another important limitation is that our lung samples were obtained from patients with end-stage disease. Consequently, the scRNA-seq data we derived may be skewed towards capturing features associated with advanced disease. Furthermore, vasodilating medications could also affect the transcriptome of the cells from diseased lungs and should be acknowledged as a potential confounding factor.

Despite these limitations, this study provides a detailed illustration of altered EC phenotypes in SSc-PH and iPAH. Specifically, we provide strong evidence for the transcriptional programs and cytokines associated with arterial endothelial dysfunction, altered angiogenesis, and EndoMT, providing new insight into transcriptional regulation and perturbed cell-cell interactions potentially targetable with future therapeutics.
