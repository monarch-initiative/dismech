# First neonatal case of primary ciliary dyskinesia caused by a pathogenic DRC1 variant presenting with recurrent atelectasis and airway hypersecretion: a case report and literature review.
**Authors:** Kato K, Yodoya N, Sawada H, Nakasato A, Makino H, Harada T, Takeoka M, Tsuboya N, Ohashi H, Mitani Y, Takeuchi K, Hirayama M
**Journal:** BMC Pediatr (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12887-026-07035-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07035-7)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a relatively rare hereditary disorder. Pathogenic variants in DRC1 are prevalent in East Asian populations and are frequently associated with situs solitus, which often obscures clinical suspicion. More than 80% of affected neonates with PCD present with respiratory distress; however, definitive diagnosis often requires specialized investigations, and many cases remain undiagnosed. Because PCD can progress to chronic obstructive lung disease and bronchiectasis, early diagnosis and appropriate management are essential. We report the first neonatal case of PCD caused by a pathogenic DRC1 variant, in which recurrent atelectasis and airway hypersecretion prompted evaluation and ultimately led to a definitive diagnosis. CASE PRESENTATION　: A female infant was born at 37 weeks of gestation via uncomplicated vaginal delivery, with a birth weight of 2,294 g. She had no respiratory distress immediately after birth but developed subcostal retractions and hypoxemia (oxygen saturation around 90%) on day 1 of life (DOL 1). High-flow nasal cannula therapy did not improve her respiratory status. On DOL 3, she developed left upper lobe atelectasis and poor feeding. She was intubated on DOL 4 and transferred to our center for further evaluation. Although she was extubated on DOL 5, right upper lobe atelectasis developed, requiring re-intubation on DOL 6. After a second extubation on DOL 7, she continued to exhibit copious airway secretions and required noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation for an additional 11 days. Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography, laryngoscopy, bronchoscopy, and upper gastrointestinal contrast studies revealed no structural abnormalities. With continued chest physiotherapy and mucolytic treatment, the right upper lobe atelectasis resolved by DOL 22; however, airway secretions remained excessive. Given the recurrent atelectasis and marked secretions, impaired ciliary function was suspected. High-speed video microscopy on DOL 29 demonstrated ciliary motility abnormalities in nearly all sampled epithelial cells. Genetic testing identified a homozygous deletion of exons 1-4 in the DRC1 gene, confirming the diagnosis of PCD. She continued mucolytic therapy and was discharged from the neonatal intensive care unit on DOL 33.

CONCLUSIONS: Recurrent atelectasis or airway hypersecretion in neonates should raise suspicion for PCD, even in the absence of situs inversus, and targeted diagnostic evaluation should be pursued.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a hereditary disorder caused by pathogenic variants in cilia-related genes and is characterized by chronic respiratory symptoms resulting from impaired ciliary motility. The clinical features of PCD are diverse, including chronic otorhinolaryngologic infections, situs inversus, and infertility, with substantial variability among patients. The prevalence is estimated to be approximately 1 in 7,500 individuals [1]; however, the number of reported cases varies substantially across regions, likely reflecting differences in diagnostic resources, referral pathways, and genetic backgrounds rather than true prevalence. Although situs inversus is a characteristic finding observed in approximately 50% of patients with PCD, it is identified in only about 25% of affected individuals in Japan, with the majority exhibiting situs solitus [2]. Takeuchi et al. have previously reported that pathogenic variants in DRC1 are relatively common in Japan and are frequently associated with situs solitus [3]. As a result, neonatal cases without a family history or situs inversus are particularly prone to delayed diagnosis. The clinical phenotype varies depending on the underlying genetic defect, and substantial ethnic differences have been described [4].

Definitive diagnosis of PCD requires specialized assessment of ciliary structure and function combined with genetic testing, often necessitating referral to experienced centers. Early recognition is challenging because neonatal respiratory distress has a broad differential diagnosis, and the clinical presentation of neonatal-onset PCD remains incompletely characterized.

Affected infants are at risk for recurrent lower respiratory tract infections that may lead to early bronchiectasis without appropriate airway clearance therapy [5], while persistent otitis media can result in hearing impairment [6]. Recognizing characteristic respiratory manifestations and pursuing timely diagnostic evaluation are therefore critical. We report a neonatal case in which recurrent atelectasis and airway hypersecretion prompted further investigation and led to the diagnosis of PCD, together with a focused review of the literature.

A female infant was born at 37 weeks and 2 days of gestation via spontaneous vaginal delivery at an outside hospital, with a birth weight of 2,294 g and Apgar scores of 9 and 10 at 1 and 5 min, respectively. The mother was a primigravida with an uncomplicated pregnancy and no significant medical or obstetric history. Family history was non-contributory. Although she had no apparent respiratory or circulatory compromise immediately after birth, she was admitted to the NICU for low birth weight management.

On day 1 of life (DOL 1), she developed subcostal retractions and hypoxemia, with oxygen saturation (SpO2) around 90% in room air. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) therapy was initiated but failed to improve her respiratory status. Empiric intravenous amoxicillin and tobramycin were started for suspected neonatal infection. On DOL 3, she developed left upper lobe atelectasis and poor feeding, accompanied by worsening retractions. Consequently, she was intubated on DOL 4 and transferred to our center for further evaluation.

Upon arrival, her body weight was 2,261 g (33 g loss from birth). Vital signs included a temperature of 37.9 ℃, heart rate of 124 beats/min, and blood pressure of 76/48 mmHg without limb discrepancy. Before transfer, she had tachypnea (respiratory rate, 65 breaths/min) and marked subcostal retractions, indicating increased work of breathing, and therefore required endotracheal intubation. On admission to our center, she remained intubated and was managed on synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV) with a positive inspiratory pressure of 18 cmH₂O, a positive end-expiratory pressure of 6 cmH₂O, an inspiratory time of 0.4 s, a respiratory rate of 40 breaths/min, and an FiO₂ of 0.25, maintaining an SpO₂ of 100%. Physical examination revealed a flat anterior fontanelle; clear breath sounds bilaterally without wheezing and normal heart sounds without murmurs. The abdomen was soft and non-distended with the liver edge palpable at the right costal margin; no hepatosplenomegaly or external malformations were noted.

Laboratory findings (Table 1) showed no evidence of respiratory acidosis or infection. Initial chest radiography revealed decreased aeration in the left lung, which had improved following positive-pressure ventilation (Fig. 1). There was no cardiomegaly or evidence of situs inversus. Echocardiography revealed normal cardiac structure and function, with no evidence of situs inversus. All bacterial cultures were negative.

Following intubation and positive-pressure ventilation, the left upper lobe atelectasis resolved promptly, and planned extubation was attempted on DOL 5. However, several hours after extubation, subcostal retractions and desaturation recurred. A chest radiograph on DOL 6 revealed new right upper lobe atelectasis, necessitating re-intubation. Her respiratory status subsequently improved, and a second planned extubation was performed on DOL 8. After extubation, the right upper lobe atelectasis persisted, accompanied by marked airway hypersecretion. Nasal continuous positive airway pressure (nCPAP) and chest physiotherapy were continued. She remained on nCPAP until DOL 18, and the atelectasis persisted until DOL 22.

Chest computed tomography revealed right upper lobe atelectasis and scattered air trapping throughout all lung lobes on axial images (Fig. 2A-C). Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography showed no vascular anomalies causing airway compression. Laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy revealed no structural airway abnormalities or laryngotracheomalacia, although a substantial amount of airway secretions was noted. Upper gastrointestinal contrast studies excluded tracheoesophageal fistula or gastroesophageal reflux (Fig. 2D). The constellation of the late-onset respiratory distress, airway hypersecretion, and recurrent atelectasis in atypical lobes strongly suggested an underlying disorder of mucociliary clearance, particularly PCD or cystic fibrosis. However, meconium-related ileus was absent and the patient showed no clinical signs of pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, such as malabsorption or poor weight gain. We therefore suspected PCD and proceeded with ciliary function testing.

A nasal epithelial biopsy was performed on DOL 29. High-speed video microscopy demonstrated predominantly metachronous but stiff ciliary beating (Supplementary File. 1). Transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 3) revealed disorganized axonemal architecture with preserved inner dynein arms, consistent with a class II ciliary defect. Genetic analysis identified a homozygous deletion of exons 1–4 in the dynein regulatory complex 1 (DRC1) gene (NC_000002.12: g.26398040_26425787del on build GRCh38/hg38), confirming the diagnosis of PCD. This large homozygous deletion is predicted to result in loss of function of the DRC1 protein, a core component of the nexin-dynein regulatory complex essential for coordinated ciliary beating.

Her respiratory condition stabilized with mucolytic therapy and airway clearance, and she was discharged from the NICU on DOL 33. At the 10-month follow-up, she demonstrated appropriate growth and developmental progress without recurrent respiratory infections, chronic productive cough, nasal congestion, or recurrent otitis media.

We describe the first reported neonatal case of PCD caused by a pathogenic DRC1 variant, presenting with recurrent atelectasis and marked airway hypersecretion in the absence of situs inversus. This case provides three important clinical insights into neonatal PCD. First, PCD-related respiratory distress may follow an atypical and fluctuating course driven by impaired mucociliary clearance. Second, the absence of situs inversus - which is common in East Asian populations due to the prevalence of DRC1 variants - can obscure clinical suspicion and contribute to delayed diagnosis. Third, while early diagnosis allows for prompt intervention, further research is needed to evaluate its impact on long-term prognosis.

Neonatal respiratory distress has a broad differential diagnosis, including transient tachypnea of the newborn, infection, congenital heart disease, and structural airway abnormalities. Unlike these conditions, respiratory distress associated with PCD often presents with a “symptom-free interval” immediately after birth, followed by progressive deterioration characterized by persistent oxygen requirements and recurrent or shifting atelectasis [7]. In animal models of PCD, reported chest CT findings include atelectasis, air trapping, and airway mucus obstruction [8]. In the present case, chest CT also demonstrated right upper lobe atelectasis and scattered air trapping in the left upper lobe, consistent with previously reported imaging findings of neonatal PCD. These findings suggest that chest CT may provide useful supportive information when neonatal PCD is suspected, particularly in cases with an atypical or progressive respiratory course. Clinically, respiratory symptoms emerged on DOL 1 and progressively worsened, with atelectasis involving different lobes despite appropriate ventilatory support and no identifiable structural or infectious cause. This atypical and dynamic clinical course provided a strong clue pointing toward a disorder of airway clearance. When impaired airway clearance is suspected in a neonate, cystic fibrosis and PCD are important differential diagnoses. Although cystic fibrosis may present during the neonatal period, it more typically manifests with meconium ileus and pancreatic dysfunction, while neonatal respiratory distress is a less common presenting feature. In the present case, neither meconium ileus nor evidence of pancreatic dysfunction was identified. Taken together, these findings made cystic fibrosis less likely, and we therefore proceeded with nasal epithelial biopsy to evaluate for PCD.

The absence of situs inversus is a major barrier to early diagnosis. While situs inversus is a classic hallmark of PCD, it is frequently absent in patients in East Asian populations due to the higher prevalence of pathogenic DRC1 variants, which do not disrupt nodal ciliary function during embryogenesis and therefore result in situs solitus [3]. Consequently, neonates with situs solitus are at greater risk of missed or delayed diagnosis [9]. Our review of 27 previously reported cases diagnosed within the first 6 months of life, together with the present case, underscores this challenge (Table 2) [10–32]. Most infants were born at term (96.4%), and although situs inversus was present in 67.9%, only 42.9% were diagnosed during the neonatal period. Notably, just two cases - including ours - had both situs solitus and no family history, emphasizing the diagnostic difficulty in this specific phenotype. Common diagnostic triggers included situs inversus and airway hypersecretion (each 67.9%), followed by persistent or recurrent atelectasis (53.6%). Mortality occurred in 17.2% of cases and was confined to infants with complex congenital heart disease, in whom impaired airway clearance contributed to severe postoperative respiratory failure.

Taken together, these findings reinforce the clinical importance of recognizing neonatal PCD at an early stage. Early diagnosis may facilitate timely initiation of airway clearance therapy and structured follow-up. In the present case, prompt recognition of characteristic respiratory features led to specialized testing - including high-speed video microscopy and genetic analysis - and enabled a definitive diagnosis during the neonatal period. Airway clearance and mucolytic therapy were initiated during the neonatal period and continued at the 10-month follow-up. The absence of respiratory complications during infancy in this patient may suggest a potential benefit of appropriate management initiated in the neonatal period. However, robust evidence demonstrating that early diagnosis improves long-term outcomes remains limited, underscoring the need for future longitudinal studies.

In conclusion, recurrent atelectasis and marked airway hypersecretion emerging after an initial period of postnatal stability should prompt consideration of PCD in neonates, regardless of laterality status. Greater awareness of these characteristic respiratory patterns, particularly in populations with a high prevalence of DRC1 variants, is essential to reduce diagnostic delays and optimize respiratory care.
