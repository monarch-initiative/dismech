# Presenting Features and Causes of Diagnostic Delays: A Report From the NUT Carcinoma Registry.
**Authors:** Walton SA, Kim JJ, Paoloni F, Liew JW, Haradon D, Haradon J, Miller KW, Paik PK, Chaft JE, Kris MG, Hsu R, Piha-Paul SA, Rotow JK, Jänne PA, Barbie DA, Sholl LM, DuBois SG, Hanna GJ, Shapiro GI, French CA, Luo J
**Journal:** JCO Precis Oncol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1200/PO-25-00526](https://doi.org/10.1200/PO-25-00526)

## Content

PURPOSE: Diagnostic delays are common for patients with NUT carcinoma (NC), a cancer driven by NUT fusion oncoprotein. Rapid diagnosis is crucial for the best outcomes. We investigated NC's presenting features and associations with diagnostic delays.

METHODS: We manually reviewed medical records from US participants in the NC Registry (2007-2024). Baseline features were analyzed descriptively, and multivariable logistic regression was used to examine odds ratios for delays.

RESULTS: We analyzed 132 patients (median age 37 years). At presentation, 55% had metastatic disease and 64% had a thoracic primary. The median interval from symptom onset to diagnosis was 10 weeks (range, 1-165 weeks). The initial histopathologic diagnosis was poorly differentiated/squamous cell cancer in 52%, insufficient malignant cells in 14%, carcinoma in 12%, and NC in 22%. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) testing revealed NUT in 100%; 93% were keratin+, 88% p63+, 89% p40+, and 72% had Ki-67 ≥50%. IHC was used to diagnose NC in 79%. Nonthoracic primaries were associated with a longer time between symptom onset and NC diagnosis (odds ratio, 3.25). Of 32 patients who started treatment before diagnosis, 78% were on agents appropriate for NC. After a diagnosis of NC, 33% participated in clinical trials.

CONCLUSION: NC almost always presents as squamous/poorly differentiated non-small cell lung or head and neck cancer. Overall, 78% of patients were initially diagnosed as non-NC. Efforts to raise awareness, recognition, and rapid diagnosis of NC by both oncologists and pathologists are critical if we are to improve outcomes.

NUT carcinoma (NC) is an aggressive solid tumor of thoracic or head and neck origin with a median survival of 6.7 months. 1 NC is defined by a fusion between the nuclear protein in testis (NUTM1) gene and an epigenetic reader, most commonly bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4::NUTM1), bromodomain-containing protein 3 (BRD3::NUTM1), or nuclear receptor binding SET domain protein 3 (NSD3::NUTM1). 1

NC is often underrecognized. 2 This may be due to the need to re-examine its presentation in light of recent work defining its underlying biology. 3 NC is currently listed under “Thymic Malignancies” in the WHO Classification of Tumours, 5th Ed, based on initial case reports. 4 Recent investigations have found that NC is defined by specific NUTM1 fusions and should not be conflated with other NUTM1 fused cancers that differ in pathogenesis. 5 Our recent work found that beyond the defining fusion oncoprotein, NC typically does not have other actionable mutations. 6 Additionally, preclinical models and transcriptional analysis have found that it is a subtype of squamous cell carcinoma. 3 , 5 , 7 In line with this finding, our experience is that most cases present as a squamous cell carcinoma of the lung or head and neck squamous cell cancer, and thymus origin NC is rare.

If a patient presents with a thoracic or head and neck primary, a diagnosis of NC can be made quickly through NUT immunohistochemistry (IHC), with 100% specificity. 8 Proper diagnosis is key to starting optimal upfront therapy and consideration of clinical trials. Recent case reports continue to show diagnoses of NC made months after the initial biopsy, often following failed treatment for an incorrect diagnosis. 9 , 10 , 11

Given these issues around the diagnosis of NC, we sought to comprehensively characterize its presentation and explore factors that might lead to diagnostic delays, which may facilitate consideration of this aggressive disease entity on the differential diagnosis. Additionally, understanding factors that contribute to diagnostic delays will help direct future efforts to streamline diagnostics.

Patients within the United States with pathologically confirmed NC who consented to participate in the International NC Registry (nc-registry.org) (IRB-approved Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center protocol 10–228) from 1/2010 to 10/2024 were included. Patients or next of kin signed informed consent. We excluded patients who did not have a primary pathology report.

Trained research staff manually reviewed records to extract patient demographics, diagnosing hospital, presenting symptoms and initial workup, pathology reports, initial treatment regimens, and survival via surgical pathology reports and clinic notes.

The initial cancer diagnosis was defined as the date the pathologic diagnosis of cancer was rendered. The initial NC diagnosis (NCDx) date was defined as the date that the NC diagnosis was rendered, established by additional molecular testing.

Univariable logistic regression was used to estimate associations between baseline characteristics and intervals of interest (definitions found in Supplemental Table 1) in separate models adjusted for age (dichotomized by median), sex, race, and primary site of disease. Intervals were dichotomized by the 75th percentile to assess delays. We calculated odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for characteristics.

From January 1, 2010, to October 30, 2024, 271 patients with pathologically confirmed NC were enrolled in the NC registry (Supplemental Figure 1). Of these, we identified 132 patients who had adequate medical records. The median age was 37 years old (range: 7–81) and 38% were female (Table 1). Patients’ self-reported race was primarily White (83%), followed by Asian (7%), Black/African American (6%), Other (3%), and American Indian/Alaska Native (1%) (n=99 known); 4% of patients self-reported their ethnicity as Hispanic.

Overall, 64% of patients had a thoracic primary and 33% had a head and neck primary. The remaining 3% of primary sites included skin/soft tissue, kidney, and liver (Figure 1A). The interquartile age range was between 28 and 47 years old, and NC diagnoses occurred across the entire age spectrum (range: 7–81) (Figure 1B). More than two-thirds of patients had a never-smoking history (69%) (median pack-years: 0; range: 0–55, n=127 known). At initial cancer diagnosis, 55% of patients had metastatic disease, with 4 patients (3%) progressing from localized to metastatic by the time of NCDx (Figure 1C). Compared to those with non-metastatic disease, those with metastatic disease had worse overall survival (18% 1-year survival) (Supplemental Figure 2).

Among patients with a thoracic primary (n=85), 47% presented with a cough, 47% with dyspnea/fatigue, and 28% with pleuritic and/or chest pain (Figure 1D). Conversely, patients with a non-thoracic primary (n=47) commonly presented with congestion (36%) and swelling and/or a palpable mass (32%) (Supplemental Figure 3). Patients frequently had overlapping symptoms, including fever, night sweats, and pain in affected areas (Figure 1E).

Common sites of metastasis included bone (55%), pleura (34%), and distant lymph nodes (21%) (Figure 1F). For those with a thoracic primary (n=85), the most common sites of metastasis were bone (62%) and pleura (41%). For those with a non-thoracic primary (n=47), the most common sites of metastasis were bone (40%) and distant lymph nodes (27%). Supplemental Figure 4 shows representative scans from a patient with metastatic disease.

Among samples with malignant cells (89%, n=117), 86% were initially diagnosed as carcinoma – 52% (n=61) as poorly differentiated/squamous, 22% (n=26) as NC, and 12% (n=14) as unspecified carcinoma (Figure 2A). Of the remaining 14% (n=16), all had histology descriptions consistent with having a limited tissue sample (e.g., malignant cells present, epithelioid, or small blue round cell tumor). There were no major histopathology differences seen for thoracic vs head and neck origin NC (Supplemental Figure 5). Histologic features of squamous differentiation were noted on 42% of initial pathology reports. NC was diagnosed via NUT IHC in 79% of cases (n=104), followed by RNA NGS (12%; n=16), NUTM1 FISH (8%; n=10), and DNA NGS (1%; n=2).

Pertinent IHC stains from biopsies diagnosing NC included NUT, keratin, p63, p40, and a high Ki-67 proliferation index (Ki-67≥ 50%). All biopsies tested for NUT were positive (100%) (Figure 2B). Of biopsies testing keratin (n=86), p63 (n=52) and p40 (n= 64), 93%, 88% and 89% were positive, respectively; 72% of biopsies that examined Ki-67 proliferation index showed ≥50% staining (n=25). Representative IHC stains can be found in Figure 2C. IHC stains suggesting alternate diagnoses included TTF-1 (NSCLC), chromogranin and synaptophysin (neuroendocrine carcinoma), and CD99 (Ewing sarcoma). Overall, TTF-1 was positive in 29% (n=56), chromogranin in 7% (n=61), synaptophysin in 24% (n=76), and CD99 in 65% (n=23) of biopsies (Figure 2D).

Of all patients, 62% (n=82) had additional testing to identify the fusion partner gene to NUTM1. BRD4 was the most common fusion partner (56%), followed by NSD3 (22%) and BRD3 (19%) (Figure 2E). The median reported PD-L1 TPS was 0.0% (range 0–70%, n=80); PD-L1 CPS was 0.5% (range 0–20%, n=12) (Table 1).

We then assessed whether NC diagnoses reflected U.S. population distribution and whether this changed over time. Compared to the general U.S. population (Figure 3A), cases were disproportionately diagnosed in Massachusetts prior to 2020 (Figure 3B). After 2020, cases were better distributed across the country, but still overrepresented in the Northeast and states with large academic centers (Figure 3C). Similar results were seen comparing diagnoses in the ten most populated states – known research centers still disproportionately diagnose NC post-2020 (Supplemental Figure 6). The distribution of NC diagnoses overall can be found in Supplemental Figure 7. In 70% of cases, the final diagnosis of NC was made at an academic center, although the initial diagnosis occurred at a non-academic center in 56% of cases (Supplemental Figure 7).

We also examined key intervals in the diagnosis of NC. Examined intervals of interest were initial symptom onset to initial scan, scan to initial biopsy, and biopsy to NCDx. The mean interval from symptom onset to initial scan was 10.5 weeks, with outliers (n=7) ranging from 33.0 to 159.7 weeks (3.1 years) (Figure 3D). From initial scan to initial biopsy, patients waited for a mean of 2.5 weeks, with an outlier of 32.7 weeks. The mean interval from initial biopsy to NCDx was 7.0 weeks, with outliers (n=7) ranging from 33.4 to 131.3 weeks (2.5 years). Overall, from symptom onset to NCDx diagnosis, the median interval was 72 days, or 10.3 weeks (Supplemental Figure 8).

Next, we asked what baseline factors may lead to a delay in diagnosis. When examining diagnostic delays in time from symptom onset to NCDx, a non-thoracic primary site of disease had a significant association with a longer delay (OR=3.25, 95% CI 1.34–8.53) (Figure 3E). This signal was no longer statistically significant when examining the time from symptoms to scan (OR=2.28, 95% CI 0.86–6.21), symptoms to biopsy (OR=2.55, 95% CI 0.95–6.23), or from biopsy to diagnosis (OR=1.75, 95% CI 0.71–4.19) (Supplemental Figure 9). Individuals of non-White race had 3-times greater odds of delay in symptoms to NCDx (95% CI 0.95–9.31) and symptoms to scan (95% CI 1.02–9.85), but this was not significant. Older age and female sex were not significantly associated with diagnostic delays.

We then examined the subset of patients who received treatment before NCDx; 24% of patients had already started systemic therapy. Of these 32 patients, 78% received NC-appropriate regimens (53% (n=17) platinum doublet, 19% (n=6) platinum doublet-IO, 6% (n=2) ifosfamide) (Figure 4A). The remaining 7 patients (22%) were on a different regimen not targeted towards NC and were switched after NCDx. After diagnosis, 33% (n=43) of patients enrolled in a clinical trial. Most patients (89%) were enrolled in a trial targeting their NC (42% BET inhibitor trials, 35% BET inhibitor combination trials, 12% non-BET inhibitor epigenetic trials (n=42 known)) (Figure 4B).

We report presenting features and factors contributing to diagnostic delays in the largest cohort of NC to date. Most patients present with thoracic or head and neck primaries, and metastases are often present at diagnosis. NC typically exhibits squamous or poorly differentiated histology; all malignant cases were classified as carcinomas or had nonspecific features due to limited tissue; 78% of cases were originally not diagnosed as NC. Diagnostic delays from biopsy to diagnosis were common, leading to under-enrollment in clinical trials and use of ineffective treatments in 22% of cases. Moreover, patients with non-thoracic primaries and those of non-White race should not be overlooked when considering this diagnosis. Only 33% of patients enrolled in clinical trials, indicating a need to improve diagnosis, referral, and trial access to advance treatment options.

The first reported cases of NC were thought to be of thymus origin, and NC is still classified under the “Thymic Malignancies” section of the WHO histopathologic classification (5th Ed) and as a separate category under head and neck carcinoma. 4 , 12 , 13 , 14 However, our findings show that two-thirds of cases are lung primaries and one-third are head and neck primaries. Moreover, metastatic patterns resembled those of squamous cancers in both thoracic and head and neck regions, consistent with recent data that NC is of squamous origin. 3 Recent NC cohorts also report similar ratios between thoracic and non-thoracic primaries. 15 , 16

While NC has been initially misdiagnosed as a sarcoma, lymphoma, or small cell/neuroendocrine tumor, we found that all cases of NC in our cohort are either carcinomas or include non-specific histopathological descriptions suggesting insufficient cancer cells. 17 , 18 , 19 Terms like “epithelioid neoplasm” (n=3) and “small blue round cell tumor” (n=4) should prompt oncologists and surgeons to discuss with pathologists about obtaining additional tissue for a more definitive diagnosis. Nonspecific findings should elicit a multidisciplinary review to consider molecular testing, further diagnostics, or repeat biopsy.

The IHC characteristics we found are consistent with previously reported literature, including pitfall IHCs that may steer a diagnostician to alternative diagnoses. 20 , 21 Farooq et al. (2023) found focal TTF-1 expression in 26% of 38 biopsies, chromogranin in 0% (n=33), and CD99 in 21% (n=14). 20 Viswanathan et al. (2024) found 33% of 30 biopsies positive for neuroendocrine markers. 21 We found a numerically higher percentage of pitfall IHCs in our cohort of 132 patients than previously reported, indicating the importance of considering NC in the differential diagnosis of poorly differentiated carcinomas with positive IHC staining for CD99, synaptophysin, chromogranin, and/or TTF-1.

We found signals for delay in those with non-thoracic primary and non-White races, suggesting that these populations may need particular efforts to improve diagnosis. Patients with lung cancer likely have targetable mutations and have genomic testing early to identify the best treatment methods. 22 This could lead to early identification of the NUTM1 fusion. In contrast, head and neck cancers lack actionable genomic targets. 23 This could explain why non-thoracic patients face significant delays, as patients are less likely to undergo testing. Racial disparities exist in both lung and head and neck cancer treatment, with patients from minority racial/ethnic groups more commonly diagnosed at later stages. 24 , 25 When examining the interval from biopsy to diagnosis, there was no association found between baseline characteristics and delays, suggesting that the bulk of delays occur before biopsy.

When imaging suggests malignancy, tissue biopsy and diagnosis should proceed without delay. Within the U.S., studies have found the median time from initial abnormal radiography to final diagnosis of lung cancer to be between 13 and 16 days. 26 , 27 We found that patients with NC typically have a median time of 28 days for the same interval. After the biopsy has been collected, clinical guidelines recommend a turnaround time of 10 working days for molecular testing results to be released to the treating physician. 28 Patients with NC have a median interval of 21 days from biopsy to diagnosis (with 5% having a >6-month delay), despite an in-house NUT IHC usually taking less than a week to result. While we found significant improvement in the diagnosis of NC across the U.S. over time, the distribution of NC diagnoses in the past several years still does not mirror the population distribution of the U.S. NC diagnosis remains concentrated in the Northeast, underscoring the need for broader recognition and access to in-house testing. This suggests that more pathologists and clinicians need to consider a diagnosis of NC and ask whether a diagnostic test for NC should be sent, especially in a younger patient with a low pack-year/never-smoking history. Our recent work comparing NGS testing to other modalities of detecting NC found that patients diagnosed via NUT IHC had overall better survival outcomes, possibly associated with IHC rapid turnaround, compared to patients diagnosed via NGS testing. 6 Furthermore, standard (ct)DNA NGS misses >75% of NC cases. This further emphasizes the need for widespread NUT IHC accessibility and proactively thinking about diagnosis and testing.

More than half of lung cancers are diagnosed as stage IV, contributing to an overall lower survival rate among those with thoracic malignancies compared to other cancers. 29 A similar finding was observed in NCs with thoracic origin, including 3% of cases that were upstaged from scan to diagnosis due to a delay. Patients with non-metastatic cancer, including those in NC, have better outcomes with locoregional interventions such as surgery and radiation, indicating the importance of early diagnosis for this aggressive malignancy. 30 Early accurate diagnosis is critical to ensuring that patients start the most appropriate regimen as quickly as possible. Given the aggressive nature of NC, many patients may no longer be fit to enroll in a clinical trial when there are delays in diagnosis.

Delayed diagnosis of NC can result not only in delayed treatment, but also in sub-optimal treatment. Of patients that started treatment prior to NCDx, 22% were on a likely inadequately effective treatment. Only 33% participated in a clinical trial following diagnosis. Because outcomes of standard-of-care chemotherapy are limited, existing clinical trials, such as those testing BET bromodomain combinations (e.g., NCT05019716, NCT05372640, and NCT05488548), should be considered in all patients. 5 Government, industry, and foundations should prioritize funding to rationally designed clinical trials in NC and preclinical science that will lead to treatments for this disease. Recent work by our group has found that the incidence of NC likely exceeds some uncommon subsets of NSCLC that have effective FDA-approved targeted therapies (e.g., BRAF V600E NSCLC). 16 In our cohort, the median age was 37, which is four decades shorter than the average life expectancy in the U.S. Broad clinical trial enrollment is essential to accelerate development of effective treatments for this underserved cancer.

Our study has several limitations. Patients included in our cohort had to have sufficient diagnostic information. By default, these were patients who survived long enough to be diagnosed with NC, excluding patients with short survival. Therefore, we chose not to analyze survival outcomes based on diagnostic delays as we were unable to include patients who likely had NC but progressed before diagnosis. Additionally, as patients with a non-thoracic primary typically have a better prognosis, this may partially explain the longer diagnostic delay compared with patients who had a thoracic primary. We also limited the cohort to the U.S. to reduce confounding from international differences in diagnostic practices. Nevertheless, we have reported the largest cohort to date analyzing these questions. Future directions include a larger cohort that would allow more in-depth analysis of diagnostic delays and better identify and adjust for potential confounders.

We confirmed that NC presents as a poorly differentiated/squamous cancer. Furthermore, it always presents as a carcinoma and nearly all cases are of thoracic or head and neck origin, and was not initially diagnosed in 78% of cases. NC can test positive for IHC stains that can be pitfalls for misdiagnoses (e.g., CD99 and TTF-1). When patients present with a poorly differentiated/squamous lung or head and neck origin cancer, clinicians and pathologists should consider asking whether a NUT IHC test should be performed on the case, particularly in young patients with a never-smoking history. A prompt diagnosis of NC leads to accurate prognostic information, ensuring patients are on a treatment targeting poorly differentiated/squamous carcinomas and are directed to enroll in NC-directed clinical trials. Increasing the proportion of patients on rationally informed clinical trials to rapidly develop effective therapies for this aggressive disease is a vast unmet need in this cancer. Widespread education on the presenting features of NUT carcinoma and diagnostic pitfalls is critical to ensuring the best outcomes for these patients.
