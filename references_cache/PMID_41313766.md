# Metabolic reprogramming in Fanconi anemia: Evidence of compromised glucose oxidation, enhanced ketogenesis, and metabolic inflexibility.
**Authors:** Vicente-Muñoz S, Summer SS, Galletta TJ, Mehta KY, Lane AN, Davies SM, Romick LE
**Journal:** Sci Adv (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1126/sciadv.ady6117](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.ady6117)

## Content

Fanconi anemia (FA) is a rare genetic disorder characterized by congenital abnormalities, bone marrow failure, and cancer predisposition. Approximately 80% of individuals with FA exhibit metabolic abnormalities, including failure to thrive and increased diabetes risk. Current management relies on physical examinations and glucose tolerance tests, which lack dynamic metabolic assessment. We used stable isotope labeling with13C6-glucose to track nutrient metabolism in individuals with FA. Unlike controls, persons with FA showed no postprandial increase in energy expenditure, sustained hyperglycemia accompanied by elevated glycolysis, and a markedly higher ketogenic response supporting a shift toward lipid utilization. Hormonal analysis revealed secretion of pancreatic hormones and ghrelin in individuals with FA, while incretins were unaffected. These results reflect a profound alteration in substrate utilization involving glucose intolerance, insulin resistance, and β-cell dysfunction. This innovative approach provides unprecedented insights into FA pathophysiology, which will inform more targeted nutritional and therapeutic interventions for this complex disorder.

Fanconi anemia (FA) is a rare inherited syndrome caused by germline loss of function in any of the 23 known FA DNA repair genes. Affected persons show a high incidence of bone marrow failure and a strong predisposition to hematopoietic and epithelial malignancies (1, 2). Many individuals with FA also present with congenital malformations such as limb anomalies, microcephaly, and/or microphthalmia (3–6). The premature aging phenotype of FA is characterized by many endocrine and metabolic sequelae including decreased sex hormone production (7), hepatic dysfunction (8), upper and lower gastrointestinal disorders (9, 10), and hormonal imbalance (11, 12). FA can affect virtually every organ system in the body (13). Recent advances in the management of hematological complications, such as improved techniques for bone marrow transplantation to treat marrow failure have extended the life expectancy of individuals with FA and increased the prevalence of endocrine and metabolic disorders. Despite the growing recognition of endocrine and metabolic anomalies in persons with FA, there remains a critical gap in our mechanistic understanding of their etiology and consequences. These aspects of the rare disease have not been explored in depth in the clinical setting.

Studies describing FA endocrine and metabolic abnormalities are limited. A portion of the FA population exhibits short stature. Previous reports have indicated that adult height is −2 standard deviation score or more below the normal reference range in greater than 50% of persons with FA (14). In those affected, short stature is apparent by the age of 3 years, and most remain below the third percentile for height (12). Growth hormone insensitivity, associated with altered serum insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3 (15), is frequent in idiopathic short stature and studies of response to growth hormone replacement therapy (16). Etiologic factors contributing to growth failure in some individuals with FA include altered growth hormone activation of Janus kinase 2 signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 signaling (17), as well as restricted energy intake, altered metabolism, abnormal vitamin D metabolism, and chronic illness (14). Nearly two decades ago, a study noted that a substantial percentage of persons with FA manifest an altered carbohydrate and lipid profile (18). However, the biochemical basis of abnormal carbohydrate sensitivity and metabolic regulation has yet to be defined and is the focus of this study.

FA genes play a central role in repairing cellular and organellar DNA, while also interacting with other proteins and pathways. Mutations in these genes can lead to widespread metabolic dysfunctions across multiple organ systems. Besides studies on individuals in both Japanese and Chinese cohorts relating to aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 metabolic modifiers and early disease progression (19, 20), the relationship between endocrine-derived factors and metabolic perturbations in the FA population has not been largely explored. It is important to critically assess indicators of diabetes before the development of life-debilitating complications by identifying the causes of insulin deficiency and developing new methods for the treatment. Although endocrine abnormalities, especially insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, have been highlighted and noted for decades in individuals with FA, these findings have largely been observational and anecdotal in nature. Hoover et al. (13) provides an in-depth review of current knowledge of the endocrine metabolism dysregulation axis in FA, further emphasizing the lack of research and urgent need in this area. Controlled and targeted tracking of any key metabolic nutrient to evaluate its handling and processing within the body of a person with FA has not previously been performed.

Stable isotope resolved metabolomics (SIRM) provides a powerful analytical approach to elucidate the connections between nutrient uptake and utilization for understanding FA comorbidities at the mechanistic level. Metabolites have been found to show sudden changes in concentration and composition within a biological system in response to either external stimuli such as diseases or treatments. The advent of tracing metabolomics has revolutionized the field, enabling researchers to track the metabolic fate of individual small molecules into their downstream products (21, 22). This advanced technology allows for the direct tracing of 13C6-glucose as it is absorbed, processed, and used in persons with FA. We aim to explore the underlying pathophysiology of endocrine and metabolic dysfunctions in FA and its clinical consequences, with a focus on improving FA population care and outcomes.

Our study uses an isotopically labeled nutrient in persons with FA, and we have found a robust metabolic phenomenon in this population. This metabolic phenomenon is similar in individuals with mutations in three different FA repair gene mutations including our FA population, supporting generalizability of the observations. Our findings have important implications in multiple organ systems and suggest that FA proteins, as well as other related proteins or pathways, play an important role in endocrine-sensitive tissues, and targetable pathways can be leveraged in a genetic, genomic, or therapeutic manner.

This case-control study comprised eight individuals diagnosed with FA and 21 age-, sex-, and ethnicity-matched healthy controls. Among individuals with FA, ages ranged from 13 to 29 years, including three pediatric study participants (ages 13, 15, and 16 years). Individuals with FA were recruited from the Fanconi Anemia Comprehensive Care Center at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC). Those taking medications that could affect their metabolic status were excluded. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in table S1. Notably, the individuals with FA exhibited significant reductions in body weight (~60% lower) and BMI compared to healthy controls (Table 1). Whereas muscle mass was markedly lower in individuals with FA, fat mass parameters remained relatively unaffected (Table 1). Measurements of arm muscle and strength also highlighted differences between the two groups. Both mid-upper arm muscle area (MUAMA) and grip strength [as a validated marker of strength and muscle function; (23)] were significantly lower in FA compared to controls (Table 1).

Following overnight fasting, all participants underwent a comprehensive metabolic assessment protocol involving oral 13C6-glucose administration (25 g), with measurement of energy expenditure by indirect calorimetry (IC), metabolomic profiling by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and hormone analysis at baseline and up to 4 hours postadministration (Fig. 1).

To begin understanding the metabolic status of persons with FA, we quantified whole-body energy expenditure in individuals with FA and controls by IC, the gold standard method in pediatric populations (24). First, resting energy expenditure (REE; kilocalories per day) in overnight-fasted individuals was analyzed by IC (Fig. 2), and data were normalized to skeletal muscle mass (SMM; kilocalories per kilogram) (25). Despite differences in body weight and muscle mass, individuals with FA had comparable REE to matched controls (FA: 42.90 ± 6.00, controls: 48.94 ± 1.35; P = 0.1636). Next, subjects were given an oral bolus of 13C6-glucose, and energy expenditure was measured again after 30 min to determine the thermic effect of food (TEF). Unexpectedly, individuals with FA showed a significantly decreased postmeal energy expenditure compared to controls (FA: 33.08.90 ± 4.91, controls: 51.41 ± 1.54; P < 0.0001). When comparing REE at baseline and REE after 13C6-glucose administration in healthy control and individuals with FA, overall and based on the paired t test, the control group responded as expected, with an increase in energy expenditure after the 13C6-glucose administration (P = 0.0084, mean difference = 2.465), whereas in the FA group, there was a decrease in energy expenditure following glucose ingestion (P = 0.1964, mean difference = −9.824) (Fig. 2A). This reduction was attributed solely to the ingestion of 13C6-glucose in the FA group, since the energy expenditure did increase in control individuals after 13C6-glucose administration as expected. To further test whether energy expenditure is altered in FA, we compared predicted REE, calculated using the Harris-Benedict equation (26), to measured REE, to assess whether predicted REE accurately reflects the measured metabolic status of individuals with FA (Fig. 2B). In controls, predicted (1616.90 ± 61.63) and measured REE (1408.24 ± 51.13) were close; however, low variability led to a statistically significant difference (P < 0.0001). Conversely, in persons with FA, the predicted REE (1263.75 ± 77.16) was markedly higher than measured (760.75 ± 101.23). The considerable interindividual variability reduced the statistical power (P = 0.0004) to detect this clinically meaningful difference, despite the large magnitude of the mean deficit observed. Our findings reveal that individuals with FA exhibit both impaired postprandial metabolic response and altered basal energy metabolism. Persons with FA demonstrated a reduced ability to increase energy expenditure following 13C6-glucose intake and had lower measured REE compared to predicted values at baseline. These metabolic abnormalities, which standard predictive models fail to capture, indicate comprehensive energy metabolism dysfunction in the FA population.

Age-stratified analysis revealed distinct relationships between BMI, predicted basal metabolic rate, and measured REE in persons with FA compared to controls (Fig. 2, D to F). Across all age groups, individuals with FA exhibited markedly lower BMI and reduced REE relative to age-matched controls. In controls, REE tracked with BMI and remained stable across age, whereas in persons with FA, REE was consistently reduced and did not increase in parallel with age or BMI, both at baseline and after 13C6-glucose administration.

We next evaluated substrate utilization before and after 13C6-glucose bolus in individuals with FA and matched controls using the respiratory quotient (RQ), calculated as the ratio of the volume of carbon dioxide released to the volume of oxygen consumed during respiration (27, 28). In a fed human, an RQ of ~0.8 indicates a mixed use of substrates (carbohydrates and fats) for energy; RQ values <0.8 suggest a greater use of stored fat for energy (such as in the fasted state), and a higher RQ indicates use of primarily carbohydrates for energy. The RQ was similar at baseline between individuals with FA and controls (0.84 ± 0.07 and 0.81 ± 0.03), respectively (Fig. 2C). Thirty minutes after glucose bolus, the controls had a slight increase in RQ to 0.84 ± 0.06, as expected given the influx of carbohydrates. However, the RQ in individuals with FA decreased to 0.78 ± 0.07 at 30 min, becoming significantly different than controls (P = 0.027), indicating the use of fat stores for energy despite the recent consumption of glucose (Fig. 2C).

Our findings of changed postprandial energy expenditure and substrate preference in individuals with FA suggest a potential dysregulation in glucose metabolism. To further investigate the metabolic impact of FA in our population, we used orally administered 13C-enriched glucose to understand the possible alterations of glucose metabolism in FA. Following an overnight fast, participants [FA (n = 8) and heathy control volunteers (n = 21)] received 25 g of uniformly 13C-enriched glucose (13C6-glucose) in drinking water. Plasma samples were collected at baseline (preadministration) and at 1-hour intervals up to 4 hours post–13C6-glucose challenge. An NMR-based SIRM approach was used in plasma samples to monitor the fate of 13C-labeled glucose and their downstream 13C products. This methodology allows us to determine how loss of a functional FA pathway in humans leads to metabolic dysregulation and to potentially identify targetable metabolic pathways to improve health in individuals with FA.

The resulting NMR profiles provided a metabolic fingerprint of each study participant. The assignments of the 1H-13C-HSQC spectra represent the 1H directly attached to 13C, demonstrating the incorporation of 13C atoms from the 13C-labeled glucose into several downstream metabolites. These metabolites include lactate, alanine, acetate, pyruvate, glutamine, and citrate. The isotopomer analysis along time will identify differences in 13C consumption and subsequent metabolic transformation between FA and controls.

To track the metabolic fate of glucose in individuals with FA, we monitored 13C6-glucose and its downstream products (Fig. 4A). The initial analysis of plasma metabolites prior to 13C6-glucose consumption (t = 0 hours) revealed key differences in unlabeled metabolites (glucose, lactate, and alanine) between the FA and control groups (Fig. 4, B to D). While total glucose consumption was similar during the first 2 hours, individuals with FA exhibited prolonged unlabeled glucose utilization, extending to 3 hours post–13C6-glucose ingestion (Fig. 4B). In contrast, the control cohort showed an increase in glucose levels at this time point, suggesting the onset of gluconeogenesis (Fig. 4B). This suggested potential differences in glucose clearance or utilization mechanisms between these populations. Notably, baseline measurements revealed statistically significant differences in unlabeled lactate and alanine levels between the two study populations (Fig. 4, C and D). Following the administration of 25 g of 13C6-glucose, ~70% of the circulating glucose was labeled (13C6-glucose) in both groups after 1 hour (Fig. 4H). However, the time course of 13C6-glucose appearance in the bloodstream differed between groups. In healthy non-FA controls, 13C6-glucose levels peaked at 1 hour postadministration. In contrast, the 13C6-glucose peak was delayed in individuals with FA, reaching maximum concentrations 2 hours postadministration, and circulating 13C6-glucose levels in FA remained significantly elevated throughout the experiment compared to controls (Fig. 4E). To further investigate the implications of the delayed glucose peak time observed in individuals with FA, we analyzed the 13C6-glucose–derived metabolites.

Despite the delayed 13C6-glucose peak, the overall 13C6-glucose consumption, calculated as the difference between the initial 13C6-glucose spike in blood and the final time point (Fig. 4E), was comparable between study groups (Fig. 4E). As expected, 13C6-glucose was metabolized via glycolysis. However, levels of 13C3-lactate [generated via lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)] and 13C3-alanine production [produced via alanine aminotransferase (ALT)] were approximately twofold higher in persons with FA at time points between 2- and 4-hour post–glucose bolus, mirroring the pattern observed for 13C6-glucose (Fig. 4, F and G).

The ingestion of 13C6-glucose resulted in rapid labeling of circulating lactate (Fig. 4F), indicative of robust glycolysis and lactic fermentation. Analysis of 13C fractional enrichment revealed distinct patterns between individuals with FA and healthy non-FA controls (Fig. 4, H to J). In individuals with FA, lactate reached maximum labeling at 48%, and alanine at 46%, both at 3 hours post–tracer administration. In both cases, the 13C enrichment was significantly higher in the FA population when compared to controls (Fig. 4I and J). Conversely, controls achieved maximal 13C enrichment earlier, at 2 hours, followed by a progressive decrease. This pattern reflects an enhanced glycolytic flux to pyruvate and subsequent metabolism via LDH and ALT in individuals with FA.

The enhanced glycolytic activity in individuals with FA was accompanied by increased mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, as revealed by the analysis of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates (Fig. 5A). Using 1H-13C-HSQC spectra, we observed and quantified the abundance of various TCA 13C-labeled intermediates and derived metabolites (Fig. 5, B, C, and G). Consistent with the increased aerobic glycolysis observed in FA, 13C-pyruvate levels were significantly elevated in persons with FA compared to controls (Fig. 5B). This elevation in 13C-pyruvate led to a concomitant increase in excreted 13C-citrate levels (Fig. 5C), indicating that at least some of the glucose was absorbed by tissue and differentially metabolized via mitochondria. Notably, even before glucose bolus administration, persons with FA had elevated baseline levels of unlabeled pools of citrate and succinate (Fig. 4, D and E), with citrate levels remaining consistently high throughout the experiment. Individuals with FA also exhibited higher glutamine consumption (Δ glutamine t4h − t0h: FA = −142.3 versus control = 4.654; P = 0.014) (Fig. 5F) and enhanced de novo glutamine synthesis (Fig. 5G). The incorporation of 13C into TCA cycle intermediates peaked 3 hours after glucose tracer administration.

Oxidative metabolism remains active in individuals with FA despite the prevalence of oxygen-independent glycolysis. A portion of 13C6-glucose–derived pyruvate enters mitochondria and is converted to acetyl–coenzyme A (CoA) via pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), resulting in increased 13C-citrate levels via citrate synthase (Fig. 5C), indicating a functional entry point into the TCA cycle. In persons with FA, 13C incorporation into TCA cycle intermediates reached its maximum at 3 hours post–13C6-glucose tracer administration. In addition, we observed significantly enhanced de novo glutamine synthesis in individuals with FA compared to healthy controls (Fig. 5G). This finding implies ongoing TCA cycle activity to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), followed by transamination and reduction via glutamate dehydrogenase and glutamine synthetase activities, an adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP)–dependent process, further supporting the presence of active oxidative metabolism in persons with FA.

The metabolic rewiring in FA extended beyond glycolysis and TCA cycle affecting ketone body production. During periods of fasting, decreased insulin levels are expected, which promote lipolysis, leading to the breakdown of fats into free fatty acids that are used as a main source of energy. In this context, the liver uses fatty acids to generate ketone bodies (acetoacetate, acetone, and 3-HB) through β-oxidation of fatty acids (Fig. 6A). Ketone bodies serve as auxiliary fuel during dietary shortages of carbohydrates (29, 30).

In our study, at baseline following an overnight fast, the control and FA study populations exhibited similar levels of circulating glucose (Fig. 4B). Despite this, the FA group displayed a significantly higher ketogenic response, as evidenced by threefold higher levels of each of the three ketone bodies (i.e., acetoacetate, acetone, and 3-HB) at baseline before 13C6-glucose administration (Fig. 6, B to D). One hour post–13C6-glucose challenge, both groups had equal blood glucose levels (Fig. 4E). In response to the elevated blood glucose, ketone body levels in the control participants rapidly decreased near to zero, indicating normal glucose utilization. In contrast, the FA cohort did not respond immediately to the presence of elevated 13C6-glucose, and their ketone blood levels remained elevated or showed minimal change (Fig. 6, B to D), only returning to baseline after 2 hours when circulating glucose levels peaked (Fig. 4E), suggesting an impaired initial response to 13C6-glucose. The delayed return of ketone levels to baseline at 2 hours when glucose peaked reflects insulin-mediated suppression of ketogenesis. The elevated glucose triggers insulin release, which inhibits hormone-sensitive lipase activity, reducing free fatty acid availability for ketone production.

Metabolic disruption in individuals with FA is not limited to carbohydrate metabolism. We found a broader dysregulation related to ketone bodies, as well as increased levels of BCAAs and aromatic amino acids in the FA groups compared to controls. BCAAs have mixed effects on metabolic health. While BCAA-rich diets can positively affect metabolic health (31), several observational studies indicate that elevated circulating levels of BCAAs are associated with metabolic disorders including obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (31–34). These alterations in fasting blood levels of BCAAs may be explained by alterations in BCAA appearance and clearance rates, as well as reduced activity of BCAA catabolic enzymes (31). In our study, individuals with FA had significantly higher levels of BCAAs leucine and valine, both at baseline (fasting conditions) and following glucose challenge (Fig. 7, A and B).

We also found significant elevations in aromatic amino acids, specifically in phenylalanine and tyrosine in individuals with FA compared to the control group (Fig. 7, C and D). These findings aligned with previous studies that showed increased levels of aromatic amino acids, including phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan, in individuals with insulin resistance (33–36). Notably, glycine levels exhibited an opposite trend to the aforementioned amino acids, showing significant depletion in individuals with FA (Fig. 7, C to E). This divergent pattern is particularly significant given glycine’s role as a limiting substrate for glutathione synthesis, the primary cellular antioxidant defense system.

Given the central role of hormones in regulating systemic metabolism (37–39) and our prior findings of impaired postprandial energy expenditure, a preference for lipids as energy source despite glucose availability and the wide metabolic dysregulation in persons with FA led us to investigate potential hormone imbalance in our study subjects. Key metabolic hormones such as insulin, glucagon, leptin, ghrelin, cortisol, and incretins play essential roles in coordinating nutrient metabolism, energy balance, and stress adaptation. Based on our evidence of altered energy substrate utilization, persistent hyperglycemia, and impaired metabolic flexibility in FA, we hypothesized that dysregulation of hormonal signaling might contribute to or exacerbate these metabolic phenotypes.

As previously seen, the control group exhibited a typical glycemic response for nondiabetic individuals. When representing the total circulating glucose concentrations (12C6-glucose + 13C6-glucose) (Fig. 9A), in the control group, glucose peaked ~60 min after meal initiation and returned to preprandial levels within 2 to 3 hours (40). In contrast, individuals with FA demonstrated a markedly different metabolic profile. Their plasma glucose peak occurred at 2 hours, reaching levels 1.4 times higher than the control cohort and stayed elevated for the duration of the experiment (Fig. 9A).

Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) are the two most potent incretin hormones responsible for stimulating insulin secretion from pancreatic β cells in response to oral glucose. These hormones exert opposing effects on glucagon secretion: GLP-1 suppresses glucagon and GIP enhances glucagon secretion (38). Individuals with FA displayed significantly higher circulating levels of both GIP and GLP-1 compared to controls (Fig. 9, B and C), which correlates with the higher circulating 13C6-glucose levels found in individuals with FA (Fig. 9A). Conversely, the peptide tyrosine-tyrosine (PYY) response showed an opposite trend in persons with FA. Both at baseline and following glucose uptake, individuals with FA displayed lower PYY levels compared to controls (Fig. 9D).

Corresponding to the levels of glucose in control participants, maximum insulin levels were also detected 1 hour after 13C6-glucose administration, gradually decreasing to their preprandial levels by 4 hours post–glucose bolus (Fig. 9E). Despite hyperglycemia and increased levels of the insulinotropic hormones GIP and GLP-1, insulin levels in the FA group did not differ from those of the healthy controls (Fig. 9E). Levels of C-peptide, which is cosecreted with insulin in equimolar amounts and is a surrogate marker of insulin secretion (39, 41), showed a shifted peak in individuals with FA compared to controls but the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 9F).

We extended our analysis to other pancreatic hormones, including total amylin, glucagon, and pancreatic polypeptide (PP) (Fig. 9, G to I). While baseline levels of amylin and glucagon were comparable between FA and the control group, post–13C6-glucose administration revealed divergent trends. Individuals with FA exhibited opposite patterns in amylin and glucagon levels compared to controls (Fig. 9, G and H). In controls, amylin levels peaked at 1 hour post–13C6-glucose administration and then dropped rapidly back to baseline levels. While amylin levels also peaked at 1 hour post–glucose administration in individuals with FA, these levels persisted through the 2-hour postadministration time point and declined gradually with levels still above baseline at the final time point. While plasma glucagon levels fell below respective baseline values for both study populations, the control subjects show a sharp increase in levels following the 1-hour post–13C6-glucose time point and levels recovered to baseline by 4 hours post–13C6-glucose administration. Plasma glucagon levels in persons with FA continued to decrease over the course of the study and did not recover back to baseline. PP demonstrated a unique profile, with FA levels showing an inverse trend both at baseline and following glucose administration (Fig. 9I).

Collectively, while incretin hormone secretion was not impaired in FA, with GIP and GLP-1 levels correlating positively with circulating glucose concentration, the pattern of C-peptide, insulin, and amylin secretion was discordant with the degree of incretin stimulation and glucose plasma levels.

Incretins also play a key role in regulating appetite by controlling leptin and ghrelin levels (42). Circulating concentrations of total ghrelin rise before a meal or after fasting and decrease to baseline levels upon refeeding or oral glucose administration (43, 44). In humans, ghrelin levels typically display an inverse relationship with weight gain, adiposity, and insulin resistance (43). Together, these findings support ghrelin’s role as an endogenous regulator of appetite, food intake, and energy balance. No differences in leptin levels were observed (Fig. 9K). Our study revealed a marked difference in ghrelin levels between FA and healthy controls. Ghrelin concentrations were below the detection threshold in 68% of the individuals with FA, compared to only 22% of the control subjects.

Persons with FA had elevated levels of plasma cortisol immediately following glucose administration (Fig. 9L). These levels normalized to those of the control group by 3 hours after the 13C6-glucose bolus.

Our study of individuals with FA has revealed significant metabolic alterations that contribute to the complex phenotype of this disease. Anthropometric assessments confirm failure to thrive in the persons with FA tested, while the paradoxical reduction in energy expenditure following glucose uptake suggests a fundamental alteration in energy metabolism and metabolic inflexibility to carbohydrates, which may contribute to the pathophysiology of the disease.

The 13C6-glucose SIRM analysis showed a critical divergence between individuals with FA and controls at 2 hours post–glucose uptake. This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating that a delayed glucose peak time has been associated with increasing insulin resistance and impaired pancreatic β-cell function in T2DM (45, 46). This delayed peak and sustained elevation of blood 13C6-glucose in individuals with FA may indicate a hepatic alteration in glucose clearance or utilization mechanisms (47).

The metabolic analysis revealed that individuals with FA exhibited increased glycolysis and lactic fermentation producing 13C3-lactate and 13C3-alanine, indicating increased activity of LDH and ALT compared to controls due to either increased substrate availability or enhanced enzyme activity. These findings indicate significant alterations in glucose metabolism and energy production in individuals with FA.

However, the TCA cycle is still active in these individuals, and a portion of the 13C6-glucose–derived pyruvate is shunted into the TCA cycle for mitochondrial oxidation. Our findings corroborate previous research (48) demonstrating that lactic fermentation does not necessarily suppress oxidative phosphorylation. The metabolic fate of pyruvate would determine the extent to which cells engage in glycolysis in the cytosol versus glucose oxidation via the TCA cycle in the mitochondria.

The persisting elevation of circulating ketone bodies levels in persons with FA, even after 13C6-glucose administration, indicates similarities with insulin-resistant individuals. Despite producing and releasing insulin levels comparable to controls in the postprandial state, the cells from individuals with FA exhibit an impaired ability to take up and use the available glucose. Consequently, the persons with FA demonstrate increased reliance on ketone bodies as an alternative energy source, reflecting traits of glucose intolerance and/or insulin resistance. This metabolic adaptation may represent a clinically protective mechanism, as ketone bodies provide enhanced metabolic efficiency under specific conditions (49–51) and cellular protection through the role of ketone bodies, particularly β-hydroxybutyrate, in activating pathways against oxidative stress (52). Given that FA is characterized by progressive cellular stress, DNA repair deficiencies, and oxidative damage, the enhanced ketone body utilization may serve as an endogenous protective mechanism that helps mitigate cellular dysfunction by providing both improved energy metabolism and resistance to oxidative and inflammatory stress.

This was corroborated by the elevated levels of branched-chain and aromatic amino acids in persons with FA, both in fasting and postglucose states. This altered amino acid signature may serve as a valuable biomarker for identifying individuals at risk of developing insulin resistance and offers the potential opportunity for reducing progression to diabetes. Elevated levels of BCAAs have emerged as promising biomarkers for metabolic syndrome (33). However, current research has not definitively established whether BCAAs are merely indicators of insulin resistance or whether they actively contribute to its development (31, 53).

The observed glycine depletion in our FA cohort reflects a metabolic pattern with potentially important clinical implications. Numerous studies have reported decreased plasma glycine concentrations in individuals with obesity (54, 55), insulin resistance, T2DM, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (54). Notably, prospective studies have identified reduced plasma glycine levels as a robust predictive marker for the development of glucose intolerance and T2DM (54, 56). These investigations suggested that glycine may play its role in insulin resistance and T2DM through alteration in the glutathione synthesis rate. Enhanced glycine transport in diabetic β cells has been demonstrated to augment glutathione synthesis, thereby conferring protection against oxidative stress (54). In the context of FA, where cells face continuous oxidative stress (57, 58), glycine depletion may represent a metabolic bottleneck that exacerbates cellular dysfunction. This suggests that glycine supplementation could potentially offer therapeutic benefits by supporting cellular antioxidant capacity.

The broad changes in systemic metabolism observed in individuals with FA led us to the hypothesis that FA individuals may have a potential hormone imbalance that may drive, at least in part, the drastic metabolic changes observed. The baseline and postprandial plasma hormone profile provided intriguing insights into the metabolic dysfunction in FA. While the incretin response aligned with glucose levels, the inadequate β-cell response characterized by the attenuated insulin response and delayed C-peptide peak may imply an intrinsic β-cell dysfunction. However, the interpretation of GLP-1 requires careful consideration. GLP-1 presents significant analytical challenges: GLP-1 circulates in plasma at very low concentrations (<10 pM) and has a rapid clearance with a very short half-life, which translates into sensitivity and stability challenges for detection and quantification (59, 60). In our study, ~50% of the GLP-1 values fell below the detection limit. The markedly reduced ghrelin levels in persons with FA present an important finding that warrants further investigation into its role in appetite regulation and energy homeostasis within the context of FA (61).

The insufficient response to glucose challenge observed in persons with FA may be attributed to a mechanism similar to that seen in Cushing syndrome. In this condition, relative insulinopenia can result from direct or indirect toxic effects on pancreatic β cells, leading to suppressed endogenous insulin secretion (62). This phenomenon could explain the observed inadequate insulin levels in individuals with FA for effective glucose regulation following the glucose challenge. While excesses of cortisol negatively affect insulin secretion, insulin sensitivity, and glucose tolerance, it does not primarily induce hyperinsulinemia (62).

Our study demonstrates the power of SIRM in detecting nutritional and metabolic changes in persons with FA without inducing stress, offering insights into their nutritional alteration and predisposition to develop insulin resistance or T2DM. Our integrated analysis corroborates and extends upon previous findings of impaired glucose metabolism endocrine abnormalities in FA (18), showing hyperglycemia and a tendency toward insulin resistance, which aligns with the higher incidence of diabetes mellitus in individuals with FA compared to the general population (11, 12, 14, 63, 64).

These alterations likely represent adaptive responses to chronic oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and defective DNA repair pathways intrinsic to FA. The persistent oxidative stress state may impair pancreatic β-cell function and disrupt hormonal feedback loops, leading to systemic endocrine dysfunction and abnormal glucose metabolism, a hallmark of FA (18, 63). It has been proposed that β-cell dysfunction/insulin resistance can be caused by the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in FA and that therapies directed at reducing ROS, such as the antioxidant quercetin, would alleviate insulin resistance and maintain glucose homeostasis (65).

The metabolic alterations observed in our study may contribute to the failure to thrive and the high predisposition to developing aggressive squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck in individuals with FA (1, 66). Our findings highlight the urgent need for markers of insulin resistance in the FA population for primary and secondary prevention of associated high-morbidity and high-mortality diseases.

We acknowledge that excluding diabetic patients limits our ability to fully characterize the metabolic spectrum in individuals with FA. However, not all persons with FA have T2DM, which is an additional metabolic confounding factor. In this study, we focused on the FA-only subpopulation, which demonstrates extensive complexity of glucose metabolism disorders. Future studies should include individuals with FA and control subjects with established T2DM to characterize metabolic dysfunction progression when both conditions coexist, to determine whether metabolic alterations in persons with FA represent unique pathophysiological mechanisms or common pathways to insulin resistance. This expanded study design would enable validation and extension of our current findings, determination of whether individuals with FA with T2DM exhibit more severe metabolic perturbations, identification of common metabolic pathways between FA-associated and diabetes-associated metabolic dysfunction, and potential identification of persons with FA at highest risk for T2DM development.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive metabolic profile of persons with FA, revealing significant alterations in energy metabolism, glucose utilization, and hormone regulation. These findings not only contribute to our understanding of FA pathophysiology but also offer potential targets for early therapeutic interventions and personalized disease management.

The groups of persons diagnosed with FA (n = 8) and nonrelated matched healthy volunteers (n = 21) over 12 years old (sex, age, and ethnicity) were selected and consented for oral administration of uniformly 13C-labeled glucose (25 g in 50 ml of water; Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, 99%) and consequent stable isotope metabolomics study under approved Institutional Review Board (IRB) protocol (CCHMC IRB 2019-1125).

At the time of admission, relevant clinical variables (age, gender, ethnicity, BMI, cancer incidence, and bone marrow transplant status) were documented to be evaluated as potential confounding variables. All FA and control volunteers underwent anthropometric measurements and in-depth body composition testing, to assess nutritional status using a series of standard and noninvasive measurements, such as triceps skinfold measurements, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) measurements, bioelectrical impedance analysis, and hand grip strength assessment by dynamometer.

Following an overnight fast, participants received oral 13C6-glucose with serial blood collection at baseline (0 hours) and 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 hours postadministration for comprehensive metabolomic and hormone analyses. Energy expenditure was measured by IC at baseline and 30 min following the 13C6-glucose administration to assess metabolic response.

IC was used to determine REE and postmeal energy expenditure, also known as the TEF. Use of IC to measure REE has been shown to be reliable and valid when compared to other methods (67–69).

Participants arrived at the research clinic early in the morning after an overnight fast and a normal pattern of activity and sleep on the prior day. Participants were instructed to engage in minimal physical activity after waking on the day of the test. After voiding, the participant rested for 30 min in a quiet, darkened room at a stable, comfortable temperature to attain a resting state, as described by Matarese (70). The instrument (Vmax Encore 29 N, CareFusion, Yorba Linda, CA) was calibrated before each test using reference gases. During testing, a ventilated hood is placed over the participant’s head to capture oxygen (VO2) consumption and carbon dioxide (VCO2) production on a computerized, open-circuit indirect calorimeter operated by a trained technician. If the participant achieved “steady state” [defined as a minimum of 5 min during which the average VO2 and VCO2 both change less than 10%, and the RQ changes by less than 5%; (70)] within the first 15 min, the test was ended. If a participant did not achieve a steady state in the first 15 min, the test continued until either steady state was achieved or the test time reached 30 min, whichever occurs first. The final REE result was determined from an average of values collected while the participant is in steady state, after the first 5 min of the test. If steady state was not achieved after 30 min, the final REE result was determined from the average of all values collected between minutes 5 and 30. In all cases, data from the first 5 min were excluded, as the REE during this initial phase has been shown to be higher than REE measured during subsequent minutes (71).

Following the baseline REE test, participants consumed 25 g of 13C6-glucose in 50 ml of water. At +30 min after ingestion of the glucose drink, the IC test was repeated to measure TEF, following the same method as for REE. Participants remained in a reclined position for the majority of the time between measurements to maintain a rested state throughout testing. For both REE and TEF, the measured energy expenditure (in kilocalories) was calculated using the Weir equation (72).

A Jamar hydraulic hand dynamometer (Performance Health, Warrenville, IL) was used to measure hand grip strength. To perform this test, the patient’s arm was positioned close to the side of the body but not touching, with the elbow at a 90° angle, and held the dynamometer in a vertical position with the pressure dial facing out toward the measurer. The patient was then instructed to squeeze as hard as they can and hold the tension for 3 s while maintaining the elbow at 90°. The measurer captured the highest value in kilograms of force achieved during the squeeze and recorded the measurement to the nearest 0.1 kg. After a brief rest period, the test was repeated on the other arm.

The MUAC was measured using a retractable, nonmetal tape measure with a colored bead tensioner to ensure consistent tension between measurements (Gulick II). To locate the landmark for measuring MUAC, the participant’s right arm was positioned alongside the body with elbow bent at a 90° angle and palm facing upward. The measuring tape was placed with its zero mark at the lateral tip of the acromial process of the scapula and pulled down to the olecranon process of the ulna. The full length of the upper arm is noted, and the midpoint of this distance was marked on the posterior of the arm. Next, with the participant’s arm relaxed and straightened at the side, the zero end of the tape was placed at this mark, and the tape pulled around the arm in a perpendicular plane until it fully circled the arm and lined up with the zero end with the two tensioner beads visible. The measurement is read on the tape where it lined up with the “0” mark and recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm.

Triceps skinfold thickness was measured using Holtain skinfold calipers (Holtain, Crymych, UK) at the same landmark as the MUAC measurement, over the triceps muscle. Participants were instructed to stand or sit upright with their right arm down at the side and fully relaxed. Standing behind the participant, a trained measurer lifted the fold of fat and skin away from the underlying triceps muscle ~2 cm above the MUAC landmark using the thumb and middle finger. The calipers were then positioned around the fold at the MUAC marking, and tension gradually released on the calipers while still holding the fold firmly above with thumb and middle finger. The reading was taken after 2 to 3 s and recorded to the nearest 0.1 mm. The MUAC and triceps skinfold thicknesses were measured three times, and the averages were included in the study data.

The MUAMA (cm2) is an estimation of bone and muscle in the upper arm. MUAMA is calculated using an equation that incorporates the MUAC and triceps skinfold measurements described above (73).

Amylin, C-peptide, ghrelin, GIP, glucagon, interleukin-6, insulin, leptin, Monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), PP, PYY, tumor necrosis factor–α, and GLP-1 concentrations in the sample supernatants were determined using MILLIPLEX Multiplex Kits (Millipore Sigma, Darmstadt, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, in a 96-well black plate, 25 μl of sample in duplicate was incubated with 25 μl of antibody-coated beads overnight at 4°C on a plate shaker. Plates were then washed two times using the BioTek 405 TS (BioTek, Winooski, VT), and 25 μl of secondary antibody was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour while shaking. Last, 25 μl of streptavidin-Rhodophyta Phycoerythrin (RPE) was added directly to the secondary antibody and incubated for 30 min at room temperature with shaking. Plates were then washed two more times, and 150 μl of sheath fluid was added. Plates were shaken for 5 min and then read using Luminex technology on the MILLIPLEX Analyzer (Millipore Sigma, Darmstadt, Germany). Concentrations were calculated from standard curves using recombinant proteins and expressed in picograms per milliliter using Bio-Plex Manager 6.2 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA).

Tsukushi (TSKU) and cortisol concentrations were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 50 μl of sample and 50 μl of antibody were added to precoated plates, which were then incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Plates were then washed two times using the Agilent 405 plate washer, and 100 μl of horseradish peroxidase conjugate was added and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Plates were then washed two times again. Trimethylboron substrate (90 μl) was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 20 min. Stop solution (50 μl) was added, and optical density was measured at 450 nm using an Agilent Epoch microplate reader. Concentrations were calculated from standard curves using recombinant proteins and expressed in nanograms per milliliter.

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from each participant after overnight fasting at baseline (before glucose administration) and at 1, 2, 3, and 4 hours after 13C6-glucose consumption. The blood was collected into Li-heparin tubes (BD Vacutainer, Li-heparin). After homogenization, samples were centrifuged at 1400g for 10 min at 4°C. Aliquots of 250 μl of the plasma fraction were carefully transferred into different new tubes, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until analysis. For NMR analysis, one aliquot of 250 μl of plasma from each volunteer was thawed on ice. Plasma small metabolites were separated from macromolecules by centrifugal ultrafiltration using a 3-kDa molecular-weight-cutoff membrane. Culture media (250 μl) were added to a prewashed 3-kDa spin filter (Avantor, VWR) and centrifuged at 14,000g for 90 min at 4°C. The filtrate flow through was collected in a new tube, weighed, and lyophilized for subsequent analysis by NMR.

For the analysis of the circulating metabolites in plasma, the lyophilized plasma extracts were resuspended in 220 μl of NMR buffer (100 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.3), 1 mM trimethylsilyl propionic acid-d4 sodium salt (TSP) as internal standard, and sodium azide (1 mg/ml) in 100% deuterium oxide (D2O). Each sample (200 μl) was transferred to a 3-mm NMR tube for analysis. All NMR spectra were acquired at 288 K on a Bruker Avance III HD 600 MHz spectrometer (Bruker BioSpin) equipped with a 5-mm Broadband Observe Prodigy probe. Two one-dimensional (1D) experiments were recorded per sample: (i) 1D 1H spectra were acquired using the noesyppr1d pulse sequence. The water signal was suppressed using presaturation with a bandwidth of 25 Hz. 1H spectra were collected with 512 transients using a spectral width of 12 parts per million (ppm) with a relaxation delay of 4.0 s and an acquisition time of 2.0 s corresponding to 44,640 data points. 1H spectra were zero-filled to 128 K data points, baseline corrected, and apodized with 1-Hz exponential function. (ii) 1D 1H-13C-HSQC was recorded using the hsqcetgpprsisp2.2 pulse sequence with a spectral width of 12 ppm, 1024 transients, a relaxation delay of 2.0 s, and an acquisition time of 0.25 s, during which GARP decoupling was applied. 1H-13C-HSQC spectra were processed with zero filling to 16 K data points, baseline corrected, and apodized with a cosine square function as well as a 4-Hz exponential line broadening. All NMR data were acquired at 288 K using TopSpin 3.5 (Bruker BioSpin, USA) and processed and analyzed with MestreNova software (MNova v12.0.3, Spain). 1H and 13C chemical shifts were referenced using 3-trimethylsilyl 2,2,3,3-d4 propionate (TSP) as internal reference at 0 ppm. Metabolite assignment was performed by comparison with in-house multinuclear database, public databases, or literature reports (74, 75).

To characterize the metabolite profile of both groups at different time points, the area of each assigned and well-resolved metabolites in 1D 1H and 1D 1H-13C-HSQC spectra were manually integrated using global spectra deconvolution function in MestreNova software (MNova v12.0.3, Spain). The peak area was divided by the number of protons contributing to that signal. For absolute quantification, the corrected peak areas were converted into nanomoles by calibration against the known amount of the internal standard TSP or lactate (methyl group resonance) at 1.32 ppm (quantified from 1D 1H spectra), respectively. The nanomoles of metabolites in each sample were then normalized to the weight of the filtrated plasma fraction analyzed by NMR. Metabolite quantification from baseline to 4-hour samples was used to define the rates of nutrient uptake and product excretion.

An unsupervised multivariate statistical analysis was carried out in R software. Data were scaled to unit variance, and PCA was used as an exploratory technique on normalized data to evaluate patterns, intrinsic clusters, and outliers. This unsupervised analysis was also used to assess sample homogeneity within each group and examine potential influence of different clinical variables in the overall distribution of the data as potential confounding factors.

Univariate statistical analysis of the data was performed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare FA and control plasma samples and hormones collected and analyzed at different time points and paired t test for the binary comparisons. Significance was defined as P < 0.05. Data were displayed as means ± SEM. An unsupervised multivariate analysis by PCA was performed with the use of the “prcomp” R package, to summarize the relationship among the observations at different time points.
