Collective cell migration (CCM) is a pivotal driver of tumor progression and 
metastasis in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), yet its upstream regulatory 
mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we investigated the functional role 
and molecular mechanism of MTAP deficiency in modulating CCM in NSCLC. Clinical 
sample analysis revealed that NSCLC patients with MTAP deficiency exhibited 
significantly shorter overall survival. Using a three‑dimensional culture 
system, we verified that MTAP deficiency had little effect on cell 
proliferation, but markedly promoted CCM by inducing cytoskeletal rearrangement 
of leader cells within tumor clusters. Mechanistically, MTAP deletion leads to 
intracellular MTA accumulation, which suppresses PRMT5 activity and reduces 
global protein symmetric dimethylarginine (sDMA) modification. Decreased sDMA 
further enhances USF2 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, facilitates 
USF2 binding to the WNT3A promoter, and concurrently reduces DNA CpG methylation 
of the WNT3A promoter, thereby synergistically activating WNT3A transcription. 
Upregulated WNT3A strengthens intercellular crosstalk between leader and 
follower cells, elevates adhesion among follower cells, and drives cytoskeletal 
remodeling in leader cells, ultimately accelerating CCM progression. 
Pharmacologically, the WNT3A specific inhibitor C59 effectively blocked CCM 
formation and mitigated collective metastasis in MTAP‑deficient NSCLC. In 
summary, our results define a novel MTAP-PRMT5-sDMA-WNT3A regulatory axis that 
controls CCM and malignant progression of NSCLC. Targeting WNT3A represents a 
promising therapeutic strategy for the clinical treatment of MTAP‑deficient 
NSCLC.

Plain Language Summary: Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related 
deaths, mainly due to its tendency to spread to other organs. We aimed to 
explore why some lung cancers spread more aggressively than others, focusing on 
a common genetic change: the loss of the MTAP gene. Our study found that when 
the MTAP gene is missing, cancer cells tend to move in groups rather than 
individually. This group movement significantly accelerates cancer spread. We 
also discovered that the loss of MTAP triggers the activation of a specific 
signal called WNT3A, which instructs cancer cells to “move together”. 
Importantly, we demonstrated that a specific drug can block this signal and 
prevent cancer cells from moving in groups. This research is significant because 
it reveals a new way to stop lung cancer from spreading, offering hope for 
personalized treatment for patients with MTAP deficiency and potentially 
improving their survival and quality of life.
