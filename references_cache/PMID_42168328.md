# Optimized nuclei isolation and snRNA-seq reveal oligodendrocyte pathway dysregulation in MOGHE brain tissue from pediatric patients.
**Authors:** Tuccari di San Carlo C, Semeraro R, Cetica V, De Vita D, Mazzinghi B, Falace A, Pelorosso C, Romagnani P, Magi A, Guerrini R, Conti V
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-026-54112-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54112-z)

## Content

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a powerful tool for exploring cellular diversity, but isolating intact cells from complex tissues like the brain remains challenging. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) overcomes these limitations by profiling nuclear RNA from frozen or archived tissue, reducing dissociation bias. Here, we present a simplified protocol for nuclei isolation from frozen human brain biopsies optimized for high yield and minimal debris, enabling robust snRNA-seq analysis. To validate it, we applied the protocol to biopsies from two pediatric patients with mild malformation of cortical development with oligodendroglial hyperplasia and epilepsy (MOGHE) carrying somatic SLC35A2 variants. snRNA-seq of isolated nuclei revealed a marked increase in oligodendrocytes in MOGHE samples, consistent with histopathological observations. Differential gene-expression analysis of oligodendrocyte-derived nuclei showed potential dysregulation of key pathways, including NOTCH, WNT/β-catenin, SLIT/ROBO, and Rho-GTPases signaling, as well as pathways associated with oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, and impaired neuron-glia communication. Despite advances in transcriptomic chemistries allowing fixed-cell analyses, reliable and debris-free nuclei isolation remains essential for generating high-quality single-nucleus data. Our streamlined protocol offers a reproducible, adaptable approach compatible with current and emerging snRNA-seq technologies.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful tool for elucidating cellular diversity in healthy tissues and aberrations in pathological conditions. However, the fundamental requirement for scRNA-seq is the processing of tissues into a homogeneous single-cell suspension1. The isolation of intact cells for transcriptome profiling from certain cell types poses challenges due to their fragility, large size, tight interconnections, and other contributing factors2. Tissues with highly interconnected processes, such as those in the brain, present unique obstacles3. Furthermore, as single-cell transcriptomic experiments are performed on surgical residues from epilepsy surgery following histopathological diagnosis, such analyses are difficult to conduct using fresh tissue4.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) has been introduced as a promising method to overcome these challenges1,5. Despite certain limitations, such as the enrichment of nascent or nuclear-localized transcripts in the nuclear RNA pool, there is a good correlation between the nuclear and whole-cell RNA pools6–8. The use of snRNA-seq offers several advantages, as it can reduce biases in cell populations resulting from cell dissociation, minimize stress-induced transcriptional artifacts, mitigate cell capture bias due to cell size, and the potential to study nascent mRNA. Nuclei isolation can be applied to frozen tissues, which is particularly relevant in human studies, where these samples are often the highest-quality material available9.

The initial and most critical step for successful snRNA-seq experiments lies in obtaining high-quality, debris-free nuclei suspensions. Several methods have been proposed, including extended ultracentrifugation, sucrose or iodixanol gradients, fluorescence-activated sorting, and commercial kits providing pre-formulated buffers and optimized workflows5,10,11. Each approach has advantages and limitations depending on tissue type, quality, and experimental purpose. Commonly used methods, however, may involve long centrifugation times, multiple washing steps, or sorting procedures that can compromise nuclei integrity and reduce yield, especially when working with fragile human brain tissue.

Although new transcriptomic chemistries now enable the use of fixed cells12, an efficient and standardized workflow for obtaining clean, intact nuclei remains an essential prerequisite for generating high-quality single-nucleus data.

Here, we developed a simplified nuclei isolation protocol, originally validated using the 10x Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ LT kit, which we expect to be fully compatible with all currently available chemistries. To validate it, we applied the protocol to biopsies from two patients with mild malformation of cortical development with oligodendroglial hyperplasia and epilepsy (MOGHE) carrying somatic loss-of-funcion (LOF) variants in the SLC35A2 gene.

MOGHE is a malformation of cortical development causing drug-resistant epilepsy, developmental delay, and intellectual disability in pediatric patients13. It exhibits several histopathological characteristics, including clusters of increased oligodendroglial cell density in the white matter and deep cortical layers, augmented oligodendroglial proliferation activity, patchy areas of hypomyelination, and excess of heterotopic neurons in the white matter14.

The mechanism by which LOF variants in SLC35A2 contribute to oligodendroglial and neuronal cortical abnormalities, ultimately leading to seizures, remains to be elucidated. Recently, Galvão and collaborators performed a multiomic profiling of single nuclei from gray and white matter regions of brain lesions from two patients clinically diagnosed with MOGHE but mutation negative after whole-exome and somatic mosaicism analysis15. The authors identified significant alterations in cellular composition, including heterotopic neurons and disease-specific oligodendrocyte populations within the subcortical white matter.

MOGHE-specific oligodendrocytes exhibited upregulation of genes associated with synaptic function and neuron–glia communication, suggesting a potential supportive role in synaptic regulation. Heterotopic neurons showed increased expression of genes related to neuronal migration and WNT signaling, pointing to mechanisms that may underlie their aberrant localization15.

We analyzed genetically confirmed SLC35A2-related MOGHE tissue, focusing specifically on the oligodendroglial lineage, using a reproducible and simplified nuclei isolation workflow that demonstrated consistent performance across multiple samples while avoiding lengthy and disruptive procedures for debris removal. The results we obtained take a step forward towards understanding the contribution of oligodendrocytes to MOGHE.

We received the patients’ brain samples directly from the neurosurgeon in the operating theater and cut them into small pieces (around 20 mg each), froze them immediately in dry ice upon receipt, and stored them at − 80 °C until use. We obtained postmortem brain specimens used as normal controls from the Semmelweis University Brain Tissue Bank of Budapest, Hungary (https://semmelweis.hu/hbtb/). We matched control tissues with patients’ samples by gender and cortical area.

Single nuclei isolation followed the serial filtration protocol described below.

Briefly, we removed human brain biopsies of around 20 mg from − 80 °C storage and minced them into 0.5–1 mm pieces on dry ice with a sterile scalpel blade. After cutting the tissue, we homogenized samples in ice-cold lysis buffer with a glass-on-glass Dounce homogenizer (10 strokes with pestle A, followed by 10 strokes with pestle B). We left samples to lyse in 5 mL of lysis buffer for 5 min. Then, we added 5 mL of wash and resuspension buffer. After lysis, we filtered samples through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged them for 5 min at 500×g. We repeated this step two more times, first using a 37 μm and then a 30 μm strainer.

To evaluate the number and integrity of recovered nuclei, we stained nuclear preparations with 0.4% Trypan Blue and DAPI staining solution and imaged them using an EVOS XL Core microscope (Invitrogen) and a fluorescent microscope (Olympus IX50). We manually counted nuclei with a Bürker counting chamber following standard procedures.

We diluted nuclei suspensions with wash and resuspension buffer to achieve the desired concentration (400 nuclei/µL), targeting ~ 1,000 nuclei per reaction. We performed gel-bead emulsions, cDNA synthesis, and library preparation following the 10x Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ LT v3.1 User Guide (cat. no. PN-1000325). We analyzed libraries using a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent) and quantified them with the Quantifluor ONE dsDNA System (Promega). We sequenced libraries on a NextSeq 500/550 system (Illumina) at a depth of ~ 60 million read pairs per sample.

To increase the number of nuclei for downstream data analysis, we pooled data from the two patients and compared them with data pooled from the two controls. We processed raw sequencing data using the 10x Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline (v10.0). We demultiplexed libraries with cellranger mkfastq command, and aligned reads to the GRCh38 reference genome with cellranger count command. We removed poor-quality nuclei and genes with insufficient read coverage.

We loaded count data into the Scanpy (v1.10) environment, filtering out nuclei with > 30% mitochondrial reads or < 50 genes expressed in fewer than 2 nuclei. We normalized counts using scran’s computeSumFactors function, followed by log-transformation. We mitigated batch effects using the mutual nearest neighbors (MNN) algorithm (using 4,000 highly variable genes) implemented in the mnnpy package.

We performed dimensionality reduction using PCA (50 components) and computed neighborhood graphs with the pp.neighbors function, visualized using UMAP.

We performed cell-cycle analysis using gene lists for S and G2/M phases18 and the tl.score_cell_cycle_genes function. We conducted clustering using the tl.louvain function at different resolutions (0.5, 1, 2); we selected a resolution of 1 for downstream analysis.

We based cluster annotation on canonical marker expression for oligodendrocytes, microglia, excitatory and inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, and OPCs.

We identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in oligodendrocytes using the Scanpy Welch t-test and DESeq2, with thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 1. We performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using Enrichr with Gene Ontology, Reactome, and MSigDB Hallmark databases.

We constructed the PPI network by importing 199 protein-coding DEGs into the STRING database v12.0 with a high-confidence interaction score of 0.700. We defined the network’s modular architecture using the Markov Clustering (MCL) algorithm with an inflation parameter of 3.0, which partitioned the interactome into 23 distinct clusters. We derived the functional identities for these modules from the automated cluster enrichment descriptions provided by the STRING database. We provided detailed information regarding all 23 clusters in Supplementary Table S1.

The protocol effectively reduces cellular debris and allows to recover a high yield of intact and high-quality nuclei suitable for library preparation and sequencing.

The progressive filtration strategy we applied prevents clogging of the finer meshes, limits shear stress, and minimizes debris (Fig. 1B), yielding a clean nuclear suspension without the need for density gradients or Fluorescence Activated Nuclei Sorting (FANS).

Using this approach, from ~ 20 mg of tissue, we obtained an estimated recovery of ~ 1,000,000 nuclei/mL. DAPI staining confirmed high nuclear integrity and yield (Fig. 1B). The resulting workflow, optimized for frozen human cortical samples, is conceptually aligned with—but considerably simpler than—the 10x Genomics ‘demonstrated protocol’ for frozen brain nuclei preparation. Our optimized protocol is straightforward and can be completed in less than 90 min. The isolated nuclei are directly compatible with the 10x Chromium Controller platform.

To assess applicability to human samples, we performed snRNA-seq on nuclei isolated from temporal lobe tissue of two MOGHE patients harboring somatic SLC35A2 variants (p.G282R, 2% mosaicism; and p.F280Tfs10, 23% mosaicism), whose functional impact on SLC35A2 was previously demonstrated by our team19, and compared the results with those obtained from two normal autoptic controls. Bioanalyzer profiles of cDNA before and after library preparation showed the expected fragment sizes and peaks (Fig. 2). Libraries were generated from 4 to 43 ng of total cDNA and sequenced to ~ 60 million read pairs per sample.

After quality control and clustering, we retained 1,427 nuclei for further analysis.

Based on canonical markers, clustering of nuclei from patients and controls revealed seven major cell populations: excitatory neurons, caudal ganglionic eminence (CGE)-derived interneurons, medial ganglionic eminence (MGE)-derived interneurons, oligodendrocytes, OPCs, microglia, and astrocytes (Figs. 3A, B). While we detected these seven populations in both control samples, in neither of the two MOGHE samples, we detected MGE- and CGE-derived interneurons. This is potentially due to their low representation relative to other cell types and the limited capture efficiency of the LT kit. However, we cannot exclude that this absence may reflect technical problems intrinsic to biological features of MOGHE tissue, such as differential nuclei fragility, or a genuine reduction of these specific neuronal subtypes.

As expected, the proportion of oligodendrocytes in MOGHE samples was significantly higher than in controls (Fig. 3C), consistent with histological findings showing increased oligodendrocyte density in deep cortical layers of resected MOGHE tissue.

Differential expression analysis between patients’ and controls’ samples, focused on oligodendrocyte nuclei, revealed 214 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), identified through the integrated use of both Scanpy and DESeq2 (Supplementary Fig. 1). This integrated approach allowed for the identification of both group-specific markers characterizing oligodendrocyte identity in MOGHE and controls, as well as potential pathologically relevant genes with robust statistical significance.

Gene Ontology enrichment emphasized protein trafficking and localization processes, including “Cellular macromolecule localization”, “Protein localization”, and “Establishment of localization in cell”, as well as defects in post-translational maturation and structural assembly, including “Macromolecule modification” and “Protein modification process”.

Consistent with these findings, Reactome pathway analysis (dot plot) confirmed the dysregulation of post-translational protein maturation (“Post-translational protein modification”) and membrane trafficking defects with “Vesicle-mediated transport”. In addition, the analysis pointed to dysregulation in cytoskeletal dynamics and intracellular transport (“Signaling by Rho GTPases”, Miro GTPases and RHOBTB3” and “Rho GTPase cycle”), which are fundamental processes for the structural integrity of the myelin sheath. Notably, several key structural myelin genes, including MBP, PLP1, CNP, and SCD, were among the most significantly up-regulated DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 1). The analysis further highlighted pathways associated with neural precursor migration and oligodendrocyte renewal (“Signaling by ROBO receptors” and “Regulation of expression of SLITs and ROBOs”).

The analysis of upregulated and downregulated Reactome pathways (bar plots) integrated these observations by showing that the up-regulation of Rho GTPase activity is accompanied by an enrichment of terms involved in axonal interaction and developmental signaling, such as “L1CAM interactions” and “Axon guidance”. This activation of the machinery for surface recognition and cytoskeletal remodeling was, however, contrasted by a significant down-regulation of processes essential for functional neuronal support, including “Transmission across chemical synapses”, “Neurotransmitter release cycle”, and “Synaptic plasticity”, and specific pathways involved in “Glutamate binding”, “Activation of AMPA receptors”, and “Trafficking of GluR2-containing AMPA receptors” (Fig. 4). This down-regulation appeared to be associated with a marked decrease in the expression of key sensing and synaptic genes, most notably SNAP25, SYT1, GRIA2, GRIN2B, SLC1A2, and CNTNAP2 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

MSigDB Hallmark analysis suggested four major altered gene sets (Fig. 5). Upregulated: “Reactive oxygen species” and “IL-6/JAK/STAT signaling” (associated with MOGHE), downregulated: “Notch signaling”, and “WNT/β-catenin signaling” (associated with controls).

Of the 214 identified DEGs, 199 (93%) were protein-coding and were included in the PPI analysis. The network exhibited significantly higher interconnectivity than expected by chance (p < 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁶; Fig. 6). While the MCL algorithm partitioned the interactome into 23 distinct modules, we prioritized the characterization of the 10 primary functional clusters containing three or more proteins. This selection criteria ensured that the analysis focused on biologically robust pathways with higher statistical confidence, while smaller clusters and the singleton—representing an isolated signaling event—remained documented in Supplementary Table S1.

Traditional nuclei isolation techniques are often labor-intensive, expensive, and frequently involve nuclei sorting or ultracentrifugation. The protocol we developed streamlines the procedure while maintaining RNA integrity and yield. In addition, the use of standard laboratory reagents and simple filtration steps facilitates the widespread adoption of single-nuclei transcriptomic analyses across laboratories.

We tailored our nuclei isolation protocol for compatibility with droplet-based, single-nuclei RNA-sequencing platforms, optimizing it for efficient nuclei isolation and library preparation from frozen brain cortical tissue.

Using this approach, we investigated how oligodendrocytes—the cell population with markedly increased proliferation in MOGHE—are affected by pathogenic variants in SLC35A2. Our single-nuclei transcriptomic analysis of brain samples from two pediatric MOGHE patients carrying distinct brain-confined mosaic SLC35A2 pathogenic variants revealed, as expected, a significant increase in the proportion of oligodendrocytes compared to controls. This observation aligns with the histological evidence of increased density of oligodendrocytes in the deep cortical layers of MOGHE.

In our small cohort, we identified 214 differentially expressed genes. GSEA of these genes highlighted potential alterations in inflammatory, metabolic, and signaling pathways. STRING network analysis confirmed a high level of connectivity among the differentially expressed genes.

According to the MSigDB analysis, the reactive oxygen species (ROS) gene set and interleukin-6 (IL-6) signaling pathway were among the most significantly enriched pathways in our MOGHE-derived oligodendrocyte samples. ROS gene set increase has been frequently associated with neuroinflammation20. ROS can have several damaging effects on oligodendrocytes because these cells have high metabolic activity and relatively low antioxidant defenses21,22. IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine involved in both neuroprotective and neurodegenerative processes, influencing neuronal and glial development, and modulating responses to injury23. While IL-6 can promote proliferation and differentiation of oligodendrocyte-lineage cells, it can also impair myelination under specific conditions, emphasizing its dual role in neuroinflammatory disorders24. Increased IL-6 signaling can affect oligodendrocytes by multiple inter-linked mechanisms that can alter their survival, differentiation, and myelination capacity25.

Increased neuroinflammation in MOGHE samples could have two possible explanations. Seizures — particularly prolonged or recurrent ones — can directly trigger inflammatory processes in the brain, which can persist long after the seizure ends and contribute to neuronal injury and epileptogenesis26,27. Another possible explanation for the positive correlation we observed between neuroinflammatory pathways and MOGHE samples could be related to SLC35A2 mutation. Altered glycosylation resulting from impaired SLC35A2 activity may rapidly modify the structure and function of glycans, potentially disrupting immune homeostasis, activating microglia, and promoting the release of inflammatory mediators, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammatory responses28. Our data suggest that, in MOGHE lesions, oligodendrocytes may participate in a coordinated biological response to brain injury and inflammation. This hypothesis is consistent with recent evidence indicating that oligodendrocytes and OPCs can sense and react to inflammation and actively contribute to or modulate the immune response in various neurological disorders29.

Our analysis also showed altered SLIT/ROBO-related signaling in MOGHE-derived oligodendrocytes, based on the presence of Reactome terms such as “Signaling by ROBO receptors” and “Regulation of expression of SLITs and ROBOs”. These pathways have been previously implicated in SVZ-derived precursor migration and oligodendrocyte renewal in demyelinating contexts30, suggesting that similar guidance- or repair-related mechanisms may be engaged within dysplastic MOGHE tissue. The enrichment of processes associated with SVZ-derived neural precursor migration and oligodendrocyte renewal observed in MOGHE samples points to an attempt at regenerative response in which neural stem cells migrate, and oligodendrocytes differentiate to repair hypomyelinated brain regions.

Recently, by performing single-nuclei multiomic sequencing on gray and white matter regions of MOGHE lesions from two patients without molecular diagnosis and comparing their results with publicly available control datasets, Galvão and collaborators identified disease-specific oligodendrocyte populations with transcriptional signatures indicating altered maturation and neuron–glia communication15. Although their pathway-level analysis did not highlight SLIT/ROBO signaling, ROBO-family genes do appear among the marker genes of specific oligodendrocyte clusters in their supplementary dataset, indicating that guidance-related pathways may be variably engaged across MOGHE lesions. The data we obtained may extend these observations by correlating altered SLIT/ROBO-associated signaling with SLC35A2 functional anomalies. Altered glycosylation due to SLC35A2 mutations may impair SLIT ligand secretion and destabilize the abundance or localization of ROBO receptors, thereby disrupting SLIT/ROBO signaling and affecting cell migration and guidance31. SLIT2 is expressed by mature oligodendrocytes, where it contributes to axon–glia organization32. Thus, its dysregulation may also disturb axon–oligodendrocyte interactions, which are critical for myelin stability and may, in turn, contribute to neural hyperexcitability and seizure propagation.

A possible defect in the initial interaction between oligodendrocytes and axons is also suggested by Reactome analysis, which identified a significant enrichment of “Signaling by Rho GTPases” and “RHO GTPase cycle”. The analysis of upregulated and downregulated pathways confirmed the significant upregulation of “Signaling by Rho GTPases” and “L1CAM interactions”. L1CAM is essential for the initial adhesion of oligodendrocyte processes to the axon33; however, its upregulation, combined with sustained Rho GTPase activity—known to suppress oligodendrocyte differentiation and inhibit the actin cytoskeleton reorganization required for membrane expansion and axonal wrapping34—suggests that MOGHE oligodendrocytes may remain in a state of persistent surface adhesion, failing to establish stable myelination. This possible failure in oligodendrocytes terminal maturation appears to be associated with a systemic downregulation of the machinery required for functional neuron-glia coupling. In fact, our analysis revealed a marked enrichment of downregulated terms, including “Transmission Across Chemical Synapses”, “Glutamate binding”, “activation of AMPA receptors”, and “Trafficking of GluR2-containing AMPA receptors”. In oligodendrocytes, the activation of AMPA receptors by neuronal glutamate acts as a critical trigger for the final stages of axonal wrapping and sheath consolidation35–37. The downregulation of these pathways may indicate an impairment in sensing neuronal activity, explaining why, despite the upregulation of structural genes such as MBP and PLP1, the oligodendrocytes fail to coordinate the final assembly of the myelin sheath.

The systemic downregulation of the machinery required for functional neuron–glia coupling is also supported by our PPI network analysis, which identified as central hubs the synaptic proteins SNAP25 and SYT1—showing reduced expression in our transcriptomic dataset—two key components of the vesicle fusion machinery involved in neuron–glia coupling and membrane protein trafficking38,39. In line with these results, interactome analysis revealed physical associations between myelin constituent proteins and MAG-mediated RHOA inhibitory signaling, consistent with persistent surface adhesion and impaired transition to stable axonal wrapping40. Oligodendrocytes maturation failure may be further confirmed by the presence of the prefoldin-CCT/TRiC complex (Cluster 3), which is essential for the folding of actin and tubulin required for cytoskeletal remodeling41,42. Finally, disruption of the protein complex responsible for organizing juxtaparanodal potassium channels (KCNA2), which contributes to myelin–axon adhesion43,44, may offer a unified molecular explanation for both the mechanical fragility of the myelin–axon interface and the chronic hyperexcitability observed in MOGHE.

Beyond the structural and sensing defects identified in the mature oligodendrocyte compartments, our data also point toward broader regulatory imbalances. In our small dataset, NOTCH and WNT/β-catenin signaling pathways emerged as more prominent in control oligodendrocytes. NOTCH and WNT/β‑catenin signaling pathways are also regulated by glycosylation, which critically modulates receptor function, ligand interactions, and downstream activity. In the Notch pathway, O‑fucosylation and O‑glucosylation of extracellular EGF-like repeats, as well as Fringe-mediated elongation, control receptor folding, trafficking, and ligand binding, thereby fine-tuning signaling outcomes45. Similarly, WNT/β‑catenin pathway activity is influenced by glycosylation of WNT ligands and Frizzled receptors, which is essential for proper ligand secretion, receptor recognition, and pathway activation46. Elevated NOTCH signaling suppresses OPCs differentiation, maintaining a pool of these cells in the adult brain47. The downregulation of this pathway we observed in MOGHE samples may suggest a disruption of oligodendrocyte homeostasis, potentially affecting the balance between OPC maintenance and oligodendrocyte differentiation. An increased number of oligodendrocytes due to NOTCH signaling downregulation may also contribute to altered myelination and white matter abnormalities in MOGHE. Multiple studies showed that canonical Wnt/β-catenin signalling acts as a negative regulator of OPC differentiation and that forced Wnt activation delays maturation and myelination48,49. Hence, reduced Wnt/β-catenin signalling may favour OPC to mature oligodendrocyte transition.

Our Reactome and GO process analyses also showed significant alterations in Membrane Trafficking, Vesicle-mediated transport, and Protein localization.

Oligodendrocytes utilize multiple pathways to support both myelination and non-myelinating functions. Classical ER-to-Golgi trafficking, mediated by COPII vesicles, directs newly synthesized proteins such as trophic factors to the extracellular space50, while SNARE-dependent exocytosis regulates the release of signaling vesicles essential for oligodendrocyte development and myelination51. Oligodendrocytes also secrete extracellular vesicles, including exosomes derived from multivesicular bodies, which carry proteins, lipids, and RNAs that promote neuron-glia communication, extracellular matrix remodeling, and glial homeostasis52,53. Altered expression of RNAs involved in membrane trafficking and vesicle-mediated transport pathways observed in MOGHE-derived oligodendrocytes suggests reduced oligodendrocyte support to neurons, which may contribute to impaired axonal conduction, synaptic plasticity, and network excitability—all of which are relevant to epilepsy.

Our findings support a model in which altered glycosylation of key pathways—including NOTCH, WNT/β-catenin, SLIT/ROBO, and Rho GTPase signaling, as well as Membrane Trafficking and Vesicle-mediated transport—resulting from altered SLC35A2 function may lead to changes in oligodendrocyte signaling, differentiation, migration, and function. These alterations, coupled with a downregulation of synaptic sensing and signaling pathways— such as AMPA receptor trafficking and synaptic transmission—may underlie key hallmarks of MOGHE, such as increased oligodendrocyte density and disrupted myelination, and may contribute to enhanced seizure susceptibility. Such changes, and in particular recurrent seizures, may also promote microglial activation and astrocytic reactivity, fostering a pro-inflammatory environment that may further exacerbate neuronal hyperexcitability. Consequently, SLC35A2-related glycosylation defects may not only impair oligodendrocyte support of neurons but also trigger or amplify neuroinflammatory cascades, thereby contributing to disrupted myelination and increased seizure susceptibility.

Our study, however, has limitations. The small sample size and the use of low-throughput chemistry limited the detection of rare cell populations, and technical challenges inherent to droplet-based snRNA-seq of human brain tissue are well recognized5,6. Thus, validation in larger cohorts and at the functional level is warranted. Finally, as our analysis focused exclusively on oligodendrocytes, including additional cell types in future studies will be crucial to fully elucidate the cellular interplay underlying MOGHE pathology.
