# Real-world use of the updated refractory epilepsy screening tool for Lennox-Gastaut syndrome.
**Authors:** Wolf SM, Boyce D, Peña P, Piña-Garza JE, Roland JJ, Thomas B, Zogejani D, McGoldrick PE
**Journal:** Epilepsia Open (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/epi4.12952](https://doi.org/10.1002/epi4.12952)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the Refractory Epilepsy Screening Tool for Lennox-Gastaut Syndrome (REST-LGS) for real-world identification of LGS in adults and to develop a scoring system for the tool.

METHODS: A retrospective chart review of adults with drug resistant epilepsy (DRE) and intellectual development disorder (IDD) was conducted by 2 primary care providers blinded to diagnosis. The REST-LGS was designed via the Modified Delphi Consensus and was previously validated. This tool consists of 8 criteria (4 major, 4 minor) considered indicative of LGS. To account for missing data in the earlier validation study and to evaluate applicability in a real-world setting, the REST-LGS was refined to include a scoring system in which major criteria were more heavily weighted than minor criteria, producing categories of "likely" (>11 points), "possible" (8-11 points), and "unlikely" (<8 points) LGS. Statistical analyses were descriptive.

RESULTS: Of the 100 patients included in the analysis, data for slow spike-waves in electroencephalography and seizure onset age - both major REST-LGS criteria - were missing for 46% and 42% of patients, respectively. The majority of patients met 4 of the 8 REST-LGS criteria (cognitive impairment since childhood, 71%; persistent seizures despite a trial of ≥2 antiseizure medications, 65%; seizure onset before the age of 12 years, 57%; ≥2 seizure types, 56%). All 4 major criteria were met in 22 patients (22%). The percentages of patients considered "likely," "possible," or "unlikely" to have LGS were 26%, 30%, and 44%, respectively. Of the 74 patients without a previous LGS diagnosis, 42 (57%) were identified as "possible" or "likely" to have LGS using REST-LGS.

SIGNIFICANCE: In this analysis, the validated REST-LGS was evaluated in a real-world setting. The majority of previously undiagnosed patients were identified via REST-LGS as "possible" or "likely" to have LGS. Extensive missing data highlights challenges of LGS diagnosis in adults.

PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY: There is a need to identify adult patients with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome (LGS) so they can receive appropriate treatment. The Refractory Epilepsy Screening Tool for LGS (REST-LGS) questionnaire was designed by experts to identify whether patients with seizures that are not controlled by medications may have LGS. In this study, 2 physicians completed the REST-LGS using charts for 100 patients who experience seizures not controlled by medications. Of the patients who were previously diagnosed as not having LGS, the majority were "likely" or "possible" to have LGS based on the REST-LGS; therefore, the REST-LGS can identify patients for further evaluation.

Key points The Refractory Epilepsy Screening Tool for Lennox Gastaut syndrome (REST‐LGS) can be used to screen patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy in the clinical setting. Of the 100 patients in this retrospective analysis, the majority (56–71%) met 4 of 8 REST‐LGS criteria, and all 4 major REST‐LGS criteria were met in 22 patients (22%). The percentages of patients considered “likely,” “possible,” or “unlikely” to have LGS via the REST‐LGS were 26%, 30%, and 44%, respectively. Of the 74 patients without a previous diagnosis of LGS, 42 (57%) were found to be “possible” or “likely” to have LGS utilizing the REST‐LGS. Data for slow spike–waves in electroencephalography and seizure onset age – both major REST‐LGS criteria – were missing for 46% and 42% of patients, respectively.

Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS) is an epileptic encephalopathy that begins in childhood and has a peak onset between the ages of 3 and 5 years. A triad of features are commonly used for an LGS diagnosis: childhood onset of multiple seizure types, intellectual disability, and electroencephalographic (EEG) abnormalities. 1 Recent guidelines from the International League Against Epilepsy indicate that LGS can be distinguished by the presence of tonic seizures early in the disease and slow spike‐wave (SSW) complexes and generalized paroxysmal fast activity in sleep on EEG. 2 The degree of intellectual disability may range from mild to severe and can include learning disabilities and significant developmental delays, 3 with disabilities that can worsen over time. 4 Although childhood onset is a hallmark trait of LGS, the disease is not always recognized and diagnosed during childhood. 5

LGS is especially difficult to identify in adults due to several factors. 6 First, the disease evolves over time, and some of the classic symptoms evident in childhood may no longer be present in adulthood. 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 For example, the characteristic multiple seizure types (tonic, atonic, generalized tonic–clonic, myoclonic, atypical absence, and focal [with or without bilateral movement]) and the EEG pattern (generalized 1.5–2.5 Hz SSW complexes) may no longer be seen in adult LGS. 7 , 9 , 10 Second, details of patients' medical histories may be lost during transfer of pediatric testing results to adult health care providers. 11 Third, patients may no longer tolerate EEG testing. 12 , 13 Finally, comorbid conditions, such as behavioral difficulties, cognitive impairment, and sleep disturbances, can develop in adolescence and adulthood, further complicating diagnosis and delaying treatment. 11 , 14 For these reasons, LGS frequently remains undiagnosed in adulthood, and without appropriate diagnosis, these patients may not receive timely and optimal care. Early intervention, including treatments specifically tailored for adults, is critically important for patients with LGS and has been shown to significantly improve outcomes in this population. 15 , 16

There is a substantial unmet need for methods to identify LGS in adult patients with treatment‐resistant epilepsy. For healthcare professionals with varying degrees of experience in diagnosing patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE), a method to accurately identify LGS could help provide specialized patient care, leading to improved outcomes. To that end, the Refractory Epilepsy Screening Tool for LGS (REST‐LGS) was previously developed by a group of experts who care for patients with DRE and intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDDs). 13 In brief, the REST‐LGS consists of 8 criteria: 4 major symptoms associated with LGS (e.g., ≥2 seizure types) as well as 4 minor features considered indicative of LGS and treatment‐resistant epilepsy (eg, evidence of seizure‐related helmet use or head/face injury). The REST‐LGS was validated in a retrospective study conducted at 2 medical centers where it demonstrated detection of multiple features of LGS, moderate to very good inter‐rater agreement on a majority of criteria (indicating it could be used effectively by both experts and nonexperts), ease of use in a clinical setting, and an ability to be populated with data across various medical record formats. 13 Interestingly, the study also found that the majority of patients with confirmed LGS met 3 major criteria and 2 to 3 minor criteria. Although these findings indicate the potentially wide applicability of the REST‐LGS for the identification of adults with LGS, and initial validation has been done, further testing in the real‐world setting is needed to further confirm the applicability of this tool. Therefore, we conducted this retrospective chart review to evaluate the REST‐LGS in the real‐world identification of potential LGS in adults with DRE and IDDs and to develop a scoring system that will simplify use of the tool.

Adult patients with DRE and IDD who lived in a residential care facility and received care there or received care at a facility for persons with IDD and had received care there for more than 5 years, were included in the analysis. Patients were either living in and receiving care at Richmond Children's Center, Yonkers, NY or were living in personal residences or group homes and receiving care at the Westchester Institute for Human Development, Valhalla, NY. DRE was defined as failure of adequate trials of 2 tolerated, appropriately chosen and used antiseizure medication schedules (whether as monotherapies or in combination) to achieve sustained seizure freedom, per International League Against Epilepsy criteria. 17

Approval from ethical standards committees to conduct this study was obtained from New York Medical College. All data were deidentified, and none of the 18 Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act identifiers were revealed. Retrospective chart reviews were performed by 2 primary care providers that routinely care for persons with refractory epilepsy and IDD and were blinded to the prior diagnoses. Each reviewer performed retrospective chart reviews for 50 patients (100 total with no overlap between reviewers). The reviewers received anonymized patient chart notes from the last 3 visits and used the REST‐LGS case report form (CRF) to identify which, if any, of the criteria the patients met. In addition to chart review, information related to patient and caregiver demographics, medical and educational history, and concomitant medications were gathered for each participant. Study data were collected on paper CRFs, which were entered directly into a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database. Each independently completed CRF for each patient with DRE was analyzed to determine the validity of the REST‐LGS. After chart review, patient information was unblinded to reveal whether they had a previous diagnosis of LGS.

The REST‐LGS was designed using the Modified Delphi Consensus approach, a rigorous method in which available scientific evidence and collective judgment of a group of experts (including epileptologists, advanced practice nurses trained in epilepsy, neurology residents, and scientists) were combined to develop the screening tool. 13 The REST‐LGS consists of 8 criteria – 4 major and 4 minor – that are indicative of LGS, based on panelists' ratings of the likelihood of each item being specifically associated with LGS. The 4 major criteria consist of ≥2 seizure types, seizure onset at <12 years of age, a history of SSW (<2.5 Hz) on EEG, and cognitive impairment since childhood (may include past or current learning difficulties, a history of special education, autism, intellectual disabilities, or developmental delays). The 4 minor criteria include persistent seizures despite trial of ≥2 antiseizure medications; a history of vagal nerves stimulation, ketogenic diet therapy, or epilepsy surgery; evidence of seizure‐related helmet use or head/face injury; and a history of other EEG abnormalities (multifocal spikes, symptomatic generalized discharges, generalized periods of attenuation of background/electrodecrement, or paroxysmal fast activity). A “yes” answer for all 4 major criteria is considered “Definite LGS”. 13 The majority of patients with confirmed LGS in the original REST‐LGS validation study exhibited 3 major criteria and 2 to 3 minor criteria, suggesting that meeting these thresholds would be indicative of LGS.

In this real‐world study, a high prevalence of missing EEG data was observed. To address this potential limitation, the screen was further refined to include a scoring system (Figure 1) with greater weight and a score of 3 given to the “major” criteria and a value of 1 given to the “minor” criteria. Summing the individual criteria scores produces the following 3 categories: “likely LGS” (>11 total score), “possible LGS” (8–11 total score), or “unlikely LGS” (<8 total score).

Statistical analyses performed were descriptive only and included frequencies, percentages, means, and medians. Given the nature of this study, sample size was not determined based on statistical power to test a formal hypothesis or to detect potential differences between reviewers. Based on the previously published study in which a sample of 100 was determined to be statistically powered to detect differences between reviewers, 13 100 patients were targeted for analysis in this study as well. As each of the 2 reviewers evaluated a unique 50‐patient set (no overlap), inter‐rater reliability in using REST‐LGS could not be assessed in this study.

Of the 100 patients included in the analysis, 54 (54%) were male. No information was available on the history of SSW in EEG in 46% of patients and the age at seizure onset in 42% of patients (Table 1; Figure 2). The chart reviewers reported a “yes” response for most patients for 4 of the 8 REST‐LGS criteria (cognitive impairment since childhood, 71 patients [71%]; persistent seizures despite a trial of 2 or more antiseizure medications, 65 patients [65%]; seizure onset before the age of 12 years, 57 patients [57%]; two or more seizure types, 56 patients [56%]) (Table 1). More “yes” than “no” responses were also reported for history of EEG with generalized SSW discharges (“yes” in 36 patients [36%]; “no” in 18 patients [18%]) and for EEG abnormalities (“yes” in 36 patients [36%]; “no” in 15 patients [15%]). Most patients were reported not to have a history of vagal nerve stimulation, ketogenic diet, or epilepsy surgery (59 patients [59%]), and more “no” than “yes” responses were reported for evidence of seizure‐related helmet use or head/face injuries (“no” in 46 patients [46%]; “yes” in 15 patients [15%]). There was evidence that vagal nerve stimulation was considered but not administered in two patients; a third patient had failed vagal nerve stimulation. High rates of “unavailable” data were reported for EEG abnormalities (49%), history of EEG with generalized SSW discharges (46%), seizure onset before the age of 12 years (42%), evidence of seizure‐related helmet use or head/face injuries (39%), history of vagal nerve stimulator, ketogenic diet, or epilepsy surgery (31%), and cognitive impairment since childhood (28%).

Most patients (90%) were found to exhibit at least 1 of the 8 criteria (Figure 3A). In 10 patients (10%), there were 0 “yes” responses, and in 1 patient (1%), all 8 criteria for LGS were met. Zero major criteria were met in 12 patients (12%), and all 4 major criteria were met in 22 patients (22%) (Figure 3B). Twenty‐nine patients (29%) exhibited 0 minor criteria, and all 4 minor criteria were met in 3 patients (3%) (Figure 3C).

Patients with 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 major criteria were further stratified by the number of minor criteria exhibited (Figure 4). Of note, of the 19 patients that exhibited 3 major criteria, 11 (58%) also exhibited 2 or more minor criteria (i.e., potential LGS per the original validation study). Additionally, of these 19 patients with “yes” responses to 3 major criteria, 7 patients (37%) had unavailable data for the history of EEG with generalized SSW discharges (<2.5 Hz) criteria.

Using the scoring system developed to mitigate the effects of missing data, the median (range) total score was 8 (0–16). Forty‐four percent of patients were considered “unlikely” to have LGS (<8 total score), 30% of patients were considered “possible” to have LGS (8–11 total score), and 26% of patients were considered “likely” to have LGS (>11 total score).

After unblinding, it was revealed that 26 patients (26%) had previously been diagnosed with LGS, and 74 patients (74%) were not previously diagnosed with LGS (Figure 5). Of the 26 patients previously diagnosed with LGS, 14 (54%) were found to be “possible” or “likely” to have LGS, and 12 (46%) were considered “unlikely” to have LGS using the REST‐LGS assessment.

Of the 74 patients that did not have a previous diagnosis of LGS, 42 (57%) were found to be “possible” or “likely” to have LGS utilizing the REST‐LGS assessment, while 32 (43%) were considered “unlikely” to have LGS.

There is a need for effective tools to screen for LGS and improve access to care. In this retrospective chart review of 100 patients with DRE, the previously developed REST‐LGS was evaluated in a real‐world setting. Importantly, the majority of patients who had not been previously diagnosed with LGS were identified as “likely” or “possible” to have LGS using REST‐LGS, highlighting the potential of this screening tool to identify patients with LGS who may benefit from further diagnostic evaluation at specialized epilepsy centers. However, extensive missing data, which may be due to medical records that were incomplete or lost, or ultimately, did not follow the patient into adulthood highlights the challenges of diagnosing LGS in adults.

Missing EEG data were the most commonly missing data (both the EEG abnormalities and the history of EEG with generalized SSW discharges [<2.5 Hz] criteria). Though routine EEGs are indicated for patients with seizures, 18 EEG testing can be difficult to tolerate in patients with developmental difficulties; thus, inability to perform with this test is common. 19 In addition, though an abnormal EEG is required for the classical diagnosis of LGS, 20 missing pediatric EEG data in adults with IDD or DRE cannot simply be replaced by new EEG readings because of the evolving nature of LGS as patients age, such as the decreased presence of EEG abnormalities. 7 Expansion of diagnostic criteria to include other minor signs/symptoms, as included in this REST‐LGS screen, can help identify adults with LGS who were previously undiagnosed and for whom a pediatric EEG analysis was not performed.

Other criteria that were often missing included seizure onset before the age of 12, evidence of seizure‐related helmet use or head/face injuries, and cognitive impairment. Although some of these criteria are observable during an in‐person evaluation, they may be difficult to ascertain through a retrospective chart review, particularly for an adult patient who had incomplete records from childhood and was not present for a complete, in‐person evaluation or where injuries and helmet use may not be noted as part of the visit record.

Including minor criteria in the REST‐LGS scoring system may improve the identification of patients with undiagnosed LGS that would otherwise be missed. There were 19 participants in this study that had 3 out of the 4 major criteria and thus did not meet the 4‐item definition of LGS in the previous REST‐LGS study. However, of those 19 patients, 11 (58%) also presented with at least 2 of the minor criteria, thus receiving the designation “likely” LGS (a total score >11). These patients can now be identified with REST‐LGS and referred for specialized care. There may also be advantages in the weighted scoring system developed in the current study versus the definition of potential LGS used in the original validation study (3 major criteria plus at least 2 minor criteria). Using the previous definition, 15 of 26 people with a previous LGS diagnosis would have been potentially missed, whereas only 12 were considered as “unlikely” LGS per the weighted scoring system.

In addition to missing data or patients not meeting 4 major criteria for LGS, barriers to a timely initial diagnosis may have included lack of access/transportation, distance to an epilepsy center, insufficient staff to accompany them if they were residing in a residential facility (potentially exacerbated by the COVID‐19 pandemic), behavioral issues, or poor communication at the time of visit. Providers for these adult patients may be unfamiliar with the criteria for an LGS diagnosis or may not know that LGS continues into adulthood. Treatment complacency could also be an issue, particularly for patients not seeing a neurologist or specialist regularly. Prioritizing these patients by utilizing the REST‐LGS tool can encourage resource allocation to alleviate these issues and connect patients with appropriate care. A formal LGS diagnosis can help connect patients with an LGS advocacy community as well as a personalized, symptom‐guided treatment regimen that prioritizes seizure reduction, a critical need in this population, particularly as patients with LGS exhibit seizure types that can put them at risk for sudden unexpected death in epilepsy. 21 Due to the treatment‐resistant nature of seizures associated with LGS, many patients receive multiple antiseizure medications, which can increase the risk of adverse events and drug–drug interactions. An accurate LGS diagnosis can aid in the selection of effective, approved treatments, potentially reducing polypharmacy. By reducing seizure burden, appropriate LGS treatments can improve patient quality of life, allowing for participation in activities of daily living and socialization.

The study was limited by a small sample size (accompanied with descriptive statistics only), the inclusion of only the last 3 entries from patients' charts in the evaluation, and retrospective design. Inter‐rater validity could not be assessed due to the nature of the study, in which two independent raters each analyzed 50 patient records. Additionally, the analyses relied on patient records only, and no consultation with patients or caregivers was conducted during the screening phase. Depending on the thoroughness of a patient's medical records and the format of the electronic health records (EHRs) attained, certain criteria of the REST‐LGS may not be readily available, necessitating a more detailed review of patient notes to populate the results. The lack of widespread use of standardized EHRs 22 can make this task more difficult, and as such, future widespread use of standardized EHRs may improve the amount and quality of available data and the efficiency with which it is accessed when utilizing the REST‐LGS screen. There is a need for improved quality and reliability of real‐world evidence under the 21st Century Cures Act, a US law that is designed to accelerate the development of treatments and medical products for patients. 23 Another limitation of this study is that, although all individuals identified as “likely” or “possible” to have LGS in this study were referred for further diagnostic evaluation at a specialized epilepsy center, patient follow‐up data were not available at the time of publication. As such, validation of whether “likely” or “possible” REST‐LGS scores would result in an eventual confirmed LGS diagnosis remains unclear and needs further validation.

Proper diagnosis of LGS is essential to pair patients with an appropriate and tailored treatment regimen 10 , 24 and can improve outcomes for this patient group. REST‐LGS can enable the user to identify potential LGS in previously undiagnosed patients with DRE, thus opening the door to specialized care. Further education is needed to promote the use of novel screening tools among physicians, nurse practitioners, and physician associates who care for these patients to identify adult patients with undiagnosed LGS. This includes neurology specialists as well as those in other medical specialties, including internal medicine, pediatrics, and family practice. Other future considerations include addressing a knowledge deficit for providers who may be unaware of recent LGS treatments, faster identification of LGS and access to epilepsy centers, an improved referral process, and improved understanding regarding treatment titration and interactions.
