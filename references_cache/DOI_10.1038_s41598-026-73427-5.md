# A randomized crossover trial with weighted blankets shows improvement in behavior and sleep outcomes in adults with Prader–Willi syndrome
**Authors:** Rocío Pareja, Alex Moreno-Armengol, Susanna Esteba-Castillo, Laura Vigil, Laura Blanco-Hinojo, Ramon Coronas, Olga Giménez-Palop, Joan Deus, Raquel Corripio, Assumpta Caixàs
**Journal:** Scientific Reports (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-026-73427-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-73427-5)

## Content

Abstract

                    Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS) is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by hypotonia, hyperphagia, challenging behaviors, sleep abnormalities, and altered body composition. Non-pharmacological interventions targeting sensory modulation may represent a safe therapeutic strategy. Deep proprioceptive stimulation (DPS), delivered via weighted blankets, has shown anxiolytic and sleep-modulating effects in other neurogenetic and psychiatric populations; however, its efficacy has not been systematically evaluated in PWS. To evaluate the effects of DPS using weighted blankets on sleep, behavior, hyperphagia, and body composition in adults with PWS. In this randomized, crossover trial, 40 adults with genetically confirmed PWS underwent two 14-day intervention periods using either a weighted proprioceptive blanket or a visually identical placebo blanket, separated by a 4-week washout period. Behavioral outcomes (including anxiety, skin-picking, and hyperphagia scales), actigraphy-derived sleep and activity metrics, and body composition measured via bioelectrical impedance analysis were assessed. Use of the weighted blanket was associated with significant improvements in anxiety, hyperphagia and self-injurious skin-picking behaviors (
                    p
                     < 0.05). Objective sleep efficiency improved, accompanied by reductions in nocturnal awakenings and daytime somnolence. Actigraphy showed reduced percentage of sedentary time and increased percentage of moderate physical activity, with concomitant increases in fat-free and skeletal muscle mass following weighted blanket use. No adverse events were reported. DPS via weighted blankets is a safe, well-tolerated, and effective non-pharmacological intervention for behavioral regulation, sleep efficiency, and body composition in adults with PWS. These findings support the integration of DPS as a complementary therapeutic tool in the multidisciplinary management of PWS.

Introduction
Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS) is a rare, complex neurodevelopmental disorder caused by the loss of expression
of paternally inherited genes in the chromosome 15q11–q13 region. The underlying genetic mechanisms include
paternal deletions (~ 70%), maternal uniparental disomy (~ 25–30%), and imprinting center defects (rare)1,2.
Clinically, PWS follows a characteristic developmental trajectory. The neonatal and early infancy stages are
dominated by severe hypotonia, poor sucking, feeding difficulties, delayed motor development and growth retar-
dation. During early childhood, hyperphagia emerges—typically between 2 and 4 years of age—leading to rapid
weight gain and severe obesity if not strictly controlled. Adolescence and adulthood are marked by persistent
hyperphagia, endocrine abnormalities (such as hypogonadism), mild to moderate intellectual disability, and
prominent behavioral disturbances such as rigidity, emotional lability, compulsivity, and self-injurious behaviors,
particularly skin picking3–5.
Body composition in PWS is distinct from that observed in individuals with primary obesity. Patients exhibit
a disproportionately high fat mass and markedly reduced lean and skeletal muscle mass, even when matched for
age, sex, and body mass index. This unfavorable composition contributes to reduced basal energy expenditure,
physical inactivity, and further metabolic vulnerability6. While growth hormone (GH) treatment improves body
composition, function and cognition7,8, additional non-pharmacological strategies are warranted to address per -
sistent metabolic challenges.
Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent in PWS and include excessive daytime sleepiness, obstructive and
central sleep apnea, altered sleep architecture, and circadian rhythm dysregulation. These disturbances further
exacerbate behavioral dysregulation, cognitive impairment, and caregiver burden9,10.
Deep proprioceptive stimulation (DPS) is a sensory-based intervention that delivers sustained deep pressure
input, modulating autonomic nervous system activity and arousal levels. Weighted blankets are a commonly
used DPS modality, grounded in Ayres’ sensory integration theory and supported by early experimental work
by Grandin in autism spectrum conditions. DPS is hypothesized to enhance parasympathetic activity, regulate
serotonergic neurotransmission, and exert anxiolytic effects analogous to pharmacological agents, but without
systemic adverse effects11,12.
Although DPS has demonstrated benefits for sleep and anxiety in other psychiatric and neurodevelopmental
populations, no studies to date have systematically examined its effects in individuals with PWS.
We hypothesized that DPS delivered through weighted blankets would improve sleep quality, reduce anxiety
and maladaptive behaviors, attenuate hyperphagia and self-injurious behaviors, and positively influence physical
activity and body composition in adults with PWS13.

Study design
This was a randomized, longitudinal crossover clinical trial with repeated measures. Participants were random -
ized in a 1:1 ratio to one of two sequences: weighted blanket followed by placebo (sequence 1, WB/PB) or pla -
cebo followed by weighted blanket (sequence 2, PB/WB). Each sequence consisted of two 14-day intervention
periods separated by a 4-week washout period to minimize carryover effects (Fig. 1).
All participants completed the following assessments:
 ● Screening and training visit (V-1) to ensure protocol familiarity and tolerance.
 ● Baseline assessments at the start of each phase (V0 and V3).
 ● End-of-intervention visits after each treatment period (V2 and V5).
 ● Interim safety telephone follow-ups (V1 and V4) to monitor adherence and potential adverse events. Adverse
events were defined as any untoward physical or behavioural change temporally associated with blanket use
(e.g., skin irritation, respiratory discomfort, worsening of sleep or behavioural symptoms) and were ascer -
tained non-systematically through caregiver report during interim telephone follow-ups (V1, V4) and at each
in-person visit.
No changes were made to the trial protocol, eligibility criteria, interventions, or prespecified outcomes after the
trial commenced.
Ethical considerations and trial registration
The study protocol was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (Comitè d’Ètica d’Investigació amb
Medicaments, CEIM) of Hospital Universitari Parc Taulí (ref. 2021/3016). The trial was prospectively registered
at ClinicalTrials.gov (TRN: NCT05044000, Registration date: 14 September 2021). The study was conducted
in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guidelines. The full trial
protocol and statistical analysis plan are available from the corresponding authors upon reasonable request.
Written informed consent was obtained from all legal guardians. Additionally, all participants provided written
or verbal assent after being informed alongside their parents or caregivers, with the study procedures adapted to
their cognitive level to ensure voluntary participation.
Recruitment took place between 10 February 2022 and 14 October 2024; the last participant completed follow-
up on 10 December 2024.

Intervention
Weighted blankets (Protac Ball Blanket®; Protac, Denmark) weighing either 7–10 kg were selected to approxi -
mate 10% of the participant’s body weight. The placebo consisted of a visually identical duvet weighing approxi-
mately 1 kg. To maintain visual similarity between conditions, both the active and placebo blankets were fitted
with identical covers, maintaining a consistent external appearance throughout the trial.
The study followed a randomized crossover design where all participants received both the WB intervention
and the PB condition or vice versa. The randomisation sequence was generated by a statistician independent of
outcome assessment using R software and was stratified by body weight (≤ 70 kg and > 70 kg) using permuted
block randomization to ensure balance between sequences within each stratum. Allocation concealment was
achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared in advance by personnel independent
of recruitment and enrolment. Participants were enrolled and assessed for eligibility by the clinical study team at
Hospital Universitari Parc Taulí. Sequence assignment was performed by an independent statician, who released
allocation only after enrolment, ensuring that clinical staff had no access to the sequence in advance.
During the WB condition, the blanket weight was adjusted according to participants’ body weight: participants
weighing ≤ 70 kg received a 7-kg blanket, and those weighing > 70 kg received a 10-kg blanket. Conversely,
the PB condition consisted of an approximately 1-kg blanket for all participants. A 4-week washout period was
included between intervention periods to minimize potential carryover effects. Both interventions were used dur-
ing full sleep time.
One week prior to the start of the intervention, participants completed a graded tolerance protocol (1–2 nights)
to familiarize them with the WB proprioceptive materials and ensure sensory comfort. This protocol consisted in
gradually increasing the area of the body covered by the WB, according to the participant tolerance, until achiev-
ing full sleep time tolerance of WB covering from feet to shoulders.
Outcome measures
Primary outcome: behavioral and psychological assessment
To evaluate the multi-faceted behavioral phenotype of PWS, primary outcomes were assessed using validated
scales. Hyperphagia-related behaviors were quantified using the Hyperphagia Questionnaire for Clinical Tri -
als (HQ-CT)14,15. The frequency and severity of skin-picking and other self-injurious behaviors were measured
via the Scale for the Assessment of Self-Injurious Behaviors (SRCA) 16. Broader behavioral disturbances were
captured through the Aberrant Behavior Checklist – Community (ABC-C) 17, while anxiety symptomatology
was screened using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) 18. All psychological instruments were administered by
a psychiatrist and a psychologist to the participants’ primary caregivers to ensure data reliability and minimize
reporting bias. Both professionals were PWS experts and blinded with regard to the intervention sequence.
Primary outcome: sleep and circadian metrics
Subjective sleep assessments were conducted using a comprehensive battery of questionnaires: the Pittsburgh
Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) 19, the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) 20, adapted for populations with intellectual
disabilities, the Composite Sleep Score (CSS)21, and the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI)22.
Objective sleep architecture and activity patterns were monitored via wrist-worn actigraphy (wGT3X-BT;
ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, FL, USA). Participants were instructed to wear the device on their non-dominant
wrist continuously for each 14-day intervention period. Key metrics extracted from the actigraphic data included
total sleep time (TST), sleep onset latency (SOL), sleep efficiency (SE), and frequency of nocturnal awakenings.
Prior to the intervention, a single-night home polysomnography (WatchPAT ® 300; Itamar Medical, Caesarea,
Israel) was performed to establish a respiratory baseline and screen for sleep-disordered breathing.

Secondary outcome: physical activity
Physical activity was also measured via wrist-worn actigraphy (wGT3X-BT; ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, FL,
USA), following the same wear protocol as for sleep assessment. Measurements obtained included total meta -
bolic rate, total sedentary time, and total moderate activity time.
Secondary outcome: anthropometry and body composition
Changes in physical parameters were assessed using multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA)
with the Inbody 770 S system (Microcaya, Bilbao, Spain). To maintain high measurement precision, assessments
were performed under standardized conditions (fasting, empty bladder, and consistent time of day). The variables
of interest included total body weight, Body Mass Index (BMI), fat mass (FM), fat-free mass (FFM), and skeletal
muscle mass (SMM).
Statistical analysis
No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample of 40 participants corresponds to the full
cohort of adults with genetically confirmed PWS followed at this national reference centre who met eligibility
criteria during the recruitment period, an approach commonly adopted in rare-disease trials where the target
population is inherently limited. No formal interim efficacy analyses or statistical stopping guidelines were pre -
specified. Safety was monitored on an ongoing basis through interim telephone follow-ups (V1, V4), with no
prespecified stopping rule linked to these checks.
Data were analyzed using SAS v9.4. The normality of the distribution for all variables was assessed using the
Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median and inter-
quartile range (IQR), as appropriate.
For behavioral variables, subjective sleep questionnaires, and body composition variables, all measured only
before and after each intervention, treatment response was defined as change from the corresponding baseline,
calculated as post-intervention minus pre-intervention. Changes were first assessed within each intervention con-
dition, and differences between WB and PB effects were then tested by comparing paired intervention change
scores. For actigraphy data, full intervention period means were compared directly between WB and PB treat -
ment conditions.
Parametric paired-samples t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used, as appropriate, depending on the
distribution of the corresponding change scores or paired differences. To test period, sequence and carryover
effects, repeated-measures models were used with intervention as the within-subject factor and order (WB first or
PB first) as the between-subject factor. Baseline values were additionally compared between periods within each
allocation group and between allocation groups, using paired and two-sample t-tests, respectively. Intervention
effects were also compared between sequences within the first period alone.
As an exploratory analysis, participants were classified according to the presence or absence of an anxiety
disorder diagnosis, and the difference between WB and PB effects on the BAI scores was compared between
subgroups. Participants were also classified according to genetic subtype (deletion versus non-deletion), and the
treatment effect was compared between subtypes for all outcomes.
Analyses were performed using available paired data for each comparison. Statistical significance was set at a
two-tailed p < 0.05. P values for all the between-condition contrasts were adjusted for multiple comparisons with
the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure (FDR < 0.05).

Patients and caregivers were not involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this trial. However, two patient

Results
Behavioral outcomes
WB use resulted in a significant reduction in anxiety scores (BAI), whereas no significant changes were observed
during the PB phase. When participants were classified according to the presence ( n = 21) or absence (n = 19) of
an anxiety disorder diagnosis, this reduction was significant only in the former; however, the effect of the inter -
vention did not differ significantly between subgroups (Supplementary Table S1). Hyperphagia scores (HQ-CT)
decreased significantly following WB use and differed significantly between interventions. Self-injurious skin-
picking behaviors (SRCA) improved significantly in terms of frequency and intensity, predominantly during the
WB phase (Table 2).
Significant improvements were also observed in multiple ABC-C domains, including agitation, lethargy, ste -
reotypies, hyperactivity, and inappropriate speech; during WB use. In contrast, PB exposure did not yield compa-
rable improvements and was associated with a slight increase in hyperactivity (Table 2).
Sleep outcomes
Subjective sleep quality scores did not significantly change across interventions, except for daytime sleepiness
that decreased significantly following WB use, with no change during PB exposure (Table 3).
Objective sleep outcomes also improved, with higher sleep efficiency, reduced wake after sleep onset, and
fewer nocturnal awakenings during the WB condition compared to PB (Table 4).
Physical activity and body composition
WB use was associated with significant improvements in physical activity distribution and body composition.
Total metabolic equivalent rate was higher during the WB condition compared to PB. While absolute sedentary
time did not differ significantly between conditions, sedentary time expressed as a percentage was significantly
lower during the WB intervention prior to correction for multiple comparisons ( p = 0.033), although this did not
survive correction (p = 0.058). Similarly, moderate activity did not show significant differences in absolute min -
utes but was significantly higher during WB intervention when expressed as a percentage (Table 4).
Physical activity changes reported by actigraphy were accompanied by significant increases in fat-free mass
and skeletal muscle mass, without significant changes in body weight, BMI, waist perimeter or fat mass percent-
age (Table 5).
PWS genetic subtype comparisons
Across the behavioral, sleep, physical activity, and body composition outcomes, exploratory analyses by genetic
subtype (deletion, n = 26; non-deletion, n = 14) showed no significant between-group difference in the treatment
effect; where the effect was significant, it was in the same direction in both subtypes (Supplementary Tables
S2-S5).

Discussion
This study provides the first systematic evidence that deep proprioceptive stimulation delivered via weighted
blankets may confer clinically meaningful benefits in adults with PWS in terms of behavior, sleep efficiency,
physical activity, and body composition.
Deep proprioception is the sense that informs the organism about the position of its muscles and joints and
can be defined as the ability to perceive the relative position of adjacent body parts23. It plays a fundamental role
in the individual’s relationship with space by regulating movement direction and enabling automatic responses
such as balance control, bilateral coordination, and postural stability 24. Deep proprioception also contributes to
maintaining an appropriate level of central nervous system alertness and influences emotional development and
behavior24.
The design of proprioceptive stimulation blankets, also known as weighted blankets, is based on A. Jean Ayres’
sensory integration theory and her work with children with learning disabilities25–27. This theory describes a neu-
rological process through which sensory information from the body and the environment is organized, allowing
the individual to use the body effectively within the surrounding environment13,28.
In children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), Olson et al. 29 reported that 92.2% of therapists from the
American Occupational Therapy Association recommended weighted vests, and 70% observed improvements
in attention during tasks performed while wearing them. Since the underlying mechanism of action, modulat -
ing autonomic arousal, is comparable between weighted vests and weighted blankets, these findings support
the rationale for extending deep proprioceptive stimulation approaches to other neurodevelopmental conditions.
Likewise, evidence from psychiatric and psychosocial settings suggests that weighted blankets may reduce the
need for caregiver assistance and healthcare services, while also alleviating depressive and anxiety symptoms,
thereby representing a potentially useful non-pharmacological intervention for individuals with specific sensory
regulation needs30.
As the behavioral phenotype of PWS shares several neurobehavioral features with ASD, including anxiety-
related self-injurious behaviors 31,32, the reductions observed in the present study in anxiety and maladaptive
behaviors are consistent with DPS-mediated modulation of autonomic arousal and align with previous findings
in ASD and psychiatric populations12,33. This evidence supports the biological plausibility of DPS efficacy in the
PWS population. In this sense, our results showed a significant improvement in anxiety after the intervention with
WB, with a mean reduction in BAI of 2.19 points, whereas virtually no change was observed after PB exposure.
Anxiety is a central driver of hyperphagia, compulsivity, and self-injurious behaviors in PWS 34; therefore, its
attenuation likely underlies the broad behavioral improvements observed in our cohort, despite the lack of statis-
tically significant treatment effect. This potential anxiety reduction was reflected not only in food-related behav-
iors, reflected by a mean reduction of 4.59 points in the Hyperphagia Questionnaire for Clinical Trials (HQ-CT)
after WB, but also in the reduced frequency and intensity of skin-picking and other characteristic maladaptive
behaviors (Table 2).
Sleeping disorders in PWS have traditionally focused on the well-established association with central sleep
apnea and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Concerns regarding OSA arise early, often from the time of diag -
nosis, because profound hypotonia, progressive weight gain, and tonsillar hypertrophy may exacerbate airway
obstruction, particularly after initiation of growth hormone therapy10. However, clinical experience and growing
evidence indicate that additional sleep disturbances are highly prevalent in PWS, including excessive daytime
sleepiness, narcolepsy-like and cataplexy-like phenotypes, and insomnia, all of which may significantly impact
behavioral functioning35. In the present study, these conditions were either excluded or adequately controlled
before participant enrollment.
In this context, the sleep-related improvements observed after DPS are also biologically plausible. Objec -
tive actigraphy demonstrated a significant improvement in sleep efficiency during WB use, despite this effect
not being fully reflected in questionnaire-based assessments. Moreover, sleep fragmentation and arousals were

significantly reduced during the WB period. The discrepancy between subjective and objective sleep measures
may reflect caregiver-reported bias and the limited sensitivity of questionnaires when applied to intellectually
disabled populations, particularly when subtle physiological improvements occur without obvious perceived
behavioral change36.
Nevertheless, although not statistically significant, sleep quality also showed a favorable trend following DPS,
as indicated by a 0.68-point reduction in Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index score, whereas PB exposure was asso -
ciated with worsening sleep quality, reflected by a 1.41-point increase. By contrast, negligible changes were
observed in overall sleep health as measured by CSS, and insomnia severity assessed through ISI remained
largely unchanged after both interventions. Nonetheless, basal ISI scores for both intervention periods were low,
hindering the finding of significant differences for this parameter.
Interestingly, the only subjective sleep parameter that clearly improved after DPS was daytime sleepiness, with
an almost 3-point reduction in Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores and no relevant difference after PB exposure.
This finding is consistent with the objective increase in sleep efficiency and suggests that improved nocturnal
sleep may have translated into better daytime functioning.
Our findings partially differ from those reported by Gringras et al. 37, who conducted a randomized controlled
trial in children with ASD and found no significant objective effect of WB on sleep disturbances, although both
parents and children subjectively favored their use. In contrast, in our cohort of adults with PWS, objective
improvements were evident while subjective measures remained less sensitive. This difference may be related to
disorder-specific sensory processing patterns, age differences, or the distinct mechanisms underlying sleep dys -
regulation in PWS. This discrepancy underscores the importance of objective metrics.
The sleep improvement observed in the present work is also in line with previous studies conducted in other
clinical populations. Children with Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder experienced improved sleep pat -
terns while using weighted blankets, including shorter sleep-onset latency, fewer nocturnal awakenings, and less
sensitivity to environmental disturbances38. Similar benefits have also been reported in individuals with sensory
hypersensitivity39, as well as in people with intellectual disability, in whom DPS improved sleep efficiency and
reduced the need for hypnotic medication40. Improved sleep quality is known to have relevant downstream effects
on daily functioning and physical activity41,42. Adequate sleep is associated with higher levels of movement and
reduced sedentary behavior 43, a pattern that was also observed in our cohort. In the present study, improved
sleep efficiency was accompanied by increased physical activity and metabolic expenditure. Importantly, this
increase occurred in a regulated manner, since hyperactivity frequency, a common behavioral feature in PWS,
was reduced after DPS, suggesting better behavioral regulation rather than simple motor overactivation.
Furthermore, the increase in physical activity and metabolic rate ultimately translated into favorable changes
in body composition, including increased fat-free mass and skeletal muscle mass, but without significant changes
in fat mass percentage nor in body weight. These findings are particularly relevant given the profound sarcopenic
obesity phenotype that characterizes PWS and the limited availability of effective non-pharmacological interven-
tions targeting body composition 44–46. Nevertheless, these findings need to be cautiously interpreted due to the
short-term duration of the intervention.
Limitations
This study has several strengths, including its randomized crossover design, which allows each participant to
serve as their own control, thereby increasing statistical power despite the challenges of rare disease research.
Furthermore, the use of both objective (actigraphy, bioelectrical impedance) and validated clinical scales pro -
vides a comprehensive overview of the intervention’s impact.
However, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size, while typical for
rare disease research, may have limited the statistical power of our exploratory analyses to detect differences
between genetic subtypes, and precluded any comparison by GH treatment status. Second, the short duration of
the intervention (14 days) precludes conclusions about the long-term sustainability of the observed effects. Future

longitudinal studies are warranted to determine if these benefits persist and are enhanced over extended periods.
Additionally, environmental factors such as potential blanket intolerance during warmer seasons could influence
adherence in different climates. Finally, while the blankets were visually identical, the nature of the intervention
makes a perfect double-blind design challenging, as participants may perceive the weight difference.
A further limitation concerns carryover effects. Although a complete counterbalancing prevents inflation of
either intervention option, ABC-C baseline values in the “WB first” group had not returned to study entry levels
before the second period. Nonetheless, intervention effects estimated within the first period alone, not subject
to carryover, confirmed the behavioral findings. Although this persistence would interestingly suggest that the
behavioral effect of DPS is long-lasting, washout time should be better adjusted in future studies.
Nevertheless, as stated in the inclusion criteria, participants remained clinically stable throughout the study
in terms of psychopathological status and psychotropic medication. This reduces the likelihood that medication
changes or acute psychopathological instability accounted for the observed improvements and further reinforces
the interpretation that deep proprioceptive stimulation (DPS) exerts a genuine therapeutic effect in adults with
PWS.
Conclusions
In conclusion, DPS via weighted blankets represents a safe, well-tolerated, non-pharmacological, and easily
implementable intervention for improving behavioral regulation, sleep efficiency, and body composition in adults
with Prader–Willi syndrome. Our results highlight a significant reduction in anxiety and hyperphagia, suggesting
that DPS targets the core neurobehavioral challenges of the PWS phenotype. These findings support the incorpo-
ration of WBs into multidisciplinary care guidelines for the management of PWS, although further research with
larger, longer-term studies is needed to confirm the durability of these clinical benefits.
