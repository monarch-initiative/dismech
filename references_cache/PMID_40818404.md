INTRODUCTION AND IMPORTANCE: Yolk sac tumor (YST) is a rare germ cell 
malignancies that occasionally emerge in unexpected extragonadal sites, 
complicating early recognition and diagnosis. When arising in the vagina, YST 
may present with misleading symptoms and delayed recognition, given its rarity 
and concealed anatomical site.
CASE PRESENTATION: We present a case of vaginal YST involving a one-year-old 
girl who presented with prolonged vaginal bleeding and urinary retention. Early 
radiologic assessment revealed a large pelvic lesion displacing pelvic 
structures, including the bladder and uterus. These findings initially 
misinterpreted as a uterine mass. Urethrocystoscopy, vaginoscopy and laparoscopy 
uncovered a friable, hemorrhagic mass occupying the vaginal canal and 
compressing adjacent organs. Histopathologic analysis confirmed YST, supported 
by characteristic Schiller-Duval bodies and markedly elevated alpha-fetoprotein 
(AFP) levels. Lymph node biopsy identified metastatic spread to the left 
inguinal region.
CLINICAL DISCUSSION: The clinical presentation mimicked more common pediatric 
conditions, such as rhabdomyosarcoma and vaginal polyps, complicating the 
diagnostic process. Early imaging findings suggested uterine or adnexal 
pathology, which led to initial misdiagnosis. However, targeted endoscopic and 
histopathological evaluation were instrumental in identifying the true vaginal 
origin.
CONCLUSION: This case provides a valuable clinical insight into the rare 
presentation of vaginal YST in pediatrics. This report contributes to the 
limited literature on vaginal YST and emphasizes the imperative of timely 
diagnosis and comprehensive management in rare but aggressive pediatric 
malignancies.

YST also known as endodermal sinus tumor, is a rare and aggressive malignant germ cell tumor that predominantly arises in the gonads. However, extragonadal YSTs can occur in various sites, including the mediastinum, sacrococcygeal region, and less commonly, the female genital tract. Vaginal YST is an exceedingly rare entity, accounting for a small fraction of pediatric malignancies, with most cases reported in infants and young children under three years of age [1]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of vaginal YST in Indonesia, although a few other extragonadal cases in other locations have been previously documented [2,3].

Due to its rarity and overlapping clinical presentation with other pediatric vaginal masses, diagnosing vaginal YST possess a significant challenge. Patients often present with abnormal vaginal bleeding, a protruding vaginal mass, or urinary symptoms, which can mimic other conditions such as urethral prolapse, vaginal polyps, or rhabdomyosarcoma [4,5]. In certain cases, the vaginal origin of YST may not be immediately apparent due to the tumor's deep-seated location. Imaging plays a pivotal role at diagnosis for identification of organ of origin. Histopathological examination, along with immunohistochemical markers such as AFP, cytokeratin and Glypican-3, plays a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis [1].

In this case report, patient initially presented with prolonged vaginal bleeding. However, the actual lesion of deeply situated vaginal mass escaped early detection, illustrating the potential for underdiagnosis when the mass is not readily visible or palpable. Early imaging findings from abdominal ultrasound (USG) suggested uterine enlargement, which led to a preliminary impression of a uterine mass, such as uterine leiomyoma a condition extremely rare in children resulting in frequent underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis of vaginal YST [6].

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges, the importance of considering a broad differential diagnosis, and the critical role of early recognition supported by a multidisciplinary approach. We discuss the clinical presentation, imaging findings, histopathological characteristics, and treatment strategies, aiming to enrich the current literature on this rare malignancy. A 1-year-old girl presented with persistent vaginal bleeding that had occurred seven months prior to admission. The bleeding was often scant and self-limiting over the first few months, which contributed to delayed medical attention. Six days before admission, she developed urinary retention and was brought to a secondary hospital in West Java where urinary catheterization was performed and an abdominal USG was conducted. The ultrasound revealed that the uterus was obliterated by a heterogeneous hypoechoic mass with relatively well-defined borders, measuring 4,11 × 4,31 × 5,97 cm in the retrovesical area, appearing to be connected to the intraluminal urinary bladder. In addition to the symptoms, there was a history of unintentional weight loss (3 kg over five months). She had no drug history and allergies. There was no history of cancer in her family.

The patient referred to Dr. Hasan Sadikin General Hospital in Bandung for further evaluation. One day after admitted to our facility care, she developed hematuria. On physical examination, there was no hepatosplenomegaly or mass in the abdomen. Upon inspection of vagina, the vulva was normal, a moderate amount of blood was found in the vaginal orifices and the friable, hemorrhagic mass was visible from the vaginal opening. In the left inguinal region, there were immobile, solid, conglomerated lymph nodes measuring approximately 4,5 × 4 × 1 cm [Fig. 1A-B]. Digital rectal examination (DRE) identified a firm, immobile extraluminal mass located anterior to the rectum (position 10–3 o'clock) with a smooth surface that bled easily upon palpation. Abdominal USG showed uterine enlargement measuring 6,73 × 3,13 × 7,76 cm with heterogeneous myometrial echotexture and a poorly visualized endometrial stripe. Additionally, an inhomogeneous isoechoic mass arising from the adnexal region, measuring 2,30 × 4,41 × 5,32 cm, was observed. The lesion had well-defined borders, regular margins, and no calcifications [Fig. 2]. It appeared to originate bilaterally from the adnexal region, predominantly on the left side, causing grade III left-sided hydronephrosis. The patient underwent urethrocystoscopy, vaginoscopy, diagnostic laparoscopy and biopsy of the vaginal mass and inguinal lymph node at Dr. Hasan Sadikin Hospital, Bandung, Indonesia, by pediatric surgery consultant. Urethrocystoscopy showed a normal urethra, but the proximal scope could not pass due to extraluminal mass obstruction [Fig. 4]. Vaginoscopy demonstrated a mass on the left vaginal wall extending from the vaginal orifice to the proximal area [Fig. 5]. Intraoperative laparoscopy findings identified normal bladder, and uterus enlargement (4 × 3 × 3 cm) with hydrocolpos [Fig. 6A-B]. Conglomerated left inguinal lymph nodes were biopsied. Samples from the vaginal mass and lymph node [Fig. 7A-B], along with hydrocolpos fluid were submitted for histopathological examination [Fig. 8]. Histopathological analysis of the vaginal biopsy exhibited round-to-oval tumor cells with reticular, microcystic, and tubular patterns, exhibiting pleomorphic, hyperchromatic nuclei and prominent nucleoli, along with Schiller-Duval bodies, confirming a yolk sac tumor. Positive staining of AFP and negative staining of myogenin from immunohistochemical methods provide an accurate diagnosis [Fig. 9A–C]. Based on clinical, imaging, histopathological and immunohistochemistry findings, the patient was diagnosed with vaginal yolk sac tumor with inguinal lymph node metastasis.

Post-operative period was uneventful and patient was discharged home post-operative day one. Three weeks after surgery, the patient was re-admitted for chemotherapy. She received BEP regimen consisting of cisplatin 200 mg/m2, etoposide 100 mg/m2, bleomycin 15 Unit/ m2, administered over five consecutive days at weeks 0, 3, 6, and 9. Unfortunately, the patient deceased one week after the initiation of treatment, most likely due to sepsis. One day prior to death, she developed respiratory distress and altered mental status. Post-chemotherapy laboratory findings revealed leukopenia 660/uL (6.000–17.000/ uL), severe thrombocytopenia 10.000/ uL (150.000–450.000/ uL), elevated C-reactive protein 27,19 mg/dL (<0,5 mg/dL), and a markedly increased procalcitonin level 614,06 ng/mL (<0,1 ng/mL), all of which were suggestive of a severe systemic inflammatory response consistent with sepsis.

Patient or family perspective could not be obtained due to the patient's young age and rapid clinical deterioration, and adherence or tolerability to treatment could not be formally assessed. This was further compounded by the family's deep emotional distress and lack of readiness to participate in interviews during the brief course of hospitalization. The diagnosis of vaginal YST presents a considerable challenge due to its rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation with other pediatric vaginal masses [1]. Vaginal bleeding, dysuria, hematuria, or the presence of a vaginal mass are often the initial complaints, yet these can easily mimic other conditions such as urethral prolapse, vaginal polyps, or more commonly, rhabdomyosarcoma, which is a more frequent pediatric vaginal malignancy [4,5]. In this case, the initial diagnostic interpretation based on abdominal ultrasound was confounded by the appearance of an enlarged uterus, leading to a preliminary suspicion that the mass originated from the uterus and was possibly a uterine leiomyoma a highly uncommon tumor in pediatric populations [6].

Vaginal YST may be underdiagnosed when the mass is located deeper within the vaginal canal, as it may escape detection during routine physical examination, especially in uncooperative young children. As seen in this patient, the vaginal mass extended proximally and was not readily visible externally, contributing to diagnostic delay. The coexistence of hydrocolpos and mass effect on the bladder further masked the primary lesion. In fact, early imaging studies revealed uterine enlargement and adnexal mass, initially pointing toward a uterine or adnexal pathology rather than a vaginal tumor. Consequently, a thorough diagnostic approach, including imaging, endoscopic evaluation, and histopathological examination, was essential to confirm the diagnosis.

The finding of a firm, immobile extraluminal mass in the anterior rectum on DRE is strongly suggestive of a vaginal origin. Given the close anatomical relationship between the vagina and the rectum, a mass located within the posterior vaginal wall or a deeply seated vaginal lesion such as a yolk sac tumor can exert anterior pressure on the rectal wall. This may result in a palpable mass during DRE despite no visible abnormalities on the perineum. In this case, the rectal finding further supports the presence of a significant vaginal mass that was not readily apparent externally but had already exerted pressure on surrounding pelvic structures. In addition to this abnormalities, the patient exhibited unintentional weight loss (3 kg in five months) a significant red flag in pediatric patients. This symptom is frequently associated with systemic effects of malignancy [9]. Tumor-secreted inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 (IL-1) have been implicated in the pathogenesis of cancer cachexia, a multifactorial syndrome characterized by involuntary weight loss, muscle wasting, and metabolic alterations [9]. In pediatric patients, weight loss exceeding 10 % of body weight over a few months should raise strong clinical suspicion of an underlying serious condition, including neoplasm.

Serum AFP levels are a key marker in the diagnosis and monitoring of germ cell tumor [8]. Elevation of the serum AFP level is usually detected in patient with YST and is correlated with the severity of the disease. In this case, the markedly elevated AFP level strongly indicated the presence of a YST [9]. Elevated LDH, as seen in this patient likely reflects high tumor burden and cellular turnover, consistent with the metabolic activity of rapidly proliferating tumor cells. Although LDH is not specific, its elevation complements AFP and supports the diagnosis and monitoring of aggressive germ cell tumor such as YST [9]. Abdominal USG in this patient suggests that the tumor had extended locally, exerting mass effect on adjacent urinary structures, particularly the left ureter, which led to urinary obstruction and subsequent dilatation of the renal pelvis and calyces. The accompanying enlargement and inhomogeneous appearance of the uterine parenchyma further confounded the diagnostic picture, raising suspicion of a primary uterine mass such as a pediatric uterine leiomyoma—an exceedingly rare entity in this age group. However, MRI provided clearer visualization that the mass most likely originated from the vaginal region. MRI is indeed the preferred modality for evaluating soft tissue tumors, which underscores the diagnostic limitation in secondary hospitals that rely solely on USG. Urethrocystoscopy findings reinforces the presence of a significant space-occupying lesion obstructing the proximal urethra from the outside, correlating with the patient's clinical symptoms of urinary retention. The inability to visualize the bladder fully also reflects the degree of anatomical distortion caused by the tumor. Vaginoscopy was critical in localizing the origin of the mass. It demonstrated a friable, bleeding tumor arising from the left vaginal wall and extending from the vaginal orifice to the upper vaginal canal.

The presence of hydrocolpos, a fluid-filled distension of the vaginal canal, is likely due to partial or complete obstruction of normal vaginal outflow caused by the tumor itself. Cytological evaluation of the hydrocolpos fluid confirmed the presence of malignant cells, consistent with direct tumor shedding into the vaginal canal. This finding highlights the importance of endoscopic visualization in evaluating deep-seated pediatric vaginal tumors that are not fully appreciated on external examination. Laparoscopic exploration further confirmed the extent of tumor involvement. Intraoperatively, the uterus was found to be enlarged (4 × 3 × 3 cm), possibly due to reactive changes, vascular congestion, or infiltration by the tumor. The vaginal mass was described as fragile and hemorrhagic, consistent with the highly vascular nature of yolk sac tumors. Additionally, conglomerated lymph nodes were noted in the left inguinal region. These findings suggest early lymphatic spread, which is a known behavior of aggressive germ cell tumors like YST. Overall, the combined imaging and intraoperative findings provide a comprehensive picture of an aggressive, deeply situated vaginal malignancy with local extension to pelvic structures and early regional lymph node metastasis. These modalities complement each other in delineating tumor extent, guiding biopsy, and ruling out alternative diagnoses such as Müllerian anomalies or benign pelvic masses.

Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of yolk sac tumor, characterized by Schiller-Duval bodies, reticular and microcystic patterns, and tumor cells with pleomorphic, hyperchromatic nuclei and prominent nucleoli features that are pathognomonic of YST. Immunohistochemical confirmation with positive AFP and negative of myogenin was also valuable in supporting the diagnosis. These findings were critical in differentiating YST from other vaginal malignancies in children, particularly rhabdomyosarcoma, which remains the most common pediatric vaginal tumor. The final diagnosis was thus established based on a combination of clinical presentation, markedly elevated tumor markers of AFP and LDH, and characteristic of histopathological and immunohistochemical findings [10]. The presence of inguinal lymph node metastasis is an uncommon finding in pediatric vaginal YST and indicates more advanced disease with lymphatic spread. While YST typically spreads via hematogenous routes, lymphatic spread has also been documented in a limited number of cases. This again highlights the aggressive nature of YST and the potential for rapid dissemination. Delays in diagnosis may allow for such progression, leading to more complex treatment regimens and potentially poorer outcomes.

Differentiating YST from embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is crucial, as the latter remains the most frequent soft tissue tumor of pediatric primarily arises in the head and neck or the genitourinary tract [11]. Clinically, RMS often presents with vaginal bleeding and a polypoid, grape-like mass protruding from the vaginal orifice, which was similar to the findings in this case, while YST tends to be deeply situated and may be initially misdiagnosed as an uterine or adnexal pathology [11]. Radiologically, RMS typically exhibits a heterogeneous, sometimes cystic mass without specific tumor markers, whereas YST often appears as a solid-cystic pelvic mass accompanied by markedly elevated serum AFP levels [[10], [11], [12]]. Histopathologically, YST is defined by reticular, microcystic, and papillary patterns, Schiller-Duval bodies, and hyaline globules, whereas RMS demonstrates round to spindle-shaped cells with a cambium layer in botryoid type. Immunohistochemically, YST expresses AFP, cytokeratin, SALL4, and Glypican-3, while RMS is positive for desmin, myogenin, and MyoD1 [12]. Understanding these distinctions is vital for appropriate diagnosis and treatment planning.

Unfortunately, the patient deceased one week after the initiation of chemotherapy consisting of cisplatin, etoposide, and bleomycin despite the fact that extragonadal YSTs generally demonstrate favorable responses to cisplatin-based regimens, especially in pediatric populations [13]. The mortality in such cases may result from a combination of critical factors. At the time of diagnosis, the tumor had already caused significant local invasion, including urethral obstruction and hydrocolpos, and had metastasized to the inguinal lymph nodes, indicating an advanced stage with high tumor burden. This condition may have increased the risk of systemic complications during early chemotherapy, such as sepsis [14].

One day prior to her death, the patient developed respiratory distress and altered mental status. The laboratory assessment after chemotherapy revealed a leukopenia and severe thrombocytopenia, along with elevated C-reactive protein and markedly increased procalcitonin level. These findings were consistent with a severe systemic inflammatory response, strongly suggestive of sepsis. The presence of obstructive uropathy due to tumor compression, including hydronephrosis and hydrocolpos, could have predisposed the patient to ascending urinary tract infection. Additionally, the initiation of chemotherapy in an already immunocompromised host might have triggered overwhelming infection and sepsis. The combination of extensive local tumor invasion, early lymphatic spread, cachexia, and rapid clinical decline strongly suggests sepsis as the most probable cause of death.

This case provides a valuable clinical insight into the rare presentation of vaginal yolk sac tumor (YST) in pediatric patients, particularly in an extragonadal location that is often overlooked. It illustrates the diagnostic complexities posed by non-specific symptoms, atypical imaging findings, and overlapping clinical features with more common pediatric genital tumors. The diagnostic delay in this case highlights the necessity of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis, especially when encountering persistent genital bleeding and pelvic masses in young children. Moreover, the aggressive progression and early metastasis observed emphasize the critical importance of timely recognition, comprehensive imaging, histopathological confirmation, and prompt initiation of therapy. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of vaginal YST in Indonesia, reinforcing the need for heightened clinical awareness and interdisciplinary collaboration when managing unusual malignancies in pediatric oncology.
