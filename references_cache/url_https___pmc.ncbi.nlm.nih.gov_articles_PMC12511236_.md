<p>Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) has a high degree of genetic heterogeneity, with numerous mutated genes that contribute to deafness. <em>GIPC3</em> gene is one of the mutated genes that can cause congenital hearing loss, which has been identified in recent years; however, the exact mechanism behind this condition remains unclear. Mitophagy is the process of selectively encapsulating and lysing damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria in order to prevent the accumulation of damaged mitochondria from damaging the cells and is of great importance in the maintenance of homeostasis in the inner ear. This paper aims to investigate the potential mechanism of sensorineural hearing loss brought on by <em>Gipc3</em> mutations by observing the impact of Gipc3 expression on mitochondrial metabolism and autophagy in inner ear hair cells. In this study, The House Ear Institute Organ of Corti 1(HEI-OC1) cells and cochlear explants were cultured to change the expression level of Gipc3 by transfection, and the knockdown efficiency was examined by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and Western blot. Knockdown of <em>Gipc3</em> inhibited cell viability and its proliferation ability. When tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP) was used to induce oxidative stress injury and knockdown of <em>Gipc3</em>, inner ear hair cells had a weakened ability to resist oxidative stress injury, mitochondrial metabolism was altered, and there was an accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and a reduction of mitochondrial membrane potential. Immunofluorescence and Western blot techniques demonstrated autophagy abnormalities in the mitophagy-related proteins LC3B and p62. Early endosome-dependent mitophagy is mediated by a PH domain and leucine zipper motif 1 (APPL1), and mitophagy is hampered by APPL1 deletion. We discovered that there is probably co-localization between Gipc3 and APPL1 by confocal microscopy imaging and that their trends show a positive association. In summary, <em>Gipc3</em> mutations may lead to decreased mitochondrial function by inhibiting the APPL1-mediated mitophagy process. This may lead to a reduction in oxidative metabolism in hair cells, which is one potential mechanism via which <em>Gipc3</em> mutations suppress mitophagy.</p>
<section><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Sensorineural hearing loss, <em>Gipc3</em>, Mitophagy, Reactive oxygen species, Hair cell</p></section></section><section><h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>According to World Health Organization (WHO) statistics, approximately 466 million people currently experience hearing impairment, with prevalence rates rising annually. While not immediately life-threatening, deafness significantly compromises patients’ quality of life and occupational achievement [<a>13</a>, <a>28</a>]. Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) represents the most common congenital sensory disorder in children [<a>18</a>], with genetic factors contributing to &gt; 60% of cases. Major SNHL-associated genes include <em>GJB2</em>, <em>GJB3</em>, <em>OTOF</em>, and <em>CDH23</em> [<a>3</a>], while mutations in the <em>GIPC3</em> gene—essential to SNHL pathogenesis—constitute a significant cause of congenital SNHL [<a>32</a>]. Specifically, <em>GIPC3</em> mutations underlie hearing loss linked to <em>DFNB15</em>, <em>DFNB72</em>, and <em>DFNB95</em> loci [<a>1</a>, <a>4</a>, <a>35</a>].
</p>
<p>As a member of the GAIP-interacting protein C-terminal (GIPC) family, <em>GIPC3</em> mRNA is ubiquitously expressed in adult and fetal tissues. The gene comprises six exons at chromosome 19p13.3 [<a>17</a>], encoding a protein with GH1, PDZ, and GH2 domains that regulates diverse biological processes including cell proliferation, migration, division, and signaling. Current evidence indicates <em>GIPC</em>3 mutations primarily cause SNHL through three mechanisms: (1) Disrupting outer (OHC) and inner hair cell (IHC) stereocilia development, leading to ciliary disorganization, maturation defects, and sparse stereocilia bundles that impair mechanical conduction and signal amplification in the cochlea [<a>4</a>],(2) Altering Ca<sup>2</sup>⁺ homeostasis: Neurotransmitter release at IHC ribbon synapses depends on L-type voltage Ca<sup>2</sup>⁺ channels [<a>11</a>, <a>12</a>]. <em>Gipc</em>3 mutations in mice shift presynaptic Ca<sup>2</sup>⁺ influx activation toward hyperpolarized potentials, elevate spontaneous spiral ganglions neuron (SGNs) discharge, and reverse the spatial gradient of maximal Ca<sup>2</sup>⁺ influx in IHCs, enhancing both Ca<sup>2</sup>⁺ influx and exocytosis while increasing SGN firing rates at sound onset [<a>27</a>],(3) Accelerating age-related hearing loss 5 (<em>ahl5</em>) in Black Swiss mice (BLSW), compromising long-term survival of hair cells and SGNs [<a>4</a>], though precise mechanisms remain uncharacterized.</p>
<p>GIPC3 orchestrates multiple cellular functions, including cytokinesis, migration, intracellular trafficking, and IHC structural/functional maintenance [<a>5</a>], through interactions with leucine zipper motif 1 (APPL1) and myosin VI (MYO6) <a>42</a>, <a>43</a>]. MYO6—an actin-based motor protein—localizes to the pericuticular zone via GIPC cargo adapters [<a>24</a>, <a>39</a>]. APPL1, an early endosomal marker, regulates adiponectin/insulin signaling [<a>6</a>, <a>8</a>] and complexes with GIPC3, exhibiting co-regulated expression [<a>9</a>]. Crucially, APPL1 mediates early endosome-dependent mitophagy; its deficiency impairs mitochondrial clearance, promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and cytoplasmic mitochondrial DNA accumulation that drive cellular damage [<a>42</a>, <a>43</a>].</p>
<p>Consequently, elucidating molecular regulation of GIPC3 is critical for understanding hereditary SNHL pathogenesis and central auditory gain. This study investigates the mechanistic link between <em>Gipc3</em> mutations, mitochondrial dysfunction, APPL1 expression dynamics, and mitophagic impairment.</p></section><section><h2>Materials and Methods</h2>
<section><h3>Cell Culture and Grouping</h3>
<p>The House Ear Institute Organ of Corti 1 (HEI-OC1) cell line (OriCell, Guangzhou, China) was utilized in this work. This cell line was one of the most common used mouse auditory cell line available for research. The HEI-OC1 cells were cultivated at 37 °C in a cell incubator with a volume fraction of 5% carbon dioxide in DMEM high glucose medium (HyClone, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (OriCell, Guangzhou, China). Other experimental procedures were performed after the cells enter the logarithmic growth phase.</p>
<p>HEI-OC1 cells were cultured under standard conditions and divided into four experimental groups (<em>n</em> = 3 biological replicates per group): (1) Blank control: transfected meaningless sequence (sense: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′, antisense: 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′ with transfer-mate transfection reagent (GenePharma, Shanghai, China); (2) t-BHP group: treated with 80 µM tert-Butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP) (RHAWN, ShangHai, China) for 24 h; (3) si-Gipc3 group: transfected with Gipc3-targeting siRNA (sense: 5′-CCUUCACCCUGCGUCUAG-UTT-3′, antisense: 5′-ACUAGACGCAGGGUGAAGGTT-3′); and (4) si-Gipc3 + t-BHP group: subjected to <em>Gipc3</em> knockdown followed 24 h later by t-BHP treatment at identical parameters to Group 2. All transfections used 5 µL siRNA with 24 h incubation prior to subsequent interventions.</p></section><section><h3>Animal Model</h3>
<p>C57BL/6 J mice (Changsheng Bio-Technology, LiaoNing, China) were used in this investigation. During the experiment, the experimental animals were all raised at the SPF level Experimental Animal Center of Dalian Medical University. They were newborn mice 3 days after birth and were dissected for cochlear explant culture (<em>n</em> = 3 biological replicates per group). The experimental procedures were in accordance with the relevant regulations of the Ethics Committee of Dalian Medical University (Ethics Number: <a>AEE18062</a>).</p></section><section><h3>Cell Transfection</h3>
<p>Once the logarithmic growth phase HEI-OC1 cells had been counted, evenly inoculate them into a 6-well plate incubator for a full day. After that, transfect them in accordance with the transfer-mate transfection reagent’s instructions (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). Perform studies on cell growth or switch the media for ensuing drug-addition tests after 24 h of transfection. The control group was transfected with meaningless sequence. There were three siRNA Gipc3 used, from which valid sequences can be identified. All sequences are as follows: S1—sense: 5′-CCUUCACCCUGCGUCUAGUTT-3′, antisense: 5′-ACUAGACGCAGGGUGAA-GGTT-3′; S2—sense: 5′-GCUUCACCAAUGUCCGCGATT-3′, antisense: 5′-UCGCGG-ACAUUGGUGAAGCTT-3′; S3—sense: 5′-GCAGCGGGAGGGAAACAUUTT-3′, antisense: 5′-AAUGUUUCCCUCCCGCUGCTT-3′; NC—sense: 5′-UUCUCCGAA-CGUGUCACGUTT-3′, antisense: 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′. The tissue explants were transfected in the same way as the cell lines.</p></section><section><h3>Cell Viability Assay and t-BHP Concentration Gradient</h3>
<p>HEI-OC1 cells from the control group and si-Gipc3 group were inoculated into a 96-well plate, and at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, the cell viability of the two groups was measured. Different concentrations of t-BHP were added to the medium of HEI-OC1 cells. Add the CCK8 detection reagent to the culture after it had been grown for 24 h. To determine the cell viability value and the 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50), measure the absorbance at 450 nm after an hour of dark incubation. The cell survival rates of each group were compared.</p></section><section><h3>Cochlear Culture</h3>
<p>Newborn mouse pups at postnatal day 3 (P3) were decapitated, followed by meticulous isolation of the cochlea from the temporal bone. Under microscopic guidance, the cochlear shell, stria vascularis, spiral ligament, and modiolus were surgically removed while preserving the organ of Corti complex containing the basilar membrane and sensory epithelium. Collagen hydrogel matrices were prepared by mixing nine volumes of rat tail type I collagen (BD Biosciences, USA), 1 volume of 10 × Basal Medium Eagle (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 1 volume of 2% sodium carbonate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Aliquots (10 µL) of this solution were pipetted onto 35-mm culture dishes and polymerized at 37 °C for 15 min. After gelation, culture medium was gently overlaid. Cochlear explants were mounted as flat-surface preparations by positioning the sensory epithelium downward onto collagen gels using fine forceps. Surface tension from the liquid-medium interface stabilized tissue adherence. Cultures were maintained for 24 h at 37 °C/5% CO<sub>2</sub>.</p></section><section><h3>Immunofluorescence Staining</h3>
<p>HEI-OC1 cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (4 °C, 1 h) while cochlear basilar membrane-sensory epithelium complexes underwent overnight fixation (4 °C), followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 and blocking in 10% normal goat serum (RT, 30 min and 1 h, respectively). Primary antibodies (Gipc3 [Biorbyt orb316977, 1:500], APPL1 [Santa Cruz sc-271901, 1:100], LC3B [Cell Signaling 3868, 1:2400]) were applied overnight at 4 °C; after rewarming (RT, 30 min) and PBS washing, secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor® 594 anti-rabbit IgG [Abcam ab150080, 1:500], Alexa Fluor® 488 anti-mouse IgG [Proteintech SA00013-3, 1:500]) were incubated protected from light (RT, 1 h). Samples underwent DAPI counterstaining (5 µg/mL, 10 min), three 5-min PBS washes, and mounting in antifade medium prior to confocal imaging (0.5 µm Z-stacks); mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ with rolling ball background subtraction (50 pixel radius).</p></section><section><h3>Confocal Microscopy Imaging and Co-localization Analysis</h3>
<p>Prepared slides were mounted onto a Leica confocal microscope (XMY-50, Germany) and visualized under a 63 × oil immersion objective lens. Z-stack imaging was performed with a layer spacing of 0.35 µm per layer to ensure high-resolution sectioning. Fluorescence signals were sequentially excited at wavelengths of 405 nm (blue), 488 nm (green), and 568 nm (red) to capture multichannel laminar scans. Scanning commenced upon initial signal detection and terminated when fluorescence diminished; all acquired layers were superimposed using Zeiss software to generate composite images. To assess co-localization between two specific proteins (APPL1 and Gipc3), double immunostaining was performed. Subsequently, co-localization analysis was conducted in the acquired images by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficient and Manders overlap coefficients using the ImageJ software measurement tools as standardized for quantitative fluorescence-based imaging in cellular models. Mean fluorescence intensity values were quantified in defined regions of interest (ROIs), with each ROI encompassing approximately 60 outer hair cells to ensure consistency across measurements. This integrated approach allowed for precise determination of protein interactions and co-localization efficiencies.</p></section><section><h3>RNA Extraction and qPCR Detection</h3>
<p>During transfection in 6-well plates, total cellular RNA was extracted with TRIzol lysis solution, and RNA purity and concentration were determined by ultra-micro spectrophotometer. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using All-in-One First-Strand Synthesis MasterMix (with dsDNase) kit (Yugong Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China). Taq SYBR Green qPCR Premix kit (Yugong Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China) was utilized for gene expression assay. The reaction conditions were as follows: Following 30 s of pre-metamorphosis at 95 °C, the 40 cycles were performed: deformation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. The gene expression level of each sample was normalized by using the <em>GAPDH</em> gene as the reference gene. GADPH primer sequences, GADPH forward: 5′-CCTTCATTGACCTCAACTACAT-GG-3′, and GADPH reverse: 5′-CTCGCTCCTGGAAGATGGTG-3′. Gipc3 primer sequence, Gipc3 forward: 5′-GAGCCGGATACGCCTTCATC-3′, and Gipc3 reverse: 5′-AGCCCACAATAGAGAGTGGTCATT-3′.</p></section><section><h3>Protein Extraction</h3>
<p>Whole protein extraction was performed on collected specimens using a total protein extraction kit (KGB5303-50, Keygentec, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the samples were homogenized/lysed in ice-cold extraction buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant containing total protein was aliquoted for quantification and subsequent analyses. The protein standard applied to calculate the protein concentration was the BCA protein content detection kit. The protein was denatured by high heat and then stored at − 80 °C.</p></section><section><h3>Western Blot Analysis</h3>
<p>The protein samples were separated by 10% and 12% SDS-PAGE. Using a membrane transfer device, the protein was moved to the polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) (4 °C, 250 mA). After being blocked in 5% skimmed milk (Solarbio, Beijing, China) powder at room temperature, the primary antibodies (1:1500, Gipc3; 1:500, APPL1; 1:1000, LC3B; 1:1000, p62, Cell Signaling Technology, UK) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. While being treated for an hour at room temperature with various species of secondary antibodies (1:10,000, abclonal, Wuhan, China), the immunoreactive bands were washed three times with TBST buffer for 10 min each time. PVDF membrane with a dropwise addition of the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) luminous reagent. The changes of bands in each group were observed by a luminescence meter. Using ImageJ software, band analysis was carried out to compare the target protein expression levels between the groups based on the target protein/β-actin internal reference protein ratio.</p></section><section><h3>Detection of Reactive Oxygen Species</h3>
<p>ROS detection kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used for this experiment, which was diluted with serum-free medium: probe = 1:1000. Remove the original medium of each group of cells, properly wash the cells, and add an appropriate volume of medium containing the probe. The cells were incubated in a cell culture chamber for 20 min (37 °C, 5% CO<sub>2</sub>) in the dark. The cells were fully washed three times with serum-free cell culture medium, and the expression of reactive oxygen species in each group was observed by green excitation light of a fluorescence microscope.</p></section><section><h3>Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit with JC-1</h3>
<p>Mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was adopted for detection. The original medium of each group of cells was removed. The JC-1 staining working solution was added in the same amount after the proper amount of cell media had been supplied. The working solution was diluted JC-1 according to the ratio of JC-1 (200 ×): ultrapure water = 1:160. The JC-1 was fully dissolved and mixed, and then a quarter volume of JC-1 dyeing buffer (5 ×) of ultrapure water was added. The cells were incubated in the dark for 20 min (37 °C, 5% CO<sub>2</sub>). After that, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed three times with the JC-1 staining buffer (1 ×). The expression of green fluorescence of the JC-1 monomer and red fluorescence of JC-1 polymer was observed under a fluorescence microscope.</p></section><section><h3>Statistical Analysis</h3>
<p>Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4 and SPSS 25.0 software. The measurement data were expressed as (<em>x̅</em> ± s). <p>In this study, siRNA technology was used to specifically knock down <em>Gipc3</em> in a short time, and <em>Gipc3</em> gene expression in each group was determined using qPCR and gene-specific primers [<a>38</a>], elucidating the role of <em>Gipc3</em> mutations in mitochondrial dysfunction within inner ear hair cells. This highlights the critical relationship between Gipc3 expression and mitochondrial metabolism. The results indicated that the knockdown of <em>Gipc3</em> lead to impaired cell viability and proliferation, exacerbating oxidative stress responses. Specifically, the observed alterations in mitochondrial metabolism, characterized by increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and diminished mitochondrial membrane potential, underscore the detrimental impact of <em>Gipc3</em> mutations. Furthermore, the identification of autophagy abnormalities, particularly in mitophagy-related proteins such as LC3B and p62, suggests that Gipc3 plays a pivotal role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis. The interaction between Gipc3 and APPL1, as evidenced by fluorescence probably co-localization, further emphasizes the importance of early endosome-dependent mitophagy in this context.</p>
<section><h3>Deafness and Gipc3 Mutations</h3>
<p>Deafness occurs due to the inability of sound stimulation signals to reach the auditory nerve pathway, resulting from the loss of expression of ear deformity proteins in inner hair cells [<a>29</a>]. Genetic mutations can lead to changes in protein expression at different sites and can also cause sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) by affecting various physiological processes. <em>Gipc3</em> gene mutations may result in SNHL through multiple mechanisms.</p>
<p>Extensive research had shown that <em>GIPC3</em> mRNA is highly expressed in the jejunum, followed by brain lymph nodes, parietal lobes, fetal spleen, and fetal thymus [<a>36</a>]. Gipc3 was essential for the survival of spiral ganglions and hair cells long after birth, as well as for the maturation of cilia. In inner hair cells (IHCs) and outer hair cells (OHCs), there is noticeable punctate staining at the base, surrounding the nucleus, and on top of the entire hair cell. Individuals with <em>Gipc3</em> mutations exhibited significant degeneration of Corti’s organ and loss of spiral ganglion [<a>4</a>].</p></section><section><h3>Mitophagy and Its Role in Age-Related Hearing Loss</h3>
<p>Age-related hearing loss (ARHL) was often accompanied by ROS imbalance and progressive deterioration of inner ear metabolism. Mitochondria are considered to be the main intracellular source of ROS [<a>23</a>]. The dynamic balance between continual division and fusion in mitochondria resulted in changes in the normal morphological structure, number, and distribution of mitochondria [<a>45</a>].</p>
<p>To maintain function and cell viability and prevent the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, the process of selectively encapsulating and lysing dysfunctional mitochondria in cells was termed mitophagy [<a>16</a>]. Mitophagy-mediated mitochondrial elimination was crucial in many biological processes. Disorders in mitophagy could adversely affect normal mitochondrial function and metabolic activities. Studies had revealed a close relationship between mitophagy and SNHL, highlighting the significance of mitophagy in preserving inner ear homeostasis [<a>45</a>]. Several studies indicated that impaired mitophagy could exacerbate hearing loss caused by medications and other factors [<a>7</a>, <a>19</a>, <a>21</a>], while upregulation of mitophagy could reduce drug-induced ototoxic damage [<a>15</a>]. The process of mitochondrial fission was also critical for mitophagy. Research had demonstrated that GIPC influence mitochondrial fission through its protein interactions [<a>31</a>] which triggered reduction in mitophagy.</p></section><section><h3>Mechanisms of Gipc3 Mutation Impacting Mitophagy</h3>
<p><em>Gipc3</em> mutations might decrease mitophagy through the following mechanisms: (1) Gipc3 promoted early endosomal maturation by binding MYO6 to APPL1. The interaction between APPL1 and Rab5 facilitates endosomal-mediated mitophagy [<a>42</a>, <a>43</a>]. A <em>Gipc3</em> mutation blocked the binding of these proteins and reduces APPL1 expression, thereby diminishing early endosomal-mediated mitophagy, inhibiting the transport of damaged mitochondria to lysosomes for degradation [<a>14</a>, <a>43</a>]. Research had demonstrated an interaction between GIPC3 and APPL1 in HEK293T cells, with a positive correlation in their expression [<a>9</a>]. Studies indicated that endosomes were involved in ubiquitination-related mitophagy [<a>20</a>, <a>25</a>], including the recognition of ubiquitinated proteins through specific receptors [<a>44</a>], guiding selective targets to the autophagy pathway [<a>14</a>]. Additionally, the involvement of the ubiquitin system in endocytic recycling further supported the role of ubiquitination in endosomal-related processes. (2) Dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) was a vital mediator of mitochondrial division and participated in initiating mitophagy [<a>26</a>, <a>40</a>]. Research had validated the interplay between GIPC and Drp1, which primarily acted on mitochondria in cell membranes and cytoplasm. The interaction between GIPC and Drp1 was mediated by the conserved C-terminal residues of Drp1 as a PDZ binding motif, guiding the aggregation of Drp1 around perinuclear mitochondria and promoting mitochondrial division [<a>33</a>]. <em>Gipc3</em> mutations might disrupt the interaction between Gipc3 and Drp1, inhibiting Drp1 aggregation in perinuclear mitochondria, reducing mitochondrial division, and affecting mitophagy. (3) Studies had shown that increased ROS production might regulate mitophagy through various pathways, such as NF-κB, mTOR, p38-MAPK, and SIRT [<a>22</a>]. The accumulation of ROS activated NF-κB signaling, which promoted mitophagy by upregulating P62 expression, exerting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, and mitigating NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated mitochondrial damage [<a>37</a>]. Early endosome-dependent mitophagy mediated by APPL1 limited NLRP3 inflammasome activation [<a>42</a>]. Whether <em>Gipc3</em> mutations led to excessive activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by reducing APPL1 expression remained to be further verified by experiments.</p></section><section><h3>In Vitro<em> Culture Techniques in This Study</em>
</h3>
<p>Tissue cell culture technology had become an essential auxiliary research method for the development of cell biology, molecular biology, genetics, immunology, and other disciplines [<a>34</a>]. The methods of inner ear culture in vitro include inner ear cell culture, inner ear tissue culture, and inner ear organ culture. Among these, inner ear organ culture was the closest to the in vivo environment. Inner ear organ culture technology involved removing part or the entire organ of the cochlear basilar membrane and the vestibular from the inner ear cavity and transferred it to a medium to survive and grow in vitro. This method was suitable for studying inner ear development, differentiation, and the interaction between the inner ear and neurons. Applications for the inner ear organs included gene expression, stereocilia movement, cell and molecular biology, and hair cell regeneration [<a>30</a>].</p>
<p>This study has several constraints that warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, the functional impact of Gipc3 was exclusively evaluated in vitro, lacking in vivo validation through animal models to assess its physiological effects. Secondly, while fluorescence co-localization indicated a positive correlation between Gipc3 and APPL1 expression, direct molecular interaction evidence (e.g., co-immunoprecipitation or structural analyses) remains unverified. Finally, mitophagy abnormalities were inferred indirectly without ultrastructural confirmation limiting morphological substantiation of these defects. However, the findings in this research contributed to a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying SNHL associated with <em>GIPC3</em> mutations, paving the way for potential therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondrial dysfunction in affected individuals.</p></section></section><section><h2>Acknowledgements
