BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Autoimmune encephalitis (AIE) and paraneoplastic 
neurologic syndromes (PNSs) encompass a heterogeneous group of 
antibody-associated disorders. Both the number of syndromes and commercially 
available antibody tests have increased considerably over the past decade. 
High-quality population-based data on epidemiology of these disorders and 
real-world performance of antibody tests are needed.
METHODS: In this nationwide retrospective cohort study, we identified all serum 
and CSF samples tested for antibodies against intracellular antigens (IAs: Hu 
[ANNA1], Yo [PCA1], CV2 [CRMP5], Ri [ANNA2], Ma1, Ma2 [Ta], amphiphysin, GAD65, 
GFAP, KLHL11, CARP VIII) or extracellular antigens (EAs: NMDAR, LGI1, Caspr2, 
GABA-B-R, GABA-A-R, AMPAR, DPPX, GlyR, mGluR1, VGCC, IgLON5, Tr [DNER]) between 
January 2016 and December 2021 in the Netherlands. Nationwide coverage was 
guaranteed for all antibodies except anti-GAD65 and anti-VGCC. We calculated 
sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative 
predictive value (NPV); obtained clinical information about patients who tested 
positive; assigned diagnosis of AIE/PNS according to diagnostic criteria; and 
calculated incidence rates for IA, EA, and individual antibody-associated 
syndromes.
RESULTS: In the study period, 2,877 (9.5%) of 30,246 samples, belonging to 1,228 
patients, tested positive. Sensitivity and specificity were high (>95%) to very 
high (>99%) for most tests in both serum and CSF. PPVs for several tests were 
moderate to poor, especially for serum testing of IA antibodies (25%-80%). 
Clinical data were available for 940 (76.5%) of 1,228 patients. A total of 578 
AIE/PNS diagnoses were made. The incidence rate for AIE/PNS (per million 
person-years) increased from 4.70 (95% CI 3.72-5.85) in 2016 to 5.76 (4.69-7.00) 
in 2021. Overall, the incidence rate was 5.57 (5.13-6.05), 2.96 (2.64-3.31) for 
the EA and 2.61 (2.31-2.94) for the IA subgroup. The 4 most common AIE/PNS types 
were anti-NMDAR, anti-LGI1, anti-Hu, and anti-GAD65, together comprising almost 
two-thirds of all diagnoses (364/578, 63.0%).
DISCUSSION: Most commercial antibody tests perform well overall, but important 
pitfalls remain. Although almost all tests had high specificity, PPV was only 
modest in the setting of these rare diseases and mass testing. We observe trends 
toward increasing incidence of antibody-associated AIE/PNS.

Autoantibodies against neuronal or glial antigens are associated with a wide range of immune-mediated neurologic disorders, including paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes (PNSs) and autoimmune encephalitis (AIE).1,2 Once considered rare disorders, in recent years, it has become apparent that the incidence of AIE might equal that of infectious encephalitis.3 Nevertheless, the number of population-based epidemiologic studies in AIE and PNS remains limited.3-6 However, these studies are critical to enable the development of health care strategies for affected patients and the planning of clinical trials.

The number of antibodies available for testing has increased considerably over the past decade, and it is not uncommon that only serum or CSF is sent for testing. This might lead to missed or delayed diagnoses and worse patient outcomes. Furthermore, outside referral centers, samples are often only tested with commercial immunoblots or cell-based assays (CBAs), without confirmation by immunohistochemistry (IHC) or indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) on tissue sections as recommended.7 This increases the risk of finding false-positive or clinically irrelevant antibody results in patients without AIE or PNS, leading to misdiagnosis and exposing patients to unnecessary and potentially harmful treatments.8,9

To address these issues, we performed a nationwide retrospective cohort study looking at AIE/PNS epidemiology and antibody testing performance, providing new data to assist physicians in their daily practice.

This study adheres to the STROND guideline for the reporting of incidence and prevalence in neuroepidemiology.10

We performed a nationwide retrospective cohort study to identify all Dutch patients with antibodies against intracellular antigens (IA subgroup: Hu [ANNA1], Yo [PCA1], CV2 [CRMP5], Ri [ANNA2], Ma1, Ma2 [Ta], amphiphysin, GAD65, GFAP, KLHL11, CARP VIII) or extracellular antigens (EA subgroup: NMDAR, LGI1, Caspr2, GABA-B-R, GABA-A-R, AMPAR, DPPX, GlyR, mGluR1, VGCC, IgLON5, Tr [DNER]). We identified all serum and CSF samples tested for these antibodies between January 2016 and December 2021 through a search of the databases of the Laboratory Medical Immunology (Department of Immunology) and the Neuro-Oncology Research Laboratory (Department of Neurology) of the Erasmus University Medical Center (EMC). Testing for all antibodies was available throughout the study period, except for GFAP (available since June 2019) and KLHL11 (available since July 2020).

The EMC Department of Neurology is a European Reference Network site (ERN-RITA) and the national referral site for patients with suspected AIE or PNS; the EMC Laboratory Medical Immunology is the EN ISO 15189:2012 accredited national referral site for antineuronal antibody testing. To ensure nationwide coverage, we also included tests performed at the 6 other Dutch laboratories that offer local testing for some AIE/PNS antibodies (eTable 1). One of these hospitals, the Maastricht University Medical Center, also performs local testing for antibodies against NMDAR, LGI1, Caspr2, GABA-B-R, and AMPAR; these data were also included. Anti-VGCC is also tested at another hospital (Leiden University Medical Center), and anti-GAD65 is tested at multiple sites across the Netherlands with varying techniques and cutoff values. Therefore, nationwide coverage could not be obtained, and only samples tested at the EMC were included in this study. Antibody testing techniques are described in eTable 1. We did not include antibodies primarily associated with demyelinating disorders (MOG, AQP4), (para)nodopathies (Caspr1, CNTN1, NF155), or myasthenic syndromes other than LEMS (AChR, MuSK).

We obtained clinical data from patients with positive antibody testing from detailed interviews with patients or relatives during a visit to the national referral center, from phone consultation with the treating physicians at referring hospitals, from medical records, or—when none of these were available—from reviewing the clinical information filled in on the request forms. Furthermore, as the national referral site, we have consulted (and, therefore, have access to clinical information on) approximately 1,500 patients per year with suspected AIE/PNS (who make up a large part of the group that tested negative). Patients with a positive antibody result tested with 2 or more independent techniques in either serum or CSF and with matching clinical syndrome were considered to have AIE/PNS (eTable 2 for details on the clinical syndromes considered relevant). If applicable, we used the diagnostic criteria for probable or definite AIE/PNS.1,2

All analyses were performed in R version 4.1.2. We calculated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) for the presence or absence of AIE/PNS for all antibody tests with 3 or more positive patients in either serum or CSF. If patients had testing performed multiple times during the study period, we only included the first test in a certain sample type (serum or CSF) in our analysis. We compared diagnostic test evaluation using the McNemar paired test and χ2 test for proportions, and we used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare VGCC antibody concentrations in patients with VGCC-associated neurologic syndromes and non-AIE/PNS patients. For the calculation of incidence rates, Dutch population data from the Central Bureau for Statistics were used.11

IRB approval was waived, but informed consent for use of medical information for research purposes was obtained from all patients or proxies that could be reached. Both parents consented in case of children.

We identified 30,246 samples from 20,940 patients during the observation period (22,373 serum [74.0%], 7,476 CSF [24.7%], and 397 unspecified [1.3%]; eFigure 1). Most of these samples (27,094/29,202 [92.8%]) were tested at the national referral center. We noted a year-by-year increase in the number of samples with a drop in 2020, after which the increasing trend resumed in 2021 (eFigure 2, eTable 3).

A total of 2,877 samples (9.5% of all samples; 2,297/21,329 serum [10.8%] and 580/7,476 CSF [7.8%]) belonging to 1,228 patients tested positive for 1 or more antibodies. Of 1,228 patients, 80 (6.5%) tested positive for 2 or more antibodies, including 5 patients with 3 or more antibodies (eTable 4). Clinical data could be collected for 940 (76.5%) of 1,228 patients, and we evaluated 494 of 940 (52.6%) in person at the national referral center.

Sensitivity, specificity, PPVs, and NPVs are given in Tables 1–3. In serum, sensitivity was high (>95%) for all antibodies except for those against NMDAR, KLHL11, GFAP, Ri, and AMPAR. CSF testing was more sensitive for those first 4, but for antibodies against AMPAR, the commercial CBA performed relatively poor in both sample types (sensitivity of 80% and 50%, respectively). In the case of anti-Ri, the apparent lower sensitivity in serum was due to a single patient who was only positive in CSF on a total of only 3 patients with Ri-PNS overall.

CSF provided equal or better specificity when compared with serum for all antibodies but was somewhat less sensitive for almost half of them (LGI1, Caspr2, GABA-B-R, AMPAR, IgLON5, GlyR, Ma2, CV2, Hu, GAD65). Of interest, 11 of 20 patients with LGI1 and 2 of 6 patients with Caspr2, in whom commercial CBA was negative in CSF, still had positive CSF IHC, indicating a failure of the commercial CBA to detect CSF antibodies in these cases rather than these patients being serum-only positive. Specificity was >99% for all antibodies in both serum and CSF, with the exception of serum testing for anti-GAD65 (97.3%), anti-VGCC (98.9%), and anti-GFAP (81.8%). For GAD65 antibodies, test characteristics were calculated for both the total group of anti-GAD65–positive patients and the high-concentration group only, using previously proposed cutoffs of >10,000 IU/mL in serum or >100 IU/mL in CSF to define this subgroup.12 While positive anti-GAD65 results in serum and CSF had PPVs for neurologic GAD65 autoimmunity of only 44% and 88.8%, respectively, PPVs increased to 97.7% and 98.5%, respectively, for high-concentration GAD65 antibodies, with almost no impact on sensitivity and NPV. For VGCC antibodies that are also assessed by a quantitative test, there is no clear cutoff to distinguish between neurologic VGCC autoimmunity and nonspecific positive results. However, we found VGCC antibody concentrations to be significantly higher in patients with VGCC-associated neurologic syndromes compared with nonspecific results (median concentration 218 vs 60 pmol/L, p < 0.0001).

Despite very high specificity, PPVs for testing serum with CBA only were 69.7% for anti-NMDAR, 71.7% for anti-Caspr2, 78.8% for anti-GABA-B-R, and 91.8% for anti-LGI1. When adding confirmation with IHC, these PPVs increased to 97.1%, 94.3%, 100%, and 96.4%, respectively. An even bigger improvement was seen with PPVs for serum testing of some of the classical onconeuronal antibodies such as anti-Yo (PPV of 28.8% with immunoblot alone vs 77.8% with immunoblot and IIF). NPVs were >99% for all antibodies in both serum and CSF except for IgLON5 (97.6% in CSF), GlyR (93.3% in CSF), and GFAP (93.8% in serum).

When interpreting PPVs reported in this study, it is important to bear in mind that we only considered presence or absence of AIE/PNS when calculating PPVs. However, antibodies found in patients without AIE/PNS can still be clinically relevant by pointing to the presence of a yet undiscovered malignancy. For example, 22 anti-Hu–positive patients did not have Hu-PNS, but 16 of these patients still had tumors. Moreover, Hu antibodies could be confirmed by IIF in 10 of 22 of these patients, and all 10 patients with confirmed antibodies had tumors. Similarly, 52 anti-Yo–positive patients did not have Yo-PNS. Only 6 of 52 could be confirmed by IIF, and 5 of 6 of those had tumors (in the remaining patient, no tumor screening was performed). In all patients with Hu or Yo antibodies and Hu-PNS or Yo-PNS (87 and 21 patients, respectively), antibodies could be confirmed by IIF.

We also analyzed patients with discordant antibody status, i.e., the antibody test performed in both serum and CSF but positive in only one of these. Because immunotherapy may influence antibody status, we only included pretreatment samples in this analysis. Patients with unknown treatment status were only included when serum and CSF samples were collected on the same date or no more than 3 days apart. We identified 180 patients with discordant antibody status (eTable 8). While 29 (91%) of 32 patients with a positive antibody test only in CSF had probable or definite AIE/PNS, this was true for just 46 (31%) of 148 serum-only positive test results. The most common serum-only positives that were considered not clinically relevant were GAD65 (n = 34, all with concentrations <10,000 IU/mL), NMDAR (n = 21), Yo (n = 13), GABA-B-R (n = 7), and CV2 (n = 6). Using the McNemar exact test, we confirmed superior accuracy of CSF testing for anti-NMDAR (p < 0.0001) and of serum for anti-LGI1 (p < 0.0001) and anti-Caspr2 (p = 0.03). Ultimately, 626 (66.6%) of 940 patients had a diagnosis of antibody-associated AIE/PNS. Of these, 36 patients had a first positive antibody result before January 1, 2016 (samples tested during the observation period were repeated samples), and 18 patients did not live in the Netherlands but their samples were sent from abroad for testing at the referral center. These patients were excluded from the epidemiologic analysis described in the remainder of this section.

Six patients had 2 antibody-associated neurologic syndromes, accounting for a total of 578 new AIE/PNS diagnoses between 2016 and 2021 in 572 patients (Table 5, Figure, A, eFigure 3, A and B). Of these, 325 (56.8%) were female and the median age at diagnosis was 64 years (interquartile range 21.9 years). Two hundred and twenty-five (43.4%) of 518 patients in whom sufficient screening was performed had a tumor: 82 (28.8%) of 285 in the EA subgroup and 143 (61.4%) of 233 in the IA subgroup (136/165 (82.4%) when excluding patients with GAD65 antibodies, which are seldom paraneoplastic). This number increased to 48/69 (70%) in patients with more than 1 antibody and to 5/5 (100%) in patients with more than 2 antibodies.

In this study, we show that antibody test results should be interpreted within their clinical context because very good specificity can still result in low PPV. This was most obvious for serum tested for intracellular targets by immunoblot only but was also seen in CBAs for antibodies against extracellular targets. Furthermore, we report an incidence rate of 5.57 per million person-years, showing a trend toward a gradual increase over time, and EA antibodies were identified slightly more frequently compared with IA antibodies.

Most tests performed well in both serum and CSF. In serum, sensitivity was high (>95%) for all antibodies except for anti-NMDAR, anti-KLHL11, anti-GFAP, anti-Ri, and anti-AMPAR. CSF testing was more sensitive for those first 4 (as has been established previously for anti-NMDAR13 and anti-GFAP14,15). CSF provided equal or better specificity when compared with serum for all antibodies but was less sensitive for almost half of them. We confirmed superior accuracy of CSF testing for anti-NMDAR and of serum for anti-LGI1 and anti-Caspr2, as has been shown previously.13,16,17

Specificity was very high (>99%) for all antibodies in both serum and CSF, with the exception of serum testing for anti-GAD65 and anti-VGCC (mentioned later) and serum anti-GFAP (as shown previously).14,15 Nevertheless, despite this very high specificity, PPVs for several antibody tests were moderate to poor. This was especially true for serum testing of classical onconeuronal antibodies, but also applicable to a lesser degree to other neuronal antibody tests. This apparent paradox is a direct consequence of the high number of tests requested in panels in combination with the low prevalence of these antibody-associated disorders. In this setting (low previous chance of positivity), even tests with very good specificity can produce a relevant number of false-positive results, which is an important pitfall in clinical practice. Indeed, overinterpretation of false-positive or clinically irrelevant serum antibodies is one of the most common reasons for AIE/PNS misdiagnosis.18-20 Therefore, PPV is the most relevant test parameter in everyday clinical practice for these rare disorders.

Because of this possibility of false positives, confirmation of a positive result by a second independent (tissue-based) technique is the recommended method for diagnosis of neuronal autoantibodies. Unfortunately, many diagnostic laboratories worldwide only use commercially available line blots or cell-based assays, leading to misdiagnosis and exposure of patients to ineffective and potentially harmful treatments.8,9 At our national referral center, we confirm all positive results by a tissue-based technique. As expected, this 2-step approach improved PPVs for all antibody tests in both serum and CSF.

Notably, PPVs for serum testing of classical onconeuronal antibodies were considerably lower than those for serum EA antibodies. This might be explained in part by the use of an immunoblot for the former, which tests for the presence of multiple antibodies at once, thereby increasing the risk of false-positive or clinically irrelevant results. In addition, the antigens used on immunoblot are mostly produced by recombinant techniques and might expose irrelevant, denatured (neo)epitopes on attachment to solid-phase surfaces, also increasing risk of false positivity.21 By contrast, EA antibodies were tested by individual CBAs in this study, which allows for a more rational testing strategy tailored to the clinical syndrome of the patient. Furthermore, the use of native antigens inherent to the CBA technique renders this technique more prone to mediate true positivity.

Previous studies have shown that high concentrations of GAD65 antibodies are found in classical anti-GAD65–associated clinical syndromes while low-concentration antibodies lack specificity and can be encountered in a heterogeneous group of neurologic and non-neurologic diseases.12,22 This is reflected in the test characteristics we found: while PPVs were low (serum) to moderate (CSF) when considering all anti-GAD65–positive patients, they were high (>97%) in the high-concentration subgroup. Of note, several laboratories worldwide use an immunoblot, which only gives a qualitative result (i.e., “positive” or “negative”) and does not allow differentiation between neurologic GAD65 autoimmunity and a clinically irrelevant result.8 A positive anti-GAD65 result obtained with this technique in a patient with a neurologic syndrome should, therefore, be interpreted with caution, and every effort should be made to quantify it.

For VGCC antibodies, there is conflicting evidence concerning the correlation between antibody concentration and likelihood of neurologic autoimmunity, but earlier studies showed its low specificity.23,24 In our cohort, specificity was good while PPV was poor. Higher specificity in our study is possibly due to our higher cutoff value (>40 pmol/L, compared with >20 pmol/L in the earlier studies). Furthermore, in contrast to earlier reports,23 antibody concentrations were significantly higher in patients with VGCC-associated neurologic syndromes compared with nonspecific results in our cohort.

Tissue-based tests to confirm positive CBA results can be costly and time-consuming and are not easily accessible to all physicians. In the absence of this possibility, the clinician should consider clues that a positive antibody result might be false positive or clinically irrelevant. The result itself might provide such clue: our data show that low-concentration anti-GAD65 or serum-only anti-NMDAR is almost definitely clinically irrelevant. Other clues are atypical patient demographics or clinical syndrome.

Furthermore, we describe population-based epidemiologic data for antibody-associated AIE/PNS in the Netherlands. Incidence of NMDAR-AIE and LGI1-AIE has been reported in earlier work from our group.13,25 We observe a trend toward increasing incidence of AIE/PNS over the years, probably due to better awareness and testing rather than reflecting a true increase in incidence. As awareness continues to improve (especially for the more recently discovered antibodies), we expect a further rise in incidence in the future.

Comparison of our incidence rates with those reported by others is challenging because of differences in inclusion criteria. Smaller population-based studies on PNS epidemiology in Olmsted County (Minnesota, United States)5 and northeastern Italy6 reported incidence rates of 6.0 and 8.9 per million person-years, respectively, considerably higher than 2.61 per million person-years in our IA subgroup (which includes all high-risk onconeuronal antibodies). However, these studies included patients based on clinical PNS diagnostic criteria2,26 irrespective of antibody status, and only 18 of 28 (64.3%) and 23 of 89 (25.8%) patients with PNS in their respective cohorts were antibody-positive. Furthermore, 6 (21.4%) of 28 patients in the former study had thymic tumors with associated AChR antibody–positive myasthenia gravis,5 which were excluded from our cohort. Similarly, an earlier study on encephalitis epidemiology in Olmsted County reported an incidence rate of 8.0 per million person-years for AIE (again, considerably higher than 2.96 per million person-years in our EA subgroup) but also included seronegative AIE, ADEM, and Hashimoto encephalitis.3

Conversely, another study reported a lower incidence rate of 3.2 per million person-years (95% CI: 2.9–3.4) for AIE/PNS in France between 2016 and 2018,4 compared with the 5.57 per million person-years (95% CI 5.16–6.08) we found. However, this study excluded patients with LEMS or anti-VGCC and only included patients in their antibody-positive AIE subgroup that fulfilled 2016 diagnostic criteria for AIE, thereby excluding nonencephalitic phenotypes associated with—among others—GAD65 (cerebellar ataxia, SPS, chronic epilepsy) and Caspr2 (cerebellar ataxia, Morvan syndrome) that make up a small but significant part of our cohort.

In 2020, the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, we observed the highest absolute number of new AIE/PNS diagnoses (116) while fewer samples were tested than in the years before. Viral infection can be a trigger for autoimmunity, including autoimmune encephalitis,27 and SARS-CoV-2 infection has been associated with increased risk of various autoimmune diseases,28-30 possibly explaining this increase in incidence. AIE/PNS incidence rates dropping again in 2021 to pre-COVID-19 rates while the number of SARS-CoV-2 infections in the Netherlands remained roughly the same might be explained by less virulent SARS-CoV-2 variants taking over in this period. However, the B.1.1.7 and B.1.617.2 variants of SARS-CoV-2, the dominant virus strains in the Netherlands during the first and second half of 2021, respectively, have been shown to elicit a more robust inflammatory response than earlier variants, thus casting doubt on this hypothesis.

Furthermore, a recent study showed that the proportion of positive LGI1 antibody test results (but not those of antibodies against NMDAR, MOG, AQP4, and Caspr2) was significantly reduced during the first UK pandemic lockdown in 2020 when compared with the same time window in 2019.31 The authors suggested that reduced exposure to infectious agents during lockdown might explain this result. We did not find significant differences in incidence rates for NMDAR-AIE, LGI1-AIE, or Caspr2-AIE when comparing 2019 and 2020 (data not shown). However, measures to prevent viral spread were less stringent in the Netherlands than in the United Kingdom during this period.

The major strength of this study is the participation of all Dutch centers that offer neuronal antibody testing. In this way, we could assemble a large cohort of almost 600 patients, and we were able to ensure nationwide coverage for all but 2 antibodies, optimizing accuracy of the reported epidemiologic data. Over 90% of tested samples and over half of the patients with AIE/PNS were evaluated in person at our national referral center, which allowed for confirmation of positive results by independent techniques, detailed clinical phenotyping, and diagnostic confirmation based on ample subspecialty expertise. For patients not seen in person at the national referral center, every effort was made to collect relevant clinical data from the treating physicians.

Apart from these strengths, this study also has some limitations.

First, we used expert opinion diagnosis of AIE/PNS as a gold standard to evaluate antibody test performance. While this might be considered subjective, we believe that this is the best alternative because of the lack of a true gold standard. We only adjudicated AIE/PNS diagnosis in the presence of clinical syndromes that are clearly established as associated with the respective antibodies (eTable 2), thereby allowing for reproducibility of our results in different cohorts.

Second, we did not collect clinical information on all 17,719 patients testing negative. In theory, we cannot exclude the possibility that some of these patients did have AIE/PNS but tested false negative, leading to slightly lower sensitivities than reported. However, when there is a considerable clinical suspicion of AIE/PNS, patients are often discussed with or seen at our national referral center and additional testing with IHC, in-house CBA, or hippocampal live neurons is performed when commercial CBA results are negative. Therefore, we do have access to the clinical information of a significant part of these patients with negative antibody tests (certainly the ones with high clinical suspicion), and we are confident that we did miss very few (if any) diagnoses in this group because of false-negative commercial tests.

Third, despite our efforts to obtain nationwide coverage for all antibodies, this was not possible for anti-GAD65 and anti-VGCC. As a national referral site, we expect to have been consulted about—and, therefore, be aware of—most patients with GAD65 autoimmunity related to limbic encephalitis or epilepsy, even if they were not tested at our center. However, because the national referral center for LEMS is located elsewhere, it is plausible that we did not identify all patients with anti-VGCC–related neurologic syndromes. Therefore, our incidence rate of 0.25 (95% CI: 0.16–0.37) per million person-years is likely an underestimation. Indeed, a previous study on the epidemiology of LEMS in the Netherlands (excluding other anti-VGCC–related neurologic syndromes such as cerebellar degeneration and including a few patients with LEMS without VGCC antibodies) found a slightly higher annual incidence of 0.4 (0.3–0.7) per million.32

Finally, testing for antibodies against GFAP and KLHL11 only became available in June 2019 and July 2020, respectively. While stored samples of suspected cases from earlier years were tested retrospectively for these antibodies, incidence rates for these are probably an underestimation.

In addition, we did not collect data on ethnicity of patients. Earlier studies have pointed out differences in NMDAR encephalitis incidence between populations with different ethnic backgrounds, with a higher incidence in Black and Hispanic populations.33 While it is unclear whether this also holds true for other AIE subtypes, caution is advised when extrapolating the incidence rates reported in our study to populations with a different ethnic composition.

In conclusion, this study provides robust population-based epidemiologic data for antibody-associated AIE/PNS and shows a trend toward increasing incidence over time (persisting during the COVID-19 pandemic, despite a drop in the number of tests performed). Furthermore, this study provides sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV for many of the commercially used antibody tests. Although almost all tests had high sensitivity (when used in the correct sample type) and high specificity, PPV can still be low. In patients with AIE/PNS, positive antibodies can be confirmed by multiple independent techniques while unconfirmed antibodies are mostly not clinically relevant and are found in a heterogeneous group of patients with both inflammatory and noninflammatory diseases.

These results reiterate some crucial caveats concerning neuronal autoantibody testing: (1) it is necessary to always interpret antibody test results in conjunction with clinical information; (2) positive results on commercial tests should be confirmed with independent (tissue-based) techniques; (3) the correct sample type should be tested, and when in doubt, both serum and CSF should be sent for testing. In this way, we can minimize the risk of false-positive, false-negative, and clinically irrelevant results.
