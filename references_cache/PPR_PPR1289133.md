# Effects of Growth Hormone Therapy on Sleep-Disordered Breathing and Metabolic Parameters in Patients with Prader–Willi Syndrome According to Genetic Subtype
**Authors:** Akın O, YAZARLI E, ULU NK, Sunman B, Emet DC, ÖZER E, Yardımcı G, KOÇER M, TEKİN V, GÜNEŞ SO, Torun D, YETKİN S

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>Objective  Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS) is frequently associated with sleep-disordered breathing, and the respiratory effects of recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) therapy are debated. This study assessed the anthropometric, metabolic, and polysomnographic outcomes of rhGH therapy in children with PWS and investigated whether these outcomes varied according to molecular subtype. Methods  This retrospective cohort study included 66 children with genetically confirmed PWS who received rhGH therapy for a minimum of 12 months. Patients were classified as having either a paternal 15q11–q13 deletion or maternal uniparental disomy (mUPD); deletion cases were further categorized as type I, type II, or atypical. Anthropometric and metabolic parameters were measured at baseline and after 12 months. Polysomnography was conducted prior to treatment and repeated after 3–6 months. Longitudinal polysomnographic analyses included 39 patients with a baseline apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) less than 5 events per hour who did not require additional respiratory intervention. Results  The median age at initiation of treatment was 3.53 years, with 32 patients (49%) identified as female. Forty-five patients (68.3%) exhibited a paternal deletion. After 12 months, the height standard deviation score increased significantly (P < 0.001), while weight and body mass index standard deviation scores did not change significantly. Glucose and lipid parameters remained stable. In the longitudinal polysomnographic analysis, both total AHI and obstructive AHI increased significantly during the first 3–6 months of treatment, primarily among patients with deletions rather than those with mUPD. Within the deletion group, patients with type II deletions demonstrated significant increases in both total and obstructive AHI, whereas no significant longitudinal deterioration was observed in patients with type I deletions. Conclusion  After one year of rhGH therapy, linear growth improved without adversely affecting short-term metabolic outcomes in children with PWS. However, early obstructive respiratory events increased, particularly among patients with type II deletions. These findings indicate that molecular subtype may influence the respiratory response to rhGH and support the need for close polysomnographic monitoring during the initial six months of treatment.</p>

Introduction
Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS) is a rare genetic disorder caused by abnormal imprinting, affecting
approximately 1 in 20,000 to 30,000 live births. It is marked by hypothalamic dysfunction with hormone
deﬁciencies, abnormal body composition, developmental delays, and hyperphagia, which can lead to
obesity (1). It results from the absence of expression of paternally inherited genes within chromosome
15q11–q13, most commonly caused by paternal deletions ( ∼ 65–70%) or maternal uniparental disomy
(mUPD) ( ∼ 20–30%). Imprinting defects account for fewer than 5% of cases (2). There are 2 types of
15q11-q13 deletions: Type I, which involves the proximal 15q breakpoint (BP1) and a distal 15q
breakpoint (BP3), or Type II, which involves a smaller deletion with a second proximal breakpoint (BP2)
and a distal BP3 (3). These molecular subtypes result in the same clinical diagnosis; However, some
studies indicate they are linked to variations in behavioral, cognitive, and metabolic characteristics (4)
Recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) therapy is the current standard treatment for children with
PWS. It promotes increased linear growth and lean muscle mass, reduces total body fat, and can
inﬂuence cognitive performance (5). Although its eﬃcacy and overall safety have been well established,
PWS is a rare disorder and most available studies have been limited by relatively small and genetically
heterogeneous cohorts. Therefore, ﬁndings from relatively large, well-characterized single-center cohorts
remain valuable to further characterize the anthropometric, metabolic, and respiratory effects of rhGH
therapy (6).
Children with PWS also frequently experience sleep-related breathing disorders (SRDB), including central
sleep apnea (CSA), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and nocturnal hypoventilation (7, 8). Consequently,
polysomnography (PSG) is advised both before starting and after beginning rhGH therapy. In the
literature, Conﬂicting data exist regarding the effect of GH treatment on polysomnography (PSG) results
in PWS. Some evidence indicates that GH therapy may temporarily worsen SRDB (9). It is proposed that
GH contributes to adenotonsillar hypertrophy, increased ﬂuid retention, and soft-tissue edema factors
driven by abnormal renin activity—that collectively lead to upper airway obstruction (10, 11, 12). However,

whether the respiratory effects of rhGH differ according to the underlying molecular subtype has not
been systematically investigated.
Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the anthropometric, metabolic, and polysomnographic effects of one
year of rhGH therapy in a relatively large, single-center cohort of genetically well-characterized children
with PWS. In addition, we investigated whether treatment responses and polysomnographic outcomes,
differed according to the underlying molecular subtype.
Materials and methods
Patients and Study Design
This retrospective cohort study reviewed the medical records of 149 genetically conﬁrmed patients with
PWS who were evaluated at the Pediatric Endocrinology Department of Gülhane Training and Research
Hospital from January 2017 to December 2025.Among these, 66 patients who had received rhGH
therapy for at least 12 months and had complete baseline and follow-up clinical data were included in
the study. All treatments were performed in accordance with the latest international consensus
guidelines for the diagnosis and management of PWS (13). Patients were categorized according to
molecular subtype (deletion and mUPD), and the deletion group was further classiﬁed into Type I and
Type II deletions. Data on anthropometric, biochemical, and metabolic parameters were recorded before
the start of rhGH treatment, 3–6 months after treatment initiation, and again at follow-up visits 12
months later. All patients underwent baseline polysomnographic (PSG) evaluation before initiation of
rhGH therapy. For longitudinal PSG analyses, only patients with a baseline apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) 
< 5 events/hour who did not require additional medical or surgical intervention during follow-up and who
underwent repeat PSG were included. Consequently, longitudinal PSG analyses were performed in 39
patients to evaluate changes in sleep parameters following rhGH therapy.
Study design
Genetic analysis
Peripheral blood samples for genotypic subtyping and diagnosis of PWS were obtained from 149
patients. Genomic DNA was extracted with the PureLink Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad,
California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. To conﬁrm PWS diagnosis, DNA methylation
analysis was used as the initial diagnostic step. The methylation status of the 15q11-q13 region was
assessed via methylation-speciﬁc multiplex ligation-dependent probe ampliﬁcation (MS-MLPA) (14). For
PWS diagnosis, we employed MS-MLPA ME028 kits from MRC-Holland (Amsterdam, The Netherlands),
adhering to their instructions. The fragments were separated using the 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Applied
Biosystems, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed with Coffalyser software (MRC-Holland). If methylation patterns
suggested PWS, additional molecular tests were conducted to identify the exact genetic cause. These
included high-resolution karyotyping, FISH (Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization), single nucleotide
Page 5/20

polymorphism (SNP) microarray, to perform deletion and UPD subtyping. SNP microarray analysis was
conducted with the Inﬁnium Global Screening Array and Cytogenetics-24 v1.0 BeadChip following the
manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA). The Inﬁnium Global Screening Array
contains approximately 7 million genome-wide probes and is utilized for analyzing chromosome
structure, copy number variations, and segregation. Image retrieval was conducted with an iScan System
(Illumina), and molecular karyotype analysis used GenomeStudio software (Illumina). For patients
without deletions, DNA polymorphism analysis was conducted to differentiate UPD from imprinting
defects. Imprinting defects were diagnosed after mUPD was ruled out.
Growth Hormone Therapy
Before initiating rhGH therapy, patients underwent anthropometric measurements, biochemical tests,
and polysomnography to screen for SRDB. The treatment began at 0.5 mg/m²/day and was increased to
1.0 mg/m²/day based on clinical response and serum IGF-1 levels. Serum IGF-1 was checked every three
months, with doses adjusted to keep IGF-1 levels below + 2 SDS.
Anthropometric Assessment
Anthropometric measurements were obtained every three months during follow-up by trained healthcare
professionals using standardized techniques. In children over 2 years old, body weight was recorded
using a calibrated digital scale, while standing height was measured with a Harpenden stadiometer
(Holtain Ltd., Crosswell, UK). Recumbent length was measured in children younger than 2 years. Body
mass index (BMI) was determined by dividing weight by height squared (kg/m²). Height SDS, weight SDS,
and BMI SDS were calculated using the Turkish national reference standards (Neyzi et al.) with Endo-C
software (15).
Biochemical Assessment
Venous blood samples were obtained after an overnight fast and analyzed at Gülhane Training and
Research Hospital's Biochemistry Laboratory. Tests included fasting plasma glucose, fasting insulin,
HbA1c, total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-C, LDL-C, and serum IGF-1. Insulin resistance was assessed
using the HOMA-IR formula: fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL) / 405. Serum IGF-1 levels
were converted to age- and sex-speciﬁc SDS values using validated reference data.
Polysomnographic Assessment
Polysomnographic studies were conducted to evaluate SRDB. PSG recordings were performed in the
Sleep Laboratory of Gülhane Training and Research Hospital. PSG recordings were performed using the
Compumedıcs Greal 4K ampliﬁer system in accordance with standard montage guidelines. Recordings
included electroencephalography [EEG; F3-M2, F4-M1, C3-M2, C4-M1, O1-M2, O2-M1], electrooculography
(EOG), chin electromyography (EMG), anterior tibial EMG, electrocardiography (ECG), oronasal thermistor,
nasal airﬂow cannula, thoracic and abdominal respiratory effort bands, pulse oximetry, and body position
channels.
Page 6/20

Respiratory events were scored following the pediatric criteria in the American Academy of Sleep
Medicine (AASM) Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events (Version 3). Apneas were
detected when airﬂow, measured via the oronasal thermal sensor, decreased by at least 90% from
baseline for at least two breaths. These apneas were classiﬁed as obstructive, central, or mixed
depending on whether inspiratory effort was present or absent. A central apnea was scored when the
reduction in airﬂow was accompanied by absent inspiratory effort throughout the event and either
persisted for at least 20 seconds or lasted for at least two missed breaths in association with an oxygen
desaturation of ≥ 3%, an EEG arousal, or bradycardia in infants. Hypopneas were identiﬁed when the
nasal pressure signal dropped by at least 30% from baseline for at least two breaths and was linked to
either a minimum 3% oxygen desaturation or an EEG arousal.
The apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) was determined by dividing the total number of apneas—covering
obstructive, central, and mixed types—and hypopneas by total sleep time in hours, resulting in a value
expressed as events per hour of sleep. Sleep apnea–hypopnea severity was categorized based on the
apnea–hypopnea index (AHI): normal (< 1 event/h), mild (1 to < 5 events/h), moderate (5 to < 10
events/h), and severe (≥ 10 events/h). These classiﬁcations align with standard pediatric sleep medicine
criteria. An AHI greater than 1 event per hour was deemed suﬃcient for diagnosing sleep apnea (16).
Mild sleep apnea was characterized by an AHI of 1–5 events per hour, and patients with this level of
severity typically began GH therapy (17). Patients with an AHI > 5 events/hour were evaluated by
pediatric pulmonology and otolaryngology specialists, and GH therapy was initiated after appropriate
medical and/or surgical treatment. Follow-up polysomnography (PSG) studies were repeated within 3–6
months.
Ethics
The Institutional Ethics Committee of Gülhane Training and Research Hospital approved the study
protocol. The research was carried out following the Declaration of Helsinki. Since it was a retrospective
study, the ethics committee waived the need for written informed consent.
Statistical Analyses
Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp.,
Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test,
complemented by visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Data are presented as mean ± SD for
normally distributed variables and as median with interquartile range (25th-75th percentile) for non-
normal data. Categorical variables are shown as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between
independent groups employed the Student's t test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on data
distribution. Within-group comparisons utilized either the paired Student's t test or the Wilcoxon signed-
rank test. Categorical variable comparisons were made using the χ² test or Fisher's exact test as
suitable. All tests were two-sided, with a signiﬁcance threshold set at P < 0.05.

mUPD. Among patients with deletions, 16 (35.5%) had type   deletions, 19 (42.3%) had type II deletions,
and 10 (22.2%) had atypical deletions (Table 1).

Growth and metabolic outcomes
After one year of rhGH therapy, height SDS increased signiﬁcantly compared with baseline (p < 0.001).
However, changes in weight SDS and BMI SDS were not statistically signiﬁcant (p = 0.070 and p = 0.751,
respectively).
Metabolic parameters, including fasting glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol,
total cholesterol, and IGF-1 SDS, remained unchanged after one year of treatment (all p > 0.05). Although
HOMA-IR values increased during follow-up, the change did not reach statistical signiﬁcance (p = 0.054).

Outcomes according to genetic subtype
Baseline age and sex distributions were comparable between patients with deletions and those with
mUPD. After one year of rhGH therapy, no signiﬁcant between-group differences were observed in
changes in height SDS, weight SDS, or biochemical parameters. However, at the end of follow-up, BMI
SDS was signiﬁcantly higher in patients with mUPD than in those with deletions (1.65 ± 1.12 vs. 0.65 ± 
1.83, p = 0.047). In the ﬁrst year of GH treatment, a signiﬁcant increase in height SDS was observed in

1.20], p = 0.023). No signiﬁcant changes were observed in BMI SDS or metabolic variables in either
subgroup.
Among patients with deletions, baseline anthropometric characteristics did not differ between the type  
and type II deletion subgroups. Height SDS increased signiﬁcantly after one year of treatment in the type
  deletion group (1.08 ± 1.60 vs. 0.42 ± 1.20, p = 0.037), whereas HDL cholesterol increased signiﬁcantly

Polysomnographic outcomes
Overall, 39 patients (59%) had an AHI < 5 events/h, including 5 (7.5%) with an AHI < 1 event/h, whereas
27 patients (41%) had an AHI ≥ 5 events/h. The median AHI was 4.20 (2.50–8.50) events/h.
Among patients with a baseline AHI < 5 events/hour, total AHI increased signiﬁcantly from baseline to
the 3-6-month assessment (2.66 [2.10–3.80] vs. 2.80 [2.00–11.70] events/hour, p = 0.011). Obstructive
AHI also increased signiﬁcantly, whereas central AHI and ODI remained unchanged. In patients with
baseline and 3-6-month AHI values < 5 events/h, total AHI did not differ signiﬁcantly from baseline at the
12-month evaluation (p = 0.609).
Baseline AHI values were similar between the deletion and mUPD groups (Table 3). Analysis by deletion
subtype showed that baseline obstructive AHI was signiﬁcantly higher in patients with type I deletions
than in those with type II deletions (1,47 ± 0,54 vs. 0,26 ± 0,20, p = 0.001). In the deletion subgroup, total
AHI increased signiﬁcantly during the ﬁrst 3–6 months of rhGH therapy (p = 0.021), whereas no
signiﬁcant change was observed in the mUPD subgroup (p = 0.172) (Table 4).

Discussion
The primary ﬁnding of this study was the early deterioration of obstructive sleep apnea in patients with
PWS, which appeared even at the initial stages of rhGH therapy, despite no negative impact on metabolic
parameters like lipid proﬁle and carbohydrate metabolism. This deterioration in sleep disordered
breathing was more pronounced among patients with deletions when subgroups were analyzed by
genetic subtype. Notably, within the deletion subgroup, patients with the smaller Type II deletion
exhibited a more marked increase in both the development and severity of apnea following growth
hormone therapy. To our knowledge, this is among the ﬁrst studies to evaluate longitudinal
polysomnographic outcomes after rhGH therapy by molecular subtype.
The positive impacts of rhGH on linear growth, body composition, muscle strength, and psychomotor
development in children with PWS are well established and form the basis of current treatment
recommendations (18, 19). In prepubertal children with PWS, rhGH therapy has been linked to increased
insulin resistance, with some cases of impaired glucose tolerance emerging after roughly two years of
treatment (20). Analysis of data from the KIGS study revealed that hyperglycemia and type 2 diabetes
mellitus (T2DM) were infrequent among those receiving rhGH treatment for PWS (21). In contrast, other
longitudinal studies and recent systematic reviews have suggested that abnormalities in glucose
homeostasis are more strongly related to age, obesity, and the natural course of PWS than to rhGH
treatment itself (22. 23). As aligned with these reports, our study found no adverse effects on glucose
metabolism or lipid proﬁle after a year of growth hormone therapy. (24). We suggest that short-term
rhGH therapy is metabolically safe when patients are appropriately monitored.
Page 12/20

Treatment with rhGH has been shown to enhance respiratory function in some PWS individuals, mainly
by improving body composition and muscle strength, potentially reducing obstructive breathing issues.
Nonetheless, the evidence remains inconclusive (23, 25). Some studies indicate that the early phase of
rhGH therapy may worsen SRBD (26. 27). This may be attributed to rhGH-induced increases in IGF1,
which may contribute to adenotonsillar hypertrophy (26). Regardless of rhGH treatment, SRBD, including
both CSA and OSA, is well documented to occur frequently in individuals with PWS. OSA in patients with
PWS can be attributed to various factors, including obesity, narrowing of the upper airways, adenoid
and/or tonsillar hypertrophy, hypotonia of the respiratory muscles, reduced saliva production, and
scoliosis. In contrast, CSA is thought to result primarily from hypothalamic dysfunction (28).
Furthermore, the protective ventilatory and cardiovascular responses to hypoxia and hypercapnia are
impaired in PWS (29). Collectively, these abnormalities may adversely affect the physical health,
psychological well-being, and social functioning in individuals with PWS (26, 27, 30, 31). Several studies
have reported no detrimental effect of rhGH therapy on SRDB. One study found that although rhGH
therapy improved BMI z-scores, It did not inﬂuence the presence or severity of SRDB. (32). Similarly, no
deterioration in SRDB was reported over a 6-month period of rhGH treatment (33). Tan et al. (30)
reported that respiratory parameters remained largely unchanged in children with PWS receiving rhGH
treatment. Schaefer et al. (31) observed a decrease in the severity of respiratory events after rhGH
treatment in children with PWS. On the other hand, Khayat et al. (32) indicated that rhGH therapy might
exacerbate OSA in individuals with PWS, particularly in those with adenotonsillar hypertrophy. Pavone et
al. (33) observed that certain patients starting rhGH therapy may need upper-airway surgery or non-
invasive ventilation. According to Lecka-Ambroziak et al.(6) rhGH treatment may affect the severity of
SRDB in children with PWS. Furthermore, the risk of mortality appears to be increased in children with
PWS undergoing rhGH therapy, particularly during the ﬁrst nine months of treatment (34). Moreover, the
severity of OSA does not appear to correlate with age or obesity in PWS patients (35). These differences
could stem from various factors, such as variations in study populations and the methods employed to
evaluate SRDB. Additionally, the impact of rhGH therapy on sleep patterns is probably affected by
elements like the dosage, treatment length, pre-existing conditions, and individual differences in
response to therapy (26). Furthermore, most studies have not reported the distribution of genetic
subtypes within their patient cohorts. Our study suggests that differences in the distribution of genetic
subtypes may contribute to the variability in outcomes reported across studies.
Emerging evidence indicates that responses to rhGH treatment may differ by molecular subtype. While
earlier research generally shows similar growth responses between deletion and mUPD, differences by
genotype have been noted in metabolic outcomes, particularly insulin resistance and IGF-1 ﬂuctuations
(36). For example, Zhou et al. (37) reported greater insulin resistance and higher IGF-1 levels in patients
with deletions, despite similar growth improvements compared with those with mUPD, suggesting
differential biological responses to rhGH by genotype (38). Similarly, Mahmoud et al. (35) demonstrated
that molecular subtype inﬂuences several phenotypic characteristics in PWS, although overall growth
responses remain largely comparable across genotypes. In contrast, we did not observe signiﬁcant
genotype-related differences in anthropometric or metabolic responses after one year of treatment. This
Page 13/20

discrepancy could be due to differences in study populations, follow-up periods, treatment protocols, or
sample sizes. Notably, our ﬁndings contribute to existing research by indicating that genotype-based
variability may also affect respiratory responses to rhGH therapy.
The relationship between genotype and phenotype in Prader–Willi syndrome has been thoroughly
studied (39, 40). However, few studies have explored whether the molecular defect inﬂuences respiratory
outcomes or modiﬁes the response to rhGH therapy (26, 5). Many studies have shown that the deletion
subtype contributes to phenotypic variability; type I deletions are generally associated with more
pronounced cognitive impairment, behavioral problems, and compulsive traits than type II deletions (41).
More recently, Clinical observations of individuals with the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion, also known
as Burnside–Butler syndrome, have further highlighted the functional signiﬁcance of genes located
within this interval (42). In total, these ﬁndings suggest that genes located between BP1 and BP2 may
inﬂuence multiple biological pathways beyond neurodevelopment and contribute to differences in
respiratory regulation, upper respiratory anatomy, or susceptibility to sleep disturbances (43, 44).
Nevertheless, direct evidence to support this hypothesis is still scarce. Biologically, genetic differences
among deletion subtypes may inﬂuence lymphoid tissue growth, upper airway structure, or craniofacial
development, thereby impacting the risk of upper airway obstruction during rhGH therapy. Since adenoid
hypertrophy has been proposed as one of the primary mechanisms by which rhGH may worsen OSA,
patients with type I deletions may have a lower tendency for lymphoid tissue proliferation and adenoidal
growth compared to those with type II deletions. This hypothesis is supported, at least in part, by
previous reports showing that genes located in the BP1–BP2 interval are involved in immune regulation
and that deletions in this region are associated with recurrent infections, immune responses, and altered
lymphoid tissue development (45).
A key ﬁnding of our research is that respiratory impairment during rhGH treatment varies across
molecular subtypes. While anthropometric growth and metabolic responses were largely comparable
between patients with deletions and those with mUPD, worsening of obstructive sleep apnea was
particularly observed in patients with deletions and was especially pronounced in those with type II
deletions. Interestingly, previous studies evaluating respiratory outcomes after rhGH treatment have
rarely considered molecular subtype as a potential modiﬁer. This deﬁciency might partly account for the
inconsistent results in the literature on rhGH's impact on sleep disorders, since genetically diverse
patient groups may respond differently to the treatment even if they share similar clinical features.
Although these observations offer a plausible biological explanation for our ﬁndings, the underlying
mechanisms remain speculative and should be explored in future mechanistic and translational
research. Our ﬁndings enhance current knowledge of genotype–phenotype interactions in PWS by
indicating that molecular subtype may affect not only neurobehavioral and metabolic features but also
respiratory responses during rhGH therapy. If conﬁrmed in larger prospective studies, molecular
stratiﬁcation could play a crucial role in risk assessment prior to rhGH treatment, enabling early
identiﬁcation of patients who may need more intensive polysomnographic monitoring.

Several limitations of this study need to be considered. As noted, our study lacks a control group that
received no rhGH. Second, in this retrospective study, when patients were stratiﬁed by genetic subtype,
the sample size within each group became substantially smaller. In particular, when analyzing
polysomnographic data, patients who required positive airway pressure therapy during follow-up had to
be excluded. Consequently, the polysomnographic ﬁndings at the end of the ﬁrst year could not be
directly compared with the baseline data.
Our research ultimately validated the necessity of speciﬁc molecular diagnosis for every patient with
PWS. This practice is crucial not only for enhanced genetic counseling but also for gaining deeper
insight into the potential future phenotype.
Given that PWS involves a large genomic region, it is not surprising that different genetic subtypes
exhibit distinct clinical phenotypes. Larger, well-designed studies are essential to further characterize
these genotype–phenotype differences. Our ﬁndings also demonstrate that genetic subtyping in patients
with PWS is important for anticipating the clinical course during recombinant human growth hormone
(rhGH) therapy and may help guide patient monitoring. Based on our results, we recommend that follow-
up polysomnography during the ﬁrst six months of rhGH treatment not be overlooked, particularly in
patients with type II deletions.
