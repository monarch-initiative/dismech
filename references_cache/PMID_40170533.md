# Christianson Syndrome Family Experiences: Results From Caregiver Interviews.
**Authors:** St Pierre DG, Best CR, Elacio J, Kissel N, Morrow EM
**Journal:** J Child Neurol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1177/08830738251327619](https://doi.org/10.1177/08830738251327619)

## Content

Background: Christianson syndrome is a rare X-linked disorder characterized by intellectual and developmental disability, epilepsy, and regressions, requiring lifelong care. This study explored family experiences and treatment priorities from the caregiver perspectives. Methods: Qualitative semistructured interviews were conducted with 18 caregivers of 20 patients (aged 4-29 years) to discuss symptom onset, diagnosis, progression, coping, and priorities. Transcripts were thematically analyzed. Results: Initial symptoms included seizures, delayed developmental milestones, and lack of speech. Caregivers described sadness, anger, and feeling overwhelmed after diagnosis. Concerns included seizures, communication challenges, and sleep disruptions. Only half reported robust support networks. Coping strategies included exercise, work, and partner support. Despite challenges, caregivers highlighted the happy, affectionate demeanors of the children. Caregivers emphasized connecting with other families and prioritized treatments for seizures, communication, and preventing regressions. Conclusions: These findings reflect caregiver experiences, enhance knowledge of Christianson syndrome impacts, and highlight common challenges for families managing disabilities.

Christianson Syndrome (CS) is a rare X-linked neurogenetic disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in the SLC9A6 gene, which encodes the endosomal Na+/H+ exchanger 6 (NHE6) 1,2. First described in a South African pedigree in 1999 3, CS may be among the most common X-linked disorders, comprising 1–2% of X-linked developmental brain disorders. Current clinical prevalence estimates for CS range from 1:16,000 to 1:100,00 4,5. Males with this disorder present with intellectual disability, inability to talk, epilepsy, ataxia, postnatal microcephaly, hyperkinesis, and regressions 6. Symptoms of CS can become evident within the first few years of life once key developmental milestones are missed 3. Seizures also may begin around this age. Happy demeanor has also been noted in many patients, sometime resulting in an initial misdiagnosis with Angelman’s Syndrome (AS). In CS, phenotypic variability is reported in the literature 4,6: although there are core diagnostic symptoms affecting all or almost-all males with CS, the severity and impact of symptoms such as seizures and ataxias varies between individuals. Going into adulthood, many males with CS experience a slow rate of growth and challenges in maintaining a healthy weight 6. Reduced adaptive functioning, loss of motor skills, and decreases in hyperkinesis have also been noted with age. Across all patients, Christianson Syndrome results in severe disability, but a full understanding of the natural history of this disease remains limited.

Christianson Syndrome is a lifelong condition requiring around-the-clock care. This care is often provided by family members, such as parents, grandparents, and siblings. Managing and caring for a child with CS poses unique challenges. While it is clear throughout the existing literature of the past several decades that simply serving as a familial caregiver is associated with increased levels of stress and poorer health 7–9, issues specific to caregiving in CS may exist. For example, since CS is a rare condition, affected families are unlikely to personally know any other individuals with this condition at the time of the first diagnosis within the family. Resources for families affected by CS to learn more about the syndrome are limited, especially first-hand accounts. Furthermore, misdiagnosis at the onset of symptoms has also been reported, given that CS has clinical similarities to Angelman Syndrome, such as autism spectrum disorder features, developmental delay, and ataxia 2,10,11. Many families may have a negative experience during the diagnostic process, and the journey to diagnosis in CS is not well understood.

It is important to note that CS is both neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative. Cerebellar atrophy has been observed in patients 4,12 and animal models of NHE6 mutations 13. This may be reflected in the decline of motor function and associated ataxias which develop with age 6. Symptom onset, severity, and frequency could change relatively often over time. Children with CS experience functional regressions, losing abilities they once had such as verbalizations or mobility 14. This uncertainty about the future, alongside the lack of a supportive community, could further impact caregiver wellbeing and mental health.

Little is known about caregiver experiences in providing support for their loved ones with CS. The purpose of the current study was to use an in-depth qualitative interview approach to learn about caregiver experiences related to symptom onset, diagnosis, disease progression, and current concerns, as well as expectations for the future and treatment priorities. Recent guidance from US regulatory agencies calls out the importance of qualitative approaches in patient-focused drug development 15–18. Learning about the specific experiences that matter most to caregivers, as well as caregiver perspectives on treatment priorities and expectations for the future, will contribute to an important foundation for future work in selecting outcome measures in future studies – for example, using identified themes as a starting point in selecting and validating clinical outcome assessments in this rare disease 18.

Caregivers were recruited through an existing Christianson Syndrome international patient registry study. Participants met the following eligibility criteria: identify as a familial caregiver of a child or adult diagnosed with Christianson Syndrome; be at least 18 years of age; have access to a telephone or computer with internet access; and be able to communicate in English. The study was approved by an Institutional Review Board (at Lifespan Healthcare and Brown University, Providence RI), and all caregivers completed an informed consent process via video conference with the study team. Caregivers were recruited via email invitation and social media advertisements. Based on published guidance 19,20, twelve or more interviews were planned. However, all caregivers who responded to invitations within the study timeframe were permitted to participate, allowing for sixteen total interviews. In-depth interviews lasting approximately one hour were conducted in 2022 by an experienced research psychologist trained in qualitative research methods via video conference.

A semi-structured interview guide was developed by clinical and research staff with experience in Christianson Syndrome. A broad literature review was conducted to inform the guide. The discussion guide consisted of open-ended questions about caregiver experiences related to Christianson Syndrome, such as the journey to diagnosis, current concerns, coping mechanisms, and expectations about their child’s future. Additionally, the interview guide also included a symptom checklist, wherein caregivers were asked about frequency and severity of all core and secondary symptoms related to Christianson Syndrome 4, including verbal status, autism spectrum related symptom, seizures, and challenges with mobility and gait.

A single researcher conducted telephone interviews using this guide. Caregivers were permitted to speak freely on their thoughts at length; although caregivers received the same broad probes, not all topics were discussed by all caregivers. All interviews were audio-recorded with permission. Participants had the opportunity to ask questions or discuss anything with the study team both before and after recording. Participants were alerted when the recording was turned on and off. Audio recordings were transcribed near-verbatim.

Multiple members of the clinical research team collaborated to identify broad categories within the first 3 interview transcripts. Transcripts were coded for emerging themes in each category of the interview guide using principles of directed qualitative content analysis 21,22. Initial themes were generated by the research team from the content of the responses, rather than made to fit pre-existing themes. Responses were coded in an iterative process and merged into broader themes when necessary. Illustrative quotes provided here were selected by the research team to represent identified themes and provide depth to the analysis. Quotes presented in-text were selected to illustrate the most common theme in each category. Given the fact that many participants in the Christianson Syndrome family community know one another, the age and gender of participants is not provided for each quote to protect participants identity. However, care was taken to ensure even distribution of quotes taken across interviews (such that each participant is quoted at least once) to be thoroughly representative of different viewpoints, and to provide deeper context in each category. Qualitative thematic data were coded using NVivo software. Quantified data were analyzed using IMB SPSS. Demographic and core medical data for the family were available via the existing registry study. These data were reviewed by the interview team prior to each interview. Quantitative data were summarized using mean and range value, unless otherwise stated.

Sixteen interviews were conducted with caregivers of males (children and young adults) diagnosed with Christianson Syndrome (CS patients). Caregivers were interviewed individually, or as spousal dyads; some caregivers represented multiple people with CS (for example, two affected siblings). Therefore, eighteen caregivers in total were interviewed, representing twenty patients (Table 1). For purposes of accurate data presentation (not double-counting interviews wherein two people participated), all percentages related to emerging themes are given using the total number of interviews (16). Given the semi-structured interview format, interview length varied greatly, ranging from 36 minutes to 125 minutes; the average length of recorded portions of the interviews was 68 minutes. All caregivers identified as parents or grandparents of a CS patient or multiple CS patients; therefore, questions were phrased as ‘your child/children’ or ‘your grandchild/grandchildren’ during interviews.

When asked ‘Can you tell me about when your child started experiencing symptoms related to Christianson Syndrome?’, caregivers described many initially presenting symptoms, including seizures (n=11, 69%), missing milestones related to speech, mobility, or social skills (n=9, 56%), differences or difficulties with nursing or eating (n=3, 19%), not crying as an infant (n=2, 13%), and high muscle tone (n=2, 13%; Table 2).

Some medical practitioners demonstrated a limited understanding of the disease and offered minimal assistance or guidance to the parents.

Caregivers reported a wide range of emotions experienced at the time of their child’s diagnosis. Half reported feelings of intense sadness and grief, using words such as ‘heartbroken’, ‘devastated’, and ‘depressed’ (n=8, 50%). Other emotions included anger (n=5, 31%) and being overwhelmed (n=4, 25%). However, multiple caregivers discussed feeling a sense of relief at receiving a definitive diagnosis for their child (n=6, 38%; Table 3).

After discussion related to the diagnostic journey, caregivers were asked to discuss their current experiences. Specifically, they were prompted to describe their child’s symptoms, how these symptoms changed over time, and to identify the worst or more concerning symptom overall. They were also asked to consider how their child’s symptoms are overall compared to one year prior. Next, caregivers were prompted to describe the impacts of each symptom on the child’s day-to-day wellbeing. The most commonly discussed most concerning symptoms were seizures (n=6, 38%) communication challenges (n=5, 31%), gastrointestinal problems (n=4, 25%), sleep-related problems (n=3, 19%) and changes or difficulties with mobility (n=3, 19%; Table 4).

Half of caregivers endorsed having robust support networks, including friends, family members, and in-home nursing staff (n=8, 50%); the other half of caregivers felt under-supported. Of those, 6 people reported that although they are approved for in-home nursing care or other aides, they have difficulty actually accessing those services. The main reason cited was challenges related to hiring skilled care workers. When prompted to ‘tell me about some things you do to take care of yourself’, a wide range of topics were elicited. Coping strategies caregivers use to relieve stress include exercise (n=6, 38%), taking time alone/out of the house (n=6, 38%), spending time with their spouse or close friends (n=4, 25%), focusing on work (n=3, 19%), and relying on a sense of humor (n=3, 19%). Two caregivers (13%) brought up crying and allowing themselves to be actively sad as a form of catharsis; one caregiver (6%) mentioned the importance of attending therapy. Unprompted, most (n=14, 88%) mentioned the role of connecting with other families impacted by CS or other severe developmental conditions for support, camaraderie, and practical information and advice regarding their child.

Familial caregivers of children and young adults with Christianson Syndrome (CS) in this study described a wide range of experiences and emotions at the time of diagnosis; reported on current symptoms and impacts; discussed their own well-being and coping strategies; and identified their treatment priorities. These findings, while specific to CS, have relevance to other neurodevelopmental conditions which require lifelong care, such as profound autism, intellectual disability, Angelman Syndrome, and many others.

While exploring the caregivers’ experiences, several negative aspects emerged. Caregivers expressed frustration and disappointment with perceived failures within the healthcare system, particularly at the time of diagnosis. Structural support deficiencies were also highlighted, with many parents feeling they lacked adequate support. Additionally, caregivers voiced concerns about the potential impacts on siblings and worried about their ability to continue caring for their child with CS as they both age, including what will happen after they are no longer able to provide care.

Despite these challenges, there were notable positive takeaways from the study. It was observed that the well-being of most caregivers and patients was described as either stable or improving over the past year, with few reporting worsening conditions. Moreover, there was an abundance of love evident within these families, and caregivers often spoke of the happiness of their children with CS, including affectionate and cheerful demeanors. Deep connections were reported among caregivers within the CS community, indicating a strong social support network.

While this study offers valuable insights into the experiences of caregivers of individuals with CS, it is important to acknowledge several limitations. The relatively small number of interviews conducted could be considered a limitation in interpreting these data. However, CS is not well-studied, and the largest reported dataset within the existing literature included only 44 individuals. Therefore, this study represented a large proportion of the known families affected by CS.

Another methodological challenge to address is that interviews were all conducted remotely. While holding all interviews in-person is not feasible, given the rarity of CS and global distribution, a subset could be done in-person. However, virtual vs. in-person interview methods in rare diseases have been demonstrated to be comparable in findings 23.

It is important to note that the majority of caregivers interviewed were women (89%), perhaps reflecting a higher frequency of women taking on caregiver roles; collecting additional data from male caregivers would likely yield different thematic patterns. Furthermore, interviewing additional family members, such as siblings, would provide a more holistic view of the impacts of CS.

Given the hereditary nature of CS, future qualitative studies could include questions for caregivers regarding their understanding of genetics, experiences with genetic testing, and how their own genetic status (carrier vs. non-carrier) and other genetic information impacted them. This is especially important, as many caregivers may also be carriers of a mutation in the SLC9A6 gene. Carriers have been noted to have complex neurological phenotypes, sometimes emerging later in life, which could impact carriers’ own health and experiences as a caregiver 12.

Finally, the age range of the patients represented was limited, with the eldest in their late twenties. This reflects the recent discovery of the SLC9A6 and subsequent characterization of CS. Additionally, data from substantial cohorts of CS patients indicates reduced lifespan 6; however, more information about the natural course of this disease and symptom progression over time is needed.

Taken together, the findings of this study hold significant clinical and practical implications for healthcare professionals, researchers, and support organizations involved in the care of individuals with CS and their families. Enhancing accessibility to support services for caregivers, parents, and families is essential. This includes addressing various aspects of support such as financial assistance, emotional support networks, and access to specialized medical and therapeutic resources. Moreover, the study underscores the importance of community among CS families. Continuing to provide avenues for community engagement and connection can play a vital role in alleviating feelings of isolation and providing caregivers with a sense of solidarity.

Addressing the specific treatment priorities and most concerning symptoms described by caregivers will be key in clinical research of Christianson Syndrome going forward. Caregivers emphasized the importance of seizure control, preventing regressions, and increasing communication with their child. Researchers should take these priorities into account when pursuing clinical targets. Future directions of this work could use a mixed-methods approach combining quantitative measures of well-being with qualitative methods in clinical target selection. This methodology may yield particularly strong insights, especially when investigating knowledge and attitudes towards genetic information.

In summary, the experiences shared by caregivers of individuals with Christianson Syndrome during qualitative semi-structured interviews offer valuable lessons for healthcare professionals, researchers, and support groups. To the best of our knowledge, no qualitative work has yet been published in Christianson Syndrome. Despite facing challenges within the healthcare system and concerns about the future, caregivers showed resilience and strong bonds within their families and communities. While this study sheds light on the specific challenges and joys encountered by families, its implications extend beyond this syndrome, with relevance to caregivers of individuals with similar neurogenetic conditions. This work provides valuable insights that will inform future research planning and priorities.
