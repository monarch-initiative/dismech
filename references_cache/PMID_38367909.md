# Bone Marrow-Derived ABCC6 Is an Essential Regulator of Ectopic Calcification In Pseudoxanthoma Elasticum.
**Authors:** Brampton C, Pomozi V, Le Corre Y, Zoll J, Kauffenstein G, Ma C, Hoffmann PR, Martin L, Le Saux O
**Journal:** J Invest Dermatol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jid.2024.01.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jid.2024.01.026)

## Content

Physiological calcification of soft tissues is a common occurrence in aging and various acquired and inherited disorders. ABCC6 sequence variations cause the calcification phenotype of pseudoxanthoma elasticum (PXE) as well as some cases of generalized arterial calcification of infancy, which is otherwise caused by defective ENPP1. ABCC6 is primarily expressed in the liver, which has given the impression that the liver is central to the pathophysiology of PXE/generalized arterial calcification of infancy. The emergence of inflammation as a contributor to the calcification in PXE suggested that peripheral tissues play a larger role than expected. In this study, we investigated whether bone marrow-derived ABCC6 contributes to the calcification in PXE. In Abcc6‒/‒ mice, we observed prevalent mineralization in several lymph nodes and surrounding connective tissues and an extensive network of lymphatic vessels within vibrissae, a calcified tissue in Abcc6‒/‒ mice. Furthermore, we found evidence of lymphangiogenesis in patients with PXE and mouse skin, suggesting an inflammatory process. Finally, restoring wild-type bone marrow in Abcc6‒/‒ mice produced a significant reduction of calcification, suggesting that the liver alone is not sufficient to fully inhibit mineralization. With evidence that ABCC6 is expressed in lymphocytes, we suggest that the adaptative immune system and inflammation largely contribute to the calcification in PXE/generalized arterial calcification of infancy.

Physiological calcification is a complex metabolic process normally restricted to the bones and teeth. Under normal circumstances, calcium and inorganic phosphate concentrations are near saturation in most soft tissues, which requires strong calcification inhibition systems ( Atzeni et al, 2006 ). Abnormal vascular calcification is a common occurrence in aging but also results from common diseases such as diabetes, end-stage renal disease, atherosclerosis, and certain inherited disorders ( Rutsch et al, 2011 ). Such abnormal calcification is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular events ( Ho and Shanahan, 2016 ). In recent years, the identification of disease-causing variants in ABCC6 ( Bergen et al, 2000 ; Le Saux et al, 2000 ; Ringpfeil et al, 2000 ) and the characterization of its function ( Jansen et al, 2013 ) have provided new molecular insight into the regulation of ectopic calcification. Although the mechanism is not entirely understood, ABCC6 mediates the cellular efflux of nucleotides, notably adenosine triphosphate, which is rapidly converted into pyrophosphate (PPi) and adenosine at the cellular surface by the ectonucleotidases ENPP1 and CD73 (encoded by NT5E) ( Jansen et al, 2014 , 2013 ; Markello et al, 2011 ; Miglionico et al, 2014 ). The generated PPi directly inhibits hydroxyapatite crystal growth, whereas adenosine indirectly restrains tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase expression, which would otherwise hydrolyze PPi. ABCC6 deficiencies underlie the progressive calcification of elastic fibers in pseudoxanthoma elasticum (PXE) (MIM number 264800) and a dystrophic cardiac calcification (DCC) phenotype described in mice ( Aherrahrou et al, 2008 ; Brampton et al, 2014 ; Meng et al, 2007 ). ABCC6 is also likely responsible for the PXE-like manifestations seen in patients with β-thalassemia ( Hamlin et al, 2003 ; Martin et al, 2011 ). Generalized arterial calcification of infancy (MIM number 208000) is primarily caused by defective ENPP1, the key enzyme that generates PPi ( Nitschke and Rutsch, 2012 ). However, some cases of generalized arterial calcification in infancy are linked to ABCC6 pathologic variants (MIM number 614473), whereas some patients with PXE only carry ENPP1 disease-causing changes ( Nitschke and Rutsch, 2012 ). The central role of ABCC6 in PXE, generalized arterial calcification of infancy, and DCC is now well-established in humans ( Le Saux et al, 2012 ) and animal models ( Li et al, 2017 , 2014 ). Moreover, ABCC6 and PPi deficiency were recently implicated in the development of Randall’s plaque and kidney stones as well as in arterial calcification resulting from liver failure ( Laurain et al, 2022 ; Letavernier et al, 2019 , 2018 ).

The bulk of ABCC6 is found in the liver ( Beck et al, 2003 ), with the kidneys being a distant second in expression levels ( Douet et al, 2007 ). Some expression has been demonstrated in other cell types and tissues ( Beck et al, 2005b , 2003 ), but its relevance there has yet to be investigated.

The high hepatic expression of ABCC6 has given an overall impression that the liver is a critical organ in PXE. However, many aspects of the pathophysiology resulting from ABCC6 dysfunction are unexplained, and there are still important gaps in the mechanistic understanding of these diseases that are critical barriers to developing comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic strategies: (i) is the liver expression of ABCC6 necessary and sufficient for calcification inhibition? (ii) The level of plasma PPi is not sufficient to explain the wide range of calcification severity in humans ( Leftheriotis et al, 2022 ; Nitschke and Rutsch, 2012 ) and mice ( Le Corre et al, 2012 ; Zhao et al, 2017 ), and the search for modifier genes has not produced practical results ( Hendig et al, 2013 ; Hosen et al, 2015 ; Vanakker et al, 2013 ). Along with the peculiarity of skin manifestations in patients with PXE, which seems to coincide with subepidermal lymph nodes, the recent evidence of inflammation in PXE ( Mention et al, 2018 ; Omarjee et al, 2020 ; Peng et al, 2018 ; Rocour et al, Personal Communication) suggests that the liver-centric model might be incomplete, and thus determining how other peripheral tissues contribute to calcification regulation has become an important goal.

In this report, we specifically investigated the contribution of bone marrow–derived ABCC6 to the calcification phenotype of PXE both in humans and mice. From observational data and molecular and pathological analyses, we found that immune cells, most likely lymphocytes, dramatically influence the development of calcification in PXE.

Typical PXE lesions consist of small yellowish papules that coalesce into larger plaques over time and lead to large, redundant, and inelastic folds of skin. Most published descriptions of the skin manifestations of PXE report lesions on the lateral and posterior aspects of the neck, the axilla, and the groin ( Chassaing et al, 2005 ; Choi et al, 2000 ; Kaimbo et al, 2011 ; Marconi et al, 2015 ; Neldner, 1988 ) (also see www.PXE.org). There are other smaller affected regions such as the antecubital and popliteal fossae, the periumbilical skin ( Chassaing et al, 2005 ), and the chin ( Lebwohl et al, 2003 ). With the exception of the periumbilical region, which is not commonly affected, the cutaneous manifestations of PXE are mainly confined to flexural, areas and the reason behind these specific locations has thus far remained unexplained. However, we noted that the main skin lesions appear to overlap with regions of subcutaneous lymph nodes (Figure 1a). Even less suggestive PXE-specific features affecting the chin, which were described as prominent mental creases ( Lebwohl et al, 2003 ), are in close proximity to the submental nodes just below the chin.

To explore experimentally a possible correlation between calcified lesions and lymph nodes, the main nodes from Abcc6−/− and wild-type (WT) mice (n = 5), approximately matching the human nodes (Figure 1b), were sampled at various ages from 6 to 24 months. Histological analysis with Alizarin red staining showed extensive ectopic calcification in all lymph node areas studied (Figure 2). We found in the inguinal region the most significant mineralization, and this was detected as early as age of 6 months. As reported for other tissues, the overall calcification around lymph nodes worsened with age and primarily affected the intima of smallsized arteries, including those in the hilum of lymph nodes, although the large common iliac arteries showed both intimal and medial calcification (Figure 2c, f, g, and i–k). Moreover, we mostly detected calcifications in the hilum region, affecting the afferent arteries and also within the lymph node cortex (Figure 2b). Notably, in the inguinal lymph nodes in the older animals, we found early and more severe mineralization than in any of the other lymph nodes examined. In the cervical region, numerous calcifications were found in the parotid, submaxillary, and sublingual glands surrounding cervical lymph nodes (Figure 2k and l). Finally, we only observed relatively limited subcutaneous calcification in the regions of the inguinal and cervical lymph nodes (Figure 2d and m). No abnormal calcification was found in distal regions or in age-matched WT mice (data not shown).

Considering the extensive calcification of the lymph node regions, to further evaluate the link between calcifications and lymphatic system, we looked for the presence of lymphatic vasculature in a prototypic tissue developing mineralization in Abcc6−/− mice, the vibrissae ( Brampton et al, 2011 ). For this purpose, we performed immunostaining on muzzle skin samples with an anti–LYVE1 antibody, a marker specific to lymphatic endothelial cells. The results revealed a relatively high density of lymphatic vessels in murine vibrissae. These lymphatic vessels were found in the cavernous sinus in the deeper half of the vibrissae and also throughout the surrounding dermis (Figure 3a and b). We confirmed the specificity of the immunofluorescent labeling of LYVE1 by the lack of any colocalization with CD31, which is specific to vascular endothelial cells (data not shown). We also combined LYVE1 immunolabeling with calcein staining to compare the regions of the vibrissae collagen-rich capsule with hydroxyapatite deposits to the cavernous sinus containing the lymphatic vasculature. We observed that calcification was primarily associated, although not exclusively, with the capsule surrounding the cavernous sinus (Figure 3c). PXE is mostly characterized by elastic fiber calcification ( Neldner, 1988 ). Because multiple studies have structurally and physiologically linked elastic fibers to lymphatic drainage ( Gerli et al, 1991 ; Kajiya et al, 2007 ; Mortimer et al, 1983 ; Ryan and De Berker, 1995 ; Skobe and Detmar, 2000 ; Solito et al, 1997 ), we examined the colocalization between elastin, calcification (through calcein staining), and LYVE1 in mouse vibrissae. Interestingly, no colabeling between calcein and elastin immunofluorescent staining was observed (data not shown), but LYVE1 and elastin did colocalize in multiple areas around the vibrissae (Figure 3d, arrows). Remarkably, this experiment also revealed the near complete absence of elastic fibers within the capsule of the vibrissae, which is the only calcified structure in the whiskers of Abcc6−/− mice, although the surrounding tissue contained abundant amounts of uncalcified elastic fibers.

With an apparent relationship between the calcification phenotype and lymphatic-related structures, we also looked for differences in the network of lymphatic vessels in the inguinal region, because of its higher susceptibility to calcification (Figure 2), between Abcc6−/− and WT mice. We compared the results with chest skin, which is reasonably distant from any lymph nodes. The skin samples were immunolabeled with the LYVE1 antibody as well as with the CD31 antibody for control purposes. Immunofluorescent images were collected, and the fluorescent signals were quantified with ImageJ software (see Materials and Methods). The results were expressed as a ratio of LYVE1/CD31 with the underlying assumption that the overall dermal vascularization is unaffected by the absence of ABCC6. Interestingly, we observed that Abcc6−/− mice display a significantly higher density of lymphatic vessels than their WT counterparts in both chest and inguinal skin samples (Figure 4a and c). The LYVE1/CD31 ratio in the chest skin was increased 4.8-fold in Abcc6−/− mice compared with the WT value (P = .007), whereas the ratio was 5.2 times higher in the inguinal region of the ABCC6-null mice (P = .02). The difference between the chest and inguinal LYVE1/CD31 ratios for the WT and Abcc6−/− mice was not statistically significant (Figure 4a). We have repeated this same experiment using human skin biopsies from patients with PXE taken from an affected area and healthy control samples. We were able to detect 2.9-fold elevation in LYVE1/CD31 ratio (P = .016) in the PXE samples compared with that in the controls (Figure 4b and d).

We pursued 2 separate approaches to determine whether the calcifications in lymphatic-related areas observed in Abcc6−/− mice produced changes in lymphatic integrity or draining function. Because of the limited number of mice aged 24 months, we used younger age groups (aged 6 and 13 months) of WT and Abcc6−/− mice that still showed significant calcification in the lymph node regions. These mice were subcutaneously injected with Evans blue in the hind leg footpads or the cervical region to measure their lymphatic drainage efficiency. Thirty minutes after injection, the gross examination of the inguinal lymph nodes of both Abcc6−/− and WT mice revealed blue staining of similar intensity (representative images are shown in Supplementary Figure S1a). We also found that the Evans blue drainage (ie, lymph fluid drainage) into various lymph nodes of Abcc6−/− mice was as efficient as for WT mice in both age groups tested (Supplementary Figure S1b). The Evans blue quantifications were performed with the inguinal, lumbar, axillar, cervical, and renal lymph nodes.

Furthermore, we investigated the integrity of the lymphatic and blood vessels. Indeed, in the setting of inflammation, the lymphatic system is critically important, ensuring interstitial fluid homeostasis by draining extravasated fluid originating from increasingly leaky, inflammatory blood vessels. For this experiment, we monitored leakage under normal conditions and in response to an acute inflammatory agent (mustard oil). The Evans blue extravasation was detected as a blue patch on the inner side of the skin 30 minutes after injection into the mice footpads and the application of mustard oil (representative images are shown in Supplementary Figure S2a). Again, we examined 2 age groups of both WT and Abcc6−/− mice (6 and 13 months). We found that there was a significant difference between the 2 strains of mice at the older age of 13 months but not in younger mice aged 6 months (Supplementary Figure S2b). In the older age group, the dye extravasation under acute inflammatory conditions was reduced by 59% in ABCC6−/− mice compared with that in WT mice (P < .01). Interestingly, the lymphatic leakage was also reduced by 33% in Abcc6−/− mice aged 13 months (P < .05) under control conditions, without mustard oil. Overall, the treatment with mustard oil increased the lymphatic leakage in both WT and Abcc6−/− mice, but it appeared that whereas the induced leakage increased in WT mice with age, it was not the case in Abcc6−/− mice, which perhaps reflect changes in endothelial structure and/or response ( Pober and Sessa, 2014 ) or increase in arterial stiffness and vasoconstrictor tone of Abcc6−/− mice ( Kauffenstein et al, 2014 ).

In this study, we discovered abundant lymphatic vessels in the ring sinus of vibrissae, a mineralized tissue in Abcc6−/− mice. Remarkably, we observed extensive calcification in lymph nodes and surrounding connective tissues, primarily in aging Abcc6−/− mice (Figure 2). Because of the abundance of calcification within and around lymph nodes, we examined lymphatic vessel’s quality and quantity and found strong evidence of lymphangiogenesis in both human and mouse skin samples (Figure 4). Considering the recent reports suggesting the presence of inflammation in patients with PXE ( Mention et al, 2018 ; Omarjee et al, 2020 ; Peng et al, 2018 ; Rocour et al, Personal Communication), we hypothesized that the lack of ABCC6 in immune cells (likely lymphocytes) could play a role in the development of calcification. To test this possibility, we performed reciprocating bone marrow transplantations between WT and Abcc6−/− knockout (KO) mice and tested the effects on ectopic and dystrophic calcification in mice. We used 2 groups of mice to evaluate that transplant procedures were not interfering with calcification development (WT → WT and KO → KO). Two other experimental groups (WT → KO and KO → WT) were used to test whether reconstituting ABCC6 expression in the marrow (or removing it) affected calcification in target tissues: whiskers and kidneys (passive ectopic calcification) and heart (acute dystrophic calcification or DCC). We found that transplant procedures did not affect the phenotype outcome in the control mice. However, restoring Abcc6 expression in the marrow of Abcc6−/− mice produced a drastic effect on calcification development, whether chronic or acute. Indeed, we observed 38 and 30% reduction in mineralization in both whiskers and kidneys, respectively (P = .001 and .05, respectively), whereas DCC was decreased by 60% (P = .001) (Figure 5a and c). Deleting ABCC6 expression in the marrow alone did not produce any noticeable change in the calcification outcome. Remarkably, despite the significant changes in mineralization, the WT → KO bone marrow transplantations had no measurably impact on plasma PPi (Figure 5b). Note that we have previously observed a lack of correlation between plasma PPi levels and calcification severity ( Pomozi et al, 2017a , 2017b ), which is further corroborated by the results we obtained. Indeed, Abcc6−/− KO in a C57BL/6J background and C3H/H1J mice carrying naturally occurring inactivating variant ( Aherrahrou et al, 2008 ) display the same levels of plasma PPi but have vastly different vibrissae calcification at age 12 months (Supplementary Figure S3a and b).

As described earlier, on the basis of our results and those of Rocour et al (Personal Communication), it appears likely that ABCC6 is expressed in a lymphocyte lineage. We verified this possibility using isolated lymphocytes to detect mRNA and protein expression. Although mRNA and protein levels were minimal in total lymphocyte isolated from WT mice (Figure 6a and b), we were able to reliably detect both.

In this study, we notably challenged the liver-centric hypothesis in PXE. To this end, we examined the contribution of bone marrow–derived ABCC6 to the calcification phenotype using human and mouse samples. From histological, molecular, and pathological analyses, we found that bone marrow–derived ABCC6, most likely from lymphocytes, regulates between 30 and 60% of the calcification phenotype in Abcc6−/− mice.

Most published descriptions of the skin manifestations in PXE present them as located on flexural areas ( Chassaing et al, 2005 ; Choi et al, 2000 ; Kaimbo et al, 2011 ; Marconi et al, 2015 ; Neldner, 1988 ). These cutaneous manifestations appear restricted to flexural areas, whereas adjacent and other skin regions generally do not show visible signs. The reason behind these specific locations is unexplained, but we noted that the visible skin lesions predominantly overlap with regions of subcutaneous lymph nodes. Because skin manifestations can be highly heterogeneous in presentation and severity, with sometimes subtle, barely noticeable lesions ( Chassaing et al, 2005 ; Neldner, 1988 ), the overlap with regions of subcutaneous lymph nodes is also likely to show variability in humans. Indeed, elastic fiber calcification does not necessarily translate to visible skin lesions ( Vos et al, 2018 ) but the extent and context for which it probably does ( Lebwohl et al, 1993 ) suggested that the overlap between skin lesions and subcutaneous lymph nodes is more than just a coincidence. The significant presence of calcification in the human lymph nodes–equivalent regions in mice (Figure 1), including within lymph nodes (Figure 2), but not in the distal regions or control mice, was the first clue of possible pathophysiological connection with immune-related organs.

Since we first characterized its expression profile ( Beck et al, 2005b , 2003 ), the vast majority of studies on ABCC6 have logically focused on the liver because hepatocytes display the highest levels of expression ( Blazquez-Medela et al, 2015 ; Brampton et al, 2014 ; de Boussac et al, 2010 ; Douet et al, 2007 , 2006 ; Jiang et al, 2012 , 2010 , 2009 ; Li et al, 2017 ; Martin et al, 2011 ; Matsuzaki et al, 2005 ). Little attention was given to the pathophysiological relevance of other tissues, with the exception of a few reports on kidneys ( Blazquez-Medela et al, 2015 ; Lau et al, 2014 ; Letavernier et al, 2019 , 2018 ). It has been generally assumed that elevated ABCC6 expression in liver correlated with physiological relevance ( Jiang et al, 2009 ; Jiang and Uitto, 2006 ; Uitto et al, 2001 ). Over time, this has led to the general belief that the lack of an endogenous inhibitor of calcification originating from the liver (now known as PPi) was sufficient to cause the systemic manifestations of PXE ( Li et al, 2017 ). Some proposed an alternate cellular hypothesis, in which resident cells in affected tissues, such as dermal fibroblasts, play a prominent role ( Boraldi et al, 2014 , 2009 ). However, this has never gained much traction primarily owing to the limited ABCC6 expression in peripheral tissues and because most studies were conducted on isolated cells rather than on tissues or animal models. For nearly 2 decades, both hypotheses have coexisted, with limited direct evidence that conclusively verified either model. This changed somewhat after Ziegler et al (2017) showed that the contribution of peripheral tissues was in fact non-negligible, revealing at the same time the important role of tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase.

There was also the early publication of Beck et al (2003) and the follow-up reports ( Beck et al, 2005a , 2005b ) to consider. The significance of these articles was largely overlooked primarily because so little was known of PXE’s etiology at the time, which was still considered an elastinopathy. These articles revealed convincingly the presence of ABCC6 in many peripheral tissues and, remarkably, in circulating cells using a combination of molecular and histochemistry approaches, including in situ hybridization with full-length mRNA sequence. ABCC6 expression was found in circulating nucleated cells but also in erythrocytes. The surprise came from the detection of ABCC6 in red blood cells, but 2 subsequent studies provided supporting molecular evidence consistent with erythrocyte expression ( Douet et al, 2006 ; Martin et al, 2011 ). These 2 reports showed that HNF-4a and Sp1 transcriptional factors as well as methylation imparted a restricted tissue specificity to ABCC6, which is similar to the human sequence ( de Boussac et al, 2010 ; Ratajewski et al, 2012 ). However, they also discovered that NF-E2 was a major transactivator of the Abcc6 promoter activity. NF-E2, a heterodimer composed of two 45 kDa and 18 kDa proteins, is found almost exclusively in hematopoietic progenitors of the erythroid/mega/mast cell lineage ( Gothwal et al, 2016 ). NF-E2 is specifically involved in the transcriptional regulation of globin genes. The study by Martin et al (2011) not only added credence to the presence of ABCC6 in erythrocytes but has also provided a molecular explanation for the PXE-like manifestation in patients with β-thalassemia. Follow-up studies to determine plasma PPi in these patients and the exact role of ABCC6 in erythrocytes would be very useful. Of note, there are now some indications that the transcription factor HNF-4a is also active in lymphocytes, notably in inflammatory conditions ( Jeffries et al, 2011 ; Lei et al, 2022 ), which have been shown to drive ABCC6 expression ( Jiang et al, 2006 ).

In this study, we have demonstrated with animal models that bone marrow–derived Abcc6 has a large and significant impact on both ectopic and dystrophic calcification, responsible for an average of 38% of vibrissae mineralization, 30% in kidneys and 60% in myocardium. Bone marrow transplantations clearly showed that the liver-centric hypothesis is not an accurate description of the pathophysiology of PXE, as previously suggested ( Ziegler et al, 2017 ). The liver is a major regulator of plasma PPi levels and a determinant of ectopic calcification in PXE ( Jansen et al, 2014 ; Pomozi et al, 2017b ). The observation that plasma PPi does not correlate with calcification levels in the vibrissae of Abcc6-deficient mouse models further supports the notion that the liver alone is not sufficient to fully inhibit mineralization. Moreover, the prevalence of lymph node calcification, the apparent lymphangiogenesis in both mice and human samples, and the evidence of ABCC6 expression in lymphocytes (our data and those of Beck et al [2003 ]), in addition to the published literature reporting the presence of inflammation markers in mice and humans ( Beck et al, 2003 ; Brampton et al, 2021 ; Mention et al, 2018 ; Omarjee et al, 2020 ; Peng et al, 2018 ; Rocour et al Personal Communication), all suggested that the adaptative immune system largely contributes to the calcification phenotype in PXE. Note that a recent article by Casemayou et al (2023) confirmed the expression of Abcc6 in lymphocytes (CD45+) and reported an Abcc6-related immune modulation in the context of rhabdomyolysis.

In summary, the presence of lymphatic vessels in vibrissae, which to our knowledge was previously unreported, illustrates some of the differences how extracellular matrix is affected between patients with PXE and Abcc6−/− mice and reinforces the notion that proximity to lymphatic-related structures seems to promote calcification. More importantly, our data redefine the notion that peripheral expression of ABCC6 is far more relevant than previously imagined. An obvious limitation of this study is that a majority of our data were obtained with an animal model with a calcification phenotype that replicates the human phenotype but with some notabe differences, notably in the skin ( Gorgels et al, 2005 ; Klement et al, 2005 ). However, the study by Rocour et al (Personal Communication) provides strong evidence and validation that lymphocyte-mediated inflammation is a significant contributor to this process where altered purinergic signaling is also likely to play a significant role ( Kauffenstein et al, 2018 ). More work will be needed to better understand the molecular underpinning of this inflammatory mechanism so that targeted treatment in conjunction with other therapies currently under development ( Shimada et al, 2021 ) could be applied to achieve better care for patients with PXE.

C57BL/6J mice, designated as WT mice in this study, were derived from mice purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME). AbccC6tm1Aabb mice were generated on 129/Ola background ( Gorgels et al, 2005 ) and backcrossed into a C57BL/6J >10 times. These mice are designated Abcc6−/− mice in this study. Both male and female age-matched Abcc6−/− and WT mice were used because gender had no significant impact on results. All mice were housed in approved animal facilities at the University of Hawaii School of Medicine (Honolulu, HI). Mice were kept under routine laboratory conditions with 12-hour light–dark cycle with ad libitum access to water and chow. The University of Hawaii Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees approved these studies. Experiments were conducted according to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Biopsies from lesional skin of patients with PXE were obtained from liquid nitrogen storage and processed at Center of the Angers University Hospital (Angers, France). The diagnosis of PXE was based on a combination of established criteria for indisputable PXE, including clinically suggestive skin lesions, angioid streaks, and histologically proven fragmented and calcified elastic fibers on skin biopsy ( Lebwohl et al, 1994 ). Control skin samples came from discarded plastic surgery tissues from individuals without evidence of PXE or other pathology and were approximately matched for gender and age. The institution review board of the Angers University Hospital has approved these studies, and the participants have been given written informed consent when required in addition to consenting to the publication of anonymized images. Samples were obtained as part of a biocollection, clinicaltrial.gov: Identifier NCT01446380. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, which was indicated in the informed consent. All human samples were deidentified prior to processing

For myeloablative preconditioning, restrained and unanesthetized mice were irradiated with an X-ray tube irradiator (Faxitron CP-160). After receiving a sublethal dose (~750 rads) of irradiation), the mice were allowed to recover 24 hours before the transplantation assay.

Briefly, marrow cells were harvested from dissected femur and tibia and flushed out with PBS and gently pipetted up and down into PBS to break down as many cell aggregates. The marrow was filtered to generate a single-cell suspension prior to injection into the recipient mice to prevent the presence of any large aggregates in the circulation. Donor and recipient mice were aged about 8 weeks at the time of procedures at the average onset of calcification for Abcc6−/− mice ( Brampton et al, 2011 ). The recipient mice were placed on a warm pad to dilate the tail vein, and under isofluorane-induced anesthesia, the bone marrow cells were transplanted. One to 3 million donor cells (200 μl of PBS) were injected into the recipient mouse by tail vein injection. Four types of transplantations were performed: 2 controls (WT → WTand KO → KO) and 2 experimental (WT → KO and KO → WT). Transplanted mice were allowed to recover for 1 month for the mice selected to undergo DCC experiments, whereas those mice chosen to measure chronic calcification (whisker, kidneys) were kept for 5 months after transplantation. As controls, the presence of Y chromosomes was verified through PCR in few female mice that received marrow from a male donor.

Cardiac injury was instilled through transdiaphragm cryoinjury, as we previously described ( Brampton et al, 2014 ). Briefly, 10-second freeze-thaw injuries using a liquid nitrogen–cooled probe were applied to the heart through the diaphragm from a 10–12 mm incision on the abdomen. This approach limits the area of cardiac injury to a single cardiac location and offers a relative uniform size of the necrotic tissue and a very high survival rate (>90%). Shamoperated Abcc6−/− mice underwent the same surgical procedure using a room-temperature probe. Mice were killed by carbon dioxide asphyxiation 7 days after injury to ensure that the cardiac calcification phenotype was fully developed. Hearts were quickly removed, rinsed in PBS, minced, and placed into 0.15 N hydrogen chloride for 48 hours for calcium content measurements.

Cell lysates were prepared using a low-salt buffer as previously described ( Verma et al, 2011 ). A total of 30 μg of total protein was loaded per well on a 20% Criterion polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad Laboratories), transferred to nitrocellulose (VWR International), and incubated with blocking buffer (Millipore). The primary antibody included the anti-mouse ABCC6 (S-20, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and anti–β-actin (2A3, sc-517582; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). The secondary antibodies (Li-Cor Bioscience) were added at final dilutions of 1:5000–10,000, and fluorescent signals were detected using a Li-Cor Odyssey infrared imaging system.

T cells were prepared as previously described ( Ma et al, 2021 ). Briefly, spleens and lymph nodes (inguinal and axillary) were excised and homogenized to create a single-cell suspension. CD3+ T cells were isolated using a Mouse Pan T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Gaithersburg, MD), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified CD3+ T cells were counted using a Millipore Sceptor and then activated through the TCR in 96-well plates precoated with anti-CD3 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) (clones 145–2C11 and OKT3; 10 μg/ml) and anti-CD28 (BioLegend) (clones 37.51 and CD28.2; 1 μg/ml). Cells were incubated in RPMI-1640 media containing 10% fetal bovine serum (VWR International) at 37 °C with 5% carbon dioxide for 18 hours.

Direct histological visualization of calcium deposition after Alizarin Red S staining on paraffin-embedded sections was carried out on the left muzzle skin of each mouse as previously described ( Brampton et al, 2011 ). The level of mineralization in whiskers, hearts (minus the atria), or kidneys was quantified following several methodologies. We first used the Calcium LiquiColor colorimetric assay ( McGee-Russell, 1958 ) that measures directly the amount of excess calcium, which is normalized to the weight of the excised tissues, as previously described ( Brampton et al, 2014 ), and expressed in μg/dl per milligram of tissue.

The concentration of PPi in plasma was measured as described previously ( Jansen et al, 2013 ).

Tissues harvested for immunofluorescence were immersed in Tissue-Tek OCT Compound (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA), frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80 °C. Sections (6 μm) were cut from various frozen tissues. Air-dried sections were fixed with methanol for 30 minutes at −20 °C and rinsed in PBS. The sections were then processed for standard immunostaining and were immersed in mounting medium containing either propidium iodide or DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA). To image lymphatic vessels, the lymphatic endothelial cell marker, LVE-1, was used: rabbit polyclonal (ab33682) or rat monoclonal (ab56312). Blood vessels were detected with the vascular endothelial marker CD31 with a rat mAb (ab7388). These antibodies were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA). Elastin was detected using the rabbit polyclonal PR396 from the Elastin Product (Owensville, MO). For fluorescent calcein-based detection of calcification, frozen sections were incubated in a solution of Tris-Buffered Saline containing 0.1 mM calcein for 30 minutes and then washed several times with Tris-Buffered Saline. Slides were examined and imaged with an Axioscope 2 fluorescent microscope (Zeiss, Thornwood, NY). Individual images were collected and processed with Photoshop CS3 (Adobe, San Jose, CA). Immunofluorescence staining for LYVE1 and CD31 was quantified using the National Institutes of Health ImageJ software (http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij). Briefly, 10 or more color fluorescent images for each stain were randomly obtained with a Zeiss Axioscope 2 fluorescent microscope from both the chest and inguinal skin areas of 2 WT and 3 Abcc6−/− mice. The images were then converted to 8-bit grayscale and the number of positively stained pixels for LYVE1 and CD31 was counted and expressed as percentage of DAPI-stained pixels for each image to normalize to the fluorescent signal to the tissue cell number. The data in Figure 4 are presented as the average ratio between LYVE1 and CD31 for each set of skin samples.

Direct histological visualization of calcium deposition after Alizarin red S staining on paraffin-embedded sections was carried out on whiskers as well as on the inguinal, mesenteric, lumbar, axillary, and cervical areas from Abcc6−/− mice aged 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months and WT controls. Briefly, 5-μm slides were deparaffinized and incubated in an Alizarin Red S solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for up to 5 minutes, excess dye was removed with a 50% mixture of acetone and xylene, and the slides were mounted with Permount (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA). Selected sections from each treatment group were examined for calcium deposition, and images were obtained on an Axioscope 2 fluorescent microscope (Zeiss).

To compare the lymphatic flow in WTwith that in Abcc6−/− mice, a modified version of the Miles’ assay, previously published by Sugaya et al (2005) , was applied. Briefly, a 3% solution of Evans blue (Sigma-Aldrich) in sterile PBS was injected into the footpads of an anesthetized mouse at a volume of 1 μl/g of mouse. To quantify the lymphatic flow, several lymph nodes downstream of the injected area (ie, inguinal, lumbar, cervical, and renal) were harvested 30 minutes after injection. The lymph nodes were minced, and the accumulated dye was extracted by incubating the tissues in formamide (Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at 55 °C. The amount of Evans blue was then quantified by measuring the absorbance at 620 nm.

To evaluate the leakage of lymphatic vessels in WT and Abcc6−/− mice, another modified version of the Miles assay ( Sugaya et al, 2005 ) was performed. A 3% solution of Evans blue was injected at 1 μ/g mouse into the footpads of the hind limbs of an anesthetized mouse. After 5 minutes, a 5% solution of mustard oil, an inflammatory agent, in mineral oil (Sigma-Aldrich), was injected subcutaneously into the ventral skin. For negative control, mineral oil alone was injected. Thirty minutes after the injection of the mustard oil, the mice were killed, and the leakage was visualized as a blue spot under the skin (Figure 6). The stained region of the skin (or equivalent-sized control skin) was then excised, and its content of Evans blue was quantified as described earlier.

Real-time PCR was used to determine the level of mRNA expression of various genes in cultured macrophages and mouse tissues. Total RNA was extracted from lymphocytes using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Valencia, VA). RNA was converted into first-strand cDNA using a SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham MA). The level of gene expression was detected by qRT-PCR with a StepOnePlus Real-Time System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) using commercially available TaqMan probes from Life Technologies for Abcc6 and Gapdh.

Results are presented as the mean ± SEM. Mouse numbers used for individual sets of data varied between experiments and are shown in the figures. Student’s t-test was used for detecting differences between 2 groups by comparing the effect of 1 variable such as calcification or image fluorescence analysis. A P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Relative gene expression was calculated using the delta–delta threshold method ( Livak and Schmittgen, 2001 ). Differences between relative gene expressions >1.5-fold were considered statistically significant.
