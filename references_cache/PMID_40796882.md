# Gut microbiota contributes to polystyrene nanoplastics-induced fetal growth restriction by disturbing placental nicotinamide metabolism.
**Authors:** He Y, Li Q, Sun Q, Li H, Yu T, Chen M, Zhang G, Zhang B, Wang W, Ju S
**Journal:** J Nanobiotechnology (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12951-025-03650-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-025-03650-1)

## Content

Polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs) are ubiquitous in the environment, eliciting significant concerns about their possible risks to human health, especially reproductive health. Various reproductive toxicities of PS-NPs have been reported, however, information regarding the effects of PS-NPs exposure during pregnancy on offspring development and the underlying mechanisms remains limited. In this study, pregnant mice were orally administered PS-NPs (approximately 100 nm in diameter) at different concentrations (1, 10, and 100 mg/kg/day) for 17.5 consecutive days, from gestational day (GD) 0.5 to GD 17.5. The relevant samples were collected on GD 18.5 to investigate the intergenerational effects. The results indicated that PS-NPs induced placental injury and metabolic abnormalities, leading to adverse pregnancy outcomes. Specifically, PS-NPs exposure observably reduced the levels of nicotinamide (NAM) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) in the placenta, resulting in decreased ATP production, increased oxidative stress and ferroptosis. Meanwhile, PS-NPs disrupted the maternal gut microbiome, specifically manifested as a reduction in Lactobacillus levels and abundances of norank_f_Muribaculaceae, Turicibacter, Alloprevotella, Parabacteroides and Ruminococcus. Fecal microbial transplant (FMT) experiments demonstrated that the microbiota from PS-NPs-administered pregnant mice could similarly induce intestinal barrier damages and placental injury. Treatment with NAM effectively mitigated disruptions in placental metabolism and reversed the adverse pregnancy outcomes caused by PS-NPs. These findings highlight the novel role of the gut microbiota in PS-NPs-induced placental injury and adverse pregnancy outcomes, and suggest that NAM could serve as a promising preventative strategy against this intergenerational damage caused by PS-NPs.

Plastics products are widely utilized in daily life, with global production projected to reach nearly 500 million tons by 2025 [1]. Improper disposal results in the accumulation of substantial plastic waste, such as bottles, food containers, and plastic bags in the environment. This waste gradually breaks down into microplastics (MPs) and nanoplastics (NPs) due to the synergistic effects of ultraviolet irradiation, mechanical abrasion, and biochemical degradation [2, 3]. NPs are extensively distributed across various environmental compartments, including soil, air and marine ecosystems [4]. These particles can accumulate in animals and plants, ultimately entering the human food chain. Consequently, dietary intake has become a primary source of human exposure to NPs [5, 6].

The contamination of NPs has become an urgent issue of global environmental significance. Research on NPs has progressed from characterizing their distribution and physicochemical properties to investigating their toxicological effects on living organisms. Several studies in mice have demonstrated that exposure to NPs during pregnancy has the potential to induce spontaneous abortion, and adversely affect fetal development [7, 8]. Furthermore, NPs exposure during pregnancy has been shown to cause multiorgan abnormalities in offspring [9–11]. Notably, the placenta, a transient organ that supports fetal development by connecting the embryo to the mother, plays a critical role in nutrient and oxygen supply during pregnancy. Placental defects can lead to fetal growth restriction and other pregnancy-related complications. However, research on the mechanisms of placental damage and fetal developmental abnormalities caused by NPs exposure is still limited. Addressing these knowledge gaps is essential for evaluating the broader implications of NPs exposure on mammalian health and development.

A well-balanced maternal gut microbiota is essential for maintaining pregnancy and is closely associated with offspring development [12]. The maternal gut microbiota contributes to energy extraction from indigestible polysaccharides and plays a crucial role in regulating maternal and fetal energy homeostasis [13, 14]. The efficiency of placental nutrient transport is determined not only by trophoblast membrane barrier functions through endocrine signaling and specific transporters, but also by metabolic interactions with maternal microbial communities [15]. Consequently, the placenta must adapt to the maternal environment to support fetal intrauterine growth. Research has shown that NPs have adverse effects on the intestinal barrier and gut microbiota, thereby affecting host health [16]. However, the regulatory mechanisms linking the placenta and fetus development mediated by gut microbiota following PS-NPs exposure during pregnancy, particularly the role of specific metabolites, remains to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we attempted to explore the possible roles of gut microbiota in PS-NPs-induced placenta injury and fetal growth restriction, as well as the potential mechanism of gut microbiota regulation. The results indicated that PS-NPs exposure during pregnancy exerted toxic effects on the maternal intestinal tract and placenta. Gut microbial dysbiosis appears to be a significant mediating factor in PS-NPs-induced placental oxidative stress and ferroptosis, which may contribute to subsequent placental dysfunction and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Additionally, metabolomic sequencing revealed a significant decrease in the level of NAM in the placenta, and NAM supplementation effectively mitigated PS-NPs-induced placental barrier damage and oxidative stress.

The morphology of PS-NPs (Jiangsu Zhichuan Technology Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China) was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi Regulus 8100, Japan), and the size of PS-NPs was calculated via ImageJ software. In addition, attenuated total reflectance infrared (ATR-IR) spectrum of PS-NPs was accomplished on a Nicolet iS5 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer. Endotoxin concentrations were quantified using Kinetic Turbidimetric LAL Endotoxin Assay Kit (C2071, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The endotoxin levels of PS-NPs suspensions were < 0.5 EU/mL, which is below the safety limit for biomedical applications of engineered nanomaterials [17].

Special pathogen-free ICR mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from the Animal Research Center of Yangzhou University (Yangzhou, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Center Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (Ethical Approval Code: NJAULLSC2023001). Following one week of acclimatization, female and male ICR mice were mated at a ratio of 2:1 to obtain pregnant mice. The occurrence of vaginal plugs in female mice was recorded as gestational day (GD) 0.5.

A previous study reported that the global average human intake of microplastics ranges from 0.1 to 5 g per week, corresponding to approximately equivalent to 0.2–11.9 mg/kg/day [18]. For the mouse assay, based on the body surface area (BSA) standardization method, the human-to-mouse conversion factor is 12, resulting in an estimated microplastics exposure range of 2.4–142.8 mg/kg/day for mice [19, 20]. Therefore, pregnant mice were exposed to 100 µL of ddH2O containing 0 mg/kg PS-NPs (control group), 1 mg/kg PS-NPs (low-dose group, LD), 10 mg/kg PS-NPs (medium-dose group, MD), and 100 mg/kg PS-NPs (high-dose group, HD) through continuous gavage during pregnancy from GD 0.5-GD 17.5 in this study (Fig. 1C, n = 20 per group). 10 pregnant mice from each group were randomly selected and euthanized for the collection of placental and maternal intestinal tissues on GD 18.5. The remaining 10 mice in each group were allowed to give birth naturally, and their offspring were monitored until 6 weeks of age to assess the effects of maternal PS-NP exposure on postnatal development.

To investigate the protective effects of NAM (N814605, Macklin) against PS-NP-induced placental injury, pregnant mice were orally administered NAM at doses of 200 mg/kg and 500 mg/kg (dissolved in ddH₂O) throughout gestation. The 200 mg/kg dose was derived from studies showing that NAD⁺ precursors, including nicotinamide mononucleotide and nicotinamide riboside, effectively restored ovarian function and reversed oocyte aging in female animals [22, 23]. Fresh fecal samples were collected daily from pregnant donor mice in the ddH₂O group and the 10 mg/kg PS-NPs exposure group throughout the entire gestational period (from GD 0.5 to GD 17.5). and then feces were immediately mixed with 0.9% NaCl at a concentration of 1 g/10 mL [24]. The bacterial mixture was centrifuged (900 rpm, 3 min, 4 °C). Prior to transplantation, recipient mice were administered antibiotics (ABX) ocontaining 200 mg/kg metronidazole (S17079, Yuanye Biotech), 100 mg/kg vancomycin (S17059, Yuanye Biotech), 200 mg/kg ampicillin (S17018, Yuanye Biotech) and 200 mg/kg neomycin sulfate (S17028, Yuanye Biotech) for one week [25]. Following antibiotic treatment, female mice were mated with males for fertility. Pregnant mice were randomly divided into FMT-ddH2O and FMT-PS-NPs groups (Fig. 8A, n = 10 per group). Staring from GD 0.5, recipient mice received 10 mL/kg of the corresponding bacterial suspension during pregnancy. Throughout the fecal microbiota transplant period, the recipients were not given any PS-NPs. Placental and maternal intestinal tissues were collected on GD 18.5 for analysis.

Fresh samples of placenta and small intestine tissue were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The placenta and ileum were subsequently embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 3 μm slices. The placenta sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), while the ileum was stained with both HE and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS). The samples were observed with an Ocus digital pathology slide scanner (Grundium, Finland).

Placental sections were first deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by immersing the sections in EDTA buffer, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. After three washes with PBS, the sections were incubated with a recombinant rabbit anti-CD34 primary antibody (GB15001, Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) at a dilution of 1:200 for immunohistochemical analysis.

Total RNA was extracted from the ileum and placenta issue using TRIzol reagent (AG21101, Accurate Biology, Changsha, China) and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted on a Real-time PCR instrument (QuantStudio 6 Flex, ThermoFisher). The primer information was listed in Supplementary Table 1. The data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method to quantify relative gene expression levels.

Proteins from the ileum and placenta were extracted using RIPA buffer (G2002, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The proteins were spilted by SDS-PAGE and shifted to PVDF membranes. After being blocked with blocking buffer (G2052, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), the membranes were exposed with primary antibody (Supplementary Table 2) for 8–12 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were treated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C. Ultimately, the proteins bands were detected with an enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) system. Images were captured using the ChemiDoc™ Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (National Institutes of Health, USA) by measuring the integrated optical density (IOD) and area of interest (AOI) of each band. The optical density was calculated as the ratio of IOD to AOI, and the relative expression levels of target proteins were normalized to loading control protein.

The sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 (Illumina, San Diego, USA) following standard protocols provided by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Total genomic DNA from the gut microbiota was extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (51604, Qiagen, Germany). Subsequently, the concentration and quality of DNA were assessed. The amplification and sequencing of the V3-V4 hypervariable regions within the bacterial 16S DNA gene were conducted utilizing the Bio-Rad T100 System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The average read length was 424 bp, and the read length was 42,336. For species classification, the Silva138/16S_bacteria database was used, and the clustering method applied was USEARCH11-uparse. Finally, the bioinformatic analysis was performed to interpret the sequencing data.

The untargeted metabolomics were determined by Majorbio (Shanghai, China). The placenta tissues (100 mg per sample) were homogenized with 80% methanol using a high-throughput tissue crusher. The supernatant was separated using ultracentrifugation and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis. Principal co-component analysis. Differentially abundant metabolites between groups were identified with VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 as thresholds.

The suspensions of placenta and ileum tissue samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min. The homogenate was used for assessment of SOD (A001-1, Jiancheng, China), CAT (A007-1, Jiancheng, China), MDA (S0131, Beyotime, China), GSH/GSSH (A061-1, Jiancheng, China), NAM (YG041527, Meilian, China), NAD+/NADH (S0175, Beyotime, China) and ATP (S0026, Beyotime, China).

Bacterial strains were streaked from − 80 °C glycerol stocks onto MRS agar (M8330, Solarbio, Beijing, China) plates. Single colonies were then inoculated into MRS broth (HB0384, Hopebio, Qingdao, China) and cultured overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 160 rpm. The overnight cultures were subsequently diluted 1% (v/v) into fresh MRS medium containing various concentrations of PS-NPs (25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL). A volume of 200 µL of the bacterial suspension was added to each well of a 96-well microplate. The bacterial growth was monitored over 24 h by measuring the optical density at 600 nm using a multimode microplate reader (Spark, TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

The IBM SPSS 23.0 software was used to conduct the statistical analyses. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s protected least significant difference test were used to examine the data in multiple groups. Student’s t-test was employed to evaluate pairwise comparisons between two independent groups. The homogeneity of variances was confirmed by Levene’s test (P > 0.05), and the normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for 16S rRNA sequencing analysis, and Spearman’s rank test was used for correlation analysis. The results are exhibited as mean ± standard deviation (SD) values. The significance differences were showed as of *P < 0.05 and ** P < 0.01.

The morphological and structural characteristics of the PS-NPs were visualized using scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 1A). The mean diameter of the PS-NPs was determined to be 98.07 ± 6.95 nm, with the particles exhibiting a nearly circular shape. As shown in Fig. 1B, the structures of the PS-NPs were further examined via an ATR-IR spectrometer, revealing a peak at 3027.34 cm−1 attributed to the stretching vibration of the unsaturated hydrocarbon group on the benzene ring (= CH). The additional peaks observed at 1449.49, 2914.64 and 2852.88 cm−1 were associated with the vibration of methylene (CH2), whereas the peaks at 1602.33 and 1494.26 cm−1 were indicative of the vibration of the benzene ring skeleton (C = C). The peaks at 748.56 and 688.35 cm−1 were attributed to unsaturated hydrocarbon groups (= C-H).

Pregnant mice were orally administered PS-NPs at dose of 0, 1, 10, and 100 mg/kg from GD 0.5 to GD 17.5, as outlined in Fig. 1C. Intestinal and placental tissues were collected from a subset of mice on GD 18.5, while the remaining mice were allowed to give birth naturally. The development of the F1 offspring was monitored until postnatal day (PND) 42. Figure 1D indicated that the maternal final weight was markedly decreased in the MD and HD groups. Concomitantly, an elevated liver/body weight ratio was observed in the HD group, whereas no significant differences were found in the kidney/body weight or uterus/body weight ratios among the groups (Fig. S1A-C).

As illustrated in Fig. 1E-G, PS-NPs exposure during pregnancy resulted in marked growth restriction in fetal mice at GD 18.5, characterized by decreased fetal weight and crown-rump length in the MD and HD groups. The incidence of fetal growth restriction (FGR) was markedly higher in the HD group (Fig. 1H). However, no significant differences were observed in litter size or sex ratio among the different groups (Fig. S1D and E). Exposure to PS-NPs led to intrauterine growth retardation, resulting in a significant increase in mortality rates in the HD groups, which adversely affecting offspring development (Fig. 1I). By PND 42, body weights of F1 offspring were significantly decreased in both the MD and HD groups (Fig. 1J). The liver/body weight ratio was notably elevated in the MD and HD groups, whereas the heart/body weight, spleen/body weight, testis/body weight and thymus/body weight ratios were significantly decreased in PS-NPs-treated groups (Fig. S1F-N).

The placenta serves a pivotal role in mediating nutrient transfer between the maternal and fetal circulations. In this study, we further investigated the effects of PS-NPs on placental health. The placental weights significantly decreased at GD 18.5 in the MD and HD groups (Fig. 2A and B). Additionally, the placental/body weight ratio was markedly lower in the PS-NPs-treated groups, while no significant differences were observed in placental diameter (Fig. 2C and D).

Histological staining revealed that PS-NPs administration reduced the percentage of the labyrinth zone relative to the total placental area (Fig. 2E and F). We then proceeded to assess the proportion of the blood sinusoid area within the labyrinth zone and observed a notable decrease in the PS-NPs treatment groups (Fig. 2G and H). Additionally, markers of vascularization (CD34) and cell proliferation (PCNA) in the placenta were also examined. As shown in Fig. 2I and J, the optical density of CD34-positive cells was markedly downregulated in the MD and HD groups. The protein expression of PCNA was significantly suppressed in the PS-NPs-treated groups (Fig. 2K). These data suggested that PS-NPs exposure could disrupt vascular development in the placenta.

Moreover, the expression of key placental development genes, including eomesodermin (Eomes), achaete-scute family bHLH transcription factor 2 (Ascl2), fos-related antigen 1 (Fra1), and heart and neural crest derivatives expressed 1 (Hand1), was decreased in the MD and HD groups (Fig. 2L). The study also quantified the expression of tight junction proteins, including zona occludens protein 1 (ZO1), Claudin1 and Occludin, which may modulate the placental microenvironment and consequently affect nutrient transport efficiency [26]. Notably, the expression of the blood-placental barrier tight junction protein ZO1 was markedly decreased in the HD group, whereas the expressions of Occludin and Claudin1 were markedly decreased in the MD and HD groups (Fig. 2M and N).

The metabolism of placental nutrients is crucial for fetal development. Subsequently, we determined alterations in the placental metabolome following PS-NPs exposure. The partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) model and permutation test revealed a markedly distinct metabolic profile in the PS-NPs-treated group (Figs. 3A and S2A). Exposure to PS-NPs resulted in the upregulation of 36 metabolites and the downregulation of 60 metabolites (Fig. 3B). These differentially expressed metabolites were significantly enriched in various KEGG top-level categories and subfunctional metabolic pathways (Fig. 3C and D), suggesting their involvement in placenta injury induced by PS-NPs. Topological analysis indicated that pathways related to nicotinate and NAM, glycerophospholipid and alpha-linolenic acid were primarily affected (Fig. 3E). Based on the results from untargeted metabolomics and core metabolite analysis (Fig. S2B), the nicotinate and nicotinamide pathways may serve as potential biomarkers for understanding the mechanistic basis of PS-NPs-induced placental injury.

To further assess the influence of PS-NPs exposure on placental metabolism, we quantified the concentrations of NAM and NAD+ in the placenta. As shown in Fig. 3F and G, the concentrations of NAM and NAD+ were notably decreased in the PS-NPs treated groups. The biosynthesis of NAD+ involves nicotinamide nucleotide adenylyltransferase (NMNAT) enzymes, and the protein levels of NMNAT1, NMNAT2, and NMNAT3 were markedly reduced in the PS-NPs-treated groups (Fig. 3H-J). These results indicated that PS-NPs reduced NAD+ concentrations by downregulating the expression of NMNAT enzymes.

Abnormal mitochondrial energy metabolism is closely related to the oxidative damage. Further experiments revealed that the ATP concentration in the placenta was lower in the MD and HD groups (Fig. 4A) Superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels were substantially upregulated in the PS-NPs-treated group, whereas the GSH/GSSH ratio was downregulated (Fig. 4B and C). Meanwhile, the catalase (CAT) and malondialdehyde (MDA) contents were increased in the HD groups (Fig. 4D and E), illustrating that PS-NPs exposure can induce oxidative damage to the placenta. To further elucidate the role of the gut microbiota in mediating intestinal and placental damage, the FMT experiment was completed. Female mice were pretreated with ABX, mated with male mice, and then transplanted with fresh feces from either the ddH2O or PS-NPs-treated groups (Fig. 8A). Compared to the FMT-ddH2O group, maternal final body weight notably decreased in the FMT-PS-NPs group, whereas the maternal liver/body weight ratio was notably increased, and a similar trend was observed in the PS-NPs treatment groups (Figs. 8B and S3A). No significant differences were observed in maternal kidney/body weight ratio or uterus/body weight ratio among these groups (Fig. S3B and C). Mice that received gut microbiota from PS-NPs-treated donors exhibited adverse pregnancy outcomes, including decreased fetal weight and crown-rump length, similar to those observed in PS-NPs-treated group (Fig. 8C-E). Furthermore, recipients of FMT-PS-NPs displayed placental injuries, characterized by reduced placental weight and suppressed expression of genes related to placental development, including Eomes, Ascl2, Fra1 and Hand1, as well as genes associated with tight junctions such as ZO1, Claudin1 and Occludin (Fig. 8F-I). However, FMT-PS-NPs administration did not affect litter size or the ratio of placental/body weight compared to the FMT-ddH2O group (Figure S3D and E). FMT treatment inhibited cell proliferation, as evidenced by a significant downregulation in the expression of PCNA in the FMT-PS-NPs group compared with that in the FMT-ddH2O group (Fig. 8J). Consistent with the effects on placental metabolism, FMT-PS-NPs treatment also resulted in notable decreases in the concentrations of NAM and NAD+ (Fig. 8K and L). Additionally, FMT-PS-NPs led to a significant decrease in the protein level of NMNAT1 (Fig. 8M). Collectively, the data in this research indicated that FMT-PS-NPs can impair maternal placental function and lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Next, we analyzed the contribution of gut microbiota in PS-NPs-induced placental oxidation while elevating injury and ferroptosis. Compared with the FMT-ddH2O group, FMT-PS-NPs decreased ATP levels while disrupting the concentrations of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, CAT, MDA and the GSH/GSSH ratio. These disturbances were replicated by the administration of PS-NPs (Fig. 8N-R), suggesting that the gut microbiota of PS-NPs-treated mice could lead to decreased placental ATP content and increased oxidative stress. Additionally, PS-NPs-induced placental ferroptosis was replicated by FMT-PS-NPs, as demonstrated by the increases of TfrC and decreases in Slc7a11 and GPX4 at both gene and protein levels (Fig. 8S and T). Overall, this study illustrated that FMT-PS-NPs could result in adverse pregnancy outcomes and placental injuries.

As described above, PS-NPs treatment negatively affected the intestinal barrier, and FMT-PS-NPs also resulted in decreased expression of the mucosal barrier markers MUC2 and Klf4 (Fig. 9A-C) and tight junction genes ZO1 and Occludin (Fig. 9D-F). Additionally, we evaluated the effects of the gut microbiota on intestinal oxidative stress. These findings revealed that, according to the abnormal content of antioxidant enzymes, FMT-PS-NPs also resulted in oxidative stress in the ileum, characterized by increased SOD and decreased CAT content (Fig. 9G and H). In summary, these findings demonstrated that FMT-PS-NPs can induce oxidative injury to the small intestine and compromise its barrier function.

Plastic particles of various sizes can be ingested by organisms through multiple pathways, including bottled water, food packaged in plastic containers, and a variety of seafood. Upon ingestion, these plastic particles pose potential health risks. Previous studies have demonstrated the harmful effects of PS-NPs on multiple organs [30], establishing that such toxic damage can be transmitted to offspring [31]. However, the fundamental processes driving the intergenerational toxicity of PS-NPs are not yet fully understood. Several studies have demonstrated that gestational PS-NPs exposure adversely impacts both maternal and fetal development. However, there is still controversy regarding whether MPs/NPs can directly cross the placental barrier, which may be attributed to differences in exposure routes and variations in particle diameter and surface modifications [9, 32]. This study specifically investigates the indirect damage of PS-NPs to the placenta through gut microbiota-mediated mechanisms. Our results revealed that exposure to PS-NPs during gestation led to placental injury and metabolic disorders via the gut-placental axis, indirectly resulting in adverse pregnancy outcomes and restricted fetal growth. A key factor in this process appears to be the disruption of NAM metabolism in the placenta. Exogenous supplementation with NAM can effectively alleviate placental damage and adverse pregnancy outcomes, suggesting its potential as a candidate for preventing PS-NPs-related placental dysplasia and tissue damage.

The placenta is crucial for deciding fetal development as it serves as the bridge for nutrient exchange between the maternal and fetal circulations, however, placentas are highly susceptible to environmental pollutants. Studies have indicated that PS-NPs can cause damage to various systems, including reproductive organs, potentially leading to further genotoxicity [25, 33]. The pathological effects of PS-NPs on the placental development demonstrate considerable complexity. While subchronic exposure to PS-NPs does not cause significant pathological damage to placental tissues, it promotes vascular dilation and congestion in placental tissues, impairing trophoblast cell expansion and invasion [34]. Our study reveal that higher exposure concentrations impacts angiogenesis, particularly reducing vascular density in the placental labyrinth zone, which is consistent with the research of Tang et al. [9]. Notably, different exposure models of plastics particles effects on both maternal metabolism and offspring development. During pregnancy and lactation, exposure to low concentration of PS-NPs cause maternal metabolic dysregulation, which subsequently perturbs fetal lipid homeostasis and predisposes to offspring obesity [35]. Data from this study showed that PS-NPs treatment significantly affected various metabolic pathways in the placenta. Notably, the nicotinate and NAM metabolism pathway changed significantly. The metabolism of NAM is closely related to the synthesis of ATP, and NAM and its metabolites are indispensable redox factors in cells [36]. NAD+ is converted to NADH upon receiving electrons, providing reducing substrates for the mitochondrial respiratory chain and generating ATP through oxidative phosphorylation [37, 38]. This study also demonstrated that PS-NPs exposure during pregnancy induced oxidative damage and aberrant ATP formation in the placenta, contributing to placental dysplasia and barrier injury. Excessive oxidative stress, ROS accumulation, and lipid peroxidation are commonly associated with placental dysfunction and related diseases [39, 40], with ferroptosis playing a vital role in these placental disorders [41]. Placental trophoblasts contain abundant iron, which is actively transferred to the developing fetus [42]. During pregnancy, the transferrin receptor TfrC is highly active in the placenta, and when the placenta is injured, the impediment of iron transport increases the susceptibility of the placenta to ferroptosis [41]. Concomitantly, GPX4 is the primary inhibitory factor of ferroptosis, and placental trophoblast cells require GPX4 for their survival [43]. The downregulation of GPX4 hampers the conversion of GSH to GSSH, thereby inhibiting the decay of ferroptotic signals and exacerbates cellular ferroptosis [44, 45]. Our data revealed that PS-NPs exposure induced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in the placenta, significantly upregulating the expression of the lipid synthesis regulatory protein ACSL4 and the transferrin receptor TfrC while concurrently downregulating GPX4 expression. These data imply that PS-NPs exposure triggers ferroptosis in the placenta, potentially representing the primary cause of placental damage. Placental damage is a primary contributor of restricted fetal development. The F1 offspring in the PS-NPs treatment group also displayed negative impacts on offspring development and exhibited organ index abnormalities, indicating that maternal exposure to PS-NPs causes toxic damage to the placenta, which can be transmitted to the offspring.

The gut microbiota is essential for maintaining host homeostasis through nutritional absorption, barrier protection and immune regulation [46]. Disruption of gut microbiota homeostasis and metabolic dysregulation are central to the progression of various diseases, such as fatty liver [47], obesity [48] and inflammatory bowel disease [49]. Maintaining maternal gut microbiota homeostasis is vital during gestation, as the maternal gut microbiota undergoes dramatic remodeling to accommodate physiological changes and nourish the developing fetus [50]. The composition of the maternal gut microbiota is closely associated with pregnancy outcomes and offspring development. Studies have indicated that microbial abundance and dysbiosis are significantly lower in women with hyperglycemia during pregnancy than in healthy women [51]. The offspring of mothers with hyperglycemia during pregnancy are more predisposed to type 2 diabetes mellitus development due to significant changes in alpha diversity and flora composition [52]. Research has suggested that different sizes of polystyrene particles can cause intestinal damage, disrupt the gut microbiota, reduce the levels of probiotics, and increase the levels of pathogenic bacteria [53]. Our study demonstrates that gestational PS-NPs exposure causes damage both to the intestinal physical barrier and mucus barrier, aligning with the research conclusions on intestinal damage in pregnant mice exposure to microplastic [54]. We that oral administration of PS-NPs led to a significant decreased in the relative concentration of Firmicute, whereas that of Bacteroidota was markedly increased in PS-NPs-treated groups. The Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio, a typical marker of gut microbiota imbalance, is related to overall health and disease susceptibility [55, 56]. Concurrently, PS-NPs administration significantly reduced the abundance of Lactobacillus at the genus level. Previous studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus abundance is significantly decreased in patients with preeclampsia and abnormal placental development compared to healthy controls, indicating that Lactobacillus is beneficial for maintaining a healthy pregnancy [57]. Integrated with Tax4Fun-based functional prediction, our findings demonstrate that PS-NPs exposure disrupts maternal gut SCFA metabolism. Both propionate and butyrate are well-documented modulators of intestinal barrier function. Butyrate enhances mechanical barrier integrity by upregulating the expression of tight junction proteins such as claudin-1 and ZO-1 [58], whereas propionate promotes the differentiation of intestinal regulatory T cells and strengthens mucosal immune defenses [59]. In vitro experiments further confirmed that PS-NPs directly inhibit the growth of g_Lactobacillus. Therefore, we propose that PS-NPs exposure may impair intestinal microbiota, cause abnormal SCFA metabolism, which may further lead to intestinal tissue injury and potentially adversely impact pregnancy outcomes.

Metabolic changes are necessary to support a healthy pregnancy. Previous studies have revealed the relationship between microbial dysbiosis and metabolic changes during pregnancy [60, 61]. Changes in the gut microbiome balance are closely associated with numerous pregnancy-associated diseases, including gestational hypertension and gestational diabetes mellitus [62, 63]. The placenta serves as a transient support organ connecting the mother and fetus, mediating the supply of nutrients and oxygen necessary for fetal development. Abnormal placental metabolism can directly impact the transfer of nutrients to the fetus, leading to fetal growth restriction and associated pathologies [64]. Results in this study indicated that PS-NPs exposure inhibited placental development, disrupted the placental barrier, and induced metabolic disorders. We hypothesize that placental metabolic abnormalities and adverse pregnancy outcomes caused by PS-NPs exposure are closely linked to alterations in the maternal gut microbiota. FMT experiments successfully replicated the intestinal injury and adverse pregnancy outcomes caused by PS-NPs, indicating that gut microbial dysregulation plays a crucial role in PS-NPs-induced adverse pregnancy outcomes. However, it is important to acknowledge a methodological limitation in the FMT procedure. The bacterial suspension used for transplantation was prepared exclusively by centrifugation to remove large particulate matter [25, 65], without further separation of PS-NPs adhering to the bacteria. This approach may be insufficient to completely eliminate the potential contamination from fecal-derived nanoplastics, which are known to readily bind to microorganisms through electrostatic interactions or become encapsulated within microbial biofilms [66]. Furthermore, a previous study has demonstrated the efficacy of sucrose density gradient centrifugation in separating multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) from Pseudomonas aeruginosa [67]. While this methodology could theoretically be adapted to reduce nanoparticle contamination in FMT samples, several practical limitations precluded its application in the current study. Specifically, the heterogeneous composition of gut microbiota and the potential for sucrose-induced metabolic disturbances in recipient mice [68] rendered this approach unsuitable for separating PS-NPs from FMT samples. Given the current technical constraints in achieving complete removal of potentially residual PS-NPs in FMT samples, we cannot definitively exclude the possibility of direct NP-mediated effects on FMT experimental outcomes. Therefore, the possible presence of residual nanoplastics in the bacterial suspension and their impact on FMT experimental outcomes warrant further investigation.

In this study, PS-NPs exposure significantly affected NAM metabolism in the placenta, inhibiting the expression of NMNATs and markedly decreasing the concentrations of NADH and NAD+. NMNAT activity is present in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, with distinct NMNAT deletions resulting in various causes of mortality [69]. A significant decrease in NAD+ can directly result in an insufficient ATP supply in the placenta, which may be a critical factor contributing to fetal growth restriction in utero.

Metabolic alterations in the placenta are intimately connected to the metabolism of the maternal gut microbiota. The body derives energy through the fermentation of food by the gut microbiota, which generates essential metabolites such as amino acids and B-vitamins [70, 71]. B-vitamins are necessary auxiliary factors for carbohydrate and fat metabolism. Mammalian cells are unable to synthesize B-vitamins in sufficient amounts, thus they must obtain these vitamins from the human diet and the gut microbiota [72]. The biosynthesis of NAD+ occurs in the majority of the gut microbiota, with the phyla Bacteroidetes, Fusobacteria and Proteobacteria contributing to the de novo pathway, whereas the phyla Actinobacteria, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria are involved in the salvage pathway [71, 73]. Alterations in the gut microbiota composition can affect influence NAD+ homeostasis through multiple pathways. For example, microbial-derived secondary bile acids modulate host lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses, thereby regulating NAD+-dependent enzyme activities [74]. Changes in Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species, which are associated with insulin resistance, may reduce NAD+ biosynthesis via oxidative stress [75]. Our findings reveal a significant correlation between maternal gut dysbiosis and placental levels of NAM and its downstream metabolites. We hypothesize that alterations in the maternal placental NAM metabolic pathway are associated with dysregulation of the gut microbiota, which is supported by our FMT experiments. However, this study did not focus on investigating the regulatory effects of individual microbial species on systemic nicotinamide metabolism, and the precise molecular mechanisms still require further exploration.

We further supplemented with NAM to investigate its potential ability to protect against PS-NPs-induced placental injury. NAM, an active form of water-soluble vitamin B3, is a constituent of NAD and NADP, and it plays a crucial role in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, thereby maintaining energy homeostasis [76, 77]. Additionally, NAM modulates hormone levels, including insulin sensitivity. Insulin resistance during pregnancy can reduce the utilization of glucose in peripheral tissues, thereby ensuring an adequate energy supply for both mothers and fetuses [78]. Meanwhile, NAM can promote vasodilation and increase blood flow by inhibiting ADP-ribosyl cyclase, which in turn lowers blood pressure [79]. It has been demonstrated that dietary supplementation with NAM can extend gestation time in preeclampsia models and prevent fetal growth restriction by lowering blood pressure, improving endothelial damage, and restoring ATP supply [80]. NAM is transformed into NAD+ via enzymatic reactions in the nucleotide salvage pathway, which is the main source of NAD+ in mammals [81]. NAD+ is crucial for redox reactions that generate ATP and serves as a substrate for enzymes involved in variety of cellular metabolic processes [82]. NAD+ depletion reduces NADPH availability, inhibits GPX4 activity, and induces ferroptosis [83]. In addition, NAD+-dependent Sirtuin family proteins (such as SIRT1 and SIRT2) modulate mitochondrial function and antioxidant gene expression, which are closely associated with the occurrence of ferroptosis [84]. Our results demonstrate that PS-NPs exposure significantly downregulates the expression of NAD+ synthase NMNAT1, NMNAT2, and NMNAT3, leading to NAD+ deficiency, mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation. These ultimately lead to significant decrease in GPX4 expression. In this study, exogenous supplementation with NAM effectively alleviated the reduction in NAD+ levels and adverse pregnancy outcomes compared to the PS-NPs treated group, suggesting that NAM has a significant protective effect against reproductive damage induced by PS-NPs exposure.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the use of engineered spherical PS-NPs and oral gavage in an animal model does not fully replicate real-world human exposure. Realistic exposure typically involves chronic, low-dose exposure to heterogeneous plastic particles (varying in size, shape, and surface chemistry) via the food chain or inhalation, often in combination with other environmental pollutants, such as heavy metals or plastic additives. Secondly, although fecal microbiota transplantation suggested the involvement of gut microbiota in placental damage, the molecular mechanisms linking key microbial metabolites to NAM metabolism via the gut-placenta axis remain to be fully elucidated. Thirdly, the FMT procedure in this study used centrifugation to prepare the bacterial suspension, which may not fully eliminate fecal-derived nanoplastics, thereby posing a contamination risk. Given the current technical limitations, complete removal of residual PS-NP from FMT samples remains challenging, and thus, their potential direct influence on experimental outcomes cannot be ruled out. Future work should prioritize the development of advanced methods to effectively remove nanoplastics from FMT samples and investigate the potential impact of residual nanoplastics on FMT outcomes.

Additionally, quantifying and comparing the relative contributions of direct (PS-NP transfer to the placenta) and indirect (microbiota-mediated) effects will provide a more comprehensive assessment of the impact of PS-NP exposure on placental and fetal development and elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms involved. Subsequent experiments will also analyze the core regulatory networks of the gut-placenta axis through methods such as gut metagenomics and placental metabolomics, and further explore the regulatory mechanism of the gut-placenta axis in the PS-NPs exposure model. In the future, it is necessary to further establish an exposure model that is closer to the real environment, such as the combined exposure of heavy metals and PS-NPs, and the co-exposure of MPs/NPs with different particle sizes/surface modifications.

Collectively, this study highlights that the gut - placenta axis represents a significant pathway through which maternal exposure to PS-NPs induces placental metabolic disturbances and oxidative stress, thereby contributing to adverse pregnancy outcomes and fetal growth restriction. Furthermore, NAM supplementation effectively alleviated the placental developmental impairment and oxidative damage induced by PS-NPs, thereby improving pregnancy outcomes. These results revealed the potential pathways of placental injury induced by PS-NPs from the perspective of the maternal gut microbiota and offered new avenues for evaluating the health risks of PS-NPs exposure in pregnant women.
