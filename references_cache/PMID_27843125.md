# Recessive Mutations in ACPT, Encoding Testicular Acid Phosphatase, Cause Hypoplastic Amelogenesis Imperfecta.
**Authors:** Seymen F, Kim YJ, Lee YJ, Kang J, Kim TH, Choi H, Koruyucu M, Kasimoglu Y, Tuna EB, Gencay K, Shin TJ, Hyun HK, Kim YJ, Lee SH, Lee ZH, Zhang H, Hu JC, Simmer JP, Cho ES, Kim JW
**Journal:** Am J Hum Genet (2016)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ajhg.2016.09.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2016.09.018)

## Content

Amelogenesis imperfecta (AI) is a heterogeneous group of genetic disorders affecting tooth enamel. The affected enamel can be hypoplastic and/or hypomineralized. In this study, we identified ACPT (testicular acid phosphatase) biallelic mutations causing non-syndromic, generalized hypoplastic autosomal-recessive amelogenesis imperfecta (AI) in individuals from six apparently unrelated Turkish families. Families 1, 4, and 5 were affected by the homozygous ACPT mutation c.713C>T (p.Ser238Leu), family 2 by the homozygous ACPT mutation c.331C>T (p.Arg111Cys), family 3 by the homozygous ACPT mutation c.226C>T (p.Arg76Cys), and family 6 by the compound heterozygous ACPT mutations c.382G>C (p.Ala128Pro) and 397G>A (p.Glu133Lys). Analysis of the ACPT crystal structure suggests that these mutations damaged the activity of ACPT by altering the sizes and charges of key amino acid side chains, limiting accessibility of the catalytic core, and interfering with homodimerization. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed localization of ACPT in secretory-stage ameloblasts. The study results provide evidence for the crucial function of ACPT during amelogenesis.

Abstract

Amelogenesis imperfecta (AI) is a heterogeneous group of genetic disorders affecting tooth enamel. The affected enamel can be hypoplastic and/or hypomineralized. In this study, we identified ACPT (testicular acid phosphatase) biallelic mutations causing non-syndromic, generalized hypoplastic autosomal-recessive amelogenesis imperfecta (AI) in individuals from six apparently unrelated Turkish families. Families 1, 4, and 5 were affected by the homozygous ACPT mutation c.713C>T (p.Ser238Leu), family 2 by the homozygous ACPT mutation c.331C>T (p.Arg111Cys), family 3 by the homozygous ACPT mutation c.226C>T (p.Arg76Cys), and family 6 by the compound heterozygous ACPT mutations c.382G>C (p.Ala128Pro) and 397G>A (p.Glu133Lys). Analysis of the ACPT crystal structure suggests that these mutations damaged the activity of ACPT by altering the sizes and charges of key amino acid side chains, limiting accessibility of the catalytic core, and interfering with homodimerization. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed localization of ACPT in secretory-stage ameloblasts. The study results provide evidence for the crucial function of ACPT during amelogenesis.

Keywords: amelogenesis imperfecta, testicular acid phosphatase, ACPT, hypoplastic amelogenesis imperfecta, autosomal-recessive

Main Text

The formation of human teeth involves a series of molecular interactions between the dental lamina and underlying ectomesenchyme in the oral cavity.1 Covering the tooth crown, enamel is the hardest tissue in the human body and has a Mohs hardness similar to that of zirconium and a Young’s elastic modulus between 70 and 115 GPa.2 Dental enamel forms in an extracellular matrix generated and controlled by ameloblasts. Amelogenesis passes through a sequence of stages (secretory, transition, and maturation) that involve strict cell control and matrix surveillance. Deviations from normal genetic regulation often lead to developmental enamel malformations or amelogenesis imperfecta (AI), a genetically and clinically heterogeneous collection of inherited disorders affecting the quantity and/or quality of tooth enamel. AI is described clinically as hypoplastic, hypocalcified, or hypomaturation in form, according to variations in the enamel phenotype.3 When the pattern of inheritance is included, 14 clinical forms of non-syndromic AI are recognized.3 The prevalence varies from 1:700 to 1:14,000, depending on the study population and location.4

A recent review of the Online Mendelian Inheritance of Man (OMIM) database identified 91 conditions (isolated and syndromic) associated with an enamel phenotype, including 71 with a known molecular etiology or linked genetic loci.5 Mutations in more than a dozen genes are known to cause non-syndromic AI, and these account for only about half of all non-syndromic cases. The rest are of unknown etiology.6, 7 Mutations in amelogenin (AMELX [MIM: 300391], Xp22.2) cause X-linked hypoplastic and/or hypomaturation AI.8 Mutations in enamelin (ENAM [MIM: 606585], 4q13.3) and ameloblastin (AMBN [MIM: 601259], 4q13.3) cause hypoplastic AI in autosomal-dominant and autosomal-recessive modes, respectively.9, 10 Autosomal-dominant hypocalcified AI is caused by mutations in family with sequence similarity 83 member H (FAM83H [MIM: 611927], 8q24.3).11 Some autosomal-dominant forms of AI are caused by single-allele defects in genes (COL17A1 [MIM: 113811], 10q24.3; LAMA3 [MIM: 600805], 18q11.2; LAMB3 [MIM: 150310], 1q32)12, 13, 14, 15 that cause junctional epidermolysis bullosa (MIM: 226650) when both alleles are defective.16

Mutations in genes encoding the secreted enamel matrix proteinases kallikrein 4 (KLK4 [MIM: 603767], 19q13.41)17 and enamelysin (MMP20 [MIM: 604629], 11q22.2)18 cause hypomaturation AI with autosomal-recessive inheritance, as do mutations in WD repeat-containing protein 72 (WDR72 [MIM: 613214], 15q21.3),19 solute carrier family 24 member 4 (SLC24A4 [MIM: 609840], 14q32.12),20 integrin subunit beta 6 (ITGB6 [MIM: 147558], 2q24.2)21, 22 and chromosome 4 open reading frame 26 (C4orf26 [MIM: 614829], 4q21.1).23

We selected six apparently unrelated Turkish families with individuals with similar clinical presentations of generalized hypoplastic AI (Figure 1, Figures S1–S5). We initially screened the families, according to their clinical features and inheritance pattern, for the known AI-associated genes by using candidate gene sequencing, array comparative genomic hybridization, or exome sequencing. The proband of family 1 was a 10-year-old daughter from a consanguineous marriage. She and her younger sister presented with generalized thin enamel in their deciduous and permanent dentitions and had no other medical conditions (see Supplemental Note). Their fetal development and deliveries were uneventful, and their past medical histories were unremarkable. This study was approved by the institutional review boards of University of Istanbul, University of Michigan, and Seoul National University Dental Hospital. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Using DNA from the proband, we performed Sanger sequencing of the candidate genes (AMELX, ENAM, AMTN, AMBN, and FAM20A) as described previously,24, 25, 26, 27, 28 but disease-causing mutations were not identified.

Array comparative genomic hybridization was performed with DNA samples from the father, mother, and proband of family 1 via an Affymetrix Genome-Wide Human SNP Array 6.0 (DNA Link). Data analysis revealed no copy-number variation under the criteria of at least one marker in a segment and a minimum genomic segment size of 1 kb. Whole-exome sequencing of the father, mother and the proband of family 1 was performed with the TruSeq Exome Enrichment System (Illumina). 101-bp paired-end sequencing reads were generated with the Illumina HiSeq 2000. The sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome (UCSC Genome Browser hg19) by the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner, and single-nucleotide variants and short insertions or deletions were obtained after a series of bioinformatic analyses performed with SAMtools and GATK. The single-nucleotide polymorphism database (dbSNP build 138) was used to annotate the sequence variations. Annotated variants were selected in consideration of recessive inheritance or a de novo germline mutation. A total of 13 candidate variants were selected in family 1 (Table S1). DNA samples of participating individuals in families 2 and 4 and the probands of families 3, 5, and 6 were also characterized by whole-exome sequencing (Table S2). Annotated lists of variants were examined for common variants or variants in the shared gene(s).

A single common homozygous variant was identified in families 1, 4, and 5. This was a transition from cytosine to thymine (c.713C>T [GenBank: NM_033068.2]) in exon 7 of ACPT (acid phosphatase, testicular; 19q13.4), substituting serine to leucine at amino acid position 238 (p.Ser238Leu [GenBank: NP_149059.1]). This variant was listed in the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC) database (rs763573828) with an allele frequency of 2/120,550. Sanger sequencing confirmed that this variant segregated perfectly with the enamel defects in families 1, 4, and 5 (Figures S6 and S7). Haplotype analyses using exon-specific primers (Table S3) on families 1, 4, and 5 showed they carried the allele on a common founder haplotype and suggested that the c.713C>T (p.Ser238Leu) change is a founder variant (Figure S6). A homozygous mutation (c.331C>T) in exon 4 was identified in the affected individuals of family 2; this mutation substitutes cysteine for arginine at amino acid position 111 (p.Arg111Cys). This variant was listed in the ExAC database (rs202073531) with an allele frequency of 13/120,060. A homozygous mutation (c.226C>T) in exon 3 was identified in the affected individuals of family 3; this mutation substitutes cysteine for arginine at amino acid position 76 (p.Arg76Cys). This variant was not listed in the ExAC database. In addition, compound heterozygous ACPT mutations were identified in family 6. The proband of family 6 had a paternally transmitted guanine to adenine transition (c.397G>A), substituting lysine for glutamic acid at amino acid position 133 (p.Glu133Lys), and a maternally transmitted guanine to cytosine transversion (c.382G>C), substituting proline for alanine at amino acid position 128 (p.Ala128Pro). Both mutations localized in exon 4 of ACPT. These mutations were listed in the ExAC database with allele frequencies of 4/118,064 (rs767907487, c.382G>C) and 2/115,374 (rs779823931, c.397G>A) (Table S4).

Acpt was reported to be downregulated in the mid-late maturation stage, as compared to expression in the secretory stage, of amelogenesis by genome-wide transcript profiling of the developing enamel organ in a rat model.29 To investigate the localization of ACPT during tooth development, immunohistochemistry was performed on a developing molar from 8-day-old mice with rabbit polyclonal anti-ACPT antibody at a 1:100 dilution (Global Biotech). Rabbit polyclonal anti-amelogenin (1:400 dilution, Cosmo Bio) antibody was used for comparison. Histostain-Plus rabbit primary (DAB) kit (Zymed Laboratories) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ACPT immunoreactivity localized in ameloblasts, follicular cells, odontoblasts, and osteoblasts. There was strong localization in secretory-stage ameloblasts, but ACPT was not detected in maturation-stage ameloblasts (Figure 2). ACPT localization overlapped that of amelogenin, the major structural enamel matrix protein, indicating that ACPT functions during the secretory stage of amelogenesis. Previously, we showed the expression of Acpt in an odontoblastic cell line and it’s possible functional role in odontoblast differentiation and mineralization.30 However, we could not identify any dentin defects in the affected individuals clinically and/or radiographically.

Acid phosphatases (orthophosphoric monoester phosphohydrolases) are a group of enzymes whose normal function is to hydrolyze esters of orthophosphoric acid.31
ACPT encodes an acid phosphatase expressed highly in the testis and that belongs to the histidine acid phosphatase family.32
ACPT has 11 exons spanning 4.8 kb (UCSC Genome Browser hg19, chr19: g.51,293,672–51,298,481) on chromosomal region 19q13.33. ACPT is a single-pass type I membrane protein with a long extracellular domain (29–390 amino acids) following the signal peptide (28 amino acids) and a short cytoplasmic domain (415–426 amino acids) and transmembrane domain (391–414 amino acids), as predicted by the Phobius web server. Arginine at amino acid position 111 (Arg111), glutamic acid at amino acid position 133 (Glu133), and serine at amino acid position 238 (Ser238) are all well-conserved among eutherian mammal orthologs. Arginine at amino acid position 76 (Arg76) and alanine at amino acid position 128 (Ala128) are also well-conserved, except in a few species. For example, a pig has histidine and serine instead of Arg76 and Ala128, respectively (Figure 3).

In silico analysis of the tertiary structure of the ACPT extracellular domain containing the mutations identified in this study via SWISS-MODEL (Uniprot: Q9BZG2) reveals that both Ala128 and Glu133 are located in a loop that is involved in homodimerization. Arg76 are located in an α-helix, which can interact with a loop of the homodimerization partner. Arg111 can potentially interact with amino acid residues in the catalytic core, and Ser238 is located in an α-helix forming an entrance to the active catalytic core (Figure 4).33 Mutations would damage the activity of ACPT by altering the sizes and charges of key amino acid side chains, limiting accessibility of the catalytic core, and interfering with homodimerization.

In Silico Analysis of the Mutations

(A) Three-dimensional structure of a homodimer generated with the PyMOL program. Mutated amino acids are indicated in the diagram. Both Ala128 and Glu133 are located in a loop involved in homodimerization. Arg76 is located in an α-helix that interacts with a loop in the homodimer partner. Arg111 is located in an α-helix near the catalytic core and has direct interactions with catalytic motifs (His41 and His288), and Ser238 is located in an α-helix near the active catalytic core.

(B) The p.Arg111Cys substitution results in total loss of interactions with two important amino acids (His41 and His288) residing in the catalytic core of ACPT.

(C) The p.Ser238Leu substitution loses the interaction with Leu234 in the same α-helix. The size of the side chain is increased significantly (red arrows).

(D) The p.Arg76Cys substitution results in total loss of the interactions with Pro132 within a loop of the homodimer partner; in addition, both the charge and size of the side chain are altered significantly (from polar with a −4.5 hydropathy index to nonpolar with a 2.5 hydropathy index).

(E) The p.Ala128Pro substitution creates new interactions with Ala127 and Ser131. The size of the side chain is increased significantly.

(F) The p.Glu133Lys substitution results in a loss of interaction with Arg137 and a change of the charge from negative to positive.

ACPT was first cloned and characterized using human testis cDNA and bacterial artificial chromosome clones.32 Lysosomal acid phosphatase 2 (ACP2) and prostate acid phosphatase (ACPP) are the two most homologous genes, sharing 63% and 60% of amino acid sequence similarity, respectively.32 The expression of ACPT has been detected in many tissues, including testis, ovary, liver, brain, small intestine, heart, and bone marrow.34 ACPT localization was detected in secretory ameloblasts, odontoblasts, and osteoblasts in this study. ACPT has three disulfide bonds (Cys159–Cys378, Cys214–Cys312, and Cys353–Cys357) and four potential N-glycosylation sites (Asn191, Asn269, Asn330, and Asn339).34 Glycosylation levels vary depending upon the tissue examined. ACPT has two conserved catalytic motifs, RHGxRxP (40–46 amino acids) as a nucleophile and HD (288-289 amino acids) as a proton donor, composing the active phosphate pocket. Even though the active sites in each subunit of the dimeric form are far apart from each other, the enzyme is active only as a dimer, which shows positive cooperativity upon substrate binding.35

The compound heterozygous mutations (c.382G>C [p.Ala128Pro] and c.397G>A [p.Glu133Lys]) and the homozygous mutation (c.226C>T [p.Arg76Cys]) would result in partial or complete loss of dimerization. The homozygous mutations (c.331C>T [p.Arg111Cys] and c.713C>T [p.Ser238Leu]) would disrupt the active catalytic core by the loss of interactions with the nucleophile and proton donor motif, thus limiting substrate binding by conformational distortion of the entrance to the active catalytic core. Reduction of ACPT activity by these mutational effects is presumed to be responsible for hypoplastic enamel formed in these families. The study results provide evidence for the significance of ACPT during amelogenesis. Determining the role of ACPT by characterizing animal models with ACPT absent could provide additional insights into the molecular basis of ACPT-related dental enamel defect.
