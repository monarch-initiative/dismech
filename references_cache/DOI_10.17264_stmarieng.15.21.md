Abstract
　　In patients with atrial standstill (AS), a lack of atrial contraction will likely cause blood flow stagnation in 
the atria. Thromboembolic complications are highly prevalent in such cases. We present a case involving a ten-
year-old boy diagnosed with AS after presenting with a cerebral embolism. Subsequently, the patient underwent 
a successful endovascular thrombectomy and pacemaker implantation. Atrial electrical activity recovered six 
months following dual-chamber pacemaker implantation. Prompt recognition and management pose challenges 
in patients with previously undiagnosed AS who present with acute ischemic stroke. Additionally, anticoagula -
tion therapy and monitoring of atrial activity are crucial for preventing thromboembolic events.
Key words: Atrial standstill, acute ischemic stroke, pediatric
Introduction
Atrial standstill (AS) is a rare cardiac condition 
characterized by the transient or persistent absence of 
electrical and mechanical activity in the atria. It is 
characterized by the absence of P waves, bradycardia 
with a junctional or ventricular escape rhythm, and an 
inability to electrically stimulate the atria. Without 
atrial contraction, blood flow is prone to stagnation in 
the atria, increasing the risk of thrombus formation. 
There is limited data on AS because of the rarity of 
this syndrome in the pediatric population. Herein, we 
report the case of a ten-year-old boy with AS who 
presented with acute ischemic stroke (AIS).
Case presentation
A ten-year-old boy with no family history of ar -
rhythmia, syncope, or sudden cardiac death presented 
to the hospital’s emergency department with sudden 
syncope. At the age of eight, auscultation during a 
routine school physical checkup suggested the pres -
ence of arrhythmia. He was subsequently diagnosed 
with sinus bradycardia and sinus arrhythmia via a 12-
lead electrocardiogram (ECG), which was retrospec -
tively reviewed as a second-degree sinoatrial nodal 
exit block. No further evaluation was conducted as 
the patient remained asymptomatic with normal find -
ings on transthoracic echocardiography (TTE). On 
the day of admission, the patient reported discomfort 
in the left occipital region of his head. He was found 
unconscious at school and regained consciousness 
(E4V1M6) but developed aphasia and right hemipa -
resis upon arrival at the hospital. His blood pressure 
measured 132/83 mmHg, and his heart rate (HR) was 
irregular, averaging 38—42 beats per minute (bpm) at 
initial presentation. The HR increased to 70—80 bpm 
following a dose of atropine. Chest radiography re -
vealed an enlarged heart with a cardiothoracic ratio of 
66%. Immediate computed tomography scans of the 
brain and chest revealed no abnormalities.

Discussion
In this case report, a pediatric patient presented 
with AIS caused by AS. The occurrence of previously 
undiagnosed AS leading to stroke is exceedingly rare, 
with only one documented case reported  
1)
. Previous 
literature indicates that most patients with AS typical-
ly present with arrhythmias or symptoms related to 
bradycardia, such as syncope. During the initial pre -
sentation, differentiating AS from sick sinus syn -
drome can be challenging because both have similar 
ECG characteristics. Therefore, a definitive diagnosis 
of AS is often confirmed through electrophysiological 
studies or during pacemaker implantation surgery.
Rapid diagnosis of AIS is crucial for enhancing 
the feasibility of hyperacute therapy. In pediatric cas-
es, limited clinical recognition of AIS by physicians 
often delays its identification. Previous studies have 
reported a median time of 15—24 hours from onset 
to diagnosis 
2, 3)
. Given the rarity of AIS caused by ar -
rhythmia in children, which has been reported in 4% 
of pediatric AIS patients with cardiac disease  
4)
, early

In patients with AS, thromboembolic complica -
tions are notable and markedly prevalent comorbidi -
ties. In the most extensive cohort study on the matter, 
which involved 20 pediatric patients with AS, 35% 
experienced thrombotic or thromboembolic events. 
Additionally, the atrial pacing capability has been re -
ported to directly correlate with outcomes  
5)
. There -
fore, efforts to achieve atrial pacing are critical in pa -
tients with AS, although VVI pacemakers are 
frequently selected owing to the difficulty of achiev -
ing atrial capture. A previous report suggested that 
electrical anatomical mapping of the atrium may 
identify capturable atrial sites, enabling atrial pac -
ing  
6)
. In the present case, atrial pacing was not 
achieved at the time of pacemaker implantation, but 
the placement of an atrial lead allowed atrial pacing 
six months later once the electrical activity of the 
atria had recovered.
The mechanism underlying the electrical recov -
ery of the atria remains unclear. Transient AS has 
been reported to be caused by digitalis or quinidine 
intoxication, hypoxia, hyperkalemia, or myocardial 
infarction  
6)
. However, none of these conditions ap -
plied to our patient. On the other hand, persistent AS 
has been associated with chronic myocarditis, cardio -
myopathy, and cardiac sodium channelopathy associ -
ated with SCN5A mutations, with many cases demon-
strating the involvement of not only atria but also 
ventricles 
5, 7, 8)
. The similar abnormalities observed in 
our patient’s ECG two years prior to the admission 
and the presence of LV systolic dysfunction might be 
associated with a long-standing cardiac disease such 
as cardiomyopathy. Due to the pacemaker’s incom -
patibility with MRI, a detailed assessment of the 
myocardium has not been possible in this patient. 
Therefore, given that most pediatric cases of AS are 
reported to exhibit progressive myocardial fibrosis 
and electrophysiological deterioration  
7-9)
, the atrial 
activity and the cardiac function should be carefully 
monitored for recurrence.
The duration of anticoagulation therapy in pedi-
atric AS is controversial. Although there are no spe -
cific guidelines for the secondary prevention of pedi -
atric stroke, long-term treatment for two years or 
more is generally recommended  
10)
. Some groups rec -
ommend anticoagulation therapy for primary preven -
tion only in those without atrial pacing capability  
5, 11)
. 
In this case, considering its classification as second -
ary prevention and the risk of AS recurrence, continu-
ation of the anticoagulant for at least two years was 
deemed necessary.
Conclusion
Thromboembolic complications are significant 
and highly prevalent in patients with AS. Prompt rec -
ognition and management can be challenging in pa -
tients with previously undiagnosed AS who present 
with AIS. Additionally, anticoagulation therapy and 
follow-up of atrial activity are key for preventing 
thromboembolic events.
