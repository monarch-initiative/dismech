# Central Involvement in Pure Autonomic Failure: Insights from Neuromelanin-Sensitive Magnetic Resonance Imaging and (18)F-Fluorodopa-Positron Emission Tomography.
**Authors:** Trujillo P, O'Rourke KR, Roman OC, Song AK, Hett K, Cooper A, Black BK, Donahue MJ, Shibao CA, Biaggioni I, Claassen DO
**Journal:** Mov Disord (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mds.30119](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.30119)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Central synucleinopathies, including Parkinson's disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and multiple system atrophy (MSA), involve alpha-synuclein accumulation and dopaminergic cell loss in the substantia nigra (SN) and locus coeruleus (LC). Pure autonomic failure (PAF), a peripheral synucleinopathy, often precedes central synucleinopathies.

OBJECTIVES: To assess early brain involvement in PAF using neuromelanin-sensitive magnetic resonance imaging (NM-MRI) and fluorodopa-positron emission tomography (FDOPA-PET), and to determine whether PAF patients with a high likelihood ratio (LR) for conversion to a central synucleinopathy exhibit reduced NM-MRI contrast in the LC and SN compared with controls and low-LR patients.

METHODS: Participants with PAF (n = 23) were categorized as high-LR (n = 13) or low-LR (n = 10) for conversion to central synucleinopathy. Additional participants included PD (n = 22), DLB (n = 8), and age- and sex-matched healthy controls (n = 23). NM-MRI at 3 T was used to quantify contrast ratios in the LC and SN, while FDOPA-PET measured presynaptic dopamine synthesis. Linear regression analyses, adjusted for age and sex, were used to compare NM-MRI contrast across groups.

RESULTS: High-LR PAF patients showed reduced contrast in the LC and SN compared with controls and low-LR PAF patients, with values similar to PD and DLB. The NM-MRI contrast in the SN correlated with dopamine uptake in the striatum. Longitudinal imaging in PAF patients (n = 6) demonstrated reduced NM-MRI and PET values in individuals who developed central synucleinopathies.

CONCLUSIONS: NM-MRI and FDOPA-PET may serve as potential biomarkers for early brain involvement and predicting progression to central synucleinopathies in PAF and could help identify patients for early intervention. © 2025 The Author(s). Synucleinopathies, including dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), Parkinson's disease (PD), and multiple system atrophy (MSA), are neurodegenerative diseases characterized by abnormal alpha‐synuclein accumulation in neurons and glial cells. These disorders can initially present with autonomic dysfunction in the peripheral autonomic nervous system.
1
 Pure autonomic failure (PAF) is a rare peripheral synucleinopathy marked by alpha‐synuclein inclusions in autonomic nerve fibers,
2
 typically manifesting with orthostatic hypotension and often supine hypertension. PAF is associated with sympathetic postganglionic denervation and low norepinephrine levels.

Though PAF is primarily considered a peripheral disorder, patients are at increased risk of developing central synucleinopathies, such as PD, DLB, and MSA.
1
,
3
 Most PAF patients eventually progress to a central synucleinopathy,
3
,
4
 though conversion rates vary based on factors like disease severity and follow‐up duration. Symptoms such as rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD), anosmia, subtle motor signs, and severe bladder dysfunction suggest a higher conversion risk.
4
 Many PAF patients already display anosmia and REM sleep behavior disorder, indicating early central nervous system (CNS) involvement. Despite this, most research has focused on autonomic dysfunction, with limited exploration of brain changes.
5
 Investigating early CNS alterations in PAF is essential for understanding the early stages of these disorders and identifying markers for conversion, which could aid in diagnosis, drug efficacy assessments, and disease progression monitoring.

Central synucleinopathies are marked by alpha‐synuclein accumulation and dopamine‐producing cell depletion in the substantia nigra (SN). Early involvement of the locus coeruleus (LC), a key norepinephrine source, is another hallmark of these conditions. Developing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques to assess the LC and SN in vivo could provide insights into early disease stages, aid in differential diagnosis, and quantify disease severity. Neuromelanin‐sensitive MRI (NM‐MRI) has shown promise in visualizing the SN and LC, offering a non‐invasive means to study catecholamine function in vivo.

,
7
,
8
,
9
 In PD, DLB, and MSA, neuronal loss in the SN and LC reduces NM‐MRI contrast. This technique is particularly useful for detecting early LC damage, which often precedes dopaminergic neuron degeneration in the SN.
10
 The LC's role in autonomic and cognitive functions, such as memory, attention, sleep, and stress response,

,

,
13
 makes it a critical target for early disease detection. Studies suggest that NM‐MRI can detect LC alterations in early disease stages,
14

This study leveraged the Autonomic Rare Diseases Clinical Research Consortium (U54 NS065736) to recruit PAF patients to investigate prodromal synucleinopathies. Prior studies from the consortium showed that 34% of PAF patients developed a central synucleinopathy within 4 years.
3
 This study aimed to evaluate early CNS involvement by quantifying NM contrast in the LC and SN in prodromal and clinically manifest synucleinopathies. We hypothesize that PAF patients with a high likelihood ratio (LR) for conversion to central synucleinopathy

,
16

Additionally, we explored the clinical and neurochemical implications of NM‐MRI SN contrast, hypothesizing that increased NM‐MRI signal reflects greater dopamine synthesis capacity in the nigrostriatal network.

This study adhered to the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring that all participants provided written, informed consent before taking part in the research. MRI scans were performed using a 3 T MRI scanner equipped with a body coil for transmission and a 32‐channel SENSE array for reception. Foam pads were used to immobilize the head to reduce motion artifacts. Standard structural imaging sequences included T1‐weighted (MPRAGE) and T2‐weighted fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, which were used to assess tissue atrophy and rule out other diagnoses. The NM‐MRI scan consisted of a T1‐weighted two‐dimensional (2D) multi‐slice TSE sequence with magnetization transfer (MT) preparation using a 20‐ms single‐lobed sinc‐gauss pulse with Δω = 2 kHz and αMT of 600° (field of view = 180 × 160 × 30 mm3, repetition time/time to echo (TR/TE) 670/12 ms, acquired/reconstructed spatial resolution = 0.65 × 0.65 × 2/0.38 × 0.38 × 2 mm3, four signal averages, acquisition time = 6 min). Oblique axial slices were defined perpendicular to the floor of the fourth ventricle, covering the region from the posterior commissure to the inferior border of the pons (Fig. 1A).

Structural images were reviewed to exclude participants with prior infarcts or vasculopathy. NM‐MRI data were analyzed in subject space. For each participant, ROIs were defined for the LC and a reference region located in the pontomesencephalic area (PT), following established protocols from previous studies (Fig. 1B,C).

 The segmentation process was carried out independently by two raters, ensuring that the ROIs were consistently and accurately delineated. All final ROIs were then visually inspected to confirm their precision before proceeding with further analyses.

The bilateral SN was divided into medial (SNmedial), central (SNcentral), and lateral (SNlateral)
33
 regions on the axial plane (Fig. 1D). For the three subregions, 6 × 6 voxel ROIs were delineated on the four consecutive axial slices where the SN was most visible. Control ROIs were also delineated in the cerebral crus (CC). The contrast ratio for each slice and the side of the LC was calculated as CRLC = (SLC−SPT)/SPT, where SLC and SPT denote the mean signal intensities of the LC and PT, respectively, and then averaged across sides and slices. Similarly, the SN contrast ratio was estimated as CRSN = (S

A subset of 10 participants completed 18F‐fluorodopa‐positron emission tomography (18F‐FDOPA‐PET) scans as part of the exploratory objectives of the study. PET scans were performed on a Philips Vereos PET/computed tomography (CT) scanner, following administration of carbidopa and entacapone to enhance signal quality.
34
,
35
 After a low‐dose CT scan for attenuation correction, participants underwent a 95‐min dynamic PET scan, starting with a slow bolus injection of 6–8 mCi 18F‐FDOPA. The PET data were reconstructed using a three‐dimensional (3D) Fourier method, resulting in isotropic 2.0 mm spatial resolution.

To quantify presynaptic dopamine synthesis and storage, regional influx constants (Ki) were computed using the Patlak reference tissue model, with the cerebellum serving as the reference region.
36
 ROIs for the putamen, caudate, and SN were manually defined
37
 and were used to quantify Ki values within the nigrostriatal regions to explore the relationship between NM‐MRI SN contrast and dopamine synthesis capacity.

For comparison, 18F‐FDOPA data from five healthy individuals were obtained from age‐matched controls (mean age 68 ± 5 years) in a separate study, using the same acquisition and processing protocol.

First, standard descriptive statistics were conducted to summarize demographic and clinical scores, as well as imaging measures. Group differences in age and sex compared with controls were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test and Fisher's exact test, respectively. The primary hypothesis of this study was that NM‐MRI contrast ratios would be lower in a cohort of PAF‐High participants compared with HC. To test this hypothesis, we performed a linear regression analysis, with the contrast ratios as the dependent variable and group as the independent variable, adjusting for age and sex as covariates. We used similar models to evaluate the group differences between controls and PAF‐Low, PD, and DLB.

For PAF participants, Spearman correlation analyses were used to assess relationships between NM‐MRI contrast ratios and age, motor symptoms, cognitive function, and apathy scores. Exploratory analyses examined the correlation between NM‐MRI contrast in the SN and 18F‐FDOPA Ki values using Spearman correlation.

A total of 76 participants were included: 23 with PAF (13 PAF‐High, 10 PAF‐Low), 22 with PD, 8 with DLB, and 23 HC. Gender distribution between PAF and HC was not significantly different (p = 0.13), though the control group had more females. PD and DLB had higher male‐to‐female ratios, reflecting the higher prevalence of these diseases in males. Mean ages were similar for PAF and HC (p = 0.165) and PD and HC (p = 0.381), but DLB participants were older than HC (p = 0.007). There was no significant age difference between PAF‐High and PAF‐Low (p = 0.225). Table 1 shows demographic and clinical data. In PAF participants, plasma norepinephrine levels were 115 ± 56 pg/mL, indicating reduced sympathetic activity. Four PAF participants could not stand for 3 min due to significant blood pressure drops. No PAF participants had anemia or major neurological conditions on neuroimaging. At enrollment, no PAF patients were on dopaminergic medications; the most common treatments were midodrine (41%), fludrocortisone (29%), and droxidopa (29%). All PD patients were on dopamine replacement therapy (LEDD 728.8 ± 365.1 mg/day), and DLB participants primarily received clonazepam/melatonin for RBD (23.5%), carbidopa‐levodopa (76.5%), and dopamine agonists (88.2%).

In the PAF‐High group, three had subtle motor symptoms, RBD, and anosmia; four had subtle motor symptoms and RBD; one had subtle motor symptoms and anosmia; and five had RBD and anosmia. Among the PAF‐Low patients, two had anosmia, seven had RBD, and one had no symptoms.

In PAF patients, NM‐MRI contrast ratios showed no significant gender differences and tended to decrease with age, particularly in the lateral and central subregions of the SN (SNLateral: rho = −0.41, p = 0.054; SNCentral: rho −0.36, p = 0.093). UPDRS‐III scores negatively correlated with contrast ratios in the lateral (rho = −0.56, p = 0.005) and central (rho = −0.48, p = 0.021) SN, as well as in the LC (rho = −0.42, p = 0.064). Although no significant correlations were found between SN contrast ratios and MoCA scores, a positive trend was observed for the SNCentral (rho = 0.36, p = 0.09). A significant positive correlation emerged between LC contrast ratios and MoCA scores (rho = 0.47, p = 0.037). Additionally, LARS scores negatively correlated with LC contrast ratios (rho = −0.64, p = 0.002). Among participants, only three patients had LARS scores ≥−21, indicating mild to moderate apathy, while others scored <−21, reflecting no apathy. Ten patients (six PAF and four PD) completed an 18F‐FDOPA‐PET scan. The results from 18F‐FDOPA suggest a correlation between NM‐MRI signal and dopamine synthesis capacity in the nigrostriatal network. Patients with higher NM‐MRI SN contrast showed greater 18F‐FDOPA uptake in the putamen, caudate, and SN. Specifically, the mean SN contrast ratio was significantly correlated with the 18F‐FDOPA uptake constant (Ki) in the caudate (rho = 0.782, p = 0.008) (Fig. S1). The SN contrast ratio was positively associated with Ki in the putamen and SN, though these relationships were not statistically significant (rho = 0.564, p = 0.090, rho = 0.588, p = 0.074) (Fig. S1).

A subgroup of PAF patients underwent longitudinal assessments. Seven PAF patients (five PAF‐High, two PAF‐Low) had one to four follow‐up MRI scans over up to 4.5 years. PAF‐High patients had lower initial NM‐MRI contrast ratios, especially in the LC. Contrast ratios in the SN decreased for all patients, while LC ratios decreased in those with initially higher values but remained stable for those with lower values (Fig. 4A,B). Six PAF patients (four PAF‐High, two PAF‐Low) had one or two follow‐up 18F‐FDOPA scans within 3.5 years. All PAF‐High patients converted to a central synucleinopathy (two to PD, two to DLB) and had lower initial 18F‐FDOPA values, particularly in the putamen, compared with PAF‐Low patients who did not convert. PAF‐High patients also showed greater reductions in 18F‐FDOPA uptake in the SN and striatum compared with PAF‐Low patients (Fig. 4C–E).

This study aimed to investigate CNS involvement in patients with PAF, a peripheral synucleinopathy that may precede central synucleinopathies such as PD, DLB, and MSA. Our findings suggest that high‐LR PAF patients progressing to central synucleinopathies exhibit structural changes in the LC and SN, indicative of neurodegenerative processes.

In PAF patients, we identified a significant negative relationship between the contrast ratio in the lateral SN and UPDRS‐III scores, which assess motor symptoms such as tremor, rigidity, and gait. At enrollment, none of the PAF patients met clinical criteria for central alpha‐synucleinopathy or were on dopaminergic medication, yet some exhibited motor symptoms. By year 4, all patients in the PAF‐High subgroup were diagnosed with a central synucleinopathy, with two PAF‐Low patients also progressing to PD. This indicates that the NM‐MRI contrast ratio in the SN may serve as an early marker of disease progression.

Higher NM‐MRI contrast ratios in the SN were associated with increased striatal 18F‐FDOPA uptake, indicating preserved dopaminergic function. 18F‐FDOPA uptake serves as a marker for presynaptic dopaminergic function, reflecting the ability of these neurons to synthesize and store dopamine. PAF‐High patients who phenoconverted to central synucleinopathies exhibited reduced 18F‐FDOPA Ki in the striatum, one of the first areas affected by dopaminergic neuron loss. Longitudinal data suggest progressive loss of presynaptic dopaminergic function in PAF‐High patients. These findings highlight the potential of NM‐MRI and 18F‐FDOPA‐PET imaging as predictors of disease progression in synucleinopathies, offering non‐invasive means to assess dopaminergic integrity and identify patients at risk of transitioning to central synucleinopathies.

Identifying biomarkers for early CNS changes in PAF is essential for timely intervention to delay or prevent progression to central synucleinopathies. Our findings highlight NM‐MRI as a non‐invasive tool for detecting early neurodegenerative changes, particularly in the LC and SN. This could enable clinicians to identify high‐risk PAF patients for closer monitoring and early neuroprotective intervention.

NM‐MRI's ability to reveal structural changes before significant motor symptoms emerge supports its role in guiding early therapy to slow disease progression. Variability in SN subregion contrast ratios suggests the potential for personalized treatment based on specific neurodegeneration patterns. Longitudinal NM‐MRI monitoring could track synucleinopathy progression and therapeutic effects. Correlations with clinical scores also provide insights into the links between structural changes and motor and non‐motor symptoms, aiding the development of targeted therapies for dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems.

Despite a relatively large sample for neuroimaging in PAF, the small cohort size and limited longitudinal follow‐up reduce this study's statistical power and generalizability. Larger, longer‐term studies are needed to validate these findings. Confounding factors, such as medication use and comorbidities, were not fully accounted for and warrant consideration in future research.

While NM‐MRI and FDOPA PET provide valuable insights into neurodegenerative processes, they are not widely used clinically. NM‐MRI is more accessible, easier to implement across sites, and increasingly integrated into clinical practice, making it more practical for multi‐site studies and wider clinical use. Standardizing data acquisition and image processing, along with clinician training on NM‐MRI interpretation, could further support its adoption in diverse clinical settings.

Although our study focused on PAF and Lewy body disorders (PD and DLB) due to their well‐defined clinical profiles, including MSA patients could have deepened insights into the disease spectrum. Early‐stage MSA lacks distinct biomarkers, complicating diagnosis in populations overlapping with PD or PAF. Notably, none of the PAF patients in our cohort phenoconverted to MSA during the follow‐up period, although this was not anticipated or part of the study design. This absence may be due to the relatively small sample size, which limits the power to detect such conversions. Other studies have shown that a subset of PAF patients may eventually convert to MSA,
3
,
68
 highlighting that phenoconversion can occur in individuals with severe autonomic dysfunction. Future studies that include MSA patients, particularly in early stages, could provide valuable insights into disease progression, enhancing our understanding of the overlap between these conditions and contributing to the refinement of diagnostic criteria.

Our approach, similar to prior studies, involved user‐defined ROIs in subregions of the SN. While user‐friendly, this approach introduces variability in ROI placement and MRI slice positioning, especially in longitudinal studies. Despite these limitations, it allowed exploration of non‐uniform SN degeneration. Future studies could employ voxel‐wise analyses to minimize user bias by normalizing images to a standard space and using template‐based ROIs. Standardizing NM‐MRI protocols and analysis methods will improve reliability, reproducibility, and clinical utility in longitudinal studies.

NM‐MRI can identify early neurodegenerative changes in the LC and SN in PAF patients with a high probability of prodromal CNS synucleinopathy. Our findings provide a foundation for further research into the role of NM‐MRI in identifying and characterizing neurodegenerative changes in patients with PAF and other prodromal stages of central synucleinopathies. Insights gained from this research may contribute to a better understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these conditions, ultimately leading to improved diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.
