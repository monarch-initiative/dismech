Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a clinically and genetically diverse group of inherited retinal degenerative diseases characterized by progressive photoreceptor cell death. With a worldwide prevalence of approximately 1:4000, RP is the most prevalent hereditary fundus dystrophy (Verbakel et al.2018). The inheritance pattern shows a rough distribution of 65% non-syndromic (20% autosomal dominant, 13% autosomal recessive, 8% X-linked, and 24% isolated or unknown), 17% syndromic (12% Usher syndrome and 5% Bardet-Biedl syndrome), 10% systemic, and 10% other or unknown (Campochiaro and Mir,2018). It predominantly begins in the rod photoreceptors, followed by gradual degeneration of the cone photoreceptors. Therefore, the initial clinical symptom is night blindness (nyctalopia), followed by a progressive constriction of the visual field, and finally causing central vision loss and complete blindness (Newton and Megaw,2020).

Since the degeneration of cones does not begin until nearly all the rods have died and the genetic mutations causing degeneration usually express on the rods, the mechanism underlying cone degeneration remains unknown. Although secondary degeneration of cones is predictable in RP, different rates appear among individuals, probably because genetic mutations regulate biochemical processes, including cone death, but differences in environmental exposures are involved in RP as well (Campochiaro and Mir,2018). Numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have attempted to explain the secondary degeneration of cones. In this review, cone degeneration refers to a process of morphological and functional change in cones, covering the early loss of outer segments, progressive dysfunction, and eventual cell death. We summarize the current five theories related to the mechanisms underlying cone degeneration in RP.

In oxidative phosphorylation and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis in the mitochondria, NADPH oxidases (NOXs) are a family of flavoenzymes that catalyze one electron reduction in molecular oxygen to generate superoxide radicals, which are the primary source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Augsburger et al.2019). ROS, including superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals, participate in immune defense and act as a second messenger in cellular signaling pathways (Takeya and Sumimoto,2003). As a major source of intracellular ROS production, NOX-derived ROS may activate NF-κB recruitment of inflammatory cells to the sites of damage and inflammation (Forrester et al.2018). To neutralize the ROS, free-radical acceptors, such as glutathione peroxidase (GPX), catalase, and superoxide dismutase (SOD), form an antioxidant defense system to protect cell organelles against ROS-induced damage, which maintains cell homeostasis (Bratic and Larsson,2013; Weydert and Cullen,2010). When NOXs are activated under pathological conditions, excessive ROS is produced due to the imbalance between ROS formation and antioxidant defense system (Prasad and Bao,2019). It affects cellular function, trans-differentiation, and survival by damaging the cellular macromolecules, such as DNA, proteins, and lipids (Murakami et al.2020b).

The choroidal circulation supplying the avascular outer retina and the retinal circulation providing the inner retina form a two-part blood supply. Unlike the autoregulation of retinal vessels in the inner retina, the choroidal vessels do not respond to the oxygen tension level in the outer retina (Yu et al.2000). In addition, photoreceptors in the outer nuclear layer (ONL) have high metabolic activity in mitochondrial function and oxygen consumption (Gallenga et al.2021). When numerous rods die in RP, oxygen consumption significantly decreases because rods form 95% of the cells in the ONL and non-autoregulation of blood flow in the outer retina, leading to a high oxygen concentration in the outer retina. In RCS rat and P23H rat model of RP (Yu and Cringle,2005), as well as in the adult Abyssinian cat model of RP (Padnick-Silver et al.2006), a dramatic decline in the oxygen consumption level because of degenerating photoreceptors causes excessive oxygen spill over from the outer retina to the inner retina, suggesting that oxygen levels increase in the retina with the degeneration of rods, followed by the markedly increased ROS in the outer retina of rd1 mice at P14 (Usui et al.2009b). Oxidative stress damages DNA, proteins, and lipids. In particular, Komeima et al. suggested that overaction of NADP(H) oxidase contributes to excessive superoxide radicals in the retinas of rd1 mice, which leads to nitrosative damage to proteins (Komeima et al.2008). The research showed that oxidative DNA damage is important for the initiation of death-signaling pathways in rd10 mice and RCS rat (Murakami et al.2012). Increased staining for 8-isprostane, a stable end product of lipid peroxidation, indicates that elevated oxidative stress damages lipids in P23H-1 rats (Wong et al.2015). As a result, ROS formation increases and causes progressive oxidative damage to cones (Campochiaro et al. 2018).

The benefit of antioxidant treatment in protecting rod and cone photoreceptors has been tested in rd10 and rd1 mice models (Komeima et al.2006). In rd10 mice, only when both SOD2 and catalase were expressed together, oxidative damage was significantly reduced and cone density was significantly improved, but without slowing of rod degeneration (Usui et al.2009a). In addition, co-expression of SOD1 and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) has been proved to maintain cone function and survival in rd10 mice, suggesting that SOD overexpression does not protect photoreceptors in RP, unless peroxide-detoxifying enzymes are expressed in the same cellular compartment (Usui et al.2011). Furthermore, AAV-mediated delivery ofNRF2gene was more effective than SOD2 and catalase to prevent oxidation since both photoreceptors and retinal ganglion cells were protected by NRF2 (Xiong et al.2015). In patients with RP, the oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) ratio in the aqueous humor, but not serum, was significantly reduced, suggesting that patients only have ocular oxidative stress rather than systemic oxidative stress. The aqueous GSH/GSSG ratio may be a useful marker to evaluate cone survival and function (Campochiaro et al.2015). Similarly, regulation of the GSH metabolism enzymes and glutamate level in the retina is probably effective in slowing cone degeneration in RP (Sanchez-Vallejo et al.2016).

Except for the dramatically increased oxygen level in the outer retina, intraretinal vascular abnormalities were described in the model of RP, which exacerbated the oxidative stress and contributed to disease progression. In the region of vascular abnormalities, both rod and cone photoreceptor abnormalities were observed, while cones were affected earlier and their condition was more severe than observed in rods (Dorrell et al.2009). The death of rods triggers retinal vascular degeneration in RP. The changes in the retinal vascular network in the P23H rats at different RP stages were previously described, which indicated that adequate supply of oxygen and nutrients to the retina is obstructed by the disruption of the capillary plexus with reduced capillary density and capillary loops, which promotes photoreceptor degeneration (Fernandez-Sanchez et al.2018). Several studies have suggested that vascular abnormalities contribute to the loss of cone-mediated central visual function due to impaired macular blood flow (Kayser et al.2017; Lang et al.2019; Murakami et al.2015). Moreover, Akiyama et al. demonstrated that topical unoprostone significantly improved central visual sensitivity by increasing macular blood flow in both treated and contralateral eyes of RP patients (Akiyama et al.2014). Therefore, increasing choroidal blood flow is a potential treatment strategy for preserving cone-mediated central vision in RP.

Oxidative stress is a critical mechanism underlying cone death in RP, but other factors should not be ignored. Increasing evidence suggests that oxidative stress acts through other mechanisms, including inflammation (Nakatake et al.2016) and metabolic stress (Donato et al.2020). The contribution of other pathways to cone death in RP remains unclear (Fig.1).

The role of oxidative stress in RP

The degeneration of rod causes the imbalance of ROS formation and the antioxidant defense system, where SOD converts O−2to oxygen (O2) and H2O2; catalase catalyzes H2O2into water and oxygen; GPx catalyzes H2O2into H2O via oxidation of GSH into its disulfide form (GSSG). Excessive ROS triggers oxidative stress, resulting in injuries to proteins, lipids, and DNA and finally the loss of cone outer segments. Meanwhile, the oxidative damage aggravates the metabolic stress, which also accelerates the disfunction and death of cone. In eyes, trophic factors are endogenously secreted proteins, which mainly bind to RPE, photoreceptors, and Müller cells to regulate cell proliferation, maturation, survival, and regeneration (Eftimiadi et al.2021). After binding to the cell surface or nuclear receptors, cell survival signal transduction is activated, which involves many cell survival pathways with numerous downstream signaling molecules (Abed et al.2015; Ivanovic et al.2011). Numerous in vitro and in vivo animal studies of retinal degeneration have proved that trophic factors have positive effects on the prevention of cone degeneration, as in RP (Byrne et al.2015; Lipinski et al.2011; Yang et al.2009). Therefore, the loss of trophic factors produced by rods leads to the death of cones.

Leveillard et al. first identified an essential trophic factor for cone viability and named it rod-derived cone viability factor (RdCVF), a truncated thioredoxin-like protein lacking thioloxidoreductase activity produced by photoreceptors (Leveillard et al.2004). There are two isoforms coded by the nucleoredoxin-like 1 (NXNL1) gene: RdCVF and RdCVFL (Elachouri et al.2015). Interestingly, both RdCVF and RdCVFL promote cone survival by two distinct mechanisms. RdCVF is expressed in the inner and outer retina. Decreased RdCVF expression is related to rod denegation (Reichman et al.2010). Furthermore, RdCVF acts on cones by stimulating aerobic glycolysis specifically through a combination containing basigin-1 (BSG1) and the glucose transporter GLUT1 (Ait-Ali et al.2015). As the sole glucose transporter in the retina, GLUT1 is a transmembrane protein facilitating passive transport to increase the access of glucose to cones, resulting in increased accessibility of cones to triglycerides for the regular renewal of outer segments (Ait-Ali et al.2015). Compared to the control groups, the injection of RdCVF prevents damage to 40% of cones in the rd1 mouse during a period of 2 weeks (Leveillard et al.2004). In the P23H rat, an autosomal dominant RP (ADRP) animal model, Yang found that RdCVF prevented secondary cone degeneration by increasing the number of cones and significantly preserving their function (Yang et al.2009). The protective effect of RdCVF after transplantation of photoreceptors has also been shown in rd1 mouse (Yang et al.2015). Therefore, the loss of RdCVF can lead to energy failure and degeneration of cones, which leads to nutrient deficiency, another mechanism of cone death in RP.

Mei et al. found that cones express only one of the twoNXNL1gene products, RdCVFL, which protects cones by reducing oxidative damage. After RdCVFL expression was silenced in cone-enriched culture, the decreasing cellular viability showed that RdCVFL is a cell-autonomous protective mechanism (Mei et al.2016). Compared to the intravitreal injection of 7m8-scCAG-RdCVF, the coinjection of 7m8-scCAG-RdCVF and 7m8-scCAG-RdCVFL increased the photopic ERG in rd10 animals, which suggested an additional protective effect of coexpression gene-therapy on cones (Byrne et al.2015). Furthermore, the effectiveness of RdCVFL depends on glucose metabolism by cones, which is stimulated by RdCVF (Mei et al.2016). Inconclusion, the non-cell autonomous action of RdCVF and the cell-autonomous activity of RdCVFL protected cone function and central vision, which correlated the mechanism of trophic factors with metabolic stress and oxidative stress (Clerin et al.2020) (Fig.2).

The pathways involved in metabolic stress in RP

Decreased production of RdCVF causes nutrient deficiency when less glucose entries into cones through impaired GLUT-1 function, which leads to reduced activation of mTOR, and the dysfunction of cones. Except for RdCVF and RdCVFL, other trophic factors have been shown to slow the progression of retinal degeneration. In rd10 mice, long-term doxycycline-inducible expression of glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) significantly improved the cone density but did not stop the process of degeneration. The effect of GDNF on cones is likely linked to the effect of GDNF on rods since only slightly better maintenance of survival and function for cones than for rods was observerd (Ohnaka et al.2012). In a novel ex vivo model of end-stage RP, GDNF had a limited, dose-dependent, protective effect on cone survival, while CNTF had a better neuroprotective effect. However, compared with cones on a background without rod photoreceptor degeneration, all groups with CNTF or GDNF treatment had significantly reduced cone numbers, indicating that the presence of rods is crucial to the survival of cones (Lipinski et al.2011). Therefore, even reduced rod photoreceptor function, coupled with greatly enhanced photoreceptor survival, may be preferable to the inevitable cell death and blindness associated with the lifetime course of this disease. This is particularly vital to the viability of cone photoreceptors. Both ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF) and oncostatin M (OSM) have been shown to be neuroprotective in both in vitro and in vivo experiments and have a high possibility to delay progressive retinal degeneration, where optokinetic response (OKR) and photopic electroretinography (ERG) demonstrated the function of surviving cones (Yang et al.2021). Additionally, CNTF not only slows down cone degeneration, but also promotes regeneration in the outer segments of cones in a transgenic rat model of RP (Li et al.2010; Wen et al.2012). In clinical trials, three patients maintained the visual improvement after implantation of encapsulated cell-based CNTF delivery (Sieving et al.2006), and CNTF resulted in a dose-dependent increase in retinal thickness, but no therapeutic benefit was observed in clinical trials (Birch et al.2013). Liang et al. have suggested that the preservation of rods improves the survival of cones, when AAV-delivered CNTF is used (Liang et al.2001).

As the first neurons converting light to electrical signals in the visual pathway, photoreceptors have high metabolic demands relying on glucose (Narayan et al.2017). Cones have greater energy requirement than rods and consume large amounts of energy for phototransduction and signal transmission at the synaptic terminals. Chinchore et al. demonstrated the importance of the glycolytic pathway in the rod outer segment maintenance and proved that aerobic glycolysis supports the biosynthetic needs of normal neurons (Chinchore et al.2017). With continuous death of rods in RP, to maintain the integrity of the neural network, neurite outgrowth in the ONL often occurs in mutant mice lacking functional photoreceptors. Moreover, compared with the fast-degeneratingrdretina, photoreceptor degeneration is significantly delayed in theCnga3−/−Rho−/−mouse, which may become a better model to study the feasibility of retinal transplantation (Claes et al.2004). The ectopic synaptogenesis between bipolar cells and photoreceptors has been observed in several mouse models of RP (Cideciyan et al.1999; Claes et al.2004; Michalakis et al.2013). In addition, surviving cones with shorter outer segments and much smaller cell body than usual were observed in late stages, which suggested that the consumption of energy was diverted from phototransduction to synaptic terminals for neurotransmitter and receptor syntheses. Besides, separate investigations of RdCVF and mTORC1 have suggested that enhancing glucose entry into cones is a common point of the two pathways for prolonging cone survival (Wong and Kwok,2016). Cone outer segment synthesis and function were restored by directly injecting glucose in the subretinal area, which suggested that glycolytic starvation of cones causes the loss of functional structures and central vision. (Wang et al.2016). Although neurite outgrowth has a positive impact on protecting cones in the early stage of RP, it increases the metabolic burden in the terminal period (Peng et al.2000).

Another hypothesis for the starvation of cones is decreased cell density. Normally, RPE provides nutrition and oxygen for retinal cells and phagocytes in the outer segment of the photoreceptors, which establishes a large number of contacts between RPE and photoreceptors. The loss of rods significantly reduces the interactions between RPE and cones, which leads to compromised glucose uptake, low mTOR activity, and shortened cone outer segments. In such restricted nutrient conditions, cones finally die due to initiated chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), a type of autophagy that preserves cell functions under nutritional stress and is featured by the lysosome-associated membrane glycoprotein 2A (LAMP-2A) (Li et al.2011). As a hormone controls glucose uptake in cells and activates mTOR functions, insulin could prolong cone survival (Bovolenta and Cisneros,2009). A cone-specific phosphodiesterase was observed after secondary rod loss in zebrafish, in which most photoreceptors in zebrafish are cones, with about 92% in zebrafish larvae and about 60% in adults (Zang and Neuhauss,2021), suggesting that the secondary photoreceptor death may be related to cell density (Chalmel et al.2007).

The expression of many genes involved in cellular metabolism changes in RP, which indicates that nutrition shortage, specifically the reduced glucose levels, could cause cone degeneration. The phosphoinositide 3 kinase/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/mTOR) signaling pathway, a key pathway that regulates cellular metabolism, plays an essential role in cone survival (Ivanovic et al.2011). Punzo et al. reported that activated mTOR gradually decreased with cone degeneration in four mice models, and that no evidence was found for increased macro autophagy. Thus, in the absence of rods, compromised glucose uptake and low mTOR activity result in the death of cones under starvation (Punzo et al.2009). Glucose deficiency decreases mTOR activation, which indicates that a low level of mTOR reflects poor nutrient supply (Swiech et al.2008). In response to diminished nutrition conditions, low mTOR activity aggravates autophagy to balance the sources of energy by re-absorbing intracellular proteins and organelles. Macroautophagy and chaperone-mediated autophagy are two types of autophagy, but neither is significantly observed only in dying cones, which suggests that the mechanism of starvation perhaps involves compromised glucose uptake and low mTOR activity (Bovolenta et al. 2009).

In particular, mTOR is a serine kinase, which contains two distinct complexes. The mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) maintains anabolic processes (such as protein translation) and catabolic processes (such as autophagy); mTORC2 catalyzes AKT phosphorylation to metabolize glucose. The key difference between these complexes is that mTORC1 contains Raptor, while mTORC2 contains Rictor, and different mTOR complexes have independent effects (Masui et al.2014). mTORC1 has been demonstrated to maintain cone function and promote long-term cone survival in RP progression (Venkatesh et al.2015; Yao and Wijngaarden,2020). The PI3K/AKT signal transduction pathway is the main pathway for signal transmission through mTOR and plays an important role in mediating cell survival and proliferation. Up-regulation of the mTOR pathway by highly active PI3K activity was found to improve photoreceptor survival, while the role of mTOR in regulating the metabolic transcriptome in healthy cones is minimal (Ma et al.2015). AKT directly phosphorylated mTOR and indirectly inhibited mTOR through the tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC1/TSC2). Knockdown of the tuberous sclerosis complex 1 (Tsc1) gene specifically in the rods of the Pde6b (H620Q/H620Q) preclinical RP mouse model prevented photoreceptor degeneration (Zhang et al.2016). As the main downstream effectors of mTOR, S6 kinase B1 (S6K1) in rd10 mice (Lin et al.2018) and 4EBP1 in rd16 mice (Saltykova et al.2021) promote photoreceptor function and visual performance. Carrying a hypomorphicNphp6allele, the rd16 mouse showed normal genesis of photoreceptors, including the formation of cilia, followed by abnormal elaboration of OS and rapid degeneration. Cideciyan et al. have suggested that cone death occurs in both rd16 retinas and extra-central human retinas due to non-autonomous processes, indicating that the degeneration of rods has a deleterious effect on the survival of cone (Cideciyan et al.2011). Thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP), a regulator of metabolism and inhibitor of the antioxidant thioredoxins, strictly regulates glucose by providing feedback to GLUT1 (Yoshioka et al.2012). RdCVF binds to BSG1, and GLUT1 causes increased glucose entry into the cones (Ait-Ali et al.2015). Wang et al. summarized that the glucose induction of TXNIP regulates AKT in cones, thereby directing aerobic metabolism for cone outer segment synthesis. In addition, TXNIP blocks some thioredoxin functions and promotes NADPH activity, which may be beneficial to the visual cycle (Wang et al.2016).

In healthy retina, with a ramified morphology containing small cell bodies and long processes, microglial cells are located in the outer and inner plexiform layers, which builds an organized territorial network for cellular interactions with other retinal neurons and macroglia (Murakami et al.2020a). However, when microglial cells are activated by cytokines, as in RP, they will transform into an amoeboid shape and migrate to the outer retina (Zhang et al.2018). Phagocytosis removes debris and preserves retinal homeostasis and is a recently recognized method for microglia to cause neuronal death by phagocytosis of viable cells (Brown and Neher,2012, 2014). The phagocytic process has been summarized as a three-step model: "find me": apoptotic cells release chemokines (for example, Fractalkine (Noda et al.2011)), so that microglia are recruited around the apoptotic cell; "eat me": the apoptotic neuron membrane expresses the signal (for example, phosphatidylserine (PS) (Segawa and Nagata,2015)), which is recognized by microglia and forms a phagosome; and "digest me": the apoptotic cell is digested and decomposed in the phagosome (Savill et al.2002).

Microglial activation has been observed in the early pro-degenerative stage of RP (Blank et al.2017). The rod photoreceptor degeneration causes activated resident microglia to move to the outer nuclear layer and clear the debris (Micera et al.2020). It has been suggested that microglia activated by primary rod cell death may kill adjacent healthy cone photoreceptors by releasing cytotoxic factors, which may lead to cone degeneration (Gupta et al.2003).

Müller glia provide essential metabolites, phagocytose waste, and control the composition of the surrounding microenvironment to meet the high metabolic requirements of photoreceptors and drive microglia to promote photoreceptor survival (Karlen et al.2020). As an inflammatory chemokine, fractalkine (CX3CL1) is expressed on retinal neurons, while its receptor CX3CR1 is located on microglia, which plays an important role in the guidance of microglial migration and regulates the microglial surveillance level (Karlstetter et al.2015). Müller cells increase the secretion of CX3CL1, microglial migration, and CX3CR1 expression on microglial cells, suggesting that Müller cells play an important role in photoreceptor homeostasis (Zhang et al.2018). Through the subretinal injection of AAV serotype 8 (AAV8) soluble CX3CL1 (sCX3CL1) in three different RP mouse models, improved cone survival and cone-mediated visual function have been observed (Wang et al.2019).InCx3crl1deficient (Cx3cr1GFP/GFP) rd10 mice, deceasing CX3CR1 in microglia increased the expression of inflammatory cytokines and microglial activation markers (Zabel et al.2016).Cx3crldeficiency dysregulated microglial activation, leading to increased cone vulnerability because scotopic and photopic ERG b-wave amplitudes were significantly smaller in rd10/Cx3cr1GFP/GFPmice than in rd10/Cx3cr1+/ GFPmice, while the dysfunction of rods remained similar (Peng et al.2014). In addition, inflammatory cytokines, including TNFα, are increased in RP patients and rd10 mice, suggesting sustained chronic inflammation (Martinez-Fernandez de la Camara et al.2014). The protective effect on cone function and survival was observed in TNF alpha deficient mice (Rana et al.,1863). Reducing microglial phagocytosis likely decreases local inflammation and protects the neighboring photoreceptors from degeneration.

Recent studies on RP patients show not only local inflammation but also peripheral inflammatory responses, such as secreted chemokine (C–C motif) ligand, which may aggravate the visual function (Olivares-Gonzalez et al.2021a). CCR2, the receptor for chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2), is involved in the pathological process of RP (Raghu et al.2017). Compared to the rd10 (Ccr2+/+rd10) mice, a smaller number of microglia, significantly thinner retina, higher rhodopsin gene expression, and better retinal function were observed in theCcr2−/−rd10mice, which indicate that the absence of CCR2 diminished microglial activation and reduced retinal damage (Guo et al.2012). Furthermore, through the depletion ofCcl2, the attenuation of circulating blood inflammatory monocytes delayed cone degeneration rather than rod degeneration in rd10 mice. Thus, the peripheral damage of circulating blood inflammatory monocytes should be considered while developing potential treatments of RP (Funatsu et al.2022).

In addition, studies have shown that the complement system is involved in phagocytosis, especially complement receptor 3 (CR3). With many protein receptors located on the microglial surface, including complement receptor 3 (CR3), a microglia-expressed receptor for the C3 activation product iC3b, phagocytosis by microglia is regulated by C3-CR3 signaling (Kinugawa et al.2013). Interestingly, genetic ablation of C3 altered retinal inflammatory gene expression. Deficiency of C3 or CR3 accelerated structural and functional photoreceptor degeneration by reducing microglial phagocytosis of apoptotic photoreceptors and increasing microglial neurotoxicity to photoreceptors. In rd10 mice with C3 deficiency at P35, reduced cone-dominated light-adapted responses suggested that C3 deficiency accelerated secondarily cone degeneration following rod death (Silverman et al.2019). It could be explained that the complement activation is adaptive to guarantee the efficient clearance of dying photoreceptors while generally limiting photoreceptor loss (Newton et al. 2020).

Microglial/macrophage activation could be caused by delayed clearance of all-trans-retinal from the retina and impaired RPE phagocytosis in RP (Kohno et al.2013). Moreover, oxidative DNA damage is involved in the regulation of microglial activation (Gallenga et al.2021). The promotion of cone survival by decreasing proinflammatory and oxidative stress was described in Gyp treatment (Li et al.2021) and nutraceuticals with antioxidant properties (NUT) treatment (Olivares-Gonzalez et al.2021b). This suggests that microglial activation is associated with other mechanisms.

More than 3,000 mutations in nearly 70 genes have been reported to accelerate the progression of RP and cause different phenotypic severities of RP (Dias et al.2018), which include phototransduction (for example, the unfolded protein response [UPR] of rhodopsin), rhodopsin recycling pathways (for example,RPE65mutations in RPE andRLBP1mutations in RPE and Müller cells), and photoreceptor structure (for example, ciliary defects ofRPGRmutations). Although some gene mutations do not affect proteins expressed in cones, secondary cone dysfunction and death do ultimately occur (Verbakel et al.2018). Complete darkness has been reported to delay the progression of RP, while light exposure accelerated photoreceptor degeneration in animal models (Naash et al.1996; Tam and Moritz,2007). In RP patients with rhodopsin mutations, the inferior retina exposed to light from above is more likely affected than the superior retina (Li et al.1994).

The mutation in the rhodopsin (RHO) gene, the light-sensitive G protein–coupled receptor involved in phototransduction exclusively in rods, is a common cause of ADRP and autosomal recessive RP (ARRP) due to several mechanisms of photoreceptor degeneration, such as cellular toxicity due to protein misfolding and aggregation in ADRP or impaired activation or reduced opsin level in ARRP (Kanan et al.2007; Travis et al.2007). Krebs et al. have indicated light-induced cone bystander damage in transgenic T17M rhodopsin (T17M) mice, where light exposure (15,000 lx, 2.5 min) led to an obviously decreased rod function (scotopic data) in the first several hours, followed by a significant deterioration of cone morphology (loss of peanut agglutinin stained outer and inner segments) within days. When compared with unilluminated mice, cone degeneration was found to be significantly accelerated under light exposure, suggesting that RP patients with rhodopsin mutations should avoid brief durations of intense illumination in daily life (Krebs et al.2009). In theRHOT4R dog model, cones were preserved initially after 24 h of light exposure. Two weeks after exposure, cone-arrestin labeling revealed loss of cone inner segments and outer segments with highest doses of light (1590 lx, 820 lx, 1 min) (Sudharsan et al.2017).

In rods, the protein encoded by the catalytic b subunit of the phosphodiesterase 6 (PDE6B) gene is a key phototransduction enzyme in the hydrolysis of cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP). A mutation inPDE6Bhas been reported to cause ARRP due to an inability to breakdown cGMP within rod outer segments (Han et al.2013). In rd10 mice carrying thePde6bmutation, dark rearing has been reported to improve function of cones by observing increased photopic ERG (Sundar et al.2020). Furthermore, Kutsyr et al. observed that an increasing housing light intensity has a role in the neurodegenerative process of RP. Differences in cone morphology under different light intensities have been described: short and swollen cone outer segments and decreased axon length at 5 lx; loss of axons and pedicles that emerge directly from the cell body at 50 lx; and only the cell body at 300 lx. Moreover, their results suggested that light induces oxidative stress and inflammation, accelerating the rate of photoreceptor degeneration (Kutsyr et al.2020).

CRALBP is coded byRLBP1and is abundantly expressed in both RPE and Müller cells, whose mutations are associated with visual impairments, such as ARRP. CRALBP accelerates the production of 11-cis-retinol. In anRlbp1knockout mouse model, rhodopsin regeneration, 11-cis-retinal production, and dark adaptation after illumination were delayed, which led to accumulation of all-trans-retinyl esters, suggesting that the isomerization of all-trans retinyl esters to 11-cis-retinol was impaired (Saari et al.2001). CRALBP a is expressed exclusively in the RPE, while CRALBP b is expressed in Müller cells (Wang and Kefalov,2011). M-cone loss and impaired cone-driven visual behavior, as well as light response, have been observed inRlbp1knockout mice. Additionally, dark rearing ofRlbp1knockout mice preserves cone function by restoring cone b-wave maximal response and improving cone sensitivity levels, but it cannot rescue cone dark adaptation, suggesting that minimizing light exposure helps protect cones in RP patients with CRALBP-based visual disorders (Xue et al.2015).

In the studies discussed above, although the genetic mutations in the visual cycle do not target cones, light accelerates both rod and cone photoreceptor degeneration. These results suggest that the survival of cones depends on the preservation of rods. Furthermore, it is necessary to minimize light exposure in RP patients with specific mutations during official examinations and regular sunlight exposure (Paskowitz et al.2006).

Understanding the mechanism underlying cone degeneration in RP is critical for developing treatments for cone protection. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that the pathology of RP cannot be explained by a single mechanism, but it likely involves several different factors. The degeneration of rods in RP leads to a metabolic abnormality of cones owing to the loss of trophic factors, which is followed by a morphological change in cones that results in cone dysfunction. In addition, retinal vascular abnormalities and inflammation cause oxidative stress, which leads to cone death because the redox power of cones relies on the metabolism of glucose through the pentose phosphate pathway. Given the role of RdCVF and mTOR in promoting cone survival, altered glucose metabolism may play a major part in cone degeneration in RP, but this does not rule out contribution by other factors. Further studies into the interplay among those factors will be significant for understanding the homeostasis between protection and degeneration in RP and for identifying the most appropriate treatment of RP.
