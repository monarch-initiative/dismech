# Clinical, cellular, and genomic consequences of a population-enriched SETD1A missense variant
**Authors:** Ament S, Lease R, Oshone R, Ahmed Y, Ali S, Arjona S, Choe J, Colantuoni C, Cortes-Gutierrez M, Herb BR, Humphries E, Mocci E, O'Hara-Payne R, Kuehner R, Damcott C, Sampath H, Shaub S, Woelfel K, Wolford C, Ahn K, Detera-Wadleigh SD, Markx S, Gogos J, Kochunov P, Pollin T, Postolache T, Shuldiner A, McMahon F, Hong LE, Mitchell B

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>  Rare variants in  <italic>SETD1A</italic>  , encoding a histone H3K4 methyltransferase, are among the strongest genetic risk factors for schizophrenia. Exome sequencing (n = 3,736) revealed a population-enriched SETD1A missense variant (P596L) in the Lancaster Old Order Amish founder population, presenting a unique opportunity to elucidate variant-specific, multi-scale mechanisms. Psychiatric and cognitive phenotyping revealed nearly two-fold increased risk for bipolar disorder, accompanied by allele dose-dependent cognitive deficits in adulthood. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from homozygous carriers exhibited signatures of SETD1A hypofunction, including reduced proliferation and heightened susceptibility to replication stress and DNA double-strand breaks. During forebrain-directed differentiation, homozygous mutant cells displayed premature activation of neurodevelopmental transcriptional programs but impaired neural rosette formation, reduced neurite complexity, and early progenitor senescence. Multi-omic profiling revealed dysregulation of gene modules converging on replication stress pathways and neuronal regulatory networks enriched for autism and psychiatric risk genes. Pharmacologic inhibition of the H3K4 demethylase KDM5 partially rescued replication stress and neurite deficits, supporting an epigenetic mechanism and suggesting therapeutic tractability. Together, these findings link a population-enriched missense variant to disrupted chromatin regulation, genome stability, and neurodevelopmental timing, bridging human genetic risk with cellular pathophysiology.  </p>

INTRODUCTION 63 
 64 
Adult-onset psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia (SCZ), bipolar disorder (BD), and major 65 
depressive disorder (MDD) are strongly heritable1,2. Recently, exome sequencing has revealed 66 
rare risk variants for these disorders, including ten high-confidence risk genes for schizophrenia3 67 
and 102 for autism spectrum disorder4. Rare risk variants are valuable for clinical and 68 
mechanistic translation, owing to their relatively large effects on disease risk and easier-to-69 
interpret consequences for the functions of protein-coding genes, compared to common risk 70 
variants. However, the infrequency of these variants in the general population poses logistical 71 
challenges for follow-up studies. 72 
 73 
A case in point is SETD1A, encoding SET Domain Containing 1A, one of the top SCZ risk genes 74 
from large exome sequencing studies3. The initial discovery of SETD1A as an SCZ risk gene 75 
raised several fundamental questions: What clinical phenotypes do SETD1A mutation carriers 76 
exhibit, through what mechanisms do these variants influence brain development and function, 77 
and how might these phenotypes be targeted for precision therapy? Clinical reports now show 78 
that SETD1A variants are associated with a syndrome of neurodevelopmental features, including 79 
cognitive deficits, developmental delay, and epilepsy, along with increased risk for psychiatric 80 

illness, though the presentation is variable and incompletely defined3,5–7. In parallel, work in 81 
model systems demonstrates that reduced SETD1A levels disrupt cortical development and 82 
synaptic function, with mouse and stem-cell studies highlighting altered gene regulation, 83 
impaired neuronal maturation, and circuit deficits8–18. Yet despite these advances, most 84 
mechanistic studies have relied on engineered loss-of-function alleles rather than clinically 85 
observed variants, limiting the ability to link specific mutations to cellular phenotypes and 86 
clinical outcomes. Moreover, clinical phenotyping has focused largely on children, and 87 
systematic characterization of SETD1A carriers in adulthood – when psychiatric symptoms 88 
emerge – remains scarce. Consequently, major gaps remain in understanding how distinct 89 
SETD1A variants shape neurodevelopment across the lifespan. Mechanistic follow-up has been 90 
even more limited for other recently described SCZ risk genes: Although a few of these genes 91 
with well-known functional roles (e.g., GRIN2A, GRIA3, CACNA1G) have enabled progress on 92 
their rapid investigation, risk genes without a prior neurobiological hypothesis remain almost 93 
completely uncharacterized19–21. 94 
 95 
Multi-scale studies in founder populations represent a unique opportunity to establish variant-96 
level genotype-phenotype relationships2,22. In founder populations, genetic drift results in certain 97 
otherwise-rare variants becoming much more common than in the broader population. Some of 98 
these population-enriched variants have substantial effects on disease risk. Studies of population-99 
enriched variants have proven invaluable in characterizing disease mechanisms and therapeutic 100 
opportunities in a variety of common illnesses23–26. However, the contributions of population-101 
enriched variants in adult-onset psychiatric disorders are poorly understood2. 102 
 103 
Here, exome sequencing of 3,736 individuals in the Lancaster Old Order Amish (OOA) founder 104 
population revealed an OOA-enriched, deleterious missense variant in SETD1A. We performed 105 
genotype-first studies to describe its clinical, neurodevelopmental, and genomic consequences. 106

RESULTS 108 
 109 
A population-enriched SETD1A missense variant associated with cognitive deficits and risk 110 
for bipolar disorder 111 
 112 
Population isolates provide unique opportunities for clinical deep phenotyping and functional 113 
studies of population-enriched variants. We analyzed exome sequences from two Lancaster Old 114 
Order Amish (OOA) cohorts to characterize population-enriched protein-coding variants in 115 
established risk genes for neuropsychiatric conditions. The Amish Connectome Project (ACP) 116 
recruited OOA families multiply-affected with mental health disorders. The Amish Wellness 117 
Study (AWS) conducted broad phenotyping of additional individuals from the same OOA 118 
parishes, including assessments of mood symptoms, as well as history of clinical diagnoses and 119 
medication use. We sequenced the exomes of 89 ACP participants with major depressive 120 
disorder (MDD), 22 with bipolar disorder (BD), ten with schizophrenia spectrum disorders 121 
(SCZ), 51 with other diagnoses, and 262 with no mental illness. We analyzed these exome 122 
sequences together with those of 3,302 unaffected AWS participants who reported no history of 123 
mood symptoms, psychiatric diagnoses, or prescriptions for medications commonly used in the 124 
treatment of psychiatric disorders.  125 
 126 

In these data, we identified 108 OOA-enriched (population-specific MAF > 1%), deleterious 127 
(CADD > 10) protein-coding variants in established risk genes for psychiatric or 128 
neurodevelopmental disorders. Although our cohort was underpowered for genome-wide 129 
association studies, several analyses support impacts on psychiatric risk in the OOA. A 130 
generalized linear mixed model controlling for polygenic background in our cohort revealed 131 
nominally significant associations with increased risk for BD or MDD for 15 of the 108 variants 132 
(P < 0.05; Fig. S1, Tables S1, S2). This included replication of two previously described OOA-133 
enriched risk variants for mood disorders -- rs78247304 (KCNH7 R394H)27 and rs201250006 134 
(CNOT1 M547I)28. More broadly, OOA-enriched variants in established neuropsychiatry-related 135 
gene sets -- e.g., risk genes for neurodevelopmental disorders, targets of the Fragile X Messenger 136 
Ribonucleoprotein -- were more likely to reach nominal significance levels for association with 137 
BD or MDD than OOA-enriched variants in other genes (Tables S3-S5). Overall, these data 138 
suggest that OOA families segregate population-enriched variants in multiple genes previously 139 
implicated in risk for psychiatric conditions, potentially valuable for genotype-first callback 140 
studies and deep phenotyping. 141 
 142 
We selected rs139187973, henceforth SETD1A P596L, for follow-up for the following three 143 
reasons: (i) SETD1A is a genome-wide significant risk gene for schizophrenia in the largest 144 
exome-sequencing study of SCZ to date (SCHEMA: P = 2.0e-12)3, and we previously reported a 145 
non-OOA pedigree with BD that segregated a distinct SETD1A variant29. (ii) SETD1A P596L 146 
was associated with a 1.8-fold relative risk for BD in our sample (P = 0.027; Fig. 1A). (iii) 147 
SETD1A P596L was the top variant from multi-parameter prioritization, combining evidence 148 
from OOA-specific genetic associations with protein-protein interactions in a gene network 149 
centered on established risk genes for neuropsychiatric traits (Fig. S2). (iv) SETD1A P596L is 150 
exceptionally common in the OOA population, facilitating recruitment of carriers: The variant 151 
had an allele frequency of 5.2% in our OOA sample, compared to 0.09% in the broader 152 
population (gnomAD exomes; 55-fold enriched), corresponding to 390 copies in our association 153 
study of BD, as well as 721 heterozygotes and 28 homozygotes in a broader database of 154 
Lancaster OOA who have participated in research studies at our institution. 155 
 156 
Little is known about the clinical characteristics of adult carriers of SETD1A variants beyond 157 
their associations with schizophrenia and related diagnoses. We recruited SETD1A P596L 158 
homozygotes (n=15), heterozygotes (n=18), and non-carriers (n=15) from the OOA population to 159 
test the hypothesis that carriers present quantitative differences in SCZ- and BD-related cognitive 160 
and behavioral dimensions, independent of diagnosis. Carriers performed worse than non-161 
carriers on two subtests of the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS)30,31, 162 
including Verbal Memory (P = 0.02) and Tokens (P = 0.04) subtests (Fig. 1E). We found no 163 
differences in performance on other cognitive dimensions assessed by the BACS (Fig. S3), nor 164 
on symptom domains assessed by the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)32,33; Fig. 165 
S3). These findings suggest selective disruption of cognitive domains commonly affected in 166 
schizophrenia, independent of diagnosis. 167 
 168 
Notably, the effects of SETD1A P596L alleles on cognition appeared additive, with 169 
homozygotes performing worse than heterozygotes, especially on the Verbal Memory subtest. 170 
Our cohort spanned the adult lifespan from 24 to 75 years. As expected, cognitive performance 171 
was lower in older participants, and the SETD1A P596L carriers with the lowest scores were 172 

>65-years-old (Fig. S4). In summary, SETD1A P596L is a population-enriched missense variant 173 
associated with bipolar disorder risk and cognitive deficits in specific sub-domains. 174

SETD1A P596L results in SETD1A hypofunction in patient-derived induced pluripotent 177 
stem cells 178 
 179 
We set out to understand the molecular and cellular consequences of SETD1A P596L in human 180 
cells. We obtained peripheral blood mononuclear cells from two homozygous carriers and 181 
reprogrammed them to produce three clonal induced pluripotent stem cell lines (iPSCs) from 182 
each donor. As controls, we studied three iPSC and embryonic stem cell (ESC) lines from non-183 
carriers. Each cell line was validated for the expression of pluripotency markers, pluripotent stem 184 
cell morphology, normal karyotype, and SETD1A P596L genotype. 185

hypofunction. Hypofunctional variants of SETD1A have previously been shown to cause 188 
increased susceptibility to DNA double strand breaks6. To measure this, we assayed the 189

SETD1A loss-of-function has also been associated with a decreased rate of cellular 198 
proliferation35,36. We assayed this via incorporation of the thymidine analogue 5-ethynyl-2′-199 
deoxyuridine (EdU). P596L iPSCs proliferated slower than +/+ iPSCs, with a median of ~10% 200 
fewer cells replicating over a 3-hour period (ANOVA: P = 0.002; Fig. 2B). Consistent with this, 201 
P596L iPSCs displayed a slower doubling time, despite their otherwise normal morphology and 202 
expression of pluripotency markers. 203 
 204 
We performed a rescue experiment to confirm that decreased cellular proliferation arose from 205 
SETD1A hypofunction. We transfected P596L and +/+ iPSCs with in vitro transcribed reference-206 
sequence SETD1A mRNA at a supraphysiological concentration (250 ng per 12-well dish, 207 
delivered via a ribonucleoprotein complex).  SETD1A overexpression rescued cellular 208 
proliferation to levels comparable to +/+ iPSCs (ANOVA: P = 0.01; Fig. 2C). By contrast, 209 
SETD1A overexpression had no effect on the proliferation of +/+ iPSCs. Together, these results 210 
suggest that SETD1A P596L results in cellular signatures of SETD1A hypofunction.  211

Transcriptomic consequences of SETD1A P596L during forebrain neurogenesis 214 
 215 
The effects of SETD1A in the brain are thought to begin during neurodevelopment13,15,37. We 216 
focused on the early stages of neurogenesis, as complete loss of SETD1A in mouse embryos 217 


resulted in a failure of neurogenesis at the neural stem cell (NSC) stage37. Proliferation of neural 218 
stem cells (NSCs) and neural progenitor cells (NPCs) is tightly regulated in the human neocortex 219 
and is a important mechanism contributing to the number and types of neurons produced38. To 220 
model these effects, we differentiated P596L and +/+ iPSC clones to forebrain-specified NPCs 221 
via embryoid bodies, using dual SMAD inhibition (Fig. 3A). NPCs were serially passaged to 222 
model intrinsic changes that contribute to their neurogenic or gliogenic characteristics39. 223

DISCUSSION 445 
 446 
In this study, we identified and functionally characterized a population-enriched SETD1A 447 
missense variant, P596L, associated with bipolar disorder risk and quantitative cognitive deficits 448 
in the Old Order Amish founder population. By integrating human genetics, stem cell modeling, 449 
multi-omic profiling, and network reconstruction, we demonstrate that SETD1A P596L produces 450 
cellular and transcriptional phenotypes consistent with partial loss of SETD1A function and 451 
implicate epigenetic dysregulation as a central mechanism linking risk variation to altered 452 
neurodevelopmental trajectories. 453 
SETD1A is among the most robust rare variant risk genes for schizophrenia, yet the clinical 454 
spectrum associated with non-truncating variants remains incompletely understood. The 455 
enrichment of P596L in the Amish population enabled quantitative phenotyping across genotype 456 
groups independent of categorical diagnosis. Carriers showed additive impairments in verbal 457 
memory and executive function without broad symptom differences, suggesting that SETD1A 458 
variation contributes to dimensional cognitive vulnerability across psychiatric disorders. These 459 
findings extend prior reports of SETD1A loss-of-function variants3,5,6 by suggesting that 460 
hypomorphic missense alleles can produce measurable cognitive effects in adulthood, even in the 461 
absence of psychosis. 462 
Our data suggest selective effects of P596L on cognitive domains commonly affected in 463 
schizophrenia and in experimental models of SETD1A loss of function. The Verbal Memory 464 
deficit is consistent with impairments in learning and memory processes linked to hippocampal–465 
prefrontal circuits, while reduced Token Motor performance may reflect slowed psychomotor 466 
and processing speed associated with fronto-striatal network function56. Notably, these results 467 
extend prior studies of SETD1A haploinsufficiency: individuals with pathogenic variants often 468 
show developmental cognitive impairment and learning difficulties, and mouse models exhibit 469 
deficits in working memory and learning tasks along with abnormalities in prefrontal circuit 470 
function 57. The relative specificity of the observed BACS deficits therefore aligns with 471 
convergent evidence that SETD1A disruption preferentially affects neural systems supporting 472 
memory formation, cognitive control, and information-processing speed rather than producing a 473 
uniform global cognitive impairment. 474 

In patient-derived iPSCs, P596L recapitulated key signatures of SETD1A hypofunction, 475 
including increased susceptibility to replication stress and reduced proliferation, which was 476 
rescued by SETD1A overexpression. These findings are consistent with prior work implicating 477 
SETD1A-mediated H3K4 methylation in genome stability and DNA damage 478 
responses14,35,36,58,59. The convergence between genetic association and cellular phenotypes 479 
strengthens the interpretation that P596L impairs SETD1A enzymatic activity and is not a benign 480 
population variant.  481 
Multi-omic profiling revealed that SETD1A P596L alters the temporal dynamics of neural 482 
induction. Early stages were characterized by accelerated activation of neurogenic transcriptional 483 
modules, whereas later stages exhibited partial convergence with controls. Importantly, 484 
premature upregulation of neurodevelopmental gene networks did not enhance differentiation 485 
efficiency. Instead, P596L cells displayed impaired rosette organization, altered neurite 486 
architecture, and reduced neurite initiation. These findings suggest that early transcriptional 487 
acceleration may disrupt coordination of developmental programs, leading to less efficient neural 488 
lineage progression. This dissociation between transcriptional activation and morphologic 489 
maturation suggests that SETD1A regulates aspects of the timing and integration of 490 
neurodevelopmental programs. Such temporal dysregulation may be particularly relevant to 491 
psychiatric risk, where subtle alterations in cortical development can have long-lasting circuit-492 
level consequences. 493 
Notably, bidirectional effects on developmental tempo have emerged as recurring phenotypes 494 
across iPSC models of neuropsychiatric risk genes, including multiple chromatin regulators60–64. 495 
In a pooled CRISPRi screen that quantified neuronal differentiation state by pseudotime, 496 
repression of several ASD-associated chromatin genes (including CHD2, ASH1L, and ARID1B) 497 
shifted cells toward delayed maturation, whereas perturbation of other high-confidence risk 498 
genes (including CHD8) shifted cells toward accelerated differentiation by the same 499 
metric64.  Complementing these pooled perturbation data, CHD8 loss-of-function models 500 
revealed defects in neuroectodermal differentiation and disorganization during early neural 501 
lineage progression63. Thus, timing effects can manifest as either premature or inefficient 502 
specification depending on the gene, allele, and differentiation context. A broader interpretation 503 
is that chromatin regulators help establish the “pace” of human neuronal maturation by shaping 504 
the accessibility and readiness of transcriptional maturation programs. Overall, these 505 
comparisons place SETD1A P596L within an emerging class of risk-associated perturbations 506 
that alter developmental timing, while underscoring that accelerated activation of neurogenic 507 
gene expression programs can coexist with impaired morphogenesis; i.e., “faster” transcriptional 508 
progression is not necessarily equivalent to more effective or better coordinated neuronal 509 
differentiation. 510 
Our results also indicate that SETD1A P596L disrupts DNA damage responses, cell-cycle 511 
regulation, and proliferative capacity across developmental stages. Mutant cells showed elevated 512 
basal γH2AX, hypersensitivity to hydroxyurea-induced replication stress, recurrent activation of 513 
replication and checkpoint genes, and reduced proliferation. With serial passaging, neural 514 
progenitors developed morphological and molecular hallmarks of cellular senescence. Together, 515 
these findings support a model in which SETD1A hypofunction produces chronic replication 516 
stress that culminates in premature progenitor arrest. 517 

These phenotypes are consistent with prior mechanistic work establishing SETD1A as a key 518 
regulator of genome stability14,35,36,58. SETD1A, the catalytic subunit of the COMPASS complex, 519 
deposits H3K4me3 at active promoters, but also plays a critical role at replication forks. In yeast 520 
and mammalian systems, loss of Set1/SETD1A or H3K4 methylation sensitizes cells to 521 
replication stress and impairs fork protection. SETD1A-dependent H3K4 methylation promotes 522 
recruitment of repair factors such as FANCD2 and stabilizes stalled forks during S phase58. Our 523 
observation of elevated DNA breaks and activation of homologous recombination and 524 
checkpoint pathways in P596L cells is therefore consistent with a primary defect in replication 525 
fork protection. Rapidly dividing neural progenitors may be especially vulnerable to such 526 
defects, given the high replicative demand during early corticogenesis. The premature 527 
senescence observed in SETD1A P596L NPCs provides a plausible developmental consequence 528 
of sustained replication stress. Persistent DNA damage signaling can drive stress-induced 529 
senescence, leading to stable cell-cycle arrest. If progenitor pools undergo early senescence, 530 
neurogenesis may become quantitatively constrained and temporally dysregulated, potentially 531 
establishing a mechanistic connection between genome instability and inefficient neural 532 
differentiation. 533 
These findings also align with a broader literature implicating DNA damage and replication-534 
associated mechanisms in neuropsychiatric disorders. Many genes in our DNA stress module are 535 
central to DNA repair and checkpoint pathways, and mutations in these genes are known to 536 
cause monogenic neurodevelopmental disorders65–67. Increasing evidence suggests that 537 
replication stress in neural progenitors can promote somatic mutation accumulation, raising the 538 
possibility that inherited chromatin variants may create vulnerability to developmental “second-539 
hit” mosaic events68. Thus, SETD1A hypofunction may contribute to psychiatric risk not only 540 
through transcriptional dysregulation but also by increasing genomic instability during critical 541 
windows of brain development. Consistent with this theme, several top schizophrenia risk genes 542 
identified by SCHEMA – including CUL1, RB1CC1, and HERC1 – encode critical regulators of 543 
cell cycle progression and DNA damage response signaling. Collectively, our data reinforce an 544 
expanded view of SETD1A as a multifunctional chromatin regulator with roles not only in 545 
promoter H3K4me3 deposition but also in replication fork protection and coordination of 546 
checkpoint signaling. A hypomorphic missense variant such as P596L can therefore plausibly 547 
produce convergent effects on genome integrity, cell-cycle control, and neural lineage 548 
progression, linking chromatin regulation to replication stress and altered neurodevelopment. 549 
Gene regulatory network reconstruction revealed interactions of SETD1A P596L with two 550 
largely separable transcriptional networks: one governing proliferation and DNA damage 551 
responses (enriched for targets of E2F and MYBL2 family regulators), and another governing 552 
neuronal lineage specification (enriched for targets of TCF4, FOXP1, RORA, and MEF2A). The 553 
presence of bridging transcription factors -- including E2F7, SP3, and the schizophrenia risk 554 
gene SP43 -- suggests layered regulatory integration between proliferative and neurogenic 555 
programs. This organization supports a mechanism in which SETD1A perturbation does not 556 
globally disrupt transcription but instead shifts the balance between canonical cell-cycle and 557 
neurodevelopmental regulatory modules. The convergence of master regulators that are 558 
themselves implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders further emphasizes shared pathogenic 559 
pathways. 560 

Pharmacologic inhibition of the H3K4 demethylase KDM5 partially rescued DNA damage 561 
susceptibility and neurite deficits in P596L cells. Although not all phenotypes were fully 562 
restored, these findings support a causal role for H3K4 methylation imbalance in mediating at 563 
least a subset of SETD1A-associated cellular abnormalities. This mechanism is reinforced by 564 
recent work from Erwin and colleagues, who reported that acute effects of SETD1A 565 
haploinsufficiency in engineered human neural models can be rescued by KDM5 inhibition15. 566 
Prior work in Setd1a+/− mice demonstrated a complementary therapeutic strategy via inhibition 567 
of a distinct histone lysine demethylase, LSD1 (KDM1A)9. Collectively, these findings suggest 568 
that restoring H3K4me2/3 tone can buffer downstream consequences of SETD1A hypofunction.  569 
These preclinical results have immediate translational relevance because brain-penetrant LSD1 570 
inhibitors are already in clinical development for neuropsychiatric indications, including a Phase 571 
IIb program evaluating vafidemstat (ORY-2001) in negative symptoms of schizophrenia69,70. 572 
While it remains unknown whether LSD1 inhibition will benefit patients specifically carrying 573 
mutations in SETD1A or other chromatin remodeling genes, the convergence of genetic evidence 574 
implicating H3K4 methylation pathways in psychiatric risk and reversibility of core 575 
cellular/circuit phenotypes in model systems motivates biomarker-driven clinical translation. 576 
Since SETD1A mutations are rare, the greatest benefit will emerge if a broader subgroup could 577 
be defined. In practical terms, this may entail the development of pharmacodynamic readouts of 578 
H3K4 methylation state and DNA damage stress signatures in patient-derived cells, which could 579 
enable stratifying future trials by molecular features that reflect SETD1A/H3K4 axis dysfunction 580 
rather than diagnosis alone. 581 
At the same time, the partial rescue we observe with KDM5 inhibition suggests important 582 
limitations: Variants in SETD1A and other chromatin remodeling genes likely perturb multiple 583 
layers of regulation -- promoters, enhancers, replication-associated chromatin, and stress 584 
responses -- so demethylase targeting may correct only those phenotypes most proximal to 585 
H3K4me imbalance. This sets up a testable model for future work: rescue should be strongest for 586 
replication stress/DNA damage and select aspects of neurite initiation/architecture, whereas 587 
phenotypes driven by longer-range developmental mistiming, accumulated cellular stress, or 588 
non-enzymatic SETD1A functions may require combinatorial or earlier intervention. 589 
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Although we analyzed 590 
multiple independent iPSC clones from carriers and non-carriers, the high GC content of the 591 
genomic sequences surrounding the P596L variant precluded generation of a clean isogenic 592 
correction line, leaving open the possibility that linked background variation contributes 593 
modestly to the observed phenotypes. While the population enrichment of P596L enabled 594 
recruitment of one of the largest cohorts of SETD1A variant carriers studied to date, the clinical 595 
and cellular effects of this specific allele may not generalize to other SETD1A missense variants 596 
or to truncating mutations associated with schizophrenia. Our in vitro neural induction model 597 
captures early forebrain development but does not recapitulate later stages of cortical circuit 598 
assembly, non-neuronal cell types such as microglia or oligodendrocytes, or the influence of in 599 
vivo environmental factors. Pharmacologic rescue experiments with KDM5 inhibition 600 
demonstrate partial reversibility of selected phenotypes but do not establish long-term functional 601 
recovery or in vivo efficacy. Together, these considerations highlight the need for 602 
complementary approaches – especially the establishment of broader allelic series -- to fully 603 
define the pathogenic mechanisms of SETD1A variation. 604 

Finally, our study underscores the value of founder populations in identifying and characterizing 605 
rare, high-impact variants for psychiatric disorders. In the Lancaster Old Order Amish, the 606 
SETD1A P596L allele is markedly enriched (~5% in our sample vs. ~0.09% in the broader 607 
population), enabling detection of its association with bipolar disorder and cognitive differences 608 
despite a modest cohort size. This enrichment also permitted recruitment of multiple adult 609 
homozygous carriers -- an exceedingly rare genotype in the general population -- for detailed 610 
cellular analyses. Founder populations thus function as natural genetic experiments, where 611 
demographic bottlenecks amplify otherwise rare alleles and facilitate genotype-first phenotyping. 612 
Leveraging this context allowed us to move from variant discovery to mechanistic dissection 613 
within a single study, a trajectory that is often difficult to achieve in psychiatric genetics. 614 
Importantly, P596L represents only one of a broader set of variants enriched in the Amish 615 
population associated with neuropsychiatric traits22,27,28,71, including variants in dozens of other 616 
well-established neuropsychiatric risk genes. This constellation of population-enriched alleles 617 
provides an immediate opportunity to expand mechanistic studies within the same well-618 
characterized cohort. Because multiple carriers can be identified for each variant, it becomes 619 
feasible to scale iPSC-based modeling approaches beyond single-gene case studies toward 620 
population-level functional genomics. Our groups have already begun systematic derivation of 621 
iPSC lines across variant carriers to enable comparative analyses of convergent and divergent 622 
cellular phenotypes, integration with deep clinical data, and testing of shared therapeutic 623 
strategies72. As sequencing efforts expand in genetic isolates, similar frameworks could support 624 
scalable, genotype-first platforms that bridge human genetics and experimental neurobiology, 625 
accelerating the translation of rare variant discoveries into biological insight and precision 626 
approaches in mental health disorders. 627

Genetic association studies. We computed associations of variants with MDD and BD affection 661 
status using generalized linear mixed models implemented with GMMAT78. We controlled for 662 
covariates of sex, age, and genetic relatedness, using a Balding-Nichols empirical kinship matrix 663

Variant filtering. We annotated polymorphic variants in our exome sequencing dataset using 671 
the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor80, as well as an in-house database of OOA-specific variant 672 
characteristics81. We defined high-quality OOA-enriched protein-coding single-nucleotide 673 
variants based on the following criteria: (i) MODERATE or HIGH impact (i.e., missense, splice 674 
site, and protein-trunacting variants) to a canonical transcript model; (ii) Combined Annotation 675 
Dependent Depletion (CADD)82 Phred score > 10; (iii) global minor allele frequency < 0.001 in 676 
both the gnomAD exomes83 and 1000 Genomes Phase 384 datasets; (iv) OOA-specific minor 677 
allele frequency >1% and <10%; and (v) a Mendelian error rate of 0 (plink –mendel)85 in the 678 
AWS dataset.   679

Prioritization of variants based prior associations with neuropsychiatric traits. Our analysis 681 
focused primarily on OOA-enriched variants in established risk genes for psychiatric and 682 
neurodevelopmental disorders, as defined by the National Advisory Mental Health Council 683 
Workgroup on Genomics (https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/priority-research-areas/genomics-684 
research). In addition, we considered evidence for each gene’s association with psychiatric traits 685 
based on other types of genetic and genomic analysis, including GWAS of common variants, 686 
transcriptomic studies of post-mortem human brain tissue, and systems biology approaches. In 687 
total, we considered 30 gene lists from these prior studies (Table S3). For each gene list, we 688 
looked up which genes overlapped associations from our OOA cohort. We tested whether this 689 
overlap was greater than expected by chance using Fisher’s exact tests. In addition, we applied a 690 
network biology approach as part of our strategy to prioritize specific variants for follow-up, 691 
leveraging protein-protein interactions from the STRING database (v12)86. Over-representation 692 
for interactions was evaluated by Fisher’s exact tests and confirmed by edge permutations, as we 693 
have described previously28. To prioritize specific genes in a network context, we used igraph87 694 
to compute each gene’s eigen-centrality in a STRING sub-graph composed of interactions 695 

between a gene set of interest and OOA-specific risk genes. The version of this analysis shown 696 
in Fig. S2 utilized targets of FMRP, which were the most strongly over-represented for 697 
interactions with OOA-specific risk genes. Similar results were obtained for other enriched gene 698 
lists. 699

Integration, dimensionality reduction, and cell type annotation. To integrate data across 862 
donors and time points, Seurat’s anchor-based integration was used. Briefly, RNA samples were 863 
first integrated across each timepoint using 3,000 highly-variable features to minimize batch 864 
effects, then new integration anchors were calculated across genotypes to harmonize RNA 865 
expression values. For the ATAC data, we first compute latent semantic indexing (LSI) 866 
embeddings for each cell. We then integrated across all donors and timepoints using reciprocal 867 
latent semantic indexing (rLSI) projection from the first 40 dimensions. Dimensionality 868 
reduction was performed by principal component analysis (PCA) on normalized, integrated gene 869 
expression values using the top 3,000 highly variable genes and by LSI on the integrated ATAC 870 
count matrix. A weighted nearest neighbors (WNN) graph was constructed using 50 PCs and 40 871 

LSI components and used to make the UMAP for clustering and visualization. Cell types were 872 
assigned based on the expression of established marker genes and confirmed by correlation with 873 
published single-cell reference datasets from developing neocortical cell types97. In addition, 874 
cells were annotated to cell cycle state using Seurat’s cell-cycle scoring pipeline. Briefly, we 875 
applied CellCycleScoring with curated S-phase and G2/M-phase gene sets (Seurat 876 
cc.genes.updated.2019) to compute per-cell S.Score and G2M.Score. Cells were assigned to 877

Gene co-expression modules. Gene co-expression modules were calculated from scRNA-seq 901 
data as previously described47. Briefly, read counts were imputed to restore correlation structure 902 
using knn-smoothing (k = 15, d= 30). We then performed k-means clustering (k = 50) to identify 903 
modules of co-expresed genes. Genes with weak correlations to the module centroid were 904 
removed (r < 0.3). Modules with strongly correlated centroids were merged by average-linkage 905 
hierarchical clustering using 1 – Pearson’s r as a distance metric, with a cut height = 0.15. To 906 
annotate co-expression modules to known functional categories and pathways, we performed 907 
gene set enrichment analysis using the fGSEA R package102. Within each module, genes were 908 
ranked by module membership (kME), the strength of the Pearson correlation between each 909 
gene’s expression and the module centroid. Enrichment was assessed against curated gene sets, 910 
including MSigDB hallmark and GO collections (MSigDB). Enrichment scores and adjusted P 911 
values were computed using 10,000 permutations per gene set. 912 

Gene regulatory networks. We reconstructed a gene regulatory network (GRN) model using 913 
our scMultiome data, similar to our prior work47. Briefly, model reconstruction began with a 914 
physical model of transcription factor binding sites within accessible chromatin peak, using 915 
motifs from JASPAR2020 within Signac96 and motif-to-TF mappings from the MotifDb R 916 
package. The resulting TF occupancy site predictions were then mapped to potential target 917 
genes, where proximal peaks were mapped to transcription start sites (TSSs) within 2 kb and 918 
distal peaks were mapped to TSSs via co-accessibility, computed with Cicero46. Next, we applied 919 
a random forest regression framework (GENIE348) to identify a set of TFs with binding sites in 920 
each gene’s regulatory elements that together predict that genes expression variability across 921 
cells in our scMultuome dataset. We retained the top 200,000 TF-gene interactions, and each TF-922 
gene interaction was assigned a regulatory type (activating vs repressing) by the sign of the 923 
Pearson correlation between the expression of the TF and the target gene across all cells. To 924 
identify master regulators of gene co-expression modules, we tested for overrepresentation of a 925 
TF’s predicted targets within a given module using Fisher’s exact tests, testing activating and 926 
repressing targets separately. We validated the computationally-derived E2F7 regulon using 927 
E2F7 ChIP–seq data from ENCODE42,103. E2F7 peak regions (ENCFF938ZPZ) were annotated 928 
to the nearest TSS (±2 kb) using ChIPseeker104.  929
