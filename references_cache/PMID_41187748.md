# A tabletop blast device for the study of the long-term consequences of traumatic brain injury on brain organoids.
**Authors:** Sirtori R, Pandey A, Shukla A, Fallini C
**Journal:** Cell Rep Methods (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.crmeth.2025.101213](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.crmeth.2025.101213)

## Content

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is the leading environmental risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases, yet its molecular link to chronic neurodegeneration is unclear. While animal models of TBI are commonly used, emerging research suggests that induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived brain organoids offer a promising human-specific alternative, particularly for studying processes like cryptic exon splicing. However, widespread use has been limited by methodological variability and the need for expensive and specialized equipment. To address these challenges, we developed a tabletop blast device capable of delivering highly reproducible pressure waves via a gravity-based pressure chamber. We validated the applicability of our approach by assessing the short- and long-term consequences of mechanical stress on brain organoids after pressure wave exposure. Our approach provides a controllable and reproducible method to apply complex pressure cycles on brain organoids, enabling broader accessibility for studying the mechanistic links between TBI and neurodegeneration in a human-relevant context.

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is the leading environmental risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases.1 A single moderate-to-severe TBI increases the risk of developing dementia by 4-folds.2 Recent studies have also linked TBI to the pathological accumulation of neurotoxic proteins such as tau,3,4 TDP-43,5 and amyloid-beta,6 which are hallmarks of neurodegenerative diseases like amyotrophic lateral sclerosis7 (ALS) and Alzheimer’s disease,8 in both animal models and in vitro systems based on stem cell-derived brain organoids.

Rodent TBI models have been instrumental in advancing our understanding of the acutely toxic events that occur after injury and of the chronic changes to the brain tissue that promote long-term neuronal dysfunction and neurodegeneration. However, the specific risk factors and pathophysiological processes underlying the connection between TBI and distinct neurodegenerative pathologies remain poorly understood. This is particularly relevant for human-specific processes that contribute to the degeneration of neurons when subjected to mechanical injury in three-dimensional contexts, which cannot be fully recapitulated in other non-human in vivo models.

Emerging research on cortical brain organoids (cBOs) and TBI suggests that this technology provides a promising model for addressing these questions. However, this is a challenging area of research for many reasons, including the methodological variability across studies. Current approaches employ a wide range of methods to deliver mechanical stress to organoids, including high-intensity focused ultrasound9, piezoelectric actuator-generated pressure,10 or external mechanical stimulation.11 These techniques often require specialized technologies, relevant costs, and engineering skills, which may not be readily available in typical biomedical laboratories.

To address these challenges, we developed a low-cost tabletop blast device capable of delivering highly reproducible pressure waves to 3D organoids. We validated the applicability of our approach for studying blast TBI-linked neurodegeneration by assessing the consequences of mechanical stress on brain organoids acutely and 1 week after pressure wave exposure. Our study demonstrates the design of a controllable, portable, reproducible, and cost-effective method to generate arbitrarily complex pressure cycles on brain organoids.

The benchtop pressure loading simulator is a portable and easy-to-use device that generates high-amplitude pressure pulses of short duration, mimicking the transient pressure loading experienced during blast injury TBI. The benchtop pressure wave simulator operates by generating a short-duration compressive stress pulse through impact, which then propagates through the material.12,13

Since the acoustic impedance of aluminum is greater than that of the liquid, stress is continuously transmitted into the liquid through multiple reverberations within the aluminum plug. The amplitude of the transmitted stress (σ˜T) in the N-th reverberation (where N = 0, 1, 2, 3 …) is given by Equation 412,13(Equation 4)σ˜T(N)=(ZP−ZLZP+ZL)Nσ˜where ZP and ZL represent the acoustic impedances of the plug (P) and liquid (L), respectively.

As the pressure pulse propagates through the liquid, it reaches the opposite interface between the liquid and the aluminum vessel. Here, the wave again splits into two components: (1) a reflected wave that remains in the liquid and (2) a transmitted wave that enters the aluminum vessel. The superposition of the reflected and incoming pressure waves in the confined liquid-filled cavity leads to an increase in net pressure. Due to the small size of the cavity, multiple reflections occur, further amplifying the pressure magnitude in the liquid.

The calibration of the blast simulator was performed to correlate peak loading pressure (Pmax) and specific impulse (I0) as a function of the height of the drop weight. The schematic of the calibration setup and a typical transient pressure history are shown in Figure S2. It is important to note that the typical pressure time course exhibits multiple peaks due to the reverberation of the pressure wave in the fluid media (Figure S2B). A secondary pressure pulse is also generated due to the impact of the drop weight after rebound. However, its magnitude (peak pressure ∼10% of first peak) is benign and hence was ignored (Figure S3). The peak pressure measured in the first pressure pulse was consistently the maximum pressure recorded in all trials and was therefore considered as Pmax. Changing the size of the drop weight (i.e., 70 mm vs. 215 mm in length) affected the magnitude of the pressure spikes, but not the first pressure peak (Figure S2B), which is in line with the pressure magnitude dependence on the drop weight height.

To quantify the max pressure and specific impulse experienced in the liquid-filled chamber, three repeat trials were carried out using a short (i.e., 70 mm) or a long (i.e., 215 mm) drop weight (Figure 2). The drop height varied in steps of 2 mm, ranging from 2 to 10 mm. Regardless of the drop weight size, the peak pressure recorded remained the same given the same drop height (Figure 2A). However, the specific impulse increased with the increase in the size of the drop weight (Figure 2B), due to the higher-pressure spikes (see Figure S2B). To test whether the pressure wave generated by the blast device could be used to mimic TBI in vitro, we generated human cBOs from two independent control induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) lines, as previously described.9 By day 45 in culture, these organoids exhibited robust neuronal MAP2 expression and possessed both deep (i.e., CTIP2-positive)- and upper-layer (i.e., SATB1-positive) cortical neurons, as determined by immunocytochemistry and western blot (WB) assays (Figures S4A and S4B). Astrocytes were detected at about 3 months in vitro (Figures S4C and S4D). At that time, we exposed cBOs to traumatic injury using a 0.9–1.2 MPa peak pressure and 0.6 MPa pressure impulse (70 mm drop weight and 6 mm height).

To determine whether our model system could reproduce processes known to occur in human TBI, we assessed acute neuronal death 24 h after pressure wave exposure, as well as well-characterized neuropathological events 1 week after injury (Figure 3A). Epidemiological and experimental evidence suggest that impact or blast TBI is one of the leading environmental risk factors for neurodegeneration, especially if repetitive injury occurs.2 For example, a recent study showed that pressure wave-mediated mechanical stress on brain organoids could cause a frontotemporal dementia (FTD)-like phenotype characterized by the accumulation of phosphorylated Tau (pTau) and the mis-localization of the RNA-binding protein TDP-43 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, leading to splicing alterations including cryptic exon retention.9 We thus wanted to test whether the mechanical injury delivered through our engineered device could similarly model the pathological long-term effects of pressure wave exposure on cBOs. TDP-43 is a well-characterized regulator of mRNA splicing, and nuclear depletion of TDP-43 leads to a widespread disruption of mRNA splicing including the de-repression of cryptic exons. For instance, cryptic exon inclusion in stathmin-215 (STMN2) and UNC13A16 introduces a premature stop codon that causes a reduction in their total mRNA and protein levels, while a cryptic exon in HDGFL217 leads to the expression of a novel protein isoform. As expected, given the observed dyshomeostasis of TDP-43 following blast injury, we found that both STMN2 and UNC13A protein levels were reduced in cBOs 1 week after injury (Figures 4D–4G), with changes in UNC13A levels strongly correlated with the degree of TDP-43 nuclear depletion (Figure 4K). Additionally, we were able to detect HDGFL2 cryptic exon-containing isoform in protein extracts from pressure wave-exposed cBOs (Figures 4L–4O), validating the presence of cryptic exons linked to TDP-43 dysfunction in our experimental setting. Altogether, these results prove our device as a powerful tabletop tool for modeling the neurodegenerative processes initiated by the blast TBI, producing pathological signs comparable to what was previously described using alternative methods.

While the previous results prove that the injury imparted by our device reproduces established phenotypes related to the TBI-like injury on cBOs, we wondered whether our experimental setup would allow us to detect biochemical and mechanical alterations linked to the long-term consequences of pressure wave exposure. Studies using rodent models of TBI have provided key insights into the consequences of trauma on neuronal function, including increased DNA damage18 and reduced activation of the pro-survival and anti-inflammatory cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) pathway.19 However, whether similar phenotypes could be observed in human systems, and persist across time, is unknown. Here we studied the presence of DNA damage following blast injury using the double-strand break marker γH2A.X. We further confirmed a global increase of γH2A.X levels by WB (Figures 5E and 5F), suggesting that pressure wave-induced mechanical stress may be a source of genomic instability. Altogether, our findings demonstrate the validity of our experimental model to study the molecular, biochemical, and mechanical underpinnings of TBI-related neurodegeneration, making this a valuable and accessible tool to advance research in this area.

TBI is a major cause of death and disability, affecting more than 65 million people annually.20 Beyond the initial injury to the brain tissue, TBI triggers a series of secondary events including neuroinflammation, excitotoxicity, and metabolic dysfunction that contribute to an increased risk of chronic diseases, including neurodegeneration12. TBI can affect people of all ages, but epidemiological data clearly suggest that some groups, including military service members and veterans, the elders, and people living in rural areas, have a greater risk of experiencing TBI caused by direct impact of blast exposure and of suffering from the long-term negative health outcomes that are linked to the initial injury21. Despite decades of promising work using animal models of TBI that generated hundreds of clinical trials, no pharmacological therapy yet exists that significantly improves TBI outcomes.

Animal models have been pivotal to assessing both the short-term effects of mild and severe TBI, and some of the systemic changes that lead to the appearance of neurodegenerative phenotypes. However, these models have so far failed in fully recapitulating the human neurodegenerative process, leading to a failure in translating therapeutic approaches from the laboratory to the clinic. On the other hand, human TBI postmortem studies, which recapitulate human-specific pathogenic events, are limited by the scarcity of tissue and are restricted to a short time window immediately after the injury. Thus, developing innovative and accessible experimental models for human TBI is fundamental to deepening our understanding of the molecular and cellular drivers that link brain mechanical stress and injury to chronic neurodegeneration.

Human iPSC-derived cBOs offer a valid and promising alternative to mimic the transient pressure loading experienced during TBI,9,10,11,22 allowing the study of human-specific pathogenic processes in a relatively complex brain-like environment. However, the current cBO-based TBI models require involved engineered devices and skills to adequately mimic pressure wave propagation though the nervous system. Because of the costs and complexity of such systems, including high-intensity focused ultrasound waves, pneumatic or piezoelectric actuators to apply rapid compression, and microfluidic systems to induce sheer stress, the use of cBOs to study TBI is still relatively limited.

In this paper, we propose a low-cost device to overcome these limitations. Our benchtop pressure wave simulator is a portable and easy-to-use tool that generates pressure pulses, mimicking the transient pressure loading that is experienced during TBI linked to blast exposure. Blast TBI (bTBI) is a form of TBI caused by the intense pressure wave of an explosion. This pressure wave, or blast overpressure, can cause injury to the brain even without direct impact to the head and is the leading cause of mild to moderate TBI in military service members and veterans.23

The physics principles governing the functioning of the simulator are easily understandable, as the pressure load generated during the injury fundamentally depends on the velocity of impact of the free-falling weight onto a piston. The impact generates a pressure wave that propagates though the piston and into the media, inducing mechanical stress on the immersed and free-floating cBOs leading to bTBI-like neuronal injury.

The elementary principles behind our device make it easy to use it and tune it depending on the type of peak pressure needed. Indeed, by modulating the height of the drop or the physical properties of the weight itself, it is possible to target a specified peak pressure. Furthermore, by changing the length of the drop weight, it is possible to modulate the duration of the pressure peak, which determines the magnitude of the pressure impulse (i.e., kPa/s), as shown in Figure 2. We also speculate that, by changing the material of the piston, therefore changing its acoustic impedance, it should be possible to change the pressure wave profile, tuning it according to the user’s needs. It is also important to note that, since the acoustic impedance of the piston is greater than that of the liquid, mechanical stress is continuously transmitted into the liquid through multiple reverberations within the plug (see Figure S2).

In this feasibility study, we compared the effects of pressure wave exposure through our engineered device to published reports that employed alternative methods to deliver mechanical stress to organoids. We found that we could easily replicate phenotypes previously described, such as the short-term increase in cell mortality through the activation of the apoptotic cascade (i.e., cleaved caspase-3) and the dramatic reduction of the expression of neuronal markers (i.e., MAP2; see Figure 3). More importantly, the injury to cBOs imparted through our device successfully replicated the longer-term neurodegenerative phenotypes associated with bTBI. In fact, we confirmed the higher susceptibility of deep-layer neurons (i.e., CTIP2-positive) to mechanical stress compared to upper-layer neurons (see Figure 3) and the effects of blast injury on the aggregation propensity of Tau and TDP-43 (see Figure 4). We further show that TDP-43’s increased aggregation following bTBI leads to changes in the splicing pattern of TDP-43 targets STMN2, UNC13A, and HDGFL2 (see Figure 4). Finally, we showed the potential of our device for future studies investigating the effects of mechanical stress on pathways never described in human TBI models. We found that pressure wave exposure increased genomic instability even 1 week after injury (see Figure 5). While increased oxidative stress has been proposed to drive DNA damage after TBI,18 we speculate that mechanical stress on the cell may be directly responsible for this phenomenon. In fact, we have recently shown in our pre-print publication that increased mechanical strain exerted on the nucleus via the linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton (LINC) complex can directly lead to loss of nuclear integrity and accumulation of DNA damage.24 These results, together with published evidence of genomic instability associated with neurodegenerative disorders, highlight the relevance of this pathway in the context of TBI-induced neurodegeneration. Similarly, the reduction we observed in the activity of the pro-survival and anti-inflammatory CREB pathway mirrored what had been previously described in rodent models of TBI.19 Interestingly, we recently found that decreased CREB activity is an early phenotype associated with FTD/ALS,25 further highlighting the large overlap in the pathogenic mechanisms leading to neuronal death in TBI and in these neurodegenerative conditions. It is important to note that the severity of the phenotypes reported here depends on several input parameters, including peak pressure loading, impulse, and the free movement of the organoids in the media. Modulating the material or the size of the weight or immobilizing the cBOs in the chamber may affect the magnitude and timing of the defects we reported here.

While we clearly show that our experimental model system can replicate previously published results obtained in other in vitro systems, it remains difficult to assess how the pressure loading experienced by the organoids in vitro compares with in vivo condition. TBI is a broad term that can refer to many different injury types, often associated with very different structural and pathological consequences. For instance, penetrating (open) TBI leads to more focal but severe damage to the brain tissue compared to non-penetrating (closed) TBI, which can lead to more diffuse damage that can be severe, moderate, or mild. Within this complex scenario, bTBI represents a type of closed TBI caused by a shock pressure wave transmitted directly into the brain, causing injury. While representing a small fraction of all TBI recorded annually, bTBI has become the subject of intense study because of its relevance to veterans’ health and a better understanding of its potential chronic neurodegenerative effects, particularly following repeated injury.26 Because of the lack of direct impact, this type of TBI can be more easily replicated using in vitro models. In conclusion, in this study, we describe a cost-effective and easy-to-use device that delivers a tunable pressure wave to iPSC-derived organoids. Our findings strongly support the use of this tool to model bTBI in vitro, toward a standardized, highly reproducible, and customizable alternative for TBI research.

While we have carefully characterized the shape and magnitude of the pressure wave generated by our tabletop device, and some of the pathological consequences on brain organoids, several limitations still apply. First, as discussed, it remains difficult to ascertain how the pressure loading experienced by the free-floating organoids compares to the in vivo condition. Further testing modulating the magnitude of the pressure and impulse and the size and freedom of movement of the organoids should be performed to further optimize the system. Importantly, in vivo studies using animal models or human postmortem tissues should be used to validate findings from this model system.

It is also important to note that our device cannot be used to effectively model blunt impact TBI, as the energy transfer from the mechanical force to the brain in such cases leads to different presentations, including focal injury with visible lesions that cannot be fully recapitulated with our device. Alternative methods or in vivo models should be used instead.

Finally, while our device is simple in its governing principles and design, its implementation still requires specialized facilities for the construction, which may limit its broad adoption.

To ensure repeatability in the experiments, it is crucial that no air bubbles remain in the liquid. Therefore, the aluminum plug must first be assembled into the vessel. The liquid should then be introduced into the cavity using a pipette through the horizontal threaded hole, filling it to the brim (Figure S5). The threaded plug, wrapped with PTFE-based sealing tape, should then be inserted into the hole. Finally, the assembly should be placed on a bench, with the other components assembled as shown in Figure S5.

The device was calibrated to create a precise correlation between the drop height (H) of the weight and the resulting peak pressure (Pmax) and specific impulse (I0) generated within the fluid-filled chamber. After filling the chamber with culture medium, a pressure sensor (PCB Piezotronics, ICP 113B22) was screwed into the vessel’s side port. The sensor was connected to a signal conditioner (PCB Piezotronics, 482C), and the output was recorded by a digital oscilloscope (Tektronix, MSO2024). Drop tests were performed using both the short and long drop weights. For each weight, tests were conducted from drop heights ranging from 2 mm to 10 mm, in 2 mm increments. Three replicate drops were performed for each height. The pressure inside the chamber was recorded at a sample rate of 50 MS/s. The highest amplitude peak was consistently defined as the maximum pressure (Pmax). The specific impulse (I0) was calculated by integrating the area under the pressure-time curve. The resulting data for Pmax vs. H and I0 vs. H were fitted using a power law regression to generate predictive equations for experimental use. A schematic of the setup and representative output data is shown in Figure S2.
