# Deficiency of DDX3X results in neurogenesis defects and abnormal behaviors via dysfunction of the Notch signaling.
**Authors:** Duan W, Huang G, Sui Y, Wang K, Yu Y, Chu X, Cao X, Chen L, Liu J, Eichler EE, Xiong B
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2404173121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2404173121)

## Content

The molecular mechanisms underlying the neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) caused by DDX3X variants remain poorly understood. In this study, we validated that de novo DDX3X variants are enriched in female developmental delay (DD) patients and mainly affect the evolutionarily conserved amino acids based on a meta-analysis of 46,612 NDD trios. We generated a ddx3x deficient zebrafish allele, which exhibited reduced survival rate, DD, microcephaly, adaptation defects, anxiolytic behaviors, social interaction deficits, and impaired spatial recognitive memory. As revealed by single-nucleus RNA sequencing and biological validations, ddx3x deficiency leads to reduced neural stem cell pool, decreased total neuron number, and imbalanced differentiation of excitatory and inhibitory neurons, which are responsible for the behavioral defects. Indeed, the supplementation of L-glutamate or glutamate receptor agonist ly404039 could partly rescue the adaptation and social deficits. Mechanistically, we reveal that the ddx3x deficiency attenuates the stability of the crebbp mRNA, which in turn causes downregulation of Notch signaling and defects in neurogenesis. Our study sheds light on the molecular pathology underlying the abnormal neurodevelopment and behavior of NDD patients with DDX3X mutations, as well as providing potential therapeutic targets for the precision treatment.

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including intellectual disability (ID), developmental delay (DD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, among others, have a prevalence of more than 2% globally (1). NDDs affect the development and function of the brain and impose significant economic and social burdens. Children with NDDs typically exhibit one or more symptoms related to motor skills, learning and memory, language and nonverbal communication, and other neuropsychiatric problems (2). The major contributing factors of NDDs are genetic variants (3–5), among which DDX3X is one of the most frequently mutated genes (6, 7). De novo nonsense, frameshift, splice site, and missense mutations in DDX3X have been associated with ID and ASD patients with complex neurological phenotypes such as microcephaly, corpus callosum hypoplasia, ventricular enlargement, hypotonia, and small anterior commissure (7–12). In addition, DDX3X mutations have also been identified in patients with severe speech disorders and childhood apraxia of speech (13, 14). Notably, patients carrying missense mutations sometimes exhibit more severe symptoms than those carrying loss-of-function mutations, suggesting that haploinsufficiency and dominant-negative effects of DDX3X variants may contribute to the pathology (7, 12).

DDX3X, encoding a member of the DEAD-box RNA helicase family, is located on the human X chromosome in the p11.3 to p11.23 region (15). DDX3X is an RNA-binding protein that participates in various RNA metabolisms, such as transcription, unwinding, splicing, stability, nuclear export, and translation (16, 17). These diverse activities have been associated with a broad range of biological processes, including cell cycle, cell growth, apoptosis, tumorigenesis, innate immunity, and virus replication. It has been proposed that the neurodevelopmental defects caused by DDX3X variants could be attributed to the disruption of RNA processes (16, 17).

Several mouse and cell models of DDX3X manipulation have been generated to examine its function during nervous system development. In vitro or in vivo knockdown of Ddx3x leads to abnormal branching complexity, spinous formation, and neurogenesis (7, 18). Female Ddx3x heterozygous mice in epiblast show DD, reductions in certain brain regions, and behavioral anomalies, including hyperactivity, anxiety, and slight contextual associative fear memory deficits (19). Mid/hind brain neural stem cells (NSCs) specific to homozygous Ddx3x knockout mice survive and show dystaxia, epilepsy, and brain developmental defects (20). Prenatal knockdown or heterozygous knockout of Ddx3x in NSCs affects cell cycle and differentiation (7, 21). These studies confirmed the critical role of DDX3X in brain development and function. However, some specific symptoms, such as abnormal social interaction and cognition, have not been recapitulated in the mouse models, and the molecular mechanisms underlying developmental and behavioral defects remain poorly understood. Moreover, there are currently no pharmacological or genetic methods to ameliorate or rescue the neurological defects associated with the DDX3X mutations in these models.

In this study, we reanalyzed DDX3X de novo variants (DNVs) in a combined DD and ASD cohort based on 46,612 trios and revealed the enrichment of DNVs in NDD, DD, and ASD. Furthermore, we generated a zebrafish ddx3x deficiency model and observed a series of morphological and neurobehavioral abnormalities, including reduced survival, DD, microcephaly, reduced social interaction and anxiety, abnormal adaptation, and deficits in spatial recognitive memory. Mechanistically, we show that the disruption of Ddx3x during early brain development reduces the stability of the crebbp messenger Ribonucleic Acid (mRNA), which in turn causes downregulation of Notch signaling, decreasing of the NSC pool, and excitation/inhibition (E/I) imbalance, eventually leading to abnormal brain development and behavior. This study provides insights into the role of DDX3X in brain development and disease, with potential translational implications.

We reanalyzed DDX3X DNVs in a large combined NDD cohort based on 46,612 families with 46,612 probands diagnosed with ASD or DD and 5,241 unaffected siblings (Dataset S01 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). In total, 120 DDX3X DNVs were identified in the NDD probands and 0 DNVs were identified in the siblings. DDX3X is among the top two commonly mutated genes in the NDD cohort (just below ARID1B with 141 DNVs), where 115 DNVs were found in 115 of 31,052 probands with DD and five DNVs were found in four of 15,560 probands with ASD. Among the DDX3X DNVs, 117 were found in 16,530 female probands, and two were identified in 29,704 male probands, with the gender of one proband remaining unknown. A significant enrichment of DNVs was supported by all three statistical models in the DD and NDD cohorts, whereas the DNV burden in ASD is significant only using the DeNovoWEST model (Dataset S01 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). In addition, DNV counts in DDX3X are significantly enriched in probands compared to unaffected siblings (P = 5.03E-06, two-tailed Fisher’s exact test), although this is driven by DD cohorts (P = 2.77E-08), when compared to ASD (P = 0.34) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). DDX3X DNVs show a greater enrichment in DD patients (P = 5.41E-15, odds ratio = 11.56) and female patients (P = 2.20E-16, odds ratio = 105.81) when compared to ASD or male patients. The DNVs affect amino acids located in various structural regions of DDX3X without obvious clustering, indicating that the importance of its functional integrity in neurodevelopment. What’s more, a majority of the DNVs in amino acid sequences are highly conserved, suggesting functional importance (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The hypergeometric distribution analysis revealed a significant overrepresentation of DNVs in conserved regions of zebrafish (Ddx3xa: P = 3.72e-11, Ddx3xb: P = 1.35e-06), Drosophila melanogaster (P = 8.01e-05), and Caenorhabditis elegans (P = 5.70e-06) (Dataset S01 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). We find that the enrichment of DNVs to evolutionary conserved sites is restricted to de novo missense mutations (Dataset S01 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D).

We then performed a meta-analysis for over 300 patients carrying DDX3X variants with phenotypic profiles available. Patients with DDX3X pathogenetic mutations exhibited a wide range of neurological symptoms, including DD (99.6% ,290/294), ID (96.9%, 315/325), delayed speech and language development (86.2%, 88/102), abnormal brain MRI (75.3%, 177/235), sensory behavioral abnormality (66.9%, 83/124), abnormality of movement (55.8, 125/224), abnormal corpus callosum (54.6%, 113/207), sleep disturbance (50.8%, 61/120), abnormal cerebral white matter (41.3%, 64/155), autistic behavior (33.7%, 92/273), and microcephaly (32%, 80/250) (SI Appendix, Table S1). These data indicated that DDX3X pathogenic mutations can cause complex defects related to neurodevelopment.

There are two ddx3x co-orthologs in the zebrafish genome, namely ddx3xa and ddx3xb, which are both highly conserved to human DDX3X (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Therefore, we subsequently used zebrafish models to further dissect the physiopathologic mechanisms of DDX3X in NDD. To determine the spatiotemporal expression patterns of ddx3xa and ddx3xb, we first analyzed published zebrafish single-cell RNA-seq datasets at various developmental stages. These data showed that both ddx3xa and ddx3xb are ubiquitously expressed in the brain, without detectable cell-type specificity (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–J). We then performed in-situ hybridization (ISH) and qPCR, and the results showed that they are both maternally and ubiquitously expressed during embryogenesis, and then mostly expressed in the brain and spinal cord after 1 dpf, suggesting a potential role in neurodevelopment (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C). Since ddx3xa and ddx3xb exhibit highly similar sequence and expression patterns, we speculated that they may function redundantly.

Using CRISPR/Cas9 system, we generated a ddx3xa allele with a 4 bp insertion and a ddx3xb allele with a 11 bp deletion, both of which caused frameshift and early truncation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 D and E). The mRNA levels of ddx3xa and ddx3xb in the corresponding homozygous alleles were mostly diminished, probably due to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G). Of note, ddx3xa mRNA levels were increased in the 3 dpf ddx3xb−/− mutants, suggesting that ddx3xa may compensate for the loss of ddx3xb (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 G and H). Therefore, we generated double mutant alleles, including ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb−/− and ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− for further analyses. The levels of mRNA for both ddx3xa and ddx3xb in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb−/− mutants were essentially abolished. In the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants, the ddx3xa mRNA expression was mostly diminished, and ddx3xb mRNA expression levels were reduced for ~35%, providing a ddx3x deficiency model (SI Appendix, Fig. S4H).

We examined the morphology of the zebrafish larvae of five genotypes, including wild-type (WT), ddx3xa−/−, ddx3xb−/−, ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/−, and ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb−/−. At 28 hpf, ddx3x deficient mutants show significantly reduced proportion of pigmentation than WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). At 32 hpf, 36 hpf, and 48 hpf, the mutants exhibited decreased area of pigmentation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). These results indicate the DD in the mutants. The head length, body length, and head size of ddx3xa−/−, ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/−, and ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb−/− mutants were reduced at 3 dpf and 5 dpf, whereas the ddx3xb−/− mutants appeared to be normal, which may be caused by the compensation effect of ddx3xa (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 G and H and S6 A and B and S7 A–D). The ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb−/− mutants exhibit 100% lethality during larval stage, whereas ~60% of the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants could survive until adulthood (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). The ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb−/− mutants exhibit massive apoptosis in the brain at 1 dpf, 3 dpf, and 6 dpf, which were increased over time and may contribute to the lethality (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and E). Whereas the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants only show increased apoptosis signal in the telencephalon at 3 dpf and 6 dpf (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and E). Adult ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutant zebrafish showed shorter body length and smaller brain size (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E–H). Collectively, these data suggest that ddx3x deficiency can cause DD and microcephaly in the zebrafish model. Since ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− is viable and fertile, we also examined the maternal effect of ddx3xa. The results showed that maternal zygotic ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− exhibited more severe DD, microcephaly, and adaptation defects compared to zygotic ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). These data indicate that the ddx3x deficiency leads to developmental defects in a dosage-dependent manner. Since ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb−/− causes early lethality, we used the maternal zygotic ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− allele for the following analyses.

To further assess the development of different brain regions, we performed ISH and found that the sizes of the dorsal telencephalon (tbr1b) and midbrain (otx2, nk2.2) were reduced, while the ventral forebrain length (nk2.2) appeared to be normal in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). Additionally, we examined the axonal tracts and connections and observed decreased branch number and projection length of the axons at 1 dpf, and reduced connections between the bilateral tecta at 3 dpf and 6 dpf (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and D). Overall, the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants exhibited brain developmental defects, particularly in the telencephalon and midbrain, including reduced brain size, increased apoptosis, and impaired axon development, which correlates with the developmental defects observed in the NDD patients with DDX3X mutations.

To investigate the neuroethological changes in the larvae, we first examined their locomotion activity in light. The ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/−mutant larvae showed no significant difference in basic average speed compared to the WT zebrafish (Fig. 1 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). In the subsequent adaptation test, ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutant larvae exhibited defects in response to repeatedly presented dark stimuli (Fig. 1 A and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and C). In the light preference test, the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants did not show a preference for either light or dark environments (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A and B). Taken together, these results demonstrate that ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutant larvae exhibited adaptation defects.

We then conducted behavioral tests on the adult zebrafish. In the open field test, ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants showed more exploratory activity as they spent significantly more time in the central zone compared to the WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 C and D). In the novel tank test, the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants spent more time in the top zone with a higher entering frequency (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 E–G). Both of the assays indicate anxiolytic-like behaviors in the mutants.

Social communication deficit was reported in NDD patients with DDX3X mutations but not well simulated in rodent models. In the three-chamber tests, WT zebrafish predominantly resides within the social zone, occasionally venturing into the middle or empty zones. In contrast, the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants exhibited reduced duration in the social contact zone with lower entering frequency and a correspondingly longer duration in the middle and empty zone, indicating a weakened preference for social interaction. (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 D and E). In the shoaling test, WT zebrafish typically swam in the same direction and maintained a tight group at all time points, whereas the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants swam toward different directions with a longer average interfish distance, indicating decreased social cohesion (Fig. 1 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 F and G). In the mirror-induced aggression test, zebrafish would attack the mirror as if its own reflection was an intruder. The ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants tended to leave the contact zone with the mirror more frequently and spent less time there, indicating reduced aggression (Fig. 1 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 H and I). Last, we tested spatial memory using a spatial fear conditioning paradigm by T-maze. Before training, both genotypes showed similar color preference. After five consecutive days of training based on electric shock, they both learned to avoid the electric shock zone after every training session. On the 6th day, the WT zebrafish still exhibited avoidance behaviors for the electric shock zone whereas the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants did not, indicating spatial recognitive memory deficits (Fig. 1 J and K and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 J and K). These data demonstrate that ddx3x deficiency in zebrafish leads to developmental and behavioral defects reminiscent of the core features of NDD patients with DDX3X mutations (SI Appendix, Table S1). Therefore, we set out to elucidate the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying these defects.

To gain insights into the cellular basis of the developmental and behavioral defects in ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants, we dissected the brains of 5 dpf larvae and performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq). After quality filtering, a dataset of 15,489 cells was retained (7,686 cells for WT and 7,803 cells for ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/−, with a median UMI counts of 2,300 and median number of detected genes of 1,328). The 15,489 cells were first divided into 23 clusters based on the 3,000 highly variable genes after batch removal (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B). The 23 clusters were further allocated to stem cells, progenitors, neurons, gliacytes, and other types in different brain regions according to the combinatorial expression of molecular markers (Dataset S02 and SI Appendix, Fig. S12 B and C). We then compared the composition of different cell types between ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− and WT. The majority of the cell types related to early developmental stages, including NSCs/radial glial cells, thalamus/pituitary progenitor cells, and neural progenitor cells (serotonergic), were decreased in ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− brains, indicating defects in maintaining the NSC pool (Fig. 2A). To further evaluate cell differentiation and the developmental state of neural cells, pseudotime analyses were performed to order the cell populations (q-value < 0.0001). Notably, the numbers of cells in the trajectory of the NSCs, glutamatergic neurons, telencephalic glutamatergic neurons, inhibitory/GABAergic neurons, and ventral diencephalic dopaminergic neurons showed a significant decrease in the early stages in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− larvae compared to the WT, while thalamus/pituitary progenitors exhibited a significant increase at later stages (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Figs. S13 A and B and S14 A and B). The developmental trajectories of the cell clusters suggested premature differentiation of NSCs. We further estimated the status of cell division and differentiation using Seurat and AUCell. The signals of the S phase of the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− larvae were enhanced in the early stage compared to the WT, whereas G2/M phase scores were similar, suggesting cell cycle arrest in the NSCs (Fig. 2 C–E and SI Appendix, Figs. S13C and S14C). In addition, the signals of positive regulation of cell differentiation were increased in the early and middle stages in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants, indicating that cell differentiation was accelerated. Therefore, we hypothesized that cell cycle arrest and premature differentiation are responsible for the reduction of the NSC pool.

To validate the changes of NSCs in ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants, we performed ISH, qPCR, and fluorescence immunohistochemistry. The RNA expression levels of NSCs markers sox2 and pax3 were reduced at both 1 dpf and 4 dpf, especially in the telencephalon and midbrain (Fig. 2 F and G). Accordingly, the number of NSCs undergoing proliferation was decreased whereas apoptosis was not affected in the dorsal telencephalon (Fig. 2 H and I). These results further support that the decrease of the NSC pool in ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants is mediated by cell cycle arrest. As a result, the numbers of neurons marked by HuC/D in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− dorsal telencephalon was also decreased, which may explain the microcephaly phenotype (Fig. 2 H and I). Overall, ddx3x deficiency causes abnormal fates of neural cells and decreased NSC pool, mediated by arrest of division or premature differentiation.

In the snRNA-seq results, we observed a decreased number of the major excitatory neurons and an increased number in inhibitory neurons (Fig. 2A). To assess the functional changes of these neuronal clusters, we performed gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) based on the gene rank of the expressional fold change. Of note, many of the glutamatergic neuron clusters showed downregulation of genes related to synaptic function, while most of the inhibitory neuron clusters exhibited upregulation of genes related to synaptic function (Fig. 3 A and B). Additionally, we calculated various receptor activities based on the rank of genes using AUCell. The results showed that the glutamate receptor activity signals were decreased in various cell types (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Furthermore, the decrease in glutamate receptor activity signals was attributed to the attenuated ionotropic glutamate receptor activity rather than the G-protein-coupled glutamate receptor activity, suggesting the hypoexcitability of the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Overall, we identified an imbalanced ratio of cell numbers and aberrant synaptic function between the excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants, which may contribute to abnormal neurological behavior.

Next, we conducted several experiments to test the hypothesis. First, we examined the expression patterns of the glutamatergic marker vglut2a and the GABAergic marker gad1b by ISH. The results showed that the expression of both vglut2a and gad1b were decreased at 1 dpf, particularly in the telencephalon (Fig. 3 D and E). Interestingly, at 4 dpf, the expression of vglut2a remained to be decreased, whereas the expression of gad1b was increased (Fig. 3 D and E). Additionally, we detected the expression of several neural markers to validate the snRNA-seq results, including agrp, oxt, glyt2, dat, sst1, pvalb6, pvalb7, and gfap (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 A and B). The expression levels of agrp, glyt2, pvalb6, and pvalb7 were increased, supporting the increased ratio of the GABAergic Purkinje cell and the glycinergic inhibitory neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 A and B). We subsequently performed protein immunoblotting analyses, including Vglut2, Gad65/67, PSD-95 (an excitatory postsynaptic marker), and Gephyrin (an inhibitory postsynaptic marker). The protein levels of the excitatory neuron marker Vglut2 and PSD-95 were decreased, whereas the expression of the inhibitory neuron marker Gad65/67 was increased in the 4 dpf larvae (Fig. 3 F and G). Therefore, the E/I ratio was decreased. Furthermore, to directly compare the brain-wide activity between ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants and WT, we colabeled pErk/tErk by immunostaining and mapping them to the Z-brain database to compute the neural activity of the whole brain (Fig. 3H). The ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants showed significantly decreased neural activity, particularly in the telencephalon and midbrain, which correlates with the decreased E/I ratio (Fig. 3H and Dataset S03).

To test whether the behavioral defects were mediated by the E/I imbalance, we used the neurotransmitter L-glu and the glutamate receptor agonist ly404039 to enhance the glutamatergic activity and examine the larval behavior. The average speeds of both interventions in light conditions were increased compared to the WT, indicating the effectiveness of the treatments (Fig. 4 A and B). To our delight, the adaptation abilities of both intervention groups were significantly improved, indicating that the E/I imbalance of the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants indeed contributes to the adaptation deficits (Fig. 4 C and D). For adults, as the blood–brain barrier prevents L-glu delivery, we used ly404039 to treat ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants for 1 wk and tested the effect on social preference behavior (Fig. 4 E and F). To our delight, the treated zebrafish exhibited ameliorated sociability (Fig. 4F). Taken together, these data suggest that the E/I imbalance of ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants is associated with the abnormal behaviors.

Considering the role of Ddx3x in RNA metabolism, we proceeded to bulk RNA-seq of the 4 dpf larval heads to analyze the genome-wide changes resulting from ddx3x deficiency. The transcriptomes of WT and ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− are highly distinctive based on PCA and correlation heatmap analyses, indicating an altered mRNA expression profile (SI Appendix, Fig. S17 A and B). Next, we identified 2,324 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the 21,905 detected genes Dataset S04 and SI Appendix, Fig. S17C). Of these, 1,025 genes were up-regulated, and 1,299 genes were down-regulated. As positive controls, the expression levels of ddx3xa and ddx3xb were significantly decreased (Dataset S04 and SI Appendix, Fig. S17C).

To assess the contribution of disrupted genes to the neurodevelopmental defects, we conducted overrepresentation analyses using human orthologs of zebrafish DEGs. Our analysis included 945 human orthologs for the down-regulated genes and 641 human orthologs for the up-regulated genes (Dataset S04 and SI Appendix, Fig. S18A). We found a significant enrichment (SI Appendix, Fig. S18B) for down-regulated genes in independently curated disease gene sets related to ASD, ID, DD, and NDD (P < 0.05), as opposed to gene sets for other mental disorders. To address potential biases in the overrepresentation analyses resulting from lack of orthologs in the zebrafish genome, we performed a permutation analysis. Given that the down-regulated gene group comprised approximately 900 genes, we randomly selected 900 genes from the pool of 13,595 human genes with zebrafish orthologs, repeating this process 1,000 times to create a null distribution. We find that down-regulated genes exhibit significantly higher odds ratios compared to the null distribution (Dataset S04 and SI Appendix, Fig. S19). Using an orthogonal approach, we find a significant enrichment of ASD, DD, and NDD risk genes among the down-regulated genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S18C). Among the identified risk genes, 15 were up-regulated and 72 were down-regulated (Dataset S04 and SI Appendix, Fig. S18D). Analysis of the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network revealed complex interactions among the products of the 72 down-regulated NDD risk genes, with EP300, CREBBP, and CHD4 identified as the top three hub proteins showing the most interactions compared to proteins with median number of interactions (EP300: P = 1.56E-19, odds ratio = 43.65; CREBBP: P = 8.94E-10, odds ratio = 13.47; CHD4: P = 1.08E-06, odds ratio = 8.66, two-tailed Fisher’s exact test), indicating the functional importance (Dataset S04).

To investigate the functional changes in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses on the up-regulated and down-regulated genes, respectively. No significant enrichment was identified in the up-regulated DEGs. The down-regulated DEGs were mainly enriched in the Notch signaling pathway, growth, skeletal system morphogenesis, ribonucleoprotein granule, transcription coregulator, and histone binding (Fig. 5A and Dataset S04). We also analyzed the alternative splicing events between the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants and WT and identified 236 differential alternative splicing events that were mainly involved in chondrocyte morphogenesis rather than neurodevelopment (Dataset S05 and SI Appendix, Fig. S17 D–F).

Given the previously reported roles of the Notch signaling pathway in the proliferation and differentiation of the NSCs (22, 23), we speculated that the dysregulation of the Notch signaling may mediate the abnormal neurogenesis in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants. Indeed, the DEGs related to the Notch signaling pathway showed a clear separation between ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants and WT (Fig. 5B). We further analyzed the DEGs related to the Notch signaling pathway using the String database to predict PPIs. Our analysis revealed that the major hub proteins with the most interaction with other proteins were Notch1b, Notch2, Ep300a, Crebbpa, and Crebbpb (Fig. 5C). Of note, EP300 and CREBBP emerged as hub proteins not only in the PPI network of down-regulated NDD risk genes but also in the PPI network of DEGs involved in the Notch signaling pathway. This observation highlights the pivotal role of these two genes in abnormal neurodevelopment of ddx3x deficiency. Since DDX3X is an RNA-binding protein, we reanalyzed three existing CLIP-seq datasets to identify reliable RNA that physically interact with DDX3X (Fig. 5D and Dataset S06). We found that EP300 and CREBBP mRNA in the DEGs of the Notch signaling pathway can physically interact with DDX3X in all three datasets (Fig. 5D and Dataset S06). Since EP300 and CREBBP are coregulators of Notch signaling that interact with NICD/RBPJ to activate the transcription of downstream targets (24, 25), we proposed that Ddx3x may regulate Notch signaling through interaction with ep300 or crebbp mRNA.

To validate the role of Ddx3x in regulating Notch signaling, we examined the expression levels of notch1b, notch2, ep300a, crebbpa, and crebbpb by ISH and qPCR, which were all reduced except for ep300a, especially in the telencephalon and midbrain region (Fig. 5 E and F). Additionally, the protein levels of the cleaved-Notch1, cleaved-Notch2, and Crebbp were decreased (Fig. 5 G and H). Moreover, the expression levels of the Notch signaling downstream targets were also decreased, including hey1, hey2, and heyl (Fig. 5I). Since the bioinformatical analyses indicate that human DDX3X binds to CREBBP mRNA, it is possible that zebrafish Ddx3x binds and stabilizes crebbpa and crebbpb mRNA. To test this hypothesis, we used Actinomycin D to block mRNA transcription and tested the RNA stability of crebbpa and crebbpb in both WT and mutant larvae. As expected, the mRNA degradation rates of crebbpa and crebbpb were significantly increased in ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants compared to WT (Fig. 5J). As negative controls, the mRNA stabilities of wnt2, gpro2, and dll4, which are down-regulated in the mutants but do not directly bind to DDX3X, were not significantly affected in ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Therefore, the reduced mRNA stability of crebbpa and crebbpb may underlie the downregulation of Notch signaling in the ddx3x deficiency model.

To determine whether Ddx3x functions through Notch signaling in early neurogenesis, we first examined the role of the Notch signaling during larval development. We used a γ-secretase inhibitor ly411575 to block the activation of Notch signaling pathway. Indeed, the downstream targets of the Notch signaling were significantly reduced (SI Appendix, Fig. S21A). As a consequence, the number and proliferation of Sox2+ NSCs were decreased (SI Appendix, Fig. S21 B and C). Moreover, the mRNA expression level of vglut2a was decreased, whereas the quantity of gad1b was increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S21 D and E). The protein expression levels of Vglut2 and PSD-95 were decreased, while Gad65/67 and Gephyrin remained comparable to WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S21 F and G). These data show that inhibition of the Notch signaling pathway can indeed lead to a reduced NSC pool and E/I imbalance, similar to the phenotypes observed in the ddx3x deficiency zebrafish.

Next, we activated the Notch signaling genetically and pharmacologically in the ddx3x deficiency model to test whether Ddx3x functions through Notch signaling during neurodevelopment. We microinjected NICD mRNA, which encodes the active form of Notch, into one-cell stage embryos and confirmed that the ectopically expressed NICD could efficiently activate the Notch signaling (SI Appendix, Fig. S22 A–C). Fluorescence immunohistochemistry results showed that the number of NSCs and their proliferation rate were significantly restored upon NICD expression in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants (Fig. 6 A and B). Moreover, ISH and qPCR results showed an increase in the expression levels of sox2 and vglut2a and a decrease in the gad1b level (Fig. 6 C and D); immunoblotting results showed a decrease in Gad65/67 protein level and an increase in PSD-95 level upon NICD expression (Fig. 6 E and F). These results suggested that activation of the Notch signaling can recover the reduced NSC pool and the E/I imbalance in the ddx3x deficiency zebrafish.

Since ddx3x deficiency reduces crebbp mRNA stability, we also used the Crebbp activator TTK21 to rescue the phenotypes. TTK21 treatment led to increases in the expression levels of Notch downstream targets (SI Appendix, Fig. S22 D–F). Similarly, as determined by immunofluorescence staining, ISH, and immunoblotting, pharmacological activation of Crebbp could restore the NSC pool and E/I imbalance defects associated with ddx3x deficiency (Fig. 6 G–L). These data indicate that ddx3x regulates early neurogenesis by promoting Notch signaling via stabilizing crebbp mRNA (Fig. 6M).

DDX3X is a frequently mutated NDD gene, whose missense or nonsense variants have been associated with ID, ASD, severe speech disorder, and childhood apraxia of speech. Genotype–phenotype correlation analyses in NDD patients with DDX3X mutations have shown that patients with missense variants or in-frame deletions are significantly more likely to have severe phenotypes than those with nonsense mutations, such as microcephaly, colpocephaly, as well as more severe language, motor, and adaptive deficits (7, 12). Therefore, it is possible that dominant-negative effects of these variants may affect the function of the WT allele (7). Accordingly, in previous studies, some of the phenotypes, such as social interaction deficits, have not been successfully modeled in heterozygous knockout rodent models (19, 20). Furthermore, rodents possess another ortholog of DDX3X, Pl-10, which may also compensate for Ddx3x function during neurodevelopment. Here, we reanalyzed DDX3X DNVs based on a large mixed NDD cohort, where 120 reliable DNVs were finally identified, and 51/120 variants are LGD (likely gene-disruptive). Given the nonspecificity of the mutation sites, proportion of the LGD variants, and conservation of the mutation sites in vertebrates, we utilized a genetic model of ddx3x deficiency (ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/−) in zebrafish to study the functions of Ddx3x in neurodevelopment and behavior. In this model, the ddx3x expression levels were reduced by approximately 70% (homozygous loss of ddx3xa, heterozygous loss of ddx3xb), which may be a better model for the cases with dominant-negative missense mutations. The zebrafish model exhibited a range of phenotypes with high similarity to the symptoms of patients with DDX3X mutations, including reduced survival, delayed development, microcephaly, reduced social interaction and anxiety, adaptation defects, and memory deficiencies. Importantly, some of these phenotypes were more severe in the ddx3xa−/−; ddx3xb+/− mutants than in the ddx3xa−/− or ddx3xb−/− mutants, suggesting a dose-dependent effect of ddx3x function. Moreover, we show that ddx3x deficiency leads to reduced NSC pool and E/I imbalance, which account for the neurodevelopmental abnormalities including microcephaly, social deficits, and adaptation defects. Notably, E/I imbalance has been proposed to be associated many neuropsychiatric disorders, including ASD, ID, seizures, and schizophrenia (26–29). Furthermore, we show that the major brain region affected by E/I imbalance as determined by hypoexcitability is the telencephalon, which reflects the function of the cerebral cortex in humans. Although previous studies have revealed that Ddx3x deficiency can disrupt neurogenesis and cell cycle (7, 20, 21), the relationship between the cytological changes and the abnormal behaviors remains unknown, the underlying mechanisms were unclear. Our study finds that ddx3x deficiency causes the dysfunction of neurogenesis and the imbalanced differentiation of excitatory and inhibitory neurons during early brain development, which are the bases of the behavioral deficits. Through bioinformatics analyses and biological validations, we show that Ddx3x functions in early neurogenesis through positive regulation of Notch signaling, which has been shown to play critical roles in neurodevelopment. To date, mutations in several genes related to Notch signaling have been reported to cause NDDs, including JAG1 (MIM: 601920, 118450) (30), DLL1 (MIM: 618709) (31), JAG2 (MIM: 619566) (32), RBPJ (MIM: 614814) (33), NOTCH1 (MIM: 616028) (34), NOTCH2NL (MIM: 603472, 619473, 618866) (35). Notch signaling is involved in the regulation of NSC proliferation, survival, and differentiation during the development of the central nervous system (22, 23). Notch signaling dysfunction has also been reported to cause reduced NSC pool, microcephaly, and hypoexcitability (35–37). Neuronal progenitors with high levels of Notch activity maintain their progenitor fate, while intermediate levels of Notch activity lead to the generation of inhibitory neurons (38). Suppression of the Notch signaling downstream factor HES1 initiates differentiation of NSCs into neurons, with the majority of these neurons developing GABAergic characteristics (39). In addition, Notch signaling is closely linked to synapse development, function, plasticity, and excitability (37, 40, 41). These previous findings fit well in our working model that downregulation of Notch signaling causes neurogenesis defects in the ddx3x deficiency zebrafish.

As an RNA-binding protein, DDX3X has been reported to regulate various RNA metabolisms, including transcription (42), RNA stability (43–45), RNA decay (46, 47), splicing (48), and translation (49–51). Here, we show that Ddx3x regulates Notch signaling by stabilizing crebbp mRNA, which is a coactivator of Notch signaling (24, 25). Of note, mutations in CREBBP have been associated with Menke–Hennekam syndrome 1 and Rubinstein–Taybi syndrome 1, which are both NDDs that share similar symptoms with DDX3X mutation patients (52–54). Moreover, CREBBP mutations are shown to cause hypoexcitability (55, 56), similar to the observations in the ddx3x deficiency zebrafish model. CREBBP dysfunction has also been reported to cause abnormalities in NSC maintenance, neuron fate determination, and synapse development (57–59). These similar phenotypes and functional changes between CREBBP and DDX3X models suggest they may share common molecular pathways in regulating neurodevelopment.

In summary, our findings established a causal relationship between ddx3x deficiency and severe neurodevelopmental defects and revealed molecular mechanisms underlying these defects. More importantly, we show that enhancing neuronal excitability or activating the Notch signaling could partially rescue the defects, providing potential therapeutic targets for the treatment of NDD patients with DDX3X mutations.

A large combined NDD cohort based on 46,612 trios was reanalyzed to explore the genetic characteristics of DDX3X DNVs, which was approved by the University of Washington institutional review board (#STUDY00000383). Meta-analysis of DDX3X syndrome phenotypic profile was performed by systematic review. CRISPR/Cas9 system was used to generate a ddx3x deficient zebrafish model. In situ hybridization and RT-qPCR were used to analyze the spatiotemporal expression profile of ddx3xa and ddx3xb, morphology of brain regions, and expression of some neural markers. Larval and adult zebrafish behavioral tests were analyzed using EthoVision XT software (Noldus). snRNA-seq was performed to explore developmental trajectory of neural cells. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay and whole-mount fluorescence immunohistochemistry were used to characterize neurodevelopment and validate the changes of neural cell fates. Brain-wide activity mapping was used to analyze the activity of neuron. Whole-tissue transcriptome, CLIP-Seq data analyses, mRNA stability assay, Notch intracellular domain ectopic expression, and pharmacological intervention were performed to explore the molecular mechanism underlying the abnormal neurodevelopment and behavior. Statistical analyses and visualization were performed using R software or GraphPad prism9. Details are provided in SI Appendix.
