# Structural basis for LMBD1-dependent trafficking and cobalamin export of ABCD4.
**Authors:** Liu Q, Li X, Wu Y, Zheng K, Zhou M, Long T
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-026-74552-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-74552-5)

## Content

Correct trafficking of lysosomal transporters is essential for intracellular homeostasis. While most lysosomal membrane proteins are directed to the lysosome via sorting motifs, the cobalamin exporter ABCD4 is distinct, instead relying on LMBD1 as a dedicated chaperone for its trafficking. Dysfunction of either protein causes inherited cobalamin metabolism disorders. Despite its physiological significance, the molecular mechanism underlying this chaperone-dependent trafficking remains unclear. Here, we report the cryo-EM structures of ABCD4 complex with LMBD1 in the lumen-open, substrate-bound and cytosol-open states. LMBD1 contains nine transmembrane-helices (TMs) and a cytosolic domain, both of which engage ABCD4. Cell imaging shows that disruption of these interactions impairs the trafficking of ABCD4 to lysosomes. Structural and biochemical analyses provide insights into cobalamin recognition and reveal conformational states associated with the proposed cobalamin transport cycle. These findings provide molecular insights into cobalamin metabolism and illustrate a chaperone-assisted mechanism that supports proper trafficking of a lysosomal transporter.

Lysosomes are acidic organelles that degrade biomacromolecules1. Coordinated action of lysosomal membrane proteins and hydrolases maintains macromolecule degradation, nutrient transport, and pH homeostasis. Lysosome biogenesis requires continuous transport of newly synthesized proteins through the ER–Golgi–endosome pathway, guided by sorting signals and recognition factors. Soluble lysosomal proteins are typically directed via the mannose-6-phosphate receptor–mediated pathway, which utilizes specific transport adapters for targeting cellular compartments2. Most lysosomal membrane proteins are guided by tyrosine- or di-leucine–based sorting motifs within their cytosolic domains, recognized by adapter protein complexes (AP1–AP4) that recruit clathrin to form coated transport vesicles3. In contrast to these conventional mechanisms, the cobalamin (vitamin B12) transporter ABCD4 lacks any known sorting motifs and depends on a membrane protein named LMBD1 for its lysosomal localization, highlighting a distinct chaperone-mediated trafficking pathway.

Cobalamin is a cofactor for 5-methyltetrahydrofolate-homocysteine methyltransferase (MTR) and mitochondrial methyl-malonyl-CoA mutase (MMUT), which serve key wide-ranging metabolic functions in mammalian cells4. Since humans can only obtain cobalamin from their diet, more than 20 proteins are encoded in an intricate pathway that enables the efficient delivery of cobalamin from its absorption, to the various tissues and cellular compartments5. Somatic complementation analysis has defined eleven known defects in the intracellular processing of cobalamin, which are classified into the following groups: cblA–cblG, cblJ, cblK, cblX, and mut6–8. Among these classes, cblF and cblJ, which are caused by mutations in LMBRD1 and ABCD4, respectively, share a similar deficiency phenotype that is the result of a loss of cobalamin transport out of the lysosome7,9.

ABCD4 is an ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter that belongs to the D subfamily of ABC half transporters. It mainly functions as a homodimer, with each monomer containing one transmembrane domain (TMD) and one nucleotide-binding domain (NBD)10. To date, four ABCD transporters (ABCD1-4) have been identified in humans11–14. ABCD1-3 are located on the peroxisomal membrane and function in the transport of various acyl-CoAs from the cytosol into peroxisomes for further β-oxidation14–19. Specifically, ABCD1 and ABCD2 are involved in the transport of CoA esters of saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids20–22, while ABCD3 transports dicarboxylic acid, branched chain acyl-CoA, and the bile acid intermediates: di- and tri-hydroxycholestanoyl (DHCA and THCA)-CoA23. On the other hand, ABCD4 is a lysosomal protein that plays a key role in the transport of cobalamin from the lysosomal lumen to the cytosol, and dysfunction of this protein results in the accumulation of cobalamin in the lysosomal lumen7.

LMBD1, encoded by LMBRD1, is an integral multi-pass membrane protein with nine putative TMs. Though LMBD1 mainly localizes to the lysosomal membrane through its AP-2 binding motif (233YERL)9,24,25, it has also been found in the plasma membrane and perinuclear region26. LMBD1 regulates a distinct clathrin-mediated endocytosis of the insulin receptor by interacting with AP-2 on the plasma membrane and mediates microtubule assembly during the process of cell mitosis27,28. Additionally, LMBD1 is essential for the expression of fibroblast growth factor 8, which ensures proper gastrulation and mesoderm formation during mouse embryogenesis28. The functional connection between LMBD1 and ABCD4 was initially established by evidence of their colocalization on the lysosomal membrane7, and their direct interaction was later demonstrated by another study29. These two lysosomal membrane proteins also form a super-complex with the downstream cytoplasmic cobalamin-processing protein MMACHC to mediate the directional transfer of cobalamin6,29,30.

ABCD4 can transport cobalamin from the interior to the exterior of lysosomes in a manner that was dependent on its ATPase activity, while LMBD1 showed no cobalamin transport activity and does not affect ABCD4-mediated transport in vitro31. Endogenous ABCD4 is localized to both the lysosomes and the ER. When LMBRD1 is knocked out, the lysosomal localization of ABCD4 is impaired25, suggesting that the translocation of ABCD4 from the ER to lysosomes depends on the lysosomal targeting ability of LMBD1. An earlier cryo-EM structure solved the ATP-bound ABCD4E549Q mutant at 3.6 Å resolution and revealed a lysosome-open conformation, in which the transmembrane domains form a cavity accessible to the lysosomal lumen while the nucleotide-binding domains dimerize with two bound ATP molecules32. This structure provided a structural framework for understanding ABCD4 function, but did not address how ABCD4 interacts with LMBD1 or how cobalamin is exported from the lysosome.

Here, we determine the structures of the ABCD4–LMBD1 complex by cryo-EM in three distinct states: lumen-open state at 2.80 Å, substrate-bound state at 2.83 Å and cytosol-open state at 2.98 Å. LMBD1 employs its TMs and cytosolic domain to interact with ABCD4. In contrast to other ABCD family members that coordinate two substrate molecules within a dimer33–38, only a single cobalamin molecule is observed in the substrate-binding cavity of the ABCD4 homodimer in our structure. Our structural and functional analyses establish a structural framework for understanding the working mechanism of the ABCD4–LMBD1 complex, shed light on the molecular basis of disease-associated mutations, and offer atomic-level insights into cobalamin metabolism and chaperone-assisted trafficking of lysosomal transporters.

To facilitate structural characterization of the ABCD4–LMBD1 complex, we used the ABCD4E549Q mutant, which impairs ATP hydrolysis while preserving ATP binding and stabilizes the transporter in a defined conformational state. We co-expressed Flag-tagged ABCD4E549Q and Strep-tagged LMBD1 in HEK-293F cells, and purified the proteins using Strep-Tactin resin followed by anti-Flag M2 resin. Gel filtration chromatographs revealed that ABCD4E549Q and LMBD1 formed a stable complex that migrated as a single peak (Supplementary Fig. 1), which was suitable for further cryo-EM structural studies. The conformation of ABCD4 in complex with LMBD1 adopts a lumen-open state, closely resembling the previously reported structure of ABCD432, with a Cα root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 0.977 Å (Fig. 1c). ABCD4 forms a homodimer, with each protomer containing six TMs followed by one NBD. Two endogenous ATP molecules and magnesium ions were observed in the NBDs, despite the absence of supplemented ATP and magnesium during sample preparation. (Supplementary Fig. 3d). During data processing, an additional density was observed on each ABCD4 protomer, suggesting that the complex adopts a dimeric arrangement with one LMBD1 protein per ABCD4. Thus, we attempted to introduce C2 symmetry during data processing. However, the cryo-EM densities of LMBD1 were weaker as the local resolution of the whole complex became higher (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Consequently, we refined the data using C1 symmetry and applied a focused mask on the transmembrane regions of one ABCD4 protomer and LMBD1 for 3D classification without image alignment. This approach resolved four similar conformations of LMBD1 with a 2:1-ABCD4:LMBD1 stoichiometry in the cryo-EM maps, only one of which enabled atomic model building for the whole complex (Supplementary Fig. 2a). To further explore the binding stoichiometry between ABCD4 and LMBD1, we performed a chemical cross-linking assay. Consistent with our cryo-EM analysis, the results indicate the coexistence of both 2:1 and 2:2 stoichiometries in the ABCD4–LMBD1 complex, with the 2:1 assembly being the predominant form (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In our structure, LMBD1 consists of 9 TMs and a cytosolic α-helix bundle (designated as CH1 and CH2) with the dimensions of ~50 Å × 40 Å × 100 Å (Fig. 1d, e). Due to flexibility and limited local resolution, several loops between the TMs, including the putative glycosylation sites and the AP-2 binding motif, were not visible and therefore were not modeled. A DALI search39 for structural homologs reveals that the overall structure of LMBD1 does not exhibit any significant fold similarity with previously characterized membrane protein structures, implying that LMBD1 presents, to the best of our knowledge, a previously unreported fold. The cryo-EM map of the lumen-open structure complex revealed two interfaces between ABCD4 and LMBD1: (1) TM4 and the β-strands between TM7 and TM8 of LMBD1 interact with the TMD of ABCD4 (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 5a); (2) the cytosolic bundle of LMBD1 forms a complex with the NBD of ABCD4 (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 5b). In the cytosolic interface, Pro260/Leu261/Pro262 in the loop between the two α-helix contribute hydrophobic contacts with five hydrophobic residues (Pro453, Leu478, Tyr482, Pro483, and Tyr539) of ABCD4 (Fig. 1g). In addition to these hydrophobic interactions, the side chains of three polar residues (Arg259, Arg264 and Lys270) and the main chain of Pro260 in LMBD1 form hydrogen bonds with four residues of ABCD4: Asp450, His454, Glu480, and Gln541 (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Furthermore, residue Lys270 also forms a π-cation interaction with Tyr482 of ABCD4. In the membrane interface, Leu156/Val157/Phe160 in TM4 and Trp391/Trp394 in the β-strand region engage in hydrophobic interactions with Phe84 in TM2 and Leu41/Met42 in TM1 of ABCD4, respectively (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Additionally, one cholesterol molecule was observed in the membrane interface, which may also contribute to the hydrophobic interactions between LMBD1 and ABCD4 via Phe393 of LMBD1 and Phe99 of ABCD4 (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 5a).

By adding cobalamin, we obtained the structure of the substrate-bound ABCD4E549Q–LMBD1 complex (Fig. 2a, b). We determined the cobalamin-bound structure at a resolution of 2.83 Å without symmetry (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Figs. 6, 7, and Supplementary Table 1). Akin to the lumen-open form, only one LMBD1 was clearly resolved in the cryo-EM map. Structural alignment of ABCD4 in this complex with the lumen-open form revealed a RMSD of 0.468 Å across 738 Cα atoms (Fig. 2c). The NBDs exhibit minimal conformational changes relative to the lumen-open structure, likely due to the retention of endogenous ATP molecules and magnesium ions (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Upon substrate binding, TM1 and TM6 tilt inwards, forming a cavity that is closed facing the lysosomal lumen (Fig. 2c). In this cavity, we observed a plate-shaped feature with an approximate volume of 2000 Å³ in the cryo-EM map (Fig. 2b). Docking attempts further confirm that cobalamin fits within the observed cryo-EM density, supporting its binding at this site. Cobalamin, in the base-on conformation with its cobalt ion coordinated by the dimethylbenzimidazole group, is positioned at the dimer interface of ABCD4 (Fig. 2a, b, d). Several residues, including Val60/Pro64/Tyr68 in TM1, Tyr286 in TM5, and Phe315/Ile318/Tyr319 in TM6 of protomer A, as well as Tyr68 in TM1, Tyr279/Tyr286 in TM5, and Phe315/Ile318/Tyr319/Ile321 in TM6 of protomer B, establish hydrophobic interactions with cobalamin (Fig. 2e, f). In addition, a number of hydrogen bonds link cobalamin to ABCD4. To validate our structural observations, we generated several ABCD4 variants and evaluated their activities using a cobalamin-enhanced ATPase assay (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Compared with the wild type, mutants that disrupt hydrophobic interactions with the corrin ring (F315A and P64W/I318W) or polar interactions (D278A/D329A) exhibited reduced substrate-enhanced ATPase activity (Fig. 2g). Furthermore, previous studies demonstrated that the pathogenic Tyr319Cys mutation abolishes both ATPase and cobalamin transport activities, whereas substituting Tyr319 with the aromatic residue Phe restores both functions in ABCD431. Additionally, replacing Asp329 with Lys significantly impairs cobalamin transport without affecting ATPase activity40. These findings support our structural observation that both hydrophobic contacts and a hydrogen-bonding network at the ABCD4 dimer interface are essential for efficient cobalamin engagement.

Distinct from ABCD4, other members of the ABCD family, such as ABCD1 and ABCD3, contain two substrate-binding sites within the dimer, with each protomer coordinating one substrate molecule (Supplementary Fig. 9a). In ABCD1 and ABCD3, the substrate-binding sites are located on opposite sides of the dimer, whereas in ABCD4 the cobalamin-binding site is positioned at the center of the dimer interface (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Sequence alignment reveals that acyl-CoA–binding residues are conserved in ABCD1–ABCD3 but absent in ABCD4, whereas residues mediating cobalamin binding are distinctively conserved in ABCD4 (Supplementary Fig. 9c), underscoring its specialization for cobalamin recognition.

In this structure, dimerization of the NBDs was disrupted, with the complete separation of the Walker A and the signature motifs. An ADP molecule was identified bound to the Walker A motif of one NBD subunit (Supplementary Fig. 11b). The overall fold of ABCD4 in a cytosol-open conformation is similar to the cytosol-facing conformation of ABCD3 with an RMSD of 3.832 Å (Fig. 3c). TM1, TM2, TM3, and TM6 of one protomer interact with TM4 and TM5 of an adjacent protomer, creating a V-shaped cleft. Compared with the substate-bound state, the cytosol-open state exhibits a scissor-like movement, accompanied by a ~48 Å displacement of the interwoven TM4 and TM5 of one protomer away from the other helices (Fig. 3d, e). Although a notable conformational change occurs, the TM1-TM2, TM3-TM6, and TM4-TM5 pairs from both states largely retain their integrity, moving as rigid bodies and maintaining their overall conformations (Fig. 3d, e). The resulting conformation generates a wide cytosolic-facing opening, facilitating substrate release into the cytosol. Remarkably, we observed a large cryo-EM density at a position similar to that of cobalamin in the substrate-bound state (Fig. 3f). However, due to the limited local resolution, we were unable to unambiguously determine the precise conformation of cobalamin. These observations further support the notion that cobalamin is released from this site in the cytosol-open conformation.

To validate the binding interface between LMBD1 and ABCD4, we generated several ABCD4 variants: mutant1 (D450A/H454A/Y482A), mutant2 (M42A/F84A/F99A), mutant3 (M42A/F84A/F99A/D450A/Y482A) and the single mutants D450A, H454A, Y482A and Q541A. The interactions between these variants and LMBD1 were assessed by co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay. HEK-293T cells were co-transfected with LMBD1-GFP-Flag and different HA-tagged ABCD4 variants. Cells were lysed in CA-630 and subsequently subjected to co-IP using anti-HA antibody-conjugated beads. In line with a previous study25, LMBD1-GFP-Flag was co-immunoprecipitated with wild-type ABCD4-HA (Fig. 4c). Mutant2, D450A, and H454A retain the ability to bind LMBD1, although their interactions are weaker compared to the wild type. By contrast, mutant1, mutant3, Y482A and Q541A demonstrated significantly impaired binding to LMBD1 (Fig. 4c). We next performed complementary mutagenesis on LMBD1, generating two triple mutants TPM1 (F160A/W391A/W394A), TPM2 (R259A/R264A/K270A), and the single mutants R259A, R264A, and K270A. Co-IP assays showed that TPM1, R264A, and K270A retained binding to ABCD4 but with reduced interaction compared with wild-type LMBD1, whereas TPM2 and R259A exhibited markedly impaired binding (Fig. 4d). Since Met42, Phe84 and Phe99 in ABCD4 and Phe160, Tyr391, and Tyr394 in LMBD1 are responsible for interactions at the membrane interface (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 5a), whereas Tyr482 and Gln541 in ABCD4 and Arg259 in LMBD1 are involved in mediating interactions at the cytosolic interface (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 5b), it is tempting to speculate that the cytosolic interface plays a more critical role in the interaction between LMBD1 and ABCD4 than the membrane interface.

The major role of LMBD1 is to mediate the transport of ABCD4 from the ER to the lysosomes25. To further validate the importance of LMBD1 binding in ABCD4 trafficking, we investigated the distribution of HA-tagged ABCD4 variants co-expressed with LMBD1-GFP-Flag using fluorescence microscopy. ABCD4 localizes to the lysosome when co-expressed with LMBD1, whereas its distribution does not overlap with lysosomes when co-expressed with the control plasmid (Fig. 4e, f). Mutant2, when co-expressed with LMBD1, displayed a punctate distribution that colocalized with the lysosomal marker LAMP1. However, mutant1, mutant3, Y482A and Q541A exhibited a reticular distribution pattern that did not overlap with LAMP1, indicating defective lysosomal localization (Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Consistently, analysis of LMBD1 variants showed that TPM1 retained the ability to transport ABCD4 to lysosomes, whereas TPM2, R259A, R264A, and K270A impaired ABCD4 lysosomal localization (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d). Together with the co-IP results, these findings indicate that the cytosolic interface is critically important for the translocation of ABCD4 to lysosomes in conjunction with LMBD1.

So far, only a few lysosomal transporters are known to require chaperones for lysosomal translocation, including ABCD4 and LAT1–4F2hc leucine transporter. ABCD4 acts as the cobalamin transporter, forming a complex with LMBD1 in the ER to facilitate its trafficking to the lysosome25,31. LAT1–4F2hc mediates the influx of essential amino acids, while LAPTM4b recruits LAT1–4F2hc to lysosomes for leucine uptake, promoting mTORC1 activation via the V-ATPase41–44. However, the molecular mechanism of chaperone-mediated targeting of these transporters to lysosomes remains unclear. In this study, our structures of the ABCD4–LMBD1 complex reveal the atomic details of their interactions, shedding light on the chaperone-dependent trafficking mechanism to the lysosome.

LMBD1 interacts with ABCD4 through two primary interfaces: a cytosolic bundle that forms hydrophobic and polar interactions with the NBD of ABCD4, and a membrane interface where TM4 and β-strands of LMBD1 interact with the TMD of ABCD4 (Fig. 1f, g). These interactions help stabilize the ABCD4–LMBD1 complex, which further facilitates the proper localization of ABCD4 to the lysosome, as shown by co-IP assay and fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 4c–f and Supplementary Fig. 12). Mutations in ABCD4 at these interfaces, especially at the cytosolic interface, disrupt the interactions between ABCD4 and LMBD1, impairing ABCD4 trafficking and preventing its efficient delivery to the lysosome.

The structures of the ABCD4–LMBD1 complex resemble those of ABC transporters containing an additional N-terminal transmembrane domain, known as TMD010. Structural comparisons with previously reported TMD0-containing proteins, including MRP145, Ycf146, SUR147, ABCB648, and ABCC349, reveal that the transmembrane segments of LMBD1 mimic the TMD0 domain found in these proteins, although the TMD of LMBD1 and TMD0s adopt distinct poses relative to the ABC core (Fig. 5a–f). Despite limited evidence, studies on SUR, TAP (ABCB2/3), and TAPL (ABCB9) provide insights into the function of TMD0. In SUR and TAP, TMD0 mediates interactions with the Kir6.2 tetramer and tapasin, forming potassium channels and peptide-loading complexes, respectively50,51. In TAPL, TMD0 deletion does not affect transport activity but causes mistargeting from the lysosome to the plasma membrane52. Thus, LMBD1 resembles TMD0 in TAPL, lacking transport activity itself but regulating ABCD4 lysosomal trafficking. Our structures of the ABCD4–LMBD1 complex in three distinct conformational states, together with functional activity assays, allow us to propose a mechanistic model for ABCD4-mediated cobalamin transport (Fig. 5g). Initially, in the lumen-open state, ABCD4 exists as a dimer with ATP bound to its NBDs. At this stage, the membrane cavity opens toward the lysosomal lumen. Cobalamin binding triggers a conformational change that leads to the formation of the substrate-bound state. In this state, the transmembrane domains of ABCD4 close inward, creating a sealed cavity that traps cobalamin within the protein complex. Following ATP hydrolysis, the NBDs of ABCD4 separate, triggering conformational changes associated with substrate release. This hydrolytic event induces further conformational changes in the transporter, disrupting the dimer interface and opening the cavity to the cytosol, resulting in the cytosol-open state. The movement of the transmembrane-helices, particularly the shift of TM4 and TM5, generates a large opening that facilitates the release of cobalamin into the cytosol. This step is essential for the active transport of cobalamin out of the lysosome. Overall, this cycle highlights the efficiency of ABCD4 as a solute cargo exporter, essential for the movement of cobalamin from the lysosome to the cytosol, which is a crucial step in cellular cobalamin homeostasis.

Furthermore, the structural determination of the ABCD4–LMBD1 complex provides a framework for mapping disease-related mutations associated with cblF and cblJ disorders (Supplementary Fig. 13). Mutations located within the cobalamin-binding pocket may directly impair cobalamin transport, while those at the cytosolic interface between ABCD4 and LMBD1 may disrupt their interaction and lead to mislocalization of ABCD4. Thus, our structures offer a foundation for future biophysical, biochemical, and cellular investigations into the molecular basis of cobalamin defects.

Notably, we identified four distinct structural classes in the lumen-open states (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The first class revealed an ABCD4 dimer associated with a single LMBD1 molecule on one side, while the second class showed an ABCD4 dimer bound to two LMBD1 molecules on both sides. The remaining two classes also exhibited 2:1 and 2:2 ABCD4:LMBD1 stoichiometries, respectively, but featured additional cryo-EM densities surrounding the NBD of ABCD4 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Structural analysis suggests that the additional densities likely correspond to the NBDs of neighboring ABCD4 molecules, with the observed interactions potentially mediated by disulfide bonds between the C-terminal cysteine residues of the two ABCD4 protomers (Supplementary Fig. 3a). However, The TM region densities of LMBD1 in all classes were weaker than those of ABCD4, suggesting that LMBD1 may adopt multiple conformations upon binding to ABCD4. To enhance structural resolution, a focused mask was applied to the transmembrane regions of a single ABCD4 protomer along with LMBD1, followed by 3D classification without image alignment in RELION58. This approach yielded four similar conformations, among which the highest-resolution map enabled atomic model building and represents the lumen-open state.
