# The effectiveness of pulpotomy in mature permanent posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
**Authors:** Hoang CM, Hiran-Us S, Limlawan P, Nguyen XT, Chantarangsu S, Vacharaksa A
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-026-61178-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61178-2)

## Content

Pulpotomy is increasingly considered an alternative to root canal treatment for preserving pulp vitality. This systematic review compared full pulpotomy (FP) and partial pulpotomy (PP) in mature permanent posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis and explored prognostic factors (patient age and bleeding time). PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Scopus, supplemented by Google Scholar, were searched up to December 2025 for randomized clinical trials. Risk of bias was assessed with RoB 2 and certainty of evidence with GRADE; single-arm proportions were pooled by random-effects meta-analysis within follow-up periods (12, 18-24, and > 24 months). Twenty-three studies were included. At the primary 12-month timepoint, pooled success was 0.92 (95% CI 0.89-0.95) for FP and 0.89 (0.81-0.95) for PP. Estimates were lower and less certain beyond 12 months. In two-arm comparisons using intention-to-treat analysis, FP showed higher success than PP (OR 1.85, 95% CI 1.16-2.94) and fewer pain-related failures (OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.07-0.64). FP success was not clearly associated with patient age or bleeding time. Both techniques achieved favorable short-term outcomes, but FP provided more consistent 12-month success and pain control. Given the low-to-very-low certainty of evidence, these findings warrant caution, and adequately powered trials with longer follow-up are needed.

Abstract
Pulpotomy is increasingly considered an alternative to root canal treatment for
preserving pulp vitality. This systematic review compared full pulpotomy (FP) and
partial pulpotomy (PP) in mature permanent posterior teeth with irreversible
pulpitis and explored prognostic factors (patient age and bleeding time). PubMed,
Cochrane Library, and Scopus, supplemented by Google Scholar, were searched up
to December 2025 for randomized clinical trials. Risk of bias was assessed with RoB
2 and certainty of evidence with GRADE; single-arm proportions were pooled by
random-effects meta-analysis within follow-up periods (12, 18–24, and >24 months).
Twenty-three studies were included. At the primary 12-month timepoint, pooled
success was 0.92 (95% CI 0.89–0.95) for FP and 0.89 (0.81–0.95) for PP. Estimates
were lower and less certain beyond 12 months. In two-arm comparisons using
intention-to-treat analysis, FP showed higher success than PP (OR 1.85, 95% CI
1.16–2.94) and fewer pain-related failures (OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.07–0.64). FP success
was not clearly associated with patient age or bleeding time. Both techniques
achieved favorable short-term outcomes, but FP provided more consistent 12-month
success and pain control. Given the low-to-very-low certainty of evidence, these
findings warrant caution, and adequately powered trials with longer follow-up are
needed.

Introduction
Within contemporary dental science, tissue preservation emphasizes
conserving tooth structure, maintaining pulp vitality, and controlling infection [1].
The dental pulp contains nerves, blood vessels, stem cells, and immune cells that
are essential for tissue repair; maintaining its vitality supports normal function and
dentine bridge formation, thereby enhancing protection against bacterial invasion
[2-4]. In addition, maintaining pulp vitality preserves sensory function, enabling the
perception of pressure, temperature, and pain, which helps prevent excessive
masticatory forces [4, 5]. Therefore, preserving pulp vitality improves long-term
outcomes while simplifying treatment and reducing costs [6].
There is growing evidence that vital pulp therapy is a viable alternative to
conventional treatment, with increasing success reported for pulpotomy in mature
permanent teeth with irreversible pulpitis [3, 6]. Pulpotomy is classified as full or
partial based on the extent of pulp removal [7, 8]. Full pulpotomy (FP) involves the
removal of the coronal pulp to the level of the root canal orifice while preserving the
radicular pulp tissue [9, 10]. In contrast, partial pulpotomy (PP) removes only the
superficial inflamed portion of the coronal pulp, preserving the remaining healthy
tissue [9, 10]. Although FP improves the likelihood of complete removal of diseased
tissue, it is more invasive; PP is more conservative but carries a higher risk of
residual inflammation, potentially leading to pain or failure [7, 11]. Because the two
techniques differ in how much potentially inflamed tissue is left behind, the optimal
extent of pulp removal in mature posterior teeth remains unresolved and may
critically influence healing and treatment outcomes [12].
The success of FP or PP depends on the complete removal of inflamed pulp
tissue, the choice of sealing materials, and potential coronal leakage [13]. Calcium
silicate–based cements, such as MTA and Biodentine, have demonstrated favorable
outcomes due to their sealing ability, biocompatibility, and capacity to promote pulp
healing [14, 15]. Several prognostic factors have been proposed; however, the
evidence remains inconclusive [1]. Bleeding time, a commonly used intraoperative
indicator, has been suggested to reflect pulpal status, with shorter durations
associated with improved outcomes and prolonged bleeding linked to a higher risk
of failure [16, 17]. However, despite its clinical practicality, bleeding time has not

been conclusively established as a reliable predictor of pulpotomy success. Patient
age has likewise been proposed as a determinant, on the basis of age-related decline
in pulpal regenerative capacity, but its influence on outcomes in mature teeth is
uncertain.
Recent syntheses have examined pulpotomy for irreversible pulpitis in mature
permanent teeth, yet none addresses the present question directly. The meta-
analysis by Li et al. pooled pulpotomy as a single modality and benchmarked it
largely against root canal treatment, and addressed the full‑versus‑partial contrast
only as a secondary comparison based on four low‑certainty trials, without strictly
isolating posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis [18]. Two further reviews
summarised existing systematic reviews rather than primary trial data — an
overview restricted to systematic reviews of RCTs, and an umbrella review whose
search closed in 2022 [19, 20]. As a result, it remains unclear which technique
optimizes success in mature permanent posterior teeth, and the role of prognostic
factors-reported inconsistently across trials-is similarly uncertain. No current
review provides a trial-level, direct comparison of FP and PP restricted to
irreversible pulpitis in posterior teeth, where anatomical complexity and occlusal
load are most relevant, or formally evaluates these prognostic factors. The present
study therefore aimed to compare the success rates of FP and PP in mature
permanent posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis, using an up-to-date search of
randomized clinical trials, and secondarily to evaluate the influence of prognostic
factors-patient age, bleeding time, and pulp-capping material-on treatment
outcomes.

Overall characteristics of the included studies
Among the 23 included studies, the evidence base was predominantly
centered on FP: 15 studies evaluated FP exclusively, three focused on PP, and only
five provided direct comparisons between FP and PP. Study aims varied across the
literature; some trials assessed FP as a conservative alternative to root canal
treatment, while others compared bioactive materials within a single pulpotomy
approach. Participant ages ranged from 9 to 65 years, with most studies focusing on
adults. Most studies were conducted on molars, with one also including premolars
(Taha & Albakri 2024), while follow-up was typically reported at 6 and 12 months.
Long-term outcomes beyond 24 months, including 5-year follow-up data, were
scarce. Hemostasis was most commonly achieved with 2.5% or 3% sodium
hypochlorite (NaOCl; 19/23 studies, including one combined with cryotherapy and
one compared with 2% chlorhexidine); the remaining four studies used normal saline
(three, all from the Asgary cohort reports) or a diode laser (one). Bleeding control
within 10 minutes or less was generally required as an eligibility criterion, with
several studies applying stricter thresholds of 6 or even 5 minutes. Calcium silicate–
based cements (MTA, Biodentine, CEM, NeoMTA, NeoPUTTY, and iRoot BP Plus)
were the predominant bioactive materials, whereas studies using calcium hydroxide
were limited to one (Taha & Khazali 2017) (Table 1).

Characteristics of the studies directly comparing FP and PP
The five studies that directly compared FP and PP included the study by
Baranwal et al., 2022 [27], Ramani et al., 2022 [34], Jassal et al., 2023 [31], Taha &
Albakri, 2024 [41], and Ramani & Sangwan 2025 [36], as listed in Supplement Table
3. While most trials focused on molars, Taha and Albakri (2024) also included
premolars, and Jassal et al., 2023 limited enrollment to teeth requiring Class I
restorations. Radiographic assessment was primarily periapical, with Baranwal’s
study additionally employing CBCT imaging. All studies reported outcomes at 12
months, with Ramani et al., 2025 extending to 5 years (Supplement Table 4). In
general, pulpotomy techniques were consistent across the included studies,
standardizing for FP and PP. Hemostasis was typically achieved using 2.5–3.0%
sodium hypochlorite within 6–10 minutes. Calcium silicate–based materials
(Biodentine, ProRoot MTA, NeoPUTTY) were used for capping, followed by glass
ionomer and composite restoration. Success criteria were generally comparable,
although Taha and Albakri, 2024 uniquely required a positive cold-test response as
part of the success criteria for PP. Overall, all studies reported success rates by
using combined clinical and radiographic outcomes.
Quality assessment
Methodological quality across the 23 included studies was evaluated using
the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, revealing a heterogeneous evidence base: seven studies
(30.4%) were classified as low risk, ten (43.5%) raised "some concerns," and six
(26.1%) were judged as high risk. High-risk designations were primarily driven by
critical deficiencies in the randomization process (Domain 1) and missing outcome
data (Domain 3), as summarized in Supplement Figure 1.
Analysis of specific domains (Supplement Figure 1) identified Domain 1
(Randomization Process) as the most frequent source of bias, with three high-risk
and six "some concerns" ratings. Domain 3 (Missing Outcome Data) also presented
challenges, totaling two high-risk and four "some concerns" judgments. In contrast,
Domain 2 (Deviations from Intended Interventions) and Domain 5 (Selection of the
Reported Result) demonstrated the highest adherence, with no high-risk studies and
18/23 and 19/23 rated as low risk, respectively. Domain 4 (Measurement of the

Success rate comparing between full and partial pulpotomy
Four of the five two-arm trials reported 12-month outcomes and were pooled
using odds ratios (ORs); the fifth (Ramani and Sangwan, 2025) reported only 5-year
data and was therefore not included in the 12-month comparison. In the intention-
to-treat analysis, FP showed a significantly higher success rate than PP (OR = 1.85;
95% CI: 1.16–2.94; P = 0.009), with no between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0%). The
per-protocol analysis was restricted to teeth with complete follow-up (FP, n = 196;
PP, n = 191). For PP, two teeth that Taha & Albakri classified as failures solely on
the basis of a non-responsive cold test-a criterion not used by the other trials-were
excluded to harmonize success definitions (PP, n = 189). FP again showed higher
success than PP (OR = 3.21; 95% CI: 1.02–10.09; P < 0.05; I² = 20%). Long-term
evidence was limited to a single 5-year study, which showed no significant difference
between techniques; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due
to limited data and attrition (Supplement Table 4).
Pain assessment comparing between full and partial pulpotomy
Postoperative pain was assessed at various follow‑up periods using different
criteria across the five reports (four independent two‑arm trials and the 5‑year
follow‑up of one cohort) and is summarized in Table 2. Four studies used a visual
analogue scale (VAS) for quantitative measurement, while one qualitatively
described pain characteristics. Some studies reported similar early pain outcomes
between groups, whereas others found better pain relief with FP, including lower
VAS scores and reduced analgesic use.

The association of bleeding time and patients’ age to the

For FP, ten studies with 12-month follow-up reported both a pooled success

heterogeneity (R² = 0%) (Supplementary Figure 3).

hemostasis thresholds of up to 10 minutes, and no consistent pattern was observed
between hemostasis cut-off and treatment success. Bleeding time was therefore
summarized descriptively (Table 1).

Discussion
Across single-arm and two-arm analyses, both FP and PP showed favorable
early outcomes but differed in consistency and pain-related results. In the single-
arm analyses, both techniques showed high short-term success that declined at
longer follow-up. Because pooling across time points would combine non-equivalent
outcomes and re-use longitudinal cohorts reported at multiple intervals, success was
pooled within rather than across follow-up windows, and no across-time overall
estimate was derived. At the primary 12-month timepoint, within-window
heterogeneity was moderate-to-substantial for FP and PP (I² = 55.3% and 78.3%,
respectively), so the pooled rates remain approximate. For PP, heterogeneity
appeared to be driven largely by the inclusion of a calcium hydroxide treatment arm
rather than by differences in follow-up duration. For FP, a substantial proportion of
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

between-study heterogeneity remained unexplained, contributing to the low
certainty of evidence. Findings for PP warrant particular caution, as the contributing
studies were few and long-term interpretation was limited by the predominance of
12-month data. Direct comparisons from two-arm trials supported these findings: at
12 months, FP tended to outperform PP in both intention-to-treat and per-protocol
analyses. Postoperative pain was a key differentiating factor, with PP showing a
significantly higher pain-related failure rate (pooled OR favouring FP), whereas such
events were uncommon after FP. Although one trial reported comparable success
between FP and PP at 60 months, this was limited by substantial attrition and should
be interpreted cautiously [36]. Overall, FP appears to provide more reliable short-
term success and better pain control; while PP may offer theoretical long-term
advantages through greater preservation of vital pulp tissue, current evidence is
insufficient to confirm long-term superiority.
Regarding prognostic factors, material choice appeared to influence
outcomes, with calcium silicate–based cements tending to show higher and less
variable success than calcium hydroxide, although the latter was evaluated in only
one PP trial. For patient age and bleeding time, study-level associations were
examined only descriptively and, for FP, by exploratory meta-regression. FP 12-
month success showed no association with study-level mean age, and a high
proportion of FP studies achieved good outcomes despite permissive bleeding time
thresholds of up to 10 minutes, suggesting that these study-level factors did not
materially influence FP success. For PP, no meta-regression or age-based subgroup
analysis was performed because too few independent studies were available; the
apparent within-study trend toward higher success at shorter bleeding times in two
reports is exploratory only and may be subject to ecological bias. These observations
should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of an
age- or bleeding-dependent effect.
Multiple clinical and histological factors may predict the success of pulpotomy
[1]. Although histological assessment remains the most accurate indicator of pulpal
inflammation, it is rarely feasible in clinical practice, underscoring the need for
reliable clinical predictors [7, 17]. These factors can be grouped into patient-related,
intraoperative, and postoperative variables [1]. Accordingly, this study included only
mature posterior teeth with a consistent diagnosis and evaluated age, bleeding time,
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

and pulp-capping material as key predictors. Age-related differences in the pulp–
dentin complex provide a biologically plausible framework for considering how
patient age might, in theory, influence PP outcomes. Younger pulp, characterized by
higher cellularity, vascularity, and regenerative capacity, may favour pulp repair
[44, 45]. In contrast, aging is associated with increased fibrosis, reduced vascularity,
and progressive narrowing of the pulp chamber due to secondary dentin deposition,
collectively leading to diminished regenerative capacity and lower tolerance to
inflammatory stress [17, 44, 45]. These changes could, in principle, modify the
response of residual pulp after PP, in which inflamed tissue is partly preserved,
whereas FP removes the inflamed coronal pulp more completely and may depend
less on intrinsic healing capacity [9, 10]. However, the present study-level data did
not demonstrate an association between age and success, and any such mechanism
remains speculative and unconfirmed. Likewise, bleeding time showed no apparent
association with success, consistent with its weak correlation with true histological
pulpal status [15, 46, 47]. This finding should nonetheless be interpreted in the
context of the treatment approach: FP involves more extensive removal of inflamed
tissue [35], whereas PP preserves residual pulp that may exhibit variable degrees of
inflammation [9, 10]. Consequently, bleeding time alone may not reliably reflect
pulpal status and should be considered alongside other clinical factors, such as
patient age, hemostatic agents, and operative technique.
Calcium silicate–based cements were most commonly used and showed
consistently high success in both FP and PP, reflecting their bioactivity and
regenerative capacity. Platelet-rich concentrate also demonstrated promising
results in limited studies. Beyond material choice, the quality of the definitive
coronal restoration is critical for long-term success [12], as effective sealing and
marginal integrity are essential to maintain pulp vitality [1, 15, 48]. Accordingly,
irrespective of the pulpotomy technique, meticulous coronal restoration is essential
for achieving and maintaining long-term treatment success.
This study included only RCTs, which best support comparison of PP and FP
with improved control of confounding and causal inference [49, 50]. However,
residual clinical confounders still contributed to heterogeneity and reduced
certainty. In the available two-arm studies directly comparing FP and PP, both
intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses were applied to capture effectiveness
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

under real-world and ideal conditions, respectively [51]. Additionally, success
definitions varied across the included studies, encompassing clinical, radiographic,
or combined criteria. In the present analysis, a combined definition was adopted, as
true long-term success requires both clinical and radiographic outcomes [12].
However, separate clinical and radiographic success rates could not be consistently
extracted for quantitative synthesis, as several studies reported only combined
outcomes, precluding back-calculation. Accordingly, to minimize overestimation, the
combined success rate was conservatively defined using the lower value when both
clinical and radiographic data were available.
Conventional endodontic treatment remains the standard of care for
permanent teeth with irreversible pulpitis [6]. However, in posterior teeth, its
effectiveness may be limited by complex root canal anatomy, restricted access from
limited mouth opening, and masticatory forces, contributing to lower survival rates
than anterior teeth [52, 53]. In this context, the present findings further support
pulpotomy as a clinically relevant alternative for posterior teeth, in line with growing
evidence demonstrating favourable outcomes of vital pulp therapy in mature
permanent teeth with irreversible pulpitis [6, 18]. Although traditionally indicated
for primary and immature teeth, pulpotomy is gaining acceptance in mature teeth
owing to its advantages, including reduced chair time and a more conservative
approach that preserves tooth structure [54]. This study provides further insight into
the clinical role of pulpotomy techniques: PP may be considered a more conservative
option when pulpal inflammation is judged to be limited and well-controlled,
whereas FP may be preferable when pulpal status is uncertain, given its more
consistent short-term outcomes.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. The small number of eligible
studies and the predominance of short-term outcomes limit the strength of the
conclusions. Because two longitudinal cohorts (the Asgary multicentre cohort,
reported at 12, 24 and 60 months, and the Ramani cohort, reported at 12 months
and 5 years) were each reported at more than one follow-up interval, part of the
apparent decline in success across follow-up windows may reflect within-cohort
change over time rather than comparison between fully independent study groups,
and the test for differences among follow-up windows should be interpreted with
this in mind. Associations between patient age, bleeding time, and pulpotomy
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

success remain preliminary and should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the
use of a derived combined success rate from clinical and radiographic criteria may
introduce bias and affect the overall findings. Collectively, these factors reduce the
certainty and generalizability of the results.
Future studies should prioritize long-term outcomes, including restoration
durability, quality, and esthetic performance, and investigate mechanisms
underlying late pulpotomy failure using standardized outcome definitions and
extended follow-up to capture delayed events. In addition, partial pulpotomy in older
patients should be specifically evaluated, with bleeding time systematically recorded
and analyzed as both a continuous variable and across predefined thresholds to
clarify its impact on treatment outcomes.
Conclusions
Both FP and PP achieve good short-term outcomes in mature permanent
posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis. In direct comparisons at 12 months, FP
showed more consistent success and a lower rate of pain-related failure than PP,
whereas long-term comparative data remain scarce. At the study level, FP success
appeared largely independent of patient age and intraoperative bleeding time; for
PP, the available evidence was too limited to assess these associations, which
therefore remain hypothesis-generating. Calcium silicate–based cements showed
higher and less variable success than calcium hydroxide, although the latter was
evaluated in only one trial. Given the low-to-very-low certainty of evidence, these
findings are preliminary and not yet sufficient to guide definitive treatment
selection. They are nonetheless consistent with FP providing more predictable short-
term success and pain control, while PP may remain a conservative option when
pulpal inflammation appears limited and well-controlled. Adequately powered trials
with standardized outcomes and extended follow-up are needed to clarify the long-
term role of each technique.

The study protocol has been registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024602116),
and reporting followed the PRISMA guidelines [55]. The PICOT framework guided
two related objectives in mature permanent posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis
(P): (i) to estimate the 12‑month combined clinical and radiographic success rate (O)

for both success rate and pain‑related failure (O), at a minimum follow‑up of 12
months (T). Study‑level prognostic factors of success — patient age, bleeding‑control
time, and pulp‑capping material — were examined as a secondary, exploratory aim.
Eligibility criteria
Inclusion criteria
The PICOT structure was applied for this study:
￿P (Population): Patients with vital, restorable mature permanent posterior
teeth diagnosed with carious irreversible pulpitis.
￿I (Intervention): Full pulpotomy (FP) or partial pulpotomy (PP)
￿C (Comparison): for the direct comparison, the alternative pulpotomy
technique (FP versus PP); single‑arm success estimates required no
comparator.
￿O (Outcomes): Success rate defined by combined clinical and radiographic
criteria, assessed for each technique and in direct FP-versus-PP comparisons;
pain-related failure was assessed as a secondary outcome in the direct FP-
versus-PP comparison only.
￿T (Time): Minimum follow-up of 12 months
Only randomized clinical trials (RCTs) were eligible. Studies involving
anterior teeth, reversible pulpitis, immature roots, or mixed dentition were included
only if data specific to mature permanent posterior teeth with irreversible pulpitis
could be independently extracted.
Exclusion criteria
Studies were excluded if they involved pulpal necrosis, radiographic root
resorption, or ill-defined success criteria. Reports were also omitted if data for

mature permanent posterior teeth could not be isolated from other tooth types or

and radiographic success (either only clinical or radiographic criteria were reported)

Literature searching strategy and study selection
A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane
Library (CENTRAL), and Scopus, supplemented by manual searching of Google
Scholar, up to December 2025, using keywords and controlled terms related to
pulpotomy and pulp disease, including full pulpotomy, partial pulpotomy, pulpitis,
and vital pulp therapy. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to combine search
terms for maximum sensitivity. After removing duplicates in EndNote, two reviewers
(C.H. and P.L.) independently screened titles and abstracts against predefined
eligibility criteria. When titles and abstracts did not provide sufficient information
for exclusion, the full texts were retrieved for further assessment. Full-text articles
were then evaluated independently by the same reviewers for final inclusion. Any
discrepancies during the screening or inclusion phases were resolved via consensus
or consultation with a third reviewer (A.V.). The full search strategy is detailed in
Supplement Table 1.
Data collection and data items
Data were extracted independently by three reviewers (C.H., S.H., and X.N.)
using a standardized data extraction table. Any discrepancies were resolved through
discussion, and when consensus could not be reached, a fourth reviewer (A.V.) was
consulted. Extracted data included author and year, treatment arm, participant
characteristics (age, tooth type), intervention details (pulp capping materials,
bleeding time and hemostatic protocol), and follow-up duration. Moreover, data on
pre- and post-treatment pain assessment, postoperative analgesic consumption, and
the number of failure cases were also collected to qualitatively describe the
therapeutic effectiveness of studies directly comparing full and partial pulpotomy.
Outcome definitions
The primary outcome was the success rate, defined as the concurrent
presence of both clinical and radiographic success. When these were reported

ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

separately without a combined measure, the lower of the two success rates was
adopted as a conservative estimate of the combined success rate. Success was
calculated as the proportion of successful cases among treated teeth and reported
according to intention-to-treat or per-protocol analyses, as specified in the original
studies [56].
Irreversible pulpitis was diagnosed according to study-specific clinical
criteria, typically including spontaneous pain or prolonged pain in the absence of
external stimuli. Clinical success was defined as complete resolution of pain, normal
tooth stability, and absence of swelling or sinus tract formation. Radiographic
success was defined as the absence of root resorption and preservation of normal
periapical tissues, or no increase in the Periapical Index (PAI) score at follow-up.
The secondary outcome was pain-related failure, assessed only in the direct
comparison between full pulpotomy (FP) and partial pulpotomy (PP). Pain-related
failure was defined as treatment failure due to persistent or recurrent spontaneous
pain, postoperative pain requiring analgesics or further intervention, or tenderness
to percussion within 12 months, regardless of radiographic findings. Failures
attributable solely to radiographic changes were not considered pain-related
failures. This outcome was extracted exclusively from two-arm randomized trials
comparing FP and PP, using both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses.
Success rate was analysed on both an intention-to-treat and a per-protocol
basis in the two-arm meta-analyses. In the intention-to-treat analysis, all randomized
teeth were included, with losses to follow-up counted as failures. In the per-protocol
analysis, only teeth with complete follow-up were analysed; where a trial applied a
success criterion not used by the others, the affected cases were reclassified to
harmonize the success definition across studies. The same intention-to-treat and per-
protocol conventions were applied to the pain-related failure analysis.
Effect measures and data synthesis
Treatment outcomes were summarized as proportions (success rates) or odds
ratios. When heterogeneity in success or failure definitions was identified, sensitivity
analyses were conducted by excluding studies or cases with discrepant criteria and
comparing pooled estimates with the primary analyses to assess robustness.

An exploratory random-effects meta-regression (REML estimator, metafor
package in R) was performed for full pulpotomy only, relating logit-transformed 12-
month success to study-level mean age, with Knapp–Hartung adjustment, a
permutation test, and leave-one-out analyses (significance at P < 0.05). Bleeding
time was not modelled, as it was reported as a protocol-defined eligibility cut-off
rather than a continuous variable, and no meta-regression was performed for partial
pulpotomy owing to the insufficient number of independent studies. Publication bias
was not formally assessed, as funnel-plot and regression-based asymmetry tests are
invalid for single-arm proportion meta-analyses-proportions are bounded between 0
and 1 and their standard errors depend on the proportion itself, inducing spurious
asymmetry-and the small number of pairwise trials (n = 4) further precluded reliable
assessment.
The quality of the evidence
The GRADEpro GDT (Guideline Development Tool) was used to assess the
certainty of evidence. The "Risk of Bias," "Inconsistency," and "Indirectness"
domains were evaluated based on the guidelines of Guyatt 2011 [57], whereas
publication bias was not formally rated (see Statistical analysis). "Imprecision" was
assessed using the updated GRADE Guidance 2022, applying the CI approach and
the optimal information size (OIS) approach when the sample size was insufficient
[58]. The certainty of evidence was assessed by two investigators (C.H. and X.N.),
with discrepancies resolved through consultation with a third investigator (A.V.)
when necessary.
