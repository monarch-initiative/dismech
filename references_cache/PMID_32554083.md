# Heterozygous IKKβ activation loop mutation results in a complex immunodeficiency syndrome.
**Authors:** Abbott J, Ehler AC, Jayaraman D, Reynolds PR, Otsu K, Manka L, Gelfand EW
**Journal:** J Allergy Clin Immunol (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaci.2020.06.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2020.06.007)

## Content

We identified in an adult with ectodermal dysplasia and immunodeficiency a germline, gain-of-function mutation, K171R, in IKBKB. The K171R mouse immunologic phenotype parallels human, suggesting IKBKB K171R underlies a novel immunodeficiency syndrome.

The importance of NF-κB to human immune signaling is demonstrated by the large number of monogenetic diseases that arise from defects in the NF-κB subunits and the proteins that regulate them. Hypomorphic variants in IKBKG and N-terminal variants in NFKBIA cause X-linked and dominant ectodermal dysplasia with combined immunodeficiency (EDA-ID), respectively. Biallelic loss of IKBKB (IKKβ) function causes a severe form of combined immunodeficiency. Monoallelic deficiency of individual NF-κB subunits is associated with CVID and autoimmunity, and defects in proteins that carry out post-translational modifications of NF-κB pathway proteins cause either immunodeficiency, severe inflammatory disease, or both. The most recent addition to this group of NF-κB-related human immune abnormalities is caused by an activating mutation, V203I, within the IKKβ kinase loop, recently reported to cause a novel syndrome of EDA-ID with additional unique features.1 We report a patient that carries a different genetic variant in IKKβ K171R, and demonstrate that, similar to V203I, K171R within IKKβ results in a complex immunodeficiency syndrome with multi-organ involvement.

The proband presented to the immunology clinic at the age of 27 with severe bronchiectasis and progressive pulmonary failure. He reported a history of lifelong recurrent respiratory infections, recurrent otitis, and severe cutaneous abscesses. CT scan of the chest revealed bronchiectasis and cystic lung changes (Fig 1, A). Examination of the skin demonstrated chronic severe scarring of the chest and back with accompanying skin thinning and cutaneous abscesses, a reticulated fibrotic appearance to the palms and soles, and nail dysplasia of the fingers and toes (Fig 1, A). Additional features gathered from the history included early-onset cataracts, granulomatous uveitis, hypodontia, and anhidrosis (Supplemental Description). Laboratory studies revealed severe lymphopenia but normal levels of IgG and IgM with a relative deficiency of IgG2 and an excess of IgA (Fig 1, B, Supplementary Table I). Immunoglobulin (IVIG) replacement and Bactrim were initiated, but he continued to experience recurrent lung infection, worsening bronchiectasis, and progressive respiratory failure. He succumbed to his disease following massive pulmonary hemorrhage during a lung infection.

The constellation of recurrent infections, ectodermal dysplasia, hypodontia, lymphopenia, and borderline-elevated IgA in a male was suggestive of NEMO deficiency.2 Long-range nested PCR of the expressed NEMO gene followed by capillary sequencing revealed no genetic variants. Likewise, no variants were found in the full-length NEMO coding sequence amplified from mononuclear cell mRNA. Immunoblot of an EBV-transformed lymphoblastoid line (EBV-LCL) generated from the patient revealed levels of NEMO protein that were comparable to controls (data not shown).

Trio exome sequencing was carried out as a means of simultaneously evaluating other known causes of EDA-ID, including monoallelic mutations in NFKBIA, or identifying a novel defect. A de novo variant in IKBKB, c.512A>G, p.lys171arg (K171R) in IKKβ, was detected with no other potential contributing variants identified. The variant was confirmed by capillary sequencing to be present in the patient’s blood and cheek swab specimen and was absent in both parents’ blood and two healthy sisters (Fig 1, C). IKBKB codes for IKKβ, a member of the IKK complex that is indispensable for activation of the canonical NF-kB signaling pathway. IKKβ phosphorylates the NF-KB inhibitor IkBα, resulting in degradation, and thus allowing NF-kB subunits to enter the nucleus to affect gene transcription. Amino acid substitution at the 171st position of IKKβ has been investigated previously due to its presence within the activation loop of the kinase domain and the identification of K171 variants in multiple B-lineage cancers as potential driver mutations.3–5 Rather than impairing kinase activity, mutation at this position appears to enhance kinase activity. In vitro, mutation of K171 to glutamate or threonine increases both phosphorylation of IκBα and transcription from an NF-κB reporter.6 Likewise, a series of K171 mutations, including the one reported here, K171R, result in dramatically enhanced auto-phosphorylation and ubiquitination of IKKβ.7 Together, these prior reports suggested that K171R acts by increasing protein function.

To determine if K171R had increased kinase activity levels of phosphorylation of the IKKβ target, p65 (phospho-P65, ser536) were measured. Phospho-p65(ser536) levels were elevated in the proband’s EBV-LCLs compared to controls (Fig 2, A). Since the amount of IKKβ detected by immunoblot was slightly higher for the proband compared to healthy controls (Fig 2, A; Fig E1), we sought to determine whether kinase activity was increased on a per molecule basis. Mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEF) were generated from a K171R knock-in mouse, and phospho-p65 levels were measured. Phospho-p65 levels increased in step with the dose of K171R alleles (Fig 2, B). This effect was observed despite similar amounts of IKKβ expression in the groups (Fig 2, B). These data support the notion that K171R in IKKβ increases baseline kinase activity ex vivo, and the effect resulted from the mutation itself rather than the presence of more IKKβ.

Degradation of IκBα is frequently investigated to assist with the diagnosis of NF-κB-related abnormalities. Prior to receiving the genetic diagnosis, we found that baseline IκBα levels were severely reduced in the patient’s peripheral blood mononuclear cells compared to healthy controls (Fig 2, C). Monocytes, however, retained the ability to further degrade IκBα following LPS stimulation (Fig 2, D). These findings indicated that signaling through NF-κB remained intact but appeared to be highly active in the probands current state. Since the proband suffered from repeated infection, a state that would be predicted to chronically activate NF-κB, we sought to determine if low IκBα levels are a baseline feature of K171R. Similar to PBMCs, the decrease in baseline IκBα was also observed in EBV-LCL from the patient (Fig 2, A). Since EBV transformation also results in chronic NF-κB activation, we then examined K171R MEFs (Fig 2, B). We found that heterozygous K171R MEFs had reduced baseline IκBα levels; however, homozygous cell lines did not have decreased baseline IκBα levels. These findings indicate that the regulation of baseline IκBα level is complex and has limited diagnostic utility, but if investigated, heterozygous carriers of K171R are likely to have low IκBα levels at baseline.

Baseline immune features of the heterozygous K171R knock-in mouse were examined. Like the proband, immunoglobulin levels of 10 to 14-week-old mice were comparable to wild type mice with the exception of increased levels of IgA and a slight increase in IgG2a (Fig E2; Fig E4 A). Spleen weights and cellularity were comparable between K171R and WT mice; however, despite similar numbers of cells, K171R spleens had increased numbers of B cells, accounted for by increases in both CD21hiCD23hi follicular B cells and CD21hiCD23lo marginal zone B cells (Fig E4, C). These findings are consistent with the B-cell-specific IKKβ constitutively-active mouse previously reported.8 As in the spleen, the thymus cellularity of K171R mice was similar to WT; however, CD4 single positive cells were reduced (Fig E4, C). This matches previous findings in thymocyte-specific IKKβ constitutively-active mouse.9 The percentage of CD3 T cells was also reduced in blood (Fig E4, B), suggesting additional problems with both CD4 and CD8 T cells following exit from the thymus. We therefore conclude that K171R results in IKKβ activation in vivo, and at least in the K171R mouse, B-cell development and function are enhanced while T-cell development and persistence appear to be impaired.

In summary, we present a complex immunodeficiency syndrome associated with K171R, a gain-of-function mutation in IKBKB. The clinical syndrome shows remarkable similarity to the features of IKKβ V203I gain-of-function patients reported by Cardinez et al; however, there are important differences (Fig E3). The similarities include recurrent cutaneous abscess, hypodontia, bronchiectasis, splenomegaly, and cataracts – a cluster of features likely unique to this condition. The patient described here also had unusual fibrotic reticulation of the palms and soles, skin thinning, nail dysplasia, anhidrosis, retinal detachment, and granulomatous uveitis. Moreover, this patient had a more profound lymphopenia that included B and NK cells, but interestingly, had preserved levels of immunoglobulins. It is unclear if these additional features reflect a unique or stronger effect of the specific mutation or if the patient’s genetic background is responsible for the modified phenotype. Identification of additional patients with the same and similar mutations will be essential to understand the full range of disease manifestations.
