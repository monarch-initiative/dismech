BACKGROUND: Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a heart muscle disease in which the 
left ventricle is enlarged, resulting in systolic dysfunction. Pathogenic 
variants in genes encoding proteins involved in cardiac contractility, 
cytoskeleton structure, and Ca2+ handling have been associated with DCM. TNNC1 
(cTnC [cardiac troponin C]) variants are implicated in DCM, hypertrophic, and 
restrictive cardiomyopathies. Unlike other sarcomere genes, most reports of 
TNNC1 variants lack segregation or pedigree data, partly because the majority of 
the variants described, to date, have been reported as de novo. Therefore, a 
critical need is warranted to further understand the mechanisms by which TNNC1 
variants could impact myofilament function, especially in response to PKA 
(protein kinase A)-mediated phosphorylation as this posttranslational 
modification modulates sarcomere function in response to β-adrenergic 
stimulation.
METHODS: Probands with the novel TNNC1-c.404A>C variant (cTnC-E135A) and family 
members were identified and consented. cTnC-depleted donor human cardiac muscle 
preparations were reconstituted with recombinant exogenous human cTnC-E135A. 
Steady-state isometric force and crossbridge kinetics were measured before and 
after PKA incubation. We used in silico modeling to further investigate 
crossbridge cycling kinetics.
RESULTS: We identified a multigenerational family carrying the TNNC1-c.404A>C 
variant with autosomal dominant DCM with both systolic and diastolic 
dysfunctions. Using reconstituted human cardiac muscle preparations, we showed 
that the cTnC-E135A abolishes the myofilament response to PKA-mediated 
phosphorylation. Furthermore, in silico mathematical modeling showed that this 
variant affects crossbridge kinetics by decreasing both Ca2+ kOFF-rate constant 
and myosin detachment rate, which could result in increased ventricular 
stiffness and reduced ejection fraction.
CONCLUSIONS: Our clinical and genetics data, combined with the in silico 
modeling and functional assays, suggest that cTnC-E135A is associated with DCM 
and disrupts kinetics of Ca2+ and crossbridge cycling by abolishing the 
myofilament response to PKA phosphorylation.

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a heart muscle disease in which the left ventricle is enlarged, resulting in systolic dysfunction. Pathogenic variants in genes encoding proteins involved in cardiac contractility, cytoskeleton structure, and Ca2+handling have been associated with DCM.TNNC1(cardiac troponin C, cTnC)variants are implicated in DCM, hypertrophic, and restrictive cardiomyopathies. Unlike other sarcomere genes, most reports ofTNNC1variants lack segregation or pedigree data, partly because the majority of the variants described to date have been reported asde novo. Therefore, a critical need is warranted to further understand the mechanisms by whichTNNC1variants could impact myofilament function, especially in response to protein kinase A (PKA)-mediated phosphorylation as this post-translational modification modulates sarcomere function in response to β-adrenergic stimulation.

Probands with the novelTNNC1-c.404A>C variant (cTnC-E135A) and family members were identified and consented. cTnC-depleted donor human cardiac muscle preparations (CMPs) were reconstituted with recombinant exogenous human cTnC-E135A. Steady-state isometric force and cross-bridge kinetics were measured before and after PKA incubation. We usedin silicomodeling to further investigate cross-bridge cycling kinetics.

We identified a multigenerational family carryingTNNC1-c.404A>C variant with autosomal dominant DCM with both systolic and diastolic dysfunction. Using reconstituted human CMPs, we showed that the cTnC-E135A abolishes the myofilament response to PKA-mediated phosphorylation. Furthermore,in silicomathematical modeling showed this variant affects cross-bridge kinetics by decreasing both Ca2+kOFF-rate constant and myosin detachment rate, which could result in increased ventricular stiffness and reduced ejection fraction.

Our clinical and genetics data, combined with thein silicomodeling and functional assays, suggest that cTnC-E135A is associated with DCM and disrupts kinetics of Ca2+and cross-bridge cycling by abolishing the myofilament response to PKA phosphorylation.

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), a primary heart muscle disease characterized by left ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction, is one of the most common cardiomyopathies in younger patients, with a predicted incidence of 1:400.1,2Clinical outcomes are poor, with a three-year mortality from diagnosis ranging from 12 to 20%, often from either heart failure or ventricular tachycardias.2Classically, there are more than 40 genes associated with disease onset and development of DCM.3Hereditary forms of the disease are often associated with pathogenic variants encoding sarcomeric and cytoskeletal proteins in the cardiac contractile apparatus. Current treatment involves targeting the neurohumoral dysregulation of HF with medications that counteract the overactive sympathetic nervous system and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.1However, there are currently no available therapeutics to treat the underlying pathogenesis, such as those that directly act on the sarcomere, in part because mechanisms for sarcomeric dysfunction in DCM are heterogeneous and still under investigation.

The cardiac troponin complex (cTn) is the key component of calcium (Ca2+) dependent thin filament regulation of muscle contraction.4Pathogenic variants inTNNT2,TNNC1, andTNNI3, encoding cardiac troponin T (cTnT), C (cTnC), and I (cTnI), respectively, are associated with DCM and other familial cardiomyopathies, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), and restrictive cardiomyopathy.5–10,11While pathogenic variants inTNNT2andTNNI3are more common and widely studied, fewerTNNC1disease-causing variants have been identified, and unfortunately, even fewer investigated functionally.11,12cTnC is the Ca2+-sensing subunit of the cTn, and is composed of four EF-hand domains.13The structural C-terminal domain (cCTnC) stabilizes an open conformation via two high-affinity Ca2+binding sites, while the regulatory N-terminal domain (cNTnC) has one inactive and a single, relatively low-affinity Ca2+binding site.13During muscle activation, Ca2+binds to cNTnC, initiating the removal of inhibitory proteins—including the C-terminus of cTnI—from actin and allowing contraction to proceed.14N-terminus of cTnI is a target for protein kinase A (PKA)-mediated phosphorylation and also interacts with the C-terminus of cTnC.13,15Importantly, PKA phosphorylation of cTnI is the predominant pathway for β-adrenergic regulation of cardiac contractility and lusitropy, both of which are impaired in DCM.2,16–20

Despite the importance of cTnC for cardiac muscle contraction, disease-associated variantsTNNC1associated with DCM are rare, and thus it is important to understand the pathophysiology of those that are associated with disease. To date, only oneTNNC1variant associated with significant family histories have been shown to co-segregate with incidence of disease in a multigenerational family.21Here, we present a family pedigree case study with a previously undescribed variant inTNNC1-c.404A>C (cTnC-E135A), that co-segregates with DCM with systolic and diastolic dysfunction via an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, and for which our pathogenicity modeling suggests is disruptive to protein function. We aimed to characterize the pathogenic nature of cTnC–E135A and to unravel the molecular mechanisms that underlies its pathogenicity through a comprehensive analysis, integrating clinical data with biophysical andin silicoapproaches. Our data demonstrates theTNNC1-c.404A>C variant is associated with an abolished myofilament response to PKA phosphorylation, which may, in turn, contribute to deficits in systolic function and contractile reserve.

A family presented with multiple generations of young adult- and pediatric-onset nonischemic DCM with a predisposition to diastolic dysfunction and ventricular arrhythmias (Figure 1A). The proband (III.2) survived sudden cardiac arrest from ventricular fibrillation when she was 38 years old and was found to have a depressed left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 35-40% with recurrent ventricular tachycardia (VT). She was subsequently diagnosed with nonischemic DCM with class I diastolic dysfunction by echocardiogram and had an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placed. The proband’s mother (II.2) was found to have a depressed EF of 20%, class II diastolic dysfunction by echocardiogram, and frequent VT episodes without evidence of ischemia resulting in a diagnosis of nonischemic DCM at 53 years of age. Her brother, II.3 had normal LVEF (60%) with blunted E/A ratio of 0.6, E’/A’ wave reversal, and biatrial enlargement suggesting diastolic dysfunction. One of the proband’s sons (IV.4) developed depressed LVEF of 33% at 16 years of age without evidence of scar or fibrosis and with normal ECGs. The reduced EF was initially seen by echocardiogram with mild dilation of the LV. Then, cardiac MRI was performed specifically to evaluate for fibrosis/scar. The dilation and reduced EF were confirmed and furthermore, there was no evidence of scar by gadolinium enhancement and no delayed enhancement of the myocardium. He also had evidence of diastolic dysfunction by strain imaging.

Given the family history of DCM, gene panel testing for cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia associated genes was performed for the proband (III.2) with cascade testing performed in II.2, II.3, IV.2, and IV.4. A heterozygous variant for the geneTNNC1encoding cTnC was found in all individuals with DCM and/or isolated diastolic dysfunction (Figure 1B). The variant is a single nucleotide substitution (c.404 A>C, p.E135A) present in exon 5 (Figure 1D). This missense variant results in a change from glutamic acid to alanine in the G helix of the C terminus, just before the fourth EF hand (one of the Ca2+binding locations) (Figure 1E). Sequence conservation analysis indicates that amino acid position 135 is very highly conserved (99%) across species (Figure 1F). This variant was not reported in the literature and was absent in ostensibly healthy control individuals in gnomAD.22Moreover, a single report of this variant was noted in ClinVar with an “uncertain” assessment of clinical status23. With application of fivein silicovariant prediction algorithms (Supplemental Table 1), we found that all predicted the variant to be deleterious to protein function. On evaluation of the variant, it was rated as likely pathogenic by current ACMG guidance.24There were no other likely pathogenic variants, or variants of uncertain significance, in genes associated with the development of cardiac channelopathies or cardiomyopathies. Taken together, these results suggest that the cTnC-E135A variant is associated with autosomal dominant DCM.

Extraction of endogenous cTnC and subsequent reconstitution with recombinant exogenous human cTnC is a methodology commonly employed to study contractile parameters in both healthy and diseased states (please seesupplemental methodsfor further details)6,18,25,26. Here, our focus was on elucidating the impact of the novel DCM-associated cTnC-E135A variant on the regulation of cardiac contractility. To achieve this, permeabilized myocardial strips from human donor were depleted of endogenous cTnC and reconstituted with either exogenous human cTnC-WT (100%) or cTnC-E135A (mixture of 50% cTnC-WT and 50% cTnC-E135A, mimicking heterozygous proband). After the CDTA extraction protocol, CMPs displayed an average residual force post-CDTA treatment of approximately 21% (Table 1). The residual force serves as a reliable indicator of the approximate amount of endogenous cTnC remaining in cardiac tissue following the extraction protocol.6,25CMPs reconstituted with cTnC-E135A displayed lower myofilament Ca2+sensitivity of steady-state isometric force generation (pCa50= 5.63 ± 0.02) with a rightward shift of 0.1 pCa units compared with CMPs reconstituted with cTnC-WT (pCa50= 5.73 ± 0.03) (Table 1andFigure 2A–B). Steady-state isometric force generated by cTnC-E135A CMPs was not statistically different than cTnC-WT (23.43 ± 2.34 mN/mm2versus24.32 ± 1.54 mN/mm2, respectively) (Table 1andFigure 1D–F).

Next, to assess the Ca2+-activation responsiveness of the reconstituted human CMPs to β-adrenergic stimulation, the samples were incubated with PKA for 30 min, and a re-evaluation of the contractile parameters was conducted. cTnC-WT + PKA exhibited a notable 0.22 pCa unit reduction (pCa50= 5.51 ± 0.03) in myofilament Ca2+sensitivity of steady-state isometric force generation, a response that is consistent with previous reports27,28. In contrast, cTnC-E135A + PKA displayed a modest increase of 0.01 pCa units (pCa50= 5.64 ± 0.04) (Table 1andFigure 2A–B). No differences in the cooperativity of thin filament activation were seen among the groups (cTnC-WT + PKA = 1.47 ± 0.08 and cTnC-E135A + PKA = 1.67 ± 0.11) (Table 1andFigure 2C). PKA incubation significantly reduced the steady-state isometric forces in WT (Table 1andFigure 2D–F). Interestingly, PKA-mediated phosphorylation showed no effects on physiological Ca2+levels range (pCa 6.0 – pCa 5.6), only on near-maximal and maximal Ca2+levels (pCa 5.4 – pCa 4) (Table 1andFigure 2D–F)., Western blot analysis revealed no differences in PKA-mediated phosphorylation of cTnI at serine 23/24 between the groups (Figure 2G). Overall, these data demonstrate that the cTnC-E135A variant abolish the effects of PKA-mediated phosphorylation.

We measured sinusoidal stiffness (SS) to assess overall number of cross-bridges. PKA-mediated phosphorylation did not show alterations in SS in cTnC-WT (Figure 3 A–BandTable 1) nor cTnC-E135A (Figure 3 C–DandTable 1). Additionally, there was no statistical differences between cTnC-E135A and cTnC-WT (Figure 3). Next, we hypothesized that cTnC-E135A variant could abolish PKA-mediated effects by altering cross-bridge kinetics, i.e., rate of tension redevelopment (kTR) as well crossbridge attachment and detachment rates. cTnC-E135A CMPs exhibited slower maximalkTRcompared to cTnC-WT and showed a diminished PKA-mediated effect on acceleratingkTR(Figure 4A–D). Importantly, the extraction and reconstitution procedures did not alter the Ca2+-dependence ofkTRin cTnC-WT-containing CMPs (Figure 4A–B). The cTnC-E135A-containing CMPs also presented Ca2+-dependence ofkTR(Figure 4C–D). However, that intrinsic property was abolished upon PKA phosphorylation (Figure 4C–D). This observation demonstrates that the employed method preserved the inherent physiological properties of cross-bridge kinetics, and the alterations seen in cTnC-E135A-containing CMPs are linked to the variant. To examine our hypothesis, we mathematically estimated the crossbridge attachment (f, transition from the nonforce-producing to the force-producing state) and detachment rates (g, transition from the force-producing to the nonforce-producing state), and thin filamentkOFF(Ca2+kOFF-rate constant) by using a three-state model of cardiac muscle contraction that models the relationship between steady-state isometric force andkTR.7cTnC-E135A variant resulted in slowerfand g compared to cTnC-WT in both absence and presence of PKA (Table 2). Upon PKA phosphorylation, WT group displayed fastergandkOFF, as expected (Table 2andFigure 4E–F). cTnC-E135A + PKA showed fasterg, but slower kOFFcompared to cTnC-E135A before PKA phosphorylation, (Table 2andFigure 4E–F). Mathematical modeling suggests that the cTnC-E135A facilitates Ca2+release from cTnC in absence of PKA phosphorylation (Table 2), while after PKA phosphorylation. Ca2+dissociation from cTnC is prolonged, counteracting the expected response to PKA phosphorylation of cTnI. This mechanism may account for the lack of force reduction observed at physiological Ca2+levels following PKA phosphorylation.

In one of the largest family pedigrees – to date – of a pathogenic variant inTNNC1linked with autosomal dominant DCM, we reveal here that the myofilament response of cTnC-E135A to PKA phosphorylation is abolished. Our findings provide a potential mechanism for deficits in systolic function in patients harboring this pathogenic variant. This discovery is significant for several reasons. While the majority of familial DCM cases are genotype negative, most genetic variants associated with DCM localize to genes that encode the cardiac sarcomere.31Secondly, this specificTNNC1-c.404A>C variant presents with stark differences in phenotypes compared to otherTNNC1variants.11,20At present, the majority ofTNNC1variants that have been identified are more frequently associated with HCM. Further, most disease-associatedTNNC1variants are associated with an early age of diagnosis and a largely pro-arrhythmic phenotype.10In contrast, our data shows theTNNC1-c.404A>C variant is associated with DCM with diastolic dysfunctional cases, manifesting in patients in their early middle-ages (35.7±13.2 yrs; mean±SEM). Finally, and of key importance, theTNNC1-c.404A>C variant abolishes the response of PKA-mediated phosphorylation of cTnI, thereby potentially reducing thin filament response to β-adrenergic stimulation. DCM-causing variants in thin filament proteins have been reported to present blunted response to PKA. However, in addition to the pedigree and multi-generational segregation for aTNNC1variant, the novelty of our work lies in the use of human myocardial strips from a healthy donor, where the endogenous TnC was extracted and reconstituted with the 50% mutant recombinant protein. Furthermore, our experiments were conducted at near-physiologically relevant temperature and under conditions that maintain myofilament lattice spacing, closely resembling those of intact muscle. Nevertheless, we assume that both alleles express proteins at equimolar concentrations, and if altered protein binding occurs, it would contribute to the underlying mechanism of the disease. This approach provides a more physiologically accurate model to study – in vitro – the effects of cTnC-E135A. These findings are clinically significant, as it suggests the use of inotropic drugs such as β-agonists and phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitors may have impaired efficacy in patients with DCM harboring this pathogenic variant.

Systole is initiated when Ca2+binds to site II of cTnC N-terminal domain (NcTnC), inducing a series of dynamic and structural events, including increased affinity between NcTnC and the cTnI switch peptide, resulting in decreased binding of the cTnI inhibitory peptide with actin32,33. The β-adrenergic system and its subsequent activation of PKA are critical regulators of inotropic and lusitropic responses to catecholamines, augmenting systolic and diastolic function. Notably, PKA phosphorylates key myofilament proteins, such as cardiac myosin binding protein-C (cMyBP-C) and cTnI, along with the Ca2+regulatory proteins, ryanodine receptor 2, phospholamban, the L-type voltage-gated Ca2+channel.15PKA augments myofilament function via the phosphorylation of cMyBP-C and TnI, a posttranslational modification that desensitizes myofilaments, increasing the magnitude of both the intracellular Ca2+transient and the acceleration rate of crossbridge formations.34,35Furthermore, PKA is a critical regulator of diastolic function and cardiac lusiptropy.13,15Phosphorylation of cTnI at serine −23 and −24 (Ser23/24) weakens its interaction with cTnC, accelerating NcTnC Ca2+release and the slow phase of relaxation in a load-dependent manner, independent of changes in cross-bridge cycling.34

The recently published structure of the fully activated Tn core32predicts that the positioning of the N-terminal part of the long TnI helix is stabilized by the two salt bridges between TnI R46 and K47 (Fig. 5, yellow atoms) and TnC D132 and E135 (Fig. 5, blue atoms), respectively. The positioning of the 41 N-terminal residues of TnI, which harbor Ser23/24, is not defined in cryo-EM maps of unphosphorylated cardiac thin filaments (cTFs)33, and there are no structures of fully phosphorylated cTFs.In silicoexperiments predicted that phosphorylated TnI N-terminus may interact with tropomyosin (Tm) to restrain the activation of the cTF by Ca2+.37We speculate one of these salt bridges is altered as a result of the E135A variant, perturbing the positioning of the TnI N-terminal helix. Based on clinical,in silico, andin vitrodata, we propose the following mechanism: cTnC-E135A variant disrupts the interaction between cTnI and cTnC, consequently impairing myocyte response to PKA-mediated cTnI phosphorylation (Figure 5). Importantly, the variant does not affect PKA’s ability in phosphorylate cTnI. The disruption of cTnI-cTnC interaction leads to sustained Ca2+binding at the N-terminus of cTnC (Table 2) even in the presence of phosphorylated cTnI, exposing the myosin binding sites on actin and facilitating cross-bridge formation (Figures 2and3). At the organ level, these alterations result in elevated left ventricular stiffness, increased filling pressure, diastolic dysfunction and reduced ejection fraction (Figure 6).

Proposed mechanism on how cTnC-E135A variant results in DCM with diastolic dysfunction.

Variants inTNNC1have been implicated in DCM and ~1% of clinical cases of HCM11,38. In HCM, the cTnC-C84Y variant has been shown to affect conformational dynamics of the D/E linker in cTnC, favoring the primed state, resulting in hypercontractility.8On the other hand, the cTnC-A31S variant increases Ca2+sensitivity by directly enhancing Ca2+affinity at binding site II on N-terminal of cTnC.39in vivostudies with cTnC-A8V mice showed that this variant increases the Ca2+-binding affinity of the cTF, as well as it elicits changes in myocyte Ca2+homeostasis and cellular remodeling, resulting in diastolic dysfunction.40Point mutations inTNNC1that encode for the D145E, Y5H, M103I, and I148V variants alter the α-helical structure of cTnC, presumed to affect Ca2+binding, all display phenotypic characteristics of DCM.41However, D145E has also been associated with HCM11. Additionally, these variants exhibit decreased β-adrenergic-induced stimulation.41Interestingly, M103I41variant display a near complete loss of PKA-mediated phosphorylation, while β-adrenergic stimulation in I4M variants18remains unexplored. We have identified one of the first multigenerational families with aTNNC1variant associated with DCM. The only DCM-causing variant that has been found is cTnC-G159D42. It has been shown in multiple families to be associated with DCM21,43–45. Dyer et al. show that in myocytes from explanted human hearts, this variant increases Ca2+sensitivity, which is affected by cTnI phosphorylation43. This increased Ca2+sensitivity is similar to what we have found with cTnC-E135A.

The present study here, unravels a mechanism by which blunted response PKA-mediated phosphorylation is a result of alterations in cTnI-cTnT interaction in myocytes harboring the cTnC-E135A variant. This is of clinical significance for several reasons. First, the influence of cTnC-E135A variant on responsiveness to β-blockers is unknown. Second, while inotropes are avoided for long-term management of DCM due to increased mortality, they are used in acute decompensated HF in patients with DCM.46Our findings presented here suggest that patients with the cTnC-E135A variant may not benefit fully from inotropy, considering these agents augment contractile function via cTnI phosphorylation. As such, direct sarcomere stimulators could be considered, primarily for management of these patients in acute settings. Danicamtiv is a possibility, although the interplay between this myotrope and impaired PKA phosphorylation of cTnI remains unclear.

One of the limitations of this study is the absence of cardiac tissue from patients or patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells, which limits the ability to assess the long-term effects of the variant in cardiomyocytes. To address this limitation, we employed a strategy involving endogenous troponin displacement and reconstitution using human recombinant troponin carrying the patient-specific variant in donor human myocardium. This approach provided a platform to investigate the functional impact of the variant in a human myofilament context. Another limitation is that this study focuses solely on cTnI phosphorylation, whereas other sarcomeric proteins can also undergo phosphorylation and contribute to altered contractile function of the heart during disease. While phosphorylation analysis of these additional proteins would not be meaningful within our simplified reconstituted system, assessing their phosphorylation status in intact myocardial tissue would provide a more comprehensive view of the disease mechanism and long-term myofilament remodeling. Finally, our proposed mechanism by which the cTnC-E135A variant blunts the regulatory effects of cTnI phosphorylation on myofilament activation is somewhat speculative, and further studies are necessary to determine whether this mechanism holds true experimentally.

A myriad ofTNNC1variants may give rise to different heart diseases and present with a variety of phenotypes. In this study, we uncovered a multigenerational family bearing theTNNC1-c.404A>C variant linked to autosomal dominant DCM. Our findings revealed cTnC-E135A abolishes the cardiac myofilament responsiveness to PKA phosphorylation. This has significant clinical implications with respect to use of β-blockers and inotropes for HF management in patients withTNNC1-c.404A>C DCM.
