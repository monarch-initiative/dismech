# A truncated CDC14A retains catalytic structure and phosphatase activity preserving male fertility but causes nonsyndromic deafness.
**Authors:** Shabbir K, Jackisch G, Belyantseva IA, Imran M, Naz S, Sele C, Murina V, Knecht W, Friedman TB, Logan DT, Imtiaz A
**Journal:** J Biol Chem (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jbc.2025.110982](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbc.2025.110982)

## Content

Pathogenic variants of human CDC14A (cell division cycle 14A) are associated either with nonsyndromic deafness DFNB32 or hearing impairment infertile male syndrome (HIIMS). The 623-residue CDC14A protein has two globular domains (residues 17-152 and 217-325) and a 278-residue C-terminal intrinsically disordered region (IDR). To date, 16 recessive variants of human CDC14A are associated with hearing loss. Variants affecting the globular N-terminal domains of human CDC14A are associated with HIIMS, whereas mutations in the IDR cause nonsyndromic deafness DFNB32. Here, we tested the hypothesis that the human CDC14A c.1033C>T variant, segregating with nonsyndromic deafness in family PKSN10, introduces a premature translation stop codon (p.R345∗), yet the mRNA escapes nonsense-mediated decay and produces sufficient active phosphatase to allow for male fertility. Quantitative analyses of CDC14A mRNA in blood leukocytes from the PKSN10 family showed that CDC14A transcripts are stable, including p.R345∗ transcripts, which evade nonsense-mediated decay. To further test this hypothesis, we performed biochemical and structural characterizations of truncated CDC14A (ΔC-CDC14A) protein retaining only residues 1 to 345. Kinetic functional studies and X-ray crystallographic findings of purified ΔC-CDC14A protein indicate that it retains structural integrity and phosphatase activity. Molecular genetic reports of DFNB32 and HIIMS, taken together with structural and functional data in this study, indicate that phosphatase activity of ΔC-CDC14A containing the two globular domains is sufficient for male fertility but insufficient for normal hearing. In addition, we show that the C-terminal IDR of CDC14A is required for normal hearing, likely because it is necessary for normal localization of CDC14A in hair cells.

Yeast phosphatase CDC14 (cell division cycle 14A; SGD: S000001924) encodes 551 residues and is essential for mitotic exit and cytokinesis (1). In Caenorhabditis elegans, CDC14 regulates the G1/S transition and developmental quiescence (2), whereas in zebrafish, this phosphatase has a debatable role in ciliogenesis (3, 4, 5). The sequence of the N-terminal catalytic domain of CDC14 is conserved across species (5). Although human CDC14A and its paralog CDC14B can each rescue a temperature-sensitive mutation of yeast CDC14 (6), mammalian CDC14A evolved for different functions. In humans and mice, CDC14A is specifically required for hearing in both sexes and fertility in males (5). In addition, mutations in the mouse paralog Cdc14b are associated with premature aging and cataracts (7) but have normal hearing (5). Deletion of both Cdc14a and Cdc14b in mouse results in brain developmental defects affecting the early survival in ∼50% of newborns, although cell cycle progression in the derived cell lines remains unaffected (8).

Human CDC14A protein (NM_033312.3, NP_201569.1) contains 623 residues encoded by 15 exons, has two conserved globular N-terminal catalytic domains, and has an N-terminal nuclear localization signal (NLS). The C-terminal region of this isoform has a nuclear export signal (NES, 352–367). The yeast NES mediates Cdc14p relocalization during mitosis (9). Subcellular localization defines substrate specificity of the CDC14 family of phosphatases (10). As established by our study, the CDC14A C-terminal region of 278 residues lacks defined secondary structure and is predominantly disordered (Fig. S1). Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) are generally longer than 40 residues, depleted in order-promoting amino acids, and exist as dynamic ensembles rather than adopting a single stable structure (11, 12). Their characterization requires a combination of computational predictions, contextual functional analyses, and advanced biophysical methods. Contextual studies show that these regions are often post-translationally modified, for example, by phosphorylation, which can modulate conformational states (13). Transcripts of genes encoding IDPs/IDRs are often alternatively spliced (14). Due to their extended conformations, IDPs/IDRs have larger apparent molecular weights and are more susceptible to proteolysis and autocleavage (15, 16). Due to low sequence conservation, it can be challenging to assign a conserved function to IDPs/IDRs. However, studies have found that orthologs of various proteins have a conserved state of disorder and interspersed functional signatures (17, 18, 19).

Seven variants in the exons encoding the C-terminal region of human CDC14A are associated with nonsyndromic hearing loss DFNB32 (4, 5, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24). DFNB32 phenotypes associated with the truncating variants p.R339∗, p.R345∗, p.S348Qfs2∗, and p.R376∗ that are located in exon 11 of human CDC14A (15 exons, 623 residues) are particularly intriguing. These premature termination codons (PTCs) are inserted in the middle of the full-length transcript (NM_033312.3) and would be expected to mark the transcript for nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), resulting in a null allele associated with hearing impairment infertile male syndrome. However, these exon 11 variants fall within the final exon of a shorter isoform of CDC14A (NM_033313.3; 383 residues). PTCs near the last exon–exon junction often evade NMD (25), escape degradation, which we hypothesize explains the nonsyndromic deafness (DFNB32) human phenotype of these exon 11 PTC variants.

In this study, we evaluated the stability of human c.1033C>T CDC14A mRNA encoding a protein that truncates the C-terminal region and provides an explanation for this mutant mRNA circumventing NMD. To assess the functional impact of p.R345∗, we performed biochemical studies and solved the crystal structures of residues 1 to 345 of mouse CDC14A alone or bound to a six-residue phosphopeptide (acetyl-ApSPRRR-amide). Functional studies demonstrated that the truncated CDC14A protein is mislocalized in sensory hair cells, but it retains phosphatase activity sufficient to support male fertility in humans. Together with our reported human and mouse genetics findings (5), we conclude that the loss of the C-terminal IDR in CDC14A uncouples catalytic sufficiency from proper subcellular targeting, preserving male fertility yet causing deafness in both males and females.

Pathogenic variants of human CDC14A (OMIM [Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man]: 608653) and their associated phenotypes are summarized in Figure 1A. All reported human DFNB32 nonsyndromic deafness-associated mutations are clustered in the C-terminal region of CDC14A. Structural predictions from AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold3 (26, 27) indicate that residues 343 to 623 of human CDC14A lack a defined 3D structure. This idea is supported by disorder predictions using PONDR and ADOPT (28), which identify residues 343 to 623 in human CDC14A, residues 343 to 603 in mouse CDC14A, as well as CDC14 homologous regions across species, as disordered (Fig. S1, A–C). Experimental verification of multiple conformational ensembles is not available. However, the CDC14A C-terminal residues exhibit contextual features of well-characterized IDPs/IDRs. Multiple sequence alignment of CDC14A from various species shows high evolutionary divergence (Fig. S2) from yeast to human; however, the state of disorder remains conserved (Fig. S1). When transcribed, CDC14A exons 11 to 15 are alternatively spliced, defining six transcript isoforms (RefSeq, Table S1). CDC14A, like other IDR-containing proteins, has multiple phosphorylation sites in its disordered C-terminal region (29, 30). As we report here, full-length CDC14A is unstable and undergoes degradation. Since human inner ear cochlear mRNA is inaccessible, the stability of CDC14A mRNA carrying the c.1033C>T (p.R345∗) mutation was assessed in mRNA derived from leukocytes of affected and unaffected individuals of the PKSN10 family (Fig. S3A). Quantitative RT–PCR analyses revealed no significant difference in CDC14A mRNA levels (Fig. 1B) between male individuals IV:8 (wildtype) and IV:4 (affected), indicating that the mutant transcript escapes NMD. mRNA levels in heterozygous carrier mother III:1 and affected female IV:2 are slightly lower (10–20%) as compared with both males (IV:8, IV:2). As the heterozygous carriers are phenotypically normal, this slight decrease in mRNA level would not be expected to have any functional significance. Sanger sequencing confirmed PCR amplimers derived from CDC14A mRNA (Fig. S4E). Our results indicate that there are stable CDC14A transcripts in homozygous c.1033C>T-affected individuals of the PKSN10 family (Fig. S4, B–D).

Attempts to express and purify full-length and 1 to 345 truncated mouse CDC14A in Escherichia coli and Pichia pastoris were unsuccessful. A shorter construct, including residues 14 to 345 expressed in E. coli, was insoluble (Fig. S5A) and catalytically inactive after solubilization (see supporting information). We refer to the mouse wildtype truncated protein (1–345) as ΔC-CDC14A and the phosphatase-dead variant as PDΔC-CDC14A in the rest of the article. We successfully expressed full-length CDC14A, ΔC-CDC14A, and PDΔC-CDC14A in Sf9 insect cells (Fig. S5, B and C). Western blot analyses confirmed expression of full-length CDC14A protein in the lysates of Sf9 cells (Fig. S5C). However, this full-length CDC14A protein degraded to multiple short fragments during purification (Fig. S6, E–H) and could not be crystalized. In contrast, both ΔC-CDC14A and PDΔC-CDC14A were purified to >98% purity (Fig. S6, A–D), and the PDΔC-CDC14A was successfully crystallized. During purification, the N-terminal methionine was removed by tobacco etch virus cleavage (Fig. S7, A and B).

PDΔC-CDC14A crystallized readily (Fig. S8). The best crystals formed in 0.2 M sodium sulfate, 20% PEG 3350, and 0.1 M Bis–Tris propane, pH 7.5. The apo structure (Protein Data Bank [PDB] ID: 9RIB) was solved at 1.62 Å resolution (Fig. 2A). The structure of PDΔC-CDC14A cocrystallized with a phosphopeptide (acetyl-ApSPRRR-amide, abbreviated PS) having the general features of a Cdk substrate was determined by refining the diffraction data directly against the apo-structure to 1.74 Å (PDB ID: 9S1S), as the crystal forms were isomorphous (Fig. 2, C and D). Both structures contained two CDC14A monomers per asymmetric unit, with residues 8 to 343 and 7 to 345 modeled in monomers 1 and 2, respectively (Fig. S9). The structure reveals two tandem DSP-like domains (domain A/DSPn and domain B/DSPc), connected by a partly helical linker between residues 157 and 179. The catalytic domain B/DSPc (residues 217–325) contains the conserved PTP signature motif (HC(X)5R). In the PS-bound crystal structure, the peptide binds to only one of the two monomers in the asymmetric unit. Clear electron density is observed for A(P – 1), pS, and P(P + 1), whereas R(P + 2) is partially visible. No electron density corresponding to the two C-terminal PS arginine residues is observed, suggesting that they are not critical for substrate binding interactions and can adopt multiple conformations when bound to the catalytic site (Fig. 2C). The peptide–protein interactions closely resemble those in the CDC14B complex (31). The phosphate group of pSer fits into the phosphate cradle formed by the PTP loop (residues 277–282). S278 hydrogen bonds to one of the oxygen atoms of the phosphate moiety (Fig. 2D). The backbone amide group of A(P-1) of the PS makes an H-bond to the side chain of D251, which is also positioned to function as a general acid, donating an H-bond to the Oγ atom of pS. A water molecule, involved in the ligand-binding network and bridging interactions, is centered between D251, the PS, and the α-helix of the DSP motif. Substrate specificity is mediated by a hydrophobic pocket formed by the residues F48, A132, A280, L282, and I317. The incorporation of the PS polar prolyl ring (P + 1) into this hydrophobic pocket within the catalytic site of PDΔC-CDC14A provides selectivity for peptides containing a pS-P motif (Fig. 2D). A DALI structural similarity search (32) showed that PDΔC-CDC14A (PDB ID: 9S1S) closely resembles CDC14B (PDB ID: 1OHC; 31), sharing 66% sequence identity and 83% similarity in the catalytic domain. The two structures superimpose with an RMSD of 1.1 Å over 333 Cα atoms with only minor differences in regions distant from the active site (Fig. S10). The next closest homolog is yeast CDC14p (PDB ID: 5XW4) with 36% identity and an RMSD of 2.4 Å over 326 residues.

Steady-state kinetics of purified ΔC-CDC14A resulted in a much lower Km value of 0.009 ± 0.0051 mM for the peptide (PS) as compared with Km pNPP (para-nitrophenyl phosphate) (3.0 ± 0.8 mM) (Fig. 2, E and F). The Km values determined for Sf9 cell lysates expressing either full length, since it degraded during purification, or ΔC-CDC14A against pNPP were similar: 3 ± 0.6 mM (ΔC-CDC14A) and 3 ± 2.1 mM (full-length) (Figs. 2G and S11, A and B) (Table S2). However, because CDC14A protein concentrations in cell lysates were undetermined, we could not compare specific activities or evaluate whether the C-terminal region influences catalytic efficiency.

In postnatal day P2 to P3 mouse inner ear explants, transfected full-length mouse enhanced GFP (EGFP)-CDC14A (1–603) was distributed throughout the cytoplasm and concentrated at hair-bundle regions of inner hair cells and vestibular hair cells and at the base and along the length of a kinocilium (Fig. 3). No detectable signal was observed in the nucleus (dotted circle, Fig. 3). In contrast, the majority of EGFP-ΔC-CDC14A, which retains an NLS, but lacks the C-terminal IDR, including NES, was trapped in the nucleus. A fraction of EGFP-ΔC-CDC14A appears to escape the nucleus and is present throughout the cytoplasm of transfected cells and in stereocilia and kinocilia in the early postnatal organ of Corti, ampulla, or saccule (Fig. 3). Nonsense mutations typically, but not always, trigger NMD, especially when a 3′ exon–exon junction lies greater than 50 to 55 nucleotides downstream of a PTC (25). For example, a truncating variant of NUSAP1 (NM_016359.5:c.1209C>A; p.Y403∗) escapes NMD, which produces a stable truncated protein associated with microcephaly and epilepsy (33). Likewise, truncations of SEMA6B in the last exon bypass NMD and are associated with progressive myoclonic epilepsy (34). In our study, we show that the CDC14A c.1033C>T; p.R345∗ variant likely evades NMD because of its proximity to a natural stop codon in exon 11 of a shorter isoform (NM_033313.3). We speculate that the isoform encoding CDC14A N-terminal 383 residues (NM_033313.3) may perform essential functions of CDC14A in male reproductive tissues.

Full-length CDC14A is present in kinocilia, stereocilia, the cytoplasm of inner ear hair cells and is colocalized with cytoplasmic microtubules in COS-7 cells (5). However, EGFP-tagged CDC14A truncated at residue R345 is concentrated in the nucleus (Fig. 3) with some signal in stereocilia, kinocilia, and cytoplasm. Such altered localization is posited to be responsible for altered interactions with protein partners and phosphosubstrates leading to hearing impairment. Similar disruptions in nuclear export were shown to be linked to other human pathologies, for example, deletion of the C-terminal NES in atrophin-1 leads to nuclear accumulation and worsens motor deficits in a DRPLA mouse model (35). An NES variant in FMR1 causes nuclear retention of FMRP resulting in fragile X-like features (36). These findings emphasize the critical role of nucleocytoplasmic transport in maintaining protein function and cellular metabolic health.

Experimentally resolved protein structures may differ from a computational predicted structure (37). However, the resolved crystal structure of the CDC14A catalytic core completely overlaps with AlphaFold-predicted structure with RMSD 0.41. The resolved globular domains closely resemble those of mammalian CDC14B and predicted models, suggesting that functional divergence arises from differences in their N- and C-termimal IDRs.

Removing the disordered C-terminal tail of budding yeast Cdc14p reduces phosphatase activity in vitro by about one-third (1). As well as phosphorylation of the C-terminal residue S429 severely decreases Cdc14p phosphatase activity (29). A similar reduction in activity between full-length and truncated forms of the Fusarium graminearum CDC14 was also observed (38), suggesting that the C-terminal region plays a conserved role in supporting enzymatic function. IDRs of multiple enzymes are involved in allosteric regulation (39). In our study, we did not draw a conclusion about a difference in catalytic efficiency or substrate specificity between the two constructs expressing full-length or ΔC-CDC14A. However, computational and contextual functional studies indicate that the C-terminal region of CDC14A is intrinsically disordered, and its function is essential for hearing. The C-terminal IDR of CDC14A may play an essential role in hearing by interacting with multiple substrates like other IDPs/IDRs (40, 41).

In summary, our investigations highlight the functional significance of the structured domain and the IDR of CDC14A in male fertility and hearing in males and females, correspondingly, as supported by human and mouse genetic data (5). Characterization of the mRNA level of CDC14A homozygous for c.1033C>T (p.R345∗) demonstrates escape of mutant mRNA from NMD and altered localization of encoded truncated protein in cellular compartments. Our data on nuclear retention of ΔC-CDC14A confirm the functional significance of NLS in the structured domain and NES in the IDR of CDC14A. Comparative analyses of CDC14A and CDC14B structures reveal an overall structural conservation of the globular DSPn and DSPc regions. These data also highlight the divergence of the C-terminal regions of CDC14A, which during evolution of higher organisms has become necessary for hearing. Our structural and functional data show that residues 1 to 345 of CDC14A provide sufficient phosphatase activity to sustain male fertility but not normal hearing. We speculate that the IDR of CDC14A expressed within the inner ear has a role in subcellular localization and perhaps in CDC14A substrate specificity. Further studies will be focused on evaluating hearing and inner ear–specific NMD in a p.R345∗ mouse model, on understanding the structural dynamics of CDC14A C-terminal IDR and identifying its in vivo substrates and protein partners necessary for normal hearing.
