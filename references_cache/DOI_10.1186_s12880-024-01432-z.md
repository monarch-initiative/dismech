Abstract
Eating disorders (EDs), including anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), binge-eating disorder (BED), and pica, 
are psychobehavioral conditions characterized by abnormal eating behaviors and an excessive preoccupation with 
weight and body shape. This review examines changes in brain regions and functional connectivity in ED patients 
over the past decade (2013–2023) using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). Key findings 
highlight alterations in brain networks such as the default mode network (DMN), central executive network (CEN), 
and emotion regulation network (ERN). In individuals with AN, there is reduced functional connectivity in areas 
associated with facial information processing and social cognition, alongside increased connectivity in regions 
linked to sensory stimulation, aesthetic judgment, and social anxiety. Conversely, BED patients show diminished 
connectivity in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex within the salience network and increased connectivity in the 
posterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex within the DMN. These findings suggest that rs-fMRI could 
serve as a valuable biomarker for assessing brain function and predicting treatment outcomes in EDs, paving the 
way for personalized therapeutic strategies.

Family and twin studies suggest a genetic basis, with

From a psychological standpoint, individuals with EDs 
often experience heightened levels of anxiety, depres -
sion, and low self-esteem, which can exacerbate their 
symptoms. For example, anxiety may lead to excessive 
preoccupation with food and weight, while depression 
can contribute to episodes of binge eating [ 10]. EDs are 
closely intertwined with cognitive distortions, difficul -
ties in emotional regulation, and maladaptive behavioral 
patterns. Those with AN frequently exhibit extreme per -
fectionism and a strong desire for weight control, traits 
closely associated with pathological concerns regarding 
body image and fear of weight gain [ 11]. Individuals with 
BN and BED commonly display impulsivity and emo -
tional instability, which are linked to binge eating and 
purging behaviors. These psychological issues underscore 
the significant role of emotional dysregulation in the 
pathophysiology of eating disorders. Moreover, research 
indicates that psychological traits may also be corre -
lated with abnormalities in brain function, particularly 
in regions involved in emotional regulation and cognitive 
control [12].
Social factors play a crucial role in the onset and main -
tenance of eating disorders. In contemporary society, 
the idealization of thinness and the pursuit of a perfect 
body image, influenced significantly by media and social 
platforms, greatly amplify individuals’ dissatisfaction 
with their body image and anxiety about weight. These 
societal pressures in cultural environments particularly 
impact adolescents and young adults, making them more 
susceptible to EDs. Sociocultural expectations regard -
ing body image and the stigmatization of overweight 
individuals further exacerbate the risk of developing eat -
ing disorders. Research indicates that social comparison 
and peer pressure are significant risk factors, especially 
among females [ 13]. When faced with pressures regard -
ing body image and appearance from peers and media, 
young women often resort to extreme dietary behaviors 
in pursuit of an ideal body image, which can lead to EDs 
[14].
Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging 
(rs-fMRI) is a non-invasive brain imaging technique that 
measures functional connectivity between brain regions 
during rest, revealing dynamic activity patterns of brain 
networks [15]. This technique has played a crucial role in 
eating disorders research, aiding in the identification of 
specific brain regions and networks implicated in these 
disorders, and understanding how neuronal circuits 
relate to behavior and pathophysiology. However, find -
ings across multiple studies have been inconsistent [ 3–5, 
16], and the specific limitations of neuroimaging studies 
are continually challenged as our understanding of the 
disorder evolves, particularly in studies involving mul -
tiple individuals [ 17]. For instance, studies have found 
increased functional connectivity within the default 
mode network (DMN) and decreased connectivity within 
the salience network (SN) in patients with AN [18]. These 
changes in functional connectivity may be associated 
with abnormal responses to food rewards and excessive 
preoccupation with body image in patients. In BN and 
BED, studies have identified abnormal functional con -
nectivity patterns in reward circuits, including the stria -
tum and orbitofrontal cortex, regions involved in food 
reward processing and impulsive behaviors. These find -
ings suggest that dysfunction in reward networks may 
underlie compulsive thoughts about food and binge eat -
ing behaviors [19].
Neuroimaging techniques are not only pivotal in 
understanding the neurobiological mechanisms of EDs 
but also hold significant potential for clinical applica -
tions. By identifying abnormal brain functional patterns 
in patients with EDs, neuroimaging can provide a basis 
for personalized diagnosis and treatment. For example, 
using rs-fMRI to detect changes in functional connec -
tivity in specific brain regions can help predict patient 
responses to treatment, thereby optimizing therapeutic 
strategies [ 20]. Additionally, neuroimaging techniques 
can serve as biomarkers to assess treatment efficacy and 
patient recovery progress in eating disorder patients. 
Studies indicate that monitoring changes in brain func -
tion before and after treatment can evaluate treatment 
effectiveness and patient recovery [ 21]. These findings 
suggest that neuroimaging has broad prospects in future 
research and treatment of EDs. Building on previous 
studies, this review summarizes research from 2013 to 
2023 on the application of resting-state MRI in eating 
disorders, focusing on AN, BN and BED. The review 
encompasses studies on brain structure, function, con -
nectivity, and metabolism, providing a macroscopic 
understanding of brain regions and potential functional 
connectivity mechanisms involved in EDs’ neuroimaging. 
It aims to serve as a valuable reference for future person -
alized research and treatment approaches.

eating disorders published between 2013 and 2023 from 
databases such as Web of Science, PubMed and Google 
Scholar. The search terms were identified as: ‘resting 
state functional connectivity and eating disorders’ , ‘rest -
ing state functional connectivity and anorexia nervosa’ ,

Inclusion criteria
The original research articles included in this review were 
published in English between January 2013 and Decem -
ber 2023. These articles utilized resting-state magnetic 
resonance imaging methods to investigate changes in 
brain functional imaging related to eating disorders. We 
selectively employed the following imaging techniques: 
resting-state functional MRI (fMRI).
Exclusion criteria
Studies involving healthy participants without eating dis -
orders were not included in the investigation. Addition -
ally, research on eating disorders combined with organic 
diseases or studies employing brain imaging (< 24 h) were 
also excluded. Results that did not report statistically 
significant trends ( p-value > 0.05) or results not adjusted 
for multiple comparisons by the original authors were 
excluded. Studies involving magnetoencephalography 
and electroencephalography were not considered.
Results
Over the past decade, researchers have primarily focused 
on the neuro-psychological characteristics of individuals 
with EDs. Consistent findings in the literature indicate 
that these patients often exhibit poor decision-mak -
ing abilities, high levels of cognitive rigidity, impaired 
set-shifting, and visual-spatial impairments [ 37, 38]. 
In recent years, substantial advancements have been 
achieved in the exploration of EDs through the applica -
tion of rs-fMRI [ 39]. This methodology entails the mea -
surement of blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) 
signals reflecting live brain activity during a resting 
state, allowing for the investigation of brain functional 
dynamics [ 40]. The use of rs-fMRI has unveiled distinc -
tive neural activity variations in individuals with EDs, 
encompassing changes in brain regions and alterations in 
functional connectivity associated with appetite, reward 
systems, cognitive control, and other relevant domains 
[41–44]. The included studies reported 14 articles on 
rs-fMRI research in AN, with a total of 293 subjects; 6 
articles on rs-fMRI research in BN, with 80 subjects; and 
2 articles on rs-fMRI research in BED, with 32 subjects. 
Due to the smaller number of reports on BED, and the 
mechanisms of BED and BN being closely related, this 
review includes subjects with BN and BED together for 
the study. In addition, 395 healthy subjects were included 
as controls. No patients with pica were temporarily 
observed, which may be related to the low prevalence 
rate of this disorder(Table 1).

Brain regions and mechanisms of brain networks.  
Patients with AN exhibit reduced cortical gray matter 
volume, particularly in areas involved in self-perception 
and cognitive control, such as the insula and anterior 
cingulate cortex [ 45]. These structural changes in brain 
regions may contribute to distorted body image percep -
tion and difficulties in emotional regulation. Addition -
ally, research has revealed alterations in the patterns of 
brain functional connectivity among individuals with 
EDs, affecting several critical brain networks including 
the Default Mode Network (DMN), Salience Network 
(SN), and Central Executive Network (CEN). Dysfunc -
tion within these networks may serve as the neural basis 
for characteristic symptoms of AN.
The DMN is one of the most prominent brain net -
works active during rest, comprising regions such as 
the Medial Prefrontal Cortex (mPFC), Posterior Cingu -
late Cortex (PCC), and Posterior cingulate gyrus(PCG). 
The DMN primarily facilitates self-referential thinking 
and introspection, which are crucial for studying self-
awareness and emotional processing abnormalities in 
AN patients [ 46]. Dysfunction in the mPFC may lead to 
distorted self-evaluation and cognitive biases, intensify -
ing pathological concerns about weight and body image 
[47], while the PCC plays a crucial role in self-reflection 
and episodic memory. The SN includes the Anterior 
Insula (AI) and Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC), which 
are pivotal for identifying and processing emotional and 
sensory stimuli [ 48]. The AI is essential for perceiving 
internal bodily states (e.g., hunger and satiety) and regu -
lating emotions. The ACC is involved in emotional regu -
lation and impulse control. The Limbic System involves 
emotion processing and memory formation, primar -
ily including the Amygdala and Hippocampus, with the

Other research indicates decreased functional connec -
tivity in subcortical brain regions among AN patients, 
predominantly involving the amygdala, hypothalamus, 
cingulate gyrus, bilateral caudate nuclei, and posterior 
insula. These changes in connectivity may be related to 
abnormal internal sensations and altered pain processing 
in AN patients [59, 60]. Research has identified weakened 
functional connectivity between the DMN and other 
brain areas in AN, potentially disrupting self-reflection 
and introspection processes. Specifically, reduced con -
nectivity between the mPFC and PCC may contribute to 
negative self-perception and pathological preoccupation 
with body weight [ 61]. Connectivity reductions in areas 
related to salience and emotion regulation, such as the 
anterior insula (AI) and anterior cingulate cortex ACC, 
may contribute to anomalies in emotional regulation and 
internal state perception. These anomalies provide a neu-
ral basis for the disturbed eating behaviors and weight 
management concerns observed in AN [ 62]. Studies by 
Lips MA and others have indicated decreased functional 
connectivity in the AI region of AN patients, leading to 
abnormal perceptions of hunger and satiety, which may 
underlie dysfunctional control over food intake [ 63]. 
Wang JN et al. demonstrated reduced connectivity in the 
ACC of AN patients, potentially contributing to dimin -
ished emotional regulation and exacerbating avoidance 
behaviors towards food and abnormal weight control 
[21]. Hentschel J reported decreased connectivity in the

ventral tegmental area (VTA) and NAcc regions among 
AN patients, potentially leading to diminished percep -

the lack of interest in food observed in AN patients. 
Decreased functional connectivity in the OFC of AN 
patients may result in reduced responses to food rewards, 
thereby intensifying excessive preoccupation with weight 
control and related behaviors [ 68]. Research by Wood -

behaviors when faced with food and weight issues [35]. Li

motor area (SMA) of AN patients, potentially affecting 
behavioral control over food intake and weight man -

resting-state functional connectivity in brain regions 
involved in reward processing and habit learning in AN 
patients, noting weakened connectivity between the ven -
tral tegmental area (VTA) and superior frontal gyrus, 
ventral caudate and orbitofrontal cortex, and dorsal cau -
date and frontal, temporal, and basal ganglia regions, 
with no evidence of enhanced connectivity. Scaife et al. 
conducted studies on 12 patients with restrictive AN 
and 14 patients recovering from the disorder, revealing 
reduced functional connectivity in the lateral visual net -
work during acute AN phases, with persistent changes 
even after weight restoration [ 30]. Additional studies 
have shown lower resting-state functional connectivity 
between the NAcc and superior frontal gyrus(SFG), ven -
tral caudate and frontal-posterior regions of the caudate 
nucleus, and dorsal caudate and frontal, temporal, and 
posterior regions in the AN group [ 69–71]. Moreover, 
research has indicated decreased activation in the ante -
rior ventral striatum and insula during hunger states in 
AN patients [72–75].
Functional Connectivity Increasing.  Further research 
has revealed increased functional connectivity in cer -
tain brain regions among individuals with AN, reflect -
ing abnormalities in specific cognitive and emotional 
processes. These studies highlight heightened connec -
tivity in areas involved in sensory stimulation, aesthetic 
judgment, and anxiety related to social rejection, such as 
the right inferior temporal gyrus, left superior parietal 
lobule, and lateral cortical regions of the occipital lobe 
[76–79]. AN patients also demonstrate enhanced func -
tional connectivity within the executive control network 
(ECN) and sensorimotor network (SMN), which may be 
linked to their excessive focus on weight and abnormal 
control behaviors concerning food. Moreover, increased 
connectivity between the posterior cingulate cortex PCC 
and other regions of the DMN suggests heightened atten-
tion to past negative experiences during introspection, 
thereby exacerbating anxiety related to weight and food. 
The PPC, critical for spatial cognition and self-awareness, 
shows heightened functional connectivity in AN patients, 
potentially leading to distortions in body size and shape 
perception, influencing their body image cognition [ 81]. 
Studies by Davidson TL et al. have identified significantly 
increased functional connectivity in the mPFC of AN

Discussion and conclusions
This review summarized the research over the past 
decade on eating disorders related to various resting-
state brain networks and their plasticity (Fig. 8). Due to 
the existing studies employing different analytical meth -
ods (e.g., seed-based and data-driven, theory-driven, 
whole-brain analysis), often with small sample sizes and 
overlapping reports, and the variation in study designs 
(randomized controlled trials, assessment time points 
before and after weight restoration, open-label) and clini-
cal populations, the degree of convergence in research 
findings remains relatively low. Previous studies have 
detected overall brain volume reductions and regional 
gray matter (GM) volume decreases, as well as white

matter (WM) microstructural changes. The primary 
affected regions include the prefrontal, parietal, and tem -

cerebellum, and various brain tracts such as the corona 
radiata, superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), and for -
nix. In terms of functional imaging, researchers have 
highlighted alterations in large-scale brain networks, 
such as the DMN, ECN, and SN [2, 141–144].
Furthermore, other studies have identified that the 
pathogenesis of eating disorders is also associated with 
altered insular function, reduced fornix integrity in lim -
bic pathways, and repetitive reward pathways involving 
the insula and striatum [ 145, 146]. For instance, changes 
in resting-state functional connectivity may be linked to 
abnormal self-body image perception in patients with 
anorexia nervosa. The functional connectivity of the 
auditory network is also diminished, mainly involving 
the precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, and insula. The 
precentral and postcentral gyri correspond to primary 
motor and somatosensory areas, respectively, indicating 
decreased somatosensory motor responses in patients 
with anorexia nervosa. Altered insular connectivity may 
be associated with abnormalities in appetite regulation, 
taste information processing, and pain sensitivity in these 
patients. Patients with bulimia nervosa and binge eating 
disorder exhibit hyperactive reward systems. Compared 
to healthy controls, patients with bulimia nervosa show 
higher activity in the brain’s reward centers, such as the 
ventral striatum, during the resting state [ 147–152]. 
There is evidence of disrupted connectivity in emotion 
regulation centers, such as the anterior cingulate cor -
tex and other brain regions related to emotional regula -
tion, during the resting state in bulimia nervosa patients 
[153–155]. Reduced functionality in brain regions asso -
ciated with decision-making and self-control has been 
linked to repetitive binge-eating behaviors [ 156–158]. In 
most individuals, brain function appears to reverse after 
weight restoration. However, current research continues 
to provide crucial insights and future directions regard -
ing the impact of eating disorders on functional brain cir-
cuits and clinical outcomes.

The application of rs-fMRI imaging technology has not 
only helped reveal the abnormal functional connectivity 
in the brains of patients with EDs but also opened new 
possibilities for diagnosis, intervention, and treatment. 
By analyzing the resting-state functional connectivity of 
patients with eating disorders, specific abnormal patterns 
in brain regions and networks can be identified, provid -
ing objective evidence for diagnosis and assessment. For 
example, combining the functional connectivity pat -
terns of the DMN and SN can more accurately evaluate 
patients’ abnormal cognition and emotional responses 
related to weight and food. As treatment progresses, the 
functional connectivity in the brains of eating disorder 
patients may change. Rs-fMRI technology can be used 
to monitor treatment response and evaluate changes at 
the brain function level. For instance, whether the func -
tional connectivity of the DMN and ECN returns to nor -
mal post-treatment can serve as an important indicator 
of treatment effectiveness. Based on the resting-state 
functional connectivity patterns of different patients, 
personalized treatment strategies can be developed. For 
example, neurofeedback training or neuromodulation 
techniques targeting specific functional connectivity 
abnormalities may positively affect the restoration of nor-
mal eating behaviors and weight management. Despite 
the progress made in the field of resting-state MRI stud -
ies of patients with eating disorders, research on their 
resting-state functional connectivity abnormalities still 
faces several challenges and unresolved questions.
Future research could be expanded in the following 
areas. Multimodal Imaging Studies: Integrate resting-
state fMRI with structural MRI, electroencephalography 
(EEG), and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) to 
further explore the complex relationships between brain 
structure and function in patients with BN and BED. 
Longitudinal Studies: Conduct long-term follow-up stud-
ies to monitor changes in brain functional connectivity 
in BN and BED patients post-treatment, and investigate 
the relationship between these changes and the long-
term course of the disorder, as well as the risk of relapse.
