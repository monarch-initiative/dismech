# The p.(Leu97Ile) variant expands the genetic landscape of NEFL-associated Charcot-Marie-tooth neuropathies.
**Authors:** Oeztuerk M, Walli S, Muhmann D, Choueiri C, Dobelmann V, Abicht A, Leube B, Schara-Schmidt U, Meuth SG, Horvath R, Lochmueller H, Roos A, Ruck T
**Journal:** Hum Mol Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/hmg/ddag028](https://doi.org/10.1093/hmg/ddag028)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Charcot Marie Tooth neuropathies arise from diverse genetic disturbances that impair axonal structure or myelin integrity. Variants in NEFL, encoding the light chain of neurofilaments, represent a rare cause of CMT and may disrupt filament assembly, axonal transport, and cytoskeletal stability. Because NEFL variants are uncommon and phenotypes variable, the pathogenic relevance of individual substitutions is often difficult to determine. Here, we identified a previously unreported missense variant, p.(Leu97Ile), in three affected individuals. In two, a second variant, p.(Arg206Ser), was present in cis. We therefore aimed to investigate the pathogenic impact of this allele constellation using clinical, biochemical, and structural approaches.

METHODS: Neurological examination, CMTNS scoring, and electrophysiological studies were performed. Exome sequencing established the genotype. Variant effects were assessed using serum biomarkers, in silico modelling and quantitative immunofluorescence of patient-derived fibroblasts.

RESULTS: Patients showed differing clinical presentations, including moderate adult-onset neuropathy to severe early-onset disease and neuromuscular abnormalities in childhood. Neurophysiology revealed mixed features, and conduction slowing in the child supported classification within the CMT1F spectrum. All carriers exhibited elevated serum NfL; CK was increased in the adult patients. Descriptive biomarker differences between the patients were observed for GDF15, FGF21, Periaxin, and NCAM1. Fibroblasts displayed increased cytoplasmic NfL without altered nuclear metrics. Structural modelling predicted destabilizing effects of both substitutions, with the cis combination producing the strongest deviation.

DISCUSSION: Integrated clinical, biochemical, and structural evidence support pathogenicity of p.(Leu97Ile) and suggest that p.(Arg206Ser) in cis augments its biological impact. These findings expand the genetic and phenotypic spectrum of NEFL-associated CMT1F.

Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT) comprises a group of hereditary disorders of the peripheral nervous system, marked by progressive muscle weakness and atrophy in the distal limbs, sensory impairment, and diminished or absent deep tendon reflexes [1]. Although CMT often manifests in the first or second decade of life, the age of onset and severity can vary significantly, ranging from congenital presentations to milder forms manifesting in adulthood [2, 3]. Despite its prevalence of approximately 1 in 2500 individuals worldwide, CMT presents with remarkable clinical variability, reflecting its underlying genetic complexity [2]. CMT is stratified into predominantly demyelinating (CMT1), predominantly axonal (CMT2), and intermediate subtypes, based on genetic mode of transmission and clinical presentations along with electrophysiological features [2]. Advances in genetic research have led to the identification of over 100 genes associated with CMT [4], although the majority of cases are linked to mutations in a small subset, including PMP22, GJB1, MFN2, and MPZ [5–8]. However, other genes such as NEFL are rather rarely implicated, and their clinical and molecular contributions remain less comprehensively explored [9].

The NEFL gene (located on chromosome 8) encodes the light chain of neurofilaments (NfL), one of five intermediate filament proteins that form the neurofilament network alongside the heavy (NfH) and medium (NfM) chains, α-internexin, and peripherin [10]. These filaments stabilize axons, regulate axonal diameter, and facilitate efficient conduction of electrical impulses [11]. Neurofilaments also play a central role in axonal transport, ensuring the proper delivery of organelles and proteins throughout axons [12, 13]. Dysfunction of the neurofilament network, as seen in the context of pathogenic NEFL variants, has been associated with disruptions in neuronal structure and function, including toxic neurofilament accumulation [14]. However, the consequences of such disruptions extend beyond peripheral neuropathies. Aberrant neurofilament dynamics are implicated in a range of neurological disorders, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and spinal muscular atrophy (5q-SMA), highlighting the indispensable role of neurofilaments in preserving neuronal health across both the central and peripheral nervous systems [15, 16].

Pathogenic NEFL variants were first identified in 2000 as a cause of autosomal dominant CMT [17]. Since then, various NEFL variants have been associated with a spectrum of neurological phenotypes, including demyelinating (CMT1F), axonal (CMT2E), dominant intermediate and autosomal recessive forms of the disease [18–22]. Overall, these pathogenic NEFL variants are rare, accounting for less than 1% of CMT cases as deciphered in large cohorts [23, 24]. To date, more than 30 mutations in the NEFL gene have been identified, spanning all domains of the protein, including the globular N-terminal head, α-helical central rod and C-terminal tail [25]. Notably, genotype–phenotype correlations indicate that pathogenic variants in any of these regions result in similar clinical outcomes, highlighting the equal importance of each region for neurofilament function [9, 25]. Most NEFL variants linked to CMT are amino acid substitutions in terms of dominant missense variants acting through gain-of-function mechanisms and disrupting neurofilament assembly and thus perturbing proper axonal transport [26]. Frameshift and in-frame deletion mutations, though rare, have also been reported and exhibit distinct inheritance patterns [25]. Autosomal recessive nonsense mutations are particularly rare, with only a few reported, and are typically linked to severe, early-onset forms of the disease caused by a complete loss of functional protein [19, 22, 27].


NEFL-related CMT demonstrates remarkable clinical heterogeneity, with variability in disease onset, severity, and additional features such as proximal weakness, ataxia, and hearing loss [28]. Mosaicism leading to incomplete penetrance has also been described in addition to familiar clinical and morphological variability of certain dominant variants [29, 30]. Beyond neuropathy, NEFL mutations have also been linked to broader neurological manifestations, including developmental delay and muscle involvement, evidenced by elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels and structural abnormalities in muscle biopsies [24, 30, 31]. Additionally, skeletal deformities such as scoliosis further expand the phenotypic spectrum of NEFL-related disease, thereby broadening the clinical spectrum [32].

We here report three individuals from two unrelated families carrying the previously unreported p.(Leu97Ile) missense variant in the NEFL gene. In one of these families, a father and his daughter presented with a second variant, p.(Arg206Ser), affecting the same allele (cis variant). Clinical findings included moderate to severe neuropathy in adulthood as well as early subclinical manifestations in childhood. To investigate the pathogenicity of this novel variant, we employed clinical assessments (applying the Charcot–Marie-Tooth Neuropathy Score (CMTNS; [33]), electrophysiological and biomarker studies, in silico predictions and cell biological investigations.

Patients included in this study were clinically evaluated at the Department of Neurology of University Hospital Düsseldorf and at the Department of Pediatric Neurology of University Medicine Essen. Written informed consent was obtained for clinical documentation, genetic analyses and the collection as well as publication of photographs. Clinical characterization was performed using CMTNS [33]. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of University Duisburg-Essen (24–12 197-BO).

Neurophysiological evaluations comprised conduction testing of peripheral nerves in both upper and lower extremities, along with electromyographic analysis using concentric fine needle electrodes.

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using standard laboratory procedures. Next Generation Sequencing (NGS)-based whole-exome analysis was performed using Sequencing-by-Synthesis technology (Illumina). For this, an NGS library was prepared from the patient’s genomic DNA to target all coding genomic regions (exome), including adjacent exon-intron boundaries (+/− 11 nucleotides), as well as known clinically relevant non-coding pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants. Hybridization-based capture was conducted using the Twist Exome 2.0 Plus Comprehensive Exome Spike-in and the Twist Library Preparation Kit (V2) (Twist Bioscience). Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NextSeq 1000 platform as 2x150 bp paired-end reads.

Due to the absence of an experimentally-confirmed three-dimensional structure for the NfL protein, protein structures for wild-type NfL (UniProt ID: P07196) and the p.(Leu97Ile) and p.(Arg206Ser) NfL variants were predicted alongside a per-residue confidence score between 1–100 (pLDDT) using ColabFold version 1.5.3 within the AlphaFold2_MMseqs2 Jupyter notebook [34, 35]. Predicted protein structures for p.(Leu97Ile) and p.(Arg206Ser) were then individually superimposed onto the predicted wild-type NfL protein structure using the ‘super’ command in PyMOL, which also generates a root-mean-square deviation (rmsd) score indicating the degree of structural similarity between superimposed structures [36]. Changes in non-covalent interactions between amino acid residues as a result of p.(Leu97Ile) and p.(Arg206Ser) variants were identified using the web server PremPS, which used the predicted protein structures for wild-type NfL as well as p.(Leu97Ile) and p.(Arg206Ser) to estimate unfolding free energy change (ΔΔG) for each variant [37]. ΔΔG (kcal/mol) is a measure of protein stability change, either negative for stabilizing mutations or positive for destabilizing mutations, associated with each NfL variant [37]. Predicted pathogenicity scores for each NfL missense variant were retrieved by clicking on the respective amino acid change in the ‘Variant viewer’ tab of the NfL UniProt page. This displays an ‘AlphaMiss. pred.’ score representing the pathogenicity score between 0–1 generated by AlphaMissense for each NfL variant of interest, with scores closer to 1 indicating higher likelihood of pathogenicity [38].

Neuropathy markers in patient serum, including NCAM1, GDF15, FGF21 and Periaxin, were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The following commercial kits were employed: NCAM1 (Cat. DY2408, DY008B, R&D Systems), GDF15 (Cat. DGD150, R&D Systems), FGF21 (Cat. RD191108200R, BioVendor), Periaxin (Cat. HUEB1386, AssayGenie). The analytical procedure was carried out according to the instructions provided in the protocol of the respective manufacturer. Significance was tested using t-test. Results were analyzed according to the specifications provided by the respective kits. Serum CK levels were also measured to evaluate potential muscle involvement. Additionally, serum NfL concentrations were determined using a microfluidic ELISA platform, as described previously [39].

Fibroblasts are known to express a diversity of proteins of neuromuscular relevance including NfL [40] and were thus used for further functional studies focusing on the impact of the identified variants on protein abundance and distribution. Precisely, immunofluorescence staining of NfL protein was performed on primary human fibroblasts (PHFC) cultured from skin biopsies of two adult NEFL-patients (Patients 1 & 2; see below) and one healthy donor (serving as non-disease control fibroblast cell line; NDC). Cells were cultured using High Glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, at 37°C and 5% CO2. PHFC were counted and seeded at a density of 5 x 104 cells per coverslip (10 mm in diameter) in a 24-well plate. One day post seeding, medium was changed. After reaching sufficient confluency on the coverslips, the medium was discarded and cells were washed with DPBS, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 minutes and another washing step with DPBS. Until staining, the coverslips were kept at 4°C. Firstly, a blocking solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.2% Triton X-100 for permeabilization was applied for 30 minutes, followed by the incubation of the primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA for one hour at room temperature. The coverslips were washed two times with DPBS, incubated for one hour at room temperature with the secondary antibody in 1% BSA, then washed in DPBS and ultrapure water again. Finally, the coverslips were mounted on microscope slides, covered with cover glasses using Fluoroshield™ with DAPI (Sigma, #F6057) and stored at 4°C.

The following antibodies were used for the staining procedures: Anti-68 kDa Neurofilament/NF-L antibody [DA2] (Mouse, 1:100, Abcam) and Phalloidin (FITC-labelled, 1:750, Sigma-Aldrich). The secondary antibody used was anti-mouse Cy3 (goat, 1:500, Sigma-Aldrich).

Imaging was conducted using the Keyence BZ-X810 fluorescence microscope (40x objective), acquiring 10 images per sample. Images were further analyzed at the Advanced Light Microscopy Core Facility (Ad-Light), Medical Faculty of the Heinrich-Heine-University, Düsseldorf. All images were analyzed on a HP Z8 Fury GS Workstation using Zen 2 (blue edition, version 3.11.105.05000) microscopy software (Carl Zeiss, Jena). Images were segmented using machine learning (Intellesis, ZEISS). Two separate segmentation models were established, one allowing for the precise identification of nuclei and one for total NfL protein content in the cells. To enhance precision, training was performed in ‘Single Channel’ mode. Each picture was analyzed for object count and total fluorescence area. Subsequent statistics used data on the sum of NfL protein per image, counts of nuclei and sum of nuclear area. Statistical comparison of total NfL-positive area between groups was performed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests. Significance thresholds were defined as follows: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.001 (***).

A 50-year old female presented to our neuromuscular outpatient clinic with a history of progressive sensory and motor symptoms. Development during infancy and childhood was reportedly normal, with no history of delayed motor milestones or neuromuscular symptoms prior to adulthood. Initial complaints included tingling sensations in the soles of the feet, significant gait instability, and a tendency to fall. These symptoms, first noticed at age 32, have progressively worsened, eventually leading to severe limitations in mobility. The patient reported marked difficulty walking on tiptoes and, over the past four years, an inability to continue her previous engagement in competitive dance. She also described nocturnal cramps, intermittent paresthesias affecting her entire body and stocking-like hypesthesia of the legs.

A detailed neurological examination at the age of 50 revealed stocking-like sensory loss in the legs up to the knees, severe foot drop bilaterally graded as 3−/5 on the Medical Research Counsil (MRC) scale, and toe extensor weakness graded as 4/5. Motor function in the upper limbs was unremarkable. Reflexes in the lower limbs, specifically the Achilles tendon and patellar reflexes, were absent, while those in the upper limbs, including the biceps (BSR), triceps (TSR), and radial (RPR) reflexes, were diminished but present. Pallesthesia testing showed significant vibratory deficits, with malleolar vibratory sense completely absent on the right (0/8) and reduced on the left (2/8). Similarly, vibratory sense was diminished at the patella level (right: 0/8; left: 3/8) and at the wrist (left: 6/8; right: 7/8). Further examination revealed marked atrophy of the lower legs and thenar musculature (see. Figure 1, 1A-E). The patient exhibited a waddling, unstable gait, though she remained ambulatory without assistive devices. Coordination was intact as evidenced by normal finger-nose and heel-shin testing.

Patient 2, a 40-year-old male, presented with a lifelong history of progressive motor and coordination difficulties, accompanied by atrophy of small muscles in the hands and feet. Gait instability had been present since childhood, with the patient reporting an inability to run and progressively increased difficulty walking, though he remained ambulatory without assistive devices. The patient also had bilateral hearing impairment but did not use hearing aids. Sensory symptoms were reported as numbness in the toes.

The patient was examined aged 40. Muscle weakness was prominent in the lower limbs, with the left quadriceps and foot dorsiflexors graded as 3/5 (MRC), plantar flexors graded as 4/5, and the right quadriceps and dorsiflexors graded as 4/5. Plantar flexion strength on the right was also graded as 4/5. On the left side, scores were 3/8 at the malleolus, 4/8 at the patella, and 4/8 at the wrist.

The patient’s medical history included restless legs syndrome, varicose veins, and a prior appendectomy. A brain MRI performed approximately five years earlier was unremarkable.

The patient provided limited details about his family history due to minimal contact with his father and paternal relatives. His father, whom he met only twice, was described as having a normal gait. Two paternal half-siblings were reportedly unaffected. On the maternal side, his mother died at age 61 from lung cancer, and his maternal grandmother passed away in her 70s from unknown causes. No information was available regarding his maternal grandfather. The patient has a healthy five-year-old daughter with no apparent neurological symptoms.

Electrophysiological studies revealed a sensorimotor polyneuropathy with features of both axonal and demyelinating involvement (Table 1). Motor nerve conduction studies demonstrated reduced NCVs in the tibial nerve on the right (37.5 m/s) and in the ulnar nerve (34.4 m/s on the right). Distal motor amplitudes were significantly reduced in the tibial nerve (1.31 mV) and the ulnar nerve (3.96 mV). Sensory nerve conduction studies showed no recordable responses in the sural, peroneal, and ulnar nerves bilaterally. F-wave responses were inconsistent and not reliably reproducible.

Electromyographic evaluation revealed chronic neurogenic changes predominantly affecting distal muscles. Active denervation with positive sharp waves (PSWs) and fibrillation potentials was observed in the tibialis anterior, alongside large polyphasic motor unit potentials (PMEs) with amplitudes up to 10 mV. Similar high-amplitude PMEs were recorded in the rectus femoris, interosseous dorsalis I, and deltoid muscles, with dense interference patterns but no active denervation.

For Patient 2, the CMTNS was 22, consistent with severe neuropathy [33]. The high score underscores substantial motor dysfunction, particularly in the distal muscles of the lower and upper limbs, alongside sensory impairments and marked reductions in reflexes. These findings, combined with the patient’s significant gait instability and steppage gait, reflect the advanced stage of his neuropathy and its profound impact on daily functioning.

Patient 3, the five-year-old daughter of Patient 2, was evaluated following the identification of NEFL variants in her father. Although no overt clinical abnormalities such as muscle atrophy or foot deformities were noted on physical examination (see Fig. 1, 3A-E), the parents reported a history of frequent stumbling and falls, particularly when running, and described her as generally unsteady on her feet. She is unable to ride a scooter without tipping over and often trips while walking. Hence, these symptoms, initially dismissed as benign clumsiness, were retrospectively recognized as potentially pathological in light of her father’s confirmed hereditary neuropathy. No sensory complaints were reported by the girl and her parents, and fine motor development was within normal limits for age. Gross motor performance, however, raised concern due to recurrent episodes of unsteadiness and imbalance. At the time of evaluation, the patient remained ambulatory and exhibited no need for assistive devices.

The CMTNS was formally calculated as 0, reflecting the absence of subjective symptoms or clinically detectable motor or sensory deficits at the time of examination. Muscle strength, pinprick sensibility, and vibration perception were within normal limits. Ulnar compound muscle action potential (CMAP) was preserved (>6 mV). The sensory nerve action potential (SAP) of the ulnar nerve could not be reliably assessed due to compliance limitations during the examination. Despite the formal score of 0, electrophysiological studies demonstrated clear evidence of sensorimotor neuropathy.

Genetic testing of Patient 1 was performed in August 2024 as part of a broader diagnostic workup for suspected hereditary neuropathy and exome sequencing identified a heterozygous variant in the NEFL gene (c.289C > A, p.(Leu97Ile), rs587777888). This variant, located in exon 1, has not been previously described in the literature and is classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS).

Genetic testing of patient 2 was conducted in February 2024 and revealed two variants affecting the NEFL gene: c.616C > A, p.(Arg206Ser) and c.289C > A, p.(Leu97Ile). Same variants were identified in May 2025 by exome sequencing in Patient 3, the daughter of Patient 2, confirming paternal inheritance and showing that both variants are present on the same allele (in cis). Both variants are not listed in the gnomAD population data base, supporting their rarity.

To address the pathogenicity of the identified NfL amino acid substitutions, three-dimensional protein structures for wild-type, p.(Leu97Ile) and p.(Arg206Ser) were predicted with pLDDT scores of 72–73.1 (Fig. 2A). The superimposition between wild-type and p.(Leu97Ile) NfL generated a root mean square deviation (rmsd) score of 5.210 and the superimposition between wild-type and p.(Arg206Ser) NfL generated a rmsd score of 4.661, indicating that both variants are predicted to share weak structural similarity with wild-type NfL. When both variants were superimposed together against the wild-type, the rmsd increased to 7.414, further underscoring the marked structural deviation from the native conformation (Fig. 2B) [36, 41]. PremPS analysis of non-covalent interactions between amino acid residues in the p.(Leu97Ile) and p.(Arg206Ser) NfL variants revealed that the p.(Leu97Ile) is a destabilizing mutation (ΔΔG = 0.38 kcal/mol) which results in the loss of 5 hydrophobic interactions between Leu97 and Phe101 and the creation of a new hydrophobic interaction between Ile97 and Gln93 (Fig. 2C, Table 2). p.(Arg206Ser) is also a destabilizing mutation (ΔΔG = 0.55 kcal/mol) resulting in the loss of two polar interactions and one ionic interaction between Arg206 and Glu210 as well as the formation of a new polar interaction between Ser206 and Ala203 (Fig. 2C, Table 2). Additionally, p.(Leu97Ile) is associated with an AlphaMissense pathogenicity score of 0.98 and p.(Arg206Ser) is associated with an AlphaMissense pathogenicity score of 0.9, showing that both mutations are predicted to be likely pathogenic (Table 2).

According to the ΔΔG > 0 kcal/Mol associated with each mutation, these variants are predicted to be destabilizing. Both variants have AlphaMissense pathogenicity scores ≥0.90, meaning they are predicted to be likely pathogenic.

To further evaluate the pathogenicity of the identified variants in the context of the clinical presentation of NEFL-associated CMT, CK (known to be increased in NEFL patients [24]), NfL, GDF15, FGF21, Periaxin, and NCAM1 were studied [42, 43]. Detailed serum concentrations of all biomarkers for each patient are provided in Table 3. Given the limited sample size, no statistically robust conclusions can be drawn. However, descriptive comparisons revealed distinct individual biomarker profiles. The lowest concentrations across all markers were found in Patient 3, the pediatric individual with only mild clinical manifestations, which may indicate an early disease stage. In contrast, higher serum concentrations of FGF21 and NCAM1 were observed in Patient 2, who carries both NEFL variants in cis, whereas GDF15 and Periaxin were comparatively elevated in Patient 1, who carries the p.(Leu97Ile) variant alone (Fig. 3, Table 3). Mild increases in GDF15 and NCAM1 were detectable in both adult patients. For Periaxin, an elevation was noted only in Patient 1. FGF21 levels were below control values in Patient 1 but above those in controls for Patient 2, suggesting that FGF21 may warrant further investigation as a potential indicator of disease progression in NEFL-related neuropathy. To evaluate the intracellular distribution and abundance of NfL, immunofluorescence staining was performed on PHFC derived from Patient 1 and Patient 2 and one NDC. As shown in Fig. 4A, both PHFC exhibited an increased NfL-positive area per image compared to NDC. Quantification across ten microscopic fields per sample revealed mean total NfL-positive areas of 282 337 ± 90 898 pixel2 for NDC, 437217 ± 84 309 pixel2 for Patient 1, and 360 419 ± 83 265 pixel2 for Patient 2. While formal statistical testing is limited by the small number of biological replicates (n = 2 patients), a consistent increase in NfL accumulation was observed across all analyzed fields in both patient-derived cell lines relative to control. Nuclear counts (Fig. 4B) and summed nuclear area per image (Fig. 4C) were similar across all groups. The mean nuclear count was 16.4 (NDC), 15.5 (Patient 1), and 15.3 (Patient 2), while the corresponding nuclear areas were 99 430 pixel2, 111 208 pixel2, and 104 979 pixel2. The clinical presentation of CMT associated with pathogenic NEFL variants is characterized by notable heterogeneity in onset, progression, and severity. In this study, we report three affected individuals: Patient 1 carried a heterozygous p.(Leu97Ile) variant, while Patient 2 and his daughter, Patient 3, harbored two missense variants, p.(Leu97Ile) and p.(Arg206Ser), in cis. The observed phenotypes included a moderate, adult-onset sensorimotor neuropathy (Patient 1), a severe, early-onset, functionally disabling polyneuropathy (Patient 2), and a mild pediatric-onset phenotype with electrophysiological abnormalities in a five-year-old child (Patient 3). The phenotypic variability observed in Patients 2 and 3 may be attributable to the presence of a second amino acid substitution, which could further destabilize the structure of the NfL protein. Additionally, the mild pediatric manifestation in Patient 3 suggests that the combination of two missense variants in cis may have a more pathogenic effect than the presence of the p.(Leu97Ile) variant alone. Along this line, Patient 2, carrying both variants, exhibited the most profound clinical impairment with a CMTNS of 22, whereas his daughter (Patient 3), with the same genetic constellation, is already showing early motor symptoms and generalized demyelinating changes on nerve conduction studies. In contrast, Patient 1 demonstrated a milder phenotype (CMTNS of 14) with clear functional limitations and lower limb involvement. Hence, these combined genetic and clinical observations may point towards synergistic effects of the two NEFL variants observed in Patient 2 and 3, a hypothesis further explored through in silico structural analyses in this study (see below).

Interestingly, all three individuals demonstrated elevated serum NfL concentrations, with values of 43 pg/mL in Patient 1, 74 pg/mL in Patient 2, and 49 pg/mL in the mildly affected child (Patient 3). In the two adult patients, these increases were accompanied by elevated CK levels, suggesting not only axonal degeneration but also possible neuromuscular involvement in terms of denervation causing muscle cell vulnerabilities. In this context, the concordance of clinical symptoms, altered serum NfL/CK levels and electrophysiological evidence points toward a pathogenic role of these NEFL variants. Studies of GDF15, FGF21, Periaxin, and NCAM1 suggest that NCAM1 could serve as a minimally invasive biomarker reflecting disease activity in NEFL-related neuropathy. Notably, Patient 2, who carries two missense variants in cis and presents with a more severe phenotype, also exhibited more elevated NCAM1 levels compared to Patient 1.

Building on the cellular phenotype of intracellular NfL accumulation, the in silico structural predictions provide molecular evidence for destabilizing effects of both NEFL missense variants identified in this study. The p.(Leu97Ile) and p.(Arg206Ser) amino acid substitutions each independently induced measurable structural deviations from the wild-type protein, with rmsd values of 5.210 Å and 4.661 Å, respectively, and positive ΔΔG scores (0.38 kcal/mol for Leu97Ile and 0.55 kcal/mol for Arg206Ser), indicating reduced thermodynamic stability. Both variants also achieved high AlphaMissense pathogenicity scores (0.98 and 0.90, respectively), supporting their classification as likely pathogenic. The structural consequences of each variant were further supported by specific alterations in non-covalent intramolecular interactions. For p.(Leu97Ile), five hydrophobic contacts with Phe101 were lost, and a novel hydrophobic interaction with Gln93 was introduced, possibly affecting the alignment of the coiled-coil domain. The p.(Arg206Ser) variant resulted in the loss of two polar and one ionic interaction with Glu210, along with the formation of a new, potentially weaker polar contact with Ala203, alteration that may destabilize local folding in the rod domain.

When modelling both amino acid changes together in cis, the double-mutant allele constellation exhibited an even greater rmsd of 7.414 Å, the second-highest among all NEFL variants previously analyzed within the AlphaFold structure space (WT NfL vs. Pro8Leu NfL: 7.959 Å) [29]. This structural deviation exceeds the threshold typically associated with benign polymorphisms and suggests a compounded destabilizing effect on protein conformation. Importantly, these findings align with both cellular and clinical phenotypes. The magnitude of structural perturbation observed in the cis-variant model parallels the pronounced sensorimotor phenotype in Patient 2, while Patient 3, harbouring the same variant constellation, exhibited subclinical abnormalities, suggesting that such structural changes may exert biological effects even in the absence of overt symptoms.

Consequently, these in silico findings offer compelling molecular support for the pathogenicity of the p.(Leu97Ile) and p.(Arg206Ser) variants and strengthen the hypothesis of a synergistic mechanism when both variants are inherited together.

Immunocytochemical analysis revealed a pronounced cytoplasmic increase of NfL in PHFC, consistent with disruptions in intermediate filament dynamics. While neurofilaments are primarily neuron-enriched, low-level expression of NfL has been documented in non-neuronal cells, including fibroblasts, making them a useful in vitro model for evaluating cytoskeletal integrity [45]. The observed increase in NfL signal occurred independently of changes in nuclear count or area, indicating that the identified cytoplasmic increase is not secondary to altered cell density or size but reflects a cell-intrinsic change in filament regulation. This finding aligns with previous reports showing that pathogenic NEFL variants may induce aberrant neurofilament assembly and impaired axonal transport [26, 46]. The cellular alterations were paralleled by elevated serum NfL concentrations in both individuals, suggesting a potential mechanistic link between cytoskeletal dysregulation at the cellular level and neuroaxonal damage reflected in biofluid markers. Our data suggest that these pathological features may extend beyond neurons, possibly reflecting systemic cytoskeletal vulnerability associated with NEFL variants. These observations underscore the potential utility of patient fibroblasts as a model system to investigate the cellular consequences of NEFL dysfunction. They also support the hypothesis that NfL dysregulation is an early and measurable manifestation of variant-related pathogenicity, even outside the nervous system.

This study presents converging clinical, biochemical, cellular, and in silico evidence supporting the pathogenicity of a novel NEFL variant, p.(Leu97Ile), with a very likely additive effect of a second cis variant, p.(Arg206Ser). The clinical findings observed across the three individuals, including moderate adult-onset neuropathy and subclinical manifestations in early childhood, were mirrored by consistent elevations in serum NfL and intracellular NfL increase in fibroblasts, as well as by structural destabilization in protein models.

Neurophysiological data in the adult patients revealed both axonal and demyelinating features, complicating precise subtyping. In the pediatric case, the latency prolongation with preserved amplitudes is more consistent with a predominantly demyelinating process, suggesting that the phenotypic consequences of the two missense variants in cis primarily involve demyelination, with axonal perturbation likely arising as a secondary effect. Taken together, these findings align with a classification of the variant within the spectrum of NEFL-associated CMT1F, while acknowledging that mixed electrophysiological signatures may emerge with disease progression. Our biomarker studies unveiled NCAM1 as a promising minimally invasive marker mirroring disease activity in NEFL-related neuropathy. Moreover, elevated serum NfL levels across all three individuals underscore its potential role as a supportive biomarker in the pathogenicity assessment of NEFL variants of unknown significance. The data highlight the variable expressivity and evolving nature of NEFL-related neuropathies, reinforcing the utility of a multimodal approach to variant interpretation and classification.
