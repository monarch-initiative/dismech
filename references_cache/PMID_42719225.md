# Thrombosis in individuals with high oxygen affinity hemoglobin-associated erythrocytosis: A systematic review and analysis of a national cohort.
**Authors:** Laloi M, Maaziz N, Bobée V, Joly P, Giansily-Blaizot M, Carpentier M, Gourin MP, Trouillier S, Barro C, Dumontet C, Santana C, Wemeau M, Hunault M, Luque-Paz D, Pissard S, Galactéros F, Girodon F, Aguilar-Martinez P, Garcon L, Orvain C
**Journal:** Hemasphere (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/hem3.70469](https://doi.org/10.1002/hem3.70469)

## Content

High‐oxygen‐affinity hemoglobins (HOAH) are rare conditions, yet one of the most prevalent causes of constitutional erythrocytosis (CE). 1 In some individuals, the impairment of oxygen delivery to tissues leads to secondary erythrocytosis, 2 although most are asymptomatic. 3 Nonetheless, thrombosis and hyperviscosity‐related symptoms can occur with an unknown incidence. As most data derive from individual and small case series, there is no consensus on optimal therapeutic management, and it is unclear whether antithrombotic prophylaxis and phlebotomies to reduce hematocrit can be useful, as it has been shown in patients with polycythemia vera (PV). 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 To better understand variables associated with thrombosis and hyperviscosity‐related symptoms in individuals with HOAH, we conducted a systematic review of the literature and analyzed a retrospective multicentric cohort as validation.

To conduct this review, we led a systematic search of the PubMed database with terms “hemoglobin,” “variant,” “high affinity,” “oxygen,” and “erythrocytosis.” A PubMed search with all HOAH variants described in the HbVar database was also conducted. Two reviewers (M.L. and C.O.) independently screened all identified studies. The second part of the study focused on individuals with HOAH variants at 16 French institutions. All individuals were informed, and non‐opposition to data collection was obtained. The study was approved by an institutional Research Ethics board (Angers University Hospital, #2022‐146). Data collection and statistical analyses are described in Supplementary Methods.

The systematic search identified 780 publications, of which 552 were assessed for eligibility after duplicate removal. After review, 319 publications not fulfilling inclusion criteria were excluded (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). For this analysis, we ultimately included 233 publications reaching all our inclusion criteria (Supporting Information S1: Table S1), reporting on a total of 665 individuals. Patient characteristics are detailed in Supporting Information S1: Table S2. At diagnosis, 47 thrombotic events were observed in 37 patients (6.8%) with available clinical data (N = 548), including 28 venous and 19 arterial thrombotic events (Supporting Information S1: Table S3). Patients with thrombosis were older (53 vs. 32 years, P < 0.001) and more likely to have hereditary thrombophilia (3% vs. 1%, P < 0.001), arterial hypertension (24% vs. 7%, P = 0.009), higher hemoglobin levels (18.3 vs. 17.2 g/dL, P = 0.003), higher hematocrit (56% vs. 52%, P < 0.001), and lower serum erythropoietin levels (25 vs. 11 mU/mL, P = 0.012; Table 1; Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). After multivariable logistic regression, only age (odds ratio [OR] = 15.23 [95% confidence interval: 2.34, 308.10], P = 0.02) was associated with thrombosis (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3). When analyzing arterial and venous events separately, age (OR = 11.81 [2.60, 114.75], P = 0.008) and smoking (OR = 15.25 [1.57, 329.57], P = 0.037) were independently associated with arterial thromboses while age (OR = 2.64 [1.45, 5.11], P = 0.002), hereditary thrombophilia (OR = 61.06 [12.04, 370.63], P < 0.001), and hematocrit (OR = 1.78 [1.00, 3.13], P = 0.044) were independently associated with venous thromboses, including the two patients with superficial venous thrombosis (Supporting Information S1: Tables S4 and S5). Symptoms related to high blood viscosity were reported in 90 of 535 patients (17%) with available data (Supporting Information S1: Figure S4). Patients with hyperviscosity‐related symptoms had higher hemoglobin levels (18.9 vs. 17.0 g/dL, P < 0.001) and hematocrit (56% vs. 51%, P < 0.001) and were more likely smokers (31% vs. 17%, P = 0.042; Supporting Information S1: Table S6). In multivariable logistic regression analyses, hematocrit was the only variable independently associated with hyperviscosity‐related symptoms (OR = 2.07 [1.02, 4.44], P = 0.04).

Data on antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy initiated after diagnosis were available for 174 and 179 patients, respectively. Among them, 45 (26%) were given antiplatelet and 25 (14%) anticoagulation therapy, with five patients receiving both. While most patients received antiplatelet drug as primary prophylaxis (36/45 patients [80%]; seven [16%] for prior arterial thrombosis, and two [4%] after anticoagulation therapy for venous thrombosis), patients receiving anticoagulation had previous venous thrombosis (19/25 patients [76%]; three [12%] for atrial fibrillation, one [4%] post‐operative, one [4%] for renal arterial thrombosis, and unknown for one patient). Phlebotomies were initiated in 129 (28%) patients of the whole cohort, with missing data for 222 patients. Reasons for initiating phlebotomies included hyperviscosity‐related symptoms for 54 patients (42%), thrombosis for 13 patients (10%), and both for 5 patients (4%), while 57 patients (44%) were asymptomatic. Phlebotomies resulted in symptom relief in 34 patients (79%) with symptoms persisting or worsening in 9 patients (21%); data were missing for 11 patients.

Follow‐up was available in 148 patients (22%), including 144 for whom follow‐up data regarding thrombosis were available. Among them, 14 (10%) experienced thrombosis during follow‐up. Among these patients, 5/14 (36%) had a prior thrombotic event, and two patients had multiple thrombotic events during follow‐up. Hematocrit levels at the time of thrombosis were available for eight patients with a median of 54.6% [interquartile range, IQR: 51.0–61.9]. These patients were more likely to have hereditary thrombophilia (21% vs. 3%, P = 0.02), arterial hypertension (36% vs. 12%, P = 0.03), to be smokers (60% vs. 24%, P = 0.02), to have ongoing anticoagulation therapy (50% vs. 13%, P = 0.004), and previous thrombosis (36% vs. 10%, P = 0.02; Supporting Information S1: Table S7). On the other hand, antiplatelet therapy (42% vs. 36%, P = 0.76) and phlebotomies (43% vs. 51%, P = 0.57) were similarly distributed among patients with and without thrombosis during follow‐up. After multivariable logistic regression, constitutional thrombophilia (OR = 99.16 [4.47, 4870.46], P = 0.007), arterial hypertension (OR = 13.31 [1.96, 144.07], P = 0.01), and smoking (OR = 15.39 [2.15, 237.41], P = 0.02) were associated with thrombosis during follow‐up. Among patients with hyperviscosity‐related symptoms, symptom recurrence during follow‐up was less frequent in those undergoing phlebotomies (20% vs. 50% in those without phlebotomies, P = 0.001).

Sixty‐four individuals with HOAH were diagnosed at 16 French institutions between 1983 and 2025 (Supporting Information S1: Table S8). Clinical features at diagnosis included seven prior thrombotic events (11%) and 11 patients (18%) with hyperviscosity‐related symptoms. Patients with previous thrombosis were more likely to be older (63 vs. 37 years, P = 0.002), to have arterial hypertension (71% vs. 14%, P = 0.003), obesity (57% vs. 12%, P = 0.014), sleep apnea syndrome (43% vs. 4%, P = 0.008), and higher erythropoietin levels (15 vs. 9 mU/ml, P = 0.025; Table 2). Multivariable analysis was not conducted because of the low number of events. There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics in patients with and without hyperviscosity symptoms (Supporting Information S1: Table S9). With a median follow‐up of 5.57 years [IQR: 0.88–11.88] in survivors, thrombosis occurred in three patients and death in one patient, contributing to the probability estimates for thrombosis‐free survival (TFS). The outcome of individuals with HOAH was favorable, with 94% (95% CI: 87%–100%) alive and thrombosis‐free at 5 years (Supporting Information S1: Figure S5). Patients receiving an antithrombotic drug were more likely to experience thrombosis (5‐year TFS, 91% [79%–100%] vs. 100% [100%], P = 0.054) but this did not reach statistical significance. In univariate analyses, no variable was associated with TFS (Supporting Information S1: Table S10).

Due to the rarity of HOAH, with limited individual data available, we led a systematic review of the literature to gain insight into thromboembolism in these patients. Because of reporting bias with asymptomatic patients being less likely to be reported, the 7% thrombotic rate at diagnosis may be overestimated. Additional studies are warranted to clarify the proportion of patients with thrombosis. This observation is, however, close to the 5% rate observed in a previous cohort 8 and the 11% rate observed in our validation cohort, while another previous study of 34 HOAH patients reported no thrombosis before or at diagnosis. 9 As in another study, 8 age and cardiovascular risk factors were associated with thrombosis, emphasizing the role of cardiovascular disease prevention in patients with HOAH. Although hereditary thrombophilia was significantly associated with venous thrombosis, due to potential risk of bias, systematic screening for hereditary thrombophilia in all individuals with HOAH should be confirmed in additional studies.

While antithrombotic medications and phlebotomies were not associated with reduced thrombosis, there is an absence of conclusive evidence for either intervention for preventing thrombosis, warranting additional studies. Inconclusive evidence is due to the non‐random allocation of these therapies, with highly heterogeneous treatment strategies across cohorts and time periods, and without standardized hematocrit targets or uniform indications for phlebotomy. For the same reasons, it was not possible to identify a subgroup of patients that would benefit from antithrombotic drugs. Although statistically significant in univariate analyses, the association between anticoagulation and thrombosis during follow‐up should be interpreted very cautiously as patients with a higher thrombotic risk were more likely to receive anticoagulation. In a recent 5‐year retrospective study of 206 patients with secondary erythrocytosis, thrombosis during follow‐up was not affected by phlebotomies and resolution of erythrocytosis, further emphasizing that phlebotomies in HOAH might not be as important as in PV. This may be explained by the compensatory mechanism of erythrocytosis in HOAH, while erythrocytosis is due to a clonal myeloproliferative disease in PV. 10 On the other hand, in contrast to a previous study, 8 phlebotomies were associated with a reduction in hyperviscosity‐related symptoms and can be used in these patients with individualized hematocrit targets according to symptom resolution and tolerance.

We acknowledge several limitations in this systematic review, including a vast majority of case reports, with heterogeneous clinical case descriptions, leading to missing data. Because our analysis was based on single case reports or small case series, we could not follow PRISMA guidelines that are more adequate for systematic reviews of clinical studies. It was therefore not possible to clearly assess the risk of bias. The relatively small number of thrombotic events and the large number of missing data may limit the interpretation of results. For example, the association of serum erythropoietin levels with thrombosis should be interpreted with caution as it was not statistically associated in the systematic review after multivariable adjustment. It could not be verified how thrombotic events were diagnosed in most case reports. As hematocrit targets were not reported in most publications, it was not possible to assess whether specific targets were associated with new‐onset thrombosis and/or resolution of hyperviscosity‐related symptoms. Furthermore, follow‐up data were rare, precluding us from estimating a median follow‐up. Despite publication bias, this review is probably the best methodology in such rare conditions to have sufficient statistical power to lead association studies. The French cohort also carries potential bias due to the small patient numbers and limited number of thrombotic events with heterogeneous follow‐up durations that may limit the robustness of validation analyses. This is, however, to our knowledge the largest case series reported so far in the literature.

Future international prospective cohorts could further help identify additional thrombotic risk factors, including clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential. Multicentric interventional studies are needed to clarify the role of antithrombotic prophylaxis and phlebotomies in individuals with HOAH.
