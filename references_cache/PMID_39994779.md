# Purification and validation of asparaginyl-tRNA synthetase heterodimer with indistinguishable subunits.
**Authors:** Vallee I, Shapiro R, Yang XL
**Journal:** IUBMB Life (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/iub.70000](https://doi.org/10.1002/iub.70000)

## Content

Oligomerization can influence the stability and activity of a protein. The majority of enzymes, including aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, become catalytically active upon forming homodimers. Residues located at the dimerization interface are highly conserved and mutations arising within can cause severe disease phenotypes. Beyond homozygous mutations, other disease-causing mutations, such as compound heterozygous and mono-allelic mutations, can lead to the formation of heterodimers between two distinct subunits. Purifying a recombinant heterodimer is required for its thorough characterization in vitro, yet there is a lack of established biochemical methods for the preparation. Here we describe a heterodimer purification and validation method with the example of a disease-causing mono-allelic, nonsense mutation R534* in cytoplasmic asparaginyl-tRNA synthetase (NARS1 or AsnRS). Our method involves co-expression of two separately tagged constructs to allow for purification of the wild-type and the R534* mutant heterodimers. While the two subunits can hardly be distinguished by size, their separate detection is achieved by western blotting against the tags. Quantification analysis confirmed that the subunits within the heterodimer are present in nearly equal proportions. This simple protocol can be adapted to study other size-indistinguishable heterodimers.

Most enzymes are found in oligomeric form, with approximately half of those in the UniProt database being homodimers (1). Amongst the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (aaRS) family specifically, the majority of human aaRSs are homodimeric and dimerization is required for enzymatic activity. Through allosteric mechanisms, aaRSs exhibit half-of-site reactivity and only one catalytic site within the dimer is active at a given time (2–8). Key residues at aaRS dimerization interfaces are highly conserved throughout evolution (9). An increasing number of disease-causing mutations is being reported to affect aaRS dimerization interfaces (10–15). Homozygous mutations may disrupt or alter the interface of homodimers, whereas compound heterozygous and the more commonly found mono-allelic mutations can lead to the formation of heterodimers between two distinct subunits. While increased affinity for interaction between the wild-type and some mutants has been shown in human cells, presumably via heterodimerization (12, 16, 17), it remains unclear whether there is a direct allosteric regulation of the mutant on the wild-type and what the impact of heterodimerization is on enzymatic activity.

To study the direct impact of heterodimerization on enzyme activities and regulations, the most obvious strategy is to purify the recombinant heterodimer. While the homodimers (wild-type or mutant) can be easily recapitulated in vitro, producing a pure recombinant heterodimer can be challenging. Previous protocols described methods to reconstitute heterodimers after separately purifying homodimers of each subunit (18,19). However, recombinant homodimers of WT and mutant are often too stable or too slow to dissociate to allow the formation of WT/mutant heterodimers in vitro, resulting in heterogeneity and low yields (20). Expressing both subunits together in the same cell recreates the cellular conditions under which both subunits are translated and folded concomitantly, allowing stable heterodimer formation to facilitate the subsequent purification (21).

Since dimeric interface residues are highly conserved, single missense mutations can be sufficient to modify the whole interface. However, missense mutations and short deletions that do not disrupt protein conformation or stability cannot be differentiated from the wild-type protein by commonly used methods like size exclusion chromatography or gel electrophoresis. Methods of using two separate affinity tags to purify the heterodimers and to differentiate the two subunits have been described (22, 23), but such a method has not yet been adapted to the purification of aaRS heterodimers.

Here we describe a research protocol to purify an aaRS heterodimer with the example of a severe disease-causing nonsense mutation R534* in cytoplasmic asparaginyl-tRNA synthetase (NARS1 or AsnRS) (24). The mono-allelic mutation causes a premature stop codon that removes the last 14 amino-acids of the protein originally located at the dimerization interface. Our protocol applies dual antibiotic selection, coupled with using two small inert affinity tags, respectively introduced to two different AsnRS constructs, to allow the purification of the recombinant heterodimer from E. coli cells. Quantification analysis confirmed that the subunits within the heterodimer are present in nearly equal proportions.

The goal of the vector design is to obtain two constructs that can be individually selected for antibiotic resistance and only colonies that contain both vectors can grow in the presence of both antibiotics. Additionally, we want to be able to distinguish the two constructs by different tags during purification and quantification.

Note: Small inert tags were chosen to avoid disrupting protein folding, dimerization and catalytic activity. We specifically chose 6xHis and STREP II tags for their specificity and cost efficiency. We confirmed dimer formation for each construct by mass photometry (Figure 3). Comparing AsnRS western blot and Coomassie staining, we found that the latter to be more consistent (Figure 2), leading us to use the Coomassie signal as total protein normalization for 6xHis and STREP western blots. One of the two heterodimers (6xHis-WT/STREP-II-R534*) could be separated by electrophoresis due to an increased size difference between subunits compared to the other constructs (Table 4, Figure 2). For this construct, we compared the ratios of the upper band over the lower band signal for Coomassie staining and 6xHis/STREP western blots. We found almost identical ratios, respectively 0.88 and 0.89, validating that both antibodies (Anti-6xHis and anti-STREP) were providing accurate quantification.

As the main controls, we separately expressed the single construct of 6xHis-tagged WT and STREP-II-tagged R534* mutant and purified each of them with their respective affinity column. This allowed for quantifications within 6xHis/STREP western blots, which were further normalized over the total protein signal quantified by the Coomassie staining. We calculated the His/STREP ratio of each construct, with values approaching 1 indicating equal amounts of subunits. Both 6xHis-WT/STREP-II-WT and 6xHis-R534*/STREP-II-R534* homodimers showed a His/STREP ratio below 1, indicating more STREP-II- than 6xHis-tagged proteins. A bias towards more STREP-II tag construct can be expected, considering the STREP purification is the later step and the 6xHis-tagged subunit may be dissociated and washed away from STREP-II-tagged subunit.

To avoid tag-induced bias, WT/R534* heterodimers were prepared swapping tags on each subunit. 6xHis-WT/STREP-II-R534* and 6xHis-R534*/STREP-II-WT had a His/STREP ratio of 0.89 and 1.07, respectively, both suggesting there is slightly more R534* than WT AsnRS in the sample. Although we cannot rule out variabilities in our quantification analysis, the difference in ratio could also be explained by a slight increase in stability and/or expression level of the R534* mutant, compared to the WT AsnRS. With the His-to-STREP order of the two-step purification, to maximize heterodimer yield and homogeneity, the STREP should be put onto the subunit with a lower stability or to increase the expression of the His-tagged subunit.

The 6xHis-WT/STREP-II-WT homodimer (0.63) showed a smaller His/STREP ratio than the His-R534*/STREP-R534* homodimer (0.9), suggesting that the mutant homodimer is stronger than the WT homodimer, further suggesting that the R534* mutation strengthens homodimer formation. The His/STREP ratio of the mutant homodimer (0.9) and the 6xHis-WT/STREP-II-R534* heterodimer (0.89) was almost identical, pointing to the R534* mutation strengthening the dimer formation in the context of both homodimer and heterodimer formation. This conclusion will be further validated in our future biochemical and biophysical characterizations of the purified AsnRS homo- and heterodimers.
