# Loss of MALT1 Function in a Patient With Combined Immunodeficiency: a Novel Pathogenic Variant and Immunological Insights.
**Authors:** Tian Z, Chen R, Jia Y, Jiang J, Dai R, An Y, Tang X, Zhao X, Zhou L
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-025-01921-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-025-01921-y)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Germline pathogenic variants in the mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma translocation gene 1 (MALT1) encodes a caspase-like protease that plays a crucial role in the caspase recruitment domain (CARD)-B-cell lymphoma 10 (BCL10)-MALT1 (CBM) complex. This complex mediates the activation of nuclear factor-kB (NF-kB) pathway and are associated with diverse human diseases including combine immunodeficiency (CID), lymphoproliferation and others. This study aimed to determine the underlying cause of immune deficiency and immune dysregulation in a patient presented with recurrent respiratory infections, aphthous ulcers, dermatitis, chronic diarrhea, failure to thrive and early death.

METHODS: Clinical and laboratory records were reviewed. Patients underwent next-generation sequencing (NGS), and analysis of genomic DNA was performed on the patient and her parents. Lymphocyte subsets, MALT1 expression and NF-kB signaling was evaluated by flow cytometry, RT-PCR and immunoblotting.

RESULTS: The patient carried a novel pathogenic biallelic loss-of-function variant in MALT1 (c.1411G > A; p.D471N) located in the caspase-like domain, leading to severely reduced MALT1 protein expression. Impaired CBM-mediated NF-κB activation was confirmed by reduced phosphorylation of the p65 subunit, resulting in deficient production of IL-2 and TNF-α. This functional defect caused lower Tfr and Treg cells, a normal proportion of Tfh cells, with higher expression of activation markers PD-1 and ICOS. The patient displayed low NK cell and B cell counts, together with a developmental block at the transitional B cell stage. Additionally, the proportion of marginal zone-like B cells (MZB-like) was markedly decreased, indicating impaired B cell differentiation.

CONCLUSIONS: Human MALT1 deficiency causes profound CID by impairing CBM-mediated NF-kB signaling and MALT1-paracaspase activity. Consistent with the reported variants located in the caspase-like domain, our patient presented with an inflammatory phenotype, supporting the notion that the MALT1 D471N mutation phenocopies a partial loss of both MALT1 scaffolding function and paracaspase activity. Prompt hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is highly recommended as an effective therapy for MALT1 deficiency.

Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma translocation gene 1 (MALT1) encodes a caspase-like protease that plays an essential role in B-cell lymphoma 10 (BCL10)-mediated activation of nuclear factor-kB (NF-kB) signaling. The protein is a key component of the caspase recruitment domain (CARD)-BCL10-MALT1 (CBM) complex, which acts as a primary initiator of both NF-kB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways, promoting cell activation, proliferation, and survival [1]. In response to antigen-receptor stimulation, the ubiquitously expressed BCL10-MALT1 couples with one of CARD family members (including CARD9, CARD10, CARD11 and CARD14) to form the CBM signalosome. MALT1 functions as a scaffold protein within the CBM complex, facilitating the recruitment of additional signaling intermediates and ultimately leading to the activation of transcription factors [2]. In addition to its role as a scaffold protein, MALT1 also functions as a protease, regulating NF-kB activity by cleaving a specific repertoire of substrates, resulting in activation or termination of these signaling pathways [2, 3].

Human germline MALT1 deficiency was first reported by Jabara et al. in 2013, and they identified a biallelic loss-of-function (LOF) MALT1 variant (OMIM #615468) in two siblings from a large consanguineous family [4]. Since then, a total of 22 patients have been discovered. These patients typically present with combined immune deficiency (CID), characterized by recurrent bacterial, viral, and fungal infections, periodontal disease, enteropathy, dermatitis, and failure to thrive. Immunological phenotyping of MALT1 deficiency generally reveals normal lymphocyte and B-cell counts, although B-cell maturation may be arrested in some patients. Other findings involve variable immunoglobulin levels (with decreased IgM or increased IgE levels), reduced specific antibody titers, severely diminished Treg cell numbers, impaired T-cell proliferation in response to mitogens or antigen receptor ligation, and normal NK cell counts [4–14]. If left untreated, MALT1 deficiency generally results in a severe disease course, with 11 of 22 patients reported to have succumbed [4, 9, 11, 12, 14]. Therefore, patients with symptoms indicative of MALT1 deficiency should be promptly investigated and receive timely treatment.

Herein, we report the clinical and immunological findings of a patient with a novel pathogenic variant in MALT1, marking the first case in China. We demonstrate that the biallelic LOF mutation (c.1411G > A; p.D471N) leads to severely reduced MALT1 protein expression. Additionally, our patient displayed low NK cell and B cell counts, together with a defective B cell development with a transitional B cell block.

The patient presented to our institution for further evaluation following the identification of a genetic defect associated with primary immunodeficiency. Medical records were obtained through interviews with the patient’s guardians and copies of records from previously visited hospitals. The clinical history was further supplemented by reviewing the electronic medical records of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University and conducting a physical examination of the patient. Written informed consent was obtained, clinical and immunological assessments for inborn errors of immunity (IEI) were conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

A commercial primary immunodeficiency gene panel was performed on genomic DNA from the patient and her parents by KANGSO MEDICAL INSPECTION (Beijing, China). A potentially pathogenic MALT1 variant (c.1411G > A; p. D471N) was identified and confirmed in the patient and carriers through standard Sanger sequencing, using the following primers: forward: 5’- CCAATGCCAGAGTTCAAATGC-3’; reverse: 5’-CAGCCTGGGTGACAGAGTTA-3’. The mutated amino acid was aligned across multiple species using Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo) to assess its evolutionary conservation.

Total RNA was extracted from EDTA-anticoagulated blood samples of patient and age-matched health control (HC) using the Blood RNA Purification Miniprep Kit (Cat No: AP-MN-BL-RNA-250 AXYGEN; 250 Preps). The RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA by PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Expression of MALT1 transcripts was quantified using TB Green Premix ExTaq II (Takara) on a Real-Time PCR System (Biorad CFX96). The following primers were used for RT-PCR: for MALT1, forward, 5’-AACAGCTTCATGGTCCCCG-3’, and reverse, 5’-ACGTGGCATATCCAAACACA-3’; and for the housekeeping gene GAPDH (glyceraldehyde phosphate dehydrogenase), forward, 5’-TGGTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAG-3’, and reverse, 5’-CCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAAATTC-3’.

Total PBMCs were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche). Lysates were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Immobilon-FL; Millipore Sigma). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TRIS-buffered saline with Tween (TBST) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: a rabbit anti-MALT1 antibody (carboxy terminus, 2494S; Cell Signaling), a rabbit monoclonal MALT1 antibody (amino terminus, EP603Y; Abcam), a HRP-conjugated GAPDH monoclonal antibody (1E6D9; Proteintech) and an alpha tubulin polyclonal antibody (11224–1-AP; Proteintech). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ZB-2301; ZSGB-BIO). The signals were detected using the LI-COR Odyssey infrared imager (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE).

Adherent HEK293 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were passaged upon reaching approximately 80% confluency. For detachment, cells were washed with 1–3 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and treated with 1 mL of 0.05% trypsin solution for 3–5 min. Trypsinization was halted by adding fresh DMEM. Cells were then either diluted into culture flasks or seeded into appropriate plates for subsequent experiments.

The 7.1-pCMV-3 × Flag wild-type human MALT1 expression plasmid (transcript: NM_006785) was obtained from YouBio. The D471N point mutation was introduced by performing overlap extension PCR with the wild-type construct as the template. Transfection of wild-type and mutant MALT1 plasmids into HEK293T cells was carried out using the standard polyethyleneimine (PEI) method. HEK293 cells (5 × 10⁶) were seeded in 24-well plates one day prior to transfection. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cycloheximide was added at a final concentration of 100 μg/mL, and cells were harvested at multiple time points (0, 6, 12, and 24 h). MALT1 protein stability was assessed by Western blot analysis.

T cell receptor excision circles (TRECs) and kappa-deleting recombination excision circles (KRECs) was evaluated using genomic DNA extracted from whole blood samples of the patient and age-matched HCs (QIAmp DNA Blood MiniKit; Qiagen) according to manufacturer’s recommendations. The amount of signal joint TREC or KREC copies per unit of DNA was estimated by quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), as previously described [15].

Activation of the NF-κB pathway was assessed by measuring Inhibitor of NF-κB Alpha (IκBa) degradation and phosphorylation of P65 subunit. PBMCs (1 × 106 cells) were stained with a cocktail of antibodies against surface markers for 30 min on ice and subsequently washed once with FACS buffer (phosphate-buffered saline containing 2% FBS). The PBMCs were then incubated with either medium or 162 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, M4647; AbMole) and 1ug/ml ionomycin (inh-ion-3; InvivoGen) at 37 °C, for 15 min. After incubations, cells were fixed with BD™ Phosflow Fix Buffer I (557870; BD Biosciences) at 37 °C for 10 min. The cells were then washed and permeabilized with BD™ Phosflow Perm Buffer III (558050; BD Biosciences) for 30 min on ice. Next, the cells were stained with fluorescent-labeled antibodies specific for phosphorylated residues of NF-κB p65 Ser536 (93H1; Cell Signaling) and IκBa (L35A5; Cell Signaling) in FACS buffer for 30 min at room temperature. Negative controls (NC) and isotype controls were used separately for p65 phosphorylation assay and IκBa degradation assessment.

For cytokine assays, thawed PBMCs (2 × 105 cells) were stimulated with 400 nM PMA and 500 ng/ml ionomycin in 96-well plate at 37 °C. GolgiStop (554724, BD Biosciences) was added 11 h later. After 16 h incubation, cell surface markers and intracellular cytokines were stained for 30 min on ice. Thereafter, cells were fixed with fixation/permeabilization solution (554714; BD Biosciences,) for 30 min on ice. Peripheral blood analysis of the patient conducted at 3.86 and 3.87 years of her age revealed leukocytosis with concurrent neutrophilia, while tests at other time points remained generally normal. Hemoglobin levels were slightly decreased during the early observation period but returned to normal between ages of 3.85 and 3.89 years. Eosinophilia, elevated platelet counts, and increased CRP levels were consistently present during the period between 2.93 and 3.89 years of her age. Lymphocytopenia was not evident during the period of observation. (Fig. 1d). IgA and IgM levels were transiently decreased, while IgG levels remained within the normal range. IgE level was elevated in two out of two tests (Fig. 1e). Initial immunophenotyping of lymphocytes revealed generally normal CD8+ T cell percentages, along with elevated CD3+ and CD4+ T cell percentages, resulting in an increased CD4/CD8 ratio. In contrast, CD19+ B cell percentages were temporarily decreased, while NK cell percentages remained consistently low throughout the observation period (Fig. 1f). To assess naïve T cell production and B cell neogenesis, TRECs and KRECs were quantified using RT-qPCR. Compared to age-matched HCs, the patient exhibited significantly reduced TRECs and KRECs levels, measuring only 13 TRECs/μL and 2 KRECs/μL, respectively (Fig. 1g).

Due to recurrent infections and hypogammaglobulinemia, most MALT1 deficient patients have been receiving antibiotic prophylaxis and IVIG replacement therapy (Fig. 1a). Despite of the combined therapies, severe infections and other complications persist, with an overall survival rate of 12 out of 23 patients (52%), including our case. However, HSCT has been proven to be an effective treatment for MALT1-deficient CID. Of the 22 patients reported to date, six (27%) underwent HSCT, all of whom were reported to be in good health [5–7, 11, 13] at the time of publication (Table 1).

The patient was born to a first-cousin marriage, consistent with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (Fig. 2a). This aligns with previous reports showing that 87% of MALT1-deficient patients had parental consanguinity (Table 1). Due to the patient's manifestation of severe immunodeficiency phenotypes consistent with CID, the family was advised to investigate a potential underlying genetic defect by conducting a commercial primary immunodeficiency gene panel sequencing. A novel bi-allelic missense variant (c.1411G > A) was identified, substituting aspartic acid with asparagine (p.D471N) in the caspase-like domain of the MALT protein (Fig. 2b). Sanger sequencing of genomic DNA confirmed that the missense mutation was homozygous in the patient and heterozygous in her parents (Fig. 2c). Multiple sequence alignment demonstrated that the mutated residue p.D471N is highly evolutionary conserved. To evaluate the effect of this rare MALT1 variant, MALT1 mRNA level and protein expression were analyzed using RT-qPCR and Western blotting respectively. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the MALT1 transcript abundance was significantly reduced comparing our patient with age-matched HCs (Fig. 2e). Two different MALT1 antibodies targeting its carboxy-terminal or the amino-terminal was used, we found that the MALT1 p.D471N mutation resulted in decreased protein expression in the patient compared to HC, with markedly reduced expression detected by the antibody against the carboxy terminal of the MALT1 gene (Fig. 2f). To further investigate the effect of the D471N mutation on MALT1 expression, we constructed wild-type and mutant MALT1 expression plasmids and transfected them into HEK293T cells. The cycloheximide chase assay was subsequently performed, revealing that the mutant MALT1 protein was unstable and underwent more rapid degradation compared to the wild-type protein (Fig. 2g).

A more detailed immunologic phenotyping of the patient, conducted through flow cytometric analysis of a blood sample, confirmed several findings from the initial immune workup. There was an elevated fraction of CD3+ and CD4+ T cells among lymphocytes, accompanied by increased absolute numbers of CD4+ T cells, together with normal CD8+ T cell numbers, leading to an increased CD4/CD8 ratio (Table 2). Notably, the patient had a decreased proportion but normal amounts of CD4+ naive T cells, in contrast to elevated proportion and absolute numbers of CD4+ central memory (CM) T cells. Additionally, there was a decrease in the percentage of TCRγδ+ T cells among CD3+ T cells, while both the proportions and absolute counts of natural killer (NK) cells remained low (Table 2). Detailed immunologic phenotyping of T cells revealed an elevated proportion of CD4+CD45RA-CXCR5- conventional T cells compared to health controls, which was consistent with the increased fraction of CD4+ T cells shown in Table 2. Similarly, the proportions of T-cell subsets, including Th1, Th2, Th17, and Th1/17 cells, were comparable to those in age-matched HCs, except for slightly increased Th2 cells in the patient (Fig. 4a and b). Nevertheless, the proportion of CD4+ CD45RA-CXCR5+ Tfh cells, as well as Tfh cell subsets including Th1-like, Th2-like, Th17-like, and Th1/17-like Tfh cells, were within the normal range (Fig. 4a, 4b). Evaluation of the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of PD-1 and ICOS on Tfh cells demonstrated enormously elevated levels of both markers in the patient's Tfh cells compared to that of HCs (Fig. 4c). Alongside with these findings, CD4+CD45RO+CXCR5+CD25+ CD127- Tfr cells were found to be reduced in the patient compared to HCs (Fig. 4a and b). B-cell immunophenotyping of our patient was characterized by markedly reduced total B cell and B cell subpopulations, with a particularly significant decrease in transitional B cells, showing lower levels in both proportions and absolute numbers (Table 2). These findings are in accordance with previously described defective B cell development with a transitional B cell block [20]. In contrast to disclosed cases, which demonstrating abundant naive B cells, absent marginal zone B cells and profound depletion of switched memory B cells, flow cytometric analysis of our patient displayed a rather normal expression of naïve B cells and switched memory B cells. However, the fraction of marginal zone-like B cells (MZB-like) was significantly decreased compared to age-matched controls, reminiscent of previously reported cases in the literature [6, 13, 14] (Fig. 4e).

Human MALT1 deficiency is an extremely rare monogenic inborn error of immunity (IEI), classified as combined immunodeficiency [21], offering valuable insights into human immune biology. To date, only 22 cases have been described [4–14]. Among them, Sefer et al. reported a large cohort of MALT1 deficiency patients through a multicenter study consist of 9 patients of Kurdish ethnicity background [11]. We presented a novel case of MALT1 deficiency caused by a bi-allelic missense mutation (c.1411G > A) in the MALT1 gene, resulting in the substitution of aspartic acid with asparagine (p.D471N). This is the first case of MALT1 deficiency identified in China. Extended genetic study was conducted to confirm the homozygous mutation in this patient. Overall, the 22 reported cases of MALT1 deficiency typically presented with recurrent bacterial, viral, and fungal infections affecting the skin, respiratory system, and intestinal tract, along with aphthous ulcers, dermatitis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and failure to thrive. Except for periodontal disease, our patient had all these typical symptoms. Our patient displayed persistent elevated levels of IgE and platelets, accompanying by a reduced proportion of B cells and Tregs, a severely diminished proportion of transitional B cell but normal proportions of naïve B and memory B cell subsets. Due to progressive diarrhea and septicemia, the patient passed away at the age of 4.

MALT1 plays a dual role in lymphocytes. As a scaffold protein, it associates with BCL-10 and CARD11 to form the CBM signalosome, which is essential for the activation of NF-κB transcriptional targets upon lymphocyte stimulation. The paracaspase MALT1 cleaves and removes negative regulators of NF-κB signaling, thereby promoting lymphocyte responses in NF-kB activation and in B-cell lymphoma subtypes [22]. Consistent with this, two mouse models—Malt1-/- and protease-dead Malt1PD/PD mice—have been employed to investigate the mechanisms underlying the phenotype observed in MALT1-deficient patients. Malt1-/- mice lack detectable MALT1 expression and are therefore deficient in both scaffold and protease functions. These mice exhibit agammaglobulinemia and increased susceptibility to infections yet display normal behavior and lifespan and are even protected from autoimmune inflammation [16, 22–26]. Our patient exhibited uniformly impaired CBM-mediated NF-κB activation in primary CD4+ and CD8+ T cells demonstrated by reduced phosphorylation of the p65 subunit, leading to deficient production of IL-2 and TNF-a. In contrast, Malt1PD/PD mice have intact MALT1 protein expression and scaffolding function but specifically lack proteolytic activity due to a knock-in mutation at the active site cysteine (C472A) [16, 22, 23, 26]. Unlike Malt1-/- mice, which typically have reduced levels of IgE and IgG antibodies, Malt1PD/PD mice develop hyper-IgE, multiorgan infiltration of lymphocytes, immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, and IPEX-like gastroenteritis [16].

In line with these findings of the murine models, our patient mainly presented with IPEX-like disorders (such as progressive eczema, chronic diarrhea, consistent high IgE levels and severe food allergy) in the first two years of her life. Recurrent bacterial and viral sinopulmonary infections did not become apparent until the age of 2, and the patient ultimately succumbed to uncontrollable sepsis. Interestingly, we found a similar pattern in patients with mutations located in the caspase-like domain of the MALT1 gene [5, 7, 11–13]. In resemblance with previous cases, our patient exhibited severely decreased but not undetectable Treg cells. Tregs are profoundly impaired in Malt1-/- mice, with markedly reduced Treg numbers observed in the peripheral blood of young animals [17, 18, 23, 27–31]. However, this defect appears to be masked in aged Malt1-/- mice due to progressive peripheral Treg expansion [19, 32, 33]. Despite this compensatory expansion, aged Malt1-/- mice develop atopic dermatitis (AD)-like skin inflammation, with no evidence of inflammation in other organs [34]. Notably, hyper-IgE levels also have been reported in aged Malt1-/- mice [24]. T cells and keratinocytes were initially considered prime candidates based on the atopic dermatitis phenotypes observed in hypomorphic variants of CARD11 and CARD14. The precise cellular source contributing to this phenotype remains unclear, as conditional deletion of MALT1 in T cells (CD4-Cre) or keratinocytes (K5-Cre) did not reproduce the hyper-IgE or AD-like phenotype [35].

Another potential contributor to the inflammatory phenotype observed in MALT1-deficient patients is the inability of MALT1 to cleave HOIL1 (heme-oxidized IRP2 ubiquitin ligase 1), a negative regulator of NF-κB signaling [34]. Inactivation of paracaspase function in Malt1PD/PD mice leads to excessive interferon-γ (IFN-γ) production by effector lymphocytes, contributing to immune pathology [23]. A similar reduction of tregs was observed in Malt1PD/PD mice, where natural Treg proportions in the CD4+ T cell compartment were reduced by∼60% [16, 24]. Thus, selective inhibition of MALT1 proteolytic activity effectively skews the immune response toward autoimmunity by impairing Treg development [22, 34]. Autoimmune manifestations (including alopecia, vitiligo and psoriasis) typically in Malt1PD/PD mice were not commonly seen in MALT1 deficiency patients, occurring in only 35% of all cases. However, our patient did not show autoimmune symptoms, except she tested positive once for anti-myeloperoxidase antibody (MPO), a type of anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) commonly associated with autoimmune diseases (data not shown).

In Malt1PD/PD murine model, there is a developmental defect in marginal zone B cells, B1 B cells, IL-10 producing B cells and mature T and B cells. In addition, Malt1PD/PD mice retained normal or partially reduced proportions of geminal center (GC) B cells, and the Tfh compartment was only partially reduced. By contrast, complete absence of GC B cells with an associated decrease in Tfh cell has been reported in Malt1-/- mice, where MALT1 has a B-cell-intrinsic role for proliferation, survival, and GC formation [16, 17, 25, 36]. In our patient, we found a normal proportion of Tfh cells, with significantly higher expression of activation markers PD-1 and ICOS. Previous studies on MALT1 deficiency from Japan indicated a significant decline in B cell numbers correlated with the age of patient, accompanied by a maturation arrest in B cells. In addition, the aberrant differentiation of B cells cannot be restored by CpG treatments [13]. To our surprise, our patient displayed normal proportion of naïve and memory B cells. In review of all the 22 reported MALT1 deficiency patients, only 7 cases had detected naïve and memory B cells [6, 8, 11, 13, 14, 20]. 2 out of 7 patients reported the percentage of naïve and memory B cells were within the normal range [11]. Furthermore, the percentage and absolute number of transitional B cell were found to be significantly reduced in our patient. Transitional B cells play an important part in regulating inflammation and tolerance [37]. Defective B cell development with a block in transitional B cell has also been announced in CARD11-deficient individuals demonstrated of imbalanced transitional B cell subsets [38]. Together, these findings highlight the role of the CBM complex in human B-cell development [37, 39, 40], indicating impaired B cell differentiation in our patient, despite the variability in B cell phenotypes observed among MALT1 deficiency patients.

Taken together, the immunological phenotypes observed in our patient support the notion that the MALT1 D471N mutation phenocopies a partial loss of both MALT1 scaffolding function and paracaspase activity. We demonstrated that the expression of both MALT1 mRNA and protein was reduced in the D471N variant. Given the variability in mRNA expression, we cannot conclude that the amino acid substitution directly causes destabilization of the MALT1 protein, as the reduced protein levels may simply reflect differences in transcript abundance. Additionally, the cycloheximide chase assay revealed that the MALT1 D471N mutant was unstable and underwent more rapid degradation compared to the WT protein. Our data suggest that the reduced expression of the MALT1 D471N variant results from both transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms. In contrast, other missense mutations resulting in amino acid substitutions did not affect MALT1 gene expression, as indicated by comparable mRNA levels measured in T-cell-derived or PBMC-derived cDNA from healthy controls using RT-PCR analysis [4, 6, 7, 10]. Although many human MALT1 mutations result in either reduced (e.g., W580S, I600N, D535N, L386P, N485S, W580R) or undetectable (e.g., S89I, D184Y) MALT1 protein expression. Interestingly, MALT1 was expressed at WT levels in a homozygous variant carrying the c.2418G > C mutation, which results in the missense substitutions E795D in the MALT1A splice isoform and E806D in MALT1B. While the resulting MALT1A/B proteins lacked protease activity, MALT1A—but not MALT1B—retained its scaffolding function. This makes it the only known hypomorphic MALT1 mutation reported to date [10]. Additionally, further cases of MALT1 deficiency are needed to determine whether hypomorphic or reduced-expression mutations give rise to distinct clinical phenotypes in humans. Unfortunately, due to the limited samples and the death of our patient, MALT1 proteolytic activity could not be assessed through cleavage of known substrates such as CYLD or HOIL-1. To evaluate the impact of the D471N mutation on MALT1 protease function, future efforts to reconstitute a MALT1-knockout T cell line with the D471N mutant would be valuable to determine whether this variant can restore MALT1 proteolytic activity.

At present, severe infections remain the primary cause of poor prognosis in MALT1 deficiency. If clinically feasible, prompt hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is highly recommended, as it has been shown to be highly effective in rectifying immune deficiency, with successful outcomes in now 6 of 6 reported cases [5, 7, 11, 13, 20]. HSCT is therefore considered a potentially curative therapy for MALT1 deficiency.
