# The yin and the yang of early classical pathway complement disorders.
**Authors:** Sullivan KE
**Journal:** Clin Exp Immunol (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1093/cei/uxac056](https://doi.org/10.1093/cei/uxac056)

## Content

The classical pathway of the complement cascade has been recognized as a key activation arm, partnering with the lectin activation arm and the alternative pathway to cleave C3 and initiate the assembly of the terminal components. While deficiencies of classical pathway components have been recognized since 1966, only recently have gain-of-function variants been described for some of these proteins. Loss-of-function variants in C1, C4, and C2 are most often associated with lupus and systemic infections with encapsulated bacteria. C3 deficiency varies slightly from this phenotypic class with membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis and infection as the dominant phenotypes. The gain-of-function variants recently described for C1r and C1s lead to periodontal Ehlers Danlos syndrome, a surprisingly structural phenotype. Gain-of-function in C3 and C2 are associated with endothelial manifestations including hemolytic uremic syndrome and vasculitis with C2 gain-of-function variants thus far having been reported in patients with a C3 glomerulopathy. This review will discuss the loss-of-function and gain-of-function phenotypes and place them within the larger context of complement deficiencies.

Keywords: periodontal disease, gingival detachment, sepsis, lupus, glomerulopathy, HUS

Early complement components have been described with variants leading to both over-activation and under-activation. These lead to distinct medical conditions.

Overall, complement defects comprise a relatively narrow sector of the overall inborn errors of immunity (IEI) population [1, 2]. Table 1 displays the frequencies of complement defects in various registries that tabulate IEI. The frequency of complement defects as a subset of all IEI ranges from less than 1% to a high of 12% in different countries or regions. There are some founder effects related to the frequency of complement deficiency that may impact these numbers, however, it is likely that undiagnosed patients represent a significant proportion of the differences between countries. The phenotypes of complement defects are not always distinctive enough that a diagnostic investigation is pursued and diagnostic testing is not widely available.

This review will focus on gain-of-function (GOF) and loss-of-function (LOF) variants in the early classical pathway component genes, covering C1q, C1r, C1s, C4, C2, and C3 variants. The recent recognition of GOF variants in this pathway has led to an improved understanding of pathologic processes, however, questions persist as to the exact mechanism of disease in some circumstances. Complement defects overall have a relatively narrow set of phenotypic expression patterns [3, 4]. In general, complement defects can be thought of as associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus, infection, hereditary angioedema, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), glomerulonephritis, or neuroinflammation (Table 2). Many of these complement defects can be identified through serum testing to define the overall level of the protein or functional testing that depends on intact hemolysis. Nevertheless, there are a number of functional defects, particularly in regulatory proteins, that are best defined genetically [5].

The classical pathway was the first pathway to be described. It is activated primarily by immune complexes, apoptotic cells, C-reactive protein, and dozens of other proteins that bind C1q. Mapping of the binding sites on C1q revealed that charged residues on the apex of the globular end C1q of the heterotrimer as well as the side chain are crucial for binding to IgG, IgM, CRP, and PTX-3 [6–9]. C1q also recognizes pathogen-associated molecular patterns, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [7], bacterial porins [10], and molecules on the surface of dying cells such as phosphatidylserine and double-stranded DNA [11–16]. Antibody-antigen complexes are the primary activators of the classical pathway and the best understood mechanistically. When the antibody binds antigen, a subtle conformational change occurs that renders the antibody molecule capable of interacting with C1q. Only immunoglobulins IgG and IgM activate complement, and IgM is more efficient than IgG. In addition, not all isotypes of IgG are equivalent with IgG3>IgG1>IgG2. IgG4 is not able to activate complement. A single molecule of IgM is sufficient to activate complement, whereas multiple molecules of IgG bound to a particle are required for activation.

C1q associates with C1r and C1s, the enzymatic components of the C1 complex. The complex is comprised of a single C1q (in turn composed of six polypeptide chains), two C1r, and two C1s proteins (Fig. 1). The globular head interacts with the Cγ2 domain of IgG or the Cµ3 domain of IgM while other activators of C1 bind to the stalk region rather than to the globular head. All protein interactions lead to a conformational change in C1q that allows autoactivation of C1r. The two molecules of active C1r then cleave the two molecules of C1s. Once cleaved, the active C1s cleave C4 into C4a and C4b. C4b binds to the pathogen, albeit inefficiently. The C4b-C1s2 complex with the enzymatically active C1s cleaves C2 into C2a and C2b. The smaller C2b is released, and the C2a becomes incorporated into the growing complex. The C4bC2a complex is referred to as the C3 convertase and binds and cleaves C3. C3a is released into the surrounding space and C3b binds to the pathogen surface (Fig. 1) and is recognized by specific receptors on neutrophils, thereby enhancing phagocytosis.

C1q, C1r, C1s, C4, and C2 comprise the activation pathway for C3, ultimately generating the classical pathway C3 convertase of C4b2a and the C5 convertase of C4b2a3b. The tulip shaped structure of C1q is formed from six heterotrimers of C1qA, C1qB, and C1qC. It is integrated with two each of the enzymes C1r and C1s, forming the C1 complex. Once activated through binding of the globular heads to the Fc region of IgG or IgM (or other activators via the stalk region), the conformational change allows C1r to cleave the other C1r and then both C1s molecules, thereby activating C1s for cleavage of first C4 and then C2. C4b covalently binds the surface of the pathogen, nucleating the assembly of the C3 and C5 convertases. Once C5 is cleaved, the membrane attack complex self assembles, stabilized by C9. The early stages of complement deposition facilitate engulfment by neutrophils (i.e., opsonization) through recognition of C3b and iC3b (inactivated C3b) by the CR1 receptor on neutrophils, also known as CD35, which augments phagocytosis mediated by Fc receptors. CR3 receptors (CD11bCD18) are upregulated on neutrophils in inflammatory states and also bind iC3b and can mediate phagocytosis. C1q receptors play a limited role for pathogen uptake but are important for the clearance of apoptotic cells. (Produced in Biorender).

As a consequence of classical pathway activation, a number of small-molecule mediators signal to initiate inflammation. C3a and C5a are termed anaphylatoxins. These fragments bind specific receptors that induce mast cell degranulation and neutrophil aggregation (Fig. 2). The anaphylatoxins increase blood flow and vascular permeability permitting complement and antibody entry into tissue. The chemotactic activity of C5a directs neutrophils to the pathogen.

soluble cleavage products of classical pathway activation are biologically active in a variety of roles. C3a and C5a have sequence and structural similarity but bind distinct G-protein coupled receptors. The C3aR is expressed on cells of myeloid origin, endothelial cells, epithelial cells and smooth muscle cells. C5aR is also highly expressed on myeloid cells, with expression on endothelial cells, neurons, microglia, astrocytes, and some specific tissues. Both C3a and C5a can trigger an oxidative burst, histamine release from mast cells and basophils and activation of eosinophils. They have additionally been implicated in tissue repair and T cell responses. C4a is structurally similar to C3a and C5a and has been found to induce comparable vascular responses but at very high doses. A receptor for C4a has been contentiously debated although some data suggest that it may bind to protease activated receptors 1 and 4, however, there is as yet little consensus on the mechanism of action of C4a and the biological role is similarly debated. The mannose-binding lectin and alternative pathways also activate C3 and provide additional mechanisms of activation. The terminal components are also called the membrane attack complexes (MAC) and are responsible for direct cytolysis of both eukaryotic cells and prokaryotic cells. These proteins (C5, C6, C7, C8, and C9) generate a physical pore.

The typical phenotypes associated with LOF of the early classical pathway components are infection, lupus, and glomerulonephritis (Table 2). The mechanism of the susceptible to infection is best understood as a defect in opsonization related to the role of C3b as a key opsonin of encapsulated bacteria. Lacking deposition of C3b onto a microbial surface people are susceptible to infection with organisms such as Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and Neisseria meningitidis. Opsonization of bacteria is critical for phagocytosis and subsequent killing of those bacteria (Fig. 1). Encapsulated organisms represent a particular challenge for neutrophils in that they are less able to utilize alternative receptors for engulfment. The characteristic infections are most often deep infections such as sepsis, meningitis, or pneumonia and a recent sequencing study of sepsis in children revealed a high number of presumed pathogenic variants in complement genes, including C3, suggesting that complement deficiencies are underdiagnosed among children with sepsis [17].

While infections with encapsulated organisms represent the most significant susceptibility in people with early classical pathway LOF variants, other infections have been described [3, 4]. Among studies that have prospectively attempted to identify complement deficiency in cohorts with significant infection, several studies have suggested that recurrent deep infections with pneumococcus warrant investigation of the complement cascade. Ascertainment of early classical pathway component deficiencies in people with recurrent sinus infections is of low yield, however, in children with a single invasive pneumococcal illness (defined as requiring hospital admission and having a positive culture), 2.4% had a complement deficiency (3/127), 9% had a sustained antibody deficiency, 7% had a transient antibody deficiency, and 1 patient had asplenia [18]. Among 59 children with recurrent S. Pneumoniae infections, the likelihood of finding a complement deficiency (all C2 deficiency) was 7% in Denmark [19]. C2 deficiency is the most common early classical pathway component deficiency in Western European backgrounds. The estimated frequency is 1:10 000 among people of Western European background [20, 21].

The second phenotype among LOF variants in the early classical pathway deficiency states is lupus. There are variations in the penetrance and the phenotype (Table 3) but the patients who develop SLE benefit from traditional lupus-directed therapeutics and have a natural history that is not very different from the polygenic SLE cohort, although their risk of infection and myocardial infection appears to be higher [20]. The mechanism for susceptibility to SLE is multifactorial, with C1 deficiency being the most strongly associated with SLE, followed by C4, and C2 being the least strongly associated with SLE. There is a roughly parallel gradient of severity, with C1 deficiency having generally the most severe phenotype. This reflects a gradient of effects with C1 (C1q, C1r, and C1s) deficiencies compromising uptake of apoptotic cells, generated daily as part of our tissue homeostasis. Persistence of the apoptotic cells due to compromised uptake leads to the release of free and histone-bound nucleic acids and stimulation of a type I interferon response, characteristic of SLE [22, 23]. The role of type I interferon is to drive the B cells past tolerance checkpoints, enhance antigen presentation, and stimulate neutrophil extracellular trap formation (NETs), which further exposes the immune system to nucleic acids [24, 25]. While true null variants are best recognized as monogenic etiologies for SLE, it is clear that copy number variants at C4 and acquired secondary deficiencies due to consumption also modestly predispose to SLE, reinforcing the importance of physiologic levels of classical pathway complement components [26–29].

The frequency of early classical pathway deficiencies in SLE cohorts is highly dependent on age. In an adult cohort, the frequency is in the range of 1% for cohorts that are largely of European ancestry [21] and this is driven almost entirely by C2 deficiency. The prevalence is much different in childhood-onset SLE. Frequencies as high as 15% have been reported in pediatric SLE [30]. In Brazil, the prevalence was 8%, with C2, C4, and C1 deficiencies nearly equivalently represented [31]. A large sequencing study of French and British patients with childhood-onset SLE (onset less than 16 years of age) found nearly 7% had a monogenic etiology and 5% of the cohort had clear C1 or C2 deficiencies [32]. Sequencing of an Indian cohort with the onset of SLE before 12 years of age led to the identification of 5% with C1 deficiency [33]. Complement deficiencies represent only a subset of genetic etiologies in pediatric-onset SLE. Lymphocyte checkpoint defects, interferonopathies, defects in apoptosis, and other types of defects in waste clearance comprise the other etiologies [34, 35].

C1 is a multi-subunit protein, C1q binds to antibodies, and C1r and C1s are the enzymatic components responsible for cleavage. C1q, C1r, and C1s deficiencies present nearly uniformly with early-onset systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [36–38]. The SLE is typically severe and can be present in infancy. The autoantibody profile is similar to that seen in other SLE patients, although anti-dsDNA antibodies may be less common [39, 40]. An additional phenotype is recurrent bacterial infections, most often systemic infections with encapsulated organisms, and infection is the most common cause of death [41]. All three deficiencies are inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Bone marrow transplant has been used successfully as a treatment for C1q deficiency as this component is produced in significant amounts by myeloid cells [42].

There are two distinct genes for C4, termed C4A and C4B. They encode highly homologous proteins, although C4A binds more avidly to protein while C4B binds more avidly to carbohydrates. C4A has additionally been shown to be critical for B cell tolerance in mice [43]. Within each C4 locus, there can be deletions or duplications or simple inactivating mutations [44, 45]. Partial C4 deficiencies are extremely common, with 1–2% of the general population and up to 15% of patients with SLE having complete C4A deficiency. A total of 1–2% of the population has complete C4B deficiency, and up to 15% of patients with the invasive bacterial disease are C4B deficient [46]. Overall, copy number variation is high at this locus in the general population, with low numbers of C4 gene copies associated with increased susceptibility to SLE and higher copy numbers associated with protection from SLE [47, 48]. Complete C4 deficiency due to four inactive alleles is quite rare, and the majority of patients with complete C4 deficiency have had SLE [49–51]. Some completely C4 deficient individuals have been asymptomatic, but most have had recurrent infections with encapsulated organisms with or without SLE. The SLE and infection phenotypes can be separate or occur together in an individual.

C2 deficiency is the most common of the inherited complement component deficiencies in people of Western European ancestry, with a frequency of 1:10 000. The common mutation is a 28bp deletion [21]. Most C2 deficient individuals are asymptomatic, although their susceptibility to infection is thought to be increased and their risk of myocardial infarction is increased. A total of 20–40% of C2 deficient individuals will develop SLE [4, 20, 52]. Anti-Ro antibodies are common in C2 deficient patients with SLE although anti-dsDNA antibodies are infrequent [53]. In spite of SLE being the most common phenotype, the most common cause of death among C2 deficient patients is sepsis [20]. Other systemic infections such as meningitis, pneumonia, epiglottitis, and peritonitis have been seen, and the most common organisms have been S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae. The risk of myocardial infarction is due to the role of early classical pathway components in the clearance of cholesterol [54]. Although the risk is most clearly defined for C2 deficient patients due to its frequency in the population, the risk is presumed to be comparable to deficiencies of the other early components, however, patients with C1 and C2 deficiencies have a shorter life expectancy overall due to the risk of death from infection [20, 41].

C3 deficiency is the least common of the complement cascade protein deficiencies, and it has a severe phenotype [55–59]. All patients have a profound predisposition to infection and the infections are characteristic of neutrophil dysfunction (abscesses), humoral deficiencies (sinopulmonary disease), and complement deficiencies (sepsis, meningitis). Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis occurs in a third of the cases of C3 deficiency and can be the presentation [4, 52]. One other feature of C3 deficiency deserves mention. During infections, a vasculitic rash may appear and symptoms of serum sickness may occasionally be seen. These unusual findings are due to the lack of immune complex solubilization by C3. C3 deficiency is rare, with fewer than 30 cases reported in the literature. There is a founder effect among the Afrikaans-speaking population [58]. Hypomorphic C3 mutations have been identified in some patients with autoimmune disease, but the phenotype and prevalence are not clear [60, 61].

The complement cascade requires exquisite tuning to prevent tissue damage and disease. Most GOF complement disorders lead to aHUS or another vasculopathy due to deposition of C3b on the surface of the vascular endothelium. Examples of this include factor B, C2, and C3 GOF function conditions. Loss of regulatory protein functions that control either activation or deposition of C3b, leads to a similar phenotype. A recent Australian registry report of aHUS found that defects in factor H were the most common (34%), followed by factor H related protein deletions (16%), diacylglycerol kinase (10%), thrombomodulin (8%), membrane cofactor protein (6%), factor B (2%), and factor I (2%) [62]. Other reports have added additional genes and have reported slightly different distributions although factor H deficiencies have consistently been the most common genetic etiology for aHUS [63]. A similar array of deficiency states has been described in HELLP (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets) occurring during pregnancy [64, 65].

In C3 glomerulopathy, an umbrella designation for renal diseases where C3 is deposited without immunoglobulins detected by immunofluorescence, including dense deposit disease and C3 glomerulonephritis, the etiologies identified have included antibodies that stabilize the C3 convertase (C3 nephritic factors), antibodies that stabilize the C5 convertase (C5 nephritic factors), C3 GOF variants, and isolated cases of factor H, factor I and CR1 deficiencies [66–69]. Monoclonal gammopathies have also been reported and are thought to act as a type of nephritic factor with, specific complement-directed antibodies identified in some cases [70]. C3 nephritic factors are found in 80% of patients with dense deposit disease and can also drive lipodystrophy [66].

When GOF variants were described for C1R and C1S, it was therefore surprising that the phenotype related to connective tissue. The mechanism remains speculative, however, these two proteins are enzymes as opposed to the other GOF disorders described. A subset of patients with GOF in C1R or C1S does have evidence of a CNS inflammatory process thought to be due to a CNS vasculopathy, more consistent with the more typical theme of GOF in the complement system.

Recognition of these newer phenotypes may allow the implementation of targeted therapeutics as the array of complement inhibitors increases [71]. The use of complement inhibitors has transformed the care of patients with HUS.

Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (EDS) are a group of clinically and genetically heterogeneous connective tissue disorders. Their overlapping features include joint hypermobility, skin and vascular fragility, and generalized connective tissue friability. Clinical criteria for periodontal EDS (pEDS) have been defined [72]. Major criteria are (1) severe and intractable early-onset periodontitis, (2) gingival detachment, (3) pretibial plaques, and (4) family history. Minor criteria consist of (1) easy bruising, (2) joint hypermobility (mostly distal joints), (3) skin hyperextensibility and fragility, (4) abnormal scarring, (5) increased rate of infections, (6) hernias, (7) marfanoid facial features, (8) acrogeria, and (9) prominent vasculature. Three major criteria and one of the minor criteria constitute the minimal criteria for diagnosis. The most specific and consistent clinical manifestation of periodontal EDS in children is a generalized lack of attached gingiva [73]. GOF mutations in C1r and C1s have been associated with this phenotype in approximately 50% of the cases [74, 75]. Some atypical features such as gingival granulomas and gingival hyperplasia have been described [76]. An additional case series of two kindreds (8 subjects with GOF variants in C1R and pEDS) reported seven patients who also suffered from various manifestations related to leukoencephalopathy [74]. The MRI pattern in the affected adults was similar to that seen in cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) [77, 78]. CADASIL can also be due to NOTCH3 mutations.

Serum levels of complement components are usually normal. One hypothesis regarding mechanisms of disease relates to the secretion of biologically active C1r and C1s proteins, which degrade collagen locally [74]. The vasculopathy may contribute to the leukoencephalopathy phenotype by altering the blood-brain barrier and the capillaries within the brain. The phenotype is somewhat similar to LOF variants in Factor I associated with aseptic meningitis and leukoencephalopathy [79, 80]. In the case of LOF Factor I variants, the mechanism is thought to relate to diffuse alternative pathway complement activation on vascular endothelium affecting the parenchyma and the blood-brain barrier.

Relatively few patients with GOF variants in C2 have been reported [81, 82]. The two variants reported (in three patients) led to stabilization of the C3 convertase and increased complement deposition in various models [83]. The phenotype has been glomerulopathy with characteristics of aHUS. The disease presented in adulthood as nephrotic syndrome, leading to chronic kidney disease in all three patients. The biopsies demonstrated intense staining for C3 with minimal IgG and IgM. C1q deposition was not detected in biopsies. Serum levels of C3 and C4 were normal.

Mutations in the genes encoding the complement regulatory factors: factor H, factor I, and membrane cofactor protein (MCP; CD46) have been identified as causal in approximately half of the patients with aHUS. Activating mutations of C3 and factor B can also be associated with aHUS [84, 85]. The patients with either GOF variants in C3 or factor B have chronic C3 activation and consumption of C3. The mechanism is thus concordant with the patients who have a loss of regulatory activity of factor H, factor I, or MCP where the loss of regulation leads to complement deposition on renal (and macular) endothelial cells [64, 65, 75]. The typical phenotype is HUS arising independently of toxigenic E. coli infection, i.e., atypical HUS. Regulatory protein variants, but not yet GOF variants in C3, have also been found in women with preeclampsia and HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets) in pregnancy. This suggests that stressors can drive the triggering of clinically evident complement dysregulation.

Mild variants in factor H, factor B, and C3, common in the general population [86], are associated with an increased risk of macular degeneration. The drusen characteristic of macular degeneration is largely comprised of complement split products. Additional rare variants in C9 and factor H have also been identified [87]. At least some of the C3 variants associated with macular degeneration lead to compromised interaction with the regulatory protein factor H and thus lead to gain of activation [88].

LOF and GOF variants define the extremes of complement function. The GOF variants in C1R and C1S revealed a previously unknown role for complement. The roles of complement in inflammatory processes impacting the brain and retina have been highlighted by the devastating CNS inflammatory processes related to complement activation on endothelial cells [79, 89]. This has directly led to the testing of complement inhibition for septic encephalopathy and other conditions where the blood-brain barrier is thought to be impacted. Similarly, the loss of complement function driving SLE pathogenesis led to the testing of various strategies to supplement complement therapeutically. Even partial deficiencies and secondary deficiencies of early classical pathway components are associated with a risk of developing SLE, suggesting that tuning this pathway is critically important. The classical pathway of complement, recognized since 1891 as an “alexin,” meaning to ward off or kill, is now recognized as central to immune homeostasis, performing diverse critical roles in host defense and removal of waste.
