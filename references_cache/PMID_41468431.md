# NFE2L1/Nrf1 forms a coactivator complex post-peptide:N-glycanase-mediated sequence editing and mitigates proteasome dysfunction.
**Authors:** Yoshida Y, Okada M, Arai N, Ando C, Kinoshita D, Nishito Y, Kametani F, Hasegawa M, Tanaka K, Endo A
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2517547123](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2517547123)

## Content

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-like 1 (NFE2L1/Nrf1), an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-associated transcription factor, is responsible for the coordinated expression of proteasome subunit genes upon proteasomal dysfunction. N-glycosylated proteins undergo protein sequence editing by peptide:N-glycanase (NGLY1)-mediated conversion of N-glycosylated asparagine residues to aspartic acid. Nrf proteins are the only transcription factors that undergo sequence editing for transcriptional activation. However, the mechanism via which sequence editing regulates the transcriptional activity of Nrf1 has remained unclear. Here, we demonstrated that sequence editing of the ninth N-glycosylation site (Asn574) in human Nrf1 is required for proteasome gene expression in HeLa cells. Editing of Asn574 is essential for interaction with host cell factor C1 and O-GlcNAc transferase, which is required for Nrf1 chromatin binding and sufficient proteasome expression. Furthermore, sequence editing of N-glycosylation sites other than Asn574 is required for the interaction with the coactivator CREBBP/EP300, thereby enhancing Nrf1's transcriptional activity. Unexpectedly, the expression of Nrf1 mutants that mimic proteolytic processing by DNA-damage-inducible 1 homolog 2 and sequence editing by NGLY1 markedly diminished the growth rate in HeLa cells, suggesting that the constitutive activation of Nrf1 exhibits cytotoxicity. Collectively, our study explains the strategy of on-demand Nrf1 activation for survival benefits. Nrf1 is synthesized as a proteasome-targeting protein and is highly glycosylated in the ER. Nrf1 is activated via sequence editing-dependent coactivator complex formation only when the proteasome needs to be compensated for.

The 26S proteasome is a large proteolytic complex composed of 33 distinct subunits, and numerous genes involved in protein ubiquitination collectively constitute the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS), an ATP-dependent protein degradation system in eukaryotic cells (1–3). The UPS plays an essential role in regulating a variety of cellular events, including the cell cycle, signal transduction, DNA repair, and maintenance of proteostasis via the degradation of damaged and misfolded proteins (4). Proteasome dysfunction has been implicated in neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s diseases (5, 6). Proteasomal integrity is regulated at multiple levels, including gene expression, complex assembly, and posttranslational modification of subunits (7). Proteasome dysfunction induces the proteasome gene expression via activation of the transcription factor, nuclear factor erythroid 2-like 1 (NFE2L1/Nrf1) (8, 9).

NFE2L (also known as Nrf) transcription factors belong to the family of basic leucine zipper (bZip)-containing transcriptional regulators. These factors are characterized by a conserved cap “n” collar (CNC) motif (10). Vertebrate genomes encode three Nrf transcription family members: Nrf1, Nrf2 (NFE2L2), and Nrf3 (NFE2L3). Nrf1 and Nrf3 are endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-associated proteins that undergo glycosylation, whereas Nrf2 is a cytosolic protein. These Nrf transcription factors heterodimerize with the small MAF (sMAF) proteins, MAFF, MAFG, and MAFK. Nrf/sMAF dimers recognize a DNA sequence motif called the antioxidant response element (ARE). All Nrf members modulate the expression of ARE-containing genes; however, they play distinct roles in various cellular functions. While Nrf2 functions in oxidative stress response, Nrf1 is responsible for the expression of the proteasome subunits during proteasome inhibition (11).

Nrf1 is a transmembrane protein in the ER with a small N-terminal portion protruding into the cytosol. Most polypeptide is modified with nine N-glycans and embedded in the ER lumen. The domains responsible for transcriptional activation and DNA binding near the C-terminal portion are located in the ER. Under steady-state conditions, Nrf1 in the ER is constitutively processed by the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) machinery. During this process, Nrf1 is ubiquitinated by the HRD1-SEL1L complex, segregated by the AAA-ATPase p97 complex, and degraded by the proteasome. Under conditions where proteasome activity is impaired, Nrf1 escapes degradation and is transported to the nucleus, where it induces the transcription of proteasome subunit genes, thereby restoring proteasome activity (12, 13). This process of Nrf1 activation, termed proteasome bounce back, requires two cytosolic enzymes, DNA-damage inducible 1 homolog 2 (DDI2) aspartic protease and peptide: N-glycanase (NGLY1) (14–16). Removal of the N-terminal ~100 amino acids of Nrf1, including the transmembrane domain, by DDI2 and de-N-glycosylation by NGLY1 are both essential for the effective nuclear translocation and transcription of proteasome subunit genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Unlike the observed in vertebrates, Nrf function depends on single skn-1 and CncC genes in Caenorhabditis elegans and Drosophila, respectively (13). In C. elegans, three SKN-1A/B/C proteins are produced from a single skn-1 gene. SKN-1A, the longest form of SKN-1, is a vertebrate Nrf1 ortholog with four N-glycosylation sites. Recent studies have demonstrated that the conversion of N-glycosylated asparagine (N) residues in SKN-1A to aspartic acid (D) by PNG-1/NGLY1, a process called “sequence editing,” is required for the activation of proteasome subunit gene expression (17). In humans, sequence editing of Nrf1 is also essential for the transcriptional induction of proteasome subunit genes (18). However, the molecular mechanisms via which Nrf1 and its orthologs are activated by sequence editing remained unclear.

In this study, we showed that the conversion of N to D at the ninth N-glycosylation site (N574) in human Nrf1 led to its interaction with a host cell factor C1 (HCFC1). Furthermore, sequence editing of other N-glycosylation sites is required for the interaction with CREBBP/EP300. The combination of these two sets of interactions with distinct coactivators is responsible for the maximal transcriptional activity of Nrf1, which is required for proteasomal gene expression. Unexpectedly, the expression of constitutively active Nrf1 mutants mimicking the sequence editing exhibited cytotoxicity. This suggests that Nrf1 must be inactivated under normal conditions but is activated on demand. Therefore, we propose that the Nrf1 activation machinery involves multistep regulation, stabilization, cleavage, and deglycosylation-mediated sequence editing.

Cytosolic NGLY1 deglycosylates Nrf1 and induces the transcriptional activity of proteasome subunit genes in response to proteasome inhibitors (16, 18). In NGLY1-knockout (KO) cells, nine high-mannose glycans conjugated to Nrf1 were retained or converted to N-GlcNAc by ENGASE, another deglycosylation enzyme, and the resultant Nrf1 was unable to induce the transcription of proteasome genes in the presence of proteasome inhibitors (18, 19) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). To investigate the effects of sequence editing on Nrf1 functions, we utilized glycan-less Nrf1 mutants, 9NA and 9ND, in which all nine putative N-glycosylation sites [(N-X-S/T; asparagine (N), serine (S), threonine (T), and X (any amino acid except proline)] were replaced with alanine (A) and aspartic acid (D). Our previous study demonstrated that 9ND phenocopied activated wild-type (WT) Nrf1 in NGLY1-KO cells when treated with the proteasome inhibitor, bortezomib (Btz). This finding confirmed that 9ND mimics the sequence-edited form of WT Nrf1.

To comprehensively understand the effects of Nrf1 sequence editing on the transcriptome, a microarray analysis was performed on NGLY1-KO HeLa cells stably expressing 9NA and 9ND (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Dataset S1). Compared to NGLY1-KO cells (control), in which endogenous Nrf1 is inactivated due to the loss of NGLY1-mediated sequence editing, the expression of 9NA and 9ND increased the transcription of 357 and 1,048 genes, respectively, with a Log2 fold change greater than 1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Although the majority of genes that were upregulated by 9ND contained AREs (MAF: NFE2 or NFE2L1 motifs) in their promoters, genes that were specifically upregulated by 9NA did not. These motifs were found in regions relatively distant from transcription start sites (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C and Datasets S2 and S3). One-fourth of the genes upregulated by 9ND expression were also stimulated by 9NA expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). These genes were enriched in several functional categories, including responses to viruses, defense responses to symbionts and glutathione catabolic processes (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D and Dataset S1). Notably, upregulation of three-fourths of genes, including all 26S proteasome subunit genes and several proteasome-related genes, was specifically induced in the cells expressing 9ND (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Figs. S2D and S3). However, the expression of immunoproteasome subunits (PSMB8-10), the thymoproteasome subunit (PSMB11), most proteasome-interacting proteins (PIPs), and proteasome activators other than PA200 (PSME4) was not induced by 9ND. Interestingly, sequence-edited Nrf1 also induces the expression of THAP1, which was recently reported as a transcription factor for the basal expression of PSMB5 (20). Reports have shown that POMP, an assembly factor for the 20S proteasome (21), is a target gene of Nrf3 (22), an Nrf1 homolog. We found that POMP was induced by 9ND, whereas PAC1-4, other proteasome assembly factors, were not (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In addition to proteasome-related genes, most Nrf1 target genes, including NGLY1 and those responsive to heavy metal ions (zinc, copper, and cadmium) such as metallothionein, were upregulated by 9ND but not by 9NA. These suggest that Nrf1 induces the majority of its target genes, including all proteasome subunits, in a sequence editing-dependent manner. We further examined the expression of some genes encoding proteasome subunits after treatment with Btz by qPCR analysis. In NGLY1-KO cells, the transcription of PSMB1 (encoding a subunit of the 20S proteasome), PSMC4 (encoding an ATPase in the 19S proteasome), and PSMD3 (encoding a non-ATPase in the 19S proteasome) was not induced by treatment with Btz, confirming that NGLY1 is required for Nrf1-mediated transcription of proteasome subunit genes (Fig. 1B). The sequence-edited forms of 9ND and WT Nrf1 induced proteasome gene expression in response to Btz treatment, whereas 9NA did not (Fig. 1 B and C). These results indicated that the sequence editing of Nrf1 by NGLY1 is essential for the transcriptional activity of proteasome genes.

To further examine whether sequence editing of Nrf1 by NGLY1 enhanced resistance to Btz, we evaluated the viability of NGLY1-KO and Nrf1-KO cells in the presence of Btz (Fig. 1D). The LD50 values for Btz in WT, NGLY1-KO, and Nrf1-KO HeLa cells were 35, 23, and 17 nM, respectively. A reduction in the LD50 of Btz due to deficiencies in NGLY1 and Nrf1 was observed. Conversely, the LD50 of Btz of cells overexpressing Nrf1, whose expression was only a few times higher than the endogenous level, was elevated to 50 nM (Fig. 1D). These results suggested that sequence editing of Nrf1 by NGLY1 resulted in resistance to Btz due to induction of proteasomal gene expression.

Nematode SKN-1A and human Nrf1 contain four and nine N-glycosylation sites, respectively. However, whether sequence editing of specific N-glycosylation sites in Nrf1 is important for the expression of proteasome subunit genes remains unclear. To identify the N-to-D conversion site(s) in Nrf1 that are critical for its transcriptional activity, we constructed Nrf1 mutants mimicking a single N-to-D conversion (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). In these mutants, the nine N-glycosylation sites were substituted with eight A residues and a single D. N9D, mimicking a single N-to-D conversion at N574, effectively induced the expression of the proteasome subunit genes after treatment with Btz, whereas the other mutants mimicking a single N-to-D conversion at glycosylation sites other than N574 were insufficient (Fig. 2A).

To verify the contribution of sequence editing at the ninth glycosylation site (N574) to Nrf1 activity, we constructed additional Nrf1 mutants (Fig. 2B). 8NXA(1–8) contains mutations in glycosylation consensus motifs other than N574. Therefore, only N574 was glycosylated and converted to D but not the other eight N. 8NXA(1–8) induced the transcription of the proteasome subunit genes upon treatment with Btz, as well as the N9D, mutant. This demonstrated that deglycosylation at the N574 site of Nrf1 is required for the transcription of proteasome subunit genes. However, compared to WT Nrf1, N9D, and 8NXA(1–8) were not sufficient to fully induce PSMC4 and PSMD3 expression, suggesting that further sequence editing at other glycosylation sites is necessary for authentic transcriptional activity of Nrf1. In contrast, the N9NXA mutant, in which N574 is not glycosylated due to the mutation of T576 in the glycosylation consensus motif, did not exhibit any induction of proteasome gene expression upon treatment with Btz. These results indicated the indispensable role of sequence editing of the glycosylated N574 of Nrf1 by NGLY1 in the transcription of proteasome subunit genes.

Next, we verified that sequence-edited Nrf1 restored proteasome activity. As previously reported, proteasome activity can recover from its inhibition through two distinct mechanisms: dissociation of inhibitors from the active site or reconstitution of active proteasomes from newly synthesized subunits (8). Recovery of proteasome activity from reversible inhibitors such as Btz or MG132 involves both pathways. Conversely, the cells exposed to covalent inhibitors, such as carfilzomib (Cfz), rely on proteasome regeneration. Treatments with MG132 and Cfz induced the transcription of proteasome subunit genes to a level comparable to that of Btz treatment in an Nrf1 sequence editing-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). To assess the functional recovery of proteasomes after the washout of Btz and Cfz, we measured chymotryptic peptidase activity (Fig. 2 C, Left). In WT HeLa cells, peptidase activity was lost after treatment with 50 nM Btz for 5 h and was fully restored 15 h after drug washout. In Nrf1-KO cells, transcription of proteasome subunit genes was scarcely induced (Fig. 1B), yet the activity was partially recovered after drug washout, suggesting the presence of proteasome-inducing factors other than Nrf1 (Fig. 2C). Notably, in Nrf1-KO cells, peptidase activity was fully restored by Nrf1 and 9ND expression after drug washout, whereas recovery by N9D expression was only partial, consistent with their transcriptional activities. Furthermore, the peptidase activity restored by the washout of Btz was higher than that restored by Cfz, confirming that recovery after Btz treatment occurs via two mechanisms: generation of new proteasomes and the dissociation of the inhibitor from existing proteasomes in cells. Indeed, the abundance of active 26S proteasomes was correlated with the extent of peptidase activity (Fig. 2 C, Right). These results indicate that the Nrf1-mediated synthesis of new proteasomes restores 26S proteasome assembly.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms via which sequence editing of Nrf1 confers its transcriptional activity, we performed interactome analysis using mass spectrometry. We first screened for proteins that interacted with WT Nrf1 when the proteasome was inhibited (Fig. 3A and Dataset S4). Focusing on the potential transcriptional regulators that cooperate with Nrf1, we identified the sMAF proteins, MAFG, MAFF, and MAFK, which form heterodimers and bind to ARE motifs (10), and FBXW11, an E3 ubiquitin ligase subunit for Nrf1 in the nucleus (23). Furthermore, OGT formed a complex with HCFC1, which has been reported to interact directly with Nrf1 (24, 25). In addition, proteins known to interact with Nrf2, such as KEAP1, EP300, CREBBP1, and p62, have been identified (26–28).

To further explore the proteins that interact with Nrf1 in a sequence editing-dependent manner, we compared the interactome of 9NA and 9ND under proteasome inhibition conditions (Fig. 3B and Dataset S5). Notably, the majority of Nrf1-interacting proteins, including sMAF, were associated with both 9NA and 9ND. However, HCFC1 and OGT specifically interacted with 9ND. We confirmed the interaction of these proteins with WT Nrf1 and its mutants by western blotting their immunoprecipitates (IP-western blot). Consistent with the results of proteomic analyses shown in Fig. 3 A and B, MAFF and p62 interacted with both 9ND and 9NA, and HCFC and OGT interacted with 9ND, but not with 9NA, in the presence of Btz (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, we observed that the interaction between HCFC1, OGT, and WT Nrf1 was abolished in NGLY1-KO cells (Fig. 3D). These results demonstrated that NGLY1-mediated sequence editing of Nrf1 is essential for its interaction with HCFC1 and OGT.

We aimed to identify the N to D conversion site required for interaction with the HCFC1-OGT complex by IP-western blot analyses (Fig. 3E). We found that N9D, but not the other mutants, interacted with HCFC1-OGT as effectively as WT Nrf1. Considering that N9D induces proteasome gene expression (Fig. 2), we hypothesized that sequence editing of the ninth glycosylation site of Nrf1 leads to its interaction with the HCFC1-OGT complex, thereby inducing proteasome gene expression.

Sequence editing at N574 of Nrf1 using NGLY1 generates the HCFC1 binding motif (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the HCFC-binding motif generated by NGLY1 was found in Nrf3, a homolog of Nrf1. Considering that the consensus sequence for HCFC1 binding is (D/E) HXY (29), we examined whether N9E interacted with the HCFC1-OGT complex and induced proteasome gene expression. However, N9E did not interact with HCFC1-OGT or promote the expression of proteasome subunit genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and C). Since the HCFC1 binding motif is inherently present in the nematode SKN-1A, SKN-1A may interact with the HCFC1-OGT complex without sequence editing. It is therefore hypothesized that the regulatory mechanism of the sequence editing-dependent interaction with HCFC1-OGT is an evolutionary adaptation in higher organisms.

Studies have shown that HCFC1 is necessary for the binding of Nrf1 to the promoter regions of proteasome subunit genes and that OGT stabilizes Nrf1 (24, 25). These effects, via direct interactions, are believed to increase Nrf1’s transcriptional activity (Fig. 4 A–C). Depletion of OGT or HCFC1 using siRNAs suppressed the induction of proteasome subunit genes in response to Btz, confirming that both OGT and HCFC1 are required for Nrf1 transcriptional activity in the proteasome, as reported previously (Fig. 4C). While the depletion of HCFC1 did not affect OGT expression, OGT depletion significantly increased HCFC1 expression (Fig. 4A). OGT is a multifunctional protein with both O-GlcNAc transferase and cleavage activities, which are required for the maturation of HCFC1. HCFC1 is synthesized as a >200 kDa protein and is cleaved by OGT at a series of six 26-residues proteolytic repeats in the central portion (30, 31). OGT requires both O-GlcNAc transferase and cleavage activities to cleave HCFC1. Proteolytic maturation of HCFC1 is believed to be essential for its function (32). Consistent with these reports, the levels of the full-length precursor, HCFC1(Fl), which was barely detected in control cells, increased, and that of the O-GlcNAc-modified form (P) decreased upon OGT depletion (Fig. 4B). However, despite the increase in HCFC1 mRNA levels upon OGT depletion, the protein levels did not appear to increase. Notably, HCFC1 depletion upregulated endogenous Nrf1 protein levels; however, OGT depletion negligibly affected Nrf1 stability under our experimental conditions (Fig. 4 B and C).

Since OGT associates with Nrf1 via HCFC1, HCFC1 knockdown abolished the interaction of OGT with Nrf1 but did not affect dimerization with MAFF in the presence of Btz (Fig. 4D). Notably, the full-length precursor of HCFC1 interacted with Nrf1 in Btz-treated cells after OGT depletion, suggesting that association of Nrf1 with the mature HCFC1 generated by OGT is required for the transcriptional activity.

Next, we examined the nuclear transport of Nrf1 mutants in the presence of Btz. We found that WT Nrf1 and its mutants that interacted with HCFC1-OGT, 9ND, and N9D, exhibited enhanced nuclear localization compared to the mutants that were unable to interact with HCFC1-OGT, 8ND(N9A), or 9NA (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). To determine whether the 9NA mutant shuttles between the cytosol and the nucleus in Btz-treated cells, we used leptomycin B (LMB) to inhibit nuclear export (33). LMB treatment resulted in the accumulation of 9NA in the nucleus, suggesting its export into the cytoplasm (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). To further verify the effect of the HCFC1-OGT interaction on the nuclear export of Nrf1, we depleted HCFC1 or OGT in HeLa cells and analyzed the nuclear localization of Nrf1 (Fig. 4F and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). Although OGT knockdown did not affect the nuclear localization of Nrf1, loss of HCFC1 reduced the rate of Nrf1 nuclear localization in Btz-treated cells, suggesting that HCFC1 enhances the nuclear retention of Nrf1. Consistently, HCFC1 knockdown increased the cytoplasmic Nrf1 levels (Fig. 4G, lanes 4 and 5). In addition, the knockdown of both OGT and HCFC1 led to the inhibition of Nrf1 chromatin binding, indicating that binding of the HCFC1-OGT complex is necessary for Nrf1 to bind to chromatin (Fig. 4G, lanes 11–15). Taken together, Nrf1 recruitment to AREs on the chromatin may require binding to HCFC1, which is activated by OGT (Fig. 4H).

We demonstrated that the binding of HCFC1-OGT to Nrf1 sequence-edited at N574 is essential for the transcription of proteasome subunit genes. However, the transcriptional activity of N9D was lower than that of WT Nrf1. In addition, depletion of OGT or HCFC1 did not completely inhibit the induction of the proteasomal genes (Figs. 2B and 4C). This implied that the sequence editing at glycosylation sites other than N574 is involved in Nrf1 activation. To determine the sequence editing sites necessary for sufficient Nrf1 activation, we constructed Nrf1 mutants lacking N-glycosylation consensus motifs and examined the expression of proteasome subunits. Although 2NXA and 4NXA did not alter the gene expression of proteasome subunits, 4NXA reduced the basal expression levels of PSMD3. In contrast, 3NXA and 6NXA partially reduced the gene expression of the proteasome subunits. These results suggested that sequence editing at multiple sites is required for sufficient Nrf1 activation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A).

We hypothesized that sequence editing at residues other than N574 would also affect the Nrf1 interactome in addition to an N574D-specific interaction with HCFC1-OGT. To identify these proteins, we compared the interactomes of 9ND and N9D (Fig. 5A and Dataset S6) and identified two histone acetyltransferases (HAT), CREBBP and its homolog EP300, as 9ND-specific interacting proteins. CREBBP/EP300 plays an essential role as a coactivator of many classes of transcription factors (34), and promotes Nrf2 activity via direct interaction (27, 35). Both proteins are associated more effectively with 9ND and 8ND(N9A) than with 9NA and N9D in the presence of Btz (Fig. 5B), suggesting that sequence editing other than N574D was involved in the interaction with CREBBP/EP300.

Next, we examined the effect of CREBBP on the transcriptional activity of Nrf1 (Fig. 5C). siRNA-mediated depletion of CREBBP suppresses the expression of proteasome subunit genes in response to Btz treatment. Importantly, double knockdown of CREBBP and HCFC1 showed an additive effect. Treatment with A485, a competitive catalytic inhibitor of acetyl-CoA CREBBP/ EP300 (36), slightly upregulated proteasome subunit gene expression without Btz treatment and reduced the expression of proteasome subunit genes in Btz-treated cells (Fig. 5D and Dataset S7). Furthermore, depletion of HCFC1 or OGT and treatment with A485 had additive effects on the suppression of Nrf1 transcriptional activity.

Collectively, these data showed that sequence editing of multiple sites in the NST region of Nrf1 facilitates its interaction with CREBBP/EP300, thereby inducing Nrf1 transcriptional activity.

Maintenance of proteostasis is critical for preventing age-related neurodegenerative pathologies characterized by the accumulation of unfolded, misfolded, or aggregated proteins (37). Recent reports indicate that Nrf1 signaling is downregulated in the brains of patients with Alzheimer’s disease (38) and that increasing SKN-1A levels promote longevity in C. elegans (39). Consequently, Nrf1 activation is essential for the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. To examine whether constant activation of Nrf1 enhances cellular resilience against stress, we expressed Nrf1 mutants that were not degraded by the ERAD pathway. We constructed and expressed cytosolic Nrf1 mutants. As these mutants lack N-terminal 103 amino acids, they are expected to evade ER targeting, degradation by the ERAD pathway, and authentic N-glycosylation-dependent sequence editing, and to mimic proteolytic processing by DDI2 (Fig. 6 A, Left). We confirmed that ΔN WT, which skips N-glycosylation-dependent sequence editing, was unable to induce the expression of proteasome subunit genes in the presence of Btz. In contrast, ΔN 9ND and ΔN N9D exhibited transcriptional activity regardless of the presence of Btz (Fig. 6 A, Right).

Contrary to our expectations, we observed that the cells stably expressing ΔN 9ND and ΔN N9D proliferated poorly. Therefore, we expressed these mutants in WT, NGLY1-KO, and Nrf1-KO HeLa cells and evaluated cell viability 7 d after lentiviral transduction. The expression of uneditable ΔN WT negligibly affected cell growth, similar to that observed with full-length WT expression. However, expression of constitutively active ΔN 9ND and ΔN N9D resulted in growth defects. This suggests that the constitutive transcriptional activity of Nrf1 in the absence of proteasome inhibitors exerts deleterious effects on cells (Fig. 6B). Although sequence-edited Nrf1 primarily induced proteasome gene expression in the presence of a proteasome inhibitor, our microarray data demonstrated that 9ND also induced the expression of NIK/NF-kappaB signaling, interleukin 1, and mitochondrial proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Dataset S1, GO_9ND_up tab). The constitutive expression of active Nrf1 may result in cellular damage, potentially due to the unregulated expression of target genes including proteasome subunits.

Taken together, these results suggest the physiological significance of temporal regulation of the Nrf1 activation machinery. The elaborate system involves the synthesis of Nrf1 as a glycoprotein in the ER and multistep activation, including stabilization by proteasome inhibition, processing by DDI2, and deglycosylation-dependent sequence editing by NGLY1, which prevents unnecessary Nrf1 activation and protects cells from detrimental effects.

In this study, we revealed that the sequence editing of Nrf1 by NGLY1-mediated deglycosylation facilitated the formation of Nrf1 transcriptional complexes with HCFC1-OGT and CREBBP/EP300 coactivators, thereby inducing the expression of proteasome subunit genes under proteasomal inhibition. Furthermore, we highlighted the significance of transient Nrf1 activation in the maintenance of cellular health by demonstrating the detrimental effects of the constant expression of active Nrf1.

We demonstrated that the HCFC1-OGT complex binds to the HCFC1-binding consensus motif extrinsically generated by sequence editing of N574 near the CNC/bZIP regions that is essential for DNA binding. The interaction of the HCFC1-OGT complex with Nrf1 is essential for inducing proteasome gene expression. The extrinsic HCFC1-binding motif generated by NGLY1 was also found in Nrf3, which is synthesized as an ER-associated glycoprotein similar to Nrf1 (Fig. 3F). Coincidentally, the PSMB5-specific transcription factor, THAP1, also binds to the HCFC1-OGT complex via an intrinsic HCFC1-binding motif (40), suggesting that the regulation of proteasome expression by different transcription factors is predominantly controlled by HCFC1-OGT. ChIP-seq analysis has revealed that the HCFC1-OGT complex is necessary for Nrf1 to bind to the promoter regions of the proteasome subunit gene loci (25). The maturation of HCFC1 requires O-GlcNAcylation and cleavage (30, 31). We showed that HCFC1 retains Nrf1 in the nucleus and that the maturation of HCFC1 by OGT is required for Nrf1 chromatin binding and transcriptional activation (Fig. 4H). SKN-1A, the nematode ortholog of Nrf1, has four N-glycosylation sites. Single-site sequence editing at any N-glycosylation sites is insufficient for the full induction of proteasome subunit genes, although editing of N338 is the most effective among them (17). Although both Nrf1 and SKN-1A require NGLY1/PNG-1-mediated sequence editing for activation, only SKN-1A possesses an intrinsic HCFC1-binding consensus motif, indicating that the sequence editing-mediated activation mechanism differs in humans and nematodes.

Sequence editing of Nrf1 was necessary for HCFC1-OGT and CREBBP/EP300 binding, leading to proteasome expression (Figs. 3B and 5 A, C, and D). Promoter motif analysis of genes upregulated by Nrf1 revealed that AREs (MAF: NFE2 and NFE2L1) were predominantly located in regions distal to the transcription start sites (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D and Datasets S2 and S3). These results are consistent with the findings of ChIP-seq profiling of Nrf2 targets (41) and the common understanding that CREBBP/EP300 coactivators preferentially act at enhancers (42). Similar to Nrf2, whose maximal transcriptional activity depends on CREBBP/EP300 (27), Nrf1 is proposed to require these coactivators for sufficient transcriptional activation of proteasome subunits. Nrf2 binds to CREBBP/EP300 via two nonstructural domains, Neh4 and Neh5 (27). These domains are structurally stabilized by binding to the TAZ1 and TAZ2 domains of CREBBP/EP300 (35). The Neh5 domain is conserved in Nrf1 (residues 206 to 235), but the sequence editing sites necessary for Nrf1 activation are located outside this region. The NST region, which contains eight glycosylation sites, was predicted to have a large, disordered structure using the AlphaFold protein structure model (AF-Q14494-F1-v4). Further studies are required to elucidate the mechanisms via which sequence editing enhances the binding between CREBBP/EP300 and Nrf1.

The TIP60/KAT5-RUVBL1-RAVBL2 complex has been reported to be crucial for the Nrf1-dependent induction of proteasome subunit genes (43). We detected interaction between Nrf1 and RUVBL1-RAVBL2 in our interactome analysis that did not rely on sequence editing.

Expression of constitutively active forms of truncated Nrf1(ΔN-9ND and ΔN-N9D) led to unexpected results. Although expression of the truncated WT, which retains the intact sequence but evades glycosylation and subsequent N-to-D editing, exhibited negligible effects on cell proliferation, cells expressing ΔN-9ND and ΔN-N9D exhibited severe defects in proliferation. Similarly, transgenic C. elegans strains expressing SKN-1A, whose N terminus was truncated and all four N-glycosylation sites were substituted with Asp, showed markedly reduced growth rates (44). These findings suggest that continuous degradation of Nrf1 via the ERAD pathway prevents constitutive activation and benefits cell survival strategies. However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. One hypothesis is that the excessive production of proteasomes, which are inherently stable and abundant in cells, may be responsible for damaging the cells. Alternatively, aberrant induction of Nrf1 target genes unrelated to proteasome biogenesis may contribute to cellular damage (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Dataset S1). Notably, proteasome inhibitors are clinically applied for the treatment of cancers such as multiple myeloma, but Nrf1-mediated restoration of proteasome activity counteracts their cytotoxic efficacy. In contrast, our observations raise the possibility that persistent Nrf1 activation elicits anticancer effects. Although the present study did not clarify the precise mechanism underlying the cytotoxicity of constitutively active Nrf1, elucidating this mechanism is important for further studies.

Two significant regulations are involved in the synthesis of Nrf1 in the ER. First, N-glycosylation of Nrf1 is essential for sequence editing by NGLY1 and its subsequent activation. Second, Nrf1 must undergo constant degradation via the ERAD pathway to prevent the detrimental effects of unnecessary activation. Consistently, Nrf1 contains large unstructured regions that are possibly recognized as ERAD-targeting signals. Cells need to restore proteasome activity via Nrf1 because age-dependent accumulation of misfolded and aggregation-prone proteins is implicated in various diseases (38, 39, 45). A comprehensive understanding of the activation mechanisms of Nrf1 would be useful for developing effective therapeutic strategies to overcome adult-onset neurodegenerative diseases. Our findings highlight the importance of mitigating the potential toxicity of Nrf1-mediated restoration of the proteasomal activity.

HeLa cells were cultured for 57 h after siRNA transfection, treated with 20 nM Btz for 15 h, harvested using trypsin-EDTA, and counted. Cells (2 × 106) were subjected to subcellular protein fractionation using a kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat#78840).

Prism 10.2 (GraphPad Software) was used for the statistical analysis of the experiments. Statistical tests are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Numerical data are shown as means ± SD of the indicated biological replicates. P < 0.05 was considered significant.
