# GIMAP5 deficiency reveals a mammalian ceramide-driven longevity assurance pathway.
**Authors:** Park AY, Leney-Greene M, Lynberg M, Gabrielski JQ, Xu X, Schwarz B, Zheng L, Balasubramaniyam A, Ham H, Chao B, Zhang Y, Matthews HF, Cui J, Yao Y, Kubo S, Chanchu JM, Morawski AR, Cook SA, Jiang P, Ravell JC, Cheng YH, George A, Faruqi A, Pagalilauan AM, Bergerson JRE, Ganesan S, Chauvin SD, Aluri J, Edwards-Hicks J, Bohrnsen E, Tippett C, Omar H, Xu L, Butcher GW, Pascall J, Karakoc-Aydiner E, Kiykim A, Maecker H, Tezcan İ, Esenboga S, Heredia RJ, Akata D, Tekin S, Kara A, Kuloglu Z, Unal E, Kendirli T, Dogu F, Karabiber E, Atkinson TP, Cochet C, Filhol O, Bosio CM, Davis MM, Lifton RP, Pearce EL, Daumke O, Aytekin C, Şahin GE, Aksu AÜ, Uzel G, Koneti Rao V, Sari S, Dalgıç B, Boztug K, Cagdas D, Haskologlu S, Ikinciogullari A, Schwefel D, Vilarinho S, Baris S, Ozen A, Su HC, Lenardo MJ
**Journal:** Nat Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41590-023-01691-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41590-023-01691-y)

## Content

Preserving cells in a functional, non-senescent state is a major goal for extending human healthspans. Model organisms reveal that longevity and senescence are genetically controlled, but how genes control longevity in different mammalian tissues is unknown. Here, we report a new human genetic disease that causes cell senescence, liver and immune dysfunction, and early mortality that results from deficiency of GIMAP5, an evolutionarily conserved GTPase selectively expressed in lymphocytes and endothelial cells. We show that GIMAP5 restricts the pathological accumulation of long-chain ceramides (CERs), thereby regulating longevity. GIMAP5 controls CER abundance by interacting with protein kinase CK2 (CK2), attenuating its ability to activate CER synthases. Inhibition of CK2 and CER synthase rescues GIMAP5-deficient T cells by preventing CER overaccumulation and cell deterioration. Thus, GIMAP5 controls longevity assurance pathways crucial for immune function and healthspan in mammals.

Longevity assurance (LASS) pathways, which ensure cell vitality and proliferation, were first defined to be under genetic control in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, where regulation of cell divisions, rather than chronological age, define lifespan1. Notably, the deletion of a gene called longevity assurance gene 1 (LAG1), which is one of a group of LASS genes that are conserved from yeast to humans encoding CER synthases (CERS), substantially increased lifespan in yeast2,3; however, the molecular mechanisms specifically regulating cellular longevity assurance pathways in mammals, especially humans, are poorly understood. CERs are structural components of cell membranes as well as second messengers for stress or receptor signaling4,5. Low, transient CER levels can transduce signals from cell surface receptors, whereas high sustained levels can cause cellular disruption and degeneration5,6. In the immune system, lymphocyte longevity is crucial for protective immunity, vaccine efficacy and successful immunotherapies. T cell receptor (TCR) signaling is known to induce a transient CER burst, but the effects of CER overaccumulation and how LASS pathways are controlled are unknown7.

In vertebrates, GIMAP5 is a member of a conserved family of eight clustered G protein genes, in humans on chromosome 7q36, encoding the GTPase of immunity-associated proteins (GIMAPs; also known as immune-associated nucleotide-binding proteins). These have especially high expression in lymphocytes and non-lymphoid endothelial cells (ECs)8,9. GIMAPs localize to different cell compartments and are structurally related to dynamin/septins, which are activated by nucleotide-dependent dimerization10-12.

GIMAP5 is perhaps the best-known GIMAP due to the prominent immunoregulatory role first observed in the classic biobreeding (BB) rat model of type 1 diabetes8,13. BB rats harbor a homozygous frameshift mutation that eliminates GIMAP5 protein expression leading to severe T cell lymphopenia, a deficit of regulatory T cells and type 1 diabetes or colitis, depending on the genetic background8,13. Mice deficient for Gimap5, for example, the ethyl nitrosourea-generated sphinx mouse (named for its enigmatic characteristics) or those with genetically engineered deficiencies, also have progressive T and natural killer (NK) cell lymphopenia14-16. Together these results suggest a specific role for GIMAP5 in regulating lymphocyte youthfulness and lifespan. Various functions of GIMAP5, such as regulation of Bcl-2 family proteins, nuclear factor (NF)-κB, mTOR, lysosomal calcium control and c-Myc stabilization by glycogen synthetase kinase have been described, but the regulatory role of GIMAP5, especially in the human immune system, is unresolved17-21. In humans, genome-wide association studies have uncovered single nucleotide polymorphisms in GIMAP genes linked to asthma, type 1 diabetes, systemic lupus erythematosus and Behcet’s disease, and a monogenic immune dysregulation in a single case has been reported21-24. Also, a genetic analysis has shown that GIMAP5 critically controls liver EC differentiation and prevents portal hypertension9. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of GIMAP5 in normal lymphocyte and EC physiology, but its precise molecular role within the cell has been mysterious. Here we describe biochemical abnormalities and a new mammalian GIMAP5 function in a clinical setting and specifically connect GIMAP5 to LASS pathways.

From our international clinical genomics research cohort, we identified 17 affected individuals in nine kindreds with GIMAP5 loss-of-function mutations (Fig. 1a). Affected individuals suffered from splenomegaly, liver disease, lymphopenia, severe recurrent lung infections with pneumonia and bronchiectasis, thrombocytopenia, lymphadenopathy, skin diseases and lymphoma (Fig. 1b-d, Extended Data Fig. 1a, Table 1, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 and Supplementary clinical descriptions). Six patients died: one from pulmonary embolus, two from liver failure, two from complications of SARS-CoV-2 infection and one from Enterococcus faecium sepsis following relapse of Hodgkin’s lymphoma and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (Fig. 1a). Longitudinal studies showed decreased absolute lymphocyte counts, immunoglobulin abnormalities and a high incidence of lymphoma (Extended Data Fig. 1b and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Portal hypertension was associated with liver disease findings (Fig. 1d, Extended Data Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1) and has been recently shown to involve liver EC abnormalities9.

The genetic abnormalities in affected individuals were either missense or stop-gain mutations in GIMAP5 with either bi-allelic homozygous or compound heterozygous inheritance, causing substitutions of amino acids L223F, I47T, P109L, L204P, N221S, W267* and C38*, which are generally conserved across species and/or the GIMAP gene family and a less-conserved R214* (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1c)9. Bioinformatics showed these single nucleotide variants to be rare and deleterious (Supplementary Table 3). To understand these mutations, we determined the X-ray crystallographic structure of GDP-bound GIMAP5 (amino acids 1–276; GIMAP51–276) with a resolution of 2.8 Å (Fig. 1e, Extended Data Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 4). GIMAP51–276 adopts a typical G domain fold with a C-terminal helical extension. The structure is closely related to that of GIMAP2 and GIMAP7, but has a short helical extension resembling the former (Extended Data Fig. 1e)11,25. Based on their position, the patient variants likely cause major structural disturbances (Fig. 1e, small panels and supplementary descriptions). Concordantly, glutathione-S-transferase (GST) fusion proteins of GIMAP51–276 containing patient mutations showed altered thermal shift profiles consistent with reduced protein stability (Extended Data Fig. 1f).

Human GIMAP5 messenger RNA is highly expressed in T cells, ECs and NK cells, but much less in CD14+ monocytes or CD19+ B cells (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 1g)26. Immunoblots (IBs) confirmed the high GIMAP5 protein expression in human NK and T cells, but not in B cells or monocytes (Extended Data Fig. 1g). Patients’ T cells showed loss of GIMAP5 protein but had normal protein levels of GIMAP1, 2, 4, 6, 7 and 8, confirming that the genetic variants cause selective GIMAP5 protein instability (Fig. 1g and Extended Data Fig. 1i). GIMAP5 mRNA levels were generally normal in patient T cells, except P8.1 who has a premature stop codon mutation at amino acid 38 leading to reduced mRNA by nonsense-mediated decay (Extended Data Fig. 1j). Thus, loss-of-function GIMAP5 mutations lead to human diseases characterized by lymphopenia, recurrent infections and liver disease.

We analyzed patient lymphocytes using 38 time-of-flight mass cytometry (CyTOF) markers and observed selectively depleted naive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and central memory (TCM) CD4+ T cells but increased effector memory (TEM) CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Extended Data Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 5, 6)27. We confirmed significantly decreased CD45RA+CCR7+ naive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and increased CD45RA−CCR7− TEM cells in patient samples; naive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were reduced to half or less compared to age-matched controls by flow cytometry (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2b28). These data suggested persistent overactivation, so we analyzed the CD57 senescence marker and found it increased on the patients’ CD8+ TEM, TCM, terminally differentiated effector memory (TEMRA) and γδ T cells but to a lesser degree on CD4+ T cells by flow cytometry and CyTOF (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 2c,d).

We further verified T cell senescence by finding increased senescence-associated-β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity and elevated surface KLRG and reduced CD27 and CD28 in patient cells (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 2e-h). We found that PD-1 was only modestly elevated and there was marked shortening of telomeres (less than the first percentile) in some samples suggesting that the patient T cells may be in a senescent state different from exhaustion29 (Extended Data Fig. 2i,j). Consistent with reduced naive cells and increased senescent cells, we observed reduced CD25 and CD69 expression and proliferation after TCR stimulation of patient T cells directly ex vivo (Extended Data Fig. 2k-m and Fig. 2d). We also examined the biochemical machinery of senescence and found that intracellular staining for p21, p53 and p16 was elevated for patient T cells (Fig. 2e). Patient plasma also had a senescence-associated secretory phenotype with increased interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-8 (Fig. 2f).

We then performed acute CRISPR/Cas9 knockout (KO) of GIMAP5 (GIMAP5CrKO) or a non-targeting control (Ctrl) in purified naive healthy human CD8+ T cells to explore the effects of acute loss of GIMAP5 function (Fig. 2g)30. GIMAP5CrKO T cells had reduced long-term proliferation and increased cell cycle inhibitors, p21, p53 and p16 (Fig. 2h,i). We did not find reduced c-Myc (Fig. 2i) as previously reported, but did observe a G2/M phase cell cycle arrest for GIMAP5CrKO T cells, which p21 causes in non-canonical senescence (Extended Data Fig. 2n)21,31. Viability was modestly reduced in GIMAP5CrKO T cells and there was no difference in the expression of the activation markers, CD25 and CD69 in GIMAP5CrKO T cells after TCR stimulation (Extended Data Fig. 2o,p). In summary, acute GIMAP5 KO in human T cells induces senescence and cell cycle arrest like the patient T cell phenotype, verifying that GIMAP5 gene deficiency accounts for disease in our patient cohort.

We next explored whether Gimap5 deficiency also drives cell senescence in mice using the sphinx mouse strain (Gimap5sph/sph) that harbors a Gimap5 point mutation leading to protein instability and loss15. In littermate control mice, Gimap5 was expressed in the spleen and liver (Fig. 3a) as well as in the lung, heart and kidney but not in the brain (Extended Data Fig. 3a). In sphinx mice, we found increased p21 in the spleen, liver and lung, but not in the kidney, heart or brain (Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3a). We first examined splenic CD4+ T cells as sphinx mice exhibit a near absence of CD8+ T cells in the peripheral lymphoid organs15. We found that levels of the senescence markers, p21, p53, p16 and SA-β-gal activity were significantly higher in CD4+ T cells from sphinx (7–12-week-old) mice compared to littermate control T cells, in a pattern resembling T cells from aged (16–19-month-old) wild-type (WT) mice (Fig. 3c). We then examined ECs, the principal non-lymphoid subtypes expressing Gimap5, from the liver and lung9. We found that CD45−CD31+ liver ECs from sphinx mice showed increased p21, p53, p16 and SA-β-gal activity (Fig. 3d). Moreover, p53, p16 and SA-β-gal activity, but not p21 were elevated in liver ECs from aged 16–19-month-old WT mice (Fig. 3d). In CD45−CD31+ lung ECs from sphinx mice, the levels of p21 and p53, but not p16 and SA-β-gal activity, were increased (Extended Data Fig. 3b). In contrast, the lung ECs of the aged WT mice did not show increased p21 or p53 but instead had elevated p16 and SA-β-gal activity, suggesting that regulation of senescence pathways is not uniform and may be tissue specific (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Further examination of Gimap5 deficiency in Rag1-deficient mice, which lack lymphocytes9, showed markedly increased p21 in the liver, but not in the spleen (Fig. 3e,f). This indicates that, in contrast to recent reports, the increased senescence in liver ECs in this instance is not induced by adaptive immune cells32. Therefore, our findings suggest that Gimap5 deficiency in mice induces senescence in T cells and ECs.

Similar to patients who are GIMAP5 deficient, previous work has shown that Gimap5-deficient mice have T cell lymphopenia with abnormally increased TEM cells14,15. As the TEM cells predominating in KO mice are not phenotypically comparable to naive T cells in age-matched WT mice, we investigated acute CRISPR/Cas9 KO of Gimap5 (Gimap5CrKO) compared to Thy1.1 KO as a control (Ctrl) in the same cell preparations to assess Gimap5 function using murine T cell blasts (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 4a). We observed that Gimap5CrKO T cells died over 3 d as the Gimap5 protein was eliminated which recapitulated the T lymphocyte loss in vivo in Gimap5-deficient mice (Fig. 4b)15,16,21. Scanning and transmission electron microscopy showed membrane breakdown, indicating necrotic death (Fig. 4c)33.

Next, we performed metabolomic analysis on Gimap5CrKO T cells due to the pivotal role of metabolism in lymphocyte function and longevity34. Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis revealed a surprising increase in CER species across a broad range of lipid subclasses in Gimap5CrKO T cells (Fig. 4d). We verified these results with targeted MS sphingolipid (SPL) analyses that showed specific abnormal increases in very long chain (VLC), 18 and 24 carbon, fatty acid (FA) CER conjugates in Gimap5CrKO T cells (Fig. 4e). We also found elevated dihydroceramides (DCERs), the immediate precursors of CER for de novo synthesis, but reduced hexosylceramides (HCERs), including β-galactosylceramide (GalCER) and β-glucosylceramide (GlcCER) (Fig. 4e). Most of the 16 to 22 carbon FA species of triacylglycerides (TAGs) were also increased (Extended Data Fig. 4b).

TCR stimulation caused death in CD4+ T cells isolated from the sphinx mice (Extended Data Fig. 4c)15,21. Of note, TCR stimulation of WT cells increased several VLC CERs (C20–C24) (Extended Data Fig. 4d) but there was a sustained overaccumulation of both long-chain (LC) and VLC CERs and a partial reduction of HCERs in Gimap5sph/sph CD4+ T cells following TCR stimulation, suggesting that Gimap5 regulates SPL homeostasis in a TCR-dependent manner (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Furthermore, we found that retroviral-mediated expression of the Gimap5WT protein can rescue the survival of activated Gimap5sph/sph CD4+ T cells, whereas expression of mutants that either inactivate (Gimap5S40N, unable to bind nucleotides) or constitutively activate (Gimap5R121D, unable to hydrolyze GTP) the enzymatic activity of GTPases11 could not (Extended Data Fig. 4e-g). Thus, it is evident that proper regulation of the GTP hydrolysis of Gimap5 is required for its function.

Of note, the CERS inhibitor, fumonisin B1 (FB1) reversed CER and DCER accumulation and prevented cell death, indicating that CER accumulation causes Gimap5-deficient T cell demise (Fig. 4f and Extended Data Fig. 4h). Due to the stringent regulation of SPL metabolism, which involves the counter-regulation of CER expression33, we observed that the KO of Cers2, the most abundant Cers in murine T cells (Extended Data Fig. 4i) caused compensatory upregulation of other Cers isoforms, specifically Cers3 and Cers6 (Extended Data Fig. 4j,k). Further in-depth lipidomic analysis of the Cers2 KO murine T cells, revealed a decrease in C22–C26 FA CER/DCERs but an increase in several other CER species, HCERs and sphingomyelin (SM) containing C16–C20 fatty acids, suggesting that this shift in the distribution of CERs and other sphingolipids likely hinders the rescue of Gimap5CrKO T cell death by Cers2 KO (Extended Data Fig. 4l,m). Furthermore, we found that administration of conduritol-β-epoxide (CBE), which induces HCER but not CER accumulation did not rescue Gimap5CrKO T cells indicating that the decreased HCER did not shorten the lifespan of the Gimap5CrKO T cells (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b). Additionally, we observed that zVAD (a pan-caspase inhibitor), necrostatin-1 (a necroptosis inhibitor) and ferrostatin-1 and liproxstatin-1 (ferroptosis inhibitors) were unable to rescue the Gimap5CrKO T cells from cell death (Extended Data Fig. 5c), suggesting that Gimap5CrKO T cells do not undergo apoptosis, necroptosis (necrosis mediated by RIP kinase) or ferroptosis as their mode of cell death.

CER has been implicated in the induction of cellular senescence and aging35. Hence, we hypothesized that chronically high levels of CER in Gimap5-deficient cells cause senescence in CD4+ T cells from sphinx mice (Fig. 3c). Given the CER abnormalities in sphinx mice, we next examined the lipidomic profiles in GIMAP5-deficient patient T cells. The requirements for multiple additional samples were challenging for the study participants, but of the nine families contacted, we successfully obtained sufficient samples from three families. We detected elevated species of VLC CERs in GIMAP5-deficient patient T cells, for example P2.2 and P7.1, which were very similar to what we observed in mice (Fig. 4g). Several VLC SPL, including SM species, were also elevated in patients but not in family members or controls (Fig. 4g and Extended Data Fig. 5d)35. Notably, we detected decreased CER/DCER but elevated HCER in GIMAP5-deficient T cells from P1.4 who currently has no symptoms (Extended Data Fig. 5d). Thus, mice and humans exhibited comparable abnormal CER accumulation when GIMAP5 was deficient. We next examined whether increased senescence markers in human GIMAP5CrKO T cells (Fig. 2i) were CER-dependent. Our findings show that CERS suppression by FB1 significantly reduces p21 and p53 levels in GIMAP5CrKO T cells, implying that GIMAP5 induces T cell senescence by modulating CER (Extended Data Fig. 5e); however, we did not observe an increase in p21 in the Gimap5CrKO murine T cells, which are undergoing cell death starting on day 2 after electroporation (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 5f). This suggests that the lack of senescent cells may be due to these cells proceeding directly to cell death. A comparison of GIMAP5 deficiency in mouse and human T cells revealed a difference: human T cells survived better than mouse cells but became senescent and non-proliferative (compare Extended Data Fig. 2o to Fig. 4b); however, the defect in cell and organismal longevity is likely attributable to cellular senescence in both humans and mice. Hence, GIMAP5 deficiency disrupts CER homeostasis in T cells, leading to cell death or senescence.

To understand the molecular mechanism by which GIMAP5 controls CER accumulation, we examined GIMAP5 interactors using GST pulldown and MS. We detected a GIMAP5 signal, indicating that GIMAP5 homodimerizes as expected for dynamin/septin-like GTPases10,11,18 (Fig. 5a). We also found the principal interactor to be LCK, explaining how TCR signals induce the diseased T cell phenotype in GIMAP5 deficiency21, and two isoforms of the catalytic subunits of protein kinase CK2 (CK2) (CK2α/CSNK2A1 and CK2α′/CSNK2A2) (Fig. 5a). CK2 is a constitutively active serine/threonine kinase that regulates cell differentiation, proliferation and survival36,37. The holoenzyme CK2 consists of two catalytic subunits (α and/or α′) and two copies of the regulatory subunit (β), forming either α2β2, α′2β2 or αα′β2 tetramers36,37. STRING analysis showed that multiple GIMAP5 interactors (mean log2 ratio >2) were either CK2 substrates or interactors (Fig. 5a)38. Notably, CK2 has been described to phosphorylate and activate CERS in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)39, suggesting a potential link between GIMAP5 and the regulation of CER synthesis. Therefore, we examined whether GIMAP5 controls CK2. Immunoprecipitation (IP) of epitope-tagged proteins showed that GIMAP5 interacted with CK2α and CK2α′, but not CK2β (Extended Data Fig. 6a). We further assessed the interaction of CK2α with GIMAP5 full-length (FL) or truncation mutants lacking the G domain (GIMAP5 NΔ) or C-terminal hydrophobic domain (GIMAP5 CΔ) by overexpressing FLAG-tagged proteins in 293T cells (Extended Data Fig. 6b). We found that GIMAP5 FL or NΔ interacts strongly with CK2α, whereas GIMAP5 CΔ has almost no interaction, suggesting that GIMAP5 requires both its hydrophobic domain for proper localization to engage with CK2α and its coiled-coil domain for interaction with CK2α (Extended Data Fig. 6c). Correspondingly, native endogenous CK2α co-immunoprecipitated with GIMAP5, LCK and CK2β kinetically after TCR activation (Fig. 5b). CK2α is constitutively associated with CK2β but GIMAP5 and LCK are most abundantly recruited to the complex at 15–30 min of TCR stimulation (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, as the peak TCR-induced association of CK2α with GIMAP5 and LCK diminishes, the CK2 complex switches at 90 min to recruitment of the enzyme CERS2 that synthesizes VLC CER (Fig. 5b). Thus, TCR-induced generation of CER may result from CK2-mediated CERS2 activation. Notably, the level of p21, a senescence mediator, increased at 90 min after TCR stimulation, the same time as the increased CK2α–CERS2 interaction (Fig. 5b) and FB1 treatment inhibited the late TCR-induced elevation of p21 (Extended Data Fig. 6d)35. Using proximity ligation analysis, we verified that GIMAP5 transiently associates with CK2α in intact cells in a time course consistent with the complex observed by co-IP (Extended Data Fig. 6e and Fig. 5b). Additionally, we confirmed that endogenous GIMAP5 co-immunoprecipitated CK2α, LCK and CERS2 in response to TCR activation (Extended Data Fig. 6f).

Next, we examined the subcellular localization of GIMAP5, LCK and CK2α complex. We found that GIMAP5 colocalizes with late endosome/lysosomal markers, LAMP1, Rab7 and LAMTOR4 and partially colocalizes with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) marker, calnexin, whereas no colocalization was detected with MitoTracker in human T cell blasts (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Furthermore, we observed that LCK adopts a notable punctate pattern upon TCR stimulation implying it is associated with vesicles (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 7b). Some LCK vesicles colocalize with GIMAP5 and Rab7 following TCR stimulation, whereas GIMAP5 exhibited constitutive colocalization with Rab7 (Extended Data Fig. 7b). These findings suggest that LCK traffics to late endosomes and partially colocalizes with GIMAP5 after TCR stimulation. The colocalization of CK2α with GIMAP5 and LCK also increased after TCR stimulation (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 7c), which aligned with our co-IP data (Fig. 5b). As reported in previous studies, CK2 has been suggested to phosphorylate the C-terminal segment of CERS at the cytosolic face of the ER membrane39. Thus, it is conceivable that GIMAP5 and CK2 interact at the contact site between the ER and late endosome/lysosome. We observed the colocalization of CK2α with both calnexin and Rab7 (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d), and following TCR stimulation, CK2α exhibited reduced colocalization with calnexin while maintaining stable colocalization with Rab7 (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d). Of note, partial CK2α colocalization with another late endosomal marker, LAMTOR4, was increased (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 7c,e). These findings indicate that CK2α binds to GIMAP5 after TCR stimulation at the late endosome, possibly near sites of association with the ER. This provides a plausible mechanism by which TCR signaling regulates CER levels at the contact site between the ER and late endosome/lysosome.

Notably, lipidomic analyses showed a transient elevation of CERs in response to TCR stimulation in control T cells, whereas GIMAP5CrKO human T cells showed the persistent increase in most CERs until 240 min after TCR stimulation (Fig. 5e), similar to what we observed in Gimap5sph/sph murine T cells (Extended Data Fig. 4d), highlighting the TCR-dependent regulation of CER by GIMAP5. We therefore hypothesized that GIMAP5 negatively controls the association of CERS2 with CK2. Indeed, whereas the Ck2α–Cers2 interaction required TCR stimulation in Thy1.1CrKO control T cells, this interaction was constitutive and sustained at a high level in Gimap5CrKO T cells (Fig. 5f). In contrast, the interaction of Ck2α with Ck2β and LCK, pErk induction and the level of CK2α were not affected (Fig. 5f and Extended Data Fig. 8a-d).

Consistent with our hypothesis, the double CRISPR/Cas9 KO of Csnk2a1 (CK2a1) and Gimap5 partially rescued cell viability although Csnk2a1CrKO T cells showed reduced viability compared to Thy1.1CrKO T cells as previously reported (Fig. 5g)36. Also, TCR-induced death of Gimap5CrKO T cells was attenuated by the CK2 chemical inhibitor, CX-4945 (Fig. 5h). Moreover, CX-4945 treatment strongly inhibited the accumulation of VLC CERs in Gimap5CrKO T cells (Fig. 5i), validating the hypothesis that CK2 plays a role in the uncontrolled CER accumulation that leads to Gimap5CrKO T cell death. Additionally, we found that several lipid abnormalities involving DCER, HCER and TAGs were reversed after CX-4945 treatment of Gimap5CrKO T cells, indicating that GIMAP5/CK2 is a key lipid regulatory axis for T cells (Fig. 5i and Extended Data Fig. 8e). We also found that CX-4945 potently suppresses levels of p21 and p53 in both control and GIMAP5CrKO T cells, showing that the GIMAP5-CK2 axis governs human T cell senescence (Fig. 5j and Extended Data Fig. 8f)35. Of note, TCR-induced elevation of CERs and p21 in human primary T cell blasts was potently reversed by CX-4945 treatment (Fig. 5k and Extended Data Fig. 8g). Hence, GIMAP5 regulates a TCR-induced LASS metabolic pathway through CK2 that plays a critical role in the regulation of T cell longevity and senescence.

Finally, to directly illustrate the effects of the GIMAP5 pathway on CER, we administered C6-CER to WT human T cell blasts. C6-CER was previously shown to induce cell death or senescence in a time- and concentration-dependent manner by stimulating CERS to generate LC CERs40,41. Increased endogenous CERs are caused by the deacylation of C6-CER to sphingosine, which is enzymatically reacylated by CERS in an FB1-inhibitable reaction to generate toxic overaccumulation of CER (Fig. 6a)40. We observed that C6-CER induces cell death in T cells but does not significantly induce senescence (Fig. 6b-d to Extended Data Fig. 9a-f). We found that both FB1 and CX-4945 reduced the lethal effects of C6-CER administration (Fig. 6b,c). Of note, we overexpressed GIMAP5 protein using a lentiviral vector in T cells and found that it similarly reduced C6-CER lethality (Fig. 6d). These data confirmed the central role of GIMAP5 in CER homeostasis. Thus, our findings demonstrate that GIMAP5 is essential for maintaining CER homeostasis by restricting CK2 activation of CERS, which in turn affects cellular senescence and lifespan (Fig. 6e and Extended Data Fig. 10).

As aging-related diseases continue to increase worldwide, it is crucial to investigate longevity pathways in humans that may enhance healthspan. SPLs, especially CERs, have been implicated in the regulation of cell senescence, proliferation, differentiation and survival in multiple cell types and organisms4. CERs have also been implicated in aging and the pathogenesis of age-related diseases, including type 2 diabetes, cancer, cardiovascular diseases and neurodegeneration35. Our finding represents new evidence of the link between CER and T cell immunosenescence and establishes a new regulatory axis involving CK2, CERS2 and GIMAP5.

GIMAP5 is the best-known member of the GIMAP gene family, but its deficiency causes severe immune pathology that is poorly understood. For example, it is the gene underlying lymphopenia and autoimmunity associated with characteristic congenital diabetes in the BB rat, the targeted gene in sphinx severely lymphopenic mice and a putative deleterious variant previously observed in a single immunodeficient individual8,13-16,21. Therefore, it is important to understand how GIMAP5 ensures the vitality of T lymphocytes in mice, rats and humans and its cellular function as a GTPase tethered to internal vesicles, lysosomes and ER membranes42. Early reports of GIMAP5 regulating Bcl-2, lysosomal Ca2+ and mTOR activity have been contested18-20. Many of these earlier studies used isolated cells from animals with systemic immune dysregulation, whereas our CRISPR-mediated acute GIMAP5 KO model enabled us to identify the specific biochemical functions of GIMAP5 using the same populations of cells as the control.

We find that GIMAP5 deficiency causes a new human genetic immunodeficiency disease with a lymphopenia phenotype and reveal its critical biochemical role in lipid homeostasis to ensure cell longevity and prevent senescence. We now call this disease ‘GIMAP5 deficiency with immunodysregulation, splenomegaly, enlarged lymph nodes, liver disease and lymphopenia’ (GISELL; pronounced with a soft G; (/dʒɪZεl/). We find that GIMAP5 governs CER levels by controlling CK2–CERS2 association in lymphocytes. The absence of GIMAP5 allows for their constitutive interaction causing CER accumulation and consequential cell senescence or death. Thus, our discovery demonstrates that the dysregulation of CER homeostasis is the underlying cause of the immunodeficiency resulting from the loss of GIMAP5 function, which is primarily attributed to its vital and distinct role in regulating T cell longevity. Murine T cells lacking Gimap5 progress rapidly through senescence to overt death. Therefore, it has been especially important to study GIMAP5 deficiency in humans as it is slower to induce death and yields T cells in a senescence phenotype that might be reversible with appropriate pharmaceuticals. It was not possible to study the pathway in immortalized cell lines such as Jurkat or HuT 78 because GIMAP5 deletion had no discernible effect, likely because immortalization circumvents cellular senescence and death processes. The defects caused by GIMAP5 deficiency are rescued by CK2 inhibition thus CX-4945 or other similar drugs may be effective as treatment options for GISELL, which has a very high mortality43,44.

A recent report showed that GIMAP5 deficiency in both humans and mice causes capillarization of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), which contributes to liver portal hypertension9. Although the mechanism of LSEC capillarization is currently unknown, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-mediated nitric oxide (NO) signaling has been implicated in the maintenance of LSEC fenestrae45. As CER has been shown to promote vasoconstriction by regulating NO signaling, future research on the roles of CER and GIMAP5 in NO signaling of LSECs could provide new insight into the underlying mechanism of portal hypertension46.

Finally, there are broader implications for understanding this pathway in different tissues as overall cell vitality and function diminish greatly with aging and senescence47. The presumed consequences of immune senescence were evident globally in many different populations during the current COVID-19 pandemic, which showed a strong age-related mortality48. Cellular senescence likely underlies the general vulnerability of the elderly to various diseases, and this will be an increasing health burden as the aging population increases globally. Our data show that similar senescence changes occur in ECs and T cells from aged mice as those we found in GIMAP5-deficient cells. We now show how to modulate the LASS pathway, which could be useful to enhance protective immunity, vaccination efficiency or immunotherapy, and potentially deploy the pathway in cases of autoimmunity or allograft rejection. The riddle of the sphinx was a metaphor for the changes caused by aging during human life. The sphinx mouse now reveals how the processes of aging can be regulated by lipid homeostasis, especially in lymphocytes. Our discovery of a regulatory axis linking CK2, CERS2 and GIMAP5 provides new biochemical insights on how to keep mammalian T cells alive and youthful.

The sample size of 3–15 per group were used for in vitro and in vivo studies. These numbers were chosen based on hundreds of experiments in our laboratory59-61. Investigators were blinded wherever possible during sample analysis by coding sample identity during data collection for mice experiment and confocal experimentation (Fig. 3 and Extended Data Figs. 3 and 7). Else, blinding was not possible as the same investigator performed experiments and data analytics. Allocation of WT and mutant mice was random in Fig. 3 and Extended Data Figs. 3 and 4. For all other experiments, randomization was not conducted because experiments either compared individuals with and without the loss-of-function mutations or were sampled from a cell suspension for treatment/evaluation. No data were excluded. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v.8.0 using a two-tailed unpaired, paired Student’s t-test or by two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-test. P values < 0.05 were considered significant, whereas P values > 0.05 were considered non-significant. Flow cytometry data were analyzed via FlowJo v.10.6.2 (TreeStar). No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Details on the test used can be found in the respective figure legends. Data distribution was assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested.

The limitations of this study are that the experimental analysis was constrained by the availability and health status of participants and their families. Due to these factors, the sample size may have been smaller than desired.

Upon TCR stimulation, the interaction of GIMAP5, CK2, and LCK rapidly increases. CK2 then dissociates from GIMAP5 and LCK when LCK-containing endosomes are transported to the plasma membrane. Following dissociation from GIMAP5 and LCK, CK2 interacts with and activates CERS thereby generating CER following TCR stimulation (left). GIMAP5 deficiency in T cells results in constitutive and sustained CK2-CERS association leading to the accumulation of CER which induces T cell death or T cell senescence through the upregulation of p21 and p53 (right).
