# Karyomegalic interstitial nephritis, a fascinating histopathologic entity for pathologists: Be watchful of the FAN1 gene mutations.
**Authors:** Walia GK, Vankalakunti M, Akal RS, Jha VK
**Journal:** Indian J Pathol Microbiol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.4103/ijpm.ijpm_984_23](https://doi.org/10.4103/ijpm.ijpm_984_23)

## Content

Karyomegalic interstitial nephritis (KIN) is an uncommon autosomal recessive disease, which is characterized by enlarged and hyperchromatic nuclei of the renal tubular epithelial cells. It is associated with mutations in Fanconi anemia-associated nuclease 1 gene, which is responsible for DNA repair, and these pathogenic mutations are responsible for progressive renal failure in young adults. We present a case of a 29-year-old female with end-stage renal disease who had a family history of early-onset renal failure in two of the siblings. Her elder sister with chronic kidney disease stage III was advised to be biopsied to look for any familial causes. Stained sections of renal biopsy revealed normal glomeruli. Tubular epithelial cells showed nuclear changes focally like hyperchromasia, karyomegaly, and anisonucleosis. On direct immunofluorescence, all glomeruli were negative for deposits with all antisera. Based on these findings, a final opinion of KIN was given.

Karyomegalic interstitial nephritis (KIN) is a rare genetic cause of chronic interstitial nephritis (CIN), leading to end-stage renal disease in young adults.[1,2]With a prevalence of less than 1%, KIN presents with a history of mild proteinuria, urinary sediment abnormalities, and recurrent upper respiratory tract infections.[2,3]This hereditary disease is linked to mutations in Fanconi anemia-associated nuclease 1 (FAN1) gene mutations with autosomal recessive inheritance.[1,2]Histologically, tubular epithelial cells show enlargement, hyperchromasia, and anisonucleosis.[1-3]Karyomegalic changes are rarely seen in other organs—liver, lungs, pancreas, and colon.[3]Keen clinical suspicion, based on clinical features and family history, is required for correct diagnosis and genetic counseling.

A 29-year-old female presented with nausea, vomiting, and decreased appetite for two months. On evaluation, she had raised serum creatinine levels of 10.0 mg/dl and USG KUB was suggestive of bilateral contracted kidneys (right kidney: 6.0 cm, left kidney: 6.1 cm). Her family history was significant with two of the three siblings having a history of medical renal disease at a young age (younger brother with ESRD who underwent an unrelated live donor renal transplant in 2022; and elder sister with chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage III). Also, there was a history of infertility in the third sibling sister and her mother died at the young age of 39 due to unknown cancer [Figure 1]. Since she had small shrunken kidneys and renal biopsy was not advisable, it was advised by the treating nephrologist to perform renal biopsy of her sister with CKD stage III to rule out any familial disorders. The hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) stained sections of renal biopsy revealed five glomeruli, out of which three were obsolescent. The remaining two glomeruli were normal in appearance. The tubular epithelial cells showed nuclear changes like hyperchromasia, karyomegaly, and anisonucleosis with some nuclei having inclusion-like nucleoli [Figure 2aandb]. There was a mild degree of interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy involving <25% of the visualized cortex. The interlobular arteries revealed mild intimal hyperplasia and medial hypertrophy. Direct immunoflorescence revealed 11 viable glomeruli and all the glomerular tufts were negative for deposits with a panel of antisera—IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, and C1q, and there was no restriction of kappa and lambda light chains. Immunohistochemistry for viral immunostains (SV 40 and CMV) was negative. Ki-67 proliferation index was less than 1% in karyomegalic cells [Figure 2c]. Based on these findings, a final opinion of KIN was given. Subsequent to this histopathology report, the patient was advised for next-generation whole exome sequencing in which a homozygous nonsense variant of FAN1 gene mutation was detected [Figure 3]. Our patient underwent a live unrelated donor renal transplant in January 2023 and has been asymptomatic since ten months.

First described 40 years ago, KIN is a rare genetic cause of CKD. With a prevalence of less than 1%, only 50 cases have been reported till now, all of which belong to 12 families.[2,3]In 1979, Mihatschet al.[4]introduced the term “KIN” when they described three cases of CIN showing systemic karyomegalic changes.[1]These patients may be asymptomatic or have mild proteinuria, urinary sediment abnormalities, and extra-renal features like recurrent upper respiratory tract infections and abnormal liver function tests.[1-3]Mutations in FAN1 gene have been found to be pathogenic for KIN.[4]FAN1 is a gene which is involved in DNA interstrand crosslink repair mechanism. It is predominantly expressed in the liver, kidney, and neuronal tissue.[4]Some cases show familial clustering of genes with increased expression of HLA-B35 and HLA-A9, increasing their susceptibility to environmental genotoxin-induced renal damage.[3,4]FAN1 gene mutations can be studied by whole exome sequencing or Sanger sequencing. In our case, since the renal biopsy of the sister was suggestive of KIN, the patient was advised to undergo whole exome sequencing studies, which showed homozygous nonsense variant of the FAN1 gene which is pathogenic for KIN.

Histologically karyomegalic changes along with anisonucleosis and hyperchromasia are noted in tubular epithelial cells. Glomeruli show global glomerulosclerosis and some case studies have shown focal segmental glomerulosclerosis also.[3,5]Interstitium shows chronic inflammatory cells and the presence of tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis. Karyomegalic changes can rarely be seen in other organs like the lungs, bronchi, liver, gastrointestinal tract, myocardium, and brain.[6]

Cytomegalovirus nephropathy and polyoma-virus-associated nephropathy show karyomegalic changes along with intranuclear inclusions, which are seen in proximal and distal tubules.[4]Tubulitis is common in viral infections, whereas, it is uncommon in KIN. Interstitial plasma cells may be seen in viral infections. Immunostains for CMV and SV40 are used to highlight viral inclusions. Our case was negative for both these markers. In reactive changes seen in tubules in acute cellular rejection, karyomegaly is not as much as seen in KIN, and these changes resolve when the patient is treated for rejection. Interstitial fibrosis is usually absent.[4]Also, Ki-67 proliferation index is high in viral infections, whereas it is low in karyomegalic cells in KIN, supporting the available literature regarding block in G2 phase of the cell cycle.[7]To further substantiate the diagnosis, electron microscopy is very helpful. Viral particles of CMV measure 70-90 nm in diameter, whereas nuclear inclusions of polyoma virus are characteristically smaller (30–45 nm) with the presence of lattice-like arrangement.[8]However, electron microscopy in KIN shows severely enlarged and giant polymorphic nuclei in the epithelium of proximal tubules, with lobulation and invagination of the nuclear membrane.[9]

There is no definitive treatment protocol for KIN, and it is largely supportive. Corticosteroids have been used with not much benefit. The renal transplant also shows variable outcomes since the data on this entity are limited.[4]Recurrence of KIN posttransplant from a related donor has also been documented, emphasizing the need to identify this genetic disease.[4]

To conclude, KIN is an underdiagnosed entity. It requires a high index of clinical suspicion in young immunocompetent patients presenting with features of CIN to timely diagnose this familial disease with the gold standard renal biopsy procedure. The absence of ultrastructural study in our case is a limitation as there is a possibility of missing any other coexisting pathology.
