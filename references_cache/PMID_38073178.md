# Genome sequencing of Pakistani families with male infertility identifies deleterious genotypes in SPAG6, CCDC9, TKTL1, TUBA3C, and M1AP.
**Authors:** Khan MR, Akbari A, Nicholas TJ, Castillo-Madeen H, Ajmal M, Haq TU, Laan M, Quinlan AR, Ahuja JS, Shah AA, Conrad DF
**Journal:** Andrology (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/andr.13570](https://doi.org/10.1111/andr.13570)

## Content

BACKGROUND: There are likely to be hundreds of monogenic forms of human male infertility. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) is the most efficient way to make progress in mapping the causative genetic variants, and ultimately improve clinical management of the disease in each patient. Recruitment of consanguineous families is an effective approach to ascertain the genetic forms of many diseases.

OBJECTIVES: To apply WGS to large consanguineous families with likely hereditary male infertility and identify potential genetic cases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS: We recruited seven large families with clinically diagnosed male infertility from rural Pakistan, including five with a history of consanguinity. We generated WGS data on 26 individuals (3-5 per family) and analyzed the resulting data with a computational pipeline to identify potentially causal single nucleotide variants, indels, and copy number variants.

RESULTS: We identified plausible genetic causes in five of the seven families, including a homozygous 10 kb deletion of exon 2 in a well-established male infertility gene (M1AP), and biallelic missense substitutions (SPAG6, CCDC9, TUBA3C) and an in-frame hemizygous deletion (TKTL1) in genes with emerging relevance.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION: The rate of genetic findings using the current approach (71%) was much higher than what we recently achieved using whole-exome sequencing (WES) of unrelated singleton cases (20%). Furthermore, we identified a pathogenic single-exon deletion in M1AP that would be undetectable by WES. Screening more families with WGS, especially in underrepresented populations, will further reveal the types of variants underlying male infertility and accelerate the use of genetics in the patient management.

In recent years, a combination of human genetics and mouse genetics research has shown unequivocally that male infertility has a substantial monogenic basis1-4. In mice, there are now hundreds of single-gene knockouts known to display spermatogenic impairment. In humans, dozens of genes have been linked to Mendelian forms of male infertility. Much work remains to identify new genes responsible for human infertility, to build evidence for previously implicated genes, and to clarify the genotype-phenotype mapping of established genes. Part of the challenge of this research is to ascertain patients with monogenic forms of male infertility. Like many Mendelian disorders, genetic forms of male infertility tend to be caused by rare recessive variants, with a very low incidence of individuals with biallelic disease-causing variants in randomly mating populations. Populations that practice non-random mating, especially mating among close relatives, increase the chance of sampling rare homozygous genotypes and are especially valuable for human genetic studies5.

The people of Pakistan are emerging as an invaluable resource for research into Mendelian disorders, due to the high rate of first-cousin marriages. With a population of over 249 million people, Pakistan is the fifth largest country in the world, and has among the highest rates of consanguinity, if not the highest; estimated at 65% over the past 30 years6,7. Most consanguineous marriages in the country are between first cousins. The research opportunity provided by this cultural environment has led to increasing recruitment of patients into a wide variety of genetics studies. Notably, a national sequencing project named the “Pakistan Genomic Resource” has been formed, with one goal to identify all possible single-gene human knockouts - individuals with biallelic loss-of-function mutations8. So far, this study has sequenced 80,000 individuals and identified over 14,000 knockouts in over 5000 genes. To achieve the same yield of knockouts in European populations, it would require sequencing over 11 million individuals by some estimates9. Consanguinity appears to impact fertility rates in Pakistan. The extent of consanguinity in Pakistani marriages has been positively correlated with longer time to first birth6 and with smaller family sizes10.

With these considerations in mind, we set out to evaluate the prospects of identifying and analyzing Pakistani families with hereditary forms of male infertility to discover new variants and genes. The cultural landscape of Pakistan is complex, with substantial representation of over a dozen ethnic groups and at least 10 different languages. Rates of consanguinity tend to be higher in rural parts of Pakistan versus urban areas (60-70% versus 40-50%)10. We recruited patients with clinically diagnosed male infertility from villages in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, a province in northwest Pakistan (Figure 1). The predominant ethnic group in this province is Pashtuns, a people that have occupied the region for hundreds of years and follow Islam. The cultural norms of the region limited the amount and types of patient contact possible: follow-up contact with patients was infeasible, as was the prospect of obtaining semen for research purposes. Nonetheless, we report here the successful identification of plausible genetic causes of male infertility in several large families with multiple affected cases, involving mutations in both well-established and previously unreported genes. Interpretation of these candidate variants was augmented with rich genomic annotations that we use in the context of GEMINI, a large multicenter study of male infertility 4. These annotations included other potentially causal mutations in genes of interest from the GEMINI cohort, and single-cell expression data from HISTA, our online database of human testicular gene expression11,12.

This study was approved by the Advanced Studies and Research Board (ASRB) of the University of Malakand, Pakistan (Reference No. UOM/2020/870). Helsinki declaration guidelines were followed, and consent forms were taken from all patients and healthy controls.

Clinical evaluation of each patient entailed a standard physical exam and semen analysis, and cases that were diagnosed as oligozoospermia, azoospermia, or asthenozoospermia were considered. Semen analysis was conducted in the health care laboratory using the 5th edition manual WHO criteria. The semen examination was performed immediately after the liquefaction of semen and no longer than 60 minutes after ejaculation. The semen was examined microscopically and the report was generated accordingly. The motility of spermatozoa was considered as total motility of spermatozoa and not progressive motility.

Probands from each family were first identified through preclinical data collection in the local community, which consisted of sites in the Upper Dir, Lower Dir, and Malakand districts (Figure 1). Male family members with a reproductive history compatible with infertility were examined by the managing reproductive endocrinologist. Seven families (numbered 3–9) with multiple affected cases and at least one unaffected sibling were recruited (Figure 2). Affected individuals of families 3 and 4 were diagnosed with asthenozoospermia but normal sperm concentration; while cases from families 5-9 were all diagnosed with either oligozoospermia or azoospermia. All patients in the families 5, 7, and 8 presented azoospermia, while families 6 and 9 included cases with both azoo- and oligozoospermia (Supplementary Table 1). Five families (3, 4, 7, 8 and 9) reported histories of consanguinity, suggesting a single-variant autosomal recessive cause for infertility, while families 5 and 6 did not report consanguinity, suggesting an X-linked, multiallelic recessive (i.e. compound heterozygous), or perhaps even dominant (with incomplete penetrance) cause of infertility in these families.

About 5ml of whole blood was collected at the recruitment from all participants in EDTA tubes and stored at 4°C until further processing. Due to the cultural norms, the follow-up contacts with patients carrying genetic findings were infeasible, as was the prospect of obtaining additional semen samples for research purposes only.

Genomic DNA was extracted using the phenol/chloroform protocol13. The quality and quantity of DNA were determined using agarose gel electrophoresis (Clever Scientific Ltd, UK) and Qubit 2.0 fluorometer using the Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) respectively.

WGS was performed using Illumina TruSeq DNA Nano Library Prep workflow protocol. DNA was profiled on a TapeStation D1000 tape (Agilent). After profiling, sequencing libraries were made using the TruSeq Nano DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). Briefly, the DNA was sheared using a S220 sonicator (Covaris). Fragmented DNA was end repaired and selected for size (~200-700bp), followed by adenylation of 3’ ends and ligation of indexed adapters. Ligated fragments were amplified by limited cycles of polymerase chain reaction. Libraries were quantified by sequencing on a NextSeq 500 (Illumina). Libraries were normalized and mixed, then sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000. Basecall files were converted to fastq files using bcl2fastq (Illumina). Raw FASTQs were uploaded to the Terra cloud environment, where they were processed into gVCFs using the DRAGEN-GATK workflow, based on reference assembly GRCh38. Briefly, this workflow uses a series of steps very similar to GATK best practices, including mapping and alignment, sorting and duplicate marking with Picard, haplotype calling with GATK HaplotypeCaller and VQSR filtering. The major difference was the use of a modified reference genome (the “DRAGEN” genome), which masks problematic regions14. Single-family VCFs containing SNVs and indels were then generated by joint genotyping using GATK. Copy number variants (CNVs) were identified using Manta15, SMOOVE (https://github.com/brentp/smoove), and GATK CNV caller, and annotated with SVAFotate16. SVAFotate was used to annotate structural variant allele frequencies using data from gnomAD, CCDG17, and 1000G whole-genome sequencing datasets18, cumulatively encompassing over 32,000 individuals. The relatedness of individuals in the study was confirmed using Somalier (https://github.com/brentp/somalier).

Indels and SNVs were annotated using ANNOVAR19 and the CADD web server20. We created an in-silico exome from WGS by selecting all variants from the VCF that overlapped the bed file for the Gencode 35 exome definition, the same gene models used for annotation in the gnomAD database. We further assessed the pathogenicity of candidate variants with PolyPhen2 and SIFT19. We calculated population sampling probabilities (PSAP) for each genotype as previously described21. To create a short list of candidate variants for each family we then selected rare variants with either: a) predicted loss-of-function or b) CADD > 15, PSAP P< 1e-4, compatible with X-linked or recessive inheritance and appropriate segregation with the trait in each family. Furthermore, we assume that genetic cause of disease is different in each family, so we disregard autosomal variants that are seen as homozygotes in multiple families, or, for X- and Y-linked variants, shared at all by multiple families.

The final list of candidates was evaluated based on the ACMG guideline for evaluation of sequencing variants to determine pathogenicity22.

Sanger sequencing was used for the confirmation and validation of variants in TUBA3C, TKTL1, SPAG6, and CCDC9 genes only on the same individuals sequenced by WGS. PCR primers and annealing temperatures were designed and optimized for this study (Supplementary Table 2).

To build additional evidence for new genes implicated in this study, we performed case-control analysis on whole-exome data from the published GEMINI cohort study, consisting of 1503 cases of male infertility and 639 normozoospermic controls4. To do this, we filtered genetic variants from GEMINI cohort using the same criteria as we used for the families. We then counted the number of cases and controls with and without a deleterious genotype and tested for association between deleterious genotypes and case/control status using a Fisher Exact Test.

In total, WGS was applied for 26 individuals from seven families (3 –5 individuals per family), including 1-2 unaffected controls per family. On average, mapped read depth per individual was 35.2X and 88.9% of reads achieved a mapping quality > 30. Candidate variants were identified as segregating appropriately within the family, protein-altering, affecting evolutionarily conserved residues, and predicted to be deleterious (see Materials and Methods). After filtering, we were left with 0-8 candidates per family (Supplementary Table 3). Further review of these candidates for gene function and expression pattern lead to a shortlist of candidate causes, with a variety of clinical interpretations (Table 1, Supplementary Table 4).

We identified plausible genetic causes in five of seven families, including a pathogenic homozygous 10kb deletion of exon 2 in a well-established male infertility gene (M1AP), biallelic missense substitutions in SPAG6, CCDC9, TUBA3C, and an in-frame hemizygous deletion in the gene TKTL1.

In the GEMINI cohort, we identified 5 additional cases of predicted deleterious mutations in TKTL1, three cases with homozygous variants in M1AP and one further case with a homozygous TUBA3C substitution (Table 2). No GEMINI cases were identified to carry biallelic disease causing variants in autosomal-recessive genes SPAG6 and CCDC9.

Next, we review the conclusion for each family.

This family presented with two brothers diagnosed with both asthenozoospermia and oligozoospermia and a third brother with azoospermia. In all three brothers, a homozygous missense mutation (ENST00000313311.10:c.C928T:p.R310W) was detected in the gene Sperm-Associated Antigen 6 (SPAG6), (Table 1, Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Figure 1). SPAG6 protein is known to be a part of the central pair complex of the sperm axoneme and ependymal cilia in mammals23. The affected position Arg310 is highly conserved from zebrafish to human, falling within a HEAT repeat of the SPAG6 protein (Supplementary Figure 2). Expression of SPAG6 appears highly specific to testis (throughout meiosis and early spermatids), with secondary expression in the lung, pituitary, and other regions of the brain (Figure 3). In the literature, three unrelated cases of asthenoteratozoospermia were attributed to mutations in SPAG6
24,25. In this study, we were unable to study the abundance of SPAG6 protein in the patients’ sperm or analyze the sperm with electron microscopy (EM), and therefore, the variant was classified as a VUS.

A homozygous nonsynonymous SNV in CCDC9 (ENST00000643617.1:c.C232T: p.R78W) was detected in all three infertile male patients in the consanguineous asthenozoospermia Family 4 (Table 1, Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Figure 1). CCDC9 is broadly expressed across the body, and in testis, it is restricted to very early spermatogonia and elongating spermatids (Figure 3). The arginine at residue 78 is conserved to drosophila (Supplementary Figure 2). A mouse knockout has not been characterized, although a strong mouse ortholog of CCDC9 exists. One prior study has attributed a homozygous CCDC9 p.S109L variant as the cause of asthenozoospermia in a consanguineous case26. In that study, EM revealed the absence of doublet microtubules and the central pair in the sperm, and sperm histology showed a reduced abundance of CCDC9 protein. As the missense variants in the current and previous study differ, and the sperm from Family 4 patients has been unavailable for additional phenotyping, this variant was also classified as VUS.

In non-consanguineous Family 6, a 3bp in-frame deletion in the X-linked gene TKTL1 segregated with spermatogenic impairment. TKTL1 gene encodes a transketolase-like protein 1, one of the three transketolase proteins encoded in the human genome. Considering the GTEx tissue panel, TKTL1 shows very strong and specific expression in testis (median TPM 169.7), with the only other appreciable expression in whole blood (Figure 3). All three transketolase genes appear to be expressed in the germline, with TKTL1 and TKTL2 being highly specific to testis and TKT more broadly expressed across the body. Curiously, these three genes show largely non-overlapping expression patterns throughout spermatogenesis, suggesting that they may serve the same, related role, modified by the specific metabolic or cellular environment at specific stages of development (Figure 4). TKTL1 is expressed in spermatogonia and pre-pachytene spermatocytes, following the expression of TKT and preceding TKTL2.

Deleterious variants in TKTL1 were identified in seven additional azoospermia cases, five in the GEMINI cohort4,27 and two in recent literature28 (Table 2). For six of these, testis histology data is available, presenting maturation arrest in four cases, Sertoli cell-only tubules in one case, and hypospermatogenesis in one case. However, deleterious TKTL1 variants were not significantly enriched in 1503 GEMINI cases compared to 639 normozoospermic controls (p=0.33, Fisher’s Exact Test).

In family 7, only a single homozygous missense SNV was identified that was perfectly segregating with male infertility trait, TUBA3C (c.G736A:p.G246R). TUBA3C encodes Tubulin Alpha 3c, which, along with TUBA3D and TUBA3E encode Tubulin Alpha 3 in humans. Alpha tubulins, along with beta tubulins, are one of the two major components of microtubules in the body. Expression of TUBA3 genes appears enriched in, or specific to, germ cells. TUBA3C is broadly expressed throughout spermatogenesis, from spermatogonia to round spermatids (Figure 3). In the GEMINI cohort, a Portuguese non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) case with maturation arrest carried a homozygous missense change of the conserved residue, TUBA3C c. p.P261L and not observed in gnomAD (Table 2). Both identified TUBA3C variants are currently classified as VUS.

We identified a pathogenic homozygous 10kb deletion encompassing exon two of the gene Meiosis 1-associated protein (M1AP) segregating with infertility in family 9 (Figure 5). This deletion was detected with all three CNV calling methods that we tested (Methods). The deletion was genotyped as homozygous in the three brothers affected by azoospermia, and heterozygous in the unaffected brother. M1AP is a well-established azoospermia gene, associated with meiotic arrest in both humans29,30 and mice31. This deletion seems rare on a global scale, as it has not been reported in gnomAD, CCDG, or 1000G datasets, cumulatively encompassing over 130,000 whole genomes. Curiously, M1AP transcripts are expressed at detectable levels across the human body, with about a 5-to-10-fold enrichment in the testis compared to other tissues (Figure 3).

Here, we describe our findings from WGS of seven families with multiplex male infertility recruited from northern Pakistan. To date, the vast majority of sequence analysis of male infertility has used cost-effective whole-exome sequencing (WES). In this study, we opted to WGS for several reasons. As the cost of sequencing continues to drop, WGS will soon replace WES; we saw this study as a good opportunity to expand our analysis capabilities to address the additional challenges and opportunities presented by WGS data. Using large, multiplex families would a) increase the chances of studying patients with a genetic form of infertility and b) allow us to use familial segregation as an additional filter to reduce the number of variants to be assessed. We identified candidate variants in five of the seven families, implicating one gene with well-established relevance to male infertility (M1AP), and genes with emerging relevance (SPAG6, CCDC9, TKTL1, TUBA3C). Despite this success, our study failed to find a plausible candidate in two of the families (5 and 8). While there is a chance that infertility in these families does not have a genetic origin, we identified several aspects of our analysis that we will target for improvement going forward. First, we only analyzed variants in the coding regions of the genome. Second, our filtering approach assumed that the same variant was the cause of all cases within a family. Third, we only assessed SNVs, indels, and CNVs. There are additional forms of variation that are more complex to identify from the short-read sequencing data that we used, including variable number of tandem repeat (VNTR) mutations, centromeric repeats, and transposable element insertions, to name a few. Research in other monogenic diseases suggests that these more exotic variants should be responsible for a rather small fraction of cases (<5%), but as experimental and computational methods progress, we see these as being integrated into WGS analyses within the next 5 years. On the positive side, the identification of the 10kb M1AP deletion, affecting a single exon, was a direct result of the WGS approach, as this causal mutation would have been missed with standard WES.

How are we to interpret the positive findings for five families? For Family 9, the M1AP deletion can be strongly interpreted as a pathogenic variant by ACMG practice guidelines22. This deletion of exon 2 is a likely loss-of-function (LoF) mutation in a gene where LoF is an established mechanism of disease in mice and humans. For the other variants, the evidence is more limited.

In Family 6, the SPAG6 p.R310W missense variant affects a gene that is necessary for normal sperm motility in mice, as demonstrated by knockout studies. Male Spag6 knockout mice are infertile due to severe motility and morphology defects23, Recent papers have described one sporadic case with asthenoteratozoospermia attributed to compound heterozygous LoF mutations in SPAG6 and two consanguineous patients, homozygous for p.A103D or p.K196Sfs*6 24,25. Notably, ultrastructural sperm defects seen in all three men were consistent with the mouse knockout- loss of one or both of the central pair microtubules. In Family 4, the best candidate was CCDC9 p.R78W substitution, whereas the implicated gene has not been characterized in mice. Homozygous CCDC9 p.S109L has been reported in asthenozoospermia with the absence of doublet microtubules and the central pair in the sperm26. In both of these cases, a limitation in this study was inability to conduct additional sperm analyses for research purposes and therefore the findings were classified as candidate variants.

In Family 7, we identified a deleterious genotype in TUBA3C. Interestingly, TUBA3C does not appear to have a direct ortholog in mice, whose tubulin alpha 3 genes consist of Tuba3a and Tuba3b
32. Single knockouts of Tuba3a were not reported to have major effects on sperm production, but double knockouts of Tuba3a and paralog Tuba3b have spermatogenic failure and are infertile 33. In a previous analysis of copy number variants in Han Chinese cases of NOA, we identified 3 large deletions spanning the related gene TUBA3E in 1000 cases but none in 1600 controls 34.

The rate of genetic findings in this study (71%) was much higher than what we observed recently using WES of unrelated singleton cases (20%). Thus, we remain enthusiastic about the value of WGS of multiplex families with infertility from consanguineous families from Pakistan and beyond. Furthermore, two of the candidate diagnostic variants (in TKTL1 and M1AP), had yet to be reported in any large population database, highlighting the fact that, in addition to enriching for genetic forms of infertility, our patient recruitment strategy also allowed us to sample causal variants that may be missing from other more highly characterized populations. Screening more families with WGS, especially in underrepresented populations, will further reveal the types of variants underlying male infertility and accelerate the use of genetics in assisted reproduction.
