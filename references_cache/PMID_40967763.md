# Reduction of systemic inflammation by elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor correlates with lung function improvement in cystic fibrosis.
**Authors:** Halle O, Graeber SY, Kontsendorn J, Kessemeier C, Falke JN, Schwabe J, Schütz K, Pallenberg ST, Dalferth R, Grychtol R, Ringshausen FC, Stahl M, Thee S, Roehmel JF, Syunyaeva Z, Duerr J, Chung J, Hirtz S, Uselmann T, Kühbandner I, Rückes-Nilges C, Bagheri-Pothoff A, Barth S, Schaub B, Brinkmann F, Weber S, van Koningsbruggen-Rietschel S, Abdo M, Weckmann M, Widder S, Hansen G, Tümmler B, Sommerburg O, Naehrlich L, Mall MA, Dittrich AM
**Journal:** Eur Respir J (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1183/13993003.00150-2025](https://doi.org/10.1183/13993003.00150-2025)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The triple cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator modulator therapy elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) rapidly improves airway and systemic inflammation in people with cystic fibrosis. However, longitudinal effects on systemic inflammation and their relationship to lung function remain unknown.

METHODS: In this prospective, observational, multicentre study, we analysed peripheral blood neutrophil counts, C-reactive protein (CRP) and six pro-inflammatory serum cytokines in a cohort of 198 people with cystic fibrosis aged ≥6 years at baseline and follow-up visits 3, 12 and 24 months after initiation of ETI, compared to 74 age-matched healthy control participants.

RESULTS: Neutrophil counts and CRP, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6 and IL-8 were reduced to 71%, 40%, 41%, 63%, 46% and 81% of median baseline values, respectively, after 3 months of therapy (all p<0.05), whereas monocyte chemotactic protein-1 reached 82% of baseline levels at 12 months only (p<0.05). Change from baseline to 3 months correlated with improvements in percent predicted forced expiratory volume for all systemic inflammation parameters except IL-8 (Spearman's r -0.17 to -0.42, p<0.05). All cytokines reached healthy control levels at or before 24 months. Decreased inflammation levels were sustained until 24 months for all parameters (p<0.05) except IL-6.

CONCLUSIONS: Our results demonstrate that ETI exerts rapid and sustained effects on systemic inflammation associated with lung function improvements in children, adolescents and adults with cystic fibrosis in a real-world, post-approval setting. However, our data also show that individual markers of systemic inflammation remain at levels above those of healthy controls, particularly in certain subgroups, suggesting persistence or resurgence of residual systemic inflammation.

Chronic pulmonary inflammation is a key driver of cystic fibrosis disease progression [ 1– 5 ], with dysfunction of the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) anion channel disturbing hydration and viscoelastic properties of airway mucus, causing mucus obstruction and sterile inflammation [ 6– 9 ]. The ensuing bacterial dysbiosis and chronic infection with hallmark pathogens Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [ 10 , 11 ] perpetuate airway inflammation, pathological mucus secretion and progressive lung damage, which remains the main cause of cystic fibrosis morbidity and mortality [ 1– 3 ].

Treatment with the CFTR modulator triple combination therapy elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI), designed to restore CFTR protein function, strongly improves lung function and other clinical outcomes in people with cystic fibrosis carrying at least one copy of the F508del allele or other responsive CFTR variants [ 6 , 12– 24 ]. ETI improves CFTR function [ 25 ] and the rheological properties of airway mucus [ 26 ], and partially normalises microbial load and composition [ 26– 28 ]. Analyses of sputum and nasal swabs demonstrate that ETI significantly reduces airway inflammation in adults and children with cystic fibrosis [ 26 , 29 , 30 ], reaching levels of non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis patients [ 29 ]. Nevertheless, these studies caution that after 3 months, inflammation might not attain healthy control levels in adults [ 26 ] nor in children [ 31 ].

Prior to the initiation of CFTR modulator therapy, elevation of white blood cell counts (WBC) and C-reactive protein (CRP), indicating waning control over local pulmonary infection and inflammation, occur primarily during exacerbations and more commonly in cystic fibrosis patients with advanced lung disease, but rarely in clinically stable people with cystic fibrosis. Nevertheless, while WBC counts in most people with cystic fibrosis typically already lie within or only slightly above the normal reference range, ETI reduces them further [ 32 , 33 ]. We thus hypothesise that in cystic fibrosis the set-point of systemic inflammation is elevated by chronic pulmonary and/or systemic hyperinflammatory processes [ 34 ] and might be lowered by ETI. Several recent studies report reductions in systemic inflammatory cytokines as early as 1 and 3 months after initiation of ETI [ 29 , 33 , 35 ], which are maintained up to 6 [ 35– 37 ] and 12 months [ 29 , 33 , 35– 37 ], but may not reach healthy control levels [ 36 , 38 ]. However, the effects of ETI on systemic, i.e. peripherally detectable, inflammation across age groups encompassing children, adolescents and adults, their durability over a time span beyond 12 months, and their relationship with clinical trajectories have not yet been addressed.

Here, we characterised the effects of ETI treatment on biomarkers of systemic inflammation and their relationship with changes in lung function in 198 people with cystic fibrosis aged ≥6 years enrolled in the prospective Modulate-CF study (clinicaltrials.gov identifier NCT04732910), compared to 74 age-matched healthy controls.

Additional details are provided in the supplementary material.

We enrolled people with cystic fibrosis in a prospective observational study at four different sites of the German Center for Lung Research (Modulate-CF) once ETI was licensed by the European Medicines Agency, starting in August 2020 with adolescents and adults (aged ≥12 years) and continuing in January 2022 with children (aged 6–11 years). Our study was approved by the ethics committees of the four sites (EA2/220/18, 10989_BO_K_2023, S-803/2019, AZ 62/14). Exclusion criteria were prior exposure to ETI, prior solid organ transplantation and acute pulmonary exacerbations at study visit. All patients not meeting the exclusion criteria were invited to participate. If a pulmonary exacerbation occurred <14 days before inclusion or subsequent study visits, inclusion or study visits were postponed and all consenting patients screened were included into the study.

Serum samples from healthy control participants were collected at a single time point and samples were subjected to cytokine analysis along with sera from people with cystic fibrosis. Adult healthy controls were recruited at the study site performing the cytokine measurements (Hannover Medical School) via a public announcement (n=34, adults only, ethical approval #10748_BO_S_2023 and #2923-2015). Paediatric healthy control samples aged <18 years were obtained from the ALLIANCE cohort [ 39 ], a cohort of asthmatic children and adults into which age- and sex-matched healthy controls are recruited (n=40, children only, ethical approval #1672-2013). Exclusion criteria for both healthy control cohorts included chronic lung disease, chronic inflammatory or immunological diseases, acute illness, use of immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory medications, leukopenia, a history of organ or bone marrow transplantation and, in case of ALLIANCE participants, prematurity. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study, their parents or legal guardians.

Data were collected before (baseline) and at follow-up time points 3, 12 and 24 months after initiation of ETI (supplementary figure S1). Clinical features of Modulate-CF participants from baseline to 12 months, outside of their association with systemic inflammation, have been published previously [ 25– 27 , 31 , 40 , 41 ]. Details on the parameters available at the different visits are provided in supplementary table S1 and information on healthy control participants in supplementary table S7.

A U-PLEX Custom Biomarker (human) Assay (K15067M-2; Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD, USA) was used to measure serum concentrations of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), interleukin (IL)-17A, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1 (supplementary methods).

Data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), SPSS 28.0.0.0 (IBM, Ehningen, Germany), and R version 4.4.3. Measurements in the text are presented as median (interquartile range (IQR)), unless otherwise stated. For statistical testing procedures, please refer to the figure legends and the supplementary methods. In figures, asterisks (*) indicate p<0.05, which was accepted as statistically significant. Pairwise comparisons without asterisks indicate p>0.05. ANOVA tests were performed between healthy control and individual cystic fibrosis measurements, as well as between baseline and later cystic fibrosis time points. p-values were corrected using Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction.

198 people with cystic fibrosis were examined in this study (table 1, figure 1), along with serum samples from 74 age-matched healthy controls (supplementary table S7). Grouped according to the ETI licensing indication, 149 people with cystic fibrosis were adolescents aged ≥12 years and adults (supplementary table S2), and 49 were children aged 6–11 years, with median (IQR) ages of 22 (16–31) years and 9 (7–11) years at baseline, respectively (supplementary table S3). In accordance with published studies [ 12 , 13 , 18 , 21 , 23 ], ETI initiation caused rapid and sustained clinical improvements in our cohort. Details on demographics and clinical characteristics at baseline are presented in table 1; age-specific trajectories are presented in supplementary tables S2 and S3.

To establish the relationship between systemic inflammation and lung disease severity before ETI initiation, we analysed correlations between systemic inflammation markers and percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 s (ppFEV1) at baseline. Neutrophil counts, CRP, G-CSF, IL-17A, IL-1β and IL-6 significantly correlated with ppFEV1 (all p<0.05, Spearman correlation coefficients ranging from −0.26 (IL-17A) to −0.58 (IL-6)), whereas no significant correlations were detected between ppFEV1 and IL-8 or MCP-1 (figure 2, supplementary figure S2). Stratification according to ppFEV1 underlined these findings (supplementary table S4).

Stratification according to S. aureus detection at baseline revealed significantly higher neutrophil counts in cystic fibrosis patients with than without S. aureus, but no differences in CRP or serum cytokine levels (p<0.05) (supplementary figure S3a and supplementary table S4). Neutrophil counts and levels of CRP, G-CSF and IL-6 in patients with cystic fibrosis were significantly higher when P. aeruginosa was detected at baseline (p<0.05) (supplementary figure S3b and supplementary table S4). Consistently, P. aeruginosa detection correlated significantly with G-CSF and IL-6 concentrations (r=0.26 and 0.29, respectively, both p<0.05) (supplementary figure S2). Stratification according to other microbial species, e.g. Burkholderia or Achromobacter, was not feasible because of the low detection rates of these species (supplementary table S6). There were no significant correlations between inflammation markers and sex (except IL-17A), F508del genotype or prior CFTR modulator therapy (supplementary figure S2, supplementary table S5).

At baseline, most cystic fibrosis patients showed neutrophil counts and CRP levels within normal reference ranges (figure 3). However, ETI induced significant reductions of neutrophil counts and CRP levels within 3 months with median values declining to 71% and 40% of baseline levels, respectively, and remaining significantly reduced compared to baseline at 12 and 24 months (all p<0.05) (figure 3, table 1, supplementary table S4a).

Compared to healthy controls, baseline levels of all cytokines except for IL-17A were significantly elevated in people with cystic fibrosis (all p<0.05) (figure 3). ETI treatment evoked different patterns of response. Median levels of G-CSF significantly declined to 41% of baseline levels within 3 months of ETI treatment (p<0.05), and remained at this level, with no more statistically significant differences compared to healthy control levels at 3, 12 and 24 months (p<0.05) (figure 3, supplementary table S4c). Median levels of IL-17A did not change significantly from baseline at any time point throughout the observation period, although the median value decreased steadily, reaching a value similar to healthy controls at 24 months (figure 3, supplementary table S4d). Median levels of IL-1β were significantly reduced to 63% of baseline levels were and no longer significantly different from healthy controls at 3 months after ETI initiation (p<0.05) (figure 3, supplementary table S4e). Compared to baseline, IL-1β levels remained significantly lower at the 12- and 24-month time points (p<0.05) (figure 3). Median levels of IL-6 were significantly reduced to 46% of their baseline levels after 3 months of ETI treatment, with these reductions remaining significant at 12 months (p<0.05) (figure 3, supplementary table S4f), but not at 24 months, where levels were once again increased significantly above those in healthy controls (p<0.05) (figure 3). IL-8 levels showed a significant decrease to 81% of baseline values after 3 months and continued to decrease until 12 and 24 months (all p<0.05 compared to baseline) (figure 3, supplementary table S4g). Nevertheless, IL-8 levels became indistinguishable from healthy controls after 24 months of ETI treatment only (figure 3). MCP-1 did not attain significantly reduced levels compared to baseline until the 12- and 24-month visits (both p<0.05) (figure 2) and reached healthy control levels after 24 months only (figure 3, supplementary table S4h).

Focused analysis of those patients with cystic fibrosis with very high baseline inflammation values, i.e. values above the age-matched reference threshold for neutrophils or CRP, or above the 75th percentile of the complete cohort for cytokines, also revealed pronounced reductions of inflammation (supplementary figure S4), with a few, but critical, differences compared to the complete cohort (figure 3). Overall systemic inflammation was substantially reduced also in the high baseline inflammation subgroup. However, for IL-1β, IL-8 and MCP-1, despite statistically significant reductions compared to baseline, median levels at the 24-month time point were still above the IQR of the healthy control group (supplementary figure S4). In particular, MCP-1 levels in the high baseline inflammation subgroup were still significantly higher than in healthy controls at the 24-month time point and, similar to the whole cohort, levels of IL-6 resurged above levels of healthy controls after 24 months (supplementary figure S4). Moreover, IL-17A levels at baseline were significantly higher in the subgroup selected for high values than in healthy controls (supplementary figure S4) and significantly reduced at 3, 12 and 24 months, with no more statistically significant differences to healthy controls after the baseline visit and with median levels at 24 months resembling those in healthy controls (supplementary figure S4). Finally, compared to the whole cohort, IL-8 levels declined more rapidly in the high baseline inflammation subgroup (supplementary figure S4).

To exclude that sustained inflammation in some patients was due to more frequent exacerbations, we stratified the cohort into three groups based on the exacerbation rate during the observation period of our study. Overall, the median (IQR) number of pulmonary exacerbations was 0.5 (0–0.5) per patient per year only. However, cystic fibrosis patients with a higher number of exacerbations throughout the observation period did not exhibit higher levels of systemic inflammation at baseline (supplementary figure S5) or at 24 months (supplementary figure S6).

To dissect age-specific effects of ETI, we subgrouped our cohort into children (aged 6–11 years), and adolescents and adults (aged ≥12 years). In both subgroups, median neutrophil counts and CRP levels at baseline and throughout the observation period were within normal reference ranges (figure 4, supplementary table S2, supplementary table S3). All cytokine levels at baseline, except IL-17A, were significantly higher in adolescents and adults with cystic fibrosis compared to age-matched healthy controls (p<0.05) (figure 4, see also supplementary table S4). In contrast, at baseline in children with cystic fibrosis, only the levels of IL-8 and MCP-1 were significantly higher than in age-matched healthy controls (figure 4, see also supplementary table S4).

Comparing the two cystic fibrosis age groups, adolescents and adults showed significantly higher baseline levels of neutrophils, CRP and IL-6 than children (all p<0.05) (figure 4), while the other parameters did not differ significantly between age groups. Consistent with these age comparisons, neutrophils, CRP, G-CSF and IL-6 correlated significantly with age (supplementary figure S2).

As observed in the all-age cohort, in adolescents and adults with cystic fibrosis, neutrophil counts, CRP and cytokines except IL-17A and MCP-1 were significantly reduced after 3 months (p<0.05) (figure 4, supplementary table S4). In contrast, in children, a statistically significant reduction between baseline and 3 months was observed exclusively for neutrophil counts (p<0.05) (figure 4, supplementary table S4), although median levels below baseline were detected for several additional parameters after initiation of ETI. Accordingly, after 3 months of ETI treatment, neutrophils remained significantly higher in adolescents and adults compared to children (figure 4, supplementary table S4a).

Consecutively, we performed correlation analyses of changes in ppFEV1 and systemic inflammation markers after 3 months of ETI treatment. Statistically significant correlations with change in ppFEV1 were detected for changes in neutrophil numbers, CRP, G-CSF, IL-17A, IL-1β, IL-6 and MCP-1 (all p<0.05; figure 5). Based on Spearman's correlation coefficients, the strength of correlation with change in ppFEV1 was weak (0.1–0.4) for changes in IL-6 (r=−0.33), CRP (r=−0.31), G-CSF (r=−0.30), MCP-1 (r=−0.26), IL-17A (r=−0.19) and IL-1β (r=−0.17), and moderate correlation strength (0.4–0.7) was detected for neutrophils (r=−0.42). Thus, neutrophils, CRP, G-CSF and IL-6 were the parameters showing the highest association with impaired lung function at baseline (figure 2) and most pertinently mirroring improvements in lung function (figure 5).

To our knowledge, this is the first report on systemic inflammation across a time span of 24 months in ETI-treated children, adolescents and adults with cystic fibrosis. Our sizeable cohort is characterised by robust participant retention at 24 months and includes a wide spectrum of lung disease severity. As such, our study demonstrates rapid and sustained reduction of systemic inflammation under ETI that correlates with improvements in lung function. Comparisons to age-matched healthy controls and of children versus adolescents and adults with cystic fibrosis uncover age-specific differences and suggest that older patients are more likely to retain low-grade residual systemic inflammation and may even rebound within a 24-month observation period. In addition to previously well-characterised inflammation markers, i.e. neutrophil counts, CRP, IL-6 and IL-8, our study provides insight into the response of G-CSF, IL-17A, IL-1β and MCP-1, which have been less comprehensively characterised previously, uncovering distinct kinetic response patterns and differences in these parameters’ association with lung function.

Our findings associating S. aureus and P. aeruginosa detection in airway samples with systemic inflammation markers that also mirror lung function improvements suggest that systemic inflammation is largely driven by airway inflammation. Consistently, previous studies observed ETI to reduce pulmonary microbial load [ 26– 28 ] and reduced airway inflammation [ 26 , 29 ]. Given the known role of IL-1β, G-CSF and IL-6 in shaping the IL-17A-rich environment in cystic fibrosis lung disease [ 42 ], and our previous findings on the role of IL-17A role in neutrophilic inflammation, tissue destruction [ 43 ] and microbial infection [ 44 ], our new results link microbial detection, lung function and neutrophil counts, CRP, G-CSF and IL-6 in a way which supports a model of “inflammation overspill”, where systemic inflammation in cystic fibrosis is reflective of the degree of pulmonary inflammation and thus lung disease severity.

To date, it remains speculative whether the 40–50% of functional CFTR improvement in biomarkers achieved by ETI [ 25 ] will suffice to durably dampen airway inflammation and thus systemic inflammation in all people with cystic fibrosis. Casey et al. [ 29 ] found that after 3 and 12 months of ETI treatment in 14 patients with advanced cystic fibrosis lung disease, serum IL-6 and CRP were lower than in people with non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis; however, another study comparing 15 people with cystic fibrosis and five healthy control participants showed that neutrophil counts at 3 months remained higher in cystic fibrosis than in healthy controls [ 36 ]. Analyses of airway inflammation support the hypothesis that ETI treatment is not sufficient to contain airway inflammation. Schaupp et al. [ 26 ] reported inflammation above healthy levels after 3 months of treatment, and even in 6–11-year-old children, single-cell analyses of nasal samples showed that ETI had not reversed all pro-inflammatory changes [ 31 ]. Corroborating and substantially extending these analyses on the insufficient effects of ETI on airway inflammation, here we show that all parameters of systemic inflammation except MCP-1 reach healthy control levels as soon as 3 months after onset of ETI treatment and that MCP-1 reaches healthy control levels after 24 months. Despite these reductions, neutrophil counts of adolescents and adults remain above those observed in children, and IL-6 levels rise again after an initial reduction, suggesting persisting age-dependent low-grade systemic inflammation.

Different mechanisms might underlie the failure of ETI to completely abolish systemic inflammation in cystic fibrosis. As in people with non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis, pre-existing lung damage might be sufficient to maintain a residual level of inflammation [ 45 ]. In that line, we have shown previously using lung magnetic resonance imaging that ETI reduces (but fails to normalise) evidence of structural lung disease even in children with preserved spirometry, a subgroup of whom are included in the present article, suggesting an association of remaining low-grade inflammation with structural lung disease [ 41 ]. Conversely, the effects of ETI on CFTR function [ 25 ] and thereby sputum viscosity [ 26 ] might be insufficient to control the inflammatory properties of mucus itself, comprising mucus-obstruction-induced hypoxia, epithelial necrosis and IL-1β-driven further mucus secretion [ 9 ]. Persistent low-grade systemic inflammation, as detected in our cohort, is found in several diseases besides cystic fibrosis, e.g. cardiovascular disease. Further studies over longer observation periods will need to gauge whether (similar to other diseases) residual low-grade inflammation under ETI therapy constitutes an increased risk for cardiovascular, malignant and neurodegenerative diseases, and thereby overall morbidity and mortality of people with cystic fibrosis [ 46 ].

Interestingly, compared to healthy controls, adolescents and adults displayed elevated baseline levels of all cytokines except IL-17A, whereas in children with cystic fibrosis, only IL-8 and MCP-1 were significantly higher than in healthy controls. These observations harbour two important clinical implications. First, early childhood might constitute a window of opportunity during which initiation of CFTR modulator therapy could durably stabilise “healthy” levels of systemic inflammation, at least in some individuals. Second, given the close correlations of neutrophil counts, CRP, G-CSF and IL-6 levels with lung function and its improvements, monitoring these parameters might be useful to identify patients with insufficient responses and/or risk for adverse disease course.

An intriguing mechanistic observation is the kinetic difference in inflammation marker response. Inflammatory markers responding early, such as neutrophil counts, CRP, G-CSF, IL-1β and IL-6 might be direct indicators of microbial infection and lung function [ 47 , 48 ] and thus mirror changes in innate immunity and early adaptations of the tissue environment. In contrast, “slow responder” markers of systemic inflammation, such as IL-8 and MCP-1, might possibly reflect additional disease processes driven by adaptive immunity [ 49 ] or entailing tissue remodelling [ 50 ], which take more time to revert or may even be less amenable to treatment with ETI.

The limitations of our study lie within its real-world design. First, this design cannot fully exclude the effects of pulmonary exacerbations on systemic inflammation. Our exclusion criterion encompassing a 14-day time-limit (rather than the more commonly used 30 days) was chosen to accommodate the different clinical practices at the participating study sites, but might have resulted in inclusion of some patients with recent exacerbations, which could theoretically impact on the levels of systemic inflammation. However, consistent with previous reports on the effect of ETI on pulmonary exacerbations [ 51 ], the overall exacerbation rate in our cohort was low after initiation of ETI, i.e. median 0.5 (IQR 0–0.5) exacerbations per patient per year, and analyses stratified for exacerbation frequency failed to detect association with higher levels of systemic inflammation at either baseline or after 24 months. Second, the real-world design of our study did not allow us to control for further confounding factors, in particular reduction and subsequent surges in seasonal respiratory infection associated with social distancing and other mitigation measures during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic. Third, we cannot account for participant-initiated changes in therapy, especially following improvement of clinical symptoms, although we would expect such changes to cause underestimation rather than overestimation of the effect of ETI on systemic inflammation. A further limitation pertaining to the observational design is unbalanced recruitment between age groups due to the 17-month time difference in ETI licensing for children versus adolescents and adults. In addition, samples from healthy controls were acquired at a single time point only rather than at several consecutive time points, providing us with no information on whether cytokine levels in healthy controls change over time, although there is evidence to suggest that at least IL-1β, IL-8 and G-CSF display little seasonal variation [ 52 ]. Furthermore, neutrophil and CRP measurements were not available for healthy controls, requiring us to make comparisons with reference datasets. Finally, with a median age of 19 years, our cohort might not be representative of older patients. Our results demonstrate a significant association between the severity of lung disease and systemic inflammation with reduced responses of systemic inflammation to ETI in some individuals with high baseline inflammation. These results suggest that older patients, who are more likely to suffer from more severe lung disease, might not experience the same anti-inflammatory benefit from ETI therapy observed in our cohort of people with cystic fibrosis. In that line, our relatively young cohort may anticipate future cohorts of older cystic fibrosis patients, in whom the underlying CFTR protein defect has been addressed at an increasingly early age.

In summary, we show that ETI exerts significant and sustained anti-inflammatory effects for up to 24 months in a multicentric cohort, largely reducing systemic inflammation in adolescents and adults with cystic fibrosis to the lower levels observed in children with cystic fibrosis in a manner that correlates with lung function improvements. Clinically, our results thus advocate monitoring of systemic inflammation during ETI therapy as a proxy for disproportionate loss of lung function. However, intriguingly, in comparison with healthy controls, a certain degree of residual systemic inflammation remains in many patients. Long-term studies will need to address the relevance of this finding in more detail. Particularly considering the increased lifespan projected to result from ETI [ 53 ], the benefits and shortcomings of ETI will critically depend on its ability to reduce low-grade systemic inflammation in the long-term.
