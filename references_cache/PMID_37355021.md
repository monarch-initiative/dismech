PURPOSE: Cystinosis is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disease (LSDs) 
caused by mutations in the gene encoding cystinosin (CTNS) that leads to cystine 
crystal accumulation in the lysosome that compromises cellular functions 
resulting in tissue damage and organ failure, especially in kidneys and eyes. 
However, the underlying molecular mechanism of its pathogenesis remains elusive. 
Two novel mice lines created via CRISPR are used to examine the pathogenesis of 
cystinosis in the kidney and cornea and the treatment efficacy of corneal 
pathology using self-complimentary Adeno-associated viral (scAAV-CTNS) vector.
METHODS: The CRISPR technique generated two novel cystinotic mouse lines, 
Ctnsis1 (an insertional mutation) and Ctnsis2 (a nonsense mutation). Immune 
histochemistry, renal functions test and HRT2 in vivo confocal microscopy were 
used to evaluate the age-related renal pathogenesis and treatment efficacy of 
the scAAV-CTNS virus in corneal pathology.
RESULTS: Both mutations lead to the production of truncated Ctns proteins. 
Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis 2 mice exhibit the characteristic of cystinotic corneal 
crystal phenotype at four-week-old. Treatment with the scAAV-CTNS viral vector 
decreased the corneal crystals in the treated mice cornea. Ctnsis 1 show renal 
abnormalities manifested by increased urine volume, reduced urine osmolality, 
and the loss of response to Desmopressin (dDAVP) at 22-month-old but Ctnsis2 
don't manifest renal pathology up to 2 years of age.
CONCLUSIONS: Both Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis2 mice exhibit phenotypes resembling human 
intermediate nephropathic and ocular cystinosis, respectively. scAAV-CTNS viral 
vectors reduce the corneal cystine crystals and have a great potential as a 
therapeutic strategy for treating patients suffering from cystinosis.

Cystinosis is an autosomal recessive disease caused by mutations in the CTNS gene encoding cystinosin, a lysosomal and cytoplasmic membrane cystine/proton cotransporter. The absence of cystinosin leads to disulfide-bonded cystine accumulation and the formation of cystine crystals in lysosomes derived from protein catabolism, e.g., autophagy, which ultimately damages organ functions[1–3]. There are three types of cystinosis according to the severity and the onset of the symptoms: nephropathic, intermediate, and ocular. Nephropathic cystinosis, the most severe type, manifests early in life with symptoms of renal Fanconi syndrome characterized by a dysfunctional proximal renal tubule, polyuria, phosphaturia, glycosuria, proteinuria, acidosis, growth retardation, and rickets[4, 5]. These patients show symptoms as early as 6–9 months of age and progress to exhibit symptoms of kidney dysfunction by 18 months of age. Continual deterioration of glomerular function leads to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) by ~10 years if untreated or at a median of ~20 years if cysteamine treatment starts by five years of age [6]. Intermediate cystinosis usually manifests around 12 years of age. It causes photophobia and kidney dysfunction at varying ages, with glomerular and tubular alterations causing marked proteinuria, eventually leading to kidney failure[6, 7]. Ocular cystinosis is characterized by adult-onset mild photophobia without renal manifestations. Excess cystine forms crystals in the cornea, conjunctiva, and retina, which causes ocular disorders, e.g., photophobia, blepharospasm, recurrent corneal erosions, and retinopathy leading to blindness[2, 5–10]. All three types of cystinosis share ocular symptoms.

More than 140 mutations have been identified in cystinotic patients worldwide. The 57 kb large deletion, which misses the promoter region and the first ten exons of the CTNS gene together with upstream of CARKL and TRPV1 alleles, was once considered the most common pathogenic mutation[11, 12]. Genetic landscape of cystinosis various widely based on ethnic population[13]. The large deletion is exclusively distributed in Northern Europe and North America. Increasingly, studies worldwide have reported other mutations such as missense and nonsense mutations, and small indels in all regions of the CTNS gene, including promoter, introns, and exons. Different mutations correlate with different clinical phenotypes. New evidence has shown that many cellular defects in cystinosis are independent of lysosomal overload, suggesting that cystinosin regulates cellular processes independently beyond its known role as a cystine transporter[1, 3, 14–16]. Therefore, studies using various animal models carrying different mutations would yield information for better understanding the molecular mechanism of cystinosis and development of novel therapeutic treatment regimens.

The two most common strategies for treating cystinosis are enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) and bone marrow transplantation (BMT) [17, 18]. However, the production of neutralizing antibodies by the host and graft versus host disease (GvHD) often compromises treatment efficacy [17–22]. Administration of cysteamine is the most effective treatment in reducing cellular cystine by converting cystine to cysteine and cysteine-cysteamine mixed disulfide, both of which can exit the lysosome thus delay the complications caused by cystine crystals[6, 23]. However, cysteamine is toxic to multi-tissues and organs. Further, the administration of cysteamine fails to ameliorate renal Fanconi syndrome. Thus, for successful treatment of cystinosis, it is imperative to reduce the accumulation of lysosomal cystine crystals and provide functional cystinosin continuously. Cell and gene therapy strategies have immense potential in accomplishing these tasks.

AAV-mediated gene therapy has been able to restore visual function in retinal degeneration disorder and restore photoreceptor structure and function in retinal degeneration slow (rds) mice[24]. Following the successful application in animal models, AAV-mediated gene therapy has been considered in clinical trials for retinal degenerative disorders. Recombinant AAV vectors can be either single-stranded or double-stranded. However, single-stranded AAV vectors have the disadvantage of delayed transgene expression, which is attributed to the need for the formation of complementary double-stranded transgene prior to efficient gene expression[25, 26]. Thus, a self-complementary AAV (scAAV) vector containing the self-complementary double-stranded transgene can readily transcribe for mRNA to synthesize Cystinosin and improves the transduction efficiency in many organ systems, e.g., the liver[27], brain cells[28], eye[29] and trabecular meshwork[30], etc. Thus, we anticipate that the use of the scAAV-CTNS vector will greatly improve the efficacy of treating cystinosis.

Here, in an attempt to elucidate possible cystinosin molecular domains involvement in the pathogenesis of cystinosis and to determine treatment efficacy of corneal pathology by gene therapy, we created two novel cystinotic mouse lines, Ctnsis1 (a 22 nt insertional mutation) and Ctnsis2 (a W138X nonsense mutation) by the CRISPR technique, both of which encode truncated Ctns proteins. Ctnsis1 mice exhibit characteristic cystinotic corneal crystal phenotype at four-weeks-old and renal abnormality at 22-months-old, while Ctnsis2 mice only develop corneal crystals beginning at 4 weeks of age without manifestation of renal abnormality up to 2 years. The Ctnsis1 mice model revealed a significant role of Ctns protein in regulating water metabolism by the kidney. The presence of the one remanent transmembrane domain could possibly contribute to the renal abnormalities observed in Ctnsis1 mice model. Both Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis2 mice are novel models with the distinct pathogenesis of cystinosis and are used to develop novel treatments with the scAAV-CTNS viral vector encoding human CTNS transgene. Lastly, we report the age-dependent treatment efficacy using a self-complementary Adeno-associated virus carrying the functional human cystinosin (CTNS) transgene for cystinosis.

Cystinosis (Ctnsis) mouse lines were generated in C57BL/6J via CRISPR gene editing. As described, three sgRNA were designed and validated by T7E1 assay with MK4 mouse cells[31]. (See the guide RNA sequences and efficiencies in Supplementary Table 1). sgRNA1, which has the best editing activity, together with SpCas9 mRNA and a 137-nt ssDNA oligo donor harboring homology arms, W138X, and silent mutations were co-injected into zygotes by the Transgenic Animal and Genome Editing Facility at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC). A schematic of the generation of cystinosis mice is shown in Fig. 1A.

PCR with primers external to the donor DNA identified two independent founder mouse lines, Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis2, as shown in Fig. 1A & 1B. Ctnsis1 is an insertional mutant of 22 nucleotides. The mRNA derived from this mutant allele encodes a frameshifted product of a N-terminal 135-amino-acid-long peptide plus extra 32 amino acids unrelated to Ctns before reaching to a stop codon. Ctnsis2 has the expected knock-in nonsense mutation c.414G>A (W138X; X, stop codon). Thus, it encodes a truncated Ctns protein with the N-terminal 137 amino acids. Fig. 1C shows that the restriction enzyme NlaIII digestion of the PCR products using genomic DNA from WT, Ctnsis1 heterozygotes and homozygotes give 2 (261bp and 127bp), 3 (410bp, 261bp, and 127bp), and 1 (410bp) bands, respectively. Similar digestion patterns for WT, Ctnsis2 heterozygotes and homozygotes, are 2 (261bp and 127bp), 3 (388bp, 261bp, and 127bp), and 1 (388 bp) bands, respectively. Sequencing of PCR products from genomic DNA (Fig. 1B) confirmed the fidelity of the mutations. Images of the entire gel are shown in Supplementary Fig 1.

Both Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis2 mice were viable and fertile. Corneal clarities of Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis2 mice were examined by HRT II in vivo confocal microscopy beginning at four weeks of age, followed by every four weeks thereafter. Corneal cystine crystals were present in both mouse lines at four weeks of age (Fig. 2A). By ten months of age, cystine crystals were detected in the cornea, lens, and iris by stereomicroscope (Fig. 2B). Quantification of corneal crystal intensity of Ctnsis1 mice showed that crystal formation was accumulated over time (Fig. 2C).

To determine the efficacy of treating Cystinosis using a self-complementary AAV vector carrying the functional human CTNS cDNA transgene, Ctnsis homozygous mice at different age group were intrastromally injected with the scAAV-CTNS virus (2 μl of 3.96 × 1013 virus particles/ml) on the left eye. The right eye was injected with PBS as a contralateral control. Cystine crystals in the corneal stroma were examined at day 0 (day of injection), 2, 4, and 8 weeks of viral administration by in vivo HRT II confocal microscopy. Further, the in vivo confocal microscopy images were analyzed using Cell Profiler software[32, 33]. Cystine crystal intensity per micron in the corneal stroma was calculated for the treated and control eyes. Different age group mice were selected to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of scAAV-CTNS viral vectors, i.e., 5-month-old, 7–9-month-old and above 10-month-old Ctnsis mice were used for the study. For statistical analysis, we performed a repeated measure two-way ANOVA followed by Šídák’s multiple comparisons tests.

Corneal cystine crystals in the untreated eye increased gradually from 20 weeks (93.09 ± 37.02) to 48 weeks (396.44 ± 198.37) as shown in Fig. 3A. The observation indicates that there is age-dependent severity of cystine crystal accumulation in corneas of Ctnsis mice. 5 and 7–9-month-old Ctnsis mice that received scAAV-CTNS viral vector had a reduction in the corneal crystals compared to the corneas that received PBS injection. At 8 weeks post treatment, there was a 2–3 times more crystal accumulation in corneas of the untreated mice compared to that of the scAAV-CTNS treated mice. However, the differences are not statistically significant as shown in Fig. 3B and 3C, except in the 5-month-old group where a statistically significant difference occurs between the scAAV-CTNS treated group and PBS group 8 weeks after viral administration (p=0.0178 <0.05) (Table 1, Fig. 3B). In 10-month-old and older mouse group, there was a statistically significant difference between treated and untreated cornea from week 2 to 8 of viral administration (week 2: p=0.0005; week 4: p=0.0043; week 8: p= 0.0047) (Fig. 3D). Representative images of in vivo confocal microscopy corneal stroma and a 3D composite image of 10-month-old cystinosis mice at day zero, weeks 2, 4 and 8 are shown in Fig. 4. All three age groups of mice that received scAAV-CTNS treatment had a baseline cystine corneal crystal intensity of 58.375 ± 12.867 at the end point. There was no sex-related therapeutic efficiency of scAAV-CTNS viral vector in treating corneal pathology cystinosis mice.

Eyeballs of the cystinosis mice were collected at the treatment endpoint. Cryosections of the cornea were collected and stained with rabbit anti-cystinosin antibody. We observed the presence of cystinosin in the scAAV-CTNS-treated mouse cornea stroma, whereas the untreated corneas did not have any positive signals of cystinosin (Fig. 5). This elucidates the successful production of human CTNS via translation of mRNA derived from the transgene of scAAV-CTNS viral vectors in the host mouse corneal stromal keratocytes, which leads to reduction of cystine crystals in treated corneas.

It should be noted that the two cystinosis mouse lines exhibit different outcomes corresponding to different genetic mutations of the Ctns gene. Ctnsis1 mice developed a significant impairment in water balance with normal proximal tubule function at 22 months of age. In contrast, the Ctnsis2 mice did not show any renal abnormalities for up to two years of age. Wild-type (WT) and Ctnsis1 homozygous (Ctnsis1−/−) mice were placed in the metabolic cages with free access to food and water at 5, 12, and 20 months of age to examine their fluid balance and acid-base homeostasis. The results indicated that_the baseline data for water balance and urine osmolality were similar among the three genotypes at 12 months of age (data not shown). To further examine the activity of the proximal tubule, the animals were given a single injection of diuretic acetazolamide (ACTZ) over a 24-hour period (Table 2). Surprisingly, the diuretic effect of ACTZ is similar among all three genotypes at 5 and 12 months of age (Data not shown). Further, to test the capacity of the proximal tubule to adapt to acid stress, 12-month-old WT and Ctnsis1−/− mice were subjected to acid loading insult using NH4Cl at 280 mM in drinking water for five days and measured urinary NH4+ production and excretion by the kidney, as previously described[34, 35]. The results showed that urinary NH4+ excretion on day 5 was similar between WT mice (0.350 ± 0.061 mmol/day, n=5) and Ctnsis1−/− mice (0.434± 0.066 mmol/day, n=4, P>0.05).

To assess if renal phenotypes may be age-dependent, 22-month-old WT, Ctnsis1+/− and Ctnsis1−/− mice were placed in the metabolic cages with free access to food and water. After three days of adjustment, food intake, water balance, and urine osmolality were measured. The results depicted in Fig. 6 show a significant increase in urine volume (Fig. 6A), which correlates with a sharp reduction in urine osmolality (Fig. 6B) in Ctnsis1−/− mice as compared to age-matched WT or heterozygous (Ctnsis1+/−) mice. Polyuria also correlates with an increase in Water intake (P<0.01) in Ctnsis1 homozygous mice, while food intake was similar among all groups (Data not shown). The results demonstrated significant impairment in water balance and urine osmolality in Ctnsis1−/− mice (Figs. 6A and 6B).

To determine whether polyuria in Ctnsis1 homozygous mice results from a central defect in the abnormal secretion of vasopressin, a hormone secreted by the hypothalamus to control water reabsorption in the kidney, animals were treated with exogenous dDAVP, a vasopressin V2 receptor analog, via subcutaneous injection (6 μg/100 g body weight), and urine produced over a 24 h period was collected. Two days after dDAVP treatment, a 24 h urine was collected again for a recovery period. As shown in Fig. 7, dDAVP treatment increased urine osmolality sharply in WT mice, as expected, and slightly but significantly in heterozygous Ctnsis1 mice, but did not alter urine osmolality in its homozygous mutant. During the recovery period, urine osmolality returned toward the baseline levels in both WT and Ctnsis1+/− mice (Fig. 7). These results demonstrate a significant urinary concentrating defect in homozygous mutant mice, which is independent of the circulating levels of vasopressin and is nephrogenic.

The apical Na+-K+−2Cl− cotransporter (NKCC2) in the thick ascending limb of Henle and water channels play an essential role in regulating water balance by the kidney[36]. The expression levels of water channels, Aquaporin 1, 2, 4 and NKCC2, were examined by immunoblotting. AQP2 is the apical vasopressin-regulated water-absorbing channel and is the limiting barrier for water reabsorption in the collecting duct systems. Immunoblotting studies demonstrated a significant downregulation in the apical water channel AQP2, with no change in the abundance of NKCC2 or proximal tubule AQP1 (Fig. 8); however, a significant upregulation with shifting in size down on the Western blot was observed in the expression of basolateral AQP4 (Fig. 8) in Ctnsis1 homozygous mice, as compared to age-matched WT and heterozygous mice. Immunofluorescence staining of AQP2 throughout the kidney regions (Fig. 9) revealed that the protein abundance of AQP2 is significantly reduced in all kidney regions in homozygous mice compared to WT mice kidney. The downregulation of AQP2 accounts for partial, if not complete, increased urine volume and reduced urine osmolality, and the resistance to vasopressin exhibited by the homozygous mutant mice (Figs. 6 and 7)

Taken together, these results suggest that in addition to the expected proximal tubular dysfunction as previously reported[3, 4, 32], the reabsorption of water in the kidney collecting duct is also significantly impaired in cystinosis mice. The impaired water balance in Ctnsis1 mutants resulted from the downregulation of AQP2 and increased water loss caused by a nephrogenic vasopressin independent mechanism.

Wild-type and age-matched Ctnsis1−/− and Ctnsis1+/− were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine hydrochloride (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) and blood was collected from the heart and analyzed using the handheld analyzer (iSTA1) with EC8+ cartridges. The results depicted in Table 3 showed a decrease in sodium and bicarbonate levels in Ctnsis1 homozygous compared to WT mice. Further, a trend toward an increase in BUN and serum K+ and a reduction in hematocrit and hemoglobin, albeit not significant, was observed in homozygous Ctnsis1 vs. WT and heterozygous Ctnsis1 mice. Unlike for 12-month-old mice (see above), the 22-month-old mice likely exhibit a proximal tubule reabsorption defect as shown by a significant reduction in serum HCO3- concentration. The increase in BUN suggests a significant volume depletion due to impaired fluid reabsorption in the proximal tubule and a reduction in vasopressin-mediated water reabsorption via AQP2 in the collecting duct system.

Aiming to mimic the human cystinotic mutation W138X in mouse by CRISPR editing technique, we obtained two distinct novel cystinotic mouse lines: Ctnsis1 has a 22 nt insertion in Exon 7, resulting in a short protein with the first 135 aa of Cystinosin followed by additional 32 amino acids unrelated to Ctns. In contrast, Ctnsis2 carries a G→A point mutation leading to desired Ctns W138X. This is not surprising because the efficiency of CRISPR/Cas9 cleavage is high, but the efficiency of homology-directed recombination (HDR) is relatively low. A large portion of the Cas9-induced DSBs will be repaired via NHEJ and lead to insertions or deletions [37]. Nevertheless, both mutant mouse lines lead to truncated cystinosin, missing the C-terminal Ctns domain essential for the lysosomal cystine transportation. Interestingly, the two novel Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis2 mice lines display pathology resembling human intermediate and ocular cystinosis, respectively.

As demonstrated by HRTII in vivo confocal microscopy scanning, as early as 4 weeks the cystine crystals were present in corneas of both mice lines. The accumulation of these crystals was progressive and consistent with the progression in human patients.

Mutations in the cystinosin gene (CTNS), which encodes the cystine transporter, are associated with cystine accumulation in the lysosome and cause lysosomal dysfunction. It is now established that nephropathic Cystinosis resulting from renal proximal tubule reabsorption defect (Fanconi syndrome) is the most severe form of Cystinosis [4, 38]. However, our current results in this specific cystinosis mouse model demonstrate that loss of functional Ctns is associated with a significant urinary concentrating defect, as shown by polyuria and reduced urine osmolality in Ctnsis1−/− vs. WT or Ctnsis1+/− mice (Fig. 6). This renal defect developed after 18 months of age, which agrees with the previous reports using Ctns null mice generated by Cherqui S, et al[39]. Further, our results demonstrated for the first time that the increased urination in Ctnsis1−/− mice results from a significant downregulation in the protein abundance of the kidney collecting duct water channel AQP2 (Figs. 8 and 9). AQP2 is the limiting barrier for vasopressin- regulated water reabsorption in the kidney[40, 41]. While AQP1 was not affected, a significant increase in the AQP4 protein expression with slightly reduced size on the Western blot is observed in Ctnsis1−/−; however, whether the shift in AQP4 is associated with a functional or inactive protein is unclear at this time. The salt transporter Na+-K+- 2Cl- cotransporter (NKCC2) in the medullary thick ascending limb is essential in the urinary concentrating mechanism. However, its expression is not altered in Ctnsis1−/− vs WT or Ctnsis1+/− mice (Fig. 8). The downregulation of aquaporin 2 is the heart of the mechanism responsible for water loss and urinary concentrating defect, which is the paradigm shifting mechanism in the etiology of impaired fluid transport balance in cystinosis. Cystinosis has always been linked to the dysregulation of fluid transport in the proximal tubule, but this model clearly identifies new sites of the kidney with significant defect in water handling.

Lastly, we further demonstrated that exogenous desmopressin (dDAVP), a vasopressin V2 receptor-specific agonist, caused a significant increase in urinary concentrating ability in WT and Ctnsis1+/− but not in Ctnsis1−/− mice (Fig. 7), indicating that the impaired water balance in Ctnsis1−/− mice is nephrogenic.

Intriguingly, the ocular crystals were shown in both mouse lines, while renal phenotype was only present in Ctnsis1 mice older than 22 months but not detected in Ctnsis2 mice even at 2 years of age, a phenotype resembling human ocular cystinosis. Similarly, all three types of cystinosis patients manifest ocular abnormalities in humans, but only patients with nephropathic and intermediate cystinosis show a renal defect.

It is not surprising that cornea was affected in both of our cystinotic mouse lines and all cystinotic patients. Cystinosis was first identified as a defect in lysosomal cystine transporter causing accumulation of cystine crystals especially in tissue like cornea where there is a lack of blood flow and fluid exchange.

However, why the renal abnormal is not present in all cystinotic patients has plagued scientists for many years. There are at least three possible explanations. Firstly, cystinosin might not be the exclusive cystine transporter in the lysosome. The cystine/glutamate antiporter encoded by solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11; also known as xCT) exchanges extracellular cystine for intracellular glutamate. The expression of xCT has been detected throughout the brain and other tissues such as the gastrointestinal tract, endocrine tissues, pancreas, and kidney (The Human Protein Atlas [42]). It regulates synaptic activity by stimulating extra synaptic receptors and performs non-vesicular glutamate release, helps maintain redox balance and cell survival[37]. It is known that xCT is in the cytoplasm membrane. However, Shih et al. demonstrated that HEK293 cells transfected with the GFP-tagged xCT and its accessory subunit 4F2hc displayed a highly clustered plasma membrane and intracellular distribution, suggesting the presence of subcellular domains[43] which is consistent with the finding reported by Mukhopadhyay, S. et al. who showed that loss of autophagy leads to xCT translocation from the plasma membrane to a lysosomal membrane in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells[44]. Although the transport function of xCT is impaired in the setting of autophagy inhibition, whether this cystine transporter is located and functional in the lysosomal membrane of cystinotic cells remains elusive. Also, the age dependent decline in the autophagy process[45] can affect the function of xCT transporters that could lead to the presence of higher crystal numbers observed in older mice.

Thus, it is highly probable that the relatively healthy lysosomes may reduce cystine accumulation by using xCT transporter, thus limiting cystine needle formation in young cystinosis mice. However, as the mice get older, the lysosomal dysfunction due to aging accompanied by dysregulation of cystine transporter function would manifest accumulation of cystine in many organ systems, if not all. This can in part explain our observations of age-dependent progressive corneal cystine crystal formation and less efficacy by scAAV-CTNS virus in young Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis2 mice. Alternatively, in young experimental mice the scAAV-CTNS treatment might in fact be able to slow down the accumulation of the cystine crystals. However, random distribution of low levels of cystine crystals led to inaccurate quantification. Also, the crystal intensity per micron was determined by averaging cystine crystals from nine scans of 1 μm areas of individual corneas which might reduce the test’s sensitivity. In contrast, presence of higher cystine crystals made it easier to demonstrate the efficacy of treatment with scAAV-CTNS virus as shown in Fig. 4. In our experiments, we observed a reduction in the cystine crystal deposition but not a complete elimination to the level of wildtype mice. There was no sex- related therapeutic efficiency in reduction of crystals in female and male mice cornea. Both genders showed similar trend in the therapeutic profile. Dosage of the viral vector can be explored in the future to determine if multiple AAV viral vector administrations can help in the complete restoration of corneas in cystinosis mice. Taken together, our results are encouraging and promising in using scAAV-CTNS viral vectors for treating corneal cystine crystals.

Emerging evidence indicates that renal pathogenesis in cystinosis patients may be independent from the loss of CTNS’s cystine transporter function with respect to distinct mutations [3]. The lost function of Cystinosin in the autophagy pathway, e.g., mTOR signaling, TFEB (transcription factor E box-binding protein) expression and chaperone-mediated autophagy may contribute to the pathogenesis of Cystinosis independent of the deposition of cystine crystals [1, 14–16].

It is notable that both the Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis2 mouse lines produce truncated cystinosin, but only Ctnsis1 shows the renal defect. We analyzed the topology and the features of the two mutant cystinosin proteins by the available online tool, Protter. The results showed that the 137 a.a. Ctnsis2 cystinosin lost all the seven transmembrane domains while the 169 a.a. Ctnsis1 cystinosin still had one transmembrane-spanning domain of the additional 32 a.a. sequence that may mediate membrane insertion (Fig. 10). It is plausible to hypothesize that the presence of such a partial membrane insertion domain seen in Ctnsis1 mice may contribute to kidney pathogenesis. However, this possibility needs to be further examined in future studies. Whether the truncated Ctnsis1 cystinosin can really reach the membrane, disturb the cystine transportation, aberrant autophagy and perturbed redox as well. Further studies are needed to elucidate the detail molecular mechanisms how the truncated Ctnsis1 cystinosin perturbs transportation of cystine homeostasis etc., which will be investigated in our future study.

To determine the treatment efficacy of the scAAV-CTNS viral vector, 2 μl of the virus (3.96 × 1013 virus particles/ml) was injected into oculus sinister (OS) and 2 μl of PBS was injected to the oculus dextrus (OD) of the Ctnsis1 and Ctnsis2 mice via intrastromal route as described previously[48]. Briefly, the cystinotic mice were anesthetized with 100 μg/g of ketamine and 12.5 μg/g of xylazine. A 32-gauge needle was used to make a tunnel in the corneal stroma. A 33-gauge needle was used to deliver 7.92 × 1010 virus particles in 2 μl into the tunnel.

In vivo examination of cornea light scatter was performed with the Heidelberg Retinal Tomograph-HRT II Rostock Cornea Module (HRT-II, Heidelberg Engineering Inc., Heidelberg, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instruction as described previously[49]. Briefly, a drop of GenTeal® Gel (Novartis Pharmaceuticals Corp., East Hanover, New Jersey) was applied to the tip of the HRT-II objective as immersion fluid. After anesthesia, a series of images were collected to cover the whole cornea as a continuous z-axis scan through the entire cornea at 2 μm increments starting from superficial corneal epithelium and ending at the corneal endothelium[50]

Corneal crystal intensity of the cystine crystals in the corneal stroma of the wild-type, heterozygotes, homozygote, scAAV-CTNS treated and untreated cornea was calculated using Cell Profiler (https://cellprofiler.org). The pipeline was designed to detect the crystals and measure their crystal intensity. Total crystal intensity was then divided by the thickness of the corneal stroma to obtain the intensity per micron of the crystals in the cornea.

To examine the kidney phenotype, wild-type, Ctnsis1 homozygous and heterozygous mice of 5, 12, and 20 months-old were placed in metabolic cages with free access to rodent chow and distilled water for metabolic analysis. To examine the activity of the proximal tube, the animals were given a single injection of acetazolamide (ACTZ) over a 24 hour period. Urine volume and urine osmolality were measured daily as previously described [34, 36].

Mice were placed in metabolic cages with free access to rodent chow and distilled water for 2 to 3 days of adjustment. After 2–3 days, baseline 24h urine was collected, and the animals were treated with a vasopressin V2 receptor analog (dDAVP, 6μg, SC), and urine produced over 24h period collected. Two days after dDAVP treatment, a 24 h urine was collected again for the recovery period[36].

Total cellular fraction containing plasma membrane and intracellular membrane proteins was isolated from whole kidneys and used for semi-quantitative immunoblotting experiments previously described[36]. Rabbit polyclonal NKCC2 and AQP2 antiserum were previously characterized and used in our laboratory[51, 52]. Anti-AQP1 and -AQP4 antibodies were purchased from Santa Crus Biotechnology (Dallas, TX).

WT and homozygous Ctnsis1 kidneys were harvested at the age of 22 months and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS at 4°C overnight, followed by paraffin embedding. Paraffin sections (6 μm) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Sections were blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4°C with the AQP2 primary antibody diluted in 1% BSA. After three washes in PBS, slides were incubated at room temperature for 1 h with Alexa 555- conjugated secondary antibodies (Life Technologies). Nuclei were counterstained with 1 ng/ml 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and mounted with Mowiol (475904; Calbiochem). Sections were examined and photographed using a Zeiss Observer Z1 inverted microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Cystinosin antibody is generated from rabbits immunized with KLH conjugated C-terminal peptide sequence Ac-CQSYNNDQWTLIFGDPTK-amide that target the cystinosin sequence 319–335 amino acids. The antibody is affinity purified through peptide affinity purification via linker using Sulfolink Immobilization kit (Catalog No 44999, Thermofisher scientific) for peptides as per manufacturer’s protocol. Purified antibodies are aliquoted and stored at −80°C until use.

scAAV-CTNS treated, and PBS control mice corneas were harvested at the end of 8 weeks and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS at 4°C. Fixed eyeballs were immersed in 15% sucrose solution for 4 hours and 30% sucrose solution overnight. Eyeballs were then embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Fisher scientific, Pittsburg, PA, USA) and 8 μm-thick cryosections were made from frozen blocks. The sections were blocked with 4% BSA for 1 hour and were stained with rabbit anti- cystinosin antibody (1:100 dilution) overnight at 4°C. After washing the slides with PBS, the sections were stained with Goat anti-rabbit IgG AF 647 (1:500 dilution, Invitrogen A21244) for 2 hours. Fluorescence images were acquired using Zeiss Observer Z1 inverted microscope (Zeiss, Germany).
