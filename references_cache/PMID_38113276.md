# Pathological mutations promote proteolysis of mitochondrial tRNA-specific 2-thiouridylase 1 (MTU1) via mitochondrial caseinolytic peptidase (CLPP).
**Authors:** Ahmad RNR, Zhang LT, Morita R, Tani H, Wu Y, Chujo T, Ogawa A, Harada R, Shigeta Y, Tomizawa K, Wei FY
**Journal:** Nucleic Acids Res (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1093/nar/gkad1197](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkad1197)

## Content

MTU1 controls intramitochondrial protein synthesis by catalyzing the 2-thiouridine modification of mitochondrial transfer RNAs (mt-tRNAs). Missense mutations in the MTU1 gene are associated with life-threatening reversible infantile hepatic failure. However, the molecular pathogenesis is not well understood. Here, we investigated 17 mutations associated with this disease, and our results showed that most disease-related mutations are partial loss-of-function mutations, with three mutations being particularly severe. Mutant MTU1 is rapidly degraded by mitochondrial caseinolytic peptidase (CLPP) through a direct interaction with its chaperone protein CLPX. Notably, knockdown of CLPP significantly increased mutant MTU1 protein expression and mt-tRNA 2-thiolation, suggesting that accelerated proteolysis of mutant MTU1 plays a role in disease pathogenesis. In addition, molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that disease-associated mutations may lead to abnormal intermolecular interactions, thereby impairing MTU1 enzyme activity. Finally, clinical data analysis underscores a significant correlation between patient prognosis and residual 2-thiolation levels, which is partially consistent with the AlphaMissense predictions. These findings provide a comprehensive understanding of MTU1-related diseases, offering prospects for modification-based diagnostics and novel therapeutic strategies centered on targeting CLPP.

Mitochondrial transfer RNAs (mt-tRNAs) undergo posttranscriptional modifications that are needed for effective intramitochondrial translation of the 13 respiratory subunits encoded in mitochondrial DNA (1,2). Currently, a total of 18 modifications have been identified at 137 sites in 22 species of human tRNAs (3). These modifications are mediated by enzymes encoded in the nuclear genome, translated in the cytosol, and imported into the mitochondria. Genetic mutations in the genes responsible for mt-tRNA modification, including MTO1 (4–6), GTPBP3 (7) and PUS1 (8), have been implicated in the development of mitochondrial diseases. This highlights the significance of mt-tRNA modifications in ensuring precise mitochondrial translation and energy metabolism.

Among the 22 mt-tRNAs, 5-taurinomethyl (τm5) modification and 2-thiouridine (s2U) modification occur in mt-tRNALys, mt-tRNAGln and mt-tRNAGlu, leading to τm5s2U formation at the ‘wobble position’ (U34) of the anticodon (9). The nuclear DNA-encoded enzyme mitochondrial tRNA-specific 2-thiouridylase 1 (MTU1), is responsible for the 2-thio modification of the three mt-tRNAs (9). The 2-thiolation of U34 facilitates U-A and U-G base pairing while inhibiting U-U and U-C base pairing (10), which enables precise codon recognition and ensures accurate decoding.

MTU1-mediated 2-thiolation of mt-tRNAs is essential for intramitochondrial protein synthesis in eukaryotes and impairment of MTU1 activity can have profound effects on cellular and tissue functions. Studies on yeast demonstrate that MTU1 deficiency leads to reduced mitochondrial protein synthesis and oxygen consumption (9). Similarly, deletion of Mtu1 in zebrafish (11) and Caenorhabditis elegans (12) results in electron transfer and oxygen consumption dysfunction. However, these animals were viable, and the overall phenotypes were moderate (11,12). In mice, MTU1-deficiency induced embryonic lethality at a very early developmental stage (13). Furthermore, liver-specific conditional knockout mice showed severe liver injury characterized by cell death and macrophage infiltration (13).

In humans, a variety of genetic mutations, including missense mutations, nonsense mutations, duplications, and frameshift mutations, have been identified in pediatric patients who develop life-threatening reversible infantile liver failure (RILF) after birth (14–17). These patients exhibit acute liver failure, which is occasionally accompanied by cardiomyopathy and other peripheral symptoms (14–17). Previous studies have identified 17 missense mutations (M1K, A10S, G14S, Y77H, F82V, F82L, N96S, A166E, E217K, R227T, L233F, L253P, G272D, V279M, A362T, G379C, G381E), with most cases being compound heterozygous (Supplementary Table S1, 14–29). A recent study reported an additional 16 mutations identified in patients (30). Notably, most mutation sites are evolutionarily conserved (9,10), suggesting that amino acids at these mutation sites are potentially important for MTU1 activity. The most severe case resulted in death due to the complete loss of MTU1 protein or thiolation activity caused by homozygous mutations at the start codon c.2T > A (M1K), activity site (C222G), heterozygous frameshifting, and exon skipping (14,15,17,30). In contrast, many patients with compound missense mutations were able to fully recover from liver failure if they survived the acute phase. In addition, a combination of mutations in the MTU1 gene and mitochondrial DNA-encoded ribosomal RNA gene has been identified in patients, who develop hearing loss (31,32).

Despite the increase in the number of MTU1 mutations, only a few mutations have been functionally characterized. The molecular pathogenesis of most disease-related missense mutations remains largely unknown. Moreover, because most missense mutations are not localized to the positions that are directly involved in catalytic residues (33), the impact of a single amino acid substitution on MTU1 activity is not predictable until recent. It also should be noted that currently there is no treatment that directly targets the mutant MTU1. Because sulfur in cysteine is required for the MTU1 activity, several groups have administered cysteine to patients carrying mutant MTU1 and showed that cysteine supplement might be beneficial to a subgroup of patients (25,27,34). However, the exact effect of cysteine supplement on MTU1 or mitochondrial translation remains elusive.

To fully understand the molecular pathogenesis of MTU1 mutations, we established a series of cell lines by stably expressing the disease-related mutant MTU1 in MTU1 knockout HEK293 cells and HepG2 cells. We also generated disease-related mutants of MnmA, the bacterial homolog of MTU1, to assess the functional conservation. We show that disease-related mutations markedly impair mt-tRNA 2-thiolation and MTU1 protein stability. We also found that the mutations accelerate CLPP-mediated MTU1 proteolysis and that suppression of MTU1 degradation can restore mt-tRNA 2-thiolation.

The initial structure of human MTU1 was obtained from the AlphaFold database (UniProt ID: O75648, version Jun-2022) (35). The C-terminal region (391–421 a.a.) was excluded due to its low confidence score. The Cys222 was treated as a deprotonated cysteine persulfide in both the WT and Y77H systems. The Y77H mutant was modeled from the WT system using the mutagenesis plugin in PyMOL. By superimposing the modeled structure with the crystal structure of E. coli MnmA (PDB ID: 2DEU) (33), AMP and tRNA were added to the system. To reduce the computational cost of molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, the tRNA was truncated, retaining only the anticodon loop (26–44 nt). The restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) charges were computed for AMP and cysteine persulfide through gas-phase HF/6–31 g(d) quantum mechanics calculations using Gaussian 16. A set of parameters was generated using the general AMBER force field 2 (GAFF2) (36) with the antechamber program from AMBER tools.

The Amber 14SB force field was employed for all proteins (37), and the Amber OL3 force field was used for tRNA (38). Missing hydrogen atoms were added using the tleap program from AmberTools. The complex was solvated with the TIP3P water model in a cubic box (39), and sodium ions were added by replacing water molecules to neutralize the system. Finally, the Amber-formatted files were converted into GROMACS format using acpype (40). All MD simulations were carried out using GROMACS 2019.6 (41). The all-atom system was equilibrated before the production run according to the following three steps: (i) energy minimization was performed for 10 000 steps; (ii) NVT equilibration was performed for 100 ps with the V-rescale thermostat at 300 K (42); (iii) NPT equilibration was performed for 100 ps with the Berendsen coupling at 300 K under 1 atm (43). Finally, a production run was performed under the NPT condition with a time step of 2 fs, utilizing the SHAKE (44) and SETTLE (45) algorithms. For the WT and Y77H systems, multiple (five) MD simulations were independently performed for 400 ns, and the initial 100 ns of each trajectory was discarded as an equilibration phase. Trajectory analysis for each MD simulation was conducted using the Python library MDAnalysis (46). For each system, the trajectories were combined to build the Markov state model (MSM), enabling each MD snapshot to be weighted along specified reaction coordinates. We utilized the Python library PyEMMA to build each MSM (47).

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Unpaired Student's t tests or Mann–Whitney tests were used to assess differences between two groups. One-way or two-way ANOVA was used followed by multiple comparisons to examine differences among multiple groups. A two-tailed P value of 0.05 was considered significant. Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. The age at the last follow up (Supplementary Table S1) was utilized to calculate the survival rate. In case of patient carrying compound heterozygous mutations, we used the mutation having a higher residual 2-thiolation for calculation.

MnmA is the bacterial homolog of MTU1 that catalyzes 2-thiolation to form 5-methylaminomethyl-2-thiouridine (mnm5s2U) or 5-carboxymethylaminomethyl-2-thiouridine (cmnm5s2U) at position U34 of tRNALys, tRNAGlu, and tRNAGln (Figure 1A and Supplementary Figure S1A). According to a previous structural study (33), MnmA (or MTU1) contains four functional domains: (i) a large N-terminal catalytic domain containing a substrate binding pocket and reactive cysteine residues, (ii) a short variable segment that acts as a lid to regulate the open or closed conformation, (iii) a central domain that recognizes the tRNA D-loop and (iv) a C-terminal β barrel domain that is involved in anticodon recognition (Figure 1A). MnmA shares moderate homology with MTU1 at the amino acid level (36.9% identity between human MTU1 and MnmA, 36.5% identity between mouse MTU1 and MnmA). Among the 16 mutation sites investigated in this study, 11 sites are completely conserved at the amino acid level, and 5 sites exhibit the similar biochemical properties among species (Figure 1B). During the preparation of this study, an additional 16 mutations were reported (30). Among those sites, 12 sites are completely conserved, 3 sites are biochemically conserved and 1 site is not conserved (Figure 1B). These disease-related mutations are found in all domains, with approximately two-thirds being found in the catalytic domain and variable segment.

We transformed MnmA-deficient E. coli with wild-type (WT) and 16 disease-related MnmA mutants and examined the levels of tRNA 2-thiolation by mass spectrometry (Figure 1C and Supplementary Figure S1A). Of the 16 mutants, 6 mutants showed severe to moderate impairment in tRNA 2-thiolation, with their protein and mRNA levels being comparable to those of WT MnmA (Figure 1D-F). On the other hand, the other 10 mutants only showed slight decrease or even unchanged in tRNA 2-thiolation levels (Figure 1D). Among all mutants, the G15S mutant, which is localized to the ATP binding domain, showed the most severe impairment (4.2% relative to WT), while Y78H, N97S, D194K, L210F and G250D showed mild (40–70% relative to WT) decreases.

We observed that many disease-related mutations largely reduced the MnmA protein solubility, as evidenced by the increased protein levels in the 1% Triton X-100 insoluble fraction (Figure 1E and Supplementary Figure S1B). Notably, G144E, G356C and G358E mutants were nearly undetectable in the soluble fraction but clearly detected in the insoluble fraction, while I229P mutant was clearly unstable and nearly undetectable even in the insoluble fraction. It should be noted that although these mutants showed poor solubility, they had only minor effects on tRNA-2 thiolation levels. While the residual modifications suggest that a trace of soluble MnmA mutants may exist and mediate 2-thiolation, it is likely that these mutations have only a limited effect on MnmA activity in E. coli. Collectively, these results showed that disease-related mutations affect not only thiolation activity but also protein solubility in E. coli.

To understand the molecular pathogenesis of disease-related MTU1 mutations in human cells, we generated MTU1 knockout (KO) HEK293 cells (Supplementary Figure S2A-S2D). We confirmed the complete the loss of 2-thiolation in mt-tRNALys, mt-tRNAGln and mt-tRNAGlu (Supplementary Figure S2E). The loss of 2-thiolation was associated with a marked decrease in MTCO1 and MTCO2, the intramitochondrially translated complex IV subunits, suggesting that protein synthesis was impaired in mitochondria (Supplementary Figure S2C). In addition, nuclear DNA-encoded mitochondrial respiratory complex proteins were also downregulated in the KO cells (Supplementary Figure S2C). Importantly, introduction of MTU1 in the KO cells restored the protein levels of MTCO1 and MTCO2 (Supplementary Figure S2C). We also examined the steady-state levels and the aminoacylation levels of mt-tRNALys, mt-tRNAGln and mt-tRNAGlu. There were no apparent changes in the steady-state levels and the aminoacylation levels between the WT and MTU1 KO HEK293 cells (Supplementary Figure S2F-S2G). Thus, MTU1-mediated 2-thiolation is indispensable for intramitochondrial translation by improving codon-anticodon base-pairing, but does not affect tRNA stability or aminoacylation, at least in HEK293 cells.

We stably expressed WT Mtu1 and the 17 disease-related Mtu1 mutants in the MTU1 KO cells using lentiviruses. Compared to E. coli, in which disease-related mutations had only mild effects, all mutants except A10S showed a more than 50% reduction in mt-tRNA 2-thiolation in HEK293 cells compared to WT MTU1 (Figure 2A). Three mutations, G14S, F82V, and A166E, almost abolished mt-tRNA 2-thiolation in HEK293 cells (G14S: 0.81%, F82V: 0.4%, A166E: 0.34% related to WT). In addition, we investigated M1K mutant and confirmed that 2-thiolation was completely abolished in M1K mutant cells (Supplementary Figure S3). Twelve mutants showed 1.7–37% residual 2-thiolation compared to WT, with mutations in the N-terminal catalytic domain and C-terminal β barrel domain showing a strong inhibitory effect. In contrast to the other mutations, the effect of A10S mutant was relatively mild (62% versus WT). A10S is implicated in the hearing loss and the clinical symptoms are mild compared to those of other mutations.

Next, we investigated MTU1 protein levels in the mutant cells. The protein levels of all mutants except R227T were significantly lower than that of WT MTU1, although the mRNA levels were comparable between WT and mutants (Figure 2B–D). Notably, there was a positive correlation between MTU1 protein levels and mt-tRNA 2-thiolation, suggesting that the steady-state levels of mutant MTU1 contribute to the residual 2-thiolation levels (Figure 2E). We also transduced cells with different doses of lentivirus and examined the protein levels. While robust protein expression of WT MTU1 can be achieved at a dose of lentivirus as low as MOI = 0.3, the mutant MTU1 proteins were only detectable when cells were infected with a high dose of lentivirus (MOI > 0.5) (Supplementary Figure S4A). Importantly, the protein levels of MTU1, including both WT and mutants, were parallel with the tRNA 2-thiolation levels, except in the G14S mutant cells (Figure 2F). Moreover, the 2-thiolation levels in the mutant cells infected with the highest dose of lentivirus (MOI = 1) were still lower than those in the cells expressing WT MTU1 (Supplementary Figure S4B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that disease-related mutations strongly affect MTU1 protein levels, which can lead to a decrease in mt-tRNA 2-thiolation.

We investigated the intracellular localization of all mutants, which might affect the steady state MTU1 protein levels (Figure 3 and Supplementary Figure S5). All mutants were colocalized with HSP60, a mitochondrial matrix protein. Nuclear DNA-encoded mitochondrial proteins usually possess mitochondria-targeting signal (MTS) at the N-terminus. However, MTU1 does not appear to have a canonical signal (Supplementary Figure S6A). We generated a mutant MTU1 (Δ28), in which 28 amino acids at the N-terminus were truncated and a new methionine was inserted (Supplementary Figure S6B). The Δ28 mutant was localized to the cytosol instead of mitochondria (Supplementary Figure S6C). Unlike many nuclear DNA-encoded mitochondrial protein, in which the mitochondrial targeting peptide is localized at the N-terminus end and will be cleaved after mitochondria translocation, MTU1 remained intact after mitochondria import (32). In line with the previous study, our results suggest the N-terminus of MTU1 needs to be preserved because it serves as both MTS and ATP binding domain. It should be noted that A10S mutant, which is localized to the putative MTS region, also showed mitochondrial localization. It is conceivable that A10S might impair mitochondrial targeting for some extent, but does not completely prevent mitochondria translocation, which might explain the considerable amount of residual 2-thiolation in A10S mutant cells.

Moreover, we found that mitochondrial morphology in HEK293 cells was strongly affected by the loss of mt-tRNA 2-thiolation. In MTU1 KO cells, tubular mitochondria were rarely detected, and nearly all mitochondria were fragmented into small and dot-like shapes. The introduction of WT MTU1 in KO cells completely restored the tubular shape. Furthermore, while G14S, F82V and A166E mutant cells exhibited aberrant mitochondrial shapes that were similar to those in KO cells, partial loss-of-function mutants were able to restore tubular shape to some extent (Figure 3 and Supplementary Figure S5). Collectively, these results suggest that the mutant MTU1 proteins can be properly incorporated into mitochondrial matrix, and that the loss of mt-tRNA 2-thiolation is linked to the aberrant mitochondrial morphology.

We suspected that the decrease in the mutant MTU1 protein might be caused by their instability. To this end, we measured the turnover of the MTU1 proteins by treating cells with the protein translation inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) for 6 h and 12 h (Figure 3B, C). MTU1 is a stable protein with a half-life of 413 h in HeLa cells and 326 h in Huh-7 cells (48). Under our experimental conditions, WT MTU1 was stable for up to 12 h in HEK293 cells in the presence of CHX. In contrast, all mutant MTU1 proteins exhibited a rapid degradation, with the A166E and V279M mutants showing the fastest degradation (Figure 3B, C). It should be noted that G144E and V261M, the bacterial counterparts of these mutants, had high accumulation in the insoluble fraction (Figure 1E), suggesting that these mutations have profound impacts on protein stability.

The rapid turnover of these mutants might explain the decrease in their steady-state levels. The ubiquitin-proteasome is the major machinery responsible for protein degradation in the cytosol. The mutant MTU1 might be degraded before the transport into mitochondria. Treatment of mutant cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 resulted in the accumulation of ubiquitinated proteins (Supplementary Figure S7). However, MG132 did not restore the mutant MTU1 proteins (Supplementary Figure S7). Notably, MG132 appeared to suppress MTCO1 levels even in WT cells (Supplementary Figure S7). Thus, the ubiquitin-proteasome is not responsible for the fast turnover of mutant MTU1.

Next, we investigated whether the mitochondrial protein quality control system is involved in the regulation of MTU1 protein levels. Mitochondrial proteases such as caseinolytic peptidase P (CLPP) and Lon protease homolog (LONP1) are the major matrix proteases and are responsible for the mitochondrial proteostasis (49). We treated mutant cells with siRNA against CLPP and LONP1, followed by evaluation of MTU1 protein levels and mt-tRNA 2-thiolation. Knockdown of CLPP, but not LONP1, effectively upregulated WT and mutant MTU1 proteins (Figure 4A, Supplementary Figure S8A), leading to an increase in mt-tRNA 2-thiolation in the mutant cells (Figure 4B, Supplementary Figure S8B). The steady state levels of MTCO1 were also upregulated in CLPP knockdown cells (Figure 4C). It should be noted that although the protein level of MTCO1 is largely decreased in mutant MTU1 cells, the level of MTCO1 was poorly correlated with 2-thiolation across different cell lines (Supplementary Figure S8A). Although the effect of CLPP knockdown on MTCO1 appeared to be valid, only some mutant cells showed significance (Figure 4C). We suspected that the variability of MTCO1 might be caused by differential degradation rate compared to protein synthesis rate in individual cell lines. In contrast to CLPP knockdown, LONP1 knockdown markedly suppressed MTU1 and MTCO1 levels in HEK293 cells. In addition, we examined the effect of knockdown of OMA1, an inner membrane protease, on several mutants (Supplementary Figure S8C, D). However, OMA1 knockdown failed to upregulate MTU1.

CLPP forms a complex with caseinolytic mitochondrial matrix peptidase chaperone subunit X (CLPX), which unfolds CLPP substrate proteins in an ATP-dependent manner (50,51). We immunoprecipitated WT and all mutant MTU1 proteins and found that CLPX can be coimmunoprecipitated with MTU1 (Figure 4D). Intriguingly, some mutants, including A166E, showed strong binding with CLPX. On the other hand, LONP1 was not coimmunoprecipitated with MTU1. These results suggest that MTU1 mutations can promote CLPP-mediated degradation and that CLPP knockdown can restore mt-tRNA thiolation via MTU1 upregulation.

Because the liver is the major organ affected by MTU1 mutations in pediatric patients, we investigated the effect of disease-related mutants on MTU1 stability and CLPP in hepatocyte-derived cells. We generated MTU1 KO HepG2 cells expressing WT and mutant MTU1 (Supplementary Figure S9A–C). Disease-related mutant HepG2 cells showed a significant reduction in mt-tRNA 2-thiolation at degrees that are comparable with mutant HEK293 cells: all mutants showed a more than 50% reduction of 2-thiolation, with G14S, F82V and A166E being the most critical mutations (G14S: 0.76%, F82V: 0.47%, A166E: 0.15% versus WT, Figure 5A).

In HepG2 cells, mutant MTU1 protein levels and the resulting mt-tRNA 2-thiolation levels were significantly lower than WT MTU1 despite cells were infected the cells with the same doses of lentivirus and expressing the same levels of mRNA (Figure 5B, Supplementary Figure S9D-S9F). Similar to the observation in HEK293 cells, mutant MTU1 proteins in HepG2 cells were also unstable as evidenced by the rapid disappearance in the presence of CHX (Figure 5C-D). The rapid turnover of MTU1 proteins was attenuated by CLPP knockdown in HepG2 cells (Figure 5D). Moreover, CLPP knockdown, but not LONP1 knockdown, restored mt-tRNA 2-thiolation levels in HepG2 cells (Figure 5E).

Taken together, our results suggest that the steady state levels of MTU1 is controlled by CLPP and that knockdown of CLPP can restore mutant MTU1 protein, consequently leading to the increase in mt-tRNA 2-thiolation in hepatocyte-derived cells.

We sought to explore the impact of disease-related mutations on MTU1 from structural point of view. Y77H is one of the mutations identified most frequently in patients and significantly affects 2-thiolation in both E. coli and mammalian cells (Figures 1 and 2). We performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations based on the predicted structure of human MTU1 with in the presence of reactive sulfur atoms on Cys222 and tRNA (Figure 6A–C). We found that Y77H has a profound impact on the structure of the catalytic pocket. In WT MTU1, the distance between Cys101SG and Cys222SD is most frequently observed at 4.20 Å. However, in Y77H MTU1, the distance from Cys101SG to Cys222SD was elongated to 8.10 Å. In addition, the distance from His77NE to Cys222SD (7.76 Å) was markedly longer than that of Tyr77OH to Cys222SD (4.54 Å), suggesting that the conformation of the catalytic pocket was distorted, which might directly affect sulfur insertion into tRNA (Figure 6C).

Interestingly, we identified a previously uncharacterized residue, Asn38, which might be involved in the conformational change. In WT MTU1, Asn38 appears to stabilize Cys222 as evidenced by the close distance between Asn38NE2-Cys222SD (3.19 Å) (Figure 6C). However, in Y77H MTU1, His77 appears to attract Asn38, which in turn destabilizes the Asn38NE2–Cys222SD interaction as evidenced by the decrease in the frequency of close contact (Figure 6C), Unexpectedly, the frequency of the Asn38NE2–Phe223CZ close interaction seems to increase in His77 MTU1 (Figure 6C), suggesting that the substitution of Tyr77 for His77 dysregulates the interaction between catalytically critical residues. To confirm the role of Asn38, we mutated Asn38 to Ala or Asp in MTU1 (N38A or N38D, Figure 6D) and MnmA (N39A or N39D, Supplementary Figure S10A). In both species, Asn38 mutation significantly impaired tRNA 2-thiolation. Moreover, N38A and N38D MTU1 protein levels, but not mRNA, showed a marked decrease compared to WT (Figure 6E, F). Likewise, N39A MnmA showed a decrease in the soluble fraction and a concomitant increase in the insoluble fraction in E. coli (Supplementary Figure S10B, C). These results suggest that the evolutionarily conserved Asn38 is needed for MTU1 activity by stabilizing catalytic Cys222 and is involved in the pathogenesis of Y77H.

In addition to Y77H, we analyzed F82V and A166E mutations, which are both severe types of mutations in mammalian cells. The predicted structure of WT MTU1 using AlphaFold2 (52) showed that F82 forms a close interaction with Trp78 (Supplementary Figure S11A). F82V mutation appears to weaken the interaction with Trp78 due to the lack of aromatic rings (Supplementary Figure S11A). Trp78 and F82 in MTU1 are localized in helices 3a and 3b, which can interact with the variable segment to control the open or closed conformation (33). It is likely that disruption of the F82-Trp78 interaction by F82V mutation might interfere with the open or closed conformation and thus impair tRNA binding. On the other hand, A166 (equivalent to G144 in MnmA) is localized to an internal loop region, which interacts with U34 and is indispensable for 2-thiolation (Supplementary Figure S11B). The predicted IDDT score showed a sharp drop in the region surrounding E166 compared to A166 (Supplementary Figure S11C) possibly owing to the newly formed interaction between E166 and other amino acids (Supplementary Figure S11B). Taken together, these severe mutations appear to affect the molecular dynamics in the catalytic domain, thereby leading to the defective tRNA 2-thiolation.

Very recently, a program named AlphaMissense has been reported to predict whether a missense mutation is pathological or benign based on the structural simulation by AlphaFold2 (53). We searched the dataset and compared it with our results (Figure 7A–C). Among the 16 disease-related mutations quantified and characterized in the study, 12 mutations were predicted to be pathologic, 3 were predicted to be benign, and 1 mutation was predicted to be ambiguous (Figure 7A). Notably, we found that the prediction score calculated by AlphaMissense showed a negative correlation with the relative residual 2-thiolation levels in HepG2 cells and HEK293 cells: the higher the prediction score is, the less the residual 2-thiolation levels is (Figure 7B, C).

We then sought to investigate whether the relative residual mt-tRNA 2-thiolation level calculated from mutant cells is associated with the clinical outcome of patients based on clinical information reported previously (14–30). Individuals with missense variants examined in this study were selected as inclusive criteria. We divided the patients into two groups based on a threshold of 10% relative residual 2-thiolation and investigated the survival rate of each group (Figure 7D). Patients carrying mutations that resulted in less than 10% relative residual 2-thiolation in HepG2 cells, showed a poor survival rate, while most patients carrying mutations that resulted in more than 10% relative residual 2-thiolation showed full recovery (Figure 7D). Taken together, our results suggest that the severity of the symptoms can be predicted by the residual 2-thiolation levels and that the AI-assisted AlphaMissense program can be useful to predict the functional impact.

MTU1-mediated 2-thiolation of mitochondrial tRNA is essential for mitochondrial protein synthesis (13). To date, 33 types of missense mutations have been identified in pediatric patients with reversible liver failure combined with other symptoms (Supplemental Table S1, 14–30). A previous study using patient-derived fibroblasts showed that the homozygous Y77H mutation and heterozygous G14S mutation are associated with a decrease in 2-thiolation (14). However, because patients usually carry compound heterogeneous mutations, the molecular pathogenesis of most mutations is unclear.

In this study, we comprehensively characterized 17 missense mutations and provided mechanistic insights into their molecular pathogenesis. We found that all mutations examined in this study significantly impaired the 2-thiolation of mt-tRNAs in HEK293 and HepG2 cells. Furthermore, we found that mutations can accelerate MTU1 degradation mediated by the mitochondrial protease CLPP. Importantly, knockdown of CLPP effectively upregulated the level of mutant proteins, leading to an increase in mt-tRNA 2-thiolation. These results demonstrate that the pathogenesis of MTU1 mutations is attributed to a decrease in both MTU1 activity and protein stability, ultimately leading to the impairment of mitochondrial protein synthesis and onset of the disease.

The present study also provides a quantitative view showing that there is a threshold effect in the pathogenesis of MTU1-related mitochondrial disease. G14S, F82V, and A166E mutations impaired mt-tRNA 2-thiolation to less than 1% of WT. Other critical mutations include G379C, G381E, E217K, and N96S mutations. The residual 2-thiolation levels of the G379C and G381E mutants were less than 10% in both HEK293 cells and HepG2 cells, and the residual 2-thiolation levels of the E217K and N96S mutants were less than 10% in HepG2 cells. Importantly, most patients carrying these critical mutations (less than 10% residual 2-thiolation levels) died after the onset of liver failure. In contrast, patients carrying mutations with a relative residual 2-thiolation higher than 10% showed a significantly good survival rate. Thus, the residual 2-thiolation levels can have a profound impact on the clinical outcome, and a relative residual 2-thiolation of 10% might be used as a threshold value to predict prognosis. This finding also suggests that mt-tRNA does not need to be fully modified to maintain tissue functions, at least for 2-thiolation, which might partially explain why most patients with >10% residual 2-thiolation fully recover.

Recently, a program named AlphaMissense was developed to predict whether a missense mutation of a given protein can be pathogenic based on AlphaFold2-based structural simulation (53). The residual 2-thiolation levels and the predicted scores showed a significant and negative correlation, suggesting that AlphaMissense can be used to predict the potential impact on the protein function. However, the fact that some pathological mutations, including G14S, which critically impair mt-tRNA 2-thiolation, are predicted to be benign or ambiguous suggests that in vitro or in vivo functional assays are still needed to validate the prediction.

An important finding of this study is that the mutant MTU1 proteins were subjected to a rapid degradation, which contributed to the decrease in mt-tRNA 2-thiolation levels. Consistent with our observations, a previous study using patient-derived cell lines carrying both the A10S mutation in MTU1 and the m.1555A→G mutation in the mitochondrial ribosomal RNA gene also showed a decrease in the mutant MTU1 protein level (54). Furthermore, a recent study showed that pathological mutation in GTPBP3, the mitochondrial protein involved in the 5-taurinomethyl modification of mt-tRNAs, can also induce protein instability (55). Thus, the destabilization of mt-tRNA modifying enzymes carrying missense mutations might be the common phenomenon underlying the pathogenesis of the mutation-related mitochondrial disease (56,57). The molecular mechanism by which mutant MTU1 proteins are prone to degradation is unclear. A previous in vitro study of MnmA has shown that the tRNA-bound or ATP-bound form of MnmA is resistant to tryptic digestion (58). We found that the disease-related mutations of MTU1 can have deleterious effects on the functionally important residues in the domains related to tRNA binding and conformational changes, which possibly leads to rapid degradation. Consistent with this view, we found that the disease-related mutations can also affect the solubility of MnmA in bacteria. Taken together, these results suggest that the instability of mutant MTU1 (or MnmA) is likely attributed to the loss of substrate binding, which renders the mutant proteins susceptible to proteolysis.

In this study, we showed that mitochondrial proteases, but not the cytosolic ubiquitin-proteasome system, were responsible for the degradation of the MTU1 protein. Both CLPP and LONP1 are involved in the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) through proteolysis of aberrant proteins (59–61). We found that MTU1 is preferentially degraded by CLPP. CLPP is an evolutionarily conserved protease that forms complex with the chaperone subunit X (CLPX) and has multiple substrates (62–65). A recent study using Clpp knockout mice analyzed by mass spectrometry identified multiple substrates, including mitochondrial respiratory complex proteins (66). Although MTU1 was not detected as a substrate of CLPP in that study (66), we found that both WT and mutant MTU1 can physically interact with CLPX. Importantly, silencing CLPP in both HEK293 cells and HepG2 cells significantly upregulated mt-tRNA 2-thiolation levels, suggesting that CLPP is a potential target for drug development, which will have a substantial impact on mitochondrial functions through direct and indirect effects on mt-tRNA modification and protein translation. In line with this view, a recent study showed that knockout of the Clpp gene can rescue abnormal cardiac phenotypes of Dars2 knockout mice, a mouse model showing defective mitochondrial translation (67).

To date, the treatment recommended for MTU1-related liver failure is very limited. Some patients have been treated with L-cysteine and/or N-acetylcysteine based on the hypothesis that a low cysteine level in early infants may contribute to the symptoms (25,27,34). However, without increasing the mt-tRNA 2-thiolation level, the effect of metabolite supplementation might be limited. In this study, we have shown that most of these mutations resulted in a decrease in the steady state protein level. As the first few days after birth are critical for survival, strategies for increasing MTU1 protein levels could result in a better prognosis. These strategies include the development of drugs targeting CLPP to prevent excessive degradation of MTU1 mutant protein or the delivery of mRNA-targeting therapy for increased MTU1 expression.

The major limitation of our study is the lack of an in vitro enzymatic assay. Although we showed that disease-related mutations significantly lowered the mt-tRNA 2-thiolation levels in cells, their in vitro activities have not been assessed. In bacteria, recombinant MnmA activity has been analyzed by incubating MnmA with TusA/B/C/D/E proteins, which are required for relaying sulfur atoms from cysteine desulfurase IscS to MnmA (68). However, because TusA/B/C/D/E are not conserved in eukaryotes, the molecular mechanism by which MTU1 acquires reactive sulfur atoms is unknown. Further study is needed to identify the eukaryotic sulfur relay factors, if they exist; to understand the molecular mechanism of MTU1-mediated sulfur transfer and to establish a methodology for assessing MTU1 activity in vitro. In addition, the current study showed that WT and mutant MTU1 physically interact with CLPX, which facilitates CLPP-mediated MTU1 in cells. Further study is needed to establish an in vivo model by crossing Clpp knockout mice with conditional knock-in mice expressing various mutant MTU1 proteins. Moreover, the lack of patient-derived cells is also the limitation of the current study. Whether mutant MTU1 can be restored by CLPP knockdown in patient-derived cells need to be investigated in the future. It is also conceivable that downregulation of CLPP or CLPX is involved in the recovery of patients. Given the ubiquitous expression of CLPP/CLPX in the tissues, examination of CLPP/CLPX expression in the biopsy or blood samples taken from the patients might be helpful to predict the stability of MTU1.

In summary, we found that disease-related MTU1 mutations cause defective mitochondrial tRNA 2-thiolation, which is attributed to decreases in both MTU1 activity and MTU1 stability. The mitochondrial protease CLPP is responsible for the instability of mutant MTU1, and inhibition of CLPP can restore mutant MTU1 protein levels, leading to an increase in tRNA 2-thiolation levels in hepatocytes. Furthermore, we propose a threshold of 10% relative residual 2-thiolation, which can be used to predict the prognosis.
