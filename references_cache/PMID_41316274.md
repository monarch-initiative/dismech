BACKGROUND: Sarcoidosis is pathologically characterized by non-necrotizing
granulomas inflammation. Despite the high spontaneous remission rate, a
remarkable number of sarcoidosis patients undergo persistent progression.
Glucocorticoids are currently recommended as the first-line treatment for
sarcoidosis, but the therapeutic responses showed a great heterogeneity in
clinical practice. We investigated the role of macrophages and Th17.1 cells in
the poor response to glucocorticoid and progression in the early-stage
sarcoidosis.
METHODS AND MAIN RESULTS: CyTOF was performed on the mediastinal lymph nodes and
peripheral blood samples to probe the alterations of immune cells in
sarcoidosis. Monocytes were enriched in sarcoidosis patients but with impaired
functions of antigen processing and presentation. Meanwhile, Th17.1 cells were
enriched and overactivated in sarcoidosis. Both macrophages and Th17.1 cells
were recruited to the granuloma lesions by CCL2 and CXCL-9/10, respectively. The
granulomas of sarcoidosis in lymph nodes were classified as 3 types: M1-like
macrophage-dominated type (type I), M1/2-mixed macrophage type (type II) and
M2-like macrophage-dominated type (type III). Type III granuloma with elevated
Th17.1 cells and M2-like macrophages predict a poor therapeutic response of
sarcoidosis patients. In addition, Th17.1 cells impaired the antigen phagocytic
and processing ability of macrophages through CD47/SIRPα interaction. Blockade
of CD47/SIRPα interaction significantly repressed the progression of sarcoidosis
in mouse model.
CONCLUSIONS: Our study reveals a novel interaction between Th17.1 cells and
M2-like macrophages in Type III granulomas potentially mediated through
CD47-SIRPα, which may contribute to impaired antigen processing and
glucocorticoid resistance in sarcoidosis. Therapies targeting this molecular
pathway represent a promising direction for developing new sarcoidosis
treatments.

Sarcoidosis characterized by non-necrotizing granulomatous inflammation can involve multiple organs. The global incidence of sarcoidosis is 2-160/100,000, and the spontaneous remission rate is about 10%-82%. However, 10%-30% of patients with sarcoidosis developed into pulmonary fibrosis and even pulmonary hypertension, with about 40% mortality rate [1, 2]. Patients with sarcoidosis exhibit substantially heterogeneous responses to glucocorticoids, a first-line treatment for sarcoidosis [2]. For those responding poorly to glucocorticoids, the etiology remains largely unknown and alternative therapeutics are still unavailable. Thus, further investigations are required to identify the factors that determine the different clinical outcomes and the heterogeneity of patient responses to glucocorticoids therapy.

Sarcoidosis has been recently reported to correlate with genetic susceptibility, specific antigen or self-antigen exposure and dysregulated antigenic responses [1–3]. The immune cells in genetically predisposed persons frequently show a dysfunction in clearance of the antigen, eventually leading to persistent antigen exposure and formation of epithelioid cell granulomas [2, 3]. Granulomas are composed of diverse types of immune cells including macrophages, among which macrophages play an important role in sarcoidosis pathogenesis [4–6]. While classically activated macrophages (M1-like type) are believed to mediate the formation of granuloma of early-stage sarcoidosis, alternatively activated macrophage (M2-like type) may be predictive of pulmonary fibrosis in end-stage sarcoidosis [3, 7, 8]. But the mechanisms underlying their dysfunction in antigen clearance in sarcoidosis are not fully understood [9]. In addition, T-helper 17.1 cells (Th17.1), defined by the co-expression of Th17-lineage markers (CCR6 or RORγt) and Th1-lineage markers (CXCR3 or T-bet) [10, 11], have recently been found enriched in sarcoidosis, and regarded as the main IFN-γ-producing cells instead of Th1 cells in granulomas from patients with sarcoidosis [12–14]. However, these findings cannot explain the heterogeneity of glucocorticoid treatment response and clinical prognosis.

In the present study, we conducted cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) using samples of peripheral blood and mediastinal lymph nodes from early-stage sarcoidosis patients, and found that macrophages and Th17.1 cells were enriched in the granulomas of sarcoidosis patients. The poor response of patients to glucocorticoid treatment is correlated with the increase in M2-like macrophages displaying impaired functions of phagocytosis and antigen processing. Mechanistically, Th17.1 cells are recruited to the lymph node during the development of early-stage sarcoidosis. These Th17.1 cells express high level CD47, which binds to SIRPα on the M2-like macrophages, and impair phagocytosis and antigen presentation activities of these macrophages, thereby leading to sustained granulomatous inflammation and promoting the progression of early-stage sarcoidosis. Thus, these results provide rationale for subtyping and prognostic evaluation of sarcoidosis based on the polarization and phagocytotic characteristics of macrophages and the abundance of Th17.1 cells in the granuloma lesions.

PBMCs from peripheral blood samples of sarcoidosis patients (n = 9) and healthy controls (n = 8) were collected for CyTOF assay (Fig. 1A). In-depth analyses of immune cells by t-SNE and FlowSoM with 42 surface markers identified 36 clusters in PBMCs (Fig. 1B and C). The frequency of CXCR3+CCR7+CCR6+ monocytes was significantly higher in sarcoidosis group than that of healthy control (Fig. 1D), suggesting an increase in the adhesion and migration ability of monocytes to the sarcoidosis lesions. However, the levels of monocyte activating receptor CD119 (IFNγRa), co-stimulation molecule CD86 and TLR2, which recognizes the most extensive pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP), decreased significantly in sarcoidosis patients compared with those of control group (Fig. 1E). Thus, despite the higher expression of HLA-DR probably due to persistent antigen stimulation, the antigen processing and presentation functions were impaired in the monocytes of sarcoidosis patients. Compared with the healthy control, the frequency of active Th1 cells (C10, CD127+, CXCR3+, CD28+, Ki67+) and active Th2 TCM cells (C06, CD38hi, CD28+, ICOS+) was significantly reduced in the sarcoidosis group (Fig. 1G, I). While, the frequency of the inactive Th2 TCM cells (C13) was higher in the sarcoidosis than that of control (Fig. 1G, I). These data indicated that adaptive immunity mediated by Th1/Th2 cells was suppressed in the peripheral blood of sarcoidosis. Sunburst plots for the distribution of all CD4+ T cell populations indicated that the proportion of Th17.1 cells (CD4+, CCR6+, CXCR3+) increased dramatically in PBMCs of sarcoidosis patients compared with those of control group (Figs. 1H, I). Therefore, our findings suggest that Th17.1 cells abnormally elevated in the peripheral blood of early-stage sarcoidosis might play an essential role in the initiation of granuloma formation.

Mediastinal lymph nodes of early-stage sarcoidosis patients (n = 9) and healthy controls (n = 8) were also collected for CyTOF assay (Fig. 1A). In-depth analyses of immune cells by t-SNE and FlowSoM with 42 surface markers identified 32 clusters in lymph nodes, and clusters C07, C17, C18 and C19 were enriched dramatically in sarcoidosis patients when merged two t-SNE figures together (Fig. 2A). The expression of each surface marker and the proportion of each one of the 32 clusters was displayed in a heatmap (Fig. 2B). Sunburst plots showed the average distribution of all immune cell populations in sarcoidosis group and the control group, and found that the proportion of B cells, especially naïve B cells increased dramatically, while the proportion of T cells, especially CD4+ T cells decreased dramatically in the lymph nodes of sarcoidosis patients, compared with those of control group, respectively (Figs. 2C). Further analysis found that, compared with control group, sarcoidosis patients exhibited a significant increase in the proportion of total B cells and naïve B cells (Fig. 2D), along with downregulation of key functional molecules on B cells including the homing receptors CXCR5 and CCR6 (Fig. 2H). Together with the observed reduction in T follicular helper (Tfh) cell frequency (Fig. 2F), these findings suggested a potentially suppressed state of humoral immunity within sarcoidosis lymph nodes. In addition, the proportions of total T cells, CD4+ T cells, naïve CD4+ T cells, Tfh cells and Th1 cells were significantly decreased, while the ratio of Th17.1 cells increased dramatically in sarcoidosis lymph nodes compared with control groups (Fig. 2D), which was also confirmed in the analysis of CD4 + T cell subsets (Fig. 2E, F). However, the proportion of Treg cells had no difference between the two groups (Fig. 2D). These data illustrated an insufficiency of Th1-mediated cellular immune responses and Tfh-mediated activation of B cells in sarcoidosis lymph nodes. In addition, the activation markers for Th17.1 cells, including HLA-DR, CD25, CD28, CD38, CD69 and ICOS were significantly higher than that of control group (Fig. 2G). Ki-67 staining indicated a rapid proliferation of Th17.1 cells in sarcoidosis lymph nodes (Fig. 2G). And it is documented that Th17.1 cells in sarcoidosis secrete higher levels of IFN-γ [12]. These findings indicated that Th17.1 cells were aberrantly enriched and over-activated, while Th1-mediated cellular immunity and Tfh-mediated humoral immunity were suppressed in the lymph nodes of early-stage sarcoidosis.

Chemokines play a central role in recruitment of immune cells into the granuloma lesions of sarcoidosis [18]. CCR2-CCL2 axis is important for monocytes trafficking during inflammation, while CXCL9/10-CXCR3 axis is important for Th17.1 cells recruitment in the granuloma lesions [18]. We thus analyzed the profiles of chemokines in sarcoidosis lymph nodes using GEO database, and found that the levels of chemokines (CCL2, CXCL9 and CXCL10) in lymph nodes and cognate receptors (CCR2 and CXCR3) in peripheral blood were significantly up-regulated in sarcoidosis patients compared with normal controls (Fig. 3A and B).Consistently, we detected significant upregulation of the above chemokines in lymph node samples of sarcoidosis patients compared with healthy volunteers (Fig. 3C, D). CD68+CCR2+ macrophages and CXCR3+ Th17.1 cells were witnessed in the granuloma lesions (Fig. 3E and F). Consistent with established definitions [10], we identified CXCR3+CCR6+ cells as Th17.1 cells. This identification was further confirmed by the co-expression of the key transcription factors T-bet and RORγt in this population (Fig. 3F). These findings suggest that both macrophages and Th17.1 cells are recruited to the granuloma lesions.

We next analyzed the phenotypes of macrophages in the granuloma lesions using a GEO database, and found that monocytes (M0), M1-like macrophage (CD86+) or M2-like macrophage (CD163+) were enriched in sarcoidosis lymph nodes compared with controls (Fig. 4A). Multi-immunofluorescent study revealed varied proportion of M1 and M2 subtypes of macrophages in mediastinal lymph nodes from different patients (Fig. 4B). Follow-up chest CT showed that the M2/M1 ratio was significantly higher in patients with resistance than those displaying favorable responses to glucocorticoid treatment (Fig. 4C). Accordingly, we divided sarcoidosis granulomas into three types according to the M1 and M2 proportion: M1 macrophage-predominant granulomas (M1 proportion ≥ 30%, Type I) (Fig. 4D), M1 and M2 macrophage mixed granulomas (M1 proportion<30% and M2 proportion<95%, Type II) (Figure S1A-B), and M2 macrophage-predominant granulomas (M2 proportion ≥ 95%, Type III) (Fig. 4E). The prognosis and clinical characteristics of sarcoidosis patients included in this study were summarized in Table S3 and Table S5 respectively, which showed that sarcoidosis remission mainly displayed in type I granuloma (Figure S2A). Sarcoidosis patients with type II granulomas were partially responsive to glucocorticoids (Figure S2B). Progressive sarcoidosis mainly happened in type III granulomas (Figure S2C). Since type II granuloma lesions represents an intermediate status, we carried out the comparison study between the type I and type III granulomas to elucidate the immunological mechanism of early- stage sarcoidosis progression. We further investigated the proportion of Th1 and Th17.1 cells in different types of granulomas (Fig. 4F, G), and found that type III granulomas consisted of more infiltrated Th17.1 cells than type I granulomas (Fig. 4H). The lymph nodes from sarcoidosis expressed high levels of cytokines IL-12 and IL-23 (Fig. 4I), which were consistent with the immune microenvironment known to facilitate Th17.1 cell differentiation [19, 20]. However, FOXP3+ Treg cells had no difference between type I and type III granulomas (Figure S1C, D). In agreement with the CyTOF data showing impaired function of antigen processing and presentation in monocytes/macrophages of sarcoidosis patients (Fig. 4E), we found that macrophages from Type I granuloma displayed more active phagocytosis with more figure-like structures and phagocytic vesicles (Fig. 4J) and more abundant phagosomes with elevated lysosome membrane proteins LAMP2A (Fig. 4K, L) and LAMP1 expression (Figure S1E) than those from Type III sarcoidosis patients. Thus, it can be speculated that the interaction between Th17.1 cells and macrophages in type III granulomas might impair the antigen phagocytic and processing ability of macrophages.

To further explore the molecular mechanism of phagocytosis inhibition of M2-like macrophages, we analyzed the expression of phagocytosis inhibitory molecules like SIRPα, LILRB1, LILRB2, LILRB4 and SIGLEC10 [21] based on GEO database, and found that the level of SIRPα (Fig. 5A, S3) was significantly higher in CD14 + cells from PBMCs of sarcoidosis patients than those from healthy individuals .SIRPα -CD47 plays a role in macrophage phagocytosis inhibition [22–26] Further experiments showed that the mRNA and protein expression levels of SIRPα in sarcoidosis lymph nodes, especially those from type III lymph nodes, were much higher than those in control lymph nodes (Figs. 5B, C). And SIRPα was expressed more abundantly on M2-like macrophages (CD163+) than M1-like macrophages (CD86+), especially in type III granuloma (Figs. 5D, E). These observations suggest that SIRPα-mediated inhibition of macrophage phagocytosis may occur in M2-like macrophage-predominant sarcoidosis. SIRPα suppresses phagocytosis of macrophages upon engagement of its cognate ligand, CD47 [23, 27, 28]. Consistently, we detected enhanced CD47 expression in the lymph node samples from sarcoidosis patients (Fig. 5F). And the expression of CD47 on the surface of Th17.1 cells in Type III granulomas was much higher than that on Th1 cells in Type III granulomas (Fig. 5G, H). Immunofluorescence staining verified the co-localization of CD47 and SIRPα (Fig. 5I). Since Propionibacterium acnes (PA) is one of the well-established pathogenic microorganisms in sarcoidosis, we incubated cells with PA and detected increased SIRPα expression on THP-1-derived macrophages and upregulated CD47 on Jurkat cells when cocultured with PA and macrophages (Figure S4A). We then investigated the effect of CD47-SIRPα interaction on the phagocytosis function of macrophages and found that the phagocytosis of macrophages was significantly inhibited by Jurkat cells (Figure S4B, C). Blockade of CD47-SIRPα interaction via a CD47 antibody, Hu5F9-G4, rescued the phagocytosis ability of patient PBMC-derived macrophages (Figure S5A, B). These results suggest that CD47/SIRPα-mediated interplay between Th17.1 cells and macrophages may play a role in the pathogenesis of sarcoidosis. In addition, the expression of HLA-DP in M2-like macrophage-dominant granuloma was lower than M1 like macrophage-dominant granuloma (Fig. 5J, K), suggesting insufficient antigen presentation by M2-like macrophages. Together, these data indicated that Th17.1 cells might induce a deficit of M2-like macrophages in phagocytosis and antigen presentation, which contributes to insufficient antigen clearance, development of sarcoidosis.

To further verify the above mechanisms, we established a mouse model of sarcoidosis by intratracheal administration of PA (Fig. 6A). H&E staining and immunohistochemical staining for CD68 performed on lung specimens of mice showed that granulomas appeared in the lung tissue as early as 35 days after induction (Figs. 6B, C). We observed that intraperitoneal injection of a CCR2 antagonist benzamide, which ablates the migration of monocytes to the lungs, resulted in significantly decreased number and size of pulmonary granulomas (Figs. 6D-F), suggesting an important role of CCR2 + peripheral-derived macrophages in sarcoidosis granuloma formation. In addition, intraperitoneal injection of InVivoMAb anti-mouse CD172a (SIRPα)/P84, which blocked the interaction between CD47 and SIRPα, also repressed the formation or decreased the size of pulmonary granulomas in mice (Figs. 6D, G and H). These results indicated that impeding monocyte recruitment to the lung tissue or restoring phagocytosis through blocking CD47/SIRPα interaction can suppress the pathogenesis of sarcoidosis in vivo.

Although most pulmonary sarcoidosis patients undergo spontaneous remission, some of them continue to progress into advanced stages, the mechanism of which remains largely elusive. Accumulating studies have shown a close relationship between sarcoidosis and the immune system [29, 30]. In particular, a higher proportion of Th17.1 cells was found in mediastinal lymph nodes and BALF specimens of sarcoidosis patients than that of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis patients [10, 31]. Consistently, we detected over-activated and increased frequencies of Th17.1 cells rather than Th1 cells in peripheral blood and lymph node samples of sarcoidosis at early stages. The idea that Th1 cells play a key role in sarcoidosis is largely based on experimental observations of increased numbers of CD4+ effector T cells, which produce IFN-γ, in sarcoidosis patients [32, 33]. However, combined use of surface markers and functional assays revealed that significantly amplified Th17.1 cells were the main CD4+ T cell type producing IFN-γ [12].

Meanwhile, we found increased number of monocytes in the peripheral blood of sarcoidosis patients with impaired function of antigen processing and presentation. After a comprehensive investigation employing combined CyTOF and multi-immunofluorescent staining, we proposed that the granulomas of sarcoidosis lymph nodes can be classified as 3 sub-types: M1-like macrophage dominated (type I), M1/2-like macrophage mixed (type II) and M2-like macrophage dominated (type III). Type III granuloma was enriched for M2-like macrophages and Th17.1 cells compared with that of type I granuloma. While M1-like signatures dominate in lesional skin and BALF, the enriched CD163⁺ M2-like macrophages in mediastinal lymph nodes of glucocorticoid-resistant patients were reported to be associated with chronicity and radiological progression in pulmonary sarcoidosis, and fibrotic remodeling in pulmonary and cardiac sarcoidosis [7, 34–38]. According to the 3 sub-type classification of granuloma, we propose that macrophage polarization is dynamic, compartment-specific, and stage-dependent process.

In term of the origin of macrophages and Th17.1 cells in sarcoidosis, we found that CCR2-CCL2 axis and CXCL9/10-CXCR3 axis were important for monocyte trafficking and Th17.1 cells recruitment in the granuloma lesions, respectively. Taken together, the enrichment of both M2-like macrophages and Th17.1 cells in type III granuloma lesions permits a close interaction between these two cell groups that potentially contributes to the pathogenesis of progressive sarcoidosis.

The exaggerated immune reaction in sarcoidosis is attributed to the impeded degradation of unidentified antigens and the genetic susceptibility of hosts to these antigens [39–41]. We established here that the defect in phagocytosis can be ascribed to a direct interplay between M2-like macrophages and Th17.1 cells through CD47-SIRPα axis. CD47-SIRPα axis as a key innate immune checkpoint mediating inhibition of phagocytosis [24], has been reported to play an important role in arthritis, colitis, cancer, pulmonary fibrosis, and obesity [42–45]. However, its potential involvement in the pathogenesis of sarcoidosis remains unexplored. We found that the interaction between CD47 on Th17.1 cells and SIRPα on M2-like macrophages lead to impaired phagocytosis and probably insufficient clearance of antigens in vitro and in vivo, which might contribute to persistent local inflammation and then granuloma formation. Using a sarcoidosis mouse model induced by the extracts of P. acnes, we found that ablation of MDMs homing to lymph nodes via a CCR2 antagonist or blockade of CD47/SIRPα interaction significantly repressed the progression of sarcoidosis. Despite the aforementioned findings, several limitations in our study need to be addressed. First, to avoid the bias resulted from the limited patients, a larger number of patients with early-stage sarcoidosis might be enrolled in a future investigation to confirm the correlation between the granuloma types and prognosis. Second, to make sure our findings representing a common mechanism of sarcoidosis, the immunological mechanism might to be confirmed in those tissues other than mediastinal lymph nodes and blood samples. Third, there is so far no animal model fully reproducing the granulomatosis for the complexity of sarcoidosis. Furthermore, the longitudinal assessment of sarcoidosis lesions in vivo in these models remains technically challenging. In conclusion, we propose a novel classification of sarcoidosis granulomas into Types I–III according to their corresponding clinical prognosis. Our results delineate a novel role of the CD47-SIRPα checkpoint in the impaired phagocytosis of M2-like macrophages in sarcoidosis. Future research should focus on validating the immunological mechanism in the other sarcoidosis lesions, and targeting CD47-SIRPα checkpoint for translational study.
