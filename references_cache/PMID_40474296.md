BACKGROUND: Angiosarcoma, a rare and highly aggressive malignancy originating
from vascular endothelial cells, is characterized by its rapid progression, high
invasiveness, and poor prognosis. Due to the limited understanding of its tumor
microenvironment (TME) and the absence of effective treatments, further research
is essential to elucidate its pathogenic mechanisms and improve therapeutic
strategies.
OBJECTIVE: This study aims to characterize the cellular heterogeneity and unique
TME of primary breast angiosarcoma using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq),
to identify potential therapeutic targets and improve clinical outcomes.
METHODS: Tumor samples were obtained from a patient with bilateral primary
breast angiosarcoma and two patients with invasive breast cancer. Single-cell
RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was conducted to capture the transcriptomic profiles
of individual cells within the tumor samples. Following stringent quality
control, a total of 31,771 cells were analyzed using comprehensive
bioinformatics approaches. Cell populations were identified and classified into
distinct cell types, and differential gene expression analysis was performed to
explore key signaling pathways. Functional enrichment analysis was used to
identify pathways related to tumor progression and immune evasion. Additionally,
cell-cell communication networks were mapped to understand interactions within
the TME, with a focus on pathways that may serve as therapeutic targets.
RESULTS: The scRNA-seq analysis revealed significant differences in the
distribution of perivascular cells, fibroblasts, T cells, endothelial cells, and
myeloid cells in breast angiosarcoma compared to invasive breast cancer. Key
pathways enriched in angiosarcoma samples included growth factor binding,
platelet-derived growth factor binding, and ribosome biogenesis, with abnormal
expression of several ribosomal proteins. Notably, genes such as FAT4, KDR, FN1,
and KIT were highly expressed in angiosarcoma endothelial cells, correlating
with poor prognosis. Cell communication analysis highlighted the CXCL12-CXCR4
axis as a crucial mediator of the TME in angiosarcoma.
CONCLUSION: This study provides critical insights into the TME of primary breast
angiosarcoma, highlighting potential molecular targets and pathways for
therapeutic intervention. These findings may inform the development of more
effective treatment strategies for this rare and challenging tumor type.

Angiosarcoma is a rare and aggressive malignancy originating from the endothelial cells of blood and lymphatic vessels. These tumors are characterized by rapid proliferation and poorly differentiated vascular channels, leading to a highly invasive disease course [1]. Angiosarcomas are notorious for early metastasis, high recurrence rates, and overall poor prognosis. They most commonly arise in the skin, soft tissue, and visceral organs, with the head and neck regions, particularly the scalp, being frequent sites of occurrence [2]. However, angiosarcomas can also develop in less common areas such as the breast, liver, and spleen.

Breast angiosarcoma is exceptionally rare, constituting less than 1% of all malignant breast tumors [3]. It is classified into primary and secondary types. Primary breast angiosarcoma arises de novo from endothelial cells within the breast, while secondary breast angiosarcoma typically develops as a complication of prior radiation therapy or chronic lymphedema following breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy.

Primary breast angiosarcoma typically presents as a rapidly enlarging mass, often with purplish skin discoloration, and is associated with high rates of local recurrence and distant metastasis [4]. Secondary breast angiosarcoma is often linked to prior radiation therapy and manifests as skin changes such as erythema, purple patches, and nodules, primarily in the irradiated area [5]. Both types are aggressive and pose significant management challenges due to their diffuse and multifocal nature.

Current treatment options for breast angiosarcoma include surgical resection, chemotherapy, and occasionally radiation therapy. However, these treatments are often limited by the difficulty in achieving clear margins, high recurrence rates, and the toxicity associated with chemotherapy. Anti-angiogenic therapies, though promising, have shown variable efficacy, with many patients developing resistance after an initial response, underscoring the need for more effective therapeutic strategies [6, 7].

Despite advances in cancer research, the molecular and cellular characteristics of breast angiosarcoma remain poorly understood. The tumor’s heterogeneity and rarity have hindered a comprehensive investigation of the mechanisms driving its aggressive behavior. There is a critical need for detailed studies exploring the tumor’s genetic landscape, cellular heterogeneity, and key molecular pathways involved in its progression. Additionally, the immune microenvironment of breast angiosarcoma is not well-characterized, limiting the development of effective immunotherapies. Understanding immune checkpoint expression and the broader immune landscape within these tumors is essential for developing new treatment strategies.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a cutting-edge technology that enables the analysis of gene expression at the single-cell level, revealing the diversity and complexity of cellular populations within a tumor. This technology is particularly suited to address the knowledge gaps in understanding the molecular and cellular characteristics of primary breast angiosarcoma. By dissecting the tumor at the single-cell level, scRNA-seq can identify key cellular populations, map interactions within the immune microenvironment, and pinpoint novel therapeutic targets associated with aggressive tumor behavior.

The primary aim of this study is to explore the cellular heterogeneity and immune microenvironment of primary breast angiosarcoma using scRNA-seq. By providing a detailed understanding of the tumor’s molecular landscape, the study seeks to identify novel therapeutic targets and improve treatment outcomes. This research not only advances our understanding of breast angiosarcoma but also lays the foundation for future studies and clinical applications, with the ultimate goal of improving patient outcomes in this challenging disease.

Post-excision, the harvested tissues were immediately processed to preserve cell viability. The tissues were washed in ice-cold RPMI1640 medium to remove blood and other contaminants. Subsequently, they were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using the Tissue Dissociation Reagent A Pro (Seekone K01801-30) from SeekGene, following the precise protocol recommended by the manufacturer. The dissociation process was conducted under sterile conditions to prevent contamination. Following dissociation, cell count and viability were assessed using the Countstar® Rigel S2 Fluorescence Cell Analyzer, with an AO/PI reagent employed post-erythrocyte removal (Solarbio R1010) to ensure accurate viability assessment. To further refine the cell population, debris and dead cells were selectively removed using Miltenyi Biotec’s Dead Cell Removal Kit (130-109-398/130-090-101). The viable cells were then washed twice in RPMI1640 and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.04% bovine serum albumin, achieving a final concentration of 1×10⁶ cells per ml, ready for downstream applications.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) libraries were constructed using the SeekOne® Single Cell Whole Transcriptome Kit (SeekGene, Catalog No. K00801), adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s protocol to ensure high-quality data. Briefly, the isolated cells were combined with reverse transcription reagents and carefully loaded into the sample wells of the SeekOne® DD Chip S3. This was followed by the addition of Barcoded Hydrogel Beads (BHBs) and partitioning oil into their respective wells in the Chip S3. The cells, along with the reverse transcription reagents and BHBs, were encapsulated into emulsion droplets using the SeekOne® Digital Droplet System, a critical step for generating barcoded single-cell cDNA.

The emulsion droplets were subsequently transferred to PCR tubes, where they underwent fifteen cycles of thermal cycling, ranging from 8 °C to 42 °C, to facilitate reverse transcription and cDNA barcoding. A 5-minute heat inactivation step at 85 °C was then applied to terminate the reaction. The barcoded cDNA was extracted from the emulsions and subjected to two rounds of PCR amplification to eliminate ribosomal and mitochondrial cDNA contaminants, utilizing AMPure beads for cDNA purification.

Following purification, the cDNA was fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with sequencing adaptors in preparation for next-generation sequencing. The indexed libraries, now containing polyA or non-polyA RNA sequences along with unique cell barcodes and molecular indices, were further purified using AMPure beads to ensure high-quality input for sequencing. For the enrichment of target sequences, the final scFAST-seq libraries were hybridized with biotinylated probes from the Target Cap® Solid Tumor Fusion RNA-Cap Panel and captured using M270 streptavidin beads, as outlined in the detailed protocol (PD00696, Boke Bioscience). A final PCR amplification was conducted using P5 and P7 primers, after which the libraries were purified, quality-checked using the TapStation 4200 (Agilent), and finally sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, employing a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) read length strategy to ensure comprehensive transcriptome coverage.

This meticulous methodological approach was designed to maximize the integrity and resolution of single-cell transcriptomic data, providing a robust foundation for subsequent bioinformatic analyses.

The raw sequencing data obtained from the single-cell RNA-seq experiments were first subjected to initial quality control using Fastp [8], a tool specifically designed to trim primer sequences and remove low-quality bases from the dataset. This crucial preprocessing step ensured that only high-quality reads were retained for further analysis. Following this, the processed sequencing data were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using SeekSoulTools, which facilitated the construction of a comprehensive gene expression matrix for each cell. To further refine the dataset, Seurat (version 4.0.0) [9] was employed for rigorous quality filtering. This involved the exclusion of low-quality cells that either exhibited fewer than 200 detected genes or more than 5000 detected genes, which could indicate potential doublets or poor-quality cells. Additionally, MAD variance normalization [10] was applied to account for and exclude cells disproportionately affected by mitochondrial gene expression, which could otherwise skew the analysis. The resultant dataset, comprising high-quality, normalized cells, was then prepared for subsequent in-depth analyses.

The next phase of the analysis involved clustering the high-quality single-cell data to identify distinct cell populations. This was accomplished using the Seurat R package, which, in combination with Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) [10], facilitated the filtering, normalization, and dimensionality reduction of the data. The use of CCA allowed for the integration of data from three distinct sample groups, thereby enabling a more robust and comprehensive analysis.

Upon integration, the data were clustered into 27 distinct cell clusters, each representing a unique population within the samples. These clusters were subsequently annotated using cell-type-specific signatures and well-established marker genes, resulting in the identification of ten major cell types: T cells, B cells, Fibroblasts, LumHR cells, Myeloid cells, Endothelial cells, Perivascular cells, Basal cells, LumSec cells, and Mast cells. The annotation process involved cross-referencing known markers with the gene expression profiles within each cluster, ensuring accurate identification and classification of the cell types present in the samples. Additionally, further subgroup annotations within cell types, such as T cells and B cells, were conducted using the same rigorous methodology to ensure the granularity and accuracy of the analysis.

To gain a deeper understanding of the biological roles and functional significance of the identified marker genes, we conducted a comprehensive enrichment analysis using several established bioinformatics tools. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed utilizing the ClusterProfiler R package [11], which provides a robust method for identifying overrepresented GO terms while correcting for gene length bias, thereby ensuring accurate representation of gene functions. GO terms that achieved a corrected P value of less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched, highlighting key biological processes, molecular functions, or cellular components associated with the identified cell populations [12].

In parallel, we conducted pathway enrichment analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), a highly regarded resource that provides insights into the high-level functions and utilities of biological systems, including cells, organisms, and ecosystems. KEGG is particularly useful for analyzing large-scale datasets generated by genome sequencing and other high-throughput technologies [13]. The ClusterProfiler R package was again employed to assess the statistical enrichment of the identified marker genes within KEGG pathways, applying a stringent corrected P-value threshold of 0.05. This analysis enabled us to identify key pathways that may be critically involved in the biological processes of the studied cell types.

Additionally, we utilized the Reactome Knowledgebase to further explore the involvement of the marker genes in specific molecular processes. Reactome offers detailed and curated information on a wide range of cellular activities, including signal transduction, transport, DNA replication, metabolism, and other molecular transformations that occur within an ordered network. The statistical enrichment of the marker genes within Reactome pathways was also evaluated using the ClusterProfiler R package, with a corrected P-value threshold of 0.05 to ensure the reliability and relevance of the results.

Together, these enrichment analyses provided a multifaceted view of the functional landscape of the identified marker genes, revealing critical biological pathways and processes that could be driving the cellular behaviors observed in the study. These insights lay the groundwork for further experimental validation and offer potential avenues for therapeutic intervention.

The identification of insertion and deletion (Indel) variants was carried out using VarScan2, a robust Java-based tool, following the samtools mpileup process [13]. This approach allowed for the sensitive detection of mutations within the dataset. The incorporation of panel probes into the workflow significantly enhanced the detection sensitivity, enabling the identification of even low-frequency variants. Once the mutations were detected, ANNOVAR [14] was utilized to annotate the functional consequences of these genetic alterations. This annotation process involved querying several comprehensive databases, including ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar), COSMIC (Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer, v99, https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic), and the 1000 Genomes Project (v2015-08–24) [15–17]. These databases provided critical insights into the clinical significance, somatic mutation profiles, and population genetics of the identified variants, thereby offering a deeper understanding of their potential roles in disease processes.

RNA velocity, an emerging concept in single-cell transcriptomics, was estimated using the scVelo Python package (v0.2.5) [18]. This sophisticated analysis enables the prediction of the future transcriptional states of individual cells by modeling the splicing kinetics of RNA. The process began with the extraction of spliced and unspliced RNA reads using the standard velocyto pipeline (v0.17.17), which generated a loompy file—a specialized format for large-scale single-cell data. This file was then imported into an AnnData object, setting the stage for subsequent computational analysis.

The scVelo workflow followed a methodical sequence, starting with data preprocessing to ensure high-quality inputs. A dynamic model was then implemented to capture the complete transcriptional dynamics of RNA splicing. This model provided a temporal snapshot of gene expression, from which latency times—representing the time taken for RNA molecules to mature from unspliced to spliced forms—were derived. These latency times were crucial for understanding the progression of cellular states.

Finally, the derived latency times were projected onto the MAP (Manifold Approximation and Projection) embeddings, which had been previously obtained from Seurat analysis. This projection allowed for the visualization of RNA velocity within the context of the broader transcriptomic landscape, offering novel insights into the directional dynamics of gene expression and cellular differentiation pathways. This comprehensive analysis of RNA velocity provided a predictive framework for understanding cellular trajectories and their implications in biological processes.

To investigate the intricate communication networks between different cell types, the CellPhoneDB Python package (version 2.1.7) [19] was employed to identify ligand-receptor interactions and predict cell-cell communication. CellPhoneDB is a well-established, publicly accessible repository that provides curated data on receptors, ligands, and their interactions. For this analysis, only those interactions in which ligand/receptor expression was detected in more than 10% of the cells within a specific cell type were selected, ensuring that the analysis focused on the most biologically relevant interactions. Pairwise comparisons were then conducted between the identified cell types to explore potential communication pathways. To validate the specificity of these interactions, cluster labels for all cells were randomly permuted 1000 times, generating a null distribution for each receptor-ligand pair. This permutation allowed for the calculation of the mean receptor and ligand expression levels within interacting clusters. The proportion of means that exceeded the actual observed mean was then used to compute an F-value, providing a measure of the likelihood that the identified receptor-ligand interactions were cell type-specific. From this, biologically significant interactions were selected for further analysis, shedding light on the complex intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment.

For the analysis of gene expression and survival data related to sarcoma marker genes, the GEPIA (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis) and GEPIA2 platforms were utilized [20]. These tools provide a user-friendly interface for accessing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), allowing for in-depth exploration of gene expression patterns and their correlation with patient survival outcomes.

IHC staining was performed on 4-µm sections of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue to visualize the expression of specific markers. The slides were first deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval, which is a critical step to unmask the epitopes for antibody binding. Immunostaining was conducted using a Dako Autostainer (Dako Corporation, Carpinteria, CA), and the following antibodies were employed: anti-CD31 rabbit polyclonal antibody (ZA-0658, Clone EP78, Beijing Golden Bridge Biotechnology), anti-CD34 monoclonal antibody (Kit-0004, Clone QBEnd/10, Fuzhou Maixin Biotechnology), anti-Factor VIII monoclonal antibody (ZM-0022, Clone OTI9 F3, Beijing Golden Bridge Biotechnology), and anti-FLI-1 monoclonal antibody (ZM-0108, Clone G146-22, Beijing Golden Bridge Biotechnology). Tonsil tissue sections served as positive controls to ensure the specificity and accuracy of the staining process.

Statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.0.5). Throughout the analysis, a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, ensuring that the findings were robust and reliable. This threshold was applied consistently across all tests to determine the significance of the results.

We retrieved single-cell dataset (GSE161529) from GEO database, which includes 19,536 cells from two triple negative tumours and 14,501 cells from normal tissues. In order to further validate the characteristics of breast angiosarcoma, these four public datasets were integrated with breast angiosarcoma data. For quality control and downstream analysis, the raw gene expression matrices underwent analysis using Seurat(V4.1.1). Cells containing fewer than 500 unique molecular idetifiers(UMIs) were excluded, as well as cells with more than 10% mitochondrial counts. To cluster all cell types, 2000 variable genes were identified. The top 30 principal components, containing the most informative data, were employed for clustering. Each cell cluster was visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), which were further characterized based on the expression of marker genes. The differential expression analysis was carried out using the Wilcoxon test implemented in the FindAllMarkers function of Seurat. The enriched pathways of differentially expressed genes across all clusters were calsulated by clusterProfiler package(V4.2.0) using GO, KEGG and Reactome Pathway Database.

The breast angiosarcoma sample was obtained from a 31-year-old female patient who discovered a mass in her right breast during lactation. The mass was initially left untreated, leading to rapid enlargement, and subsequent biopsy results suggested a diagnosis of breast angiosarcoma. The patient had no history of chest wall or breast irradiation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed two distinct masses located in the central and lower outer quadrants of the right breast, measuring approximately 6.8 cm by 6.4 cm and 3.7 cm by 2.9 cm, respectively (Fig. 1A). The MRI findings were characterized by reduced signal intensity on T1-weighted images, mixed high signals on T2-weighted FLEX water images, and high signals on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). The contrast-enhanced MRI exhibited a “plateau” pattern on the time-intensity curve (TIC) during both early and late phases. Additionally, multiple masses and mass-like shadows with similar signal characteristics were detected in the left breast.

Computed tomography (CT) further revealed multiple masses in the right breast, the largest measuring 7.2×5.5 cm, with significant heterogeneous enhancement and thickening of the right breast skin (Fig. 1B). In the left breast, three nodules with similar enhancement patterns were identified, prompting a biopsy for further evaluation. The patient underwent a bilateral nipple-sparing mastectomy, and subsequent pathological examination confirmed the presence of bilateral breast angiosarcoma (Fig. 1C). IHC staining was performed to verify the diagnosis. The IHC analysis demonstrated positive staining for CD31, CD34, vascular markers F8, FLI-1, and smooth muscle actin, confirming the vascular origin of the tumor cells. Conversely, the cells were negative for cytokeratin and GATA3, excluding a ductal epithelial origin (Fig. 1E). The other two samples analyzed were diagnosed as invasive breast cancer, with their clinical data summarized in Table S1.

To further investigate the microenvironmental characteristics of breast angiosarcoma, single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on the three samples, providing a deeper understanding of the cellular and molecular landscape of this rare malignancy.

A total of 31,771 cells were obtained from the three samples, with a median gene count of 1,559 per cell. Following the initial quality control measures, all 31,771 cells were retained for further analysis. At a resolution of 0.1, the integrated data revealed nine distinct cell clusters, including luminal hormone-responsive ductal epithelial cells (LumHR), luminal secretory ductal epithelial cells (LumSec), basal-myoepithelial cells, endothelial cells, four types of immune cells (T cells, B cells, mast cells, and myeloid cells), and two types of mesenchymal stromal cells (fibroblasts and perivascular cells) [21] (Fig. 2B). Notably, perivascular cells, fibroblasts, T cells, endothelial cells, and myeloid cells collectively constituted over 80% of the total cell population (Table S3).

When the resolution was increased to 0.6, a more granular analysis identified 26 distinct cell clusters, each characterized by unique gene expression patterns (Fig. 2C,D). The distribution of these cell types across the samples is depicted in the accompanying figures (Fig. 2E). Notably, certain subgroups within the primary breast angiosarcoma samples exhibited unique clustering distributions, particularly in clusters 7, 23, and 26 of the perivascular cell subgroups at this higher resolution (Fig. 2F). In contrast, the number and proportion of B cells were significantly lower in primary angiosarcoma samples compared to those in breast cancer samples (Fig. 2G).

The samples from BC_0 and BC_2 included 14,647 and 10,723 cells, respectively, while the PR_BA sample contained 6,401 cells, all of which were included in the analysis. Cells from the PR_BA sample accounted for approximately 20% of the total cell count across the study. Notably, subclusters with a concentration higher than 20%, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, perivascular cells, and mast cells, were instrumental in defining the distinct microenvironment of primary breast angiosarcoma, distinguishing it from the invasive breast cancer samples.

A comparative analysis between breast angiosarcoma and invasive breast cancer samples identified differential expression in 1,010 genes, with 223 genes being down-regulated and 777 genes up-regulated (Fig. 3A, Table S4). The top 20 differentially expressed genes, as determined by an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2(FoldChange)| > 2, are listed in Table 1 for the breast angiosarcoma dataset. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the upregulated genes are primarily enriched in pathways related to ribosome biogenesis, ribosomal subunit biogenesis, and ribosome assembly, indicating enhanced protein synthesis, a feature commonly associated with increased cell growth and proliferation in angiosarcoma (Fig. 3B). Additionally, pathways involved in vascular proliferation, including those related to growth factor and platelet-derived growth factor binding, as well as smooth muscle contraction, were also upregulated, highlighting the aggressive nature of angiosarcoma (Fig. 3B). Recent studies have shown that excessive ribosome production is critical for the accelerated protein synthesis and increased translation observed in the proliferation of malignant cells [22]. Furthermore, abnormalities in ribosomal proteins have been linked to malignant tumor behavior, resistance to treatment, and poor clinical outcomes. Aberrant expressions of Ribosomal Protein Large (RPL) and Small (RPS) subunits were detected in the PR_BA sample, as summarized in Table 2. According to data from the TCGA-SARC database, these abnormalities in ribosomal subunits are associated with poorer overall survival rates (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the downregulated genes were primarily involved in cellular connectivity, adhesion, and immune responses (Fig. 3D). Notably, reduced expression of genes related to the immunoglobulin complex and immunological synapse components may impair immune function [23, 24]. Additionally, diminished cell adhesion facilitates tumor cells in evading immune surveillance by altering the surrounding stroma to create an environment that supports tumor growth and metastasis, while also reducing interactions between tumor cells and immune cells [25]. To assess whether these differentially expressed genes are unique to angiosarcoma, we compared our findings with the public database GSE163359, which contains gene expression data from 13 angiosarcoma and 6 benign tumor tissue samples. Commonly differentially expressed genes across both datasets were identified, and Venn diagrams were used to visualize these shared genes (Table S5, Fig. S1-2). This study employed a panel probe technology targeting 532 genes to detect genetic mutations across three different tumor samples. The use of panel probes significantly enhanced the sensitivity of mutation detection. The analysis of samples PR_BA, BC_0, and BC_2 revealed 1,952, 2,257, and 3,180 mutation sites in their respective genomes (Tables S6-8). A particular focus was placed on mutations within the endothelial cell cluster (cluster 6), where 247, 447, and 1,078 mutations were identified, respectively. Comparative analyses were performed to determine the specificity of these mutations to angiosarcoma, and Venn diagrams were utilized to compare mutated genes between the angiosarcoma and breast cancer sample groups (Fig. S3). This analysis identified 127 genes with specific expression related to endothelial cells in the angiosarcoma dataset (Table S9). Additionally, the mutation spectrum in breast angiosarcoma was predominantly characterized by C>T and G>A transitions, with the mutation patterns mainly consisting of nonsynonymous single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and frameshift insertions (Fig. S4).

EndMT, a specialized subtype of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), is a critical mechanism that facilitates the invasion and metastasis of epithelial malignancies [26]. Although extensive research on EMT has provided a comprehensive understanding of this process, the specifics of EndMT, particularly in the context of angiosarcoma, remain less explored. This study aimed to investigate the signaling pathways implicated in EndMT within two breast malignancies, with a particular focus on the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)/Smad and Notch pathways [27]. Comparative analysis of endothelial cell clusters from both breast angiosarcoma and invasive breast cancer revealed a notable downregulation of key components within the TGF-β/Smad pathway in angiosarcoma, including TGFBR2, SMAD3, SERPINE1, CCN2, and SNAI1. This downregulation suggests a suppression of TGF-β/Smad pathway activity in breast angiosarcoma cells (Table S10). In contrast, components of the Notch signaling pathway, such as NOTCH3, JAG1, and MAML3, were upregulated. Moreover, increased expression of downstream effectors and target genes of the Notch pathway, including HES1, HEY1, and HEY2, was observed across various cell populations in angiosarcoma—specifically within endothelial, perivascular, and myeloid cells—when compared to breast cancer (Tables S10-12). A particularly noteworthy finding was the marked underexpression of FBXW7, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that plays a crucial role in the ubiquitination and degradation of Notch pathway proteins, within the endothelial cells of angiosarcoma [28] (Table S10). Additionally, a significant increase in FN1, a multifunctional extracellular matrix protein critical for cell adhesion, migration, growth, and differentiation, was detected in these cells [29]. The elevated FN1 levels could be indicative of an active EndMT process, potentially mediated predominantly through the Notch signaling pathway [30]. These findings suggest that the Notch signaling pathway may have a dominant role in driving the endothelial-mesenchymal transition process in angiosarcoma, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying this aggressive malignancy.

A previous study had identified 127 mutated genes in Cluster 6, which is associated with endothelial cells, and among these, mutations in the KDR gene were particularly significant (Table S9). KDR (kinase insert domain receptor), also known as VEGFR-2, is a critical driver in the development of primary breast angiosarcoma. Mutations in KDR are known to influence angiogenic signaling pathways, thereby contributing to the progression of the disease [31]. A comprehensive analysis of KDR expression levels across the five distinct endothelial cell subclusters revealed significantly elevated KDR expression in the PR_BA samples, particularly within Subclusters 1 and 5 (Fig. 4D).

Further examination of the endothelial cell subpopulations in the PR_BA samples, through violin plots, indicated that genes highly expressed within these subpopulations were significantly associated with inflammatory processes and angiogenesis (Fig. 4E). The clinical relevance of these highly expressed genes was further underscored by survival analyses using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), which demonstrated their potential impact on overall survival outcomes (Fig. 4F).

Reactome enrichment analysis provided additional insights, showing that Subclusters 1 and 5 were enriched in several critical signaling pathways related to angiogenesis, cell differentiation, and cell proliferation. These pathways include the “VEGFA-VEGFR2 Pathway,” “VEGF Signaling,” “Notch Signaling,” “Signaling by PDGF,” “Beta-catenin independent WNT signaling,” “Platelet activation, signaling and aggregation,” “MAPK family signaling cascades,” and “PIP3 activates AKT signaling.” These findings highlight the functional diversity within the endothelial cell population in PR_BA, and suggest that specific subclusters may play pivotal roles in the aggressive behavior and progression of breast angiosarcoma.

Single-cell sequencing data identified immune cells, such as B cells, T cells, mast cells, and myeloid cells, within two malignancies (Fig 2B). The proportion of immune cells differed between breast angiosarcoma and breast cancer samples (Fig 2G). B cells were significantly less prevalent in PR_BA samples compared to BC_0 and BC_2, whereas T cells maintained similar levels across both malignancies (Fig 2E). To delve into immune cell diversity, the immune cells in PR_BA were further clustered and annotated using canonical immune marker genes. Analysis of 10,633 T cells via scRNA-seq revealed 13 subclusters (Fig 5A). Identified CD4+ T cell subtypes included naive T cells (CCR7), TH-like cells (ARID5B, MAF), T effector memory (TEM) cells (DNAJA1, HSPA1 A), T regulatory (Treg) cells (BATF, TBC1D4), and T central memory (TCM) cells (ANXA1, IL7R). Two CD8+ T cell populations were recognized: T resident memory (TRM; CD8 A, AUTS2) and T exhaustion (TEX; LAG3, ITGAE). A cluster of γδ T cells (GD-T; CEMIP2, GPCPD1) emerged, with sub-clustering further delineating NK (AREG) and NKT (NKG7, TRGC1) cell populations. Additionally, a small proliferative cluster (T_prol; STMN1) and a subset of cells expressing high levels of CST7 (CST7_T) were identified (Fig 5B). The tumor microenvironment of breast angiosarcoma featured a high proportion of memory T cells (CD4_TCM, CD8_TRM) and a low proportion of effector T cells (CD8_TEX, CD4_TH_like, CD4_Treg) (Fig 5C). Single-cell RNA sequencing metadata was leveraged to analyze intercellular communication among various cell clusters in breast angiosarcoma. After preprocessing the experimental data, endothelial cell populations exhibited complex interactions with fibroblasts, perivascular cells, T cells, and myeloid cells through chemokines and growth factors, with the strongest cellular communication occurring between endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and perivascular cells (Fig 6A, Fig S5-7). Analyzing cell clustering and functional annotation identified 26 clusters within the related cell populations. These clusters include Endothelial, iCAF, myoCAF, apCAF, Macro_C1QC, Macro_NLRP3, Mono_CD16, cDC2_CD1 C, GD_T, CD8_TRM, NKT, CST7_T, THEMIS_T, CD4_TEM, CD4_TCM, CD4_Treg, CD4_TH_like, CD4_naive, Myeloid_CD1 A, cDC1_CLEC9 A, pDC_LILRA4, NK, CD8_TEX, T_prol, cDC3_LAMP3, and Perivascular cells (Fig 6B). Cell communication among clusters was estimated using CellPhoneDB, focusing on interaction pairs involving growth factors and members of the CCL and CXCL families (Fig 6C-D). Tumor cells express high levels of CXCL12, which binds to CXCR4 and DPP4 receptors. Given CXCR4’s expression in a diverse array of immune cells, CXCL12 secreted by tumor cells may represent the primary mechanism underlying immune resistance in breast angiosarcoma [32]. Previous studies have shown that the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis promotes tumor immune evasion in triple-negative breast cancer [33]. Analysis of the top 30 interaction pairs of growth factors demonstrated that iCAFs produce VEGF, including VEGFA, VEGFB, VEGFC, and VEGFD, which bind to VEGF receptors (FLT1, KDR, NRP1, and FLT4) on tumor cells, thereby promoting angiogenesis. FGFR1 expression was observed on fibroblast clusters (iCAF, myoCAF, apCAF), perivascular cells, tumor cells, and macrophages (Macro_C1QC), binding to FGF and exhibiting proliferative effects. PDGFRB, expressed in tumor cells and perivascular cells, is also present in iCAF, myoCAF, and apCAF, which express PDGFRB and PDGFRA, binding to tumor cell-secreted PDGFs, such as PDGFA, PDGFB, and PDGFD, promoting proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. MET receptors on tumor cells can bind to HGF secreted by iCAF and myoCAF, facilitating tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [35]. In the cell-cell communication analysis, we identified that endothelial cells, perivascular cells, and fibroblasts in breast angiosarcoma exhibit the strongest intercellular interactions. Therefore, we focused on these three cell types and further analyzed their differentially expressed genes (DEGs) compared to HR+ breast cancer, triple-negative breast cancer, and normal breast tissue, aiming to identify the unique molecular characteristics of breast angiosarcoma.

In the endothelial cell population, we identified 87 significantly upregulated genes in BAS, including FAT4, FN1, PLCG1, and EGR1 (Fig S8 A). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were predominantly enriched in biological processes related to angiogenesis and endothelial cell function, including endothelial cell proliferation, endothelial cell differentiation, endothelial cell development, and establishment of the endothelial barrier (Fig S8B). These results suggest that endothelial cells in breast angiosarcoma may exhibit enhanced proliferative and differentiation capabilities, playing a crucial role in the formation and maintenance of the tumor vasculature.

In the perivascular cell population, we identified 258 significantly upregulated genes in BAS, including HEY2, ANGPT1, ANGPTL4, and JUN (Fig S8 C). GO enrichment analysis showed that these genes were mainly enriched in biological processes related to the muscle system, involving muscle system processes, muscle tissue development, muscle cell differentiation, smooth muscle contraction, and regulation of muscle system processes (Fig S8D). The enrichment of these biological processes suggests that perivascular cells in breast angiosarcoma may contribute to vascular stability, smooth muscle regulation, and tumor vascular remodeling, further highlighting their distinct role in the tumor microenvironment.

In the fibroblast population, we identified 355 significantly upregulated genes in BAS, including VEGFD, PDGFRB, PDGFRA, and CXCL12 (Fig S8E). GO enrichment analysis demonstrated that these genes were significantly enriched in the platelet-derived growth factor receptor signaling pathway (Fig S8 F), suggesting that fibroblasts in breast angiosarcoma may promote extracellular matrix remodeling and dynamic alterations in the tumor microenvironment through PDGF-mediated signaling, ultimately influencing tumor growth and invasiveness.

The primary objective of this study was to elucidate the molecular and cellular characteristics of primary breast angiosarcoma using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). Given the rarity and aggressive nature of this malignancy, a detailed understanding of its underlying biology is essential to improve diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Although based on a single patient, our findings suggest generalizable features of breast angiosarcoma, offering insights into its cellular composition and transcriptional landscape. Our approach demonstrates the potential of single-cell RNA sequencing for personalized cancer analysis, particularly in rare tumors. This method not only captures patient-specific variations but also identifies broader tumor characteristics, making it a valuable tool for precision oncology. Our comprehensive scRNA-seq analysis yielded several critical insights into the pathogenesis of primary breast angiosarcoma: 1) Identification of Key Differentially Expressed Genes and Signaling Pathways: We found significant differences in gene expression between primary breast angiosarcoma and invasive breast carcinoma, identifying 1,010 differentially expressed genes, with 223 down-regulated and 777 up-regulated in angiosarcoma. These genes are involved in critical cellular processes like ribosome biogenesis, angiogenesis, and cytoskeletal remodeling, contributing to the aggressive nature of angiosarcoma. 2) Unique Characteristics of Endothelial Cells in Breast Angiosarcoma: The study revealed considerable heterogeneity within the endothelial cells in breast angiosarcoma, identifying five distinct subclusters. One subcluster was unique to angiosarcoma, suggesting specific molecular features, including mutations in the KDR gene, which is crucial for angiogenic signaling. 3) The Role of EndMT and Notch Signaling in Angiosarcoma Pathogenesis: Our findings indicate a significant role for endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) and Notch signaling in breast angiosarcoma’s development and progression. We observed downregulation of the TGF-β/Smad pathway, typically linked to EndMT, and upregulation of the Notch pathway, particularly in endothelial, perivascular, and myeloid cells, which may promote tumor invasiveness and metastasis. 4) Differences in the Immune Microenvironment Between Breast Angiosarcoma and Invasive Breast Carcinoma: The study also highlighted differences in the immune microenvironment between breast angiosarcoma and invasive breast carcinoma. Breast angiosarcoma samples had fewer B cells and a distinct immune cell composition, with more memory T cells and fewer effector T cells. There was also reduced expression of immune checkpoint genes, suggesting a less immunosuppressive environment in breast angiosarcoma. Collectively, these findings enhance our understanding of the molecular and cellular landscape of primary breast angiosarcoma and underscore the importance of further research aimed at developing targeted therapies that address the specific molecular abnormalities identified in this study.

Our findings align with and extend the current understanding of breast angiosarcoma, particularly regarding its aggressive nature and propensity for metastasis. Previous studies have established breast angiosarcoma as a highly malignant tumor with a marked tendency for distant metastasis to atypical sites such as the lungs, bones, and liver [36, 37]. Our study corroborates these observations, further confirming the aggressive behavior of breast angiosarcoma at the molecular level. Specifically, we identified significant upregulation of genes involved in ribosome biogenesis and angiogenesis, processes that are known to drive rapid tumor growth and dissemination [38]. This molecular profile is consistent with the high metastatic potential previously reported in clinical observations.

Moreover, the downregulation of genes involved in immune response and cell adhesion observed in our study reinforces the concept of immune evasion as a key feature of breast angiosarcoma. This is in line with earlier research that highlighted the tumor’s ability to avoid immune surveillance, thereby facilitating its aggressive spread [39]. The identification of unique endothelial cell subclusters with specific molecular alterations, such as KDR mutations, further supports the established role of abnormal angiogenesis in the pathophysiology of angiosarcoma, as previously described [40].

While our findings confirm several aspects of breast angiosarcoma that have been documented in the literature, they also provide novel insights into the disease’s molecular landscape. One of the most significant contributions of our study is the identification of unique molecular signatures that differentiate breast angiosarcoma from other breast malignancies. The discovery of specific gene mutations, including KDR, FN1, and PLCG1, sheds new light on the genetic drivers of angiosarcoma progression.

The identification of KDR (VEGFR-2) mutations is particularly noteworthy, as this gene plays a crucial role in angiogenesis [41]. The upregulation and mutation of KDR in our study suggest that it may be a key driver of the aberrant vascularization observed in breast angiosarcoma, contributing to the tumor’s rapid growth and metastatic behavior. This finding not only confirms the role of KDR in angiosarcoma but also highlights its potential as a therapeutic target.

Similarly, the upregulation of FN1 (fibronectin 1), a gene involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, suggests that alterations in the tumor microenvironment may play a significant role in facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis [42]. The high expression of FN1 in breast angiosarcoma underscores its importance in tumor biology and could provide a new avenue for therapeutic intervention. Another novel contribution of our study is the identification of mutations in PLCG1, a gene involved in signal transduction and cellular proliferation [43]. The presence of PLCG1 mutations in breast angiosarcoma adds to the growing body of evidence that this gene may be implicated in the pathogenesis of various vascular tumors [44]. Given its role in key signaling pathways, PLCG1 represents a potential target for future therapeutic strategies. Overall, our study not only confirms the aggressive and metastatic nature of breast angiosarcoma as documented in previous research but also introduces new molecular insights that could guide future research and therapeutic development. The identification of specific gene mutations and their associated pathways offers promising targets for personalized medicine approaches in treating this challenging malignancy.

Our study highlights the downregulation of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway in breast angiosarcoma, which is crucial for endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) [45]. Typically, TGF-β/Smad signaling drives EndMT, contributing to tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis. However, in breast angiosarcoma, the reduced activity of this pathway, as indicated by the downregulation of TGFBR2, SMAD3, and related genes, suggests a deviation from the typical EndMT process observed in other cancers. This suppression may be compensated by other pathways, particularly the Notch signaling pathway, which was significantly upregulated in our study. The activation of Notch signaling in endothelial, perivascular, and myeloid cells appears to promote mesenchymal characteristics, enhancing tumor invasiveness and metastasis. These findings suggest that while TGF-β/Smad signaling is commonly associated with EndMT in many cancers, Notch signaling might play a more dominant role in the vascular context of breast angiosarcoma, presenting a potential target for therapeutic intervention.

The immune landscape in breast angiosarcoma revealed a high proportion of memory T cells and low expression of immune checkpoints, which contrasts with other breast malignancies. The elevated levels of memory T cells, such as CD4_TCM and CD8_TRM, indicate a persistent immune response, yet the reduced presence of effector T cells (CD8_TEX, CD4_TH_like, CD4_Treg) suggests a weakened immediate immune response [46]. This imbalance could facilitate tumor immune evasion. Additionally, the low expression of immune checkpoint molecules like PD-1, CTLA-4, and BTLA may reflect a less immunosuppressive environment but also complicates the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors. These findings suggest that breast angiosarcoma may utilize a unique immune evasion strategy, highlighting the need for tailored immunotherapies, potentially combining checkpoint inhibitors with agents that enhance effector T cell function or reactivation.

Our analysis of breast angiosarcoma revealed a mutation spectrum characterized by C>T and G>A transitions, commonly linked to oxidative damage and DNA repair errors [47]. Key mutations were identified in genes associated with critical cancer pathways, including KDR, FN1, and PLCG1. KDR mutations, which drive angiogenesis, suggest enhanced tumor vascularization and align with the aggressive nature of angiosarcoma, making it a promising target for anti-angiogenic therapies. FN1 mutations, involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, likely contribute to the tumor’s invasive properties by altering cell-matrix interactions. PLCG1, which plays a role in signal transduction and proliferation, was also mutated, indicating its potential involvement in tumor growth and resistance to apoptosis. These mutations provide critical insights into the molecular drivers of breast angiosarcoma and highlight potential targets for developing novel therapeutic strategies tailored to the unique genetic profile of this malignancy.

The diagnostic and prognostic potential of FN1, PLCG1, and other identified molecular markers could lead to more accurate diagnoses and better predictions of clinical outcomes in breast angiosarcoma. These insights could guide the development of more personalized treatment protocols, improving patient care and outcomes in this challenging malignancy.

The findings from this study have significant clinical relevance, particularly in identifying potential therapeutic targets for treating breast angiosarcoma. One of the most promising avenues is the targeting of the Notch signaling pathway. Our study revealed a marked upregulation of Notch pathway components, including NOTCH3, JAG1, and MAML3, in breast angiosarcoma. The Notch pathway is crucial in regulating cell differentiation, proliferation, and survival, and its aberrant activation is associated with increased tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential [48].

Given these findings, targeting the Notch pathway could be a viable therapeutic strategy. Gamma-secretase inhibitors (GSIs), which block the activation of the Notch receptor by preventing the cleavage of its intracellular domain, represent one potential approach. GSIs have shown promise in preclinical models of various cancers by reducing tumor growth and enhancing the efficacy of other treatments [49, 50]. Additionally, Notch monoclonal antibodies that specifically inhibit the interaction between Notch receptors and their ligands could be used to block the signaling cascade more selectively [51]. These therapies could be particularly effective in tumors where Notch signaling is a driving force behind tumor progression, such as breast angiosarcoma.

Moreover, the immune landscape observed in breast angiosarcoma, characterized by low expression of immune checkpoints, suggests that immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) alone may have limited efficacy [52]. However, combination therapies that include anti-angiogenic drugs and ICIs could offer enhanced therapeutic benefits [53, 54]. The upregulation of angiogenesis-related genes, such as KDR (VEGFR-2), underscores the critical role of angiogenesis in breast angiosarcoma. Anti-angiogenic therapies, such as those targeting VEGF or VEGFR pathways, could disrupt the tumor’s blood supply and inhibit its growth. When combined with ICIs, these therapies might overcome the tumor’s immune evasion mechanisms by normalizing the tumor vasculature, improving immune cell infiltration, and enhancing the overall anti-tumor immune response.

In addition to therapeutic implications, our findings have potential diagnostic and prognostic applications. The identification of molecular markers such as FN1 and PLCG1 offers new tools for diagnosing and predicting outcomes in breast angiosarcoma. FN1, which is involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and cell adhesion, was found to be highly expressed in breast angiosarcoma. Its elevated expression could serve as a biomarker for identifying this tumor type, particularly in distinguishing it from other forms of breast cancer. Moreover, given its role in facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis, FN1 expression levels might correlate with disease aggressiveness and patient prognosis. High FN1 expression could indicate a higher risk of metastatic spread, guiding clinicians in making more informed decisions regarding treatment intensity and monitoring strategies.

Similarly, PLCG1 mutations, which are implicated in signal transduction and cellular proliferation, could also serve as important diagnostic and prognostic markers. The presence of PLCG1 mutations in breast angiosarcoma may indicate a more aggressive tumor phenotype, associated with poorer clinical outcomes. Monitoring PLCG1 expression or mutation status could help stratify patients based on risk, allowing for more personalized treatment approaches. Furthermore, these molecular findings could influence patient management strategies, particularly regarding the decision to perform axillary lymph node staging. While the necessity of lymph node evaluation in breast angiosarcoma is still debated, the identification of specific molecular markers could help identify patients at higher risk for lymph node involvement [55]. For example, high expression of angiogenesis-related genes or mutations in pathways associated with metastasis could indicate a greater likelihood of lymph node spread, prompting the inclusion of axillary lymph node staging in the surgical management plan.

This study’s primary limitation is the small sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings and underrepresent the heterogeneity of breast angiosarcoma. Additionally, technical limitations associated with single-cell RNA sequencing, such as potential dropout events, technical variability during sample preparation, and the complexity of data interpretation, may have influenced the results. These factors highlight the need for further validation in larger, more diverse cohorts and the integration of additional methodologies to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the disease’s molecular landscape.

Despite these limitations, the study has made several important contributions to our understanding of breast angiosarcoma. Key findings include the identification of unique molecular signatures, the potential role of EndMT and Notch signaling in tumor progression, and the distinct immune landscape of this malignancy. These insights have significant implications for the development of targeted therapies and diagnostic tools.

Future research should focus on validating these findings in broader patient populations and exploring the role of EndMT in other types of sarcomas. The potential for therapeutic targeting of the Notch pathway, as well as the investigation of combination therapies involving immune checkpoint inhibitors and anti-angiogenic drugs, presents promising avenues for improving treatment outcomes. Additionally, the development of new diagnostic markers based on the molecular signatures identified in this study could enhance early detection and prognostication in breast angiosarcoma. These efforts will be crucial in advancing our understanding of this aggressive cancer and improving patient care.

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the molecular and cellular heterogeneity of primary breast angiosarcoma, a rare and aggressive malignancy. Our findings reveal significant differences in gene expression and cellular composition between breast angiosarcoma and invasive breast cancer. Key pathways, including those involved in ribosome biogenesis, angiogenesis, and smooth muscle contraction, were notably upregulated in angiosarcoma, emphasizing their role in tumor progression. The identification of mutations in critical genes such as KDR, ANGPT2, LOXL2, and TGFBR1 offers valuable insights into the molecular underpinnings of angiosarcoma and highlights potential therapeutic targets. Moreover, the study underscores the downregulation of immune response genes and the unique immune microenvironment of angiosarcoma, which may facilitate immune evasion and contribute to the tumor’s aggressive behavior. These findings suggest that targeted therapies aimed at specific signaling pathways, along with strategies to bolster the immune response, could enhance the effectiveness of treatments for this challenging cancer type. Future research should prioritize the validation of these findings in larger and more diverse populations and explore combination therapies that may improve treatment outcomes for patients with breast angiosarcoma.
