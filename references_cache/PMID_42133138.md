# Diagnostic accuracy of 1H-MRS in detecting the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG) in adult-type diffuse gliomas.
**Authors:** Hebda A, Wawrzyniak P, Heinze S, Mazgaj P, Sadowski D, Oczko-Wojciechowska M, Nowicka E, Chmielik E, Bobek-Billewicz B
**Journal:** J Neurooncol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s11060-026-05600-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11060-026-05600-9)

## Content

PURPOSE: To evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of three proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) strategies-short echo (TE 30 ms), intermediate echo (TE 97 ms), and MEGA-PRESS-for detecting the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG) to non-invasively determine isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status in adult-type diffuse gliomas.

METHODS: A cohort of 152 patients with adult-type diffuse gliomas (84 IDH-mutant, 68 IDH-wild-type) underwent preoperative 3T MRI incorporating standard morphological sequences and 1H-MRS acquisitions. Spectra were evaluated to assess the presence of a 2HG peak. Diagnostic performance was analysed independently for each method and collectively for a subgroup of patients receiving all three sequences.

RESULTS: The intermediate echo (TE 97 ms) method demonstrated 100% specificity and 51.2% sensitivity, while the short echo (TE 30 ms) sequence achieved 97% specificity and 88.9% sensitivity. The MEGA-PRESS method yielded 86.7% specificity and 72.2% sensitivity but was limited by a high non-diagnostic rate (17.5%) due to artifact susceptibility. When combined, the spectroscopy methods achieved 100% sensitivity, 85.7% specificity, and 93.7% overall accuracy in detecting 2HG.

CONCLUSION: Conventional PRESS sequences (TE 30 ms and TE 97 ms) outperform MEGA-PRESS for the routine clinical detection of 2HG due to higher technical reliability. An optimized conventional PRESS protocol offers a robust, highly accurate, and non-invasive preoperative tool for identifying glioma genotypes and predicting tumour phenotypes.

In the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of Central Nervous System (CNS) tumours, molecular diagnostics have assumed a significant role in the categorization and management of gliomas [1, 2]. Among the key molecular markers, the isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status is one of the most important prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers, especially in adult-type diffuse gliomas. IDH mutations result in the accumulation (in the cytoplasm of glioma cells with IDH1 mutation and in the mitochondria of cells with IDH2 mutation) of the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG) within tumour tissue, providing a critical molecular signature for diagnosis and further prognosis [3–7].

Despite histopathological analysis remaining the gold standard, the non-invasive detection of IDH mutation status is important for prognostication and therapeutic decision-making. Nowadays, radiogenomics is an evolving field of study that utilises advances in image acquisition and analysis to correlate imaging features with molecular markers of diseases. It focuses on the relationship between genomics and imaging phenotypes, and has been widely applied to address tumour heterogeneity and predict immune responsiveness and progression [8]. Preoperative non-invasive methods for verifying mutation status could greatly influence patient management, especially when considering the risks associated with biopsy in eloquent brain areas or in differentiating gliomas. The development of a protocol including 1 H-MRS spectroscopy focused on the detection of 2HG will enable the non-invasive determination of IDH [3, 5, 9–12].

This study investigates the effectiveness of 1H-MRS in detecting 2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG) in brain tumours, specifically in adult-type diffuse gliomas. We implemented three 1H-MRS strategies to check how effective these spectroscopy methods are in detecting 2HG. We used a short echo time (TE 30 ms) sequence, an intermediate echo time (TE 97 ms) PRESS sequence, and the MEGA-PRESS method to improve non-invasive diagnostic methods.

A total of 152 adult-type diffuse glioma patients treated between January 2020 and October 2025 were included in the analysis. All patients underwent preoperative multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging prior to planned surgery or biopsy, and had available histopathological and genetic test results. The protocol included standard morphological sequences and optimized spectroscopy 1H-MRS providing detection of the oncometabolite 2HG. Clinical data, tumour characteristics, and genetic markers such as IDH1 mutation, MGMT promoter methylation, TERT pathogenic variant, and the appearance of microvascular proliferation and/or foci of necrosis were collected. In this study, we focused on the evaluation of IDH1 mutation status. IDH2 mutations were not considered in our analysis, as IDH2 status was not evaluated in a subset of the included cases.

All MR examinations and spectroscopy acquisitions were performed on 3T clinical scanners (MAGNETOM Vida or MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 20-channel head coil. Different analytical approaches were applied depending on the acquisition sequence.

Data from TE 97 ms and MEGA-PRESS were analysed using LCModel software (version 6.3). Custom basis sets were simulated via FID-A in the MATLAB environment using real pulses. Macromolecules were not modelled in either basis set. PRESS 97 ms Basis Set included simulated spectra for Ala, Asp, Cr, PCr, GABA, Glc, Gln, Glu, GPC, PCh, GSH, 2HG, Ins, Lac, NAA, NAAG, Scyllo, and Tau; MEGA-PRESS Basis Set included simulated spectra for 2HG, NAA, NAAG, Glu, Gln, GSH, and GABA. The primary output metric for both sequences was the Cramer-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) of the 2HG peak. In the LCModel analysis, the reliable separation of 2HG from the overlapping Glx complex was facilitated by the fitting algorithm’s simultaneous evaluation of the entire simulated basis set, utilizing the distinct 2HG resonance at 4.02 ppm—where Glx lacks overlapping signal—as an anchor to prevent signal misattribution.

Short TE data were analysed using the MR Spectroscopy application on Syngo.Via software (Siemens Healthineers). Processing steps deviated from the standard product pipeline by fitting two specific Gaussian peaks: Glx3 (between 2.24 and 2.34 ppm) and Glx4 (between 2.33 and 2.42 ppm). The underlying assumption of this model is that 2HG will selectively increase the integral of the Glx3 Gaussian peak (with which it overlaps) but not the Glx4 peak. The Positive Glx3/Glx4 ratio shifts relative to the normal-appearing reference voxel indicated the presence of 2HG, whereas negative or neutral shifts denied it.

To ensure the reliability of the quantification models, strict data exclusion criteria were applied across all sequences. Spectra from any sequence were excluded if the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) was < 2. For LCModel fits (PRESS 97 ms and MEGA-PRESS), a CRLB of < 50% for 2HG was required to accept the model fit. For syngo.via post-processing (PRESS 30 ms), visually distinct/resolvable Glx3 and Glx4 peaks were required. Specific mean SNR and linewidth numerical values across the cohort were not systematically reported; however, adherence to the above thresholds was strictly maintained. Sample spectra illustrating these criteria can be found in Figs. 1, 2, and 3.

Statistical analysis was performed on the data obtained from both tumour samples and reference spectra, alongside dedicated phantoms (two phantoms containing brain metabolites in nominal concentrations, one with 2HG and one without, validated under the same protocol). The correlation between 2HG presence and molecular diagnosis, as well as the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of the validated methods, was statistically assessed, including a formal comparative analysis using ROC curves and AUC.

To directly compare the diagnostic efficacy of the three spectroscopic methods and minimize selection bias, we specifically restricted our analysis to a strictly paired subset of 63 patients. Every single patient in this analysed subgroup successfully underwent all three MRS sequences (TE 97 ms, TE 30 ms, and MEGA-PRESS) and yielded diagnostic-quality spectra for each. As described in the methodology, for the combined evaluation, a result was considered positive (+) if a 2HG peak was detected in any of the three methods, and negative (-) only if none of the methods showed a 2HG peak.

Furthermore, we expanded our statistical analysis to rigorously evaluate the differences in diagnostic performance among the three methods using a paired sample approach. We utilized Cochran’s Q test to assess differences in the proportions of true and false results across the three methods. For this analysis, a result was defined as “true/correct” if it yielded 2HG(+) for an IDH-mutant tumour or 2HG(-) for a wildtype tumour, and “false/incorrect” if it yielded 2HG(+) for a wildtype tumour (false positive) or 2HG(-) for a mutant tumour (false negative). To precisely identify differences between specific pairs of methods, post-hoc McNemar tests were performed. To adjust for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied, establishing a corrected threshold for statistical significance at p < 0.0167.

A total of 152 patients with adult-type diffuse gliomas underwent proton MR spectroscopy as part of a 3T MRI examination protocol extended with single-voxel PRESS and MEGA-PRESS acquisitions for the non-invasive detection of 2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG). The study cohort comprised 69 women and 83 men, with a mean age of 46 ± 13 years. IDH mutation status was available for all patients: 84/152 (55%) were IDH-mutant and 68/152 (45%) were IDH-wild-type.

The analysed group comprised 54 out of 152 (36%) WHO G2 tumours, 26 out of 152 (17%) were G3 tumours, and the remaining 72 out of 152 (47%) were wild-type G4 tumours. The analysed group comprised 66 cases of IDH1-mutant astrocytoma (43 G2, 19 G3, 4 G4), 18 cases of IDH1-mutant oligodendroglioma with 1p/19q codeletion (11 for G2, 7 for G3), and the remaining 68 cases were G4 wild-type, of which 32 were histopathological glioblastomas with necrosis and proliferation of small vessels, whilst the remaining 34 were wild-type astrocytomas.

Most patients exhibited high-grade features in histopathological evaluation with MVP and necrosis for G4 tumours, including prominent microvascular proliferation (MVP). Furthermore, nearly half of the G4 cases had TERT promoter mutations, the assessment of which has recently become a standard component of molecular diagnostics. In some patients, necrosis within tumours classified as molecular glioblastomas (molecular GBM) caused underscoring of the histopathological and molecular heterogeneity of these lesions.

The Cochran’s Q test revealed a statistically significant difference in the proportion of dichotomous diagnostic results (true/correct vs. false/incorrect) among the three methods (p = 0.0049). To precisely identify where these differences occurred, we performed post-hoc McNemar tests for pairwise comparisons. To account for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied, establishing a new significance threshold of α = 0.0167. The pairwise comparisons yielded the following results - TE 97 ms vs. MEGA-PRESS: No significant difference was observed (p = 1.0000), indicating both methods produce false results at a comparable rate. TE 97 ms vs. TE 30 ms: A statistically significant difference was observed (p = 0.0033); the TE 97 ms sequence yielded significantly more incorrect indications compared to the TE 30 ms sequence. MEGA-PRESS vs. TE 30 ms: A statistically significant difference was observed (p = 0.0153); the MEGA-PRESS method also produced significantly more incorrect indications compared to the TE 30 ms sequence.

Our comparative analysis of three distinct 1H-MRS acquisition strategies—short echo (TE 30 ms), intermediate echo (TE 97 ms), and spectral editing (MEGA-PRESS)—presents the diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, and technical feasibility in the detection of the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG). We attempted to distinguish the 2HG oncometabolite peak in the spectroscopy spectrum as a marker for the presence of IDH mutations in diffuse gliomas.

Our primary goal was to compare the diagnostic accuracy (the proportion of true versus false indications) of three distinct 1H-MRS acquisition strategies—short echo (TE 30 ms), intermediate echo (TE 97 ms), and spectral editing (MEGA-PRESS)—on a single, well-characterized patient cohort. We aimed to evaluate the clinical utility of these methods for detecting the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG) as a non-invasive marker for IDH mutations in diffuse gliomas.

A key consideration in this study is that the different detection criteria employed (CRLB versus Glx ratios) represent a methodological divergence. However, this difference is dictated by the inherent physical properties of the sequences used, and we deliberately chose this approach to maximize the clinical utility of our study while acknowledging the limitations of each individual method. As established in previous literature, it is standard practice to analyze the optimized PRESS 97 ms and MEGA-PRESS spectra using LCModel. Because these sequences are specifically designed to isolate the 2HG signal (by minimizing overlap or editing the spectrum), we can directly utilize the Cramer-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) as an objective cutoff threshold to determine the presence or absence of a significant 2HG peak.

In contrast, short echo time (TE 30 ms) spectra are routinely acquired as part of the standard clinical protocol for these patients. We wanted to leverage these readily available data to validate all three methods simultaneously without subjecting the patient to additional scanning time. However, due to the specific chemical shift at 2.25 ppm, one of the major resonance peaks for 2HG at TE 30 ms directly overlaps with the Glx complex. Therefore, direct CRLB quantification of 2HG is highly unreliable at this echo time. To utilize the 30 ms data, we had to employ an established indirect method based on the Glx3/Glx4 ratio, as described by van Dijken et al. The only reliable way to infer the presence of the 2HG peak at this position is by comparing this ratio within the lesion to a contralateral reference voxel. False-negative results can still occur if intratumoural 2HG concentrations are below the detection threshold of conventional MRS, a limitation similarly observed in prior literature [15].

To overcome the issue of limited comparability between continuous variables (CRLB vs. Glx ratios) and avoid biasing performance results, we abstracted the outputs of all three methods into a binary diagnostic outcome (Positive/Negative for 2HG). This allowed us to fairly compare the final diagnostic truthfulness (true positive, true negative, false positive, false negative) against the genetic ground truth across all methods within the same cohort. We believe that utilizing the data from all three methods—despite their differing post-processing requirements—significantly enriches the comparative value of the study and reflects real-world clinical scenarios where different sequences might be available.

When comparing our findings with the previously reported diagnostic performance of 2HG MRS techniques, the intermediate echo time (TE 97 ms) method proved to be a highly specific tool. By optimizing the echo time to minimize signal overlap, this sequence achieved a specificity of 100%. While its sensitivity was moderate (51.2%)—aligning with previous reports noting the challenges of 2HG detection at intermediate echo times due to T2 relaxation effects—its high positive predictive value confirms its utility as a reliable confirmatory sequence.

Conversely, the short echo time (TE 30 ms) method demonstrated the highest sensitivity (88.9%) and overall accuracy (92.5%) in our cohort. Historically, short-TE sequences in the literature boast high sensitivity but often struggle with specificity due to complex metabolite overlap. However, by strictly utilizing the comparative Glx3/Glx4 ratio, our TE 30 ms approach maintained a high specificity of 97.0%, demonstrating performance that compares highly favorably with established standard-of-care spectroscopic analyses. The absence of a 2HG peak in diagnostic-quality TE 30 ms spectra serves as a strong marker for IDH-wildtype status, correlating with a more aggressive, high-grade glioma phenotype.

Finally, the MEGA-PRESS method, while theoretically superior for isolating the 2HG resonance via J-difference editing, presented significant translational challenges. It yielded high specificity (86.7%), which is consistent with prior literature highlighting the precision of spectral editing; however, it suffered from a substantial non-diagnostic rate (17.5%). This failure rate is largely due to its known susceptibility to magnetic field inhomogeneities and motion artifacts common in clinical settings. Furthermore, a notable observation in our dataset was the presence of a recurrent, unexplained artifact near 4 ppm—critically overlapping the edited 2HG signal—which accounted for a substantial proportion of the excluded datasets. The etiology of this artifact remains unclear and highlights an important gap in current understanding that warrants further investigation through phantom studies and multi-vendor comparisons.

Consequently, while MEGA-PRESS offers refined spectral resolution, our findings support the prioritized use of conventional PRESS sequences—specifically the high-sensitivity TE 30 ms combined with the high-specificity TE 97 ms—as a more robust and clinically reliable protocol for the non-invasive genotyping of diffuse gliomas.

Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy targeting the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG) represents a highly informative, non-invasive technique that significantly enhances the diagnostic work-up of adult-type diffuse gliomas. Our study demonstrates that while spectral editing via MEGA-PRESS offers theoretical precision, its clinical utility is constrained by a high rate of non-diagnostic acquisitions and susceptibility to artifacts. Consequently, conventional PRESS sequences emerged as the superior approach for routine practice.

Specifically, the intermediate echo time (TE 97 ms) method provides optimal diagnostic confidence due to its 100% specificity and ease of technical implementation, making it an ideal confirmatory tool. Complementing this, the short echo time (TE 30 ms) method is performed in all standard MRS examinations and offers high sensitivity, provided that careful spectral analysis is applied to distinguish the 2HG peak from the overlapping Glx/NAA slope. Clinically, the absence of the 2HG signature across these methods is a critical finding, serving as a reliable surrogate marker for IDH-wildtype status and predicting a more aggressive tumour phenotype. Therefore, we recommend a protocol utilizing optimized conventional PRESS spectroscopy as a robust, widely applicable solution for the preoperative determination of glioma genotypes.
