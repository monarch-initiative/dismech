# Malignant Melanotic Nerve Sheath Tumors: A Review of Clinicopathologic and Molecular Characteristics.
**Authors:** Hammad RM
**Journal:** J Microsc Ultrastruct (2023)
**DOI:** [10.4103/jmau.jmau_5_22](https://doi.org/10.4103/jmau.jmau_5_22)

## Content

Malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumor (MMNST) which was formerly known as melanocytic schwannoma, is an uncommon aggressive type of nerve sheath tumor. It originates from nerve roots with clonal Schwann cell proliferation and melanin pigment production. MMNST which was once thought to be a benign tumor is now considered a malignant disease based on the latest 2020 World Health Organization classification of soft tissue tumors. Interestingly, despite the histologic features appearing benign with a low proliferation index, the clinical course of this tumor is malignant, which was demonstrated in case series with high rate of recurrences and metastasis. This tumor can occur sporadically or in patients with an underlying familial predisposition syndrome called, Carney's complex. Affected patients will often harbor a germline mutation in the PRKAR1A gene. MMNST can be histologically difficult to distinguish from malignant melanoma, other melanocytic tumors, and Schwannoma. Having a better understanding of its clinic pathologic characteristics and associated conditions is essential in properly diagnosing and managing affected individuals. This includes the possible need for genetic testing to detect germline mutations, genetic counseling, and surveillance according to published recommendations. In this article, we summarize the clinic pathologic and molecular features of MMNST and discuss what is known about its molecular biology and its associations with predisposing conditions. The review was conducted through an extensive PubMed search using keywords then relevant publications were selected.

Malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumors (MMNSTs), previously known as melanocytic schwannoma, is a rare tumor arising from clonal Schwan cells with the ability to produce melanin pigment.[ 1 ] They were first described by W. Gilbert Miller in 1932 in a patient with a thoracic sympathetic ganglion. These tumors typically arise from the paraspinal nerve roots or sympathetic chain.[ 2 , 3 ] Other extremely rare primary tumor locations include intracranial, visceral, soft tissue, and skin.[ 4 5 6 ] The median age of presentation is 38 years.[ 7 ] These tumors typically present as a paraspinal mass with symptoms of spinal and/or nerve root compression.

Historically, it has been described as a benign tumor based on the absence of aggressive feature on histologic examination and short-term follow-up clinical data.[ 1 ] Interestingly, over the past decade, multiple case series and long-term data led to its reclassification into a malignant tumor. This was a result of many observations of high incidence of metastases and recurrences, contributing to a poorer prognostic outlook compared to what have been initially observed.[ 8 ] This change is reflected in both the recently published 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of tumors of the Soft Tissue and the 2021 WHO Classification of The Central Nervous System in which, the term melanocytic Schwannoma has been changed to MMNST.[ 9 , 10 ] This change also signifies the recognition of MMNST as a distinct tumor entity, under the umbrella of cranial and paraspinal nerve tumors and is no longer considered a variant of conventional Schwannoma.

MMNST can be a diagnostic challenge owing to the histologic overlap with metastatic melanoma, primary leptomeningeal melanocytic neoplasms, melanocytoma, and conventional Schwannoma.[ 11 ] It is also important to recognize, that a subset of MMNST containing psammoma bodies [Figure 1] is associated with the neuroendocrine syndrome Carney’s complex; a rare autosomal dominant syndrome with a wide range of benign and malignant tumors some of which includes adrenal hyperplasia, pituitary adenoma, cardiac myxomas, and lentigines.[ 12 ] Therefore, obtaining an accurate diagnosis is essential given its distinct clinical course, management considerations, variable prognosis, and possible need for genetic counseling. In this review, we provide an up-to-date summary of MMNST highlighting the clinical, histopathologic, and molecular features in the context of the recent WHO changes.

A PubMed search was conducted, using the terms “Melanocytic Schwannoma,” “MMNSTsm” “nerve sheath tumours,” “Schwannoma,” “Carney’s complex” with “histology,” “pathology,” and “molecular.” Relevant publications were reviewed for clinical data of interest. References from these publications were also reviewed for relevant data. The publications were reviewed to obtain data on pathogenesis, histology, molecular, clinical features, and association with Carney’s complex.

MMNST is a rare aggressive tumor accounting for <1% of primitive nerve sheath tumors.[ 7 ] It affects males and females equally and is often diagnosed in young adults between the ages of 10–40 years. There is no racial predilection as it has been described worldwide.[ 8 , 13 ] Similar to other solid neoplasms, MMNST typically presents with a mass with or without symptoms related to compression of surrounding structures. One of the most common presentations is radiculopathy which was seen in 92% of patients presenting with a paraspinal tumor.[ 3 ]

Radiographically, MMNST has nonspecific magnetic resonance imaging findings. Most tumors on T1-weighted imaging are hypointense, and on T2-weighted imaging are hyperintense with variable degrees of contrast enhancement.[ 14 ]

Despite its low MIB-1 proliferation index and slow growth overtime, MMNST are considered aggressive tumors irrespective of its benign histologic features. This is supported by multiple case series demonstrating delayed metastases and relapses when long-term follow-up was obtained.[ 15 , 16 ] One study by Torres-Mora et al. reported a recurrence rate of up to 35% and a metastatic rate of 44% of the 26 patients with adequate follow-up.[ 17 ]

Owing to the rarity of disease and variability in reported outcomes, there is no standard of care when it comes to the management of MMNST. Nonetheless, the accepted treatment strategy generally involves complete surgical resection with negative margins for localized tumors. Radiation therapy with or without chemotherapy maybe considered for residual disease or unresectable tumors. In addition, long-term follow-up is recommended as recurrences have been documented up 20 years following the diagnosis.[ 18 ]

Since the 1970s MMNST cell of origin was identified as Schwann cells, however, there was controversy around the genesis of melanin in these tumors. Following extensive histopathologic examination, the identification of ultrastructural characteristics and the use of electron microscopy, it has been generally accepted that MMNST is tumors that originate from clonal Schwann cells with capabilities of producing melanin pigment.[ 1 ] Although some reports still consider the combination of Schwann cells and melanocytes as co-contributors given their common neuroectodermal origin.[ 19 ]

Gross examination of MMNST often reveals a well-circumscribed and pigmented tumor in close proximity to a nerve root.[ 7 ] Necrosis and hemorrhage maybe infrequently seen as well as cystic lesions.[ 17 ] Microscopic examination typically displays neoplastic Schwann cells with spindled and epithelioid morphology. These cells are organized in clusters and contain intracellular melanosomes representing the melanin pigment. The cells have a small round nucleus with a small nucleolus.[ 20 ]

Immunohistochemical studies are extremely helpful in securing the diagnosis in conjunction with the histological appearance. MMNST often stain positive for S100, SO × 10 and Vimentin. In addition, they tend to stain positive for melanocytic markers such as HMB45 and Melan-A and negative for Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein, which differentiates them from conventional Schwannoma.[ 7 , 14 , 20 , 21 ] In most reported cases, mitotic activity is low, with a MIB-1 proliferation index of <5%, which, as mentioned earlier, does not necessarily reflect a benign behavior clinically.[ 8 , 17 ]

There are two major histologic subtypes of MMNST, namely psammomatous and nonpsammomatous MMNST. Psammomatous MMNST is associated with carney’s complex, which are neoplastic cells containing psammoma bodies.[ 22 ] These will sometimes be associated with adipose-like cells or vacuolations within the cytoplasm.[ 23 ]

The pathogenesis and genetics of MMNST remain poorly understood, however over the past few years with the advances in molecular diagnostics including high-resolution Next Generation Sequencing techniques, as well as copy number change detection methods, the biology of MMNST is beginning to unravel.

The discovery of the association between carny’s complex and MMNST have led to some insight into the molecular alterations involved in this tumor, namely genetic alterations in the tumor suppressor gene PRKAR1A.[ 12 ] Since then, germline PRKAR1A mutations have been implicated in the pathogenesis of Carney’s complex.[ 24 ] The genetic mutations reported in PRKAR1A include but not limited to, single base pair substitutions, insertions, and deletions. Furthermore, patients with suspected Carney’s complex who were PRKAR1A-negative using conventional methods of detection such as polymerase chain reaction were found to have large deletions in this gene.[ 20 , 25 ] Eventually, somatic PRKAR1A mutations with subsequent loss of function is seen in both sporadic and carney’s complex-associated MMNSTs.[ 26 ]

The PRKAR1A gene is located on chromosome 17q, which encodes a regulatory subunit of the type-1 protein kinase A enzyme. Any genetic alteration leading to loss of function of this tumor suppressor gene product will cause an unopposed catalytic subunit activity, promoting increased cell proliferation in cAMP-responsive cells.[ 26 , 27 ]

In addition, Wang et al. reported that sporadic MMNST exhibited its own unique genomic patterns when it comes to copy number alterations, supporting the notion of it being a distinct entity as opposed to a variant of conventional Schwannoma. Copy number alterations observed in MMNST included hypodiploidy; more specifically, monosomies 1, 2, and 17. That is in addition to the mutations detected in the PRKAR1A gene.[ 26 ]

The use of molecular diagnostics is also helpful in excluding other tumor entities that may resemble MMNST which helps in ruling them out. For instance, GNAQ mutations if detected, are indicative of leptomeningeal melanocytic lesions.[ 28 ] Another important differential to consider is malignant melanoma (MM). MM biology has been well studied, and in histologically ambiguous cases, molecular findings such as TERTp, CDNK2A and BRAFV600E mutations will favor the diagnosis of MM.[ 29 ]

Carney’s complex was initially described by Dr. J. Aidan Carney in the 1980s as a complex constituting cardiac myxomas, spotty pigmentations, and adrenocortical disease, the description involved multiple family members hinting to its heritable potential.[ 30 ] Further studies of multiply affected family members revealed an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance.[ 31 ]

In the 1990s two genetic loci were mapped to chromosomes 17q and 2p.[ 32 , 33 ] The exact gene namely the PRKAR1A gene on chromosome 17q was sequenced in 2000, and it was determined that germline mutations in the PRKAR1A gene are the culprit to this syndrome due to the loss of its tumor suppressor effects when the gene is mutated.[ 24 ] As mentioned above, a wide range of genetic alterations have been reported including large deletions.[ 25 ]

The exact prevalence of carney’s complex is unknown but remains a rare disease despite the increasing number of families being diagnosed over the years due to better diagnostic tools and understanding of this syndrome.[ 27 ] Carney’s complex can be inherited, which is seen in 70% of cases or acquired as a de novo germline mutation.[ 34 ] Among those with inherited carney’s complex, the penetrance observed was 100%.[ 27 ]

Carney’s complex is a multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome characterized by skin pigmentations called lentigines, cardiac myxomas, adrenocortical hyperplasia with Cushing’s syndrome, pituitary adenomas with acromegaly, gonadal, thyroid tumors, and MMNST.[ 12 , 24 , 34 ] There is variable expressivity with a wide range of tumors occurring over the life span of an affected individual. For example, some features manifest in early infancy such as cardiac myxomas and adrenocortical hyperplasia, whereas skin pigmentations are only apparent during the peripubertal period and gonadal tumors manifest in adulthood. Nonetheless, the mean age of diagnosis is 20 years in most cases.[ 27 ] Diagnostic criteria were developed to aid in identifying and diagnosing patients suspected of having carney’s complex, which continues to evolve over the years as more knowledge is acquired.[ 24 , 35 ] Moreover, surveillance recommendations have been proposed and have been shown to improve prognosis.[ 36 , 37 ]

MMNST was first described in patients with carney’s complex in 1990 as a benign nerve sheath tumor.[ 12 ] It is considered one of the rare tumors seen in this syndrome and is thought to be seen in 10% of patients.[ 38 ] The most common primary tumor locations are the gastrointestinal tract and the sympathetic chain but can occur anywhere.[ 34 ] The MMNST seen in carney’s complex is quite distinct as it is associated with the presence of psammoma bodies and adipose-like cell. Moreover, it is often described as heavily pigmented, calcified, with a high incidence of metastatic disease.[ 12 , 37 , 39 ]

The only other genetic syndrome where MMNST have been described is Neurofibromatosis type 1. This has been described in case reports and is exceedingly rare.[ 40 ]

The diagnosis of MMNST can be made with adequate family history, histopathologic examination and immunohistochemistry stains. However, the addition of molecular ancillary testing in ambiguous cases can help in establishing the diagnosis, enable appropriate therapy, and offer surveillance and counseling for affected patients and their families. Over the past few decades, the clinical behavior and biology of MMNST have been better characterized and we now have a much better understanding of this tumor, necessitating its reclassification. Future studies on the genomics and biology of this tumor will help further help understand its behavior, identify clinically feasible diagnostic markers, and potentially therapeutic targets.
