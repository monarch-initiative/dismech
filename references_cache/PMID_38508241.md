# Phenotypes and ontogeny of senescent hepatic stellate cells in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis.
**Authors:** Yashaswini CN, Qin T, Bhattacharya D, Amor C, Lowe S, Lujambio A, Wang S, Friedman SL
**Journal:** J Hepatol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jhep.2024.03.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhep.2024.03.014)

## Content

BACKGROUND & AIMS: Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are the key drivers of fibrosis in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), the fastest growing cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) worldwide. HSCs are heterogenous, and a senescent subset of HSCs is implicated in hepatic fibrosis and HCC. Administration of anti-uPAR (urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor) CAR T cells has been shown to deplete senescent HSCs and attenuate fibrosis in murine models. However, the comprehensive features of senescent HSCs in MASH, as well as their cellular ontogeny have not been characterized; hence, we aimed to comprehensively characterize and define the origin of HSCs in human and murine MASH.

METHODS: To comprehensively characterize the phenotype and ontogeny of senescent HSCs in human and murine MASH, we integrated senescence-associated beta galactosidase activity with immunostaining, flow cytometry and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNAseq). We integrated the immunohistochemical profile with a senescence score applied to snRNAseq data to characterize senescent HSCs and mapped the evolution of uPAR expression in MASH.

RESULTS: Using pseudotime trajectory analysis, we establish that senescent HSCs arise from activated HSCs. While uPAR is expressed in MASH, the magnitude and cell-specificity of its expression evolve with disease stage. In early disease, uPAR is more specific to activated and senescent HSCs, while it is also expressed by myeloid-lineage cells, including Trem2+ macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, in late disease. Furthermore, we identify novel surface proteins expressed on senescent HSCs in human and murine MASH that could be exploited as therapeutic targets.

CONCLUSIONS: These data define features of HSC senescence in human and murine MASH, establishing an important blueprint to target these cells as part of future antifibrotic therapies.

IMPACT AND IMPLICATIONS: Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are the primary drivers of scarring in chronic liver diseases. As injury develops, a subset of HSCs become senescent; these cells are non-proliferative and pro-inflammatory, thereby contributing to worsening liver injury. Here we show that senescent HSCs are expanded in MASH (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis) in humans and mice, and we trace their cellular origin from the activated HSC subset. We further characterize expression of uPAR (urokinase plasminogen activated receptor), a protein that marks senescent HSCs, and report that uPAR is also expressed by activated HSCs in early injury, and in immune cells as liver injury advances. We have integrated high-resolution single-nucleus RNA sequencing with immunostaining and flow cytometry to identify five other novel proteins expressed by senescent HSCs, including mannose receptor CD206, which will facilitate future therapeutic development.

Hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are resident liver pericytes that activate upon liver injury from a quiescent state into myofibroblasts, which are the major cellular source of extracellular matrix, or fibrosis1. Features of HSC activation and their response to injury in liver disease have been well characterized2,3 and their transition to cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) contributes to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) pathogenesis2,3. Recently, single cell technologies have revealed significant HSC heterogeneity, with subsets that have discrete and sometimes opposing functions in injury and cancer4. Accounting for only ~10% of hepatic cells2, HSCs are remarkably pleiotropic and their divergent functions may be explained by these different subsets.

The senescent HSC subset has been proposed as an important source of inflammatory and fibrogenic signals through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which includes expression of IL1A, IL1B and IL65,6 genes, among others. Cellular senescence is a state of irreversible cell cycle arrest in cells that remain metabolically active7. Senescence can be induced by DNA damage, oncogene activation, unfolded protein response, or other stress-mediated signals8,9. Senescent cells exhibit flattened morphology with enlarged nuclei, elevated lysosomal activity, and upregulated senescence-associated genes including GLB1, CDKN2A, CDKN1A, TP53, and SPI110–13.

The exact contributions of senescent HSCs to fibrosis and cancer are debated, with data supporting opposing roles of this subset in liver. On the one hand, senescent HSCs reportedly express less collagen, promote liver regeneration after partial hepatectomy via SASP14 and promote a tumor-suppressive microenvironment through macrophage interaction15, suggesting that HSC senescence could be beneficial and limit fibrosis progression. On the other hand, senescent HSCs have been described as pro-inflammatory through their SASP in obesity-associated experimental liver cancer16,17 and pro-tumorigenic through secreted extracellular vesicles that increase epidermal growth factor signaling18. The contradictory conclusions about the role of senescent HSCs in liver fibrosis and cancer could reflect inconsistent or incomplete characterization of their senescence phenotype, or contributions of different senescent cell subtypes. Therefore, it is vital to definitively establish features of senescent HSCs in human and rodent MASH, in order to establish whether senescent HSCs are a suitable therapeutic target.

Based on the prospect that clearing senescent HSCs could be beneficial, senolytic therapy has been explored to reduce fibrosis and improve liver function in mouse models of chemically-induced fibrosis19. To specifically target senescent HSCs in liver disease, we previously identified urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) as a senescent HSC marker, and demonstrated that clearing this subset by administering anti-uPAR chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells attenuated fibrosis and improved liver function19. Because metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), a subgroup of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), is expanding worldwide, we previously evaluated CAR T therapy in a mouse model of MASH and in toxic liver injury due to carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)19.

Currently there are no FDA-approved treatments for MASH, which is the fastest growing cause of HCC20, and management of MASH is restricted to diet and lifestyle modifications21, which are difficult to sustain22. While there is hope that new agent(s) will be approved soon, no candidate therapies in phase 3 clinical trials have direct antifibrotic activity towards HSCs23, so effective antifibrotics are still needed.

Because features of senescence in MASH have not been rigorously defined, we sought to comprehensively characterize HSC senescence in human and rodent MASH. To do so, we integrated the senescence-associated beta galactosidase (SA-β-gal) assay, the gold-standard for senescence detection24,10,25, with immunofluorescence to localize senescent HSCs in murine and human MASH. We complemented these findings with data from our recent single nuclear RNA sequencing data (snRNAseq)26, allowing us to identify different HSC subtypes, including senescent HSCs in human and murine MASH. We incorporated pseudotime trajectory analysis to reveal the origin and evolution of HSC subtypes, from healthy quiescent, to quiescent precursors of activated HSCs, to activated HSCs, and ultimately to senescent HSCs. Using complementary methods including single cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) and flow cytometry, we also characterized uPAR expression and its cell-specificity during progressive MASH, revealing that uPAR is expressed not only by senescent HSCs, but also by activated HSCs, immunosuppressive Trem2+ macrophages and polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs). Finally, by analyzing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between HSC subclusters, we have identified alternative markers to uPAR that are more highly restricted to senescent HSCs in human and murine MASH livers, including CD206, encoded by MRC1. Our work conclusively establishes the presence of senescent HSCs in human and murine MASH and identifies new, targetable markers to facilitate evaluation of therapies directed at this HSC subtype.

Detailed Materials and Methods are described in the Supplementary Information

To establish the clinical relevance of exploring senescence in MASH, we analyzed patient liver tissue (Table S1). SA-β-gal activity was greater in patients with MASH (Fig 1A–B). We interrogated and subclustered human MASH HSCs from snRNAseq data (Fig S11A) to establish the presence of senescent HSCs (Fig 1C–D, S1A). By analyzing expression of previously published genes4,26–28 and top genes in each cluster (Table S4), we discerned five distinct HSC subclusters – quiescent-like HSCs present in healthy liver (Fig S1B), quiescent precursors to activated HSCs (Fig S1C), activated HSCs, (Fig S1D), and activated and deactivated HSCs (Fig S1D). By examining expression of seven senescence-associated genes (TP53, CDKN1A, CDKN2A, SPI1, IL1A, IL1B and IL6), a senescent HSC cluster was identified (Fig S1E). This senescent HSC cluster comprises 7.05% of captured HSCs in the human MASH snRNAseq dataset (Table S2). Interestingly, this cluster has a gene expression pattern most like the activated cluster and the quiescent precursors to activated HSC cluster (Fig S1A), which both have strong fibrogenic potential, suggesting that senescent HSCs may also be pro-fibrogenic.

Serial sections of patient tissue were analyzed for SA-β-gal activity, or stained for myeloid marker CD68 and HSC marker αSMA (antibodies specified in Table S6 and supplementary CTAT table), revealing that senescent cells were abundant, and did not align specifically with HSC or macrophage/monocyte distribution (Fig S1F). By applying the gene module score of the senescence signature described above (Fig S1E) to all populations, other cells including hepatocytes and endothelial cells had higher senescence scores (Fig S1G). Thus, while senescent HSCs are present in human MASH, and may significantly impact tissue inflammation and fibrosis, there is greater overall expression of senescence markers in non-HSC populations.

uPAR, encoded by PLAUR, is a cell surface protein that we previously identified as a targetable cell-surface senescence marker in HSCs19. Here we further characterized HSC expression of uPAR by co-immunostaining of αSMA and uPAR, which indicated that there are more uPAR+ HSCs in human MASH (Fig 2A–C). Furthermore, while uPAR expression was highest in the senescent HSC subcluster, expressed by ~30% of senescent HSCs, it was also expressed by ~15% of HSCs in the cluster containing mostly deactivated HSCs (Fig 2D), which are known to contribute to fibrosis resolution29, suggesting that antifibrotic HSCs may also express uPAR.

uPAR+ αsma− cells were also observed. Serial staining for uPAR and CD68 identified these non-HSC uPAR+ cells as myeloid-lineage cells (Fig S2A), which is reinforced by human snRNAseq data, where uPAR expression was strongest in macrophages (Fig S2B–C).

In view of our findings that uPAR is only expressed by ~30% of human senescent HSCs, and is also expressed by some deactivated HSCs (Fig 2D), we aimed to identify additional, targetable markers that are more strongly expressed by senescent HSCs, and are not expressed by non-fibrogenic HSCs. Analysis of DEGs between HSC subsets revealed distinct genes upregulated in senescent HSCs (Fig 3A–C). Of the top genes in the senescent HSC cluster, nine are membrane-expressed and minimally present in quiescent healthy, quiescent precursors of activated, and activated + deactivated HSC clusters (Fig 3D). Seven of these genes were expressed by >50% of senescent HSCs – STAB2 (51.72%), PTPRB (72.41%), MERTK (77.59%), MS4A6A (79.31%), FRMD4B (86.21%), MRC1 (87.93%) and SLC9A9 (87.93%). Further analysis determines that these genes are not HSC-specific, and are also expressed by macrophages and endothelial cells (Fig S3).

MRC1, which encodes mannose receptor CD206, was one of the most highly expressed transcripts within the senescent HSC subcluster. To verify protein expression of MRC1 in HSCs, human tissues were stained for αSMA and CD206 using co-immunostaining (Fig 3E). MASH livers have significantly more CD206+ HSCs compared to healthy liver (Fig 3F). While only ~5% of cells were double positive, this finding aligns with snRNAseq data suggesting that only ~7% of human HSCs are senescent (Table S2) and ~90% of them express CD206 (Fig 3D)

To explore these findings in a relevant mouse model suitable for preclinical studies, we utilized the Fibrosis and Tumor (FAT) model (Fig 4A), a diet- and chemically-induced mouse model that recapitulates human MASH30. To gauge the degree of senescence in the FAT model, SA-β-gal activity was assessed at different timepoints. In age-matched healthy mice, few senescent cells were visualized, although significantly more senescent cells were present in aged mice, as expected (Fig 4B–C). At 6, 12 and 29 weeks of MASH, SA-β-gal+ cells were abundant and distributed along fibrotic septa. These FAT model timepoints had significantly more SA-β-gal+ area compared to age-matched controls (Fig 4B–C).

To further explore the degree of senescence at the transcriptomic level, published bulk RNAseq data from whole liver30 were interrogated to assess levels of senescence-associated transcripts from a Hallmark gene set31,32. Of the 78 senescence-associated genes, 74 were upregulated in 24-week FAT liver compared to healthy tissue. Several well-known senescence-associated transcripts like Cdkn1a, Cdkn2a, Spi1, and Trp53 were strongly upregulated, as well as Plaur (encodes uPAR) (Fig 4D). These data establish that murine MASH has elevated senescence compared to healthy mice.

While whole liver bulk RNAseq data was informative to assess general levels of senescence-associated transcripts, we aimed to localize senescent HSCs in situ. Thus, serial sections were stained for SA-β-gal activity and desmin, a standard HSC marker, revealing co-localization of the two, especially at 6 and 29 weeks of FAT MASH (Fig 5A–D).

In a murine model of chemically-induced fibrosis, senescent HSCs influence the hepatic immune microenvironment by recruiting macrophages15. Therefore, we examined macrophage distribution relative to senescent HSCs in situ. Serial sections stained for SA-β-gal activity, αSMA and monocyte/macrophage marker CD68, indicated that senescent cells in murine MASH were most abundant in αSMA+ areas that are close to CD68+ areas (Fig S4). These results suggest that murine MASH livers have more senescent cells, including HSCs that closely interact with macrophages.

To further characterize HSC subsets, we analyzed snRNAseq data from 24-week FAT MASH liver26 (Fig 6A, S11B), where HSCs were subclustered (Fig 6B–C, S5A) to reveal four distinct clusters based on expression of previously published genes4,26–28. These clusters included quiescent-like HSCs in healthy liver (Fig S5B), quiescent precursors to activated HSCs (Fig S5C), and activated HSCs (Fig S5D), which contain some deactivated HSCs (Fig S5E, S5F).

A senescent HSC cluster was identified by examining expression of 7 senescence-associated genes (Trp53, Cdkn1a, Cdkn2a, Spi1, Il1a, Il1b and Il6) (Fig 6D), and comprises 8.0% of captured HSCs in this murine MASH snRNAseq dataset (Table S3). Furthermore, the senescent cluster expressed no Mki67 — the gene encoding Ki-67 (Fig 6E) — and was the most inflammatory (Fig S5G).

To assess the temporal relationship between HSC subclusters, we performed Monocle3 pseudotime trajectory analysis33, which measures transcriptional change from one cluster to the next, and tracks this change as a function of progress along a trajectory. This demonstrated the distinct evolution of HSC subtypes, from quiescent healthy, to quiescent precursors of activated, to activated, and then to deactivated or senescent (Fig 6F).

To understand the processes involved in HSC senescence, gene ontology analysis was performed with PANTHER34 on the top DEGs by the senescent HSC subcluster (Table S5). Of the 14 GO Biological Processes associated with this cluster, 5 are related to inflammation and senescence based on published literature, further strengthening the argument that targeting senescent HSCs may provide an antifibrotic benefit (Fig 6G).

Based on our prior identification that uPAR is a targetable marker expressed by, but not specific to, senescent HSCs19, we evaluated this finding in murine MASH by staining serial sections for SA-β-gal activity and uPAR. There was little uPAR expression in healthy mice, but robust uPAR expression in FAT MASH mice, which overlapped closely, but not completely with the localization of SA-β-gal activity (Fig 7A–B). Whole liver bulk RNAseq data reinforced that FAT MASH stroma has strong uPAR expression compared to healthy liver (Fig S6A). Co-immunostaining of uPAR and Ki-67 and colocalization quantification with JaCOP35 indicated that there was no significant overlap between the two; however, there is a trend of increased uPAR+, Ki-67+ area in MASH liver compared to healthy liver, suggesting that some but not all uPAR+ cells are non-proliferative (Fig S6C–D).

Based on snRNAseq analysis, ~2% of the activated HSC subcluster expresses uPAR (Fig S6B), in addition to ~10% of a macrophage subset, and ~20% of other immune cells (Fig S7A), indicating that uPAR is not restricted to senescent HSCs. Flow cytometry of 24-week FAT MASH liver (antibodies specified in Table S7 and supplementary CTAT table), confirmed that uPAR is expressed by both activated and senescent HSCs (Fig 7C, S12A). To further investigate the spatial and temporal expression of uPAR+ HSCs and immune cells as MASH progresses, co-immunofluorescence and quantification of uPAR and αSMA (Fig 7D–E), desmin (Fig S6E–F) or macrophage/monocyte marker F4/80 (Fig S8A–B) were employed, in addition to serial sections stained for each individual marker (Fig S7B–D). After 6 weeks of FAT MASH, uPAR co-localized with desmin and αSMA but not with F4/80 (Fig 7D–E, S7B, S8A–B). By 12 weeks, uPAR significantly overlapped with F4/80 and was concentrated in a pattern resembling hepatic crown-like structures (hCLS), indicative of infiltrating monocyte-derived macrophages that localize near HSCs and eat dead or dying lipid-laden hepatocytes36,37 (Fig S7C, S8A–B). By 24 weeks, uPAR overlapped with desmin, αSMA and F4/80, in a sinusoidal distribution indicative of HSCs, and in a hCLS pattern (Figs 7D–E, S7D, S8A–B).

To clarify features of uPAR+ immune cells in murine MASH, snRNAseq and scRNAseq data from FAT liver were analyzed. snRNAseq data revealed that uPAR+ macrophages express the immunosuppressive Trem2+ signature (Fig S8C). scRNAseq allowed for more detailed analysis of the immune compartment (Fig S11C), revealing uPAR expression by PMN-MDSCs, Trem2+ macrophages and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) (Fig S8D–F), and that as MASH progresses, there is a reduction in the Trem2+ macrophage and pDC fractions and an increase in PMN-MDSC fractions (Fig S8G).

Because uPAR is not sufficiently expressed by, or restricted to senescent HSCs in murine MASH, we sought other, novel cell-surface markers of senescent HSCs via DEG analysis (Fig 8A–C). This uncovered 22 genes encoding cell-surface proteins (Fig S10C). Of these, seven were expressed by >50% of the senescent HSC subset (Fig 8D), including five genes also significantly expressed by human senescent HSCs (Sema6a in 57.14%, Mrc1 in 60.71%, Stab2 in 67.86%, Ptprb in 75%, and Slc9a9 in 75%) (Fig 3D). In alignment with human findings, most of these genes are also expressed by endothelial cells and/or macrophages (Fig S10A).

Because CD206, encoded by MRC1, was significantly expressed by senescent HSCs (Fig 8E), we evaluated CD206 protein expression. Co-immunostaining showed a significant increase in αSMA+ CD206+ cells in murine MASH (Fig 8F–G, S9A–B). Additionally, flow cytometry of 24-week FAT liver confirmed that CD206 expression within αSMA+ cells is restricted to senescent cells (Fig 8H, S10D–F, S12A–B).

Senescent HSCs are a proposed target for anti-fibrotic therapies, based on their reported roles in hepatic fibrosis, MASH, and HCC development15–17,19,38. This study establishes the origin and distribution of senescent HSCs in a well-validated murine MASH model30 that parallels human MASH, and has uncovered alternative senolytic targets beyond uPAR that are more enriched in, and specific to senescent HSCs.

Our data demonstrate that HSC senescence is almost absent in healthy liver and increases in MASH. Senescent HSCs interact closely with recruited immune cells39,15, likely in response to SASP mediators5,6,40. Based on gene ontology analysis34 of senescent HSCs, we identified several pathways related to elevated inflammation and fibrosis.

The novel analyses of human and rodent MASH snRNAseq datasets26 have yielded new insights into HSC behavior in this disease. For example, clustering analysis definitively identifies an HSC subcluster with elevated senescence-associated transcripts but low Ki-67. Only 7–8% of captured HSCs in human and rodent MASH are senescent, consistent with in vivo studies where senescent cells comprise 2–5% of total cells, but were still sufficient to reduce fibrosis when depleted41. Importantly, we have traced the evolution of senescent HSCs from other HSC subpopulations using pseudotime analysis33, demonstrating that they arise from activated HSCs. Establishing the presence and origin of senescent HSCs in MASH allows us to clarify their contribution to fibrosis and tumor development15–17,19,38, contradicting a report that disputes the presence of senescent HSCs in MASH42.

Our analysis reinforces the heterogeneity amongst HSC subtypes4,26–28,43,44, and clearly defines the origin of activated HSCs from quiescent cells as previously reported4,27. We establish that “deactivated”29,45,46. and “inactivated’4,47 HSCs are the same cell type. Moreover, the “inflammatory” HSC subtype transcriptome43,44 aligns with our senescent HSC cluster, consistent with the inflammatory phenotype of senescent cells.

Interestingly, there is transcriptomic resemblance of senescent HSCs to endothelial cells. These findings align with studies highlighting the existence of endothelial-like HSCs, which are implicated to have roles in MASLD and HCC48–50. This finding is unlikely to represent cellular doublets, because snRNAseq rarely generates doublets, and we further filtered the data with scDblFinder51,52 to remove doublets. Moreover, the flow gating strategy utilized excludes doublets and endothelial cells.

Based on SA-β-gal activity and snRNAseq, hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and other cells displayed higher overall senescence scores. Thus, while senescent HSCs are present in human MASH, there is greater overall senescence in non-HSC populations.

We have also definitively characterized uPAR expression in MASH. uPAR is weakly expressed in healthy liver, but strongly expressed in areas of senescence in MASH. Importantly there is a clear evolution of uPAR distribution during MASH progression; uPAR is expressed by HSCs in early MASH, but transitions to presence in hCLS in late-stage MASH, which are associated with infiltrating CCR2+, Ly6Chi bone marrow-derived monocytes36,37,53.

Based on these data, reduced fibrosis following anti-uPAR CAR T therapy likely results not only from clearance of some senescent and activated HSCs, but also of Trem2+ macrophages and PMN-MDSCs. It is important to emphasize that uPAR is expressed by a minority of senescent HSCs, thus, while targeting these other uPAR+ immune cells may contribute to CAR T antifibrotic efficacy, newer methods could enhance targeting of senescent HSCs via dual specificity CAR T cells54,55 that target an HSC marker combined with a highly expressed HSC senescence marker.

We identify alternate senolytic targets on HSCs that are more highly expressed than uPAR, which include markers encoded by MRC1, SLC9A9, PTPRB, STAB2 and SEMA6A. In particular, MRC1, encoding mannose receptor CD206, is present in ~90% of human senescent HSCs and ~60% of murine senescent HSCs, and is also expressed by sinusoidal endothelium and macrophages. Thus, dual specificity targeting of CD206 and an HSC marker could allow significant depletion of senescent HSCs while sparing other cells. CD206 is a marker of immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages that often promote tumor growth56–58, so there is potential added benefit in targeting CD206+ macrophages but not liver endothelial cells, which maintain architectural integrity and sinusoidal homeostasis59.

There are some limitations of our study. While assessment of SA-β-gal activity, and immunofluorescence for senescence markers were informative, we were limited by lack of some senescence antibodies suitable for co-staining with HSC markers. A second limitation is that the snRNAseq data captures limited time points within the FAT MASH model, which restricted our analysis of senescence in murine MASH. We observe a relative dip in hepatic senescence at 12 weeks of FAT MASH, and it would be interesting to identify the senescent populations at this timepoint. In the future, snRNAseq of additional FAT MASH timepoints will help address this outstanding question, and define the onset of HSC senescence, thereby informing strategies for depletion of senescent HSCs in future clinical studies.

In summary, our data greatly clarify the origin of senescent HSCs in murine and human MASH and their markers of senescence, thereby establishing a more definitive template for developing therapies that target senescent cells in this disease.
