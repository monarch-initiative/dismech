# TUBB2A related epilepsy: novel variants and genotype-phenotype correlation.
**Authors:** Liu W, Chen M, Tang X, Zhu Y, Li Y, Liang L, Wu Z, Jiang Y, Yin Y, Mei F, Zhang Y
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-026-44992-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-44992-6)

## Content

This study delineated the clinical phenotypes of epilepsy patients harboring TUBB2A variants and explored the pathogenesis of novel variants in HEK293T cells. The clinical data of 5 epilepsy patients in our cohort and 23 epilepsy patients from published studies carrying TUBB2A variants were evaluated. TUBB2A-FLAG and TUBB2A-EGFP plasmids were constructed to investigate the functional implications of TUBB2A variants. In 28 patients with epilepsy, the seizure onset age spanned from neonatal period to 8 years old, with 61% experiencing seizures before 1-year-old. The prevalent seizure types were epileptic spasms (32%) and focal seizures (32%). All 28 patients showed varying degrees of developmental delay, and 93% had malformation of cortical development (MCD). All identified TUBB2A variants were missense variants. Three-dimensional structure predictions revealed that 83% of patients diagnosed with pachygyria possessed variants situated near or at the linkage of α-β tubulin. We investigated 4 novel variants and 4 reported variants and found that all 8 variants affected the morphology of spindle apparatus and dynamics of microtubules. Variants located at or near α-β tubulin dimer interface exerted a pronounced influence on the morphology and dynamic of microtubules. Most patients with TUBB2A-related epilepsy experience seizures before 1-year-old. Over 90% of these patients presented with MCDs, and all displayed varying degrees of developmental delay. Patients with TUBB2A variants that perturb tubulin dimer linkage were observed to have a higher proportion of severe phenotypes, such as pachygyria.

TUBB2A is located at chromosome 6p25.2 and encodes a specific β-tubulin isoform. It is highly enriched in the brain and predominantly expressed in neurons.1,2 The TUBB2A protein comprises 445 amino acids and contains a GTPase domain, a two-layer sandwich domain, and C-terminal domain. It plays an important role in cell proliferation, specification, and differentiation. In the brain, TUBB2A is the second most abundantly expressed β-tubulin isoform.1 Its expression is highest in neurons, followed by astrocytes, and is minimal in oligodendrocytes. RNAseq analysis indicates that among all β-tubulin isoforms, only TUBB2A maintains high expression in neurons into adulthood.3 In mouse models, homozygous deletion of Tubb2a results in a slight reduction in cell numbers within both motor and somatosensory cortical areas. Furthermore, the pyramidal fibers of these homozygous mice exhibit reduced staining intensity in the corpus callosum.4.

The phenotypic spectrum of tubulinopathies was first outlined by Cushion et al. in 2013, and is characterized by epilepsy, developmental delay, and complex malformations of cortical development (MCDs).5 These disorders result from dysfunctions in tubulins or microtubule-associated proteins. To date, three types of tubulins and eight types of microtube-associated gene variants have been identified as causative. Among the tubulin genes, TUBA1A, TUBB2A, TUBB2B, TUBB3, TUBB5, and TUBG1, have been linked to MCDs. Notably, Tubulin beta-2 A chain (TUBB2A), a member of β-tubulin subunits, exhibits the highest expression level of all β-tubulins in neurons. Nevertheless, reported cases worldwide remain relatively scarce. This study aims to investigate the clinical phenotypes and potential genotype-phenotype correlations in TUBB2A variant-related epilepsy.

Between November 2019 to August 2023, seven children with TUBB2A variants were enrolled. Among these, five patients with epilepsy were selected for this study. Clinical information including patients’ names, sex, date of birth, perinatal conditions, age at the seizure onset, clinical manifestations, family history, genetic data, electroencephalography (EEG) results, neuroimaging findings, and therapeutic regimens were collected. The patients were followed up either at the pediatric neurology clinic or via telephone. (Tables 1 and 2)

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (approval number 20126), and adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all patients participating in this study.

Research articles published in PubMed and Web of Science from 2014 to 2022, focusing on the term “TUBB2A” and “epilepsy”, have been searched. To date, 14 published articles have reported on 30 patients with TUBB2A-related neurological diseases. Of these, 23 patients with epilepsy have been selected for this study. Their epilepsy onset age, seizure types, treatments, prognosis, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and other clinical data are summarized in Table S1.

Variant screening of TUBB2A (NM_001069.3, GRCh37/hg19) was performed using targeted next-generation sequencing of epilepsy or whole-exome sequencing from peripheral blood sample collected from probands and their parents. The genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) database (http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) was used to retrieve the gene intolerance scores for TUBB2A, specifically the probability of being loss-of-function intolerant (pLI) and metrics for predicted loss-of-function variants (pLOF). All variants were further evaluated using various tools, including the Polymorphism Phenotyping version 2 (Polyphen-2) (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), Mutation Taster server (http://www.mutationtaster.org/), ‌Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner‌ (REVEL) (hthttps://sites.google.com/site/revelgenomics/), and Combined Annotation Dependent (CADD) Depletion GRCh37-v1.7 (hthttps://cadd.gs.washington.edu/download). The Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) data, as sourced from population databases, includes the allele frequencies reported in both the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC) and the gnomAD. The interpretation of variant pathogenicity adhered to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines6. The potential pathogenic variants identified were subsequently validated by Sanger sequencing conducted at CIPHER GENE and MyGenostics (Beijing, China).

HEK293T TUBB2A shRNA knockdown cells and HEK293T scrambled shRNA knockdown cells were obtained by lentivirus infection. The knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qPCR and Western blot (WB) (Supplemental data Fig. 2). Flag-tagged and EGFP-tagged TUBB2A expression vector were constructed through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of cDNA libraries (NCBI website) using specific primers designed to anneal at specific regions. The single-nucleotide changes resulting in the p.Arg2Leu, p.Asp203Glu, p.Val229Ala, p.Arg391His and p.Asn247Lys substitutions were introduced by site-directed mutagenesis (KOD-Plus-Neo, TOYOBO). Refer to supplemental data.

After 24 h transfection, the TUBB2A knockdown HEK-293T cells were treated with 100 µg/ml cycloheximide (CHX) at 37℃ to block protein synthesis. At specified time points (0, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h), the cells were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) Following this, protein level of TUBB2A was detected by WB.

After 24 h transfection, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 30 min, blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin and 0.3%Triton X-100 at room temperature for 1 h, and then incubated with mouse anti-α tubulin (1:5000, RayAntibody, RM2007) at 4 °C overnight. After incubation with secondary antibodies (abcam, Alexa Fluor® 488, Alexa Fluor® 555) at room temperature for 1 h and staining with 0.5 µg/mL DAPI for 10 min, the coverslips were mounted and evaluated with confocal microscopy (Nikon TCS A1 microscope).

The human TUBB2A chain utilized the AlphaFold entry Q13885 as the template for its modeling. InterPro was then employed to predict the protein domains of TUBB2A. Homology modeling was applied to construct TUBB2A as a complex with the tubulin alpha-1B chain (TUBA1B) and the KIF1A motor domain. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) entries 4LNU and 5FNV served as templates for this process. Within the modeled range specified by 4LNU, TUBB2A, TUBA1B and KIF1A exhibited amino acid identity of 98, 99 and 39% compared to their homologs. Similarly, in the modeled range defined by 5FNV, TUBB2A and TUBA1B showed amino acid identity of 99 and 99% with their corresponding homologs.

Each of the in vitro experiments in HEK293T described above was repeated a minimum of three times. Statistical analysis was performed using the GRAPHPAD/Prism 9 Software (SanDiego, CA, USA) and SPSS version 26.0. Protein bands were analyzed and quantified using Imagin J software. Significant differences between groups were evaluated using two-tailed unpaired t-test. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

We analyzed the clinical data of five epilepsy patients from our own cohort and 23 epilepsy patients with TUBB2A variants identified from nine published studies. The age at seizure onset ranged from neonatal period to 8 years, with 61% (17/28) experiencing their first seizure before the age of one. Seizure types among were detailed for 22 of the 28 patients, as summarized in Fig. 1C. Six patients presented with multiple seizure types, such as epileptic spasms (9/28, 32%), focal seizure (9/28, 32%), myoclonic seizures (4/28, 14%), GTCS (3/28, 11%), tonic seizure (2/28, 7%) and absence seizure (1/28, 4%) (Fig. 1C). In addition, six patients (21%) were diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and one patient was reported to have sleep disturbance and self-injuries behavior (Table S1). All 28 patients were diagnosed with intellectual disability/global developmental delay (ID/DD).

The video-EEG findings in all five patients from our cohort showed background slowing. Multifocal discharges were observed in all patients. Additionally, two patients (Patient 2 and 4) exhibited electrical status epilepticus in slow-wave sleep (ESES) (Fig. 1B). Seizures were recorded in two patients, with one patient (Patient 3) experiencing a focal seizure and another (Patient 4) experiencing absence seizure.

93% (26/28) of patients exhibited MCDs, although detailed descriptions were unavailable for three cases. Among the 23 patients with clearly characterized MCDs, microcephaly was observed in 48% (11/23) of them. Dysgyria was nonted in 43.5% (10/23), pachygyria in 26% (6/23), polymicrogyria in two patients, and cortical heterotopia in one patient. Agenesis of corpus callosum (ACC) was present in 74% (17/23), reduced white matter volume in 26% (6/23), and white matter hypomyelination in two patients. Cerebellar dysplasia was evident in 30% (7/23) of the patients, basal ganglia dysplasia in 30% (7/23), brain stem dysplasia in two patients, and hippocampus dysplasia in one patient. Enlarged ventricles were noted in 52% (12/23) of the patients. Two patients had normal brain MRI findings (Table 2; Fig. 1D and E).

14% (4/28) of patients had ophthalmic diseases, including two with cortical visual impairment, one with optic nerve hypoplasia, and one with both conditions. 7% (2/28) of patients suffered from dystonia. 18% (5/28) exhibited dysmorphic features outside the nervous system, such as bilateral epicanthal folds, long prominent ears, and a broad nasal bridge. One patient was diagnosed with Russell-silver syndrome.

The 28 TUBB2A-related epilepsy patients had 20 different heterozygous variants (Tables 2 and 3, and S1). All these variants were missense variants. Four variants (p.Arg2Leu, p.Asp203Glu, p.Val229Ala, p.Arg391His) were novel variants in TUBB2A related epilepsy. We used PyMOL to model and assess the potential functional impact of these novel TUBB2A variants. In silico analysis suggested that variants p.Arg2Leu and p.Arg391His, located at the tubulin dimer interface, potentially impacted the microtubule’s dimer interface. Conversely, two other variants (p.Asp203Glu, p.Val229Ala) within β tubulin did not appear to affect the dimer interface. (Fig. 2B)

Among 20 identified TUBB2A variants, 16 were observed in a single individual, while 4 variants (p.Arg2Leu, p.Gly98Arg, p.Ala248Val, p.Pro357Leu) were reported in more than one patient (Fig. 2A). Variant p.Arg2Leu was identified in two siblings and was inherited from their mosaic mother (Table 2; Fig. 1A and S1). The most recurrent variant was p.Ala248Val, which accounted for 21% (6/28) of the patients carrying the recurrent variants.

Structural localization analysis of the 28 identified TUBB2A variants revealed a distinct distribution pattern. 64% (18/28) of the variants were located at or near the α-β tubulin dimers interface. 4% (1/28) of the variant (p.Arg262His) was positioned at the interaction interface between β tubulin and kinesin, while the remaining 32% (9/28) were situated within β tubulin protein (Table 3). Five variants (p.Arg2Leu, p.Gly98Arg, p.Asn247Lys, p.Ala248Val, p.Arg262His) identified in the six patients diagnosed with pachygyria mapped to either the α-β tubulin dimer interface or the β tubulin-kinesin interaction interface.

Spatial transcriptomics sequencing and single cell sequencing data were used to analyze β-tubulin isoform expression in the human frontal cortex and hippocampus.7 The analysis revealed that TUBB2A is broadly and highly expressed throughout the brain. In contrast, other neuronal β-tubulin isoforms, TUBB2B, TUBB3 and TUBB5, were detected at significantly lower levels, particularly within neurons (Figure S2).

To determine the functional impact of the variants p.Arg2Leu, p.Asp203Glu, p.Val229Ala, p.Arg391His on protein stability compared to the wild type (WT). TUBB2A knockdown HEK-293T cells were transfected with plasmids expressing either WT or variant TUBB2A tagged with EGFP. Protein stability was analyzed by measuring the half-life of TUBB2A after inhibition of new protein synthesis with cycloheximide (CHX). Compared to WT, all four variants exhibited a significantly reduced half-life, with differences evident at 8 and 12 h post-CHX treatment. Two variants (p.Val229Ala, p.Arg391His) showed significant reduced after 4 h post-CHX treatment. These results suggest that TUBB2A variants can compromise short-term protein stability. (Table 4; Fig. 3A)

To delve deeper into the influence of variants on microtubules morphology and dynamics in vitro, C-terminally (EGFP)-tagged TUBB2A plasmids were used to transfect HEK-293T cells. Immunofluorescence analysis was then used to assess the incorporation of the recombinant protein into the microtubule network. Compared to WT, which integrated normally into microtubules, the variant p.Arg2Leu failed to integrate into the polymeric microtubule network and instead remained dispersed in the cytoplasm in a non-polymerized state. In contrast, the other variants exhibited partial incorporation into the microtubule cytoskeleton. (Table 4; Fig. 3B)

We next examined the effect of TUBB2A variants on spindle assembly and mitosis in cultured HEK-293T cells. Expression of variants p.Arg2Leu, p.Asp203Glu, p.Val229Ala, and p.Arg391His frequently resulted in malformed spindles during the M phase. In most transfected cells, microtubules appeared disorganized, radiating irregularly from the spindle poles toward the cell periphery. (Table 4; Fig. 3C).

The variants p.Asp203Glu, p.Val229Ala, and p.Arg391His exhibited delayed reintegration into microtubules compared with the wild type. During the early phase of repolymerization, their incorporation was notably slower, and fully recovery of microtubule polymerization was not observed until 45 min. These findings suggested that these variants impair the efficient incorporation of TUBB2A into elongating microtubules. (Table 4; Fig. 4)

A summary of the in vitro experimental data for our four novel and four previously reported TUBB2A variants is provided in Table 4.3,8,9 These eight variants are situated in distinct domains of TUBB2A protein. Integration of the functional outcomes revealed that all variants perturbed spindle morphology and microtubule dynamics. The severity of these defects correlated with the structural location of the variant. Specifically, variants located at or near α-β tubulin dimer interface exerted the pronounced disruptive effects on microtubule architecture and dynamics. (Table 4)

TUBB2A-related diseases constitute a spectrum of tubulinopathies, including epilepsy, MCDs, DD, and dystonia. In 2014, Cushion et al.8 initially reported two patients with TUBB2A-related epilepsy, MCD, severe global developmental delay (GDD), and verified the function of variants p.Asn247Lys and p.Ala248Val in vitro. To data, 30 patients have been diagnosed with TUBB2A-related neurological disorders.3,9,10.

A review of reported cases from literatures and our cohort reveals a high incidence of epilepsy among individuals with TUBB2A variants (28/35, 80%). Among the 28 TUBB2A-related epileptic patients, the most prevalent seizure types were epileptic spasms (32%), focal seizures (32%), and myoclonic seizures (14%), while tonic seizures and absence seizures were less frequent3,8,9,11,12(Fig. 1C). Compared with epilepsies associated with other β-tubulin genes, TUBB2A variants are linked to a higher overall risk of epilepsy.13–15 This might be explained by the fact that TUBB2A is the predominant β-tubulin isoform in neurons, accounting for approximately 30% of total brain β-tubulin.1,3,8.

Analysis of our cohort and the literature indicated that a high proportion (93%, 26/28) of patients with TUBB2A-related epilepsy exhibited MCDs. The most common types include ACC, dysgyria, and microcephaly (Fig. 1E).3,8,9,11,12 Unlike tubulinopathies linked to TUBA1A and TUBG1, characteristic MCDs such as classic pachygria are less frequently reported in TUBB2A-related patients.10,16,17 analysis of brain RNAseq data by Park et al.3 revealed that TUBB2A is expressed continuously throughout brain development, suggesting it played a role across a broad developmental window rather than during a specific, narrow period.8,18,19 Regarding patients with normal brain imaging, Yuskaitis et al. proposed that subtle malformations might not be detectable on MRI before two years of age due to incomplete myelination.20 However, two of the 28 patients who underwent MRI at the ages of 2 and 5 years still showed normal results.10 This aligns with findings from Bittermann et al., where loss of Tubb2a in mice did not affect survival but can led to relatively mild cortical phenotypes.4 Together, this evidence indicated that TUBB2A variants might cause epilepsy through pathogenic mechanisms that extend beyond gross structural MCDs detectable by MRI.

This study compiled a total of 20 TUBB2A variants in patients with epilepsy, comprising four novel and 16 previously reported variants. All these variants were missense variants, suggesting that TUBB2A is a highly conserved and that large deletions may lead to severe, potentially embryonic lethal phenotypes. However, this inference contrasts with findings from mouse models, in which homozygous Tubb2a deletion is viable.4 The discrepancy may reflect fundamental interspecies differences. The precise role of TUBB2A in brain development requires further investigation.1,3.

Among the four novel TUBB2A variants investigated in this study, p.Arg2 was reported, but the specific amino acid change differed from our patient. Brock et al. reported a patient with p.Arg2His variant.10 The seizure onset in this patient occurred at 10 months. She exhibited focal seizure and febrile seizure during the disease progression, ultimately diagnosed with drug-refractory epilepsy. Her head circumference was below the 3rd percentile upon initial presentation. Brain MRI at 10 months revealed ACC, reduced white mater volume, and enlarged ventricles. She experienced developmental milestones delay, ambulant at 2 years and 2 months, and speaking single words until 3 years and 8 months. Additionally, she presented with facial dysmorphisms and clinodactyly of toes.

In our research, patient 4 and 5 were siblings who both carried the p.Arg2Leu variant, yet their clinical presentations differed markedly. Patient 4 experienced multiple seizure types and four episodes of status epilepticus, leading to a diagnosis of drug-refractory epilepsy. In contrast, patient 5 had only febrile seizures. A similar pattern of intrafamilial heterogeneity was noted in the literature, where three family members carrying p.Gly98Arg variant were reported, only two of them developed seizures.18 Together, this observations indicate that TUBB2A related epilepsy may exhibit phenotypic heterogeneity, even within a family.

Structural mapping revealed that nearly two-thirds of the TUBB2A variants identified in patients with epilepsy are located at or near α-β tubulin dimer interface (Table 3). Analysis of the correlation between variant location and clinical severity showed that all five patients diagnosed with pachygyria carried variants situated at or near α-β tubulin dimer interface or at β tubulin-kinesin interaction interface.8,11,18,21 This points to a potential genotype-phenotype correlation, wherein variants affecting these critical interaction interfaces may predispose individuals to more severe cortical malformations. However, this preliminary association requires validation in larger, independent cohorts.

In Table 4, our study observed that all the variants impacted the morphology of the spindle apparatus and the dynamics of microtubules, with the degree of these impacts correlating to the location of the TUBB2A variants. In vitro observations indicate that certain TUBB2A variants, particularly those located near or within the α-β tubulin dimer interface, can disrupt microtubule morphology and protein stability. These variants appear to have a more pronounced effect on microtubule networks compared to variants located solely within the β-tubulin domain. Based on these cellular findings, we hypothesize that such disruptions might affect the mitosis of neural progenitor cells and neuronal migration during brain development. This could provide a potential mechanistic explanation for the severe MCDs, such as microcephaly and pachygyria. However, directly linkage between in vitro phenotypes to specific in vivo neurodevelopmental outcomes requires further investigation.

The abnormal spindle apparatus observed in vitro might suggest potential mitosis defects in neural progenitor cells in vivo, which could underlie the microcephaly seen in patients. Variants located at or near the tubulin dimer interface perturb microtubule architecture and compromise protein stability. Notably, variants affecting the dimer interface exert a more pronounced disruptive effect on microtubule morphology than those situated solely within β tubulin. Based on these in vitro findings, we hypothesize that variants located at or near α-β tubulin dimer interface may more severely impair neuronal migration compared to variants in β tubulin. It may potentially lead to severe clinical symptoms such as pachygyria.

Certain TUBB2A variants could trigger a reduction in the stability of TUBB2A, consequently affecting its expression during brain development. However, the extent of protein stability decline does not directly correlate with the structural location of these variants. This lack of correlation may stem from the current scarcity of fundamental functional studies on TUBB2A variants.

We acknowledge that this study has several limitations. First, the patient cohort size remains relatively small, which may constrain the statistical power to establish definitive genotype-phenotype correlations. Second, our functional investigations of the variants were primarily conducted in vitro, relying on immunofluorescence and protein stability assays in HEK293T cell models. While informative, these findings necessitate further validation through in vivo studies using animal models to fully understand the pathogenic mechanisms within a complex organismal context.
