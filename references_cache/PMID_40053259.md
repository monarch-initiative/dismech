# The phenotype of "pure" autonomic failure.
**Authors:** Coon EA, Badihian N, McCarter SJ, Sletten DM, Berini SE, Benarroch EE, Sandroni P, Low PA, Singer W
**Journal:** Clin Auton Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10286-025-01121-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10286-025-01121-z)

## Content

PURPOSE: Identifying features of patients who remain pure autonomic failure has implications on disease definition and offers insights into synucleinopathy progression. We sought to determine symptom timeline and autonomic features in patients who retain the pure autonomic failure phenotype with prolonged follow-up.

METHODS: We reviewed all patients diagnosed with pure autonomic failure from 2001 to 2011 evaluated at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, with autonomic reflex screen and over 1 year of in-person follow-up. Clinical evaluations and patient telephone calls were used to assess timeline of symptoms.

RESULTS: Of 202 patients, 133 remained pure autonomic failure with median follow-up time of 9.05 years (interquartile range (IQR) 4.2-13.1). Additional autonomic symptoms included constipation (N = 60; 45%), bladder symptoms (N = 78; 59%), which were severe in 50 patients (37.6%) with incontinence or requiring catheterization, sexual dysfunction (N = 53; 40%) and thermoregulatory dysfunction (N = 51; 38%). Assessment of dream enactment behavior was completed in 86 patients and endorsed in 45 patients (52%). Median time to dream enactment behavior onset from orthostatic hypotension was 7.00 years (1.55-13.50). Other autonomic symptoms tended to occur near orthostatic hypotension. Autonomic testing showed moderate to severe autonomic failure with median composite autonomic score of 6 (IQR 4-8; N = 133) and median percentage anhidrosis of 51% (IQR 3-93%; N = 105).

CONCLUSIONS: Patients with pure autonomic failure typically have symptom onset near development of orthostatic hypotension while dream enactment behavior may occur later. Our findings underscore that not all patients with pure autonomic failure will develop motor or cognitive symptoms, even with prolonged follow-up.

Pure autonomic failure (PAF) is a synucleinopathy, one of several neurodegenerative disorders characterized by α-synuclein aggregation in the central and peripheral nervous system that leads to distinct disorders based on affected neuronal populations, protein misfolding characteristics, and cellular localization of α-synuclein aggregates [1, 2]. Classically, PAF is characterized by predominantly peripheral accumulation of α-synuclein as neuronal cytoplasmic inclusions [3, 4] similar to the neuronal α-synuclein inclusions of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites in Parkinson’s disease (PD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) [1] and in contrast to the neuropathological hallmark of multiple system atrophy (MSA) of oligodendroglial cytoplasmic inclusions [5, 6]. The synucleinopathies share a common theme of autonomic failure with variable manifestations and severity [7] as well as rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD) [8, 9]. The importance of autonomic failure and RBD as early or prodromal phases in the synucleinopathies is increasingly delineated [10] and highlighted as part of prodromal or research criteria for the synucleinopathies [11, 12].

Clinically, neurogenic orthostatic hypotension is the hallmark of PAF, which is diagnosed using the consensus criteria from 1996 [13]. The seminal description in 1925 of three men with orthostatic hypotension with fixed heart rate, constipation, anhidrosis, and erectile dysfunction led to the original eponymous terminology of Bradbury–Eggleston syndrome [14]. PAF is also considered a prodromal state, as it has been well established that a proportion of patients with PAF develop motor and/or cognitive symptoms that leads to a diagnosis of a synucleinopathy such as MSA, PD, or DLB [15–20]. However, a substantial proportion of patients retain the PAF phenotype for years or decades without overt motor or cognitive features. Understanding the clinical and autonomic features of patients that retain purely the PAF phenotype has important prognostic implications and may represent a unique opportunity to study patients with a neurodegenerative disorder who have limited or very slow progression over decades. Therefore, we sought to expand follow-up in our PAF cohort to determine clinical and autonomic features indicative of PAF without phenoconversion.

The primary research questions were as follows: Are there clinical and autonomic function test features that suggest a patient with PAF will not phenoconvert to motor or cognitive involvement in the setting of prolonged follow-up? Do patients with PAF with dream enactment behavior (DEB) indicative of REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) have a different clinical course than those without DEB?

We performed a retrospective review of all patients evaluated by an autonomic specialist with autonomic function testing of autonomic reflex screen (ARS) at Mayo Clinic Rochester between 1 July 2001 and 1 July 2011 who fulfilled the following criteria:

Inclusion criteria included the presence of: (1) orthostatic hypotension (OH) defined as a systolic blood pressure (BP) drop of ≥ 30 mmHg or diastolic BP drop ≥ 15 mmHg during a 5-min head-up tilt; (2) patient age 18–80 years at time of onset of symptoms; and (3) greater than 1-year of clinical in-person follow-up with diagnosis of PAF (without meeting diagnostic clinical criteria for MSA [12], PD [21], or DLB [22]).

Exclusion criteria included: (1) autonomic testing not consistent with neurogenic OH (normal heart rate response and BP recovery time on Valsalva maneuver based on normative data [23]); (2) clinical evidence of central neurodegeneration (Parkinsonism, cerebellar ataxia, or dementia) at initial presentation for PAF; (3) evidence of clinically significant peripheral neuropathy, Adie’s pupils, or prominent upper GI symptoms; (4) medications or medical conditions that could interfere with the results of autonomic testing, including significant heart disease, neck radiation, or chemotherapy; and (5) expert diagnosis not consistent with PAF.

This study extends follow-up from the original cohort [19] and reports phenotypic data from the telephone interview that has not been previously published. All phone calls occurred between 7 September 2016 and 5 January 2018, with telephone script provided (Supplementary). The final review of the charts completed in May 2024. This study was reviewed and approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB). All patients who were interviewed by telephone were mailed consent forms, which were required to be signed and returned.

Patients that fulfilled inclusion and exclusion criteria were considered PAF. At subsequent evaluations, diagnostic clinical criteria were used to make the diagnosis of MSA [24], PD [21], and DLB [22]. The patients were contacted by telephone and following consent and were verbally given a survey regarding development of autonomic, motor, cognitive, and sleep symptoms, including timing of specific symptoms and diagnosis of other neurologic disorders including MSA, PD, and DLB. For deceased patients, the next of kin were queried regarding symptoms prior to death, cause of death, and neurologic diagnosis at the time of death. Phone assessments and clinical chart review were completed in May of 2024. Disease duration was calculated on the basis of time from onset of symptoms until death or last follow-up. Survival data were obtained from the clinical record or from information by phone call with next of kin.

Details regarding clinical data extraction has been published [19] and timed from symptom onset and time of presentation. Bladder symptoms of frequency, urgency, and nocturia were recorded as mild while incontinence, retention, and use of catheterization were recorded as severe. The presence and timing of dream enactment behavior was extracted from clinical documentation. For those contacted by phone, dream enactment behavior was assessed by asking the patient or patient’s bed partner the core question from the Mayo Sleep Questionnaire; if positive, onset and frequency were assessed [25].

Standardized autonomic function testing with ARS was completed on all patients. The ARS tests postganglionic sudomotor, cardiovagal, and cardiovascular adrenergic function [26]. Patients were instructed to discontinue medications for at least 48 h prior to testing that may inference with autonomic function. Quantitative sudomotor axon reflex testing (QSART) was used to evaluate postganglionic sympathetic sudomotor function, which involves iontophoresis of an acetylcholine solution at four sites [27]. Cardiovagal function was assessed using heart rate response to deep breathing and heart rate response to Valsalva maneuver. Blood pressure responses to Valsalva maneuver and passive head up tilt were used to assess cardiovascular adrenergic function [28]. Consensus criteria were used to define supine hypertension and severe supine hypertension [29].

Results from the ARS were used to compute the composite autonomic severity score (CASS). CASS quantifies severity and distribution of autonomic failure through a validated formula consisting of three subdomains: sudomotor (score range 0–3), cardiovagal (0–3), and adrenergic (0–4). Total CASS may range from 0 to 10, with a higher number indicating more severe autonomic failure [26]. The ARS from the time of presentation for PAF was used for analysis and CASS.

The integrity of the central and peripheral thermoregulatory system was assessed using thermoregulatory sweat test (TST). TST involves exposing the anterior body surface and covering with an alizarin red indicator powder that changes from yellow to purple when exposed to sweat. The patient was warmed to a core temperature of 38 °C in a heat chamber. The percentage of anhidrosis was quantified using digital photography [30].

Results from transthoracic echocardiography were obtained, including ejection fraction and presence of left ventricular hypertrophy. The presence of pacemaker was documented.

Laboratory variables collected from the time of presentation for PAF included supine and standing norepinephrine (NE) levels (pg/mL) performed as part of plasma catecholamine testing and kidney function with creatinine.

Summary statistics were used to describe demographic and clinical variables including mean (standard deviation), median (interquartile range, IQR), and frequency (percentage). Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using a Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier estimates for symptom onset to death.

With extended follow-up of 202 patients who initially presented with PAF, two additional patients had phenoconverted (N = 69; 34%), an additional patient was diagnosed with MSA (N = 35; 51%), and one was diagnosed with PD (PD/DLB N = 34; 49%) (Fig. 1). That left 133 patients that remained PAF for at least 1 year. Of those patients that remained PAF, the majority were men (N = 78; 59%) with median onset at 62 (IQR 54–71) years of age. Median follow-up time was 9.05 (IQR 4.2–13.1) years. Of the 91 patients with confirmed date of death, median survival was 14.95 (IQR 11.36–20.40) years with a median age of death of 82.69 (IQR 74.70–86.69 years).

Phone interviews were completed in 56 patients. The average patient age at the time of the phone call was 77.51 years (standard deviation 9.81). An additional 33 patients were contacted by phone but did not agree to be part of the study (N = 5) or did not sign a mailed IRB consent form after two mailed requests following the phone interview (N = 28). An additional 15 patients had been seen within 6 months of the phone call period by an autonomic neurologist at our institution, and there were data recorded on progression. Of the 104 patients with phone call or assessment in the clinical record on progression, 60 (58%) reported worsening over time, 38 (37%) endorsed stability, while 6 (6%) felt that symptoms had improved or became more manageable over time.

Clinical features of patients with PAF are presented in Table 1. Additional autonomic symptoms frequently occurred in patients with PAF. The most common symptoms were bladder symptoms (58%), which were graded as severe in 37% of patients although they were less likely to be graded as severe at the time of presentation for PAF. Constipation was reported in nearly half of patients with PAF and present at the time of PAF presentation in less than a third of patients. Thermoregulatory symptoms were reported in 38% of patients. Overall, only 40% of men had sexual symptoms documented in their chart. Of those men, 87% endorsed sexual dysfunction. Data on sexual function was not captured systematically and only reported in 3 of the 55 women (5%), of which 1 (33%) endorsed sexual dysfunction. Regarding sleep issues, dream enactment behavior was able to be assessed in 133 patients and was positive in 86 (52%) of patients over the course of the study with polysomnogram (PSG) confirmation in 10 patients. Four of those patients were contacted by phone; 2 had concordance between PSG and dream enactment behavior question, 1 patient had onset of dream enactment behavior 6 years after the last PSG (which had been negative for RBD), and 1 patient was deceased and next of kin was not asked about dream enactment behavior based on the phone script (Supplementary). Sex differences were also notable with dream enactment behavior found in 64% of men and 35% of women (p = 0.011), which is slightly less than sex discrepancies reported in isolated RBD [31].

The patient’s initial reported symptom was of orthostatic intolerance in 69 patients (52%). Symptoms were timed to the onset of OH symptoms which showed that additional autonomic symptoms were frequent near OH onset (Fig. 2). Additional autonomic symptoms occurred less than 3 years after OH onset with median time to thermoregulatory symptom onset of 0 (IQR −36, 24) months, bladder symptoms 1.92 (0, 63.6) months, and constipation 0 (0, 54) months later. The median time from OH to sexual symptoms was 0 (−36, 24) months. Median time from OH to dream enactment behavior was 7.0 (1.55, 13.5) years with variability among patients. Of the 26 patients for whom a specific date was recorded for onset of dream enactment behavior, only 5 patients (19%) reported occurrence before onset of OH, with an additional 3 patients reporting concomitant onset of DEB and OH. Of the 45 patients who were still living and completed the phone call interview, sleep apnea was reported in 8 patients (6%) with 3 of those patients reporting use of continuous positive airway pressure. Stridor was reported in 1 of those 45 patients (1%) while cognitive symptoms were reported in 14 (10%) with visual hallucinations in 3 patients (2%).

ARS showed moderately severe autonomic failure with a median CASS of 6 that when calculated with TST percentage anhidrosis rose to 7 (Table 1). Median systolic BP drop at 5 min was 52 mmHg (38–80) while diastolic BP drop was 18 mmHg (8–38). HR rose by median of 11 beats per minute (3.75–18.25) at 5 min of tilt. There were no differences in mean systolic BP drop in women (61.10 mmHg) compared with men (62.41 mmHg; p = 0.834). Supine hypertension was present in 92 (69%) patients; criteria was met for severe supine hypertension in 16 (12%). The presence of supine hypertension or severe supine hypertension did not influence survival (p = 0.540; p = 0.216). While the median percentage anhidrosis on TST was 51, there was variability with interquartile range of 3–93.

Initial catecholamine tests showed median supine norepinephrine of 100 pg/mL (47.50–172.50) with a median increase upon standing levels of 52 pg/mL (19.25–133). Median creatinine was 1.20 (1.00–1.50) at presentation. For the 58 patients with transthoracic echocardiogram, median ejection fraction was 61% (60–65%) with left ventricular hypertrophy noted in 12 (9%). Fifteen patients underwent pacemaker placement during their disease course (11%).

Patients with report of dream enactment behavior (N = 45) were compared with those with no personal or bed partner history of dream enactment behavior (N = 41; Table 2). The 47 patients with no mention of dream enactment behavior in the clinical record (N = 47) were excluded from this analysis. Demographic variables including sex, age at onset, time from onset to death and follow-up time were similar between those with and without documented dream enactment behavior. There were no differences between report of additional autonomic symptoms including bladder, severe bladder, constipation, sexual, and thermoregulatory symptoms. The two groups also looked similar on autonomic function testing including blood pressure drop on tilt and total CASS. Catecholamine levels with supine and standing norepinephrine were lower in patients with documented dream enactment behavior although statistical significance was only seen with standing norepinephrine levels.

In this cohort of patients with PAF with prolonged follow-up, we found that patients may retain a “pure” PAF phenotype for decades. In these patients, widespread autonomic failure is common and tends to manifest around the onset of OH, which is typically the initial manifestation. Dream enactment behavior was typically reported following the onset of orthostatic hypotension. This work has implications on diagnosis and understanding the disease course in patients with synucleinopathies, as well as how the disease may be defined.

One goal of our study was to describe the clinical syndrome of patients with “pure” PAF. Patients tend to initially present with symptoms of OH while other features of autonomic failure, such as genitourinary, gastrointestinal, and thermoregulatory features tend to occur near the onset of OH. The autonomic function data from autonomic reflex screen and TST highlights the severity of autonomic failure in patients with PAF, with substantial blood pressure drop and frequency of supine hypertension. We did not find that the presence of supine hypertension or severe supine hypertension was associated with shortened survival or end-organ damage such as heart or renal failure in this cohort, although that may be due to sampling of laboratory and cardiac testing at the time of initial presentation.

Much work has focused on the phenoconversion of patients with PAF which documents clinical and autonomic features that portend progression to MSA, PD, or DLB [16, 17, 19, 20]. After initially studying this cohort, we suspected that with time, many patients would later develop motor or cognitive features, suggestive of central Lewy body disease, as suggested by earlier studies [17, 20]. Interestingly, by extending the follow-up period, only two additional patients who phenoconverted were captured. With the median follow-up period of nearly a decade in this cohort, this emphasizes the importance of considering PAF as its own disease entity and not solely a prodromal state [12]. Conceptually, PAF may be considered as a manifestation of “body-first” subtype of synucleinopathies [32], with commonly affected sites involving the autonomic ganglia and enteric nervous system [3]. Importantly, the prolonged state of peripheral-only involvement, especially considering those who do not manifest REM sleep behavior disorder, questions whether progression of the prion-like spread seen in the synucleinopathies can stall or whether certain patients manifest protective effects from central nervous system involvement or spread of pathology within the central nervous system. Some of these patients who do not eventually manifest motor or cognitive impairment may not harbor a synucleinopathy, consistent with a published case of Lewy body-negative pure autonomic failure [33].

Studies on PAF phenoconversion have shown that stable patients with PAF were less likely to have dream enactment behavior at presentation [17, 18] with autonomic function and laboratory evidence of peripheral denervation [16, 17]. This led us to question whether patients diagnosed with PAF but without dream enactment behavior were truly patients with synucleinopathies and whether dream enactment behavior or polysomnogram-confirmed REM sleep behavior disorder should be used for diagnosis of PAF. We compared patients with and without dream enactment behavior and found that while patients with dream enactment behavior tended to be slightly older, there were no clinical differences in symptoms reported and autonomic function testing was similar. Of note, standing norepinephrine levels were higher in those without reported dream enactment behavior, which could raise the question of whether some of these patients could have had neurogenic OH due to alternative causes, such as autonomic neuropathy. While the 1996 diagnostic criteria cites reduced supine plasma norepinephrine levels as characteristic of PAF, cutoff values are not well delineated [13].

Our study highlights the complex relationship between autonomic failure and RBD. While both PAF and isolated RBD (iRBD) may represent prodromal alpha-synucleinopathies in many cases, our cohort may represent a unique group of RBD patients. Only 27% of iRBD patients have documented OH, and most are unaware of symptoms [34]. This is distinctly different from our cohort in which autonomic symptoms prompted clinical evaluation, and all had OH first and then developed RBD later. It is rare for patients with iRBD to phenoconvert MSA in large clinical cohorts of iRBD (4% versus 96% DLB/PD) despite the very high frequency of RBD in MSA [31, 35]. Our study would suggest patients with predominant autonomic failure often have autonomic failure before or at the time of RBD symptom onset. Additional supporting evidence for a different RBD phenotype is that our cohort had relatively more women with dream enactment than large cohorts of iRBD (27% versus 18%) [31]. Whether different neuroanatomical trajectories of neurodegeneration are present in patients with iRBD who develop OH versus those who develop autonomic failure prior to RBD symptom onset requires additional study.

This cohort highlights some of the issues surrounding the difficulty in making a diagnosis of PAF, particularly during this study period. While dream enactment behavior could be used an indicator of underlying synucleinopathy, it was rare in our cohort for patients to report preceding dream enactment behavior when presenting for OH, illustrating an issue with relying on dream enactment behavior for diagnosis of PAF. While imaging testing may be helpful, such as cardiac 18F-dopamine PET imaging, which can determine phenoconversion from PAF to MSA, this is not available on a clinical basis [36]. The use of alpha-synuclein biomarkers in skin or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), could improve diagnostic accuracy, which raises the question as to whether the availability of these tests should eventually lead to a revised definition of PAF [37–39]. The biological criteria recently proposed for synucleinopathies do not capture PAF well, either subsuming it under Parkinson’s disease [40] or considering it as a mild clinical form [41]. The movement and autonomic communities would be best served by updating a consensus of the clinical definition of PAF, while increasing the number of patients with biomarker testing to better inform biological criteria.

Limitations to our study include the use of a single tertiary center contributing to referral bias and limit generalizability. A high percentage of patients were deceased which could lead to bias based on reliability of reported findings from interviewing next of kin. Additionally, standardized and validated scales were not used in the phone interview, which limits data on sensitivity and specificity of the phone survey results. We relied on self-reported history of dream enactment behavior for RBD diagnosis and did not have polysomnographic confirmation of REM sleep without atonia to confirm RBD diagnosis in most patients. The percentage of patients with dream enactment behavior was lower than anticipated and may be related to our study methodology but also was likely influenced by the evolution in understanding the importance of the association between REM sleep behavior disorder and synucleinopathies, which occurred in the latter portion of study period. Dream enactment behavior may have been less likely to be assessed or documented at the start of the study period, which began in 2001. While the length of follow-up was long for many patients, follow-up by phone call may have limited the ability to pick up motor or cognitive features that would lead suggest phenoconversion. Additionally, patients who phenoconverted to MSA, PD, or DLB may be more likely to be lost to follow-up which leads to selection bias.

In summary, not all patients with PAF will phenoconvert into MSA, PD, or DLB even during prolonged follow-up. While it is of utmost important to capture the earliest stages of a synucleinopathy, PAF may remain in its “pure” form. With better understanding of PAF and the use of biomarkers for detection of synucleinopathies, the scientific community will need to discuss the right timing for a revised definition of PAF.
