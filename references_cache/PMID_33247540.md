# A de novo pathogenic BMP2 variant-related phenotype with the novel finding of bicuspid aortic valve.
**Authors:** Ahluwalia N, Gelb BD
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet A (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmg.a.61992](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmg.a.61992)

## Content

A rare autosomal dominant syndrome with craniofacial dysmorphisms, skeletal abnormalities, short stature, and congenital heart defects has recently been described, associated with monoallelic truncating and frameshift bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) variants and deletions. We describe a patient harboring a novel de novo BMP2 nonsense variant, who exhibited craniofacial and skeletal features previously described for this trait and the novel findings of bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) and aortic root and ascending aortic aneurysm. This first instance of aortic valve involvement provides another potential cause of BAV and confirms the role of BMP2 in left ventricular outflow development.

Bone morphogenic proteins (BMP) belongs to the class of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) superfamily of secreted proteins involved in numerous cellular functions including prominent roles in musculoskeletal and cardiac development. Previous reports have described a craniofacial and skeletal phenotype along with cardiac malformations in patients with loss of function of BMP2 (T. Y. Tan et al., 2017). Distinctive facial features included broad forehead, a long philtrum, a thin upper lip, crowded dentition, and a cleft or high-arched palate along with commonly associated skeletal anomalies of short stature, fifth finger clinodactyly, and wide sandal gap.

While alterations of Bmp2 in mouse models leading to heart defects have firmly established Bmp2’s role in endocardial cushion development, its role in semilunar valvulogenesis is less clear (Délot, Bahamonde, Zhao, & Lyons, 2003; Sugi, Yamamura, Okagawa, & Markwald, 2004). Here, we describe the first case of aortic valve involvement in a patient with pheno-typical traits similar to the described syndrome seen with BMP2 mutation.

The patient, who is male, was born at full term weighing 6 lb 1 oz. During the initial few years of life, he was noted to have hypotonia and feeding difficulties. Physical exam was notable for facial dysmorphic features with downslanting, wide palpebral fissures, and hypertelorism. Genetic evaluation showed a normal 46, XY karyotype. The hypotonia gradually improved over the first few years of life with occupational and physical therapy. He developed short stature, underwent an endocrinology work-up, and was treated with growth hormone (8–13 years of age). Skeletal survey at 10 years 7 months of age revealed a bone age of 10 years, which was within 2 SDs of his chronological age. Other skeletal abnormalities included progressive thoracic scoliosis, which required surgical intervention at 13 years of age. He was referred to cardiology for evaluation of palpitations at 3 years of age, and an electrocardiogram showed evidence of preexcitation consistent with Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome (WPW). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a bicommissural aortic valve (BAV) due to fusion of the left–right commissure with mild aortic regurgitation. The left ventricular function was normal. As the episodes of palpitations were infrequent and given his age, the decision was made to defer ablation until he was older.

During his childhood years, the patient’s physical exam was notable for prominent pectus excavatum, joint laxity, velvety soft skin without atrophic scars or transparency, the previously known facial dysmorphia with downslanting, wide-palpebral fissures, hypertelorism, large corneas, prominent eyes, and a long, flat philtrum. These physical findings in the presence of BAV and aneurysm suggested an underlying connective tissue disorder. Clinical molecular genetic testing was performed to look for variants in COL3A1 underlying Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, vascular type as well as the two genes known at the time to underlie Loeys–Dietz syndrome, TGFBR1 and TGFBR2, but no causal variant was identified.

The patient was monitored annually with echocardiograms for progression of the cardiovascular involvement. Slow progressive dilation of aortic root and ascending aorta was noted without aortic stenosis and, at most, mild aortic insufficiency (Figure 1).

Episodes of palpitations became more frequent at 13 years of age. Given the underlying WPW, he was referred for an electrophysiology study for risk stratification. His accessory pathway, which was left sided, was found to have a shortest conducted R–R interval of greater than 250 ms. Radiofrequency ablation was successful.

Due to the high suspicion of a genetic trait, clinical whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on the peripheral leukocyte genomic DNA from the patient and his parents. This reveal the presence of a de novo BMP2 c.313 C>T (p.R105X) variant in the patient, which was deemed pathogenic using the American College of Medical Genetics criteria. There were no additional de novo variants identified on the clinical WES. Rereview of the medical history and reexamination of the patient in light of the molecular diagnosis revealed some additional shared features previously associated with BMP haploinsufficiency, including the facial dysmorphic gestalt, dental crowding requiring extractions, high-arched palate, pectus excavatum, dysplastic nails (toes > fingers), and a sandal gap bilaterally.

The human BMP2 gene, located on chromosomal region 20p12.3, encodes a 396-amino acid secreted ligand belonging to the TGF-β superfamily of proteins. In our patient, a nonsense variant, which, if transcribed, would generate a truncated protein lacking the last 292 amino acids. In 2017, Tan and colleagues described a syndrome caused by loss-of-function BMP2 variants identified in 12 individuals from 8 families, which matched well with 2 prior reports of a total of 6 individuals from 3 families harboring genomic deletions that removed only BMP2 (T. Y. Tan et al., 2017). Our patient shared many of the distinctive craniofacial and skeletal features associated this trait (Table 1). Of note, most of the previously reported probands have had cleft palate or Robin sequence, whereas our patient had only a high-arched palate. In the series of eight families harboring BMP2 loss-of-function variants, 5 of 12 affected individuals had cardiac malformations, including Ebstein anomaly, transposition of the great arteries, ventricular septal defects, and pulmonary valve stenosis. Our patient was noted to have BAV and aortic root aneurysm, neither of which has not been reported previously in association with BMP2 haploinsufficiency. Cardiac arrhythmias (WPW syndrome, paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, and nonspecific palpitations) were observed in one-third of the previously reported individuals and also present in our patient.

BAV is the most common congenital heart defect with a prevalence of 1–2% and is highly heritable (Cripe, Andelfinger, Martin, Shooner, & Benson, 2004; Galian-Gay et al., 2019). BAV is often accompanied by thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA). While TAA was previously ascribed to the effects of abnormal blood flow patterns above the BAV, its presence in individuals with BAV with laminar postvalve blood flow and the increased rates of aortic dilation among first-degree relatives of probands with BAV have strongly suggested that the ascending aortic abnormalities can arise independently of the aortic valve developmental defect (Galian-Gay et al., 2019). In our patient, there was minimal blood flow disturbance due to the BAV so we speculate that the BMP2 haploinsufficiency may be directly responsible for his ascending aortic dilation. BAV presents primarily as an isolated anomaly but can also occur in the context of genetic syndromes (e.g., familial patent ductus arteriosus, BAV, and hand anomalies [OMIM: 604381], Adams–Oliver syndrome, Williams–Beuren syndrome). Causal genetic variation associated with BAV has been described in NOTCH1, SMAD6, MAP3K7, TAB2, NKX 2.5, GATA6, and several other genes, although, collectively, they account for a very modest percentage of BAV cases (AbuBakr, Jeffries, Ji, McGrath, & Lakhani, 2020; Garg et al., 2005; Gharibeh et al., 2018; Gillis et al., 2017; McKellar et al., 2007; Mohamed et al., 2006; Prall et al., 2007; Qu et al., 2014; H. L. Tan et al., 2012; Thienpont et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2018). BMP2, which encodes a protein belonging to a subgroup of the TGF-β superfamily, which is implicated in cardiac development, including for valvulogenesis. In the developing atrioventricular canal and outflow tract myocardia, BMP2 and other TGFβ genes (e.g., BMP4 in the outflow tract), limited in their expression domains by NOTCH signaling in the underlying endocardium, drive development of the endocardial cushions, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT; MacGrogan, Luna-Zurita, & de la Pompa, 2011; Wang, Greene, & Martin, 2011). While mouse models altering Bmp2 expression have generally resulted in atrioventricular canal defects, it is interesting that the congenital heart disease observed in patients with BMP2 haploinsufficiency include Ebstein anomaly, a specific defect of tricuspid valve development, and BAV. It is also notable that five genes associated with BAV have biologically related roles in valvulogenesis: NOTCH1, BMP2, SMAD6, which encodes an inhibitor of TGF-β receptor signaling that limits EMT, MAP3K7, which encodes TGF-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1), an activator of signaling downstream from TGF-β and TAB2, which encodes TAK1 binding protein 2 that activates TAK1 (Desgrosellier, Mundell, McDonnell, Moses, & Barnett, 2005; Thienpont et al., 2010). In cardiac development, the myocardium at the atrioventricular and outflow junctions secretes a thickened extracellular matrix (ECM) in the atrioventricular region and outflow tract (Person, Klewer, & Runyan, 2005). Endocardial cushions are formed by delamination and migration of the endocardial cells into to ECM and transformation into mesenchymal cells. BMP2 signaling is essential in EMT of the atrioventricular cushion endocardium, which eventually remodels into the cardiac valves. Cardiac-specific loss of Bmp2 impairs endocardial EMT in the heart-valve forming region (Rivera-Feliciano & Tabin, 2006). Studies have further elucidated the role of Alk2-mediated BMP signaling in cardiac valve development, with mutant mice with deleted Alk2 function in the cushion mesenchyme after EMT developing aortic valve defects characterized as “functional” BAV (Thomas, Sridurongrit, Ruiz-Lozano, & Kaartinen, 2012).

In summary, we describe a patient with the syndrome caused by BMP2 haploinsufficiency. In addition to confirming features previously described for this trait, our patient exhibited BAV and ascending aortic aneurysm. This finding highlights the importance of BMP2 in semilunar valvulogenesis and adds BMP2 to a growing list of biologically related genes underlying the pathogenesis of BAV.
