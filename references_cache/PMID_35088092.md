# Mouse Knockout Models for Pelvic Organ Prolapse: a Systematic Review.
**Authors:** Allen-Brady K, Bortolini MAT, Damaser MS
**Journal:** Int Urogynecol J (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00192-021-05066-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00192-021-05066-5)

## Content

INTRODUCTION AND HYPOTHESIS: Mouse knockout (KO) models of pelvic organ prolapse (POP) have contributed mechanistic evidence for the role of connective tissue defects, specifically impaired elastic matrix remodeling. Our objective was to summarize what mouse KO models for POP are available and what have we learned from these mouse models about the pathophysiological mechanisms of POP development.

METHODS: We conducted a systematic review and reported narrative findings according to PRISMA guidelines. Two independent reviewers searched PubMed, Scopus and Embase for relevant manuscripts and conference abstracts for the time frame of January 1, 2000, to March 31, 2021. Conference abstracts were limited to the past 5 years.

RESULTS: The search strategy resulted in 294 total titles. We ultimately included 25 articles and an additional 11 conference abstracts. Five KO models have been studied: Loxl1, Fbln5, Fbln3, Hoxa11 and Upii-sv40t. Loxl1 and Fbln5 KO models have provided the most reliable and predictable POP phenotype. Loxl1 KO mice develop POP primarily from failure to heal after giving birth, whereas Fbln5 KO mice develop POP with aging. These mouse KO models have been used for a wide variety of investigations including genetic pathways involved in development of POP, biomechanical properties of the pelvic floor, elastic fiber deposition, POP therapies and the pathophysiology associated with mesh complications.

CONCLUSIONS: Mouse KO models have proved to be a valuable tool in the study of specific genes and their role in the development and progression of POP. They may be useful to study POP treatments and POP complications.

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is the descent of one or more of the following: anterior vaginal wall, posterior vaginal wall, the uterus, or the apex of the vagina [1]. POP is a significant health problem for women as the cause of bladder, bowel and sexual dysfunctions [2]. The lifetime risk for surgery to treat POP is 12–19% [3, 4], and severe POP can significantly affect quality of life [5].

POP has a multifactorial pathology. Pregnancy and vaginal childbirth are the major risk factors for POP, but others such as aging, menopause, obesity, neuropathies, ethnicity, family history and genetic predisposition, separately or collectively, may play a role in POP development [6–10]. It is widely believed that POP results from failure of the structures supporting the pelvic floor (PF) and pelvic organs: the connective tissue in the form of ligaments, endopelvic fascia, the levator muscles and the smooth muscle fibers of the vaginal wall [11]. PF tissues of women with POP are known to have structural, histological, molecular and functional differences from women without POP [12–17].

Accumulating evidence supports that connective tissue defects, inherited or acquired, particularly in collagen and elastin proteins in the extracellular matrix (ECM), may be contributing factors to POP [18–20]. It has long been known that women with concurrent joint hypermobility, particularly at the elbow, have a higher prevalence of cystocele, rectocele and uterine/vault POP [21, 22], as well as bladder neck mobility [23]. In addition, women with Marfan’s syndrome or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, both connective tissue disorders, have higher rates of POP than the general population [24].

POP is understood to be a polygenetic condition [25]. Nonetheless, mouse knockout (KO) models can serve as monogenic models, in which a specific gene is inactivated, resulting in POP.

The first genetic mouse model for POP was published in 2004 [26], approximately 10–20 years after the association between joint hypermobility and POP was established [21, 22]. Liu et al. were investigating the role of lysyl-oxidase like-1 (LOXL1) in elastogenesis through development of a Loxl1 KO mouse [26] when they unexpectedly found that disruption of elastic fiber homeostasis with LOXL1 deficiency leads to POP [27]. The first genome-wide genetic linkage study for POP in humans was published 3 years later [28].

Genetic manipulation studies are typically done with mice because they are amenable to the technology, have a relatively short lifespan and have a predictable and short estrous cycle. Because they are relatively inexpensive and are easy to handle, large numbers of subjects can be obtained and studied. Normal wild-type (WT) mice have fully functional genes of interest; in contrast, KO mice have a target gene that is permanently inactivated in every cell (i.e., there is no gene expression of the target gene). Mouse models of POP typically involve null mutations (−/−) in genes associated with ECM fiber synthesis and assembly. However, anatomical differences exist between rodents and humans including a horizontal pelvic floor, smaller fetal head-to-birth canal ratio, quadruped mobility and the musculature of the mouse pelvic floor being situated dorsally to control the tail [29].

Mouse KO models have contributed mechanistic evidence for the role of ECM defects to POP, particularly as they involve impaired elastic matrix remodeling [28, 29]. Among the enzymes responsible for elastin fiber assembly are LOX, LOXL1, fibulin-3 and fibulin-5 (Fig. 1).

We believe that mouse KOs have played important roles in understanding critical contributing factors to POP, such as aging and mode of delivery, as well as the relationships between those factors in maintaining homeostasis of vaginal ECM [30]. The objective of this study was to systematically review the literature on existing mouse KO models used to study POP and to provide a narrative review of the findings to advance understanding of POP etiology and pathophysiology.

This review was structured according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement. The research question was: “What mouse KO models for POP are available, and what have we learned about the pathophysiological mechanisms of POP development?”

A systematically complete computerized literature search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus and Embase databases to identify mouse KO models for POP; all records were independently screened by two authors (KAB and MD) by reading the title and abstract or both of every record. Any discrepancy was solved through consensus.

In PubMed, we used the following search terms: (prolapse OR “pelvic organ prolapse”[MeSH] NOT mitral NOT carcinoma [Title] NOT cancer [Title]) AND ((“animal knockout”) OR (animal AND genetic) or mouse). For the Embase and Scopus search we used the following search terms: “mouse AND pelvic organ prolapse.” The search timeframe was from January 1, 2000, to March 31, 2021, restricted to English language articles. Relevant conference abstracts were limited to the past 5 years (January 1, 2016, to March 31, 2021). We also manually reviewed the reference lists of original articles to identify further relevant publications. A manual search was used to eliminate duplicate reports.

We included any experimental KO mouse in which POP was studied, either naturally occurring or provoked, that report the effects on the vagina, bladder, urethra or the pelvic floor support apparatus, either qualitative or quantitative. Exclusion criteria were KO mouse reports on the effect of other organs and tissues, review articles, case reports, commentaries or letters to the editor.

All potentially relevant articles were assessed as full-text and checked for agreement. The information from these studies was tabulated according to the age of the animals, silenced gene, tissue evaluated, outcome measures and main findings.

Given the heterogeneity of the studies and outcomes, it was not possible to perform a meta-analysis and standard risk-of-bias assessment. With that, all studies were appraised for reporting as a narrative review.

The search strategy resulted in 294 total titles and abstracts after removing duplicates, while 128 out of them were selected for full-text review. We ultimately included 25 articles and an additional 11 conference abstracts, after excluding 92 publications that did not meet the eligibility criteria (Fig. 2). Table 1 lists the included publications, their characteristics and main findings of research on KO mice related to POP.

Five mouse KO models have been demonstrated to show evidence of deficiency of pelvic tissue integrity and POP. Most of these involve deficiency of enzymes involved in ECM metabolism and/or repair, implicating pathologies of ECM development and repair pathways in POP. The two KO models that provide the most reliable and predictable POP phenotype, the Lysyl Oxidase-Like 1 (Loxl1) KO and the Fibulin 5 (Fbln5) KO mouse models, involve disruption of elastin assembly and/or crosslinking, implicating elastin metabolism specifically in development of POP.

The lysyl oxidase (LOX) family of enzymes consist of five known copper-dependent monoamine oxidases: LOX and LOXL1–4 [26, 31]. LOX and LOXL1 are secreted as inactive pro-enzymes which interact directly with the ECM, directing their deposition onto elastic tissues. LOX mediates crosslinking of collagen as well as elastin, whereas LOXL1 is more specific for crosslinking elastin [32]. The gene lysyl oxidase like 1 (Loxl1) encodes for the LOXL1 enzyme, which is required for polymerizing tropoelastin monomers into elastin polymers to generate mature elastin fibers [33].

LOXL1 is active primarily in elastin homeostasis and matrix remodeling after injury, fibrosis and cancer development [31] and is essential for the postnatal deposition of elastic fibers during the massive remodeling process that the female reproductive organs undergo through pregnancy, birth and postpartum [26]. Experimental studies have reported physiological variation of LOXL1 levels in mice uterine cervix through the reproductive cycle [27]. LOXL1 expression dramatically reduces just before parturition and perinatally but returns to baseline 6 days postpartum [27]. LOXL1 expression also decreases in the reproductive tract with aging and with cessation of reproductive activity [27].

We identified 12 manuscripts published in peer-reviewed journals and 6 additional abstracts presented in conferences addressing Loxl1 KO mice and POP (Table 1).

Mice with ablation of Loxl1 are viable and can procreate. Overall, this model strongly demonstrates that LOXL1 has an important role in elastic fiber homeostasis in adult tissues. Mice with deficiency of Loxl1 develop diverse systemic pathologies that can be attributed to elastic fiber defects including emphysematous lungs, loose and redundant skin, intestinal diverticula, rectal prolapse and vascular abnormalities, in addition to POP [26, 32].

POP presents in Loxl1 KO mice as a permanent bulge in the urogenital area [26, 34]. POP in parous Loxl1 KO mice involves descent of the uterus, bladder or vaginal tissues into a distended lower vaginal cavity [34]. The vaginal wall circumference from Loxl1-deficient mice is much larger than in WT mice and is thin and fragile [27].

Loxl1 KO mice develop POP spontaneously but slowly in nulliparous mice, while WT animals do not develop POP during their lives even after parturition, aging or estropause [27]. Discrepant rates of POP in nulliparous Loxl1 KO mice across the lifetime range from 15% [34] to 44% [35] and may be explained by age at evaluation, environment or differential genetic background.

Anatomically, Loxl1 KO mice have wider vaginal diameters [35]. Histologically, the subepithelial and muscularis layers of the vagina are disorganized, with areas of disruption, and of variable thickness not observed in WT mice [35]. In response to downward distension along the longitudinal axis of the vagina, a 31% decrease in load at failure was detected in vaginal and supportive tissues from Loxl1-deficient mice compared to age-matched WT animals, suggesting that tissues from Loxl1 KO mice are weaker [35].

The natural weakening of connective tissues due to the absence of Loxl1 was also evidenced in vitro [36]. Single smooth muscle cells from vaginas of Loxl1 KO mice in culture showed decreased surface adhesion, but increased stiffness compared to WT cells. However, confluent cells from vaginas of Loxl1 KO mice as a monolayer are significantly more compliant than cells from WT mice, as evidenced by decreased stiffness, and produce less collagen and elastin, which impacts the cytoskeletal structure [36].

Changes in other key molecules for vaginal ECM homeostasis were studied with Loxl1 KO mice [37]. There is a reduction in elastin synthesis, while the ratio of matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9) and tissue inhibitors of MMPs-1 (TIMP-1) is increased in in vitro culture of non-epithelial vaginal cells from nulliparous mice lacking Loxl1 compared to WT cells, reflecting the decreased production of TIMP-1 by fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells [37]. In addition, Loxl1 KO mice cells showed altered relative expression of several clusters of genes involved in synthesis and degradation of the ECM, reinforcing that those tissues are of different composition and regulation [37].

Morphometric analyses of elastic fibers in vaginal tissue culture showed structural changes, including a reduced aspect ratio of the elastic fibers (ratio of the major to the minor axes) isolated from multiparous Loxl1 KO mice compared to multiparous WT mice, providing insights that not only the quantitative but also qualitative aspects of the elastic fibers may contribute to the pathophysiology of PF disorders [38].

There is a strong body of evidence showing that POP in Loxl1 KO mice is primarily driven by injury during vaginal pup delivery [26]. Differences in expressions and concentrations of inflammatory cytokines [e.g., chemokine (C–C motif) ligand (Ccl7) and chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 12 (Cxcl12)] have been shown to be altered in response to tissue injury in the vagina, urethra, bladder and rectum in pregnant and post-partum Loxl1 KO mice compared to WT mice, suggesting that these mice are more prone to childbirth injury particularly to the urethra and vagina [39]. Indeed, 50% of parous animals develop POP at 25 weeks of age. Approximately 1/3 of Loxl1 KO mice develop POP after the first litter and another third after the second litter. With increased parity, the age at POP presentation decreases [34]. Multiparous Loxl1 KO mice develop POP at nearly three times the rate of primiparous Loxl1 KO mice [40, 41].

One week postpartum, the uterus of Loxl1 KO mice shows fragmented elastin polymers and loss of elastic fibers; the uterine cervix is stretched to several times the normal size, consistent with a loss of tissue resilience [26]. Elastin polymerization defects with tropoelastin accumulation in Loxl1 KO mice are more pronounced in uterine tissue from older postpartum mice than from young virgin mice [26]. Old multiparous Loxl1 KO mice have wider vaginas, with total vaginal area 150% larger than age- and parity-matched WT mice [26]. This effect was similar in the vaginal muscularis and submucosal tissue [42]. These findings suggest the inability of Loxl1-deficient mice to restore normal elastic fibers during connective tissue remodeling of the reproductive tract after pregnancy and birth [26].

Cesarean delivery reduces and delays but does not eliminate the development of POP in Loxl1 KO mice [43]. Approximately 60% of Loxl1 KO mice develop moderate/severe POP by 7.2 weeks after spontaneous vaginal delivery, whereas only 31% had POP by 10.5 weeks after cesarean delivery [43].

Additional studies have shown the distinct behavior of vaginal ECM regulation after vaginal birth trauma in Loxl1 KO mice during the tissue remodeling process postpartum [37, 44]. A number of ECM genes are differentially expressed between Loxl1 KO and WT mice during the puerperium, as evidenced by the persistent upregulation of Bmp1 in the vagina of KO mice late postpartum and the lack of co-expression with other ECM genes seen in WT animals. In addition, the gene expression profile suggests reduced fibroblastic activation but increased proteolytic activity of MMPs in the vaginas of Loxl1 KO compared to WT mice.

In vitro cultures of cells harvested from Loxl1 KO mice demonstrate that elastin synthesis increases with parity as evidenced by higher amounts of elastin, cell proliferation in the elastin matrix and increased transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) in cultures of non-epithelial vaginal cells from multiparous Loxl1 KO mice compared to cells from Loxl1 KO nulliparous mice [37]. Also, MMP regulation appears to be crucial for the postpartum remodeling process as cells from multiparous Loxl1 KO mice expressed significantly more Mmp2 and Bmp1 and less Mmp9 than vaginal cells from nulliparous KO mice. These findings may influence the turnover of elastin fibers and the ability of ECM to properly and fully repair itself after delivery, ultimately leading to POP [37]. This link between pathological molecular processes and POP is consistent with demonstration of altered mechanical properties in the vagina. Overall vaginal tissue stiffness and extensibility increase postpartum, but persist in KO mice after puerperium, which is not observed in WT mice, confirming an insufficiency of the process of tissue recovery from birth trauma in Loxl1 KO mice [45].

Loxl1 KO mice develop POP that frequently involves the descensus of the bladder, while the urethra is typically detached from the upper vaginal wall allowing for greater mobility of the lower urinary tract [27]. Severe POP may lead to bladder outlet obstruction and hydronephrosis in some animals [27, 35].

Histologically, the paraurethral connective tissues are disorganized with fewer elastic fibers. The urethral walls are also disorganized, with reduced number of smooth muscle cells [27]. In addition, striated muscle fibers of the external urethral sphincter (EUS) are less organized, shorter and thinner in Loxl1 KO mice [39].

Loxl1-deficient mice present abnormal voiding patterns as evidenced by increased urinary frequency, with correspondingly smaller urine volume per void compared to WT mice [27]. Urodynamics confirms reduced bladder capacity, micturition pressure and volume for leak point-pressure (LPP) measurement in Loxl1 KO mice compared with age- and parity-matched WT mice [46]. POP mice have increased elastic fiber fragmentation and disorganization as demonstrated by more elastin clusters in the EUS compared with KO non-prolapsed mice [34].

Loxl1 KO mice with POP have lower LPP after the third delivery compared with after the first delivery, demonstrating that LPP is decreased with increased parity [34]. Twelve weeks postpartum, Loxl1 KO mice with POP have an increased number of bladder contractions not associated with voiding, possibly due to partial bladder outlet obstruction [34]. However, POP is not associated with any other outcomes of cystometry at this time point. Similarly, route of delivery does not affect LUT function in Loxl1 KO prolapsed mice [43].

Analysis of age-matched Loxl1 KO and WT mice, although limited, has demonstrated that the age of mice has little overall impact on the biomechanical behavior of the surrounding vaginal-supportive tissue complex [35]. However, no investigation of elderly mice after mouse estropause (similar to menopause) has been done. Additionally, aging does not appear to influence the overall expression of ECM genes in vaginal tissues of mice when controlled for parity or time postpartum [44].

Loxl1 KO mice can be used to test therapies for POP. The addition of the elastogenic factors, hyaluronan oligomers and TGFβ-1 in cultures of smooth muscle vaginal cells harvested from Loxl1 KO mice results in upregulation of elastin mRNA by 3.6-fold, a 2.6-fold increase in total elastin and denser elastic matrix compared to untreated cells [47]. However, additional research is necessary since histological morphometric analyses characterized the elastic content as tortuous clusters of elastin deposits and not mature fibers [38]. Another study of elastogenic treatment of vaginal cells from Loxl1 KO mice compared to untreated tissues showed a significant increase in the mean area and perimeter length of elastic fibers [38]. These findings suggest a continuous proteolytic activity in the cultures of Loxl1 KO mice resulting in increased elastic fiber breakdown as well as aberrant new fiber formation that needs to be addressed by future therapeutic modalities [38].

Loxl1 KO mice have more recently been used to better elucidate the pathophysiology associated with mesh complications in POP surgical treatment. Initial findings indicate that mesh decreases the elasticity of the surrounding tissues as evidenced by decreased amount of elastin (percent area). Collagen and inflammation increased in the recto-vaginal tissue of Loxl1 KO mice implanted with polypropylene mesh [48].

Fibulin-5 (FBLN5) is a calcium-dependent, elastin-binding protein and a member of the short fibulin family. It localizes to the surface of elastic fibers and promotes elastogenesis [49, 50]. FBLN5 binds tropoelastin with high affinity and facilitates coacervation or self-aggregation of tropoelastin, enabling subsequent cross-linking and assembly of mature elastic fibers [32]. FBLN5 likely plays a regulatory role by targeting LOXL1 to elastic fiber assembly sites in the ECM; FBLN5 involvement occurs in the early stages of elastogenesis [51]. FBLN5 may also play a role in regulating oxidative stress in the vaginal wall after delivery as studies have shown that FBLN5 is required for binding of extracellular superoxide dismutase (ecSOD) to vascular tissue [52, 53]. Of the mouse KO genes studied, FBLN5 is the only gene that has also been consistently and significantly associated with POP in humans [8, 54–56]

Like Loxl1 KO mice, Fbln5 KO mice live until adulthood and demonstrate emphysematous lungs, loose skin, dilated and tortuous great vessels, and disrupted elastic fiber morphology as well as POP. These systemic elastic fiber defects suggest that a developmental defect rather than destruction of elastic fibers contributes to POP [32, 57]. Newer technologies including CRISPER/Cas9 have been used to efficiently induce the Fbln5 mutation in mice [58, 59].

The Fbln5 KO mouse model was used to develop a POP quantification scoring tool, the Mouse pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (MOPQ) system, which is useful for quantifying POP in mice [60]. This tool includes: grade of perineal bulge, cervical descent, anal prolapse, height of perineal bulge, perineal body length and vaginal diameter.

We identified 13 publications addressing the pelvic floor and POP in Fbln5 KO mice: 8 full peer-reviewed manuscripts and 5 abstracts presented in conferences.

Mice deficient in Fbln5 demonstrate true urogenital POP since dissection of the Fbln5 KO mouse urogenital bulge shows urethra, vaginal wall and sometimes, the bladder inside the bulge [57]. The vagina and cervix are descended and stretched, and Fbln5 KO mice have an increased genital hiatus [57]. Uterosacral ligaments are either absent or poorly attenuated in older Fbln5 KO mice. Fbln5 KO mice also appear to have increased size and weight of their vaginal wall compared to WT mice [57].

Other biomechanical changes of the Fbln5 KO prolapsed vaginal wall include decreased stiffness, decreased maximal load and increased distensibility; these changes are similar to changes that are observed during pregnancy in WT mice [61, 62]. Interestingly, the small minority of Fbln5 KO mice that do not develop POP (~ 10%) have similar vaginal wall biomechanical properties to nonpregnant WT mice for factors such as maximum stress, maximum distention and stiffness [61].

FBLN5 plays a dual role in vaginal ECM homeostasis in terms of both assembly of elastic fibers as well as regulation of proteinases. MMP9 and MMP2 are upregulated in vaginal tissues of adult Fbln5 KO mice, and MMP9 is upregulated at least 6 weeks prior to typical development of POP [60]. POP was significantly attenuated in a double KO mouse model involving deficiency in both Fbln5 and Mmp9; these double KO mice showed increased elastic fiber density and thicker, more continuous collagen fibrils [53]. Only 42% of double KO mice developed POP compared to 96% of mice that developed POP mice deficient in only Fbln5 [53]. In addition to upregulation of MMP9, additional serine proteases (i.e., V1) and serine protease inhibitors (i.e., serpina1a and elafin) are upregulated in Fbln5 KO mice [63]. These findings suggest that protease activation and deficient elastic fibers are important contributors to POP in KO mice [64, 65].

Most investigations of Fbln5 KO mouse models have focused on homozygous Fbln5 null (Fbln5 −/−) mice; however, researchers have also investigated a heterozygous phenotype (Fbln5 ±). Although sample sizes were small, no difference was observed between heterozygous mice and control mice for mechanical properties of the vagina including perineal bulge, bulge grade, cervical descent, vaginal diameter, length of perineal body and magnitude of MOPQ scores [60].

Mice deficient in Fbln5 develop POP as a function of age even without vaginal delivery [57, 60]. Nulliparous Fbln5 KO mice as young as 3 months old exhibit a genitourinary bulge [57]. By 6 months of age, > 90% of Fbln5 KO mice have substantial stage 3 POP [57, 60], whether nulliparous or parous.

No changes have been observed in the size of the perineal bulge during pregnancy in Fbln5-deficient mice; however, increased severity of POP occurs approximately 1 week postpartum [57]. Other morphological changes that occur in the early (2–24 h) postpartum period of Fbln5 KO mice include fragmented and disoriented elastic fibers and marked increases in monocytes and macrophages in the vaginal wall, representing a rich source for elastolytic enzymes [57].

Additional evidence for the importance of FBLN5 in the recovery from parturition is found in a tissue-specific conditional Fbln5 KO mouse model where only the stromal cells of the pelvic floor are deficient in Fbln5 [66]. These tissue-specific mice did not develop POP after parturition; however, they did show significantly increased perineal body length, bulge, increased Mmp9 and nondetectable levels of Fbln5 that did not return to baseline after 12 weeks compared to control mice whose values returned to baseline within 6–8 weeks [66]. These results suggest that compromised levels of FBLN5 in the vaginal wall led to subclinical POP that likely continues to accumulate with increasing number of deliveries.

Various investigations have used vaginal distention to simulate the effects of parturition in Fbln5 KO mice. In non-pregnant Fbln5 KO mice, vaginal distention resulted in rapid acceleration of POP without recovery [67, 68]. Cellular senescence as part of the aging process occurs when senescent cells secrete proinflammatory and matrix-degrading molecules. Markers of cellular senescence appear altered in mice deficient in Fbln5, including failure to downregulate the senescent gatekeeper P53 and H2Ax, as well as downregulation of p16, which leads to activation of p21, resulting in long-term senescence [68]. These findings suggest that markers that protect from senescence fail in animals predisposed to POP [68]. Proteins involved in elastogenesis, including protein tyrosine kinase 7 (PTK7), and β-catenin are differentially expressed in Fbln5 KO mice compared to WT mice, suggesting that these genes might also be involved in POP in Fbln5 KO mice [59].

Fibulin-3 (FBLN3) is a member of the fibulin family of ECM proteins. The protein is encoded by the EGF Containing Fibulin Extracellular Matrix Protein 1 (EFEMP1) gene [69, 70]. FBLN3 displays little interaction with elastin, fibrillin-1 and other elastic fiber or basement membrane components, suggesting that FBLN3 has distinct and non-interactive functions from the other fibulins [71].

We identified two peer-reviewed publications about Fbln3 KO mice and POP [72, 73].

Fibln3 KO mice are born with no gross phenotypical abnormalities, indicating that Fbln3 is not essential for early survival [73]. They can breed and procreate, but their reproductive capacity is reduced, being sterile in about 40% and producing very few pups in the remaining mice [73].

Fbln3 KO mice appear to develop a complex pheno-type that reflects early aging. They have a shorter lifespan, looser skin, reduced vigor, hair changes, reduced body size, reduced bone density and muscle mass, lordokyphosis, atrophy/degeneration of various internal organs and frequent periophthalmic lesions compared to age-matched or even older WT animals [73]. Some aging features such as hair graying, skin ulceration or cataracts, or changes in lungs, heart, intestines and blood cell counts were not observed [73].

Bulges and protrusions were visually observed at several sites in both female and male Fbln3 KO mice at 2 months old and older, which progress with age [73]. Around 53% of Fbln3 KO female mice > 40 weeks old developed multiple herniations: abdominal and inguinal hernias, protrusion of xiphoid process, rectal prolapse and POP [72]. Biopsies confirmed that FBLN3 has a specific effect on fascial connective tissues demonstrated by a significant reduction or absence of the fascial elastic fiber network in Fbln3 KO mice, resulting in thin and fragile fascial membranes compared to WT mice; however. no elastic changes were detected in aorta, skin or lungs [73].

Pelvic organ support is impaired in female Fbln3 KO mice, and stage I or greater perineal bulge is observed in 26.9% of animals ≥ 11 weeks of age [72]. Similar to other KO models, the perineal bulge comprised the bladder, vaginal vault and descended uterine horns caudal to the pubic ligament. Concomitant with perineal bulge, rectal prolapse occurred in 34% and hernias in 32% of animals > 40 weeks old [72].

Compared with WT controls, perineal bulge, perineal body length, vaginal diameter and vaginal weight increased significantly in Fbln3 mice even without obvious POP [72]. The paravaginal and uterosacral ligaments are either absent or attenuated in Fbln3 KO mice. Histologically, elastic fibers are disrupted in Fbln3-deficient vaginal tissues. The elastic fibers are blunt, thick and fewer in number in the muscularis and grossly more abundant, fragmented, tortuous and tangled in the adventitial layer [72].

MMP activity in Fbln3 KO mice without prolapse does not differ from that in WT mice, while Mmp2 and Mmp9 activity increased in Fbln3 KO mice with POP. Timp1 and Timp3 gene expression and Fbln5, desmosine and tropoelastin levels are increased in vaginal tissues from Fbln3 KO animals regardless of POP. These findings suggest that both elastogenesis and proteolysis are upregulated in response to Fbln3 loss in the vaginal wall, together with potential upregulation of the inhibitory mechanisms of elastin breakdown [72]. However, it appears that proteolysis dominates, resulting in progressive deterioration of elastic fibers in the vagina and failure of pelvic organ support [72].

Approximately 90% of Fibln3 KO mice developed POP after 20 weeks of age [72]. In Fbln3 KO mice, disruption of the elastin core of the vaginal wall was apparent in young females several months before the onset of POP, and the disrupted fibers were more severe in the vagina of older Fbln3 KO mice, showing an age-dependent relation even in the absence of vaginal delivery [72]. Approximately 66% of older Fibl3 KO mice with POP were nulliparous [72]. No association was found between parity and POP in Fbln3-deficient mice, suggesting that while Fbln3 plays a role in maintaining pelvic organ support during aging, it does not impact the ability of pelvic floor connective tissue to recover after parturition [72].

Homeobox (HOX) genes are evolutionarily conserved genes encoding transcription factors that regulate mammalian embryonic growth and development of the urogenital tract, mediating segmental differentiation of the paramesonephric duct into morphologically distinct organs of the female reproductive tract [74, 75]. HOXA11 is responsible for the development of the lower uterine segment and cervix and is thought to play an important role in maintaining plasticity of the uterus under the influence of hormonal variations and structural changes across the lifetime of women and other mammals, including mice [74, 75].

We identified a single publication on Hoxa11 KO mice that addresses the pelvic floor [76]. Deletion of Hoxa11 gene in mice results in the absence of uterosacral ligaments and increased mobility of the uterus; however, overt POP was not observed [76]. In contrast, the ligaments were identified in all WT and heterozygous mice, with no anatomical differences between them, confirming the role of Hoxa11 in uterosacral ligament development. In vitro assays using murine fibroblastic cells transfected with virus expressing Hoxa11 showed increased procollagen type III expression and decreased MMP2 expression, favoring collagen synthesis over catabolism [76].

An additional animal study linked the Hoxa11 gene with ECM metabolism in the uterus and uterosacral ligament [77]. Using liposome-mediated gene transfection technology, the uterine horns and peritoneal cavity of WT mice were surgically assessed in vivo and were locally injected with a media solution to deliver shRNA vectors with Hoxa11 inserts to knock down the Hoxa11 gene in the genital tract. This resulted in decreased expression of Hoxa11 gene by approximately 50% in the uterosacral ligaments and uterus 24–48 h after the injection, together with overall decreased levels of collagens and TIMPs genes and proteins and increased levels and activity of MMPs in those organs [76].

We found a single report of incidental POP in Upii-sv40t transgenic mice, which express the simian virus 40 T antigen protein specifically in urothelium, driven by the uroplakin II promoter [78], which is typically used to express oncogenes. Spontaneous descent of the pelvic organs distal to the pelvic floor was observed in both male and female Upii-sv40t mice [79]. Necropsy of female Upii-sv40t transgenic mice showed a bulge below the pelvis, with the bowel and bladder prolapsed through the pelvic floor, with distal displacement of the horned uterus compressed by the bladder and bowel. MRI images showed the bladder prolapsed distal to the ischia, the vagina compressed and displaced laterally, and loops of bowel in the place of the normal location of the bladder [79]. In addition, the appearance of the kidneys and ureters suggested lower urinary tract obstruction.

This transgenic mouse model may have translational value due to their spontaneous POP. However, analysis of breeding colony records suggests that POP was potentially inherited independent of the Upii-sv40t transgene in both males and females and appeared to be an autosomal dominant trait [79]. Further investigation may identify candidate genes responsible for POP development in these mice.

Although investigation of the pathophysiology of POP is in its infancy, several KO mouse models of POP have been developed or discovered in the past decade and half. The ability to genetically manipulate animals to assess the role of specific genes in POP development remains a relatively new development for the field, and therefore the full implications of these models remain unknown. Nonetheless, these models have unequivocally demonstrated the changes in ECM homeostasis with age, with pregnancy and vaginal delivery playing a role in POP development. The research to date has demonstrated that KO mouse models have great utility for investigation of the role of specific genes and proteins in pelvic floor stability and POP development and progression. In addition, despite being a quadruped animal, they are useful for preclinical investigation of novel therapies for POP, an area of great current clinical need. The choice of which KO mouse model to use for each investigation depends on the hypothesis being tested and the outcomes being investigated.

While there are several different animal models of stress urinary incontinence (reviewed in [80, 81]), none of these develop POP. In contrast, Loxl1 KO mice have been shown to develop SUI concomitant with POP [27, 34]. Thus, this mouse model may provide the only available model of the full spectrum of female pelvic floor disorders and a means for investigating the pathophysiological links between the different pelvic floor disorders. However, little to nothing is known of the impact of Loxl1 deficiency on fecal incontinence so that is a priority area for future investigation and characterization of this model system.

Fbln5 KO mice demonstrate the importance of proper elastin development for normal pelvic floor function. When FBLN5 is missing, female mice develop POP regardless of parturition or parity. Thus, these mice are useful for investigation of mechanisms of elastin homeostasis and its impact on POP. Fbln3 KO mice further demonstrate that pathology in elastin structure is not the only contributor to POP since absence of FBLN3 can also result in POP with increased aging of the deficient mice. Thus, as in humans, POP has a complex pathophysiology in mice, which depends on genetic predisposition along with the impact of confounding and inciting factors such as aging, pregnancy, parturition, parity and other life events. Together, these two KO mice along with results from studies using Loxl1 KO mice demonstrate the importance of connective tissue support to the pelvic floor and that impaired ECM, particularly that involving the elastin pathway, contributes to POP.

Hoxa11 KO mice demonstrated that the absence of a uterosacral ligament does not produce POP. Even though POP was not observed or induced in Hoxa11 KO mice, this animal model could serve to further explore the pathway of Hoxa11 as modulator of the ECM in uterosacral ligaments. Further investigation using Upii-sv40t transgenic mice may identify candidate genes responsible for POP development.

Thus, although utilization of KO mouse models to understand the pathophysiology of POP and test potential therapies is still young, a number of advances have been made using these models. Future investigations should be encouraged since insight would be gained into the genetics and molecular mechanisms of POP, which could lead to improved treatments and perhaps preventative measures. For example, investigations using multiphoton microscopy may be useful for studying ECM microarchitecture in POP mouse models [82].
