# A novel linker region truncating variant in BCL10 underlies a leaky immunodeficiency phenotype.
**Authors:** Tong L, Wei Q, Li Y, Tian Z, Dai R, Gong X, Feng Z, Jia Y, Du H, Wu J, Yang X, An Y, Zhao X, Zhou L
**Journal:** Pediatr Allergy Immunol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/pai.70441](https://doi.org/10.1111/pai.70441)

## Content

BACKGROUND: BCL10 is a core CBM (CARD-BCL10-MALT1) complex component required for antigen receptor-mediated NF-κB activation. BCL10 deficiency is an exceptionally rare autosomal recessive combined immunodeficiency, with only six patients reported to date. We aimed to characterize the clinical, immunologic, and molecular features of a novel homozygous BCL10 variant and explore mechanisms associated with its leaky phenotype.

METHODS: Clinical/immunologic phenotyping, whole-exome and Sanger sequencing, transcript and protein studies, signaling, cytokine, and T cell proliferation assays, and scRNA-seq were performed.

RESULTS: A novel homozygous BCL10 c.345_346dup (p.Gly116GlufsTer3) variant was identified in a patient with combined immunodeficiency and immune dysregulation, with relatively mild infections, terminal effector-skewed lymphocytes, and partial Treg preservation. In PBMCs, the variant reduced full-length BCL10 transcripts and BCL10 protein while increasing N-terminal transcripts; HEK293T overexpression showed that the mutant construct could produce a low-abundance truncated protein in vitro. NF-κB signaling was stimulus dependent, with near-absent p-p65 induction in T cells after PMA/ionomycin but relative preservation or enhancement after TNF-α or LPS. T cell proliferation was partially preserved with anti-CD3/CD28 but nearly absent with anti-CD3 alone. ScRNA-seq revealed aberrant Treg-associated gene expression, increased activation/apoptosis/senescence programs in T and NK cells, enhanced inflammatory signaling in B cells and monocytes, and enrichment of AP-1/MAPK, inflammatory NF-κB, and IFN-response pathways.

CONCLUSION: This novel linker-region truncating BCL10 variant was associated with leaky combined immunodeficiency with immune dysregulation. Defective CBM-dependent NF-κB activation coexisted with secondary activation of CBM-independent inflammatory and AP-1/MAPK-related programs, suggesting that inflammatory remodeling may contribute to the nonclassical phenotype.

We report the seventh known patient with BCL10 deficiency, carrying a novel linker‐region truncating variant and showing a leaky clinical and immunologic phenotype. In contrast to previously reported cases with severe early‐onset disease and predominantly naïve lymphocyte profiles, this patient showed a relatively milder course, terminal effector‐skewed lymphocytes, and partial preservation of Treg cells. Mechanistically, defective canonical NF‐κB signaling coexisted with secondary activation of CBM‐independent inflammatory and ERK/MAPK‐related pathways, including increased basal p‐ERK levels and AP‐1/MAPK‐related transcriptional programs. These observations suggest that inflammatory remodeling may contribute to the atypical phenotype, but they do not establish true functional compensation for the CBM defect. Together, these findings expand the clinical and molecular spectrum of BCL10 deficiency and improve recognition of leaky BCL10 deficiency.

Inborn errors of immunity (IEI) are a rapidly expanding and genetically heterogeneous group of disorders, representing an important cause of severe pediatric disease and a powerful model for elucidating the molecular basis of human immune development, signaling, and homeostasis.1The CARD‐BCL10‐MALT1 (CBM) complex is a central signaling platform that transduces receptor‐proximal signals to downstream NF‐κB and MAPK pathways across lymphoid, myeloid, and barrier cells.2,3Within this complex, BCL10 and MALT1 are shared core components, whereas signaling specificity is largely determined by CARD family members, including CARD11 in lymphocytes, CARD9 in myeloid cells, CARD10 in nonhematopoietic cells, and CARD14 in epithelial tissues.4In T cells, TCR stimulation triggers CARD11 activation, promotes BCL10 polymerization and MALT1 recruitment, and subsequently leads to TRAF6 recruitment, TAK1/IKK activation, and ultimately NF‐κB and JNK/AP‐1 activation, driving cytokine transcription, proliferation, survival, and effector differentiation.5

BCL10, located on chromosome 1p22.3, encodes a 233‐amino acid cytosolic adaptor protein, containing an N‐terminal CARD that mediates CARD family interactions and polymerization, and a C‐terminal serine/threonine‐rich region involved in MALT1 recruitment and CBM signaling, thereby playing an essential role in immune homeostasis and host defense. Its deficiency causes combined immunodeficiency with immune dysregulation.4Since the first description of human germline BCL10 deficiency in 2014,6only 6 patients from several unrelated families have been reported to date.6,7,8,9,10,11Reported disease‐causing variants include splice‐site, missense, and truncating variants. Affected patients typically present with early‐onset severe infections and hypogammaglobulinemia, along with profoundly impaired T cell proliferation after TCR stimulation, largely preserved total T and B cell counts with a predominantly naïve phenotype, and markedly reduced or absent Treg cells, resulting in severe clinical courses and early death in most reported cases.7,8,9,10,11

Here, we report a patient with IEI harboring a novel pathogenic BCL10 variant, representing the seventh reported case worldwide. Distinct from previously reported BCL10 defects, this variant maps to the linker region between the CARD and the serine/threonine‐rich domains, resulting in a C‐terminal truncation. The relatively mild clinical course and atypical immunologic features observed in this patient suggest broader phenotypic heterogeneity of BCL10 deficiency than previously appreciated, and further expand its clinical spectrum while providing additional insight into the role of BCL10 in human immune homeostasis.

A pediatric patient with a homozygous BCL10 variant was enrolled. Clinical data, longitudinal follow‐up information, and peripheral blood samples were obtained from the patient and available family members. Age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls were included for comparison. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient's guardian, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children's Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. 2021‐138).

Whole‐exome sequencing was performed to identify the candidate BCL10 variant, which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing in the patient and family members. For transcript analysis, total RNA was extracted from peripheral blood (Axygen), reverse‐transcribed into cDNA (Takara), and analyzed by quantitative real‐time PCR using primer sets targeting the N‐terminal region and the full‐length BCL10 transcript (TableS1). GAPDH was used as the internal control, and relative expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCtmethod.

BCL10WTand BCL10G116Efsconstructs were generated and transiently expressed in HEK293T cells (TableS2). Cells were harvested 24 h after transfection for subsequent immunoblot analysis. To determine whether the variant altered protein stability, cycloheximide chase experiments were performed, and the decay kinetics of BCL10WTand BCL10G116Efswere compared over time.

PBMCs were isolated from peripheral blood using Ficoll–Hypaque density gradient centrifugation and were processed immediately for downstream assays. PBMCs were stimulated with PMA (100 ng/mL, Abmole) plus ionomycin (1 μg/mL, Invivogen), TNF‐α (20 ng/mL, PeproTech), or LPS (10 ng/mL, Sigma‐Aldrich). NF‐κB activation was assessed by phospho‐flow cytometry or immunoblotting of p‐p65. For cytokine measurements, PBMCs were stimulated with LPS for 24 h, and IL‐6 and IL‐8 levels in culture supernatants were quantified by ELISA (4A Biotech).

T cell and B cell subpopulations were evaluated in whole‐blood samples by standard flow cytometry methods. Briefly, samples were incubated with fluorochrome‐conjugated antibodies against TCRγδ, TCRαβ, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD27, and CD45RA (BioLegend) for T cell analysis, or against CD19, CD27, IgD, CD38, and CD24 (BioLegend) for B cell analysis for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Red blood cells were then lysed, and the remaining cells were washed and resuspended for acquisition. For Treg analysis, cells were stained with surface antibodies followed by intracellular staining for FOXP3 and Helios (eBioscience) and CTLA‐4 (BioLegend). For phospho‐flow analysis, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained intracellularly with phospho‐NF‐κB p65 (Ser536) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology). Data were acquired on a flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Total protein was extracted from PBMCs or transfected HEK293T cells, separated by SDS‐PAGE, and analyzed by immunoblotting with an anti‐BCL10 antibody recognizing the N‐terminal epitope (Abcam, ab40752), an anti–phospho‐p65 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology), and anti‐β‐actin or anti‐α‐Tubulin (Proteintech) as loading controls.

PBMCs were labeled with CFSE (Invitrogen) and stimulated with PHA (2.5 μg/mL, Invivogen), anti‐CD3 alone (1 μg/mL, Biolegend), or anti‐CD3 plus anti‐CD28 (5 μg mL−1/2 μg mL−1, Biolegend). After 72 h, proliferative responses of CD4+and CD8+T cells were evaluated by flow cytometric analysis of CFSE dilution.

PBMCs from the patient and two age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls were processed using the Chromium Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kits v2 (10x Genomics), with paired TCR/BCR libraries prepared according to the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150‐bp paired‐end reads. Raw data were processed using bcl2fastq and Cell Ranger for alignment, barcode counting, and UMI counting. Downstream analyses were performed using Seurat. Genes expressed in more than three cells were retained. Scrublet‐predicted doublets were removed, and low‐quality cells were excluded if they had fewer than 200 detected genes, fewer than 400 total UMIs, or more than 20% mitochondrial transcripts. Datasets were integrated using Seurat canonical correlation analysis. Clustering was performed at a resolution of 0.6, followed by UMAP visualization and cell annotation. Differential expression was analyzed using the Seurat workflow with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using GOseq and KOBAS 3.0, with adjustedp< .05 considered significant. Given the limited sample size, the scRNA‐seq analysis was interpreted as exploratory.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.3.0. Data are presented as mean ± SD. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student'st‐test or Mann–WhitneyUtest was used as appropriate according to sample size and data distribution. Analyses involving the single patient were interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory. Apvalue < .05 was considered statistically significant (*p< .05, **p< .01, ***p< .001, ****p< .0001).

The patient was a 4‐year‐10‐month‐old boy born to consanguineous parents (TableS3). Disease onset occurred in the neonatal period, with recurrent eczema‐like pruritic rash as the earliest manifestation (Figure1A,D). In addition, he had recurrent diarrhea with mucus and blood streaked stools, fever, and pneumonia from infancy onward. Anemia and urinary tract infection were documented at 7 months of age, followed by recurrent oral candidiasis. Bronchoalveolar lavage identified mixed infection withStreptococcus pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, andCandida albicans. Antibiotic and supportive therapy provided only transient improvement, with persistent relapses leading to a total of 13 hospitalizations. At 3 years and 4 months of age, he was referred to our hospital because of disease progression, where severe gastrointestinal ulceration was identified (Figure1D). Regular IVIG replacement and longitudinal health management were subsequently initiated, followed by gradual clinical improvement (Figure1B,D). The patient also showed growth retardation and localized suppuration at the BCG vaccination site (Figure1A,C). Together, these findings indicate an early‐onset disorder characterized by recurrent infections, chronic mucocutaneous and gastrointestinal inflammation, and growth impairment.

Given the early‐onset recurrent infections and inflammatory manifestations, we next investigated the patient's immunologic profile. Persistent eosinophilia, low IgA/IgM, markedly elevated IgE, reduced naïve CD4+and CD8+T cells with relative effector/memory expansion, and severely impaired memory B cell differentiation despite largely preserved total T and B cell numbers were observed (Figure1E, Table1). Among CD4+T cells, Th1 and Th17 proportions were increased, whereas Tfh and Tfr cells were at the low end of the age‐matched normal range (Figure1F,H). Notably, Foxp3+Treg cells were markedly reduced in the patient, but a low residual population was still preserved (Figure1G). Overall, the patient exhibited combined humoral and cellular immune abnormalities, characterized by impaired lymphocyte differentiation, reduced Treg cells, contraction of naïve T cell compartments with relative effector/memory expansion, profound impairment of memory B cell differentiation, and skewing toward inflammatory Th cell subsets.

Whole‐exome sequencing identified a homozygous BCL10 c.345_346dup (p.Gly116GlufsTer3) variant in the patient, whereas both parents were heterozygous carriers (Figure2A,B). MutationTaster predicted the variant to be disease causing with a score of 1.0, and T‐Coffee alignment showed that the affected residue is highly conserved across species (Figure2C). Unlike previously reported pathogenic BCL10 variants clustered in the CARD domain, this variant was located in the linker region between the CARD and serine/threonine‐rich domains, further expanding the mutational spectrum of human BCL10 deficiency (Figure2D).

To further place this case in the context of previously reported BCL10 deficiency, we compared its clinical and immunologic features with those of published patients (TableS4). Unlike most reported cases with severe early developmental arrest and a profound naïve‐skewed immune phenotype,6,7,8,9,10,11this patient showed partial preservation of lymphocyte development, residual Treg cells, limited but measurable immune function, less severe infectious manifestations overall, survival beyond early childhood, and partial clinical improvement after IVIG therapy. Together, these findings indicate a leaky clinical and immunologic phenotype of BCL10 deficiency.

To define the molecular consequence of the variant, BCL10 expression was examined. Full‐length BCL10 transcripts were markedly reduced, whereas N‐terminal transcripts were relatively increased, and endogenous BCL10 protein was undetectable in patient PBMCs, indicating severe impairment of normal BCL10 expression at both the transcript and protein levels (Figure2E,F). Consistently, HEK293T overexpression showed that the mutant construct produced a truncated protein with markedly reduced expression, whereas cycloheximide chase did not demonstrate clearly accelerated protein decay, suggesting that the G116Efs variant primarily impairs protein production or steady‐state expression rather than causing overt posttranslational instability (Figure2G–I).

Given the central role of BCL10 in CBM signaling, canonical NF‐κB activation was assessed in lymphocytes and found to be profoundly impaired, with nearly abolished p‐p65 induction in both CD4+and CD8+T cells after PMA/ionomycin stimulation (Figure3A). In contrast, CBM‐independent inflammatory responses were relatively preserved or enhanced, as shown by maintained or increased p‐p65 responses after TNF‐α stimulation, elevated basal p‐p65 followed by gradual decline after LPS stimulation, and increased IL‐6 and IL‐8 secretion (Figure3B,C,E,F). In addition, immunoblot analysis showed higher basal p‐ERK levels in patient PBMCs than in healthy controls (Figure3D). Together, these findings suggest a dissociation between defective CBM‐dependent NF‐κB activation and secondary activation of CBM‐independent inflammatory and ERK/MAPK‐related pathways. However, these data do not establish that these pathways functionally compensate for the impaired CBM signalosome.

In the patient, proliferation after PHA stimulation was reduced in both CD4+and CD8+T cells relative to controls. Under anti‐CD3 stimulation alone, proliferative responses were further diminished, with CD4+T cells showing almost no detectable proliferation. Combined anti‐CD3/CD28 stimulation partially restored CD8+T cell proliferation, whereas CD4+T cell proliferation remained substantially impaired, indicating a severe defect in TCR‐dependent proliferation that was particularly pronounced in CD4+T cells (Figure3G).

Single‐cell transcriptomic profiling further suggested that the patient exhibited a cellular phenotype clearly distinct from the profoundly naïve lymphocyte pattern reported in previously described BCL10‐deficient cases. Instead, the patient displayed broader immune imbalance characterized by skewing of lymphocyte differentiation, marked reduction but incomplete loss of the Treg compartment, and widespread transcriptional dysregulation across lymphoid and myeloid lineages. Major immune populations were preserved, but T cell composition shifted toward effector‐associated compartments with relative reduction of CD4Treg cells, consistent with the preceding flow‐cytometric findings that lymphocyte development was not completely arrested at a naïve stage (Figure4A,B). Concordantly, the patient exhibited a more oligoclonal TCR repertoire (Figure4C), with clonal expansion concentrated in cytotoxic/effector T cell subsets, particularly CD8GNLY cells, a GNLY‐high CD8 T cell cluster corresponding to cytotoxic effector/effector memory‐like CD8 T cells, as well as γδT cells and proliferating T cell populations, whereas naïve compartments remained largely singleton‐dominated (Figures4H–J,S1A,S2B).

The B cell compartment showed aberrant activation, inflammatory/IFN‐stress responses, and impaired differentiation, with upregulation of AP‐1 family genes (FOS, FOSB, JUN, JUNB, andJUND) together withCD69, CD83, HLA‐DQA2, HLA‐DRB5, andNFKBIA, most prominently in memory B cell and plasmablast‐related populations (Figures5C,S1B). In the BCR repertoire, naïve and transitional B cells predominated, with only a limited plasmablast fraction, whileIGHM/IGHDpredominated over switched immunoglobulin transcripts, particularlyIGHGandIGHA, indicating impaired terminal differentiation and restricted class‐switch recombination (Figures5D,E,S1C).

Innate immune compartments showed broad inflammatory dysregulation. NK cells displayed concurrent activation, cytotoxicity, and inhibitory/exhaustion features, with increasedIFI27, GSTM1, FOS/JUN, IFNG, GZMB, PRF1, GNLY, LAG3, andPDCD1in both CD56brightand CD56dimsubsets (FigureS1C), which broadly correspond to conventionally recognized cytokine‐producing/regulatory‐like and more mature cytotoxic NK cell populations, respectively. Monocyte and myeloid dendritic‐cell populations, corresponding to conventional monocyte and antigen‐presenting myeloid compartments, showed enhanced interferon and proinflammatory activation, characterized by increasedIFI44L, XAF1, IL6, EREG, CXCL8, CD14, NFKBIA, andTNFAIP3(Figures5F,S1A).

KEGG analysis showed shared enrichment of inflammatory and stress‐response pathways across T, B, NK, and myeloid cell compartments, including NF‐κB, MAPK, TNF, IL‐17, and innate immune receptor signaling, together with HIF‐1 signaling and oxidative phosphorylation (Figures5G,S1D,E). These exploratory findings were consistent with defective CBM‐dependent signaling and secondary CBM‐independent inflammatory activation.

BCL10 is a key CBM‐complex component that couples antigen receptor signaling to NF‐κB, MAPK, and downstream lymphocyte activation. By 2025, more than 200 patients with germline defects affecting CBM‐complex proteins had been reported, predominantly involving CARD‐family adaptors,12,13,14,15followed by MALT1 deficiency,16whereas germline BCL10 deficiency remained exceptionally rare, with only 6 patients described worldwide.6,7,8,9,10,11All previously reported pathogenic variants were located in the N‐terminal CARD domain, and the affected patients typically presented in early infancy with severe combined immunodeficiency, profound lymphocyte maturational defects, and frequent early death from infectious and inflammatory complications.6,7,8,9,10,11Here, we report the seventh patient with BCL10 deficiency, carrying a homozygous BCL10 c.345_346dup, p.Gly116GlufsTer3 variant. Although the overall phenotype overlapped with prior reports, the disease course was relatively milder and the peripheral immune phenotype was not dominated by a typical naïve pattern, but instead showed partial effector/memory skewing, suggesting a broader clinical and immunologic spectrum of BCL10 deficiency than previously recognized.

A notable feature of this case is that the variant lies in the linker region between the CARD and Ser/Thr‐rich domains, rather than in the canonical CARD domain reported previously.6,7,8,9,10,11It markedly reduced full‐lengthBCL10transcripts and abolished endogenous BCL10 protein in PBMCs, whereas the mutant construct showed low‐level expression in vitro, confirming functional BCL10 deficiency. Increased N‐terminal transcript levels and the absence of clearly accelerated mutant protein decay suggest that impaired protein production or steady‐state expression, rather than rapid degradation, is the dominant mechanism. Although the mutant construct produced a low‐abundance truncated protein in HEK293T cells, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because it was observed only in an overexpression system. A plausible explanation is nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay triggered by the premature termination codon, preferentially depleting endogenous mutant transcripts in primary cells. By contrast, the CMV‐based overexpression construct likely drove supraphysiologic transcript output that partially overwhelmed transcript surveillance, permitting detection of the truncated protein in vitro despite its apparent absence in PBMCs; similar discrepancies have been observed in other monogenic immune disorders, includingPTPN2‐,NFKB1‐, andCTLA4‐related disease models.17,18,19Because this truncation also removes the downstream Ser/Thr‐rich regulatory region involved in posttranslational control of BCL10 stability and NF‐κB signal termination,20,21,22it likely disrupts both BCL10 integrity and downstream regulatory functions, thereby compromising CBM signaling output.

BCL10 deficiency is classically characterized by defective CBM‐dependent canonical NF‐κB activation, impaired TCR‐driven proliferation, and failure of memory T and B cell formation.23,24,25,26,27,28,29Our patient shared the core defects of BCL10 deficiency but differed from previously reported cases by showing effector/memory skewing, enhanced TNF‐α– and TLR4‐mediated inflammatory responses, and broad single‐cell remodeling across T, B, NK, and monocyte compartments toward effector, terminally differentiated, chronically activated, apoptotic, and senescent states, consistent with preserved or increased p‐p65 and IL‐6/IL‐8 responses to TNF‐α or LPS, as well as increased basal p‐ERK levels in PBMCs. Notably, partial proliferation was retained under strong anti‐CD3/CD28 stimulation, which may reflect engagement of additional costimulatory, metabolic, and inflammatory pathways. However, because AP‐1 activity assays, JNK/p38 phosphorylation analyses, and pharmacological inhibition experiments were not available, we cannot conclude that MAPK/AP‐1 signaling functionally compensates for the CBM defect. Therefore, we interpret the increased ERK activation and AP‐1/MAPK‐related transcriptional enrichment as evidence of secondary inflammatory remodeling rather than definitive proof of true functional compensation. In the B cell compartment, the BCR repertoire remained dominated byIGHM/IGHDwith limited switched isotypes, indicating restricted class‐switch recombination and ineffective terminal B cell maturation despite inflammatory activation. In addition, persistent eosinophilia, markedly elevated IgE, and fluctuating inflammatory markers may also be interpreted in the context of chronic inflammatory activation. These observations suggest that antigen receptor hyporesponsiveness coexists with secondary activation of nonantigen receptor inflammatory signaling, which may help explain the mixed immunodeficiency and immune‐dysregulation phenotype observed in this patient.

Another distinctive feature of this patient was reduction, rather than complete absence, of Treg cells, whereas previously reported BCL10‐deficient patients often lacked Tregs entirely.8,10,11Residual Tregs showed reducedFOXP3andIL2RAexpression but partial preservation ofIKZF2, CTLA4, andTIGIT, suggesting impaired lineage stability and homeostasis with incomplete loss of suppressive features. However, because no functional Treg suppression assays were performed, the presence of residual Treg cells should not be interpreted as evidence of preserved suppressive function. Given the established dependence of Treg development and maintenance on NF‐κB, including c‐Rel‐dependent programs,30,31,32,33,34,35,36one possible hypothesis is that limited residual or alternative inflammatory signals may allow partial phenotypic preservation of the Treg compartment. However, this remains speculative and cannot be distinguished from secondary effects of the inflammatory milieu in the present study. Future studies will be required to determine whether these residual Treg cells retain regulatory capacity and how BCL10 deficiency affects Treg development, stability, and function.

Taken together, this patient remained within the spectrum of BCL10 deficiency‐associated combined immunodeficiency, but showed a nonclassical leaky phenotype with partial preservation of lymphocyte differentiation and proliferative capacity despite chronic inflammatory immune remodeling. Conceptually, this phenotype may be interpreted at four levels: primary defective canonical CBM‐dependent signaling, secondary activation of CBM‐independent inflammatory pathways, chronic immune remodeling, and partial functional preservation. Impaired PMA/ionomycin‐induced p‐p65 activation and TCR‐dependent proliferation reflect the core CBM defect. Increased TNF‐α/LPS‐associated p‐p65 responses, elevated IL‐6/IL‐8 production, increased basal p‐ERK levels, and AP‐1/MAPK‐related transcriptional enrichment are consistent with secondary inflammatory activation. Effector skewing, altered Treg phenotypes, and impaired B‐cell maturation suggest chronic immune remodeling. Residual lymphocyte differentiation and partial proliferative responses indicate partial functional preservation, rather than proven adaptive compensation for the CBM defect. Given the single‐patient design and limited sample availability, mechanistic causality could not be determined and the scRNA‐seq findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. Further studies incorporating ERK/JNK/p38 phosphorylation kinetics, AP‐1 activity assays, and pharmacological inhibition will be needed to define the functional relevance of these pathways, and validation in additional patients or disease models will be required.

From a therapeutic perspective, previous reports indicate that supportive care for BCL10 deficiency includes infection control, immunoglobulin replacement, and prevention of opportunistic infections, whereas hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only potentially curative approach.6,7,8,9,10,11Some reported patients underwent HSCT and survived with clinical remission,7,8whereas others died before or around transplantation because of rapidly progressive infection,6,9,11emphasizing the importance of timing. Although our patient appeared less severely immunodeficient than previously reported cases, single‐cell profiling revealed persistent inflammatory activation, including a prominent pDC IFN‐stimulated signature and broadly enhanced AP‐1‐related programs, consistent with his longstanding skin and gastrointestinal involvement and indicative of ongoing inflammation with potential long‐term risk of immune dysregulation and inflammation‐related organ injury. Accordingly, in addition to regular IVIG replacement andPneumocystis jiroveciiprophylaxis, the patient is currently undergoing active preparation for HSCT, which is planned once infection and inflammation are better controlled to enable definitive immune reconstitution and improve long‐term outcome.

In summary, this case expands the clinical and immunologic spectrum of BCL10 deficiency, improves recognition of leaky phenotype, and suggests that even nonclassical presentations warrant early molecular diagnosis, immune monitoring, and timely evaluation for immunoglobulin replacement, infection prophylaxis, and HSCT.
