# Tricyclic Antidepressant Poisoning: A 6-year Retrospective Observational Cross-sectional Analysis of Emergency Department Presentation, Clinical Severity, and Hospital Outcomes in a Tertiary Academic Center.
**Authors:** Sahayaraj RG, Hazra D, Chandy GM, Thangaraju L
**Journal:** Indian J Crit Care Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.5005/jp-journals-10071-25219](https://doi.org/10.5005/jp-journals-10071-25219)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND AIMS: Tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) poisoning remains a significant cause of toxicological morbidities due to its cardiotoxic and neurotoxic effects. We aimed to study the clinical profile, management, and outcomes of patients presenting with TCA overdose to an Indian emergency department (ED).

PATIENTS AND METHODS: This retrospective study analyzed patients with TCA poisoning over a 6-year period. A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to identify factors independently associated with poor outcomes.

RESULTS: Our study analyzed 75 patients with TCA overdose (mean age: 35.1 ± 13.01 years, 84.0% female). Amitriptyline was the most common drug (69.3%), followed by imipramine (17.3%). Interpersonal issues were the most common trigger (40 cases). Clinical presentations included altered mental status (46.7%) and drowsiness (29.3%). Emergency department management included intubation (20.0%), gastric lavage/charcoal (29.3%), and sodium bicarbonate (70.7%). Patients with poor outcomes had a higher median amitriptyline dose than those with good outcomes [474.47 mg (interquartile range [IQR]: 590) vs 266.43 mg (IQR: 375), p = 0.028]. Patients who required intubation had poor outcomes (p < 0.001). Kaplan-Meier analysis of time to in-hospital death showed significantly lower survival in the amitriptyline overdose group than in the other TCA overdose group (log-rank p = 0.017). There were 12 deaths overall.

CONCLUSIONS: Tricyclic antidepressant overdose, predominantly involving amitriptyline, was commonly triggered by interpersonal issues and frequently presented with altered mental status. Poor outcomes were associated with a higher amitriptyline dose and need for intubation.

This first-of-its-kind study from India analyses clinical outcomes in tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) poisoning over 6 years. Amitriptyline emerged as the most frequently implicated and highest-risk agent, with central nervous system (CNS) depression and need for intubation associated with poor outcomes. The findings underscore the importance of risk stratification, supportive care, and drug regulation.

Deliberate self-harm (DSH) is a significant public health problem, with patterns varying by region.1 Deliberate self-harm is a pressing public health issue, particularly in developing countries, with prevalence estimates ranging from 11.5 to 33.8%.2 Among adolescents, rates reach 4.5% in boys and 3.2% in girls, often linked to emotional stress, family conflict, and internet exposure.2 In India, agrochemical ingestion is most common, followed by drug overdoses.3,4 Easy access to prescription drugs and weak regulatory controls increase the risk of misuse.5 Among toxic exposures, tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) poisoning is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality and requires rapid recognition and intervention.6,7

Tricyclic antidepressants, introduced in the 1950s, have now largely been replaced by selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and other newer antidepressants.6 However, they remain in use for treatment-resistant depression, neuropathic pain, migraine prophylaxis, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and nocturnal enuresis.6,7 Despite their reduced prescription, TCA overdoses continue to pose serious risks due to their life-threatening sodium channel-blocking effects.6–8 Tricyclic antidepressants inhibit norepinephrine and serotonin reuptake in the central nervous system (CNS).6,7 In overdose, this can trigger seizures. They also block alpha-adrenergic, histaminic, muscarinic, and central serotonin receptors—causing hypotension, anticholinergic symptoms (tachycardia, fever, dry mouth, and delirium), and CNS depression.6,7 Cardiovascular effects are especially dangerous: TCAs block fast sodium channels in myocardial cells, leading to QRS complex (QRS) widening, conduction delays, QT prolongation, and arrhythmias such as Torsade's de Pointes.7,9,10 Their quinidine-like action also depresses cardiac contractility, compounding hypotension.7 After ingestion, TCAs are quickly absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract, though anticholinergic effects may slow motility and delay absorption. Co-ingested anticholinergic agents can worsen this.6,7 Tricyclic antidepressants have high protein binding, are lipid-soluble, and undergo first-pass hepatic metabolism via cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6).11 Acidosis can raise free drug levels, amplifying toxicity.12 Symptoms usually appear within 2 hours. If patients remain asymptomatic with a normal electrocardiogram (ECG) and bowel sounds after 6 hours, serious toxicity is unlikely. However, symptoms can persist for 24–48 hours, and rarely longer, especially with amitriptyline.6,7,9,10,12

Though TCA overdoses are less common than other drug poisonings, they carry a disproportionately high fatality rate due to severe CNS and cardiac toxicity. Understanding their clinical course is vital for effective management. This study evaluates the severity and hospital outcomes of TCA poisoning in a tertiary academic center. To our knowledge, it is the first such report from India.

This was a retrospective observational study on TCA poisoning conducted in the emergency department (ED) of a large tertiary care center in Southern India.

This study included patients over a 6-year period, from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2022. The inclusion criteria included the medical records of patients aged 18 years and older who were diagnosed with TCA poisoning. The exclusion criteria were charts with missing data and cases in which patients were brought dead on arrival.

For the comparative outcome analysis, patients were stratified into two groups based on the final hospital outcome. Survivors included patients discharged after clinical improvement. The non-survivor/poor-outcome group included patients who died during hospitalization or were discharged against medical advice, as definitive in-hospital recovery could not be confirmed in the latter subgroup.

Patient data were retrieved from the hospital's electronic database and recorded in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Collected variables included age, gender, vital signs at presentation, triage priority level, psychiatric history, presenting symptoms, and reasons for DSH. Additionally, information on the number/dose and type of ingested TCAs—including amitriptyline, imipramine, desipramine, clomipramine, doxepin, and nortriptyline—was documented, along with details of any co-ingestants such as other drugs, alcohol, or substances. Data on prior management at other facilities (if referred), ED interventions, and both ED and hospital outcomes were also recorded. Triage classification was based on physiological parameters according to institutional protocol, with priority I assigned to patients with compromised airways, breathing, or circulation, while priority II included those with stable airways and minor circulatory or sensorium disturbances.

Emergency department management details were recorded retrospectively from case records and included airway support, intubation and mechanical ventilation, gastric lavage or activated charcoal administration, intravenous fluids, sodium bicarbonate therapy, vasopressor support, seizure management, and intensive care monitoring where indicated.

Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New york, United States of America). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), according to data distribution, whereas categorical variables were described using frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Univariate analysis was performed to assess associations between clinical variables and poor outcomes. Continuous variables were compared using the Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate, and categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed using the backward stepwise elimination method. Variables that were statistically significant in univariate analysis and clinically relevant were considered for inclusion. The need for intubation and sodium bicarbonate administration were entered into the final model to identify factors independently associated with poor outcomes. Amitriptyline dose was analyzed separately using the Mann–Whitney U test because of its non-parametric distribution and was not included in the final regression model to avoid model instability. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Box-and-Whisker plots were used to visualize the distribution of isolated amitriptyline dose and total TCA/polydrug dose across Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and hospital stay categories, and group differences were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis H statistic. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to compare time to in-hospital death between patients with amitriptyline overdose and those with overdose involving other TCAs. Time zero was defined as ED presentation. Patients discharged alive were censored at discharge, and patients discharged against medical advice were censored at the time of the last documented in-hospital follow-up. Survival distributions were compared using the log-rank test, and hazard ratios were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression.

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the study (IRB Min no: 2506142, dated June 25, 2025). Patient confidentiality was maintained by assigning unique identifiers and utilizing password-protected data entry software with limited access permissions. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the IRB granted a waiver of consent.

Our study analyzed 75 patients over 6 years (mean age: 35.1 ± 13.01 years), with a predominance of females (63, 84.0%) and a majority being single (50, 66.7%). Twenty patients (26.7%) had also inflicted physical self-injury in addition to the overdose. Interpersonal issues were the most common precipitating factor, reported in 40 patients (53.3%), followed by financial issues in 21 patients (28.0%), work-related stress in seven patients (9.3%), psychiatric disorders in four patients (5.3%), and accidental ingestion in three patients (4.0%). The most commonly ingested drugs were amitriptyline (52, 69.3%), imipramine (13, 17.3%), and dothiepin (12, 16.0%), sometimes in combination with other TCAs or additional drugs such as benzodiazepines (6, 8.0%) or benzodiazepine/SSRIs (4, 5.3%) (Table 1).

Clinical presentations included altered mental status (35, 46.7%), nausea/vomiting (28, 37.3%), and drowsiness (22, 29.3%). Sensorium at presentation ranged from normal responsiveness to critical impairment (Table 2). Thirty-four patients (45.3%) received initial treatment outside our facility. Among these, one patient (1.3%) underwent intubation and mechanical ventilation, 21 patients (28.0%) received gastric lavage, seven patients (9.3%) received activated charcoal, and seven patients (9.3%) received sodium bicarbonate. In our ED, 15 patients (20.0%) required intubation and mechanical ventilation, 22 patients (29.3%) received gastric lavage with activated charcoal, 53 patients (70.7%) received sodium bicarbonate, three patients (4.0%) required vasopressors, and one patient (1.3%) received flumazenil. Overall, 56 patients (74.7%) survived to discharge, while 19 patients (25.3%) had poor outcomes, including in-hospital death or discharge against medical advice.

Factors associated with poor outcomes are detailed in Table 3. Patients with poor outcomes had a higher median amitriptyline dose than survivors (474.47 mg, IQR: 590 vs 266.43 mg, IQR: 375 mg; p = 0.028). Patients who required intubation and mechanical ventilation had poorer outcomes on univariate analysis (OR: 40.00, 95% CI: 4.53–352.90; p < 0.001), likely reflecting greater poisoning severity rather than an effect of intubation itself. In multivariate analysis, intubation and mechanical ventilation remained significantly associated with poor outcomes (adjusted OR: 36.00, 95% CI: 3.93–329.98; p = 0.002). Sodium bicarbonate administration was more frequent among patients with poor outcomes on univariate analysis (OR: 2.74, 95% CI: 1.71–10.58; p = 0.044), but this association was not significant after adjustment (adjusted OR: 1.41, 95% CI: 0.33–5.93; p = 0.642). Lower GCS scores were associated with higher isolated amitriptyline dose (Kruskal–Wallis H = 10.944, df = 1, p = 0.001) and higher total TCA/polydrug dose (Kruskal–Wallis H = 6.887, df = 1, p = 0.009) (Figs 1 and 2). No significant association was observed between hospital stay category and isolated amitriptyline dose. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated significantly lower survival in the amitriptyline overdose group than in the other TCA overdose group (log-rank test, χ² = 5.702, df = 1, p = 0.017) (Fig. 3).

In this retrospective study, amitriptyline was the most frequently implicated TCA, interpersonal conflict was the most common precipitating factor, altered sensorium was the most frequent clinical presentation, and poor outcomes were associated with higher amitriptyline dose and markers of severe poisoning, particularly the need for intubation and mechanical ventilation. Although TCA poisoning is less common than many other drug overdoses, it remains clinically important because severe overdose can cause life-threatening neurological depression, seizures, QRS prolongation, ventricular dysrhythmias, hypotension, and death.6,7,13–15

The predominance of young to middle-aged women in our cohort is consistent with our studies on DSH, where self-poisoning is commonly reported among females with underlying psychosocial stressors and interpersonal conflict.4,16–18 Interpersonal issues were the leading trigger for overdose in our study, similar to patterns observed in other Indian ED cohorts evaluating DSH and self-poisoning behavior.16–19 Amitriptyline was the most common TCA involved in overdose in our study, followed by imipramine and dothiepin. Similar trends have been described in published toxicology literature, where amitriptyline remains one of the most commonly implicated TCAs in overdose because of its widespread availability and continued clinical use despite the increasing prescription of SSRIs.6,7,9,20 Tricyclic antidepressants continue to carry disproportionately high morbidity and mortality because of their narrow therapeutic index, sodium-channel blockade, and anticholinergic effects.

Neurological manifestations were prominent in our cohort, with altered mental status and drowsiness being the most common presenting features. Lower GCS scores were associated with higher isolated amitriptyline dose and higher total TCA/polydrug dose, suggesting that increasing overdose severity was associated with greater CNS depression. Previous studies have similarly shown that severe CNS depression, seizures, respiratory compromise, and coma are important indicators of severe TCA toxicity and are associated with increased intensive care utilization and mortality.8,13,21 Cardiovascular toxicity remains the most feared complication of TCA poisoning. Tricyclic antidepressants block myocardial fast sodium channels, resulting in QRS widening, corrected QT interval (QTc) prolongation, hypotension, ventricular arrhythmias, and, in severe cases, cardiac arrest.10,14,22 In our cohort, QRS or QTc prolongation was observed in a subset of patients, highlighting the importance of early electrocardiographic monitoring and close observation in suspected TCA overdose. Previous studies have demonstrated that electrocardiographic abnormalities, particularly QRS prolongation, are associated with increased toxicity severity and adverse outcomes.

Patients who required intubation and mechanical ventilation had poorer outcomes; however, this should be interpreted as a marker of severe poisoning rather than evidence that intubation itself caused poor outcomes. In multivariate analysis, intubation and mechanical ventilation remained significantly associated with poor outcomes, suggesting that patients with severe neurological depression and airway compromise were at greater risk. Similarly, the higher median amitriptyline dose observed among patients with poor outcomes supports the relationship between increasing overdose severity and worse clinical outcomes. Sodium bicarbonate was administered frequently, particularly in patients with more severe clinical presentations. Although sodium bicarbonate administration was associated with poor outcomes on univariate analysis, it was not independently associated with poor outcomes after multivariate adjustment. This likely reflects confounding by indication, where sodium bicarbonate was preferentially administered to patients with greater poisoning severity rather than acting as an independent risk factor itself. Published literature supports sodium bicarbonate as an important component of supportive management in TCA toxicity, particularly in the presence of QRS widening, hypotension, arrhythmias, or severe cardiovascular instability.7,20,23,24

These findings should be interpreted within the limitations of a retrospective observational study. Higher amitriptyline dose, depressed sensorium, and the need for airway support were associated with poor outcomes; however, causality cannot be inferred.

This study has several limitations. Many cases were excluded because of incomplete documentation. In non-verbal patients, history was obtained from bystanders, making the exact dose and co-ingestion difficult to confirm. Alcohol levels were not routinely measured, limiting pharmacokinetic interpretation. Several patients were discharged against medical advice, preventing complete outcome follow-up. Time to presentation was inconsistently recorded, and the single-center design may limit generalizability and introduce referral bias.

Tricyclic antidepressant poisoning remained associated with significant neurological and cardiovascular toxicity in this cohort, with amitriptyline being the most frequently implicated agent. Poor outcomes were associated with higher amitriptyline dose, depressed sensorium, and the need for intubation and mechanical ventilation, likely reflecting greater poisoning severity. These findings should be interpreted within the limitations of a retrospective single-center observational study.
