# Nationwide Survey of Association Between Fever and Epileptic Seizure in CDKL5 Deficiency Disorder Revealed Therapeutic Implications.
**Authors:** Wang S, Du J, Liu J, Hu D, Wang H, Man X, Gao L, Hu S, Meng X, Zhao H, Hu M, Yang Y, Xiong Z, Ren L
**Journal:** Ann Clin Transl Neurol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/acn3.70517](https://doi.org/10.1002/acn3.70517)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: CDKL5 deficiency disorder (CDD) is a rare, severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. There is a pressing need to develop effective and sustainable therapeutic strategies. We aimed to investigate the causal association between febrile episodes and epileptic seizures for therapeutic implications in CDD patients.

METHODS: The study was a nationwide, cross-sectional survey on CDD patients in China (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06663163). Detailed phenotypic and genotypic data were collected through an online questionnaire with uploaded original medical records, genetic testing results, and peri-fever seizure diaries. The primary outcome was changes in epileptic seizure frequency during and post-fever phases compared to a 1-month pre-fever phase based on seizure diaries.

RESULTS: Between October 2024 and December 2024, we received 131 questionnaires. Forty-seven questionnaires were removed after excluding duplicates and missing data. Ultimately, 84 eligible participants with complete uploads were included, from 26 of 34 (76.5%) province-level regions in China. Among these, 47 (56.0%) patients had significant decreased seizure frequency only during febrile episodes, and 27 (32.1%) patients with daily seizures achieved at least a seizure-free day. Notably, 20 patients (23.8%) exhibited post-fever seizure reduction: 12 (25.5%) for 3 days to 1 week, and 6 (12.8%) for more than 2 weeks (maximum > 40 days). The effect was independent of patients' clinical and genetic characteristics.

INTERPRETATION: Our findings, for the first time, revealed that fever-related seizure reduction is a distinctive and prevalent genotype-phenotype for CDD, thereby providing evidence for further fundamental research into the underlying mechanisms and offering potential clinical implications for seizure control in CDD.

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs) comprise a heterogeneous group of the most severe and devastating epilepsies, characterized by refractory epileptic seizures that typically begin in infancy, resulting in a significant seizure burden, intellectual disability, and global developmental delays [1]. Cyclin‐dependent kinase‐like 5 (CDKL5) deficiency disorder (CDD) has been recognized as a rare and severe DEE, caused by loss‐of‐function mutations in the CDKL5 gene located on the X chromosome (Xp22.13), accounting for approximately 1 case per 40,000–60,000 live births [2, 3, 4]. Since its first description in 2003 [5], great efforts have been directed toward alleviating the drug‐resistant seizures.

Current treatment strategies for CDD remain constrained, with considerable variability in response among patients and inevitably provide temporary relief, including antiseizure medications (ASMs), ketogenic diet, and surgical interventions [6, 7]. Notably, Knight et al. [8] reported the results of ganaxolone treatment in CDD patients in 2022, demonstrating a 30.7% overall improvement rate in motor seizure frequency. Adjunctive soticlestat produced comparable efficacy, with 27.3% of patients achieving seizure reduction [9]. Therapeutic responses to cannabidiol and fenfluramine are highly variable, and access remains limited worldwide [6]. Accordingly, addressing the necessity for safe and more effective therapeutic interventions for CDD is increasingly urgent.

Characterizing the genotype–phenotype correlations can provide new insights into the treatment of CDD. In recent years, we have noted, through communications with parents and caregivers at CDD Patient‐Clinician Exchange Forums in China, an interesting trend in children with CDD: the occurrence of fever appears to correlate with a significant reduction of seizure burden. While the impact of fever remains heterogeneous across epilepsies, this reduction is contrary to the canonical view that fever induces seizures acknowledged for centuries observed in stereotypical febrile seizures [10, 11], Dravet syndrome [12, 13], PCDH19‐related epilepsy [14, 15], and other diseases caused by mutations in sodium channels [16, 17, 18]. However, the critical question of whether the paradoxical phenomenon that fever can diminish seizure frequency is prevalent in CDD, especially conferring therapeutic benefits, remains unknown.

To determine whether fever can indeed mitigate seizure frequency and potentially offer therapeutic benefits for CDD patients, we conducted a nationwide, cross‐sectional online survey in China. We investigated the genotype–phenotype spectrum of CDD, particularly focusing on the causal association between febrile episodes and epileptic seizures. Our study may facilitate fundamental research investigations into the underlying mechanisms and offer potential clinical implications for seizure control in CDD.

We conducted a nationwide, cross‐sectional survey from October to December 2024 in China. An online questionnaire investigating the relationship between fever and epileptic seizures was distributed via the WeChat (a popular social media platform in mainland China) groups of “CDKL5 Baby Care Family,” China's largest CDD patient community under the Chinese Organization for Rare Disorders (https://www.cord.org.cn).

Patients were included in the study if they fulfilled the subsequent criteria: (1) A diagnosis of CDKL5 deficiency disorder, including molecular confirmation of a pathogenic CDKL5 variant and exhibiting epileptic seizures; (2) Parents or guardians are able to read the questionnaire and understand the purpose and content of the study; (3) Parents or guardians are willing to provide complete source data and related original medical records after agreeing and filling out the survey questionnaire online. Key exclusion criteria included: (1) Patients who had any history of previous brain disease (including trauma, encephalitis, etc.) that were likely to precipitate seizures; (2) Patients who presented any clinical condition that in the opinion of the principal investigator made the patient not suitable to participate in the survey.

All participants were volunteered and informed of their rights and interests before filling out the questionnaire. E‐consent was provided for all patients by their guardians or legal representatives. The consent information specified that uploaded information would be kept confidential, analyzed statistically, and reported only in fully anonymized form, with no identifiable personal information disclosed. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines and received ethical approval from the Medical Ethical Committee of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing, China. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06663163).

We invited families of CDD patients through the WeChat platform. The questionnaire was designed via the Wenjuanxing platform, a widely accepted and validated platform in China for survey data collection (https://www.wjx.cn, ID: 256528960). Once participants agree and scan a specific quick response (QR) code generated by Wenjuanxing, they can readily read and respond to the 8‐min online questionnaire, allowing for its convenient completion on their electronic devices (mobile phones, computers, tablets, etc.) at home. Withdrawal was permitted at any time.

Participants were required to offer related original medical records (video of epileptic seizures, EEG, neurodevelopmental profiles, medical history records, and others), genetic testing results, and peri‐fever seizure diaries covering at least 1 month before, during, and after febrile episodes during the survey; otherwise, the participant was classified as having missing data.

The questionnaire comprised four sections as follows: (1) Patients' basic demographic information, including age, gender and home address. (2) Clinical information, including age of seizure onset, seizure frequency and seizure types at baseline, current ASMs use, neurodevelopmental impairments including motor, fine motor and speech; All outcomes based on medical records and physician assessment. Seizure types were classified based on the International League Against Epilepsy guidelines and definitions [19]. (3) Genetic testing results about chromosome location, mutations' sites and types, and the identification of de novo mutations; Gene variants outcomes were sourced from whole genome or whole exome sequencing reports furnished by certified genetic companies as provided by the patient's family. To account for the heterogeneity of genetic reports across different centers, categorization and analysis were based on the exon positions provided in the original reports. (4) Fever profiles (defined as body temperature > 38°C), including experiences of febrile episodes, febrile etiologies, peak body temperature during febrile episodes, duration of febrile episodes and fever‐related seizure reduction, and seizure frequency changes during and after febrile episodes; Questionnaires were retrospectively collected from caregivers; the complete design is detailed in Supporting Information.

At the design level, the web‐based instrument (Wenjuanxing) employed conditional skip logic and mandatory field restrictions to prevent logical inconsistencies and item non‐response. Following data collection, a rigorous, multi‐tiered validation process was implemented. First, raw data were adjudicated by a specialized panel of pediatric and adult neurologists to ensure clinical plausibility. Second, invalid questionnaires were systematically excluded based on the following criteria: (1) Duplicate submissions, identified via cross‐referencing participant IDs, IP addresses, and demographic variables; (2) Incomplete verification, defined as the failure to upload corroborating source documents (original medical records, genetic testing reports, or peri‐fever seizure diaries); and (3) Logical inconsistencies, such as contradictory responses regarding fever history (such as selecting both “I have experienced febrile episode” and “no” for the highest recorded temperature of febrile episode) or clinically implausible temporal sequences. Suspected erroneous data points were reviewed by two independent investigators. To resolve ambiguities, follow‐up verification was conducted by certified medical staff via telephone or text‐based messaging. Finally, a random subset of 10% of entries underwent manual review to assess internal consistency.

The primary outcome was the changes in fever‐related seizure frequency, which were classified based on the percentage change from baseline (1 month preceding the onset of fever) to observation time points. In the questionnaire, seizure frequency changes in CDD patients were categorized into three distinct fever‐related response profiles: decreased seizures (more than 25% reduction), increased seizures (more than 25% increase), and insignificant change (less than 25% change). The seizure frequency changes were determined by the peri‐fever seizure diary. Secondary outcomes included the duration of post‐fever seizure reduction, and the proportion of patients with at least a seizure‐free day due to fever.

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the characteristics of the participants and their responses to the survey. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (SD) or median (IQR) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to evaluate the data normal distribution. The Kruskal–Wallis test, Chi‐square test, or Fisher's exact test were used to test group differences. Statistical tests were two‐sided, and we considered p < 0.05 to show a significant difference. The statistical analysis was done in SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Between October and December 2024, we received 131 questionnaires. Forty‐seven questionnaires were removed after excluding duplicates and missing data (unavailability of original medical records, genetic testing results, and peri‐fever seizure diaries). Finally, 84 eligible patients with e‐consents and complete uploads were included, yielding a 64.1% valid response rate (Figure 1A). Participants came from 26 out of 34 (76.5%) provinces and municipalities directly under the central government and autonomous regions in China (Figure 1B).

The clinical features of these 84 patients are summarized in Table 1. The age distribution of patients ranged from 0.5 to 11 years. The study population exhibited a female‐to‐male ratio of approximately 3.9:1. The median onset age of patients was 2 months, with 1/3 of patients experiencing seizure onset at or before 1 month. At baseline, we examined seizure frequency and types; The majority of patients reported a seizure frequency exceeding 30 times per month in our cohort. The seizure types varied, with 54 (64.3%) patients experiencing epileptic spasms and 42 (50.0%) having tonic–clonic (Table S1). Of the 84 patients, 62 (73.8%) received polytherapy (two ASMs and above), and 45 (53.6%) were currently using sodium valproate (VPA) (Table S2). Neurodevelopmental impairments including motor, fine motor and speech domains were reported by caregivers in all patients.

The genetic profiles of our CDD population are detailed in Figure 2 and Table S3. Within this cohort, CDKL5 mutations were distributed across exons 2–18, with the highest frequency observed in exons 8 and 12. Structurally, 52 (61.9%) patients had mutations located in the catalytic domain and 32 (38.1%) were located in the C‐terminus. Genetic testing identified 5 mutation types: nonsense, missense, frameshift, splicing, and deletion. In this cohort, missense mutation predominated (33 [39.3%]), followed by frameshift mutation (22 [26.2%]) (Figure 3). All identified mutations were de novo.

Among the 84 CDD patients, 47 (56.0%) showed a significant decrease in seizure frequency only during febrile episodes despite heterogeneous etiologies including cold, upper respiratory infections, and gastroenteritis (Figure 1C). The median duration of febrile episodes was 2 days (IQR: 2–3). No specific infections associated with decreased seizure frequency were identified in any of the patients. The impact of fever occurred in every febrile episode in 22 (46.8%) patients, in most episodes (> 75%) in 18 (38.3%) patients, and occasionally (< 25%) in 7 (14.9%) patients. Twenty‐seven (32.1%) patients who had daily seizures nearly achieved a seizure‐free day during fever episodes. Significantly, among the 47 patients who experienced a reduction in seizure frequency, 20 patients (42.6%) exhibited post‐fever reduction in seizure frequency. This effect persisted for a duration ranging from 3 days to 1 week in 12 patients (25.5%), while 6 (12.8%) demonstrated a sustained impact lasting beyond 2 weeks, with the longest duration exceeding 40 days (Figure 1D). Meanwhile, 19 (22.6%) experienced increased seizure frequency, and 18 (21.4%) showed insignificant change. Our analyses showed a lack of significant differences (p > 0.05) in clinical and genetic characteristics among all three groups classified by varying responses in seizure frequency due to fever (Table 1 and Tables S1 and S2).

To our knowledge, this study is the first to identify a previously unrecognized phenotype in CDD, in which febrile episodes were causally associated with seizure reduction. Over 50% of patients experienced fever‐related seizure reduction in CDD patients, indicating significant implications for treatment. Most importantly, such novel effect appears to be independent of clinical and genetic characteristics, revealing a distinctive genotype–phenotype of CDD.

Our study relied on social media that have revolutionized the management of rare diseases. Social media has become an indispensable part of people's daily activities for interpersonal communication over years [20]. WeChat is the widest mobile instant communication application used in mainland China. The use of WeChat for epidemiological surveys and communication offers unprecedented opportunities for us to efficiently gather patients with rare diseases, overcoming traditional geographic, resource, and informational barriers [21, 22].

In our cohort, the median age of onset for CDD patients was approximately 2 months consistent with previous studies, reflecting the early‐onset characteristic of the disease [2]. The predominant seizure types observed were epileptic spasms, followed by tonic–clonic seizures, which aligns with the documented seizure semiology in CDD [2]. Although seizure type alternations had been documented in several patients after febrile episodes, the linkage between these changes and fever is indeterminate. Notably, all patients presented with generalized neurodevelopmental impairments. The prevalence and severity of clinical phenotype increase with age [23]. The high prevalence of drug‐resistant epilepsy in CDD patients necessitates the exploration of novel and effective interventions. We observed that the distinctive phenotypes manifested independently of the utilization of ASMs. Our data showed that fever‐related seizure changes were not determined by clinical profiles.


CDKL5 encodes a serine threonine kinase protein that consists of a catalytic domain and a long C‐terminal extension. The catalytic domain is responsible for the modulation of catalytic activity and the C‐terminal accounts for the sub‐nuclear proteins' localization and the negative catalytic activity regulation [24]. Variants that influence the kinase activity of CDKL5 play a role in protein function and the severity of phenotypic manifestations [25]. Despite our survey presented varying distributions of mutation locations and types, the analysis failed to establish a link between the mutation location or types and the fever‐related seizure frequency changes.

The nationwide survey revealed that fever‐related seizure reduction is a prevalent and distinctive phenotype in CDD. More importantly, fever‐related seizure reduction exhibited a prolonged and sustained effect in a proportion of patients. Given the profound epileptic burden typically observed in CDD, this significant reduction of seizure frequency appears to transcend mere disease variability and is undocumented in previous natural history studies [4, 6, 26], strongly suggesting the novel, temperature‐sensitive physiological mechanism that warrants further rigorous investigation for its potential role in treatment of CDD.

The underlying mechanisms by which fever reduces seizure frequency in CDD remain unclear. Classically, genetic and inflammatory factors influence both the onset and outcomes of fever‐related epilepsy, with mutations that impact ion channel functionality rendering individuals particularly susceptible to seizure [27]. Elevated temperature accelerates synaptic vesicle (SV) recycling and enlargement, potentially contributing to the occurrence of seizure [28]. We hypothesize that fever may modulate temperature‐sensitive signaling pathways and metabolic processes, or influence immune responses [29, 30]. Additionally, this effect may involve alterations in temperature‐responsive gene expression unique to CDD. Further fundamental research is needed to elucidate the precise underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of fever‐related seizure reduction.

Our study has several limitations. First, despite the availability of source data, retrospective bias and selection bias cannot be entirely excluded. Second, our study was a descriptive study to delineate genotype–phenotype correlations, the prevalence and related factors of seizure frequency changes due to fever in CDD patients, without conducting experiments to explore underlying mechanisms behind this novel phenomenon. Future studies in appropriate animal models and investigations into biomarkers correlating with clinical seizure responses are needed. Finally, while the questionnaire was open to all, patients who had seizure frequency changes due to fever may have been more inclined to respond, potentially leading to an overestimation of prevalence. Further validation in prospective, multicenter and multinational studies with larger sample sizes are needed.

In contrast to the prevailing concept of fever induces seizures, our nationwide survey has identified fever‐related seizure reduction as a distinctive genotype–phenotype in CDD. Moreover, our findings pave the way for future fundamental research into the underlying mechanisms, and might provide potential clinical implications for seizure control in CDD.
