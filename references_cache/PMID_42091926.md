# Bridging the gap: an emerging link between tubulinopathies and ciliopathies.
**Authors:** Steiman S, Omer S, Pastore S, Ivakine EA
**Journal:** NPJ Genom Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41525-026-00576-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41525-026-00576-x)

## Content

Genetic neurodevelopmental disorders arise from variants in genes critical for brain development, including those causing ciliopathies and tubulinopathies. Both disorders are linked to brain malformations, intellectual disability, and motor impairments. Since cilia rely on microtubules for ciliogenesis, axoneme formation, and intraflagellar transport, defects in tubulin genes can directly disrupt ciliary function. This review highlights emerging evidence connecting these two disease groups and reveals how tubulin variants impact ciliopathy mechanisms.

Cilia are microtubule-based organelles essential for a wide range of biological processes such as transduction of signalling pathways, cell migration, fluid movement, and developmental patterning. Cilia are classified into primary and motile types based on structural differences, particularly the presence or absence of a central microtubule pair within the cilium core structure, the axoneme1. Primary cilia have a 9 + 0 microtubule arrangement, a structure that lacks the central pair of microtubules required for motility and is present on the surface of nearly all vertebrate cells, with few exceptions such as lymphocytes and red blood cells2. Enriched in receptors and ion channels within the membrane, primary cilia function as a sensory hub for critical signalling pathways such as Sonic Hedgehog (Shh) and Wnt3–5. In contrast, motile cilia exhibit a 9 + 2 architecture and are found in high abundance in tissues such as the respiratory epithelium, reproductive tract, and ependymal cells in the brain ventricles, where they play essential roles in mucociliary clearance and cerebrospinal fluid movement6,7.

Disruption of ciliary function gives rise to a diverse class of inherited disorders known as ciliopathies, including Joubert syndrome, primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), Bardet-Biedl syndrome, Meckel-Gruber syndrome, nephronophthisis, and Jeune asphyxiating thoracic dystrophy, among others8,9. Ciliopathies are genetically heterogeneous, having linked to over 180 risk genes; they often involve multiple organ systems, and present with overlapping phenotypes9,10. While most ciliopathies result from mutations in genes encoding structural or regulatory components of the cilium, recent studies suggest that mutations in tubulin, the building blocks of microtubule filaments, may also contribute to disease pathogenesis11–13. Recent reports using whole-genome sequencing have begun to bridge the longstanding gap between tubulinopathies and ciliopathies by implicating β-tubulin isotypes TUBB and TUBB4B in the dysfunction of both primary and motile cilia, respectively12. This emerging convergence calls for a re-valuation of how we categorize and study cilia-related disorders. Furthermore, the overlap carries potential diagnostic implications, as atypical or undiagnosed ciliopathy cases may be explained by mutations in tubulin genes. Therapeutically, strategies that modulate microtubule dynamics, such as stabilization or regulation of microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs), may hold promise for treatment of relevant cilia disease categories17,18.

The detailed architecture of primary and motile cilia structures has been extensively reviewed elsewhere19–22. Despite their functional diversity, both cilia types share a conserved microtubule-based structure, the axoneme, anchored to a centriole-modified basal body that is located at the base of cilia, serving as a foundation for their formation and enclosed by the plasma membrane. While primary cilia exhibit a 9 + 0 microtubule configuration, motile cilia possess a 9 + 2 arrangement with nine outer microtubule doublets surrounding a central pair6,7,19. The central microtubule pair that regulates beat frequency and waveform is linked to the outer doublets via radial spokes. Surrounding the nine outer doublets are the ATP-hydrolysing motor complex dynein-2 (also known as axonemal dynein) that generates the mechanical forces between microtubules to power the strokes for fluid propulsion23. Though structurally distinct, both primary and motile cilia membranes are enriched with receptors and ion channels that sense environmental cues and modulate responses to environmental changes19,22. Both motile and primary cilia rely on IFT to shuttle proteins bidirectionally along the axoneme. Anterograde trafficking is driven by kinesin motor proteins to deliver molecules and cargo from the base to the tip of the cilium, while retrograde trafficking mediated by dynein motors returns compartments back to the cytoplasm for recycling and signal maintenance1,24.

Primary cilia are solitary organelles that act as critical sensory and signalling hubs. Signalling of pathways such as Sonic hedgehog, Wnt/β-catenin, Receptor Tyrosine Kinase (RTK) signalling, TGF-β/BMP, Notch, mTOR, and Hippo rely on enriched receptors and ion channels embedded in the ciliary membrane5,25,26. Primary cilia regulate neurogenesis, neuronal migration, and brain patterning during development and influence adult neuroplasticity27. For example, hedgehog signalling is crucial for maintaining neural stem cell populations in the adult brain28. In the kidney, primary cilia are enriched in mechanosensitive and calcium-permeable ion channels to detect the flow of urine in the nephron tubes29. The dysregulation of ciliary ion channels has been implicated with ciliopathies such as Bardet-Biedl syndrome and polycystic kidney disease (PKD)30,31. In addition, primary cilia play roles in the photoreceptors of the retina to transport phototransduction proteins, in the endothelium of blood vessels to sense shear stress, and in the pancreatic islet cells to regulate insulin and glucose sensing32–34.

Motile cilia serve diverse and tissue-specific roles such as facilitating endolymph flow in the cochlea for sound detection, aiding in oocyte transport towards the uterus in the fallopian tubes, and supporting sperm movement in the efferent ducts7,35,36. Beating coordination is achieved through metachronal waves, which ensure a unidirectional flow that is crucial for mucus clearance in the lungs and cerebrospinal fluid circulation in the brain6,7,36.

Primary and motile cilia are both crucial to establish left-right patterning and the asymmetric position of internal organs or “situs solitus”37. They are also present during embryogenesis at the embryonic node or “Hensen’s node” to establish left-right organization37,38. In the central region of the node, motile cilia generate unidirectional leftward extracellular flow through a clockwise rotation at approximately 600 revolutions per minute39,40. Primary cilia sense the flow generated by the motile cilia and relay mechanical and chemical cues to the lateral plate mesoderm, in turn leading to the asymmetric expression of key genes such as Nodal41,42.

Ciliopathies represent a heterogeneous group of genetic disorders resulting from mutations in genes that impair ciliary structure or function. Most ciliopathies exhibit an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, with some exceptions that display autosomal dominant and X-linked inheritance8,43–45. These disorders are classified as primary ciliopathies, motile ciliopathies, secondary ciliopathies, and atypical ciliopathies, based on the subtype of cilia affected and the underlying genetic etiology43. However, significant overlap in clinical features between the categories of ciliopathies often complicates diagnoses45. Primary ciliopathies result from dysfunction in the primary cilia, typically affecting multiple organ systems including the brain, kidneys, retina, and liver. Primary ciliopathies also display shared phenotypes that can be present in patients, such as retinal degeneration, cerebellar ataxia, cystic kidney disease, polydactyly, and intellectual disability10,19. Currently, there are over 240 genes associated with 34 distinct primary ciliopathy disorders10,43. The encoded proteins localize to the cilia basal body, transition zone, and ciliary membrane, reflecting the essential roles these domains play in ciliary function and signalling10,45.

Primary ciliopathies range in severity, with diseases such as Meckel syndrome, lethal oral-facial-digital syndromes, and lethal skeletal ciliopathies exhibiting increased severity relative to ciliopathies with milder phenotypes, such as to Joubert syndrome, nephronophthisis, Senior-Løken syndrome, and BBS that show more moderate and milder phenotypes41. Diagnosis is often challenging due to overlapping phenotypes and specific patient mutations in ciliary genes45. For example, BBS has been linked to mutations in over 26 genes with the most common mutations in the BBS1 (Bardet-Biedl syndrome 1) and BBS10 genes46. Definitive diagnoses for ciliopathy patients may require further insight in addition to sequencing. For example, Joubert syndrome is characterized by a hallmark brain malformation known as a molar tooth sign that requires a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to detect. Despite the progress in gene discovery, a significant proportion of patients remain without a definitive diagnosis, indicating a subset of genes yet to be identified13.

In contrast to primary ciliopathies, patients with motile ciliopathies often present recurrent respiratory infections, laterality defects, and infertility9,12. Currently, approximately 75 genes have been associated with motile ciliopathies, with the most common motile ciliopathy being primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD)9. PCD impacts the structure and function of motile cilia that line the airways, brain ependyma, reproductive tracts, and the transient embryonic node. Mutations associated with PCD follow autosomal recessive or, less frequently, X-linked recessive inheritance47. Genes that have been associated with PCD include Dynein regulatory complex subunit 1 (DRC1), Growth Arrest Specific 8 (GAS8), a component of the nexin-dynein regulatory complex, and Axonemal central pair apparatus protein (HYDIN) that is involved in central pair formation. More recently, mutations in FOXJ1 and TUBB4B have expanded the genetic spectrum of PCD, further highlighting the complexity of motile ciliopathy causative genes. Similar to primary ciliopathies, there is a population of patients lacking definitive diagnoses, and current genetic testing for PCD can only identify genetic defects in ~70% of patients47,48. To date, no therapies can correct ciliary dysfunction. Approaches to alleviate certain symptoms, such as mucociliary clearance, are similar to those already used to treat cystic fibrosis, namely chest percussion and postural drainage48.

Secondary ciliopathies result from mutations in genes that impact ciliary function indirectly, as they do not encode genes that directly localize to the cilium. This disruption in cilia is often due to defects in upstream regulators, trafficking pathways, or transcription factors43. For example, MCIDAS (multiciliate differentiation and DNA synthesis-associated cell cycle protein) is not a motile cilia structural protein but is essential for multiciliogenesis. Mutations in genes such as MCIDAS can lead to ciliary dysfunction and mimic classical ciliopathy phenotypes49,50.

Atypical ciliopathies present with a wide range of phenotypes seen across many ciliopathies or involve symptoms that overlap with both primary and motile ciliopathies. These cases defy conventional classification and may be caused by hypomorphic mutations in ciliary genes or by mutations in shared machinery proteins such as basal body regulators and IFT proteins43. Atypical ciliopathies highlight the phenotypic continuum of ciliary disorders and the importance of recognizing ciliopathies as a spectrum and not rigid classes. The category of atypical ciliopathies has expanded recently with new associations of β-tubulin genes with both motile and primary ciliopathy phenotypes12,13.

Many ciliopathies, including Joubert syndrome, nephronophthisis, and Meckel-Gruber syndrome, are genetically heterogeneous, as mutations in a single gene can potentially result in diverse ciliopathy phenotypes. For example, TMEM67 mutations have been associated with three different primary ciliopathies10. Clinical diagnoses are further complicated by the frequent involvement of multiple organ systems such as the brain, eyes, and kidneys10,45. The overlapping phenotypes and spectrum of ciliopathies necessitate the involvement of bioinformaticians to interpret sequencing results. Despite technological advances and our increased understanding of the genetic etiologies of ciliopathies, a significant number of patients lack a definitive genetic diagnosis10,51,52.

Over the past two decades, significant progress has been made in the characterization of ciliopathies, driven by advances in whole-exome and genome sequencing in tandem with the annotation of cilia-related genes to specific disease phenotypes43. While the discovery of ciliopathy-associated genes has enhanced our understanding of ciliopathies, accurately linking specific genes and mutations to distinct ciliopathies remains challenging. This is largely due to the genetic heterogeneity of ciliopathies and the pleiotropic nature of many cilia-related proteins, which often participate in multiple cellular processes and signalling pathways5. In 2023, a team addressed this challenge by curating a human database called CiliaMiner, which classifies ciliopathies and related disorders based on the subcellular localization and molecular function of the disease-associated genes43. This functional-genomic framework provides a detailed understanding of the genetic etiologies of ciliopathies, aiming at improving and expediting current genetic diagnoses. There currently exists no cure for ciliopathies, and most therapeutic options are primarily limited to symptom management52.

Microtubules are dynamic cytoskeletal polymers composed of globular α- and β- tubulin subunits that assemble into an extensive filamentous network53. Microtubules are essential for subcellular organization and intracellular trafficking of vesicles and organelles, in addition to the formation of the bipolar mitotic spindle for proper chromosomal segregation during cell division. Microtubules form the core structural components of cilia and the flagella axoneme, and also serve as dynamic tracks to transport signalling molecules by specialized motor complexes: kinesins and dyneins53,54.

The structure of tubulin proteins is highly conserved among eukaryotes55. Tubulin genes are thought to have originated through gene duplication events in early eukaryotic ancestors, giving rise to six major families in humans: alpha (α), beta (β), gamma (γ), delta (δ), zeta (ζ), and epsilon (ε)55,56. Tubulin proteins belong to the GTPase superfamily, characterized by their ability to bind and hydrolyse guanosine triphosphate (GTP) into guanosine diphosphate (GDP)57,58. While homologs of α- and β-tubulin have been identified in bacteria (FtsZ), bacterial protofilaments typically consist of five linear strands instead of the 11–15 protofilaments, depending on the species and the cell type, found in eukaryotic microtubules56.

In humans, the majority of cells contain microtubules with 13 protofilaments that interact laterally to form the ~25 nm diameter hollow cylindrical structure53,59. The classification of tubulin genes in humans is complicated by the presence of numerous pseudogenes. Currently, there are nine α-tubulin, ten β-tubulin, two γ-tubulin, one δ-tubulin, and one ε-tubulin gene12,55. By comparison, the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae genome contains only two α- and one β-tubulin gene60. Although the α- and β-tubulin heterodimer is the fundamental unit of microtubules, other tubulin families have specialized roles. γ-tubulin forms the γ-tubulin ring complex, a critical structure for microtubule nucleation at the centrosome, which caps the minus (non-growing) end of microtubules61. δ-tubulin contributes to the formation and stability of the triplet microtubule in basal bodies and is required for the assembly and stability of centriolar microtubules. Mutations or deletions of ε-tubulin can lead to compromised microtubule structures, disrupted mitosis, and impairments in basal body formation62.

Microtubule assembly starts with heterodimer formation of α- and β-tubulin via chaperone-assisted folding before their integration end-to-end, resulting in polarized protofilaments. Protofilament formation requires many tubulin-specific chaperones (TBCs) and co-chaperone proteins, such as the cytosolic chaperonin CCT63. In addition to their end-to-end arrangement forming linear protofilaments, microtubules are stabilized by lateral interactions between adjacent protofilaments, which create a hollow cylindrical lattice54,64. Microtubules exhibit dynamic instability, a behaviour characterized by alternating phases of growth and shrinkage, interspersed with abrupt transitions called catastrophe (switch from growth to shrinkage) and rescue (switch from shrinkage to growth)65. Polymerization and depolymerization of microtubules are key mechanisms for generating mechanical forces in cells. These forces drive essential processes, such as chromosome segregation, organelle positioning, and cell motility54,66.

Microtubules serve as polarized tracks for the mechanoenzyme protein complexes and a platform for the binding of MAPs, which play crucial roles in IFT and subcellular organization54. Cytoplasmic dynein-1 (Dynein 1) and axonemal dynein-2 (Dynein 2) are the two major dynein families that transport cargo toward the minus end of microtubules67. Dynein proteins are large ATP-hydrolyzing motor proteins that facilitate trafficking along the cytoplasm and in the axoneme of cilia. In addition, in motile cilia, dynein generates sliding forces between the microtubule doublets to drive ciliary beating68,69. With the exception of KIFC, all kinesin motor family proteins move cargo anterogradely towards the microtubule plus-end70,71. Microtubule binding proteins (MBPs) are categorized by function: stabilizers, destabilizers, cross-linkers, end-binding proteins, and nucleators, and can play a wide range of important roles in development, mitosis, and stress response. MAPs are a subset of MBTPs that stabilize, bundle, or crosslink microtubules64.

Tubulin subunits undergo a variety of post-translational modifications (PTMs) to influence both microtubule function and interaction with MBPs54,55. The α- and β-tubulin families display about 63% sequence homology and 36-42% polypeptide identification, respectively (Fig. 3)72. The α- and β-tubulin C-terminal tails are highly variable between isotypes, and they play crucial roles in PTMs73,74. Most PTMs on α- and β-tubulin, such as polyglutamylation, detyrosination, and glycylation, occur at the unique C-terminal tails of the subunits75–77. However, PTMs can also occur on the luminal residues, such as K40 on α-tubulin, in addition to the outer surface78. Detyrosination, the removal of C-terminal tyrosine, can impair the binding of end-binding protein (CLIP170) while enhancing kinesin-1 activity79. Glutamylation and polyglutamylation, the addition of glutamate side chains, are mediated by tubulin tyrosine ligase-like (TTLL) enzymes and are enriched in neurons and cilia80. Knockout of TTLL1 in the mouse brain did not show a disruption in neuronal development, suggesting a tolerance to this modification74. Phosphorylation at S172 on β-tubulin isotypes is conserved and mediated by Cdk1, and is proposed to modulate mitotic microtubule dynamics81,82. Other kinases and PTMs have been shown to influence mitotic function, immune cell activity, and MTB interactions83,84. Tubulin subunits undergo a variety of PTMs that influence microtubule function and interaction with MAPs and motor proteins. PTMs are often enriched in specific subcellular compartments and domains and are thought to locally fine-tune microtubule properties to meet specific demands in the cell74. For example, in wound healing assays, microtubules oriented towards the wound edge in confluent monolayers are enriched in detyrosinated and acetylated tubulin85,86. Similarly, central spindle microtubules during mitosis are marked by detyrosination, glutamylation, and acetylation, whereas astral microtubules are not. Polyglycylation and polyglutamylation are rare, and in mammals are primarily restricted to axonemal structures in motile cilia and flagella82,86.

Tubulinopathies represent a spectrum of neurodevelopmental disorders primarily affecting brain formation and function. They stem from mutations in tubulin genes resulting in dysregulation in microtubule structure and dynamics66,87. In the nervous system, microtubules are vital for maintaining neuronal architecture, guiding neuron migration, supporting axon growth, and mediating synaptic transport. Furthermore, processes such as neuronal progenitor cell division are dependent on mitotic spindles, shaping the overall brain size and structure66. The spatial and temporal expression of tubulin isotypes is tightly regulated in neurons, indicating a distinct role in neuronal development88Pavone et al. (2023). describe the seven tubulin genes involved in cerebellar malformations: TUBA1A, TUBA8, TUBB2A, TUBB2B, TUBB3, TUBB, and the γ-tubulin isotype TUBG187,89. In addition, TUBA4A and TUBB4A have been associated with neurodegenerative diseases90–92. TUBB1 and TUBB8 have been implicated in diseases outside of the nervous system93,94. The majority of tubulinopathies are caused by de novo heterozygous missense mutations, typically inherited in an autosomal dominant manner92,95,96.

Disruption of microtubule assembly and stability impacts many critical brain developmental processes, such as axon guidance and neuronal migration, resulting in brain abnormalities88,97. The severities in the phenotypic spectrum of tubulinopathies range from isolated brain malformations to complex syndromes with multisystem involvement. However, there are many common neuroanatomical features associated with tubulinopathies. These include lissencephaly (smoothening of the cerebral cortex due to reduced or absent gyri) and polymicrogyria (the excessive and abnormal folding of the cerebellar cortex)15,98. Other brain malformations include agenesis or hypoplasia of the corpus callosum and cerebellar hypoplasia or dysplasia, which contribute to ataxia and impaired motor incoordination99,100. The severity of these malformations varies based on the specific mutation, the affected tubulin isotype, and the genetic background of the patient. For example, some patients present with isolated lissencephaly, while others exhibit a combination of structural malformations15,89,101. Diagnosis for fetal tubulinopathies, the most severe end of the tubulinopathy spectrum, has improved with the implementation of molecular sequencing, imaging, and fetal ultrasounds. While current literature lacks defined characteristics in the literature of fetal tubulinopathy hallmarks due to disease lethality and diagnostic constraints, key prenatal features have been identified102,103. These include enlarged germinal matrices, microlissencephaly (a kinked brainstem in severe tubulinopathy cases), asymmetric brainstem, and dysgenesis of the corpus callosum, among others, for more mild cases.

Beyond the structural abnormalities of the brain, tubulinopathies can clinically present with additional features. These can include motor and intellectual disability, which are more common and can correlate with the extent of brain malformation89. Epilepsy and frequent seizures in patients with lissencephaly or severe dysgyria are not universally present but occur in a subset of fewer than 30% of patients99,104,105. Other features such as visual impairment, ataxia, and poor motor coordination are also linked to malformations in the cerebellum. Patients with more profound malformations such as microlissencephaly often have severe hypotonia, absent motor control, and developmental delays15,105. Given their central role in mitosis, microtubules are major targets for chemotherapeutic drugs106. Drugs such as colchicine and colcemid bind to tubulin to prevent polymerization and, in turn, division of cancerous cells, while taxane drugs, such as paclitaxel, stabilize microtubules and prevent depolymerization106–108. These disruptions lead to mitotic arrest and apoptosis in cancer cells. However, drug resistance and toxicity remain clinical challenges when using these drugs109. Further understanding of tubulin dynamics and specific isotype roles may uncover new targets and strategies to improve drug therapy and overcome possible resistance.

Mutations in TUBB3 have been recognized for their impact on microtubule dynamics, specifically in neurons. TUBB3 is primarily expressed in the nervous system and is enriched during axon guidance and neuronal maturation110. When modelled in the yeast β-tubulin gene TUB2, pathogenic variants in TUBB3, including R262C and A302T, alter microtubule dynamic instability and reduce kinesin (Kip3) accumulation at the microtubule plus ends111. These mutations were proposed to impair axon guidance with preserved cortical neuronal migration, resulting in neurodevelopmental phenotypes that are distinct from other tubulinopathies112–114. TUBB2B is another isotype enriched during brain development115. Mutations in TUBB2B are associated with asymmetrical polymicrogyria and other cortical malformations and have been linked to fetal akinesia deformation sequence (FADS), a group of disorders with congenital malformations related to impaired fetal movement116. Missense mutations in TUBB2B have been shown to lead to disrupted cortical lamination and aberrant neuronal morphology in both human tissue and murine models115,117. Modelling of a TUBB2B patient mutation (C239F) showed that tubulin heterodimerization was impaired in a rabbit reticulocyte lysate with bovine brain tubulin, leading to accelerated rates of repolymerization, while a variant in TUBB (E401K) displayed disruptions in tubulin chaperone-mediated folding, in turn leading to disruptions in heterodimerization116,118. Despite TUBB mutations being less commonly reported in association with motor deficits and microcephaly, mice harbouring a TUBB E401K variant exhibited reduced brain size, impaired motor coordination, and deficits in sensorimotor gating103,119,120.

TUBA1A is essential for neuronal migration and is the most frequently mutated isotype in tubulinopathies. In humans, mutations in TUBA1A result in severe cortical malformations, including lissencephaly and pachygyria, due to impaired neuronal migration121,122. While some cases of TUBA1A tubulinopathies share common brain malformations such as brainstem hypoplasia, vermis hypoplasia, vermis anomaly, and cortical malformations, other malformations, such as polymicrogyria, may also be present103,123. The isotypes TUBB3, TUBA1A, and TUBA1B are all highly expressed in postmitotic differentiating neurons; however, unlike TUBB3 syndromes, mutations in TUBA1A and TUBB2B result in lissencephaly, pachygyria, and/or grey matter heterotopias117,124. Conversely, both humans and mice with pathogenic TUBB3 mutations do not show signs of cortical neuronal migration defects125,126. Similarly, a study of TUBA1A in non-mutant subjects, ascertained on the basis of agyria and pachygyria, failed to identify mutations in TUBB3 and instead identified mutations in TUBB2B111. This reinforces the idea that TUBB3 mutations primarily affect axon guidance rather than neuronal migration. Mutations in TUBA8 and TUBA4A have been linked to brain malformations with additional features, such as spinal-onset classical ALS, but their roles and severity of associated phenotypes are less well-defined compared to those of TUBA1A15,127,128. The higher representation of TUBA1A mutations may stem from its high expression in neuronal progenitors and postmitotic neurons122. In addition, mouse models harbouring TUBA1A pathogenic mutations (S140G and N102D) exhibit severe defects in cortical lamination and reduced perinatal survival, suggesting a non-redundant and unique role during brain development98.

The TUBG1 isotype, while critical for microtubule nucleation at the centrosome, has been associated with microcephaly and simplified gyral patterns when mutated15,89. The diagnostic imaging phenotypes associated with variants in TUBG1 overlap with variants found in other genes, such as LIS1 (Platelet-activating factor acetylhydrolase 1b regulatory subunit 1), leading to reduced white matter. These differences may be caused by the roles of γ-tubulins in microtubule nucleation89.

Both primary and motile cilia are microtubule-based organelles structured around a core axoneme that extends from the basal body, a modified centriole that anchors the cilium1. Microtubules are required for cilia formation, basal body migration, axoneme assembly, and vesicular transport129. While the specific isotypes of α- and β-tubulin that comprise motile and primary cilia have not been clearly identified, the stability, dynamics, and function of ciliary microtubules are highly dependent on tubulin PTMs. These modifications include glutamylation, glycylation, acetylation, detyrosination, tyrosination, and ubiquitination63,74,130.

Tubulin glutamylation, the addition of glutamate side chains to the C-terminal tails of α- and β-tubulin, is one of the most well-characterized PTMs in cilia131,132. This PTM is catalysed by a subset of tubulin tyrosine ligase-like (TTLL) enzymes133. Among these, specific enzymes such as TTLL1 and TTLL6, have been identified as critical for motile cilia function131,134,135. For example, in ependymal cilia in the brain, TTLL6 was shown to promote ciliary beating by modifying α-tubulin with long glutamate side chains136. Knockout of TTLL1 in murine models results in reduced tubulin glutamylation, structural disorganization of cilia, and phenotypes consistent with PCD80,131. Beyond structural roles, glutamylation also regulates beating behaviours through the modulation of dynein motors. This regulation is remarkably sensitive; despite the expression of multiple glutamylases in ciliated cells, depletion or knockout of a single enzyme is sufficient to cause ciliary defects in motile cilia137–139. The consequences of impaired glutamylation lead to photoreceptor cell degeneration140, and loss of the catalytic subunit of TTLL1 results in defective sperm axonemes and sterility in male murine models76.

The specific roles of glutamylases are highly context-dependent, varying across organisms and cell types. In mouse ependymal cilia, ciliary beating is regulated by the α-tubulin-specific polyglutamylase TTLL6, whereas in Tetrahymena, a similar function has been attributed to TTLL6A and TTLL6F135,136,141. In Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, flagellar beating is regulated by a homologue of TTLL9, which has no function in ciliary beating in mouse ependymal cells142. Tissue-specificity is also evident within the same organism. In mice, TTLL1 is important for motile airway cilia and is not detected in the ependymal layer138. Similarly, TTLL5, which plays no role in ciliary stability or beating in ependymal cilia, was found to be essential for sperm flagella function and for polyglutamylation in primary cilia of hTERT-RPE1 cells136,143.

Tubulin glycylation involves the addition of glycine residues to the C-terminal tails of tubulin and is primarily enriched on axonemal microtubules in cilia and flagella144. This modification is catalysed by TTLL3 and TTL8, which are essential for the maintenance rather than assembly of motile cilia. Glycylation is more predominant in mature cilia, and its depletion can lead to axonemal disassembly, suggesting a critical role in structural stabilization and cilia length76,136,144–146. The prominent glycylation activity in mature motile cilia indicates that this modification might not be required for ciliogenesis per se, but rather for maintaining axoneme stability once assembly is complete139,147. Interestingly, glutamylation and glycylation appear to be reciprocally regulated; overexpression of a glycylase such as TTLL3 reduces glutamylation levels, and vice versa. This balance may be necessary to preserve the structural integrity and proper function of axonemes76,148. Despite these insights, many aspects of tubulin glycylation roles remain unknown, largely because several modifying enzymes have yet to be discovered.

Acetylation of α-tubulin at L40 is commonly associated with stabilization of microtubules149–152. This modification has been linked to ciliary dynamics across multiple organisms. In C. reinhardtii, for example, α-tubulin is deacetylated during cilia resorption, coinciding with an increase in methionine synthase within the flagella153,154. In mammalian cells, aurora A kinase links cell cycle progression to ciliary disassembly by stimulation of HDAC6, a deacetylase that accelerates the requisite loss of the cilium before cell division155–157. Beyond acetylation, ubiquitination also plays a role in ciliary regulation. In C. reinhardtii, α-tubulin is polyubiquitinated during flagellar resorption, and these ubiquitinated proteins can accumulate in the flagella of mutants that are defective in retrograde IFT158,159. Some tubulin PTMs are irreversible, such as the removal of two amino acids at the C-terminal domain to generate ∆2-tubulin. This modification is most common in the mature microtubules of differentiated neurons and axonemes17,74,160. Its irreversible nature may point to an unexplored role in ciliary stability and maintenance. A recent study by Mollica et al. (2025) identified Tubulin Beta Class I (TUBB) as a novel Joubert syndrome-associated gene. Joubert syndrome, a primary ciliopathy, is diagnosed based on the presence of a hallmark radiological feature called the molar tooth sign for diagnosis10,161. This ciliopathy is genetically heterogeneous, and remarkably up to 30% of Joubert syndrome cases lack a defined genetic diagnosis, suggesting that additional undiscovered genes are potentially contributing to primary ciliopathies162. The authors reported a dominant de novo missense variant in TUBB, G308S, identified through whole-genome sequencing. Patient-derived fibroblasts and isogenic cell models harbouring this variant exhibited phenotypes characteristic of both tubulinopathy and a ciliopathy. These included a dysregulation in the microtubule landscape, increased acetylation in mutant microtubules, and impaired microtubule nucleation. Most notably, cells showed both a delay and reduction in primary cilia formation, a phenomenon never before observed in a tubulinopathy model.

To further explore any in vivo relevance of this newfound connection, Mollica et al. generated a chimeric murine model carrying the TUBB variant. These mice recapitulated several ciliopathy-associated features, including reduced primary cilia in the brain, renal cysts, and cystic dilation of Bowman’s capsule. Furthermore, a plasmid-based screen of clinically relevant pathogenic missense variants in TUBB presented additional mutations, E108K and E401K, as variants causing a reduction in primary cilia formation. This finding further reinforces the pathogenic potential of TUBB variants in the context of ciliopathy and establishes TUBB as a novel causative gene for the disease class.

Altogether, these findings suggest the existence of a subset of patients with Joubert syndrome-like features driven by primary cilium-dependent tubulinopathies. While more research is required to fully understand the mechanistic overlap between these disease classes, accumulating evidence supports a greater connection than previously understood.

While the majority of PCD cases have been historically attributed to mutations in dynein arm components, radial spokes, and axonemal structural proteins, emerging evidence suggests that core axonemal microtubule components, particularly β-tubulin isotypes, play a direct and previously unidentified role in motile cilia dysfunction48,163. Tubulin Beta Class IVb (TUBB4B) has been identified as a key isotype enriched in motile cilia axonemes12Dodd et al. (2024). demonstrated that mutations in TUBB4B impair axonemal microtubule assembly, resulting in defective ciliary beating and compromised fluid transport. They report that TUBB4B is the predominant β-tubulin isotype expressed in centrioles and axonemes of mouse respiratory and ependymal cells. Furthermore, functional disruption of TUBB4B modelled using patient mutations led to disorganized axonemal structure and impaired mucociliary clearance. Clinically, patients with TUBB4B mutations presented phenotypes resembling PCD, such as recurrent respiratory infections and hydrocephalus12. These findings raise the possibility that a subset of genetically undiagnosed PCD cases may, in fact, represent undiagnosed tubulinopathies, with TUBB4B mutations acting as a novel causative factor for motile cilia dysfunction. Sewell et al. (2024). further explored the specific role of TUBB4B in multiciliated cells. Using a TUBB4B knockout murine model, they showed that the absence of TUBB4B results in several abnormalities of motile cilia in the trachea and oviduct, without impacting the formation or function of primary cilia11. Their findings suggest that TUBB4B contributes unique regulatory functions specifically for motile ciliary axonemes. This further supports the emerging view that tubulin isotypes are not merely redundant proteins, but rather possess specialized, context-specific roles within the microtubule landscape.

Further linking TUBB4B dysfunction to motile ciliopathy phenotypes, Luscan et al. (2017) reported that dominant missense variants in TUBB4B are associated with Leber’s congenital amaurosis, a severe retinal dystrophy characterized by early-onset vision loss and linked to ciliary dysfunction in photoreceptor cells. While retinal phenotypes are more commonly associated with defects in primary cilia, these findings raise an interesting question about how TUBB4B mutations impact both motile and primary cilia, possibly through a shared mechanism of axonemal destabilization164,165. Additional studies have also implicated TUBB4B in the structural integrity of cochlear supporting cells and the development of motile cilia in the inner ear. Murine TUBB4B null models exhibited disorganized microtubules in pillar cells and defective motile cilia in the middle ear epithelium, leading to hearing impairments166.

Collectively, these studies highlight a paradigm shift in our understanding of motile cilia biology, wherein tubulin isotypes are emerging as causative factors of motile cilia structure and function. However, significant gaps remain, such as whether the observed phenotypes are driven solely by TUBB4B mutations or whether disruptions in tubulin PTM patterns contribute to pathogenesis. These findings warrant a broader reconsideration of tubulinopathies and the role of tubulin gene variants in the context of motile ciliopathies.

Although ciliopathies and tubulinopathies are categorized as distinct disease groups, they exhibit overlapping clinical features and face similar diagnostic and therapeutic barriers. Currently, these disease classes have no cure, and available treatments largely prioritize symptom management99,167. Compounding this challenge, the same mutation can result in variable expressivity and penetrance, complicating efforts to establish reliable genotype-phenotype correlations. MRIs remain a critical tool for diagnosing tubulinopathies and primary ciliopathies, such as Joubert syndrome, where the hallmark signs, like the molar tooth sign or cortical dysplasia, are needed to support diagnosis10,51,161. However, these features can considerably vary in both severity and subtlety, often leading to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis9,168,169. Whole-exome sequencing and whole-genome sequencing are valuable diagnostic tools, but they may miss pathogenic variants in non-coding regions, splicing elements, or epigenetic modifiers170–172. In tubulinopathies, functional validation of novel potential pathogenic variants remains particularly difficult due to the lack of in vitro and in vivo models173,174. Due to the essential roles of tubulin genes, many murine models harbouring tubulin mutations exhibit embryonic lethality, limiting the ability to study both neurodevelopmental and potential ciliary phenotypes such as kidney cysts. While chimeric mice were employed to study certain TUBB mutations, fully heterozygous models may be required to recapitulate the complete phenotype.

Currently, neither ciliopathies nor tubulinopathies benefit from disease-modifying therapies. In the case of tubulinopathies, restoring normal microtubule function is complicated by the essential, dosage-sensitive, and isotype-specific roles of tubulin subunits167,175. Unlike enzymatic disorders, where small molecules may restore partial function, correcting dysfunctional tubulin would require allele and isotype-specific intervention. Targeting a specific tubulin isotype or mutant allele can pose significant risks, particularly given that tubulin genes are highly expressed, share sequence redundancy, and are essential for neurodevelopment176. The tubulin code adds another layer of complexity that can limit therapeutic efficacy and suggests that certain isotypes or PTMs may require individualized therapies74,176,177. Additionally, most microtubule-targeting agents used in cancer therapy are neurotoxic and are unsuitable for long-term use178–180. High-throughput drug screening has the potential for identifying FDA-approved drugs that could be repurposed181,182. However, previous screens have been limited to cancerous cell lines, have identified drugs with off-target cell cycle arrest effects, and are constrained by challenges with image analyses183,184.

In ciliopathies, some promising therapeutic leads have emerged from targeting dysregulated signalling pathways downstream of primary cilia, such as Shh and Wnt185. For example, in nephronophthisis models, compounds that modulate these pathways have shown promise, though none have yet advanced to clinical trials186,187. High-throughput drug screens have identified hits that may enhance ciliogenesis or ciliary function, but robust in vivo validation remains a persistent bottleneck184. Generating accurate animal models for tubulinopathies has proven difficult, with some research groups turning towards conditional murine models or chimeric models13,120. Additionally, in mice, the context-dependent roles and reduced number of tubulin isotypes compared to humans may complicate modelling efforts and limit true patient recapitulation188,189.

Currently, no gene therapy exists for either tubulinopathies or ciliopathies. While CRISPR/Cas9, base editing, and prime editing technologies may be explored in the future, they face substantial obstacles, especially for potential delivery to the nervous system190,191. In tubulinopathies, dominant-negative mutations pose a major challenge, as traditional gene-replacement strategies are unable to revert the pathogenic allele98.

In the treatment of ciliopathies, AAV-based delivery has shown promise in the retina, particularly for Leber congenital amaurosis, but extending this success to other affected organs such as the kidney and lungs remains difficult190,192–194. Kidney-targeted gene therapy for nephronophthisis is under development but hurdles include achieving effective targeting, sustaining long-term expression, and avoiding toxicity195,196. Furthermore, since ciliary dysfunction disrupts multiple downstream signalling pathways in primary ciliopathies, restoring a single mutated protein may not be sufficient to reverse the phenotype.

Lastly, efforts to study individual tubulin isotypes for targeted drug design also face considerable challenges. Efforts to reconstitute functional α- and β-tubulin in bacterial systems have often failed due to the requirement of eukaryotic-specific folding cofactors and chaperones74. in vitro studies using post-translationally modified tubulin purified from HeLa cells have provided key insights into how specific PTMs, such as polyglutamylation, influence microtubule dynamics197,198. These approaches, while powerful, are technically challenging and are not yet scalable for variant screening or therapeutic development.

Historically, research on ciliopathies and tubulinopathies has proceeded along distinct trajectories. Ciliopathy studies have primarily focused on identifying cilia-related genes responsible for disease and characterizing the structural and signalling roles of cilia, particularly dyneins, IFT proteins, and centrioles10,43. In contrast, tubulinopathies have largely been examined in the context of neuronal development and clinical neuroanatomical phenotypes, without a direct connection to ciliary function. This divide has delayed the recognition that tubulin gene variants can directly contribute to ciliopathy phenotypes.

Recent publications have demonstrated that heterozygous missense variants in β-tubulin genes can lead to both tubulinopathies and ciliopathies, such as TUBB mutations in Joubert syndrome and TUBB4B mutations in PCD12,13. These overlapping symptoms underscore the need for a novel and improved diagnostic approach for ciliopathies. Both disease classes face common challenges, including difficulties in diagnosis, mutation modelling, and the development of effective treatment strategies190.

Current genetic panels for conditions such as Joubert syndrome and PCD typically exclude tubulin genes as causative factors. However, the growing body of evidence supports the pressing need for broader sequencing approaches to explore unclear cases presenting with ciliary dysfunction. In particular, in vitro models of tubulinopathies that also present ciliopathy symptoms should be routinely assessed for defects in ciliogenesis. The inclusion of tubulin genes in the list of ciliopathy-associated genes and incorporating them into genetic panels may lead to improved diagnostic outcomes for patients and open new avenues for identifying prospective therapeutic targets.

This review presents an emerging framework linking tubulinopathies and ciliopathies through their shared dependency on microtubule dynamics and ciliary function. While tubulin gene variants have long been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, only recently has their role in primary and motile cilia dysfunction been recognized. Despite the potential for microtubule-targeting therapies to influence ciliary phenotypes, this remains a largely unexplored area with significant therapeutic promise.
