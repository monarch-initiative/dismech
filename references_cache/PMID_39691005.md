Sporadic late-onset nemaline rod myopathy is a rare, acquired, sub-acute, adult-onset myopathy characterized by proximal muscle weakness and nemaline rods in the myofibers. In contrast to its congenital form, the prevalence in adult population is comparatively rare. Herein, we report a case of 60-year-old male who presented with insidious onset proximal muscle weakness with myopathic pattern on electromyography. Histopathological examination showed type 1 and 2 muscle fiber atrophy with minimal chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate. Modified Gomori trichrome staining revealed granular deposits in the myofibers. Ultrastructure examination showed numerous nemaline rods in the myofibers; hence, the diagnosis of sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy was rendered. Its association with monoclonal protein has been reported in the past. One should be aware of this rare entity as without correct diagnosis, there can be treatment failure and unfavorable outcome.

In a clinically unsuspected case of nemaline rod myopathy, reddish granular material highlighted by modified Gomori’s trichome stain hints towards SLONM which can be further confirmed by ultrastructure studies.

Nemaline myopathy (NM) was first described in 1963 by Shyet al.[1]and Conenet al.[2]in a group of congenital myopathies. NM is a neuromuscular disorder characterized by muscle weakness, hypotonia, and reduced or absent reflexes. The diagnosis is clinched by identifying the subsarcolemmal or intermyofibrillar dark red structures in the muscle fibers highlighted by modified Gomori’s trichome stain (MGT),[3]which can be confirmed on electron microscopy as electron dense bodies. Sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy (SLONM) is a rare, distinct form of NM which is acquired and late in onset. It has a variable clinical presentation with the cardinal complaint of proximal muscle weakness and atrophy. Involvement of respiratory muscle is frequent and is the leading cause of death in these patients.[4]Association with monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance (MGUS) and HIV infection has been reported in the literature and shows different therapeutic and prognostic implications.

A 60-year-old male with history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension since 2015 presented with the complaints of insidious onset upper and lower limb weakness with waddling gait for past one year. On physical examination there was progressive asymmetric (left > right) lower and upper limb proximal muscle weakness. He did not have any sensory, cranial, bowel or bladder involvement. There was no history of seizure, vomiting, headache, gaze deviation, fever, back pain, exercise intolerance, calf muscle enlargement or family history of similar illness. Upper limb distal muscles were also involved. Sensory/motor, deep tendon/superficial reflexes were within normal limits.

Creatine phosphokinase (CPK) level was 125U/L and was within normal limits. Electromyography (EMG) showed myopathic pattern. Nerve conduction studies were normal. Repetitive nerve stimulation test (RNST) did not reveal any abnormality. Positron emission tomography (PET) scan did not show any active uptake. Antiinflammatory autoantibodies, PM-Scl 100 and PL-7 were +1 (borderline) and +2 positive, respectively. Urine protein electrophoresis revealed the presence of a protein band in beta globulin region (“M” spike). Radiological evaluation and bone marrow examination revealed no evidence of plasma cell dyscrasia. A muscle biopsy was performed with a clinical diagnosis of paraproteinemia associated myopathy. The patient was administered intravenous immunoglobulin (IV IG) in the doses 2 g/kg for 5 days and showed clinical improvement in the follow-up of 5 months.

Pathological Examination: Muscle biopsy was rapidly processed in precooled isopentane placed in liquid nitrogen and 8µ thick sections were cut for histochemistry and immunohistochemistry. Histopathological examination revealed loss of fascicular architecture with marked variation in fiber size and numerous atrophic fibers [Figure 1a and b]. ATPase stain (9.4 ph) showed atrophy of both types of fibers (type 1 and 2) and absence of type grouping, thereby ruling out the possibility of neurogenic etiology [Figure 1c]. MGT stain showed reddish granular deposits in the myofibers [Figure 1d]. The major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and MHC class II were not upregulated on the myofibers and granular membrane attack complex (MAC) deposits on endomysial blood vessels were absent. Immunostaining for dysferlin, caveolin 3, sarcoglycans (α,β,ϒ, Ϩ), and dystrophins (1,2,3) showed normal staining pattern. Immunoblot for dysferlin and calpain was normal.

Ultrastructure examination: A small piece of muscle was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and processed for ultrastructural examination. Electron microscopy revealed numerous sarcoplasmic electron dense rods arranged parallel to the long axis of muscle fibers seen both in the subsarcolemmal and intermyofibrillary region [Figure 2a-c]. Intranuclear inclusions were absent.

(a-c) Longitudinal sections of nemaline rods in subsarcolemmal and intermyofibrillary region demonstrated by electron microscopy

A final diagnosis of sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy with paraproteinemia was made.

Nemaline Myopathy is a disorder that primarily affects the skeletal muscle. In accordance to severity, clinical presentation, and inheritance, six types of NM have been recognized, namely, (1) severe congenital NM, (2) intermediate congenital NM, (3) atypical congenital NM, (4) mild childhood-onset NM, (5) adult-onset NM, and (6) other forms.[5]Schnitzleret al.[6]published a metanalysis in 2016 which comprised of a cohort of 76 SLONM patients. Authors observed that in SLONM, the predominant phenotype was weakness of the proximal muscle of upper limbs (84%), followed by proximal muscles of lower limbs (80%), both upper and lower limbs (67%), axial weakness (68%), dyspnea (55%), and dysphagia (47%). The age of onset of symptoms ranged from 25 years to 78 years with mean age of 52 years. Interestingly, neck drop secondary to neck extensor muscle weakness was seen in almost 50% of the patients. MGUS was observed in 53% of the patients. Naddafet al.[7]published a series of 28 patients with mean age of presentation at 62 years and noted that 50% of the SLONM patients were associated with MGUS. The prognosis of SLONM association with paraproteinemia is controversial.[6,7]Schnitzleret al.[6]observed negative association with prognosis, whereas Naddafet al.[7]noted rapid progression of disease but also show favorable therapeutic response. Five-to-ten-year survival was comparable in both groups of patients. Association of paraproteinemia is not clear; however, it is postulated that it may cause aggregation of sarcomeric proteins and cause formation of rods in the myofibers.[8]

HIV associated nemaline myopathy (HIV-NM) show similar features to SLONM as proximal muscle weakness, myopathic pattern on EMG, and elevated CPK levels. Facial and respiratory muscles are generally not involved. Autoimmune pathogenesis has been suggested for HIV associated NM;[9]however, it is still unclear that SLONM and HIV-NM represent same or two distinct entities.

The most important differentials of NM are idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM). The clinical presentation with dropped head, bulbar weakness, dyspnea, and fasciculation observed in sporadic inclusion body myositis (sIBM) can be considered as a close differential diagnosis of SLONM. Absence of inflammation, lack of MHC I and II expression on the myofibers and membrane attack complex (MAC) on the endomysial blood vessels mitigate against the diagnosis of IIM. Myopathic pattern on EMG and presence of nemaline rods are helpful features of SLOMN. Rarely neurogenic pattern on electromyography can be seen in severe chronic myopathies.[6]

Several clinical symptoms like perinatal hypotonia, delayed motor milestones, scoliosis, high arched palate, age of onset, and microscopically type I fiber predominance differentiates SLONM from congenital forms of NM. Known genes causing congenital nemaline myopathy areTPM3, NEB, ACTA1, TPM2, TNNT1, KBTBD13, CFN2, KLHL40, KLHL41, LMOD3andMYPN[5]and none of these genes showed any pathogenic variations in next-generation sequencing studies done in 6 cases.[6]Nemaline rods are easily missed on light microscopy because of their small size and sometimes not highlighted distinctly on modified Gomori trichrome. Electron microscopy shows characteristic lattice structure and periodicity to that of a Z-line.

Treatment with IVIG and autologous blood stem cell transplant have shown promising results[6]as seen in the index case which showed improvement on the administration of IV IG.

SLONM is diagnostically challenging because of its rarity, close clinical mimickers, and lack of awareness of this entity at a late age. The high index of awareness is required for its diagnosis in patients presenting with proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and respiratory weakness. These patients should be investigated thoroughly which include a muscle biopsy with modified Gomori trichrome in order to identify the nemaline rods. Electron microscopy should be performed for the confirmation of the diagnosis. Patients should be investigated for concomitant MGUS and HIV as both have different clinical implications.
