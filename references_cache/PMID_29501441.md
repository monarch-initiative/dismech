# Change in Populations of Macrophages Promotes Development of Delayed Gastric Emptying in Mice.
**Authors:** Cipriani G, Gibbons SJ, Miller KE, Yang DS, Terhaar ML, Eisenman ST, Ördög T, Linden DR, Gajdos GB, Szurszewski JH, Farrugia G
**Journal:** Gastroenterology (2018)
**DOI:** [10.1053/j.gastro.2018.02.027](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.gastro.2018.02.027)

## Content

BACKGROUND & AIMS: Muscularis propria macrophages lie close to cells that regulate gastrointestinal motor function, including interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC) and myenteric neurons. In animal models of diabetic gastroparesis, development of delayed gastric emptying has been associated with loss of macrophages that express cytoprotective markers and reduced networks of ICC. Mice with long-term diabetes and normal gastric emptying have macrophages that express anti-inflammatory markers and have normal gastric ICC. Mice homozygous for the osteopetrosis spontaneous mutation in the colony-stimulating factor 1 gene (Csf1op/op) do not have macrophages; when they are given streptozotocin to induce diabetes, they do not develop delayed gastric emptying. We investigated whether population of the gastric muscularis propria of diabetic Csf1op/op mice with macrophages is necessary to change gastric emptying, ICC, and myenteric neurons and investigated the macrophage-derived factors that determine whether diabetic mice do or do not develop delayed gastric emptying.

METHODS: Wild-type and Csf1op/op mice were given streptozotocin to induce diabetes. Some Csf1op/op mice were given daily intraperitoneal injections of CSF1 for 7 weeks; gastric tissues were collected and cellular distributions were analyzed by immunohistochemistry. CD45+, CD11b+, F4/80+ macrophages were dissociated from gastric muscularis propria, isolated by flow cytometry and analyzed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction. Cultured gastric muscularis propria from Csf1op/op mice was exposed to medium that was conditioned by culture with bone marrow-derived macrophages from wild-type mice.

RESULTS: Gastric muscularis propria from Csf1op/op mice given CSF1 contained macrophages; 11 of 15 diabetic mice given CSF1 developed delayed gastric emptying and had damaged ICC. In non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice, administration of CSF1 reduced numbers of gastric myenteric neurons but did not affect the proportion of nitrergic neurons or ICC. In diabetic Csf1op/op mice given CSF1 that developed delayed gastric emptying, the proportion of nitrergic neurons was the same as in non-diabetic wild-type controls. Medium conditioned by macrophages previously exposed to oxidative injury caused damage to ICC in cultured gastric muscularis propria from Csf1op/op mice; neutralizing antibodies against IL6R or TNF prevented this damage to ICC. CD45+, CD11b+, and F4/80+ macrophages isolated from diabetic wild-type mice with delayed gastric emptying expressed higher levels of messenger RNAs encoding inflammatory markers (IL6 and inducible nitric oxide synthase) and lower levels of messenger RNAs encoding markers of anti-inflammatory cells (heme oxygenase 1, arginase 1, and FIZZ1) than macrophages isolated from diabetic mice with normal gastric emptying.

CONCLUSIONS: In studies of Csf1op/op and wild-type mice with diabetes, we found delayed gastric emptying to be associated with increased production of inflammatory factors, and reduced production of anti-inflammatory factors, by macrophages, leading to loss of ICC.

Macrophages distributed throughout the gastrointestinal tract regulate gastrointestinal function and their phenotype changes in response to changes in the extracellular environment1. Diabetic gastroparesis, a complication of diabetes characterized by delayed gastric emptying (DGE) in the absence of obstruction2-4 is associated with changes in macrophage number and phenotype. In patients with diabetic gastroparesis and DGE, fewer (Cluster of differentiation 206) CD206+ macrophages in gastric smooth muscle correlates with fewer interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC)5 and loss of ICC associates with DGE in mice6 and humans7. In diabetic mice, macrophage polarization towards anti-inflammatory, CD206+ phenotypes associates with normal gastric emptying (NGE) whereas diabetic mice with DGE did not have CD206+ macrophages in the muscularis propria (MPM)8, 9. Csf1op/op (op/op) mice that lack macrophages due to a mutation in macrophage colony stimulating factor (CSF1)10 do not develop DGE when diabetic11. Therefore macrophages appear to be required for protection from, and development of, DGE depending on their phenotype12.

The mechanism for interactions of macrophages with cells in the gastric muscularis propria of diabetic mice is not clear. There is evidence of cross talk between macrophages and myenteric neurons through effects of growth factors released by these cells13. MPMs are close to myenteric ganglia as well as nerve endings and ICC in circular muscle8, 11-14. Based on these observations, macrophages are proposed to contribute to normal development and maintenance of the enteric nervous system13.

Csf1op/op mice lack macrophages because CSF1 is required for survival, proliferation and differentiation of myeloid lineage cells15-17. Therefore Csf1op/op mice have osteopetrosis, no teeth, reduced weight and depleted macrophages18. Administration of CSF1 to these mice results in restoration of macrophages in various tissues15. While it is known that there is ongoing replenishment of mucosal macrophages by circulating monocytes, the contribution of monocytes to the population of the muscularis propria is not known19. Therefore the effect of administering CSF1 to Csf1op/op mice is a good test to determine whether monocytes can populate the gastric muscularis propria with macrophages.

Our overall goal is to identify therapeutic targets that could be used to prevent or treat gastroparesis in the subset of people who develop this serious complication of diabetes. These targets could be the damaging factors generated by polarized macrophages, or factors associated with diabetes that might polarize macrophages towards damaging phenotypes. Therefore, our aims were first, to determine if gastric MPM are necessary for the cellular changes associated with DGE in diabetic mice, and second to identify macrophage-derived factors that determine whether diabetic mice do or do not develop DGE.

Csf1op/op mice had no macrophages in the gastric muscularis propria, even after induction of diabetes11 (Figure 1C). CSF1-treated, diabetic Csf1op/op mice maintained weight compared to control Csf1op/op mice (Suppl Fig 1). After CSF1 treatment for >2 weeks, F4/80+ macrophages were detected in the gastric muscularis propria and mucosa/lamina propria of diabetic and non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice (Figure 1C, Suppl Fig 2A). Macrophages had a patchy distribution across the muscularis propria (Suppl Fig 2B) and similar morphologies to gastric MPM previously identified in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice9. Cells in the muscle layers were bipolar whereas more abundant multi-polar cells were found in the myenteric region (Figure 1D). The densities of MPM of CSF1-treated, diabetic and non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice were similar (Figure 1E) and significantly greater than the zero macrophages in untreated mice (Figure 1E). These data suggest that F4/80+ macrophages in CSF1-treated Csf1op/op mice originated from circulating monocytes generated from myeloid progenitor cells following administration of CSF110.

More F4/80+ cells were identified in WT mice than in age-matched CSF1-treated Csf1op/op mice (Figure 1E) and as previously reported for NOD mice9, onset of diabetes was associated with more F4/80+ MPM in WT mice (Figure 1E). FACS confirmed that CD45+ CD11b+ F4/80+ MPM were present in non-diabetic CSF1-treated Csf1op/op mice at a lower percentage of the total cell count than in non-diabetic WT mice (Figure 1F-G). We previously reported that F4/80+ MPMs in non-diabetic NOD mice did not express markers characteristic of polarized macrophages9. Macrophages sorted from non-diabetic CSF1-treated, Csf1op/op mice displayed an activated phenotype characterized by higher expression of anti-inflammatory (FIZZ1, HO1) and pro-inflammatory markers (interleukin-6-IL6, tumor necrosis factor-α –TNFα) (Suppl Fig 3). Therefore MPMs of CSF-1-treated, Csf1op/op mice appear more poised for activation and phenotypically different from the macrophages normally present in the stomachs of WT mice.

Immunohistochemistry was used to determine whether the distribution of macrophages was different between WT and CSF1-treated Csf1op/op mice. Many round, F4/80+ cells were found within, and adjacent to, small blood vessels in CSF1-treated Csf1op/op mice suggesting active extravasation of monocytes into the muscle layers (Suppl Fig 2C). Further, the distribution of F4/80+ MPM in CSF1 treated Csf1op/op mice was similar to that of MHCII+ cells in WT23. MHCII+ cells were in the myenteric plexus region and through the muscle layers (Suppl Fig 4). There were more MHCII+ MPMs in WT compared to CSF1 treated Csf1op/op mice (Suppl Fig 5). To further test for an association of macrophages with blood vessels or neuronal structures, we doubly labeled for MHCII and Neuropilin-1 (NRP-1), a protein expressed in neurons and endothelial cells24 (Suppl Fig 4A). The distributions of macrophages in relation to blood vessels and neuronal structures were assessed in the myenteric region. In CSF1-treated Csf1op/op mice, MHCII+ cells were closer to blood vessels than in WT mice (Figure 2B). Diabetes did not change the proximity of macrophages to blood vessels in Csf1op/op mice (Figure 2B), whereas in WT mice macrophages were closer to blood vessels after onset of diabetes (Figure 2B). In CSF1- treated, non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice, macrophages were less associated with neuronal structures than in non-diabetic, WT mice (Figure 2C). In diabetic CSF1-treated Csf1op/op mice, macrophages appeared even less associated with neuronal structures (Figure 2C) but diabetes had no apparent effect on the association of macrophages with neuronal structures in WT mice. These studies on the distribution and morphology of macrophages together with the transcriptional profiling data on sorted cells indicate that CSF1 treatment of Csf1op/op mice populates the gastric muscularis propria by monocyte invasion with macrophages that are phenotypically poised for activation. We then investigated whether the resistance11 to DGE in diabetic Csf1op/op mice was affected by CSF1 treatment.

The normal range for the T½ values of GE in Csf1op/op mice was 34-96min with a median of 63min (Figure 3A). Previously, diabetic Csf1op/op mice never developed DGE whereas 4 out of 13 diabetic WTs became delayed11 indicating that macrophages were necessary for development of DGE in diabetic mice. Therefore, we treated Csf1op/op mice with CSF1 and followed GE for up to 8 weeks after induction of diabetes (Figure 1A). A significantly higher proportion of diabetic Csf1op/op mice developed DGE (11 out of 15) following CSF1 treatment compared to untreated Csf1op/op mice (0 out of 15) and compared to non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice that received CSF1 (Figure 3A, 1 out of 7). Diabetic Csf1op/op mice treated with CSF1 had significantly slower GE compared to non-diabetic, CSF1-treated Csf1op/op mice (Figure 3A) and compared to diabetic, non-treated Csf1op/op mice (Figure 3A). CSF1-treated, diabetic Csf1op/op mice developed DGE 5.6±1.6 weeks (Suppl Fig 6A) after onset of diabetes, which was not significantly different from WTs (6.3±2.5 weeks). As previously reported, T½ values were not significantly changed after CSF1 treatment of non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice (Figure 3C). In comparison, GE was significantly slower in diabetic Csf1op/op mice at the end of CSF1 treatment compared to the values after induction of diabetes in the same mice (Figure 3D). These data indicate that MPMs are required to develop DGE and furthermore, treatment with CSF1 in non-diabetic mice is not sufficient to cause DGE even though the muscularis propria is populated with macrophages.

Diabetic Csf1op/op mice had similarly elevated blood glucose levels regardless of GE status (red dots compared to black dots in Figure 3B) and mice treated with CSF1 had similar blood glucose levels to untreated diabetic mice (Figure 3B). As already reported for NOD mice8, glucose levels did not change after onset of DGE (Figure 3B). Insulin was used to maintain blood glucose in diabetic Csf1op/op mice below 600mg/dl. Diabetic Csf1op/op mice receiving CSF1 required similar average daily insulin doses compared to untreated diabetic Csf1op/op (Suppl Fig 6C).

Higher levels of systemic oxidative stress, detected by measuring serum MDA are associated with diabetes and development of DGE in mice8. In diabetic Csf1op/op mice, MDA levels were higher than in diabetic WTs11. MDA levels in CSF1-treated, diabetic Csf1op/op mice with NGE were not different from levels in non-treated diabetic Csf1op/op mice (Figure 3E). However, diabetic Csf1op/op mice treated with CSF1 with DGE had significantly higher MDA levels than the other groups (Figure 3E).

These differences in MDA in CSF1-treated, diabetic Csf1op/op mice, with and without DGE indicate that increased levels of systemic oxidative stress associate with higher susceptibility to development of DGE in diabetic mice.

Reduced NOS1 expression in myenteric neurons and damage to ICC are both associated with diabetes25 although DGE in diabetic humans and mice has been specifically linked with loss of ICC networks11, 5, 9. Therefore we investigated how CSF1 treatment of Csf1op/op mice would affect ICC and myenteric neurons. In non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice, ICC were well preserved and treatment for 7 weeks with CSF1, which resulted in population of the muscularis propria with F4/80+ macrophages adjacent to the ICC (Figure 4A), had no significant effect on the integrity, density or distribution of the ICC in either the gastric corpus or antrum. Overall, images of Kit-immunoreactivity from CSF1-treated and Csf1op/op controls could not be distinguished on the basis of blind scoring (Figure 4B-E). However, diabetic Csf1op/op mice that developed DGE after treatment with CSF1 had patchy loss of ICC, reduced connectivity between the cells and a reduction in overall number of ICC in the gastric corpus and antrum (Figure 4B-E). No difference was observed in ICC networks of Csf1op/op mice with or without diabetes as previously reported11. Parameters for ICC network quality were not different between non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice following treatment with CSF1 compared to non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice (Figure 4B-E). However, ICC densities were significantly lower in diabetic Csf1op/op mice with DGE following CSF1 treatment compared to both diabetic treated Csf1op/op mice CSF1 and non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice (Figures 4B,C). In addition, individual ICC were less well connected in mice with DGE when compared to the other groups (Figures 4B,D) and the distribution of ICC networks was more patchy (Figure 4B,E). Consistent differences in the parameters for ICC network quality were found in the gastric antrum and the corpus, (Suppl Fig 7A-H).

Depletion of NOS1 in gastric myenteric neurons from diabetic mice with NGE and DGE has been reported8, 25. However, numbers of enteric neurons were not affected by diabetes in NOD mice and treatment of diabetic mice to achieve normoglycemia restored NOS1 expression25. In juvenile Csf1op/op mice, the density of small intestinal myenteric neurons was higher and the distribution of neurons was abnormal based on βIII-tubulin-immunolabelling compared to WT mice13. To study the relative effects of diabetes and macrophages on all myenteric neurons, we labeled and quantified HuC/D+ neurons in gastric muscularis propria. The distribution and proportion of neurons were not different across the whole stomachs of WT and Csf1op/op mice (Suppl Fig 8A-B). However, Csf1op/op mice had an approximately two-fold higher density of HuC/D+ neurons than WT siblings (Figure 5A,B). In CSF1-treated non-db Csf1op/op mice, the HuC/D+ neuron densities were lower compared to untreated Csf1op/op mice (Figure 5A,B). As result of this, the density of HuC/D+ neurons in CSF1 treated Csf1op/op mice was not different from WTs (Figure 5A,B). Diabetic Csf1op/op mice had lower HuC/D+ neuron densities compared to non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice (Figure 5A,B) and treatment with CSF1 resulting in DGE did not alter the density of neurons in diabetic Csf1op/op mice. This indicates that although MPMs influence the number of myenteric neurons in the stomach as well as the small intestine13, 26, diabetes alone can cause a depletion of myenteric neurons in the absence, but not the presence, of macrophages.

There was also a significantly higher density of NOS1+ myenteric neurons in Csf1op/op mice (Figure 5A,C) and a higher proportion of the HuC/D+ neurons were NOS1+ in Csf1op/op mice (Figure 5A,D). CSF1-treated Csf1op/op mice had a lower density of NOS1+ neurons in comparison to Csf1op/op mice (Figure 5A,C), so the proportion of NOS1+ neurons was not affected by CSF1 treatment (Figure 5A,D). Densities of NOS1+ neurons in diabetic Csf1op/op mice were not different compared to non-diabetic Csf1op/op mice (Figure 5A,C). In CSF1-treated, diabetic Csf1op/op mice with DGE, we found a lower density of NOS1+ neurons (Figure 5A,C) but the proportion of NOS1+ neurons was also not different compared to the other groups of mice (Figures 5A,D) and the density of both HuC/D+ and NOS1+ neurons, while decreased, remained at least as high as that in healthy WTs. No correlation between GE status of diabetic Csf1op/op mice and the density of either all HuC/D+ neurons, or the subset of NOS1+ neurons, was found. This supports the observation that loss of ICC following macrophage population is the defining cellular pathology in this animal model.

Our in vivo data indicate that higher levels of systemic oxidative stress, as reported by MDA levels, may underlie susceptibility to development of DGE in diabetic mice. Therefore, we tested whether exposure of macrophages in vitro to either high glucose or oxidative injury, or both, caused production of soluble factors that might injure ICC using a newly developed in-vitro model (Suppl Fig 9). Following 3 days of incubation, ICC in cultured gastric smooth muscle preparations from adult mice (3 days in culture) had a similar density and organization as in tissues that were not cultured (Figure 6A). To first determine how diabetic conditions affected macrophage activation we isolated and cultured bone marrow-derived macrophages (Figure 6B) before applying tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBH), an inducer of oxidative injury27 and/or high glucose. High glucose and TBH resulted in phenotypic changes to the macrophages (Figure 6B). Macrophages exposed to TBH expressed more IL6 compared to macrophages exposed to high glucose and this effect was suppressed in high glucose (Figure 6C). No effect of the different treatments was observed on TNFα levels (Figure 6C). CD206 levels were higher in macrophages exposed to high glucose but not to the other treatments (Figure 6C). To understand whether soluble factors from polarized macrophages could affect adult ICC in culture, media conditioned by incubation with macrophages was tested on cultures of macrophage-free, gastric muscularis propria from Csf1op/op mice. ICC networks were significantly degraded compared to untreated tissues after application of media conditioned by TBH-treated macrophages (Figure 6D,E). In contrast, application of conditioned media from macrophages exposed to hyperglycemia and media from the combined treatment did not significantly alter ICC (Figure 6D,E). Importantly, adding neutralizing antibodies against the IL6 receptor and TNFα to the media conditioned by TBH-treated macrophages preserved ICC from damage (Figure 6D,E). This result indicates that high glucose treatment and oxidative injury to macrophages result in generation of soluble factors with opposing effects on ICC survival in culture and the data are consistent with a major contribution of oxidative injury to the cellular pathology that underlies development of DGE in mice.

The in vitro experiments and our previous studies indicate that gastric MPMs should have different phenotypes between diabetic mice with NGE and DGE. To test this in WT mice we isolated CD45+ CD11b+ F4/80+ cells from non-diabetic WTs and from diabetic WTs with NGE and DGE. As previously reported11, a proportion of diabetic WTs developed DGE, which was associated with higher levels of systemic oxidative stress (Figure 7A-C). MPMs were dissociated, sorted by FACS and RNA was extracted for Q-RT-PCR (Figure 7D-E). These MPMs expressed a mixture of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory markers regardless of the GE status. As predicted from our previous profiling of gastric muscularis propria tissue from NOD mice9, the macrophages from diabetic WTs with NGE expressed high levels of anti-inflammatory markers including HO1, FIZZ1, TGFβ and Arg1 and all of these transcripts except TGFβ were expressed at significantly lower levels in macrophages from diabetic WTs with DGE (Figure 7F). Macrophages from diabetic WTs with DGE, expressed high levels of pro-inflammatory markers, especially IL6 and iNOS, but also detectable levels of FIZZ1 and TGFβ (Figure 7F). TNFα and IL1β levels were not significantly different between MPMs from the two groups of diabetic mice (Figure7F). This complex profile is consistent with a population of macrophages developing in diabetic mice with NGE that protects the tissue from diabetic injury, whereas in mice with DGE, high oxidative stress activates macrophages that produce molecules including IL6 that are injurious to ICC. Our data from Csf1op/op mice and mapping of macrophage distributions in diabetic WT mice indicate that these pro-inflammatory macrophages may be derived from invading monocytes.

Despite a well-established role in regulation of innate immunity28, it is only recently that macrophages have emerged as regulators of the fate and function of other types of cell including interactions with gastrointestinal ICC and myenteric neurons13, 14, 29, 30. In this study, we showed that systemic treatment with CSF1 populates the gastric muscularis propria of adult Csf1op/op mice with macrophages. As result of this, 73% of diabetic Csf1op/op mice treated with CSF1 developed DGE and had disrupted ICC networks. The cellular changes in the diabetic mice with DGE indicated that monocyte derived cells had invaded the tissue and caused changes to ICC. Therefore the loss of ICC was the key to the development of DGE, which is a defining characteristic of gastroparesis2-4. We also found, using an in vitro model that oxidative injury, but not high glucose, was sufficient to re-capitulate both the macrophage activation phenotypes and the cellular injury patterns seen in diabetic mice with DGE. Finally, we found overlapping patterns of expression of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory molecules in MPMs from diabetic mice with normal and DGE. We conclude from these studies that in diabetes, maintenance of normal ICC networks and NGE depends on the ability of cytoprotective macrophages to suppress the damaging effects of cytokines released from macrophages polarized by high levels of oxidative stress.

It is unclear whether macrophages derived from the embryonic yolk sac that become resident in the gastrointestinal tract persist into adulthood and how these cells contribute to maintenance of populations of tissue macrophages. In other tissues, resident macrophages are generated by either embryonic progenitors from the yolk sac or fetal liver that, upon different stimuli, undergo cell division and produce macrophages by self-renewal in situ31. In contrast, mucosal macrophages are continuously replenished by circulating monocytes19. The origin and turnover of macrophages in the adult muscularis propria has not yet been addressed. CSF1 is the primary regulator of the mononuclear phagocyte lineage and absence of functional CSF1 in Csf1op/op mice, or its functional depletion by the administration of a CSF1 neutralizing antibody, results in absence of MPMs in the small intestine13. Since myenteric neurons produce CSF113, these studies indicate that MPMs can be maintained by local and systemic CSF1. As result of their anatomical association with other cell types, MPMs interact functionally with ICC and enteric neurons9, 13, 23. After population of the muscularis propria following CSF1 treatment of Csf1op/op mice, macrophages were more closely associated with blood vessels than were macrophages in WT mice, suggesting vascular origin of the cells following CSF1 treatment. Our data support the hypothesis that circulating monocytes can be a source of MPMs although further studies are needed in order to understand the mechanisms regulating the invasion of monocytes into the tissue under normal and diseased conditions and their polarization towards a specific phenotype.

MPMs by producing BMP2 directly interact with enteric neurons that in response release CSF113. This circuit is not present in Csf1op/op mice, where due to the absence of CSF1, enteric neurons cannot establish a functional interaction with MPMs. Our data support this conclusion since CSF1 treatment only promotes active extravasation of monocytes to the tissue but does not restore macrophage-enteric neurons interactions most likely due to the inability of enteric neurons to produce active CSF1 in Csf1op/op mice.

MPMs play a fundamental role in development of DGE. In animal models of diabetes, DGE is associated with the presence of pro-inflammatory phenotypes of macrophages9,5. On the other hand, long standing diabetes without development of DGE is associated with high levels of CD206 and HO1, two markers of anti-inflammatory macrophages5. In addition to that, diabetic op/op mice, lacking gastric MPM, do not develop DGE whereas wild type littermates do 11The data from our work further clarify the role of macrophages in development of DGE. Introduction of macrophages into the stomach is not sufficient for DGE to develop in Csf1op/op mice since, in non-diabetic mice, CSF1 treatment results in the presence of MPMs, but the mice have NGE.

The high proportion of diabetic Csf1op/op mice that developed DGE within 8 weeks of induction of diabetes is in contrast with the rate previously reported for other strains of mice, including diabetic NOD and WT controls9. These observations strongly support the concept that macrophages are required for development of DGE in diabetic mice and also indicate both macrophages and the diabetic condition are required for the cellular injury that causes DGE. The higher rate of development of DGE in diabetic Csf1op/op mice treated with CSF1 may be due to several factors. In this model, there is an abrupt switch from none to many MPMs, also those macrophages are introduced into an environment of established diabetes and that may not be favorable for polarization of the cells towards a cytoprotective, M2 phenotype. Alternatively, monocyte-derived macrophages may be less likely to generate anti-inflammatory macrophages whereas tissue resident macrophages can expand and polarize towards the anti-inflammatory phenotype and in Csf1op/op mice, there are no tissue resident macrophages until CSF1 is delivered. Finally systemic CSF1 delivery, rather than local release from myenteric neurons, may favor development of pro-inflammatory MPMs. In diabetes, extracellular conditions that may contribute to macrophage polarization include high levels of glucose, generation of advanced glycation products (AGE) and increased levels of oxidative stress32, 33. The literature does not support the hypothesis that hyperglycemia by itself can cause DGE8, and it may actually protect ICC from injury, and mice from DGE34. The identification of the mechanism(s) by which diabetes promotes polarization of macrophages or their precursors is fundamental to development of potential therapies not only for diabetic gastroparesis but also for other pathologies where MPMs may play an important role. In this regard, systemic oxidative stress appears to be essential for a fuller understanding of the required sequence of events leading to damaged ICC networks and DGE.

High MDA levels have been widely reported in diabetes and were found to be elevated further in diabetic NOD mice with DGE8. In diabetic Csf1op/op mice, MDA levels were higher than in diabetic WT littermates11 possibly due to an up-regulation of macrophages in WT mice that generate anti-oxidants such as carbon monoxide or bilirubin in response to oxidative stress. As reported previously for other strains8, diabetic Csf1op/op mice with DGE show further elevation of MDA levels compared to diabetic Csf1op/op mice with NGE. Importantly, the level of oxidative stress detected in diabetic op/op mice with DGE is much higher than that in NOD and WT mice with DGE. Our results support a correlation between different levels of oxidative stress and development of DGE, and oxidative stress levels as a marker for diabetic gastroparesis.

We have previously hypothesized that presence of pro-inflammatory MPMs is associated with loss of ICC, possibly due to release of TNFα22, which results in the development of DGE8, 9. Our study demonstrates that IL6 but not TNFα is selectively over-expressed in macrophages from diabetic mice with DGE and that exposure of macrophages to oxidative injury in vitro can up-regulate IL6 expression more effectively than high glucose exposure. The data also demonstrate that neutralizing TNFα and IL6 activity preserves ICC networks after exposure to medium conditioned by macrophages exposed to diabetic conditions. These results further indicate that pro-inflammatory macrophages are responsible for the damage occurring to ICC networks and anti-inflammatory macrophages are protective due to the production of molecules like IL1035. Further investigation is required to understand how either oxidative stress and/or hyperglycemia alter the dynamic balance of the different phenotypes of macrophage might lead to ICC loss and, consequently, development of DGE over the progression of diabetes. Pro-inflammatory macrophages, by releasing injurious substances such as cytokines and chemokines are capable of damaging various cell types. Thus, neutralizing their function may be a therapeutic approach for treating gastroparesis. Importantly, the data also reveal a potential contribution of circulating monocytes to production of pro-inflammatory macrophages in diabetic conditions. Blocking monocyte invasion into the tissue might also represent a therapeutic strategy to prevent damage to ICC and development of DGE.

Numerous studies based on electron microscopy and immunohistochemistry have shown that macrophages are in close association with myenteric neurons and that the two cell types interact13, 14, 23, 30. MPMs can affect numbers of myenteric neurons in the healthy small intestine23 and consistent with this finding, the density of myenteric neurons in CSF1 treated Csf1op/op mice was lower than in untreated Csf1op/op mice and comparable to that in WT mice. The development of diabetes is associated with loss of NOS1+ neurons, which is maintained after development of DGE8, 25. In Csf1op/op mice, onset of diabetes reduced the density of HuC/D+ neurons but did not have effect on the proportion of NOS1+ neurons. This finding suggests that, in this model, DGE is not influenced by changes to enteric neuron number, but the loss of ICC is the only cellular pathology drive the development of DGE.

This finding suggests a potential and novel role for macrophages in the regulation of gastric myenteric neurons in diabetes. However, the contribution of myenteric neurons to development of DGE is still unclear. As already reported for the juvenile small intestine, adult Csf1op/op mice have a higher density of gastric myenteric neurons compared to WT littermates, suggesting that macrophages may have a regulatory role for gastric myenteric neurons as well as the small intestine13. The density and percentage of NOS1+ neurons was higher in Csf1op/op mice compared to WT mice, suggesting that macrophages regulate a sub-population of myenteric neurons. For our studies, we used a streptozotocin induced diabetic mouse model, which does have some limitations including the possibility that the toxin might cause neuropathic injury. However the overlap of our data with respect to changes in neurons and ICC with previous studies on NOD mice8, 9 make it unlikely that the findings are due to the streptozotocin model used.

In summary, population of the gastric muscularis propria with macrophages following treatment of diabetic mice with systemic CSF1 results in development of disrupted ICC networks, and development of DGE suggesting that activated pro-inflammatory macrophages release factors that injure ICC22 and are required for the cellular damage associated with development of DGE. Strategies to modulate macrophage population of the stomach and their polarization may therefore hold promise for the treatment of DGE in diabetes.

Experimental design for the in vitro model of the effects of diabetes on gastric muscularis propria Bone marrow derived macrophages were polarized by exposure to either hyperglycemic conditions (10 g/L) or increased oxidative stress (100 μM tert-butyl hydroperoxide – TBH). After removing the polarizing medium, the polarized macrophages were used to condition serum-free M199 medium by incubation for 24 hrs. The conditioned media with or without neutralizing antibodies to IL6Rα (1 μg/mL) and TNF-α (0.4 μg/mL) were then added to preparations of gastric muscularis propria established in organotypic culture from Csf1op/op mice. After 24 hrs of exposure, tissues were fixed and immuno-labeled for the ICC marker, Kit. For each tissue confocal images of the immunofluorescence were collected from 7 fields and the changes to the density, distribution and connectivity of the Kit+ ICC network were scored in random order by 2 investigators blind to the source of the images.
