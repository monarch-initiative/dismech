Certain Enterobacteriaceae strains contain a 54-kb biosynthetic gene cluster 
referred to as "pks" encoding the biosynthesis of a secondary metabolite, 
colibactin. Colibactin-producing E. coli promote colorectal cancer (CRC) in 
preclinical models, and in vitro induce a specific mutational signature that is 
also detected in human CRC genomes. Yet, how colibactin exposure affects the 
mutational landscape of CRC in vivo remains unclear. Here we show that 
colibactin-producing E. coli-driven colonic tumors in mice have a significantly 
higher SBS burden and a larger percentage of these mutations can be attributed 
to a signature associated with mismatch repair deficiency (MMRd; SBS15), 
compared to tumors developed in the presence of colibactin-deficient E. coli. We 
found that the synthetic colibactin 742 but not an inactive analog 746 causes 
DNA damage and induces transcriptional activation of p53 and senescence 
signaling pathways in non-transformed human colonic epithelial cells. In MMRd 
colon cancer cells (HCT 116), chronic exposure to 742 resulted in the 
upregulation of BRCA1, Fanconi anemia, and MMR signaling pathways as revealed by 
global transcriptomic analysis. This was accompanied by increased T>N 
single-base substitutions (SBS) attributed to the proposed pks+E. coli signature 
(SBS88), reactive oxygen species (SBS17), and mismatch-repair deficiency 
(SBS44). A significant co-occurrence between MMRd SBS44 and pks-associated SBS88 
signature was observed in a large cohort of human CRC patients (n=2,945), and 
significantly more SBS44 mutations were found when SBS88 was also detected. 
Collectively, these findings reveal the host response mechanisms underlying 
colibactin genotoxic activity and suggest that colibactin may exacerbate 
MMRd-associated mutations.

Members of the intestinal microbiota appear implicated in the development of CRC by virtue of bacterial-derived virulence factors that induce an inflammatory and genotoxic host response, at least in pre-clinical models [1,2]. For example, some E. coli strains carry a 54-kb biosynthetic gene cluster encoding a hybrid polyketide non-ribosomal peptide synthase (referred to as pks or clb) that generates a secondary metabolite(s) named colibactin [3]. Bacteria carrying the pks island possess DNA damaging properties [3,4] that have been associated with CRC [5], [6], [7]. In humans, pks+ E. coli are present in a higher proportion of individuals with CRC (55-66%) and IBD (40%) than healthy individuals (20%) [7], [8], [9]. Infection of eukaryotic cells with pks+ E. coli results in DNA interstrand cross-links (ICLs) [10,11] that are detectable after colonization in vivo
[12]. Importantly, neoplastic lesions with a concomitant increase in histological γH2AX staining are observed in pks+ E. coli-colonized Apcmin/+; Il10−/- and AOM-Il10−/- mice [8,13], suggesting that these bacteria foster tumor growth mediated by their genotoxic activity. Two recent studies have described a specific pattern of somatic mutations generated by pks+ E. coli exposure, characterized by single base substitutions (SBS) or short indels occurring within AT-rich DNA motifs [14,15]. These mutational signatures are found in a subset of patients with CRC, urinary tract cancer, head and neck cancer, and oral squamous cell carcinoma [16], suggesting pks+ E. coli may have contributed to oncogenic mutation in these cases. Interestingly, the prevalence of pks+ E. coli in CRC patients far outweighs the observed prevalence of pks-associated mutational signatures, suggesting environmental context may influence colibactin's carcinogenic propensity [5]. For example, patients consuming a Western diet exhibit a significantly higher risk for CRC when colonized by pks+ bacteria [17]. Additionally, colibactin may promote tumorigenesis through mechanisms that do not cause a canonical pks-associated signature [18].

Here we combine results from in vivo pks+ E. coli infection and in vitro exposure to synthetic colibactin to reveal a novel mutagenic consequence of colibactin exposure. We show that synthetic colibactin or infection with colibactin-producing E. coli increased the total number of mutations and the relative contribution of mutational signatures associated with mismatch repair deficiency (MMRd). Furthermore, we found a significant co-occurrence of the MMRd signature SBS44 and the pks-associated signature SBS88 in a large cohort of human CRC patients.

129/SvEApcMin/+ mice were bred and all experiments were conducted at the University of Florida. Germ-free mouse colonies were maintained in isolators, supplied with sterile water and food. For all experiments, mice were randomized into treatment groups and matched to the best for age and sex. At least three cages, each harboring at least two mice, were used in each group for all experiments. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Florida (protocol no. 201609606).

Germ free Apcmin/+ mice (12–18 weeks old, males and females) were transferred to SPF condition and inoculated with 108 colony-forming units (c.f.u.) of E. coli via single oral gavage. At 1 week after E. coli gavage, mice received 1.5% DSS (molecular weight 40 kDa, Alfa Aesar, J63606) in drinking water for 7 d. After switching to regular drinking water for 4 weeks, mice were euthanized and examined.

From each group, we selected 6 tumor-bearing mice to analyze by whole-exome sequencing (WES). For each mouse, tumor tissue was collected from a single tumor in the distal colon and matched normal tissue was collected from the proximal colon. Genomic DNA was isolated using the Quick-DNA Midiprep Plus Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Mouse exome libraries were prepared using the SureSelect Mouse All Exon Kit (Agilent) and samples were sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq6000 S4.

Whole exome sequencing data was processed using sarek (version 3.0.2) [19], an nf-core [20] pipeline, written in the domain specific language, Nextflow [21]. Sarek follows the Genome Analysis Tool Kit (GATK) best practices workflow for variant calling. Soft clipping of adapter sequences was performed by sarek using fastp [22], reads were aligned to the Ensembl [23] mouse genome, build GRCm38. Somatic mutations in the tumor samples were extracted using proximal normal epithelial tissue as baseline using Strelka2 [24], the ‘exome’ flag. For mutational signature analysis, 96 trinucleotide SBS subcategory mutation spectra and percentages of each sample were calculated using the R package MutationalPatterns [25]. Previously defined SBS mutational signatures were retrieved from the COSMIC mutational signatures database version 3.3 (June 2022; https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic/signatures), and fit to each sample using MutationalPatterns.

Synthetic colibactins 742 and 746 were synthesized as previously described [26] and purity was assessed by NMR (1H and 13C) and LC/MS. Compounds were stored as solutions in DMSO at -80°C before use as separate aliquots such that each aliquot was not subjected to more than a single freeze/thaw cycle. For all studies, a mixture of ring and chain isomers of colibactin 742 (8:1 ratio) was employed, as previously described [26].

IEC-6 cells were seeded in 8-well chamber slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 5 × 104 cells per well and serum starved in 2% FBS medium overnight. Cells were treated with DMSO, 3 µM MMC, or the indicated concentration of 742 or 746 for 24 hours before washing once with cold PBS and fixation in 4% methanol free formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min on ice, washing with PBS and quenching of free aldehydes with a 50 mM NH4Cl solution for 10 minutes. Cells were permeabilized using 0.25% Triton-X 100 (Sigma Aldrich) for 5 min on ice, washed with PBS, and blocked with blocking solution (PBS containing 1% BSA and 5% normal goat serum) for 1 hour on ice and incubated with the Phospho-Histone H2AX (Ser139) rabbit monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling, 9718) diluted 1:800 in blocking solution overnight at 4°C. Cells were washed with PBS and incubated in the secondary Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) antibody (Life Technologies, A-11034) for 45 minutes at room temperature. Cells were washed with PBS and mounted with VECTASHIELD mounting medium with DAPI (Vector Labs). Cells were examined for immunofluorescence using a Leica DM6000B upright microscope and counted as positive if more than five foci/nucleus were detected.

Colonoids were isolated from biopsies taken from healthy subjects undergoing routine colonoscopy as previously described [27]. Colonoids were cultured in domes of Matrigel (Corning) and covered by human colonoid media (HCM), prepared by mixing 50% IntestiCult organoid growth media (StemCell Technologies) with 50% L-WRN conditioned media prepared as previously described [28] in advanced DMEM/F12, supplemented with 50 ng/ml EGF (Peprotech). Human derived colonoids (HDCs) were passaged by manually dissociating Matrigel domes using a pipette in cold PBS, pelleting, and incubating in 1:5 TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before passing through a 26G syringe needle prior to re-seeding dissociated HDCs.

For experiments measuring changes in HDC diameter or survival, HDCs were passaged and seeded uniformly with approximately 50,000 cells per well in a 6-well tissue culture plate before treating with the indicated compounds for 48 hours. Diameter was quantified using ImageJ by measuring the diameter of each colonoid at its widest point. For each data point, a single well was imaged and a 5 × 5 grid was overlayed before randomly selecting grid cells to quantify using a random number generator. All organoids with any point touching the selected grid cell were measured, and a new grid cell was selected until a total of 30 organoids were measured. In the case that an organoid contacted multiple cells, it was only measured once. Relative survival was calculated by counting the total number of organoids visible by bright field microscopy with approximate size > 30 µM, and counts were normalized to the average number of HDCs present in the untreated condition.

Immunofluorescent detection of γH2AX in HDCs was carried out as previously described [29]. Briefly, dissociated colonoids were plated in CELLview culture dishes (Greiner Bio-One), cultured for 24-48 hours in HCM until approximately 100 µm in diameter, and treated with MMC, 742, or 746 at the indicated concentration for 48 hours. HDCs were washed with room temperature PBS and fixed in 4% methanol free formaldehyde (Fisher Scientific) for 30 minutes at room temperature before washing twice with PBS, and free aldehydes were quenched with a 50 mM NH4Cl solution for 30 minutes. Cells were washed twice with PBS and permeabilized using 0.1% Triton-X 100 (Sigma Aldrich) for 30 minutes at room temperature. HDCs were then washed twice with PBS and blocked with blocking solution for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with the Phospho-Histone H2AX (Ser139) rabbit monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling) diluted 1:400 in PBS overnight at 4°C. Cells were washed with PBS and incubated in the secondary Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) antibody (Life Technologies) in PBS overnight at 4°C. Cells were counterstained with 1:5000 DAPI solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged using a Leica TCS SP5 confocal microscope. Z-stacks are merged by maximum intensity projection to produce a three-dimensional image. The fraction of nuclei containing γH2AX foci relative to the total number of nuclei is shown as one datapoint per organoid.

The indicated compounds were administered to Galleria mellonella larvae (Carolina Biological Supply) via 10 µl intrahemocoelic injection (7.42 µg of the indicated compound per larva, or approximately 30 mg/kg assuming an average larval weight of 250 mg) at the last proleg using a 29G1/2 insulin syringe (EXEL International). 24 hours post-injection, G. mellonella health index and melanization scores were quantified using a previously described standardized rubric [30].

To assess DNA damage in the G. mellonella intestinal epithelium, the indicated compounds were administered by oral gavage in a final volume of 10 µl as previously described [31]. Blue food coloring (Badia) was added at a ratio of 1:10,000 to visualize successful gavage. 24 hours after administration, the midgut from 5 larvae per treatment were dissected, pooled, and epithelial cells dissociated by incubation in 20 mM EDTA before resuspending cells in PBS at 1 × 105 cells/ml for electrophoresis. DNA strand breaks were analyzed by alkaline comet assay using the CometAssay Single Gel Electrophoresis Kit (4250-050-K, R&D Systems) following the manufacturer's protocol as previously described [32]. DNA was stained with 40 µl 1x SYBR Gold nucleic acid gel stain (S-11494, Life Technologies) per well and immediately visualized using the Leica DM6000B upright microscope. DNA damage was quantified using the OpenComet [33] ImageJ plugin.

Chronic treatment and derivation of subclones for whole-genome sequencing and RNAseq analysis was performed following previously recommended guidelines [34]. A clonal grandparent HCT 116 cell population was treated with 742 or 746 at 20 µM for 48 hours (approximate LC50) after which cell were washed and fresh culture media added. Following these treatments, cells were grown for 48-96h until approximately 80-90% confluency and then passaged. This process was repeated 10 times for each condition, with a total experimental duration of 34 (746) and 51 (742) days. Following the tenth exposure cycle, cells were subcloned for downstream application. All subcloning steps were conducted by passaging cells and filtering through a 40 µm cell strainer before sparse seeding (1 × 103 cells) in a 100 mm petri dish. After 9-13 days, when colonies reached approximately 200 µm diameter, single colonies were manually scraped, aspirated with a pipette tip and dissociated in trypsin before repeating this process to ensure clonality. After cloning, cells were expanded for approximately 1-2 weeks before long-term storage in CryoStor cell freezing media (StemCell Technologies). The total duration of sub-cloning and expansion was 30 (746) and 32 (742) days. Cells were thawed and passaged once before collection for RNAseq or WGS.

FHC cells were cultured in media containing HEPES (10 mM), insulin (0.005 mg/ml), EGF (Peprotech, 20 ng/ml), and 10% FBS. Cells were seeded at 2 × 105 per well in 6-well plates, treated with 742 or 746 and collected after 4- or 12-hour exposure. For chronic exposure, sub-clonal populations described above and analyzed by whole-genome sequencing were seeded at 2 × 105 per well in 6-well plates before collection. For all collections, cells were collected by cell scraping in ice-cold PBS. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNeasy isolation kit with on-column DNase treatment (Qiagen). RNA samples were measured by the QUBIT fluorescent method (Invitrogen) and Agilent Bioanalyzer. 250 ng of total RNA (RIN >7) was used for library construction using the reagents provided in the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs) and the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer's user guide. Briefly, 250 ng of total RNA was used for mRNA isolated using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs). Then the poly A enriched RNA was fragmented in NEBNext First Strand Synthesis Buffer via incubation at 94°C. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using reverse transcriptase and a random hexamer primer followed by ds cDNA synthesis, end-repair and dA-tailing. Illumina adaptors were ligated to the sample, amplified, and purified using AMPure beads (Beckman Coulter). The library size and mass were assessed using a High Sensitivity DNA1000 Screen Tape by Agilent TapeStation.

Normalized libraries were treated with the the Free Adapter Blocking Reagent (Illumina). The library pool was diluted to 0.8 nM and and 120 pM with 1% PhiX spike-in control was sequenced on one S4 flow cell lane (2 × 150 cycles). Thirty-nine barcoded libraries were pooled equimolarly for sequencing on a single 0.52 lane of NovaSeq 6000 S4 (2 × 150 cycles).

Reads were quality trimmed at Q20 (sequencing quality score of 20) and filtered to remove sequencing adaptors using Trimmomatic (v.0.39) [35]. This resulted in an average of 74,350,818 reads per sample (37,175,409 reads for each end) that were then used for subsequent analysis. Alignment and gene expression quantification were done through STAR (v.2.7.9a) [36] using the human reference genome (GRCh38) with its corresponding annotation file obtained from the European Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-EBI, http://ftp.ebi.ac.uk). More than 90% of the input reads for each sample were successfully aligned to GRCh38. Gene counts were then imported into edgeR (v.3.26, R v.4.1.2) [37] for normalization and differential expression analysis. We considered a transcript differentially expressed if its edgeR FDR adjusted P-value is less than 0.05. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was done using R prcomp function using amatrix of log transformed values generated with R rlog function with the option blind set to true. Heatmaps were generated using R pheatmap package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap). Pathway analysis, upstream regulator analysis, and disease or function annotation were conducted using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; Qiagen). For upstream regulator analysis, targets identified with a ‘bias’ flag by the IPA sofwatre were excluded. For chronic treatment, further pathway mapping using the protein interaction database (PID) and REACTOME was done using R EnrichAnalyzer [38] employing the hypergeometric test (HGT). Pre-ranked gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out using an unweighted scoring scheme, 1000 permutations, and gene lists pre-ranked by normalized fold change [39].

Total genomic DNA was isolated from HCT 116 clones using the Quick-DNA MIDIprep plus kit (Zymo Research) following the manufacturer's instructions after cell collection by manual scraping in ice-cold PBS. Genomic DNA (250 ng) was fragmented in 50 ul microTUBES-50 (AFA fiber screw-cap) using an S220 sonicator (Covaris) following the manufacturer's recommendation to generate 300-400 bp fragments. Fragmentation was followed by AMPure clean-up (0.9:1.0 beads:sample ratio) and 50 ng of DNA was used as input for library construction. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext UltraTM II DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). Barcoding was performed using the indexing reagents provided in the NEBNext Unique Dual Index Oligos kit (Illumina). The final libraries were quantified by fluorescence (QUBIT, ThermoFisher Scientific), and sized on the Agilent TapeStation (DNA5000 Screen Tape). A yield of 50-60 ng of library was obtained of an average size ranging from 500-800 bp. Libraries were normalized, pooled equimolarly and treated with the Free Adapter Blocking Reagent (Illumina). The library pool was diluted to 2.5 nM and sequenced (two S4 lanes for 12 samples, 2 × 150 cycles) according to Illumina NovaSeq6000 sequencing protocol, using 120 pM loading concentration and 1% PhiX spike-in control. Approximately 2.7 billion paired-end reads were obtained per lane (Q30%>= 87%; Cluster PF= 70%).

Raw sequencing reads were QC trimmed using Fastp [22]. Reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) using Minimap2 [40]. Somatic mutations were called using GATK Mutect2 (v4.2.2.0) using as normal reference the baseline sample (the parental HCT 116 clone before colibactin 742 exposure). The quality of the variants was evaluated using GATK VariantFiltration using the default settings. Clonality was assessed by comparing variant allele frequency in sequenced HCT 116 clones after 742 exposure (n=8) or 746 exposure (n=3), and one 742 treated sub-clone was omitted because VAF suggested this sample consisted of two clonal cell populations. Mutational signatures were extracted using the R package SigProfilerExtractorR (v1.1.6). To identify the mutational signatures induced exclusively by 742, we first subtracted the relative proportion of mutations present in 746-treated samples. Mutational signatures and mutational profiles of samples were decomposed into COSMIC or SIGNAL signatures using SigProfilerExtractorR (v1.1.6) or MutationalPatterns (v3.6.0) [25]. To determine if 742-associated signature was present in cancer patients, we downloaded mutational profiles from 18,641 samples from 21 types of cancer and 3 different genome projects [41] (available in the supplementary material). Similarly, mutational profiles were refitted to COSMIC or SIGNAL signatures using MutationalPatterns.

Co-occurrence analysis was performed by first generating a presence/absence table for all SBS identified in all CRC patients from https://signal.mutationalsignatures.com
[41]. Statistical significance of the co-occurrence for each pairwise combination of SBS was assessed using hypergeometric test in R.

All experiments in this section were carried out using frozen cell stocks of HCT 116 cells after chronic exposure to colibactin 742 or 746 for ten 48-hour cycles as previously described. For these experiments, polyclonal cell populations preserved and expanded for approximately 2 weeks, but prior to sub-cloning, were utilized. Soft-agar colony formation was performed as previously described [42]. Briefly, a bottom layer of 1% noble agar (BD Biosciences) was overlaid with a 0.6% noble agar mixture containing 5 × 103 cells/well in a 6-well tissue culture plate, covered with cell culture media, and cultured at 37°C for 21 days. Colonies were stained with 0.1% crystal violet in 10% ethanol for 30 minutes before 5x rinsing with dH2O. Images were captured using the BioRad GelDoc XR+ with ImageLab software. C57Bl/6 Rag1−/− mice were implanted subcutaneously with 2 × 106 HCT 116 cells after chronic exposure to 742 or 746. Following manual palpation of tumors, tumor volume was measured every three days before termination of experiment 32 days after implantation. Tumors were excised and weighed before overnight fixation in 10% formalin and long-term storage in 70% ethanol at 4°C.

All statistical tests were were performed using Graphpad Prism V.6. All tests are two-tailed; parametrical tests were used only for normally distributed data, otherwise the Mann-Whitney U test was used.

Although recent studies demonstrated that pks+ E. coli infection generates a unique mutational signature in vitro [14,15], these signatures have not been experimentally derived in murine models. As such, we aimed to investigate genomic changes occurring in tumors from germ-free DSS-exposed ApcMin/+ mice after colonization by pks+ E. coli or a ΔclbP mutant immediately after transfer to specific pathogen free (SPF) housing. As previously reported [8,13], infection with the pks+ E. coli strain NC101 significantly increased tumorigenesis in the distal colon relative to mice colonized with an isogenic ΔclbP mutant that does not produce colibactin (Fig. 1a-c). To quantify somatic mutations in tumors derived from NC101- and ΔclbP-treated animals, we compared single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) present in tumor tissue relative to normal tissue from the proximal colon by whole exosome sequencing (WES). This analysis revealed relatively few changes in the overall 96-base SBS spectra between treatments (Fig. 1d), but a significantly higher rate of somatic SNPs in the mice colonized by NC101 (Fig. 1e). Mutational signature analysis revealed that in both groups most mutations could be attributed to SBS89 or SBS37 (Fig. 1f). Surprisingly, the MMRd-associated signature SBS15 [43] accounted for a significantly higher proportion of somatic mutations in NC101 treated mice relative to ΔclbP treated mice (Fig. 1g). No other signature was significantly different between these groups. To further investigate whether colibactin could exacerbate MMRd-associated mutagenesis, we employed a reductionist approach utilizing previously described stable synthetic colibactins (742 and 746) [26]. Molecular docking experiments predict that cyclized colibactin (771) alkylates adenine-N3 residues on opposing strands by cyclopropane ring-opening, forming ICLs that may progress to double-strand breaks (Fig. 2a) [14]. It has been shown that replacing cyclopropane residues in 742 with unlinked methyl groups as in colibactin 746 (Fig. 2a) abrogates the genotoxic effects of 742 in HeLa cells [26]. However, the genotoxic potential and host-response mechanisms of colibactin 742 exposure in intestinal epithelial cells remains uninvestigated. We found that IEC-6 cells and human colonoids exposed to 742 but not 746 showed increased levels of γH2AX (Fig. 2b-e), and the impact of 742 on colonoid growth and viability was significantly more pronounced than 746 (Fig. 2f-h). Because synthesizing enough synthetic colibactins to conduct murine experiments was not feasible, we chose to test the genotoxic capacity of 742 and 746
in vivo using larvae of the wax moth, Galleria mellonella by intrahemocoelic injection or oral gavage. Intrahemocoelic injection of 7.42 µg 742 caused increased cuticular melanization relative to injection of 746 (Fig. S1a,b), and 7.42 µg oral gavage with 742 caused a higher level of DNA damage in the intestinal epithelium relative to 746 assessed by comet assay (Fig. S1c-e). A recent study described the synergistic effect between the presence of another carcinogenic bacterium, Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis, and MMRd in colon tumorigenesis [47]. Moreover, our data demonstrated that pks+ E. coli colonization enhanced MMRd-associated mutations in ApcMin/+/DSS mouse tumors (Fig. 1). Thus, we hypothesized that repeated exposure to colibactin 742 in a MMRd cell line (HCT 116) may exacerbate MMR-associated mutations. To assess the impact of chronic 742 exposure on transcriptomic and genomic changes in MMRd cells, we performed RNA-seq and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on sub-clonal HCT 116 cells after exposure to 20 µM 742 (LC50) or 746 in ten 48-hour cycles with 48- to 96-hour recovery, followed by a toxin-free sub-cloning and expansion period of 30-32 days (Fig. 4a). 742 and 746 treated cells had unique transcriptional signatures (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table 5), characterized by a significant increase in the expression of cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP4F3, CYP4F12; Fig. 4c), a superfamily involved in xenobiotic metabolism enriched in CRC tissue [48], and BRCA-mediated DNA repair genes (BRIP1, BRCA2, BARD1; Fig. 4c). Pathway mapping with IPA confirmed a significant enrichment of the BRCA1/BARD1/RAD51/FANCD2 complex facilitating DNA repair (Fig. 4d; Fig. S2a), supported by a significant enrichment of Fanconi and BARD1 signaling pathways when analyzed by PID (Fig. 4e). 742-treated cells also showed a significant upregulation of transcriptional networks regulated by IFNL1 (Fig. 4f), with a concomitant increase in the expression of multiple downstream ISGs (Fig. S2b). When 746-derived mutations were subtracted, the resulting spectra showed a high proportional increase in single T deletions within <6 bp homopolymers and a slight increase in the number of single T insertions within <6 bp homopolymers (Fig. 5g). Fitting known COSMIC signatures to the observed indel profiles in our 742-treated cells showed that the colibactin signature ID18 was one of three signatures with known etiology detected in most of our samples, along with the MMR-associated signatures ID1 and ID2 (Fig. 5h). Collectively, this data show that chronic 742 treatment in HCT 116 cells generates a unique pattern of SBS and INDELs that are a mixture of several signatures with known etiology, including pks+ E. coli infection and MMRd.

To determine if there was an association between pks- and MMRd-associated mutations in human CRC patients, we analyzed the pairwise co-occurrence of SBS signatures identified by a prior study of 2945 CRC samples [41]. Out of the 20 COSMIC SBS signatures with proposed etiology identified in this CRC cohort, 3 showed a significant co-occurrence with SBS88, including the MMRd-associated SBS44 (Fig. 5i). A co-occurrence of SBS88 and SBS44 was found in a total of 9.8% of CRC patients. Moreover, the number of mutations attributed to SBS44 was significantly higher in SBS88-positive CRC patients than SBS88-negative CRC patients (Fig. 5j). These findings show that in a subset of CRC patients, the presence of SBS88 (attributed to pks) is associated with a higher number of MMRd mutations.

Since the discovery of the pks cluster in 2006 and the observations that this cluster is correlated with human CRC shortly thereafter, there has been intensive interest in determining if pks+ bacteria and the final product of the pathway – colibactin (771) – play a causal role in CRC. Our results demonstrate that exposure to pks+ E. coli or synthetic colibactin itself increases the number of mutations in mammalian cells. While two recent studies established that pks bacteria induce mutagenesis in vitro [14,15], linking this phenotype directly to colibactin (771) has not been previously possible due to an inability to isolate colibactin (771) in homogenous form. To circumvent this, we employed the derivative colibactin 742
[26]. which was designed to mimic the bioactivity of natural colibactin (771) and have greater stability owing to the conversion of the central α-aminoketone of colibactin 771 to a stable two-carbon linker. The former is a known locus of instability that prevents purification and handling of natural colibactin 771 [49,50]. While our experiments revealed that high concentrations of colibactin 746 inhibited colonoid growth, this observation was independent of DNA damage. Using the same concentration (100  µM) we do not observe an increase in γH2AX foci formation in IEC-6 cells or human colonoids. Moreover, we saw no increase in transcriptional pathways related to DNA damage. While the mechanism for colibactin 746 inhibiting colonoid growth remains unclear, our evidence suggests that colibactin 746 is incapable of damaging DNA. In tandem, we utilized a murine model of colitis associated cancer (ApcMin/+/DSS) to determine the mutagenic consequence of pks infection in vivo. Colibactin 742 exposure in a MMRd cell line increased the number of SBS signatures associated with pks+ E. coli (SBS88) and MMRd itself (SBS44). Similarly, colonization of ApcMin/+ mice with pks+ E. coli increased the number of SBS attributed to the MMRd-associated signature SBS15. Given that prior studies [14,15] have shown that the mutational signature SBS88 is derived from pks+ E. coli infection, and that this signature is prevalent in colorectal cancer patients, it was surprising that we did not detect this signature in ApcMin/+/DSS colon tumors after NC101 colonization. Importantly, SBS88 has not yet been detected in murine tumors after pks+ E. coli infection. Prior epidemiological studies have suggested that colibactin's carcinogenic activity may result from early-life exposures that take decades to develop into CRC [51]. In the context of this study, it remains unclear if predisposed animal models (such as ApcMin/+/DSS mice over a period of six weeks) develop more colonic tumors after pks+ E. coli colonization through an identical mutagenic mechanism. These results support the notion that, in addition to generating SBS88, colibactin exposure may cause mutations through mechanisms that do not yield a canonical signature [18,52,53].

Accumulating evidence suggests that colibactin alkylates DNA to form ICLs, a hypothesis supported by our finding that colibactin 742 but not 746 exposure activates ICL repair pathways. Moreover, prior studies utilizing cell-free systems have demonstrated that colibactin 742 (but not 746) forms interstrand DNA cross-links [26]. However, our study did not investigate the binding specificity of colibactin 742. Our observation that 742 treatment causes mutations that only partially fit the previously reported colibactin motif may be the result of multiple scenarios. First, removing the hydrophobic diketone linker in 742 to stabilize the compound may alter the molecule's DNA-binding motif, resulting in more promiscuous lesion formation and consequently, mutagenesis. Alternatively, MMRd mutations may render DNA more susceptible to off-motif mutagenesis through an unclear mechanism. For example, when DNA lesions cannot be effectively removed, cells may utilize translesion synthesis (TLS) to bypass alkylated nucleotides using error-prone DNA polymerases [54]. Indeed, the MMR pathway plays a critical role in maintaining genomic integrity after errors associated with (TLS) [55]. During TLS, specialized error-prone polymerases are recruited to the lesion site but also exhibit high activity within an approximately 100 base-pair window, generating off-lesion mutations that are similarly corrected via MMR [56]. This scenario may explain why we observe an increase in MMRd-associated mutations in regions that do not fit the predicted colibactin target motif (AAWWTT). This hypothesis is consistent with our observation that chronic colibactin 742 treatment caused a fixed upregulation of MMR pathways. Future studies characterizing interactions between colibactin 742 and DNA are needed to understand the biological relevance of the observations reported here, and to determine to what extent colibactin 742 recapitulates the biology of natural colibactin.

These findings may have important implications for accurately assessing the contribution of pks+ E. coli in human CRC. Mutational signature analysis can be used to identify the biological processes underlying individual cancers [57], and identification of pks-associated signatures can provide evidence that colibactin is the causative environmental carcinogen in these cases. However, the generation of these signatures is influenced both by the nature of DNA damage and the mechanisms used to resolve it [58]. Therefore, if colibactin exposure generates non-canonical mutational phenotypes we may underappreciate the extent of the genotoxin's effect in human CRC. A related question is whether patients with MMRd are then more susceptible to CRC caused by pks+ E. coli than other individuals. Our analysis suggests that the MMRd-associated signature SBS44 co-occurs with SBS88, suggesting that MMRd patients may be at a higher risk of CRC after pks+ E. coli infection. However, these associations must be assessed using natural colibactin in the context of MMRd to draw firm conclusions regarding the translational relevance of these findings. In conclusion, our study demonstrates a novel mutational consequence of colibactin exposure (i.e., SBS44) and suggests that MMRd-associated mutations may contribute to pks+ E. coli-mediated carcinogenesis.
