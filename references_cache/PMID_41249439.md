# NDRG2 upregulation induces astrocytic apoptosis via AKT inhibition and FoxO3a-Puma activation in depression.
**Authors:** Wang Z, Yang J, Zhang H, Ke D, Zhang L, Liu Y, Lai Y, Zhang Y, Qin X, Yang J, Kang M, Yang W, Wang X, Zeng J, Liu R
**Journal:** Commun Biol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s42003-025-08968-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-025-08968-y)

## Content

The loss of astrocytes represents a key pathological hallmark of major depressive disorder, yet its mechanisms remain elusive. Here, we identified NDRG2 as a critical regulator of astrocyte apoptosis in depression. NDRG2 expression is upregulated in depression models, concomitant with astrocyte loss. Overexpression of NDRG2 in astrocytes triggered apoptosis and depressive-like behaviors, with inhibition of AKT and activation of FoxO3a-Puma apoptotic signaling pathway. Mechanistically, NDRG2 recruits PP2A to AKT, facilitating its dephosphorylation and subsequent inactivation, then promotes the nuclear translocation of FoxO3a, leading to the transcriptional activation of the pro-apoptotic factor Puma. Crucially, NDRG2 knockdown in astrocytes effectively prevented astrocytic apoptosis, with effective rescue of depressive-like phenotypes. These findings reveal a key mechanism of astrocyte death in depression, wherein NDRG2 functions as an adaptor to promote PP2A-mediated AKT dephosphorylation, thereby initiating the pro-apoptotic signaling pathway, and disclose a druggable pathway for developing antidepressants targeting astrocyte preservation.

The high global prevalence and disability make major depressive disorder (MDD) one of the most common and burdened psychiatric diseases. Approximately 300 million people worldwide are suffering from this illness, with the incidence increasing annually1. As one of the most debilitating psychiatric conditions, MDD’s complex etiology involves genetic predisposition, environmental stressors and psychological factors, in which chronic stress is the most common risk factor2,3. There are several recognized hypotheses of MDD pathogenesis, including monoamine hypothesis, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysfunction hypothesis, inflammatory hypothesis and neuroplasticity hypothesis, but none is able to fully elucidate the mechanism of MDD4,5. This knowledge gap has severely limited treatment options, leaving many patients without effective therapies6,7. Therefore, it is extremely urgent to disclose the molecular mechanisms and explore new therapeutic strategies of MDD.

Astrocytes are the predominant glial cells in the central nervous system (CNS), constituting approximately 30% of the total number of cells in mammalian brains8. Astrocytes execute a lot of essential functions in the developing and mature brain. During development, they orchestrate the formation of neural circuits by participating in synapse formation, pruning, elimination and maturation9,10. In the mature nervous system, astrocytes participate in the synthesis and transport of neurotransmitters, maintain the ion balance and the integrity of blood-brain barrier, and are deeply involved in neuronal energy metabolism11–13. Astrocyte dysfunction has been firmly established as a key contributor to depressive-like behaviors through multiple pathological mechanisms14. Disrupted glutamate-glutamine cycles in astrocytes contribute to neurotoxicity and induce depressive phenotype in rats exposed to chronic social defeat stress (CSDS)15. Metabolic disturbance of glucose in astrocytes is also highly associated with depression. Both MDD patients and depression models consistently reveal significant impairments in astrocytic glucose metabolism and glycolysis pathways, which actively contribute to depression pathogenesis16,17.Moreover, astrocytes release ATP which mediates antidepressant-like effects, and deficiencies in astrocytic ATP release cause depressive-like behaviors in mice18. Besides cell dysfunction, the cell number of astrocytes is also reduced in individuals with depression, which is recognized as one of the main pathological hallmarks of depression. Postmortem studies and preclinical investigations consistently demonstrate significant reductions in astrocyte density across depression-relevant brain regions, particularly in the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex (PFC), and amygdala19,20. This pathological feature has been functionally validated through experimental models using L-alpha-aminoadipic acid (L-AAA), where selective astrocyte ablation in either the PFC or hippocampus reliably induces depression-like behavioral phenotypes in rodents21,22. Despite these compelling clinical and experimental observations, the precise molecular mechanisms driving astrocyte loss in depression remain poorly understood.

N-myc downstream-regulated gene 2 (NDRG2) is a member of the NDRG family. As a tumor suppressor, NDRG2 exerts anti-tumor effect by enhancing cell apoptosis23. Beyond its role in cancer biology, NDRG2 has been identified as a stress-responsive gene, which is upregulated by cellular stress stimuli, such as hormones, hypoxia, and DNA damage24,25. In the brain, NDRG2 is selectively and predominately expressed in astrocytes26. Emerging evidence highlights its pathological significance in neurological disorders. In subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), NDRG2 is upregulated and indirectly promotes expression of matrix metalloproteinases 9 (MMP-9), which further triggers blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption27. Another study shows that suppression of NDRG2 could rescue glutamate neurotoxicity through NF-κB/glutamate transporter 1 (GLT-1) signaling, thus alleviate brain damage after intracerebral hemorrhage28. Moreover, overexpression of NDRG2 elevates extracellular glutamate by downregulating GLT-1, and subsequently induces neuropathic pain in rats29. However, the critical role of NDRG2 in mediating astrocyte dysfunction and cell death in MDD pathogenesis remains unclarified.

Previous studies revealed that NDRG2 expression was increased in the hippocampus of chronic psychosocial stress model rats30. And NDRG2 mRNA levels were upregulated by dexamethasone in a concentration-dependent manner in astrocytes31. Notably, antidepressants, such as imipramine and sertraline, could reserve the stress-induced NDRG2 upregulation32. These evidences raise the possibility that NDRG2 participates in the pathogenesis of depression through regulating the function and cell fate of astrocytes. In this study, we have uncovered a previously unrecognized molecular cascade in which NDRG2 induces astrocyte apoptosis via the PP2A/AKT/FoxO3a/Puma signaling pathway, thus contributing to the development of depression. These findings provide a molecular explanation for stress-induced astrocyte death and identify NDRG2 as both a key pathological mediator and a promising therapeutic target in depression.

To explore the potential role of NDRG2 in loss of astrocytes in depression, we established chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) rat model to assess the correlation between NDRG2 and loss of astrocytes. The results of sucrose preference test (SPT) and three-chamber social interaction test (SIT) confirmed the successful establishment of chronic stress model with depressive-like phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). NDRG2 was observed specifically expressed in GFAP-positive astrocytes, not in NeuN-positive neurons or Iba1-positive microglial cells, which was consistent with previous studies26 (Supplementary Fig. 1C). We further analyzed the RNA-sequencing transcriptome of different types of cells in mouse brain cortex using available Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset (GSE52564)33, and found that NDRG2 was expressed at a much higher level in astrocytes compared to neurons and microglia (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Compared with control, the number of astrocytes was decreased in CA1, CA3 and DG regions of depressed CUMS rats. Astrocyte dysfunction, as characterized by significantly decreased number and average length of branches (Fig. 1A–C), was also revealed. Decreased GFAP protein level was observed in hippocampus of CUMS models by Western blotting (Fig. 1E, F), which is consistent with the astrocyte loss. With the astrocyte loss and dysfunction, the mRNA and protein level of NDRG2 were significantly upregulated in hippocampus of CUMS rat models (Fig. 1D–F). To confirm the astrocyte-specific upregulation of NDRG2 in depressed rats, we used immunofluorescence staining to show NDRG2 in GFAP-positive cells. NDRG2 was co-localized with GFAP and its fluorescence intensity was increased in astrocytes in hippocampus of CUMS rat models (Fig. 1G, H). Next, we explored whether apoptosis was induced by NDRG2 in astrocytes. The results showed that overexpression of NDRG2 resulted in increased level of cleaved-Caspase 3, however, it had no impact on the expression and activity of Mixed lineage kinase domain-like (MLKL) (Fig. 3C, D, Supplementary Fig. 2D-E). MLKL is the main necroptosis executor and mediates the plasma membrane permeabilization34. LDH assay revealed cytotoxicity induced by upregulation of NDRG2 (Fig. 3E). In addition, TUNEL staining also exhibited the pro-apoptotic effect of NDRG2 overexpression in astrocytes (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. 2F). These findings indicated that overexpression NDRG2 induced astrocytic apoptosis, it may be the underlying mechanism by which chronic stress leads to loss of astrocytes.

Could NDRG2 be targeted to protect astrocytes under chronic stress? To answer this question, primary astrocytes were infected with shNDRG2 lentivirus to knockdown NDRG2. We found that NDRG2 knockdown effectively reduced cleaved-Caspase 3 level (Fig. 3G, H), ameliorated cytotoxicity induced by CORT (Fig. 3I), and rescued apoptosis (Fig. 3J, Supplementary Fig. 2G). Taken together, we confirmed that upregulation of NDRG2 induced apoptosis and its knockdown protected astrocytes from cell death induced by CORT. Thus, NDRG2 is an important mediator of chronic stress-induced apoptosis of astrocytes.

To explore the molecular mechanisms by which NDRG2 induces astrocytic apoptosis, we analyzed the DEGs between astrocytes with and without NDRG2 overexpression, and found the significant upregulation of p53 up-regulated pro-apoptotic factor (Puma) (Fig. 3A). Puma is a Bcl-2 family member which drives apoptosis triggered by a wide range of stimuli, including DNA damage, nutritional deprivation, and dexamethasone35,36. Increased Puma expression induced by NDRG2 was further identified through validating the mRNA and protein levels of Puma in NDRG2-overexpessing primary astrocytes (Fig. 4B–D). Puma was transcriptionally activated in a p53-dependent manner35,37. Additionally, it was also induced by p53-independent signaling, such as Fork head box O3a (FoxO3a) and nuclear factor-kappaB (NF-kappaB)38–40. Through KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs between astrocytes with and without NDRG2 overexpression, we found that FoxO signaling pathway was highly associated with NDRG2 overexpression (Fig. 4A). Thus, we speculated that FoxO3a may be involved in Puma transcription induced by NDRG2 overexpression. To verify this speculation, FoxO3a protein levels were detected and found notably decreased in the cytoplasm and increased in the nucleus after overexpression of NDRG2 (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence also revealed that NDRG2 overexpression promoted FoxO3a nuclear translocation (Supplementary Fig. 3I, left). Furthermore, ChIP-qPCR assays verified the direct binding of FoxO3a to the PUMA promoter. And NDRG2 overexpression potentiated FoxO3a recruitment, thereby upregulating PUMA transcription (Supplementary Fig. 3C). These results indicate that NDRG2 overexpression induced FoxO3a translocation into the nucleus, thus inducing Puma transcription. Transport of FoxO3a across the nucleus is regulated by its phosphorylation. Phosphorylated FoxO3a binds to 14-3-3 proteins, and this interaction keeps FoxO3a in the cytoplasm41,42. Cytoplasmic FoxO3a interacts with Murine double minute 2 (MDM2) and results in FoxO3a degradation via ubiquitin-proteasome pathway43. On the contrary, dephosphorylated FoxO3a entered the nucleus and mediated Puma transcription. We detected the phosphorylation level of FoxO3a at pS253 and found that NDRG2 induced significant dephosphorylation of FoxO3a (Fig. 4C, D), which is consistent with its nucleus translocation. We also found that NDRG2 overexpression increased the total protein level of FoxO3a (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 3A), which may be a result of less degradation in cytoplasm due to nucleus translocation. Next unanswered question is what is the upstream mechanism of FoxO3a dephosphorylation in NDRG2-overexpressed astrocytes? It has been reported that the serine/threonine kinase AKT is an important kinase to regulate FoxO3a phosphorylation44,45. In our study, the upregulation of NDRG2 was accompanied with decreased AKT phosphorylation at Thr308 and Ser473 (Fig. 4C, D), indicating that AKT inhibition may be responsible for dephosphorylation of FoxO3a. To further identify this signaling pathway and its key role in mediating astrocytic cell apoptosis in depression, we observed the changes of these proteins in CORT-treated astrocytes, with or without NDRG2 knockdown. Consistent with those observations in NDRG2-overexpressed astrocytes, CORT decreased AKT and FoxO3a phosphorylation, increased FoxO3a level in nucleus (Fig. 4F–H, Supplementary Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. 3I, right), and thus induced Puma expression. And all of these changes were rescued by NDRG2 knockdown (Fig. 4F, G, Supplementary Fig. 3I, right). Taken together, these data revealed that NDRG2 induced apoptosis through inhibiting AKT, thus upregulating FoxO3a-Puma pathway in primary astrocytes, and knockdown of NDRG2 inhibited Puma expression and cell apoptosis.

FoxO3a is a classic phosphorylation target of constitutive phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-AKT46,47, and active PI3K mediated downstream responses through regulating the phosphorylation and activity of AKT48,49. To explore the mechanism by which NDRG2 lead to AKT dephosphorylation and inhibition, we detected the protein levels of total and active form of PI3K. The results showed that overexpression or knockdown of NDRG2 had no impact on PI3K expression and phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 3E–H). This suggested that NDRG2 induced AKT dephosphorylation independent of PI3K regulation.

Studies implied that NDRG2 bind serine/threonine protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) and form a complex to dephosphorylate various target proteins24. AKT was revealed to be dephosphorylated by PP2A at its activation-determining sites Thr308 and Ser47350. Thus, we speculate whether NDRG2 could recruit PP2A to AKT and mediate AKT dephosphorylation (Fig. 5A). To verify this hypothesis, cell lysates from primary astrocytes treated with CORT were immunoprecipitated with an anti-NDRG2 antibody, and the proteins that interacted with NDRG2 were detected. We found that NDRG2 interacted with AKT and catalytic subunit of PP2A (PP2Ac), while did not interact with scaffolding A-subunit of PP2A (Supplementary Fig. 4A). To further verify the interaction among NDRG2, AKT and PP2A, immunoprecipitation was performed with an anti-AKT antibody, the presence of NDRG2-AKT-PP2Ac complex in primary astrocytes treated with CORT was confirmed. Furthermore, it was found that upregulation of NDRG2 by CORT promoted the interaction of AKT and PP2Ac, and the interacted AKT was dephosphorylated at Thr308 and Ser473 (Fig. 5B). Next, we overexpressed NDRG2 in primary astrocytes to further confirm the presence of NDRG2-AKT-PP2Ac complex. Astrocytes are the most numerous neural cells in human brain. They play crucial roles in maintaining CNS homeostasis and BBB integrity52,53. Through regulating the uptake, synthesis and transformation of neurotransmitters, releasing neuromodulators, and supplying energy substrates to neurons, astrocytes exert important functions in numerous physiological processes of the brain including learning, memory, and emotion54,55. Astrocytes are vulnerable to stresses. Glucocorticoid receptor (GR) in astrocytes was more sensitive to stressors than those in neurons56. Astrocytic dysfunction and loss were observed in lots of neurological diseases, including MDD. Postmortem analysis of MDD patients indicated that the number of astrocytes was significant decreased in hippocampus and prefrontal cortex57; deletion of astrocytes in the PFC or hippocampus of rats could induce depressive-like behaviors21,22. However, the characteristic and mechanism of astrocytic cell death in depression remains unrevealed.

In this study, we proved that astrocytic cell apoptosis, not necroptosis, participated in the pathogenesis of depression induced by chronic stresses. We found that the apoptosis markers, such as cleaved-Caspase 3, Puma, and positive TUNEL staining were remarkably increased in brain of depressive animal models and in astrocytes treated with stress hormone, and a pan-Caspase inhibitor reversed cell death in stress hormone-treated astrocytes. Besides, KEGG enrichment analysis also suggested that astrocytes were suffering from apoptosis. In addition, we excluded necroptosis since the expression and activity of MLKL were unchanged. We further elucidated the mechanism for astrocyte loss in cell and animal models of depression and concluded that NDRG2 play a key role in mediating apoptotic astrocyte death.

In the brain, NDRG2 is predominantly expressed in astrocytes26, and previous studies identified that upregulation of NDRG2 was induced by stress both in vivo and in vitro58. Glucocorticoid increased the expression of NDRG2 in astrocytes, which could be reversed by glucocorticoid receptor antagonist31,59. However, limited studies have elaborated on the effect of increased NDRG2 in astrocytes and its role in the development of stress-related disorders such as depression. Ichikawa et al. reported that Ndrg2-deficient mice exhibited reduced anxiety-like behaviors, and treatment of wild-type mice with the antidepressant drug imipramine decreased the expression of NDRG2 in the frontal cortex60. These findings, together with the observations in our study that NDRG2 expression is increased in animal models of depression, specific overexpression of NDRG2 in astrocytes induces depressive-like phenotype, and NDRG2 knockdown effectively improves depression-like behaviors in CRS mice, strongly indicate that astrocytic NDRG2 upregulation contributes to the development of depression.

As a stress-responsive protein and tumor suppressor, NDRG2 is downregulated in numerous tumor tissues, and its upregulation is proven to promote apoptosis of cancer cells23. In our study, we identify that under chronic stresses, increased NDRG2 induces the apoptosis of astrocytes, thus promoting the development of depressive-like phenotype. To our knowledge, this is the first report elucidating the role of NDRG2 in depression through mediating astrocyte apoptosis. Future studies employing apoptosis blockers to directly test whether preventing NDGR2-dependent astrocyte death rescues depression behavior may help further identify this hypothesis.

For the signaling pathways of NDRG2-induced apoptosis in tumor cells, several molecular mechanisms were revealed, including LKB1-AMPK pathway61, STAT3/TIMP3/MMP pathway62, and so on23. In our study, to disclose the molecular mechanism underlying NDRG2-induced astrocytic apoptosis, we performed RNA-seq and found NDRG2 overexpression induced the upregulation of proapoptotic factor Puma and the enrichment of FoxO signaling pathway. Biochemical detections on stressed cells and animal models further support that NDRG2 upregulation promotes FoxO3a dephosphorylation and translocation to the nucleus, thus increasing Puma transcription. While our RNA-seq and biochemical data clearly demonstrate NDRG2 mediates astrocytic apoptosis through FoxO3a-Puma signaling, other pro-apoptotic pathways, such as p53 and NF-κB signaling, could not be excluded. However, p53 is known to transcriptionally upregulate NDRG2 in both nucleus pulposus (NP) cells and the human tumor cell line H129963,64. For NF-κB signaling, a study showed that NDRG2 suppresses rather than activates NF-κB -- evidenced by reduced p-NF-κB and complement C3 levels upon NDRG2 overexpression65. These findings collectively argue against p53 or NF-κB involvement in NDRG2-mediated Puma induction, instead establishing FoxO3a as the dominant transcriptional regulator in stress-induced astrocytic apoptosis.

To clarify the link between NDRG2 and FoxO3a dephosphorylation, we detected the change of PI3K-AKT signaling and PP2A recruitment to NDRG2. It has been reported that AKT could regulate FoxO3a phosphorylation44,45. On the other side, NDRG2 has been revealed to recruit PP2A to various proteins and mediates dephosphorylation of these proteins24. In particular, phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted on chromosome 10 (PTEN) is a target molecule of the NDRG2/PP2A complex. Through recruitment of PP2A, NDRG2 maintains the dephosphorylated status of PTEN to suppress excessive PI3K/AKT signaling. In adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, loss of NDRG2 expression leads to the failed recruitment of PP2A to PTEN, resulting in the phosphorylation and inactivation of PTEN51. Based on these findings, we speculate that in astrocytes, NDRG2 may also act as a PP2A adaptor to regulate AKT activation and FoxO3a phosphorylation. We first examined the expression and activity of PI3K, a classical upstream kinase of AKT, and confirmed that NDRG2 did not influence the PI3K phosphorylation. Through multiple immunoprecipitation detections on samples from cells and brain tissues, we identified that NDRG2 could directly recruit PP2A to AKT and mediate AKT dephosphorylation. Under chronic stress, NDRG2 is upregulated and induces increased interaction of PP2A with AKT, which results in AKT dephosphorylation/inhibition, FoxO3a dephosphorylation/nucleus translocation, and Puma overexpression.

While our current study demonstrates that NDRG2 mediates astrocyte apoptosis via the AKT-FoxO3a-Puma axis, emerging evidence suggests upregulated NDRG2 in astrocytes may also influence neuronal viability and function through multiple mechanisms. In sepsis-associated encephalopathy, upregulated NDRG2 in astrocytes inhibits Na-K-ATPaseβ1 and EAAT2, disrupting glutamate metabolism and causing neuronal dysfunction and apoptosis -- phenotypes reversed by NDRG2 knockdown66. Similarly, NDRG2 is up-regulated in hemoglobin-treated astrocytes (cell model of hemorrhagic stroke), where it  suppresses EAAT2 expression and impairs glutamate clearance, ultimately affecting neuronal activity67. Besides, astrocytic NDRG2 overexpression enhances microglial secretion of microRNA-37-3p, which promotes neuronal degeneration68. These findings collectively suggest that beyond its pro-apoptotic effects in astrocytes, NDRG2 may contribute to depression pathogenesis by disrupting neuron-glia communication through signaling molecules such as glutamate and microRNAs. Our future research will further explore the role of NDRG2 in regulating astrocyte-neuron metabolic coupling.

In conclusion, we clarified a molecular mechanism of astrocyte death during the development of depression under stress conditions: Stress induces the upregulation of NDRG2 in astrocytes, and NDRG2 recruits PP2A to AKT and mediates its dephosphorylation, inactive AKT further promotes the dephosphorylation/redistribution of FoxO3a and transcription of pro-apoptotic factor Puma. However, although our current study provides multiple lines of evidence for the role of NDRG2-PP2A-AKT-FoxO3a-Puma axis in depression-associated astrocyte apoptosis, future investigations employing apoptosis blockers, constitutive AKT activation or direct FoxO3a/Puma manipulation could further validate this pathway’s specificity and therapeutic potential. Our findings provide compelling evidence for the molecular basis of stress-induced astrocyte loss and establish NDRG2 as both a pivotal pathological regulator and a potential therapeutic target for depressive disorders.

Each mouse or rat was kept in a cage individually and provided with two identical bottles containing 2% sucrose solution or pure water for 12 h. The sucrose preference was calculated as followed: Sucrose preference (%) = [(sucrose consumption) / (water consumption + sucrose consumption)] × 100%.

We utilized a three-chamber social interaction test to assess sociability of rats. The test was conducted in a box (40 × 20 × 20 cm) composed of three chambers. Each chamber was separated by acrylic glass walls, and have corridors (10 × 15 cm) to facilitate passage to each chamber. During the habituation phase, the rat was placed in the middle chamber to allow exploring freely for 10 min. During the testing phase, a stranger rat was kept under a transparent plastic box in one of the side chambers randomly, and the other chamber remained empty. The rat was kept in the middle chamber to travel freely through three chambers for 5 min. The time spent in each chamber were recorded to evaluate sociability to judge depression-like behavior.

The experimental field is an empty, 50 × 50 × 50 cm plastic container (Techman Software Co, Ltd, Chengdu, China), and it was divided into 25 equal squares (5 × 5 sectors), in which the middle (3 × 3 sectors) squares were regarded as central area, others were peripheral areas. Before the test, all mice were placed in the experimental room for 2 h. At the beginning of test, mice were put in center of the experimental container and allowed to explore randomly for 5 min. After each trial, the field was cleaned with 75% alcohol to eliminate olfactory cues. The total distance and time were recorded to assess the general locomotor activity. And the center duration (%) and distance (%) were calculated to assess anxiety-like behavior.

The device is composed of two open arms (25 cm long, 6 cm wide), two closed arms (25 cm long, 6 cm wide) and a central platform. The apparatus is 50 cm high from the floor. At the start of the test, mice were placed on the central platform and allowed to move freely for 5 minutes. The time spent on the open arms and the number of entries into open arms were recorded to evaluate anxiety-like behavior.

The mice were inverted on the apparatus 15 cm above the floor and tails were fixed with adhesive plaster. The struggling time and immobility time were recorded in 5 minutes.

Mice were placed in a transparent container (25 cm high × 18 cm diameter) filled with clean water of suitable temperature to force swimming for 5 min. The struggling time and immobility time were recorded to assess depression-like behavior.

Mice or rats were anesthetized and perfused with 0.9% normal saline and then 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) intracardially. Then brains were fixed with 4% PFA at 4 °C for 48 h. Next, they were immersed in 30% sucrose in Phosphate Buffer (PB) to dehydrate and embedded in OCT (optimum cutting temperature compound, Sakura, USA), sectioned at 30 μm using a cryotome (CM1950, Leica, Germany).

Brain slices were washed with PBS buffer and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin, slices were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. They were washed three times with TBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h. After stained with DAPI (Cat#C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), slices were mounted on glass slides using antifade reagent (Cat#G1401, Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China). All images were captured and analyzed by confocal microscope (LSM800, Zeiss, Germany).

Cell lysate samples and brain tissue were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with proteinase inhibitor cocktail (1:100, Yeasen Biotech, Shanghai, China Cat#20124ES03) and phosphatase inhibitors (1:100, PMSF). Then loading buffer (final concentration: 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 10 mM DTT, and 0.2% bromphenol blue) added in above solutions and boiled for 10 min. Next, they were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min and the supernatants were collected to sonicated.

Protein samples were electrophoresed on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel and then transferred to 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes (Cytiva, USA). The membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat dried milk for 1 h, and then incubated with each primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Next, membranes were washed by TBST 2 times for 15 min each and incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. After being washed again, they were visualized using Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LICOR Biosciences, USA) and analyzed quantitatively by Image J software (Rawak Software, Inc.).

ChIP was performed using the ChIP Assay Kit (beyotime, #P2078) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, primary astrocytes were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by quenching with 10X glycine solution. After washing with PBS containing 1 mM PMSF, cells were lysed in SDS lysis buffer and sonicated to shear chromatin. The lysates were diluted with ChIP dilution buffer and pre-cleared with Protein A + G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4 °C with the FoxO3a antibody or control IgG. Protein A + G Agarose was added to capture the antibody-chromatin complexes, which were then washed sequentially with Low Salt, High Salt, LiCl, and TE buffers. The precipitated DNA was eluted and purified using the PCR/DNA Purification Kit (beyotime, #D0033). RT- qPCR was performed to analyze the enriched DNA fragments. Input DNA was used as a normalization control.

To detect interaction of proteins, cell lysate samples were solubilized with RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with proteinase inhibitor cocktail (1:100) and phosphatase inhibitors (1:100, PMSF). And they were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min. The supernatants were collected and protein concentrations were measured by BCA kit (Cat#P0012, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Then, protein was incubated with primary antibodies against NDRG2 (1:50) or AKT (1:30) and control immunoglobulin G at 4 °C overnight. Protein A + G Agarose beads (Cat#P2012, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were added to continue incubation 6–8 hours. After washing three times with PBS, beads were resuspended with loading buffer and boiled for 10 min. Last, the bound proteins were collected for Western blotting.

Cytoplasmic and nuclear extracts were prepared according to the instruction of Nuclear-Cytosol Extraction Kit (Cat#P1200, Applygen, Beijing, China). The extracts were collected for Western blotting.

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol Reagent method according to the manufacturer’s instructions (RNAiso Plus, Cat#9801, Takara, Japan), and the transcription kit (Cat#RK20433, Abclonal, Wuhan, Hubei, China) was used to obtain first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted by 2X Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (Cat#RK21203, Abclonal, Wuhan, Hubei, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The primer sequences used were as follows: NDRG2 (Mus musculus): forward (F)- CAGGACAAACACCCGAGACT, reverse (R)-AGCCATAAGGTGTCTCCACAG; NDRG2 (Rattus norvegicus): forward (F)- AACTTTGAGCGAGGTGGTGAGA, reverse (R)-ATTCCACCACGGCATCTTCA; MLKL: forward (F)-GGCCAGGTCATCCACAAAC, reverse (R)-TATCTCCCCATTAGCCTCCTC; Bbc3: forward (F)-AGTGCGCCTTCACTTTGG, reverse (R)-CAGGAGGCTAGTGGTCAGGT; Puma: forward (F)- AGTGCGCCTTCACTTTGG, reverse (R)- CAGGAGGCTAGTGGTCAGGT; GAPDH: forward (F)-TAAGCAGTTGGTGGTGCAGG; reverse (R)-GAGCCAAAAGGGTCATCATC. All primers were supplied by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

The LDH cytotoxicity test was detected according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat# C0017, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Primary astrocytes were cultured in 24-well plates for staining. Cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min, and incubated with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min for permeabilization. Double staining with NDRG2 and TUNEL were performed to mark apoptosis cells. The TUNEL staining was followed by the manufacturer’s procedure (Cat# KTA2011, Abbkine, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Nuclear were stained with DAPI (Cat#C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Images were visualized with confocal microscope (LSM800, Zeiss, Germany).

Primary astrocytes were collected in Trizol reagent and sent to the sequencing company (Majorbio, Shanghai, China) to perform subsequent transcriptome sequencing. In briefly, samples were checked for quality firstly. And then, RNA extraction, RNA detection, library construction and sequencing were performed in succession. Finally, data analysis and visualization were conducted using interactive website by Majorbio.

C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized and fixed in a stereotaxic frame. The coordinates of injection site targeted the upper of dorsal DG (anteroposterior [AP] –1.9 mm, mediolateral [ML] ± 1.1 mm, dorsoventral [DV] −1.8 mm). Both sides of hippocampus were injected with 2 µl AAV (∼1013 v.g./mL) at a rate of 0.2 µl/min. The needle was kept 10 min before withdrawal. The AAV virus carry astrocyte-specific promoter GFAP to achieve specific transfection in astrocytes, and they were constructed and packaged by Syngenbio (Beijing, China). pAAV-GFAP-NDRG2-EGFP-3FLAG was used to overexpress NDRG2, and pAAV-GFAP-NDRG2 shRNA-EGFP-3FLAG was used to induce the expression of NDRG2 shRNA.

All data were presented as the mean ± SEM, and analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA). A two-tailed Student’s test was used to determine between two groups. For the differences among multiple groups, the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was used. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The value “n” denotes the number of biologically independent samples or experimental replicates, with precise numerical values provided in the respective figure legends.
