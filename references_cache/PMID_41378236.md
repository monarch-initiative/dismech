# Cobalamin J Disorder in a Teenage Boy with Recurrent Abdominal Pain Attacks: A Case Report and Literature Review.
**Authors:** Aslan D, Uctepe E, Yesilyurt A, Esen FN, Dalgıç B
**Journal:** Mol Syndromol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000544821](https://doi.org/10.1159/000544821)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Cobalamin J disease (CblJ) is an ultrarare autosomal recessive disorder of intracellular cobalamin metabolism associated with combined methylmalonic academia and homocystinuria (MAHCJ; 614857).

CASE PRESENTATION: A new patient with MAHCJ, representing the eighth documented instance, is reported here. A novel homozygous missense variant c.1591C>T (p.Arg531Trp) in exon 17 of ABCD4 (NM_005050.4) was identified. The patient, a 15-year-old male of Azerbaijani descent, presented with severe abdominal pain beginning at the age of 1 year. These episodic pain attacks were accompanied by hypotonia, pallor, nausea, and vomiting. Initial evaluations were inconclusive. At the age of 8 years, the patient developed megaloblastic anemia due to vitamin B12 deficiency, leading to the initiation of replacement therapy. The pain attacks ceased during treatment but recurred whenever vitamin B12 levels dropped after discontinuation. The patient exhibited no dysmorphology, skin hyperpigmentation, somatic abnormalities, seizures, or neurodevelopmental delays and remains in remission with ongoing vitamin B12 treatment.

DISCUSSION: This patient is the oldest diagnosed with MAHCJ and has the longest documented clinical course. This report expands the known clinical and molecular spectrum of this rare disease. We recommend remembering cobalamin defects in the differential diagnosis of unresolved abdominal pain attacks.

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is required to function two enzymes: methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Intracellularly, absorbed cobalamin undergoes a series of biochemical modifications to form methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin, which act as coenzymes for these enzymes, respectively. Methionine synthase catalyzes the methylation of homocysteine (Hcy) to form methionine, and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase catalyzes the conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA [1]. Deficiencies and inherited defects of cobalamin can result in high levels of Hcy and methylmalonic acid (MMA) in blood and urine. Inherited cobalamin defects affecting cellular cobalamin metabolism may lead to isolated homocystinuria, isolated methylmalonic aciduria, or combined homocystinuria and methylmalonic aciduria, depending on which step in cellular cobalamin metabolism is affected [2, 3]. In patients with disorders affecting early steps in cellular cobalamin metabolism or the transport of cobalamin across the lysosomal membrane into the cytoplasm, elevated levels of Hcy and MMA are observed in blood and urine [2]. In MAHCJ, cobalamin release from lysosome into the cytoplasm is defective, and two metabolites accumulate, leading to clinical complications. MAHCJ was defined in 2012 [2], and only 7 patients have been reported so far [4–8]. Despite its rarity, this disorder has a variable phenotype and age of onset. The main findings are feeding difficulties, hypotonia, neurodevelopmental delay, and hematologic abnormalities. This article presents a new patient with unusual clinical presentation and molecular features and broadens the phenotype of this ultrarare disorder.

A 15-year-old male was referred to the hematology department due to vitamin B12 deficiency dependent on parenteral therapy. He was the second child of consanguineous, healthy Azerbaijani parents, born at term with a weight and height of 2,900 g (10–25 centile) and 49 cm (25–50 centile), respectively. The patient had two elder healthy siblings. The woman had four previous pregnancy losses in the first trimester.

Severe abdominal pain was observed within the first year of life and continued as periodic episodes characterized by nausea, vomiting, anorexia, profound weakness, fatigue, feeding difficulties, and weight loss. The patient did not experience fever. The duration of these episodes varied. Extensive investigations, including stool examinations, urea breath test, tissue transglutaminase IgA antibody, MEFV gene test, sweat chloride test, abdominal ultrasound, and esophagogastroduodenoscopy, did not provide a definite diagnosis. At 8 years old, the patient developed pallor, and subsequent hematologic evaluation identified macrocytic anemia. Serum vitamin B12 level was low (150 ng/mL). Other hematinics were normal. He has initiated vitamin B12 injections (cyanocobalamin [Dodex®]). Once the levels normalized, the replacement therapy was discontinued. However, a few months after cessation, gastrointestinal complaints reemerged. The patient’s vitamin B12 levels were found to be low again, necessitating a repetition of the treatment. Subsequently, cobalamin treatment could not be discontinued as each cessation led to a decrease in levels and a recurrence of symptoms. Therefore, he has been receiving monthly intramuscular injections of cyanocobalamin for the last year without any complaints. At the age of 15 years, his anthropometric measurements were as follows: weight 40 kg (<3 centile), height 160 cm (25 centile), and head circumference 53 cm (10–25 centile). He was thin. There was no sign of skin discoloration, and his hair was black. His intellectual development was similar to that of his healthy peers. He has been able to continue his education successfully. An ophthalmologic evaluation, including retinal examination, revealed no abnormalities. There was no medical history of seizure. The medical family history was unremarkable regarding hematologic, renal, or rheumatologic diseases. In laboratory workup, complete blood count revealed Hb 14 g/dL, Hct 42.8%, MCV 83 fL, MCH 27.2 pg, MCHC 32.8 g/dL, RBC 5.2 1012/L, RDW 13.5%, reticulocyte ratio 0.8%, WBC 7400/mm3 with an absolute neutrophil count of 4,200, platelet 442,000/mm3; serum iron 86 μg/dL, total iron-binding capacity 437 μg/dL, transferrin saturation 20%, ferritin 14.2 ng/mL, vitamin B12 188 pg/mL (298–955), folate 24 ng/mL, vitamin D 25 ng/mL; blood biochemistry revealed normal results. Spot urine and 24-h urine examination, blood gas analysis, and abdominopelvic USG evaluation were normal. No unusual organic acid excretion was observed in urine organic acid evaluation. Metabolic screening (acylcarnitine profile and amino acids) from dried blood impregnated on paper revealed no change in acylcarnitine and amino acid profile. Serum MMA level was 69.8 ng/mL (0–46.6) and Hcy was 6.3 μmol/L (5–15). MMA and Hcy were negative in the urine.

These clinical and biochemical findings suggested a disturbance in vitamin B12 metabolism. Exome Sequencing was performed on the patient. In contrast, repeated serum vitamin B12 levels in the other two siblings remained within the normal range.

We ascertained proband from a consanguineous Azerbaijani family. We obtained written informed consent from the family of the patient for participation in this study.

Genomic DNA was extracted from proband blood samples using the QIAamp system (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Exome sequencing was performed with the Illumina DNA Prep with Exome 2.0/2.5 Plus Enrichment kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Bioinformatic analysis of raw data was performed with VarSome Clinical Software (Saphetor SA, Lausanne, Switzerland) after quality control and alignment to the reference genome GRCh38/hg38 [9]. The fraction of targets covered at least 20× was 97.7% and the average sequencing depth on target was 99%. Variants were filtered according to homozygosity, ClinVar (pathogenic/likely pathogenic/conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity) [10], pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant classification by VarSome Clinical Software [11], and Human Phenotype Ontology terms [12] associated with the clinical findings of the patient. All variants with a minor allele frequency less than 1% in gnomAD database were included. Variants were classified according to the Association for Clinical Genomic Science (ACGS) [13] and Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) Guidelines [3]. Deletion/duplication analysis was performed for patient 1 using the VarSome Clinical Software CNV Caller tool [11].

After variant prioritization in the data, we identified the homozygous missense variant c.1591C>T (p.Arg531Trp) in exon 17 of ABCD4 (NM_005050.4). The arginine at codon 531 is located in the ABC transporter domain and highly conserved. In silico analysis tools predicted deleterious effect on protein structure and function (REVEL: 0.96, BayesDel AF score: 0.48, MetaRNN: 0.99). This variant is present in extremely low frequency in population databases but no homozygous individuals Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD, https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) population frequency databases. It is also not present in Acibadem Labgen in-house database. This variant has not been previously reported in clinical databases and literature. It is classified as likely pathogenic according to the recommendations of ACGS and ClinGen Guidelines (Pm2_Sup, PP3_Mod, PM3_Sup, PP4_Mod).

Here, we describe a patient with vitamin B12 deficiency, severe abdominal pain attacks characterized by feeding difficulties, and hypotonia, born to a consanguineous Azerbaijani family. In this study, we report a novel homozygous variant (c.1591C>T) in ABCD4, expanding the allelic heterogeneity of this disease. It is located in the highly conserved region of the ABC transporter domain and has very high in silico pathogenic prediction scores. Nine pathogenic variants have been reported in patients with MAHCJ so far (Fig. 2a, b). These variants are scattered throughout different exons of ABCD4, but 6 out of 9 variants are located within the ATP-binding domain of ABCD4, indicating its critical role in the proper function of the ABCD protein (Fig. 2b). Also, the c.423C>G (p. Asn141Lys) variant was detected in 3 out of 8 patients, and c.1295G>A (p.Arg432Gln) was detected in 2 out of 8 patients reported so far. This suggests that these variants may represent a founder effect in the regions where the cases were reported. The prominent finding in this patient is methylmalonic acidemia resulting from the cofactor disorder of the methylmalonyl-CoA mutase enzyme. The cobalamin-dependent mitochondrial enzyme catalyzes the isomerization of l-methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA and uses adenosylcobalamin as a cofactor. The enzyme is present in the metabolic pathway of amino acids (valine, isoleucine, methionine, and threonine), cholesterol, and odd-chain fatty acids. Due to its deficiency or inactivation, as is here, MMA accumulates in blood. Clinical features are due to the accumulation of toxic metabolites, altered mitochondrial energy metabolism, carnitine depletion, and CoA sequestration. An MMA test was not conducted during the acute episodes; therefore, we cannot definitively establish a correlation between the abdominal pain complaint and MMA levels in this patient. The precise mechanism underlying MMA-induced abdominal pain remains unclear. MMA may stimulate the gastrointestinal system, irrespective of the concentration of toxic metabolites [14]. According to medical history, the attacks always co-occur with vitamin B12 deficiency and disappear when the level returns to normal with treatment (Fig. 1). The patient’s hypotonia during attacks is compatible with the impact on energy metabolism. The accumulation of toxic metabolites may also explain the patient’s nausea, vomiting, and other gastrointestinal symptoms. As a collective result, the inability to gain weight and failure to thrive are chronic complications and may be related to protein depletion. Other chronic complications have been reported as movement disorders, progressive spastic quadriparesis, epilepsy, cardiac dysfunction, progressive renal disease, pancreatitis, osteopenia, osteoporosis, vision loss, and functional immunodeficiency in this disorder [15]. Although our patient is the eldest patient to be diagnosed with the most extended duration of clinical course, other organ systems are unaffected, and he only has poor growth. Reported patients have varying degrees of Hcy elevation. Hyc is a sulfhydryl-containing amino acid formed during the metabolism of methionine. Elevated Hcy levels are associated with thrombosis via several mechanisms (e.g., increased tissue factor expression, enhanced platelet reactivity, impaired fibrinolytic potential, and vascular injury) [16]. Patients with higher total plasma Hcy concentration have an increased risk of coronary artery disease, stroke, and venous thromboembolism. Among the reported patients, the thrombotic complication case had a higher Hcy level than the others, stemming from the underlying cobalamin defect and the conjunction MTHFR variants [4]. Our patient’s slightly elevated serum MMA level and normal serum Hcy level were attributed to metabolic stability under cobalamin treatment for a long time. We observed that the MMA elevation was more prominent in laboratory and clinical manner in this patient carrying the homozygous variant of c.1591C>T. Still, we cannot interpret whether this is a variant-specific situation since insufficient patients have accumulated.

In inherited cobalamin defects, particularly in cases with CblJ and CblF disorder where intracellular cobalamin cannot be released from the lysosome into the cytoplasm, elevated levels of both MMA and Hcy can be observed with decreased serum cobalamin levels. Indeed, in 6 out of 7 previously reported CblJ cases where serum cobalamin levels were measured, they were found to be low in 2 [4, 5], as observed in CblF disorder. The underlying reason for the laboratory similarities between these two disorders may be the requirement of the ATPase activity of the ABCD4 for a nucleotide-sensitive interaction with the LMBRD1, associated with CblF disorder [6].

The treatment goal in cobalamin defects is to keep the cobalamin level in a normal range and avoid metabolite accumulation. Treatment of MAHCJ disorder therefore usually consists of high-dose vitamin B12 injection, betaine, and folate. This patient shows satisfactory clinical, metabolic, and hematologic response to cobalamin supplementation. Since the patient’s clinical complications were mainly due to metabolite accumulation, the long-term treatment was arranged as a monthly 1,000 μg hydroxocobalamin (Neurogriseovit®) injection, the preferred form of cobalamin to remove free radicals.

In daily practice, cobalamin deficiency is not thought to cause intestinal disorders; instead, it is believed to be caused by intestinal disorders. Hence, MAHCJ is not among the causes of abdominal pain in children. Based on this case, we recommend remembering this rare disease in unexplained severe abdominal attacks accompanied by cobalamin deficiency. With early treatment, it may be possible to avoid organic acidemia caused by MAHCJ and the acute and chronic complications that develop accordingly.

In conclusion, the case underscores the necessity of considering cobalamin defects in the differential diagnosis of recurrent unexplained abdominal pain, especially in the presence of hematological abnormalities. This report broadens the clinical and molecular understanding of MAHCJ. Early recognition and sustained vitamin B12 therapy are crucial for managing this condition. Further research is warranted to explore the full spectrum of clinical presentations and genetic variants associated with MAHCJ.
