OBJECTIVE: Catheter-directed intervention (CDI) use in massive pulmonary 
embolism (PE) is rarely studied due to guideline recommendations for systemic 
thrombolysis (stPA). Nevertheless, surgical embolectomy (SE) and CDI remain 
well-accepted alternatives in massive PE management, particularly when patients 
have contraindications to or do not improve after stPA. We hypothesized that CDI 
and SE have comparable outcomes in the treatment of massive PE.
METHODS: We conducted a retrospective review of patients presenting with massive 
PE who underwent CDI or SE at a multihospital health care system (2010-2024). 
Baseline characteristics, in-hospital outcomes, and long-term mortality were 
recorded. Data was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and multivariate 
Cox regression.
RESULTS: A total of 99 patients with massive PE were analyzed, with 24 (24.2%) 
undergoing SE and 75 (75.8%) receiving CDI (41 suction thrombectomies and 34 
catheter-directed thrombolysis). SE and CDI baseline characteristics were 
similar with mean age of 58.5 years in SE and 64.5 years in CDI (P = .09). The 
majority in both groups had absolute (CDI, 17.3%; SE, 16.7%; P = .94) or 
relative contraindication (CDI, 58.7%; SE, 66.7%; P = .49) to stPA. The use of 
preoperative stPA was similar in both groups (CDI, 13.3%; SE, 25.0%; P = .21). 
Median time to procedure was also similar (CDI, 14.3 hours; SE, 18.5 hours; P = 
.42). CDI was associated with a lower total intensive care unit (ICU) length of 
stay (LOS) (median, 2.2 vs 3.3 days; P = .04) and lower major bleeding 
complications (9.3% vs 79.2%; P < .001). However, there was no statistically 
significant difference in fatal bleeding (CDI, 5.33%; SE, 4.17%; P = 1.00), need 
for bailout intervention (CDI, 8.0%; SE, 16.7%; P = .25), resolution of right 
heart strain (CDI, 27.8%; SE, 41.2%; P = .37), or median hospital LOS (CDI, 8 
days; SE, 5 days; P = .12) between both groups. In-hospital mortality occurred 
equally (CDI, 21.3%; SE, 20.8%; P = 1.00). On Kaplan-Meier analysis, there was 
no survival difference between the two groups. On Cox regression, procedure type 
was not a significant predictor for mortality (adjusted hazard ratio 1.36; 95% 
confidence interval, 0.58-3.20; reference: SE).
CONCLUSIONS: CDI is a minimally invasive alternative to SE in massive PE and 
offers comparable outcomes and similar survival rates. Nevertheless, CDI offers 
advantages in terms of shorter ICU stay and fewer major bleeding complications.

Type of Research: Retrospective review of massive pulmonary embolism (PE) patients presenting to a multihospital health care system

Key Findings: Among 99 patients with massive PE (75 catheter-directed interventions [CDIs], 24 surgical embolectomies), in-hospital mortality, long-term mortality (P = .48), and clinical improvements were similar in both groups. CDI was associated with shorter intensive care unit stays (2.2 vs 3.3 days; P = .04) and fewer major bleeding complications (9.3% vs 79.2%; P < .001).

Take Home Message: For massive PE, CDI is a less invasive option than surgical embolectomy, offering similar survival and comparable outcomes, with shorter intensive care unit stays and fewer major bleeding events.

Pulmonary embolism (PE) affects nearly 900,000 people1 and causes 50,000 deaths each year in the United States.2 PEs are stratified as low-risk, submassive, and massive based on hemodynamic status, with massive PE resulting in clinically significant hemodynamic compromise.3 Massive PE accounts for the lowest proportion of PE hospitalization, ranging from 3.8% to 5.3%,4, 5, 6 but it is associated with the highest mortality rate, with in-hospital and 30-day mortality averaging 28.3% and 30.2%, respectively.7

Guidelines recommend systemic thrombolysis in addition to anticoagulation for patients with massive PE and acceptable risk of bleeding complications.3,8,9 Systemic thrombolysis has been associated with lower mortality10,11 and greater echocardiographic improvement when compared with anticoagulation alone.12 Unfortunately, systemic thrombolytic therapy has been associated with significant bleeding, including a 6% risk of major bleeding and up to 3% of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH),10,11 and treatment failure has been reported in 8.2% to 19.4% of cases.13,14

Historically, surgical embolectomy (SE) was the only option when systemic thrombolysis was ineffective or contraindicated. However, with the advent of catheter-directed interventions (CDIs), including suction thrombectomy and catheter-directed thrombolysis, now there are minimally invasive options for this select group of patients.8,15 However, comparative data is severely lacking to determine the optimal approach of interventional strategy.

Thus, we performed a retrospective chart review to compare outcomes of CDI with those of SE, hypothesizing that mortality would be similar and morbidity would be lower.

The study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY22110154) and is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

This is a retrospective study of a multihospital health care system between January 2010 and May 2024. We included adults 18+ years of age with diagnosis of PE via International Classification of Disease-9 or 10 code, confirmed by chart review. We excluded those who did not undergo SE or CDI based on Current Procedural Terminology codes (36,013, 36,014, 36,015, 37,212, 37,201, 37,213, 37,214, 37,187, 37,188, 33,916). Finally, we excluded those without massive PE (defined as systolic blood pressure [SBP] ≤90 mmHg, requirement for vasopressor therapy prior to intervention, or presentation with cardiac arrest3). Patient demographics, baseline characteristics, intervention details, in-hospital outcomes, and mortality were collected using a combination of electronic health record datasets and individual chart reviews.

Absolute systemic alteplase (stPA) contraindication was defined as a history of intracranial bleeding, active bleeding, bleeding diathesis, or significant closed head or facial trauma within 3 months.

Relative stPA contraindication was defined as SBP >180 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure >110 mmHg on admission, history of ischemic stroke more than 3 months prior, traumatic or prolonged cardiopulmonary resuscitation, major surgery within 3 weeks, recent (within 2 to 4 weeks) internal bleeding, pericarditis or pericardial effusion, current anticoagulant use with International Normalized Ratio >1.7 or prothrombin time >15, or age >75 years.

Novel oral anticoagulants were not viewed as a relative contraindication to anticoagulation. Institutional protocols for heparin in the setting of novel oral anticoagulant use were followed in these cases.

Bailout therapy was defined as the need for a second intervention therapy after clinical deterioration, including catheter-directed thrombolysis, suction thrombectomy, SE, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).

Major bleed was defined according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis as requiring more than 2 units of blood transfusion, ICH, or fatal bleeding.16

Right heart strain was defined as the presence of one or more of the following echocardiographic findings: right ventricular (RV) dilatation, interventricular septal flattening, paradoxical septal motion, right atrial enlargement, RV hypertrophy, RV systolic dysfunction with apical sparing (McConnell’s sign), RV hypokinesia, elevated RV pressures, dilated inferior vena cava, or tricuspid regurgitation that is new or worsened from baseline. The presence of right heart strain was also assessed on computed tomography angiography by identifying one or more of the following: abnormal position of the interventricular septum, RV enlargement, pulmonary trunk enlargement, or features of right heart failure, including inferior vena cava contrast reflux, a dilated azygos venous system, or dilated hepatic veins with or without contrast reflux.

Patients were managed by our Pulmonary Embolism Response Team (PERT) based on a previously published algorithm,9 although ultimate treatment decisions were at the discretion of the PERT team. The algorithm suggests treating patients with high bleeding risk with SE or suction thrombectomy, whereas those with moderate bleeding risk with catheter-directed thrombolysis. Our treatment protocol for catheter-directed thrombolysis has been previously published.17

Pulmonary embolectomies at our institution were performed by different cardiothoracic surgeons, and the operative technique, including the decision to use cardiopulmonary bypass, was left to each surgeon’s judgment.

An intention-to-treat analysis was performed (ie, patients were classified according to their first intervention). The χ2 test, Exact test, Student’s t-test, and Kruskal-Wallis test were used to compare CDI and SE groups where appropriate. Mortality was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and multivariate Cox regression. At all time points, the standard error did not exceed 10%. Variables were included in the model if they were found to have P < .10 on univariate analysis. Paired t-test was used to compare vital signs pre- and post-intervention. Subgroup analyses were conducted to compare catheter-directed thrombolysis and suction thrombectomy with SE, as well as with each other.

During the study period, a total of 99 patients presented with acute massive PE with 24 (24.2%) undergoing SE and 75 (75.8%) receiving CDI (41 suction thrombectomy and 34 catheter-directed lysis) (Fig 1). One-half of the catheter-directed thrombolysis cases (17/34) were treated with the EkoSonic Endovascular System. There was no statistically significant difference in bailout therapy between the two groups. Bailout was required in six of 75 patients (8.0%) in the CDI group and our of 24 (16.7%) in the SE group (P = .25). Among SE patients requiring bailout, all four received ECMO. In the suction thrombectomy subgroup, one patient underwent SE and second required catheter-directed thrombolysis as bailout therapy. In the catheter-directed thrombolysis subgroup, one patient required SE, two underwent suction thrombectomy, and one received ECMO as bailout therapy.

Major bleeding was less frequent in the CDI group, occurring in 7 of 75 patients (9.3%), compared with 19 of 24 patients (79.2%) in the SE group (P < .001). In the SE group, there were 18 cases of major transfusion and one fatal mediastinal bleed. In the suction thrombectomy subgroup, major bleeding included one major transfusion and two fatal bleeds (one due to ICH and another caused by groin access bleeding). In the catheter-directed thrombolysis subgroup, major bleeding events included two major transfusions and two fatal bleeds (both due to ICH). The fatal bleeding rate was similar between the 2 groups (CDI, 5.33%; SE, 4.17%; P = 1.00).

One suction thrombectomy patient developed heart block as the wires crossed the right ventricle. The patient recovered and did not require a pacemaker.

The ICU LOS was significantly shorter in the CDI group, with a median of 2.2 days (interquartile range [IQR], 3.4 days) compared with 3.3 days (IQR, 5.8 days) in the SE group (P = .04).

The hospital LOS was also shorter in the CDI group, with a median of 5 days (IQR, 6 days) compared with 8 days (IQR, 13 days) in the SE group, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = .12).

In patients presenting with massive PE, systemic thrombolysis remains the current standard of care.3,8,9 However, it is not always effective and is contraindicated in patients with an increased risk of bleeding. In these patients, guidelines recommend either SE or CDI without favoring one over the other, as limited direct comparison studies have been conducted. Our study of 99 patients with massive PE suggests that CDI is comparable to SE in terms of mortality and hemodynamics recovery while offering the benefits of lower major bleeding complications and a shorter ICU LOS. These results were consistent across the different CDI modalities including suction thrombectomy and catheter-directed thrombolysis.

Our study found no significant difference in in-hospital, 30-day, or long-term mortality between SE and CDI. This is the first study to compare these two interventions exclusively in a cohort of patients with massive PE.18, 19, 20 Nevertheless, our findings align with a meta-analysis by Ishisaka et al, which aggregated in-hospital mortality from various SE and CDI studies and found no significant difference between the two interventions on a subgroup analysis of patients with massive PE.18 A study by Winters et al including patients with massive and submassive PE demonstrated consistent results.19 Additionally, another meta-analysis of a similar sample of patients with PE reported a higher in-hospital mortality in the SE group, but preintervention cardiac arrest accounted for all the mortality effect.20

Conversely, a study by Cires-Drouet et al included patients with PE of all severity levels and found that SE was associated with improved all-cause in-hospital and long-term mortality.21 They reported a 0% mortality rate in the SE group and 13.3% in the CDI group, compared with our findings of 20.8% and 21.3% in SE and CDI, respectively. The difference in CDI in-hospital mortality rates may be explained by their inclusion of patients with low-risk and submassive PE, whereas our study focused exclusively on cases with massive PE. As for the absence of mortality in their SE group, this remains difficult to explain. It may be due to differences in patient characteristics, as our SE group appears to have a higher burden of comorbidities. However, our mortality rate aligns with previous studies that quote mortality rates between 10% and 26%,22, 23, 24 such as Keeling et al, which reported an in-hospital mortality rate of 23.7% in patients with massive PE treated with SE.25

Both CDI and SE achieved comparable hemodynamic improvements after intervention, with both showing significant improvement when comparing the worst preintervention vitals to vitals at day one. Additionally, both demonstrated similar right heart strain resolution on postintervention on follow-up imaging. This finding is in agreement with the results reported by Cires-Drouet et al et al on a longer-term follow-up period. They also found that 9.9% of their CDI patients needed bailout therapy,21 closely reflecting the 8% observed in our study. The latter rate was not significantly different from the SE group.

We found that SE was associated with a significantly higher risk of major bleeding events, both statistically and clinically, compared with CDI (79.2% vs 9.3%). In contrast, Cires-Drouet et al reported a higher major bleeding rate in the CDI group (20%) compared with the SE group (6.2%), although this difference was not statistically significant.21 This discrepancy may be attributed to patient selection factors. Similarly, Ishisaka et al found that patients undergoing CDI for massive PE had 2.71 higher odds of major bleeding compared with patients undergoing SE.18 However, this meta-analysis pooled data from multiple studies, each using different definitions of major bleeding. Our high major bleeding rate (79.2%) in the SE group was entirely driven by the need for blood transfusion (18/19). This is consistent with Keeling at al, who reported that 76.3% of patients with massive PE needed postoperative transfusion.25

Our study showed that CDI patients have statistically significant lower ICU LOS (2.2 vs 3.3 days) when compared with SE patients. Kolkailah et al similarly found that CDI was significantly associated with lower ICU LOS (0.83 days vs 2.91 days)26 in patients with submassive PE, which could explain their slightly lower LOS compared with ours. Although this one-day difference might seem clinically insignificant, one study found that an ICU LOS exceeding 48 hours after cardiac surgery was associated with a higher incidence of adverse events.27 Our CDI patients also had a lower hospital overall LOS than SE patients (5 vs 8 days), although this difference was not statistically significant. Winters et al observed a statistically significant similar trend (5.5 days in CDI vs 10.5 days in SE).19

A recently published nationwide study found a steady decrease in SE use over the years alongside a rise in CDI.28 This aligns with our study, where CDI has been the leading intervention in recent years, reaching 100% of cases in 2023, whereas SE use has declined. This shift may be explained by the implementation of a multidisciplinary PE response team, as well as the favorable safety profile and comparable outcomes of CDI.

This study had several limitations, particularly due to its retrospective design. Because patients were identified using Current Procedural Terminology codes, there is a possibility of missing cases due to coding errors. Although we have a well-established PERT team with a suggested algorithm, the algorithm does not suggest when to choose SE vs suction thrombectomy. Furthermore, the decision-making process is highly individualized and dependent on both patient and provider factors. Although the two groups had few statistically significant differences in comorbidities and presentations, unmeasured patient factors and illness severity may have influenced the intervention choice and subsequently affected the outcomes. The study also lacked a medical treatment arm for comparison. Furthermore, except for all-cause mortality, the observed outcomes were limited to the short term. Loss of follow-up prevented the assessment of long-term morbidity and patient-centered outcomes.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare CDI and SE in a cohort exclusively composed of patients with massive PE. Our findings suggest that CDI is a minimally invasive alternative to SE that provides comparable outcomes and similar survival rates. Additionally, CDI offers advantages in terms of shorter ICU stay and a lower incidence of major bleeding complications. Our results support the rise in CDI compared with SE seen in both our institution and nationwide data.
