# Ocular phenotype and therapeutic interventions in keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness (KID) syndrome.
**Authors:** Mc Lean K, Bignotti S, Callea M, Cammarata-Scalisi F, Steger B, Armstrong D, Lagan M, Sinton J, Semeraro F, Kaye SB, Romano V, Willoughby CE
**Journal:** Ophthalmic Genet (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1080/13816810.2023.2258218](https://doi.org/10.1080/13816810.2023.2258218)

## Content

BACKGROUND: To report ocular manifestations, clinical course, and therapeutic management of patients with molecular genetically confirmed keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness syndrome.

METHODS: Four patients, aged 19 to 46, with keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness syndrome from across the UK were recruited for a general and ocular examination and GJB2 (Cx26) mutational analysis. The ocular examination included best-corrected visual acuity, slit-lamp bio-microscopy, and ocular surface assessment. Mutational analysis of the coding region of GJB2 (Cx26) was performed by bidirectional Sanger sequencing.

RESULTS: All four individuals had the characteristic systemic features of keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness syndrome. Each patient was found to have a missense mutation, resulting in the substitution of aspartic acid with asparagine at codon 50 (p.D50N). Main ophthalmic features were vascularizing keratopathy, ocular surface disease, hyperkeratotic lid lesions, recurrent epithelial defects, and corneal stromal scarring. One patient had multiple surgical procedures, including superficial keratectomies and lamellar keratoplasty, which failed to prevent severe visual loss. In contrast, oral therapy with ketoconazole stabilized the corneal and skin disease in two other patients with keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness syndrome. The patient who underwent intracorneal bevacizumab injection showed a marked reduction in corneal vascularization following a single application.

CONCLUSIONS: Keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness syndrome is a rare ectodermal dysplasia caused by heterozygous mutations in GJB2 (Cx26) with a severe, progressive vascularizing keratopathy. Oral ketoconazole therapy may offer benefit in stabilizing the corneal and skin disease.

2 Introduction
3
4 Keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness syndrome (KID, MIM #148210) is a rare dominant 
5 ectodermal dysplasia syndrome characterized by vascularizing keratitis, 
6 sensorineural hearing loss and erythrokeratoderma (1,2). The most common cause 
7 of KID syndrome is dominant, heterozygous missense mutations in the connexin-26 
8 (Cx26) gene, GJB2 (3) Inheritance is usually sporadic, however autosomal dominant 
9 and recessive transmission have been reported (4,5,6). 
10 Connexins are integral membrane proteins which forms gap junctions. They work as 
11 ion and metabolites exchangers (6), but evidence suggest they play a role in other 
12 biological functions like cell growth and intercellular communications (7). Connexin 
13 structures consists of four transmembrane domains, one cytoplasmic domain and 
14 two extracellular loops. In KID syndrome all mutations have been identified in regions 
15 of the gene coding for the first extracellular domain and the N-terminal of connexin 
16 26 (4). As seen in Figure 1, the most common GJB2 mutation associated with KID 
17 syndrome is c.148G>A (p.Asp50Asn) (5). It has been suggested that Asp50Asn 
18 could alter gap junction channel gating and hemi-channel interactions (4,5). It has 
19 also been associated with anomalous hemi-channel behavior causing abnormally 
20 elevated membrane currents, enhanced ATP release in response to precise stimuli 
21 and increased permeability to small-molecule tracers (5). Other GJB2 mutations 
22 have been described in KID patients, such as p.Gly11Arg, p.Gly12Arg, p.Asn14Tyr, 
23 p.Ser17Phe, p.Ala40Val, p.Gly45Glu, p.Asp50Tyr and p.Gly54Glu (4). A shift in the
24 pattern of connexin expression in the skin has been associated with changes in the 
25 differentiation pathway of keratinocytes (4)
26 KID syndrome is not a true ichthyosis (8) as the skin lesions are more correctly 
27 classified as erythrokeratoderma forming symmetrical, well-circumscribed, 
28 erythematous, and hyperkeratotic plaques on the face and extremities [9]. Other 
29 features include scarring alopecia, dystrophic nails, dental abnormalities, and heat 
30 intolerance (3,10). Furthermore, there is an increased susceptibility to

3 The pathogenesis of the ocular phenotype of KID syndrome is poorly understood. 
4 The main ocular manifestations include corneal surface instability and dry eye (11), 
5 limbal stem cell deficiency (12) with resulting corneal  abnormalities and 
6 vascularizing keratitis. (913,134). Managing these ocular features of KID syndrome 
7 is challenging. Publications regarding surgical intervention are limited and the 
8 outcome is generally poor (134). Hazen et al. published a case report of a 43-year-
9 old female patient with KID syndrome who was treated with oral ketoconazole, 200 
10 mg twice a day, for a culture positive Candida albicans skin infection. The treatment 
11 improved the ichtyosiform dermatosis and un-expectantly her rapidly progressing 
12 vascularizing keratopathy stabilized such that she was able to avoid additional 
13 corneal transplantation for four years (145).
14
15 In this report we describe the ocular manifestations and management of four patients 
16 with a molecular diagnosis of KID syndrome.  Two patients were beneficially treated 
17 with oral ketoconazole.

16 Case reports
17 All four patients with KID syndrome were Caucasian of Northern European decent. 
18 Table 1 summaries their systemic and ocular manifestations alongside their 
19 treatment and outcome. Genetic sequencing demonstrated they each had a 
20 heterozygous transition 148G>A at codon 50 of GJB2 (connexin-26) encoding 
21 asparagine instead of aspartic acid (D50N). This sequence variation was not seen 
22 in unaffected relatives and 112 control chromosomes suggesting the 148G>A 
23 change was not a polymorphic variant. No other sequence variants were identified 
24 in the patients' coding sequences of GJB2. This non-conservative mutation (D50N) 
25 in connexin 26 (Cx26) replaces an uncharged polar amino acid by a charged polar 
26 one at position 50 in the first extracellular loop.

15 This female, with a known diagnosis of KID syndrome, presented aged 22 years old 
16 with a two-year history of reduced vision and photophobia. She was diagnosed with 
17 sensorineural hearing loss in early childhood and was attending dermatology with 
18 palmoplantar keratoderma and hyperkeratotic plaques on face or extremities. There 
19 was alopecia and hyperkeratotic nails. Patient communicated by sign language and 
20 was referred to ophthalmology aged 16. Peripheral corneal neovascularization and 
21 ocular surface disease was detected and KID syndrome suspected. The diagnosis 
22 was confirmed genetically and a D50N mutation in connexin-26 identified. She was 
23 initially managed with topical lubricants but developed a paracentral epithelial defect 
24 aged 18. Aged 20, she was commenced on long-term oral ketoconazole 100 mg 
25 once a day. Her corneal disease stabilized and  BCVA remain RE 6/12 and LE 6/9.

10 One patient (case 1) had multiple surgical procedures in the left eye, including two 
11 lamellar grafts and two PK, which failed to prevent severe visual loss. Case reports 
12 regarding surgical intervention in KID syndrome are of limited availability. A case 
13 series published by Messmer et al. detailed another patient who had multiple surgical 
14 procedures including superficial keratectomies, limbal allograft transplantation with 
15 systemic immunosuppression, amniotic membrane transplantation, later 
16 tarsorrhaphies, and lamellar keratoplasty (189). Like our patient, these procedures 
17 were unable to preserve useful vision.
18
19 In contrast, oral therapy with ketoconazole stabilized the corneal and skin disease in 
20 two other patients with KID syndrome (case 3 and 4). This is consistent with another 
21 case report demonstrating similar improvements with oral ketoconazole in a patient 
22 with KID syndrome (145). Ketoconazole is an imidazole antifungal agent effective in 
23 the management of recalcitrant dermatophyte and/or Candida albicans infections of 
24 the skin. It acts primarily by interfering with ergosterol synthesis in the cell membrane 
25 of susceptible fungi (189). It is thought that improvements in the skin disease are a 
26 result of controlling the cutaneous mycosis infections that KID syndrome patients are 
27 susceptible to. The mechanisms by which oral ketoconazole improves corneal 
28 disease are unclear; ketoconazole is known to have effects other than  its antifungal 
29 properties. In high doses, it interferes with testosterone and cortisol synthesis, thus 
30 justifying its use in the management of Cushing’s syndrome and metastatic prostate
