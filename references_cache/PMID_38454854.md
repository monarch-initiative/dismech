Acute Zonal Occult Outer Retinopathy (AZOOR) is characterized by sudden visual
impairment, often presenting with scotoma and photopsia in young to middle-aged
adults, showing a female predominance. This condition, distinct from genetic
disorders like retinitis pigmentosa, lacks a hereditary basis and exhibits
unique fundus changes and imaging features indicative of outer retinal
dysfunction. Recent advancements have broadened our understanding, identifying
variants like Acute Annular Outer Retinopathy (AAOR) and Multizonal outer
retinopathy and retinal pigment epitheliopathy (MORR), each with specific
clinical presentations and imaging characteristics. The diagnosis of AZOOR and
its variants primarily relies on excluding other conditions through
comprehensive evaluation, including imaging and serological testing. Treatment
approaches, including the use of corticosteroids and immunosuppressives, remain
debated, with some evidence suggesting benefits in the early stages. The
prognosis of AZOOR varies, with most patients experiencing stabilization,
although complications like choroidal neovascularization may occur, requiring
targeted therapy. This manuscript elucidates the complexity of AZOOR,
emphasizing the necessity of high clinical suspicion and the role of advanced
imaging in diagnosis and management.

Acute zonal occult outer retinopathy (AZOOR) is a rare inflammatory disease characterized by outer retinal dysfunction. The disease was first described by Gass in 1993 in 13 young females.[1] Subsequently, Gass extended the spectrum of AZOOR and postulated the AZOOR complex, which included multiple evanescent white dot syndrome, punctate inner choroidopathy, multifocal choroiditis, acute macular neuroretinopathy, and acute idiopathic blind spot enlargement.[2] The onset of AZOOR is acute and begins with the scotoma contiguous to the optic disk and photopsia. The disease is predominantly seen in young to middle-aged adults, with a female predominance. Usually, there is minimal or no anterior segment inflammation, and vitreous cells are often seen in the late stage of the disease. Fundus findings are variable and can range from normal in the beginning, followed by a grayish-white line at the border of normal and an involved retina in the peripapillary area, to pigmentary disturbances simulating retinitis pigmentosa. Unlike AZOOR, which is characterized by a sudden onset of symptoms, retinitis pigmentosa involves nyctalopia and a gradual loss of peripheral vision, often with a positive family history. Additionally, genetic testing can be useful in identifying retinitis pigmentosa and other hereditary retinochoroidal dystrophies, whereas AZOOR is not a genetic condition. With increased awareness among ophthalmologists and improved ophthalmic imaging in recent years, AZOOR has been increasingly recognized, and several other variants of the condition have been described in the literature. Acute annular outer retinopathy (AAOR) and multizonal outer retinopathy and retinal pigment epitheliopathy (MORR) are similar to classically described AZOOR, but have distinct differentiating features. AAOR is usually unilateral and presents with an annular grayish peripapillary demarcation line on clinical examination. Similar to AZOOR, patients with AAOR present with acute-onset visual field loss and photopsia. This entity is characterized by outer retinal affliction, as demonstrated by photoreceptor loss and outer nuclear layer thinning on spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT). MORR has an acute onset and a progressive course. This entity involves multiple zones of the retina, including the macula, peripapillary, and far peripheral retina with annular patterns. Unlike AZOOR, the lesions are not occult and manifest as fundus changes corresponding to areas of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and photoreceptor disturbance. Imaging suggests that predominant involvement is in the RPE layer along with outer retinal involvement.

The diagnosis of AZOOR rests on the exclusion of other closely associated conditions, often necessitating determining coexisting clinical conditions in the AZOOR complex spectrum. Autoimmune retinopathies (AIRs) are a very close differential of AZOOR. AIRs can be neoplastic or nonneoplastic. AIR usually presents with vision loss and scotomas, accompanied by minimal fundus abnormalities. Some patients with AIR may show retinal pigment abnormalities, vascular attenuation, and disk pallor. Usually, they have minimal or no intraocular inflammation. They are characterized by a wider area of outer retinal loss at presentation as compared to AZOOR. Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) features that are characteristically seen in AZOOR are very rare in AIR. Before coming to a diagnosis of paraneoplastic AIR, extensive investigations should be undertaken to rule out any malignancy. The diagnosis of AIR is confirmed by the presence of circulating antiretinal antibodies. However, it is important to remember that anti-retinal antibodies can be found in other autoimmune diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease and systemic lupus erythematosus and in normal controls. Interestingly, ocular conditions such as age-related macular degeneration and retinitis pigmentosa can also have circulating anti-retinal antibodies, which makes the clinical diagnosis confounding. Syphilitic outer retinopathy (SOR) is a distinct form of syphilitic posterior uveitis, and it is closer to AZOOR in presentation. SOR presents with vision loss, scotoma, and photopsia. Anterior segment findings are usually unremarkable. Some patients may present with the demarcation line as seen with AZOOR, but the fundus is often normal in appearance or may show subtle retinal pigment epithelial changes. Disruption of the ellipsoid zone on OCT and increased FAF are observed in SOR. Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) in SOR may show phlebitis in the area of active outer retinitis, and this may help differentiate it from AZOOR. Thus, patients suspected of having AZOOR should be tested for syphilis.

The role of anti-inflammatory agents in the management of AZOOR remains controversial. While some reports have shown a beneficial role for corticosteroids and immunosuppressives, many authors, including Gass himself, believed that corticosteroids have no added benefit in the long-term management of the disease.[256] Improvement of central visual function has been reported in patients with AZOOR following pulse corticosteroid therapy, and there are reports on the use of intravitreal corticosteroid in the management of AZOOR. The precise role of these agents in managing AZOOR is not yet well established. In light of the limited literature, it can be suggested that oral corticosteroids may have a limited role in halting the progression of the early stages of the disease, if any role at all. There are reports of recovery of the ellipsoid zone at the foveal area following corticosteroid therapy in the early stages of the disease.[7] It is probable that recovery from outer retinal damage in the advanced stages of the disease is irreversible, and these anti-inflammatory therapies have no or limited role when instituted later in the disease.[6] Though a possible viral etiology was suspected, antiviral treatment did not show any benefit in AZOOR.

The natural course of AZOOR is variable, and the data available in the literature are contradictory. Bilateral involvement is often asymmetrical, and recurrences of inflammation can occur. According to Gass, the majority of cases completely or partially stabilize within 6 months, while a few patients (4%) experience gradual deterioration with the progression of the disease.[2] Good visual outcomes were noted spontaneously or after systemic corticosteroid therapy in 52 eyes of 38 Japanese patients.[8] However, the exact number of patients showing gradual deterioration in terms of both visual acuity and the visual field is likely underestimated due to the lack of longer follow-up.[9] There are a few reports of choroidal neovascular membrane (CNVM) complicating the course of AZOOR.[10111213] The exact cause of CNVM in AZOOR, which remains a sequelae of inner choroidal inflammation, remains unknown. The majority of the patients reported in the literature received treatment with intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors. Introini et al.[11] reported that although intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment effectively stabilized the CNVM, they observed a concomitant progression of the zonal lesions of AZOOR.[11]

To conclude, AZOOR remains a rare clinical entity, mysterious in terms of its etiopathogenesis, and shadowed by a spectrum of several closely mimicking clinical entities. Clinicians can miss the diagnosis of the disease if they do not have a high index of suspicion and apply proper use of multimodal imaging.
