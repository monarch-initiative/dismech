ABSTRACT
Purpose: Females with biallelic CHEK2 germline pathogenic variants (gPVs) more often
develop multiple breast cancers than individuals with monoallelic CHEK2 gPVs. This study is
aimed at expanding the knowledge on the occurrence of other malignancies.
Methods: Exome sequencing of individuals who developed multiple primary malignancies
identiﬁed 3 individuals with the CHEK2 (NM_007194.4) c.1100del p.(Thr367MetfsTer15) loss-
of-function gPV in a biallelic state. We collected the phenotypes of an additional cohort of
individuals with CHEK2 biallelic gPVs ( n = 291).
Results: In total, 157 individuals (53.4%; 157/294 individuals) developed ≥1 (pre)malignancy.
The most common (pre)malignancies next to breast cancer were colorectal- (n = 19), thyroid- (n =
19), and prostate (pre)malignancies (n = 12). Females with biallelicCHEK2 loss-of-function gPVs
more frequently developed ≥2 (pre)malignancies and at an earlier age compared with females
biallelic for the CHEK2 c.470T>C p.(Ile157Thr) missense variant. Furthermore, 26 males
(31%; 26/84 males) with CHEK2 biallelic gPVs developed ≥1 (pre)malignancies of 15 origins.
Conclusion: Our study suggests that CHEK2 biallelic gPVs likely increase the susceptibility to
develop multiple malignancies in various tissues, both in females and males. However, it is
possible that a substantial proportion of individuals with CHEK2 biallelic gPVs is missed as
diagnostic testing for CHEK2 often is limited to individuals who developed breast cancer.

Introduction
Females with a monoallelic germline (likely) pathogenic
variant (gPV) in CHEK2 (OMIM; 604373) are at low-to-
moderate increased risk to develop breast cancer, depending
on their CHEK2 germline variant.
1,2 CHEK2 is a cell-cycle
checkpoint regulator and is involved in the DNA damage
response pathway.
3,4 Several founder variants have been
described in CHEK2, as well as a variety of both (predicted)
loss-of-function (LoF) and pathogenic missense variants. The
risk to develop cancer is highly dependent on the gPV in
CHEK2, ranging from variants with low risk, which do not
seem to warrant close clinical management, to variants for
which the risk is substantially higher and surveillance advices
are in place.
5,6 For example, compared with the general
population, the odds ratio (OR) for breast cancer risk in fe-
males heterozygous for the CHEK2 (NM_007194.4)
c.470T>C p.(Ile157Thr) missense variant is estimated to be
1.28 to 1.58, and for females heterozygous for the CHEK2
c.1100del p.(Thr367MetfsTer15) LoF variant, the OR is
estimated to be 2.66.
5,7-10 Furthermore, it was shown that
females, who are heterozygous for the CHEK2 c.1100del
variant, are younger when they develop breast cancer, and
they are more frequently diagnosed with multiple breast
cancers compared with individuals without CHEK2 hetero-
zygous gPVs.
11,12 Depending on the population and variant,
frequencies of individuals with heterozygous CHEK2 LoF
gPVs may be as high as 1.5% in the general population, which
is the case for the CHEK2 c.1100del gPV in individuals from
North-West Europe.
13 Therefore, it is not unexpected that
CHEK2 gPVs are occasionally observed in a biallelic or
compound heterozygous state. Several case series of in-
dividuals, mostly females, with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2
report on a higher risk for developing multiple (bilateral)
breast cancers and at a younger age, compared with in-
dividuals with monoallelic gPVs in CHEK2.
1,13,14 In addi-
tion, females with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 are also reported
to present with additional cancers, such as ovarian-, colo-
rectal-, and thyroid cancer.
1,14,15 As such, CHEK2 is sug-
gested to be a multi-cancer predisposing gene. 16 Although
most data on individuals with monoallelic and biallelic gPVs
in CHEK2 are available for females, several reports on breast
cancer in males heterozygous for gPVs in CHEK2 are pub-
lished.
17,18 The risk for breast cancer is approximately 10-fold
higher in males with the CHEK2 c.1100del gPV compared
with the general population, and various CHEK2 gPVs are
also suggested to be associated with a low-to-moderate
increased risk for prostate cancer.
19-21 However, data
regarding males with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 are scarce.
Via exome sequencing of 70 individuals who developed
multiple primary malignant and benign tumors, we identi-
ﬁed 2 males and 1 female with the CHEK2 c.1100del gPV
in a biallelic state (4%). This ﬁnding raises the question
what the phenotypic spectrum of malignancies in both fe-
males and males with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 is. Here, by
collecting the largest cohort of individuals with biallelic
gPVs in CHEK2 described to date, we investigate the cancer
phenotypes in both females and males with biallelic gPVs in
CHEK2.
Materials and Methods
Study cohorts
We collected genomic DNA (gDNA) from a cohort of in-
dividuals who developed ≥2 primary malignancies <65
years of age ( n = 58) or 1 malignant and 1 benign tumor
<65 years of age ( n = 12) who were counseled by a clinical
geneticist at Radboud university medical center (Radbou-
dumc, Nijmegen, The Netherlands; Supplemental Figure 1 ).
For validation on the occurrence of CHEK2 c.1100del
gPV in individuals with cancer, we collected a total of 781
individuals (n = 667 females) who developed 1 malignancy
≤35 years of age ( n = 377) or ≥2 malignancies ≤60 years of
age ( n = 404), counseled by a clinical geneticist at Rad-
boudumc, Nijmegen, The Netherlands ( Supplemental
Figure 1 ). No (clear) causative gPVs in any cancer predis-
posing gene at the time of diagnosis were identi ﬁed.
Furthermore, we collected the data of 271 individuals
diagnosed with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 via diagnostic
centers involved in the European Reference Network on
Genetic Tumour Risk Syndromes (ERN GENTURIS,
Supplemental Figure 1 ).
22 For each individual with biallelic
gPVs in CHEK2 we collected information on the type of
gPV, family history, selection for genetic testing, histo-
pathological information about presence of (pre)malignant-
and— when available— benign tumors, and age at diagnosis.
We included ductal carcinoma in situ, carcinoma in situ
adenoma, dysplastic nevi, essential thrombocythemia, and
Bowen’s disease in the (pre)malignant group. From litera-
ture, we were able to collect 17 individuals diagnosed with
biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 for which their full phenotypic
cancer spectrum was described ( Supplemental
Figure 1 ).
1,15,23-26 Only individuals with (likely) gPVs ac-
cording to ACMG criteria, or for which additional func-
tional evidence for pathogenicity were available, were
included in the study.
27
To compare the phenotypic spectrum of individuals who
have the CHEK2 c.1100del gPV in a monoallelic or biallelic
state, we collected the malignant and benign tumor pheno-
types from 179 individuals diagnosed via routine diagnostic
testing with monoallelic LoF gPVs in CHEK2 identiﬁed at
Radboudumc, Nijmegen, The Netherlands. 28 This study was
approved by the local medical ethics committee of the
Radboud university medical center (CMO; study numbers
2019-5082, 2019-5738, and 2020-7036).

Statistical analysis
To determine the differences in frequency of individuals
with certain phenotypes, a Fisher ’s exact test was used. P
values, odds ratio (OR), and 95% con ﬁdence intervals were
calculated using R (version 4.1.3). P value correction for
multiple testing (Bonferroni correction) was applied and
results were labeled as statistically signi ﬁcant when P <.05.
For comparisons between 2 groups, unpaired t tests were
performed, and when more than 2 groups were compared,
one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey ’s post-hoc
test and non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test followed by
Dunn’s correction for multiple comparisons were applied
using GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1).
Results
Exome sequencing identiﬁes biallelic gPVs in CHEK2
in individuals who developed multiple primary
malignancies
By exome sequencing analyses of gDNA from individuals
(n = 70) who developed multiple primary malignancies
diagnosed <65 years of age, we identi ﬁed 3 unrelated in-
dividuals (2 males and 1 female) who presented with the
CHEK2 (NM_007194.4) c.1100del gPV in a homozygous
state (4%; 3/70 individuals; we also identi ﬁed 1 individual
with monoallelic CHEK2 c.1100del gPV). Individual
UPN001 (male; last age of follow-up 67 years), developed 9
different (pre)malignancies (dysplastic nevi, age 40 years;
melanoma, age 43 years; thyroid cancer, age 49 years;
essential thrombocythemia, age 59 years; colorectal cancer
(CRC), age 60 years; chronic lymphocytic leukemia, age 64
years; Bowen ’s disease, age 66 years; squamous cell car-
cinoma of the skin, age 67 years; and a prostate cancer, age
67 years) and multiple benign tumors, including adenoma-
tous colorectal polyps and a sessile serrated polyp
(Supplemental Table 1 ). Co-segregation analyses identi ﬁed
a sister who is also homozygous for the CHEK2 c.1100del
gPV and she (UPN002; last age of follow-up 65 years)
developed 3 primary malignancies (breast cancer, age 44
years; CRC, age 65 years; and chronic lymphocytic leuke-
mia, age 65 years). Furthermore, she developed 2 breast
ductal carcinoma in situs (age 32 and 42 years) and multiple
adenomatous colorectal polyps. The second individual
identiﬁed through exome sequencing is individual UPN009
(male; who died at age 65 years). UPN009 developed 2
CRCs at age 64 years and multiple adenomatous colorectal
polyps, a lipoma and a trichilemmoma ( Supplemental
Table 1 ). The third individual identi ﬁed through exome
sequencing is individual UPN008 (female; last age of
follow-up 64 years). UPN008 developed 4 primary malig-
nancies (thyroid cancer, age 53 years; endometrial cancer,
age 53 years; breast cancer, age 54 years; and urothelial
cancer, age 62 years), a carcinoma in situ in an adenoma
(age 54 years), and multiple adenomatous colorectal polyps
(Supplemental Table 1 ). No other germline (likely) PVs in
known cancer-associated genes were identi ﬁed in the exome
sequencing data from the 3 individuals with the homozy-
gous CHEK2 c.1100del gPV.
Collection of a large cohort of individuals with
biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 suggests phenotypic
heterogeneity in cancer presentation
To identify more individuals with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2
and to get further insight in the prevalence, we screened the
CHEK2 c.1100del variant in a validation cohort of 781 in-
dividuals (69.5% of the cohort developed at least 1 breast
cancer). This approach resulted in the identi ﬁcation of 3
females (0.38%; UPN020, UPN021, and UPN063) homo-
zygous for CHEK2 c.1100del gPV ( Supplemental Table 1 ).
In addition, we identi ﬁed 30 individuals (3.8%) monoallelic
for the CHEK2 c.1100del gPV in the cohort, which is
comparable to literature.
1,13 Via 10 genetic diagnostic cen-
ters participating in ERN GENTURIS, we collected another
270 individuals diagnosed with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2.I n
addition, via a literature search, we identi ﬁed 17 individuals
with a biallelic gPV in CHEK2 for which a full individual
phenotypic description was provided.

Discussion
In this study, we collected the largest cohort of individuals
with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 to date. Phenotypic analysis
of this cohort supports the observation that females with
biallelic LoF gPVs in CHEK2 frequently develop multiple
breast cancers at young age and that they also develop other
cancer types throughout their lives. Moreover, our results
suggest that males with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 may also
present with other cancer types than breast cancer. There-
fore, it is likely that a substantial proportion of individuals
with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 is missed because diagnostic
testing for CHEK2 often is limited to those who have
developed (early-onset) breast cancer or who have a family
history for breast cancer.
In line with the existing literature on the role of CHEK2
in cancer susceptibility, we observe that most females with
biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 developed breast cancer. How-
ever, this observation is potentially biased because, in most
cases, the development of breast cancer was the most likely
reason for genetic testing of CHEK2. Moreover, our data
suggest that biallelic LoF gPVs in CHEK2 may confer a
higher risk to develop multiple (breast) cancers and at a
younger age compared with the homozygous p.(Ile157Thr)
variant in CHEK2. More than half of the females with
biallelic LoF gPVs in CHEK2 group developed at least 2 or
more cancers, and in approximately half of them, cancers
other than breast cancer were observed. Although we
observed phenotypic differences between individuals with
biallelic LoF gPVs and missense gPVs in CHEK2,i ti s
possible that these are attributable to the ascertainment bias
in genetic testing at various ERN GENTURIS genetic
centers. For example, although females with breast cancer in
The Netherlands undergo panel testing for breast cancer
predisposition that includes CHEK2, all individuals with
cancer in Poland, irrespective of the tissue type, are tested
for various founder variants in cancer predisposing genes,
among which is the CHEK2 p.(Ile157Thr) variant.
Although we could only make limited comparisons for
females with monoallelic LoF gPVs in CHEK2,w e
conﬁrmed that females with biallelic LoF gPVs in CHEK2
develop breast cancer earlier than females with monoallelic
LoF gPVs in CHEK2, and they more often develop non-
breast (pre)malignancies.
11,14 Interestingly, we found that
females with biallelic LoF gPVs develop more often CRC
than females with monoallelic LoF gPVs in CHEK2. This
observation is important because no surveillance advice for
CRC for individuals with biallelic LoF gPVs in CHEK2 are
currently in place, and this ﬁnding warrants further inves-
tigation. For CHEK2, compared with most other cancer
susceptibility genes, such as the mismatch repair (MMR)
genes and several genes in homologous recombination (HR)
repair, the lifetime risks for developing cancer are much
lower when gPVs are present in a monoallelic state. How-
ever, the fact that biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 may cause a
more severe and slightly different cancer phenotypic spec-
trum is in line with other genes involved in DNA repair
because biallelic gPVs in MMR genes (eg, Constitutional
MMR deﬁciency) and HR genes (eg, Fanconi Anaemia) also
predispose to syndromes with much earlier cancer onset and
different cancer types compared with their monoallelic
counterparts.

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network provides
risk management guidelines for people with CHEK2 gPVs.35
However, these are almost exclusively focused on females
heterozygous for CHEK2 gPVs, for which surveillance ad-
vices for breast cancer are provided. 35 Although extensive
literature exists for females with monoallelic gPVs in
CHEK2, most literature does not address individuals with
biallelic gPVs in CHEK2.11,36 As such, more research is
needed to estimate cancer risks in both females and males with
biallelic CHEK2 gPVs. In the absence of cancer risks for in-
dividuals with biallelic CHEK2 gPVs, it is too early to provide
tailor-made surveillance guidelines. However, yearly breast
cancer surveillance by MRI from age 25 and CRC surveil-

for individuals with biallelic LoFCHEK2 gPVs. Furthermore,
more than half of the individuals with biallelic CHEK2 gPVs

gene panel used for genetic testing, addition of CHEK2 to
identify biallelic CHEK2 gPVs may be considered for in-
dividuals who developed multiple primary (pre)malig-
nancies, especially before age of 70 years.
Our study has several limitations. Unfortunately, as also
described above, because of the ascertainment bias of testing
for gPVs in CHEK2 in various countries, in combination with
geographical differences in incidence of LoF and missense
variants and our retrospective collection of individuals with
biallelic gPVs in CHEK2, we cannot estimate lifetime cancer
risks either based on sex or for groups of individuals with
different CHEK2 gPVs. For follow-up studies, more exten-
sive clinical data collection is needed that includes extensive
family history, age at genetic testing, detailed treatment in-
formation, more complete follow-up time, and control series
of individuals heterozygous for the various CHEK2 gPVs and
individuals without CHEK2 gPVs.

In conclusion, the presence of biallelic gPVs in CHEK2
in an individual potentially increases the susceptibility to
develop multiple primary malignancies in various tissues,
both in females and in males. Our results suggest that more
research on the presence of biallelic gPVs in CHEK2 in
individuals with and without cancer should be performed.
More than 20 years after the discovery of CHEK2 as a
cancer susceptibility gene, it is time to accurately assess the
type of cancers that may develop in individuals with bial-
lelic gPVs and to determine their lifetime cancer risks in
order to provide clinical management guidelines of both
females and males with biallelic gPVs in CHEK2.
