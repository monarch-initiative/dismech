# LPIN-1 gene variant in Egyptian children: acute recurrent myoglobinuria
**Authors:** Tarek M. A. Abdallah, Said S. El-Feky, Nourhan A. A. Salem, Ghada M. Mashaal, Zaghloul E. Gouda
**Journal:** Journal of Rare Diseases (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s44162-025-00106-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44162-025-00106-w)

## Content

Abstract

Background
Children with LPIN-1 gene variant have recurrent acute myoglobinuria, rose-colored urine, general weakness, and fatigue. Treatment for this gene variant remains ameliorative and supportive. Here, we report the clinical data of three cases from Egypt. The age at onset of clinical symptoms was above 4.0 years.


Purpose
This work aimed to delineate the clinical manifestations exhibited by affected pediatric cases of recurrent rhabdomyolysis with a novel LPIN-1 variant and to provide a molecular characterization of this disease in Egypt, in order to raise awareness among both pediatricians and patients.


Subjects and methods
The study included three male cases: one from Damanhour city and two from Shebin El-Kom city. Gene expression analysis was performed using the “3billion” platform, which provides genetic testing for rare diseases and includes unlimited reanalysis that considers newly discovered diseases until diagnosis. Gene sequencing was conducted using the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). A total of 9,346,770,115 bases of sequence were generated and uniquely aligned to the Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 37 (GRCh37) and the Revised Cambridge Reference Sequence (rCRS) of the mitochondrial genome. This resulted in a mean depth of coverage of 127.51 within the 34,412,807 bases of the captured regions, including approximately 99.3% of the Ref Seq protein coding region. About 99.00% of the targeted bases were covered to a depth of ≥ 20 × . Gene or exon level depth-of-coverage information is available upon request. Urine analysis, AST, CK, CKMB, troponin, creatinine, and electrolytes profiles were also conducted. These biochemical analysis were performed in Damanhour Teaching Hospital Lab.


Results
Patients had creatinine kinase (CK) levels of 147,660.70 ± 28,144.50 U/L compared to baseline levels (438.0 ± 215.90 U/L) and aspartate transaminases (AST) levels of 2034.20 ± 1770.00 U/L compared to baseline levels (110.66 ± 105.13 U/L), and myoglobinuria and positive LPIN-1 gene deficiency expression with heterozygous likely pathogenic NM_001349206.2:c.26211GA variant and a heterozygous likely pathogenic 1.8-Kb deletion variant were identified. CK, AST, and urine color showed improvement after protective treatment.


Conclusion
Rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria in children can occur as a consequence of genetic mutation in the LPIN-1 gene. The available treatment aims to ameliorate muscle damage, provide supportive care, and ensure general protective management.

Introduction
LPIN-1 is a protein sequence that is primarily expressed 
in adipose tissue and muscle. It serves as a phosphatidate 
phosphatase 1 for the biosynthesis of phospholipids and 
triacylglycerol. Furthermore, it is comprised of numer -
ous amino acids [9]. LPIN-1, a 890-amino acid enzyme 
encoded by the LPIN-1 gene on chromosome 2p25.1, is 
magnesium dependent and has two conserved domains: 
an N-terminal lipin (N-LP) region spanning the first 108 
residues and a C-terminal lipin (C-LP) domain at the 
amino acids. The LPIN-1 gene, which contains 20 exons, 
is 149,865 bases long [18].
Acute recurrent myoglobinuria is attributed to vari -
ants  in the LPIN-1 gene. Acute renal failure, myalgia, 
hypotonia, weak muscles, impaired reflexes, and myo -
globinuria are some of the symptoms. Clinical monitor -
ing of rhabdomyolysis can be achieved by measuring 
levels of CK [16]. This condition is characterized by the 
breakdown of injured skeletal muscle, which releases the 
contents of the muscle fibers into the blood. Even though 
there is no clear cause for rhabdomyolysis, it is com -
monly seen following a fever, intense physical activity, or 
fasting [14].
A new class of hereditary metabolic disorders known 
as defects of complex lipid biosynthesis and remod -
eling includes lipin-1  deficiency, an autosomal recessive 
genetic disorder linked to rhabdomyolysis [15]. Impor -
tant signaling cascades are affected by lipin-1’s (a protein 
encoded by the LPIN-1 gene) capacity to regulate diacyl -
glycerol and phosphatidic acid levels outside of adipose 
tissues. This causes changes in the homeostasis of cells in 
mammals [24]. Adipocyte differentiation is dependent on 
the presence of the LPIN-1 gene-encoded magnesium-
ion-dependent phosphatidic acid phosphohydrolase 
enzyme, which dephosphorylates phosphatidic acid to 
produce diacylglycerol [27]. Abnormal mitochondrial 
function, altered fatty acid oxidation, impaired formation 
of auto-lysosomes in muscle, and rhabdomyolysis owing 
to muscle cell death are all consequences of LPIN-1’s dis -
rupting roles on communications to various organelles 
during stress responses [11]. An environmental risk fac -
tor may coexist with a hereditary one to produce LPIN-
1 variant-induced rhabdomyolysis, according to recent 
observations [5]. Since LPIN-1 gene variants remain 
relatively rare and under-recognized, many clinicians 
are encountering this condition for the first time. Conse -
quently, additional education and training are needed to 
ensure appropriate diagnosis, monitoring, and manage -
ment of patients with LPIN-1 deficiency [10].
Recently, low-dose hydroxychloroquine sulfate has 
shown promise in improving physical capacity in patients 
with LPIN-1 deficiency. It has been reported to reduce 
oxidative stress in patient-derived myoblasts and may 
serve as a supportive treatment during the baseline phase 
in chronically symptomatic patients. In cases of acute 
rhabdomyolysis, it may also be combined with corticos -
teroids to improve outcomes [20].
The pathophysiology of rhabdomyolysis-induced acute 
kidney injury (AKI) involves damage to the muscle sar -
colemma, which results in the leakage of intracellular 
contents, including myoglobin and electrolytes such 
as potassium, into the bloodstream. This cascade may 
impair renal glomerular function and lead to life-threat -
ening complications [4]. The accumulation of myotoxic 
substances including myoglobin, potassium, and lactic 
acid can cause multi-organ dysfunction, with AKI being 
one of the most frequent and severe complications, par -
ticularly among patients requiring intensive care [1].
Genetic testing for suspected muscular dystrophies, 
including LPIN-1-related rhabdomyolysis, is crucial not 
only for diagnosis but also for optimizing patient man -
agement. This includes educating caregivers and health -
care providers about lifestyle modifications to prevent 
muscle breakdown, such as avoiding strenuous physical 
activity and inappropriate therapies like aggressive physi-
cal rehabilitation [2]. In our series, such practices may 
have contributed to the progression of chronic kidney 
disease in Case 2.
The typical onset of rhabdomyolysis in LPIN-1-defi -
cient patients occurs before the age of 6 years, often with 
extreme elevations in serum creatine kinase (CK) levels 
exceeding 10,000 U/L [25]. However, later-onset cases 
have also been reported, including a 9-year-old boy with 
recurrent rhabdomyolysis and compound heterozygous 
LPIN1 variants [3]. These findings underscore the impor -
tance of early evaluation and recognition of LPIN1-asso -
ciated rhabdomyolysis to guide appropriate treatment, 
avoid harmful interventions, and establish standardized 
care recommendations tailored to each patient’s clinical 
picture.
Subjects and methods

the pediatric clinic and/or pediatric intensive care unit 
(PICU) at Damanhour Teaching Hospital and Faculty 
of Medicine Menoufia University, Egypt. The data were 
derived from the records of patients. The target cases 
were three patients: one of them was in the pediatric 
age range (4.5–8.5 years), and the other two were adults, 
but the retrograde study of their records and presenting 
symptoms was at the pediatric age range.
Permission was obtained from the hospital’s institu -

The LPIN gene family comprises three members: LPIN-
1, LPIN-2, and LPIN-3, each encoding a ~ 100-kDa 
protein that shares 44–48% amino acid homology [6]. 
Among these, LPIN-1 is predominantly expressed in 
skeletal muscle and plays a crucial role in muscle func -
tion. This explains the clinical manifestations observed 
in our patients, including fatigue, recurrent fever, dark-
colored urine, generalized weakness, and elevated cre -
atinine levels particularly in Case 2, who led a highly 
active lifestyle with limited medical supervision. The 
LPIN-1 variant identified in our patients was classified 
as likely pathogenic based on the ACMG criteria, fulfill -
ing the following evidence codes: PVS1, PS3, PM1, and 
PP4. This frameshift mutation leads to a significant loss 
of LPIN-1 enzymatic activity, which disrupts mitochon -
drial function and lipid metabolism, fulfilling the criteria 
for very strong pathogenicity (PVS1) and strong func -
tional evidence (PS3). Additionally, the variant affects a 
highly conserved catalytic domain of phosphatidic acid 
phosphatase, supporting its moderate pathogenicity clas -
sification (PM1). The clinical presentation of recurrent 
rhabdomyolysis in early childhood further supports its 
pathogenic nature (PP4).
Comparison with existing literature
Previous studies have reported that severe rhabdomyoly -
sis episodes are often linked to complete loss of function 
mutations in LPIN-1, such as frameshift variants, which 
lead to extremely elevated CK levels [16]. In our patients, 
significantly elevated CK levels were also observed, indi -
cating ongoing muscle breakdown due to LPIN-1 dys -
function. While more severe mutations result in total 
loss of enzymatic activity, missense mutations may pre -
serve some transcriptional regulatory functions, resulting 
in milder phenotypes [22]. Our findings align with the 
work of Donkor et al. [8], who described the dual role of 
LPIN-1 in lipid metabolism and transcriptional regula -
tion. Additionally, elevated levels of inflammatory media-
tors such as TNF-α and IL-1β may explain the high body 
temperatures and physical deterioration seen during epi -
sodes [17]. In our cohort, Case 2 developed chronic renal 
failure and has been on hemodialysis for 3 years. This is 
consistent with Jigar et  al. [12], who described similar 
renal deterioration due to prolonged myoglobinuria and 
rhabdomyolysis-induced acute tubular necrosis. Contrib-
uting risk factors include dehydration, sepsis, metabolic 
acidosis, and iron-mediated tubular injury.

tial to prevent acute kidney injury in rhabdomyolysis. 
Renal injury in these patients results from vasoconstric -
tion, tubular obstruction by myoglobin casts, and direct 
myoglobin toxicity [21]. These mechanisms align with 
our care strategy, which emphasizes early hydration 
and avoidance of harmful stimuli. Serum CK remains 
a key biomarker for assessing rhabdomyolysis severity 
and predicting complications such as renal impairment 
[26]. In our study, CK levels was more than 500,000 µ/L 
in aggressive disease attack for case number 3 and var -
ied significantly among the three patients, despite simi -
lar LPIN-1 genotypes. This variation was likely due to

Conclusion
We emphasize the importance of genetic testing for 
LPIN-1 variants in pediatric patients presenting with 
recurrent rhabdomyolysis. Early detection allows for 
targeted interventions and the implementation of avoid -
ance care protocols including hydration, avoidance of 
prolonged fasting, reduction of physical exertion, and 
infection control. CK and transaminase levels, along with 
urine analysis, are essential tools for monitoring disease 
progression. With proper management, prognosis in 
these rare cases can be significantly improved, reducing 
long-term complications such as chronic kidney disease.
