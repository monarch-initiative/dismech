# Pseudomonas infections persisting after CFTR modulators are widespread throughout the lungs and drive lung inflammation.
**Authors:** Durfey SL, Kapnadak SG, Pena T, Willmering MM, Godwin JD, Teresi ME, Heltshe SL, Vo AT, Villacreses RA, Aliukonyte I, Boyken L, Stroik MR, Morgan SJ, Wang GM, Betts HL, Zhang S, Goss CH, Clancy JP, Aitken ML, Steele C, Feder AF, Esther CR Jr, Tiddens HAWM, Woods JC, Stoltz DA, Singh PK
**Journal:** Cell Host Microbe (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.chom.2025.07.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chom.2025.07.009)

## Content

Cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulators improve the physiological defect causing cystic fibrosis, but the lungs of most people remain infected and inflamed. A leading hypothesis implicates damaged segments as the cause of persistent infection and predicts that mildly diseased segments within an individual's lungs will clear after treatment, whereas severely diseased segments will not. Our findings contradict this hypothesis. We used bronchoscopy to sample the least- and most-damaged lung segments in Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Pa)-infected individuals before modulators and returned to these same segments after 1.5 years. Surprisingly, we find an "all-or-none" infection clearance response: the most-diseased segments clear if any other lung segment in that person clears, and the least-diseased segments remain infected if others in that person do. Furthermore, neutrophilic inflammation completely resolves where Pa clears but remains elevated where Pa persists. These data indicate that post-modulator infections are not limited to severely diseased segments and that Pa infections drive persistent lung inflammation after modulators.

People with cystic fibrosis (PwCF) have a shortened lifespan due to chronic lung infections, and the pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Pa) has historically been linked to poor outcomes 1– 3 . CF treatment has recently been transformed by drugs called modulators that markedly improve the activity of the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) channel, which is defective in CF 4 . Elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) is one of the most effective modulators in use, and it markedly improves sweat chloride levels (which reflect in vivo CFTR activity) in PwCF and responsive CFTR mutations 5 , 6 . However, the sputum of most PwCF remain infected with the pathogens present pre-treatment and neutrophilic inflammation persists 7– 9 . Understanding why lung infections and inflammation persist is critical to devising new treatment approaches.

Several mechanisms could explain why PwCF remain infected after modulators. A leading hypothesis posits that localized areas with high lung damage are the central cause 7 . This is plausible because lungs of most individuals with CF contain segments with potentially reversible lesions like inflammation and mucus plugging where modulators could restore host defenses, and segments with severe damage that may not benefit from treatment 10– 13 . Thus, modulator treatment could make CF lung disease analogous to non-CF bronchiectasis. In this condition, infection is thought to be localized to structurally damaged segments, while the remainder of the lung is thought to be uninfected 14 , 15 .

Other mechanisms could also cause persistent infection. For example, Pa populations genetically diversify and adapt during CF infections 16 and they grow to high cell densities in mucus-encased aggregates 17 . These or other bacterial phenotypes could make organisms in any lung region resistant to killing. Likewise, insufficient CFTR correction 18 or immune tolerance from chronic antigen stimulation 19 could result in ongoing and widespread lung host defense defects. These or other mechanism could cause infection to persist throughout the lungs, including in minimally diseased segments.

Understanding whether lung infection and inflammation are widespread or localized post-ETI is important for several reasons. First, widespread infection will likely degrade lung function globally, whereas localized infection may have more limited effects. Second, widespread and localized infection may require different therapeutic approaches. Localized infection suggests that particular conditions interfere with the beneficial effects of modulators, so improving these could enhance outcomes. Widespread infection that includes minimally diseased segments may require new strategies against adapted, high-density bacterial populations or measures that generally boost host defenses. In addition, total lung bacterial burden could be higher if most lung segments remain infected as an expansive area is colonized. This could also accelerate bacterial adaptation by increasing the range of selective conditions that operate on infecting bacteria.

Here, we used bronchoscopy to sample multiple lung segments within PwCF before and after ETI treatment to ask four main questions. First, how much baseline segment-to-segment heterogeneity exists in conditions that could affect infection responses? Second, does ETI produce similar effects in lung segments within an individual or do some segments clear while others remain infected? Third, do segments become colonized with other pathogens after clearing infections present pre-ETI? Finally, what factors drive persistent lung inflammation after ETI treatment?

We addressed these questions by sampling the 5 lung segments including those with the most mild and severe disease in each of 9 individuals before ETI. We then returned to these same segments after 1.5 years of treatment. At both time points we measured structural lung damage with high-resolution imaging, and microbiologic, inflammatory, and metabolomic parameters in segmental lung washings. This work generated a granular understanding of ETI’s effects on CF lung disease and insights on mechanisms driving post-modulator lung inflammation. It also provides a case study on how a paradigm chronic bacterial infection responds when host defense defects that initially caused the infection are reversed.

Our objective was to investigate how disparate lung regions within individuals respond when CFTR channel activity is improved. Thus, we performed invasive multi-segment lung sampling in a small number of selected CF adults (n=9, average age 33.5yrs) with chronic Pa infection, and moderate lung dysfunction (average percent predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1pp) was 68%). Participant’s lung function was slightly higher than the >3000 comparably aged subjects with chronic Pa infection in the CF Foundation’s patient registry (average FEV1pp = 62%). We excluded people with low lung function for safety, which likely accounts for this difference.

Participants had robust clinical responses to ETI, similar to prior studies 5 , 6 . Their mean (range) FEV1pp improved from 68% (58–81%) to 84% (68–121%; mean change +16%) and mean (range) sweat chloride decreased from 96 (68–131 mmol/L) to 36 mmol/L (17–72 mmol/L; mean change −60 mmol/L). Baseline demographics including other lung function values are shown in Table S1, and comorbidities and concurrent medications are in Table S2. All but 1 participant had post-ETI sweat chloride values lower than the diagnostic criteria for CF (60 mmol/L), and 4 participants had normal values (<30 mmol/L). Individual responses are shown in Table S3.

The same 5 segments in each participant were sampled before and 1.5 years after ETI (Fig. S1A and methods). The lower lobes were excluded to avoid poor lavage return as we could use only ~20 mL/segment (to avoid instilling >100 mL/person). To maximize the range of conditions sampled, an expert chest radiologist (J.D.G.) selected the 2 most diseased and 3 least diseased lung segments for sampling in each participant based on high-resolution chest CT scans performed within 2 weeks of the bronchoscopy. Antibiotics (except azithromycin) were disallowed 2 weeks prior to each bronchoscopy.

We made major efforts to reduce contamination and carryover between segments. Bronchoscopy was performed under general anesthesia through cuffed endotracheal tubes to reduce oropharyngeal contamination, a new disposable bronchoscope was used for each of the 5 segments per person, and the least damaged segment was sampled first followed successively by segments with greater damage. Control analyses showed these measures effectively reduced cross contamination, as many segments had undetectable levels of one or more measured analyte, when segments sampled immediately beforehand did not (Fig. S1B-F).

We focused primarily on 5 conditions (called “disease conditions” below) that previous work has implicated in infection responses. These included: (1) structural lung damage on CT scans measured by adapting the Brody scoring method to measure segmental damage (see methods) 15 , 20 ; (2) Pa density measured by quantitative culture 7 ; (3) inflammation measured by neutrophil elastase (NE) 21– 24 ; (4) mucus concentrations measured by sialic acid levels (sialic acid terminates glycans, the primary constituents of mucins) 25 ; and (5) levels of carbon and energy sources shown to be most preferred by Pa in sputum of PwCF (e.g. proline, lactate, and others) 26 . Structural damage and inflammation could affect ETI’s ability to improve lung host defenses, and high bacterial density, abundant mucus, and plentiful nutrients could counteract ETI-mediated bacterial clearance.

All five disease conditions exhibited large variations within the lungs of individuals before ETI was initiated. While all but two sampled lung segments were Pa-culture positive pre-ETI, the average range in segmental Pa density within individuals was 3.1 log10 CFU/mL (highest range found in a participant was 6.0 log10 CFU/mL; Fig. 1A). The average range in NE in segments within a person’s lungs was 1.3 log10 μg/mL (highest 2.2 log10 ug/mL); sialic acid was 1.1 log10 mM (highest 1.8 log10 mM); proline was 1.3 log10 mM (highest 2.1 log10 mM); and in segmental Brody score was 15.1 (highest 26.5) on a 40-point scale (Fig. 1B-E). Marked within lung heterogeneity was also seen in many other conditions (Fig. S2 and Table S4).

We calculated the intraclass correlations 27 of segmental values for the 5 main disease conditions (using study participant as the class) to measure the extent to which segmental values depended on study participant. Intraclass correlations values for all conditions were low (0.022 to 0.25; Table S5), indicating that the values of these parameters in lung segments were largely independent of other segments in the same lung. Thus, it seemed reasonable to examine infection outcomes by segment, as well as by study participant, and we do both below.

We explored relationships between different disease conditions (measured pre-ETI) in individual lung segments and found values of the 5 main disease conditions strongly associated (all p <0.001; R2 = 0.24–0.91; Fig. 2A-J). Other conditions we measured were also associated (Fig. S3). This finding suggests that segments with extremes of severity in one condition often have extremes of severity in others, and vice versa.

We tested this by ranking each segment by its cohort-wide severity for each condition and examined the frequency at which segments scored in the worst, middle, and best tertiles for each disease condition (i.e. levels of Pa, structural damage, NE, mucus, and Pa nutrients), and in an “overall” segmental disease measure that summed severity rankings for each condition (see methods). Consistent with the strong associations noted above, adverse conditions often co-localized in the same lung segments, as did favorable conditions (Fig. 2K). The co-localization of multiple severe and mild disease conditions could accentuate within-lung differences in ETI responses.

We began studying ETI’s effects by examining responses of all lung segments independent of participant or baseline condition and found Pa density, CT-measured lung damage, neutrophilic inflammation, mucus, and Pa nutrients all markedly decreased after 1.5 years of treatment (p < 0.0001; Fig. 3A-E). The average decrease in Pa was −2.4 log10 CFU/mL, segmental Brody score was −3.3 points, NE was −0.9 log10 ug/mL, sialic acid was −0.57 log10 mM, and proline was −0.92 log10 μM. Other parameters also significantly decreased (p < 0.05), including Staphylococcus aureus (Sa) density (Fig. 3F), total bacterial load (Fig. 3G) and cumulative Pa + Sa abundance (Fig. S4B) measured by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR); IL-1β (Fig. 3H), IL-8 (Fig. 3I) and 18 additional inflammatory markers (Fig. S4C); and other nutrients preferred by Pa in vivo including arginine, lactate and 11 more (Fig. S4D-F). The decreases in Pa, NE, other cytokines, and amino acids are consistent with changes detected in expectorated sputum in previous work 7 , 8 , 28– 30 (Table S6). The interpatient range we detected pre-ETI and post-ETI is similar to other pre-modulator BAL studies (Table S7). Notably, the average Pa density change reported above includes the 18/45 segments in which Pa became undetectable after ETI (see below). In segments remaining positive, Pa density decreased by only 1.1 log10 CFU/mL (p < 0.05).

Our findings that adverse conditions generally co-localize to same segments, and knowledge that severely diseased segments cause persistent infection in non-CF bronchiectasis 14 , 15 led us to hypothesize that mildly diseased lung segments within individuals would clear infection and inflammation after ETI whereas severely diseased segments would not.

To test this, we examined changes in segmental Pa density by participant and were surprised to find that Pa generally cleared from either all or none of the sampled lung segments within individual participants (Fig. 3J). In 3 of 9 participants, all 15 sampled segments became Pa-negative after ETI. This means that segments with the worst baseline structural damage, and highest Pa density, inflammation, mucus, and Pa nutrients in each person cleared Pa if other segments in that lung did so. Notably, 27% (4/15) of the segments that became Pa-negative were in the worst overall disease severity tertile across the entire cohort (red points, Fig. 3J).

Findings in the 6 persistently Pa-infected participants were analogous, as almost all segments, including segments with the mildest disease, remained Pa-positive after ETI: only 2 of 30 segments in persistently infected participants became Pa-negative. Thus, segments with the least structural damage, and lowest baseline Pa density, inflammation, mucus, and Pa nutrients within participants’ lungs remained infected if other segments in that lung remained infected. Indeed, 30% of the persistently infected segments were in the lowest overall disease severity tertile across participants (green points, Fig. 3J).

The six Sa-infected participants showed a similar response, as Sa generally cleared in either all or none of the segments in individuals (Fig. 3K). Similar to Pa, segments with the worst overall disease cohort-wide (red points, Fig. 3K) generally cleared Sa if other segments in that lung cleared, and segments with the least overall disease (green points, Fig. 3K) remained infected if other segments in that lung remained infected. Multi-locus sequence typing showed that same Pa and Sa MLST types were present pre-and post-ETI in persistently infected subjects (Table S8). Thus, the severity of local lung conditions did not independently determine which lung segments cleared Pa and Sa, and participants with persistent Pa and Sa infections generally remained infected throughout their lungs rather than in localized segments.

Our finding of an “all or none” segment clearance pattern suggests that factors inherent to individual people or the Pa strains they are infected with have a dominant effect on infection responses. While we sampled 45 lung segments pre- and post-ETI, only 9 participants were involved (3 cleared and 6 remained Pa-infected). Thus, the study was underpowered to draw conclusions on participant-level characteristics (i.e. FEV1pp, sweat chloride, whole lung CT parameters, time since first Pa-positive culture, etc.) associated with infection persistence. These data are shown in Fig. S5 and Table S4 but are not emphasized here.

We did perform an exploratory analysis of conditions associated with Pa persistence after ETI, using pre-treatment disease parameters in the 45 sampled lung segments. Of the 5 main disease conditions we studied, only Pa’s preferred nutrients (including proline (Fig. 4A), arginine, lactate, and 4 additional amino acids (Fig. 4B)) were different, with participants remaining Pa-infected having higher baseline levels than those clearing Pa. The uncorrected p-values increased when applying multiple testing corrections or repeated measures analyses (Table S9).

In contrast, we found no significant differences between segments clearing Pa vs. those remaining infected in the other 4 main disease conditions including segmental levels of Pa, segmental Brody score, NE, and mucus (Fig. 4C-F), or in overall cohort-wide disease severity of segments that included all five main disease conditions (Fig. S6A). There was also no difference in segmental Sa or total bacterial density, (Fig. S6B-C), microbiome parameters (Fig. S6D-F) or levels of 60 of 63 other inflammatory markers we measured (Fig. 4G).

Because structural lung disease is thought to be a primary cause of persistent infection in non-CF bronchiectasis, we examined the CT scans further using segmental Brody subscores and two computer image analysis systems (developed by Thirona 31 and VIDA Inc 32 ). We found no differences in segmental bronchiectasis or mucus plugging between participants who did and did not clear Pa, as measured by both segmental Brody scoring and the Thirona system (Fig. 4H-I). However, baseline segmental air trapping was significantly higher in participants remaining persistently Pa-positive (as measured by both segmental Brody and VIDA) (Fig. 4J).

Finally, we studied the phenotypes of Pa populations in segments that did and did not clear Pa, and consistent with previous work 33 found phenotypic heterogeneity in Pa within individual lung segments. Segments remaining persistently Pa infected were more likely to contain Pa isolates that were auxotrophic, nonmotile, and lacked protease secretion (Fig. 4K). We found no differences in pyoverdine production or antibiotic resistance (Fig. S6H). Pa strains in participants that did and did not clear Pa also had similar mutation frequencies (Fig. S7). We view these results as exploratory. Additional work will be needed to determine if elevated Pa nutrients, air trapping, or particular Pa phenotypes contribute to infection persistence post-ETI.

Segments clearing Pa after ETI could develop secondary infections because of residual CFTR dysfunction or other abnormalities. We tested this using 16S amplicon sequencing and ddPCR on lavage fluid in the participants clearing Pa. Reagent controls were found to have 0–300 16S copies/mL matching CF pathogen genera 34 , and samples from segments clearing Pa (including those with the worst baseline disease conditions) had similar levels (range: 0–400 copies/mL) (Fig. 5). Thus, we found no evidence that segments clearing Pa after ETI became infected by other CF pathogens.

The long-term prognosis for lung function in ETI-treated individuals likely depends on whether lung inflammation persists or resolves 35– 37 . Thus, we measured inflammation changes after ETI and examined factors associated with persistent inflammation.

We focused first on NE, as NE levels predict future lung disease in both CF and non-CF bronchiectasis 35– 37 . NE became undetectable in almost all (16/18) segments clearing Pa and remained elevated in most (i.e. 22/27) segments remaining Pa-infected (Fig. 6A). Consistent with the Pa responses described above, NE largely exhibited an “all-or-none” by-participant response. NE became undetectable in segments with the most severe baseline disease (red points, Fig. 6B), if any other segment in the lung resolved NE. Likewise, NE remained elevated in segments with the least baseline disease (green points, Fig. 6B), if it did in any other lung segment.

Changes in IL-1β, IL-8, and 17 other inflammation markers showed a similar pattern, with markedly greater reductions in segments clearing Pa as compared to those remaining infected (p < 0.05; Fig. 6C). The absolute post-ETI concentrations of NE, IL-1β, IL-8, and 30 other markers were also significantly lower in segments clearing Pa as compared to those remaining infected (p < 0.05; Fig. 6D).

Multiple mechanisms are postulated to cause inflammation in CF 38 , 39 . These include mechanisms unrelated to infection such as mucus plugging and airway hypoxia 40 and dysregulated inflammatory signaling in epithelia and immune cells due to CFTR dysfunction 41 . ETI could have differential effects on these pro-inflammatory mechanisms, and it is important to understand which mechanisms diminish after ETI and which persist.

To explore this, we examined treatment-associated changes in the goodness of fit (i.e. R2 value) between Pa density and inflammatory markers to determine how well infection accounted for residual inflammation. The R2 value between Pa and NE markedly increased after ETI (pre-ETI R2 = 0.28; after ETI R2 = 0.76; Fig. 7A), as did R2 values between Pa and IL-1β, IL-8, IL-17, and other inflammatory markers implicated in CF lung disease (Fig. 7B) 41 . All told, 11 of 43 inflammatory markers that significantly correlated with Pa had significantly higher R2 values after ETI as compared to before ETI, almost all others showed trends toward increased R2 values, and none showed significant decreases (Fig. 7B). These findings suggest that Pa density better accounts for lung inflammation after as compared to before ETI. This finding is consistent with previous work showing that ETI reduces other pro-inflammatory mechanisms thought important in CF including mucus plugging 42 and mutant CFTR’s direct effects on cellular inflammatory signaling 43– 46 .

We also examined the slopes of regression lines between Pa density and inflammatory markers before and after ETI to determine whether ETI affected the magnitude of inflammation associated with each unit of Pa (i.e. per Pa cell, or per Pa log10 CFU/mL). This was of interest because CFTR dysfunction in epithelial and immune cells is postulated to amplify pro-inflammatory responses 41 , so one might expect slopes to decrease after ETI. However, slopes of the relationships between Pa and 42 of the 43 markers described above (including NE, IL-1β, and IL-8) were either unchanged or exhibited increased trends after ETI (Fig. 7C). Thus, ETI treatment did not decrease the magnitude of lung inflammation associated with Pa.

Finally, we investigated how Pa persistence affected inflammation mechanisms thought to be independent of infection. Previous work has shown that high mucus levels and hypoxia can induce lung epithelia to secrete IL-1β 40 . Consistent with these results, we found statistically significant relationships between markers of mucus (sialic acid) and IL-1β in segments that did and did not clear Pa after ETI (Fig. 7D). However, the goodness of fit was stronger (R2: 0.26 vs. 0.58) and the slope much steeper (0.46 vs. 1.6, p < 0.05) in segments where Pa persisted as compared to segments where Pa cleared (Fig. 7D). These data and ETI’s effects on the R2 and slopes of Pa-inflammation marker regressions suggest that Pa is a both a primary driver of post-ETI lung inflammation and can amplify the effect of non-infectious pro-inflammatory mechanisms in CF.

This study generated four main findings. First, at baseline, the lungs of these participants with mild to moderate CF lung disease exhibited marked segment-to-segment heterogeneity in bacterial density, structural damage, inflammation, mucus, and bacterial nutrients. Moreover, conditions that we thought would favor infection clearance (e.g. low pathogen density, damage, inflammation, etc.) generally co-localized to the same lung segments within an individual, as did severe disease conditions that we expected to favor infection persistence. Second, despite these highly disparate segmental conditions, infection clearance generally occurred in either all or none of the segments in an individual’s lungs. This finding contradicts the hypothesis that post-modulator infections are primarily localized to highly diseased lung regions.

Third, after 1.5 years of treatment, lung segments clearing Pa and Sa had not become persistently infected with other bacterial pathogens. Thus, when it occurs, infection clearance could be durable and may not be followed by secondary infections. Fourth, neutrophilic inflammation as measured by NE almost completely resolved in segments clearing Pa, but it persisted in segments remaining Pa-infected. Many other inflammatory markers were also much higher in Pa-infected as compared to Pa-cleared segments. These data are consistent with the hypothesis that persistent Pa is a primary driver of post-ETI lung inflammation. Key findings are discussed below.

Heterogeneity in structural lung damage is a hallmark of CF 10– 13 , and work in the pre-modulator era in adults found regional heterogeneity in infection and inflammation 11 , 33 , 34 , 47 , including differences in Pa density and inflammation similar to those described here 47 , 48 . We extend these findings by showing marked segment-to-segment differences in many disease parameters including Pa and Sa density, CT-based measures of structural lung disease, a wide range of inflammatory markers, and mucus and bacterial nutrient levels. Notably, within-lung differences in key parameters far exceed changes produced by effective CF treatments. For example, the ~1000-fold or higher differences in Pa density between lung segments exceed ~10-fold reductions achieved with inhaled antibiotics 49 , and the ~30-fold differences in NE exceed the 2-fold reductions produced by the most-commonly prescribed CF anti-inflammatory agent 50 .

This heterogeneity may be due to the combined effects of anatomical variations in host conditions and bacterial responses. For example, known variations in blood and lymphatic flow, oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, mucociliary clearance, and other conditions 51 , 52 could illicit different pathogenic responses from regional bacteria, even if they were genetically identical. Adding to this, Pa subpopulations in different lung segments genetically diverge from each other causing regional populations to differ in phenotypes like invasiveness, inflammatory potential, and resistance to killing 33 . Thus, genetically diverged Pa populations are exposed to different ambient conditions. The strong correlations between disease conditions suggests a cyclical relationship between factors that may amplify regional differences (e.g. higher pathogen densityin➔creased inflammation➔more bacterial nutrients & mucus➔higher pathogen density, and so on).

We were surprised to find that lung segments with the lowest baseline levels of Pa density, structural damage, neutrophilic inflammation, mucus, and nutrients remained Pa colonized in people with persistent infections. It was particularly notable that neither the CT scan analyses by human Brody scoring or computerized methods found differences in bronchiectasis in segments that did and did not clear Pa. Bronchiectasis a hallmark lesion of CF lung disease 10 , 36 and postulated to be a root cause of persistent infection 15 .

These finding may seem at odds with observations from non-CF bronchiectasis and other chronic infections, where damaged regions are thought to be the nidus of infection 14 , 15 . However, it is important to note that the link between localized disease conditions and infection is derived from observations on initial infection susceptibility; i.e. chronic infections generally arise preferentially at damaged sites. Here we studied the effect of reversing pre-existing host defense defects after chronic infection had been established.

These scenarios differ in important ways. For example, initial infections are generally caused by a low inoculum of bacteria from environmental sources that may not be host-adapted 53 . Thus, infection initiation could depend on permissive host defenses enabled by localized tissue damage. In contrast, established CF infections involve high density Pa populations that have genetically adapted to host conditions and extensively diversified 16 . High density and adaptation could make established infections resistant to clearance, even if host defenses were markedly improved.

Another mechanism that could explain infection persistence in lung regions with minimal disease relates to the finding by others and us that pathogens can migrate from segment to segment within the lungs of PwCF 33 , 54 . Bacterial migration could enable a scenario wherein some segments clear after ETI initiation, but then subsequently become re-infected by bacteria from other lung locations. Our previous work showing that segmental Pa have genetically diverged from each other may enable us to detect intra-lung bacterial migration 33 . This is because genetic differences of regional Pa can serve as markers to determine where they originated from. This work is underway.

The all or none pattern of infection clearance suggests that characteristics particular to individuals may have a dominant effect on infection responses. The critical characteristics could be host-specific traits, such as sex 55 , the extent of CFTR correction (including effects of CFTR genotype 5 ), efficacy of systemic immunity, the total burden of structural lung damage, inflammation, or other systemic factors, including comorbidities like CF-related diabetes 56 . Residual CFTR dysfunction is of particular interest as CFTR dysfunction impairs multiple host defenses including mechanical clearance 38 , efficacy of soluble antibacterial factors 57 , macrophage 58 , 59 and neutrophil 60 function, and perhaps others. Infection clearance may require marked improvement in many or all of these together, and this may rarely occur. It is also possible the “by-participant” pattern of infection clearance is due to differences in infecting Pa. Most people with CF are infected by different strains that can vary substantially in infection-related phenotypes including resistance to killing 53 , 61 , and strains can evolve differently in vivo.

Because we focused on high intensity sampling in a small number of PwCF, we lacked statistical power to identify relationships between participant-level traits and infection clearance. However, our exploratory analysis found lung segments of persistently infected participants had higher concentrations of nutrients preferred by Pa in vivo 26 , higher levels of air trapping by two independent analysis methods, and a higher proportion of Pa isolates with phenotypes associated with chronic CF infections 62 including auxotrophy, and loss of motility and protease production. Loss of motility and protease production could contribute to infection persistence by making the bacteria less visible to the immune system.

However, additional work is needed to confirm these potential associations and investigate the directionality of the effect. It is plausible that these parameters contribute to infection persistence (e.g. high nutrients, air trapping, and/or Pa adaptation infection persistence). It is also possible that they are consequences of infection (e.g. infection persistence nutrient liberation, air trapping, and increased Pa adaptation).

Multiple pro-inflammatory mechanisms are thought to operate in CF, including pathogen-induced inflammation 63 , epithelial hypoxia from mucus stasis 40 , and the direct pro-inflammatory effects of CFTR mutations 41 . However, it is unknown which mechanisms are most consequential after modulator treatment.

Four observations generated here suggest that persistent Pa infection are a primary driver of post-ETI lung inflammation. First, NE was almost exclusively found in segments remaining Pa-infected and was absent in segments clearing Pa. Second, many other inflammatory mediators including those produced mainly by monocytes and macrophages (Il-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-23), T-cells (IL-17), and multiple cell types (IL-8, MIP-1α&β, eotaxin) 41 , 64 , 65 exhibited greater reductions in segments becoming Pa negative versus those remaining Pa-infected. These findings suggest persistent Pa infection has pervasive pro-inflammatory effects. Third, we found marked increases in the goodness of fit (the R2 value) between Pa density and NE and many other inflammatory markers. Thus, Pa better explained the extent of inflammation after as compared to before ETI. Fourth, after ETI the slopes of regression lines between Pa and key inflammatory markers were either unchanged or marginally increased. This indicates that each Pa cell likely induces a similar or increased amount of inflammation after as compared to before ETI. While cause/effect relationships cannot be concluded from these observations, the findings are consistent with the hypothesis that Pa is likely a primary driver of lung inflammation after ETI treatment.

These findings are also consistent with previous work suggesting that modulators diminish other pro-inflammatory mechanisms. For example, modulators improve the CF mucociliary clearance defect 66 , 67 and mucus plugging 42 , 68 which should reduce hypoxia-induced inflammation. Modulators also reduce pro-inflammatory signaling in CF cells 43– 46 , 69 , 70 . Notably, we found markers of mucus concentration were associated with IL-1β after ETI, which suggests that mucus plugging may still contribute to inflammation in treated patients. However, the strength of the association and the slope of the relationship between mucus abundance and IL-1β were far higher when Pa was present.

This study had several limitations. First, the intensity of the study and the COVID-19 pandemic limited enrollment. Thus, we did not attempt to draw conclusions about participant-level characteristics associated with infection persistence like CFTR genotype, complications like CF-related diabetes, participant’s Pa clone type, or other factors. Larger studies could assess these factors. However, we were able to intensively study 45 lung segments in nine PwCF, and think the information generated here complements larger studies underway using sputum sampling. Second, we selected participants known to have chronic Pa infections as these infections are most strongly linked to poor outcomes in CF. While the people infected with Sa also generally exhibited an all-or-none infection clearance pattern, our findings may not apply to other organisms, or for patients with very early or late-stage disease.

Third, because the risks and costs of bronchoscopy, we used two time points and a relatively long sampling interval (1.5 years) as we thought longer-term infection and inflammation outcomes were most important. Thus, we do not know when clearance occurred (in people becoming Pa-free), if persistently infected segments were transiently infection-free or if Pa-cleared lungs become transiently infected.

Fourth, we could not measure ETI-mediated changes in CFTR activity in each segment, and this could be an important variable. For example, recent work shows that ETI may be more effective when inflammation is increased 18 . However, all participants exhibited robust systemic CFTR responses. Thus, if marked segment-to-segment differences in CFTR activity existed, these could be due to differences in structural lung damage or inflammation 18 , 71 (rather than systemic differences in ETI responses) and might be reflected by the imaging and inflammatory parameters we measured. We also did not examine the functioning of epithelia, macrophages, neutrophils, or other cell types from participants, and any of these cell types or others could affect infection responses.

Fifth, while we intentionally targeted segments with the mildest (and most severe) disease in each participant, none of the sampled segments were normal before treatment. Almost all harbored Pa or Sa and were inflamed. Thus, this work does not provide insights into the natural history of completely un-diseased lung segments after ETI, or segments infected with other organisms.

Sixth, we note that different views about the nutrient composition of CF sputum exist 72 . However, 8 of 9 media recipes that attempt to model sputum nutrient conditions contain amino acids, which we focused on as markers of Pa nutrient availability 26 , 73– 79 . Moreover, the nutrients highlighted here have been shown to be preferentially consumed when Pa is grown ex vivo in sputum collected from PwCF 26 .

Finally, it is challenging in human studies to prove cause and effect relationships. While clinical experience in CF argues strongly that improvements in infection, inflammation and other parameters were due to ETI, we cannot definitively determine if changes in a given parameter (e.g. Pa density) caused changes in others (e.g. inflammation). However, previous work on modulators show that pathogen density declines before inflammation 68 , 80 , and Pa is known to induce inflammation in many animal and cell culture systems. Moreover, the most prescribed anti-inflammatory medicine used in CF (azithromycin) effectively suppresses inflammation without reducing bacterial density 81 . If an ETI-mediated decrease in inflammation caused decreases in infection (rather than decreased infection decreased inflammation), one might expect decreases in pathogen density after anti-inflammatory treatment. Together, these observations suggest reduced Pa contributed to reduced inflammation.

Our finding that PwCF who remain infected after ETI have Pa and Pa-associated inflammation throughout their lungs has important clinical implications. Over time, widespread infection and inflammation could be more detrimental to lung function (and overall health) than localized disease. Widespread infection also means that infecting bacteria are likely replicating in many lung regions with disparate conditions. This could increase the range of selective pressures operating on pathogen populations and enable a higher total-lung bacterial burden. Both of these factors could accelerate in vivo bacterial adaption and worsen disease. Finally, infection throughout the lungs may not be amenable to regionally targeted approaches like surgical resection or new approaches like localized tissue regeneration. Rather, the findings suggest that infection outcomes could be improved by sensitizing bacteria to killing by existing therapeutics, developing new antimicrobial strategies, or improving host defenses throughout the lungs. Future work to identify pathogen and host factors responsible for infection persistence could enable such strategies.

Low dose CT scanning protocol was adapted from the protocol used for the multi-center SPIROMICS trial 82 and consisted of obtaining multi-detector CT (MDCT) images of the entire lung at full inspiration (total lung capacity or TLC) and at Functional Residual Volume (RV). Scans were collected at 120 kV and current of 110 mA for inspiratory scans and 65 mA for expiratory scans. The slice thickness was 0.625 mm with an interval of 0.5 mm and a pitch of 0.984:1. Spatial resolution was matched at baseline and follow-up using a similar diameter field of view for both scanning sessions. Scans were reconstructed with filtered back projection and standard kernel. Participants were instructed in specialized breathing techniques to acquire the scans. Briefly, the participant was instructed to inhale deeply and exhale 2 times and then hold their breath two different ways: with the lungs full of air (TLC) and at the end of a full expiratory effort (RV). Scans were de-identified prior to transfer between the Universities of Washington, Iowa, Cincinnati and Erasmus University for subsequent analyses.

To maximize the range of lung conditions sampled, an expert chest radiologist with >20 years of experience (J.D.G.) selected the 2 lung segments that qualitatively appeared to be the most damaged within each participant and the 3 lung segments with the least apparent damage at baseline. The lower lobes were excluded because lavage fluid return is often poor from lower lobes, and we were limited to 20 mL of lavage fluid per segment since 5 segments/person were sampled.

Quantitative measurements from CT scans were preformed 3 ways: (1) An expert (M.M.W.) applied the Brody scoring to sampled segments using segmentally delineated CT’s generated by Apollo software (VIDA Diagnostics). The Brody score consists of an aggregate of 5 measurements including bronchiectasis, peri-bronchial wall thickening, mucus plugging, parenchymal damage, and air trapping 83 . Each sampled segment was scored identically to how lobes are scored in the standard Brody scoring system for a maximum score of 36.5. Additionally, the standard lobar-based Brody scoring was completed to calculate the whole-lung score (maximum of 219). (2) We used Lung Print software (VIDA Diagnostics) disease probability measure on the segmentally delineated CT scans to measure the voxel-to-voxel difference in Hounsfield units between matched inspiratory and expiratory images to estimate the probability of air trapping 32 . (3) Furthermore, a fully automated Bronchus-Artery (BA) analysis was performed using LungQ® (Thirona, Nijmegen) 84 .

LungQ consists of two artificial intelligence-based algorithms. One segments the bronchial tree from the inspiratory CT scans and the second identifies adjacent arteries. Bronchial widening is defined as the ratio between bronchial outer diameter (Bout) and artery diameter (A). The ratio between bronchial wall thickness and A (Bwt/A) is used to calculate the severity of bronchial thickening. For both Bout/A and Bwt/A, all measurements are averaged within each bronchial branch, from segmental generation G1 to G6 and then the median value of Bout/A and Bwt/A are computed for G1–6. For the mucus plug analysis, the LungQ-MP algorithm detects, localizes, and counts obstructive mucus plugs, including the first plug in a path and the finger-in-glove pattern up to the first bifurcation. It provides the mucus plug count and location, allowing for reports on plug counts, affected segment scores, and volumes 85 .

Participants underwent bronchoscopy under anesthesia after a cuffed oral endotracheal tube (ETT) was placed utilizing established procedures standard at U. Washington (laryngoscopy after sedation and paralytics) and U. Iowa (bronchoscopic intubation using a separate bronchoscope). Segmental airway washings were obtained via sterile single use/disposable bronchoscopes (Ambu, Ballerup, Denmark). Lavage volumes were 20 mL of normal saline per segment and did not exceed 100 mL / person to reduce risk of lung edema. The 2 “most diseased” and 3 “least diseased” lung segments were sampled, based on qualitative review of pre-ETI CT scans, as described above.

Lavage fluid was homogenized with needle and syringe. One aliquot of homogenized fluid was serially diluted and plated on MacConkey agar (Difco) to quantify Pa. Aliquots were also processed for quantification of inflammatory markers, DNA extraction, and metabolites, as follows.

The aliquots used to measure inflammatory markers were processed according to the CF Therapeutics Development Network Coordinating Center standard operating procedure, as previously described 68 . Briefly, aliquots of BAL fluid were centrifuged at 250xg for 10 min at 4°C, and supernatants were then centrifuged at 4000xg for 20 min at 4°C before freezing. One aliquot of supernatant from the second spin were frozen directly at −80°C until analysis. This aliquot was analyzed for free neutrophil elastase (NE) activity [Sigma Diagnostics; St. Louis, MO] at the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation Therapeutics Center for Biochemical Markers at the University of Colorado. The lower limit of detection for this assay was 0.5 ug/mL free neutrophil elastase. Samples below the limit of detection were assigned a value of half the limit of detection (0.25 ug/mL). A second aliquot of the centrifuged supernatant was treated with protease inhibitor and frozen at −80°C until analysis. This aliquot was analyzed for 95 cytokines using Milliplex Human Cytokine/Chemokine assay Panels I, II, III, and IV (Millipore Sigma). Standards for linear curve determination included two control samples representing high and low levels for each target protein in the assay. Values below the limit of detection were imputed as half the minimum detectable value. Cytokines where >30% of the samples were below the limit of detection were excluded (n = 32 cytokines excluded).

DNA was extracted from 200 μL of lavage fluid using the Qiagen DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Qiacube Kit and eluted in 100 μL Solution C6 (10 mM Tris). The purified DNA sample was stored at −20°C. Negative controls (no sample added) were run with every assay. We measured bacterial density using 3 DNA-based droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) assays for: total bacterial load, Sa density, and Pa density using previously validated primers and probes (Table S11) 7 , 68 . ddPCR was performed using 1 μL of undiluted DNA (BioRad QX200 ddPCR system) using the ddPCR Multiplex Supermix (BioRad). PCR was performed following manufacturer’s recommendations for cycling conditions, with an annealing temperature of 56°C and extension time of 1 minute. Results were analyzed with QX software to determine target DNA concentrations. Positive controls (DNA isolated from bacterial cultures) and negative controls (no sample added) were run with each assay. Values below the limit of detection were imputed as half the limit of detection.

The V3-V5 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified from each sample with primers containing Illumina adapter sequences (Table S11). Triplicate PCRs were performed for each sample in a total 60 μL reaction volume containing 30 μL 1x KAPA HiFi HotStart Ready Mix (KAPA), 12 μL of each primer, and 6 μL of DNA template using the following reaction conditions: an initial incubation at 95°C for 3 minutes followed by 25 cycles of 95°C for 30 seconds, 55°C for 30 seconds, and then 72°C for 30 seconds. After PCR the triplicate amplicons for each sample were pooled. Pools were cleaned, indexed using 8bp sequences (Illumina and 86 ), and cleaned again as described in the 16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library Preparation guide (Illumina). Amplicon concentration was measured using the Qubit dsDNA assay kit (Life Technologies). Sequencing libraries for each sample were pooled in equal concentrations before sequencing for 600 cycles on an Illumina MiSeq desktop sequencer using the Miseq Reagent Kit v3. The sequencing reads for each sample were de-multiplexed using the MiSeq Control Software. De-multiplexed sequencing reads were assigned to amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using DADA2 denoising pipeline 87 (version 1.18). Taxa tables were filtered to remove genera present at <1% across samples. Calculated absolute abundance of genera were determined by multiplying the total bacterial abundance (determined by 16S rRNA gene ddPCR) by the relative abundances of each genera (determined by bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequencing), as previously described 7 , 34 . Chao1 richness and Shannon diversity were calculated using the vegan package in R 88 . Cumulative Pa and Sa load was calculated by adding the ddPCR-based number of Pa and Sa genome copies per mL, then dividing by the number of genome copies per mL (also based on ddPCR; calculated by dividing the number of 16S copies by 4). This proportion was then converted to percent.

We extracted DNA (Qiagen) from three isolates each of Pa and Sa from each person at pre- and post-ETI timepoints. We performed whole genome sequencing (Illumina) on the Pa isolates with targeted examination of MLST sequences as previously described 68 and traditional MLST typing (via PCR and sanger sequencing) for Sa. Where MLST loci were not a 100% match with currently available (as of May 29, 2025) MLST types in the PubMLST database 89 , we report them as ”no match”.

We collected up to 95 Pa isolates from each segment. Cryopreserved isolates were grown overnight in 96-well plates containing 600 μl of Psueomonas Broth medium (1 ml deep well plates, Nunc, Thermo Scientific). Cultures were then tested for ability to produce protease, produce pyoverdine, swim in 0.3% agar, grow in media without amino acids, and grow on agar containing tobramycin, ciprofloxacin, and aztreonam by using a pin replicator (V&P Scientific) to transfer overnight cultures to the test plates. Test plates were incubated for 24 hours at 37°C. Every isolate was scored for the presence or absence of each phenotype (i.e. growth vs. no growth or motile vs. non-motile on the appropriate test media). Tests were repeated three times for each isolate, and the mode of results was used.

One isolate from each participant at both timepoints was selected for hypermutation analysis. Isolates were grown overnight in Pseudomonas broth, normalized by OD, and serially diluted and plated on Pseudomonas agar containing 300 μg/ml rifampicin and Pseudomonas agar without antibiotics. Plate counts were performed to determine the frequency of spontaneous rifampicin resistant colonies. Three biological replicates were performed for each strain, and strains WT mPAO1 and ΔmutS mPAO1 were included as negative and positive controls, respectively.

Recovered BAL fluid was frozen directly at −80°C, without centrifugation. Metabolomic profiling was performed by Metabolon, Inc. (Durham, NC, USA) as previously described 90 using three independent platforms (ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometry for acidic and basic metabolites as well as gas chromatography/mass spectrometry). Metabolites were identified by automated comparison of ion features to a reference library. Units for metabolites are as follows: mM – sialic acid; μM – adenine, arginine, leucine, ornithine, phenylalanine, proline, tryptophan, and tyrosine; MS signal – glycine-leucine, lactate, leucine-proline, lysine-leucine, methionine, and uridine.

Spirometric measurements were obtained in accordance with the American Thoracic Society Standards 91 . The forced expiratory volume in one second percent predicted (FEV1pp) values are based on the GLI 2012 values for adults 92 . We also computed CF-specific percentiles using https://cfpercentiles.statup.solutions/, described 93 . Both survival corrected and uncorrected values are given in Table S4.

Pre-ETI sweat chloride values were obtained from participants’ medical records. Post-ETI sweat was collected with the Macroduct® collection system (Wescor, Logan UT), and sweat chloride levels were measured using routine standard laboratory techniques.

Five segmental disease conditions were included in the “overall” disease severity calculation: Pa culture density, segmental Brody score, sialic acid concentration (mucus marker), proline concentration (Pa nutrient), and neutrophil elastase. We ranked each segment by its cohort-wide severity for each of these 5 conditions, and then summed the ranks. This sum of the ranks was used to determine best, middle, and worst tertiles for “overall” disease severity.

For “overall” disease severity, segments were ranked cohort-wide for each condition and the sum of the ranks determined best, middle, and worst tertiles. All log-normally distributed data were log-transformed before statistical analyses were performed. Simple linear regressions were used to test for associations between quantitative parameters. Pre- vs post-ETI p-values were calculated with paired t-test, and 2-tailed p-values are reported. Pa-negative vs positive segment and participant p-values were calculated with unpaired t-test, and 2-tailed p-values are reported. Multiple comparisons corrections were calculated using the false discovery rate (FDR) with Q of 5% (using the two-stage linear step-up procedure of Benjamini, Krieger and Yekutieli) 94 . These statistical analyses were performed in Prism (Graphpad). Repeated measures correction was calculated with linear mixed effects (REML) model, as implemented in the lmerTest package in R 95 . Significance after correction was assessed with the Satterthwaite method. Intraclass correlation was calculated with the one-way, agreement, single score method 27 , as implemented in the psych package in R 96 .
