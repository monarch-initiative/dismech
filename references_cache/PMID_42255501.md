Psittacosis caused by Chlamydia psittaci has re-emerged in China as sporadic
cases and localized outbreaks. However, current knowledge remains fragmented
across the clinical, veterinary, epidemiological, and public health fields. This
scoping review mapped studies on psittacosis in China, identified major
knowledge gaps, and defined priorities for research, clinical management, and
prevention and control. Following the Arksey and O'Malley framework and
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for
Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI),
Wanfang, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase were searched for studies published
between 1 January 1985 and 31 December 2025 and synthesized eligible studies
with descriptive statistics and thematic analysis. A total of 424 studies were
included. Research interest showed recent sharp increases and was concentrated
in Eastern and Central China. Case reports and series dominated the literature,
whereas analytic epidemiology, standardized surveillance, and high-resolution
molecular studies remained limited. Reported cases were most often documented in
middle-aged and older adults with avian exposure, including pet birds and
poultry, and the reported occurrence showed a winter-spring pattern. Pneumonia
was the predominant clinical presentation, and severe cases could progress to
acute respiratory distress syndrome and multi-organ dysfunction. Metagenomic
next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was the most frequently reported diagnostic
method in recent studies, while PCR and serology remained important
complementary tools. Overall, the literature is growing rapidly, but remains
uneven in geographic coverage, study design, and integration across human,
animal, and environmental sectors. These findings support broader One Health
surveillance, stronger analytic and molecular epidemiology, and more
standardized approaches to diagnosis, source investigation, and prevention in
China.

Psittacosis is a zoonotic infection caused by the bacterium Chlamydia psittaci primarily acquired through inhaling aerosols contaminated with respiratory secretions or feces from infected birds [1]. Recognized globally since the late 19th century, its clinical manifestations range from asymptomatic infection to severe pneumonia with life-threatening systemic complications [2]. Despite its long history and global distribution, psittacosis remains underdiagnosed [3]. The non-specific nature of its clinical presentation, compounded by persistent limitations in diagnostic capacity, including reliance on technically demanding culture methods and serological assays with cross-reactivity, results in underreporting and an underestimated disease burden worldwide [4].

China has an extensive but fragmented evidence base for psittacosis, spanning clinical medicine, veterinary science, epidemiology, and public health. Etiologically, C. psittaci was first isolated from domesticated ornamental birds in 1985, with the first documented human case a decade later [5]. Although clinical reports have markedly increased recently, case identification remains largely opportunistic and dependent on individual hospital-based studies and case series, without unified active surveillance or a routine reporting mechanism. Inclusion of psittacosis on the national List of Zoonotic Infectious Diseases in 2022 indicates growing recognition of this issue [6]. However, China’s exposure landscape is distinct, featuring intensive poultry production systems, vast and active ornamental bird trade, and emerging evidence implicating non-psittacine birds, particularly pigeons, as significant sources of human infection, which diverges from the European experience [3,[7], [8], [9]]. Diagnostic accessibility remains a formidable barrier. The lack of routine molecular assays and ambiguous serological interpretation contribute to frequent misdiagnoses of atypical or community-acquired pneumonia [10]. The literature is dominated by descriptive retrospective analyses that, although valuable, collectively fail to delineate the full scope and dynamics of psittacosis in China.

We conducted a comprehensive scoping review of psittacosis research in China. This review aimed to map the temporal and geographic distribution of research activity and reported human cases; synthesize current evidence on epidemiology, clinical features, diagnosis, treatment, pathogen and laboratory research, and public health practice; and identify key methodological limitations and priority areas for future research and surveillance.

This scoping review followed Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and was reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [11,12]. Detailed methods and operational procedures are provided in the Supplementary Material (Sections S1–S6). We searched PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Data for studies conducted in China published between 1 January 1985 and 31 December 2025 in English or Chinese. Full search strategies are provided in Table S1. We manually searched the reference lists of included studies to identify additional eligible studies.

Eligibility was prespecified according to the Population, Concept, and Context framework (Supplementary Material, Section S1) [13]. We included studies conducted in China reporting human, animal, and/or environmental evidence relevant to psittacosis or C. psittaci, regardless of design. We excluded studies without full text, non-academic materials, studies addressing other Chlamydia species without clear differentiation, and duplicate studies.

Studies were managed in EndNote 21. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts, resolving disagreements by discussion and third-reviewer adjudication. De-duplication procedures (including bilingual/translated-metadata duplicates) are described in the Supplementary Material (Section S3). We screened for overlapping/redundant publications during the full-text review and identified no duplicates. We used descriptive statistics and thematic synthesis to summarize the evidence across four domains: epidemiology, etiological and laboratory research, clinical features, diagnosis and treatment, and public health strategies and surveillance. We identified key findings, recurring patterns, and areas of divergence within each domain. We integrated findings across domains to characterize the current evidence base, summarize diagnostic approaches, and identify major evidence gaps and research priorities. Analytical outputs were presented in structured tables and figures to support a clear and coherent synthesis of the available evidence. No meta-analysis or formal critical appraisal was performed. The rationale and approach are described in the Supplementary Material (Section S6).

The initial database search yielded 2558 studies pertaining to C. psittaci. After removing duplicates, 2109 studies remained. Following title and abstract screening, 1664 studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded. Full-text review was performed on the remaining 445 studies, and eight studies that did not address psittacosis, nine studies for which full text could not be obtained, two studies conducted outside China, and two popular science news pieces were excluded. The final analysis included 424 studies, comprising 330 published in Chinese and 94 published in English (Fig. 1).

The publications spanned from 1985 to 2025. The volume of relevant research remained low, characterized by sporadic publications up to 2019. A marked increase was observed from 2020 onwards (10 studies in 2020; 39 studies in 2021), with sustained high publication counts during 2022–2025 (80, 81, 76, and 75 studies annually, respectively) (Fig. 2A).

Studies reporting the administrative region of case origin demonstrated pronounced geographic disparity, with most studies concentrated in Eastern and Central China (Fig. 2B). Zhejiang Province contributed the highest number of publications (n = 57), followed by Jiangsu Province (n = 43), Guangdong Province (n = 32), Beijing (n = 31), and Hunan Province (n = 24), all of which showed relatively high concentrations of case-based studies. In contrast, geographically underrepresented regions (e.g., Qinghai Province, Ningxia Huizu Zizhiqu [also known as Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region], Heilongjiang Province) contributed only a few publications, predominantly isolated case reports or small case series (Fig. 2B). An additional 72 studies did not provide sufficient geographic information for regional attribution and were excluded from the regional distribution analysis.

Clinical case-based studies constituted the predominant research type, with case reports/series representing the largest category (n = 281). A substantial body of pathogen genomic research (n = 59), outbreak/epidemiological investigations (n = 33), observational studies (n = 23), and diagnostic methods studies (n = 21) was also identified. Synthesis of case report data indicated that human psittacosis infection can occur across age groups, with a preponderance observed among middle-aged and older adults (40–70 years). Several series reported a higher proportion of male cases. Occupational exposure was a major risk factor, with over 60% of case reports documenting a definitive history of avian contact. Affected occupations included poultry farming, slaughtering, and trading personnel, pet shop staff, veterinarians, and pigeon fanciers. A considerable proportion of cases resided in urban settings, with exposure histories frequently linked to keeping pet birds (e.g., parrots, finches) or proximity to live poultry markets.

Psittacosis incidence exhibited a distinct seasonal pattern, with higher detection rates during winter and spring (November–April) and a peak between December and February [14]. Higher occurrence was reported during winter and spring, although no climatic or ecological factors that might explain this pattern were formally assessed. However, notable regional variations were observed. Surveillance data and epidemiological investigations from Guangzhou City (January 2021–June 2024) identified case peaks in December and March, predominantly between winter and spring [15]. Northern, northwestern, and Qinghai–Xizang regions reported higher incidence during spring and summer [16].

Reported human cases and case-based evidence were concentrated in Southeastern China. At the provincial level, Zhejiang, Guangdong, and Hunan provinces contributed the highest cumulative cases (1216, 880, and 512, respectively). Reported cases have substantially increased since 2023, with broader molecular diagnostic use and greater clinical recognition in multiple settings.

Although C. psittaci transmission predominantly manifests as geographically dispersed sporadic cases, clustered outbreaks occur. The three primary contexts are household clusters, occupational exposure-related clusters, and common-source exposure clusters at specific sites. An occupational cluster was documented among poultry processing workers in Shandong Province, with symptomatic cases emerging in a single facility. C. psittaci was detected in all seven patient samples, with confirmatory PCR in a subset. Epidemiological investigation implicated processed poultry as the likely common source [17]. Household clusters include multiple reports involving families exposed to pet birds or shared environmental sources. In a five-case household cluster in Northwest China, a newly purchased ill parrot provided a critical epidemiological clue [18]. Another household case series, documented during the COVID-19 pandemic, suggested that prolonged cohabitation and increased shared exposure opportunities may facilitate household clustering [19]. Common-source clusters have been associated with high-risk exposure sites. In the Shenzhen area, a case cluster was linked to exposure at a bird and flower market [20].

C. psittaci infection in China primarily stems from avian species, with parrots and other pet or ornamental birds recurrently implicated as important reservoirs or exposure sources [21]. In addition to psittacine birds, pigeons and domestic poultry, particularly chickens, ducks, and geese, are frequently reported as potential sources of human infection. Some reports grouped avian exposure into broader categories, such as backyard birds, poultry, wild birds, or unspecified bird contact [7,8,22].

Most cases reported bird or poultry-related exposure prior to disease onset [15,[23], [24], [25]]. Common exposure scenarios included keeping or purchasing ornamental birds, sustained exposure at bird and flower markets or pet shops, occupational contact during poultry farming and slaughtering/processing, and activities such as cleaning cages, handling avian excreta, or contact with sick or deceased birds or their secretions [[26], [27], [28]]. Pathogens may contaminate the environment via avian feces, respiratory secretions, and feathers, generating pathogen-laden dust or aerosols that are subsequently inhaled, leading to human infection [29]. Several outbreak investigations detected positive signs of C. psittaci in avian feces, sewage, and environmental surface samples collected from patients’ residences or trading sites. Contact tracing generally failed to identify secondary cases. However, a recent outbreak documented secondary and tertiary transmission chains within a larger cluster event [27].

Comparative evidence between occupational and non-occupational high-contact groups was limited and mostly from separate studies with different outcome metrics. Poultry farm/zoo workers showed quantitative PCR (qPCR) positivity of 3.82% (6/157) and indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) seropositivity of 42.59% (46/108) [30], whereas racing pigeon fanciers in Beijing showed antibody seropositivity of 24.10% (19/79) and throat-swab antigen positivity of 23.88% (16/67) [7].

Urban exposure settings were frequent. In a Guangdong case series (n = 162), among 108 cases of documented avian exposure, the most frequently reported sources were food (38.89%) and bird (33.33%) markets. Commonly reported exposure-related activities included breeding (51.23%), farming (29.63%), and coop cleaning (25.92%) [23]. Environmental contamination was documented in a live poultry wholesale market in Changsha, where C. psittaci was detected in sewage (12.50%), cage swabs (6.59%), and air samples (2.29%), supporting the plausibility of aerosol/dust exposure in densely trafficked market environments [29]. A case–control study in Hangzhou identified bird habitats within 500 m of residence (odds ratio [OR] = 3.81, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.19–6.61) and keeping parrots (OR = 2.95, 95% CI: 1.10–7.89) or poultry (OR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.02–4.53) as independent risk factors [31], underscoring peri-domestic and urban interfaces beyond traditional agricultural workplaces.

Occupational risk groups for C. psittaci exposure are predominantly concentrated among poultry farming, slaughtering, and processing workers; bird and flower market/pet bird trade operators and employees (including retail staff and exhibitors); and farmers or occupationally active individuals in peri-urban or rural settings with intensive pigeon and poultry contact. Non-occupational risk is primarily represented by household pet bird keepers and their cohabiting family members [28,31]. C. psittaci is an obligate intracellular bacterium with a ∼1.17 Mb genome (GC content ∼39%) encoding ∼1026 genes, and a cell envelope dominated by lipopolysaccharide plus the major outer membrane protein (MOMP) and other cysteine-rich proteins [34]. A biphasic developmental cycle alternating between the infectious elementary body (EB) and replicative reticulate body (RB) defines its pathobiology. Under stressors such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ) exposure, β-lactam pressure, nutrient limitation, heat shock, or co-infections, C. psittaci can enter a viable but non-cultivable persistent state that may contribute to prolonged carriage and later recrudescence [[35], [36], [37]]. Classic in vitro work demonstrated long-term maintenance of a non-infectious yet viable state under stress (e.g., penicillin/antiserum), followed by reactivation and renewed EB production after stress removal [38].

C. psittaci shows substantial intra-species genetic diversity linked to host preference, making strain typing central to molecular epidemiology for source tracing and reservoir attribution [39]. Early phenotypic serotyping using MOMP-targeting monoclonal antibodies classified avian strains into serovars A–F [40]. Expanded microimmunofluorescence panels distinguished up to 10 serovars, but reliance on cultured isolates and specialized workflows limited scalability [41]. Molecular approaches became dominant, and ompA-based genotyping was widely adopted because ompA encodes MOMP and contains variable domains amenable to discriminatory PCR-restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR-RFLP) or sequencing [42]. Nine classical genotypes (A–F, E/B, M56, WC) are widely recognized and show reproducible (non-exclusive) host associations (e.g., A with psittacines, B with pigeons, C with ducks/geese, D with turkeys; E/E-related groups are reported across a broader host range) [43]. Subsequent ompA surveys expanded the framework to 16 genotypes by adding G, 6 N, Mat116, YP84, R54, 1 V, and CPX0308 [42]. In China, published ompA data most consistently reported A, B, and E/B across poultry- and pigeon-related settings, with CPX0308 additionally documented in peacocks [7,29,30,44].

To improve resolution beyond a single locus, multilocus sequence typing (MLST) has been adopted, most commonly the 7-locus scheme (enoA, fumC, gatA, gidA, hemN, hlfX, and oppA) that assigns standardized sequence types using housekeeping genes, enabling finer discrimination and more robust population-structure and outbreak interpretation, including patterns consistent with host association and jumps [45,46]. At the current frontier, genome-scale typing further increases the resolution through culture-independent genome recovery and genome-wide comparisons. Probe-based/culture-independent whole genome sequencing (WGS) can generate C. psittaci genomes directly from host material, while single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based analyses enable high-resolution clustering for outbreak investigations (including near-identical genomes separated by only a few SNPs in multistate zoonotic outbreaks) [47]. C. psittaci infection has a typical 5–14 day incubation period in humans [49]. C. psittaci pneumonia usually presents with acute onset and non-specific manifestations, ranging from mild influenza-like illness to rapidly progressive severe pneumonia with multi-organ involvement. Fever is the most common symptom, often accompanied by chills, fatigue, myalgia, and headache. Respiratory manifestations mainly include cough, dyspnea, tachypnoea, and chest tightness with occasional pleuritic pain or hemoptysis. Some patients develop extrapulmonary manifestations, particularly gastrointestinal symptoms and neurocognitive disturbances, such as delirium. Multi-system involvement may include liver dysfunction, hypoalbuminemia, muscle injury, renal impairment, and acute myocardial injury.

Laboratory findings commonly show normal or mildly elevated leukocyte counts with neutrophilia and lymphopenia, together with markedly elevated inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), whereas procalcitonin (PCT) is variable. Imaging typically demonstrates lobar or segmental consolidation, sometimes with ground-glass opacities, most often involving the lower lobes, and frequently accompanied by air bronchograms. A subset of cases shows pleural effusion. Severe disease may progress to respiratory failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and sepsis or septic shock [50]. Worse clinical trajectories have been associated with underlying comorbidities, particularly chronic cardiovascular disease, as well as dyspnea, neuropsychiatric symptoms, gastrointestinal symptoms, and multilobar infiltrates at presentation, although proposed prognostic indicators remain heterogeneous and should be interpreted cautiously [51].

Current laboratory diagnostic modalities for C. psittaci include culture, serology, nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT), and high-throughput sequencing, which differ in evidential strength, turnaround time, and clinical applicability. Culture provides direct evidence of viable organisms and supports downstream genotyping, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and mechanistic studies, but is labor-intensive, has limited clinical sensitivity, and requires biosafety level 2 containment under China’s Catalogue of Pathogenic Microorganisms Transmissible between Humans, largely restricting it to research settings [52]. Serological assays, including ELISA, complement fixation, immunofluorescence, and immunoperoxidase tests, are relatively simple and useful for retrospective confirmation and seroepidemiological surveys. However, cross-reactivity with other Chlamydia species limits their specificity, and interpretation generally requires paired acute- and convalescent-phase sera in conjunction with clinical findings [53]. NAATs, including PCR, nested PCR, qPCR, and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), provide more sensitive and specific detection of C. psittaci nucleic acids and are preferred for case confirmation and epidemiological investigation, but cannot distinguish viable from non-viable organisms [54,55].

Among sequencing-based approaches, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is the most frequently used diagnostic modality, particularly for respiratory specimens such as sputum, airway secretions, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and has served as the principal confirmatory tool in hospital-based case reports and case series. Its main strengths include unbiased pathogen detection and relatively rapid reporting, with identification possible within 24 h in some studies. However, high host-background reads and substantial costs may limit routine implementation, particularly in resource-constrained settings [56,57]. Targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) can better detect low-abundance targets using multiplex PCR or probe hybridization to enrich specific pathogen sequences, although panel design restricts its detectable range [58]. Comparative studies of respiratory pathogen detection have demonstrated comparable overall accuracy between tNGS and mNGS, despite trade-offs between positive and negative percent agreement [59].

Only a small subset of studies provided comparative evidence on diagnostic yield and timeliness. In a Zhejiang study of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) mNGS for suspected pneumonia in immunocompromised patients, mNGS returned results within 24–48 h and showed a sensitivity/specificity of 80%/73%, whereas conventional microbiological tests required (119 ± 87) h, detected fewer pathogens (42 vs. 76), and showed a sensitivity/specificity of 36%/82% [60]. In a Beijing series of 14 patients with C. psittaci pneumonia confirmed by mNGS, the mean reporting time was 38 h (range: 24–62 h) for mNGS, compared with 56 h (range: 34–111 h) for IgM serology, whereas routine culture yielded no positive results and required 64 h (range: 50.5–75.0 h) [61].

Overall, mNGS was the most frequently reported diagnostic approach in recent hospital-based studies, whereas PCR, serology, and culture were used less frequently and mainly as complementary or retrospective tools.

C. psittaci is an obligate intracellular bacterium intrinsically resistant to β-lactams. Thus, many patients initially received empirical β-lactam- or carbapenem-based regimens before pathogen identification, usually following treatment principles for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) or severe community-acquired pneumonia (SCAP). The 2019 ATS/IDSA guidelines define SCAP as CAP with one major criterion (i.e. invasive mechanical ventilation or septic shock requiring vasopressors) or at least three minor criteria [62]. β-Lactam plus macrolide or β-lactam plus respiratory fluoroquinolone combinations were the most common empirical regimens in clinical practice. After etiological confirmation, most cases were switched to or supplemented with anti-chlamydial therapy. Tetracyclines were the first-line agents for adults, with treatment courses generally lasting 10–14 days and extended when clinically indicated in severe, prolonged, or immunocompromised cases. Macrolides, including azithromycin and erythromycin, were used as alternatives for patients with contraindications to tetracyclines, such as pregnant women or children under eight years of age [49]. Combination regimens, particularly tetracycline plus respiratory fluoroquinolone, were also frequently reported, reflecting concerns regarding severe disease, possible co-infection, and variable accessibility of doxycycline in some Chinese healthcare settings. However, evidence supporting fluoroquinolone efficacy in psittacosis is limited and mainly from experimental and retrospective studies [63].

Within-study comparisons reported generally favorable outcomes after timely targeted therapy, although regimen-specific differences were inconsistently reported. In a multicenter cohort of severe psittacosis CAP with acute hypoxic respiratory failure, intensive care unit mortality was 8.9%, with no significant differences across three treatment strategies involving continued fluoroquinolone use, fluoroquinolone-to-tetracycline switch, or fluoroquinolone-tetracycline combination therapy [64]. In another severe series of 13 cases, 11 patients recovered and 2 died from secondary multidrug-resistant infection. Several patients who failed to respond to initial fluoroquinolone therapy improved after switching to tetracycline-class agents [65].

Critically ill patients also required oxygen therapy and escalating respiratory support, including non-invasive or invasive mechanical ventilation for ARDS and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) as rescue therapy for refractory hypoxemia [66]. Systemic corticosteroids were used in selected severe ARDS cases, together with nutritional support and comprehensive intensive care [67,68]. Case fatality is generally low with standardized antimicrobial treatment. However, delayed targeted therapy may be associated with substantially increased mortality [69].

Available evidence indicates that human C. psittaci infection is mainly acquired through animal exposure, whereas human-to-human transmission is rare. Accordingly, strict isolation measures equivalent to those used for highly transmissible pathogens are not routinely required. The Center for Disease Control and Prevention does not recommend routine patient isolation or antimicrobial prophylaxis for close contacts [49]. However, patients often present with pneumonia or severe pneumonia, may have cough, and may undergo aerosol-generating procedures, and limited human-to-human transmission has been reported under specific circumstances. Thus, World Health Organization advises droplet precautions in addition to standard precautions for hospitalized patients in recent European outbreak notifications [3].

In emergency departments, fever clinics, and admission assessments, the available literature and guidance suggest that C. psittaci warrants consideration in the differential diagnosis of CAP/SCAP when recent bird-, poultry-, or pigeon-related exposure is present, suggestive imaging with negative conventional cultures or rapid progression, or planned bronchoscopy, suctioning, or other procedures with splatter or aerosol risk. Reported infection-control recommendations generally favored single-room placement when feasible, otherwise emphasizing adequate bed spacing, ventilation, respiratory hygiene, and droplet precautions in addition to standard precautions. Environmental management should focus on high-touch surfaces and procedure areas, with routine and terminal cleaning/disinfection of bedrails, bedside tables, respiratory equipment surfaces, and workstations. Visible contamination should be removed before disinfection, and medical waste managed according to routine healthcare-associated infection and regulations without special handling requirements.

Infected birds, including asymptomatic carriers, can persistently shed C. psittaci, contaminating environmental matrices such as cages, fecal matter, wastewater, and feed, thereby posing exposure risks in household settings and bird and flower or agricultural wholesale markets. Synthesized evidence suggests that reported source-control practices generally focus on rapid source tracing, isolation and disposition, environmental remediation, retesting, and sustained surveillance. Following case confirmation and epidemiological linkage to an exposure site, reported control measures commonly include isolation management of implicated birds and their cohort. Where necessary, humane euthanasia or standardized tetracycline-based therapeutic regimens (typically a 45-day course for pet birds) may be implemented, with laboratory-confirmed negativity as the prerequisite for release from isolation and re-entry into commerce [70]. Affected pet shops or trading stalls may be subject to temporary closure, stock, or removal of birds, followed by thoroughly cleaning and disinfecting cages, floors, feces, and wastewater, and sequential rounds of nucleic acid testing of avian and environmental samples until achieving consecutive negative results, mitigating the risk of ongoing exposure [71]. Reports commonly linked longer-term prevention with routine quarantine inspection of traded birds, market access regulation, daily sanitation management, and multisectoral coordination involving disease control, agriculture and rural affairs, animal husbandry, forestry, and market regulatory authorities.

Populations at heightened risk for psittacosis exposure are concentrated among bird and flower market or pet shop workers and customers, poultry farmers, and agricultural workers with frequent bird contact. Collectively, available reports suggest that public health responses have focused on exposure reduction, early recognition, and rapid response. Core measures include intensified cleaning and disinfection of cages and coops, safe feces and wastewater disposal, consistent glove and mask use during bird handling and cleaning, and targeted health education to improve recognition of key symptoms (e.g., fever, cough, dyspnea) and promote timely healthcare seeking. At the event level, regional reports commonly described active case finding, contact tracing, health monitoring, and animal and environmental specimen collection, including avian swabs, cage-surface samples, feed, and wastewater, as key components of source identification and transmission risk assessment. Common responses to suspected household- or occupationally related clusters included prompt epidemiological investigation, risk communication, and remediation of implicated premises, including temporary closures where necessary.

At the systems level, psittacosis has not been uniformly incorporated into China’s national notifiable disease surveillance framework. Therefore, early warning relies on complementary mechanisms, including integrated reporting, sentinel surveillance, unexplained pneumonia surveillance, and cluster-outbreak investigation. Strengthening routine enquiries into bird-keeping and bird-contact history through hospital-based surveillance and clinician training may improve case detection. Effective prevention also requires cross-sectoral coordination among health, agriculture and rural affairs, market regulation, forestry, and quarantine authorities to regulate bird trade, reinforce quarantine supervision, and improve environmental management. Local practices have already emerged. Hangzhou has mandated reporting of human psittacosis cases since 2021, Jiangsu applies Category C management, and healthcare institutions in Guangzhou must report confirmed cases to the Chinese Infectious Disease Surveillance and Control Project [15].

The primary public health value of C. psittaci vaccination lies in reducing infection and bacterial shedding in avian and other animal reservoirs, thereby interrupting zoonotic transmission at its source. C. psittaci is an obligate intracellular pathogen. Therefore, protective immunity requires humoral responses and, critically, T helper 1 (Th1)/IFN-γ-driven cellular immunity capable of limiting intracellular replication and persistent shedding. Animal chlamydial vaccines have evolved from inactivated and live attenuated whole-organism approaches to subunit, live-vector, DNA, and more recently, mRNA platforms [72]. Early work focused mainly on whole-pathogen preparations; however, stringent biosafety requirements, modest protection, and safety concerns constrained their application [73,74]. Antigen development subsequently expanded from elementary bodies and crude outer membrane preparations to recombinant proteins, synthetic peptides, expression vectors, and naked DNA. Among these, MOMP remains the leading candidate because of its surface exposure, strong immunogenicity, and consistent association with protective efficacy. A systematic review of 220 chlamydial vaccine trials identified MOMP as one of the most strongly validated alternatives to whole-cell antigens, while adjuvantation and antigen-combination strategies increased partial protection from 58% to 82% and complete protection from 0% to 6% [74].

Current vaccine research has shifted towards recombinant, subunit, DNA, and mRNA platforms for poultry and pet birds. In China, the first recombinant C. psittaci vaccine for broiler chickens was commercialized in 2006, followed by approval of a recombinant vaccine for sheep and goats in 2014. However, limited protective efficacy has restricted wider use. Other candidate antigens have been explored, including polymorphic membrane proteins (Pmps) [75]. Multivalent PmpG formulations combined with MOMP have been reported to induce humoral and cellular immune responses and protect against respiratory and reproductive tract challenges. mRNA vaccines represent a promising emerging platform because of their flexibility and immunogenicity. In budgerigars, C. psittaci mRNA vaccines elicited high-titer serum antibodies and partial protection, with α-galactosylceramide-containing lipid nanoparticle formulations outperforming conventional lipid nanoparticle preparations [76]. Across the mapped evidence, gaps clustered into four broad domains and mainly reflected limitations in study design, reporting completeness, and integration across human, animal, and environmental sectors, rather than a complete lack of research activity. Descriptive and case-based studies dominated the epidemiology literature, with limited denominator data for incidence estimation, inconsistent exposure intensity measurement, and insufficient evidence to evaluate person-to-person transmission. In pathogen and laboratory research, genotyping relied largely on ompA, with limited use of MLST and WGS. Sampling was uneven across host species, geographic regions, and specimen types, and culture-based isolates and phenotypic validation were infrequently reported. In clinical diagnosis and treatment, the available literature was weighted towards hospitalized and severe cases, and reporting of diagnostic workflows, mNGS interpretation, treatment regimens, and clinical outcomes was heterogeneous, restricting comparability across studies. This scoping review synthesized 424 eligible studies and found that psittacosis research in China has expanded substantially in recent years. The available evidence indicates increasing attention to the epidemiology, pathogen characterization, clinical diagnosis, and public health management of psittacosis; however, it also reveals an uneven evidence base. Most studies remain descriptive and case-based, while higher-resolution molecular epidemiology, comparative clinical evidence, and standardized surveillance data are limited. Collectively, these findings suggest that, despite increased research activity, the literature remains more informative for identifying patterns and gaps than for supporting robust epidemiological inference or evidence-based intervention planning.

Recently, research interest has increased substantially, alongside broader use of mNGS in Chinese hospitals and growing recognition of psittacosis as an underappreciated zoonosis. Nevertheless, case reports and series dominate the available evidence, whereas analytic designs capable of quantifying exposure–risk relationships, strengthening transmission inference, and informing intervention design remain scarce. A matched case–control study from Hangzhou provided an important example of how peri-domestic avian exposures can be quantified in the Chinese context [31], and international outbreak investigations illustrate the value of case–control and cohort approaches for identifying high-risk exposures and informing targeted control [77,78]. These observations suggest that an important next step for research in China is moving beyond descriptive case accumulation towards better-designed analytic studies that integrate standardized exposure assessment with animal, environmental, and molecular data.

Reported cases and research activity were concentrated in eastern and central China, whereas western and remote regions were less represented. Furthermore, a considerable proportion of publications did not provide precise geographic attribution. These patterns may reflect differences in diagnostic access, surveillance capacity, and reporting practice, rather than true differences in disease occurrence alone, and should be interpreted cautiously. A similar caution applies to temporal patterns. Although winter and spring predominance was frequently reported at the national level, with some regional variation, studies rarely examined the meteorological, ecological, or behavioral factors that might explain these observations. The recent increase in reported cases is more appropriately interpreted in the context of improved molecular diagnostics, greater clinical awareness, and changing ascertainment conditions, rather than as evidence of a single epidemiological shift. Overall, these findings highlight the need for broader and more standardized surveillance to better distinguish true epidemiological variation from differences in detection and reporting.

Across reported cases, psittacosis was more frequently documented in middle-aged and older adults, and avian exposure histories were commonly reported [79,80]. These findings are consistent with psittacosis being a predominantly avian-interface zoonosis. However, the exposure profile observed in China appears broader than traditional farming-related risk alone. Repeated reports involving pet-bird keeping, bird-and-flower markets, pet-bird shops, and other peri-domestic settings indicate an emerging dual exposure pattern in which urban consumer-chain exposures coexist with classical occupational risks. This broader exposure profile is epidemiologically important because it expands the range of potentially affected populations and indicates that prevention efforts should address occupational settings and urban and household bird-contact environments.

Etiological evidence indicates a gap between pathogen detection and epidemiological interpretation. Chinese studies frequently implicate ornamental birds, pigeons, and domestic poultry, although the observed host distribution likely reflects local exposure patterns and uneven sampling across species and settings. At the molecular level, ompA-based genotyping provides an initial description of dominant strains. However, continued reliance on single-locus typing limits stronger inference on transmission linkage, host association, and source attribution. Broader application of MLST and genome-scale approaches, particularly combined with standardized host, geographic, and outbreak metadata, would improve interpretation of whether observed genotype patterns reflect underlying ecological structure, repeated introductions, or sampling bias.

Available evidence is broadly consistent with the current understanding of psittacosis transmission. Avian infection, environmental contamination, and human acquisition through inhalation of contaminated aerosols or dust remain the dominant pathways. Although human-to-human transmission appears rare, a recently reported large cluster with possible secondary and tertiary transmission should be cautiously interpreted. Distinguishing true person-to-person spread from shared-source exposure requires stronger genomic and spatiotemporal evidence than most reports provide [27].

Hospitalized and relatively severe cases dominate the literature, likely skewing the reported clinical spectrum towards febrile pneumonia with rapid systemic progression. Diagnostic practice has increasingly shifted towards mNGS, improving etiological recognition. However, test indications, result interpretation, reporting standards, and affordability demonstrate substantial heterogeneity. These features support consideration of tiered diagnostic pathways, in which targeted PCR may be used preferentially for epidemiologically suggestive cases, whereas mNGS may be reserved for severe, atypical, or outbreak-associated presentations. Tetracyclines, particularly doxycycline, remain the main treatment option, although comparative evidence across therapeutic strategies remains limited.

Public health measures remain largely reactive and outbreak-driven, with uneven implementation of source control, market management, environmental monitoring, and cross-sector coordination. Although vaccination of reservoir hosts remains a potentially valuable strategy, evidence supporting C.psittaci vaccination is still limited [81].

The literature indicates six broad priorities for future research in China. First, stronger epidemiological infrastructure is required, including consideration of nationally notifiable status for human psittacosis, integrated sentinel surveillance with standardized protocols, and multicenter cohort studies to quantify exposure–risk relationships. Second, molecular epidemiology should be strengthened through a national C. psittaci isolate biobank, wider use of MLST and whole-genome sequencing in outbreak-associated and sporadic cases, and systematic surveys of understudied hosts and regions. Third, diagnostic practice would benefit from validated tiered algorithms that combine targeted PCR with more selective mNGS use, combined with cost-effectiveness evaluations across healthcare settings and national external quality assessment schemes. Fourth, the treatment evidence base remains limited and would benefit from pragmatic comparative effectiveness studies, better safety and pharmacokinetic data for special populations, and clearer evidence of treatment duration in severe or immunocompromised patients. Fifth, prevention at the human–animal interface should be strengthened through scalable interventions such as targeted education, low-cost decontamination measures, market hygiene certification, integration of C. psittaci screening into animal health programs, and renewed veterinary vaccine research with field-effectiveness and cost–benefit evaluations. Sixth, more formalized One Health coordination is required, including joint outbreak investigation and environmental management guidance and interoperable data-sharing between human and animal health surveillance systems.

Although this review focuses on China, the identified patterns are also relevant to other settings with frequent avian contact and active bird trade. In such contexts, changes in diagnostic capacity can substantially influence case ascertainment, and surveillance may need to extend beyond farms to include live-bird markets, pet-bird commerce, and peri-domestic environments. Recent increases in reported psittacosis cases in parts of Europe highlight the broader importance of strengthened surveillance and cross-sector coordination [3]. These findings support One Health approaches that link human, animal, and environmental surveillance for earlier detection and more targeted control [82].

This review had some limitations. First, restricting the sample to English and Chinese publications may have omitted a few relevant studies in other languages, although this is unlikely to have materially affected the overall comprehensiveness of evidence from China. Second, the included studies were highly heterogeneous in design and reporting quality, with descriptive studies predominating, which precluded quantitative synthesis and limited direct comparisons across studies. Third, this review relied on published literature and could not overcome broader problems of under-detection and under-reporting without centralized and standardized surveillance. Finally, consistent with the scoping review approach, we did not undertake formal critical appraisal or make certainty judgments regarding individual studies. Therefore, the identified evidence gaps and priorities should be interpreted as reflecting the characteristics and completeness of the available literature rather than the true epidemiological burden of psittacosis or comparative effectiveness of specific interventions.

In China, psittacosis remains under-characterized despite increases in published studies. The literature has expanded rapidly but remains dominated by descriptive case-based evidence and uneven in geographic coverage, study design, and integration across human, animal, and environmental sectors. Therefore, current studies are more informative for identifying major patterns and knowledge gaps than for defining disease burden, transmission dynamics, or prevention and control strategy effectiveness. Future progress will require broader One Health surveillance, stronger analytic and molecular epidemiology, more standardized diagnostic approaches, and better coordination between clinical, veterinary, and public health systems.
