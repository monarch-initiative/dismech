Congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) is among the most common 
congenital lung disorders. Possible findings are foci of mucinous cell clusters 
(MCCs), harboring KRAS mutations. Studies combining morphology, 
immunohistochemistry (IHC), and molecular data are scarce. A total of. 46 CPAM 
lesions were assessed by conventional histomorphology. IHC was performed to 
analyze lung-specific markers, transcription factors, and epithelial markers 
reflecting the structural composition of the bronchial tree and alveolar system. 
Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was conducted to identify mutations and 
fusions. CPAM type 1 was found in 50%, type 2 in 22%, and type 3 in 6%. A mixed 
pattern of types 1 and 2 was observed in 22%. The epithelial lining was strongly 
positive for CK7 and TTF-1 in all samples. Expression for Napsin A, Surfactant 
A, p40, CK5/6, and CK20 varied within and between subtypes. P40 and CK5/6 were 
more enriched in type 1 compared to type 2. MCCs occurred in 17%. Mutations were 
found in 24% (9 × KRAS; 2 × FGFR2). Besides classical KRAS mutations (G12D, 
G12V), two cases showed a double-mutation pattern (G12D/G12V; G12D/TP53). In all 
MCC cases, the same mutation with comparable allele frequencies was found in 
mucinous and non-mucinous areas. No fusions were detected. The presence of 
mixed-type patterns supports the hypothesis that CPAM represents a continuum of 
developmental disturbances occurring at various stages of lung branching 
morphogenesis. This is further corroborated by the divergent protein expression 
patterns. Our study confirms recent findings that the same KRAS mutations occur 
in mucinous and non-mucinous areas. The identification of additional mutations, 
including potential co-mutations, underscores the need for further investigation 
into molecularly defined CPAM subtypes.

Introduction
Congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM), 
formerly known as congenital cystic adenomatoid malfor -
mation (CCAM), is a cystic hamartomatous lesion of the 
lung [1, 2]. With an estimated incidence ranging from 1 in 
7200 to 1 in 35,000 live births [3 , 4], it is among the most 
common congenital pulmonary anomalies [2 ]. The Stocker 
classification (types 0–4) categorizes CPAMs based on 
macroscopic appearance and histopathological features [5 , 
6]. Types 0 and 4 are considered distinct entities; notably, 
type 4 is discussed as the cystic variant of pleuropulmonary 
blastoma [6, 7]. Types 1 to 3 are thought to originate from 
developmental disturbances during different stages of lung 
branching morphogenesis, particularly the pseudoglandular

Mucinous cell clusters (MCCs) have been identified in up 
to 83% of CPAM type 1 cases [9 ], but are less frequently 
observed in other subtypes [10, 11]. These clusters commonly 
harbor KRAS mutations [10, 12–15], raising concerns about 
their potential role as precursors to malignancy [10, 12, 15, 
16]. Several case reports have documented progression from 
MCCs to mucinous adenocarcinoma [4, 13, 17–21]. In some 
cases, diagnosis of carcinoma has been based primarily on the 
presence of a KRAS mutation [19, 21], prompting the ques-
tion of whether similar genetic alterations might also be pre-
sent in the adjacent non-mucinous cystic epithelium. Recent 
molecular studies have compared MCCs with neighboring 
non-mucinous cystic epithelia. While Fakler et al. found no 
KRAS-mutations outside of MCCs [10], other studies—such 
as a case report by Koopman et al. [22] and larger analyses by 
Hermelijn et al. [12] and Nelson et al. [16]—identified iden-
tical KRAS mutations in both mucinous and non-mucinous 
regions. These findings highlight ongoing controversy regard-
ing the molecular landscape of CPAM. Moreover, comprehen-
sive immunohistochemical analyses of CPAMs—including 
comparisons of lung-specific markers, transcription factors, 
and epithelial markers reflecting the composition of the bron-
chial tree and alveolar system—are scarce. To address these 
gaps, we conducted a comparative analysis of 46 CPAM cases 
at the morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular 
levels.
Material and methods
Patients and samples
We analyzed formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tis-
sue from 46 CPAM lesions obtained from 44 patients across 
three centers (Leipzig: 27 cases; Munich: 12 cases; Berlin: 
7 cases). The median age at the time of surgery was 6.8 
months (range: 3 days to 40 years). Surgical intervention 
consisted of at least a lobectomy, performed between 2003 
and 2024. Further details are provided in Supplementary 
Table 1.
Conventional morphology
Where available, cyst dimensions were extracted from 
pathological reports, adhering to the established size cri-
teria (> 2 cm for type 1; ≤  2 cm for type 2). CPAM clas -
sification into types 1, 2, and 3 was based on the histopatho-
logical criteria originally described by Stocker [5 , 6], with 
refinements from more recent literature [9, 23]. Type 1 was 
referred to as “wavy,” type 2 as “punched-out,” and type 3 
as “microcystic” pattern. For a detailed histomorphological 
description and definition of these types—which may also 
serve as a basis for routine diagnostic practice—please refer 
to Table 1, based on the most recent literature.
Mucinous cell clusters (MCCs) were measured in mil-
limeters, and their distribution/localization was documented 
as further outlined in Table 1.
The histopathological evaluation included a total of 349 
slides (mean: 7.6 slides per lesion; range: 1–36), all stained 
with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E). Additionally, peri-
odic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was performed on 98 slides 
(mean: 2.1 slides per lesion; range: 1–10).
Immunohistochemistry
For immunohistochemical analysis (IHC), one representative 
FFPE block per CPAM lesion was selected, displaying the 
most prominent diagnostic features—including large cysts, 
characteristic epithelial patterns, and mucinous cell clusters 
(if present)—as summarized in Table  1. Staining encom-
passed lung-specific markers (Napsin A and Surfactant A), 
transcription factors (TTF-1, CDX2, and SATB2), and epi-
thelial markers reflecting the composition of the bronchial 
tree and alveolar system (p40, CK5/6, CK7, and CK20).
Evaluation was scored as follows: negative (−): total 
absence of positively stained cells (0%); partial positive (+): 
incomplete, discontinuous staining of lower intensity in up 
to 40% of the epithelial cells; strong positive (+ +): pre-
dominantly continuous, intense staining in more than 40% of 
the epithelial cells. Staining was performed on the Ventana 
BenchMark Ultra (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to 
the manufacturer’s instructions (for further information, see 
Supplementary Table 2).
Nucleic acid isolation and next‑generation 
sequencing (NGS)
All 46 different CPAM types were separately analyzed by 
NGS on the DNA and RNA level. Guided by pre-evaluated 
H&E-stained reference slides, manual macrodissection was 
performed on paraffin-embedded tissue sections using a 
scalpel to selectively isolate mucinous and non-mucinous 
epithelial regions prior to DNA and RNA extraction. Special 
care was taken to prevent cross-contamination between the 
two compartments. Macrodissection was carried out on 46 
CPAM cysts without mucinous cell clusters (MCCs) and 
on all 8 distinct CPAM cases with MCCs, resulting in a 
total of 54 separately processed samples for downstream 
NGS analysis on the DNA and RNA level. For targeted 
DNA sequencing, a custom QIAseq targeted DNA panel 
(Qiagen) covering the partial or complete coding region 
of 40 genes was used. Fusion gene analysis was performed 
by a custom QIAseq targeted RNAscan panel covering the 
breakpoints of 110 genes. Sequencing was performed on the 

Virchows Archiv 
Miseq instrument (Illumina). For a detailed description and 
gene list, see SupplementaryMethods 1 and Supplementary 
Tables 3 and 4.
Statistics
Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 
4.3.2) [24]. Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to analyze dif-
ferences between types 1, 2, and mixed for the variables 
cyst size, Napsin A, Surfactant A, p40, CK5/6, and CK20. 
Significant differences were further analyzed using Dunn’s 
tests as post hoc tests. Given the limited number of type 
3 cases, this subtype was not included in the statistical 
analyses. To compare (previously named markers) between 
KRAS-mutated vs. KRAS wild-type samples, Mann–Whitney 
U tests were performed. P -values were adjusted using the 
false discovery rate (FDR) method (q-values) and considered 
significant for q < 0.05.
Results
Conventional morphology
A total of 50% (23/46) of the samples were classified as 
stocker type 1 (showing a “wavy” pattern), 22% (10/46) 
as type 2 (“punched-out” pattern), and 6% (3/46) as type 
3 (“microcystic” pattern). 22% (10/46) showed mixed his-
tological features encompassing patterns of type 1 and 
2. Here, cysts lined by pseudostratified ciliated cells and 
those resembling dilated bronchioles were found within 
one lesion (Fig.  1).
A total of 43% (20/46) of all samples showed cyst sizes 
≤ 2 cm, 52% (24/46) > 2 cm; for two samples, no size 
was documented. All type 3 lesions showed spongiform 
cystic areas (diameters: 4.5 cm, 5.5 cm, and 11.0 cm) with 
small microcysts with a few millimeters of diameter only 
(Table  2). No statistically significant differences were 
found between CPAM types (1, 2, mixed) and cyst size 
concerning the 2 cm cut-off (p  = 0.16; q = 0.24).
MCCs occurred in 17% (8/46) of all samples, with a 
frequency of 30% (7/23) in type 1 and 33% (1/3) in type 
3 (Fig.  1). Sizes ranged from 0.5 to 1.6 mm (mean 0.9 
mm). All eight cases showed intra-lesional arrangement 
of MCCs, seven with a multifocal and one with unifocal 
pattern. The unifocal cluster (size 0.5 mm) was initially 
not visible by H&E section but became apparent within 
deeper slide sections by means of PAS reaction. Six of the 
multifocal MCCs showed both intra- and (predominantly) 
extracystic distribution. One case had an intracystic pat-
tern only. No extra-lesional MCCs were found in the sur -
rounding regular lung tissue.

Immunohistochemistry
The cyst epithelia were strongly positive for CK7 and 
TTF-1 in all CPAMs. Partial or strong positivity for Nap-
sin A, Surfactant A, p40, CK5/6, and CK20 was found in 
61% (28/46), 57% (26/46), 100% (46/46), 83% (38/46), 
and 46% (21/46), respectively. Intensity of the expression 
patterns varied within and between subtypes. All MCCs 
showed combined positivity for CK7 and CK20 only. 
All samples were negative for CDX2 and SATB2. For a 
detailed description of IHC results, see Table  3 and Fig. 1.
To further analyze differences between CPAM types (1, 
2, mixed) statistically, Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed. 
A significant difference was apparent in p40 expression (p = 
0.005; q = 0.03). Thereby, the Dunn’s test revealed a signifi-
cant difference between type 1 and type 2 (p = 7.18e−5; q = 
0.0002), and no significance between type 1 and mixed (p = 
0.081; q = 0.081) as well as type 2 and mixed (p  = 0.059; 
q = 0.081). The CPAM types showed a tendency to differ 
in CK5/6 (p = 0.017; q = 0.051). Albeit, these results were 
not statistically significant after correcting for multiple test-
ing. A consequently performed pairwise Dunn’s test showed 
a higher frequency of CK5/6 in CPAM type 1 compared 
to CPAM type 2 (p  = 0.0003; q = 0.0009). No differences 
were shown in CK5/6 between type 1 and mixed (p = 0.051; 
q = 0.076), as well as between type 2 and mixed (p  = 0.16; 
q = 0.16). In addition, no statistically significant differences 
were observed for Napsin A (p = 0.37; q = 0.37), Surfactant 
A (p = 0.095; q = 0.19), and CK20 (p = 0.29; q = 0.35).

Discussion
To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the most 
comprehensive analysis of CPAM types 1–3 to date, inte-
grating morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecu-
lar data. Our immunohistochemical analyses aimed to delin-
eate protein expression profiles across different pulmonary 
regions (central vs. peripheral), while NGS was employed 
to identify potential molecular drivers. Notably, we sought 
to validate prior findings suggesting that KRAS mutations 
are not restricted to mucinous cell clusters (MCCs), but may 
also be present in surrounding non-mucinous epithelium.
Conventional morphology 
and immunohistochemistry
Type 1 CPAM is widely regarded as the most prevalent 
subtype, comprising 60–70% of cases, followed by type 2 
(15–20%) and type 3 (5–10%) [6, 14, 17, 25, 26]. Our find-
ings (50% type 1, 22% type 2, and 6% type 3) align with this 
distribution, though variations have been reported [12, 27], 
with some studies identifying type 2 as the most frequent 
subtype [8, 9, 28]. Such discrepancies may be attributed to 
enhanced prenatal detection of asymptomatic lesions [9 ].
Stocker’s original 1977 classification [5] was expanded in 
2002 to include five subtypes, primarily based on cyst size 
and morphology [6]. Conventionally, type 1 lesions are char-
acterized by cysts > 2 cm, whereas type 2 lesions contain 
smaller cysts (≤ 2 cm), and type 3 lesions exhibit sponge-
like microcystic areas [1 , 5]. However, emerging evidence 
suggests that cyst size alone may not reliably predict histo-
logical subtypes in up to 18% of cases [9, 18, 23], a finding 
corroborated and even exceeded in our study (Table 2). This 
may be partially explained by the fact that 36% of the cysts 
in our cohort measured near the 2 cm threshold (Supple -
mentary Table 1). Previous studies [9, 12] have emphasized 
that cyst size may be influenced by factors such as inflation 
and infection, leading to proposals for standardized histo-
pathological assessment protocols (see supplementary data 
in [12]).
Beyond classical CPAM subtypes, hybrid lesions (CPAM 
with sequestration) [ 28] and mixed CPAM types have 
increasingly been recognized. The latter may occasionally 
show overlapping features of type 1 + 3 [23] or even type 
1 + 4 [29], but typically involve type 1 + 2 [4, 13, 30] (most 
common, 22% in our cohort). This suggests that CPAM 
lesions may represent a developmental continuum, reflect-
ing disruptions at different stages of lung morphogenesis, an 
idea further supported by our protein expression patterns.

and CK7 positivity across all CPAM subtypes, consistent 
with their fundamental roles in pulmonary epithelial dif-
ferentiation. TTF-1 plays a crucial role in embryonic and 
fetal lung development [31], with expression persisting in 
type 2 pneumocytes during later gestation [7 ]. The strong

Napsin A, an asparagine protease, is routinely used in con-
junction with TTF-1 for the diagnosis of pulmonary carci-
nomas and metastases [34]. Its expression begins around 
the 12 th week of gestation in fetal pulmonary glandular 
structures and is thought to decline to adult-like levels 
by around the 30 th week [35]. To date, only two case 
reports have documented Napsin A expression in CPAM. 
Hasegawa et al. [36] described combined Napsin A and 
TTF-1 positivity in a CPAM type 1 associated with adeno-
carcinoma in an 80-year-old patient, while Koopman et al. 
[22] reported Napsin A-positive papillary epithelial cells 
in a type 1 lesion of a 6-week-old infant. Partial expression 
of Surfactant A in CPAM types 1–3 is known from the 12 
th week of gestation [25]. In our cohort, approximately 
60% showed partial and discontinuous expression of both 
Napsin A and Surfactant A. As with CK5/6 and p40, these 
diverse expression patterns likely reflect varying cellular 
compositions at distinct phases of lung development.
The role of MCCs as potential precursors of malignancy 
has been a topic of debate. MCCs are most frequently asso-
ciated with type 1 CPAM, with reported prevalence rang-
ing from 32 to 83% [5 , 9, 26]. In their recent larger study, 
Nelson et al. [23] stated: “we have never seen mucinous 
cells in classic type 2 cases.” However, MCCs have also 
been documented in type 2 [10, 12, 21, 23], type 3 [10, 16, 
23], and mixed types [23, 37]. In our cohort, no MCCs or 
molecular alterations (see below) were detected in either 
type 2 or mixed-type CPAMs. Given that type 1 and type 
2 components may co-occur within the same lesion, previ-
ously reported MCCs in type 2 CPAMs could potentially 
reflect sampling errors in cases of mixed-type morphology. 
This observation may further support the continuum model 
hypothesis, which proposes a morphological and possibly 
molecular spectrum rather than strictly distinct CPAM sub-
types. One large study of 671 MCCs noted multi-focal distri-
bution in 82% of cases, most often with extracystic growth, 
followed by combined extra and intracystic involvement, 
and lastly, purely intracystic growth [16]. Consistent with 
prior studies [16, 17], MCCs in our series appeared in 17% 
of all samples (30% in type 1; 33% in type 3). A total of 
75% displayed multi-focal distribution, combining extra- and 
intracystic localization, with a predominance of extracystic 
growth. Only one sample exhibited an exclusively unifocal, 
purely intracystic MCC. Because such small clusters can be 
overlooked on H&E staining alone, adding PAS-staining to 
routine protocols is advisable.
Next‑generation sequencing
KRAS functions as a molecular switch in signal transduc-
tion, playing a critical role in regulating cellular growth and 
differentiation. Mutations in KRAS are among the most fre-
quently observed alterations in human cancers [38] and are 
well-documented in MCCs [12, 15, 20, 23].
These mutations are considered potential drivers of 
malignant transformation, including progression to meta-
static mucinous lung adenocarcinoma [10, 12, 14, 17, 19, 
21]. Notably, some studies have based the diagnosis of car-
cinoma solely on the detection of KRAS mutations within 
MCCs [19, 21]. This raises the question of whether adjacent 
non-mucinous congenital pulmonary airway malformation 
tissue also harbors such mutations. While Fakler et al. [10] 
reported an absence of KRAS mutations in these adjacent 
regions, our findings align with recent studies [12, 22, 23], 
demonstrating that identical mutations are present in the sur-
rounding non-mucinous epithelium (with comparable allele 
frequencies). This reinforces the principle that malignancy 
diagnoses should not be based solely on the detection of 
a mutation. Analogies can be drawn to BRAF mutations 
in melanocytic nevi [39] and KRAS mutations in precur -
sor lesions of pancreatic cancer [40]. Additionally, KRAS  
mutations have been identified in CPAMs without MCCs 
[12, 23], as observed in one of our samples.
Nelson et al. [ 16] evaluated the malignant potential of 
MCCs, comparing them to adult adenocarcinomas. A clear 
histological distinction between the two entities was chal -
lenging due to similarities in nuclear features and size. 
Moreover, MCCs and the surrounding epithelium exhib-
ited a significantly higher proliferation index compared to 
adenocarcinomas. Interestingly, healthy infant lung tissue 
displayed an average proliferation rate approximately 2.5 
times higher than adult lung tissue [16]. These findings sug-
gest that diagnosing carcinoma in these contexts requires 
considerable caution. Some authors have hypothesized that 
KRAS-mutated MCCs, despite their morpho-molecular 
resemblance to mucinous adenocarcinomas, may gener -
ally exhibit benign behavior [16, 18]. Reports of malignant 
potential, including distant metastases and fatal outcomes, 
have often been attributed to incomplete surgical resection 
or delayed intervention [19, 41]. However, no recurrences or 
metastases have been reported following complete surgical

resection [23, 26]. Due to the lack of long-term data on 
asymptomatic, unresected CPAMs, the risk of malignant 
transformation in untreated lesions remains uncertain. Con-
versely, Nelson et al. [9 ] demonstrated that mucinous cell 
clusters (MCCs) in infantile CPAMs histologically resemble 
adult mucinous adenocarcinoma, yet are not associated with 
adverse outcomes when completely resected. In these cases, 
follow-up imaging within the first year after surgery showed 
no signs of recurrence or metastatic spread—independent 
of KRAS mutation status [9 , 19]. To date, no consensus or 
official guidelines exist regarding post-operative surveil-
lance protocols. Future studies should focus on gathering 
systematic follow-up data, particularly in mutation-driven 
CPAMs, to improve understanding of their clinical behavior 
and potential risks.
One might argue, by analogy to adult cancers, that addi-
tional mutations beyond KRAS may be required for tumori-
genesis. Although most studies on CPAM-associated adeno-
carcinoma have identified single KRAS mutations [10, 15, 
17, 20, 21], recent evidence has revealed KRAS/GNAS co-
mutations [17, 18]. In our study, we identified one case with 
a KRAS double mutation (G12D/G12 V) and another with a 
KRAS–TP53 co-mutation. To the best of our knowledge, this 
has not been previously reported in CPAMs, and especially 
KRAS double mutations are particularly rare in carcinomas 
overall [42]. In contrast, KRAS–TP53 co-mutations are more 
frequently observed and are often associated with poor prog-
nosis in cancers [ 43]. The co-occurrence of TP53  altera -
tions aligns with classical lung cancer biology, suggesting a 
potentially higher risk of malignant transformation in such 
cases [44]. However, in our study, early surgical intervention 
and subsequent follow-up did not reveal malignant progres-
sion, leaving this hypothesis speculative. Interestingly, our 
statistical analyses revealed that KRAS-mutated samples 
were significantly more often positive for Napsin A com -
pared to KRAS wild-type samples. We suggest that this is a 
phenomenon by chance; however, further research is needed 
to gain insight at the cellular level.
Additionally, we identified FGFR2 mutations in two of 
three type 3 CPAM samples. So far, this mutation has only 
been anecdotally reported in a mixed type 1 + 3 CPAM [23]. 
Furthermore, strong FGFR2 expression has been observed 
in cystic epithelium, MCCs of CPAM types 1–3, and nor -
mal lung tissue [10, 45]. FGFR2, a tyrosine kinase receptor, 
plays a critical role in early lung development, with disrup-
tions potentially leading to respiratory anomalies and tumo-
rigenesis [ 46]. As 24% of our samples showed mutations, 
the question arises whether these lesions might represent a 
distinct subtype. Further research is needed to identify the 
(stem) cell of origin (e.g., by single-cell sequencing) and to 
gain insight into first steps of pathogenesis.
This study has several limitations. Due to its retro-
spective design, potential biases in data collection and 
documentation cannot be excluded. Although the case 
number is relatively high for this rare entity and the study 
was conducted across multiple centers, the total number of 
included cases (n  = 46) remains limited. Moreover, long-
term clinical follow-up data were not available, restricting 
conclusions regarding the prognostic relevance of KRAS  
and/or FGFR2 mutations. In two cases, cyst size could not 
be assessed due to incomplete histopathological records, 
limiting the morphological classification. The small cohort 
size also reduced the statistical power for subgroup analy -
ses; in particular, CPAM type 3 could not be evaluated 
separately due to the low number of cases. The absence 
of mucinous cell clusters (MCCs) in type 2 and mixed 
lesions may likewise reflect the limited sample size (n  = 
10). It is noteworthy that all 11 cases with mutations were 
resected within the first 10 months of life. Nonetheless, 
we do not consider this to reflect a systematic age-related 
bias, as the vast majority of patients (97.8%, 45/46) were 
nine years old or younger at the time of surgery, and still 
76.1% (35/46) were within the first year of life (see Sup-
plementary Table 1).
Conclusions
A clear-cut assignment to CPAM classification may be chal-
lenging in a subset of samples, as patterns favoring type 1 
and/or type 2 might be seen within a single lesion. However, 
the occurrence of these mixed types supports the hypoth-
esis that such lesions rather represent a continuum of devel-
opmental disturbances occurring at different stages of lung 
development and, consequently, at different localizations 
within the bronchioloalveolar tree. This is further corrobo-
rated by the divergent protein expression patterns observed 
in the cystic epithelium. Our findings confirm that identical 
KRAS mutations can be present in MCCs and adjacent non-
mucinous CPAM tissue, underscoring the need to avoid diag-
nosing carcinoma based solely on mutation analysis. With 
advances in parallel sequencing, additional mutations—such 
as FGFR2 or double mutations—are likely to be identified. 
Given that one-quarter of our samples exhibited molecular 
alterations, a mutation-driven CPAM subtype, particularly 
within types 1 and 3, may warrant further investigation.
