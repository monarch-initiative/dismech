# From bedside to bench: A multimodal approach uncovering the molecular basis of the MYBPC1-linked Myotrem myopathy.
**Authors:** Iyer AS, Wright NT, Cook ME, Takagi Y, Johnson BA, Biancalana V, Massier M, Spodenkiewicz M, Poirsier C, Vallecillo B, Constant Boyer F, Pineau C, Hensley L, Sellers JR, Varney KM, Weber DJ, Kontrogianni-Konstantopoulos A
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2529897123](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2529897123)

## Content

Myotrem is an untreatable, early-onset, congenital myopathy characterized by hypotonia, muscle weakness, skeletal deformities, dysmorphia, respiratory insufficiency, and myogenic tremor (V. Shashi et al., Hum Mutat, 2019 and J. Stavusis et al., Ann. Neurol., 2019). It is associated with dominant variants in the pivotal M-domain of slow-skeletal Myosin Binding Protein-C (sMyBP-C) that modulates the dynamic binding to myosin and actin filaments and thereby crossbridge formation and kinetics. Herein, we report a nonmissense Myotrem variant, c.795_803dup p.(Leu266_Arg268dup), referred to as LKR-duplication. Our comprehensive studies, integrating clinical findings with biophysical, structural, and computational approaches, uncover the previously unreported structure and properties of the slow-skeletal M-domain, while elucidating the impact of the LKR-duplication. We show that the LKR-duplication stabilizes local helicity but alters global domain dynamics, leading to increased myosin binding, while impairing myosin-ATPase activity and crossbridge cycling. Critically, we pinpoint the specific amino acid residues facilitating the M-domain/myosin interaction and demonstrate that the LKR-duplicated residues not only directly contribute to myosin binding but also enhance the myosin interacting capability of neighboring and distant residues. Our multimodal approach sheds light on aspects of the pathobiology of the slow-skeletal M-domain-the Myotrem hotspot-by unveiling underlying pathogenic etiologies thereby paving the way for the development of targeted treatments.

MYBPC1, encodes the sarcomeric slow skeletal Myosin Binding Protein-C (sMyBP-C) (1), a modular protein that mainly consists of immunoglobulin (Ig) and fibronectin-III (Fn-III) domains, along with a Proline/Alanine (Pro/Ala) rich region and a unique M-domain (Fig. 1A). Unlike the fast-skeletal and cardiac isoforms, which originate from single transcripts, sMyBP-C exists as a subfamily of spliced variants expressed in both slow- and fast-twitch skeletal muscles in varying amounts, combinations, and ratios (2). True to their name, MyBP-C proteins interact with myosin thick filaments. While their COOH termini are constitutively bound to light meromyosin (LMM), their NH2 termini dynamically interact with subfragment 2Δ (S2Δ) of heavy meromyosin (HMM), and actin, regulating muscle contraction and relaxation in a phosphorylation and Ca2+-dependent fashion (3–7). Extensive work has focused on determining the minimal interacting site on MyBP-C proteins that facilitates binding to myosin S2Δ, with the current consensus implicating the highly conserved NH2-terminal M-domain with possible contributions from Ig domain C1 (6, 8). Both structural and regulatory roles have been attributed to sMyBP-C, with its structural role in thick filament organization and maintenance preceding its regulatory role in actomyosin crossbridge cycling (9). This is a unique property of sMyBP-C not shared by the cardiac and fast isoforms, which mainly possess regulatory functions (9). Consistent with this, sMyBP-C has been identified as an essential protein for skeletal muscle development and function, owing to its early expression during fetal development (10), the neonatal lethality of patients with a recessive NH2-terminal nonsense mutation (11), and the embryonic/perinatal lethality of a constitutive null MYBPC1 murine model (12).

Until a few years ago, our understanding of MYBPC1-associated diseases was limited to arthrogryposis multiplex congenita, a spectrum of disorders involving multiple joint contractures ranging from severe to lethal (13–15). In 2019, our team, in collaboration with research groups worldwide, identified a novel MYBPC1-associated, congenital myopathy characterized by hypotonia, muscle weakness, skeletal deformities, dysmorphia, respiratory deficiency, and a distinctive high-frequency, irregular tremor in the absence of neuropathy (16, 17). This myopathy has since been designated as Myotrem. All reported MYBPC1 Myotrem-linked variants involve dominant, missense substitutions within the highly conserved NH2-terminal M-domain of sMyBP-C (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) (16–18). While prior studies implicate variant-specific biochemical alterations of the M-domain (16, 17), they are hindered by the lack of information on the slow skeletal M-domain structure, function, and dynamics, contrary to the extensively researched cardiac M-domain. Critically, the slow skeletal and cardiac M-domains harbor only ~56% sequence identity (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Herein, we report a nonmissense MYBPC1 Myotrem variant and offer detailed insights on the biochemical, structural, and dynamic properties of wild-type and mutant slow skeletal M-domain, using high-resolution biophysical techniques, in tandem with computational modeling.

An adult individual with a history of distinctive myopathic musculoskeletal manifestations, including muscle weakness and abnormal spinal curvature (Fig. 1B), as well as a unique tremor in the absence of neurological deficits, underwent extensive clinical and genetic work-up. As a neonate, he demonstrated global hypotonia and muscle weakness, the latter of which was more profound in axial muscles, proximal limbs, and lower limbs. These symptoms were accompanied by moderate restrictive impairment and fine tremors of both the upper and lower extremities. Hypotrophic growth trends were present between six to nine months of age. Psychomotor developmental milestones were relatively normal, with independent sitting and walking achieved at 10 and 17 mo of age, respectively. Deltoid biopsies at ages one and five did not demonstrate any abnormalities, and subsequent immunoblot analysis showed unaltered dystrophin levels. Serum creatine phosphokinase (CPK) levels were normal. Skeletal manifestations of spinal rigidity and thoracic scoliosis emerged in early adolescence between 14 to 15 y of age. Brain MRI was normal, whereas whole-body MRI, at that time, revealed asymmetry in muscle masses of the hips and thin-appearing subcutaneous adipose layers, but was negative for fatty infiltration or muscle degeneration. Cognitive function and intellectual development were normal.

Currently at 32-y-old, the patient’s manifestations remain unchanged since disease stabilization in early adulthood. Manual muscle testing (0 to 5 scale) showed significant weakness of the hip flexors and abdominal muscles, the latter of which possibly caused a recent inguinal hernia. The combination of spinal rigidity and abdominal muscle weakness possibly contribute to difficulty transitioning from a supine to a sitting-up position (Movie S1). However, upper and lower limb strength ranged from good (4/5) to normal (5/5). The high-frequency irregular tremor is accentuated by posture and is more noticeable in the extremities (Movie S2). Muscle fatigability is still apparent, based on subjective feedback, primarily in the upper limbs. Exercise testing showed primarily peripheral limitations, characterized by cessation due to fatigue and muscle weakness, preventing sustained effective pedaling. The tremors were not alleviated by β-blockers typically used to treat cardiac arrhythmia and hypertension, or primidone commonly used to alleviate seizures. CPK levels continue to be within normal range. He currently does not have cardiac, renal, hepatic, or gastrointestinal dysfunction. The patient has consistently exhibited an underweight BMI, currently at 16.3 kg/m2, but does not manifest dysphagia and has no record of inadequate nutritional intake.

Assessment of motor nerve conduction revealed normal amplitude, latency, and conduction velocity, along with normal and symmetrical F-wave latency. Evaluation of sensory nerve conduction demonstrated appropriate amplitude and conduction velocity for the median and ulnar nerves, bilaterally, as well as for the right musculocutaneous nerve. However, amplitude was diminished for the left musculocutaneous nerve (5.3 µV) and slightly reduced for the sural nerves bilaterally (right: 9.2 µV, left 7.9 µV), with preserved conduction velocity. Electroneuromyography (ENMG) studies excluded neuropathy and confirmed a myopathic pattern, manifesting as full motor unit potential recruitment with short durations, along with the absence of spontaneous activity in the extensor carpi radialis and anterior tibialis muscles (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–D). Interestingly, ENMG traces further demonstrated short, microvoltage and brief potentials for voluntary weak effort, and intermediate potentials to recruit motor units for maximum effort, associated with the clinically observable tremor (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E).

The father of the patient presented with similar manifestations, including neonatal hypotonia, tremors, and delayed motor development (e.g., independent walking was achieved at three years of age), suggesting a potential hereditary pattern (Fig. 1C). Targeted genetic sequence analysis of the proband (individual V-1; Fig. 1C) identified a heterozygous variant in the MYBPC1 gene, NM_002465.4:c.795_803dup p.(L266_R268dup), not previously reported in gnomAD (19). This variant results in duplication of the Leu266 (L)-Lys267 (K)-Arg268 (R) tri-amino acid peptide, hereafter referred to as LKR-duplication, located within the M-domain (Fig. 1 C and D and SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2). It is strongly suspected that the proband inherited the dominant MYBPC1 LKR-duplication variant from his father, in whom it likely occurred de novo. Unfortunately, the father is deceased from an unrelated cause, therefore genetic testing could not be performed. Clinical evaluation of other family members, including the mother, sibling, and paternal grandparents, did not reveal any abnormalities, and genetic testing confirmed the absence of the MYBPC1 LKR-duplication variant.

To assess the functional impact of the LKR-duplication on the M-domain, we performed actin gliding and ATPase assays, using recombinant wild-type and LKR-mutant M-domain proteins. The LKR-mutant M-domain significantly dampened actin filament sliding velocity over immobilized purified skeletal muscle myosin compared to wild-type, in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1E and Movies S3 and S4). While the wild-type M-domain had little effect on the percentage of smooth-moving actin filaments even at higher concentrations, the LKR-mutant M-domain considerably reduced this percentage in a concentration-dependent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Importantly, the distribution of filament velocities was comparable across all conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Moreover, the actin-activated enzymatic activity of myosin was markedly inhibited in the presence of the LKR-duplication in a concentration-responsive manner (Fig. 1F). Thus, mutant sMyBP-C carrying an extra copy of the LKR-repeat exhibits altered biochemical properties, manifesting as enhanced inhibition of actomyosin crossbridge cycling and myosin-ATPase activity, likely contributing to the observed muscle pathogenicity.

Given the hydrophobic (L) and basic (K and R) nature of the LKR-duplication, we aimed to decipher how the LKR-duplication impacts the structural properties of the M-domain. As the structure of the slow skeletal M-domain had not been resolved, we employed NMR spectroscopy to address this important knowledge gap. Following collection of two-dimensional (2D) 1H,15N-heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) experiments, we unambiguously assigned backbone amide-proton correlations for isotopically enriched wild-type and LKR-mutant recombinant M-domain proteins, through triple resonance data collection and assignment (Fig. 2A). Subsequent consensus chemical shift index (CSI) analysis indicated that the wild-type M-domain is comprised of a disordered NH2-terminal subdomain and a structured COOH-terminal region, which contains three α-helices generating a tri-helix bundle (THB; Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Further structural assessment using secondary chemical shifts, residue order, and deep learning algorithms revealed that the disordered NH2-terminus contains regions with helical propensities (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B–D). Of note, although the slow skeletal M-domain exhibits a secondary structure composition similar to the cardiac M-domain, the regions with helical propensity within the disordered NH2-terminus appear to be more extended in the latter (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) (20).

CSI and secondary chemical shift analyses revealed that the gross secondary structure of the LKR-mutant M-domain remained conserved (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). The duplicated residues, L266dup (L269) and K267dup (K270), are located at the end of the final helix (i.e., helix-3) within the THB, replacing M269 and R270, respectively, while R268dup (R271) resides in the subsequent coil region. Order/Disorder analysis showed an extended decrease in the ZIRD score of the residues following the LKR-mutant THB (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C), indicating a prolonged helix-to-coil transition, compared to wild-type. This suggests that the LKR-duplication stabilizes the helical nature of the terminal THB helix, as demonstrated in the secondary structure plot (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Temperature-dependent circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy further confirmed this observation, as the LKR-mutant M-domain exhibited greater secondary structure stability compared to wild-type (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Relatedly, chemical shift perturbation (CSP) analysis exposed significant shifts in numerous correlations between residues L266 to K274 and highlighted considerable differences between the wild-type and LKR-mutant M-domain proteins (Fig. 2B), indicating substantial changes in the local chemical environment of the residues surrounding the LKR-duplication. Notably, residues W236 and K243, residing in helix-1, also exhibited noticeable, though not significant, CSPs, suggesting that the LKR-duplication impacts regions within the M-domain beyond the COOH-terminal helix-3. Collectively, our NMR studies reveal that the LKR-duplication considerably alters the chemical environment of neighboring residues and stabilizes the secondary structure of the M-domain, particularly the terminal helix-3 where it is located.

Given that the LKR-duplication induces substantial chemical shift changes to the THB, but not the disordered NH2-terminal region of the M-domain, and the challenging nature of obtaining NMR structural restraints of disordered protein segments (21), we solved the core structure of the wild-type THB (THBaa231−aa274). The selected region exclusively harbors the structured α-helices 1 to 3, allowing for maximum resolution through identification of intramolecular nuclear Overhauser effects (NOEs), which provide structural distance restraints (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 and Table S4). Our calculations for the THBaa231−aa274 structure confirmed that residues 235 to 240, 246 to 253, and 259 to 270 reside in helix 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The solved THBaa231−aa274 structure was consequently utilized as the basis of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, to predict the impact of the LKR-duplication on the THB tertiary structure. Comparison of computational simulations of the wild-type and LKR-mutant THB regions (Fig. 2C) were consistent with our CSI structural findings (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). While charge distributions on the wild-type THB revealed a basic surface interspersed with negatively charged residues, the duplicated residues resulted in a more homogenous basic patch on the LKR THB surface (Fig. 2D).

As the LKR-mutant M-domain exhibited peak broadening of several residues in 2D and 3D spectra, we subsequently explored whether altered structural dynamics disrupt M-domain behavior. Analysis of fast motion dynamics (ps to ns), through collection of R1, R2, and heteronuclear NOE (hetNOE), revealed that the LKR-mutant THB possesses a longer effective rotational correlation time, τc, which quantifies the time it takes for a protein to rotate one radian in solution, compared to wild-type (LKR-mutant τc: 9.34 ± 0.14 ns versus wild-type τc: 8.40 ± 0.13 ns; Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Of note, the calculated effective τc is greater than expected for proteins with a molecular weight of ~12 kDa, as is the case of both the wild-type and LKR-mutant proteins. This is likely due to the inherent lack of globularity of the THB and the tumbling behavior of the disordered NH2-terminus, commonly observed in structurally analogous proteins (22, 23). Two computational NMR relaxation platforms, PINT and NMRFx, corroborated these findings, yielding comparable results for R1, R2, and τc values (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Though the addition of just three residues does not markedly affect the mass of the M-domain, our studies revealed an almost one ns gain in effective tumbling time of the LKR-mutant THB. Therefore, it appears that the LKR-duplication affects the structural behavior of the M-domain by reducing its overall domain globularity.

To determine whether the LKR-duplication also alters M-domain backbone conformational dynamics on a slow-timescale (µs to ms), Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG)-based relaxation dispersion experiments were collected. Residues with similar parameters, such as motional exchange rate (kex) and the major population percentage (pA), within the wild-type and LKR-mutant M-domain proteins were clustered for final analysis. Several key findings were obtained. All identified residues undergoing chemical exchange or switching between conformational states, were localized to the THB region in the wild-type and LKR-mutant M-domain proteins (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Table S5). Notably, the LKR-mutant THB harbored a greater number of residues (eleven) that underwent exchange, compared to wild-type (eight; Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Specifically, V235, G261, and L266 underwent exchange only in the LKR-mutant M-domain. Additionally, two of the duplicated residues, L266dup (L269) and K267dup (K270), were directly involved in altered protein conformational dynamics and displayed slower kex rates (807.49 ± 242.26 s−1) compared to the respective wild-type residues M269 and R270 (2,876.66 ± 874.54 s−1). Furthermore, seven of the eight exchanging residues within the wild-type THB (E248, F252, Y254, R260, R265, M269, and R270) demonstrated a kex > 1,500 s−1, while only one (G255) exhibited a kex < 499 s−1. However, the LKR-mutant THB harbored three residues with a kex > 1,500 s−1 (V235, R260, and G261), four residues (E248, F252, Y254, G255) with a kex between 1,000 to 1,499 s−1, and four residues [R265, L266, L266dup (L269), K267dup (K270)] with a kex between 500 to 999 s−1. Interestingly, while exchanging residues were confined to the last two helices in the wild-type M-domain, all three helices contained exchanging residues in the LKR-mutant protein, supporting the global effect of the LKR-duplication in increasing internal THB conformational dynamics. These findings were further supported by tryptophan fluorescence experiments, in which the LKR-mutant M-domain displayed increased tryptophan mobility, compared to wild-type protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Thus, a greater population of residues adopts distinct conformational states in the LKR-mutant M-domain, compared to wild-type, thereby increasing the overall motion of the protein backbone; this likely also contributes to the increased τc of the LKR-mutant THB.

Given the key role of the M-domain in mediating the dynamic binding to myosin S2Δ, we utilized 2D-NMR 1H,15N-HSQC titration experiments to determine the impact of the LKR-duplication on S2Δ binding. Unlabeled myosin S2Δ was titrated into 15N-labeled wild-type or LKR-mutant M-domain proteins at increasing concentrations (0:1 to 2:1 molar ratio of S2Δ:15N-labeled M-domain). Resultant spectra were examined to determine chemical exchange behaviors of individual residues upon the addition of S2Δ. Titration of S2Δ into wild-type M-domain revealed that residues primarily located at the COOH-terminus displayed intermediate-exchange patterns, as shown by both CSP and intensity changes, indicating that the THB region is indeed responsible for facilitating myosin S2Δ binding, with minimal to no contributions from the disordered NH2-terminus (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, the exchange behavior of the same residues was notably more profound in the LKR-mutant M-domain, shifting to a slower-exchange regime, as evidenced by the loss of peak intensities at even lower S2Δ concentrations, denoting stronger binding (Fig. 4B). Binding affinity calculations (SI Appendix, Fig. S14) revealed that the wild-type and LKR-mutant M-domains have binding affinities (KD) to S2Δ of ~37 µM and ~14 µM, respectively (Fig. 4C). Thus, the LKR duplication bolsters binding of the M-domain to myosin S2Δ by more than two-fold. Subsequent MD simulations implicated residues R260, K264, K267, R268, R271, and E272 within the wild-type THB to interact with S2Δ (Fig. 4D). Conversely, residues K243, R260, K264, K267, K267dup (K270), R268, R268dup (R271), and R274 of the LKR-mutant M-domain were predicted to bind to S2Δ (Fig. 4E). This is consistent with our NMR data, as the indicated wild-type and LKR-mutant M-domain residues exhibit intermediate or slower chemical exchange upon S2Δ titration. Accordingly, our in-silico models demonstrated that the mean distance between the M-domain and S2Δ is decreased in the presence of the LKR-duplication (Fig. 4F), further underscoring the enhanced affinity of LKR-mutant sMyBP-C to myosin.

In 2019, our group in collaboration with other teams worldwide, identified Myotrem as a new myopathy linked to MYBPC1 dominant missense variants residing in the M-domain of sMyBP-C (16, 17). Herein, we report a nonmissense Myotrem variant, involving duplication of the LKR tri-amino acid repeat, also located in the M-domain. Notably, according to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) (24), the available clinical data alone are insufficient to establish pathogenicity. However, our integrated functional, structural, and computational analyses provide convergent evidence supporting the pathogenic association of the MYBPC1 LKR-duplication variant with Myotrem myopathy.

Given that the sMyBP-C M-domain has emerged as a Myotrem hotspot, we sought to investigate its structural and biophysical properties, in order to decipher the impact of these pathogenic variants at the molecular level. Our solution NMR studies revealed that the slow skeletal M-domain harbors a flexible disordered region at its NH2-terminus, with segments of helical propensity, and a tri-helix bundle at its COOH-terminus. Although the presence of the LKR-duplication minimally alters the secondary structure of the M-domain, it significantly increases the conformational dynamics of the THB region. A probable contributing factor to the augmented flexibility of the LKR-mutant THB region is that the duplicated leucine, L266dup (L269), competes with L266 to maintain hydrophobic interactions within the THB core, resulting in significant conformational changes of other hydrophobic residues (i.e., V235, Y254) and additional neighboring residues [i.e., R265, K267dup (K270)], ultimately leading to greater global THB motion.

Moreover, the LKR-mutant M-domain exhibits increased S2Δ binding capacity. The binding affinity of the wild-type M-domain–S2Δ interaction in the mid to low micromolar range is consistent with those previously reported for the NH2-terminus of the cardiac and skeletal isoforms, encompassing Ig domain C1, the M-domain, and Ig domain C2 (8, 10). However, our studies narrow down the region responsible for this interaction to the structured THB subdomain, while our computational modeling pinpoints the contributing amino acids (i.e., R260, K264, K267, R268, R271, and E272). More importantly, our structural and modeling studies uncover two key factors facilitating the enhanced LKR-mutant M-domain–S2Δ interaction. First, the increased number of positive charges, due to the duplicated lysine and arginine residues—K267dup (K270) and R268 (R271)—allows for additional electrostatic interactions between the large basic patch of the THB (Fig. 2D) and the negatively charged S2Δ residues. Second, the enhanced global motion of the LKR-mutant M-domain leads to increased accessibility of the positively charged duplicated [i.e., K267dup (K270) and R268dup (R271)] and neighboring (i.e., K243, R260, K264, K267, R268, and R274) residues. Thus, the LKR-duplication alters the structural and biochemical properties of pathogenic sMyBP-C, likely impairing its ability to modulate the formation and kinetics of effective actomyosin cross-bridges as well as the enzymatic activity of myosin, which in turn may underlie the clinical pathologies seen in the carriers.

Previous studies assessing the impact of missense MYBPC1 Myotrem variants on the myosin-binding capacity and stability of the M-domain demonstrated variant-specific outcomes. Accordingly, the Y247H and E248K substitutions were found to enhance binding to HMM, while neither was predicted to affect the stability of the M-domain’s secondary structure (17). Our relaxation dispersion experiments revealed that E248 is conformationally dynamic in the wild-type M-domain. Therefore, it is likely that the E248K substitution impacts the dynamic properties of E248 (and possibly of surrounding residues) to alter global THB motion. Moreover, the L259P variant does not alter binding to HMM, while the L263R mutation dampens binding to HMM. Although the L259P and the L263R variants introduce distinct amino acid substitutions (nonpolar versus charged), they were respectively suggested to disrupt and destabilize the secondary structure of the M-domain (16). Moreover, unlike the other Myotrem mutations that reside in the structured THB subdomain, the L219P variant is located in the unstructured NH2-terminus of the M-domain, interestingly within a region with helical propensity, where it is predicted to locally enhance disorder (18). Although the impact of L219P on M-domain’s structural and biochemical properties has yet to be experimentally or computationally determined, we predict that it will have minimal effects on myosin binding, provided that the stability of the THB secondary structure remains unaffected. Importantly, not all MYBPC1 pathogenic variants found within the THB core present as Myotrem myopathy. The dominant W236R variant, situated in helix-1 of the THB, manifests as distal arthrogryposis type 1 (DA-1), characterized by joint deformities and contractures of the upper and lower extremities (13). It is plausible that helix-1 may mediate distinct biochemical and/or structural properties of the M-domain, differing from those supported by helices 2 and 3. Accordingly, substitution of the aromatic tryptophan (W), a vital residue for maintaining hydrophobic core stability, to hydrophilic arginine (R) could potentially promote the collapse of THB’s tertiary structure. While the idiosyncrasies between Myotrem and DA manifestation of MYBPC1 variants are far from understood, we postulate that a complex interplay involving M-domain stability, dynamics, and surface charge alterations elicited by the modified residue(s) may underlie disease presentation.

Currently, there are no available treatments for Myotrem. Attempted therapies, including systemic steroids targeting muscle weakness and β-blockers or anticonvulsants aiming to alleviate tremor occurrence, provide no relief, may cause serious side-effects and/or exacerbate symptoms. The development of targeted therapeutics demands comprehensive investigation of both wild-type and pathogenic sMyBP-C at the molecular level. Our studies demonstrate that the LKR-duplication increases the M-domain conformational dynamics, rendering it more flexible, and creates a basic patch, enhancing its myosin-binding capacity. As with most known Myotrem variants, the impact of the LKR-duplication on actin binding remains unclear. Likewise, it is not yet known how phosphorylation at S203 within the M-domain influences these interactions. Future investigation is therefore warranted to address these knowledge gaps. Notwithstanding these open questions, our findings herein underscore the importance of developing targeted therapeutics, capable of restoring mutant M-domain’s native dynamics and/or biochemical properties. Such strategies are essential for the treatment of Myotrem, potentially through personalized approaches tailored to the specific functional consequences of individual variants.

Our comprehensive investigation uncovers insights into the previously understudied myopathic slow skeletal M-domain at the molecular and atomic levels, shedding light on its structural and biochemical properties in health and how they are disrupted in Myotrem myopathy.

Experimental set-up and analysis were performed at 30 °C, as previously described (27), with slight modifications. The motility assay buffer consisted of 20 mM MOPS (pH 7.4), 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EGTA, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM ATP, 2.5 mg/mL glucose, 0.1 mg/mL glucose oxidase, and 2 µg/mL catalase. Rabbit skeletal muscle myosin II heavy meromyosin (HMM; 0.2 mg/mL) was immobilized on nitrocellulose-coated coverslips with a flow cell chamber volume of 8 to 10 µL, via a 3-min room temperature incubation. To prevent nonspecific interactions and block inactive myosin heads, 1 mg/mL BSA and 5 µM unlabeled rabbit skeletal muscle F-actin (28) in the presence of 1 mM ATP were sequentially added, followed by extensive washing with motility assay buffer with and without ATP. Finally, 15 nM rhodamine-phalloidin (Invitrogen) labeled F-actin was introduced to the chamber in the presence of 1 mM ATP. All steps following HMM immobilization included a 1-min incubation time at room temperature. Recombinant wild-type and LKR-mutant M-domain proteins were added to the motility assay buffer at final concentrations ranging between 0.1 µM to 5 µM. Average velocities (µm/s) of ≥200 smooth-moving actin filaments were measured via the Cell Trak system (Motion Analysis) from five independent experiments, as previously described (27). Smooth-moving filaments were defined as those meeting the filter threshold of [SD of average filament velocity/average filament velocity] < 0.30. This filtering parameter was previously determined to be optimal for efficiently screening and selecting populations of smooth-moving filaments based on Gaussian fitting and statistical analysis (27). The percentage of smooth-moving filaments per assay was derived from the ratio of filtered filaments to total tracked filaments. Replicate assays were performed (for each recombinant protein) using a Nikon Eclipse Ti TIRF microscope equipped with an Andor EMCCD camera, to capture high quality motility videos (Movies S3 and S4). Actin gliding rates from experimental conditions (i.e., containing wild-type or LKR-mutant M-domain) were normalized to rates from sham control assays (i.e., lacking wild-type or LKR-mutant M-domain) for each independent experiment.

NADH-coupled actin-activated myosin ATPase activity was measured at 25°C (SpectraMax® iD3, Molecular Devices), as previously described (29, 30), using purified rabbit skeletal muscle F-actin and HMM. The enzymatic assay buffer was composed of 10 mM MOPS pH 7.0, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.15 mM EGTA, 10 mM KCl, 1.8 mM NaN3, 1 mM DTT, 40 U/mL L-lactic dehydrogenase, 200 U/mL pyruvate kinase, 200 µM NADH, and 1 mM phosphoenolpyruvate. The reaction was induced by the addition of 2 mM ATP. The final HMM and actin concentrations were held constant at 14 nM and 25 µM, respectively, while the final concentration of recombinant wild-type and LKR-mutant M-domain proteins varied between 2.5 µM to 5 µM. Actin-activated HMM ATPase rates in the presence of recombinant M-domain proteins were normalized to average sham control rates following subtraction of intrinsic actin ATP hydrolysis rates. Three to five independent experiments were performed per experimental condition, each consisting of two technical replicates.

For protein dynamics analysis, both fast- and slow-timescale experiments were collected on 0.45 mM 15N-labeled M-domain protein samples in buffer conditions utilized for M-domain backbone assignments. 15N-T1, 15N-T2, and 1H-15N heteronuclear NOE (hetNOE) experiments were collected at 25 °C using pulse programs, as previously described (55–58). Spectra were collected using relaxation delays of 1.21162, 80, 200, 400, 600, 800, 1,000, 200, and 200 ms for T1 times, and 0, 16, 32, 64, 96, 128, 32, and 32 ms for T2 times. 1H-15N hetNOE spectra were collected in the presence and absence of three sec saturation delay times. Spectral data were processed on NMRpipe (41), followed by peak integration and derivation of relaxation parameters using PINT v2.1.0 (59); error values were determined based on jackknife analysis of exponential curve fitting (60). Subsequent relaxation rates were utilized to determine rotational correlation time (τc): τc=14πνN6R2R1-7 (61); calculated values for residues 235 to 270, in which most HetNOE values were ≥0.6, were averaged to determine overall τc for the structured THB region (62). Error propagation of τc calculations (per residue) was performed under standard propagation of uncertainty calculations (63, 64). To verify rotational correlation time calculations, R1 and R2 datasets were also analyzed using NMRFx Analyst, with subsequent τc values calculated using the integrated RING NMR Dynamics application (65). τc values, on a preresidue basis, from PINT and NMRFx Analyst, were subjected to a simple linear regression model to assess direct correlative behavior (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Relaxation parameters of residues with extreme overlap that were of poor confidence in either wild-type or LKR-mutant M-domain proteins, were excluded from plotted data, final analysis, and/or statistical assessments. Specifically, the following residues with such behavior were excluded from plotted relaxation data: wild-type M-domain (R202, E212, K240, E246, E253, L266) and LKR-mutant M-domain (R202, E205, E212, R222, K240, E246, K267, E276, K277, K278, F282). Additionally, select residues, 271 (wild-type only) as well as 269 and 273 (LKR-mutant only), were unable to be assessed by PINT in one (or more) relaxation experiments due to peak broadening, and were therefore omitted from any calculations. Any residues excluded from the wild-type average τc calculations, were respectively omitted from the LKR-mutant τc average as well, to allow for accurate comparison (i.e., residues 240, 253, 266, 267, and 269).

Relaxation dispersion experiments (66–69) were recorded at 18 °C with a constant relaxation delay of 0.02 s and νcpmg (Hz) values of 0, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900, 1,000, 200, 200, 800, and 800. NMRFx Analyst (version 11.4.12) was used for data processing and analysis via the integrated RING NMR Dynamics application (65). The data were fitted to assume a two-state exchange model, with model selection performed using the small-sample correction Akaike information criteria (AICc). CPMG max frequency was set to 5,000 s-1 and Rex ratio value was set to 2.50. Parameter optimization was determined by minimizing the absolute values of the sum of squared deviation. Estimation of uncertainties was determined via 500 nonparametric bootstrap sampling. Error estimation for R2eff values within CPMG profiles was obtained as previously described (70), using the duplicate measures at a given field strength, across all residues within a dataset (i.e., not on a residue-by-residue basis). The error across wild-type and mutant M-domain residues at 600 MHz was 1.562 and 7.026 respectively, while the error across the wild-type and mutant M-domain at 950 MHz was 3.790 and 6.037, respectively. Residues with extreme overlap, specifically, E246, I250, E273, K274, and K275 in the LKR-mutant, were omitted from analysis. Following fits on an individual residue basis, subsets of residues were clustered based on the initial parameters (kex and pA values) and R2eff curve profiles.
