# A novel gene edited rat model of cerebral autosomal recessive arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CARASIL).
**Authors:** Monte B, Davis J, Zhu X, Xu F, Majchrzak M, Cabral MJ, Van Nostrand WM, Healey B, Koundal S, Lee H, Robinson JK, Benveniste H, Van Nostrand WE
**Journal:** Cereb Circ Cogn Behav (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.cccb.2025.100401](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cccb.2025.100401)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Cerebral Autosomal Recessive Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy (CARASIL) is a rare hereditary cerebral small vessel disease associated with loss-of-function mutations in the High Temperature Requirement Serine Protease 1 (HTRA1). While previous mouse models have provided insight into the functions of HTRA1, they have incompletely replicated the pathogenesis of CARASIL.

METHODS: A novel gene-edited rat model of CARASIL (designated CRHTRA1) was generated containing the loss-of-function Htra1 mutation p.R302Q. Quantitative cognitive, physiological, pathological and transcriptomic analyses were conducted to assess the CARASIL phenotype.

RESULTS: At 12 months, histopathological analyses revealed marked thickening of the cerebral arteriolar walls with concomitant lumen stenosis. Changes in elastic composition, vascular smooth muscle cells and extracellular matrix proteins were identified in cerebral arteries. Downstream pathological remodeling of vasculature is implicated in increased capillary tortuosity and changes in capillary coverage in specific brain regions. Focal hyperintensity regions associated with enlarged perivascular spaces were identified locally by in vivo proton density weighted MRI. Region-specific reductions of fractional anisotropy were identified using diffusion tensor imaging and correlated with a reduction in neuronal densities. Behavioral testing in CRHTRA1 rats revealed significant cognitive deficit and gait disturbances. Micro-computed tomography of spinal vertebrae revealed pronounced increases in osteophyte formation. Global cerebral RNA sequencing revealed changes in signaling pathways associated with the loss-of-function HTRA1 CARASIL mutation, including those associated with transforming growth factor-β signaling and extracellular matrix remodeling.

CONCLUSION: The CRHTRA1 rat recapitulates many pathological changes that are consistent with both clinical CARASIL pathology and studies of HTRA1 function in vitro.

Cerebral Autosomal Recessive Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy (CARASIL) is a rare hereditary cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) associated with biallelic, loss-of-function mutations in the High Temperature Requirement Serine Protease 1 (HTRA1). The majority of HTRA1 CARASIL mutations have been identified in Japan [1,2]. However, recent reports have identified novel CARASIL mutations in patients from China [[3], [4], [5]] India [6,7], Saudi Arabia [8], United States [9] and Portugal [10]. Pathological manifestations of CARASIL are multifaceted, with similar outward representation across mutations. Lacunar stroke, white matter hyperintensities, and cerebral microbleeds are present in patients using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, CARASIL patients do not develop systemic hypertension as it relates to the disease mutations. Post-mortem histological analyses show a diffuse loss of white matter with cortical U-fiber sparing, thickening and fibrosis of cerebral arterioles and leptomeningeal arteries, with subsequent reductions in lumen size [1,2]. Loss of vascular smooth muscle cells and thickening and splitting of the internal elastic lamina (IEL) are noted. Peripherally, increases in bone density, development of spondylosis deformans, and progressive alopecia are also reported. Cognitive impairments tend to manifest with disease progression [[11], [12], [13]].

HTRA1 is a serine proteinase containing four functional domains: the trypsin-like proteinase domain, a Kazal-like proteinase inhibitor domain, a PDZ domain, and an insulin-like growth factor binding protein domain. Primarily expressed as a secretory protein, HTRA1 can be found within several organs and tissues during development [14,15], and proteolytically regulates several cellular signaling pathways across the lifespan, such as TGFβ [14], Wnt [16], and NOTCH3 [17]. Both heterozygous and homozygous Htra1 mutations result in loss of proteolytic activity of the protein. Heterozygous Htra1 mutation carriers can exhibit similar, but milder, pathological changes observed with homozygous Htra1 CARASIL patients [[18], [19], [20]]. HTRA1 is also implicated in two other CSVDs: cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) and cerebral amyloid angiopathy [21,22]. Therefore, investigating HTRA1 and its associated pathways may prove to be informative in understanding common pathogenic mechanisms across distinct CSVDs.

To date, several types of mouse models exhibiting alterations in HTRA1 have been developed to investigate the pathogenesis CARASIL including Htra1 whole gene knockout mice [23,24], a transgenic mouse model expressing the familial HTRA1 mutation p.L364P [25,26], and a knock-in mouse model harboring the familial p.R274Q HTRA1 mutation [27]. While these mouse models have provided insight into the functions of HTRA1, they have incompletely replicated the pathogenesis of CARASIL, suggesting that other platforms for model development are warranted. In this regard, rat models have several advantages compared to mice for investigating CNS disease pathology. For example, rats are more evolutionarily similar to humans than mice, with a larger proportion of the genome being conserved across the two species [28]. Rats, due to their larger brain size, possess more white matter to probe for alterations. Further, rats are advantageous for investigating behavioral impairments with more translatable findings relative to cognitive deficits [29]. Therefore, we hypothesize that a rat model for CARASIL could provide a more accurate reflection of human disease.

To this end, we report on the generation and characterization of a novel gene-edited rat model containing the homozygous HTRA1 mutation p.R302Q, herein designated CRHTRA1. This mutation was chosen due to its early-onset and aggressive presentation of CARASIL pathology [30,31]. The high degree of conservation of the HTRA1 sequence allows for investigation of clinically relevant mutations in rats. At 12 months (M) of age, CRHTRA1 rats exhibited cognitive deficits and marked gait and spinal abnormalities. Notable cerebral arteriolar changes consistent with the pathological features of CARASIL patients were observed, with notable vessel wall thickening and lumen shrinkage. MRI analysis revealed significant changes in specific brain regions that were associated with decreases in neuronal densities. Lastly, cerebral transcriptomic analysis by RNA sequencing identified numerous genes and cellular signaling pathways that are associated with disrupted HTRA1 proteinase activity. Together, these findings indicate that CRHTRA1 rats provide a novel experimental model to investigate the pathogenesis of CARASIL and the biology of HTRA1.

All animal work was conducted in compliance with the United States PHS policy on the Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the University of Rhode Island and Yale University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees. Taconic Bioscience GmbH with Cyagen US Inc. (Santa Clara, CA) was commissioned to generate the Sprague Dawley (SD) CRHTRA1 rat model using their proprietary RapidKnockout™ CRISPR/Cas-mediated genome engineering technology. The CRHTRA1 rat model includes the R302Q (CGA to CAG) ‘knock in’ mutation within the endogenous rat Htra1 gene (Gene ID: 65,164; Gene accession number: NM_031721.1), located on rat chromosome 1, exon 4. Gene sequencing confirmed the presence of the point mutation in the endogenous rat Htra1 gene (Supplemental Fig. S1). Animals were bred and maintained as homozygous for the Htra1 mutation. SD rats served as wild-type (WT) controls. Body weights were not significantly different between CRHTRA1 and WT rats. Both female and male rats were evenly distributed between cohorts and aged to 12 months (M). Rats used in this study did not exhibit visible signs of pain or distress and ambulated without complication during behavioral paradigms. All rats were housed in a temperature-controlled room (22 ± 2 °C, 40–60 % humidity) on a standard 12 h light/dark cycle. Rat chow and water were available ad libitum.

Animals were sacrificed under deep anesthesia with 1 mL/kg of ketamine and 1mL/kg dexmedetomidine. Transcardial perfusion was performed with a 0.05 % solution of heparin sodium salt (Sigma, Cat# H3393-MU) in phosphate buffered saline as previously described [32]. Brains were either bisected sagitally at the midline or prepared coronally and drop-fixed in a 4 % paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h. Following fixation, samples were placed in a 30 % sucrose solution until tissue was completely saturated and mounted in optimal cutting temperature compound (Fisher Scientific, Cat#23–730–571) and stored at −80 °C. Coronally prepared brains underwent fixation in 4 % paraformaldehyde and were exported for paraffin embedding by American HistoLabs (Gaithersburg, MD).

Whole brain homogenate samples were normalized to 30 µg and denatured for 10 min at 95 °C. Homogenate proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE on 8 % gels at 70 mV for 20 min followed by 110 mV for 90 min. Proteins were electro-transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane for 2 h at 90 V. Following transfer, the membrane was blocked with Intercept Blocking Buffer (TBS-based; LiCor Biosciences; Lincoln, NE, 927–60001) for 1 h and incubated with the following antibodies: rabbit polyclonal anti-HTRA1 (ProteinTech, Rosemont, IL, 55011–1-AP, 1:600); mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, A2228, 1:1000) in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Detection of primary antibodies was performed by incubating the nitrocellulose membrane with donkey anti-mouse IgG 600RD (LiCor, 926–68,072; 1:15,000) and goat anti-rabbit IgG 800CW (LiCor, 926–32210; 1:15,000) for 2 h. Bands corresponding to HTRA1 and β-actin were quantitated using the LiCor Image Studio software version 5.2. The resulting HTRA1 band intensities were normalized to the endogenous β-actin to quantify expression.

Prior to behavioral testing, blood pressure measurements were performed on 12 M old rats (n = 11 CRHTRA1 and n = 6 WT rats) as previously described [32]. Briefly, the CODA Non-invasive Blood Pressure System (Kent Scientific, Torrington, CT, United States) was used to collect measurements. A habituation period of five consecutive days was conducted using this method before measurements were collected from each animal. Rats were handled, weighed, and placed in the appropriate size restraint with a fitted nose cone. The restrained animal was placed on an infrared warming platform to slowly increase tail temperature. Tail temperature was measured using an infrared thermometer and remained within a temperature range of 33–38 °C during measurement. Average systolic and diastolic blood pressures were calculated from three consecutive measurements at the end of the measurement protocol and were used for analysis. Rats showing visible signs of distress such as audible noise and persistent excessive movement were removed from the restraint and measured the following day. Time in the restraint system did not exceed 30 min per day.

Frozen PFA sagittal hemispheres were cut in the sagittal plane at 20 µm thick using a cryostat (Leica, Buffalo Grove, IL) and mounted on Colorfrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, 12–550–17). Slides contained two sections 200 µm apart. For vessel quantification, routine Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was performed on every tenth slide across each hemisphere (Leica CAT# 3801698). Subsequent immunolabeling was performed on adjacent slides using antigen retrieval (VectorLabs, Newark, CA, H-3301) at a 1:200 dilution in deionized water, followed by a 15 min incubation in Proteinase K (1:10,000) dilution in 1x phosphate buffered saline at 25 °C. Tissue blocking was performed with a SuperBlock solution (Fisher Scientific, 37518) containing 0.3 % Triton X100 for 30 min at 25 °C. Primary antibodies were diluted in SuperBlock solution with 0.1 % TritonX 100 and incubated overnight at 25 °C: Mouse monoclonal anti-smooth muscle cell α actin (SMA) to identify small arteries and arterioles (Novus Bio, Centennial, CO, NBP 2–33006, 1:200); rabbit polyclonal anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) to identify astrocytes (Dako, Carpenteria, CA, Z0334, 1:200); rabbit polyclonal anti-ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (IBA-1) to detect microglia (Fujifilm Wako, Osaka, Japan, 019–19741, 1:200), goat polyclonal anti-collagen IV to detect capillaries and small vessels (Novus Bio, NBP1–26549, 1:200). Extracellular matrix (ECM) protein expression was examined using either rabbit polyclonal anti-fibronectin-1 (FN1) (ProteinTech, 15613–1-AP, 1:200) or mouse monoclonal anti-LTBP-4 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, AF2885, 1:100). Detection of primary antibodies was performed using either AlexaFluor 594 or 488 fluorescent conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000 dilution) in blocking buffer with 0.1 % TritonX 100 and incubated for 2 h at 25°C. For neuron counting, slides were stained in a 0.1 % cresyl violet acetate (Sigma, C5042) solution in deionized water adjusted to a pH of 4.3. Elastic van Gieson staining (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, Ab150667) was performed on coronal sections containing the anterior cerebral artery and middle cerebral arteries to examine the integrity of the internal elastic lamina. Detection of myelin basic protein (MBP) was performed using a rabbit polyclonal antibody raised against MBP residues 50–64 followed by incubation with horse anti-rabbit IgG biotinylated secondary antibody (Vector Labs, BA-1100–1.5). Detection of diaminobenzene (DAB) chromogen was conducted using an Avidin/Biotin horseradish peroxidase (HRP) kit (Vector Labs, PK-6100), followed by antibody development with DAB substrate HRP (Vector Labs; SK-4105). All images were collected on a Keyence BZ-X710 Microscope (RRID:SCR_017202) using the Keyence BZ-X Analyzer Software Version 1.3.1.1 (Keyence Corp. Osaka, Japan) or Fiji [33].

To examine potential alterations in vessel wall thickness and/or lumen area reduction, routine H&E staining was performed as described above. Parenchymal arterioles and/or pial arteries were collected from random fields (40x magnification) from the cortex, basal forebrain, brain stem, striatum, cerebellum, hippocampus and corpus callosum. The outer area of the vessel followed by the inner area, encompassing the lumen alone, were measured. The lumen area was then divided by the outer area to yield a percentage representative of the total area comprised of vessel wall alone. The inverse percentage of this value yielded a value representative of the total lumen area relative to the entire vessel. All vessels were confirmed to be arteries or arterioles through adjacent immunolabeling with anti-SMA.

Capillary tortuosity and coverage measurements were conducted for potential downstream vasculopathy as previously described [34]. Briefly, capillaries were selected from random fields across serial sagittal sections that were immunolabeled with an antibody to collagen IV as described above. Total capillary length from point-to-point in conjunction with the total curvature of the capillary were measured. The resulting curvature value (C) was divided by the point-to-point (S) measurement to yield a “tortuosity ratio” of 1 or greater. Values greater than 1 indicate the degree of capillary tortuosity.

The levels of SMA, FN1 and LTBP-4 present in cerebral vessels were analyzed by immunofluorescent labeling. Collected images were analyzed using the Hybrid Cell Count tool in BZ-X analyzer. Images were binarized and set to a threshold that was consistent across groups. Measurement of the internal elastic lamina of the anterior and middle cerebral arteries was performed as described by Qin and colleagues [35].

Perivascular astrocyte and microglia coverage were determined by collecting vessel images from the basal forebrain, brain stem and striatum at 40x magnification. Images were cropped to encompass a 50 µm distance surrounding each vessel. Images were analyzed using the Hybrid Cell Count tool in BZ-X analyzer for both fluorescence intensity and fluorescence area coverage. The total number of neurons in the basal forebrain, striatum, and septal regions were collected from every tenth section cut at 20 μm thickness and labeled with Cresyl Violet staining. The grid overlay function in Fiji was used to manually count neurons across regions of interest.

All MRI acquisitions were performed on a Bruker 9.4T/16 magnet (Bruker BioSpin, Billerica MA, USA) interfaced to an Avance III console controlled by Paravision 6.0 software. For in vivo MRI experiments the rats were anesthetized and imaged in the supine position. Anesthesia during MRI was conducted with a subcutaneous infusion of dexmedetomidine (∼0.009 mg/kg/hr) supplemented with low-dose, ∼1 % isoflurane delivered via a nose cone in a 1:1 Air:O2 mixture. A 30-mm ID planar surface radiofrequency (RF) coil (Bruker BioSpin, Billerica, MA, USA) was placed under the head of the rats for RF signal reception and a custom-made volume transmit RF coil designed with an internal diameter of 50 mm was used as RF signal transmitter. In vivo 3D-proton density weighted (PDW) imaging was performed on 12 M old female WT (n = 3) and age-matched CRHTRA1 (n = 5) rats to screen for hyperintense areas, particularly in the context of detecting white matter hyperintensities or lacunes which appear as bright signals. A single flip angle spoiled gradient echo (SPGR) sequence was used to acquire 3D PDW MRIs: (repetition time (TR) = 50 ms, echo time (TE) = 4 ms, flip angle (FA) = 7°, Average = 1, field of view (FOV) = 30×30×15 mm, spatial resolution = 0.234×0.234×0.234 mm, scan time = 6 mins 50 s. The PDW images were corrected for intensity inhomogeneity using the N4 bias field correction algorithm [36]. An expert (HB) familiar with rat bran MRI anatomy and cerebral small vessel disease pathology screened all PDW brain scans for pathologies including focal hyperintensity signal areas using PMOD software (PMOD, version 3.908, PMOD Technologies LLC, Zürich, Switzerland).

Ex vivo MRI scans were performed on a larger cohort of 12 M old WT (n = 8; Female:Male = 6:2) and CRHTRA1 (n = 10; Female:Male = 8:2) using the active gadolinium staining technique as previously described [[37], [38], [39]]. Prior to imaging, the specimens were allowed to reach room temperature (19–21 °C) before being placed in a custom-made MRI-compatible holder containing proton signal-free susceptibility matched fluid (Galden Heat Transfer Fluids, HT230. Kurt J. Lesker Company, USA). A 3D pulsed gradient spin-echo DTI imaging protocol was implemented using the same protocol as described previously [37]. Ex vivo DTI image analyses were performed using voxel-wise analysis as described previously [37]. Following 3D voxel-wise diffusion tensor map calculation using the diffusion software toolbox (http://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/) [40], the tensor maps were spatially normalized by using the DTI-TK diffeomorphic deformable image registration algorithm (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/dtitk) to spatially normalize all tensor maps into a common space [41,42]. Fractional anisotropy (FA) was then calculated in the spatially normalized space for each subject and smoothed by a 0.4 mm Gaussian smoothing kernel. Voxel-wise statistical analyses comparing CRHTRA1 and WT were performed by a two-sample t-test using SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). A voxel was considered statistically significant at p-value < 0.05 after correcting for multiple comparison by false discovery rate (FDR).

Whole brain RNA sequencing and analyses were performed in 12 M old CRHTRA1 rats (n = 4) and age-matched WT rats (n = 4) as previously described [43]. RNA isolation, preparation, and sequencing were performed by Omega Bioservice Company (Norcross, GA, USA) according to the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) protocol (TruSeq Stranded mRNA). Available online: https://support.illumina.com/downloads/truseq-stranded-mrna-reference-guide-1000000040498.html) High quality poly mRNA (RINe > 8) was used for the subsequent sequencing analysis. The libraries were quantified, normalized, pooled, and subjected to cluster, and pair read sequencing was performed for 150 read cycles on a HiSeqX10 instrument (Illumina, Inc. San Diego, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The raw sequencing library was analyzed with Illumina Basespace app suite (Available online: https://www.illumina.com/products/by-type/informatics-products/basespace-sequence-hub/apps/rna-seq-alignment.html). Sequencing reads were aligned to the Rattus norvegicus UCSC rn5 using the STAR aligner. Salmon was used for quantification of reference genes and transcripts. The Strelka Variant caller identifies single nucleotide variants and small indels and generates output in a VCF file. Manta was used to detect gene fusions. GC and mean coverage information for every target was computed using Picard. For bioinformatics analysis, gene raw counts and differential expression results were produced by DESeq2 R package (R core team, 2024) with a cutoff of Benjamini and Hochberg p adjust value (P. adj) < 0.05 [44]. Using the Bioconductor and Cluster Profiler R packages, we performed the gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses including over-representation analysis (ORA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). ORA were done using ranked genes by log2 fold change and FDR-corrected values (P.adjust value < 0.05). The GO enrichment results show GO terms with P.adj < 0.05.

A total of n = 11 CRHTRA1 and n = 13 WT rats aged to 12 M were used. The open field (Maze Engineers, Skokie, IL) measures 60 cm x 60 cm and is constructed with matte black acrylic floor and walls. A camera is suspended above the arena to record movement. Prior to testing, the rats are habituated to the room for 20 min. After acclimation, the rat is placed in the center of the open field and allowed to freely explore the arena for 10 min after which they are returned to their home cage. During the 10 min period, the behavior was recorded using AnyMaze® behavioral tracking software (Stoelting Co., Wood Dale IL). AnyMaze® software tracks several parameters of behavior, such as total distance traveled, rotations, quadrant entries, speed, and time spent in the center of the arena. The experimenter manually records the number of rears the animal displays. Data were analyzed using unpaired student t-tests.

Working memory was assessed using a radial 8 arm maze (RAM) platform (Maze Engineers, Skokie, IL). A total of n = 11 CRHTRA1 and n = 13 WT animals aged 12 M were used. Before testing, animals were water restricted for 23 h per day (M-F), with ad libitum access to water on the weekends. Three days of maze habituation were performed prior to testing. The first day consisted of animals being placed inside the maze facing a single shape on the wall as a spatial cue. Each of the doors to the 8 arms were closed. A 100 µL drop of water flavored with orange Tang powder (Mondelez International, Chicago, IL; 1 tsp/700 mL filtered water) was placed at the indent of each arm entrance. Animals were given 5 min to drink all 8 drops of Tang. Day 2 of maze habituation allowed for exploration of all 8 arms, with reinforcement being placed at the beginning, middle, and in the recessed cup at the end of each maze arm. Day 3 of habituation consisted of 100 µL of Tang only in the recessed cup at the end of each arm. Animals were removed from the maze if all reinforcements were consumed prior to the time limit. After the completion of these 3 days of habituation, training sessions were conducted with liquid rewards available only in the food wells located at the ends of the arms. Each training session was terminated when the rat visited the ends of each of the arms or 5 min expired. An error was scored each time the rat entered an arm that had been previously entered during the session or if the rat did not enter an arm within the training session. Total number of errors [45] for each group across the 10-day period were analyzed using an unpaired student’s t-test.

A total of n = 11 CRHTRA1 and n = 13 WT animals aged 12 M were used to conduct gait analysis. The CatWalk XT® (Noldus, Leesburg VA) automated gait analysis system consists of an adjustable tunnel and a horizontal glass walkway ((W x D x H) 68×29×52.5 inches) with a lid emitting red light. Animals are positioned at the end of the walkway opposite the goal box and ambulate spontaneously towards the goal box. A green LED emits light inside the glass walkway in combination with a high-speed camera which is localized underneath the walkway that digitally records the trials of the animals and the print parameters of their forelimbs (FL) and hindlimbs (HL). Data are transmitted to a computer and analyzed with the CatWalk XT® software. The resulting output yields a large database of variables that are extracted from the rodent’s locomotor activity. This data can provide valuable insight of both ‘normal’ rodent locomotion and any gait aberrations that may be seen with genetic or pharmaceutical intervention [46]. Rats must perform at least three uninterrupted runs back and forth over the walkway to successfully complete the test. A maximum run variation of 60 % validates that no pauses or interruptions occurred during the trial. Rats have 10 s to complete the trial once they enter the walkway. A camera gain of 16.99 decibel (dB) and a detection threshold of 0.1 arbitrary units (AU) are set as calibrated detection parameters for all runs. If an animal does not complete 3 uninterrupted runs within 5 min, it is brought back later in the day when all animals have completed the test. All trials were manually reviewed and irrelevant data such as recorded contacts of the nose, abdomen, or tail with the walkway were excluded. Print data generated when the animals turn around at the end of the walkway were manually removed. Major parameters related to gait, kinetic or coordination are calculated for every run within the CatWalk XT® software, and the results of the 3 trials are averaged for every animal. Variables were extracted from the CatWalk XT® software and analyzed using unpaired student’s t-tests.

To investigate the 3D morphology of CRHTRA1 spines, samples were scanned using x-ray micro-computed tomography (micro-CT). The data were collected with a Zeiss Xradia 610 x-ray microscope operated at 80 kV and with the source and detector positioned to yield a voxel size of 26.25 µm. Given their length, each spine was imaged in six segments that were stitched together during post-processing. The micro-CT for each spinal segment consisted of 2401 image projections, captured with an exposure time of 1.2 s, and took approximately 2 h to complete (∼12 h total per spine). To preserve the interior structure of the spines and to prevent artifacts due to sample dehydration during image acquisition, each spine was imaged in a plastic graduated cylinder submerged in PBS. The spines were positioned in the graduated cylinders (cervical at the top and sacrum at the bottom) with parafilm to ensure the entire spine could be captured within the vertical range of the micro-CT instrument and would not move throughout the course of imaging. Upon completion of each scan, the data for each spinal segment was converted into a stack of tiff images that were loaded into Dragonfly Pro software (version 2024.1; Comet Technologies Canada, Montréal, Québec). Post processing was conducted using the Bone Analysis Wizard with implementation of pre-established bone assessment guidelines [47]. Briefly, cortical and trabecular bone were automatically segmented using threshold binarization in a three-dimensional space as detailed in the Buie method [48]. Automatic export of values denoting average total area (both cortical and trabecular bone) and average cortical area were conducted. The resulting data was analyzed using unpaired student’s t-tests.

Bioinformatics analysis, gene raw counts and differential expression of RNA sequencing data were conducted using the DESeq2 R package with a cutoff of p adjust value (FDR) < 0.05. Using R packages DOSE, ggplot2, enrichplot, clusterProfiler and org.Rn.eg.db database downloaded from Bioconductor and CRAN [49,50], we performed the gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses including over-representation analysis (ORA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). ORA were conducted using ranked genes by log2 fold change and p.adj ≤ 0.05. GO enrichment results show GO terms with p.adj < 0.05.

Voxel-wise statistical analyses of FA data comparing CRHTRA1 and WT were performed by a two-sample t-test using SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). A voxel was considered statistically significant at p-value < 0.05 after correcting for multiple comparison by FDR. Statistical analysis for all other studies was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA USA, www.graphpad.com) using unpaired student’s t-tests.

CRISPR/Cas-mediated genome engineering was utilized to introduce the point mutations (CGA to CAG) in the endogenous rat Htra1 gene. The resulting CRHTRA1 rats were bred to homozygosity and subsequent genomic sequencing revealed the successful insertion of the CARASIL R302Q (CGA to CAG) ‘knock in’ mutation (Supplemental Fig. S1). CRHTRA1 rats were viable and fertile. Given the introduction of a loss-of-function mutation into the endogenous rat Htra1 gene as opposed to a whole gene knock out, a difference in the level of HTRA1 protein between groups was not expected. To confirm this, quantitative immunoblotting was performed to compare the levels of HTRA1 protein in the brains of CRHTRA1 and WT rats. No significant difference in cerebral HTRA1 protein levels in CRHTRA1 mutant rats compared to age-matched WT rats was detected (Supplemental Fig. S2). Blood pressure measurements performed in CRHTRA1 rats and WT rats demonstrated that CRHTRA1 rats exhibited a normotensive phenotype (Supplemental Fig. S3), consistent with CARASIL. These findings demonstrate that the successful incorporation of the R302Q mutation into the endogenous rat Htra1 gene in CRHTRA1 rats did not alter the levels of HTRA1 protein in brain or the peripheral blood pressure in the animals.

CARASIL patients develop progressive gait disturbances along with spinal abnormalities including spondylosis [12]. Accumulating evidence indicates a role for HTRA1 in bone formation via its proteolytic regulation of TGF‐β and BMP signaling pathways [52]. Since the HTRA1 protein is proteolytically inactive in CRHTRA1 rats and they similarly exhibit gait disturbances, we next evaluated the spinal structure of the rats. Micro-CT was performed on spines isolated from CRHTRA1 rats and age-matched WT rats. Cervical, thoracic and lumbar vertebrae of CRHTRA1 rats exhibited numerous osteophyte formations along the vertebrae (Supplemental Fig. S4). Moreover, lumbar vertebrae showed excessive bone growth and shrinkage of the vertebral disc space. Quantitative measures showed increased bone volume along the spinal column. These spinal abnormalities are consistent with reported changes in CARASIL patients and may contribute to the observed altered gait function.

CARASIL is classified as a loss-of-function disease, in which the proteolytic activity of the HTRA1 protein is disabled, thereby removing this specific function. Earlier mouse models utilizing a complete gene knock-out of HTRA1 result in removal of the entire protein from the animal [23,24]. However, this approach also removes other functional regions of the protein, which normally remain in CARASIL patients and, therefore, is not an accurate representation of CARASIL pathogenesis. On the other hand, transgenic overexpression of mutant HTRA1 proteins in mice is non-physiological and is subject to interference of the endogenous mouse HTRA1 protein. Genetic knock-in models containing familial Htra1 mutations provide greater clinical relevance. Here, we present the generation and characterization of a novel rat model for CARASIL using gene-editing technology to introduce the p.R302Q mutation into the endogenous rat Htra1 gene. Our approach to knock-in a specific disease gene mutation into rats has notable advantages over earlier CARASIL mouse models. For example, rats are more evolutionarily similar to humans compared to mice, making rats a more translatable platform for CNS disease modeling. Second, CARASIL mutations introduced into the endogenous rat Htra1 gene compared to transgene overexpression and total gene knock out more closely reflects the etiology seen in CARASIL patients. However, previous models, while not as physiologically relevant to CARASIL in these aspects, do provide some insight into HTRA1-related pathology. For example, complete Htra1 gene knockout mouse models report changes in cerebral TGFβ signaling through phosphorylated SMAD2/3, elevated expression of ECM proteins, and structural alterations to the elastic lamina of cerebral arteries [23,24]. Analysis of cerebral vascular morphology are mixed, with some studies reporting no abnormalities in the composition of the elastic lamina or smooth muscle cell [53]. Mouse models that harbor the loss-of-function Htra1 CARASIL mutations p.L364P [25,26] or p.R274Q [27] have reported deficits in behavioral assays, increases in TGFβ/SMAD expression, and increased expression of ECM proteins within cerebral vasculature. Changes in cerebral arteriolar thickness, elastin structure, and smooth muscle cell composition were also reported, but not quantified [26]. The present CRHTRA1 model serves to advance these findings by providing a genetically relevant rat model of CARASIL that consistently recapitulates key pathological changes in the disease.

Concurrent with clinical reports of CARASIL, we have identified several behavioral and pathological characteristics that are present in the CRHTRA1 model. For example, CRHTRA1 rats exhibit memory impairment and notable gait abnormalities consistent with CARASIL patients. Spinal cord analysis using micro-CT scanning revealed distinct abnormalities including numerous osteophytes, increased bone volume and cortical thickness in CRHTRA1 rats, similar to spinal cord alterations observed in CARASIL patients [11,12]. These collective changes in bone structure are likely linked to role of HTRA1 in regulating bone morphogenic protein (BMP) signaling and matrix mineralization [54]. Post-mortem brain histological analysis of CARASIL patients frequently report on intimal thickening and fibrosis of cerebral blood vessels, particularly in white matter regions and the basal ganglia [1,13,55]. To identify all potential brain regions for arteriosclerotic changes, we performed a global vascular assessment prior to focusing our analysis to regions showing vascular changes. At the level of the cerebral vasculature, we identified both marked thickening of cerebral arteries and arterioles accompanied by lumen shrinkage in a region-specific manner. Of note, and consistent with clinical findings, the striatal region and brainstem showed significant thickening of cerebral vasculature. The rat brain striatum is defined to encompass the caudate, putamen, and ventral striatum, while pallidal regions contain the globus pallidus internus and externus. Therefore, we consider the term striatum to encompass major nuclei within the basal ganglia [56]. Pathological changes within the brainstem were diffuse and not confined to a specific region. We also identified vascular changes within the basal forebrain, a region which is associated with degeneration in patients with Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease [57,58]. Although major changes occurred in arteries and arterioles, there were also modest, but significant changes in downstream capillaries exhibiting increased tortuosity.

Smooth muscle cell loss is a common pathological characteristic seen in various CSVDs. For example, in CADASIL, mutations in Notch3 alter VSMC signaling and cause degeneration and loss of VSMCs in cerebral arterioles [58,59]. Similarly, in cerebral amyloid angiopathy, the cerebral vascular accumulation of fibrillar amyloid β-protein leads to degeneration and loss of VSMC [60,61]. Cerebral VSMC loss has also been reported in CARASIL [11]. In the CRHTRA1 rat, a significant global reduction in VSMC in pial arteries was identified. Additionally, in agreement with previous reports, we observed a significant thickening, but not splitting, of the IEL in cerebral arteries.

A key function of the HTRA1 protein is to serve as both a binding site and catalyst for proteolytic activity. Previous literature has reported on the increased accumulation of ECM proteins within the vasculature of HTRA1 mutant mice [23,27], and in cell cultures [24], including those associated with TGFβ signaling (i.e., LTBPs). Consistent with these findings, we report a significant increase in the levels of ECM proteins LTBP-4 and FN-1 within cerebral arteries. LTBPs, while classified as ECM proteins, are also recognized as mediators of TGFβ molecule trafficking and signaling. LTBPs are also associated with homeostatic maintenance of the ECM, promoting or inhibiting the development of microfibrillar fibulins, elastin, and fibronectins [62,63]. Thus, consistent with the increased expression of LTBP-4 in CRHTRA1 rats, an increase in FN-1 expression and elastic lamina thickening are observed.

Both changes and loss of cerebral white matter are consistent features seen in patients with various CARASIL mutations and tend to worsen with progression of the disease [30,55,64]. The PDW images of CRHTRA1 rats revealed spot-like hyperintensity areas which might represent dilated PVS and demyelination which was revealed by H&E staining and immunolabeling for myelin basic protein, respectively. It is unclear why the hyperintensity areas were confined to the piriform cortex. In rats, the piriform branch is the main MCA branch supplying the piriform cortex. Further, using MR imaging we found a significant decrease in FA values within three distinct anatomical regions, two of which show distinct vascular alterations. DTI in MRI can be tailored to target the movement of water molecules through the application of various diffusion gradient strengths in an MR sequence [65]. The resulting data allows for the quantification of diffusivity through a multitude of pipelines. Given this, we specifically chose to examine data by extraction of FA to quantify the cohesiveness of diffusivity along white matter tracts. It is important to note that the regions with significant decreases in FA values are not distinctly white matter regions, hence our reporting of lower neuronal counts in CRHTRA1 rats.

HTRA1 proteolytic activity can regulate TGFβR signaling by cleaving receptors, ligands or downstream regulators of these processes [24,54,66]. Proteolysis of these components results in a reduction of downstream transcription activation of a superfamily of genes associated with intracellular and extracellular development, organogenesis and osteogenesis [14,[17], [54],]. In CRHTRA1 rats, global RNAseq analysis revealed several pathways that are impacted by the p.R302Q mutation that confers loss of HTRA1 proteolytic activity, including elevated BMP signaling (Fig. 11), likely leading to increased bone matrix mineralization and observed abnormal spinal bone growth (Supplemental Fig. S3). Relatedly, elevated HTRA1 expression in patients with lumbago and intervertebral disk degeneration is correlated with increased expression of specific matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and is modulated by the ERK1/ERK2 signaling cascade [67]. Although CRHTRA1 rats do not exhibit overexpression of HTRA1 (Supplemental Fig. S2) they do show significant upregulation of Epor and downregulation of Nqo2 (Fig. 10A, Supplemental Table S3), which are associated with the regulation of ERK signaling and subsequent MMP secretion. This suggests that the dysregulation in specific genes associated with this cascade may play a role in the increase of specific MMPs (Fig. 11B, ECM Organization), thereby modulating the extracellular matrix of the intervertebral disks in the CRHTRA1 rat spinal cord and contributing to their degeneration (Supplemental Fig. S4D). Dysregulated TGFβR signaling is implicated in the cerebral vessel wall disturbances and accumulation of matrix proteins observed in the CRHTRA1 rats and is consistent with previous reports of HTRA1 dysfunction in vitro [19,54].

Despite the notable features of the CRHTRA1 rat, several limitations of this model are recognized. For example, CARASIL is classified as a CSVD with leukoencephalopathy. While PDW analysis revealed hyperintensities in the piriform cortex and DTI analyses revealed FA changes within the basal forebrain, septal region and ventral striatum, there were no significant alterations within major white matter structures, such as the corpus callosum or internal capsule. However, with further aging of the CRHTRA1 rats the potential for alterations within these white matter structures may arise and should be examined in future studies. Thickening of cerebral blood vessel walls in CSVD is related to reductions in cerebral blood flow (CBF), thereby increasing the risk of stroke or ischemic events [68]. Occurrence of cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) have also been reported in some CARASIL cases [4,7,64]. CRHTRA1 rats, despite showing prominent vascular alterations, did not consistently present with CMBs at 12 M, and were not evaluated for changes in CBF, both of which should be further examined in older rats in future studies. Regardless of these shortcomings, the CRHTRA1 rat provides a novel, physiologically relevant platform to further understand HTRA1 proteinase mutations in CARASIL and to uncover potential mechanistic links in the pathological development of other CSVDs.
