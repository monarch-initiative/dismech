# Mechanistic Insights and Pharmacological Approaches for Nitrogen and Sulfur Mustards and Their Implications as Therapeutic Agents.
**Authors:** Owens M, Thyagarajan A, Travers JB, Sahu RP
**Journal:** J Appl Toxicol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jat.4770](https://doi.org/10.1002/jat.4770)

## Content

Nitrogen and sulfur mustards, often acting as vesicants, have significant consequences for public health. Skin is a common site for exposure to these vesicants that can result in considerable morbidity and mortality. Given that the treatment options are limited, new insights into the mechanisms for the toxicity of these vesicants that can be translated into preventative/therapeutic strategies are desperately needed. Importantly, like most antineoplastic agents, including chemotherapy, the cytotoxic activity of vesicants such as nitrogen mustard (i.e., mustargen/mechlorethamine) and sulfur mustard is primarily mediated via their ability to act as alkylating agents. The current review highlights the underlying mechanisms, effects as well as approaches to mitigate sulfur and nitrogen mustard-induced effects, and their potential to be explored as therapeutic agents. Insights into the mediating roles and impacts of mustard agents could lead to future research and interventions that raise public health awareness to circumvent their adverse events and exploit desirable effects against proliferative diseases such as cancer.

Sulfur and nitrogen mustards have been used in warfare in World War I (WWI) and the Iraq and Iran wars, and currently, there are over 210,000 exposed victims (Ghanei et al. 2010; Nourani et al. 2017). Nitrogen and sulfur mustards cause immediate discomfort and painful reactions, but they also cause long‐term suffering and comorbidities later in life that are still being researched. Civilians and soldiers suffer from systemic inhalation of the gas form of sulfur mustard (SM) or through absorption of the liquid form through the skin. Those who inhale SM suffer from persistent lung disease and delayed deterioration later in life (Tewari‐Singh and Agarwal 2016). Victims of skin exposure to SM exhibit various cutaneous lesions with associated symptoms of itching and burning sensations, lacrimation, malaise, anorexia, vomiting, and respiratory and cardiac distress (Ghanei et al. 2010). Nonexposed areas also develop erythema, bulla, hypopigmentation, and hyperpigmentation (Ghanei et al. 2010). Of significance, mustard gas causes severe suffering after exposure, and the progression of SM has been found after more than 25 years post‐exposure, indicating chronic hindrance in victims (Nourani et al. 2017). SM causes a wide variety of side effects, and a clear mechanism of action has not yet been established. There is research identifying likely leads and pathways once exposed.

SM has been classified as a sensitizing agent, which is a pathophysiological process starting with an adverse outcome pathway (AOP) and ends with a contact dermatitis reaction (Steinritz et al. 2019). A report by Ghanei and colleagues' states that 70% of the SM resides in the epidermis and the rest in the basement membrane of the dermis (Ghanei et al. 2010). It has been found that SM manifests via vesication of the skin and then later leads to systemic toxicity that is shown through weight loss, blood cytopenia, bone marrow destruction, the pathology of tissue and blood, and delayed severe skin injury that can last for years after initial exposure (Goswami et al. 2015; Das et al. 2016). SM's alkylating properties are an active subject of research, and it has been found that it causes DNA replication cessation because it alkylates N7 of a deoxyguanosine residue and a cysteine residue (Shang et al. 2019). There are also theories that SM (1) hydrolyzes within cells as hydrochloric acid, (2) inhibits hexokinase, and (3) depletes the levels of glutathione and lipid peroxidation (Rafati‐Rahimzadeh et al. 2019). However, these theories were rejected for various reasons. The hydrolyzation theory was not accepted because the amount of vesicant action does not line up with the rate of acid release (Rafati‐Rahimzadeh et al. 2019). The inhibition of hexokinase was not accepted because the amount of SM needed for inhibition of this enzyme did not match in vivo and in vitro (Rafati‐Rahimzadeh et al. 2019). The glutathione theory was not accepted because sulfhydryl groups can deactivate lipid peroxidation processes quickly, and thus, the rate of damage by SM would be inconsistent (Rafati‐Rahimzadeh et al. 2019).

Despite the exact mechanism(s) by which SM causes damage, there are some leads on treatment options for exposed victims. There are therapies targeting oxidative stress and inflammation to help at least alleviate symptoms if the exposure damage cannot be cured. The use of N‐acetyl cysteine (NAC) has been shown to decrease release of ROS and inflammatory cells in response to SM (Rafati‐Rahimzadeh et al. 2019). There are also studies that show that the use of mesenchymal stem cells can help improve the quality of life in patients, but there was no significant improvement of effects in the body from treatment (Nejad‐Moghaddam et al. 2017). More research is being done on preventing the expansion of damage post‐SM exposure and more permanent measures to treat victims.

SM has been deemed a hazardous vesicant agent that can affect entire troops, but there may be a positive use of its effects. There is some research delving into how to use the toxic effects of SM's less potent analogue, nitrogen mustard (NM), in a therapeutic sense against cancer. Given that cancer cells overexpress a variety of genes that confer a propensity for unchecked cell division and not many medications can get inside to function, alkylating agents like NM are sometimes used for their high electrophilicity and reactivity against cells (Singh et al. 2018). NM lone pairs form an unstable intermediate called the “aziridinium ion,” which reacts with the DNA of tumor cells, but it also nonspecifically reacts with normal cells, causing unwanted side effects (Singh et al. 2018). There are still more studies to be performed to perfect the specificity of NM toward cancer cells, but there are a variety of therapeutic uses for this agent.

SM and NM are easily absorbed through the skin and can cause immediate cutaneous injury and blistering (Goswami et al. 2015). At higher concentrations, SM can cause severe multiple‐organ damage and then subsequent effects on the nervous, cardiac, and digestive systems (Goswami et al. 2015). NM is an analog of SM that was not used in warfare but does have similar mechanisms and toxicity after exposure (Goswami et al. 2015).

Not only do nitrogen and SMs have expansive systemic effects on the body but their effects also manifest very quickly after exposure and continue to affect victims years later. An in‐depth study evaluating the kinetics of post alkylating agent exposure vesication in the mouse ear vesicant model found that male mice ears that were exposed to HN2 (0.5 μmol/ear) had an increase in ear weights, morphometric thickness, edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, and signs of vesication at all time points of 15 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h compared with DMSO vehicle and untreated mice (Cuffari et al. 2018). This study also found that vesication sharply increased between 4 and 8 h after exposure, was well established, and remained elevated at 8‐ and 24‐h post‐exposure (Cuffari et al. 2018). These findings help provide insight into the kinetics of vesication and may be useful when investigating vesicant countermeasures.

SM and NM affect many different systems of the body and parts of the immune system. A study by Ghazanfari and colleagues evaluated serum levels of IgM, IgE, IgA, IgG, and IgG subclasses using ELISA in 128 SM‐exposed veterans with induced eye injuries and 31 controls (Ghazanfari et al. 2020). This study found that immunoglobulin levels can be altered after SM exposure compared with controls and the changes in IgG2 and IgG1 were associated with ocular damage (Ghazanfari et al. 2020). Other studies on topical NM exposure in mice demonstrated that SM and NM cause systemic toxicity in the hematopoietic system as indicated by significant body weight loss, 40%–80% mortality, loss of membrane integrity of the surface epithelium in the small intestine, abnormal structure of glands, and degeneration of villi, reduced white and red pulp in the spleen, dilated glomerular capillaries, and increased blood urea nitrogen/creatine ratio (Goswami et al. 2015; Sawyer et al. 2017).

SM and NM have a latent period after exposure before any symptoms manifest and are inversely proportional to the dose. The mechanism of action and pathway of toxicity is still highly unknown despite research and its prevalence as vesicant agents. Some hypotheses propose that these agents produce reactive nitrogen and oxygen species (RNS and ROS). Other studies have hypothesized that these agents also target intracellular calcium, anion, and pH deregulation, poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) activation, DNA, apoptosis, altered function of membrane‐bound receptors, and signaling molecules, including interleukin‐17 (IL‐17) producing cells (Nourani et al. 2017; Sawyer et al. 2017).

Animal models using the NM mechlorethamine [HN2] have been developed, which have provided important mechanistic insights into the toxicity associated with dermal as well as intratracheal exposures (Goswami et al. 2015). Regarding the dermal exposure models, multiple published reports have revealed that topical application of nitrogen mustard results in a potent skin vesicant response, followed by multi‐system toxicity (Goswami et al. 2015; Cuffari et al. 2018). Regarding the acute skin response, careful biochemical studies have revealed increased action of mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK), and a cytokine profile, including upregulation of tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNFα), cyclooxygenase type 2 (COX‐2), and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6). Regarding the systemic responses, mice that undergo significant topical exposure to mechlorethamine typically lose up to 70% of their baseline weight with a lethal outcome in most animals by 120 h (Goswami et al. 2015). Organs demonstrated to be involved include the spleen and hematopoietic system, as well as the intestines, liver, kidneys, and lungs Goswami et al. 2015). Mechanistic studies using in vitro epithelial cells as well as NRF2 KO mice have demonstrated an important role of ROS in the dermal and systemic toxicity of NM (Udasin et al. 2016).

SM contains a fat‐soluble structure with two 2‐chloroethyl side chains, which promote absorption in biological tissues such as the lungs, skin, and eyes (Nourani et al. 2017). Both SM and NM are alkylating agents and can cause cytotoxicity by cross‐linking deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) strands, which leads to prevention of DNA replication and repair (More et al. 2019). Similarly, NM binds DNA by irreversibly cross‐linking guanine N‐7 to the other strand of DNA, preventing replication and proliferation (More et al. 2019). NM has known cytotoxicity and has been known to cause DNA interstrand cross‐links and DNA monoalkylation. NM has more recently been found to cause DNA‐protein cross‐links (DPCs) between N7 of deoxyguanosine residue and a cysteine residue, but nitrogen‐induced DNA‐histone cross‐link formation has not been observed (Shang, Ren, Zhou et al., 2019). The authors demonstrated that in addition to DNA monoalkylation and interstrand cross‐link formation, the formation of DNA‐histone cross‐links occurs when reconstituted nucleosome core particles (NPCs) were treated with NM mechlorethamine. DNA monoalkylation had the highest yield, then DNA interstrand cross‐links, and then DNA‐histone cross‐links had the lowest yield (Shang et al. 2019). Other studies using competitive inhibition revealed that Lysine residues in the N‐ and C‐ terminal tails of histones were the predominant sites of cross‐link formation (Shang et al. 2019). These findings suggest that NM‐induced formation of DNA‐histone cross‐links and DPC‐induced cytotoxicity may occur in vivo because NCPs are fundamental repeating units in eukaryotic chromatin (Shang et al. 2019).

SM disrupts metabolism and inhibits antioxidant enzymes, including thioredoxin reductase, which leads to oxidative stress and long‐term tissue damage (Brimfield et al. 2012; Udasin et al. 2016). A study by Brimfield and colleagues demonstrated that the enzymatic reduction of SM sulfonium ions to carbon‐based free radicals was catalyzed by cytochrome P450 reductase, NADPH, and the spin trap α‐(4‐pyridyl‐1‐oxide)‐N‐tert‐butylnitrone (4‐POBN) in in‐vitro buffer (Brimfield et al. 2012). It has been found that carbon‐based radicals can subsequently create a magnified oxygen‐free radical response by reducing other molecules of oxygen and even a cyclic redox system like the herbicide paraquat (Brimfield et al. 2012).

Expanding on the role of ROS in NM and SM toxicity, a study evaluated the metabolic activation of SM and how it leads to oxygen‐free radical formation and long‐term tissue damage (Marzony et al. 2016). This study isolated the chain reaction of oxygen radicals from SM by first observing a 4.0‐mM SM concentration in an in vitro system. This large concentration of SM had a very low amount of mustard carbon radical concentrations recorded by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR). Next, the rate of mustard radical‐POBN adduct formation was observed in an in vitro system at atmospheric and reduced oxygen levels. This combination yielded a sixfold increase in 4‐POBN adduct formation (0.5 to 3.0 μM). When oxygen radical‐specific spin trap 5‐tert‐butoxycarbonyl‐5‐methylpyrroline‐N‐oxide (BMPO) was used instead of POBN, peroxyl and hydroxyl radicals were detected directly (Marzony et al. 2016). Reactive oxygen species (ROS)–BMPO EPR signals were reduced when mustard carbon radical production was also reduced. This shows that carbon radicals were a necessary precursor to ROS production (Brimfield et al. 2012). The mechanism of SM/NM‐induced toxicity is shown in Figure 2.

Despite SM and NM wide‐span exposure and fatal effects on the body, there are still no recommended in vivo treatments or repair mechanisms for SM‐induced toxicity or mortality to this day (Kumar et al. 2010; Nejad‐Moghaddam et al. 2017). Initial exposure to sulfur and nitrogen mustards can lead to symptoms of edema and erythema, which then develop into long‐lasting blisters, ulcerations, and necrosis of the skin. Despite advances in research on the mechanism of action of the effects of NM and SM, there are still limited therapies and treatments for human subjects, and most of them are designed to alleviate the symptoms rather than cure the cause (Goswami et al. 2015).

Notably, a few supplements can alleviate some of the symptoms caused by NM and SM. To that end, a study by Goswami and colleagues found that flavanone silibinin, a commercially available supplement derived from milk thistle that has anti‐inflammatory and anticancer properties that target oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways, could be used as a novel therapeutic intervention following exposure to vesicating agents (Goswami et al. 2015). Flavanone silibinin’s topical dose of 0.5 or 1 mg/mouse and 10 mM silibinin 30 min post‐exposure to 2‐chloroethyl ethyl sulfide (CEES) attenuated skin toxicity effects. The same dose applied 30 min post‐NM exposure also showed improvement in skin lesion damage from NM (Nejad‐Moghaddam). Other previous studies have also gathered that antioxidants, neutrophil depletion, and anti‐inflammatory drugs can also help alleviate the effects of SM (Nourani et al. 2017). A study on nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2), a transcription factor that regulates cellular defense against oxidative stress and toxic insults, and multidrug resistance‐associated protein 1 (Mrp1) found that Mrp1 is crucial in regulating HN2‐induced keratinocyte growth inhibition, and enhanced HN2 efflux from keratinocytes may serve as a novel strategy for the treatment of NM‐induced cytotoxicity (Udasin et al. 2016).

Decontamination of SM immediately after exposure is the most recommended protection against systemic toxicity of SM in experimental models (Kulkarni et al. 2006). To that end, a study that evaluated the prophylactic effects of oral Defence Research and Development Establishment (DRDE) analogues against lethality and toxicity of SM found that amifostine [S‐2(3‐aminopropylamino) ethyl phosphorothioate] and DRDE‐07 [S‐2(2‐aminoethylamino) ethyl phenyl sulfide] exerted sufficient protection in mouse models but had only moderate protection in rat models (Kulkarni et al. 2006). Notably, other DRDE‐07 analogues such as DRDE‐10, DRDE‐21, DRDE‐30, and DRDE‐35 were associated with 20‐fold protection compared with DRDE‐23 and DRDE‐38, which provided less protection (4.8 and 9 folds) in mouse models (Kulkarni et al. 2006). However, these compounds exerted almost 2‐fold protection against SM‐induced toxicities. Importantly, while pretreatment with DRDE‐21 significantly attenuated SM‐induced depletion in hepatic GSH levels, pretreatment with DRDE‐07, DRDE‐09, DRDE‐10, DRDE‐21, DRDE‐30, and DRDE‐35 resulted in significant protection from DNA damage induced by SM (Kulkarni et al. 2006). Moreover, SM‐induced liver and spleen lesions were also found to be decreased by pretreatment with DRDE‐07, DRDE‐09, DRDE‐10, and DRDE‐21. Overall, these studies suggest that DRDE analogues exert beneficial effects against SM‐induced toxicities when given prophylactically.

As for additional symptom prevention and treatments, other studies have used various preclinical model systems to explore and characterize SM effects. The mouse ear vesicant model (MEVM) has been utilized to quantitatively measure inflammation, edema, or tissue damage. A study by Babin and colleagues determined the protective effects of three pharmacological anti‐inflammatory agents as anti‐vesicants against SM‐induced skin injury. Mice were systemically administered hydrocortisone, indomethacin, and olvanil 24 h before and continued until 24 h post‐exposure to SM applied topically to one of the mice ears, followed by the measurements of epidermal‐dermal separation (i.e., blisters), necrosis, and edema responses (Babin et al. 2000). Overall, the data demonstrated that all these agents exhibited differential degrees of protective effects in reducing blisters and edema response, whereas only hydrocortisone resulted in attenuating epidermal necrosis when compared with only SM‐treated mice (Babin et al. 2000). Another research study done by Banin and colleagues examined the efficacy of metallocomplexes and their ability to protect against NM exposure to ocular tissues (Banin et al. 2003). Rabbits were exposed to 1% or 2% NM in one eye and then were treated with topical drops of zinc or gallium desferrioxamine, saline, zinc (chloride), or DFO alone. Masked examiners then evaluated the damage via clinical, histologic, and biochemical criteria. Rabbits with only saline treatment had severe damage to the anterior ocular segments compared with 52%–64% yielded protection in the Zn/DFO or Ga/DFO groups. Treatment with either Zn/DFO or Ga/DFO leads to lower scarring, neovascularization, inflammation in the anterior chamber, better healing of corneal epithelial erosions, better maintenance of intraocular pressure, and changes in the iris and lens (Banin et al. 2003). Another study on human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell‐derived exosomes (HMSCs‐Ex) miR‐199a‐5p demonstrates its critical role in regulating SM‐induced oxidative stress and apoptosis (Gong et al. 2023). HMSCs‐Ex‐derived miR‐199a‐5p was able to reduce oxidative stress in mouse models 24 h post‐exposure to SM by reducing ROS and increasing antioxidant enzymes in BEAS‐2B cells. It was also found that overexpression of miR‐199a‐5p led to a further decrease of Caveolin1 and activation of mRNA and protein level of NRF2, HO1, and NQO1 (CAV1/NRF2 signaling pathway) compared with HMSCs‐Ex administration (Gong et al. 2023).

Ocular surface exposure to NM has no definitive treatment currently available, but a study examining the therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cell‐conditioned media (MSC‐CM) in NM‐induced corneal wounds found that MSC‐CM partially restored mitochondrial function, decreased intracellular ROS generation, and inhibited apoptotic cell death in murine and porcine corneal tissue (Gong et al. 2023). The application of MSC‐CM after NM exposure can potentially be a helpful targeted treatment therapy for chemical eye injuries in the future (Gong et al. 2023).

As for preventative measures, a study completed by Gautam, Gupta, Lomash, and Vijayaraghavan demonstrates that DRDE‐07 and its analogues and different combinations of amifostine were able to recover biochemical alterations and histopathological changes from SM when given 30 min prior to percutaneous SM exposure (Gautam et al. 2010). Vitamin D 25(OH)D has also been found to serve as a potential countermeasure that offers protection against the mortality of SM and mitigates SM toxicity (Das et al. 2016). Additionally, it has been found that DRDE07 and its analogues on the systemic toxicity of SM and NM in rabbits state that DRDE 07 analogues can serve as effective prophylactic agents against percutaneously exposed male rodent and rabbit models (Kumar et al. 2010).

Regarding animal skin models, the agent 4‐phenylbutyric acid (4‐PBA) was used in a study by Srivastava and colleagues evaluating its ability to treat mouse skin edema, erythema, unfolded proteins, and increased production of cytokines after phenylarsine oxide (PAO) exposure (Srivastava et al. 2016). 4‐PBA was used in PAO‐treated mouse skin keratinocytes NHEK and HaCaT and significantly reduced inflammation, blistering, ROS formation, cytokines, and unfolded protein response. It was found that PAO can be used more in researching the molecular pathogenesis of chemical injuries in the future (Srivastava et al. 2016).

Upon clinical research, a randomized double‐blind placebo‐controlled trial with 89 subjects evaluated the efficacy of curcuminoids paired with piperine in subjects suffering from long‐term lung damage from SM exposure (Panahi et al. 2016). Forty‐five subjects were given curcuminoids 1500 mg/day + piperine (15 mg/day) combination, and 44 subjects were placebo for 4 weeks. Evaluation measures using high‐resolution computed tomography (CT), serum levels, and severity and frequency of respiratory symptoms were completed. Efficacy measures showed increased serum levels of GSH and decreased serum levels of malondialdehyde (MDA). All subjects’ CT suggested bronchiolitis obliterans, and the St. George Questionnaire (SGRQ) and COPE Assessment Test (CAT) suggested significant improvements in total and in subscale scores for severity and frequency of respiratory symptoms. There was a significant difference (p < 0.001) in comparison of the magnitude of changes between groups that showed elevated GSH, reduced MDA, and improved CAT and SGRQ scores in the curcuminoids‐piperine group (Panahi et al. 2016). The safety profile and antioxidant activity of curcuminoids make these polyphenols a key area of interest in the treatment of SM‐induced respiratory damage, and more research should be explored in this area (Panahi et al. 2016).

Finally, another clinical trial by Nejad‐Moghaddam and colleagues evaluated the efficacy and safety of mesenchymal stem cells on SM‐induced respiratory conditions. Stem cells were isolated from one male participant and then transfused back into him over 4 separate injections, 20 days apart (Nejad‐Moghaddam et al. 2017). Pulmonary function tests and quality of life assessments were completed before, during, and after the injections. There were no statistically significant improvements in pulmonary function, but the subject did note an improvement in quality of life (Nejad‐Moghaddam et al. 2017). The ability of NM to form electron‐deficient intermediates causes covalent bonds with bio‐macromolecules, which results in inhibition of function and cell apoptosis (Chen et al. 2018). These powerful cytotoxic and lymphoablative effects of mustards have been used since the 1940s for cancer treatment, and then later treatment of sarcomas, and hematologic malignancies (Highley et al. 2022). However, NM binding to N7 nitrogen‐atoms on DNA guanines and poor selectivity leads to high reactivity and peripheral cytotoxicity that is very damaging to normal cells (Chen et al. 2018). Therefore, efforts are being made to counter the disadvantages of poor selectivity and severe adverse reactions in the development of NM derivatives that have high activity and low toxicity (Chen et al. 2014). There are various classes of nitrogen mustards that each have medicinal aspects and developments in treatment, but cyclophosphamide, arguably the most versatile NM derivative, is used for its anti‐angiogenic and immunomodulatory effects in conjunction with stem cell therapy and treatment for autoimmune diseases (Highley et al. 2022).

NM is highly reactive and easily spreads into many organs of the body, but it can be used as a chemotherapy agent with enhanced drug delivery methods to specifically tumor cells (Singh et al. 2018; Sun et al. 2022). In a comparative study of NM and triethylene thiophosphoramide (thio‐TEPA) treatment of lung cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma, there was a tentative conclusion drawn that NM was more active than thio‐TEPA (Sun et al. 2022). Studies have indicated that the aromatic form of NM is less electrophilic and reactive and therefore can be given orally to patients (Sun et al. 2022). Although this does reduce overall toxicity, there are still severe side effects and acquired drug resistance that occurs due to the non‐specificity of the agent (Sun et al. 2022).

Recent research studies have demonstrated that prodrugs such as antibody‐directed enzyme prodrug therapy (ADEPT), gene‐directed enzyme prodrug therapy (GDEPT), and virus‐directed enzyme prodrug therapy (VDEPT) may improve NM selectivity toward cancer cells (Singh et al. 2018). Additionally, a study by Chen and colleagues found that NMs played a role in selectively killing chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) over normal lymphocytes when coupled with H2O2. There was about 40%–80% apoptosis in chronic leukemic lymphocytes isolated from CLL patients in in vitro assays and less than 25% cell death in normal lymphocytes from healthy donors when paired with H2O2 (Chen et al. 2014). Overall, these findings indicated that the selectivity of NM agents helps greatly to reduce host toxicity and enhances cancer chemotherapy and drug development.

Along similar lines, Jungk and colleagues conducted a retrospective data analysis to determine the effects of 1,3‐bis (2‐chloroethyl)‐1‐nitroso‐urea (BCNU), a chemotherapy agent with alkylating properties classified as a nitrogen mustard, on clinical outcome and side effects in recurrent glioblastoma patients (Jungk et al. 2016). BCNU treatment resulted in significant survival benefits after relapse, which were most pronounced in patients with O6‐methylguanine DNA methyltransferase (MGMT)‐hypermethylated gliomas, a prognostic biomarker associated with clinical outcomes, including improved survival, as compared with untreated control patients. BCNU was well tolerated in 46% of the patients with mild side effects, including nausea, vomiting, and fatigue, and severe side effects, including hepatotoxicity, thromboembolism, intracranial hemorrhage, and injection site reaction requiring surgical intervention (Jungk et al. 2016). While future studies are warranted, these findings indicated that BCNU was associated with favorable outcomes in recurrent glioblastoma patients.

Given that no specific treatment exists for SM and NM‐induced effects, including skin blistering/lesions, studies to understand deeper mechanistic insights are warranted to devise applicable approaches to mitigate their deleterious toxic effects. While available remedies may relieve symptoms, prevent infection, and promote healing for a short duration, in a long‐term, the sustained remission has been challenging due to SM and NM‐induced irreversible cellular damage leading to systemic toxicities. Cellular and animal models aimed at determining SM and NM‐induced toxic effects have enhanced our understanding of molecular mechanisms leading to oxidative stress, inflammation, skin lesions, and the involvement of signaling pathways and transcription factors. However, due to the complex microenvironment of immune and non‐immune cells, the development of potential prophylactic strategies and new therapeutic interventions are needed to mitigate SM and NM‐induced acute and long‐term damage to various organs, including cornea, mucous membrane, and skin.

| NM | Corneas of New Zealand albino rabbits | 1% or 2% wt/vol | Topical eye drops | 5 min | Conjunctival injury, scarring, and corneal opacity and neo‐vascularization | Banin et al. ( 2003 ) |
| NM | Hairless male mice | 3.2 mg | Topical | 24, 72, and 120 h | 24 h—Increased circulating neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils. 72 h—Decreased circulating neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils. 120 h—40%–80% mortality. | Goswami et al. ( 2015 ) |

Median number of 5 cycles (1–16 cycle range) | Intravenous | Varied and cumulative treatment exposure was evaluated | Myelosuppression, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia.
