# FBXL4-related encephalomyopathic mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome: A rare cause of hyperammonemia.
**Authors:** Kahraman AB, Çelik H, Bagci Z, Sezer A, Kılıç M
**Journal:** Mol Genet Metab Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymgmr.2025.101206](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymgmr.2025.101206)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: FBXL4- related encephalomyopathic mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) depletion syndrome is caused by pathogenic variants in the FBXL4 gene, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and multisystem involvement. Hyperammonemia is reported in 45 % of cases but extremely elevated ammonia levels are rare.

CASE PRESENTATION: A male infant presented with dysmorphic features, hypotonia, failure to thrive, and lactic acidosis and severe hyperammonemia (ammonia: 1495 μmol/L). Genetic testing identified a homozygous FBXL4 pathogenic variant.

CONCLUSION: To our knowledge, this report presents a neonatal case of FBXL4-related mtDNA depletion syndrome with the highest hyperammonemia level. This case emphasizes the importance of FBXL4 genetic testing in neonates with multisystem involvement, hyperammonemia, and dysmorphic features.

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) depletion syndromes (MDDS) are rare inherited metabolic disorders characterized by a quantitative reduction in mtDNA copy number. This group, characterized by highly heterogeneous clinical features, can primarily be categorized into encephalomyopathic, hepatocerebral, or myopathic forms. Variants identified in the FBXL4 gene have been associated with FBXL4- related encephalomyopathic mtDNA depletion syndrome. FBXL4 (F-box and leucine-rich repeat protein 4) is a nuclear encoded protein that plays a pivotal role in ubiquitination, a fundamental biochemical process crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis [1]. This type of depletion, which follows an encephalomyopathic course, can present with early infantile encephalopathy, growth and developmental delay, hypotonia, stroke-like episodes, movement disorders, lactic acidosis, and hyperammonemia [2]. Older patients have been documented to exhibit metabolic acidosis, lactic acidemia, developmental delay, neurological manifestations, and dysmorphic features. To date, approximately 112 cases have been reported [3]. This report will present a neonatal case characterized by severe hyperammonemic episodes, metabolic acidosis, and dysmorphic features.

The patient was delivered at 36 weeks of gestation with a birth weight of 1865 g, as the third living child of a 21-year-old healthy mother and a 24-year-old healthy father, who were first cousins of Arab origin. The mother's pregnancy was unmonitored, and the infant was born at another center with respiratory failure, failure to thrive, muscular hypotonia, dysmorphic features, and lactic acidosis (pH:7.21, lactate:11 mmol/L). The patient was discharged at 38 days of age following antibiotic therapy and treatment with citrate-based alkalinizing solution. At 40 days old, the patient experienced metabolic acidosis and a hyperammonemic episode (ammonia level: 1495 μmol/L) triggered by an infection. Plasma amino acid levels measured during the episode of hyperammonemia: glutamine: 742 umol/L (333–809), alanine: 498 umol/L (139–474), citrulline: 47 umol/L (8–47), lysine:238 umol/L (56–200) ornithine: 176 umol/L (20–136), proline: 424 umol/L (53–250). Urine organic acid analysis revealed elevated levels of 3-hydroxybutyric acid 173 mmol/mol creatinine (ref < 11.1), fumaric acid 212 mmol/mol creatinine (<38), malic acid 82 mmol/mol creatinine (<16), lactic acid 435 mmol/mol creatinine (<285), and succinic acid 103 mmol/mol creatinine (<81). The ammonia level was successfully reduced with enterally administired sodium phenylbutyrate and sodium benzoate, eliminating the need for peritoneal dialysis. Following a duration of 12 h, the concentration of ammonia diminished to 212 μmol/L, and subsequent to an additional 12 h, it further decreased to 99 μmol/L. The infant was discharged following treatment with a mitochondrial cocktail consisting of coenzyme Q10, carnitine, biotin, riboflavin, and thiamine, in addition to sodium benzoate. No specific diet was administered to the patient. At two months of age, the patient exhibited severe developmental delay and dysmorphic features (shown in Fig. 1). In her physical examination, her weight, height, and head circumference were 2.8 kg (standard deviation score [SDS]: −4.06), 51 cm (SDS: −3.82), and 37 cm (SDS: −3.02), respectively. The patient appeared malnourished with axial and peripheral hypotonia, lacked visual tracking and auditory responsiveness. Echocardiography revealed ventricular concentric hypertrophy. Electroencephalography showed focal epileptiform abnormalities, and brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed periventricular microcystic changes and a cavum septum pellucidum et vergae variation. A hearing test indicated bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. Fundoscopic examination revealed bilaterally pigmentary changes in the temporal region of the macula. Metabolic testing revealed a normal acylcarnitine profile. Elevated levels of blood alanine, proline, ornithine, lysine, lactate and Crebs' cycle intermediates in urine as fumaric, malic, succinic acid, and ketosis were observed, indicating mitochondrial disease. The patient was managed with sodium benzoate and a mitochondrial cocktail. Clinical exome sequencing was performed, revealing a homozygous pathogenic variant in the FBXL4 gene (NM_001278716 c.1555C>T p.(Gln519*), confirming a diagnosis of FBXL4- related encephalomyopathic mtDNA depletion syndrome. No homozygous segment associated with any other pathological condition was identified during the process of exome sequencing. To our knowledge, this case presents the highest ammonia level reported in the literature for patients with FBXL4-related encephalomyopathic mtDNA depletion syndrome. Hyperammonemia has been reported to occur in approximately 45 % of patients [4]. The second-highest value, 443 μmol/L, was reported in a case described by Morton et al. [5]. Hyperammonemia in mitochondrial disorders can be attributed to several mechanisms. Enzymes responsible for maintaining the urea cycle may fail to function due to mitochondrial energy deficiency, specifically ATP depletion. During cellular energy deficits, enhanced amino acid catabolism leads to increased ammonia production. Additionally, disruptions in pH balance impair enzymatic activity, further exacerbating ammonia accumulation. Impaired intestinal motility, a common feature in mitochondrial diseases, may lead to an overgrowth of ammonia-producing bacteria in the gut. The FBXL4 protein is hypothesized to play a pivotal role in the ubiquitination of the urea cycle proteins [5]. These combined factors contribute to the development of hyperammonemia, which is frequently observed in mitochondrial disorders.

Bonnen et al. first described this disease in 2013, reporting common features like developmental delay, microcephaly, low birth weights, metabolic acidosis, along with variable findings such as craniofacial dysmorphic features, cataracts, optic atrophy, and hyperammonemia [6]. The pathogenic variant identified in our patient, c.1555C > T [p.Gln519*] in the FBXL4 gene, is the same mutation first reported by Bonnen et al. and the second most common in FBXL4- related encephalomyopathic mtDNA depletion syndrome [7]. Our patient presented with distinct craniofacial abnormalities. Huemer et al. reported craniofacial abnormalities in 67 % of cases, without a clear genotype-phenotype correlation [8]. Ballout et al. noted that the condition is more prevalent among individuals of Arab descent, as observed in our patient, and that significant clinical variability can occur even among patients with the same genotype [9]. Our patient's early onset of lactic acidosis, low birth weight, periventricular cystic changes, and typical dysmorphic features, including a smooth long philtrum, large fontanelle, broad nasal root and bridge, low-set ears, and involvement of other systems such as sensorineural hearing loss, ventricular concentric hypertrophy, have also been reported in the literature. Ocular examination revealed pigmentary changes in the macula, suggestive of mitochondrial disease; such findings have not been previously described. It is plausible that, had the patient survived and undergone further ophthalmologic evaluations, findings such as cataracts or optic atrophy might have been observed.

The maintenance of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) integrity is a highly intricate process that requires the orchestration of numerous signaling pathways, trafficking between inter-membrane space, and regulatory proteins to sustain a dedicated nucleotide pool within the mitochondria. This complex process involves a tightly regulated network of pathways that ensure proper nucleotide availability for mtDNA synthesis and repair. Among the critical proteins involved in this process is FBXL4, which plays a pivotal role in maintaining mitochondrial DNA stability and function and is involved in mitophagy, the selective degradation of damaged mitochondria and contributes to mitochondrial fusion [10]. A defect in the FBXL4 gene disrupts mtDNA maintenance, resulting in impaired mitochondrial integrity and subsequent mitochondrial DNA depletion. A study by Kulkarni et al. demonstrated that mutations in the FBXL4 gene lead to increased mitophagy and mtDNA depletion in patient fibroblasts [11].

The genetic defect likely results in variable expression across different tissues, leading to a spectrum of functional impairment and clinical heterogeneity. Energy-intensive organs such as the heart, muscles, brain, and sensory systems (vision and hearing) are particularly vulnerable, giving rise to distinct clinical features. Clinical manifestations can range from antenatal findings such as brain MRI abnormalities and low birth weight, to neonatal presentations including metabolic acidosis and severe hyperammonemic episodes, often culminating in early mortality. The condition may present postnatally with non-specific symptoms such as feeding difficulties and neurological deficits. Previous cases have demonstrated remarkable clinical heterogeneity, including presentations of progressive cardiomyopathy and cystic encephalomalacia at 4.5 years of age, as well as notable early-life complications such as dysmotility and gastrointestinal bleeding at 2 months, or necrotizing enterocolitis and enteroencephalopathy at 6 weeks [12]. The oldest reported patient is 13.5 years old [8]. The complexity of cellular processes similarly leads to the complexity of clinical manifestations. These intricate and still partially understood mechanisms often culminate in a perfect storm of multisystem involvement, presenting with perplexing and multifaceted features that challenge even the most experienced clinicians.

Brain MRI findings in affected patients have included white matter abnormalities, thin corpus callosum, cerebral/cerebellar atrophy, basal ganglia and brain stem abnormalities, dilated ventricles, polymicrogyria, and lactate peaks on brain MR spectroscopy. One case reported periventricular cystic formations and cavum vergae variations at 26 weeks of gestation, resembling our patient's postnatal MRI findings, while another case described antenatal mega cisterna magna and subependymal hemorrhage [12,13]. These observations, combined with clinical features such as intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, microcephaly, and prominent dysmorphic features, suggest that the underlying pathophysiological process likely begins in the antenatal period.

Currently, no curative treatments exist for FBXL4- related encephalomyopathic mtDNA depletion syndrome and available interventions primarily aim to alleviate symptoms, albeit with limited effectiveness. Similar to other cases reported in the literature, our patient was started on a mitochondrial cocktail, which yielded no significant benefit. İnci et al. described a case with sepsis-like presentations where intravenous ketogenic diet markedly reduced lactate levels [14]. Conversely, Köse et al. reported a patient with intractable epilepsy who developed lactic acidosis on a ketogenic diet, highlighting the lack of benefit. These findings underscore the variability in therapeutic response, necessitating patient-centered rather than generalized treatment decisions. In some cases, dichloroacetate, which inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase and reduces lactate production, has been shown to improve lactic acidosis and reverse cardiac hypertrophy [15]. Reflecting on our patient's course, the potential use of dichloroacetate targeting concentric hypertrophy could have been considered.

In conclusion, neonates with multisystem involvement, including dysmorphic features, metabolic acidosis, and hyperammonemic episodes indicative of mitochondrial encephalopathy, should undergo prompt evaluation for FBXL4 gene defects through rapid genetic testing. Expanding reports of FBXL4- related encephalomyopathic mtDNA depletion syndrome will improve understanding of the disease spectrum, underlying pathophysiology, and potential targeted treatments. An increased number of case reports could enhance our understanding of the disease's clinical spectrum and provide deeper insights into its pathophysiology, ultimately facilitating the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.
