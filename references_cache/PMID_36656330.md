# Vitamin D-Dependent Rickets Type 3: A Case Report and Systematic Review.
**Authors:** Mantoanelli L, de Almeida CM, Coelho MCA, Coutinho M, Levine MA, Collett-Solberg PF, Bordallo AP
**Journal:** Calcif Tissue Int (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00223-022-01051-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00223-022-01051-2)

## Content

Although vitamin D deficiency resulting from insufficient sunlight exposure or inadequate dietary vitamin D intake is the most common cause of rickets, mutations in genes involved in vitamin D metabolism can cause genetic forms of rickets termed Vitamin D-Dependent Rickets (VDDR). In 2018, Roizen et al. described a new type of VDDR, named VDDR3, caused by a recurrent missense mutation in the CYP3A4 gene﻿ that leads to accelerated inactivation of vitamin D metabolites. Here, we describe the third case of VDDR3 due to the same CYP3A4 mutation in a 2-year-old boy with bone deformities associated with poor growth. As in the previously reported cases, this patient had no family history of rickets. Serial measurements of vitamin D metabolites after a single 150,000 IU dose of cholecalciferol demonstrated an accelerated inactivation of 25(OH)D and 1,25(OH)2D. Significant improvement in growth velocity and healing of bone deformities were achieved after a short period of treatment with 10.000 IU of cholecalciferol daily, showing the importance of early recognition and prompt precision therapy of this condition.

Rickets is a disorder of decreased skeletal mineralization and defective function of the growth plate in children. Although vitamin D deficiency is the primary cause of rickets worldwide, rickets can also occur in children who have a poor calcium intake or defects in phosphate metabolism [1–3]. Vitamin D deficiency most commonly occurs as a consequence of inadequate exposure to sunlight or dietary vitamin D insufficiency, but can also be due to increased clearance of vitamin D metabolites from various medications that activate the P450 enzyme CYP3A4 [4]. After the discovery of vitamin D in the 1920s and its subsequent addition to foods and supplements, the incidence of nutritional rickets decreased [1, 5] and genetic forms of rickets were recognized. Genetic forms of vitamin D deficiency rickets have been termed vitamin D-dependent rickets (VDDRs) and are characterized by early-onset rickets due to mutations in genes that encode proteins required for vitamin D homeostasis or responsiveness [6]. VDDR type 1 A (VDDR1A) [7] occurs due to a defect in the renal activation of vitamin D by a mutation in CYP27B1, which encodes the renal 1α- hydroxylase. VDDR type 1B (VDDR1B) [8] is due to a defect in conversion of vitamin D to calcidiol at the liver, by mutation in CYP2R1 encoding the principal 25- hydroxylase [9]. VDDR type 2 (VDDR-2) is characterized by a resistance to vitamin D due either to mutations in the VDR gene that encodes the nuclear receptor for vitamin D (VDDR2A) [10] or through a yet unknown mechanism (VDDR2B).

In 2018, Roizen et al. [11] described VDDR3 in two unrelated subjects with early-onset rickets who were poorly responsive to standard treatment regimens. Administration of very large doses of vitamin D or calcitriol induced an early response with a significant increment in serum levels of both forms of vitamin D followed by a rapid clearance. These individuals had identical de novo heterozygous missense mutations (c.902 T > C (p.I301T)) in CYP3A4, which encodes the predominant hepatic p450 enzyme, that led to accelerated inactivation of vitamin D metabolites. Here, we present a third case of VDDR3 due to the same CYP3A4 mutation in a young boy who presented with bone deformities associated with a poor growth. Our case provides further evidence that this recurrent CYP3A4 mutation should be considered in children with apparent vitamin D deficiency who do not respond appropriately to conventional vitamin D therapy and highlights the emerging role of precision medicine in the management of rickets.

This 2 8/12-year-old Caucasian male presented with a history of bowed legs, gait instability, and growth arrest since 12 months of age. He was born to non-consanguineous parents after a full-term normal pregnancy and was 48 cm in length and 3.635 kg in weight. His mother did not take any prenatal vitamin D supplements. He was breastfed until age 1 2/12 years (exclusively until 6 months) and did not receive any vitamin D supplements. He started to crawl with 6 months and had dental eruption at 5 months. He and his family had always lived in a sunny clime in Brazil at a latitude between the Tropic of Capricorn and the equator. The patient had no other medical conditions and was not taking any medications. There was no family history of rickets or metabolic bone disease. He was first seen by an orthopedist who made the diagnosis of rickets and referred him to a geneticist who ordered a genetic panel for 321 genes involved in bone disorders (Skeletal Diseases NGS Panel including CNV, Mendelics Análise Genômica, São Paulo, Brazil) with normal results. He was assessed by an endocrinologist who prescribed cholecalciferol 3000 IU daily. After 3 months with no improvement, he was switched to calcitriol (0.25 mcg PO twice per day). When seen at our clinic he was taking calcitriol 0.25 mcg once daily plus a calcium supplement. His physical examination revealed a height of 83 cm in height (− 2.5 SDS) with a midparental target height of + 0.7 SDS. His weight was normal. He had thickened wrists and bowed legs (genu varum). His radiographs were suggestive of active rickets—(Fig. 1A). His initial biochemical studies showed a low calcium of 1.3 mmol/L (NR 2.2–2.77), a low phosphorus of 1.07 mmol/L (NR 1.45–2.1), an elevated Alkaline Phosphatase at 18.45 μkat/L (NR < 8.3), a low 25(OH)D of 29.45 mmol/L (NR > 50), a high PTH of 371 ng/L (NR 12–88), and an elevated 1,25(OH)2D of 252 pmol/mL (NR 49.7–198). The elevated 1,25(OH)2D is probably because he was taking calcitriol 0.25 mcg PO twice per day. A modified vitamin D challenge was performed using an oral dose of 150,000 IU of cholecalciferol [8, 11]. Serum levels of 25(OH)D and 1,25(OH)2D peaked on the 3rd day after the vitamin D challenge, reducing considerably by the 30th day, as shown in Fig. 2. Serum calcium and phosphorus levels were low (1.98 mmol/L and 1.03 mmol/L, repectively) prior to administration of the 150,000 units of cholecalciferol and normalized (2.2 mmol/L and 1.64 mmol/L, respectively) 30 days later.

After appropriate parental assent, we obtained an oral swab from the patient from which genomic DNA was extracted using standard protocols. The proband underwent whole exome sequencing (WES) according to previously published protocols [12] in a College of American Pathology (CAP)-accredited commercial laboratory, Mendelics Análise Genômica, São Paulo, Brazil. Briefly, the library was constructed with Twist-Flex kit (Twist Biosciences, San Francisco, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The exome library was sequenced on an Illumina platform (NovaSeq 6000) running on paired-end mode. Reads were aligned to the reference human genome (GrCh38) with BWA (Burrows–Wheeler Aligner) aligner. Genotyping was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK). Annotation, filtering, and variant prioritization were performed using Mendelics proprietary software. Sanger sequencing was used to confirm the candidate variant in the patient and to genotype his parents.

Vitamin D metabolites were measured in the clinical chemistry lab; 25(OH) D was analyzed using the Architect 25-OH D Immunochemical Assay on Abbott Architect i-2000 with competitive chemiluminescent microparticle immuno assay (CMIA) technology (Abbott Park, IL, USA). 1,25 (OH)2D was analyzed by chemiluminescence immunoassay.

For this systematic review, we searched MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Knowledge, Scopus, CINAHL, LILACS, ScieELO, Cochrane, PubMed databases, trials registries, 3ie, Google Scholar using the terms CYP3A4 and rickets, vitamin D-dependent rickets type 3, CYP3A4 variants, CYP3A4 mutations, and VDDR3 not being identified any other case of VDDR3 besides the one described by Roizen et al. [11].

Figure 2 compares the serum concentrations of 25(OH)D and 1,25(OH)2D after administration of a large dose of cholecalciferol to our patient with those published in Thacher et al. [13] for controls and children with nutritional rickets. The baseline serum concentrations of 25(OH)D and 1,25(OH)2D are lower in our patient, and although the initial increment is larger than that of controls and children with nutritional rickets this is not entirely unexpected as the dose of vitamin D that we used (150,000 IU) was much greater than that used by Thacher et al. (50,000 IU) [11]. However, clearance of vitamin D metabolites by our patient is also much more rapid. Based on these results, we treated our patient with cholecalciferol 10,000 IU daily plus calcium supplements. Subsequent genetic testing by WES (average coverage of target exonic regions was 120x, with 99.4% of the targeted region covered more than 10x) revealed a heterozygous missense variant in CYP3A4: c.902 T > C; p.Ile301Thr (reference transcript NM_017460.6 or ENST00000651514.1; rs1815413655). This variant was absent in international (gnomAD) and local (ABraOM) available databases and is identical to the CYP3A4-p.(Ile301Thr) variant that was previously reported by Roizen et al. [11] confirming the diagnosis of VDDR3. The variant was absent in the parents’ DNA samples by Sanger sequencing. He has an older brother growing within his parental target height who was not available for testing.

At his most recent clinic visit, 3 months after the initiation of 10,000 IU of cholecalciferol daily, his height had increased to 91.4 cm (Z −1.3 SDS), with a greatly increased height velocity (5.9 cm in 3 months) and markedly improved gait. His biochemical studies now showed a normal calcium (2.25 mmol/L), PTH (38 ng/L), and 25(OH)D (126.3 mmol/L) and radiographs of his bones showed marked improvements (Fig. 1B).

Here, we describe the first case of VDDR3 in Brazil due to the previously identified gain-of-function mutation in the CYP3A4 gene that leads to an increased inactivation of vitamin D metabolites and rickets. Individuals who carry this heterozygous mutation have the same clinical and biochemical features as children with nutritional vitamin D deficiency, which makes it important to consider CYP3A4 mutations in patients with vitamin D deficiency who do not respond to conventional doses of vitamin D.

The CYP3A4 gene is highly expressed in the liver and intestine and encodes Cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) (EC 1.14.13.97), an enzyme that possesses a variety of activities, including hydroxylation, epoxidation of olefins, aromatic oxidation, and heteroatom oxidations, and modifies many drugs, steroids, carcinogens, and xenobiotics. The variant allele c902T > C directs the replacement of the amino acid isoleucine at codon 301 by threonine (p.lle301Thr) causing a gain of function [6, 11] that was demonstrated in vitro to have tenfold greater activity than the wild-type enzyme and twofold greater activity than CYP24A1, the primary enzyme that inactivates vitamin D metabolites. Normally, CYP3A4 does not play a significant role in vitamin D homeostasis, but many drugs (e.g., rifampin, phenytoin, barbiturates, and glucocorticoids) can induce overexpression of CYP3A4 and thereby lead to vitamin D deficiency [14].

Patients with VDDR3 have low serum concentrations of both 25(OH)D and 1,25 (OH)2D. In our case, the initial therapeutic trial with high-dose cholecalciferol administration and sequential measurement of vitamin D levels showed a rise in levels to values within the normal range, indicating adequate absorption of the medication, and a subsequent rapid decline due to increased degradation, similar to the pattern shown by Roizen et al. for this CYP3A4 variant [11]. Our patient showed improvements in clinical, radiological, and biochemical features with doses of cholecalciferol (10,000 IU/day) that were lower than those used by the patients in the cases reported by Roizen et al., who needed 50,000 IU of cholecalciferol per day. This difference is likely due to the smaller size of our patient. In addition, as synthesis of CYP3A4 is induced by the growth hormone signaling pathway [15], it is conceivable that lower activity of this pathway in younger children may also contribute to the lower requirement for vitamin D. Finally, similar to the other reported cases, our patient had no family history of rickets and carried a de novo mutation.

Finally, our patient experienced a rapid response to daily treatment with high-dose cholecalciferol, with a significant improvement in growth velocity and height, moving the patient from the short stature range to the normal stature range, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and treatment of this condition.

VDDR3 is a very rare disorder but given its similarity to nutritional vitamin D deficiency and the relative unavailability of genetic tests for CYP3A4, its prevalence may be underestimated, especially in sunny climes. The optimal dose of cholecalciferol for treatment still needs further investigation, in our case a dose of 10,000 IU daily of cholecalciferol was sufficient to normalize laboratory parameters and heal rickets with excellent growth recovery. The use of a single large dose of vitamin D followed by the collection of serial blood samples can point toward increased degradation of vitamin D as the cause for the rickets, and identification of the CYP3A4 mutation can facilitate precision therapy.
