## Content

BACKGROUND: Limited ancestral diversity has impaired our ability to detect risk variants more prevalent in ancestry groups of predominantly non-European ancestral background in genome-wide association studies (GWAS). We construct and analyze a multi-ancestry GWAS dataset in the Alzheimer's Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC) to test for novel shared and population-specific late-onset Alzheimer's disease (LOAD) susceptibility loci and evaluate underlying genetic architecture in 37,382 non-Hispanic White (NHW), 6728 African American, 8899 Hispanic (HIS), and 3232 East Asian individuals, performing within ancestry fixed-effects meta-analysis followed by a cross-ancestry random-effects meta-analysis.

RESULTS: We identify 13 loci with cross-population associations including known loci at/near CR1, BIN1, TREM2, CD2AP, PTK2B, CLU, SHARPIN, MS4A6A, PICALM, ABCA7, APOE, and two novel loci not previously reported at 11p12 (LRRC4C) and 12q24.13 (LHX5-AS1). We additionally identify three population-specific loci with genome-wide significance at/near PTPRK and GRB14 in HIS and KIAA0825 in NHW. Pathway analysis implicates multiple amyloid regulation pathways and the classical complement pathway. Genes at/near our novel loci have known roles in neuronal development (LRRC4C, LHX5-AS1, and PTPRK) and insulin receptor activity regulation (GRB14).

CONCLUSIONS: Using cross-population GWAS meta-analyses, we identify novel LOAD susceptibility loci in/near LRRC4C and LHX5-AS1, both with known roles in neuronal development, as well as several novel population-unique loci. Reflecting the power of diverse ancestry in GWAS, we detect the SHARPIN locus with only 13.7% of the sample size of the NHW GWAS study (n = 409,589) in which this locus was first observed. Continued expansion into larger multi-ancestry studies will provide even more power for further elucidating the genomics of late-onset Alzheimer's disease.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) affects over a third of people aged 80 years or older [1], with AD prevalence continuing to increase with the growth in the global population of elderly. Currently, there are approximately 50 million persons with AD worldwide, projected to increase to 135–200 million by 2050 [1]. Coupled with the facts that there are limited therapeutic interventions for AD and that this disease affects individuals of all ancestries, the expanding burden of AD makes this disease an urgent global public health crisis. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified over 75 risk loci associated with risk of AD and related dementias [2–5], providing critical insights into molecular mechanisms underlying disease development. Most of these studies, however, have been conducted in populations of predominantly European ancestral background, although it is clear similar genetic ancestral background impacts genotypic risk [6], with rare variants in particular usually being more frequently shared between populations with greater genetic similarity. This has limited the ability to identify variants and loci specific to non-European ancestral backgrounds [7–9], determine shared genetic risk and protective factors across diverse populations [10], and to capitalize on differential linkage disequilibrium (LD) structures across varied ancestral genetic backgrounds to improve fine-mapping of causal loci [11–14]. Thus, progress towards genetic prediction and precision medicine is significantly hampered by lack of data from populations with significant non-European background [15].


To characterize genetic ancestral background [16] while retaining statistical power to detect associations, our study examined genetic clustering of samples (excluding within-study population outliers) at the dataset level against 1000 Genomes/Human Genome Diversity Project reference panels to identify unique GSGs for population-specific analyses. As noted in the National Academies for the Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine report on the use of population descriptors in genetic studies [17] and related guidances [18], the assignment of samples to GSGs may be constrained by the limitations of the datasets used, particularly “legacy” datasets as most of our studies were. Assignment to GSGs at the dataset level allowed us to account for multiple sources of between-study heterogeneity, like study design, genotyping platform, ascertainment approach, and covariate collection in our > 60 legacy datasets. GSG classifications used multiple criteria. Studies predominantly clustering in principal components analysis (PCA) only with populations of European ancestral background sets were labeled non-Hispanic whites (NHW)); studies predominantly clustering with only with populations of East Asian ancestral background were labeled East Asian (EAS); studies whose samples clustered between and across European, Amerindian, and African ancestral backgrounds with ascertainment in heavily Spanish-speaking countries, regions, or areas and incorporating individuals identified as Latino were labeled Hispanic (HIS); and finally, studies whose samples clustered between and across European and African ancestral backgrounds with ascertainment in the USA and incorporating individuals identified as non-Hispanic black were labeled African American (AFA). This approach facilitated sufficiently well-powered discovery of novel loci across a spectrum of diverse ancestral genetic backgrounds.

To identify novel loci and explore differences in genetic association across these groups, the Alzheimer’s Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC) initiated the first large-scale multi-ancestry GWAS meta-analysis with direct genotyping and either clinical evaluation of or pathological confirmation of AD status on over 56,000 individuals across four genetic similarity groups (GSGs) with diverse ancestral genetic backgrounds, including 6728 AFA, 8899 HIS, 3232 EAS, and 37,382 NHW participants. We analyzed imputed genotypes derived from the diverse Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) R5 haplotype reference panel containing over 300 million unique variants from a large, diverse sample set. We conducted a two-tier analysis including a cross-GSG GWAS meta-analysis of AD and secondary analyses examining novel within-GSG loci including cross-GSG comparisons of GSG-specific associations to understand genetic architecture underlying novel genetic loci and to identify shared and distinct differences of AD risk and protective loci in populations of diverse ancestral genetic background.

The ADGC assembled the largest multi-GSG collection of AD samples to date, containing 24,388 participants with AD and 31,853 cognitively unimpaired individuals passing quality control (QC) from 41 NHW, 12 AFA, eight HIS, and five EAS datasets (within-GSG characteristics shown in Table 1; dataset-level characteristics shown in Table S1). Notably, in contrast to several previously reported studies that used proxy AD cases [4, 5], all samples utilized in these analyses were directly genotyped and either clinically assessed or pathologically evaluated and did not include “proxy” case/control definitions (individuals defined as cases or controls based on questionnaire response on the presence or absence of dementia in their parents) highly susceptible to misclassification and not limited to AD dementia. Descriptions of recruitment and diagnosis of AD for newly added and previously available datasets are reported in the Note S1; briefly, AD cases were defined by either pathological confirmation of the presence of AD pathology or clinical diagnosis using NIA-AA Working Group criteria, while cognitively normal elders (CNEs) (cases) were determined to have intact cognition via cognitive examinations (including mini-mental status exam (MMSE) or modified MMSE (3MS) score ≥ 26) among living CNEs and absence of AD pathology among deceased CNEs. No samples classified as having mild cognitive impairment (MCI) were used in analyses. We excluded individuals younger than age 65 years at the time of censoring (age-at-onset [AAO] of symptoms for AD cases or age-at-last-examination/age-at-death [AAE] for CNEs). Sample sizes and genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array platforms for the 66 individual datasets are reported in Table S2.

The lead variant of the LRRC4C locus (rs12576934) lies in the intergenic region 254,788 basepairs upstream from the LRRC4C (closest protein-coding gene; negative strand) transcription start site (TSS) and overlaps enhancers in cultured neurons suggesting a potential role in enhancer regulation in tissues/cell types related to AD pathogenesis (Fig. S21). It should be noted rs12576934 is a significant splicing quantitative trait locus (sQTL) for LRRC4C in brain frontal cortex (P = 5.89 × 10−4) from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Consortium [19]. However, rs12576934 does not show a significant association with LRRC4C expression in any brain-tissue related expression QTL (eQTL) data, including GTEx, Metabrain [20], the Microglia Genome Atlas (MiGA) [21], and Accelerating Medicine Partnerships-Alzheimer’s Disease (AMP-AD) [22] resources.

For the LHX5-AS1 locus, the lead variant (rs111486601) is located 170,842 base pairs upstream of the LHX5 TSS (closest protein-coding gene; negative strand). Multiple variants in linkage disequilibrium (LD; estimated using 1000 Genomes NHW populations [23–25]) with rs111486601 overlap ROADMAP [26] ChromHMM [27] brain enhancers in neuronal culture cells, hippocampus middle region dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, as well as blood enhancers in primary B and NK cells, suggesting potential regulatory roles in immune cell types and brain regions that are known to be related to AD. The variant rs111486601 overlaps and potentially disrupts multiple transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) as predicted by Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif Enrichment (HOMER [28]). Given that rs111486601 is rare in NHW (MAF = 0.0006), it is not presently observed in any QTL database (GTEx, Metabrain, MiGA, or AMP-AD), as these databases have been generated using common European GSG variants.

Of eight genes flanking intergenic SNPs or containing GWS SNPs from the five novel loci, LRRC4C and KIAA0825 were differentially expressed between autopsy-confirmed AD brains and brains of cognitively intact decedents with P < 0.05 in secondary analysis of previously reported [29] differential expression analyses using autopsied Framingham Heart Study (FHS, Table S8) brains (strongest association at LRRC4 C: log2FC (fold change) = − 0.01, P = 8.8 × 10−4), while only expression of LRRC4 C was significantly associated with Braak stage in FHS in autopsied AD brains (best association at LRRC4C: β = − 0.11, P = 4.7 × 10−3).

We performed pathway enrichment analyses including genes containing or flanking suggestively associated SNPs (P < 10−6) from the cross-GSG meta-analysis. We selected 86 and 60 genes for APOE ɛ4-unadjusted and -adjusted models, respectively. For both models, amyloid-beta related pathways were the top-ranked pathway, indicating that the relevance of this pathway in cross-GSG meta-analysis was unaffected by APOE ɛ4 adjustment. In the APOE ɛ4-unadjusted model, lipid transport, endocytosis, and classical complement pathways were in addition significantly associated (Padjusted < 0.05) (Table S9a). Examining the APOE ɛ4-adjusted model, classical complement and phospholipid efflux pathways were now more significantly associated and ranked higher than chylomicron remnant clearance, cholesterol, and endocytosis pathways with lower significance (Table S9b). The pathway enrichment patterns remained largely unchanged with the exclusion of APOE region genes (Table S9c). Pathway analysis excluding known AD genes from previous NHW studies revealed three pathways (P < 0.05) containing the novel gene LRRC4C, regulation of anatomical structure morphogenesis, synapse organization, and cell–cell adhesion, with or without APOE ɛ4 adjustment (Tables S9 d, e).

Employing the GSG-specific GWAS results from the two models, we determined pathways shared across GSGs (Table S10). Among the significant pathways in each GSG (Padjusted < 0.05) from the APOE ɛ4-unadjusted model, seven pathways were shared across all four ancestries related to lipid metabolism pathways (Fig. S22a). In the APOE ɛ4-adjusted model, no pathways with Padjusted < 0.05 were shared across ancestries (Fig. S22b).

In the largest multi-ancestry GWAS meta-analysis in AD to date, we identified two novel genome-wide cross-GSG associations near LRRC4C and LHX5-AS1, and three GSG-specific genome-wide significant loci near KIAA0825 (NHW), and GRB14 and PTPRK (HIS). Functional annotation follow-up revealed the LRRC4C variant’s potential regulatory role in enhancer regulation in tissues and cell types related to AD pathogenesis, and the involvement of the LHX5-AS1 variant in regulatory roles in immune cell types and brain regions. In silico differential expression analyses across brain tissues suggested differential expressions of LRRC4C and KIAA0825 between AD and control brains and association of LRRC4C expression with Braak stage; however, these gene expression results should be carefully interpreted since the observed effect size was relatively small. Pathway analyses implicated pathways associated with amyloid metabolism, cholesterol transport, and inflammation, consistent with previous pathway-based findings in AD.

While the available AD GWAS data from non-European GSG groups continue to be dwarfed by the sample sizes of AD GWAS in European GSG subjects, our analyses demonstrate the power of multi-GSG datasets. This was exemplified by the significant association at the SHARPIN locus in our study. While the SHARPIN association was first observed in a study of 53,689 clinically diagnosed and “proxy” AD cases and 355,900 controls individuals of European/NHW ancestral genetic background (rs34173062, MAF = 0.081, OR = 1.13, P = 1.7 × 10–6) and later confirmed in much larger sample sizes [4, 30, 31], here we observed a genome-wide significant association (rs34173062, P = 1.81 × 10−9) with a much smaller sample size. While NHW participants in this study had similar MAF (0.086 here vs. 0.081) and similar effect size (OR = 1.19 here vs. 1.13), the much larger effect size in HIS participants (ORHIS = 1.37; MAFHIS = 0.057) ensured sufficient power to detect a GWS cross-GSG association in just 56,241 subjects, only 13.7% the sample size of Schwartzentruber et al. (n = 409,589) [30], reflecting the unique power leveraging diverse populations can provide in GWAS. A post hoc power analysis [32, 33] confirmed this: For the sample size of HIS, we had Power(1 − β) = 0.89 to detect an association at P = 1.88 × 10−4 at OR = 1.37 and MAF = 0.086, whereas we would have had only Power ~ 0.23 to detect an association at the same significance level and sample size if OR = 1.19 and MAF = 0.057.

Similarly to the SHARPIN locus here, the higher frequencies of risk alleles in non-NHW GSGs may explain why the novel loci identified were not observed in earlier and much larger NHW GWAS. The association of the LRRC4C variant is relatively modest in NHW (MAF = 0.168, OR = 1.11, P = 1.81 × 10–6), but the higher frequency of the risk allele in HIS (MAF = 0.206) and EAS (MAF = 0.335) boost power to detect marginal associations of similar effect size to NHW in both groups (HIS, OR = 1.13, P = 0.00761; EAS, OR = 1.14, P = 0.0597), so that the combined effect across GSGs is detectable at the GWS level. A similar effect is observed with the LHX5-AS1 locus, which is of very low frequency in NHW (MAF = 0.0007) so that a statistically significant association is not observed even with a strong protective effect (OR = 0.50, P = 0.245), but in HIS, the much higher frequency (MAF = 0.039) coupled with a similarly strong protective effect (OR = 0.44) produces a GWS association in HIS that drives the cross-GSG association. The similar size and direction of effect in NHW and HIS suggest that this locus could be detected in NHW elsewhere, but post hoc power estimates suggested a GWS association in NHW would require ~ 123,000 cases, greater than the largest number of cases in published NHW GWAS of LOAD to date (Bellenguez et al. 2022; n = 111,326).

Among the novel loci identified in cross-GSG analyses, both LRRC4C and LHX5-AS1 are potential biological candidates for roles in AD. LRRC4C encodes leucin-rich repeat (LRR) containing adhesion molecules, which are key organizers of inhibitory and excitatory synapses. Mutations within the gene have been implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders, such as autism [34] and intellectual disability [35]. LRRC4C variants with suggestive associations with CSF b-site APP cleaving enzyme (BACE) have been previously discovered in ADNI [36]. Additionally, one study used LRRC4C knock-out mice in an experimental multiple sclerosis model and found a neuron-protective role for LRRC4C, concluding that the ectopic expression of LRRC4 C protected neurons from immune damage [37]. Associated variants in the LRRC4C locus are significantly associated with sQTL for LRRC4C in GTEx brain frontal cortex, and fall in a regulatory region that are notably enhancers in neuron tissue [38], with transcription factor (TF) Chromatin ImmunoPrecipitation Sequencing (ChIP-Seq) experiments showing the region overlapping our index variant as being a target of Srebf1 in exocrine gland, epithelium and mammary gland tissues. Srebf1 is a transcriptional activator required for lipid homeostasis and has been identified as a proneural transcription factor in radial glia [39]. Variants of SREBF1 influence AD risk by moderating the deleterious effect of the APOE ε4 allele [40].

LHX5-AS1 is an antisense RNA gene, which is complementary to the mRNA LHX5 with which it hybridizes and blocks its translation into protein. LHX5 encodes LIM homeobox 5, which is essential for the regulation of precursor cell proliferation. This gene controls neuronal differentiation and migration during hippocampal development [41]. LHX5 and LHX5-AS1 are similarly expressed in various regions of the adult central nervous system including hypothalamus, spinal cord, cerebellum, basal ganglia, and cerebellar hemisphere [42]. Significantly associated variants for this locus fall in a regulatory region, for example, bivalent enhancers in hematopoietic multipotent progenitor cells and brain [38]. Our specific index variant is an enhancer in blood and a transcription variant in neural progenitor cells [38]. Variants in 12q24 have previously been associated with hippocampal volume [43]. This novel locus is ~ 360 kb away from the recently reported locus at TPCN1 [4].

Notably, most of the novel loci identified in GSG-specific analyses act in biologically plausible pathways. The protein encoded by Grb14 functions as a negative modulator of insulin receptor (IR) activity. Both insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (T2D) and have been implicated in AD by a large body of epidemiological, neuropathological, and experimental studies [44]. Brain tissue from individuals with AD show major abnormalities in insulin signaling and increased presence of disease-specific pathological lesions, neurodegeneration, and neuronal vulnerability [44, 45]. In structural and functional neuroimaging studies, T2D and insulin resistance are associated with white increased burden of white matter lesions, decreased hippocampal volume, regional cerebral blood flow, and oxygenation [46–50]. PTPRK encodes a member of the protein tyrosine phosphatase (PTP) family regulating a variety of cellular processes including cell growth, differentiation, mitotic cycle, and oncogenic transformation. Variants in PTPRK were recently found to be associated with reaction time in a GWAS on cognitive function in 300,486 individuals of predominantly European genetic ancestral background [51]. KIAA0825 encodes a protein of largely unknown function; however, it is differentially expressed in AD vs. control in brain RNAseq data (see URL below).

Given that KIAA0825 had not been reported in previous NHW GWAS studies, we conducted a detailed examination of this locus using summary statistics from the Bellenguez et al. [4] GWAS study in NHW. We investigated the region spanning from 93,655,878 to 94,274,481 on chromosome 5 revealing multiple variants with significance levels reaching P = 1.1 × 10−4. This suggests that while the KIAA0825 locus was not highlighted in earlier studies, there is preliminary evidence of its genetic relevance in the NHW population.

In addressing the transferability of GWAS loci across ancestries, our data illustrate a compelling differential risk association. For instance, the CR1 locus shows genome-wide significance in NHW populations but is not associated with AD risk in other ancestries, suggesting a potential variance in genetic susceptibility or environmental interactions across populations. Similarly, the BIN1 locus, implicated in tau pathology in AD, demonstrates a strong association in NHW and nominal significance in AFA and HIS populations, yet this association is not replicated in EAS datasets. Variants such as APOE ε4 and APOE ε2, known respectively for their association with AD risk and protection, show significant associations across all ancestries; however, the effect size of APOE ε4 exhibits variation across populations. These examples support our hypothesis that, while some loci confer universal risk or protection against AD, others display a distinct GSG-specific effect. Such findings highlight the necessity for inclusive genetic research that recognizes the diversity of genetic ancestral backgrounds. This is important for advancing our understanding of AD and for the development of effective, personalized interventions.

Leveraging GWAS on diverse ancestries, here we observed evidence for novel loci not seen in prior multi-ancestry or large NHW-only studies. While this study yielded several novel AD susceptibility loci, the fewer non-European GSG samples relative to European GSG samples (57.2% of participants) remains a limitation. This constrains the identification of rare variants and novel GSG-specific loci in highly admixed groups including AFA and HIS, where heterogeneity in levels of European, African, and Amerindian admixture may strongly limit statistical power. Future work planned for these data includes global and local admixture analyses to provide detailed characterization of patterns of admixture and adjust for these positional and GSG-specific background differences, as well as on-going expansion of multi-population AD data resources. Despite our use of a high filtering threshold for imputation quality (R2 > 0.8) for inclusion in this analysis, variability in the frequencies of population-specific haplotypes against which to impute variants may have contributed to undetected differences in quality for the same variant between GSGs, suggesting we may have missed some non-NHW-driven associations in this study. The limited availability of non-NHW data further constrained our ability to perform replication of our findings in diverse sample sets, and splitting our non-NHW GSGs for replication would have severely limited their power for discovery. While confirmation through functional follow-up work provides support for our discovery associations, the possibility remains that the novel loci may be potential false positive associations until independently replicated. We will pursue replication/validation of our findings as the availability of non-NHW AD datasets increases. While more non-European GSG AD GWAS are valuable for cross-GSG comparisons of AD genetic risk profiles, even modest amounts of diverse data have tremendously boosted our ability to detect novel AD susceptibility loci.

This study identified several novel LOAD susceptibility loci, including those in or near LRRC4 C and LHX5-AS1, which are recognized for their roles in neuronal development. The findings were achieved with considerably smaller sample sizes compared to recent large LOAD GWAS of predominantly ancestral genetic background, highlighting the efficiency and importance of including diverse genetic backgrounds in genomic research. Expanding the diversity in genetic studies will improve our understanding of genetic risk factors across different populations, ultimately leading to more effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for AD.

We performed QC on individual ADGC datasets using a multi-step pipeline with key functions implemented in PLINK v1.9 [52–54]. All datasets included in analysis included only cases that met either NINCDS-ADRDA [55] or NIA-AA [56] criteria for clinical diagnosis of AD and had an age-at-onset > 60 years or had autopsy-confirmation of late-onset AD pathology; controls/non-cases (“cognitively normal elders”, CNEs) included individuals with a mini-mental status examination (MMSE) > 26 or modified mini-mental status examination (3MS) score > 87 at most recent exam and/or had either pathological confirmation of no AD or were reported to be cognitively intact at time of death (details in Note S1). We identified and excluded low-quality variants and samples after re-estimating all quality metrics following an initial filter excluding variants with a genotype missingness rate of > 10%. Variant-level QC filters implemented thereafter included exclusions of (a) SNPs with call rates below 98% for Illumina and 95% for Affymetrix panels; (b) SNPs with departure from Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) of P < 10−6 among controls for variants of MAF > 0.01; and (c) SNPs with informative missingness by case-CNE status of P < 10−6. For sample-level QC, samples were excluded if (a) the individual genotyping call rate was < 95%; (b) if X chromosome heterozygosity indicated inconsistency between predicted and reported sex; or (c) if population substructure analyses (described below) indicated the sample did not cluster with samples in its reported GSG grouping when compared against multi-GSG data from the 1000 Genomes (1kG) Phase 3 reference panel. For examples, reported NHW samples were expected to cluster with 1kG European GSGs while reported AFA samples being admixed were expected to cluster between and around 1kG European and African GSGs.

To identify outlier samples within each dataset, we performed a principal components analysis (PCA) using “smartpca” in EIGENSOFT [57, 58] on the subset of ~ 20,000 LD-pruned SNPs used for relatedness checks on genotypes from all samples within each individual dataset and from the 1kG Phase 3/HGDP reference panels. Individuals not clustering within 6 SD of one reference GSGs (or between GSGs for admixed subjects) were excluded from analysis. To account for the effects of population substructure in our analysis, a second within-dataset PCA was performed using only the remaining individuals in each dataset. Principal components (PCs) 1–10 were examined for association with AD case–control status and eigenvector loading, and only PCs showing nominal association with AD (P < 0.05) and eigenvector loadings > 3 were used in covariate adjustment for populations substructure (average number of PCs used was 3; range: 2–4).

For each dataset, SNPs not directly genotyped were imputed on the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) Imputation Server (TIS) [59] using samples of all ancestries available on the release 5 (R5) reference panel [60], which includes 308,107,085 SNPs observed on 194,512 haplotypes (from 97,256 participants), all with an estimated minor allele count (MAC) ≥ 5 and observed in samples from at least two separately ascertained data sources. Phasing on the TIS was done with EAGLE [61], while imputation was performed using Minimac4 [62]. Quality of imputation for all variants was assessed using R2 for imputation quality, although all variants were retained and not filtered prior to analysis. Following imputation and analysis, SNPs of all frequency were filtered using a conservative quality threshold, R2 ≥ 0.8, to assure high quality of rare variants with MAF ≤ 0.01. Analysis of SNP imputation quality by bin of MAF revealed that more than 80% of variants of MAF > 0.0005 had R2 ≥ 0.8; among variants with MAF ≤ 0.0005, approximately 50% of variants had R2 ≥ 0.8 in most datasets with n > 1000 participants. After association analyses at several cross-dataset and within-dataset filtering thresholds, reported analyses include only variants of R2 ≥ 0.8 within each dataset for the purpose of noise reduction. Genotyped and imputed variants were all mapped to the GRCh138/hg38 human genome build.

Single variant-based association analysis on datasets of unrelated cases and CNEs were performed in SNPTEST [63] using score-based logistic regression under an additive model with covariate adjustment for PCs, age (defined as age-at-onset for cases and age-at-last exam for CNEs), sex, and in certain datasets, additional adjustment for study-specific indicator variables (“APOE-unadjusted”; Model 1). A second model (“APOE-adjusted”; Model 2) included covariate adjustment for dosage of APOE ε4 (0/1/2 copies), which is commonly observed as an effect modifier. Family-based datasets were analyzed using GMMAT, an R package for performing association tests using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs). These GLMMs allow for both a binary outcome and adjustment for relatedness via a genetic relationship matrix (GRM). Score tests were performed for each genetic variant assuming an additive model with fixed effect covariate adjustment for PCs, age (defined as age-at-onset for cases and age-at-last exam for CNEs), sex, and random effect adjustment for the GRM (calculated using GEMMA) in the family-based datasets. After association analysis on imputed data, variants with regression coefficient of |β|> 5 and any erroneous estimates (negative standard errors or P-values equal to 0 or 1) were excluded from further analysis. Within-study association results for variants common (MAF > 0.01) to at least one study were meta-analyzed using a fixed-effects approach with inverse variance-weighting in METAL [64].

Rare variant association and meta-analysis was performed for individual variants using the SeqMeta [65] package in R [66]. SeqMeta performs a score-based logistic regression, estimating scores in individuals using ‘prepScores()’ and performing meta-analysis using ‘singleSNPMeta()’. Family-based datasets were analyzed using GMMAT as described previously for common variants and no datasets with fewer than 100 cases and/or CNEs were analyzed. As in common variant analyses, models evaluated included covariate adjustment for PCs. As for common variants, any SNPs with a regression coefficient of |β|> 5 and any erroneous estimates were excluded from further analysis after meta-analysis of imputed rare variants.

All cross-GSG meta-analyses were performed using the Han-Eskin random-effects (REHE) model as implemented in METASOFT [67], which is optimized for the detection of cross-population associations in the presence of effect heterogeneity between GSG groups. The REHE model has similar power to a fixed-effects model when effect heterogeneity between GSG groups is modest. Cross-GSG meta-analyses incorporated within-GSG genome-wide summary statistics from the single variant fixed-effects meta-analyses described above for NHW, AFA, HIS, and EAS GSGs (SNPTEST/GMMAT METAL for common variants [MAF ≥ 0.01]; SeqMeta/GMMAT for rare variants [MAF < 0.01]). In the primary cross-GSG analyses, all variants with a within-GSG MAF > 0.01 in at least one dataset were incorporated into analyses.

It should be noted that, in the cross-GSG meta-analysis, P-values are derived from the REHE model. This model addresses the limitations of the traditional RE model by relaxing the conservative assumption that heterogeneity is always present, even under the null hypothesis. Odds ratios and confidence intervals reported here were produced using a traditional RE model.

We used all annotations in functional genomics repository (FILER) [68] for our analyses. To reveal potential biological functions of the variants, experimental studies have shown that matching tissues/cell types to the phenotype of interest is one of the keys to success. Thus, we focused our analyses on the brain-related tracks harmonized in FILER. These brain-related tracks originated from ROADMAP [26] (for enhancer and active histone marks), Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE [69]; active histone marks), and HOMER [28] (predicted TFBSs). We annotated the five-novel genome-wide significant signals (cross-GSG and GSG-specific results) using brain-related tracks harmonized in FILER. By annotating variants with these various functional genomics data, we identified variants with converging functional evidence and determined which variants had a higher chance of being functional in the brain.

We followed up SNPS with genome-wide significant associations (GWS; P < 5 × 10−8). If the GWS SNP is intergenic, we selected closest genes of the SNP. If the GWS SNP resides in the gene, we selected the gene. The selected genes were assessed for differential expression between AD and control brains from prefrontal cortex tissue specimens of 208 participants (64 autopsy-confirmed AD cases and 129 controls) of the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) and Boston University Alzheimer’s Disease Center (BUADC). Details of diagnosis, data cleaning, and analysis methods were previously reported [29, 70]. In brief, differential gene expression between AD and control brains was performed using the LIMMA [71, 72] software. Expression of a gene was compared in AD and control brains using linear regression models including the log2-transformed normalized expression values and terms for age at death (AAD), sex, and RNA integrity number (RIN). We also evaluated associations of gene expression for Braak staging for neurofibrillary tangles and the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer Disease (CERAD) semi-quantitative criteria for neuritic plaques (CERAD Score) [73]. Values for each trait were adjusted for AAD and sex, and the residuals were rank-transformed as previously described [74]. Association of log2-transformed expression levels for each rank-transformed neuropathological trait was evaluated using linear regression models that adjusted for RIN in the FHS/BUADC dataset.

For pathway analysis, we selected flanking genes of suggestive SNPs with P < 10−6 from cross-GSG meta-analysis and each within-GSG meta-analysis. We conducted pathway analysis using GO Biological Process in the EnrichR program [75]. For cross-GSG signals, we compared significantly enriched pathways after FDR-corrected P-value (Padjusted < 0.05) between APOE-unadjusted and -adjusted models. For each analysis model, we created Venn diagrams of pathways with Padjusted < 0.05 from each GSG. We reported both unique and shared pathways across different populations. We also conducted pathway analysis using suggestive genes with P < 10−6 from both APOE ε4-unadjusted and -adjusted models after excluding known AD genes from previous NHW studies.
