# PI3Kγ in B cells promotes antibody responses and generation of antibody-secreting cells.
**Authors:** Lanahan SM, Yang L, Jones KM, Qi Z, Cabrera EC, Cominsky LY, Ramaswamy A, Barmada A, Gabernet G, Uthaya Kumar DB, Xu L, Shan P, Wymann MP, Kleinstein SH, Rao VK, Mustillo P, Romberg N, Abraham RS, Lucas CL
**Journal:** Nat Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41590-024-01890-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41590-024-01890-1)

## Content

The differentiation of naive and memory B cells into antibody-secreting cells (ASCs) is a key feature of adaptive immunity. The requirement for phosphoinositide 3-kinase-delta (PI3Kδ) to support B cell biology has been investigated intensively; however, specific functions of the related phosphoinositide 3-kinase-gamma (PI3Kγ) complex in B lineage cells have not. In the present study, we report that PI3Kγ promotes robust antibody responses induced by T cell-dependent antigens. The inborn error of immunity caused by human deficiency in PI3Kγ results in broad humoral defects, prompting our investigation of roles for this kinase in antibody responses. Using mouse immunization models, we found that PI3Kγ functions cell intrinsically within activated B cells in a kinase activity-dependent manner to transduce signals required for the transcriptional program supporting differentiation of ASCs. Furthermore, ASC fate choice coincides with upregulation of PIK3CG expression and is impaired in the context of PI3Kγ disruption in naive B cells on in vitro CD40-/cytokine-driven activation, in memory B cells on toll-like receptor activation, or in human tonsillar organoids. Taken together, our study uncovers a fundamental role for PI3Kγ in supporting humoral immunity by integrating signals instructing commitment to the ASC fate.

Production of high-affinity, class-switched immunoglobulins by antibody secreting cells (ASCs) is integral for protection from infectious diseases and can be generated without or with the help of T cells. Class switch recombination (CSR) from the IgM isotype to the class-switched isotype is critical for a proper effector response and is driven by signals B cells receive from T cells in conjunction with the cytokine milieu. ASC- and memory B cell-producing factories, organized as germinal centers (GCs), are formed in secondary lymphoid tissues to support robust, high-affinity T-dependent antibody responses. These structures facilitate B/T interactions in the light zone and rapid B cell proliferation in the dark zone. This elegant process supports B cell receptor (BCR) affinity maturation via somatic hypermutation (SHM) and the generation of long-lived, ASCs that home to survival niches primarily in the bone marrow and gut lamina propria.1–3 By contrast, T-independent responses are often dominated by more innate-like B cell subsets – marginal zone B cells that have basally elevated metabolism, leaving them poised to readily differentiate without T cell help, and B1 B cells that can spontaneously produce Igs.4–7 T-independent immunizations yield lower-affinity antibody responses dominated by shorter-lived IgM ASCs when compared to T-dependent challenges.8

T-dependent B cell responses involve coordinated cellular interactions through (1) antigen recognition by the B cell receptor of naïve B cells, (2) internalization of antigen for processing and peptide presentation by major histocompatibility complex II (MHC-II), (3) activation of CD4+ helper T cells recognizing the peptide:MHCII complex and reciprocal T cell help to the B cell via co-stimulation (i.e., CD40L/CD40) and cytokines (i.e., IL-21, IL-4), and (4) B cell differentiation into effector cells such as memory B cells or ASCs. ASC differentiation occurs after stimulation strong enough to induce pro-ASC transcription factors (e.g., IRF4) to supplant their negative regulators (e.g., IRF8) in a transcriptional arms-race.9 Other ASC transcription factors such as BLIMP-1 are then induced and lead to chromatin remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, and a persisting suppression of core transcriptional operators of alternative B cell fates.10–12

In addition to pro-ASC transcription factor upregulation, sufficient signals drive B cells to metabolically reprogram and augment their unfolded protein response (UPR), in part via phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)- and mTOR-driven ribosomal protein S6 phosphorylation, to support ASC differentiation.13 Marginal zone (MZ) B cells are responsible for rapid, low-affinity antibody responses to bloodborne pathogens and have basally elevated S6 phosphorylation and UPR gene expression, poising them to readily differentiate into ASCs upon stimulation.4,6 Conversely, follicular B cells mediate high-affinity antibody responses to pathogens and require integration of complex inputs. These cues are temporally and spatially regulated signals received during the GC response to support productive B cell activation, growth, and metabolic reprogramming.14,15 The precise molecular circuitry responsible for production of high-affinity class switched ASCs during T-dependent B cell responses remain incompletely defined.

The phosphotyrosine-activated PI3Kδ complex is well established as a critical signal transducer supporting B cell biology and the antibody response.16–18 Class I PI3Ks convert phosphatidylinositol(4,5)-bisphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol(3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3), a second messenger that recruits pleckstrin homology (PH) domain-containing proteins such as AKT to promote their activation and downstream signaling.19,20 The class of PIP3-producing PI3Ks is divided into class IA (PI3Kα/β/δ), classically activated upon recruitment to inducible phosphotyrosine residues and small GTPase Ras, and class IB (PI3Kγ), classically activated by G proteins and small GTPases.21 Expression of PI3Kδ and PI3Kγ is enriched in cells of the hematopoietic system, making the complexes largely functional in immune cells.22 Previous work has established the general paradigm that PI3Kδ is the major kinase supporting lymphocyte responses downstream of antigen (TCR/BCR) and costimulatory receptors, while PI3Kγ functions predominantly in neutrophils, macrophages, and to some extent T cells downstream of receptors for chemokines, soluble complement fragments, small molecule inflammatory mediators, and microbial products (toll-like receptors).23,24 Indeed, it has been shown that B cell development is disrupted in PI3Kδ knockout (KO) mice, but is normal in PI3Kγ KO mice, and PI3Kγ KO B cells respond normally to chemotactic migratory cues.16,25,26

In the present study, we describe a previously unknown pivotal role for PI3Kγ in B cells for supporting de novo generation of ASCs in response to T-dependent challenges. PI3Kγ facilitates induction of ASC-driving transcription factors by supporting kinase-dependent signal integration at the stage when activated B cells downregulate surface BCR (a major PI3Kδ activator) and adopt an ASC fate. This function is conserved from mice to humans and elucidates a novel ASC gatekeeping function with broad relevance for basic and translational immunology.

We previously described the first patient with monogenic PI3Kγ deficiency causing a primary immunodeficiency with humoral defects and lung/gut inflammation.27 We found that human PI3Kγ deficiency causes humoral defects from an early age, particularly in IgG, with normal levels of IgM and near-normal levels of IgA (Figure 1a and Extended Figure 1a–b). A normal response elicited by the tetanus vaccine but weak responses to other, classically weaker, immunizations was observed (Supplementary Table 1). Based on these observations, we hypothesized a previously unrecognized, fundamental role for PI3Kγ in class-switched antibody responses, which we sought to investigate using mouse models.

The PI3Kγ complex is comprised of the p110γ catalytic subunit (encoded by Pik3cg) and the p84/p101 regulatory subunit (encoded by Pik3r6/Pik3r5).28,29 We first utilized animals with disruption of Pik3cg as total PI3Kγ knockout (KO) mice to assess T-dependent (TD) antibody responses to 4-hydroxy-3-nitrophenylacetyl hapten conjugated to ovalbumin (NP-OVA) as an immunogen.23 We observed PI3Kγ KO mice had trending lower levels of antigen-specific IgM at 5 days and modestly lower IgG1 at 12 days after initial challenge with NP-OVA using alum adjuvant (Extended Figure 1c–d) and pronounced reduction in antigen-specific IgG1, both total and high-affinity, as well as IgG2c 14 days after boost (Figure 1b–c and Extended Figure 1e). We further immunized mice with NP-OVA using an alternative adjuvant, CpG, and similarly observed reduced antigen-specific total and high-affinity IgG1 and IgG2b responses (Figure 1d–e and Extended Figure 1f). These defective T-dependent antibody responses were not associated with alterations in development of follicular B cells, which were at normal levels in PI3Kγ KO mice, while transitional B cells were slightly reduced and MZ B cells were slightly increased (Figure 1f–i), consistent with published findings.25 Overall frequencies of IgM ASCs present at steady state were also normal (Figure 1j). Thus, PI3Kγ promotes optimal antibody responses induced by T-dependent antigen challenge.

We next assessed readouts of the germinal center (GC) as the site of T-B collaboration for TD antibody responses. To survey the cellular landscape and assess CSR and SHM, we immunized PI3Kγ KO mice and wild-type controls subcutaneously with NP-Ova/alum three times for strong GC induction and assessed enriched B cell populations from draining lymph nodes on Day 6 using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) with BCR repertoire sequencing. After quality control and processing, we identified 19 distinct cell clusters (Figure 2a–b) present in both genotypes of mice and designed the experiment to focus on BCR characteristics rather than cell frequencies (see Methods) or gene expression (assessed separately by bulk RNA-seq). To ensure sufficient and representative cell numbers for BCR comparisons, we combined the various subsets of naïve B cells as well as the different subsets of GC B cells (Extended Figure 2a) and found that PI3Kγ deficiency had no major impact on class switching or mutation frequency (Figure 2c–d), thereby excluding the possibility that TD IgG responses are low in the context of PI3Kγ deficiency due to CSR defects. We observed a trend toward an increased frequency of GC B cells with the W38L mutation conferring high affinity for NP (Extended Figure 2b), consistent with the possibility that PI3Kγ deficiency results in increased rounds of affinity maturation of GC B cells.30,31 In order to obtain deeper gene expression analyses, we next immunized PI3Kγ KO mice and heterozygous littermate controls with sheep red blood cells (SRBC) to induce robust and persistent GCs then performed bulk RNA sequencing.32 Consistent with similar frequencies in unchallenged mice described above, follicular and marginal zone B cell subsets were present at similar frequencies between genotypes in the spleen after immunization (Extended Figure 2c–d). While sorted naïve B cells had minimal gene expression differences (Extended Figure 2e), we observed markedly different transcriptional profiles of sorted B220+IgD−GL7+CD95+ GC B cells from spleens of mice 28 days after immunization with SRBC (Figures 2e and Extended Figure 2f). Broad transcriptional aberrations were observed in PI3Kγ KO cell populations, with genes mapping to GC B cells higher and those mapping to pre-ASC cell fates lower in KO compared to heterozygous control mice (Figure 2e). Prominent among the defective gene inductions in PI3Kγ KO GC B cells was the transcription factor IRF4, which is required for ASC fate commitment, repression of BCL-6 and reinforcement of BLIMP1 and the UPR response that supports ASC differentiation (Figure 2e). For terminal differentiation into ASCs, B cells must upregulate the unfolded protein response (UPR) to sustain the high rate of immunoglobulin protein production, and we observed by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) that expression of genes involved in UPR as well as in mTORC1 pathways were significantly reduced in PI3Kγ KO GC B cells (Figure 2f). The UPR response during the transition of activated B cells to ASCs has been shown to occur in two waves, with the first wave occurring in activated B cells and the second wave being specific for ASCs.6 We observed defective upregulation of “ASC UPR” transcripts, while “Activated UPR” genes were not defective in PI3Kγ KO GC B cells (Extended Figure 2g). Taken together, our data from in vivo GC B cells are consistent with PI3Kγ playing an essential role in integrating signals that drive cell biological and transcriptional changes as GC B cells transition to the ASC fate (Extended Figure 2h). In the absence of PI3Kγ, GC B cells form and undergo CSR and SHM but fail to properly differentiate into ASCs.

We next sought to determine which immune cell types involved in TD responses require Pik3cg expression for IgG responses in vivo. To do this, we generated conditional KO mice utilizing previously published Pik3cg floxed mice that we crossed to four different cell type-specific Cre lines to delete the gene in B cells, T cells, macrophages/neutrophils, or dendritic cells and challenged with NP-OVA immunization.33 Control mice (either expressing Cre recombinase without homozygous floxed alleles or Cre-negative with Pik3cg floxed) had levels of antigen-specific antibodies comparable to wild-type control mice post-immunization. The most striking and consistent IgG defects were observed in mice that lacked PI3Kγ conditionally in B cells from early in development (Mb1-Cre) (Figure 3a–b and Extended Figure 3a). We observed modest and less consistent reductions in antigen-specific IgG antibodies in mice with PI3Kγ conditionally knocked out in T cells (CD4-Cre) or macrophages/neutrophils (LysM-Cre) and no defect in antigen-specific IgG in mice with PI3Kγ conditionally knocked out in dendritic cells (CD11c-Cre) (Figure 3a–b and Extended Figure 3a). We therefore conclude that B cell-intrinsic PI3Kγ is required to support IgG responses.

To further dissect mechanisms underlying defective ASC generation, we quantified GC B cells and observed trending low frequencies that were not significant between PI3Kγ KO and control mice (Figures 3c and Extended Figure 3b). Consistent with this finding, B cells from PI3Kγ KO mice proliferated and upregulated the CD69/CD86 activation markers normally in response to both TD (BCR/CD40) and TI (CpG and LPS) stimulation in vitro (Extended Figure 3c–h). To further assess GC, memory, and antibody-secreting cells, we analyzed Peyer’s patches at steady state and observed PI3Kγ KO mice exhibited slightly reduced light-zone and increased dark-zone GC B cells, unperturbed IgA+ B cells reflecting memory, and reduced total CD138+ ASCs (Figures 3d–e and Extended Figure 3i–n). Consistent with this, fecal IgA levels were unperturbed in animals with PI3Kγ KO B cells (Extended Figure 3o). Since IgA responses were normal, we focused on immunization-induced IgG responses in our subsequent analyses. SRBC-induced IgG+ memory and GC B cell induction were unaffected by loss of PI3Kγ (Extended Figure 3p), consistent with normal CSR. Moreover, GC B cells could be induced in-vitro at similar frequencies when treated with PI3Kγ inhibitor IPI-549 (Extended Figure 3q–r).34 Thus, the defective antibody responses from PI3Kγ deficiency particularly impact IgG (rather than IgM or IgA) responses, can be recapitulated by B lineage cell-specific PI3Kγ deficiency, and are not due to activation defects.

Because our ex vivo RNA sequencing and Peyer’s patch phenotyping data pointed to PI3Kγ supporting GC B cells differentiating into ASCs, we next assessed the in vivo effect of deleting Pik3cg after activation using Cγ1-Cre mice. Indeed, antigen-specific antibody responses were blunted when PI3Kγ was genetically disrupted after activated B cells begin expressing Cγ1 (Figure 4a–b). These data support a model in which PI3Kγ plays a requisite cell-intrinsic role after B cell activation to transduce signals required for ASC differentiation and robust antibody responses. Consistent with this, we observe upon reanalysis of publicly available expression data from Monaco et al. for human transcripts and in our own RNAseq datasets for mouse transcripts that PIK3CG is expressed in B cell subsets and upregulated in ASCs,35 whereas PIK3CD is expressed at lower levels in ASCs (Figures 4c–d).36 Similar findings were true in data from the Immunological Genome Project (Immgen) for mouse transcripts (Extended Figure 4a).37 Additionally, innate-like B cell populations (MZ and B1 B cells) express lower levels of Pik3cg than follicular and GC B cells (Extended Figure 4b). Rapid differentiation of B1 and/or MZ B cells into ASCs occurs after challenge with highly repetitive antigens (T-independent type-I) or mitogens in T-Independent responses (T-independent type-II).8 We immunized PI3Kγ-deficient mice with NP-Ficoll and NP-LPS to assess the role for this gene in T-independent immunizations and found no difference in antigen-specific IgM 5 days after immunization for either of the immunizations (Extended Figure 4c–d). Thus, PI3Kγ is upregulated when activated B cells differentiate into ASCs, a time also marked by loss of surface-expressed PI3Kδ-linked BCR uniquely in IgG− but not IgM− or IgA-expressing cells and is dispensable for T-independent IgM responses mediated by innate-like B cells.38

Given our observation that genetic deletion of Pik3cg after B cell activation was sufficient to result in IgG defects, we next tested the prediction that pharmacologic inhibition of PI3Kγ in the days following TD immunization would similarly yield IgG defects. Indeed, the small molecule inhibitor, IPI-549, administered in-vivo by oral gavage 3 days after primary immunization reduced IgG ASC induction at 12 days (Figures 4e–f and Extended Figure 4e–f). Since we observed that genetic PI3Kγ deficiency or its pharmacological inhibition results in class-switched antibody defects and failure to generate antigen-specific ASCs, we next sought to directly measure ASC differentiation using in vitro systems. Indeed, purified splenic murine B cells cultured with the T-dependent stimuli anti-CD40 and cytokines (IL-4 and IL-5) that are known to promote proliferation, class-switch recombination, and ASC differentiation exhibited defective differentiation into ASCs when PI3Kγ activity was genetically or pharmacologically disrupted (Figure 4g–i). Utilizing the Blimp1-YFP reporter to mark early (Blimp1−CD138+) and fully differentiated (Blimp1+CD138+) ASCs, we similarly observed PI3Kγ activity was required optimal generation of fully differentiated ASCs (Extended Figure 4g–h). Thus, PI3Kγ is required for optimal TD ASC differentiation, despite being dispensable for early activation and proliferation.

To extend our findings to human cells and further dissect mechanisms, we next assessed the impact of PI3Kγ in human ASC differentiation. Human tonsillar organoids were first used to validate our findings further using a physiologically relevant system that recapitulates complex interactions and GC formation to induce plasmablast differentiation.39,40 Upon culturing tonsillar organoids from five human donors for 7 days with varying concentrations of the PI3Kγ inhibitor IPI-549, we observed a dose-dependent reduction in plasmablast generation (Figure 5a). There were no significant changes in percent viable CD19+ B cells or CD38+ plasmablasts and minimal reduction in CD4+ T cell viability, with normal proportions of CD4+ T follicular helper cells (Extended Figure 5a–h) in the organoid cultures. Next, we stimulated purified pan-B cells from healthy human peripheral blood with trimeric human CD40L and a cocktail of cytokines without or with PI3Kγ inhibition and observed a significant reduction in ASCs (by flow cytometric assessment of CD27+CD38+ cells and by ELISPOT for IgG) when PI3Kγ was inhibited (Figure 5b–e). Given the importance of secondary ASCs generated when memory B cells re-encounter stimulation, we next sorted CD27+ memory B cells from healthy human peripheral blood and stimulated them with the human TLR7/TLR8 agonist R848 to induce differentiation into ASCs, and again we observed a requirement for PI3Kγ for ASC generation (Extended Figure 5f–h).41 These findings are consistent with the larger magnitude of IgG defect observed in mice after booster immunizations compared to after primary immunization.

As PI3Kγ is a class I PI3K that induces PIP3, we hypothesized that PI3Kγ plays a non-redundant role in providing this key second messenger as activated B cells transition to ASCs and, therefore, that the differentiation defect could be rescued through experimental induction of PIP3 through other means. To test this, we employed the recently described small molecule activator of the class I PI3Kα expressed broadly, including in B-lineage cells.42 Indeed, we observed a partial rescue in the differentiation of human pan-B cells into CD27+CD38+ ASCs utilizing this PIP3 inducer (Figures 5i and Extended Figure 5i). Since we observed no effect of overnight PI3Kγ inhibition on immunoglobulin secretion by existing ASC (Extended Figure 5j–k), we conclude that PI3Kγ is particularly required for the differentiation of activated B cells into ASCs rather than maintenance of their secretory function. This is in line with existing data that similarly showed mTOR inhibition with rapamycin inhibits newly generated ASCs but not existing IgG-secreting cells.13 Lastly, to discern mechanisms contributing to ASC differentiation, we turned back to our sorted GC B cell bulk RNA-seq and performed ChIP-X Enrichment Analysis 3 (CHEA3) analysis. We observed that IRF4 scored as one of the strongest drivers of gene expression differences in the sorted GC B cells (Figure 5j). Thus, we tested the functional link between PI3Kγ activity and IRF4 induction in the human pan-B cell differentiation culture and observed defective induction (Figure 5k–l), thereby offering a mechanistic basis for the observed requirement of this kinase in ASC differentiation. Taken together, our in vitro data validate our in vivo findings and demonstrate that PI3Kγ activity is required for the transition of GC B cells to ASCs to support IgG antibody responses.

Our prior discovery of human PI3Kγ deficiency27 illuminated unexpected humoral immunity defects, including hypogammaglobulinemia and poor antibody responses to vaccination. These new observations prompted us to further assess roles for PI3Kγ in the antibody response, as the vast majority of prior work on PI3Kγ had focused on its roles in cellular responses primarily in innate immune cells and T lymphocytes. Utilizing a combination of in vivo immunizations in genetically engineered mouse models and in vitro studies, we now report novel functions of PI3Kγ cell-intrinsically in B cells after activation that support the transcriptional and functional switch of activated B cells to the ASC fate for robust production of IgG antibodies in response to T-dependent immune challenges.

We demonstrate here that mice lacking PI3Kγ have defective antigen-specific antibody responses to T-dependent challenges with both type 1- (CpG) and type 2- (alum) skewing adjuvants. We observe that antibody defects increased in severity with time and re-challenge, thereby providing evidence in support of a role for PI3Kγ in ASC outputs of the germinal center response and secondary ASC production from memory B cell reactivation. By contrast, we observe that IgM responses to TI immunizations were unaffected in mice lacking PI3Kγ. This finding is consistent with literature showing that innate-like B cells have elevated levels of mTOR/S6 signaling at baseline, partially due to nutrient sensing, and are less responsive to inhibitors of the PI3K/mTOR axis.6,43 An additional previously reported case of a patient with immune dysregulation and potentially hypomorphic PI3Kγ had normal serum IgG levels, suggesting sufficient kinase activity remained for normal antibody induction in this patient.44 Prior literature has also shown that PI3Kγ KO mice mount defective antigen-specific IgG1 responses after immunization in the footpad. However, PI3Kγ knockout B cells were shown to be able to proliferate normally in-vitro upon IgM cross linking (a finding we reproduced here), and effects on other immune cell subsets were presumed to be responsible for reduced IgG1 induction.24 In mouse models of rheumatoid arthritis, pharmacologic inhibition of PI3Kγ reduced disease severity45, and in a model of systemic lupus erythematous, pharmacologic inhibition of PI3Kγ was found to reduce pathogenic autoantibodies.46 In both instances, the prevailing hypothesis was that PI3Kγ was acting by reducing innate cell recruitment and T cell help to support B cell responses. Our findings using mice with conditional deletion of the Pik3cg gene in B cells, T cells, macrophages/neutrophils, or dendritic cells now demonstrate a B cell-intrinsic requirement for PI3Kγ. Additionally, mice lacking PI3Kγ in T cells or macrophages exhibited a more moderate defect in antibody responses. Further investigation of these T cell- and myeloid cell-intrinsic roles of PI3Kγ in the antibody response is warranted, particularly in light of the established function of PI3Kγ in T cell activation and macrophage migration.24 Despite a role for PI3Kγ in DC migration47,48, we found that PI3Kγ is dispensable in DCs for class-switched antibody induction. Together, these findings are important because they demonstrate that PI3Kγ functions directly in B cells to promote optimal class-switched, T-dependent antibody responses.

B cells must become activated, begin dividing, and properly receive and integrate cues required to turn on the transcriptional drivers of ASC differentiation. In TD responses, germinal centers act as a hub for this process to occur. The resulting gene expression leads to metabolic and functional changes that define ASCs and are required for the high levels of immunoglobulin production by these terminally differentiated cells.1 Prior research has shown that germinal center B cells become desensitized as they are activated, and it has been proposed that, because of this desensitization, only the B cells with the highest primary (BCR) and secondary (cytokine, co-stimulatory, and nutrient) cues will differentiate to antibody-secreting cells.14,15 Our results demonstrate normal B cell activation, CSR, SHM, and memory B cell formation and normal (trending low) GC B cell production when PI3Kγ is deficient. Transcriptomes of GC B cells from PI3Kγ-deficient mice exhibit marked gene expression differences that map to programs for activated GC B cells versus pre-ASC GC B cells49, with the latter (including IRF4, BLIMP1, XBP1, etc.) being defective when PI3Kγ is lacking. Moreover, in GC B cells, UPR genes found in activated B cells were expressed at comparable levels while UPR genes characteristic of ASCs were lower in PI3Kγ KO GC B cells.50 Notably, mTORC1 supports metabolic reprogramming and was also defective in GC B cells from PI3Kγ KO mice, consistent with critical roles for this pathway in supporting B cell responses.6,51 These data, together with the upregulation of PI3Kγ and downregulation of PI3Kδ as B cells become ASCs, point to a molecular switch in B cells whereby they acquire a dependence on PI3Kγ for commitment to the ASC fate. The timing for this molecular switch also raises the hypothesis that there may be a potential relevance at the time B cells downregulate surface BCR (a major PI3Kδ activator) as they become ASCs. Indeed, we hypothesize that the level of surface BCR that is maintained as activated B cells become ASCs is a key determinant of whether PI3Kγ is required for ASC differentiation. Both IgM and IgA ASCs maintain signaling-competent surface BCR expression, and IgM and IgA antibody responses remain intact in PI3Kγ KO contexts under the conditions tested. However, IgG ASCs do not maintain surface BCR expression, and it is these cells that require PI3Kγ as an additional driver of downstream signals to support their differentiation into IgG-producing ASCs.38 Thus, we propose that production of sufficient levels of PIP3 during the transition to ASCs, while surface IgG BCR signals to PI3Kδ are waning, is a key function of PI3Kγ in B cells to support IRF4 induction and IgG+ ASC differentiation.

As our data pointed to key functions for PI3Kγ after B cell activation, we predicted deletion of Pik3cg after activation would be sufficient to cause antibody defects. Indeed, using a Cγ1-cre conditional Pik3cg knockout mouse model to delete the kinase after activation, we observed reduced antigen-specific antibody titers. Moreover, acute inhibition using a clinically relevant PI3Kγ small molecule inhibitor, IPI-549, could recapitulate ASC differentiation defects in vitro (upon CD40/TLR and cytokine stimulation and in a tonsillar organoid model) and in vivo (TD immunization), even when administration of inhibitor was initiated after activation (Day 3).52,53 However, fully differentiated ASCs no longer required PI3Kγ for their secretory phenotype, as there was no impact of acute kinase inhibition on Ig production by purified ASCs. We recognize the translationally relevant therapeutic relevance of our findings for modulation of GC-dependent antibody responses utilizing pharmacological modulators of PI3Kγ. Many of the signals received by B-lineage cells during their trajectory toward differentiation into ASCs converge on AKT/mTOR signaling, and our findings illuminate PI3Kγ as a key regulator of the humoral immune response by critically supporting the transition from an activated B cell to an ASC to enable robust class-switched antibody responses.

Briefly, the transcriptome data were processed using CellRanger and aligned to the mm10 mouse reference genome. CITE-seq barcodes were used to identify cells belonging to each mouse and genotype. After data alignment, quality control assessment was performed by filtering out genes expressed in fewer than three cells and cells with fewer than 200 genes or more than 10% mitochondrial gene fraction. Thereafter, the filtered data were integrated and batch effects corrected using single-cell variational inference (scVI).60 scVI’s latent representation was used to compute the neighborhood graph for Louvain clustering and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization. Data processing was performed using the Scanpy toolkit.61 Data were normalized (scaling factor = 104) and log-transformed before downstream analysis. Using Louvain clustering with an initial resolution of three, clusters were defined using the expression of published cell-specific gene markers. For ambiguous clusters, differential gene expression analysis by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test compared with the rest of the clusters was performed to guide unbiased labeling of cell identity.

The scBCR-seq data was processed with 10x genomics Cell Ranger vdj. The AIRR rearrangement table was then split according to the sample-specific antibody barcodes and further processed for immunoglobulin gene assignment and clonal analysis with nf-core/airrflow v3.1.0 (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.8007974). Immunoglobulin V(D)J and C gene assignment was performed with IgBLAST v1.1962 with a mouse C57BL/6 reference data retrieved from the ImMunoGeneTics Information System63 and prepared as previously described.64 Change-O v1.3.0 was used to call IgBLAST and format the alignments into the AIRR rearrangement format.65 The aligned sequences were filtered to include only productive sequences with at least 200 informative positions and less than 10% N nucleotides. Cells with multiple heavy chain sequences or with only light chain sequences were removed. Clonal assignment was performed with SCOPer v1.2.1 with the hierarchicalClones function.66 B-cell clones were defined as cells sharing the IGHV and IGHJ gene calls and junction length, as well as 90% junction nucleotide similarity according to the hamming distance metric. Clonal abundance and V-gene usage was calculated with Alakazam v1.2.1, and somatic hypermutation rates were calculated with SHazaM v1.1.2.65,67 Figures and statistical analysis were performed with custom scripts in R v4.3.1 deposited under bitbucket.org/kleinstein/projects (Lanahan2023 folder). We note that for 3 mice per group the Wilcoxon rank sum test lowest possible p-value is 0.1.
