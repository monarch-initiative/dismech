# Ebola virus disease in the modern era: pathogenesis, therapeutic advances, vaccine strategies, and global health preparedness.
**Authors:** Sundeep D, Umadevi K, Masood J, Tummala N
**Journal:** Infection (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s15010-026-02924-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s15010-026-02924-6)

## Content

Ebola virus disease (EVD) remains one of the most severe and complex viral hemorrhagic fevers confronting global public health systems. Since its first recognized emergence in 1976, recurrent outbreaks caused primarily by Zaire ebolavirus have demonstrated the extraordinary epidemic potential of filoviruses, particularly in regions with fragile healthcare infrastructure. The unprecedented West African epidemic of 2013-2016 fundamentally altered scientific understanding of Ebola virus transmission, pathogenesis, clinical progression, and outbreak management. Subsequent outbreaks in the Democratic Republic of Congo and Uganda have further emphasized the persistent risk posed by Ebola virus disease despite advances in diagnostics, therapeutics, and vaccination strategies. Ebola virus infection is characterized by extensive immune dysregulation, aberrant inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, coagulopathy, and progressive multiorgan injury. Recent studies have significantly expanded understanding of viral persistence, post-Ebola syndrome, host immune responses, and mechanisms of viral immune evasion. Parallel developments in molecular diagnostics, genomic surveillance, monoclonal antibody therapy, and recombinant vaccine platforms have substantially improved survival outcomes and epidemic control measures. Although India has not experienced endemic Ebola outbreaks, globalization and increasing international mobility have heightened the possibility of imported infections, necessitating robust preparedness frameworks. Following the West African epidemic, India strengthened airport surveillance, laboratory biosafety capacity, integrated disease surveillance systems, and emergency outbreak-response protocols. However, substantial challenges remain regarding rapid diagnostics, healthcare infrastructure, biosafety training, and public awareness. This review critically examines current advances in Ebola virus epidemiology, molecular biology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, diagnostics, therapeutics, vaccine development, and public health preparedness, with particular emphasis on implications for India and future global health security.

Introduction
Ebola virus disease (EVD) is a highly pathogenic zoonotic 
infection caused by viruses belonging to the genus Ebola-
virus within the family Filoviridae [1]. The disease is rec -
ognized as one of the most lethal viral illnesses affecting 
humans, characterized by severe systemic inflammation, 
vascular injury, coagulation abnormalities, and multior -
gan dysfunction [ 2]. Since the first documented outbreaks 
in Sudan and Zaire in 1976, Ebola virus has remained an 
important threat to global health security because of its 
high case fatality rates, epidemic potential, and ability to 
rapidly destabilize healthcare systems [ 1–3]. For several 
decades, Ebola outbreaks were largely confined to remote 
regions of Central Africa and were regarded as geographi -
cally restricted zoonotic events. This perception changed 
dramatically during the 2013–2016 West African epidemic, 
which evolved into the largest Ebola outbreak in recorded 
history [ 4]. The epidemic exposed major deficiencies in 
international outbreak preparedness and demonstrated how 
delayed recognition, weak surveillance systems, population 
mobility, and inadequate healthcare infrastructure can accel-
erate the spread of highly pathogenic infectious diseases 
across national borders. More importantly, the epidemic 
transformed Ebola virus disease from a regional public 
health issue into a global biosurveillance priority [5, 6].
The expanding frequency of Ebola outbreaks over recent 
decades has intensified scientific interest in the molecular 
biology and pathogenesis of ebolaviruses. Although earlier 
investigations focused primarily on hemorrhagic manifesta-
tions, contemporary research has revealed that severe Ebola 
virus infection is driven predominantly by dysregulated host 
immune responses, endothelial injury, cytokine-mediated 
inflammation, and progressive organ dysfunction [ 7]. Viral 
proteins such as VP24 and VP35 interfere extensively with 
interferon-mediated antiviral signaling, enabling uncon -
trolled viral replication during the early stages of infection. 
Simultaneously, infected monocytes, macrophages, and 
dendritic cells amplify inflammatory cascades that contrib -
ute substantially to vascular leakage, coagulation abnormal-
ities, and shock [8].
The clinical management of Ebola virus disease has also 
evolved significantly over the last decade. Historically, 
treatment strategies relied almost exclusively on supportive 
care, including fluid replacement and hemodynamic stabi -
lization. However, recent advances involving monoclonal 
antibody therapies, antiviral agents, and recombinant vac -
cine platforms have substantially improved survival out -
comes in outbreak settings [9, 10]. The approval of vaccines 
such as rVSV-ZEBOV GP and monoclonal antibody thera-
pies including REGN-EB3 and mAb114 represents a major 
milestone in filovirus research and outbreak medicine. 
Despite the concentration of Ebola outbreaks within Africa, 
the increasing interconnectedness of global travel and com-
merce has elevated the risk of international dissemination 
[11]. Several imported cases reported outside Africa dur -
ing the West African epidemic highlighted the vulnerabil -
ity of densely populated countries to emerging infectious 
threats. India, although free from endemic Ebola transmis -
sion, remains susceptible to imported infections because of 
extensive international mobility, particularly from African 
nations. Consequently, the West African epidemic prompted 
Indian public health authorities to strengthen airport sur -
veillance, quarantine systems, laboratory preparedness, and 
emergency response frameworks [12].
In addition to its immediate mortality burden, Ebola 
virus disease presents significant long-term medical and 
public health challenges. Increasing evidence indicates that 
Ebola virus can persist in immune-privileged anatomical 
sites such as the testes, ocular tissues, and central nervous 
system even after apparent clinical recovery. This persis -
tence has important implications for viral transmission, 
relapse, and post-Ebola syndrome, which includes chronic 
musculoskeletal, neurological, ophthalmological, and psy -
chological complications among survivors [13]. The present 
review critically evaluates current developments in Ebola

Historical evolution and global 
epidemiology of Ebola virus disease
Discovery and early outbreaks
Ebola virus disease was first identified in 1976 during two 
simultaneous outbreaks occurring in southern Sudan and 
northern Zaire, presently known as the Democratic Repub -
lic of Congo [2, 5]. These outbreaks involved severe febrile 
illness associated with hemorrhagic manifestations and 
unusually high mortality rates. The causative agents were 
later identified as genetically distinct species, namely Sudan 
ebolavirus and Zaire ebolavirus [ 14, 15]. The latter dem -
onstrated significantly greater virulence and subsequently 
became responsible for most major outbreaks reported 
worldwide. The initial outbreaks generated substantial 
international concern because of the rapid transmission of 
infection within healthcare facilities and among close fam -
ily contacts. Reuse of contaminated needles, inadequate 
infection-control practices, and direct exposure to infected 
body fluids contributed extensively to early transmission 
dynamics [ 16, 17]. During subsequent decades, intermit -
tent outbreaks continued to occur across Central and East 
Africa, particularly in regions characterized by dense tropi-
cal forests and limited healthcare infrastructure [ 18]. The 
historical evolution of Ebola virus disease outbreaks is 
summarized in Table 1. The emergence of Ebola outbreaks 
in geographically distinct regions suggested the existence 
of widespread sylvatic reservoirs across equatorial Africa. 
Ecological investigations increasingly implicated fruit bats 
as potential natural reservoir hosts, although the precise 
mechanisms governing zoonotic spillover remain incom -
pletely understood [19, 20].
The West African epidemic and its global impact
The West African epidemic fundamentally changed scien -
tific and public health perspectives regarding Ebola virus 
disease. Unlike earlier outbreaks that remained relatively 
localized, the epidemic spread extensively across Guinea, 
Liberia, and Sierra Leone, eventually affecting densely 
populated urban centers. Weak healthcare infrastructure, 
delayed outbreak recognition, inadequate laboratory capac-
ity, and widespread population movement contributed sig -
nificantly to uncontrolled transmission [ 23]. The epidemic 
also exposed major vulnerabilities in international outbreak 
preparedness systems. Healthcare workers experienced dis-
proportionately high infection rates because of inadequate 
personal protective equipment and limited infection-control 
training. Simultaneously, public fear, misinformation, and 
distrust toward governmental health agencies complicated 
containment efforts [24, 25].
Importantly, the West African epidemic accelerated 
major advances in Ebola virus research. Rapid international 
collaboration enabled the development of novel molecular 
diagnostics, genomic surveillance approaches, monoclonal 
antibody therapies, and recombinant vaccines within an 
unprecedented timeframe [26, 27]. These scientific achieve-
ments significantly transformed the future management of 
Ebola outbreaks. The geographical distribution and progres-
sion of major Ebola outbreaks are illustrated in Fig. 1.
Recent outbreaks and re-emergence patterns
Recent outbreaks in the Democratic Republic of Congo and 
Uganda have demonstrated that Ebola virus disease remains 
a recurrent and evolving threat. Political instability, armed 
conflict, population displacement, and healthcare disruption 
have substantially complicated outbreak response efforts in 
several affected regions. Additionally, recurrent zoonotic 
spillover events suggest that ecological and environmen -
tal factors continue to facilitate periodic viral emergence

Virology and molecular biology of Ebola 
virus
Taxonomy and classification
Ebola viruses belong to the order Mononegavirales, family 
Filoviridae, and genus Ebolavirus. Members of the Filoviri-
dae family are characterized by filamentous virion mor -
phology and non-segmented negative-sense RNA genomes. 
The genus Ebolavirus currently includes six recognized 
species: Orthoebolavirus zairense, Orthoebolavirus sudae -
nse, Orthoebolavirus bundibugyoense, Orthoebolavirus 
restonense, Orthoebolavirus taiense, and Orthoebolavirus 
bombaliense [ 30, 31]. Among these, Zaire ebolavirus has 
consistently demonstrated the highest pathogenicity in 
humans and has been responsible for most large-scale out -
breaks documented to date. Although all pathogenic ebo -
laviruses produce clinically similar disease manifestations, 
variations in transmissibility, virulence, and case fatality 
rates have been observed between species [ 32]. Reston 
ebolavirus, unlike the African ebolaviruses, has not been 
associated with symptomatic disease in humans despite 
documented infections in non-human primates and swine 
populations. This observation suggests important differ -
ences in host adaptation and viral pathogenic mechanisms 
among filoviruses. Phylogenetic analyses indicate that ebo-
laviruses have evolved through long-term ecological adap -
tation within sylvatic environments. However, the precise 
evolutionary origins of these viruses remain incompletely 
resolved. Molecular epidemiological studies conducted dur-
ing recent outbreaks have demonstrated rapid viral diver -
sification during sustained human-to-human transmission, 
emphasizing the importance of genomic surveillance in out-
break monitoring and containment (Fig. 2).

coprotein (GP) plays a particularly important role in disease 
pathogenesis [33]. Surface glycoproteins facilitate host-cell

Viral entry and replication mechanisms
The Ebola virus replication cycle is highly dependent upon 
host-cell machinery and intracellular trafficking pathways. 
Viral entry begins through interactions between viral glyco-
proteins and host-cell attachment factors, including lectins 
and phosphatidylserine receptors expressed on macro -
phages, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, and hepatocytes 
[9, 34, 35]. Following attachment, Ebola virions enter host 
cells predominantly through macropinocytosis. Within 
endosomal compartments, viral glycoproteins undergo pro-
teolytic cleavage mediated by host cathepsins, enabling 
interaction with the Niemann–Pick C1 (NPC1) receptor [37, 
38]. Binding to NPC1 triggers membrane fusion and release 
of the viral ribonucleoprotein complex into the cytoplasm. 
The Ebola virus replication cycle is summarized in Fig. 5.
Immune evasion strategies
A defining characteristic of Ebola virus infection is the 
remarkable ability of the virus to suppress innate antiviral 
immunity during the early stages of infection. Two viral 
proteins, VP24 and VP35, function as potent interferon 
antagonists and are critical determinants of viral patho -
genicity. VP35 interferes with intracellular viral RNA 
sensing pathways by inhibiting activation of interferon 
regulatory factors and suppressing type I interferon produc-
tion. Simultaneously, VP24 disrupts nuclear transport of 
phosphorylated STAT1 proteins, thereby impairing down -
stream interferon signaling within infected and neighboring 
cells [40]. The combined effects of these immune evasion

Viral persistence and evolutionary adaptation
One of the most important discoveries emerging from 
recent outbreaks is the ability of Ebola virus to persist 
within immune-privileged anatomical sites even after 
apparent clinical recovery. Viral RNA and, in some cases, 
replication-competent virus have been detected within 
seminal fluid, ocular tissues, cerebrospinal fluid, and pla -
cental tissues months after acute infection resolution [ 9, 
42]. This persistence has major implications for both dis -
ease recurrence and viral transmission. Sexual transmission 
from convalescent survivors has been implicated in several 
flare-up outbreaks following periods of apparent epidemic 
control. Persistent infection within immune-privileged sites 
also contributes to post-Ebola syndrome, characterized by 
chronic neurological, ophthalmological, musculoskeletal, 
and psychological complications [43].
From an evolutionary perspective, sustained human-to-
human transmission during large outbreaks creates oppor -
tunities for adaptive viral mutations. Genomic sequencing 
studies conducted during the West African epidemic demon-
strated continuous viral diversification over time. Although 
most mutations appeared selectively neutral, concerns 
remain regarding the possibility of future adaptations influ-
encing transmissibility, virulence, or immune escape. Con -
sequently, genomic surveillance has become an essential 
component of modern Ebola outbreak management. Con -
tinuous monitoring of viral evolution not only assists epide-

Pathogenesis and immunopathology of 
Ebola virus disease
Following entry into the host through mucosal surfaces, 
parenteral exposure, or disrupted skin barriers, Ebola virus 
initially targets cells of the mononuclear phagocyte system, 
particularly monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. 
These cell populations play a central role in early viral dis -
semination because of their migratory capacity and extensive 
tissue distribution [35, 42, 47]. Productive viral replication 
within these immune cells facilitates rapid systemic spread 
through lymphatic and hematogenous pathways, allowing 
infection to extend to multiple organs including the liver, 
spleen, kidneys, adrenal glands, and vascular endothelium. 
Unlike classical cytolytic viral infections in which tissue 
destruction occurs primarily through direct viral injury, 
Ebola virus disease is characterized by a complex interac -
tion between uncontrolled viral replication and dysregulated 
host inflammatory responses. Consequently, disease sever -
ity reflects not only viral burden but also the magnitude of 
immune-mediated tissue injury that develops during pro -
gressive infection [48, 49].
A defining feature of Ebola virus pathogenesis is the 
profound disruption of innate antiviral immunity during 
the earliest stages of infection. Under normal physiological 
conditions, viral nucleic acids are recognized by intracel -
lular pattern-recognition receptors, resulting in activation 
of interferon regulatory pathways and transcription of anti -
viral genes. Ebola virus effectively suppresses these pro -
tective responses through the coordinated actions of viral 
proteins VP24 and VP35 [50, 51]. VP35 inhibits activation 
of interferon regulatory factors responsible for initiating 
type I interferon synthesis, whereas VP24 interferes with 
STAT1 nuclear translocation, thereby blocking downstream 
interferon-mediated antiviral signaling. This dual mecha -
nism enables extensive viral replication before effective 
host immune control can be established. Simultaneously, 
infected dendritic cells demonstrate impaired maturation 
and reduced antigen-presenting capacity, limiting activation 
of adaptive T-cell responses and further compromising anti-
viral immunity [52].
As viral replication intensifies, infected monocytes and 
macrophages release excessive quantities of pro-inflamma -
tory cytokines and chemokines including tumor necrosis 
factor-α, interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, macrophage inflam -
matory proteins, and nitric oxide. The resulting hyperinflam-
matory state contributes substantially to disease progression 
and represents one of the major determinants of mortality in 
severe Ebola virus disease [53–55]. Rather than functioning 
as a protective immune response, this uncontrolled cytokine 
release becomes maladaptive and promotes widespread 
endothelial dysfunction, vascular instability, and metabolic 
injury. Elevated cytokine concentrations correlate strongly 
with disease severity and fatal outcomes, suggesting that 
immune dysregulation rather than direct viral cytotoxicity 
alone is responsible for much of the tissue damage observed 
during advanced disease [54, 55].
Endothelial dysfunction constitutes a major pathological 
hallmark of severe Ebola virus infection. Although direct 
infection of endothelial cells may occur during later stages 
of disease, vascular injury appears to arise predominantly 
through indirect inflammatory mechanisms. Pro-inflamma -
tory mediators released from infected immune cells disrupt 
endothelial tight junctions, resulting in increased vascular 
permeability and leakage of intravascular fluid into sur -
rounding tissues [ 56, 57]. Concurrently, nitric oxide over -
production induces vasodilation and contributes to profound 
hypotension. These mechanisms collectively impair tissue 
perfusion and oxygen delivery, leading to progressive cir -
culatory collapse. Clinically, patients develop manifesta -
tions including edema, tachycardia, hypotension, metabolic 
acidosis, and shock. Severe gastrointestinal fluid losses 
secondary to vomiting and diarrhea further exacerbate intra-
vascular volume depletion and contribute significantly to 
hemodynamic instability [58].
Coagulation abnormalities observed in Ebola virus dis -
ease arise through complex interactions involving endothe -
lial injury, inflammatory activation, platelet dysfunction, 
and dysregulation of coagulation pathways. Infected mono-
cytes express elevated levels of tissue factor, promoting 
activation of the extrinsic coagulation cascade and initiating 
disseminated intravascular coagulation [ 59]. Progressive 
consumption of clotting factors and platelets subsequently 
predisposes patients to spontaneous hemorrhagic manifes -
tations. Although bleeding phenomena such as petechiae, 
ecchymoses, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and mucosal 
bleeding historically dominated clinical descriptions of 
Ebola virus disease, contemporary evidence indicates that 
overt hemorrhage is neither universal nor the principal 
cause of mortality in most patients. Instead, progressive 
shock, metabolic failure, and multiorgan dysfunction appear 
to represent the dominant terminal pathological processes.
As systemic injury progresses, extensive organ dys -
function develops across multiple physiological systems. 
Hepatic involvement is particularly prominent and is char -
acterized by hepatocellular necrosis, impaired protein syn -
thesis, metabolic dysregulation, and reduced production of 
coagulation factors. Elevated serum aminotransferase con -
centrations frequently correlate with disease severity and 
poor clinical outcomes. Adrenal cortical injury contributes

Neurological involvement is increasingly recognized 
as an important component of severe Ebola virus disease. 
Patients may develop encephalopathy, delirium, seizures, 
and altered mental status secondary to metabolic abnormali-
ties, inflammatory injury, vascular dysfunction, and direct 
or indirect effects on the central nervous system [62]. In sur-
vivors, persistent neurological and neuropsychiatric compli-
cations are frequently observed long after acute recovery, 
suggesting prolonged inflammatory or immunological con -
sequences of infection [ 63, 64]. One of the most important 
discoveries emerging from recent outbreaks has been the 
recognition of prolonged viral persistence following appar -
ent clinical recovery. Ebola virus RNA has been detected 
within seminal fluid, ocular tissues, cerebrospinal fluid, and 
placental tissues for months after clearance of detectable 
viremia [ 65]. Such persistence within immune-privileged 
anatomical sites has major implications for both transmis -
sion dynamics and disease recurrence. Several flare-up 
outbreaks occurring after periods of apparent epidemic 
containment have been linked to sexual transmission from 
convalescent survivors. Consequently, long-term monitor -
ing and counseling of survivors have become essential com-
ponents of modern outbreak management strategies [43].
Increasing numbers of Ebola survivors have also 
revealed the substantial burden of post-Ebola syndrome, a 
chronic multisystem disorder characterized by persistent 
fatigue, arthralgia, myalgia, visual impairment, uveitis, cog-
nitive dysfunction, depression, anxiety, and chronic pain 
syndromes. Although the precise mechanisms underlying 
these long-term complications remain incompletely under -
stood, persistent immune activation, residual tissue injury, 
and ongoing antigenic stimulation are believed to contribute 
significantly to post-recovery morbidity. As survival rates 
improve with advances in supportive care and targeted ther-
apeutics, long-term survivor management has emerged as 
an increasingly important dimension of Ebola virus disease 
research and public health planning [66, 67].
Initial cellular targets and systemic dissemination
The pathogenesis of Ebola virus disease begins immediately 
following viral entry through mucosal surfaces, broken 
skin, or direct inoculation into the bloodstream. Early viral 
replication primarily occurs within cells of the mononuclear 
phagocyte system, particularly monocytes, macrophages, 
and dendritic cells. These cells represent critical early tar -
gets because of their widespread tissue distribution and 
central role in initiating innate immune responses [68]. Fol-
lowing infection, macrophages and dendritic cells migrate 
through lymphatic and vascular pathways, facilitating 
rapid systemic dissemination of the virus. Viral replication 
subsequently expands to involve multiple organs includ -
ing the liver, spleen, adrenal glands, kidneys, and endo -
thelial tissues. Unlike several classical viral hemorrhagic 
fevers, severe Ebola virus disease is not driven solely by 
direct cytopathic injury but rather by an intricate interac -
tion between uncontrolled viral replication and dysregulated 
host inflammatory responses [69]. The early events in Ebola 
virus pathogenesis are illustrated in Fig. 6. A defining fea-
ture of Ebola virus infection is the simultaneous induction 
of widespread inflammation and profound impairment of 
effective antiviral immunity. This paradoxical immune state 
contributes substantially to disease severity and mortality.
Innate immune dysregulation
Innate immune dysfunction represents one of the central 
mechanisms underlying severe Ebola virus disease. Under 
physiological conditions, viral infections activate inter -
feron-mediated antiviral pathways that limit viral replication 
and promote adaptive immune activation [ 15]. Ebola virus 
disrupts these protective mechanisms extensively through 
the actions of viral proteins VP24 and VP35. VP35 inhibits 
intracellular sensing of viral RNA and suppresses activation 
of interferon regulatory factors responsible for initiating 
type I interferon production. Concurrently, VP24 interferes 
with nuclear transport of STAT1 signaling proteins, thereby 
preventing transcription of interferon-stimulated antiviral 
genes. These coordinated immune evasion strategies allow 
rapid viral replication during the early stages of infection 
before effective host immune control can be established 
[71].
Infected dendritic cells exhibit impaired maturation and 
reduced antigen-presenting capacity, further compromising 
adaptive immune activation. Consequently, antiviral T-cell 
responses often remain delayed or functionally inadequate 
in fatal disease. In survivors, however, more balanced 
immune activation and earlier adaptive responses appear 
to contribute to viral clearance and recovery [ 72]. Recent 
immunological studies have suggested that fatal Ebola virus 
disease is associated not only with excessive inflammation 
but also with profound immune exhaustion and lymphocyte 
dysfunction. Extensive apoptosis of T lymphocytes and 
natural killer cells has been observed despite limited direct

Cytokine storm and hyperinflammatory responses
The uncontrolled release of pro-inflammatory cytokines 
constitutes one of the most important determinants of Ebola-
associated tissue injury. Infected macrophages and mono -
cytes release large quantities of inflammatory mediators 
including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 
(IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), macrophage inflammatory 
proteins, and nitric oxide [36]. This excessive inflammatory 
response, frequently referred to as a cytokine storm, con -
tributes directly to vascular instability, endothelial dysfunc-
tion, and systemic inflammatory injury. Elevated circulating 
cytokine levels correlate strongly with disease severity and 
mortality. Unlike protective inflammatory responses that 
promote pathogen elimination, the hyperinflammatory state 
observed in severe Ebola virus disease becomes maladap -
tive and destructive. Sustained cytokine release promotes 
recruitment of additional inflammatory cells into tissues, 
amplifying local tissue damage. Simultaneously, nitric 
oxide overproduction contributes to vasodilation, hypo -
tension, and impaired tissue perfusion. These mechanisms 
collectively drive progression toward shock and multior -
gan failure. Importantly, contemporary evidence suggests 
that the severity of Ebola virus disease may depend less on 
absolute viral load alone and more on the host’s capacity 
to regulate inflammatory injury while maintaining effective 
antiviral responses [53].

virus disease. Endothelial dysfunction develops through 
both direct and indirect mechanisms. Although endothelial 
cells may become infected during advanced disease, much 
of the vascular injury appears to result from inflammatory 
mediators released by infected immune cells [ 57, 74]. Pro-
inflammatory cytokines disrupt endothelial tight junctions 
and increase vascular permeability, leading to widespread 
plasma leakage into interstitial tissues. Simultaneously,

Coagulation abnormalities and hemorrhagic 
manifestations
Coagulation abnormalities in Ebola virus disease arise 
through a complex interplay of endothelial injury, excessive 
inflammatory activation, platelet dysfunction, and dysregu-
lation of the coagulation cascade. Infection of monocytes 
and macrophages induces marked upregulation of tissue fac-
tor expression, which serves as a major trigger for activation 
of the extrinsic coagulation pathway [ 77, 78]. This process 
initiates widespread thrombin generation and contributes to 
the development of disseminated intravascular coagulation 
(DIC), a hallmark feature observed in severe and fatal cases 
of Ebola virus disease. Progressive activation of coagulation 
pathways results in extensive microvascular thrombosis, 
consumption of clotting factors, and depletion of circulating 
platelets, ultimately leading to a state of consumptive coag-
ulopathy. The combined effects of endothelial dysfunction, 
thrombocytopenia, impaired hepatic synthesis of coagula -
tion proteins, and systemic inflammatory injury predispose 
affected individuals to a broad spectrum of hemorrhagic 
manifestations [79]. Clinically, patients may develop pete -
chiae, ecchymoses, mucosal hemorrhage, gastrointestinal 
bleeding, hematemesis, and persistent bleeding from veni -
puncture or injection sites. The severity of bleeding varies 
considerably among patients and outbreaks, reflecting dif -
ferences in viral burden, host immune responses, and the 
extent of coagulation dysregulation [80].
Although hemorrhagic manifestations have historically 
been regarded as a defining feature of Ebola virus disease, 
contemporary clinical observations indicate that overt bleed-
ing is neither universally present nor essential for diagnosis. 
In many patients, severe disease is dominated by profound 
vascular leakage, refractory shock, metabolic derange -
ments, and progressive multiorgan dysfunction rather than 
catastrophic hemorrhage. Consequently, mortality in Ebola 
virus disease is more commonly associated with circulatory 
collapse and organ failure than with exsanguination [ 81].

Clinical manifestations and disease 
progression
The clinical presentation of Ebola virus disease is highly het-
erogeneous and evolves dynamically throughout the course 
of infection. Early manifestations are frequently nonspecific 
and resemble several tropical febrile illnesses endemic to 
outbreak regions, including malaria, typhoid fever, dengue, 
and Lassa fever [85 − 83]. This clinical overlap contributes 
substantially to delayed recognition and increases the likeli-
hood of early community transmission before appropriate 
isolation measures are implemented. The incubation period 
generally ranges from 2 to 21 days, with most symptom -
atic cases developing within the first 7 to 10 days following 
exposure. Viral load kinetics and host immune responses 
appear to influence both the timing and severity of clinical 
manifestations [84].
Initial symptoms typically include abrupt onset of high-
grade fever, severe fatigue, headache, myalgia, chills, and 
sore throat. These manifestations reflect the early systemic 
inflammatory response induced by widespread activation of 
infected monocytes and macrophages. During this phase, 
patients often exhibit profound weakness and generalized 
malaise that rapidly impair functional status [ 47]. As viral 
dissemination progresses, gastrointestinal involvement 
becomes increasingly prominent and frequently dominates 
the clinical picture. Severe vomiting, profuse watery diar -
rhea, abdominal pain, anorexia, and nausea contribute 
extensively to dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and cir -
culatory instability. In many patients, gastrointestinal fluid 
losses become sufficiently severe to precipitate hypovole -
mic shock even before overt hemorrhagic manifestations 
develop [85].

temic inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and multiorgan 
involvement. Hepatic injury commonly produces elevated 
serum transaminases, impaired coagulation factor synthesis, 
and metabolic abnormalities. Renal dysfunction develops 
through combined effects of hypoperfusion, inflammatory

Diagnostic approaches and recent advances
Rapid and accurate diagnosis remains one of the most criti-
cal determinants of successful Ebola outbreak containment. 
Delayed identification of infected individuals contributes 
substantially to ongoing transmission, particularly during 
the early stages of epidemics when clinical manifestations 
are nonspecific and overlap extensively with other endemic 
febrile illnesses [ 91]. Diagnostic strategies for Ebola virus 
disease have evolved considerably over recent decades, 
transitioning from conventional viral culture and serological 
assays toward highly sensitive molecular platforms capable 
of rapid field deployment. The unprecedented scale of the 
West African epidemic accelerated major innovations in 
point-of-care diagnostics, biosurveillance technologies, and 
decentralized laboratory systems, fundamentally reshaping 
outbreak response capabilities worldwide [92, 93].
The diagnostic approach to Ebola virus disease depends 
heavily upon the stage of infection, specimen availability, 
laboratory infrastructure, and biosafety capacity. During 
the acute phase of illness, molecular detection of viral RNA 
remains the cornerstone of laboratory diagnosis because 
viremia typically reaches extremely high levels within 
the first several days following symptom onset. Real-time 
reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) 
has therefore emerged as the diagnostic gold standard owing 
to its high sensitivity, specificity, and capacity for rapid 
confirmation of infection. RT-PCR assays targeting viral 
genes encoding nucleoprotein, glycoprotein, or polymerase 
proteins enable detection of Ebola virus RNA even before 
extensive systemic manifestations become clinically appar-
ent. The widespread implementation of RT-PCR during 
recent outbreaks significantly improved case confirmation 
and facilitated more effective isolation and contact-tracing 
strategies [92].
Despite its diagnostic utility, RT-PCR-based testing 
presents several logistical challenges in outbreak settings. 
Conventional RT-PCR platforms require sophisticated labo-
ratory infrastructure, uninterrupted electrical supply, tem -
perature-controlled reagent storage, and trained personnel 
capable of performing complex molecular procedures under 
strict biosafety conditions [ 94–96]. These requirements are 
difficult to maintain in many regions where Ebola outbreaks 
typically occur. Consequently, delays in specimen transport, 
processing, and result reporting frequently hinder rapid 
outbreak containment efforts. To address these limitations, 
portable and semi-automated molecular diagnostic systems 
were increasingly deployed during recent outbreaks, allow-
ing testing to occur closer to affected communities and 
substantially reducing turnaround times [ 96]. The major 
diagnostic modalities currently employed in Ebola virus dis-
ease are summarized in Table 4. The progression of Ebola 
virus disease and its associated clinical manifestations are

and severe systemic inflammation. Neonatal survival fol -
lowing maternal Ebola infection remains extremely uncom-
mon despite advances in supportive care [ 97–99]. Pediatric 
disease presentations may differ from adult manifestations 
and are frequently complicated by severe dehydration, 
electrolyte imbalance, and delayed diagnosis. Healthcare

Antigen-detection assays have emerged as valuable 
supplementary tools for rapid field-based screening during 
large outbreaks. These assays detect viral proteins, particu -

and can generate results within a relatively short period. 
Although antigen tests generally demonstrate lower sensi -
tivity than RT-PCR, particularly during the earliest stages of 
infection, their operational simplicity and reduced biosafety 
requirements make them attractive for deployment in remote 
or resource-limited settings. Rapid diagnostic tests capable 
of point-of-care use have become increasingly important in 
outbreak triage, preliminary screening, and decentralized 
surveillance programs [97–101].
Serological testing plays a more limited role in acute 
Ebola diagnosis because detectable antibody responses 
often develop only after several days of symptomatic ill -
ness. Immunoglobulin M responses typically emerge during 
the later phase of acute infection, whereas immunoglobu -
lin G antibodies may persist for prolonged periods follow -

useful for epidemiological investigations, retrospective 
surveillance, seroprevalence studies, and evaluation of 
vaccine-induced immunity than for early clinical diagno -

infections that may occur during outbreaks [102, 103].

sented the definitive diagnostic standard for Ebola virus 
infection. However, viral culture requires Biosafety Level-4 
containment because of the extreme pathogenicity of live 
Ebola virus. Such facilities remain exceptionally limited 
worldwide and are unavailable in most outbreak regions. As 
a result, viral culture is now largely restricted to special -
ized research laboratories investigating viral biology, patho-
genesis, antiviral susceptibility, and vaccine development 
rather than routine clinical diagnosis [ 92, 104, 105]. One 
of the most transformative developments in modern Ebola 
diagnostics has been the integration of genomic sequencing 
technologies into outbreak surveillance systems. During the 
West African epidemic, large-scale viral genome sequencing 
enabled investigators to trace transmission chains, monitor 
viral evolution, identify outbreak clusters, and evaluate the 
emergence of novel mutations in near real time. Genomic 
epidemiology subsequently became an essential component 
of modern outbreak response strategies. Sequencing-based 
surveillance now plays a crucial role not only in monitor -
ing transmission dynamics but also in assessing potential 
changes in virulence, transmissibility, diagnostic sensitivity, 
and therapeutic susceptibility [ 104, 105]. The evolution of

Recent technological advances have further expanded 
the diagnostic landscape through development of highly 
portable nucleic acid amplification platforms, isothermal 
amplification systems, CRISPR-based detection technolo -
gies, and microfluidic biosensors. These approaches aim to 
overcome many limitations associated with conventional 
RT-PCR by reducing dependence on centralized labora -
tory infrastructure. CRISPR-mediated diagnostic systems, 
in particular, have attracted considerable attention because 
of their rapid detection capability, high analytical specific -
ity, and potential applicability in field conditions. Although 
many of these technologies remain under evaluation, they 
represent promising future tools for rapid outbreak identifi-
cation and decentralized surveillance [92].
An equally important component of Ebola diagnostics 
involves biosafety and specimen management. Clinical 
specimens from suspected Ebola patients contain extremely 
high viral loads and therefore pose substantial occupational 
risks to laboratory personnel. Strict adherence to biosafety 
protocols during specimen collection, transport, processing, 
and disposal is essential to prevent laboratory-associated 
transmission [82]. Recent outbreaks highlighted major defi-
ciencies in biosafety infrastructure within several affected 
regions, emphasizing the necessity for expanded training, 
personal protective equipment availability, and standardized 
infection-control procedures within diagnostic laboratories. 
The diagnostic challenges associated with Ebola virus dis -
ease are particularly relevant for countries such as India, 
where imported infections remain a realistic possibility 
despite the absence of endemic transmission. Following the 
West African epidemic, Indian health authorities expanded 
molecular diagnostic capacity, strengthened airport surveil-
lance systems, and enhanced biosafety preparedness within 
designated laboratories. Nevertheless, substantial chal -
lenges remain regarding rapid decentralized testing, bio -
safety training, and integration of molecular surveillance 
systems into broader public health preparedness frame -
works. As global travel and ecological disruption continue 
to increase the risk of emerging zoonotic diseases, strength-

Ebola preparedness strategies worldwide [83].

Therapeutic strategies and supportive 
clinical management
The management of Ebola virus disease has undergone sub-
stantial transformation over the past decade, shifting from 
largely supportive interventions toward increasingly tar -
geted therapeutic strategies. During the earliest recognized 
outbreaks, the absence of specific antiviral therapies and lim-
ited critical-care infrastructure contributed to exceptionally 
high mortality rates. Clinical management was frequently 
restricted to symptomatic treatment under resource-con -
strained conditions, with minimal capacity for advanced 
hemodynamic monitoring, organ-supportive therapy, or 
intensive care interventions [ 18]. However, experiences 
gained during the West African epidemic and subsequent 
outbreaks significantly altered therapeutic approaches and 
demonstrated that aggressive supportive care, combined 
with emerging targeted therapies, can markedly improve 
survival outcomes [10].

disease management regardless of the availability of anti -
viral agents or immunotherapeutics. Severe gastrointestinal 
fluid loss resulting from persistent vomiting and diarrhea 
frequently leads to profound dehydration, electrolyte imbal-
ance, metabolic acidosis, and circulatory collapse. Conse -
quently, early and aggressive fluid resuscitation is critical 
for maintaining intravascular volume, preserving tissue 
perfusion, and preventing progression toward irreversible 
shock [105]. Contemporary management protocols empha -

progression to advanced multiorgan dysfunction [109, 110].

Vaccines and preventive strategies against 
Ebola virus disease
The development of effective Ebola vaccines represents 
one of the most significant achievements in contemporary 
outbreak medicine and global public health preparedness. 
Prior to the West African epidemic, vaccine research against 
Ebola virus remained relatively limited because outbreaks 
were sporadic, geographically restricted, and commer -
cially neglected. However, the unprecedented scale and 
mortality associated with the 2013–2016 epidemic acceler -
ated international investment in vaccine development and 
transformed Ebola vaccination into a major global health 
priority. The rapid progression from experimental vaccine 
candidates to large-scale field deployment demonstrated the 
capacity of coordinated scientific collaboration to address 
emerging infectious disease threats under urgent epidemic 
conditions [116–118].
Among currently available vaccine platforms, the recom-
binant vesicular stomatitis virus-based vaccine expressing 
Ebola glycoprotein (rVSV-ZEBOV) has demonstrated the 
greatest clinical impact. This live-attenuated recombinant 
vaccine induces protective immune responses by replac -
ing the native vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein with 
the glycoprotein of Zaire ebolavirus [ 116]. Clinical trials 
conducted during outbreak conditions revealed remarkably 
high protective efficacy, particularly when vaccination was 
implemented using ring-vaccination strategies targeting 
contacts and contacts-of-contacts surrounding confirmed 
Ebola cases. These findings fundamentally altered outbreak-
control strategies by demonstrating that rapid reactive vac -
cination could significantly interrupt transmission chains 
even during ongoing epidemics [116–118].
The implementation of ring-vaccination strategies dur -
ing recent outbreaks provided critical insights into both 
the strengths and limitations of emergency vaccination 
campaigns. Vaccination of frontline healthcare workers, 
laboratory personnel, burial teams, and high-risk commu -
nity contacts substantially reduced transmission risk and 
improved outbreak containment efforts. Nevertheless, oper-
ational challenges including vaccine hesitancy, community 
mistrust, political instability, transportation difficulties, 
and maintenance of cold-chain infrastructure frequently 
complicated vaccine deployment in remote outbreak 
regions. Such logistical barriers remain major obstacles to 
large-scale vaccination programs in low-resource settings. 
In addition to rVSV-ZEBOV , several alternative vaccine 
platforms have been developed to improve immunoge -
nicity, durability of protection, and broader cross-species 
coverage. Adenovirus-vectored vaccines, including Ad26.
ZEBOV-based regimens, have shown promising immu -
nogenicity profiles and may offer advantages in long-term 
preventive vaccination strategies [ 116, 118]. Heterologous 
prime-boost approaches combining adenoviral vectors with 
modified vaccinia Ankara vectors have similarly demon -

Ebola virus disease and public health 
preparedness in India
Although Ebola virus disease has remained geographically 
concentrated within sub-Saharan Africa, the increasing 
interconnectedness of international travel, trade, migration, 
and medical mobility has substantially elevated the risk of 
imported infections worldwide. India, despite the absence 
of indigenous Ebola transmission, represents a country of 
considerable epidemiological importance because of its 
large population density, extensive international air connec-
tivity, and diverse healthcare infrastructure. The West Afri-
can epidemic served as a major catalyst for strengthening 
India’s preparedness strategies against high-consequence 
emerging infectious diseases and highlighted the necessity 
for coordinated biosurveillance, laboratory readiness, and 
emergency-response planning.
The possibility of Ebola virus introduction into India 
became a realistic public health concern during the 2013–
2016 epidemic because of frequent travel between India and 
affected African countries for commercial, educational, and 
occupational purposes. Particular concern emerged follow -
ing the detection of Ebola viral RNA in the seminal fluid 
of an Indian national returning from Liberia after clinical 
recovery from Ebola virus disease [124]. Although second-
ary transmission did not occur, the incident underscored the 
importance of long-term surveillance, post-recovery moni -
toring, and preparedness for imported cases within highly 
populated regions. In response to the global Ebola emer -
gency, Indian public health authorities implemented exten -
sive surveillance measures at major international airports 
and ports of entry. Thermal screening systems, travel-his -
tory assessments, quarantine protocols, and rapid isolation 
procedures were established to identify travelers arriving 
from outbreak regions. Dedicated isolation facilities were 
simultaneously designated across selected tertiary-care 
hospitals to facilitate safe management of suspected cases 
while minimizing the risk of nosocomial transmission. 
These interventions represented one of the earliest large-
scale coordinated responses to an imported high-risk viral 
pathogen within the Indian public health system [124–126].

One of the most important aspects of India’s prepared -
ness strategy involved strengthening laboratory diagnostic 
capacity. Molecular diagnostic facilities capable of per -
forming RT-PCR-based Ebola testing were expanded within 
selected national reference laboratories operating under 
enhanced biosafety conditions. Institutions such as the 
National Institute of Virology and the National Centre for 
Disease Control played central roles in developing labora -
tory protocols, specimen-transport systems, and biosafety 
guidelines for handling high-risk infectious materials. How-
ever, the concentration of advanced diagnostic infrastructure 
within limited urban centers continues to represent a signifi-
cant challenge for rapid nationwide response capability.
Biosafety preparedness became another major focus 
area following recognition of the exceptionally high occu -
pational risks associated with Ebola virus disease. Indian 
healthcare authorities initiated training programs empha -
sizing infection-control procedures, appropriate use of 
personal protective equipment, specimen handling, waste 
disposal, and decontamination protocols. Simulation exer -
cises and emergency-response drills were conducted across 
multiple healthcare institutions to improve preparedness for 
high-consequence infectious disease outbreaks. Neverthe -
less, substantial disparities remain between tertiary-care 
centers and peripheral healthcare facilities regarding bio -
safety infrastructure, workforce training, and access to pro-
tective equipment.

extends beyond healthcare preparedness alone and includes 
major socioeconomic and demographic considerations.

retically facilitate rapid transmission if early containment 
measures failed. Simultaneously, overcrowded healthcare

Global health challenges and future 
perspectives
The recurring emergence of Ebola virus disease over recent 
decades has demonstrated that highly pathogenic zoonotic 
infections remain among the most significant threats to 
global health security. Although considerable progress has 
been achieved in diagnostics, therapeutics, vaccination, 
and outbreak management, recurrent epidemics continue 
to expose major structural weaknesses within international 
public health systems. The persistence of Ebola outbreaks 
despite scientific advances reflects the complex interaction 
between biological, ecological, political, socioeconomic, 
and healthcare-related factors that collectively influence 
epidemic dynamics. Consequently, future control of Ebola 
virus disease will depend not only on biomedical innova -
tion but also on sustained global investment in surveil -
lance infrastructure, healthcare resilience, and international 
cooperation.
One of the most important unresolved challenges involves 
understanding the ecological mechanisms governing zoo -
notic spillover events. Fruit bats are widely considered the 
most probable natural reservoirs of ebolaviruses; however, 
the precise environmental and ecological conditions facili -
tating viral transmission from wildlife to humans remain 
incompletely understood. Increasing deforestation, min -
ing activities, urban expansion, agricultural encroachment, 
and disruption of natural ecosystems may intensify human 
exposure to wildlife reservoirs and increase the frequency 
of spillover events. Climate change further complicates this 
relationship by altering wildlife migration patterns, habitat 
distribution, and ecological interactions that influence viral 
circulation within reservoir populations. These observations 
emphasize the importance of integrating ecological surveil-
lance into future outbreak-prevention strategies.
The growing frequency of emerging zoonotic diseases has 
accelerated adoption of the One Health framework, which 
recognizes the interconnected nature of human, animal, and 
environmental health. Ebola virus disease represents a pro -
totypical example of a pathogen whose emergence cannot 
be adequately addressed through purely clinical or labo -
ratory-centered approaches. Effective prevention requires 
multidisciplinary collaboration involving epidemiologists, 
ecologists, veterinarians, virologists, public health agen -
cies, and environmental scientists. Integrated surveillance 
systems capable of monitoring wildlife reservoirs, ecologi -
cal disruption, and unusual patterns of animal mortality may 
provide early warning signals before widespread human 
transmission occurs.
Another major global challenge involves disparities in 
healthcare infrastructure between high-income and low-
income regions. Most Ebola outbreaks occur in countries 
characterized by limited laboratory capacity, fragile health-
care systems, shortages of trained healthcare workers, and 
inadequate critical-care resources. These structural vulnera-
bilities contribute substantially to delayed diagnosis, inade-
quate infection control, and elevated mortality rates. During 
the West African epidemic, overwhelmed healthcare facili-
ties became major amplification centers for tra nsmission 
because of insufficient isolation capacity and inadequate 
protective equipment. Similar patterns were observed dur -
ing subsequent outbreaks despite improvements in interna -
tional response mechanisms.
Strengthening healthcare resilience within vulnerable 
regions therefore remains essential for future epidemic pre-
paredness. Investments in laboratory biosafety, molecular 
diagnostics, emergency logistics, infection-control infra -
structure, and healthcare-worker training are likely to pro -
vide benefits extending beyond Ebola virus disease alone. 
The global response to the COVID-19 pandemic further 
demonstrated that preparedness for emerging infectious

Genomic surveillance has emerged as one of the most 
transformative developments in modern outbreak science. 
Large-scale sequencing during Ebola epidemics enabled 
real-time monitoring of viral evolution, transmission 
dynamics, and epidemiological clustering. Future integra -
tion of genomic epidemiology with artificial intelligence, 
digital surveillance systems, and predictive modeling may 
substantially improve outbreak forecasting and response 
coordination. Machine-learning algorithms capable of ana -
lyzing ecological, climatic, demographic, and travel-related 
data could potentially identify regions at elevated risk for 
zoonotic emergence before sustained human transmission 
occurs.
The future of Ebola therapeutics is also likely to evolve 
considerably over the coming decade. Current mono -
clonal antibody therapies have demonstrated substantial 
efficacy; however, their cost, storage requirements, and 
limited accessibility remain significant barriers in resource-
constrained outbreak settings. Future research increasingly 
focuses on developing broad-spectrum antiviral agents 
capable of targeting multiple filovirus species simultane -
ously. Advances in structural biology, computational drug 
design, and host-directed immunomodulatory therapies may 
enable development of more affordable and scalable treat -
ment options suitable for widespread deployment during 
future epidemics.
Vaccine research similarly continues to evolve beyond 
species-specific protection toward the development of uni -
versal filovirus vaccines. Existing vaccine platforms pri -
marily target Zaire ebolavirus, leaving substantial gaps in 
protection against other pathogenic species such as Sudan 
ebolavirus. The 2022 Uganda outbreak highlighted the 
limitations of relying on single-species vaccine strategies 
and reinforced the urgency of identifying conserved viral 
epitopes capable of inducing cross-protective immunity. 
Advances in nanoparticle vaccines, messenger RNA plat -
forms, and antigen-engineering technologies may accelerate 
development of next-generation pan-filovirus vaccines with 
broader protective capacity.
An additional challenge involves the long-term man -
agement of Ebola survivors and the implications of viral 
persistence. Increasing evidence demonstrating prolonged 
viral persistence within immune-privileged sites has funda-
mentally altered understanding of Ebola virus biology. Per-
sistent infection not only creates opportunities for delayed 
transmission and outbreak re-emergence but also contrib -
utes substantially to chronic post-Ebola syndrome. Future 
research must therefore address mechanisms underlying 
viral persistence, chronic immune activation, and long-term 
organ injury in survivors. Establishing sustainable survivor-
care programs will become increasingly important as mor -
tality rates decline and the number of survivors continues to 
increase following improved therapeutic interventions.
The social and political dimensions of Ebola outbreaks 
remain equally important. Community mistrust, misinfor -
mation, political instability, armed conflict, and resistance 
to public health interventions have repeatedly complicated 
outbreak-control efforts. Scientific advances alone cannot 
overcome these barriers without parallel investment in com-
munity engagement, culturally sensitive communication 
strategies, and local public health capacity-building. Suc -
cessful epidemic containment ultimately depends upon pub-
lic trust in healthcare systems and governmental institutions.
For countries such as India, future preparedness strat -
egies must focus on maintaining surveillance readiness 
despite the absence of endemic Ebola transmission. Increas-
ing international travel, urbanization, and ecological disrup-
tion continue to elevate the risk of imported infections and 
future zoonotic threats. Strengthening molecular diagnostic 
networks, expanding biosafety infrastructure, integrating 
digital surveillance systems, and enhancing rapid-response 
coordination remain essential priorities. Importantly, pre -
paredness frameworks developed for Ebola virus disease 
may also strengthen resilience against a broader spectrum 
of emerging pathogens with pandemic potential.
The future management of Ebola virus disease will likely 
depend upon the integration of biomedical innovation with 
global public health preparedness, ecological surveillance, 
and international collaboration. Although remarkable prog-
ress has been achieved over recent decades, the recurring 
emergence of Ebola outbreaks serves as a reminder that 
emerging infectious diseases remain an enduring challenge 
within an increasingly interconnected world. Sustained 
scientific investment, equitable healthcare development, 
and coordinated global preparedness efforts will therefore 
remain critical for reducing the impact of future Ebola epi -
demics and safeguarding international health security.
The emergence of highly transmissible infectious dis -
eases in recent years has reinforced the importance of 
adopting integrated One Health approaches that recognize 
the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmen -
tal health. Although Ebola virus reservoirs are not believed 
to exist within India, ecological disruption, climate change, 
wildlife trafficking, and increasing global mobility continue 
to elevate the risk of zoonotic spillover events worldwide. 
Consequently, future preparedness strategies must extend 
beyond reactive outbreak management and incorporate 
proactive biosurveillance systems capable of identifying 
emerging infectious threats at early stages.

Conclusion
Ebola virus disease remains one of the most formidable 
emerging infectious diseases confronting modern pub -
lic health systems because of its high mortality, epidemic 
potential, and capacity to overwhelm fragile healthcare 
infrastructure within short periods of time. Since its first 
recognized emergence in 1976, scientific understanding of 
Ebola virus biology, transmission dynamics, and clinical 
pathogenesis has expanded substantially. Contemporary evi-
dence demonstrates that severe disease is driven not solely 
by direct viral cytotoxicity but by a complex interaction 
involving uncontrolled viral replication, profound immune 
dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, coagulopathy, and 
progressive multiorgan failure. The recognition of cytokine-
mediated inflammatory injury and impaired interferon sig -
naling has fundamentally reshaped current understanding of 
Ebola virus pathogenesis and provided important insights 
into potential therapeutic targets.
The unprecedented West African epidemic marked a 
turning point in Ebola research and outbreak preparedness 
by accelerating the development of rapid molecular diag -
nostics, genomic surveillance systems, monoclonal anti -
body therapies, and recombinant vaccines. These advances 
significantly improved patient survival and outbreak con -
tainment during subsequent epidemics. Nevertheless, recur-
ring outbreaks in the Democratic Republic of Congo and 
Uganda continue to demonstrate that Ebola virus disease 
remains a persistent threat, particularly within regions 
affected by political instability, healthcare disparities, eco -
logical disruption, and limited biosurveillance capacity. 
Recent investigations into viral persistence and post-Ebola 
syndrome have further expanded understanding of the 
long-term consequences of infection. The ability of Ebola 
virus to persist within immune-privileged anatomical sites 
has important implications for disease recurrence, survi -
vor management, and future outbreak dynamics. Simulta -
neously, the increasing number of survivors experiencing 
chronic neurological, ophthalmological, musculoskeletal, 
and psychological complications highlights the necessity 
for long-term healthcare strategies extending beyond acute 
epidemic control.
Although India has not experienced endemic Ebola out -
breaks, increasing globalization and international mobil -
ity continue to elevate the risk of imported infections. The 
preparedness measures implemented following the West 
African epidemic including airport surveillance, molecular 
diagnostic strengthening, biosafety training, and integrated 
outbreak-response systems represent important advances 
in national biosecurity infrastructure. However, contin -
ued improvement in decentralized diagnostics, health -
care-worker preparedness, digital disease surveillance, 
and emergency-response coordination remains essential 
to ensure rapid containment of future high-risk infectious 
threats.
Future management of Ebola virus disease will require 
sustained integration of biomedical innovation, ecologi -
cal surveillance, international cooperation, and resilient 
public health infrastructure. The development of univer -
sal filovirus vaccines, broad-spectrum antiviral therapies, 
artificial intelligence-assisted outbreak prediction systems, 
and integrated One Health surveillance approaches may 
substantially improve preparedness against future zoonotic 
emergence events. At the same time, strengthening health -
care systems in vulnerable regions and promoting equitable 
access to diagnostics, therapeutics, and vaccines will remain 
critical for reducing the global burden of Ebola virus dis -
ease. The recurring emergence of Ebola virus outbreaks 
serves as a powerful reminder that infectious diseases con -
tinue to evolve within an increasingly interconnected world 
shaped by ecological disruption, climate change, urbaniza -
tion, and global mobility. Sustained scientific investment, 
coordinated international preparedness, and proactive pub -
lic health strategies will therefore remain indispensable for 
mitigating future Ebola epidemics and safeguarding global 
health security.
