# Early-Onset Progressive Encephalopathy With Brain Oedema and/or Leukoencephalopathy 1: Report of three siblings.
**Authors:** Al-Amrani F, Al-Murshedi F, Al-Thihli K, Al-Ajmi E, Al-Musalhi B, Al Shehhi A, Al Futaisi A
**Journal:** Sultan Qaboos Univ Med J (2026)
**DOI:** [10.18295/2075-0528.3012](https://doi.org/10.18295/2075-0528.3012)

## Content

NAXE encephalopathy, also known as early-onset progressive encephalopathy with brain oedema and/or leukoencephalopathy-1 (PEBEL-1), is a rare and often lethal autosomal recessive mitochondrial disorder. Typical presentation includes psychomotor regression, ataxia, respiratory insufficiency and seizures triggered by febrile illness. This report describes three siblings from a consanguineous family who presented to a tertiary care hospital in Muscat, Oman, in 2022 and were found to have a homozygous pathogenic variant in the NAXE: NM_144772.2:c.827del, p.(Pro276Hisfs*43). Beyond the classic neuroradiological and clinical features of PEBEL-1, these patients exhibited novel phenotypic manifestations, including axonal polyneuropathy, Bull's eye maculopathy and a late-onset presentation of skin lesions. The identification of these features expands the known clinical spectrum of NAXE mutations and highlights the importance of considering this diagnosis in patients presenting with unexplained multisystemic mitochondrial symptoms involving the peripheral nerves and retina.

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP) are crucial coenzymes for various cellular and mitochondrial functions.1 The reduced form of these two metabolites is important for mitochondrial electron transport chain and mitochondrial energy production.12 Moreover, NAD and NADP hydration results in NADHX and NADPHX formation, respectively.12 NADHX and NADPHX are toxic metabolites and their accumulation inhibits crucial mitochondrial enzymes.13 Generally, these toxic metabolites are degraded by an epimerase enzyme that acts against their accumulation in the cell.3

The gene NAXE (APOA1BP) encodes NADPHX-epimerase;23 this enzyme converts the hydrated reduced form of NADPHX through a specific pathway.23 Biallelic variants in the NAXE gene cause a deficiency of NAPHX-epimerase and disruption of this crucial pathway; consequently, NADHX and NADPHX accumulate, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and enzyme inhibition.123 Febrile illness usually exacerbates this disruption, possibly because NADPHX forms spontaneously under stressful conditions, such as febrile illnesses or acidic pH.234

NAXE encephalopathy or early-onset progressive encephalopathy with brain oedema and/or leukoencephalopathy 1 (PEBEL-1) is an autosomal recessive and lethal mitochondrial disorder.2456 Usually, it manifests as psychomotor regression, hypotonia, ataxia, quadriparesis, ophthalmoparesis, respiratory insufficiency, encephalopathy and seizures within the first 2 years of life.2456 Other clinical manifestations include myopathy, elevated creatine kinase and variable skin lesions.1 The disease commonly leads to death before the age of 2 years.2456

We report three siblings with PEBEL-1 with a homozygous pathogenic variant in the NAXE who presented to a tertiary care hospital in Muscat, Oman. Several patients with PEBEL-1 have been reported in the literature.2456789 However, this report presents new PEBEL-1 clinical observations, including the presence of polyneuropathy, Bull's eye maculopathy and late appearance of skin manifestations.

Case 1 was a female child born to consanguineous parents, with normal perinatal history and early development. After her 6-month vaccination, she developed intermittent torticollis alternating with retrocollis. She was able to cruise at 12 months but never walked independently. Over time, she developed chronic cough and recurrent lower respiratory infections requiring hospitalisation. In 2020, at 18 months, she showed developmental regression, loss of head control, strabismus and encephalopathy, and was admitted with respiratory failure following a mild febrile illness. Empirical treatment for suspected meningoencephalitis was initiated. Examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 3 (E1V1M1), severe hypotonia, areflexia and equivocal plantar responses. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed no white cells, normal glucose and protein and negative cultures. CSF viral polymerase chain reaction and autoimmune encephalitis panels were negative. She was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin, high-dose methylprednisolone and plasma exchange, without improvement. Seizures later developed and anti-seizure therapy was started. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed marked oedema and diffusion restriction in the cerebellar hemispheres and brainstem. In spine-MRI, extensive abnormal signal intensity was observed in the cord. In addition, abnormal enhancement on the surface of the cord and of the cauda equina nerve roots was noted [Fig. 1]. The patient developed complete ophthalmoplegia with absent brainstem reflexes. Her course was complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) and autonomic dysfunction with fluctuating blood pressure. She later developed tense blisters, initially on both ankles, which spread over a week to the axillae, arms and abdomen, along with mucosal erosions in the oral cavity and genitalia. The affected skin area was extensive. Skin biopsy revealed focal subepidermal separation, vesicle formation, moderate hyperkeratosis, keratinocyte necrosis and perivascular lymphocytic infiltration consistent with toxic epidermal necrolysis [Fig. 2]. Fundus examination showed Bull's eye maculopathy. Liver function tests, creatine kinase and plasma amino acids were normal. Nerve conduction studies (NCS) demonstrated severe axonal motor polyneuropathy. Electroencephalography revealed diffuse delta slowing with interictal focal epileptiform discharges. She was transferred to a regional hospital, where she died shortly after arrival. She was subsequently found to harbour the same homozygous variant in NAXE: NM_144772.2:c.827del, p.(Pro276Hisfs*43), that was identified in her younger sister (Case 2).

Case 2, who was the younger sister of Case 1, presented to our hospital in 2022. She had an uneventful perinatal history and normal development until 18 months of age, when she developed intermittent anterocollis following her 18-month vaccination. As her condition progressed, she lost the ability to walk and developed truncal ataxia. Physical examination revealed mild head tilt toward the left side and truncal ataxia. Initial metabolic tests including lactate, ammonia, liver function test and creatine kinase were all within normal limits. Brain MRI was normal, with no cerebellar oedema or diffusion restriction. The patient was discharged home with unsteady gait but subjected to a close follow-up with neurology. A week later, the patient presented with sudden deterioration including severe axial hypotonia, respiratory failure and recurrent seizures with ophthalmoplegia in the context of mild febrile illness. Palliative care was provided and the patient died within a week from her admission.

Given that both siblings were affected with the same phenotype and experienced sudden deterioration with febrile illness, mitochondrial diseases were suspected. Sanger gene sequencing of the SURF gene was negative. In clinical exome sequencing, Case 2 was positive for the homozygous variant in the NAXE: NM_144772.2:c.827del, p.(Pro276Hisfs*43).

Case 3 was the youngest brother of Cases 1 and 2 [Fig. 3]. He had an uneventful perinatal history and normal initial development until the age of 14 months, when he presented with encephalopathy (reduced activity, irritability and loss of interest in exploring toys), axial ataxia and intermittent episodes of head and trunk titubation in 2024. Target mutation of the NAXE: NM_144772.2:c.827del, p.(Pro276Hisfs*43) was positive for the homozygous variant. Brain MRI showed symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the middle cerebellar peduncles bilaterally without abnormal diffusion restriction. He also had diffuse myelopathy. Thiamine, pyridoxine and niacin were then initiated, leading to the improvement of clinical status within 48 hours. However, he deteriorated despite continuous increment in niacin dosing. Despite the clinical deterioration, repeat MRI revealed significant improvement of the signal abnormality in the middle cerebellar peduncles and the spinal cord. The clinical course and neuroimaging findings of this patient have been described in detail by Al-Amrani et al.10

His course was complicated with autonomic dysfunction with blood pressure fluctuations, bradycardia and SIADH. Unfortunately, he died in the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) one week after niacin cessation.

All three patients showed signs of PEBEL-1 before 18 months of age (after 6-, 18- and 14-months post-vaccination in Cases 1 and 2 and following mild febrile illnesses in patient 3, respectively), which aligns with the typical age of presentation.2456789 All three died before age two (at 18, 19 and 16 months, respectively) shortly after disease onset, consistent with previous reports.245 While some patients survive into adulthood, they are typically non-ambulatory or ventilator dependent.45

All patients presented with acute cerebellar ataxia, a common finding seen in 80% of Cases in Manor et al.'s cohort.4 Head tilt and torticollis were also observed, in line with previous reports.2 Case 1 had bulbar dysfunction and recurrent aspiration pneumonia; similar bulbar symptoms such as dysphagia and dysarthria were reported in five patients by Manor et al.4 Ocular symptoms in Case 1, likely due to ophthalmoparesis, resemble those previously described, including gaze palsy and strabismus.4

All patients developed progressive encephalopathy with recurrent febrile illnesses, culminating in respiratory failure and PICU admission, followed by rapid deterioration and death. This progression mirrors previous findings.245 Although slower disease courses have been reported, these cases still resulted in death by the second or third decade with significant disability.45

Identified triggers in the current cases included vaccination (Cases 1 and 2) and mild upper respiratory tract symptoms (Case 1), similar to prior observations.4 Skin involvement in Case 1 appeared much later than in prior reports.2410 Unlike Malik et al.'s case, Case 1 did not show early skin manifestations.11 Kremer et al. reported skin involvement beginning 2–3 weeks after neurologic symptoms in five out of six patients, whereas Case 1 developed skin lesions almost a year later.2 Case 2 had eczematous dermatitis and Case 3 developed urticaria after niacin initiation, likely unrelated to the primary disease. Both had more rapid disease progression and shorter PICU stays than Case 1. The absence of skin manifestations in Cases 2 and 3 may be attributed to the relatively short disease course in Case 2 and the administration of niacin in Case 3. Additionally, variable expressivity and age-related penetrance should be considered, despite all siblings carrying the same homozygous variant.

Case 1's ascending skin eruption differed from Malik et al.'s descending pattern.11 Kremer et al. described similar involvement of non-sun-exposed areas.2 Malik et al. noted superficial partial-thickness burns, while Kremer et al. observed extensive bullous eruptions.211 Due to diagnostic uncertainty, skin biopsies were performed in Case 1. The biopsy in Case 1 resembled pellagra, hinting at overlapping mechanisms potentially linked to NAD+ depletion.12 Therefore, the timing and pattern of eruptions in PEBEL-1 warrant further study.

Case 1 showed Bull's eye maculopathy during PICU admission, while Case 2's early eye exam was normal. Though unreported in PEBEL-1, maculopathy is associated with mitochondrial disorders.1314 Trinh et al. reported pigmentary retinopathy in one case, suggesting this manifestation may indicate disease pathogenesis.5

Neuroimaging in Cases 1 and 3 during PICU admission demonstrated findings consistent with the literature, including cerebellar and brainstem oedema.246 Spinal MRI has not been systematically performed in previously reported cases.78 Among the eight reported patients who underwent spinal MRI, five had normal imaging whereas the remaining three patients demonstrated features of myelopathy.256

The variability in spinal cord involvement is notable and may reflect both the timing of imaging relative to disease evolution and underlying differences in metabolic vulnerability across neural tissues. Given that NAXE-related disease results in impaired NAD(P)HX repair and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction, tissues with high metabolic demand—such as the cerebellum, brainstem, and, in some cases, the spinal cord—may be differentially affected. This could account for the inconsistent detection of myelopathy across reported cases. However, further studies are required to better delineate the temporal and tissue-specific patterns of involvement.

In the current case series, Case 1 demonstrated cauda equina enhancement, a finding not previously described in PEBEL-1.24 This observation may suggest involvement of peripheral nerve roots, potentially reflecting energy failure with secondary disruption of axonal or myelin integrity. Supporting this, NCS in Case 1 showed reduced compound muscle action potentials with preserved sensory nerve action potentials consistent with a pure motor axonal polyneuropathy. In the context of concurrent myelopathy and cauda equina enhancement, these findings are most suggestive of a motor axonopathy as part of the primary disease process, rather than an alternative aetiology. Although differential diagnoses such as anterior horn cell involvement or myopathy may be considered, the overall clinicoradiological picture favours a neurogenic process; acute motor axonal neuropathy is less likely given its rarity in the paediatric population.14 Despite its clinical utility, the intricate pathophysiological basis of contrast enhancement is yet to be fully deciphered. Notably, similar imaging features have been reported in other mitochondrial disorders, including POLG-related disease and leukoencephalopathy with brainstem and spinal cord involvement and lactate elevation, supporting a shared pathophysiological mechanism related to mitochondrial dysfunction and regional susceptibility.1516 Nevertheless, it is plausible to hypothesise that contrast enhancement is a sequelae of mitochondrial defects which can increase reactive oxygen species, release mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns such as mitochondrial DNA and activate inflammasome pathways, all of which can drive cellular inflammation and tissue injury. In the central nervous system and peripheral nerves, this can contribute to blood-nerve or blood-brain barrier disruption and contrast enhancement, but the enhancement itself is not specific to mitochondrial disease and should still prompt a broad differential diagnosis.17

Case 3 also demonstrated myelopathy, although NCS was not performed. Previous reports, such as that by Trinh et al., have similarly described polyneuropathy based on clinical features alone where no NCS was performed.5 Notably, axonal neuropathy has been described in other mitochondrial disorders and may represent an underrecognised manifestation of NAXE-related disease.18

Taken together, these findings expand the neurological phenotype of PEBEL-1 and support the hypothesis that mitochondrial dysfunction may affect not only central structures but also peripheral motor pathways, likely reflecting tissue-specific vulnerability to impaired energy metabolism. Further studies are warranted to better characterise the frequency, mechanisms and clinical significance of peripheral nervous system involvement in this condition.

Niacin supplementation has been proposed as a metabolic correction strategy for NAXE deficiency due to its role in the NAD+ biosynthetic pathway. While initial clinical reports suggest that high-dose niacin or its derivatives could lead to neurological stabilisation and slow disease progression, niacin supplementation in Case 3 resulted in a transient therapeutic effect.10 This suggests that while niacin may provide a temporary metabolic rescue, it may not be sufficient to halt the underlying neurodegenerative process in all cases.10

Genetic testing revealed a homozygous variant in NAXE: NM_144772.2:c.827del, p.(Pro276Hisfs*43) variant in all three patients, with heterozygous parents. This is a loss-of-function (LOF) variant (frame shift); LOF is a known mechanism of NAXE-related disease (gene has 17 reported pathogenic LOF variants). The variant was not found in gnomAD genomes and it was not identified in a local Omani population database of 5,270 chromosomes. The NAXE variant (NM_144772.2:c.827del, p.(Pro276Hisfs*43)) was therefore classified as likely pathogenic (Class 2), in accordance with ClinGen/ACMG guidelines.

This case series confirms that, while the core presentation of post-febrile psychomotor regression and cerebellar aedema remains a hallmark in NAXE encephalopathy, clinicians should remain vigilant for atypical manifestations, specifically Bull's eye maculopathy and motor axonal polyneuropathy. The unique late-onset, ascending skin eruptions observed in Case 1, which histologically mimicked pellagra, further support the hypothesis of underlying NAD+ depletion as a central pathogenic mechanism.
