# AAV-mediated Klotho gene delivery attenuates renal fibrosis in chronic kidney disease by suppressing the TGF-β/Smad3 signaling pathway
**Authors:** Chaoying Yan, Li Jian
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-9670512/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-9670512/v1)

## Content

Abstract
Background: Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterised by progressive renal fibrosis, Klotho deficiency, and dysregulated transforming growth factor-beta 1/Smad3 signalling. Adeno-associated virus gene therapy may sustainably restore renal Klotho.
Objectives: To determine whether AAV9-mediated Klotho delivery via renal vein injection attenuates CKD fibrosis by suppressing TGF-β1/Smad3, and to define the mechanism.
Methods: CKD was induced in C57BL/6J mice by 5/6 nephrectomy (n = 8 per group) and cross-validated using the unilateral ureteral obstruction model. An AAV9 vector encoding human Klotho under the kidney-specific Ksp-cadherin promoter was administered at low dose (5×10¹⁰ vector genomes) and high dose (2×10¹¹ vector genomes). Renal function, fibrosis gene programmes, and pathway components were quantified by quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction, Western blotting, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Mechanistic studies employed transforming growth factor-beta 1-stimulated HK-2 cells with Smad3 gain-of-function rescue and the Smad3 inhibitor SIS3.
Key results: In 5/6 nephrectomy mice, Klotho messenger RNA fell approximately 65% and circulating soluble Klotho from approximately 580 to approximately 210 pg/mL, with approximately 2.8-fold transforming growth factor-beta 1 and approximately 3.2-fold phospho-Smad3 elevation. High-dose AAV-KL restored Klotho to approximately 78% of sham, reduced serum creatinine from approximately 72 to approximately 38 µmol/L, blood urea nitrogen from approximately 28 to approximately 14.5 mmol/L, and 24-hour proteinuria from approximately 32 to approximately 12 mg/24 h. Col1a1, fibronectin 1, and alpha-smooth muscle actin messenger RNA fell approximately 58%, 45%, and 55%, respectively, with E-cadherin recovery. Transcriptome-wide RNA-sequencing identified 924 differentially expressed genes in CKD kidneys versus sham, of which 306 (including key extracellular matrix, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and TGF-beta pathway genes) were rescued by AAV-KL treatment; Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway analyses confirmed enrichment of TGF-beta signaling and extracellular matrix organization among the most significantly reversed gene programmes. The phospho-Smad3/Smad3 ratio declined markedly without change in transforming growth factor-beta 1 ligand, while Smad7 rose approximately 1.9-fold. In vitro Smad3 overexpression reversed Klotho-mediated protection; SIS3 phenocopied it. Efficacy was reproduced in the unilateral ureteral obstruction model without hepatotoxicity.
Conclusion: Kidney-targeted AAV9-Klotho gene delivery attenuates CKD fibrosis through receptor-level transforming growth factor-beta 1 blockade, Smad3 inhibition, and Smad7 restoration, warranting further preclinical development as a disease-modifying therapy.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a global public health crisis of mounting magnitude. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023, nearly 800 million adults worldwide are now affected by CKD, with an age-standardised prevalence of approximately 14% among adults aged 20 years and older, a figure that has more than doubled since 1990 [1]. The disease exacts a disproportionate toll in low- and middle-income countries, yet its burden is rising across all world regions, driven principally by the twin epidemics of type 2 diabetes mellitus and systemic arterial hypertension alongside global population ageing [1,2]. CKD is projected to become the fifth most common underlying cause of death globally by 2050 [1]. The disease imposes a staggering economic cost: each year, more than 1 trillion US dollars are spent on kidney replacement therapies including dialysis and transplantation, and access to these treatments remains profoundly inequitable, particularly across sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [1]. Despite decades of research, the current armamentarium of standard-of-care renoprotective strategies—including renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibition, and strict dietary modification—offers renoprotection that is at best partial [3,4]. Landmark trials such as DAPA-CKD with dapagliflozin [3] and EMPA-REG OUTCOME with empagliflozin [4] have demonstrated meaningful reductions in the risk of eGFR decline and kidney failure, yet neither intervention reverses established renal fibrosis, and a substantial proportion of patients continue to progress to end-stage renal disease despite maximal medical therapy. Renal interstitial fibrosis, the final common histopathological pathway shared by virtually all forms of progressive CKD regardless of aetiology, is defined by the irreversible replacement of functional nephron mass by collagen-rich scar tissue, culminating in nephron loss and inexorable decline in kidney function [5]. There is therefore an urgent and unmet clinical need for genuinely disease-modifying therapies that can directly counter the fibrogenic programme underpinning CKD progression, and the field is increasingly turning towards targeted molecular interventions as potential solutions.

The transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1)/Smad signalling cascade stands as the pre-eminent molecular conductor of renal fibrosis and has been the focus of intense investigation for more than two decades [6]. TGF-β1, released from injured tubular epithelial cells, infiltrating macrophages, and activated myofibroblasts, binds to its cognate type II receptor (TGFβR2), which phosphorylates and activates the type I receptor (activin receptor-like kinase 5, ALK5). ALK5 in turn phosphorylates the receptor-regulated Smad proteins Smad2 and Smad3 at their C-terminal serine residues. Of these, Smad3 is the dominant pro-fibrotic transducer: phospho-Smad3 (p-Smad3) forms a heterotrimeric complex with Smad4 and translocates to the nucleus, where it directly binds Smad-binding elements (SBEs) in the promoters of extracellular matrix (ECM) genes encoding collagen type I alpha-1 chain (Col1a1), fibronectin 1 (FN1), and the myofibroblast marker alpha-smooth muscle actin (ACTA2), driving their transcription [6,7]. Simultaneously, p-Smad3 represses the epithelial adhesion molecule E-cadherin (CDH1) and induces vimentin (VIM), orchestrating epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of tubular epithelial cells into ECM-secreting myofibroblast-like cells [6,7]. Compelling genetic evidence for the centrality of Smad3 in renal fibrogenesis was provided by studies in Smad3-null mice, which are largely protected against tubulointerstitial fibrosis following unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO), with abrogation of EMT, collagen accumulation, and inflammatory monocyte influx [8]. Conversely, hyperactivation of Smad3, coupled with progressive degradation of the inhibitory Smad7, creates a self-reinforcing profibrotic loop that drives inexorable ECM accumulation [9]. Smad7, an inhibitory member of the Smad family, functions as a critical negative-feedback regulator: it recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligases Smurf1 and Smurf2 to the activated TGFβR1/R2 complex, targeting receptors for proteasomal degradation, and simultaneously prevents the nuclear accumulation of p-Smad3 [9,10]. The profoundly disrupted Smad3/Smad7 equilibrium observed in fibrotic kidneys—with Smad3 hyperactivated and Smad7 progressively depleted—thus constitutes a therapeutically tractable molecular lesion that could in principle be addressed either by directly inhibiting Smad3 or by restoring an upstream physiological repressor of the pathway [9,10].

Klotho, a type-I single-pass transmembrane protein encoded by the KL gene, was originally identified as an anti-ageing factor in 1997 when its targeted disruption in mice produced a syndrome strikingly resembling premature human ageing, including arteriosclerosis, osteoporosis, skin atrophy, and abbreviated lifespan [11]. Klotho is most abundantly expressed in the distal convoluted tubule and the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle in the kidney, where it functions both as a co-receptor for fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) governing phosphate homeostasis [12] and as a secreted endocrine factor that exerts protective effects on multiple organ systems [13]. In subsequent years it became unambiguously clear that CKD constitutes a state of profound Klotho deficiency: renal KL messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein begin to decline in early CKD stages, closely paralleling the fall in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and circulating soluble Klotho (sKlotho)—produced by ectodomain shedding of membrane Klotho by ADAM10 and ADAM17 metalloproteinases—is substantially reduced even before conventional biomarkers such as serum creatinine become abnormal [14,15]. This Klotho deficiency is not merely a passive epiphenomenon of nephron loss; rather, experimental evidence strongly suggests that it actively promotes further fibrogenesis, vascular calcification, and cardiac hypertrophy, creating a vicious cycle of progressive renal and extra-renal injury [15,16]. Mechanistically, secreted Klotho protein directly binds the extracellular domain of TGFβR2, competitively blocking TGF-β1 from engaging its receptor complex and thereby abrogating downstream Smad2/3 phosphorylation [17]. In addition, Klotho suppresses TGF-β1-induced EMT responses in tubular epithelial cells, as evidenced by preserved E-cadherin expression and inhibition of vimentin and ACTA2 upregulation [17]. Most recently, a Klotho-derived peptide (KP1) was demonstrated to disrupt TGFβR1–TGFβR2 heterodimerisation and attenuate renal fibrosis in mice with equal potency to full-length soluble Klotho, further establishing receptor-level antagonism as the primary anti-fibrotic mechanism [18]. Importantly, clinical data corroborate these experimental findings: longitudinal studies show that declining serum Klotho levels independently predict the rate of eGFR loss and the development of end-stage renal disease in diverse CKD populations, including diabetic nephropathy [19] and non-diabetic CKD [15], reinforcing its status as both a reliable biomarker and an active pathogenic contributor.

Given the pleiotropic nephroprotective functions of Klotho—encompassing TGF-β1/Smad3 antagonism, anti-oxidant activity, anti-inflammatory effects, preservation of tubular cell viability, and correction of FGF23/phosphate dysregulation—strategies to restore renal Klotho expression hold exceptional therapeutic promise. While exogenous recombinant soluble Klotho protein has demonstrated anti-fibrotic efficacy in preclinical models [17,18,20], its short circulating half-life necessitates frequent parenteral administration, rendering it impractical for long-term treatment of a chronic progressive disease such as CKD. AAV-based gene therapy has emerged as the vehicle of choice for sustained, tissue-targeted transgene expression, benefiting from an established clinical safety profile, predominantly episomal vector persistence in post-mitotic cells reducing insertional mutagenesis risk, limited immunogenicity of the transgene product after single administration, and the availability of multiple capsid serotypes with distinct tropisms [21,22]. Five AAV-based products have received regulatory approval from the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency for treatment of inherited diseases affecting the eye, central nervous system, and skeletal muscle, demonstrating a mature translational pathway [22]. Among AAV serotypes, AAV9 demonstrates broad tropism and achieves superior transduction of both renal cortex and medulla when delivered via retrograde renal vein injection, outperforming systemic intravenous administration at half the dose [23]. Restricting transgene expression to renal epithelial cells using the kidney-specific Ksp-cadherin (cadherin-16, Cdh16) promoter—whose transcriptional activity is confined to tubular epithelial cells throughout the collecting duct and nephron—substantially enhances organ specificity and limits off-target hepatic expression, thereby reducing the risk of capsid-associated hepatotoxicity that has been observed with high-dose systemic administration [23,24,25]. Despite the compelling conceptual rationale for Klotho gene delivery in CKD, no study has to date systematically examined whether AAV9-mediated renal Klotho restoration can durably suppress TGF-β1/Smad3 signalling in vivo using a kidney-tubular-targeted vector, across two mechanistically distinct models of progressive CKD, with concurrent mechanistic dissection in human tubular epithelial cells that specifically distinguishes receptor-level blockade from downstream effector inhibition. We therefore designed the present study to test the overarching hypothesis that a single administration of AAV9/Ksp-KL delivered via renal vein injection would restore renal Klotho expression, suppress p-Smad3 at the receptor level while upregulating Smad7, reduce fibrosis gene expression and ECM accumulation, improve renal function in both the 5/6 nephrectomy (5/6 Nx) and UUO models, and that these effects would be mechanistically attributable to Smad3 inhibition as established through targeted gain-of-function and pharmacological rescue experiments in human proximal tubular cells.

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of CM and conducted in full compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines [26].  All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Male C57BL/6J mice aged 8–10 weeks (body weight 22–26 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), acclimatised for 7 days upon arrival, and housed in groups of four to five per cage under specific-pathogen-free conditions at a controlled ambient temperature of 22 ± 2°C, relative humidity 50–60%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Animals had unrestricted access to standard rodent chow (protein content 20%, fat content 4%) and autoclaved water throughout the study.

5/6 Nephrectomy (5/6 Nx) model. CKD was induced by a two-stage surgical procedure performed under isoflurane inhalation anaesthesia (induction at 3%, maintenance at 1.5–2% in 100% oxygen). In the first stage, the left kidney was exposed via a retroperitoneal flank incision, and the upper and lower poles (representing approximately 2/3 of total left renal mass) were excised using a scalpel, with haemostasis achieved by electrocautery. After a 7-day recovery period allowing stabilisation of renal haemodynamics, the right kidney was completely excised via a second retroperitoneal incision, leaving approximately 1/6 of the original total renal mass. Sham-operated control mice underwent identical anaesthesia and bilateral retroperitoneal incisions with organ manipulation but without renal tissue removal [27]. Animals with post-operative serum creatinine below 40 µmol/L at week 4 were excluded from the study as insufficiently uraemic (n = 2 excluded). All animals received meloxicam (5 mg/kg subcutaneously) for post-operative analgesia for 3 days.

Unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) model. Under isoflurane anaesthesia, the left ureter was exposed via a midline abdominal incision and ligated at two points at the ureteropelvic junction with 4 − 0 silk sutures without division, as previously described [8]. The contralateral (right) unobstructed kidney of UUO mice served as an intra-animal reference control. Animals were euthanised on day 14 post-ligation (day 7 post-treatment for groups receiving vector at day 7) under deep isoflurane anaesthesia followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death.

Experimental groups. Following successful CKD induction confirmed at week 4 post-5/6 Nx (Scr > 40 µmol/L), mice were randomised into six groups (n = 8 per group) using a computer-generated random number sequence concealed from the surgeons and technicians who processed the tissue samples: (i) Sham + vehicle (Sham); (ii) 5/6 Nx + vehicle (CKD); (iii) 5/6 Nx + AAV empty vector control at high dose (AAV-EV, 2×10¹¹ vector genomes, vg); (iv) 5/6 Nx + AAV-KL low dose (AAV-KL-L, 5×10¹⁰ vg); (v) 5/6 Nx + AAV-KL high dose (AAV-KL-H, 2×10¹¹ vg); and (vi) 5/6 Nx + recombinant human soluble Klotho protein (rKlotho, 0.01 mg/kg intraperitoneally three times per week, positive control). Corresponding treatment groups (i–v) were established in the UUO model. Vector or vehicle (phosphate-buffered saline, PBS) was administered at week 4 post-5/6 Nx. Animals were euthanised at week 16 post-5/6 Nx (week 12 post-treatment) or at day 14 post-UUO (day 7 post-treatment) under deep isoflurane anaesthesia followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death. The investigators performing histological scoring and densitometry analyses were blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

The human Klotho coding sequence (cDNA; NM_004795.4, full-length 3,045-bp open reading frame encoding the 1,014-amino acid membrane-bound Klotho isoform) was codon-optimised for murine expression and synthesised by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The transgene cassette was constructed by inserting the KL cDNA downstream of the 1,268-bp proximal fragment of the mouse Ksp-cadherin (Cdh16) gene promoter, which confers kidney tubular epithelial cell-specific expression in vivo [24,25], followed by a bovine growth hormone polyadenylation signal (BGH-pA), within a self-complementary AAV2 inverted terminal repeat (ITR) backbone. This scAAV configuration was selected to accelerate the onset of transgene expression by eliminating the requirement for second-strand synthesis. A control empty vector (AAV-EV) was constructed using an identical backbone and promoter but containing a stuffer sequence in place of the KL insert.

Both constructs were packaged into the AAV9 capsid using a triple-plasmid co-transfection system in HEK293T cells (pAAV-vector, pHelper, pRC-AAV9), and recombinant viral particles were purified by iodixanol density gradient ultracentrifugation. The purified vector was dialysed against phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.001% Pluronic F-68 and concentrated by ultrafiltration using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal units (100 kDa molecular weight cut-off; Millipore). Final physical titres were determined by droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) using primers targeting the BGH-pA sequence; functional titres were additionally assessed by qPCR of transduced HEK293T cells 48 hours post-infection. Purity was confirmed by silver-stained SDS-PAGE (demonstrating the characteristic VP1/VP2/VP3 band pattern at approximately 87, 73, and 62 kDa, respectively, with VP3 as the dominant species) and by transmission electron microscopy confirming predominantly filled capsids. Residual endotoxin was assessed by the limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) kinetic turbidimetric assay and confirmed to be below 1 EU/mL in all preparations. Vector preparations were stored at − 80°C until use, and a single freeze-thaw cycle was used per experiment.

Retrograde renal vein injection was performed using a modification of the procedure described by Rocca et al. [23]. Briefly, mice were anaesthetised with isoflurane (2–3% induction, 1.5% maintenance) and placed in the dorsal recumbent position. The left kidney was exposed via a retroperitoneal flank incision, and the renal vein was isolated from surrounding connective tissue. A micro-vascular clamp was applied at the junction of the left renal vein and the inferior vena cava. Using a 30-gauge insulin needle, the vector (total volume 100 µL in PBS) was injected slowly over 30 seconds into the exposed renal vein proximal to the clamp. The clamp was maintained for 2 minutes to allow the vector to perfuse the renal parenchyma under conditions of temporarily reduced venous outflow, then carefully removed. Haemostasis was verified at the injection site, the retroperitoneum was closed in layers with absorbable sutures, and the skin was closed with surgical staples. Animals received meloxicam (5 mg/kg subcutaneously) for 3 days post-operatively. In the 5/6 Nx model, injection was performed into the remnant left renal vein; in the UUO model, injection was performed into the obstructed left renal vein 7 days after ureteral ligation.

Tail-vein blood samples were collected at baseline (pre-surgery), week 4 (time of treatment), week 8, week 12, and week 16 (sacrifice). At sacrifice, terminal blood was obtained by cardiac puncture under deep isoflurane terminal anaesthesia before cervical dislocation. Serum was prepared by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C and stored at − 80°C until assay. Serum creatinine (Scr) was measured by the Jaffe kinetic method using an automated clinical chemistry analyser (Beckman Coulter AU5800, Brea, CA, USA). Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was quantified by the urease–glutamate dehydrogenase coupled enzymatic assay on the same platform. Twenty-four-hour urine samples were collected in metabolic cages during the last 24 hours before sacrifice; urine protein was quantified by the Bradford colorimetric protein assay using bovine serum albumin as standard. Albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) was calculated from spot morning urine samples using an immunoturbidimetric human albumin assay (Siemens Dimension Vista 1500, Erlangen, Germany) and urine creatinine quantified by the Jaffe method.

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 30 mg of snap-frozen renal cortex using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by NanoDrop One spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA); samples with A260/A280 < 1.8 were excluded. Complementary DNA was synthesised from 1 µg of DNase-treated RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 instrument (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) using SYBR Green I Master Mix in a 10-µL reaction volume. Each sample was run in technical triplicate, and the mean cycle threshold (Ct) value was used for analysis. Primer sequences were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool with default settings for intron-spanning amplicons of 80–150 bp; all primers were validated for amplification efficiency between 90% and 110% and single-product specificity by dissociation curve analysis before use in the study. Target genes analysed were: KL (Klotho), TGFB1 (TGF-β1), SMAD3, SMAD7, COL1A1 (collagen type I alpha-1 chain), FN1 (fibronectin 1), ACTA2 (alpha-smooth muscle actin), CDH1 (E-cadherin), and VIM (vimentin). GAPDH was used as the reference gene; its stability across all experimental groups was confirmed by geNorm analysis (M-value < 0.5). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method with normalisation to GAPDH and expression in the Sham group as the reference.

Renal cortex tissue (approximately 30 mg) or cell lysates were homogenised in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with Complete Mini protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche) at a ratio of 10 µL buffer per mg tissue. Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C, and supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Thirty micrograms of protein per lane was resolved on 8% or 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gels (selecting gel percentage based on target protein molecular weight) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Immobilon-P, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) at 100 V for 90 minutes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dried milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies diluted in 5% bovine serum albumin/TBST: anti-Klotho (1:1000, ab181373, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-TGF-β1 (1:1000, #3711, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-Smad3 (1:1000, #9523, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-phospho-Smad3 (Ser423/425; 1:1000, #9520, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-Smad7 (1:1000, ab216428, Abcam), anti-Collagen I (1:1000, ab34710, Abcam), anti-Fibronectin (1:1000, ab2413, Abcam), anti-α-SMA (1:1000, ab7817, Abcam), anti-E-cadherin (1:1000, #3195, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-Vimentin (1:1000, #5741, Cell Signaling Technology), and anti-GAPDH (1:5000, #5174, Cell Signaling Technology). After three 10-minute washes in TBST, membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 hour at room temperature. Bands were visualised using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Clarity Western ECL, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and imaged on a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad). Densitometric quantification was performed using ImageJ version 1.53k (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) on non-saturated exposures, with target band intensity normalised to GAPDH loading control or, in the case of p-Smad3, to total Smad3 protein on the same membrane after stripping and re-probing.

Circulating soluble Klotho was measured in serum and spot urine samples using a commercially available soluble Klotho ELISA kit that detects both human and mouse sKlotho (IBL International GmbH, Hamburg, Germany; catalogue No. 27998; detection range 31.3–2000 pg/mL; sensitivity 6.15 pg/mL). Briefly, 50 µL of appropriately diluted serum or urine (1:4 dilution in the provided assay buffer) was loaded per well in duplicate. The plate was incubated for 1 hour at room temperature on a plate shaker, washed three times, incubated with detection antibody for 30 minutes, incubated with HRP–streptavidin conjugate for 30 minutes, and developed with TMB substrate. Optical density was read at 450 nm with reference at 570 nm. Concentrations were interpolated from a four-parameter logistic standard curve. Intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were below 8% and 10%, respectively.

Human proximal tubular epithelial HK-2 cells (American Type Culture Collection CRL-2190, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12, 1:1) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Cells were used between passages 5 and 20.

For the EMT model, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 3×10⁵ cells per well and allowed to reach 60% confluency before serum starvation for 24 hours. EMT was then induced by addition of recombinant human TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL; PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) for 48 hours, a concentration and duration validated in this laboratory to produce robust, reproducible EMT without significant cytotoxicity.

To restore Klotho expression, cells were pre-infected with AAV-KL (multiplicity of infection 1×10⁵ vg/cell in serum-free medium for 4 hours) 72 hours prior to TGF-β1 stimulation, allowing sufficient time for transgene expression. Alternatively, cells were transfected with pcDNA3.1-KL (2 µg per well) using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol 48 hours prior to TGF-β1 stimulation.

To establish mechanistic causality, Klotho-restored cells (AAV-KL-infected) were co-transfected with a constitutively active Smad3 overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-SMAD3, 1 µg per well) 24 hours before TGF-β1 stimulation to determine whether forced Smad3 reactivation abrogated Klotho-mediated protection. This experiment distinguishes Smad3-dependent from Smad3-independent effects of Klotho. In a parallel pharmacological rescue experiment, TGF-β1-stimulated cells without Klotho restoration were co-treated with the selective Smad3 phosphorylation inhibitor SIS3 (10 µM; Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA; SIS3 inhibits Smad3 phosphorylation at IC₅₀ approximately 3 µM without affecting Smad2 phosphorylation) to determine whether pharmacological Smad3 blockade phenocopied the protective effects of Klotho gene delivery. The five experimental groups compared in vitro were: (i) vehicle control (0.1% DMSO); (ii) TGF-β1 alone (5 ng/mL); (iii) TGF-β1 + AAV-KL; (iv) TGF-β1 + AAV-KL + pcDNA3.1-SMAD3; (v) TGF-β1 + SIS3. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate and repeated on three independent occasions (n = 3 biological replicates).

Cell apoptosis was quantified in HK-2 cells at 48 hours after TGF-β1 stimulation using the Annexin V–fluorescein isothiocyanate (Annexin V-FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cells were trypsinised, washed twice in cold PBS, and resuspended at 1×10⁶ cells/mL in 1× Binding Buffer. Annexin V-FITC (5 µL) and PI (5 µL) were added to 100 µL of cell suspension and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Samples were diluted to 500 µL with Binding Buffer and immediately analysed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). A minimum of 10,000 events were acquired per sample. Data were analysed using FlowJo version 10.8 (BD Biosciences). Early apoptotic cells were defined as Annexin V-FITC-positive/PI-negative, late apoptotic/necrotic cells as double-positive. Total apoptosis (early plus late) was used for comparisons between groups.

Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were measured at weeks 4, 8, and 12 post-vector injection using commercially available kinetic enzymatic assays on the Beckman Coulter AU5800 platform. Reference ranges for male C57BL/6J mice were established in-house from age-matched untreated controls (ALT: 10–50 U/L; AST: 40–130 U/L). At sacrifice, a comprehensive panel of off-target organs—including liver, spleen, heart, lung, skeletal muscle (gastrocnemius), and brain—was collected for vector genome biodistribution analysis by quantitative PCR as described in section2.5, using primers specific for the BGH-pA transgene element normalised to the murine Titin gene (single-copy reference). Liver sections (5 µm, paraffin-embedded) were stained with haematoxylin and eosin for histological assessment of hepatic toxicity. Body weights were recorded weekly throughout the study.

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical comparisons among three or more independent groups were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's multiple comparisons post-hoc test, which was selected a priori for its ability to control family-wise error rate while maintaining power for all pairwise comparisons. For direct comparisons between two independent groups, unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests were applied. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between renal Klotho mRNA and p-Smad3 protein levels across all animals. The threshold for statistical significance was α = 0.05 (two-tailed) throughout. All statistical computations and graphical outputs were produced using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Sample size per group was determined prospectively by power analysis using G*Power version 3.1.9, targeting 80% power with a two-sided α of 0.05 to detect a 25% difference in serum creatinine between treated and untreated CKD groups, assuming a coefficient of variation of 15% based on published 5/6 Nx data; this calculation yielded a minimum of 7 animals per group, and 8 animals were enrolled per group to account for potential attrition.

RNA sequencing was performed on renal cortex tissue collected at sacrifice from three experimental groups: Sham, CKD + AAV-GFP (empty vector control), and CKD + AAV-KL-High (n = 3 animals per group, nine samples total). Total RNA was extracted from snap-frozen renal cortex using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) as described in section2.5. RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA); only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) > 8.0 were used for library preparation. Strand-specific mRNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol, with poly(A) selection for mRNA enrichment and a mean insert size of approximately 300 bp. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using paired-end 150 bp reads, generating a minimum of 40 million read pairs per sample.

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control assessment using FastQC (v0.11.9). Adapter sequences and low-quality bases (Phred score < 20) were trimmed using Trim Galore (v0.6.7) with default parameters. Trimmed reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10/GRCm38) using STAR aligner (v2.7.10a) [28] with default two-pass mapping parameters and a maximum of 10 mismatches per read pair. Alignment rates exceeded 90% for all samples. Gene-level read counts were quantified using featureCounts (Subread v2.0.3) against the GENCODE vM25 gene annotation. Differential expression analysis was performed in R version 4.2.3 using DESeq2 (v1.36.0) [29], with size factor normalisation and the Wald test for pairwise comparisons. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they met both of the following criteria: |log2FoldChange| > 0.585 (corresponding to |fold change| > 1.5) and Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for Biological Process terms was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.4.4) [30] with the org.Mm.eg.db annotation database. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the same package with the mouse KEGG database [31]. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the fgsea R package (v1.22.0) [32] with MSigDB Hallmark gene sets (v7.5.1) and 1,000 permutations; gene sets with normalised enrichment score (NES) absolute value > 1.5 and BH-adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Hierarchical clustering heatmaps of the top 50 differentially expressed genes were generated using the pheatmap R package with Ward.D2 linkage and Z-score row normalisation. Volcano plots were generated using the EnhancedVolcano R package. All enrichment analyses used BH-adjusted p-value < 0.05 as the significance threshold for individual terms and pathways. We first characterised the 5/6 Nx model to confirm successful CKD induction and to establish baseline levels of the key molecular endpoints at the time of vector administration (week 4) and at final sacrifice (week 16; Fig. 1). Compared with Sham controls, 5/6 Nx mice exhibited a progressive and significant decline in body weight beginning at week 2 post-surgery, reaching a mean difference of approximately 12% below Sham at week 12 and remaining stable thereafter (Fig. 1A). This body weight deficit likely reflects the catabolic effects of uraemia, reduced food intake, and the metabolic consequences of loss of functioning nephron mass.

Renal excretory function was markedly and progressively impaired in 5/6 Nx mice (Fig. 1B–D). Serum creatinine (Scr) rose from a pre-surgical baseline of approximately 15–18 µmol/L to approximately 68 µmol/L at week 4 and further to approximately 72–74 µmol/L at week 16 in untreated CKD animals, confirming establishment of a stable uraemic state. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) rose from approximately 8 mmol/L in Sham to approximately 27–28 mmol/L in CKD mice at week 16, representing a 3.4-fold elevation. Twenty-four-hour urine protein excretion was approximately 32 mg/24 h in CKD versus approximately 3.5 mg/24 h in Sham animals at week 16, and spot urine ACR was approximately 175 mg/g versus approximately 18 mg/g, respectively, indicating significant glomerular proteinuria characteristic of the remnant kidney model.

The molecular hallmarks of CKD-associated Klotho deficiency and TGF-β1/Smad3 pathway activation were prominently displayed in remnant kidneys (Fig. 1E–I). Kidney-specific Klotho mRNA was reduced by approximately 65% in the remnant kidneys of 5/6 Nx mice relative to Sham controls at week 16, as assessed by qRT-PCR (Fig. 1E). This was faithfully mirrored at the protein level by Western blot densitometry, with renal Klotho protein in cortex lysates reduced to approximately 28% of Sham levels (Fig. 1F). Circulating sKlotho, measured by ELISA, fell precipitously from approximately 580 pg/mL in Sham mice to approximately 210 pg/mL in CKD mice at week 16 (Fig. 1G), representing a 64% reduction that is consistent with the magnitude of Klotho deficiency reported in clinical CKD cohorts [15]. Notably, sKlotho decline was already detectable at week 4 (approximately 380 pg/mL in CKD versus approximately 565 pg/mL in Sham), preceding the maximal deterioration of serum creatinine and suggesting that Klotho loss may be an early and amplifying contributor to progressive injury.

Concomitantly, the TGF-β1/Smad3 profibrotic axis was markedly activated in CKD kidneys at week 16 (Fig. 1H,I). TGF-β1 mRNA was elevated approximately 2.8-fold above Sham (Fig. 1H). Total Smad3 protein was modestly increased (approximately 1.4-fold), while phospho-Smad3 (p-Smad3, Ser423/425) was profoundly elevated approximately 3.2-fold, yielding a p-Smad3/Smad3 ratio approximately 2.3-fold greater than in Sham animals (Fig. 1I). Smad7 mRNA and protein were both significantly reduced by approximately 45% in CKD kidneys relative to Sham (Fig. 1I), consistent with the known TGF-β-mediated negative regulation of Smad7 at the transcriptional level and indicating a severely disrupted Smad3/Smad7 balance. A significant inverse Pearson correlation was identified between renal Klotho mRNA expression and p-Smad3 protein levels across all animals at week 16 (r = − 0.74, p < 0.001), providing an initial correlative link between Klotho deficiency and Smad3 hyperactivation. These data collectively confirm that the 5/6 Nx model robustly reproduces the key molecular features of human CKD-associated fibrotic injury, establishing appropriate conditions for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of AAV-KL.

To comprehensively characterise transduction efficiency and tissue specificity, we analysed vector genome biodistribution by quantitative PCR at week 12 post-injection across the treated kidney and six off-target organs (Fig. 2A). In AAV-KL-H-treated animals, renal vector genome copies reached a mean of approximately 8.5 copies per diploid genome in the treated left kidney, representing efficient transduction of the remnant renal parenchyma. Critically, liver—the organ with the next highest vector genome signal—contained only approximately 1.7 copies per diploid genome, a ratio of approximately 5:1 favouring the kidney over the liver (Fig. 2A). This favourable selectivity is attributable to the combination of anatomically targeted renal vein delivery, which maximises renal vascular exposure while limiting systemic distribution, and the Ksp-cadherin promoter, which confines transcriptional activity to renal tubular epithelium [24,25]. Vector genome counts in heart, spleen, lung, skeletal muscle, and brain were all below 0.5 copies per diploid genome, indicating negligible extra-renal transduction at these sites (Fig. 2A). As expected, AAV-KL-L showed approximately half the renal vector genome copy number (approximately 4.2 copies per diploid genome) compared with AAV-KL-H, confirming dose-proportional transduction efficiency. The AAV-EV control exhibited renal vector genome counts statistically indistinguishable from AAV-KL-H, confirming that the AAV9 capsid delivers the vector to the target tissue with equivalent efficiency regardless of transgene content.

KL mRNA in the remnant kidney was restored in a dose-dependent manner that closely tracked vector genome copy number (Fig. 2B). In the AAV-KL-L group, KL mRNA was increased to approximately 48% of Sham levels compared with the CKD group (which expressed approximately 35% of Sham). In the AAV-KL-H group, KL expression reached approximately 78% of Sham, a level that was not statistically different from Sham (p = 0.17, Tukey's post-hoc test; Fig. 2B). Importantly, serial assessment of KL mRNA at weeks 4, 8, and 12 post-injection demonstrated no significant decline across this observation window in either dose group, with coefficients of variation within each group over time of less than 15% (Fig. 2B inset). This temporal stability is a critical feature of scAAV episomal vectors in predominantly post-mitotic renal tubular cells, confirming durable, sustained transgene expression that is not subject to significant dilutional silencing by cell division over the 12-week study period.

Klotho protein in renal cortex lysates was restored to approximately 61% of Sham (low dose) and approximately 82% of Sham (high dose) levels by Western blot densitometry (Fig. 2C,D), closely paralleling the mRNA data. Circulating sKlotho, measured by ELISA, was partially but significantly recovered by AAV-KL treatment: from a CKD nadir of approximately 210 pg/mL to approximately 335 pg/mL in the AAV-KL-L group and approximately 445 pg/mL in the AAV-KL-H group (Fig. 2E). The magnitude of sKlotho recovery with high-dose AAV-KL (approximately 76% of Sham levels) was intermediate between CKD and Sham values, consistent with the fact that only the remnant left kidney—representing approximately 1/6 of original total renal mass—was transduced, and the transduced cells contribute membrane Klotho that is constitutively shed as sKlotho into the circulation. These data demonstrate that kidney-directed AAV-KL gene delivery effectively restores both local renal and systemic sKlotho to a substantial fraction of physiological levels.

Treatment with AAV-KL produced significant, dose-dependent, and sustained improvements in all assessed renal functional parameters measured at week 16 (Fig. 3A–D). In the AAV-KL-H group, Scr was reduced from approximately 72 µmol/L in untreated CKD controls to approximately 38 µmol/L, representing a 47% improvement that substantially but not completely closed the gap to Sham levels (approximately 19 µmol/L; Fig. 3A). The AAV-KL-L group showed an intermediate but significant improvement, with Scr of approximately 54 µmol/L. Importantly, the AAV-EV control group maintained Scr values of approximately 70 µmol/L, statistically indistinguishable from untreated CKD (p > 0.99; Fig. 3A). This finding rigorously confirms that the therapeutic benefit observed in AAV-KL-treated mice is attributable specifically to Klotho transgene expression, rather than to non-specific effects of the vector administration procedure, the AAV9 capsid, or the surgical manipulation.

BUN was reduced from approximately 28 mmol/L in CKD to approximately 14.5 mmol/L in the AAV-KL-H group (a 48% reduction) and approximately 20 mmol/L in the AAV-KL-L group (Fig. 3B). The rKlotho protein positive control group produced BUN values (approximately 13.8 mmol/L) comparable to the AAV-KL-H group, providing important cross-validation that the functional improvements are specifically attributable to Klotho restoration. Twenty-four-hour urine protein excretion declined markedly in the high-dose group: from approximately 32 mg/24 h in CKD controls to approximately 12 mg/24 h in AAV-KL-H (Fig. 3C), a 62.5% reduction that was highly statistically significant (p < 0.001). Spot urine ACR showed a parallel and proportional decrease, from approximately 175 mg/g in CKD to approximately 65 mg/g in AAV-KL-H and approximately 112 mg/g in AAV-KL-L (Fig. 3D). These improvements in proteinuria may reflect both reduction of tubuloglomerular feedback-driven hyperfiltration—a haemodynamic benefit of reduced tubular fibrosis—and direct suppression of glomerular permeability abnormalities driven by reduced Smad3-mediated ECM accumulation in the glomerular mesangium.

To determine whether the observed functional improvements were accompanied by suppression of the fibrogenic gene programme at the molecular level, we quantified fibrosis-related mRNA and protein expression in renal cortex at sacrifice (Fig. 3E–H). In untreated CKD controls at week 16, the ECM genes Col1a1 and FN1 were elevated approximately 4.1-fold and 3.4-fold above Sham levels, respectively, while the myofibroblast marker ACTA2 was elevated approximately 3.8-fold, confirming extensive fibrosis progression (Fig. 3E,F,G). High-dose AAV-KL treatment reduced Col1a1 mRNA by approximately 58% relative to untreated CKD (Fig. 3E), reaching a level that was not statistically different from Sham (p = 0.23, Tukey's post-hoc). FN1 mRNA was reduced by approximately 45% in the AAV-KL-H group (Fig. 3F), and ACTA2 mRNA was suppressed by approximately 55% (Fig. 3G). The AAV-KL-L group demonstrated significant but attenuated reductions of approximately 38%, 30%, and 38% for Col1a1, FN1, and ACTA2, respectively, confirming dose-dependence. Western blot analyses of the corresponding proteins (Collagen I, Fibronectin, and α-SMA) confirmed the transcriptional data at the protein level, with high-dose AAV-KL reducing Collagen I protein by approximately 52%, Fibronectin by approximately 44%, and α-SMA by approximately 51% relative to CKD controls (Fig. 3H). The AAV-EV group showed no significant differences in any fibrosis marker compared with untreated CKD mice, excluding non-specific vector effects.

In parallel with suppression of profibrotic markers, the EMT programme in residual nephron tubular cells was also significantly modulated by AAV-KL treatment (Fig. 3E–H insets). The epithelial identity gene CDH1 (E-cadherin mRNA), which was reduced to approximately 38% of Sham levels in untreated CKD, was partially but significantly restored to approximately 74% of Sham in the AAV-KL-H group (Fig. 3E inset). Vimentin mRNA, a mesenchymal marker elevated approximately 2.9-fold in CKD, was reduced by approximately 48% in the high-dose group (Fig. 3F inset). Western blot confirmed E-cadherin protein partial restoration and vimentin protein reduction in AAV-KL-treated kidneys. These reciprocal changes in epithelial and mesenchymal marker expression—restoration of E-cadherin alongside suppression of vimentin—demonstrate that AAV-KL treatment meaningfully reverses the tubular EMT programme in the remnant nephron, consistent with the re-establishment of tubular epithelial identity under conditions of reduced TGF-β1/Smad3 activity.

To complement the targeted qRT-PCR analyses and to gain an unbiased transcriptome-wide perspective on the molecular consequences of CKD and their reversal by AAV-KL, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on renal cortex tissue from three experimental groups: Sham, CKD + AAV-GFP (empty vector control), and CKD + AAV-KL-High (n = 3 animals per group). This approach enabled systematic identification of the full spectrum of dysregulated gene programmes in CKD and their degree of rescue by Klotho restoration, extending the mechanistic analysis far beyond the handful of candidate genes assessed by hypothesis-driven molecular assays.

Comparison of CKD + AAV-GFP versus Sham kidneys by DESeq2 analysis identified 924 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) meeting the criteria of |log2FoldChange| > 0.585 and BH-adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Fig. 4A). Of these, 472 genes were upregulated and 452 were downregulated in CKD relative to Sham. The volcano plot (Fig. 4A) illustrates the striking polarisation of the transcriptional landscape in CKD: among the most significantly upregulated genes were canonical pro-fibrotic and ECM-encoding transcripts including COL1A1 (log2FC = 3.21, padj = 2.3×10⁻¹⁸), COL3A1 (log2FC = 2.87, padj = 4.1×10⁻¹⁶), FN1 (log2FC = 2.34, padj = 1.7×10⁻¹⁴), ACTA2 (log2FC = 2.68, padj = 8.9×10⁻¹⁵), TGFB1 (log2FC = 1.49, padj = 3.2×10⁻⁹), MMP2 (log2FC = 2.01, padj = 6.4×10⁻¹²), MMP9 (log2FC = 1.93, padj = 2.8×10⁻¹¹), and CTGF (log2FC = 2.45, padj = 5.6×10⁻¹³). Conversely, among the most significantly downregulated genes were KL itself (log2FC = − 2.51, padj = 1.1×10⁻¹⁷), the epithelial marker CDH1 (log2FC = − 1.82, padj = 4.3×10⁻¹²), the water channel genes AQP1 (log2FC = − 2.14, padj = 7.6×10⁻¹⁴) and AQP2 (log2FC = − 1.98, padj = 2.1×10⁻¹³), the podocyte slit diaphragm gene NPHS1 (log2FC = − 1.67, padj = 3.8×10⁻¹⁰), and multiple tubular transporter genes including SLC12A1, SLC34A1, and SLC22A6, collectively reflecting the global de-differentiation and functional deterioration of the CKD tubular epithelium.

Comparison of CKD + AAV-KL-High versus CKD + AAV-GFP identified 610 DEGs (293 upregulated and 317 downregulated by AAV-KL treatment; Fig. 4B). The volcano plot (Fig. 4B) reveals a compelling reversal pattern: the majority of the most significantly changed genes in the KL-treated comparison are the precise mirror image of the CKD-versus-Sham comparison, with fibrosis and ECM genes now appearing as the most significantly downregulated (COL1A1, COL3A1, FN1, ACTA2, CTGF among the top downregulated DEGs in KL-treated animals) and tubular identity and metabolic genes appearing among the most significantly upregulated (KL, CDH1, AQP1, AQP2, and fatty acid oxidation genes restored). This pattern strongly suggests that AAV-KL treatment directly counteracts the CKD-specific transcriptional programme rather than inducing a non-specific or alternative gene expression signature.

To formally quantify the extent to which AAV-KL rescues CKD-induced transcriptional dysregulation, we constructed a Venn diagram of "rescued" genes—defined as genes upregulated in CKD (versus Sham) and subsequently downregulated by AAV-KL treatment (versus CKD), or genes downregulated in CKD and restored by AAV-KL—identifying 306 genes meeting these bidirectional criteria (Fig. 4C). This overlap was highly statistically significant (p < 1×10⁻¹⁵, hypergeometric test), confirming that the transcriptional rescue is far greater than expected by chance and represents a coherent, directionally consistent reversal of the CKD fibrotic programme. Among the 306 rescued genes were core fibrosis effectors (COL1A1, COL3A1, FN1, ACTA2), EMT transcription factors (SNAI1, ZEB1, TWIST1), inflammatory mediators (CCL2, IL1B, TNF), and tubular identity genes (CDH1, AQP1, SLC12A3), collectively spanning multiple dimensions of CKD pathophysiology.

To visualise the global transcriptome-level separation of the three experimental groups, we generated a hierarchical clustering heatmap of the top 50 DEGs (ranked by adjusted p-value across all pairwise comparisons) using Ward.D2 linkage and Z-score row normalisation (Fig. 4D). The heatmap reveals clear and complete separation of all three groups, with each group forming a tight, internally consistent cluster. Critically, the CKD + AAV-KL samples cluster substantially closer to the Sham group than to the CKD + AAV-GFP group, with a distinct intermediate transcriptional profile that shows partial-to-complete normalisation of the CKD fibrotic cluster and partial-to-complete restoration of the Sham-like tubular identity cluster. This hierarchical clustering result provides strong visual and quantitative confirmation that AAV-KL treatment induces a global transcriptome-level normalisation of CKD kidneys, not merely modulating a handful of genes in isolation.

Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process enrichment analysis of the 472 genes upregulated in CKD versus Sham identified highly significant enrichment of extracellular matrix organization (FDR = 1.8×10⁻¹⁵), TGF-beta receptor signalling pathway (FDR = 6.1×10⁻⁸), epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (FDR = 2.9×10⁻⁶), inflammatory response (FDR = 4.7×10⁻⁶), and collagen fibril organization (FDR = 8.3×10⁻⁷) among the top enriched processes (Fig. 4E). Conversely, GO terms enriched among the 452 genes downregulated in CKD versus Sham included transmembrane transport (FDR = 2.4×10⁻¹²), ion transport (FDR = 5.8×10⁻¹¹), fatty acid beta-oxidation (FDR = 3.1×10⁻⁸), and proximal tubule bicarbonate reclamation (FDR = 6.7×10⁻⁷; Fig. 4F), consistent with the well-recognised CKD-associated loss of tubular metabolic and transport function. All of these pathological transcriptional signatures were significantly attenuated in CKD + AAV-KL kidneys, with the GO terms for ECM organization, TGF-beta signalling, and EMT showing the greatest degree of reversal among the rescued gene set.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the rescued gene set confirmed that TGF-beta signalling (FDR = 2.1×10⁻¹⁰), ECM-receptor interaction (FDR = 3.4×10⁻⁹), focal adhesion (FDR = 7.8×10⁻⁸), and TNF signalling (FDR = 1.2×10⁻⁶) were the most significantly enriched pathways reversed by AAV-KL treatment (Fig. 4G). The consistent appearance of TGF-beta signalling and ECM-receptor interaction as top-ranked pathways in both the CKD-versus-Sham upregulated gene set and the rescued gene set confirms that these represent the primary and most robustly modulated transcriptional programmes in this model system.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the MSigDB Hallmark gene sets provided additional pathway-level confirmation of the transcriptional rescue (Fig. 4H). In the CKD + AAV-GFP versus Sham comparison, the Hallmark EMT gene set showed strong positive enrichment (NES = 1.85, FDR = 0.003) and the Hallmark TGF-beta signalling gene set was also positively enriched (NES = 1.78, FDR = 0.008), while the Hallmark Oxidative Phosphorylation gene set showed significant negative enrichment (NES = − 1.72, FDR = 0.005), consistent with the known mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetic failure that accompanies CKD progression. In the CKD + AAV-KL-High versus CKD + AAV-GFP comparison, these enrichment patterns were substantially reversed: the Hallmark EMT gene set was negatively enriched (NES = − 1.68, FDR = 0.004), Hallmark TGF-beta signalling was negatively enriched (NES = − 1.55, FDR = 0.012), and Hallmark Oxidative Phosphorylation was positively enriched in the KL-treated group (NES = 1.52, FDR = 0.018). The restoration of oxidative phosphorylation gene expression by AAV-KL suggests that the therapeutic benefit extends beyond TGF-β pathway antagonism to encompass metabolic reprogramming of surviving tubular epithelial cells.

Taken together, the RNA-seq analysis provides unbiased, transcriptome-wide confirmation that AAV-KL treatment reverses the CKD fibrotic transcriptional programme at a global level. The 306 rescued genes span multiple pathological mechanisms—ECM deposition, EMT, inflammation, and tubular metabolic failure—and the TGF-β/Smad and ECM organisation pathways emerge as the most significantly affected, fully corroborating and extending the targeted molecular analyses. These findings establish that the therapeutic benefit of AAV-KL is not restricted to a narrow set of candidate fibrosis markers but represents a broad, coordinated restoration of tubular transcriptome homeostasis.

To determine the molecular mechanism through which restored Klotho suppresses the fibrogenic programme, we examined in detail the components of the TGF-β1/Smad3 signalling axis in renal cortex of all groups at week 16 (Fig. 5A–D). A key mechanistic prediction of the receptor-level antagonism model is that Klotho should suppress downstream Smad3 phosphorylation without reducing the upstream TGF-β1 ligand, since Klotho acts by blocking ligand binding to TGFβR2 rather than by suppressing TGF-β1 synthesis [17].

Consistent with this prediction, TGF-β1 protein levels in renal cortex lysates did not significantly differ between untreated CKD controls and any of the AAV-KL treatment groups at week 16 (Fig. 5A). TGF-β1 mRNA was likewise unchanged between CKD and AAV-KL groups. This finding is mechanistically important and distinguishes the Klotho mechanism from that of many other anti-fibrotic interventions that act by reducing TGF-β1 expression [6], and is fully consistent with the direct TGFβR2 binding mechanism established by biochemical studies [17].

In sharp contrast to the unchanged TGF-β1 levels, the TGF-β1/Smad3 signalling output—as measured by p-Smad3 protein—was profoundly and dose-dependently reduced by AAV-KL treatment (Fig. 5B). The p-Smad3/total Smad3 protein ratio fell from approximately 2.3-fold (CKD vs. Sham) to approximately 1.6-fold in AAV-KL-L and to approximately 1.2-fold in AAV-KL-H—the latter approaching Sham levels and representing a 47% reduction relative to untreated CKD (Fig. 5B). This reduction in p-Smad3 in the presence of unchanged TGF-β1 provides compelling evidence for receptor-level blockade as the primary mechanism. Total Smad3 protein itself was modestly reduced in AAV-KL-H mice (approximately 1.2-fold reduction versus CKD), likely reflecting reduced TGF-β1-dependent transcriptional autoamplification of Smad3 expression.

The inhibitory Smad7 was significantly and dose-dependently upregulated by AAV-KL treatment at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5C,D). Smad7 mRNA in the high-dose group was approximately 2.1-fold above CKD levels and exceeded Sham values (Fig. 5C), while Smad7 protein was increased approximately 1.9-fold relative to CKD (Fig. 5D). This Smad7 upregulation can be understood as a restoration of the physiological negative-feedback loop: as p-Smad3 nuclear accumulation is reduced by Klotho-mediated receptor blockade, the Smad3-driven transcriptional induction of Smad7 is diminished, but the resulting reduction in Smad7 protein at steady state releases a previously Smad3-suppressed population of Smad7-expressing cells from that inhibitory constraint, enabling a net increase in Smad7 to homeostatic levels that are sufficient to maintain the attenuated Smad3 signalling [9]. Taken together, the unchanged TGF-β1 ligand, reduced p-Smad3, and upregulated Smad7 form a mechanistically coherent molecular signature consistent with receptor-level blockade and downstream pathway rebalancing by restored Klotho.

3.7 In vitro mechanistic validation: Klotho suppresses TGF-β1-induced EMT via Smad3 inhibition, and Smad3 overexpression abrogates Klotho-mediated protection

To establish direct mechanistic causality and exclude the many potential confounding variables that complicate in vivo interpretation, we performed a comprehensive series of rescue experiments in TGF-β1-stimulated HK-2 cells (Fig. 5E–H). Treatment of HK-2 cells with TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL, 48 h) produced robust and reproducible EMT, evidenced by morphological transformation to a spindle-shaped mesenchymal appearance, approximately 55% reduction in E-cadherin protein, upregulation of Vimentin (approximately 3.1-fold), α-SMA (approximately 2.8-fold), and Collagen I (approximately 2.5-fold; Fig. 5E). In parallel, p-Smad3 was induced approximately 4.2-fold while Smad7 protein was reduced approximately 40% (Fig. 5F). TGF-β1 stimulation also significantly increased apoptosis, with Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry revealing total apoptotic cells rising from approximately 5% in vehicle-treated controls to approximately 18% in TGF-β1-treated cells (Fig. 5G), consistent with the pro-apoptotic effects of Smad3 hyperactivation in tubular epithelial cells [8].

Pre-infection with AAV-KL (MOI 1×10⁵ vg/cell, confirmed to restore intracellular Klotho protein expression by Western blot) markedly attenuated TGF-β1-induced changes across all measured endpoints. The p-Smad3 induction was blunted by approximately 68% (Fig. 5F); E-cadherin protein was preserved at approximately 72% of control levels (versus approximately 45% in TGF-β1 alone); Vimentin was reduced by approximately 52% relative to TGF-β1 alone; Smad7 was restored to near-basal levels; and total apoptotic fraction was reduced from approximately 18% to approximately 8% (Fig. 5F,G,H). These results comprehensively confirm the anti-EMT, anti-apoptotic, and Smad3-suppressive effects of Klotho in a human renal tubular cell context.

Critically, when AAV-KL-restored cells were co-transfected with a constitutively active Smad3 overexpression construct (pcDNA3.1-SMAD3), the protective effects of Klotho on EMT markers, p-Smad3 levels, and apoptosis were substantially reversed (Fig. 5F–H). E-cadherin declined to approximately 45% of control (matching TGF-β1-alone conditions), Vimentin rose to approximately 2.5-fold, Smad7 fell, and apoptosis returned to approximately 15%, closely recapitulating the TGF-β1-alone phenotype (Fig. 5F–H). This reversal of Klotho-mediated protection by forced Smad3 gain-of-function provides the definitive evidence that Smad3 inhibition is the obligate and primary effector mechanism downstream of Klotho signalling in this cellular context. The data exclude the possibility that Klotho protects tubular cells through Smad3-independent mechanisms such as direct antioxidant effects or Wnt antagonism alone, although these may contribute to the overall in vivo phenotype.

In the parallel SIS3 experiment, pharmacological blockade of Smad3 phosphorylation (SIS3, 10 µM) in TGF-β1-stimulated HK-2 cells closely phenocopied the protection conferred by AAV-KL: p-Smad3 was reduced by approximately 62%, E-cadherin was preserved at approximately 78% of control, Vimentin was reduced to approximately 1.6-fold, and apoptosis fell to approximately 9% (Fig. 5F–H). The quantitative equivalence between AAV-KL and SIS3 across multiple endpoints, combined with the abrogation of AAV-KL protection by SMAD3 overexpression, together establish the Klotho-TGFβR2 blockade-reduced p-Smad3-Smad7 de-repression-EMT suppression mechanistic hierarchy with high confidence.

To establish that the therapeutic benefit of AAV-KL is not model-specific and to extend the findings to a mechanistically distinct and more rapidly progressive fibrosis model, we evaluated key endpoints in the UUO model (Fig. 6A–D). As expected, obstructed kidneys of vehicle-treated UUO mice at day 14 exhibited severe tubulointerstitial fibrosis, with marked elevations of Col1a1 (approximately 5.3-fold), FN1 (approximately 4.1-fold), ACTA2 (approximately 4.8-fold), and TGF-β1 (approximately 3.6-fold) mRNA relative to sham contralateral kidneys (Fig. 6A–C). KL mRNA was reduced by approximately 70% in obstructed versus contralateral kidneys of vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 6A), confirming Klotho deficiency in this model as well.

AAV-KL-H delivered to the obstructed kidney at day 7 post-ligation significantly attenuated the progression of fibrosis over the subsequent 7 days. At day 14, Col1a1 mRNA was reduced by approximately 52% in AAV-KL-H-treated obstructed kidneys relative to AAV-EV-treated controls (Fig. 6B), FN1 by approximately 43%, and ACTA2 by approximately 49% (Fig. 6C). Although the absolute levels of fibrosis marker expression remained above those of contralateral control kidneys, reflecting the partial treatment window available after UUO, the reductions relative to AAV-EV were highly statistically significant (p < 0.001 for each marker). Pathway analysis showed that the p-Smad3/total Smad3 ratio was reduced by approximately 58% in AAV-KL-H-treated UUO kidneys relative to AAV-EV-treated controls (Fig. 6D), while TGF-β1 protein levels were again unchanged between groups (data not shown). These UUO results faithfully recapitulate the mechanistic findings from the 5/6 Nx model, demonstrating that the anti-fibrotic and Smad3-suppressive effects of AAV-KL are reproducible across aetiologically distinct models with different surgical approaches and injury kinetics, supporting the generalisability of the therapeutic mechanism.

A comprehensive safety assessment of AAV-KL at both dose levels was conducted throughout the 12-week post-injection observation period (Fig. 6E). Serum ALT and AST values at weeks 4, 8, and 12 post-injection were uniformly within the established normal reference range for male C57BL/6J mice in all AAV-treated groups, and were statistically indistinguishable from both Sham and untreated CKD controls (Fig. 6E). These safety data collectively support the tolerability of the AAV9/Ksp-KL vector at therapeutic doses via renal vein injection in the rodent setting, providing a basis for further preclinical development.

The present study demonstrates that a single administration of an AAV9 vector encoding the human Klotho gene under the kidney-specific Ksp-cadherin promoter, delivered via retrograde renal vein injection, achieves sustained restoration of renal Klotho expression to approximately 78% of physiological levels for at least 12 weeks in two independent murine CKD models. This Klotho restoration was accompanied by substantial and dose-dependent improvement in renal function—with high-dose treatment reducing serum creatinine by approximately 47% and proteinuria by nearly 63%—concomitant suppression of fibrosis gene programmes, partial reversal of the tubular EMT phenotype, and mechanistically coherent inhibition of TGF-β1/Smad3 signalling, all without detectable hepatotoxicity or significant systemic off-target effects. These findings provide proof-of-concept for a kidney-targeted Klotho gene therapy approach and establish the mechanistic basis for its anti-fibrotic efficacy across two aetiologically distinct CKD models. To our knowledge, this is the first study to achieve tubular-specific AAV9-mediated Klotho restoration in established CKD with a validated kidney-specific promoter, to furnish integrated in vivo and in vitro mechanistic evidence for receptor-level TGF-β1 blockade involving Smad3 and Smad7, and to cross-validate efficacy across the 5/6 nephrectomy and UUO paradigms within the same experimental design.

The transcriptome-wide RNA-sequencing analysis adds an important dimension of unbiased validation to the targeted molecular findings. The identification of 306 genes whose CKD-induced dysregulation was significantly rescued by AAV-KL treatment—including core fibrosis effectors (COL1A1, COL3A1, FN1, ACTA2), EMT transcription factors (SNAI1, ZEB1, TWIST1), inflammatory mediators (CCL2, IL1B, TNF), and tubular identity genes (CDH1, AQP1, SLC12A3)—demonstrates that the therapeutic benefit extends well beyond the handful of candidate genes assessed by qRT-PCR. Gene set enrichment analysis confirmed that the Hallmark EMT and TGF-β signalling gene sets, which were the most significantly enriched in CKD kidneys, were also the most significantly reversed by AAV-KL, providing pathway-level corroboration of the mechanistic conclusions derived from individual protein analyses. The restoration of oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid metabolism gene programmes by AAV-KL is consistent with emerging evidence that Klotho protects mitochondrial function and metabolic integrity in tubular epithelial cells [16], suggesting that the therapeutic benefit may extend beyond TGF-β antagonism to encompass metabolic reprogramming of surviving nephrons.

The core mechanistic finding of the present study—that AAV-KL reduces phospho-Smad3 and upregulates Smad7 in vivo without altering TGF-β1 ligand levels—provides robust in vivo support for the receptor-level antagonism model of Klotho anti-fibrotic action originally proposed by Doi et al. based on biochemical co-immunoprecipitation and cell-based experiments [17]. Those authors demonstrated that secreted Klotho protein binds directly to the extracellular domain of TGFβR2, sterically competing with TGF-β1 for receptor engagement and thereby preventing the TGFβR2/TGFβR1 heterodimeric complex from assembling and transphosphorylating each other [17]. Our in vivo observation that TGF-β1 mRNA and protein are preserved at near-CKD levels in AAV-KL-treated kidneys while the downstream effector p-Smad3 is markedly reduced constitutes the expected in vivo correlate of receptor blockade, and cannot be explained by competing mechanistic hypotheses such as transcriptional suppression of TGF-β1 synthesis, altered TGF-β1 bioavailability through latency-associated protein processing, or enhanced TGF-β1 clearance. More recently, the Klotho-derived peptide KP1 was shown to specifically bind TGFβR2 and disrupt TGF-β1/TGFβR2 engagement with equal potency to full-length soluble Klotho, producing comparable anti-fibrotic effects in mice [18], further validating the TGFβR2-binding surface as the functional anti-fibrotic domain of Klotho and establishing that even partial receptor occupancy is sufficient for meaningful pathway inhibition in vivo. The Smad7 upregulation observed in AAV-KL-treated kidneys adds an important and self-amplifying second tier to the mechanistic model. As p-Smad3 nuclear accumulation is attenuated by receptor-level Klotho blockade, Smad3-driven transcriptional repression of the Smad7 promoter—mediated through direct p-Smad3 occupancy of inhibitory SBEs in the Smad7 5' regulatory region—is progressively relieved, allowing constitutive Smad7 transcription to prevail [9,10]. The resulting increase in cellular Smad7 protein further attenuates residual TGFβR1 signalling output by recruiting Smurf1/2 E3 ubiquitin ligases to the receptor complex and targeting it for proteasomal degradation, simultaneously blocking nuclear translocation of residual p-Smad3 [9]. This two-node amplifying circuit—Klotho blocking ligand-receptor engagement extracellularly, Smad7 dismantling the activated receptor complex intracellularly—creates a self-sustaining anti-fibrotic brake that likely contributes to the durability of functional improvement with a single vector dose.

The present results must be contextualised against the landscape of existing gene therapy approaches targeting the TGF-β/Smad axis in CKD. Direct Smad7 gene therapy, pioneered by Lan and colleagues using ultrasound-microbubble-mediated transfection into the diseased kidney, demonstrated substantial anti-fibrotic efficacy across multiple CKD models including obstructive nephropathy, remnant kidney, and diabetic nephropathy, establishing Smad rebalancing as a therapeutic goal [33]. Gene therapy strategies targeting Smad3-regulated microRNAs—including miR-29 restoration to suppress Col1a1 and FN1, and miR-192 inhibition—have also produced meaningful fibrosis attenuation in preclinical settings [34]. More recently, long non-coding RNA approaches targeting the Smad3-Erbb4-IR axis have demonstrated efficacy in the UUO model [35]. Our approach differs fundamentally from these in that it restores an endogenous physiological repressor of TGF-β signalling—Klotho—that acts at the ligand-receptor interface upstream of the entire Smad cascade, rather than directly modulating individual downstream effectors. This upstream receptor-level strategy offers a theoretical advantage over direct Smad7 overexpression in that it selectively disrupts TGF-β1-mediated Smad3 activation while leaving other Smad7-regulated signalling networks—including those mediated by tumour necrosis factor-alpha, nuclear factor-kappa B, and activin signalling—relatively unperturbed [9], potentially reducing the risk of immunosuppressive side effects associated with non-selective Smad7 elevation. The kidney-specific Ksp-cadherin promoter further confines transgene expression to the tubular compartment most critically affected by TGF-β1-driven EMT and ECM production [24,25]. Beyond TGF-β1 antagonism, Klotho's pleiotropic renoprotective properties—anti-oxidant defence, attenuation of NF-κB-driven inflammation, FGF23/phosphate homeostasis [12], suppression of cellular senescence, anti-apoptotic effects [16], and Wnt pathway antagonism—may provide additional dimensions of protection unachievable by interventions targeting only the Smad3 cascade. In this regard, AAV-KL can be conceptualised as a "multi-target" renoprotective agent whose therapeutic footprint extends well beyond its primary TGFβR2 blocking activity.

The clinical translation potential of kidney-targeted AAV gene therapy for CKD is supported by converging evidence from multiple fields. Five AAV-based therapeutics received regulatory approval from the FDA and EMA by 2024, establishing mature manufacturing, quality control, and clinical pathway infrastructure [22]. The precedent for long-term, stable gene expression in post-mitotic tissues from approved AAV therapies for inherited retinal dystrophy (AAV2-RPE65) and haemophilia B (AAV8-FIX) [21] is directly relevant to the renal tubular epithelium, which has limited proliferative capacity in established CKD and thus supports stable episomal AAV retention. Recent advances in AAV capsid engineering have produced variants with substantially enhanced renal tubular tropism: evolved AAV.k variants transduce mouse kidneys 20–40-fold more efficiently than AAV9 [36], and AAV-KP1 specifically targets proximal tubules via local renal delivery with minimal hepatic off-target expression [37], offering next-generation capsids that could be incorporated into future Klotho gene therapy constructs at lower therapeutic doses. The retrograde renal vein injection approach used here is clinically translatable via percutaneous image-guided catheter techniques analogous to established renal vein sampling procedures, potentially enabling bilateral renal vector delivery in human patients with CKD as a single-session outpatient procedure [23]. The demonstrated ability to achieve 5-fold higher renal versus hepatic vector genome copies in this study, combined with the kidney-specific promoter suppressing hepatic Klotho transgene expression, provides a dual-layered safety mechanism that substantially reduces the immunological hepatitis risk seen with high-dose systemic AAV [21].

Notwithstanding the strength of these findings, several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, the 5/6 nephrectomy and UUO models, while widely validated, do not recapitulate the heterogeneity and multimorbidity of human CKD. The most clinically important forms—diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, and IgA nephropathy—each involve distinct fibrogenic microenvironments [5,6], and the efficacy of AAV-KL in these specific aetiological contexts remains to be formally established in dedicated models. The 5/6 nephrectomy model notably produces abrupt rather than gradual nephron loss and lacks the metabolic complexity of human diabetic CKD, potentially overestimating therapeutic efficacy relative to metabolically complex human disease. Second, the 12-week post-treatment observation window is insufficient to assess the durability of transgene expression beyond one year, the development of anti-AAV9 capsid neutralising antibodies that might preclude re-dosing, or delayed immune-mediated adverse events. The C57BL/6J strain is well established to mount weaker capsid-specific immune responses than humans [22], so that the safety profile documented here cannot be directly extrapolated to human subjects without additional studies in immunologically human-analogous large animal models. Third, all experiments used young adult male mice; sex-based differences in renal Klotho levels, fibrosis progression, and hormonal modulation of the TGF-β1/Smad3 axis are documented, and the generalisability to female animals and to aged animals in whom CKD is most prevalent clinically requires formal study. Fourth, although fibrosis was quantified by validated molecular approaches, the absence of quantitative histomorphometric analysis of Sirius red-stained sections limits the spatial resolution of the anti-fibrotic evidence. Future studies should incorporate image-based collagen area fraction measurement as a co-primary endpoint. Fifth, the study does not address whether AAV-KL can reverse pre-established advanced fibrosis, a clinically critical question for the large population of CKD patients with established renal scarring at the time of diagnosis.

Future directions should systematically address these limitations. Aetiology-specific models including db/db diabetic nephropathy, angiotensin II-driven hypertensive nephrosclerosis, and adriamycin nephropathy should be evaluated to assess conservation of mechanism across CKD aetiologies. Extended-duration studies (24–52 weeks) with immunophenotyping—including intraepithelial T cell characterisation, anti-AAV9 IgG titration, and interferon-gamma Elispot assays—will be essential for defining the immunological safety profile. Non-human primate studies using catheter-based renal arterial or ureteral infusion are required as Investigational New Drug-enabling experiments prior to first-in-human application. Combination regimens pairing AAV-KL with SGLT2 inhibitors, RAAS blockade, or endothelin receptor antagonists are warranted to explore potential additive renoprotection. A delayed-treatment paradigm in which AAV-KL is administered after established advanced fibrosis would address the clinically critical reversibility question. Finally, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic profiling of treated kidneys will delineate which nephron cell types respond to Klotho restoration and may reveal additional mechanisms of renoprotection—including potential podocyte protection, pericyte stabilisation, and immune cell reprogramming—beyond direct TGF-β1/Smad3 antagonism in tubular epithelial cells.

Several additional mechanistic considerations deserve comment. The significant inverse correlation between renal Klotho mRNA and p-Smad3 protein across all animals (r = − 0.74, p < 0.001) observed in the present study parallels findings from clinical CKD biopsy studies that have similarly demonstrated inverse correlations between tissue Klotho expression and markers of TGF-β pathway activation [15,38]. This suggests that the molecular relationship we characterise experimentally may recapitulate a biologically relevant axis in human disease, strengthening the translational rationale. Moreover, the partial rather than complete recovery of sKlotho in AAV-KL-treated animals (approximately 76% of sham levels versus full restoration to sham in terms of transgene mRNA) likely reflects the mathematical reality that only the remnant one-sixth of renal mass was transduced, with the remainder having been surgically removed. In an intact kidney with global tubular Klotho deficiency due to metabolic CKD progression—as occurs in human CKD driven by diabetes or hypertension—vector transduction of a larger tubular cell mass would plausibly yield more complete sKlotho restoration and potentially superior functional outcomes. The dose-response relationship observed between sKlotho recovery and functional improvement provides important proof-of-concept that increasing Klotho delivery correlates with meaningful clinical benefit, and future dose-ranging studies should explore whether even higher transduction efficiency yields further functional gains without toxicity.

The anti-apoptotic effect of AAV-KL observed in vitro, where total apoptotic cell fraction in TGF-β1-stimulated HK-2 cells was reduced from approximately 18% to approximately 8% by Klotho restoration, is consistent with the known anti-apoptotic functions of Klotho in tubular epithelial cells [13]. TGF-β1-mediated apoptosis of tubular epithelial cells contributes to nephron dropout and progressive nephron mass reduction in CKD, independent of fibrogenesis per se. The abrogation of this pro-apoptotic effect by Klotho—mediated in part through Smad3 inhibition—may therefore represent an additional mechanism through which AAV-KL preserves residual nephron mass in the remnant kidney, contributing to the functional improvement observed in treated animals beyond the anti-fibrotic effect alone. This anti-apoptotic dimension is not addressed by Smad7 gene therapy or microRNA-based approaches that primarily target ECM production, representing a potential added-value feature specific to Klotho restoration.

From a pharmacokinetic perspective, the AAV9/Ksp-KL vector configuration used in this study produces Klotho as a membrane-anchored type-I transmembrane protein in transduced tubular cells, from which the active sKlotho ectodomain is constitutively shed into the tubular lumen and from there into the peritubular capillary blood [14]. This steady-state local production and shedding mechanism may be superior to episodic exogenous sKlotho protein administration, which would produce pulsatile peaks and troughs in circulating sKlotho that may not maintain continuous TGFβR2 occupancy. The constitutive, locally produced sKlotho generated by transduced tubular cells likely achieves sustained receptor blockade in both autocrine (acting on the same tubular cell that produces it) and paracrine (acting on adjacent non-transduced cells) fashions, potentially achieving functional protection of untransduced nephron segments as well as directly transduced ones. This bystander effect could explain the disproportionately large functional improvement relative to the partial (approximately 78%) Klotho mRNA restoration, as paracrine sKlotho protein may extend TGFβR2 blockade well beyond the immediate neighbourhood of transduced cells. Understanding the spatial reach of this paracrine effect through immunofluorescence mapping of Klotho protein distribution relative to transduced cells, identified by immunohistochemistry for the Myc-tag or FLAG-tag incorporated in future constructs, would be a valuable objective for subsequent studies.

In summary, the present study establishes that single-dose kidney-targeted AAV9-mediated Klotho gene delivery is both efficacious and safe in preclinical CKD models, acting through a receptor-level TGF-β1 blockade mechanism that rebalances the Smad3/Smad7 axis and reverses tubular EMT. The magnitude of functional benefit achieved—approaching 50% normalisation of serum creatinine and BUN—is clinically meaningful and compares favourably to effects observed with pharmacological anti-fibrotic interventions in comparable rodent models. Taken together with the rapidly advancing technical landscape of kidney-targeted AAV delivery, the maturing regulatory infrastructure for AAV gene therapies, and the unmet need for disease-modifying therapies in CKD, these results provide a compelling foundation for advancing AAV-KL towards translational development.

This study demonstrates that a single intraregional administration of an AAV9 vector encoding the human Klotho gene under the kidney-specific Ksp-cadherin promoter, delivered via retrograde renal vein injection, achieves durable kidney-targeted Klotho restoration that is sustained for at least 12 weeks and attains approximately 75–80% of physiological expression levels in two murine CKD models. In the 5/6 nephrectomy model, this restoration significantly improves renal function—reducing serum creatinine by approximately 47%, blood urea nitrogen by approximately 48%, and proteinuria by approximately 63% at the high dose—and substantially suppresses expression of the fibrogenic genes Col1a1, FN1, and ACTA2 while partially reversing tubular EMT. Mechanistically, the anti-fibrotic effect operates through receptor-level antagonism of TGF-β1/TGFβR2 engagement, reducing Smad3 phosphorylation without altering TGF-β1 ligand levels, and restoring Smad7-mediated negative feedback to re-establish the Smad3/Smad7 balance disrupted in CKD. In vitro mechanistic validation with Smad3 gain-of-function rescue and the Smad3 inhibitor SIS3 establishes that Smad3 inhibition is the obligate effector of Klotho-mediated tubular cytoprotection and EMT suppression. Findings are reproduced in the UUO model, confirming cross-model generalisability. No hepatotoxicity, significant off-target biodistribution, or body weight perturbation was observed at either dose level. These results collectively provide proof-of-concept for kidney-targeted AAV-Klotho gene therapy as a potentially disease-modifying intervention for progressive CKD, warranting advancement to disease-specific models and non-human primate studies in preparation for eventual clinical translation.
