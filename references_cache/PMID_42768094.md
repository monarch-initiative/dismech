# 5q31 duplications encompassing PURA are associated with a neurodevelopmental disorder.
**Authors:** Challeat L, Remize S, Alouane T, Laurenceau D, Boisseau C, Hubert C, Celton N, Le Du N, Vonwill S, Gevrin C, Kerbellec L, Pebrel-Richard C, Egloff M, Navarro C, Perthus I, Niclass T, Caumes R, Fauqueux J, Jeanne M, Smol T, Laumonnier F, Vuillaume ML
**Journal:** Eur J Hum Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41431-026-02230-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41431-026-02230-7)

## Content

PURA heterozygous microdeletions and pathogenic variants have been previously associated with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), grouped into PURA-NDDs, characterized by severe neonatal hypotonia, feeding difficulties, developmental delay, intellectual disability, epilepsy, and distinctive facial features. Here we report four individuals carrying overlapping 5q31 duplications encompassing PURA, in addition to five cases from the DECIPHER database and one previously described in the literature. All patients share common features including developmental delay and mild to moderate intellectual disability. The minimal region of overlap spans 215 kb and contains only the PURA gene, suggesting PURA as the candidate gene underlying the observed phenotype. Using transcriptomic and biochemical analyses on cultured lymphocytes from one patient, we found that 5q31 duplication causes a significant upregulation of PURA mRNA and PURA protein, respectively. RNA Sequencing also revealed dysregulated expression of various genes involved in human pathology, especially in neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by intellectual disability. Using primary hippocampal neuronal cultures from mouse embryos, we demonstrated that PURA overexpression impairs neuronal morphology in vitro, inducing a reduction in dendritic arborization and in dendritic spine density. Our findings support that 5q31 duplications encompassing PURA represent a novel genomic disorder presenting with a milder phenotype than the reciprocal deletion syndrome and implicate PURA dosage dysregulation as a key contributor to the associated clinical features.

INTRODUCTION
Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) constitute a broad and
heterogeneous group of conditions with onset during childhood,
characterized by a wide spectrum of clinical symptoms and
variable degrees of severity (DSM-5). They can affect multiple
brain functions, notably cognition, communication, adaptive
behavior, language, and social and motor abilities. Genetic factors,
including chromosomal abnormalities, Single-Nucleotide Variants
or Copy Number Variants (CNVs) are estimated to contribute to at
least 30% of cases of NDDs [ 1]. To date, 2027 genes have been
implicated in NDDs (SysNDD database, https://sysndd.dbmr.unibe.
ch/) and many recurrent CNVs (microdeletions or microduplica-
tions) have been extensively characterized. However, there might
still be unidenti ﬁed CNV syndromes [ 2, 3]. Deletions and
duplications at the same genomic locus, such as 16p11.2,
22q11.2, 1q21.1, and 15q11.2, can sometimes produce mirrored
phenotypes and are associated with opposite phenotypes such as
obesity versus underweight or macrocephaly versus microcephaly,
illustrating a clear gene-dosage impact on reciprocal phenotypes
[4, 5]. Generally, overlapping CNVs encompass multiple genes,
including several involved in human pathology, especially in
NDDs. Candidate causative genes that contribute to the observed
phenotypes are often included in the critical minimal overlapping
region.
In this context, the PURine-rich element binding protein A gene
(PURA) (OMIM #600473) became a candidate gene for NDDs after
the identi ﬁcation of several overlapping 5q31.2q31.3 deletions in
patients sharing many similar features including severe global
developmental delay, intellectual disability, profound hypotonia,
cerebral abnormalities, feeding dif ﬁculties, abnormal breathing
pattern, distinct facial features and seizures [ 6–11]. In 2014, more
than 70 heterozygous de novo variants were ascribed to cause
PURA-related severe neonatal hypotonia-seizures-encephalopathy
syndrome (OMIM #616158), an NDD characterized by a wide range
of clinical features similar to those observed in the 5q31
microdeletion syndrome [ 12–19].

PURA is a single-exon gene located at 5q31 that encodes the
PURA protein [ 20], a member of the Pur family that includes four
protein members: Pur- α (PURA), Pur-β (PURB), and two isoforms of
Pur-γ (PURG) [ 21].
Until now, only one de novo large heterozygous 5q31
duplication encompassing this gene has been described in the
literature. This duplication of 3.36 Mb was identi ﬁed in a 4-year-old
male patient with global developmental delay, intellectual
disability, hypotonia, relative microcephaly, and dysmorphic
features [ 22]. Here, we report for the ﬁrst time the phenotypic
spectrum associated with 5q31 duplications, describing four
unrelated individuals carrying heterozygous 5q31 duplications,
three of which occurred de novo. They share common features
including developmental delay, mild to moderate intellectual
disability, and predisposition to overweight and obesity. These
duplications overlap with the previously reported duplication and
range in size from 215 kb to 3.5 Mb. The minimal overlapping
region spans 215 kb and contains only the PURA gene, previously
known in human pathology.
In this study, we show that 5q31 duplications are associated with
an NDD that is less severe than that associated with reciprocal
deletions. We also show, by RNA sequencing and western blot
analyses conducted in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)
from one patient, that the 5q31 duplication leads to increased
PURA transcript and protein expression. Furthermore, functional
experiments reveal reduced dendritic arborization and a decrease
in dendritic spine density in primary hippocampal neurons from
mouse embryos overexpressing PURA. Altogether, these results
suggest that 5q31 duplications encompassing PURA represent a
novel genomic disorder and implicate PURA dosage dysregulation
as a key mechanism underlying the associated phenotype.

Confocal microscopy and neuronal morphological analyses
Acquisitions were performed using a confocal microscope Leica
SP8 and the software Leica Application Suite X (LAS X). A 63×
immersion objective was used to acquire z-stack images. A
z-interval of 0.3 μm was used for full-neuron imaging, while a
0.2 μm interval was used for dendritic spine imaging. Images were
analyzed using ImageJ software ( https://imagej.net/ij/).
A Sholl analysis was performed upon conversion to binary
images using the SNT module of ImageJ software ( https://imagej.
net/ij/) as explained previously [ 23]. The cell center was de ﬁned
using an active ROI, from which concentric circles were generated
at 10 μm intervals, extending up to a radius of 80 μm. For each
cell, the number of neurites crossing the circles was determined
automatically. Manual corrections were performed when
necessary.
Counting of the GFP-labeled dendritic spines was performed
manually using ImageJ software ( https://imagej.net/ij/) using the
multi-point tool. Spine density was calculated as the number of
spines per 10 μm of dendrite length. Spine maturity was assessed
based on the co-localization of GFP with PSD-95, which is
predominantly expressed in mature spines [ 24].
Statistical analyses
Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed
using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Normality of the data was
assessed using the D ’Agostino–Pearson test, while homogeneity
of variances was evaluated using the Brown –Forsythe test prior to
group comparisons. For normally distributed data with similar
variances, multiple group comparisons were performed using one-
way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey ’s multiple comparisons
test. When variances were unequal, Welch ’s ANOVA was used,
followed by Games –Howell’s multiple comparisons test. Results
are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) or
mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences were
considered signi ﬁcant at p < 0.05.
RESULTS
Identiﬁcation of overlapping 5q31 duplications in individuals
with a neurodevelopmental disorder
A de novo 3.3 Mb heterozygous duplication at 5q31 was identi ﬁed
in Patient 1 (Tours, France) by array CGH through routine clinical
and genomic analyses (Genome build GRCh38/hg38, chr5:
139,585,558–142,862,369). She presented with global develop-
mental delay without neonatal hypotonia, moderate intellectual
disability with special school needs, and overweight since the age
of 3 (+3.6 SD/age at last examination). Through a national
collaboration using the French network Achro-puce (https:/acpa-
achropuce.com/), we collected three other unrelated patients (two
females and one male) carrying heterozygous 5q31 duplications,
two of which were de novo. These duplications have also been
identiﬁed by array CGH ranging in size from 215 kb to 3.5 Mb,
deﬁning a minimal critical common region of 215 kb including
only PURA (Fig. 1A). Subsequent ﬂuorescent in situ hybridization
(FISH) analysis using a BAC DNA probe (RP11-79K4) and Long ‑read
Oxford Nanopore sequencing targeting the 5q31 region indicated
that the duplication was present in the same chromosomal region
in Patient 1, and was arranged in tandem in Patient 4 (Fig. 1B, C
and Supplementary Fig. S2). Five additional patients were
identiﬁed through the DECIPHER database, in addition to the
patient reported in the literature (Fig. 1A). In total, ten patients
carried overlapping 5q31 duplications. Following informed con-
sent, phenotypic data were obtained by clinical care providers
using a standardized collection form. A summary of clinical
ﬁndings in the ten affected individuals is provided in Table
1 when
available.
The size of the reported duplications ranges from 215 kb to
3.5 Mb, de ﬁning a minimal critical region of 215 kb. Among the
eight individuals for whom inheritance data were available, seven
duplications occurred de novo, and one was maternally inherited,
with no available information regarding the associated pheno-
type. For two patients from DECIPHER (#276570, #501379), the
phenotypic data were limited, and the mode of inheritance was
unknown. The patients were born following uncomplicated
pregnancies, with spontaneous vaginal deliveries, and were
appropriate for gestational age at birth (4/5), except for Patient
5, who presented with intrauterine growth restriction of suspected
vascular origin. No neonatal hypotonia was reported, and feeding
difﬁculties were uncommon (1/4). Neurodevelopment was char-
acterized in all cases by global developmental delay, predomi-
nantly affecting language (9/9). Independent walking was
achieved between 11 and 21 months of age. Fine motor skills
appeared impaired (4/5). First words were markedly delayed,
ranging from 18 months to over 4 years among ﬁve of them (data
unavailable for four patients from DECIPHER and for Rosenfeld ’s
patient). Ultimately, patients were able to produce short
sentences. All individuals (7/7) were diagnosed with mild to
moderate intellectual disability. No developmental regression was
observed. Two patients from the DECIPHER cohort were reported
to have autism spectrum disorder (without further speci ﬁcation),
whereas none of the patients in our cohort had such a diagnosis.
Two patients presented with seizures (2/7). Some individuals
exhibited anxiety (2/4), low frustration tolerance (2/4), aggressive
behaviors (1/4), social interaction dif ﬁculties (1/4), and eating
behavior disturbances (1/4). There was no evidence of growth
retardation or tall stature, and head circumference remained
within the normal range. Except for the patient reported in the
literature, who is also the youngest described, all present (6/7)
with overweight or obesity (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S3).
Among the four patients who underwent brain MRI, ﬁndings were
normal in two cases (2/4), while one showed vermian atrophy (1/
4) and another exhibited a white matter abnormality (1/4). From
an extra-neurological perspective, 4/7 patients presented with
visual impairment (refractive errors and strabismus), while none
had hearing impairment. Two individuals had orthopedic mani-
festations, including scoliosis (1/2), pectus excavatum (1/2), gait
abnormalities with internal hip rotation (1/2), and joint hyperlaxity
(1/2). A tendency toward constipation was reported in 2/5
patients, with apparent improvement over time in one case.
Two out of ﬁve patients experienced precocious puberty, although
no biological data were available. No structural organ malforma-
tions were reported except for one ventricular septal defect (VSD)
in patient 10 from the literature. Moreover, some patients seem to
exhibit non-speci ﬁc and non-recurrent dysmorphic features, but
no photographs were available to con ﬁrm this.

PURA overexpression impairs in vitro neuronal development
In line with the increased PURA protein expression level identi ﬁed
in Patient 1 lymphocytes, we then assessed the functional impact
of PURA overexpression on in vitro neuronal development.
Interestingly, to date, functional analyses on PURA have only
focused on loss-of-function variants. However, the consequences
of its overexpression on brain development remain poorly
explored, particularly on dendritic arborization complexity and
on the formation and maturation of dendritic spines.
To study dendritic arborization, primary hippocampal neuronal
cultures from mouse embryos were transfected at DIV3. Three
transfection conditions were used: pCAG-GFP and pCAG-GFP/
pMYC-DDK co-transfection as negative controls, and pCAG-GFP/
pPURA-MYC-DDK co-transfection as the experimental condition.
The expression of PURA-MYC-DDK was con ﬁrmed using anti-DDK
labeling (Fig. 3A) and we found no signi ﬁcant difference on
dendritic arborization between neurons transfected with pCAG-
GFP or with pCAG-GFP/pMYC-DDK plasmids (Fig. 3A–C). Neurons
transfected with pCAG-GFP/pPURA-MYC-DDK, leading to PURA
overexpression, showed a signi ﬁcant decrease in global dendritic
arborization complexity and branching (Fig. 3A–C, Supplementary
Fig. S5 and Table S3).
To determine whether a functional impact is also present at
later stages of neuronal development, primary neurons were
transfected at DIV11, a stage characterized by the onset of
dendritic spine formation. While there was no difference between
control conditions (Fig. 3D–F), neurons overexpressing PURA
exhibited a signi ﬁcant decrease in dendritic spine density
compared to controls (Fig. 3D, E). Notably, the maturation status
of the dendritic spines was not impaired when compared to
control neurons (Fig. 3F). Taken together, these ﬁndings suggest
that PURA overexpression in developing neuronal cultures alters
proper neurodevelopment, especially dendrite formation in young
neurons, and synapse density in mature neurons.
DISCUSSION
Genetic alterations are estimated to contribute to at least 30% of NDD
cases [ 1], and almost 2000 genes have been identi ﬁed to date
(SysNDD database, https://sysndd.dbmr.unibe.ch/), illustrating the
clinical and genetic heterogeneity of these early-onset conditions.
Here we report the identi ﬁcation of four heterozygous
duplications involving the 5q31 region associated with NDD, in
addition to ﬁve cases from the DECIPHER database and one
previously described in the literature [ 22], highlighting a minimal
overlapping region of 215 kb that includes only the PURA gene.
Previous reports of larger 5q31 duplications involved multiple
OMIM morbid genes, which limits our ability to determine
whether PURA is the only contributing gene in the clinical
phenotype [ 25, 26].
The 5q31.3 microdeletion syndrome and the PURA syndrome
resulting from PURA haploinsufﬁciency are now referred to PURA-
related neurodevelopmental disorders (PURA-NDDs), sharing a
phenotype characterized by a global developmental delay,
hypotonia, feeding impairments, and seizures [ 27, 28]. PURA
haploinsufﬁciency has been associated, in some patients, with MRI
brain abnormalities such as delayed myelination and atrophy of
brain parts, as well as respiratory issues and severe intellectual
disability [ 7, 8, 12, 17, 18]. Despite our small cohort, we observed
similar brain MRI ﬁndings: half were normal, while the others
showed either vermian atrophy or white matter abnormalities [ 29].
These observations suggest that PURA dosage appears to be
important in brain development, likely through different
mechanisms.

that appears milder than that reported in individuals with PURA
haploinsufﬁciency [ 28]. Furthermore, whereas patients with
deletions of the 5q31.3 region typically present with neonatal
hypotonia and feeding dif ﬁculties often requiring enteral nutri-

observed in most clinically characterized individuals with PURA
duplication, suggesting that altered energy homeostasis may
represent a recurrent component of the phenotype. Given the
high expression of PURA in the central nervous system, including
the pituitary gland (Human Protein Atlas, www.proteinatlas.org/),
one possible explanation is that increased PURA dosage disrupts
hypothalamic neuronal circuits controlling appetite regulation and
energy expenditure. Interestingly, this appears to represent a
mirror phenotype with respect to gene dosage, with poor weight
gain frequently reported in individuals with PURA haploinsufﬁ-
ciency and excessive weight gain observed in those with PURA
duplication. Nevertheless, given the limited number of clinically
characterized individuals, additional cases will be required to
determine whether obesity is a consistent feature of 5q31
duplication syndrome.

Moreover, pathway enrichment analysis revealed a signi ﬁcant
representation of genes involved in apoptosis-related signaling,
including BAG3, HSPA1A, and NDFIP1. BAG3, a co-chaperone of
HSP70, modulates many biological processes, especially apoptosis
and cell proliferation, through interactions with upstream effectors
of several pathways. Overexpression of BAG3 leads to activation of
the NF- κB cell survival pathway [ 30] and increased ERK1/2
activation [ 31], contributing to apoptosis resistance [ 32]. Consis-
tent with BAG3-mediated ERK activation, HSPA1A has also been
reported to regulate ERK1/2 activity, and its decreased expression
inhibits cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis [ 33]. In a
neuronal context, NDFIP1 is overexpressed in surviving neurons
following brain injury, and in vitro studies in cortical neurons have
shown that NDFIP1 overexpression promotes neuronal survival
through ubiquitination of damaged proteins [ 34, 35]. Together,
these ﬁndings indicate that 5q31 duplication is associated with
transcriptional modulation of pathways regulating apoptosis and
cell survival. However, further functional studies are required to
determine the causal contribution of these pathways ’ dysregula-
tions to the neuronal phenotype.
A limitation of this analysis is the relatively modest cohort size used
for OUTRIDER. Although the 35 samples retained after quality control
exceeded the documented minimum requirement for the DROP
aberrant expression module, this number remains below the sample
size generally recommended for optimal OUTRIDER performance and
stable correction of latent factors. Therefore, OUTRIDER results were
interpreted as expression-outlier prioritization rather than standalone
diagnostic evidence. The biological relevance of the prioritized genes
was assessed in conjunction with the genomic ﬁndings, particularly
their concordance with the duplicated interval, including PURA.
Another limitation is the use of cultured lymphocytes, which do not
express all brain-expressed genes. For example, the 5q31 duplicated
region in Patient 1, Patient 3 and four Patients from DECIPHER
(#292874, #401667, #501379, #276570), includes the NRG2 gene,
which encodes the Neuregulin 2 (NRG2) protein [ 36], that is a
candidate gene for Autism Spectrum Disorders [ 37]. Notably, as
DECIPHER patient #501379 was reported to present with ASD
(Table 1), a contribution of NRG2 dosage may not be excluded.
However, it is not expressed in blood cells (Human Protein Atlas).
Similarly, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses were per-
formed in a previous study to assess the impact of PURA
knockdown (KD) on gene expression in HeLa cells, mimicking
PURA haploinsufﬁciency. In this context, CUX1 was identi ﬁed as a
potential PURA target, showing signi ﬁcant reduced expression at
the transcript and protein levels [ 38]. CUX1 acts as a transcription
factor and is involved in neuronal differentiation, especially during
dendritogenesis and synaptogenesis [ 39]. Interestingly, despite its
strong expression in blood (Human Protein Atlas), CUX1 was not
signiﬁcantly misregulated in Patient 1 ( p > 0.6).
When focusing on genes commonly misregulated in both
studies, ZDHHC8 encoding for a palmitoyltransferase, showed
opposite expression patterns: it was signi ﬁcantly downregulated
in the PURA KD model (fold change: 0.64; p < 0.01) [38], whereas it
was signi ﬁcantly upregulated in Patient 1 (fold change: 1.33; p:
0.015). ZDHHC8 has been primarily studied in the context of the
22q11.2 microdeletion syndrome and has been proposed to
contribute to the associated neurodevelopmental phenotype [ 40].
In a Zdhhc8-deﬁcient mouse model, it has been shown that
Zdhhc8 deﬁciency induced decreased spine density and excitatory
synapses [ 40–42] and impairments in axonal growth, branching,
and synaptic transmission [ 42]. Taken together, the altered
expression of ZDHHC8 in our patient suggests that PURA dosage
may regulate its expression, potentially contributing to the
observed phenotype.
Finally, functional studies were conducted to evaluate the
consequences of PURA overexpression during neurodevelopment
using a neuronal model that mimics the overexpression observed
in Patient 1. We observed a reduced dendritic arborization and a
decrease in dendritic spine density in primary hippocampal
neurons from mouse embryos overexpressing PURA.
Notably, similar structural abnormalities have been reported in
Pura-deﬁcient mouse models. Studies in acute hippocampal slices
from Pura knockout mice revealed reduced numbers of neurons,
decreased synaptic connectivity, and abnormal dendritic devel-
opment compared to wild-type littermates [ 43]. In addition,
morphological alterations were observed in the cerebellum and
hippocampus of Pura-deﬁcient mice, explained by mislocalization
of Staufen and Map2, respectively [ 44]. Altogether, these
observations indicate that both reduced and increased PURA
expression lead to overlapping structural impairments, as
observed at the clinical level. It suggests that tightly regulated
PURA expression is critical for normal neurodevelopment.
Moreover, no mouse model overexpressing PURA has been
developed; only heterozygous and homozygous Pura knockout
models exist. Homozygous knockouts exhibit severe neurological
deﬁcits and feeding problems leading to early death 2 weeks after
birth, whereas heterozygous mice display milder phenotypes,
including hypotonia, gait abnormalities, and spatial memory
deﬁcits [9, 43, 45, 46]. These phenotypes re ﬂect aspects of human
PURA-NDDs, highlighting the essential role of this gene in
postnatal neurodevelopment.
Taken together, our results indicate that PURA may represent
one major actor that would explain the phenotypes observed in
patients carrying 5q31 duplications, which also suggests that
PURA gene dosage is critical for brain development and cognition.
PURA would be another example of dosage-sensitive neurode-
velopmental genes, for which both reduced and increased gene
dosage may result in distinct clinical disorders, with a phenotype
observed in cases of PURA haploinsufﬁciency being more severe
than what is described in PURA duplications. This paradigm is
illustrated by MECP2, where loss of function causes Rett syndrome,
whereas gene duplication results in MECP2 duplication syndrome,
both characterized by ID but with markedly different neurological
manifestations [ 47]. Similarly, dosage alterations of RAI1 underlie
two reciprocal genomic disorders which share neurodevelop-
mental features despite distinct clinical presentations [ 48].
We acknowledge that the transcriptomic and protein analyses
that were performed only in a single patient and the unavailability
of additional biological samples with 5q31 duplications represent
a limitation of our study. The addition of individuals with 5q31
duplications, together with transcriptomic analyses of larger
patient cohorts and complementary cellular models, such as
patients’ iPSC-derived neuronal cultures will provide important
complementary ﬁndings to further delineate the molecular
mechanisms underlying the phenotype and to re ﬁne
genotype–phenotype correlations.
