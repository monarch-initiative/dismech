# Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Management, and Surveillance of LMNB1-Related Autosomal Dominant Leukodystrophy.
**Authors:** Dhamija R, Tobin WO, Cortelli P, Padiath Q, Muthusamy K, Singh Sekhon UD, Singh BJ, Harris D, Billings H, Mamillo K, Appleberry H, Giorgio E, Ratti S, Fogel BL, Gavrilova R, Raininko R, Cousin MA
**Journal:** Neurol Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1212/NXG.0000000000200287](https://doi.org/10.1212/NXG.0000000000200287)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: There is limited published evidence to guide the diagnosis and management of LMNB1-related autosomal dominant leukodystrophy (ADLD), an adult-onset progressive neurodegenerative genetic disorder caused by pathogenic variants in LMNB1.

METHODS: We used a modified Delphi process to systematically combine literature evidence with international experts' recommendations to determine best-practice clinical care guidelines in the areas of diagnosis, genetic testing, surveillance, and management.

RESULTS: Diagnosis: The diagnosis of ADLD should be considered in an individual with characteristic brain MRI findings and/or clinical symptoms of autonomic dysfunction, with or without a positive family history of autosomal dominant inheritance. The typical disease onset is insidious, followed by progression of manifestations.Genetic testing: The diagnosis of ADLD should be considered established in an individual with suggestive clinical and MRI findings, positive family history, and either a heterozygous pathogenic LMNB1 duplication or deletion upstream of the LMNB1 promoter identified on genetic testing. Single-gene testing that can identify LMNB1 structural variants (duplications/deletions) at a high resolution is currently the optimal means of confirming a genetic diagnosis. Exome sequencing is not recommended as a first-tier test.Surveillance: Careful physical examination, tilt table testing, brain and cervical spinal cord MRI, and neuropsychometric assessment should be conducted at first presentation, with follow-up evaluations determined by baseline findings and the clinical course. Symptoms localizing to other segments of the spinal cord may necessitate additional neuroimaging.Management: Specialists in neurology, physiotherapy, and genetics/genetic counseling are key to providing optimal care. Other specialists whose input will be valuable for some affected individuals include urologists, mental health specialists, physical medicine and rehabilitation physicians, and dieticians. For the management of myelopathy, a stepwise approach should be considered, beginning with oral antispasticity medications, followed by botulinum toxin injections, and, if needed, progressing to intrathecal baclofen therapy. All individuals with ADLD and those at risk who wish to conceive should be offered prenatal genetic counseling.

DISCUSSION: Our consensus-based approach allowed us to formulate guideline recommendations in the setting of limited scientific evidence. Our analysis highlights the need for rigorous, collaborative studies on ADLD, including natural history studies, outcome assessments, and biomarker development, to improve our understanding and care of this devastating rare condition.

LMNB1-related autosomal dominant leukodystrophy (ADLD) is an ultra-rare but underreported adult-onset leukodystrophy.1 It was initially described in 19842 as a differential diagnosis of primary progressive multiple sclerosis and has since been described in over 33 families from diverse ethnic backgrounds.1 The genetic etiology and clinical phenotype are now well defined.1 It is a slowly progressive disorder characterized by autonomic dysfunction, followed by pyramidal and cerebellar abnormalities resulting in spasticity, tremor, and ataxia. Autonomic dysfunction is universal and may include bladder dysfunction, constipation, postural hypotension, and/or erectile dysfunction.3-5 Cognitive dysfunction can occur as a late manifestation.6 Affected individuals may survive for more than 2 decades after onset, but quality of life is significantly affected.7-9

Cerebellar peduncles are involved early. In the cerebrum, symmetric hyperintense areas spread into frontal and parietal lobes first, followed by occipital and temporal lobes. Periventricular areas are less affected.3,10 Progression of radiologic changes eventually culminates in brain atrophy.4,11-14 Clinically presymptomatic individuals may harbor white matter changes in the brain and spinal cord, accompanied by spinal cord atrophy.3,10,15 Pathologic accumulation of lactate has been demonstrated by magnetic resonance (MR) spectroscopy in CSF in lateral ventricles but not in cerebral white matter.12,16

Genetically, ADLD is characterized by alterations of the LMNB1 gene (chr5q23.2), resulting in an excessive lamin B1 protein production, a component of the nuclear lamina.17 At least 2 types of genetic alterations have been identified: LMNB1 gene duplications or upstream deletions affecting LMNB1 regulatory regions.17-22 Although demyelination is one of the most significant aspects of ADLD, the molecular and functional mechanisms underlying this pathology have not been fully elucidated. An early neuropathologic study demonstrated vacuolar myelin loss with preservation of oligodendrocytes.10,23 However, recent studies have demonstrated a role for both oligodendrocytes24-26 and astrocytes behind demyelination caused by lamin B1 accumulation.27-29 The exact pathomechanism of ADLD is yet to be established.30,31

Management is symptomatic and there is no cure, although active research for novel therapies is in progress.32-34 There are no current guidelines for diagnosis, management, or surveillance of this rare entity.1 Established clinical practice guidelines help standardize clinical care and improve outcomes.

The aim of this study was to assemble ADLD clinical experts and use a modified Delphi method to develop consensus guidelines for the diagnosis, management, and surveillance of individuals with ADLD. Standardized care is a key initial step to improving outcomes and initiating natural history studies that form the basis of any future therapeutic development. Consensus expert recommendations for management of comorbidities are provided because literature evidence is weak.

This study was initiated by R. Dhamija, M.A. Cousin, and B.J. Singh who also determined the methods and scope. An expert panel was formed by the project leads R. Dhamija and B.J. Singh (scientific lead of the ADLD Center patient advocacy group) and comprised individuals with expertise in ADLD who have published on the condition or have clinical experience caring for individuals with ADLD. Experts were also identified through collaborations and attendance at relevant scientific meetings such as the 1st International Symposium on ADLD held in Bologna, Italy, on July 1, 2022, and the 2nd International Symposium on ADLD held at the Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, in 2024.

We identified 9 clinicians and scientists (Europe and United States) from 5 different institutions as expert panel members (EPMs), 1 biostatistician (D.H.), a guidelines methodologist (H.B.) with expertise in medical guideline development using the modified Delphi approach, and a clinical research fellow (K.M.) who helped with literature review and article preparation. The EPMs included neurologists, clinical geneticists, and molecular geneticists. Representatives from the ADLD Center (2) were also included to review the clinical guideline statements for relevance to the patient and family community, review final recommendations, and provide feedback. Final recommendations from the EPM were reviewed by an additional neuroradiologist experienced in evaluating MRI from individuals with ADLD and 2 scientists with extensive expertise in ADLD including the genetic and molecular mechanisms of disease.

The target population to which the guidelines will apply are individuals with or suspected of having LMNB1-related ADLD across diverse ethnic backgrounds. However, in clinical practice, at-risk family members may receive care or education from the proband's neurologist, geneticist, or another clinician involved in the care. These guidelines aim to summarize available information to assist general neurologists in the clinical diagnosis, genetic test selection, clinical management, and surveillance of individuals with ADLD. These guidelines do not intend to replace clinical judgment.

With the support of a medical librarian, 4 electronic databases (Embase, Medline, Scopus, and Web of Science) were searched for “autosomal dominant leukodystrophy” from inception to July 2023, including only English-language articles. The literature review identified a total of 165 retrospective or observational case series, reviews, and case reports from diverse ethnic backgrounds. All abstracts were reviewed, and 78 publications met criteria to be included in the literature review on LMNB1-related ADLD.

These publications were divided into 5 categories based on their primary conclusion: clinical features and diagnosis, genetic testing and diagnosis, neuroimaging and management, and therapeutics (eAppendix 1).

Literature review formed the basis of the survey questions encompassing the topics of diagnosis, genetic testing, surveillance, and management (eAppendix 2).

The literature review is summarized further.

The largest longitudinal study on ADLD describing the natural clinical and radiologic course was published in 2015.3 Twenty-three participants from 2 unrelated Swedish families with LMNB1 duplications (each family had a unique duplication) were studied over 2 decades with clinical assessment and MRI of the brain and spinal cord. Repeated MRI scans were performed in 14 participants over a period of up to 17 years. MRI abnormalities were found in the brain and spinal cord in all, with uniform progression of MRI changes and clinical symptoms. Autonomic dysfunction (100% of participants) appeared in the fifth to sixth decade, preceding gait and coordination difficulties in most individuals. Motor signs developed in an ascending pattern, from spastic paraplegia to tetraplegia and pseudobulbar palsy, in the seventh decade. Pyramidal signs were seen in 91% (20) of the participants, ataxia in 91% (20), and tremor in 45% (10). Median survival was 18 years (range 3–24 years). Seventeen participants reported worsening with infection.3 One family with a duplication of LMNB1 has been reported to lack the early dysautonomia, which occurred at later stages of disease.35

MRI can reveal abnormalities in the brain and spinal cord more than 10 years before the first clinical symptom. The pyramidal tracts are first affected. T2 hyperintensity is first detected under the motor cortex and extends downward to the pyramids in the medulla oblongata. Cerebellar peduncles are involved early before clinical symptoms. Such findings are very rarely seen in other diseases. In the cerebrum, T2 hyperintense areas spread into frontal and parietal lobes first. Periventricular areas are less affected, and therefore, characteristic darker rims around the lateral ventricles are always seen in T2-weighted (T2w) spin-echo and often also in T2w fluid-attenuated inversion recovery images. Later, the hyperintensities also extend to the occipital lobes and eventually to the temporal lobes. All signal intensity changes are principally symmetric. Substance loss is not a prominent feature in LMNB1-related ADLD, but some atrophy is seen in advanced stages. However, thinning of the corpus callosum can be revealed in early stages, and subtle callosal signal intensity changes can be found. Affected individuals with a duplication of the LMNB1 gene have an early spinal cord involvement even before clinical symptoms. The spinal cord appears thin, and the entire white matter usually emits high T2 signals. In a few cases, only local hyperintensities were detected (Figure).3,10-15

In 2010, an ADLD family was described with lamin B1 overexpression but with neither gene copy-number defects nor single nucleotide variant and without the key ADLD autonomic symptoms present at clinical onset.36 For the same family,18 it was later demonstrated for the first time that lamin B1 overexpression was caused by a deletion upstream of LMNB1 eliminating a topologically associating domain (TAD) boundary and allowing interactions between a foreign enhancer and the LMNB1 promoter (enhancer-adoption mechanism). This second genetic cause of ADLD was corroborated by the identification of 5 additional affected individuals from 3 families with deletions upstream of LMNB1.22 Individuals with pathogenic deletions show clinical peculiarities compared with those with LMNB1 duplications, such as an earlier age at symptom onset and a lack of early dysautonomia.22 Individuals with deletions may show the same brain MRI findings as those with duplications, but some have no signal intensity abnormalities in the middle cerebellar peduncles and in the lower half of the brainstem. Individuals with a deletion may have more marked brain atrophy, and signal intensity changes can be seen earlier than in those with a duplication.22

Autosomal dominant inheritance of LMNB1-related ADLD is well established.17 Most families with ADLD have a unique LMNB1 duplication or (very rarely) a heterozygous deletion upstream of the LMNB1 promoter. This results in LMNB1 overexpression and lamin B1 protein accumulation, which is believed to be the basis of pathogenesis.17,31,37 ADLD is almost always familial. De novo alterations have not yet been seen in clinical practice (EPM observation). It is important to note that, for genetic testing and counseling considerations, the exact size, orientation, and insertional location of the LMNB1 duplication are critical because not all duplications cause ADLD.26,38,39

Autonomic dysfunction typically includes bladder dysfunction, constipation, postural hypotension, and erectile dysfunction and is seen in most of the affected individuals.3 Selective impairments in executive function can be seen,4 and cognitive dysfunction usually occurs as a late manifestation.6 Affected individuals may survive for decades after onset.3,4 Quality of life is significantly affected.7-9 There are no previously published guidelines on surveillance and management of symptoms in ADLD. Typically, it has been symptom based (EPM observation).

The initial guideline content was developed by RD based on literature review and discussion with the ADLD Center, encompassing the topics of diagnosis, genetic testing, surveillance, and management. A modified Delphi approach (a process used in medical settings to achieve consensus for a defined clinical problem) was used to reach expert consensus of the guidelines.40-42 This approach uses repeated rounds of voting via surveys, culminating in a panel discussion for final resolution. The basis of modified Delphi is that the collective input of a group of experts is more reliable than that of an individual.

Our modified Delphi approach consisted of 2 structured questionnaire rounds (eAppendix 3) using surveys and 1 virtual consensus meeting (web-based platform Zoom Video Communications). Consensus was defined as >80% agreement. Surveys were built and distributed using Qualtrics survey software. Qualtrics has been approved by the Mayo Clinic Office of Information Security and Privacy as a vendor for distributing, collecting, and storing survey data.

The initial survey was distributed among the EPMs. Each EPM was asked to answer based on their expertise in the area. The option to agree or disagree with the statement and provide comments was present. Each EPM could also choose not to answer the question if outside their area of expertise. There were 3 questions on clinical diagnosis, 4 on genetic testing, 3 on neuroimaging, and 9 on surveillance and management (eAppendix 2).

Based on the first EPM survey results and feedback, the guideline statements in the survey were modified and the EPMs were surveyed again. The final set of clinical guidelines was discussed and refined by the EPMs as a group, and consensus was achieved. Consensus EPM recommendations for management of comorbidities are also provided because of limited evidence in literature.

This study is exempt from Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval because guidelines are based on available data from the literature review and expert opinion. No patient information is included in this article.

At the conclusion of the modified Delphi process, our recommendation statements were finalized and approved, forming the LMNB1-related clinical care guidelines. These guidelines cover the domains of clinical diagnosis, genetic testing, surveillance, and management.

Recommendations relevant to the identification of individuals affected by ADLD and general clinical overview are strongly supported by published work on LMNB1-related ADLD as discussed previously in methodology. Clinical suspicion of LMNB1-related ADLD should arise when characteristic brain MRI findings are identified along with symptoms of autonomic dysfunction, a family history demonstrating dominant inheritance, or characteristic spinal cord MRI.1,3-6 Typically, the onset is insidious in adulthood with gradual progression. Occasional acute worsening may be triggered by illnesses, followed by recovery to baseline.1,3,5 These diagnostic recommendations are strongly supported by consistent evidence from published case series of LMNB1-associated ADLD and expert consensus.

Using this clinical overview, recommendations will enable early consideration of this diagnosis and significantly reduce the time to diagnosis for individuals with LMNB1-associated ADLD.

Genetic testing serves as a key diagnostic tool, and these recommendations have strong evidence supported by case studies and expert consensus for LMNB1-related ADLD. This fully penetrant disorder is caused by either a pathogenic duplication of the LMNB1 gene or an upstream deletion in the promoter region.1,3,17-19,21 It is important to note that detection of an LMNB1 deletion or duplication alone, without clinical, MRI, or family history evidence, does not confirm an ADLD genetic diagnosis. Recent findings have demonstrated that not all LMNB1 duplications cause ADLD.26,38,39 Larger duplications that include a recently described silencer element are predicted not to cause ADLD.26 Single-gene testing with an orthogonal approach to sequencing for confirmation of structural variants is recommended over genome sequencing because of its cost-effectiveness and superior accuracy in detecting LMNB1-specific genetic alterations.1,43-45 These may include aCGH with a resolution of 100–200 kb, digital karyotyping, quantitative PCR, and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA). Exome sequencing is not recommended because of the limited capacity of this technology to detect relevant variants; genome sequencing optimized for accurate detection of duplications or deletions is a better option. It is critical to determine the precise size, orientation, and insertion site to assess whether the LMNB1 duplication will result in ADLD.

Establishing a genetic diagnosis provides significant benefit to the individual by defining the genetic cause of disease. This enables the use of the 2025 ICD-10-CM diagnosis code G90.B for LMNB1-related ADLD, sets the appropriate expectation of disease progression, guides management as described by these guidelines, and may help individuals feel a sense of belonging to a community of similarly affected individuals. An accurate genetic diagnosis will be necessary for participation in future clinical trials or to receive disease-modifying therapies when they become available.

Owing to the rarity of ADLD and limited available literature, the recommended guidelines for specific clinical assessments or surveillance protocols have weak evidence. These practical recommendations were largely adapted from other adult leukodystrophies with overlapping clinical features, such as X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy and Krabbe disease, and relied heavily on expert opinion from experience with these and similar disorders. Neurologic examinations, brain and cervical spinal cord MRI, autonomic function testing, and neuropsychometric evaluations49,50 should be performed at baseline (first contact with the neurologist) with neurologic evaluations repeated annually, given the slow progression of the disease to monitor disease progression.1,3,6,46 If there are spinal cord symptoms, the clinicians can consider including the entire cord at next MRI. Autonomic function testing, MRI, and neuropsychometric testing49,50 can be repeated based on symptom progression.4,47,48 Urology consults for neurogenic bladder51,52 should follow a similar approach, with assessments conducted as needed over time.

Annual neurologic examination will enable clinicians to improve clinical management. A symptom-guided and clinical progression–guided schedule for the other assessments will provide objective evidence of decline and change and will enable the treating physician to deliver individualized symptom management addressing specific symptoms. Management strategies are based primarily on expert consensus and practices from comparable neurologic disorders because little to no literature evidence exists for clinical management of ADLD. Recommendations are listed in Table 2. Individuals with LMNB1-related ADLD should be managed by a multidisciplinary team including a neurologist, physiotherapist, and geneticist.1 Treatment may involve spasticity management with medications or, in severe cases, invasive interventions, such as those used for other neurologic disorders associated with spasticity.53,54 Given the autosomal dominant inheritance leading to 50% risk of transmission to offspring, genetic counseling is strongly recommended,1,55-57 with prenatal counseling recommended for carriers and at-risk individuals.1,55,56,58 These management recommendations will benefit patient and physician communities in informing that multidisciplinary care models like those used in other neurodegenerative disorders can be extended to LMNB1-related ADLD and will lead to uniform care. Genetic diagnosis will benefit families as it provides opportunities for at-risk family members to engage with genetic counselors and geneticists early and seek genetic counseling and testing.

Limitations of this study include the limited amount of published data on individuals affected with ADLD, especially longitudinal, and the small number of available disease experts and their varied clinical experience with ADLD. These are inherent challenges in studying an ultra-rare condition. Through the Delphi process, repeated review of questions can also create a bias; however, only 2 rounds of surveys were needed to reach consensus.40-42 Any remaining points of nonconsensus were resolved during the final discussion. This consensus-based approach among ADLD experts enabled us to formulate recommendations despite limited direct scientific evidence. While we reached consensus on all topics, several recommendations relied heavily on expert opinion drawn from experience with similar disorders or symptom management, indicating the need for further studies on clinical variability, genotype-phenotype correlations, and outcomes in ADLD.

Management of disabling autonomic symptoms and spasticity can be areas of future study because these are the 2 most common clinical symptoms. Radiologic progression is uniform in all affected individuals, and a grading scale has been proposed.3 Further validation in additional families is needed, and genotype-driven differences (especially LMNB1 duplication vs deletion) need to be assessed. However, MRI findings will likely serve as a biomarker for future therapeutic interventions. In vivo CSF lactate elevation has also been seen in 4 participants with LMNB1 duplication and could be a potential biomarker but needs further validation.12

Despite these limitations, this guideline addresses a need in the ADLD community, an ultra-rare disease population. This study highlights knowledge gaps that can direct future research and foster collaboration among physicians, researchers, and patients to improve medical care and prepare for clinical trials. Longitudinal studies on disease course and progression are needed to further refine our understanding of the symptomatology, genotype-phenotype correlations, and ideal management strategies. Currently, there are no therapeutic strategies for ADLD, but LMNB1 knockdown approaches have been proposed and model systems developed to further this aim.30,32 Functional outcomes and end points for future trials have not yet been studied. The relative uniformity in the course of this disease among affected individuals can potentially make determining end points and course of action relatively easier; however, rigorous longitudinal studies of individuals with causative duplications and deletions, representing all sizes and breakpoints, are needed to precisely understand variability in clinical course. Another potential advantage is the large therapeutic window available for this disease, given its late onset and the possibility of earlier carrier detection. The prolonged course of the disease makes clinical trials challenging; hence, biomarker development is important.

Our EPMs have put forth these recommendations to promote uniform care for this ultra-rare disease and pave the way for future collaborative research efforts toward a natural history study, defining end points for future clinical trials and a cure.
