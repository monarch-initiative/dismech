itemprop="dateModified">September 22, 2026</span>.</p><p><em>Estimated reading time: 44 minutes</em></p></div><div class="body-content whole_rhythm" itemprop="text"><div id="rfc1-canvas.Summary" itemprop="description"><h2 id="_rfc1-canvas_Summary_">Summary</h2><div><h4 class="inline">Clinical characteristics.</h4><p>The phenotypic spectrum of <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders ranges from <i>c</i>erebellar <i>a</i>taxia, <i>n</i>europathy, <i>v</i>estibular <i>a</i>reflexia <i>s</i>yndrome (CANVAS) to phenotypes that involve predominantly or exclusively the peripheral sensory nerves with or without vestibular and cerebellar involvement. Onset is in adulthood (range: age 25-80 years). A chronic, spasmodic, dry cough can occur up to three decades before any neurologic manifestations. Sensory neuropathy seems to be a constant feature of <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders. Other features can include oscillopsia due to impaired vestibuloocular reflex, dysarthria, dysphagia, autonomic dysfunction (postural hypotension, erectile dysfunction, chronic constipation, urinary dysfunction, and altered sweating), and brisk reflexes. Less frequently, additional manifestations include upper and/or lower motor neuron involvement (spasticity and/or Babinski sign, muscle atrophy, fasciculations, and weakness), cognitive impairment, and extrapyramidal signs (resting tremor, bradykinesia, postural instability). <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders progress slowly. Life expectancy does not appear to be affected.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Diagnosis/testing.</h4><p>The diagnosis of an <i>RFC1</i>-related disorder is established in a proband with suggestive findings and biallelic pathogenic variants in <i>RFC1</i> identified by molecular genetic testing.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Management.</h4><p><i>Treatment of manifestations:</i> Multidisciplinary management of ataxia, sensory neuropathy, vestibular dysfunction, and autonomic dysfunction per neurologist, neurorehabilitation specialist, physiatrist, ENT, occupational therapist, and physical therapist; speech-language therapy for dysarthria; modify food consistency to decrease aspiration risk in those with dysphagia; management of cough per pulmonologist, ENT, and gastroenterologist; family and social work support. </p><p><i>Surveillance:</i> Annual neurologic assessment including consideration of monitoring using validated scaled for ataxia; neurologic assessment for any acute exacerbation; annual physiatry, occupational therapy, and physical therapy assessment; assess need for alternative communication device or speech therapy for dysarthria as needed; assess aspiration risk and feeding methods as needed; assess for cognitive issues as needed; assess family and social work needs. </p><p><i>Agents/circumstances to avoid:</i> Medications toxic to peripheral nerves, cerebellum, or vestibular system may worsen the features of <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders, but there is currently no evidence-based guidance recommending avoidance of specific medications, and treatment decisions should be based on clinical need. Alcohol, which can exacerbate ataxia, should only be consumed in moderation.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Genetic counseling.</h4><p><i>RFC1</i>-related disorders are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. (Pseudodominance may be observed due to the high heterozygote carrier frequency of <i>RFC1</i> AAGGG repeat expansions.) Assuming that both parents are heterozygous for an <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variant, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of inheriting biallelic <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variants, a 50% chance of inheriting one pathogenic variant, and a 25% chance of inheriting neither of the familial pathogenic variants. Sibs who inherit biallelic <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variants will likely be affected; sibs who inherit one <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variant are presumed to not be at risk of developing an <i>RFC1</i>-related disorder. Once biallelic <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variants have been identified in an affected family member, carrier testing for at-risk relatives and prenatal/preimplantation genetic testing are possible. </p></div></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Diagnosis"><h2 id="_rfc1-canvas_Diagnosis_">Diagnosis</h2><p>Formal diagnostic criteria for <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders have not been established.</p><div id="rfc1-canvas.Suggestive_Findings"><h3>Suggestive Findings</h3><p><i>RFC1</i>-related disorders <b>should be suspected</b> in individuals with onset after age 25 years of one or more of the following clinical findings with supportive electrodiagnostic, vestibular, and imaging findings as well as family history.</p><p>
<b>Common neurologic findings</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">
<b>Sensory neuropathy or neuronopathy</b>
</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Unsteadiness, loss of feeling, pins and needles, pain, and cramps</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Altered sensation in all limbs in either a length-dependent (distal extremities worse) or non-length-dependent pattern. Most commonly, widespread pinprick sense reduction, reduced vibration sensation in the lower limbs, and milder reduction of position sense distally.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Reflexes can be decreased/abolished, but retained and even brisk reflexes are possible. Frequently, decreased/abolished ankle reflexes with normal/brisk reflexes in the knees and in the upper limbs. </div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Gait ataxia</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Positive Romberg and dysmetria worsened by eye closur</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Usually, normal muscle bulk, strength, tone, and flexor plantar responses</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">
<b>Bilateral vestibulopathy</b>
</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Unsteadiness and oscillopsia, particularly with head movements</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Impaired vestibuloocular reflex at bedside on head impulse test</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Impaired visually enhanced vestibuloocular reflexes (indicating the coexistence of vestibular and cerebellar pathology)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Note: Vertigo is NOT a symptom of <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">
<b>Cerebellar dysfunction</b>
</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Unsteadiness, dysarthria, and dysphagia</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Cerebellar oculomotor signs including gaze-evoked nystagmus, downbeat nystagmus, saccadic pursuit, and dysmetric saccades</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Gait and limb ataxia</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Dysdiadokokinesia</div></li></ul><div class="half_rhythm">Note: Isolated cerebellar ataxia (without sensory neuropathy) is NOT a feature suggestive of <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.hadjivassiliou.2024.171">Hadjivassiliou et al 2024</a>].</div></li></ul><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Chronic cough</b> (can be the main presenting symptom)</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Dry or minimally productive with duration &#x0003e;8 weeks</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Refractory after addressing treatable cause (e.g., asthma, gastroesophageal reflux&#x000a0;/ ineffective esophageal motility, and rhinitis)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Laryngeal paresthesia (e.g., tickle, irritation, or globus sensation)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Can be the main presenting symptom</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Chest examination findings usually normal</div></li></ul></li></ul><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Autonomic dysfunction</b> (mild and rarely disabling)</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Postural hypotension</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Erectile dysfunction</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Urinary dysfunction</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Chronic constipation and/or diarrhea</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Nausea, vomiting, or bloating after a small meal</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Anhidrosis or increased sweating</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Dry mouth/eyes</div></li></ul></li></ul><p>
<b>Less common neurologic findings</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Cognitive impairment typically involving executive function and attention&#x000a0;/ working memory</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Upper and lower motor neuron involvement: brisk reflexes, particularly in the upper limbs and at the knees. Muscle atrophy, fasciculations, and weakness are rare [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.huin.2022.2121">Huin et al 2022</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Extrapyramidal involvement: resting tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.trasch_tz.2021.e1369">Trasch&#x000fc;tz et al 2021</a>]</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Electrodiagnostic findings</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Nerve conduction studies. Sensory neuropathy or neuronopathy with reduced or absent sensory action potentials. Motor study is usually normal. </div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Abnormal blink reflex (trigeminal neuronopathy) and preserved H-reflex (Hoffmann reflex) are reported in few individuals [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.infante.2018.1454">Infante et al 2018</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Electromyography is usually normal.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Vestibular testing</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Bilateral abnormal video head impulse test</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Reduced caloric response</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Reduced vestibuloocular reflex time constants on rotatory chair</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Imaging</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Brain MRI. Cerebellar atrophy, particularly of the vermis, in later disease stages; spinal cord atrophy and T<sub>2</sub>-weighted hyperintensity of the posterior columns.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Nerve ultrasound. Reduced cross-sectional area of upper and lower limb nerves, both proximally and distally [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.salvalaggio.2024.464">Salvalaggio et al 2024</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.garvey.2025.fcaf434">Garvey et al 2025</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b>Autonomic testing</b> can confirm the presence of sympathetic and/or parasympathetic dysfunction.</p><p><b>Family history</b> is typically absent (the proband is the only family member known to be affected) or suggestive of autosomal recessive inheritance (e.g., affected sibs and/or, more rarely, parental consanguinity). Note: Pseudodominant inheritance has been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.falcone.2024.2622">Falcone et al 2024</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.grosz.2025.e70070">Grosz et al 2025</a>] due to the high carrier frequency of <i>RFC1</i> AAGGG repeat expansions (see <a href="#rfc1-canvas.Prevalence">Prevalence</a>). </p></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Establishing_the_Diagnosis"><h3>Establishing the Diagnosis</h3><p>The diagnosis of an <i>RFC1</i>-related disorder <b>is established</b> in a proband with <a href="#rfc1-canvas.Suggestive_Findings">suggestive findings</a> and biallelic pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variants in <i>RFC1</i> identified by molecular genetic testing (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK564656/table/rfc1-canvas.T.rfc1related_disorders_mole/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobrfc1canvasTrfc1relateddisordersmole">Table 1</a>).</p><p>Note: (1) Per American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics&#x000a0;/ Association for Molecular Pathology variant interpretation guidelines, the terms "pathogenic variant" and "likely pathogenic variant" are synonymous in a clinical setting, meaning that both are considered diagnostic and can be used for clinical decision making [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.richards.2015.405">Richards et al 2015</a>]. Reference to "pathogenic variants" in this <i>GeneReview</i> is understood to include likely pathogenic variants. (2) Identification of biallelic <i>RFC1</i> variants of uncertain significance (or of one known <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variant and one <i>RFC1</i> variant of uncertain significance) does not establish or rule out the diagnosis. </p><p>The two classes of <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variants are (1) intronic pentanucleotide expansions in <i>RFC1</i> (most commonly AAGGG repeat expansions) and (2) <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic sequence variants or deletions. Most individuals (&#x0003e;99%) with <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders have biallelic intronic repeat expansions of <i>RFC1</i>.</p><p>
<b>Repeat motifs</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>
<b>Normal (non-pathogenic)</b>
</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>(AAAAG)<sub>9-11</sub> repeats</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>(AAAAG)<sub>exp</sub></div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>(AAAGGG)<sub>exp</sub> (as part of complex motif with possible AAAAG and AAAGG interruptions)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>(AAGAG)<sub>exp</sub></div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>
<b>Variable (size-dependent) penetrance</b>
</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>(AAAGG)<sub>exp</sub> usually in the range of 30-110 repeats are non-pathogenic. </div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Rarer expansions greater than 600 repeats of uninterrupted (AAGGG)<sub>exp</sub> or two-motif (AAAGG)<sub>exp</sub>(AAGGG)<sub>exp</sub> can be pathogenic (multiple families reported) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.dominik.2023.5060">Dominik et al 2023</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.erdmann.2026.993">Erdmann et al 2026</a>].</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>
<b>Pathogenic</b>

However, cough can precede other symptoms by several decades, with typical onset in the third to fifth decade of life. </p><p><b>Impairment of balance and coordination.</b> Progressive imbalance is the presenting symptom in two thirds of affected individuals. Imbalance is often worse in the dark, indicating a prominent contribution from peripheral neuropathy and vestibular loss. Upper limb coordination and hand dexterity are better preserved than gait. </p><p><b>Sensory symptoms,</b> which include loss of feeling, pins and needles, pain, and cramps, occur in one third of individuals early in the disease course and in more than two thirds of individuals at any point. Sensory examination in individuals with <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders commonly reveals widespread pinprick sense reduction, reduced vibration sensation in the lower limbs, and milder reduction of position sense distally.</p><p><b>Vestibular hypofunction.</b> Oscillopsia, defined as a visual disturbance in which objects appear to oscillate during head movements, is a common sequela of a bilaterally impaired vestibuloocular reflex; it is reported by one fourth of affected individuals and can be the presenting manifestation in some. Examination reveals absent/reduced vestibuloocular reflex at bedside or video head impulse test. An impaired visually enhanced vestibuloocular reflex indicates the coexistence of vestibular and cerebellar pathology.</p><p>Note: Vertigo and hearing loss are not features of <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders but, as they are common in the general population, may occur independently of <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders. </p><p><b>Cerebellar dysfunction.</b> Dysarthria and dysphagia, attributed to cerebellar dysfunction, are possible complications later in the disease course. Examination reveals oculomotor signs (including gaze-evoked nystagmus, downbeat nystagmus, saccadic pursuit, and dysmetric saccades) and gait and limb ataxia.</p><p>Note: Isolated cerebellar ataxia (without sensory neuropathy) is not a feature suggestive of <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders. </p><p><b>Cough.</b> A chronic spasmodic dry cough is frequently associated and can be reported as early as the second decade of life, up to three decades before any neurologic symptoms develop. It is commonly misdiagnosed as asthma or gastroesophageal reflux, although these conditions may coexist. </p><p><b>Autonomic dysfunction.</b> Signs and symptoms including postural hypotension, erectile dysfunction, chronic constipation, urinary dysfunction, and altered sweating are relatively common but rarely disabling. More severe dysautonomia with symptomatic orthostatic hypotension resembling multisystem atrophy and cough-induced syncope was reported in a few individuals [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.record.2022.311">Record et al 2022</a>]. Autonomic testing confirms the presence of a sympathetic and/or parasympathetic dysfunction in half of individuals undergoing specific investigations. Autonomic testing for sympathetic dysfunction can include measurement of blood pressure response to change in posture and handgrip and sympathetic skin response; for parasympathetic dysfunction, testing can include EKG monitoring of heart rate variation during Valsalva maneuver, deep breathing, and standing. </p><p><b>Cognitive impairment</b> has been reported in those with biallelic <i>RFC1</i> expansions. Deficits most frequently involve executive functions and attention&#x000a0;/ working memory. Two case series in which cognition was systematically assessed found that most affected individuals met criteria for probable or definite cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome (CCAS) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.albajar.2024.1246">Albajar et al 2024</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.dujardin.2024.892">Dujardin et al 2024</a>]. However, dementia is not common in <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders.</p><p><b>Motor neuron involvement.</b> Brisk reflexes, particularly in the upper limbs and at the knees, are commonly observed. Other signs of upper and lower motor neuron involvement, including spasticity and/or Babinski sign, muscle atrophy, fasciculations, and weakness, are uncommon.</p><p><b>Extrapyramidal features,</b> including resting tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability, appear rare in several series (0%-0.5%) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.malaquias.2023.e200190">Malaquias et al 2023</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.curro.2024.1887">Curro et al 2024</a>] but more prevalent in others (17.1%-23.1%) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.trasch_tz.2021.e1369">Trasch&#x000fc;tz et al 2021</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.azevedo.2023.105854">Azevedo et al 2023</a>]. Several case reports and small case series (including 19 individuals) described DaTscan findings, with results ranging from absent to severe dopaminergic denervation [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.nakamura.2020.475">Nakamura et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.barghigiani.2022.5431">Barghigiani et al 2022</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.kyt_vuori.2022.6">Kyt&#x000f6;vuori et al 2022</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.azevedo.2023.105854">Azevedo et al 2023</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.horowitz.2024.879">Horowitz et al 2024</a>]. The association between <i>RFC1</i> expansions and extrapyramidal features warrants further investigation in larger cohort studies. </p><p><b>Prognosis.</b> Disease progression is slowly progressive, from early involvement of sensory neurons to later appearance of vestibular and cerebellar dysfunction. In a study on 392 individuals, clinical features of CANVAS were seen after ten years of disease duration in half of individuals with <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.curro.2024.1887">Curro et al 2024</a>]. A complex sensory ataxia with cerebellar or vestibular involvement was identified in 33% of affected individuals. A sensory neuropathy, or sensory neuropathy with cough, was the only clinically detectable disease manifestation in 14% of affected individuals. No affected individuals had isolated cerebellar ataxia or isolated vestibular failure. Half of individuals needed a cane after ten years of disease duration, and one fourth were wheelchair dependent five years later. Life expectancy did not appear to be affected. </p></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.GenotypePhenotype_Correlatio"><h3>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations </h3><p>The size of the pathogenic AAGGG repeat expansion shows a mild inverse correlation with age of onset; larger repeat sizes are associated with an earlier age of onset [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.curro.2024.1887">Curro et al 2024</a>]. Also, larger AAGGG repeat sizes appear associated with increased disease severity, earlier loss of independent walking, and more rapid onset of dysarthria or dysphagia. </p><p>Although genotype-phenotype correlation data for non-AAGGG pentanucleotide expansions are limited, more frequent involvement of lower motor neurons (atrophy, cramps, fasciculations) has been reported in those with ACAGG expansions [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.scriba.2020.2904">Scriba et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.miyatake.2022.1139">Miyatake et al 2022</a>].</p><p>Due to the limited number of reported families, there is no conclusive evidence that individuals with truncating variants in <i>RFC1</i> in compound heterozygous state with a monoallelic AAGGG repeat expansion have a more severe phenotype compared to individuals with biallelic AAGGG repeat expansions, although this was suggested in few reports [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.benkirane.2022.3770">Benkirane et al 2022</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.king.2022.e200036">King et al 2022</a>, <a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/authored-by/Arteche%E2%80%90L%C3%B3pez/Ana" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">Arteche-L&#x000f3;pez</a> et al 2023, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.ronco.2023.e543">Ronco et al 2023</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.weber.2023.e14">Weber et al 2023</a>].</p></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Germline_Instability"><h3>Germline Instability</h3><p>Since <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders are typically caused by inheritance of pathogenic repeats from both parents, germline instability has limited clinical implications. Moreover, change in repeat size from paternal or maternal transmission appears to be small in magnitude (&#x0003c;10%) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.curro.2024.1887">Curro et al 2024</a>].</p></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Prevalence"><h3>Prevalence</h3><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The heterozygote carrier frequency of <i>RFC1</i> AAGGG repeat expansions is 8% in populations of predominantly northern European origin, while lower frequences are reported in American (5.3%), East Asian (4.7%), South Asian (2.9%), and African (0.7%) populations [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.iba_ez.2024.3357">Iba&#x000f1;ez et al 2024</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The prevalence of individuals with biallelic AAGGG repeat expansions is 1:517 to 1:951 [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.iba_ez.2024.3357">Iba&#x000f1;ez et al 2024</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.erdmann.2026.993">Erdmann et al 2026</a>]. The number of individuals presenting with manifestations of <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders may be lower than predicted by short-read genome sequencing data due to reduced penetrance, particularly of AAGGG repeat expansions of &#x0003c;250 repeats.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Biallelic pathogenic expansions in <i>RFC1</i> have been identified in 15%-22% of individuals with late-onset ataxia in European cohorts, with higher percentages observed in persons with sensory and cerebellar involvement (62%) and persons with ataxia with chronic cough (~100%) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.cortese.2019.649">Cortese et al 2019</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.montaut.2021.3337">Montaut et al 2021</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.trasch_tz.2022.e6">Trasch&#x000fc;tz et al 2022</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.erdmann.2023.1831">Erdmann et al 2023</a>]. More than one third of individuals with ataxia in an Australian cohort had biallelic <i>RFC1</i> expansions (mostly AAGGG, but also [ACAGG]<sub>exp</sub>, [AAAGG]<sub>&#x0003e;500,</sub> and the M&#x00101;ori [AAAGG]<sub>10-25</sub>[AAGGG]<sub>exp</sub>[AAAGG]<sub>4-6</sub> repeat motif) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.davies.2025.111">Davies et al 2025</a>]. A lower prevalence of biallelic pathogenic <i>RFC1</i> expansions (0.6%-4.3%) has been reported in cohorts from North and South America and East Asia [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.ak_imen.2019.1219">Ak&#x000e7;imen et al 2019</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.aboud_syriani.2020.e440">Aboud Syriani et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.wan.2020.1132">Wan et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.ando.2022.952493">Ando et al 2022</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Repeat expansions in <i>RFC1</i> account for ~33% of idiopathic, pure sensory or predominantly sensory axonal neuropathies [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.curro.2021.1542">Curro et al 2021</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.tagliapietra.2021.4280">Tagliapietra et al 2021</a>].</div></li></ul><p>Genetic data and cohort studies suggest that <i>RFC1</i> expansions are a common cause of adult-onset recessive ataxia and sensory neuropathy.</p></div></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Genetically_Related_Allelic"><h2 id="_rfc1-canvas_Genetically_Related_Allelic_">Genetically Related (Allelic) Disorders</h2><p>No phenotypes other than those discussed in this <i>GeneReview</i> are known to be associated with germline pathogenic variants in <i>RFC1</i>. </p></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Differential_Diagnosis"><h2 id="_rfc1-canvas_Differential_Diagnosis_">Differential Diagnosis</h2><p>Given the multisystem involvement of <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders and the possible asynchronous involvement of different systems during disease progression, the genetic differential diagnosis is broad and includes:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Genetic causes of ataxia (see <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/ataxias/">Hereditary Ataxia Overview</a>);</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Genetic causes of neuropathy (see <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/cmt/">Charcot-Marie-Tooth Hereditary Neuropathy Overview</a>); </div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Mitochondrial disorders that can manifest with ataxia, neuropathy, and (more occasionally) bilateral vestibular areflexia (see <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/mt-overview/">Primary Mitochondrial Disorders Overview</a>). </div></li></ul><p>Note: Isolated cerebellar ataxia (without sensory neuropathy) is not a feature suggestive of <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.cortese.2020b.480">Cortese et al 2020b</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.hadjivassiliou.2024.171">Hadjivassiliou et al 2024</a>]. </p></div></dd></dl></div></div></div><p><b>Sensory neuronopathy.</b> The differential diagnosis should encompass acquired causes of sensory neuronopathy including:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Paraneoplastic syndrome</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Sj&#x000f6;gren syndrome [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.kumar.2020">Kumar et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.fern_ndezeulate.2023.460">Fern&#x000e1;ndez-Eulate et al 2023</a>]</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Acute and chronic immune-mediated disorders including anti-MAG neuropathy [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.johnson.2026">Johnson et al 2026</a>]</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Metabolic disorders (diabetes mellitus, vitamin B<sub>12</sub> deficiency, copper deficiency)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Toxins (alcohol, pyridoxine, platinum derivatives, pyridoxine intoxication) </div></li></ul><p>Note: It is important to consider testing for <i>RFC1</i> expansions in individuals with cerebellar ataxia and/or sensory neuropathy attributed to acquired causes, particularly when phenotype, disease course, or response to treatment are atypical. Biallelic <i>RFC1</i> expansions have been identified in multiple persons initially diagnosed with acquired sensory neuronopathy (e.g., Sj&#x000f6;gren syndrome, anti-MAG neuropathy) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.hadjivassiliou.2024.171">Hadjivassiliou et al 2024</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.johnson.2026">Johnson et al 2026</a>].</p><p><b>Late-onset cerebellar ataxia.</b> The two main differential diagnoses to take into consideration in an individual with late-onset cerebellar ataxia are <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/fgf14-ataxia/">GAA-<i>FGF14</i>-related ataxia</a> (see <a href="/sites/books/NBK564656/table/rfc1-canvas.T.rfc1related_disorders_gene/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobrfc1canvasTrfc1relateddisordersgene">Table 3</a>) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.pellerin.2024.175">Pellerin et al 2024</a>] and multisystem atrophy [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.fan.2020.1">Fan et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.sullivan.2020.1277">Sullivan et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.wan.2020.1132">Wan et al 2020</a>]. </p><p>Multisystem atrophy (MSA) is a rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disease that can present with a wide range of clinical manifestations, including cerebellar ataxia and autonomic failure [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.wenning.2022.1131">Wenning et al 2022</a>]. The following clinical characteristics distinguish MSA from <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The average survival time in MSA is 9.3 years, while disease progression in <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders is very slow and life expectancy does not appear to be reduced [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.fanciulli.2015.249">Fanciulli &#x00026; Wenning 2015</a>]. </div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Unlike <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders, sensory neuropathy and vestibular dysfunction do not occur in MSA, or, if coexisting, are most likely unrelated. </div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Autonomic dysfunction, a common and highly debilitating feature of MSA, is usually mild in <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders. </div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Additional features that favor an MSA diagnosis include rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder, parkinsonism, and an MRI pattern showing putaminal, pontine, and middle cerebellar peduncle atrophy and "hot cross bun" sign cruciform T<sub>2</sub>-weighted hyperintensity in the pons [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.chelban.2019.1036">Chelban et al 2019</a>]. </div></li></ul><p>Additional causes of progressive cerebellar impairment to consider include toxic, nutritional, vascular, neoplastic, inflammatory, and paraneoplastic conditions.</p><p><b>Bilateral vestibulopathy.</b> The differential diagnosis of bilateral vestibulopathy includes (among other conditions) drug-induced ototoxicity (e.g., aminoglycosides, immune checkpoint inhibitors), Meniere disease, tumors compressing both vestibular nerves (e.g., bilateral schwannomas in <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/nf2/"><i>NF2</i>-related schwannomatosis</a>), and infectious and/or inflammatory systemic disorders. </p><p>Bilateral vestibular impairment can be observed in several hereditary neurodegenerative conditions including spinocerebellar ataxia (<a href="/sites/books/n/gene/sca3/">SCA3</a> as well as <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/sca1/">SCA1</a>, <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/sca2/">SCA2</a>, and <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/sca6/">SCA6</a>), <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/friedreich/">Friedreich ataxia</a>, <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/gaucher/">Gaucher disease</a>, and <a href="/sites/books/n/gene/cmt/">Charcot-Marie-Tooth hereditary neuropathy</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.poretti.2013.2099">Poretti et al 2013</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.p_rezgarrigues.2014.824">P&#x000e9;rez-Garrigues et al Note: There may not be clinical trials for this disorder.</p></div></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Genetic_Counseling"><h2 id="_rfc1-canvas_Genetic_Counseling_">Genetic Counseling</h2><p><i>Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with information on the nature, mode(s) of inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them make informed medical and personal decisions. The following section deals with genetic risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or ethical issues that may arise or to substitute for consultation with a genetics professional</i>. &#x02014;ED.</p><div id="rfc1-canvas.Mode_of_Inheritance"><h3>Mode of Inheritance</h3><p><i>RFC1-</i>related disorders are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. </p><p>Pseudodominance (the occurrence of an autosomal recessive disorder in two generations of a family without consanguinity) may be observed in <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders due to the high heterozygote carrier frequency of <i>RFC1</i> AAGGG repeat expansions in some populations [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.falcone.2024.2622">Falcone et al 2024</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.grosz.2025.e70070">Grosz et al 2025</a>] (see <a href="#rfc1-canvas.Prevalence">Prevalence</a>).</p><p>
<b>Parents of a proband</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The parents of an affected individual are presumed to be heterozygous for an <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variant (i.e., a pathogenic <i>RFC1</i> intronic pentanucleotide repeat expansion [most commonly AAGGG repeat expansion] or an <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic sequence variant or deletion). </div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Molecular genetic testing of the parents can be used to confirm that both parents are heterozygous for an <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variant and to facilitate reliable recurrence risk assessment; however, given the mean age of onset of <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders is in the sixth decade, molecular genetic testing of the parents is often not possible.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>To date, <i>RFC1</i>-related disorders have not been reported in heterozygotes (carriers) and heterozygotes are presumed to not be at risk of developing an <i>RFC1</i>-related disorder (additional long-term testing is needed to confirm this observation). However, a single study suggested that carriers of a pathogenic <i>RFC1</i> AAGGG expansion are at higher risk of developing a moderate/severe sensory neuropathy after exposure to neurotoxic chemotherapy (i.e., cisplatin) (see <a href="https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.11.18.688292v1" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">bioRxiv</a>; pre-peer review). </div></li></ul><p>
<b>Sibs of a proband</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Assuming that both parents are heterozygous for an <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variant, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of inheriting biallelic <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variants, a 50% chance of inheriting one pathogenic variant, and a 25% chance of inheriting neither of the familial pathogenic variants.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Sibs who inherit biallelic <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variants will likely be affected. Limited variability in age at disease onset is expected among sibs who inherit biallelic pathogenic <i>RFC1</i> repeat expansions. Intergenerational changes in <i>RFC1</i> repeat expansion size appear to be small in magnitude.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Sibs who inherit one <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variant (i.e., carriers) are presumed to not be at risk of developing an <i>RFC1</i>-related disorder. However, a single study suggested that carriers of a pathogenic AAGGG expansion are at higher risk of developing a moderate/severe sensory neuropathy after exposure to neurotoxic chemotherapy (i.e., cisplatin) (see <a href="https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.11.18.688292v1" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">bioRxiv</a>; pre-peer review). </div></li></ul><p><b>Offspring of a proband.</b> Unless an individual with an <i>RFC1-</i>related disorder has children with an affected individual or a carrier, offspring will be obligate heterozygotes (carriers) for an <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variant (see <b>Family planning</b>).</p></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Carrier_Detection"><h3>Carrier Detection</h3><p>Carrier testing for at-risk relatives requires prior identification of the <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variants in the family.</p></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Related_Genetic_Counseling_I"><h3>Related Genetic Counseling Issues </h3><p>
<b>Family planning</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The optimal time for determination of genetic risk and discussion of the availability of prenatal/preimplantation genetic testing is before pregnancy. </div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is appropriate to offer genetic counseling (including discussion of potential risks to offspring and reproductive options) to young adults who are affected, are carriers, or are at risk of being carriers.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Due to the high heterozygote carrier frequency of <i>RFC1</i> AAGGG repeat expansions in some populations (see <a href="#rfc1-canvas.Prevalence">Prevalence</a>), carrier testing may be considered for the reproductive partners of individuals known to have an <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variant(s). For example, in populations of predominantly northern European origin, the likelihood that the reproductive partner of an individual with an <i>RFC1</i>-related disorder would be a carrier of an <i>RFC1</i> AAGGG repeat expansion is 8%. Based on this carrier frequency; the likelihood that offspring would inherit biallelic <i>RFC1</i> AAGGG repeat expansion is estimated to be ~4%-5%</div></li></ul></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Prenatal_Testing_and_Preimpl"><h3>Prenatal Testing and Preimplantation Genetic Testing</h3><p>Once biallelic <i>RFC1</i> pathogenic variants have been identified in an affected family member, prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing are possible. </p><p>Differences in perspective may exist among medical professionals and within families regarding the use of prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing. While most health care professionals would consider use of prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing to be a personal decision, discussion of these issues may be helpful.</p></div></div><div id="rfc1-canvas.Resources"><h2 id="_rfc1-canvas_Resources_">Resources</h2><p>

RFC acts as a clamp loader, facilitating the loading of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) onto DNA [<a class="bk_pop" href="#rfc1-canvas.REF.gaubitz.2020.23571">Gaubitz et al 2020</a>]. PCNA ensures that the polymerase and the template DNA maintain a high-affinity interaction during the elongation process. </p><p>The repeat locus is located at the polyA tail of an AluSx3 element in the second intron of <i>RFC1</i>. The variable pentanucleotide repeats identified in the population differ in terms of motif and size from the reference sequence (AAAAG)<sub>9-11</sub>.</p><p>The repeat expansion is intronic and is not predicted to be translated into protein by canonical AUG-initiated translation. </p><p><b>Mechanism of disease causation.</b> The mechanism underlying neurodegeneration in <i>RFC1-</i>related disorders is still a matter of debate and both toxic gain-of-function and loss-of-function mechanisms have been investigated.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Loss of function is suggested by the autosomal recessive pattern of inheritance and identification of <i>RFC1</i> nonsense and truncating pathogenic variants in compound heterozygous state with an expanded pathogenic pentanucleotide repeat.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The AAGGG repeat motif can adopt complex secondary structures, predominantly parallel G-quadruplexes [<a class="bk_pop"
