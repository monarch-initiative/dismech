</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>The iron regulatory hormone <em>hepcidin</em> is transcriptionally up-regulated in response to iron loading, but the mechanisms by which iron levels are sensed are not well understood. Large-scale genetic screens in the zebrafish have resulted in the identification of hypochromic anemia mutants with a range of mutations affecting conserved pathways in iron metabolism and heme synthesis. We hypothesized that transferrin plays a critical role both in iron transport and in regulating <em>hepcidin</em> expression in zebrafish embryos. Here we report the identification and characterization of the zebrafish hypochromic anemia mutant, <em>gavi</em>, which exhibits transferrin deficiency due to mutations in <em>transferrin-a</em>. Morpholino knockdown of <em>transferrin-a</em> in wild-type embryos reproduced the anemia phenotype and decreased somite and terminal gut iron staining, while coinjection of <em>transferrin-a</em> cRNA partially restored these defects. Embryos with <em>transferrin-a</em> or <em>transferrin receptor 2</em> (<em>TfR2</em>) deficiency exhibited low levels of <em>hepcidin</em> expression, however anemia, in the absence of a defect in the transferrin pathway, failed to impair <em>hepcidin</em> expression. These data indicate that <em>transferrin-a</em> transports iron and that <em>hepcidin</em> expression is regulated by a <em>transferrin-a</em>–dependent pathway in the zebrafish embryo.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Transferrin, a serum protein with 2 iron binding sites, is the major carrier of ferric iron in human plasma and is required to deliver iron to developing erythroid and other cells. Iron-bound holotransferrin binds transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) on the surface of the cell, resulting in endocytosis of an endosome containing both transferrin and TfR1 (reviewed in Aisen<sup>1</sup>). After acidification of the endosome, ferrous iron exits the endosome via the divalent metal transporter (DMT1).<sup><a href="#B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2">2</a>,<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a></sup> Transferrin receptor 2 (TfR2), a homolog of TfR1, with restricted expression to hepatocytes and erythroid cells,<sup><a href="#B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4">4</a></sup> binds transferrin at lower affinity than TfR1<sup><a href="#B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5">5</a>,<a href="#B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6">6</a></sup> and is involved in the regulation of iron homeostasis</p>
<p>As humans have no active means of iron excretion, maintaining iron homeostasis requires adjustment of iron absorption to reflect the body's iron stores. Hepcidin is a peptide hormone, primarily expressed in the liver,<sup><a href="#B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7">7</a></sup> which modulates iron absorption and iron delivery to erythrocytes by binding the iron transporter ferroportin (fpn), resulting in internalization and degradation of fpn.<sup><a href="#B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8">8</a></sup> In mammalian models, <em>hepcidin</em> is transcriptionally up-regulated in response to inflammation<sup><a href="#B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9">9</a>,<a href="#B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10">10</a></sup> or iron overload<sup><a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">11</a></sup> and down-regulated in response to anemia, iron deficiency, or hypoxia.<sup><a href="#B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9">9</a></sup> Although mutations in <em>HFE</em>,<sup><a href="#B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12">12</a></sup> <em>transferrin receptor 2</em> (<em>TFR2</em>),<sup><a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">13</a></sup> <em>hepcidin</em> (<em>HAMP</em>),<sup><a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">14</a></sup> or <em>hemojuvelin</em> (<em>HJV</em> or <em>HFE2</em>)<sup><a href="#B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15">15</a></sup> have each been associated with hemochromatosis and impaired <em>hepcidin</em> expression,<sup><a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">16</a></sup> the mechanisms by which iron overload is sensed and <em>hepcidin</em> is regulated are not completely understood.</p>
<p>The zebrafish, <em>Danio rerio</em>, provides an excellent system for the identification and analysis of genes involved in iron metabolism and erythroid development. Large-scale genetic screens for embryonic hypochromic anemia have identified recessive mutations in genes affecting iron acquisition and use including <em>DMT1</em>,<sup><a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">17</a></sup> <em>transferrin receptor 1a</em> (<em>TfR1a</em>),<sup><a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a></sup> and <em>ferroportin1</em> (<em>fpn1</em>).<sup><a href="#B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19">19</a></sup> Zebrafish embryos at 48 to 72 hours postfertilization (hpf), provide an excellent in vivo model for the study of <em>hepcidin</em> gene expression. At this stage, zebrafish embryos do not ingest food and are kept free of inflammatory stimuli, reducing the variables that may affect <em>hepcidin</em> expression. Our previous work demonstrated that <em>fpn1</em> is required for normal cycling of iron through the reticuloendothelial system in the zebrafish and that induction of <em>hepcidin</em> expression in response to iron loading occurs 18 hours after injection of zebrafish embryos at 48 hpf with iron dextran.<sup><a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">20</a></sup> The zebrafish hepcidin peptide is 52% identical to human hepcidin and has been shown to mediate degradation of ferroportin and cellular iron retention in vitro.<sup><a href="#B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21">21</a></sup></p>
<p>In this report, we identify a zebrafish strain, <em>gavi</em> (<em>gav</em>), with mutations in zebrafish <em>transferrin</em> (<em>transferrin-a</em>), which exhibits reduced <em>transferrin-a</em> expression and impaired hemoglobin production. We demonstrate that morpholino knockdown of <em>transferrin-a</em> reproduces the anemia phenotype and reduces nonheme iron stores in the somites of the embryo, while overexpression of <em>transferrin-a</em> partially restores hemoglobin production and iron stores in the somites and terminal gut. We also compare the effects of <em>DMT1</em>, <em>TfR1a</em>, <em>TfR1b</em>, <em>TfR2</em>, and <em>transferrin-a</em> deficiency on <em>hepcidin</em> expression in zebrafish embryos. We demonstrate that both <em>transferrin-a</em> and <em>TfR2</em> are required for <em>hepcidin</em> expression in zebrafish embryos, while <em>TfR1a</em> and <em>DMT1</em> are required for increased <em>hepcidin</em> expression in response to iron loading. These data support the hypothesis that <em>transferrin-a</em> is required for iron transport from the yolk sac to the embryo and that <em>transferrin-a</em> participates with <em>TfR2</em> in the regulation of <em>hepcidin</em> expression in zebrafish embryos.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Zebrafish strains, maintenance, and determination of genotype</h3>
<p>Zebrafish were maintained as described.<sup><a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a></sup> Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Children's Hospital and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. <em>Gav</em><sup>IT029</sup> and <em>gav</em><sup>HE067</sup> were identified as mutants with hypochromic anemia in a large-scale ENU-mutagenesis screen conducted by the Tübingen 2000 consortium. Genetic mapping strains were created by mating <em>gav</em>/Tü heterozygotes to the polymorphic WIK strain. Embryos were collected from pairwise matings of mapping strain <em>gav</em> heterozygotes and scored at 72 hpf for hypochromic anemia. Genomic DNA extraction from individual embryos and bulk segregant analysis were performed as described<sup><a href="#B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23">23</a></sup> using primers designed to amplify SSLP markers from the Massachusetts General Hospital Zebrafish Server website (<a href="http://zebrafish.mgh.harvard.edu" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://zebrafish.mgh.harvard.edu</a>). The <em>gav</em><sup>IT029</sup> and <em>gav</em><sup>HE067</sup>mutations were amplified from genomic DNA using the following primer pairs: IT029 E4F2 5′-CCCCAATCTGATTCAGTTCC-3′ and IT029 E4R1 5′-TGCCCTGGTGACAACTCTTA-3′, and HE067 F9 5′-CAGATGCTTCCAAAATGCACGACTCC-3′ and HE067 R6 5′-ACCATGCAATCCCAGGTCGGTCAA-3′. For amplification of the aberrant splice forms of <em>transferrin-a</em> in the mutants, RNA was extracted from pools of mutants using the RNeasy kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA), cDNA was generated as described,<sup><a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">20</a></sup> and PCR was performed using the primers trf F 5′-GCCAGAAATCGCTGGTGAAG-3′ and trf R 5′-GAGCTAATACAGTTGCCAAAAGTGC-3′. PCR products were purified and sequenced directly. For characterization of gene expression, the following zebrafish anemia alleles were used: <em>cia</em><sup>Tu25f</sup>,<sup><a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a></sup> <em>cdy</em><sup>Te216,17</sup> and <em>gav</em><sup>IT029</sup>.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Morpholino injection and cRNA injection</h3>
<p>Antisense morpholino oligonucleotides,<sup><a href="#B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24">24</a>,<a href="#B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25">25</a></sup> obtained from Gene Tools (Philomath, OR), were designed either to interfere with translation or to impair appropriate splicing of transcripts (morpholino sequences shown in Table S1, available on the <em>Blood</em> website; see the Supplemental Materials link at the top of the online article). Morpholino sequences for <em>TfR1a</em>, <em>TfR1b</em>, and <em>GATA1</em> were as described.<sup><a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a>,<a href="#B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26">26</a></sup> For each <em>transferrin-a</em> morpholino (MO), a mismatch control morpholino was synthesized with identical sequence, except for 5 nucleotide substitutions. Morpholinos that did not produce an anemia phenotype were tagged with 3′-fluorescein to indicate adequacy of injection. Embryos were injected at the one-cell stage with 3 nanoliters in 1× Danieau medium, supplemented with phenol red. For single morpholino injections, the concentration was 0.25 mM, or 0.2 mM for GATA1 MO, while for combination morpholino injections, the injection solution contained 0.125 mM of each morpholino. For cRNA injections, zebrafish <em>transferrin-a</em> was cloned into the pCS2 + expression vector. The vector was digested with NotI, and the sense cRNA was synthesized using the SP6 mMachine RNA transcription kit (Ambion, Austin, TX). <em>TfR1a</em> cRNA was prepared as described.<sup><a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a></sup> The cRNA was injected at a concentration of 1600 ng/microliter, with or without Trf-MO2 (0.0625 mM).</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Whole mount in situ hybridization</h3>
<p>Whole mount in situ hybridizations were performed as previously described.<sup><a href="#B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27">27</a></sup> The development of endogenous pigments was inhibited by supplementing the embryo medium with 1-phenyl-2-thiourea (PTU) at a final concentration of 0.2 mM. The following antisense riboprobes were generated for use in the in situ hybridizations: <em>transferrin-a</em>, <em>TfR2</em>, <em>hepcidin</em>, and <em>liver fatty acid binding protein</em> (<em>LFABP</em>). <em>Transferrin-a</em> and partial <em>TfR2</em> were each cloned into pCS2 +. The vectors were digested with <em>Eco</em>RI, and antisense riboprobes were synthesized from the T7 promoter. A full-length <em>hepcidin</em> clone in PCRII-Topo (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) was digested with NotI, and the antisense riboprobe was synthesized from the SP6 promoter. The <em>LFABP</em> plasmid was a gift from W. Goessling. The antisense riboprobe was generated by digesting with SalI and transcribing from the T7 promoter. Photomicrographs of in situ hybridizations were obtained using a BX51 compound microscope (Olympus, Center Valley, PA) at 100× magnification using Nomarski optics and a Q-capture 5 digital camera (QImaging, Surrey, BC).</p></section><section id="sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Whole mount embryo staining for hemoglobin and iron</h3>
<p>Live anesthetized embryos were stained for hemoglobin with <em>o</em>-dianisidine, as described.<sup><a href="#B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28">28</a></sup> Diaminobenzidine (DAB)–enhanced staining for ferric iron was performed as described.<sup><a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">29</a></sup> After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde-phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and dehydration in methanol, the embryos were incubated in 2.5% potassium ferrocyanide/0.25 M HCl (no. HT20-1KT; Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO) for 30 minutes at room temperature. The embryos were rinsed 3 times in phosphate buffered Tween (PBT), followed by incubation in 0.3% H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> in methanol for 20 minutes at room temperature. The embryos were rinsed twice in PBT and then incubated in DAB substrate for 5 to 7 minutes at room temperature. The DAB substrate was generated by dissolving one tablet of Sigma Fast 3,3′-DAB tetrahydrochloride with cobalt chloride enhancer (no. D-0426, Sigma-Aldrich) in 5 mL of water. This DAB-substrate preparation results in a dark blue or black stain. Photomicrographs of iron-stained embryos were obtained using an SZX51 zoom stereomicroscope (Olympus) at 40× magnification with a DP-71 camera (Olympus).</p></section><section id="sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Iron injection and phenylhydrazine treatment</h3>
<p>Embryos in the iron-injected cohorts were injected with iron dextran 100 mg/mL (Sigma-Aldrich) at 52 hpf, as described.<sup><a href="#B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19">19</a></sup> For induction of hemolysis, embryos were maintained in phenylhydrazine 2 mg/L from 48 to 72 hpf.</p></section><section id="sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Quantitative analysis of gene expression</h3>
<p>At specified time points, embryos were pooled in groups of 18 to 20, anesthetized, and placed in RNAlater (Ambion). RNA extraction, generation of cDNA, and multiplex quantitative PCR assay for <em>hepcidin</em>, <em>ferroportin1</em>, and <em>transferrin-a</em>, normalized to β-<em>actin</em> expression, were performed as previously described.<sup><a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">20</a></sup> Number (N) equals 2 to 4 pools per condition or time point. Detection and analysis were performed on an ABI 7000. Transcript abundance was expressed as a fold increase over calibrator, according to the method as described.<sup><a href="#B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30">30</a></sup> For each experiment, the calibrator pool consisted of sibling controls, at the same developmental stage, which were neither treated with iron, nor heat shock, nor morpholinos. Data presented are the means and standard errors.</p></section><section id="sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Biostatistical analysis</h3>
<p>Biostatistical analysis was performed using the method previously developed for analysis of zebrafish embryonic transcript levels.<sup><a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">20</a></sup> Heterogeneity among cohorts was analyzed by ANOVA using Instat 3.0 (GraphPad Software, LaJolla, CA). Tests for heterogeneity used the natural log for assessment of transcript levels. All estimates and standard errors presented have been converted back to the original units. When the global <em>P</em> value obtained from the ANOVA analysis was statistically significant, pairwise comparisons between the cohorts were performed using a 2-tailed student <em>t</em> test. <em>P</em> values less than or equal to .05 were deemed statistically significant. Multiple sequence alignments were constructed using the ClustalV algorithm with the MegAlign program (DNASTAR, Madison, WI). Residues that matched by 2 distance units in the PAM-250 weight table were considered homologous.</p></section></section><section id="sec10"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Mutations in transferrin-a produce the gavi phenotype</h3>
<p>The zebrafish mutant, <em>gavi</em> (<em>gav</em>), was identified as part of a large-scale screen for mutations resulting in hypochromic anemia. Two alleles were identified, <em>gav</em><sup>HE067</sup> and <em>gav</em><sup>IT029</sup>, both of which exhibit a recessive pattern of inheritance, hypochromic anemia at 48 hpf, as evidenced by <em>o</em>-dianisidine staining for hemoglobin (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>A,B), and lethality by 14 days of development. Using bulk segregant mapping, <em>gav</em><sup>HE067</sup> was localized to chromosome 2. Intermediate resolution mapping with a panel of 88 mutant embryos identified flanking microsatellite markers z9354 (3 recombinants/88) to the north and z26250 and z4300 (3 recombinants/88) to the south. This placed the <em>gav</em> gene near the <em>transferrin-a</em> locus (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>C) that encodes the full-length zebrafish <em>transferrin</em> cDNA, which had been cloned previously and sequenced (GI:62204609).<sup><a href="#B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31">31</a>,<a href="#B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32">32</a></sup> The <em>transferrin-a</em> protein sequence is 49% identical to human <em>transferrin</em>, but 67% identical to carp <em>transferrin</em> (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>D). The <em>gav</em><sup>HE067</sup> and <em>gav</em><sup>IT029</sup> cDNA's for <em>transferrin</em> were sequenced. Each was found to have a single alternative splice form of <em>transferrin-a. Gav</em><sup>HE067</sup> was found to have an A to G mutation (zebrafish assembly version 7 chromosome 2, nucleotide 12509998), creating a new exon donor leading to inclusion of 19 nucleotides from the intron following exon 7 (GenBank, accession number <a href="https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nucleotide/EU360800" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">EU360800</a>). This would result in truncation of the protein at residue 298. On the other hand, <em>gav</em><sup>IT029</sup> was found to have a G to A mutation (zebrafish assembly version 7 chromosome 2, nucleotide 12507046), resulting in a new exon donor and elimination of the last 89 nucleotides of exon 4 (GenBank, accession number <a href="https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nucleotide/EU360801" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">EU360801</a>). This splicing error would lead to truncation of the <em>transferrin-a</em> protein at residue 170 (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>D).</p>

<p>We used antisense morpholinos (MOs) designed to knock down <em>transferrin-a</em> and the transferrin receptors (TfRs) to confirm the requirement of <em>transferrin-a</em> for hemoglobin production. In comparison to uninjected WT embryos (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>A), injection of trf-MO1, directed against the ATG of the <em>transferrin-a</em> gene, resulted in severe anemia at 48 hpf (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>B) in 100% of the embryos injected (n = 177), compared with none in uninjected embryos (n = 3477). Coinjection of trf-MO1 and trf-MO2 produced anemia (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>D) in 96% of the injected embryos (n = 553). To confirm that the morpholinos exert a sequence-specific effect, mismatch control morpholinos for trf-MO1 and trf-MO2 were devised (Table S1), which were identical to the original morpholinos with the exception of 5 mismatched bases. Coinjection of the mismatch control morpholinos (trf-MO1M and trf-MO2M) failed to produce anemia (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>E) in any embryos (n = 213).</p>

<p>To explore the effects of <em>transferrin-a</em> deficiency on transcript levels of genes important in iron metabolism, RNA was obtained from pools of <em>gav</em><sup>IT029</sup> embryos and pools of WT siblings at 72 hpf, after injection with iron dextran to detect iron-induced transcript levels, or without injection to detect “basal” transcript levels. <p>Morpholino knockdown of <em>transferrin-a</em> produced a similar effect on <em>hepcidin</em> expression to that seen in <em>gav</em><sup>IT029</sup> mutants. After coinjection of trf-MO1 and trf-MO2, <em>transferrin-a</em> expression (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>A) was significantly reduced (0.015 ± 0.007 AU vs 0.988 ± 0.012 AU, <em>P</em> = .02), and <em>hepcidin</em> expression (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>B) was also significantly decreased (0.023 ± 0.016 AU vs 0.972 ± 0.028 AU, <em>P</em> = .04), compared with uninjected controls. <em>Hepcidin</em> expression increased in <em>transferrin-a</em> morphants after iron injection, but was not significantly increased compared with morphants that were not injected with iron (1.07 ± 0.53 vs 0.023 ± 0.016, <em>P</em> = .32). In contrast, knockdown of <em>TfR1a</em> (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>E) did not significantly lower the basal level of <em>hepcidin</em> expression (5.12 ± 0.88 AU vs 1.90 ± 0.90 AU, <em>P</em> = .18), although the effect of iron injection on <em>hepcidin</em> expression was blunted in <em>TfR1a</em> knockdown embryos (5.89 ± 1.03 AU vs 5.12 ± 0.88 AU, <em>P</em> = .63), as was seen in the <em>TfR1a</em>-deficient <em>chianti</em> mutant (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a>D). <section id="sec17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Transferrin-a is required for hemoglobin production in the zebrafish</h3>
<p><em>Gavi</em>, the first <em>transferrin-a</em>–deficient mutant characterized in the zebrafish, exhibits severe hypochromic anemia and embryonic lethality, demonstrating that <em>transferrin-a is</em> required for hemoglobin production in the zebrafish. These findings complement studies in the zebrafish mutant <em>chianti</em>, which demonstrated that the erythroid transferrin receptor 1 (<em>TfR1a</em>) is required for hemoglobin production.<sup><a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a></sup> <em>Gavi</em> serves as a zebrafish model for congenital atransferrinemia, a rare condition in humans resulting in severe hypochromic anemia and premature death,<sup><a href="#B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38">38</a>,<a href="#B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39">39</a></sup> in the absence of intermittent plasma infusions, which partially restore serum transferrin levels.<sup><a href="#B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B40">40</a></sup></p>
<p>Both alleles of <em>gav</em> (<em>gav<sup>IT029</sup></em> and <em>gav<sup>HE067</sup></em>) are due to mutations that cause aberrant splicing of the <em>transferrin-a</em> message. This resembles the gene defect in hypotransferrinemic (<em>hpx/hpx</em>) mice, which display low serum transferrin levels due to a mutation that results in aberrant splicing of <em>transferrin</em> mRNA.<sup><a href="#B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41">41</a></sup> In the <em>hpx/hpx</em> mouse, provision of transferrin protein improves the anemia phenotype and reverses the lethality of the phenotype.<sup><a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">42</a></sup> Similarly, in our study, injection of normal <em>transferrin-a</em> cRNA into zebrafish embryos ameliorated anemia.</p></section><section id="sec18"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Transferrin-a is required for normal iron distribution in the zebrafish embryo</h3>
<p>Supporting a role for <em>transferrin-a</em> in iron transport from the yolk sac to the developing embryo, we demonstrated that <em>transferrin-a</em> deficiency resulted in a deficit of stainable iron stores in the somites and terminal gut of the zebrafish embryo, which improved after experimental overexpression of <em>transferrin-a</em> cRNA. Further supporting this hypothesis, <em>transferrin-a</em> (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>A,B) is expressed in the yolk syncytial layer,<sup><a href="#B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32">32</a></sup> as is <em>fpn1</em>, which is also required for transporting iron from the yolk sac to the embryo.<sup><a href="#B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19">19</a></sup> Although histologic iron accumulation has been demonstrated in nonerythropoietic tissues in atransferrinemic patients<sup><a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">42</a>–<a href="#B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44">44</a></sup> and the <em>hpx/hpx</em> mouse at 45 days of age,<sup><a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">42</a></sup> the absence of stainable iron accumulation in the <em>gavi</em> mutant embryos is consistent with the newborn <em>hpx/hpx</em> mouse, in which iron deposits were not stainable.<sup><a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">42</a></sup></p></section><section id="sec19"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Transferrin-a and TfR2 are required for hepcidin expression in zebrafish embryos</h3>
<p>In this report, we provide the first in situ hybridization expression data for <em>hepcidin</em> during embryonic development. We found that strong hepatic expression of <em>transferrin-a</em> and <em>TfR2</em> at 52 hpf preceded strong hepatic expression of <em>hepcidin</em> at 72 hpf and that <em>transferrin-a</em>–deficient mutants and morphants exhibited low levels of <em>hepcidin</em> expression, which remained inappropriately low after iron dextran injection. Taken together, these data demonstrate that <em>transferrin-a</em> is required in the zebrafish embryo for normal <em>hepcidin</em> expression. These findings complement recent work, in animal models and human subjects, supporting the hypothesis that transferrin plays an important role in regulating postnatal <em>hepcidin</em> expression. <em>Hpx/hpx</em> mice exhibit low levels of <em>hepcidin</em> expression,<sup><a href="#B45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45">45</a></sup> while provision of holotransferrin to cultured primary hepatocytes stimulates <em>hepcidin</em> expression.<sup><a href="#B46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B46">46</a></sup> Furthermore, administration of plasma to an atransferrinemic patient resulted in an increase in urinary hepcidin levels.<sup><a href="#B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B40">40</a></sup> Low levels of <em>hepcidin</em> expression in atransferrinemic patients may explain the increased intestinal iron absorption previously observed in these patients.<sup><a href="#B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B43">43</a>,<a href="#B47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B47">47</a></sup></p>
<p>Of the hypochromic anemia mutants that we have studied in the zebrafish (reflecting deficiencies in <em>fpn1</em>,<sup><a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">20</a></sup> <em>TfR1a</em>, <em>DMT1</em>, and <em>transferrin-a</em>), only the <em>transferrin-a</em>–deficient <em>gavi</em> exhibited decreased embryonic <em>hepcidin</em> expression. Furthermore, we demonstrated that elimination of circulating erythrocytes, by treatment with phenylhydrazine or by knocking down <em>GATA1</em>, failed to impair embryonic <em>hepcidin</em> expression. This differs from studies of mouse strains with hypochromic anemia, such as <em>mk/mk</em> and <em>sla/sla</em> mice, which have defects in <em>DMT1</em> and <em>hephaestin</em>, respectively, or phenylhydrazine-treated mice, all of which exhibit low levels of <em>hepcidin</em> expression.<sup><a href="#B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9">9</a>,<a href="#B45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45">45</a></sup> This discrepancy could be due to differences in <em>hepcidin</em> regulation across species or to differences in regulation at different stages of development. The <em>mk/mk</em> and <em>sla/sla</em> mice were evaluated 4 to 10 weeks after birth (Cindy N. Roy, Johns Hopkins University, e-mail, April 29, 2008), while we evaluated zebrafish embryos. Supporting a role for developmental differences in the regulation of <em>hepcidin</em> expression, <em>hepcidin</em> expression was markedly reduced in the livers of severely anemic <em>fpn1</em>-deficient adult zebrafish, but not in <em>fpn1</em>-deficient embryos.<sup><a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">20</a></sup></p>
<p>The expression and function of <em>TfR2</em> have not been evaluated previously in the zebrafish. We demonstrate here that <em>TfR2</em> is coexpressed with <em>transferrin-a</em> in the liver of the zebrafish embryo and that knockdown of <em>TfR2</em> fails to produce anemia or a morphologic defect. Instead, we found that morpholino knockdown of <em>TfR2</em>, but not <em>TfR1a</em> or <em>TfR1b</em>, abrogated <em>hepcidin</em> expression. This is consistent with experimental data in mouse models and human patients, which support a critical role for <em>TfR2</em> in regulating <em>hepcidin</em> expression. Liver-specific knockout of <em>TfR2</em> in mice results in decreased <em>hepcidin</em> expression and increased liver iron stores.<sup><a href="#B48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B48">48</a></sup> In addition, patients who are homozygous for <em>TfR2</em> mutations develop early onset hemochromatosis<sup><a href="#B49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B49">49</a></sup> and exhibit low levels of urinary hepcidin.<sup><a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">13</a></sup></p></section><section id="sec20"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Transferrin-a, TfR1a, TfR2, and DMT1 facilitate increased hepcidin expression in response to iron loading in zebrafish embryos</h3>
<p>Although <em>transferrin-a</em> and <em>TfR2</em> were the only genes identified that were required for basal <em>hepcidin</em> expression in zebrafish embryos, we found that knockdown of <em>transferrin-a</em>, <em>TfR2</em>, <em>TfR1a</em>, or <em>DMT1</em> impaired the enhancement of <em>hepcidin</em> expression in response to iron injection, while deficiency of <em>TfR1b</em> did not. As <em>TfR1a</em> and <em>DMT1</em> are required for erythroid iron acquisition, while <em>TfR1b</em> is not,<sup><a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a></sup> these data imply that erythroid iron acquisition via <em>transferrin-a/TfR1a/DMT1</em> facilitates iron-induced increases in <em>hepcidin</em> expression in zebrafish embryos. In mammalian systems, DMT1 has previously been implicated in the release of iron from the endosome after endocytosis of holotransferrin/transferrin receptor.<sup><a href="#B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2">2</a>,<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a></sup></p>
<p>How transferrin and the transferrin receptors interact to regulate <em>hepcidin</em> expression is not yet understood. Recently, it has been shown, in mammalian cell culture, that TfR2 competes with TfR1 to bind HFE,<sup><a href="#B50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B50">50</a></sup> an atypical MHC class I protein (reviewed in Pietrangelo<sup><a href="#B51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B51">51</a></sup>), and that holotransferrin competes with HFE to bind TfR1.<sup><a href="#B52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B52">52</a></sup> Genomic analysis has failed to identify a close ortholog for human HFE in the zebrafish<sup><a href="#B53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B53">53</a></sup> or in the other fish species for which sequence data are available (P.F. and Y.G., unpublished data, April 14, 2008). We have identified 9 orthologs of MHC class I in the zebrafish with low sequence homology to human HFE (25.9%-31.6% sequence identity). It remains to be determined if any of these zebrafish MHC class I orthologs interacts with <em>TfR2</em>. While many aspects of iron homeostasis appear to be conserved in the zebrafish, further dissection of the pathways involved in regulating iron metabolism may reveal important differences, which may provide insight into the evolution of iron homeostasis.</p></section></section><section id="sec21"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
