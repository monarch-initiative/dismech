# Heterozygous Predicted Loss-of-function Variants of TRAF3 in Patients with Common Variable Immunodeficiency.
**Authors:** Urban B, Batlle-Masó L, Perurena-Prieto J, Garcia-Prat M, Parra-Martínez A, Aguiló-Cucurull A, Martinez-Gallo M, Moushib L, Antolín M, Rivière JG, Soler-Palacin P, Dieli-Crimi R, Franco-Jarava C, Colobran R
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-024-01833-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-024-01833-3)

## Content

TRAF3, a versatile adaptor protein within the TRAF family, participates in various signaling pathways involving the tumor necrosis factor receptor, toll-like receptor, and retinoic acid-inducible gene I-like receptor families. In 2010, autosomal dominant TRAF3 deficiency was reported in a patient with herpes simplex virus-1 encephalitis, consistent with the role of TRAF3 in type I interferon production. Recently, a novel, completely different clinical phenotype was described in patients with TRAF3 haploinsufficiency (TRAF3Hl), characterized by recurrent bacterial infections, autoimmune features, systemic inflammation, and hypergammaglobulinemia. In this study, we conducted a TRAF3-targeted reanalysis of next-generation sequencing data from 800 patients with inborn errors of immunity. Through this reassessment and additional familial investigations, we identified three previously unidentified cases of TRAF3Hl within two different families. These individuals harbored stop-gain variants (p.Arg163* and p.Gln407*) and experienced recurrent bacterial infections with hypogammaglobulinemia. Previously, the patients had been diagnosed with common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) and were receiving immunoglobulin replacement therapy. In addition, a TRAF3 start-loss variant (c.3G > A) was identified in a fourth patient, but after familial and molecular studies, it was not considered disease-causing, excluding TRAF3Hl in this patient. This study illustrates the usefulness of targeted reanalysis of genes with reported novel phenotypes. We rescued three patients with TRAF3Hl, presenting similarities and differences with the previously reported patients. The most significant differences were hypogammaglobulinemia and a CVID-like presentation. These data expand the clinical phenotype of TRAF3Hl and pave the way for further investigation into loss-of-function variants in patients with CVID.

Abstract
TRAF3, a versatile adaptor protein within the TRAF family, participates in various signaling pathways involving the tumor 
necrosis factor receptor, toll-like receptor, and retinoic acid-inducible gene I-like receptor families. In 2010, autosomal 
dominant TRAF3 deficiency was reported in a patient with herpes simplex virus-1 encephalitis, consistent with the role 
of TRAF3 in type I interferon production. Recently, a novel, completely different clinical phenotype was described in 
patients with TRAF3 haploinsufficiency (TRAF3 Hl), characterized by recurrent bacterial infections, autoimmune features, 
systemic inflammation, and hypergammaglobulinemia. In this study, we conducted a TRAF3-targeted reanalysis of next-
generation sequencing data from 800 patients with inborn errors of immunity. Through this reassessment and additional 
familial investigations, we identified three previously unidentified cases of TRAF3Hl within two different families. These 
individuals harbored stop-gain variants (p.Arg163* and p.Gln407*) and experienced recurrent bacterial infections with 
hypogammaglobulinemia. Previously, the patients had been diagnosed with common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) 
and were receiving immunoglobulin replacement therapy. In addition, a TRAF3 start-loss variant (c.3G > A) was identified 
in a fourth patient, but after familial and molecular studies, it was not considered disease-causing, excluding TRAF3Hl 
in this patient. This study illustrates the usefulness of targeted reanalysis of genes with reported novel phenotypes. We 
rescued three patients with TRAF3Hl, presenting similarities and differences with the previously reported patients. The 
most significant differences were hypogammaglobulinemia and a CVID-like presentation. These data expand the clinical 
phenotype of TRAF3Hl and pave the way for further investigation into loss-of-function variants in patients with CVID.

response by promoting the production of type I interferons

In 2010, the importance of TRAF3 in type I IFN generation

c.352C>T/p.Arg118Trp, was associated with impaired, but 
not abolished, type I IFN production [3].
In the adaptive immune response, TRAF3 plays a vital 
role in regulating B and T cell functions by modulating 
recruitment of negative regulators. In T cells, it oversees 
signal transduction by antigen, costimulatory, and cytokine 
receptors [4]. In B cells, TRAF3 acts as a critical regula -
tor of mature B cell survival, B cell activation, and plasma 
cell differentiation and maturation [ 5]. Consistent with the 
importance of TRAF3 in B cell biology, somatic loss-of-
function (LOF) mutations in TRAF3 are regularly docu -
mented in a wide variety of B cell malignancies [6, 7].
In 2022, Rae et al. provided the first report describing 
the clinical repercussions of TRAF3 haploinsufficiency 
(TRAF3Hl) [8]. The study included 9 patients from 5 unre -
lated families, all harboring heterozygous LOF variants 
in TRAF3. These individuals exhibited recurrent bacterial 
infections, autoimmune features, and systemic inflamma -
tion [8].
In this research, we identified 3 new patients with 
TRAF3Hl through targeted reanalysis of NGS data from 800 
patients with suspected inborn errors of immunity (IEI). The 
patients carried stop-gain variants in the TRAF3 gene and 
presented with hypogammaglobulinemia and a common 
variable immunodeficiency (CVID) phenotype. While there 
are similarities with the previously reported cases, the find-
ings in these patients expand the spectrum of the TRAF3 Hl 
clinical manifestations to include CVID as an additional 
feature of this novel IEI.
Methods
Patients and Samples
The patients included in this study (designated P1, P2, P3, 
and P4) were followed at the Vall d’Hebron Barcelona Hos-
pital Campus (Barcelona, Catalonia, Spain). Previously, P1, 
P2, and P3 had been diagnosed with CVID according to the 
European Society for Immunodeficiencies (ESID) criteria 
[9]. Written informed consent for the samples obtained and 
analyses reported here, and for publication of the article, 
was obtained from each patient, according to the procedures 
of the local Ethics Committee [code: PR(AG)202/2021].
Reanalysis of Genetic Data
In this retrospective study, we reanalyzed genetic data 
acquired by next-generation sequencing (NGS), specifi -
cally focusing on identification of rare variants in TRAF3. 
The sample included 720 patients who underwent genetic 
analysis using our IEI-targeted gene panel. Among these, 
460 patients had been assessed with the initial version of 
the panel, encompassing 323 genes, and 260 patients with 
the second version, covering 425 genes (both versions 
incorporated the TRAF3 gene). Furthermore, data from 80 
patients who underwent whole-exome sequencing were also 
included (supplementary methods).
Sanger Sequencing
Sanger sequencing served as the standard technique for 
family segregation studies (all families), and for assessing 
the consequences of allelic expression in complementary 
DNA (cDNA) (supplementary methods).
Deep Amplicon Sequencing
Deep amplicon sequencing experiments were conducted to 
precisely quantify the contribution of each of the two alleles 
in cDNA from P1, P2 and P3, and to confirm and quantify 
the splicing defect in P4 (supplementary methods).
TRAF3 Western Blot
Western blotting (WB) of TRAF3 was used to assess the 
presence of wild-type TRAF3 protein and possible truncated 
forms in P1, P3, and P4. WB was performed using total pro-
tein extracts from PBMCs (supplementary methods).
Immunophenotyping
In all patients, deep immunophenotyping was performed 
by flow cytometry to identify and quantify the various 
immune cell populations and subpopulations (supplemen -
tary methods).

Results
Genetic Reanalysis of our Cohort of IEI Patients 
Revealed Deleterious Variants in TRAF3
The recent description of TRAF3Hl in humans prompted us 
to reassess the genetic data from our cohort of patients with 
clinically suspected IEI, seeking rare variants in the TRAF3 
gene. Our database contained standardized NGS data from 
IEI-targeted gene panels ( n = 720 patients) and whole-
exome sequencing ( n = 80 patients). Annotated files were 
filtered to prioritize TRAF3 variants meeting the follow -
ing criteria: (1) located within an exonic or splicing region; 
(2) rarity in healthy reference populations (minor allele 
frequency < 0.01); and (3) predicted loss of function or 
missense effect on the protein. We identified 12 rare hetero-
zygous variants in TRAF3: 2 stop-gain variants, 1 start-loss 
variant, and 9 missense variants (Table 1). One of the stop-
gain variants, c.487C >T/p.Arg163*, had been described 
previously in a patient in the original TRAF3Hl study [ 8]. 
The other stop-gain variant, c.1219C >T/p.Gln407*, was 
a novel finding located in the TRAF-C domain (Fig. 1A). 
The start-loss variant, c.3G>A/p.Met1?, was identified in a 
patient who also harbored a missense variant in the adjacent 
residue (c.6G>T/p.Glu2Asp). The two variants were in close 
proximity, confirmed by NGS data to be in the same allele 
(in cis). The other 8 variants were low-frequency missense 
variants. Most were identified in a single patient (includ -
ing the p.Arg118Trp variant, previously associated with a 
single case of HSE), although two were present in three and 
four patients, respectively (Table 1). In the remainder of the 
study, we focused on clinical and molecular characterization 
of the patients carrying the three potentially deleterious vari-
ants in TRAF3 (two stop-gain and one start-loss variant). In 
these patients, no other relevant variants in IEI genes were 
identified (see gene list of IEI-targeted gene panel version 2 
in supplementary methods).

Impaired B Cell Maturation and Low naïve T Cell 
Levels in Patients with TRAF3 LOF Variants
The three patients (P1-P3) with TRAF3 stop-gain LOF vari-
ants exhibited hypogammaglobulinemia and started IGRT. 
Although T, B, and NK cell percentages and absolute values 
were generally within normal ranges, CD4 cell counts in all 
three patients were at the lower end or even below refer -
ence values over follow up (Figure S2), resulting in a low 
CD4/CD8 ratio (Table 2). Extensive immunophenotyping 
showed impaired B cell maturation with markedly reduced 
class-switched memory B cell counts and elevated naïve B 
cells. The CD21low B cell subpopulation, which is typically 
increased in autoimmune conditions, was not elevated in our 
patients. T cell analysis demonstrated significant abnormali-
ties, with a notable decrease in naïve CD4 and CD8 T cell 
subpopulations in all three patients. In addition, there was 
an increased proportion of CD4 central memory cells with 
skewed differentiation to the Th1 subset, while CD8 cells 
predominantly exhibited a TEMRA phenotype. Accord -
ingly, CD4 and CD8 cells expressed HLA-DR at high lev -
els in all patients. P1 and P2 had a higher proportion of T 
regulatory cells compared to P3, whose levels were within 
the normal range (Table 2). On in vitro stimulation, lym -
phocyte proliferation in P2 was markedly impaired, with 
significantly decreased cell division after PMA + ionomycin 
and PHA stimulation, and a less pronounced reduction after 
anti-CD3/CD28 + IL-2. P3 also showed a somewhat altered 
proliferation capacity, but it was less evident and stimulus-
dependent (Figure S3). P4 presented essentially normal cell 
populations with only minor reductions in the percentages 
of pre-switched and switched memory B cells.

TRAF3Hl. The selection of patients in the two studies could 
also have had an influence on the phenotypes observed. We 
took an unbiased approach, conducting TRAF3-targeted 
genetic reanalysis across our entire cohort of IEI patients 
(n = 800), without prior phenotype-based selection. The two 
patients initially identified with LOF variants in TRAF3 (P1 
and P3) had both been previously diagnosed with CVID. 
Despite undergoing genetic evaluation in our center using a 
custom NGS IEI panel, their results had been inconclusive. 
Of particular note, the TRAF3 variants had been detected 
in these patients, but they were categorized as variants of 
uncertain significance (VUS) in the genetic report. This 
classification resulted from the association of TRAF3 at that 
time with HSE, a condition unrelated to the patients’ clinical 
phenotype [3].
Reanalysis of our data led us to rescue these genetically 
undiagnosed patients and identify an additional patient 
(P2), through familial studies. This outcome underscores 
the importance of periodic reassessment of genetic data, 
particularly in the light of emerging phenotypes. Typi -
cally, this reassessment is based on comprehensive genomic 
data obtained through whole-exome or whole-genome 
sequencing. In our case, the description of a novel pheno -
type (TRAF3Hl) caused by a gene previously described as 
causing another IEI (HSE) facilitated reanalysis of data not 
only from whole-exome sequencing but also from our gene 
panel, as TRAF3 was included in the panel because of its 
previous association with HSE.
P4 is a challenging case, with two variants positioned 
very close together in the same allele. One variant (c.3G>A) 
disrupts the start codon and creates a novel acceptor splice 
site, leading to a partial splicing defect. Although it is tempt-
ing to assume that start-loss variants are always deleterious, 
their effects actually vary from fully pathogenic to likely 
benign, depending on several factors [ 11]. Evaluating the 
clinical implications of these variants is complex due to cel-
lular mechanisms that can diminish their impact, such as 
use of non-canonical (non-AUG) translation start sites or 
alternative in-frame AUG codons close to the original one 
[12]. In the case of TRAF3, an AUG codon at the seventh 
indicated a C/T allele ratio of 42:58 (Table S1), suggesting 
that the mutated RNA was not degraded and could poten -
tially produce a truncated TRAF3 protein consisting of 406 
aa. The WB findings in P3 showed a band corresponding to 
wt TRAF3 (64 kDa) and a thicker band of lower molecular 
weight, absent in healthy donors and in the other patients. 
This additional band could correspond to the truncated form 
of TRAF3 (1-406), predicted to have a molecular weight of 
46 kDa (Fig. 1I).
P4 carried the heterozygous c.3G >A/p.Met1? and 
c.6G>T/p.Glu2Asp variants on the same chromosome. 
Both variants are located in exon 3, which is the first cod -
ing exon in TRAF3 (exons 1 and 2 are non-coding). The 
first variant disrupted the initiation codon and, therefore, 
the second variant would no longer be a missense mutation. 
On examination of the TRAF3 wild-type cDNA sequence, 
we found an ATG codon at the seventh residue, potentially 
serving as an alternative initiation codon downstream of the 
original one (Figure S4). Analysis of cDNA by Sanger and 
DAS showed an equal presence of the two alleles, at a ratio 
of 53:47, ruling out possible degradation of the allele car -
rying the start-loss variant (Fig. 1G, Table S1). In addition, 
cDNA analysis showed an unexpected double-peak pattern 
immediately following the c.3G >A variant. This double-
peak pattern, absent in control cDNA, corresponded to the 
non-coding exon 2. The explanation lies in the creation of 
a novel acceptor splice site by the c.3G >A variant, which 
is selected at low frequency and leads to a partial splic -
ing defect (Figure S4). Interestingly, this aberrant splicing 
event eventually enabled the use of methionine at position 
7 as an alternative initiation codon. WB of P4 depicted a 
single band aligning with wt TRAF3 (Fig. 1J). However, it 
is uncertain whether this band exclusively consisted of wt 
TRAF3 or also included TRAF3 generated via the alterna -
tive initiation codon, which would be only 6 aa shorter at 
the N-terminus and indistinguishable from the wt on WB 
analysis.
Discussion
In a recent study, Rae et al. described TRAF3Hl as an immune 
dysregulation syndrome associated with recurrent bacterial 
infections, autoimmune features, and systemic inflamma -
tion. All patients examined harbored heterozygous LOF 
variants in the TRAF3 gene and had predominant clinical 
findings of hypergammaglobulinemia, lymphadenopathy, 
splenomegaly, and autoantibodies [ 8]. Here, we describe 
three additional patients (P1-P3) with TRAF3Hl caused by 
stop-gain variants. Our patients experienced recurrent sino-
pulmonary infections, lymphoproliferation with spleno -
megaly and lymphadenopathy and multiple gastrointestinal

would generate a TRAF3 protein 6 aa shorter than wild-type 
at the N-terminus (TRAF37–568). However, we were unable 
to determine whether this translation reinitiation did indeed 
occur and if the resultant TRAF3 7–568 isoform retained full 
functionality. Moreover, the partial splicing defect gener -
ated by the c.3G >A variant may not necessarily abrogate 
translation reinitiation and production of the TRAF3 7–568 
isoform. In summary, the current evidence does not suf -
fice to classify the c.3G>A variant as pathogenic or benign, 
maintaining its status as a VUS. The patient’s clinical phe -
notype is restricted to respiratory infections without hypo -
gammaglobulinemia or significant B cell abnormalities, 
although he is only 7 years old and his situation may change 
in the future. At present, the diagnosis of TRAF3Hl cannot 
be established. Notably, the fact that P4 inherited the vari -
ants from his unaffected mother argues against their patho -
genicity. In addition, the complete penetrance of TRAF3Hl 
(100%) reported to date in the 7 families with TRAF3 LOF 
variants (5 families reported by Rae et al. and 2 families in 
this study) supports this notion.
Our reanalysis also identified 13 patients carrying 8 rare 
heterozygous missense variants in the TRAF3 gene. How -
ever, whether any of these variants are deleterious remains 
to be demonstrated. Interestingly, one patient harbored the 
p.Arg118Trp variant, previously associated with a single 
case of HSE [ 3]. Due to its relatively high population fre -
quency (0.002 in gnomAD, with > 3300 heterozygous and 
8 homozygous individuals reported) this variant is currently 
viewed more as a risk factor than a highly penetrant cause of 
HSE. In vitro studies have demonstrated that p.Arg118Trp 
leads to a reduction in TRAF3 expression, while healthy 
individuals heterozygous for the variant show reductions in 
the proportion of marginal zone-like B cells [8]. Of note, the 
patient carrying the p.Arg118Trp variant had CVID, with 
hypogammaglobulinemia (under IGRT) and recurrent respi-
ratory tract infections. Although it seems clear that TRAF3 
p.Arg118Trp is not a disease-causing variant, its potential 
role as a contributory factor for CVID in a polygenic model 
cannot be excluded. The remaining patients with TRAF3 
heterozygous missense variants exhibit a broad spectrum of 
phenotypes. Further investigation is needed to understand 
the contribution of the variants to these manifestations.

TRAF3 mRNA and protein for each mutation. Even in the 
case of protein, they observed > 50% reduction in the four 
patients tested, and suggested that in TRAF3Hl an increased 
ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of the wild-
type protein may occur [8]. We have not been able to reliably 
quantify the TRAF3 protein in our patients, but we found

(p.Arg163* and p.Gln407*) indeed resulted in TRAF3 func-

has been already described as LOF and causing TRAF3Hl

demonstrated to be LOF by Rae et al. [8].
TRAF3 is a constitutive negative regulator of the alter -
native NF-κB2 pathway. TRAF3Hl results in heightened 
activity of this pathway, leading to B cell hyperactivity, 
increased inflammation, and enhanced metabolism [ 8]. 
This fits with the findings of hypergammaglobulinemia and 
autoantibody-associated diseases in most patients reported 
by Rae et al. However, the paradoxical development of 
hypogammaglobulinemia in some individuals with the same 
molecular defect ( TRAF3Hl) suggests another aspect of B 
cell dysregulation. For example, patients P1 and P2 in our 
study carried the same variant (p.Arg163*) as patient D1 in 
Rae et al., who exhibited elevated levels of IgG, IgA, IgM, 
and IgE. Conversely, one patient in Rae’s study also pre -
sented hypogammaglobulinemia requiring IGRT [8]. Future 
characterization of additional patients could help clarify 
whether TRAF3Hl predominantly manifests as a CVID phe-
notype or as an immune dysregulation syndrome. In sum -
mary, the findings in this study expand the clinical spectrum 
of TRAF3 HI, potentially facilitating the identification of 
similar cases by reexamining CVID patients who have not 
undergone screening for TRAF3 defects.
