</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="s1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Context</h3>
<p>Acquired partial lipodystrophy (APL) is an ultra-rare disorder characterized by unique loss of subcutaneous fat affecting mostly the face, neck, trunk, and upper extremities. The precise prevalence of metabolic derangements and other comorbidities among patients with APL is not clear.</p></section><section id="s2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h3>
<p>We report clinical features and metabolic and autoimmune derangements in a large cohort.</p></section><section id="s3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>A total of 86 participants (77 female, 9 male; median age 40 years [range 8-78 years]) with APL from 2 US tertiary referral centers, UT Southwestern and National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases, were recruited into prospective observational studies. Demographic, health history, and laboratory data at the initial evaluation and follow-up were systematically collected and analyzed.</p></section><section id="s4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>The median age of onset of lipodystrophy was 7 years (range, 2-51 years). About 15% had autoimmune diseases, 38% had either diabetes mellitus or glucose intolerance, 43% had hypertriglyceridemia, and 61% had fatty liver or metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH). A total of 71% of patients had low serum complement 3 (C3) levels, 8% had membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, while drusen occurred in 62% of those with fundus examination (n = 21). Patients with C3 hypocomplementemia, compared to those with normal serum C3 level, reported earlier onset of diabetes or glucose intolerance (median age 36 vs 56.5 years, <em>P</em> = .007), hypertriglyceridemia (30 vs 48 years, <em>P</em> = .03), and drusen (33 vs 60 years, <em>P</em> = .14).</p></section><section id="s5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p>Our data reveal high risk of metabolic comorbidities and drusen in patients with APL, with earlier onset of these complications in those with C3 hypocomplementemia.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> acquired partial lipodystrophy, Barraquer–Simons syndrome, complement 3, hypocomplementemia, drusen retinopathy, diabetes mellitus, hypertriglyceridemia</p></section></section><hr class="headless">
<p>Acquired partial lipodystrophy (APL), also known as Barraquer–Simons syndrome, is an ultra-rare disorder, first described in 1885 (<a href="#dgaf315-B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B1">1</a>). Patients affected with APL generally present with a specific progression of bilaterally symmetrical subcutaneous fat loss in the cephalo-caudal fashion, starting from the face and neck and then affecting mostly the truncal region and upper extremities, but sparing the hips and lower extremities (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B3">3</a>). In fact, there is usually excess fat accumulation in the lower parts of the body (lower abdomen, hips, gluteal regions, and legs), especially during adulthood. A sharp demarcation line can occasionally be identified between the lipodystrophic and non-lipodystrophic areas (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>).</p>
<p>APL is reported to be more common in females (F) than in males (M) (F:M; ranging from 8:1 to 4:1) (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B4">4</a>). Fat loss usually occurs around late childhood to adolescence, although later onset in the fourth to fifth decades has also been reported (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>). The alteration of the alternative complement pathway by the autoimmune process, as suggested by the high prevalence of low serum levels of complement 3 (C3), is claimed to be a part of the causative pathophysiology of APL (<a href="#dgaf315-B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B4">4</a>). Extra-adipose tissue autoimmune manifestations, such as membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), drusen retinal macular degeneration (the extracellular material accumulation beneath the retinal pigment epithelium associated with a normal aging process of the retina which could be accelerated by environmental and genetic factors) (<a href="#dgaf315-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B5">5</a>), and other autoimmune diseases, are also documented in APL (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B6">6</a>).</p>
<p>About 2 decades ago, we reported 35 patients with APL and reviewed the literature about associated comorbidities (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B3">3</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B7">7</a>). We concluded that, in contrast to the other 3 major types of lipodystrophies—acquired generalized lipodystrophy (AGL), congenital generalized lipodystrophy, and familial partial lipodystrophy—metabolic abnormalities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertriglyceridemia, and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), were uncommon in APL (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>). However, due to its rarity, there is a paucity of reports of APL’s presenting clinical features, associated autoimmune diseases, metabolic abnormalities, and overall follow-up outcomes (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8-10</a>). Therefore, now we aim to describe clinical features, biochemical abnormalities, metabolic derangements, related autoimmune diseases, and long-term follow-up outcomes in a large cohort of patients with APL seen by us at UT Southwestern Medical Center (UTSW) and National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK).</p>
<section id="dgaf315-s1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="dgaf315-s1.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Patients</h3>
<p>The study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of UTSW, Dallas, TX and NIDDK, Bethesda, MD. Participants in this study were diagnosed with APL and were seen by the physicians at UTSW or NIDDK. Written informed consents were obtained from the participants or their legal representatives if they were under 18 years. Participants' data were collected by medical record reviews, phone interviews, physical examination, and either web-based questionnaires or questionnaires sent via postal mail (Supplementary Methods S1) (<a href="#dgaf315-B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B11">11</a>).</p>
<p>The diagnosis of APL was based on the accepted criteria mentioned in the multi-society practice guideline (<a href="#dgaf315-B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B12">12</a>) and previous literature (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B6">6</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B13">13</a>). The essential criterion was documentation of cephalocaudal, bilaterally symmetrical subcutaneous fat loss starting from the face and neck and variably progressing to the upper body and upper extremities while sparing the lower body. The supportive criteria consist of clinical features and laboratory data, including the onset of fat loss during childhood or adolescence, absence of familial lipodystrophy, presence of autoimmune diseases, low serum C3 level, presence of C3 nephritic factor (C3NeF) autoantibody, proteinuria, and MPGN on renal biopsy.</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s1.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Questionnaire</h3>
<p>We collected demographic data and health care history from patient-completed lipodystrophy questionnaires (Supplementary Methods S1) (<a href="#dgaf315-B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B11">11</a>) and interviews. The presence of diabetes mellitus, hypertriglyceridemia, fatty liver, and polycystic ovarian syndrome, hypertension, coronary heart disease, stroke, cancer, autoimmune diseases, and psychiatric disorders were self-reported by the participants and/or documented in laboratory or imaging studies.</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s1.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Blood Tests for Biochemical Parameters</h3>
<p>Overnight fasting blood samples were collected and analyzed for laboratory variables. Blood samples of the patients evaluated at UTSW were sent to Quest Diagnostics (Irving, TX) for analysis. Serum glucose, lipids, creatinine, and liver enzymes of UTSW participants were measured by spectrometry method (Beckman Coulter AU clinical analyzer) and blood glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured by the enzymatic method (Roche Integra 800 chemistry analyzer). Serum complement C3 and C4 were measured by immunoturbidimetric method. Serum C3NeF was measured by immunofixation electrophoresis. Serum leptin was measured by electrochemiluminescence. Serum glucose, lipids, renal and hepatic function tests, and blood HbA1c for the NIDDK samples were measured at the National Institutes of Health Clinical Center Laboratory using the standard methodology.</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s1.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Definitions and Diagnosis of Metabolic Disorders</h3>
<p>Diagnosis of diabetes mellitus was based on self-reported history, previous medical records, and laboratory findings, according to the American Diabetes Association guideline (<a href="#dgaf315-B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B14">14</a>): fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, blood HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, 2-hour postprandial plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL after oral glucose tolerance test, and random plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia. Impaired fasting glucose (IFG) was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL, but &lt; 126 mg/dL, and impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) was defined as 2-hour plasma glucose after oral glucose tolerance test ≥ 140 mg/dL but &lt; 200 mg/dL, or blood HbA1c ≥ 5.7%, but &lt;6.5%. The diagnosis of dysglycemia was made if the participant had either diabetes mellitus or IFG/IGT. Hypertriglyceridemia was defined as fasting serum triglyceride concentration ≥ 150 mg/dL, or if the participant was on triglyceride-lowering therapy (fibric acid derivatives and/or high-dose omega-3 fatty acid derivatives containing eicosapentaenoic or docosahexaenoic acid), or self-reported. Low concentration of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was defined as less than 40 mg/dL and 50 mg/dL in males and females, respectively (<a href="#dgaf315-B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B15">15</a>). MASH was defined based on the elevation of liver enzymes (aspartate transaminase and/or alanine transaminase) with either pathologic or radiologic findings. Drusen retinopathy was diagnosed by an ophthalmologist by retinal examination. Serum C3 and C4 levels were defined as low when less than lower limits of normal.</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s1.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Anthropometric Measurements</h3>
<p>Height and body weight were measured with standard procedures. Skinfold thickness was measured at the following 12 sites; chin, chest, midaxillary, abdominal, subscapular, suprailiac, biceps, triceps, forearm, hip, thigh, and calf with Lange caliper (Cambridge Scientific Industries, Cambridge, MD) on the right side of the body as reported previously (<a href="#dgaf315-B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B16">16</a>). The mean of 3 repeat measurements at each site was calculated.</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s1.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical Analysis</h3>
<p>Descriptive statistical methods were used to analyze all parameters. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percent). Continuous variables are presented as median (minimum and maximum values) or mean ± SD depending on distribution. The distribution of the continuous variables was assessed by the skewness and kurtosis normality tests. Differences in categorical variables between the 2 subgroups were analyzed using Chi-square or Fisher's exact test (when the number of observations were fewer than 5). Differences in continuous variables between the 2 subgroups were analyzed using independent <em>t</em> test or Mann–Whitney <em>U</em> test, where appropriate.</p>
<p>All analyses were performed using SPSS software version 26.0 (SPSS, Inc.) and GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad software, Inc.). A 2-tailed <em>P</em> &lt; .05 was the criterion for statistical significance.</p></section></section><section id="dgaf315-s2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="dgaf315-s2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study Population, Demographic Data, and Clinical Features</h3>
<p>A total of 86 participants with APL were included in this study; 36 people were seen in person (41.9%) while the others were seen virtually, with remote laboratory investigations. Of the participants, 77 (89.5%) were female and 9 (10.5%) were male; the approximate female to male ratio was 8:1. Most subjects were White (93.1%), and the others were Asian or other race; none were African Americans. The median age at presentation to us was 32 years (range, 7-70 years). In our series, the median age at the onset of lipodystrophy was 7 years (range, 2-51 years). The onset of fat loss usually occurred during childhood to early adolescence; however, 2 patients were diagnosed in the fourth to sixth decades; with the maximum age of onset of 51 years after being treated for dermatomyositis for 7 years, and the other after being diagnosed with sicca syndrome (<a href="#dgaf315-T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). Forty-three (50%) participants had at least one follow-up visit and/or updated their health status with us. The median follow-up duration was 11 years (range, 2-29 years). Six participants (6.9%) died at the median age of 61 years (range, 50-72 years).</p>

<p>We reviewed all self-reported preceding illnesses within a year before the onset of fat loss. Most patients or their parents could not identify any specified illness (61%). Viral/bacterial infection was the most frequently identified antecedent illness (20 out of 86 [23%]), of which ear infection (3 patients) and chicken pox (3 patients) were reported in approximately one-third. Other infections (measles (1 patient), mumps (2 patients), gastroenteritis (2 patients), pharyngitis (1 patient), and bronchitis (2 patients)) were less commonly reported. The severities of infections varied from mild to severe, requiring hospital admission. Other less frequent antecedent events were autoimmune diseases, fever of unknown cause, and miscellaneous events (including sunburn, breast implantation, appendectomy, blood loss from accident, and pregnancy).</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Body Fat Distribution</h3>
<p>Clinical features and pattern of fat loss of a few APL patients are shown in <a href="#dgaf315-F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>. The skinfold measurement data were available in 27 out of 86. Fifteen patients were adults (14 were female, 93%) and the other 12 were children (prepubertal) of whom 9 patients were female (75%). The upper body skinfolds (at least one site of axillary, chest, subscapular, and triceps) of most patients (23 out of 27 [85.2%]) were less than the 10th percentile for healthy subjects (<a href="#dgaf315-B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B17">17-19</a>), while 40.7% (11 out of 27) showed fat accumulation at the lower body (skinfold values &gt; 90th percentile at least one site of abdominal, suprailiac, and thigh) (<a href="#dgaf315-F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>). Unfortunately, normal data for chin, biceps, and forearm are not available for comparison. We also observed the heterogeneity of the skinfold thickness values measured among male and female adults and children. The severity and extension of fat loss varied slightly individually. We report that 22% of our series (17 out of 79) experienced at least one reconstructive procedure to mitigate the fat loss in their lifetime.</p>

<p>A total of 15 participants had diabetes mellitus (18%), with the median age at diagnosis of 36 years (range, 11-68 years). The earliest onset of diabetes, at 11 years old, occurred after corticosteroid treatment for immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) (APL4200.3). Seventeen (20.5%) patients had IFG/IGT without diabetes, with a median age of 44 years (range, 9-67 years). The overall prevalence of dysglycemia, including both diabetes mellitus and IFG/IGT in our series, was 38.5%.</p>
<p>Forty-three percent (35 of 81) had hypertriglyceridemia with median age of onset of 32.5 years (range, 10-68 years). Furthermore, 2 out of the 4 people with hypertriglyceridemia at age 10 had concomitant autoimmune diseases requiring corticosteroid therapy (APL109.4 and APL1200.3). Only eicosapentaenoic acid was used as triglyceride-lowering therapy. About 60% of patients had low levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).</p>
<p>Hepatomegaly was reported in 20.3% and elevation of aspartate transaminase (AST) or alanine transaminase (ALT), at least once during follow-up, was noted in 25.9% of patients. Most participants (61%) who underwent liver imaging and/or biopsy had fatty liver or MASH at the median age of 33 years (range, 9-66 years) (<a href="#dgaf315-T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>).</p>
<p>Hypertension was reported in one-third (33%), and the median age at onset was 35 years (range, 7-72 years). The 7-year-old patient with hypertension developed glomerulonephritis 3 years before (APL109.4). During the follow-up, 5 female participants and 1 male participant, median age 50 years (45-57 years), developed coronary heart disease, with confirmation by coronary angiogram in 4 or them (3 females and 1 male).</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s2.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Association With Autoimmune Diseases</h3>
<p>Thirteen participants from our cohort (15%) had systemic autoimmune diseases. The 2 most common were dermatomyositis (including juvenile dermatomyositis) (4 patients, 30.8%) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (3 patients, 23.1%). Others included rheumatoid arthritis (RA), mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD), idiopathic (immune) thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), autoimmune hepatitis, and sicca syndrome. The median age of diagnosis of autoimmune diseases was 19.5 years (range 1.5-58 years). Most autoimmune diseases (SLE, RA, MCTD, ITP, and autoimmune hepatitis) occurred after fat loss with a median time from fat loss to autoimmune disease of 15 years (range, 4-54 years). By contrast, 3 out of 4 patients with dermatomyositis were diagnosed prior to onset of lipodystrophy by 1.5 to 7 years, but in the fourth patient, lipodystrophy was antedated by a year. One-quarter of patients had autoimmune thyroid disorders (Graves' disease or Hashimoto thyroiditis); these were considered separately from other autoimmune conditions due to their high prevalence even in the general population. Among patients who were tested for serum antinuclear antibodies (ANA), 41% had positive results.</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s2.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Membranoproliferative Glomerulonephritis and Drusen Retinopathy</h3>
<p>Seven of our patients (8%) had MPGN and all but one had low serum C3 levels. C3NeF was positive in both the patients with MPGN in whom it was checked. The median onset of MPGN was 10 years after onset of lipodystrophy (range, 0-29 years). Renal biopsy reports were available in 6 participants, with dense-deposit disease in 3 patients (APL1300.3, APL5900.3, and APL9400.3), and C3 glomerulonephritis (C3GN) (APL105.3), lupus nephritis (APL4700.3), and postinfectious glomerulonephritis (APL6800.3) in 1 patient each. Interestingly, we found drusen retinopathy prevalence as high as 62% among those who underwent retinal examination (13 out of 21 patients) at the median age of 42.5 years. Seventy percent of these patients developed drusen before the age of 50 years.</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s2.7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Serum Complement 3 and Complement 3 Nephritic Factor</h3>
<p>In our series, serum C3 levels were available in two-thirds of patients (56 out of 86). We compared clinical features and laboratory parameters of patients with hypocomplementemia on any measurement (71.4%) to participants with normocomplementemia at all measurements (28.6%), as shown in <a href="#dgaf315-T2" class="usa-link">Tables 2</a> and <a href="#dgaf315-T3" class="usa-link">3</a>. There were no sex or racial differences. Although the median ages of onset of fat loss were not different in the 2 groups (7 years in low C3 and 7.5 years in normal C3, <em>P</em> = .86), the median age of official lipodystrophy diagnosis was significantly earlier in patients with hypocomplementemia than in those with normocomplementemia (12.6 years and 32.5 years, respectively; <em>P</em> = .03), which may signify more severe phenotype in those with C3 hypocomplementemia leading to early diagnosis. The prevalence of diabetes mellitus, IFG/IGT, and hypertriglyceridemia, was comparable between the 2 groups. However, patients with hypocomplementemia, compared to those with normocomplementemia, had earlier onset of dysglycemia (median age 36 years and 56.5 years, respectively, <em>P</em> = .007) and hypertriglyceridemia (median age 30 years and 48 years, respectively, <em>P</em> = .03); and were diagnosed with drusen retinopathy 2 times more often (80% vs 33.3%, <em>P</em> = .16) and at a younger age (median age 33 years and 60 years, <em>P</em> = .14). The autoimmune diseases prevalence was similar in the 2 groups. In our series, 63.6% (7 out of 11) in whom data were available had presence of C3NeF.</p>

</div></section></section><section id="dgaf315-s2.9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Follow-Up Clinical Outcome and Laboratory Parameters</h3>
<p>During the median follow-up of 11 years, metabolic parameters including fasting triglycerides, plasma glucose, and HbA1c remained in the normal range for most participants (<a href="#dgaf315-F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3A-3C</a>). However, 3 participants reported a new diagnosis of diabetes mellitus during follow-up (median time 11 years; range, 8-21 years). Of those participants, the median age of diabetes onset was 47 years (range, 42-57 years). Blood HbA1c of only one of these patients with new-onset diabetes (APL109.4) was available (<a href="#dgaf315-F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>). In addition, new-onset hypertriglyceridemia was reported in only one patient at the age of 36 years. APL109.4 developed clomiphene-induced hypertriglyceridemia at age 31 years, with serum triglyceride level of 1104 mg/dL. <a href="#dgaf315-F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3D</a> shows initial and follow-up serum C3 of 4 patients who initially had low C3 level. Interestingly, in 2 patients (APL104.3 and APL121.4) serum C3 levels increased to the normal range after 9 and 21 years, respectively.</p>

<p>Although APL was reported in the early 1900s by Barraquer and Simons (<a href="#dgaf315-B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B20">20</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B21">21</a>), only a few case series have been reported in the last 3 decades. Consequently, the precise prevalence of various phenotypic features and associated morbidity and mortality of APL remain unknown due to lack of systematic clinical and laboratory data collection. As the largest and longest follow-up cohort of patients with APL, our data reveal several new observations.</p>
<p>
<a href="#dgaf315-T6" class="usa-link">Table 6</a> compares previous APL cohorts from Turkey (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B9">9</a>), and from Italy and Spain (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>), (28 and 26 participants, respectively), with ours and an aggregate of previous anecdotal reports of patients with APL in the literature (220 patients from Misra's review (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>) and 31 patients from our literature review from 2005 to 2024). Our present cohort includes the patients reported by Misra et al in 2004 (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>). All participants from Spain (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>) and our cohort were diagnosed based on the same accepted criteria (essential and supportive criteria) first mentioned by Misra et al (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>). On the other hand, the Turkish cohort (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B9">9</a>) recruited patients with APL mainly based on the pattern of body fat loss, so there was discrepancy of some clinical characteristics from our cohort. For example, (i) the female to male ratio was 1:1.15 in the Turkish cohort (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B9">9</a>) in contrast to our data and of others with female to male ratio of 7-8:1; (ii) C3 hypocomplementemia occurred in less than half of Turkish patients (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B9">9</a>), while the other investigators reported C3 hypocomplementemia in about 60% to 70% of their patients with APL (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>); and (iii) the onset of fat loss of some patients in the Turkish cohort (without significant autoimmune diseases) were in their 40s to 50s (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B9">9</a>), far beyond their childhood and adolescence, while in the other reports, including ours, the late-onset fat loss occurred in the patients with autoimmune diseases (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>). Thus, there is a possibility of inclusion of APL phenocopies, such as localized lipodystrophy of the face, SHORT syndrome, and atypical progeroid syndrome in the Turkish cohort (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B9">9</a>).</p>

</div></section><p>Almost all our patients were White. However, our literature review from the last 2 decades showed an increase in the number of Asian patients, as many as 37%, which might be explained by more awareness among clinicians. Nevertheless, so far there are no reports of any patient of African origin. The onset of fat loss mostly occurred before puberty, with a median age of 7 to 13.5 years. Interestingly, Magno et al (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>), our cohort, and the review of 31 patients with APL reported more patients with late-onset fat loss, in fourth and fifth decades (<a href="#dgaf315-B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B22">22-24</a>). This late-onset fat loss mainly occurred after the flare of autoimmune diseases, especially dermatomyositis (<a href="#dgaf315-B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B22">22</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B23">23</a>). Unfortunately, there is no information regarding a patient from Magno et al (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>) whose onset of lipodystrophy was at 48 years of age.</p>
<p>Since Misra et al (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>) documented 13.6% prevalence of diabetes in 2004, the prevalence rose in the next 20 years to 18% with longer follow-up. Nevertheless, only 3 out of 43 patients who provided health status updates to us were newly diagnosed with diabetes during the median follow-up time of 11 years. These could possibly be explained by the increased mean age of the present cohort (40 years) compared to the previous cohort (29.9 years) (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>). Patients with APL and dysglycemia in our series had higher BMI, hypertension, and older age. A few participants documented a complicated insulin regimen or poorly controlled diabetes (<a href="#dgaf315-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B9">9</a>).</p>
<p>Hypertriglyceridemia seemed comparable among cohorts at one-third to one-half of the participants. Interestingly, the prevalence of MASH among APL in our series was quite high, up to 61%. Upon follow-up, we noted that older patients, in their 40s to their 70s, were more frequently diagnosed with new-onset metabolic complications, such as diabetes, hypertriglyceridemia, and MASH (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B25">25</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B26">26</a>). Moreover, APL with fat accumulation in the lower body tended to have higher BMI and rate of hypertriglyceridemia (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>). Although the metabolic abnormalities in APL are much less severe than in other lipodystrophies, long-term follow-up and age-appropriate screening of metabolic profiles, beginning in the fifth decade, seems advisable.</p>
<p>Autoimmune diseases were reported more frequently in our cohorts (15%) compared to the other 2 (7% (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B9">9</a>) and 4% (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>)), which could be due to the larger number of patients and longer follow-up duration. We found dermatomyositis to be the most prevalent autoimmune disease in our series. Although the other 2 groups did not report any patient with dermatomyositis (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8-10</a>), the literature reviews (both Misra's (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>) and ours) revealed that dermatomyositis and SLE were both frequently diagnosed among patients with APL. Dermatomyositis has already established a causative relationship with acquired generalized lipodystrophy (AGL) (<a href="#dgaf315-B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B27">27</a>) but not yet for APL. Several case reports previously mentioned the development of APL after diagnosis of dermatomyositis, both juvenile- and adult-onset (<a href="#dgaf315-B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B23">23</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B28">28-30</a>). While the other autoimmune diseases, including SLE, usually present after the onset of fat loss, dermatomyositis is almost always diagnosed before or at least simultaneously with the lipodystrophy, ranging from 2 months to 20 years (<a href="#dgaf315-B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B23">23</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B28">28-30</a>). This unique time sequence is also observed in dermatomyositis-associated AGL. Therefore, we suggest that patients with any type of dermatomyositis should be thoroughly examined annually to detect partial or generalized lipodystrophy.</p>
<p>C3 hypocomplementemia is the well-known hallmark of APL. Among APL cohorts (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>), including ours and literature reviews (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>), low serum C3 was reported in approximately 61% to 72%, which was comparable to the previous literature (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B31">31</a>). Our finding is also similar to the observation of Magno et al (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>) that APL patients with C3 hypocomplementemia tended to lose fat earlier than those with normal serum C3 level. Moreover, our data reveal that the metabolic abnormalities (diabetes, IFG/IGT, and hypertriglyceridemia) occurred at a younger age in patients with APL and C3 hypocomplementemia and may require screening at a young age. Interestingly, after an extensive follow-up (range, 9-35 years), 2 people from our cohort and 1 from Magno et al (<a href="#dgaf315-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B10">10</a>) spontaneously normalized serum C3 levels without any specific treatment. It is likely that with time, the autoimmune process that caused APL abates and serum C3 levels normalize.</p>
<p>Although the precise pathophysiology and underlying autoimmune basis of APL remains unclear, the dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway is claimed to play a significant role (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B3">3</a>). Not only C3NeF, but also anti-C3, anti-properdin, and anti-factor B autoantibodies were simultaneously detected in a recent report in patients with APL and C3 hypocomplementemia (<a href="#dgaf315-B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B31">31</a>). Interestingly, C3NeF is commonly positive in immune-related glomerulonephritis but these patients rarely develop lipodystrophy (<a href="#dgaf315-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B32">32</a>) and therefore, the specificity of C3NeF is currently questioned as the marker of APL. We reported positive C3NeF in around two-thirds of the participants, slightly less than in the previous literature mentioned in Misra et al (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>). Unfortunately, we did not screen our patients' sera for the other autoantibodies mentioned above.</p>
<p>It is also suggested that the regional loss of fat in APL may be related to the expression of adipsin and other complement factors in the different adipocyte depots (<a href="#dgaf315-B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B33">33</a>). Whether C3NeF and other autoantibodies in patients with APL are directed against adipocyte-specific antigens causing fat loss remains unclear. Recently, our group (<a href="#dgaf315-B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B34">34</a>) and Corvillo et al (<a href="#dgaf315-B35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B35">35</a>) reported association of perilipin 1 (PLIN1) autoantibodies in patients with AGL and autoimmune diseases. However, sera of none of our 52 patients with APL had PLIN1 autoantibody (<a href="#dgaf315-B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B34">34</a>). This suggests that other autoantibodies directed against other adipocyte-specific antigens or other autoimmune processes may be involved in the pathogenesis of APL. We did not detect any particular antecedent illness linked to onset of APL.</p>
<p>MPGN has been the most concerning complication for APL because it can lead to end-stage renal disease. In contrast to previous literature (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B36">36</a>), which reported up to 20% to 22% of patients with APL developed MPGN within 8 to 10 years after diagnosis, our recent data reveal prevalence rates of less than 10%, which is consistent with other series (<a href="#dgaf315-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B8">8-10</a>). This could be explained by the publication bias of more severe APL with MPGN in the past.</p>
<p>Drusen retinopathy is commonly known as age-related macular degeneration (AMD), especially in White people with ages beyond 50 to 60 years (<a href="#dgaf315-B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B37">37</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B38">38</a>). Patients with variants in alternative complement pathway genes, such as complement factor H (<em>CFH</em>), complement 3 (<em>C3</em>), and complement factor H related 3 (<em>CFHR3</em>) have increased risk of late-stage AMD (<a href="#dgaf315-B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B39">39</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B40">40</a>). Drusen macular degeneration shares the same pathophysiology as MPGN, that is, the dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, particularly the overactivation of C3 convertase (<a href="#dgaf315-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B5">5</a>). Early-onset drusen retinopathy has been reported previously in patients with APL (<a href="#dgaf315-B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B41">41</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B42">42</a>), regardless of the presence of MPGN, but the true prevalence is still unclear. Our report of drusen retinopathy affecting as many as two-thirds of patients with APL who underwent retinal examination is concerning. This relatively high prevalence was confirmed by our recent review of the literature within the past 2 decades; All seven patients with APL who had an eye examination were diagnosed with drusen retinopathy (<a href="#dgaf315-B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B26">26</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B41">41-46</a>). Moreover, patients with APL and C3 hypocomplementemia had drusen retinopathy earlier than those with normal serum C3, which supports the dysregulation of alternative complement pathway as the central mechanism of disease. The presentations of drusen vary from asymptomatic to abnormal visual acuity. Nevertheless, drusen retinopathy needs to be monitored by an ophthalmologist due to serious complications later, such as retinal atrophy and choroidal neovascularization (<a href="#dgaf315-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B5">5</a>, <a href="#dgaf315-B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B44">44</a>). Therefore, patients with APL should undergo a screening retinal examination starting in their 20s to 30s and then annually thereafter.</p>
<p>People with APL typically lose fat from the face, neck, and upper body, while some accumulate fat in lower body. This very often leads to psychological stress and psychiatric comorbidities (<a href="#dgaf315-B47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B47">47</a>). We report the actual burden of psychiatric illnesses in APL at approximately 20%, of which depression disorder was the most common, and anxiety and eating disorders were in second and third place, respectively, as reported previously (<a href="#dgaf315-B47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B47">47</a>). The reconstructive and cosmetic techniques to correct the disfigurement are one of the biggest concerns among patients with APL (<a href="#dgaf315-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dgaf315-B2">2</a>). We report that more than one-fifth of our series experienced at least one procedure in their lifetime, although fat loss frequently recurred later.</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Limitations and Strengths</h2>
<p>Our study's main strength is that it represents the world's largest cohort and the longest follow-up of an ultra-rare disease, APL. We present a systematic analysis of the initial clinical characteristics, metabolic comorbidities, immunologic profiles, and the specific complications (MPGN and drusen retinopathy) as well as on follow-up. Furthermore, none of our patients had a family history of APL, so our data strongly speak against monogenic inheritance among patients with APL. However, whether patients with APL have a predilection for a particular human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genotype which may predispose them to develop APL remains a possibility.</p>
<p>We acknowledge some limitations as well. Most of our patients with APL were from the United States and were White; thus, our findings may not be applicable to patients from the other parts of the world. Also, only a few male patients had APL and therefore, firm conclusions cannot be made about the prevalence of comorbidities among them. The serum C3, C4, and C3NeF were measured by Quest Diagnostics and the National Institutes of Health clinical center laboratory, respectively. Since our study is retrospective with follow-up over nearly 30 years, we could not provide the inter-and intra-assay variability between the 2 centers. Whether patients with APL lack visceral adipose tissue due to lipodystrophy or have increased fat accumulation there remains unclear as our study did not include quantification of visceral fat measurement using specific protocol of volumetric magnetic resonance imaging. Establishment of a multi-country registry would be helpful in ascertaining the underlying immune basis and genetic predilection of this disorder, as well as factors predisposing to disease initiation, progression, and outcomes.</p></section><section id="dgaf315-s5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p>Our study reveals that patients with APL develop more metabolic complications than previously known; however, the prevalence was still much less as compared to other lipodystrophies (AGL, congenital generalized lipodystrophy, and familial partial lipodystrophy) even after 30 years of follow-up. Second, patients with APL and C3 hypocomplementemia were at risk of dysglycemia and hypertriglyceridemia at a younger age. Third, among autoimmune diseases, dermatomyositis presented a close relationship to developing APL later, particularly in late-onset patients. Fourth, early-onset drusen retinopathy was highly prevalent in patients with APL. Lastly, the body disfigurement among patients with APL led to social isolation, anxiety, and depression, thus emotional well-being should be evaluated at every appointment.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
