# Dietary fibre blend improves gut health in patients with Irritable Bowel Syndrome following a low FODMAP diet: a randomised, double-blind control trial
**Authors:** Yan R, Devine A, Lo J, Marlow E, Dunican IC, Kunaratnam K, Andrew L, Christophersen CT
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-9893113/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-9893113/v1)

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>  Purpose:  A diet low in FODMAP negatively affects the gut microbiota of Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) patients, despite alleviation of symptoms. This study examines whether ur gut®, a mix of dietary fibres, when given to patients with stable symptoms on an existing low FODMAP diet, can improve gut microbiota, mental health, sleep and quality of life (QOL) in IBS patients without symptom exacerbation. <h4>Methods:</h4> Adult participants (n = 26, 3 males) completed a randomised, double-blind controlled study. Participants were provided with either ur gut® (n = 13) or placebo (n = 13) for 3 weeks after a 1-week baseline. At baseline and end of intervention, blood, 24-h faecal samples and 3-day weighed-food diaries were collected. Validated questionnaires were used to evaluate mental health, sleep, QOL and gut symptoms. Participants reported daily IBS symptoms and wore a wrist-based actigraphy to capture daily sleep. <h4>Results:</h4> During the intervention, both groups maintained baseline stable gut symptoms and sleep patterns. After the intervention, the ur gut® group had lower energy (P < 0.05) and protein (P < 0.01) intake compared to the placebo group, and increased resistant starch intake (P < 0.001) compared to baseline. Changes in overall genus-level gut microbiota composition (P < 0.01), including  <italic>Ruminococcus E</italic>  (5.5-fold increase, P < 0.01), were observed. ur gut® improved GI-specific anxiety (P = 0.01) and sexual subscale of QOL (P = 0.018). <h4>Conclusion:</h4> A specific mix of fibres can increase fibre intake, improve gut microbiota and gastrointestinal-specific anxiety without impacting sleep and symptoms of IBS patients on a low FODMAP diet as compared with placebo, despite no changes found in faecal and blood biomarkers. This provides a dietary avenue to improve gut health with no exacerbation of symptoms in IBS patients.  </p>

ABSTRACT   
Purpose: 
 A diet low in FODMAP negatively affects the gut microbiota of Irritable Bowel 
Syndrome (IBS) patients, despite alleviation of symptoms. This study examine s 
whether ur gut®, a mix of dietary fibres, when given to patients with stable symptoms 
on an existing low FODMAP diet, can improve gut microbiota, mental health,  sleep 
and quality of life (QOL) in IBS patients without symptom exacerbation. 
Methods: 
 Adult participants (n=26, 3 males) completed a randomised, double-blind 
controlled study. Participants were provided with either ur gut® (n=13) or place bo 
(n=13) for 3 weeks after a 1-week baseline. At baseline and end of intervention, blood, 
24-h faecal samples and 3-day weighed-food diaries were collected. V alidated 
questionnaires were used to evaluate mental health, sleep, QOL and gut s ymptoms. 
Participants reported daily IBS symptoms and wore a wrist-based actigraphy to 
capture daily sleep.  
Results: 
 During the intervention, both groups maintained baseline stable gut symptoms 
and sleep patterns. After the intervention, the ur gut® group had lower ene rgy (P < 
0.05) and protein (P < 0.01) intake compared to the placebo group, and  increased 
resistant starch intake (P < 0.001) compared to baseline. Changes in overall g enus-

increase, P < 0.01), were observed. ur gut® improved GI-specific anxiety (P = 0.01)

INTRODUCTION 1 
Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) affects around 5% of people globally [1], 2 
inflicting mental and life quality loss on individuals and imposing an economic burden 3 
on communities [2,3]. A diet low in fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides,  4 
monosaccharides and polyols (FODMAP) has been demonstrated as efficient in 5 
symptom management [4]. As a group of short-chain carbohydrates that occu r 6 
naturally in varied foods, FODMAP is poorly absorbed in the small intestine and is  7 
rapidly fermented in the proximal colon, causing increases in water volume, osmotic 8 
activity, fermentation and gas production, which in turn leads to distention and 9 
symptom induction [5]. Thus, despite the existence of similar physiological responses 10 
for both IBS individuals and healthy people following FODMAP consumptio n, GI 11 
symptoms associated with visceral hypersensitivity can be induced in IBS patients 12 
[6,7]. Moreover, IBS patients have been shown to have lower pain thresholds with  13 
higher perceptions of gut-related pain than the general population [8].  14 
A low FODMAP diet, however, has been suggested to have unfavourable 15 
impacts on gut microbiota, including: a reduction in total bacterial abundance [9];  16 
prebiotic bacteria abundance, such as Bifidobacteria [10] ; butyrate-producing bacteria 17 
(Faecalibacterium prausnitzii ); and gut health related bacteria ( Akkermansia 18 
muciniphila) [11]. Moreover, despite symptom amelioration [12], low FODMAP diets 19 
have not been shown to restore the dysbiotic gut microbiome of IBS, and ca n 20 
negatively impact the gut microbiota of healthy subjects [13,14], while the clin ical 21 
response can be dependent on the original microbiota signature in IBS p atients [15]. 22 
This was demonstrated in a longer-term study with 18 IBS patients who had completed 23 
a 3-phase FODMAP diet over a 12-month period showed that sho rt chain fatty acid 24 
(SCFA) concentration was reduced compared to baseline, despite symptomatic 25 
improvement and unchanged Bifidobacteria abundance with a relative abundance at 26 
1.2% - 1.3% [13].  27 
Emerging research has shown the link between gut microbiota and IBS 28 
comorbidities, including problems in sleep [16] and mental health [17]. Moreove r, gut 29 
microbiota has been indicated as a potential target for health-related imp rovement. 30 
Previous animal studies have found microbiota-derived butyrate to enhance sleep [18], 31 
where butyrate supplementation ameliorated damage in intestinal homeostasis  32

induced by sleep deprivation [19]. SCFA are rapidly absorbed in the human co lon, 33 
whereby SCFA levels decline along the large intestine, with approximately 5% of 34 
bacteria-derived SCFA being excreted in faeces [20], if excess amount is pro duced. 35 
Butyrate, one of the main SCFA, produced via fermentation of dietary fibre residue 36 
that enters the colon by local obligate anaerobic bacteria, is the preferre d metabolic 37 
fuel for healthy colonocytes, providing approximately two-thirds of their energy (> 60%) 38 
[21]. Additionally, butyrate drives the energy metabolism of colonocytes t owards 39 
mitochondrial beta-oxidation, which in turn consumes oxygen and maintains epithelial 40 
hypoxia [22,23]. Butyrate can also modulate microbial tryptophan metab olites that 41 
appear to be dysfunctional in IBS patients, which are in turn linked to psychiatric 42 
comorbidities [24] and poorer sleep quality [25]. Therefore, existing evidence supports 43 
the pivotal role of microbiota-derived butyrate in functions such as sleep and m ental 44 
health [26]. Notably, a low intake of dietary fibre limits the function of gut microb iota, 45 
including producing SCFA, especially butyrate [27]. Longer-term studies of restri ctive 46 
food patterns, such as a low FODMAP diet, have demonstrated that dieters habitually 47 
consume low fibre (10 –17g/d) [13,28], which is below Australian dietary 48 
recommendations [29]. However, fibre intake has also been positively associated with 49 
faecal output and SCFA, whereby reaching a daily faecal output of 150 g may reduce 50 
colon cancer risk via improving varied fermentation-derived products, including 51 
butyrate [30].  52 
Gut health, according to newest consensus statement from the International 53 
Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP), refers to both subjective 54 
experience and objective measures of gut function, and absence o f symptoms is not 55 
equivalent to good gut health [31]. To date, little research has focus ed on the triad of 56 
gut microbiota, sleep and mental health when identifying the effectiveness of a fibre-57 
related dietary intervention in IBS patients who follow a low FODMAP diet [32]. 58 
Research has suggested the inadequate fibre intake in the IBS population  [33], and 59 
the predominant role of IBS individuals’ baseline microbiome composition on  the 60 
response to fibre consumption [34]. Emerging human studies have evidenced  61 
beneficial effects of resistant starch (RS) type 2 via reshaping the gut microbiome, 62 
such as managing non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [35], facilitating weight loss [36],  63 
reduce inflammatory mediators[37]. However, RS type 2 alone is not well tolerated in 64 
patients with IBS as its readily fermentable capacity [38,39]. Nevertheless, animal  65

MATERIALS AND METHODS  73 
Participants and Sample Size  74 
Inclusion and exclusion criteria have been included in the protocol paper [42] 75 
and are detailed in the supplementary material. In summary, adult IBS patients (18-65 76 
years old) were included if they had stable symptoms (self-reported), a clinical 77 
diagnosis of IBS by a gastroenterologist or other medical professional, and had been 78 
on a low FODMAP diet for at least one month prior to the intervention . Participants 79 
were excluded if they were current smokers or had a known diagnosis of other 80 
gastrointestinal illness. 81 
The participant recruitment began in July 2020 in Perth, Western Australia. The 82 
initial sample size calculation proposed in the protocol indicated a total of 50 83 
participants was required to detect a change in log SCFA concentration of 0.4, 84 
corresponding to a small-to-medium effect size of 0.2. However, the recruitment was 85 
significantly impacted by the COVID 19 pandemic, resulting in multiple lockdowns and 86 
reduced interest/engagement from the public. As a result, a revised sample size 87 
calculation was performed using G*Power version 3.1.9.2 [43]. The calculation was 88 
based on a within-between interaction design to minimally detect a medium effect size 89 
of 0.3 with 80% power and an α -error at 5%, whereby a minimum sample size of 24 90 
was required. Accounting for a 15% attrition rate, the final minimum require d sample 91 
size was 28 participants.  92 
Trial Protocol  93 
The study protocol has been published elsewhere [42], and methodolog ical 94 
details are outlined in supplementary methods. In summary, this study was designed 95 
as a randomised, double-blind, control trial, including a one-week baseline a nd a 96

anthropometric measures, such as body weight (kg), waist and hip circumference (cm) 98 
and percentage of lean and fat mass (determined by BOD POD, an Air Displacement 99 
Plethysmograph). Fasting blood samples were collected to assess blood bioma rkers 100

and excretion amount of SCFA were examined from a 24-h faecal sample  (all faecal 103

This was recorded by each participant for analysing of habitual dietary intake.  The 105 
general diet quality was assessed using Aussie-Dietary Quality Index [44], detailed in 106 
the supplementary material. Varied validated questionnaires were used to assess IBS 107 
symptoms [45,46], FODMAP intake [47], sleep[48-52], mental health [53,54], QOL 108 
[55,56] and physical activity[57], at T1 and at Timepoint 2 (T2) - the en d of the 109 
interventional period, as detailed in the study protocol [42]. After the  baseline week, 110 
participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups,  either the ur gut® fibre 111 
powder or placebo (maize flour without resistant starch with psyllium husk), for a 112 
period of three weeks. The intervention of ur gut® fibre consists of HAMS and psyllium 113 
husk ( International Patent Application (WO/2025/007187)). To ensure that an y 114 
observed intervention effects were not due to psyllium husk, the placebo incorporated 115 
an identical psyllium husk component in the same amount.  Both groups increased 116 
their dose from 5 g to 40 g/d over 11 days until the full dose was consumed for a further 117 
10 days (40 g /d), as described in the study protocol [42]. For every 100 g o f ur gut®, 118 
it provides 67 g carbohydrate consists of 56 g dietary fibre (36 g resistant starch) and 119 
11 g other carbohydrate, as well as 1 g protein and less than 1 g of f at. Education 120 
about consumption, calculation of compliance and specimen collection and analysis 121 
are detailed in the supplementary material. A wrist-actigraphy monitor, a Readiband™ 122 
v5 (Readiband, Fatigue Science Inc., Canada) (validated [58]), was provided to each 123 
participant to capture daily sleep data. Throughout the four weeks, participan ts 124 
recorded their daily symptoms via an online checklist . At T2, the assessments 125 
conducted at T1 were repeated. The primary outcome was severity of IBS symptoms, 126 
with other analyses listed as secondary outcomes. 127

Statistical Methods 133 
The per-protocol analysis was undertaken using data from participants who 134 
completed the study, whereby non-compliant participants were excluded. Linear 135 
Mixed Modelling was used to determine both within- and between-group d ifferences 136 
in health outcomes via the group x time interaction effect and the main effects (if the 137 
interaction is not significant), with BMI and age included as covariates. Gender was 138 
not included due to the small number of male participants (n=3). Post-ho c tests were 139 
conducted if a significant effect was detected. The significance level was set at P ≤ 140 
0.05. Furthermore, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was performed on all raw P-141 
values to minimise false positive results, and an effect was noted as marginal when 142 
the raw P ≤ 0.05, but the FDR P > 0.05. For gut microbiota, a combination of R, Primer 143 
7 and Permutation Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) + (PRIMER-E, 144 
Plymouth), was used to analyse bacterial community structure at three taxonomic 145 
levels; phylum, genus and Amplicon Sequencing Variant (ASV). The relative 146 
abundance data were square root transformed and a Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix  147 
was calculated, followed by the deployment of Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) 148 
to visualise findings of the three taxonomic levels at T1 and T2. For determinatio n of 149 
significant associations at baseline and longitudinal dissimilarities between the two 150 
groups, PERMANOVA and dissimilarity percentage (SIMPER) were conducted. 151 
Distance-based linear models (DistLM) and distance-based redundancy ana lysis 152 
(dbRDA) were used to integrate gut microbial findings with all other relevant aspects. 153 
Further details of the statistical analyses are provided in the supplementary methods.  154

DISCUSSION 302 
This randomised trial is the first study to comprehensively explore the effects of 303 
a mixed fermentable dietary fibre powder (ur gut®) on gut microbiota, sleep, mental 304 
health and QOL in IBS patients who are following a low FODMAP diet. The study 305 
outcomes showed that ur gut® increased in fermentable fibre intake, benefited overall 306 
gut microbial composition, selectively promoted gut microbes, and improved GI-307 
specific anxiety and QOL (sexual subscale), without symptomatic exacerbation or 308 
impacts on participants’ habitual sleep throughout the study . Moreover, ur gut® 309 
boosted participants’ fibre intake above Australian dietary recommendations and 310 
helped maintain overall diet quality and pattern. The presence of fibre is crucia l to 311 
protect the intestinal barrier integrity and to balance microbial metabolic networks, 312 
whereby fibre-supplemented/fibre-rich diets can maintain the balance between mucin-313 
degrading microbes and fibre-degrading microbes [63].  314 
The demonstrated effect of ur gut® is due to the unique blend of H AMS and 315 
psyllium. Rat studies have shown that the fermentation was shifted further distally 316 
when HAMS was mixed with psyllium [40]. Rats fed with HAMS + psyllium 317 
demonstrated a higher butyrate concentration, which was maintained throughout the 318 
colon; however, for rats taking HAMS only, their butyrate concentration decre ased 319 
along the colon, with the concentration in the cecum being threefold o f that in the 320 
faeces [40]. The fibre mix in ur gut® is believed to slow proximal fermentability, where 321 
psyllium, with its strong gel-forming and water-holding capacity [64] can positively trap 322 
RS granules. It prevents or slows proximal colonic fermentation, preventing or 323 
reducing gas-production and related symptom-causing responses, and delive ring RS 324 
further distally in the colon where it is better tolerated. previous research has  shown 325 
that RS alone is not tolerated by IBS sufferers [39]. Gut microbes are highly dependent 326 
on their substrate preference [65], and as such, we found bacteria were  selectively 327 
promoted in response to ur gut®. Our results showed that ur gut® consiste ntly 328

current recommendations of an adaption period for most fibre supplements [80]. 386

Limitations  395 
          Limitations include a small sample size, dominance of female participants an d 396 
differing IBS subtypes. IBS is more prevalent in women than men with fem ales 397 
accounting for approximately 60 –70% of diagnosed cases in Western populations. 398 
Gender differences are also evident across IBS subtypes, with IBS-C predominatin g 399 
in women, while IBS-D is more commonly reported in men. Several results showed 400 
marginal significances (namely raw P ≤ 0.05, but FDR-adjusted P > 0.05), which could 401 
be attributed to our relatively small sample size, especially in gut microbial an alysis. 402 
Gut transit time and faecal pH were not assessed in the present study, which shoul d 403 
be incorporated in future research to strengthen methodological completeness. 404 
Further research needs to identify effects or impacts of long-term intake of ur gut® 405 
fibre on weight management and metabolic health. 406 
CONCLUSION 407 
In conclusion, this study comprehensively explored the effects of 3-week ur 408 
gut® supplementation on gut microbiota, gut health, dietary intake, sleep, mental 409 
health and QOL in participants living with IBS and following a low FODMAP diet. 410 
Despite no change found in faecal SCFA level between groups, adding ur gut® to this 411 
group’s habitual low FODMAP diet  increase d fibre intake, improved  GI-specific 412 
anxiety and benefited  gut microbial composition, without impacting participa nts  413 
symptoms severity, sleep and dietary quality and patterns.  414
