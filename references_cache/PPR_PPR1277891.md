# Trypanosoma cruzi genetic diversity associates with serological diagnostic assay performance
**Authors:** Thompson J, Dumonteil E, Herrera C
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-10119431/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10119431/v1)

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>  Background  Infections with  <italic>Trypanosoma cruzi</italic>  can lead to Chagas disease. Diagnosis principally relies on serological assays, although there is no gold standard assay, and discordant results among tests are increasingly reported. Parasite diversity has been proposed as a potential factor underlying variable diagnostic performance, but clear associations have not been detected yet. Therefore, we tested here how parasite strain composition in infected hosts -the “cruziome”- may associate with serological outcomes in this cohort. Methods  Archived blood samples from patients from Argentina, Honduras and Mexico were stratified as confirmed positive or serodiscordant based on previous serological testing, and  <italic>T. cruzi</italic>  parasites were successfully genotyped in 73 patients. Results  A total of 532 mini-exon sequences were obtained (7.3 ± 0.5 haplotypes/individual), covering TcI, TcII, TcIV, TcV and TcVI DTUs, in similar proportions among countries, but variable proportions among individuals. Phylogenetic analysis indicated a significant geographic structuring of TcI, TcII, TcV and TcVI among countries, as well as clustering associated with discordant serology for TcI, TcII and TcV DTUs. Between 5–12 SNPs among these DTUs supported the association with discordant serology, and their frequency among countries mirrored that of serodiscordance. Conclusion  These results point to the cruziome and genetic differentiation within TcI, TcII and TcV DTUs to explain a large part of geographic differences in diagnostic performance. Thus, it is critical to account for the local diversity of  <italic>T. cruzi</italic>  strains throughout the continent for the development of improved diagnosis.  </p>

Introduction 51 
Infections with Trypanosoma cruzi, a protozoan parasite, can lead to Chagas disease, a 52 
major health problem in the Americas. Indeed, there are an estimated 10 million infected 53 
persons worldwide [1], of whom 30-40% may progress to severe clinical disease characterized 54 
by cardiac and/or digestive manifestations [2]. Diagnosis relies primarily on serological assays 55 
detecting antibodies against the parasite. However, there is no gold standard assay. Thus, 56 
current recommendations require two distinct assays for the confirmation of cases, and when 57 
discordant, a third assay needs to be performed [3]. This testing algorithm is lengthy and 58 
complicates case identification, leading to important underdiagnosis of infections [4-8]. 59 
Critically, serodiscordance is increasingly reported, particularly with samples from 60 
Central and North America [8-12] and it poses a challenge for accurate diagnosis as cases are 61 
often dismissed as false positives given their apparent non-consistent reactivity across assays. 62 
Some studies suggested that serodiscordance may be associated with low level antibodies 63 
lingering after a cured infection [13]. However, our previous work indicated that parasite burden 64 
was identical between serodiscordant and confirmed seropositive samples [10]. On the other 65 
hand, differences in antibody levels against diagnostic antigens suggested that serodiscordance 66 
reflected a distinct humoral response, possibly attributed to the infecting parasite strains or host 67 
factors [10]. Indeed, T. cruzi parasites are classified into seven major lineages, TcI to TcVI and 68 
TcBat referred to as discrete typing units (DTUs). The initial description of their geographic 69 
distribution suggested that TcI was the predominant DTU circulating in the northern hemisphere, 70 
while non-TcI (TcII, TcV and TcVI) predominated in the southern hemisphere [14,15]. Thus, 71 
difference in circulating DTUs were also thought to contribute to serodiscordance. However, 72 
new evidence over the past decade clearly shows that the geographic distribution of DTUs is 73 
more complex, with TcII, TcV and TcVI increasingly detected in Central America, Mexico and 74 
the US [16-27]. Furthermore, these studies and others [28] also highlighted that many infections 75 
were composed of multiple parasite strains, including from different DTUs. This multiplicity of 76 

 4 
infection can have important implications for the host immune response and disease 77 
progression [29,30] and we proposed the term “cruziome” to describe the parasite assemblage 78 
of strains infecting a host [31].  79 
Previous efforts to associate parasite DTUs and genetic diversity with serodiscordance 80 
suggested a possible association, but the evidence remained weak due to a small sample size 81 
and limited phylogenetic resolution to assess T. cruzi genetic diversity and the complete 82 
cruziome [17]. In dogs, serodiscordance among tests is also an issue [32,33], and a previous 83 
analysis similarly suggested that TcI genetic diversity could be associated with discordant 84 
serology [20]. Based on the above, we tested here for the potential role of T. cruzi strain 85 
diversity on diagnostic test accuracy by performing high resolution parasite genotyping based 86 
on next generation sequencing for optimal resolution of the cruziome, applied to a large cohort 87 
of patient samples from Argentina, Honduras and Mexico. We examined how different aspects 88 
of the cruziome composition may associate with serological outcomes in this cohort.  89

Materials and methods 91 
Ethics Approval and Consent to participate  92 
The present study was approved by Tulane University Institutional Review Board (No. 93 
2018-2237) and used de-identified archived samples from a previous study on congenital T. 94 
cruzi transmission and collected following written informed consent. 95 
 96 
Patient samples 97 
Samples consisted in maternal blood/DNA samples collected at birth in Argentina 98 
(N=160), Honduras (N=233) and Mexico (N=110) for a total of 503 samples [34]. T. cruzi 99 
parasites were successfully genotyped in a subset of maternal samples (see below). Metadata 100 
associated with these samples included extensive serological testing for T. cruzi infection based 101 
on Stat-Pak (Chembio Diagnostics) and T-detect (InBios) rapid tests and a confirmatory 102 

 5 
recombinant ELISA (Wiener), as well as T. cruzi PCR and parasite burden measured by qPCR 103 
as detailed before [34]. Serological and PCR data were also available from their matching 104 
umbilical cord blood samples. The samples were stratified as confirmed positive when all three 105 
serological assays (two rapid tests and one ELISA) were reactive and concordant, and 106 
serodiscordant when the ELISA was negative while zero, one or two rapid tests may be 107 
reactive. 108 
 109 
T. cruzi genotyping 110 
Parasite genotyping was performed by amplifying the mini-exon marker  using a 111 
multiplex PCR as described by Souto et al., which generates products of different sizes 112 
according to the DTU [35]. In parallel, an alternative primer set amplifying a larger fragment of 113 
500 bp of the mini-exon gene was used as previously described. [36]. PCR products from both 114 
reactions were mixed, purified and processed for sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform. 115 
Raw FASTQ reads were demultiplexed for each sample and competitively mapped to reference 116 
sequences of the mini-exon covering all seven DTUs in Geneious Prime, and SNPs were called 117 
from each individual DTU present using FreeBayes or Geneious SNP utility. Haplotypes from 118 
each DTU were reconstructed based on these SNPs, and their relative abundance was 119 
estimated based on the proportion of reads covering each haplotype. Only sequences present 120 
at a frequency >1% of reads were considered for further analysis. These haplotype sequences 121 
were aligned using MAFFT [37], and phylogenetic analysis was performed in FastTree 2, which 122 
infers approximately-maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees from alignments based on a 123 
generalized time-reversible (GTR) model of nucleotide evolution [38]. Mini-exon reference 124 
sequences from all DTUs were included in the trees to identify the respective DTU clusters (TcI: 125 
H1, EF576846; SilvioX10, CP015667; TcII: Tu18, AY367125; TcIII: M5631, AY367126; TcIV: 126 
92122102r, AY367124; TcV: SC43; AY367127, TcVI: CL, U57984, and TcBat: TCC949cl3, 127 
KT305873). Trees were visualized and formatted with FigTree. A total of 73 DNA samples from 128 

 6 
maternal blood were successfully genotyped, including samples from Argentina (N=20), 129 
Honduras (N=24) and Mexico (N=29) from which we obtained their detailed cruziome DTU and 130 
haplotype composition. Sequences have been deposited into NCBI Genbank database under 131 
accession number PZ560951-PZ561482. 132 
 133 
Data analysis 134 
The samples were classified as confirmed positive (N=44) or serodiscordant (N=29). Of 135 
these discordant samples, 3 (10%) were negative in all serological assays, 22 (76%) were 136 
reactive in a single rapid test, and 4 (14%) were reactive with 2 rapid tests. We then tested for 137 
potential differences in their cruziome by considering its diversity at multiple levels. First, we 138 
tested for differences in overall cruziome complexity/size by comparing the average number of 139 
T. cruzi DTUs and sequence haplotypes infecting individuals (alpha diversity) with Student t 140 
tests.  141 
Second, we tested for an association with the presence/absence of specific parasite 142 
DTUs based on X2 tests, and with the average composition of the cruziome (beta diversity). This 143 
was tested by one-way permutation ANOVA (PERMANOVA) with 10,000 permutations. 144 
Differences in cruziome composition among countries were also assessed by linear discriminant 145 
analysis (LDA).  146 
Third, we assessed the potential contribution of individual sequence haplotypes by 147 
performing phylogenetic analysis of T. cruzi sequences from each DTU. Sequences were 148 
aligned using MAFFT and trees generated with FastTree as above with each DTU analyzed 149 
separately. The statistical significance of sequence clustering according to country of origin and 150 
with diagnostic discordance was assessed by one-way PERMANOVA based on Tamura-Nei 151 
genetic distances among sequences as implemented in Past 4 [39] and linear discriminant 152 
analysis (LDA ) was also performed. 153 

 7 
Finally, we identified SNPs in the sequence alignments of the mini-exon sequences from 154 
each DTU, which were numbered based on SNP position within reference sequences (H1cl2, 155 
EF576846.1 for TcI; Tu18, AY367125.1 for TcII and SC43, AY367127.1 for TcV). SNPs were 156 
tested for their association with diagnostic outcome by comparing SNP frequencies in confirmed 157 
positive and serodiscordant samples with 2 tests and odds ratios were calculated. Bonferroni 158 
correction was applied to account for multiple testing of SNPs. Multivariate logistic regression 159 
models were then built to integrate multiple SNPs from the respective DTUs as predictors of 160 
diagnostic performance. Akaike information criteria (AIC) was used to rank the models and only 161 
the best models were retained. The association of SNPs with the performance of individual 162 
serological assays was also tested in the same manner for Stat-Pak and T-detect rapid tests 163 
and for Wiener ELISA. Finally, SNP frequencies were also calculated among the different 164 
countries and compared by 2 tests. 165 
 166 
Results  167 
Serodiscordance is a stable characteristic of plasma samples 168 
We first characterized the serodiscordance of our panel of maternal plasma samples by 169 
testing its stability and reproducibility by comparing these samples with their matching umbilical 170 
cord plasma samples, which are considered to mostly contain maternal antibodies [40,41]. 171 
Comparison of anti-T. cruzi IgG levels measured by ELISA between maternal and cord blood 172 
indicated a nearly perfect correlation (Figure 1A, R2=0.90, P<0.0001), and serodiscordance was 173 
similar for maternal samples and corresponding cord blood samples (Kappa index=0.95, 174 
P<0.0001). This strongly supported the presence of a distinct antibody profile among these 175 
samples, as suggested before [10]. Also, as reported before [10,34], the proportion of 176 
serodiscordance was highest in samples from Mexico, intermediate in samples from Honduras, 177 
and lower in samples from Argentina (Figure 1B). 178 

 8 
 179 
Genotyping of T. cruzi reveals extensive genetic diversity and frequent mixed infections 180 
The sequencing of T. cruzi mini-exon amplicons from 73 maternal blood samples 181 
generated a total of 532 sequences, corresponding to an average of 7.3 ± 0.5 haplotypes per 182 
individual. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that these sequences clustered with TcI, TcII, TcIV, 183 
TcV and TcVI reference sequences, highlighting the extensive diversity of DTUs infecting these 184 
subjects. The average cruziome included multiple DTUs in variable proportions across 185 
countries, but this did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2B and C; PERMANOVA F=1.87, 186 
P=0.10), suggesting broadly comparable geographic distribution of DTUs among this sample of 187 
infected mothers from these countries. Analysis of individual cruziomes also highlighted that the 188 
large majority of infections included multiple DTUs in variable proportions (72/73 (98.6%) had 189 
multiple DTUs present) (Figure 2D). Individual cruziomes could then be stratified according to T. 190 
cruzi serological testing outcome. 191 
 192 
Cruziome complexity/size does not associate with serological discordance 193 
We explored how the cruziome composition and diversity may associate with serological 194 
diagnostic discordance by considering different levels of diversity. First, we tested for potential 195 
differences in cruziome complexity/size and compared the number of parasite DTUs and 196 
sequence haplotypes infecting individuals (Figure 3A and B), but neither the number of DTUs 197 
nor the number of haplotypes were associated with serodiscordance (t test, P=0.88 and P=0.82 198 
for DTUs and haplotype number, respectively).  199 
 200 
Unique T. cruzi sequence haplotypes associate with serological discordance. 201 
Next, we tested for differences in cruziome composition based on the proportion of the 202 
different DTUs (Figure 4A) or the presence/absence of specific DTUs (Figure 4B) associated 203 
with discordant serology. None of these variables presented significant differences between 204 

 9 
confirmed positive and serodiscordant samples (PERMANOVA, F=1.99, P=0.12 for overall 205 
composition, and 2, d.f.=1, P=0.81, P=0.32, P=0.22, P=0.68 and P=0.57 for TcI, TcII, TcIV, 206 
TcV and TcVI, respectively).  207 
Lastly, we examined the potential contribution of individual haplotype sequences, which 208 
may reflect the presence of genetically different parasite strains. Phylogenetic analysis of mini-209 
exon sequences from TcI, TcII, TcV and TcVI DTUs indicated extensive sequence diversity 210 
within DTUs (Figure 5), as observed before [42-44]. Accordingly, this diversity reflected a 211 
geographic structuring that reached statistical significance for TcI, TcII, TcV and TcVI 212 
(PERMANOVA F=3.03, P=0.039; F=5.99, P=0.008; F=9.29, P=0.0001 and F=61.73, P=0.034, 213 
for TcI, TcII, TcV and TcVI, respectively), indicating a significant level of genetic clustering 214 
among parasite sequences according to the country of origin within each DTU (Figure 5).  215 
Remarkably, sequences derived from serodiscordant samples were also over- or under-216 
represented in some tree branches for TcI, TcII and TcV, and this phylogenetic clustering was 217 
statistically significant (PERMANOVA F=3.83, P=0.023; F=4.73, P=0.034 and F=7.24, P=0.002 218 
for TcI, TcII and TcV, respectively)(Figure 6). Such an association was not detected for TcVI 219 
(F=3.231, P=0.183). These data strongly suggested that strain genetic diversity within DTUs is 220 
associated with serodiscordance for at least TcI, TcII and TcV DTUs. To explore this association 221 
in more detail, we examined SNPs within the mini-exon sequences, and identified several SNPs 222 
in TcI, TcII and TcV sequences which were associated with discordant serology, and the 223 
statistical significance of several of these SNPs remained after Bonferroni correction, which is 224 
highly conservative (Table 1). Furthermore, the frequency of these SNPs varied significantly 225 
among the three countries (Figure 7), mirroring the observed frequency of serodiscordance. 226 
Lastly, we built a multivariate logistic regression model which explained diagnostic outcome 227 
(confirmed positive or serodiscordant) with high accuracy (80% correct prediction, P<0.0001) 228 
based on a small number of SNPs from TcI, TcII and TcV parasites (Figure 8). Similar models 229 
were built to assess the contribution of parasite SNPs on the outcome of individual assays, and 230 

 10 
SNPs subsets were found to be major factors driving the outcome of all assays (Supplementary 231 
Figure 1; R2=0.37, R2=0.27 and R2=0.26 for Stat-Pak, T-Detect, and Wiener ELISA, 232 
respectively). Thus, 75-88% of diagnostic outcomes could be correctly explained by T. cruzi 233 
SNPs combinations. Together, these results strongly supported the circulation of genetically 234 
diverse T. cruzi strains from TcI, TcII and TcV DTUs within the three countries, with some of 235 
these strains significantly associated with discordant serology. 236

Discussion 238 
The complex diagnostic algorithm for T. cruzi infections is a major limitation for the rapid 239 
identification of cases and accurate disease surveillance, and discordance among serological 240 
assays has been a lingering problem [8-12], particularly relevant in Central America and Mexico. 241 
Understanding the causes of serodiscordance is critical to guide the development of improved 242 
diagnostic tests with better performance, and previous work suggested that T. cruzi strain 243 
diversity could be a contributing factor. Indeed, a complex geographic distribution of parasite 244 
strains and DTUs is emerging, adding to the observation that antigens used in diagnostic tests 245 
may not be as conserved as initially thought [42]. Furthermore, the multiple strains and DTUs 246 
composing the cruziome infecting hosts may also affect immune response and diagnostic 247 
performance, acting as an additional confounder. Thus, we examined here the potential 248 
association between serodiscordance and cruziome composition at multiple levels following the 249 
detailed genotyping of parasites from a large cohort of infected patients. 250 
A first major observation was that most (>98%) of patients were infected with multiple 251 
DTUs, which overall distribution was highly similar among patients from Argentina, Honduras 252 
and Mexico. This largely confirms our previous genotyping effort on some of these samples with 253 
a less sensitive method [17,34], strengthening the idea that TcII, TcV and TcVI are important 254 
DTUs circulating in Central America and Mexico, while TcI is also highly relevant for Argentina. 255 
Together with many other studies [16-26], these results indicate that the DTU distribution in the 256 

 11 
Americas initially proposed [14,15] needs to be revised and better defined. In addition, we also 257 
detected a significant geographic structuring for TcI, TcII, TcV and TcVI sequences, suggesting 258 
some level of local differentiation within these DTUs. Geographic structuring of T. cruzi across 259 
the Americas has been described before within TcI [42,43,45-47], as well as within TcIII and 260 
TcIV which have been divided into northern and southern clades [48,49]. TcII also seems to 261 
present some level of geographic variability within Brazil [50]. Thus, our observations expand 262 
these findings and suggest that most if not all T. cruzi DTUs are geographically structured. This 263 
has important implications as biological properties of parasite strains may also vary accordingly, 264 
leading to potential differences in clinical disease progression or response to treatments. For 265 
example, geographical variations in clinical outcomes in the BENEFIT trial have been observed 266 
[51], stressing the need to better understand T. cruzi diversity throughout the continent to 267 
account for this heterogeneity. 268 
Our data also suggest that most infections involve multiple T. cruzi strains/DTUs, 269 
strengthening the concept of cruziome and its relevance to understand host response to 270 
infection, with implications ranging from diagnosis to pathogenesis and treatment [31]. 271 
Regarding the role of cruziome composition in affecting serological results, we found that 272 
serodiscordance was not associated with cruziome complexity/size, overall DTU composition, or 273 
the presence/absence of any specific DTU. Rather, we detected an association with unique 274 
mini-exon sequence haplotypes for TcI, TcII and TcV, which differed from haplotypes from these 275 
DTUs associated with a confirmed positive diagnosis. This was further strengthened by the 276 
identification of several SNPs within these sequences that were significantly associated with 277 
serodiscordance, and which frequency followed a matching geographic distribution. Indeed, 278 
SNP variants associated with serodiscordance were detected at lower frequencies in Argentina 279 
and higher frequencies in Mexico, and seven SNPs from TcI, TcII and TcV were sufficient to 280 
accurately predict >80% of serological diagnostic outcomes. Remarkably, this pattern was 281 

 12 
consistent across serological diagnostic assays as their individual performance was similarly 282 
well predicted by parasite SNP combinations. 283 
Together with initial observations in humans [17] and dogs [20], these results provide 284 
strong evidence for the major contribution of T. cruzi parasite genetic diversity to 285 
serodiscordance. While SNPs within the mini-exon marker are of limited usefulness to infer 286 
specific biological differences, these likely reflect broader genomic differentiation of parasites 287 
within DTUs as observed before [42,44]. Thus, we may hypothesize that infections with some T. 288 
cruzi strains within these DTUs may lead to antibody repertoires targeting parasite antigens 289 
different than those included in current diagnostic assays, leading to serodiscordance. Indeed, 290 
these assays rely mostly on antigens derived from strains from the southern cone, which may 291 
not reflect strains from the northern hemisphere [42,52]. A growing number of observations 292 
indicating that epitope profiles recognized by antibodies from T. cruzi infected patients are 293 
mostly private, i.e. unique to individuals, with very few shared epitopes recognized by antibodies 294 
from multiple patients [42,53] further support this idea. Analysis of antibody repertoires detected 295 
in T. cruzi infected dogs and macaques also indicate mostly private repertoires [54,55].  296 
 297 
Conclusion 298 
In conclusion, the analysis of the cruziome of a large cohort of T. cruzi infected patients 299 
confirmed the strong predominance of infections with multiple parasite strains/DTUs, with a 300 
comparable DTU composition among patients from Argentina, Honduras and Mexico. 301 
Furthermore, significant geographic structuring was detected within TcI, TcII, TcV and TcVI, 302 
confirming and expanding earlier observations, which has important implications for diagnosis. 303 
Remarkably, a significant association between SNPs within TcI, TcII and TcV with 304 
serodiscordance was detected and seven SNPs were sufficient to explain most of the 305 
geographic differences in diagnostic performance. These results imply that it is critical to 306 

 13 
consider the local diversity of T. cruzi DTUs throughout the continent for the development of 307 
improved diagnosis with optimal performance. 308
