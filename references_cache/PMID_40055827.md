# Sodium azide (NaN(3)) intoxication, "the man who lived": potential effective antidote and treatment strategy.
**Authors:** Geerdink JX, van der Wal G, de Jong LAA, Olyslager EJH, van den Goorbergh TW, Kluijtmans LAJ, Spronk PE
**Journal:** Crit Care (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13054-025-05330-w](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-025-05330-w)

## Content

This case report describes the successful treatment of a suicide attempt involving the ingestion of a supralethal dose of sodium azide (NaN3), presenting a prospective novel antidote and therapeutic approach. Treatment encompassed the implementation of high-volume continuous veno-venous hemofiltration (HV-CVVH) alongside the administration of levocarnitine. The latter demonstrated a substantial mitigation of lactate concentration. Comprehensive analyses of serum, ultrafiltrate, and urine revealed the efficacy of HV-CVVH in elimination of NaN3. Our case report presents a potential therapeutic approach for managing otherwise fatal NaN3 intoxications.

Sodium azide (NaN3) is a white, odorless, tasteless salt that is highly water soluble [ 1 ]. It serves as a potent preservative in laboratories and hospitals, leveraging its biocidal properties, and is commercially employed in agriculture as a pesticide and herbicide. Upon ingestion, NaN3 rapidly binds irreversibly to the heme-group of cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV in the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation system), obstructing the transport of high-energy electrons throughout the respiratory chain [ 2– 5 ]. Since our metabolism primarily relies on oxidative phosphorylation for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, the toxic potential of NaN3 is evident. Additionally, NaN3 is metabolized to nitrogen oxide, contributing to the characteristic hypotension and arrhythmias observed in sodium azide intoxication [ 6 , 7 ].

While NaN3 poisoning is infrequent, its incidence is increasing worldwide [ 8 , 9 ]. In the Netherlands NaN3 poisoning has surged, exhibiting an eightfold increase between 2012 and 2022, based on data from the Nationaal Vergiftigingen Informatie Centrum (center in the Netherlands for documenting intoxications) and post-mortem autopsy results from unexplained deaths in the Netherlands. The increase is probably, in part, attributable to end-of-life organizations promoting NaN3 as a preferred compound for self-euthanasia. Fittingly labelling it: “suicide powder” or “Compound X”, promising a quick and painless death resulting in numerous reported cases of deliberate exposure with fatal outcomes over the past decades [ 8– 11 ]. Oral doses surpassing 10 mg/kg or > 700 mg total are almost universally fatal [ 1 , 8 , 12 , 13 ]. Despite various proposed therapies, patients typically succumb within hours to days post-ingestion [ 7 , 11 ]. This case report details treatment of a suicide attempt involving ingestion of high-dose NaN3, resulting in complete recovery after brief intensive care admission, employing a novel treatment strategy.

A 27-year-old male, weighing 70 kg, was found in his room, after ingesting a potentially lethal substance procured online. Next to him, a package containing empty domperidone strips (20mg), an empty container marked '2 × 1.5 g NaN3’, along with detailed instructions for the 'appropriate use of compound X' (Pictures S1–3 in Supplementary Appendix) was discovered. The time of ingestion was unknown. Emergency services were summoned, and patient was transferred to hospital. Upon arrival in the emergency department, the patient presented with a blood pressure of 135/78 mm Hg, a variable pulse (60 to 160/min), and a respiration rate of 26/min and a temperature of 32.6 °C. Patient was unresponsive (E1M1V1) and exhibited profuse sweating. Arterial blood gas analysis on supplemental oxygen via non-rebreather mask (15 L/min) showed a pH of 7.38, pCO2 of 2.1 kPa, bicarbonate of 9 mmol/L, lactate of 18 mmol/L, and pO2 of 32.5 kPa.

The patient was intubated and admitted to ICU where he entered profound shock with persistent severe lactic acidosis (lactate > 20 mmol/L), necessitating hemodynamic support with noradrenalin infusion and initiation of amiodarone to address persistent tachyarrhythmias. Given the absence of a known antidote for NaN3, supportive care included high-volume continuous veno-venous hemofiltration (HV-CVVH) using substitution fluid replacement containing 45 mmol/l NaHCO3 to correct worsening metabolic acidosis. The HV-CVVH settings employed included pre-dilution at 4500 ml/h and post-dilution at 3500 ml/h, optimizing clearance and fluid balance. As part of the decontamination process, a gastric lavage was previously carried out in the emergency room, where no pill fragments were recovered. Activated charcoal was subsequently administered.

During stabilization, the potential counteraction of NaN3 toxicity was considered, leading to the administration of levocarnitine (1 g four times a day, intravenously). To mitigate the possibility of a concomitant intoxication with cyanide we also administered 5 g of hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit). The start of HV-CVVH in combination with levocarnitine promptly resulted in a marked decrease in lactate concentration and improved metabolic acidosis. Hemodynamics stabilized within 12 h, leading to the discontinuation of noradrenaline (cumulative dose 60 mg) and subsequent cessation of amiodarone infusion 6 h later. HV-CVVH ceased 32 h post-admission, and the patient was extubated after 36 h of mechanical ventilation.

After ICU admission patient was admitted to the psychiatric ward. During his 3-week admission he remained hemodynamically stable. His metabolic state remained stable under levocarnitine administration. Lactate concentration was measured daily and was used as a marker of impaired oxidative phosphorylation. Interestingly, lactate concentration recurrently rose after discontinuation of levocarnitine administration; and subsequently normalized after reintroduction of levocarnitine. This phenomenon was observed until day 18 after presentation (Fig. 1). Eventually, the patient left hospital with no discernible permanent damage to vital organs or otherwise. We measured NaN3 concentrations in serum, ultrafiltrate, and urine using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS; Agilent 7890A, 5975C). After sample preparation with pentafluorobenzyl bromide and sodium thiosulphate, we extracted and evaporated the samples. Two microliters were injected into the GC-MSD, with helium as the carrier gas and a temperature gradient from 50 to 300 °C.

Organic acids, amino acids, and acylcarnitine concentrations were measured using routine assays for inborn errors of metabolism. Organic acids in urine and ultrafiltrate were analyzed by high-resolution liquid chromatography quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry in negative ionization mode [ 14 ]. Amino acids in plasma were quantified by multiple reaction monitoring using stable isotope-labeled standards [ 15 ]. Free carnitine and acylcarnitine esters were measured by tandem mass spectrometry with on a Waters XEVO TS-Q system [ 16 ].

Full method details are provided in the supplementary section.

We measured NaN3 concentration in serum, ultrafiltrate and urine in short intervals (Table 1). Serum NaN3 concentration at arrival in hospital was 41.5 mg/L, confirming a lethal concentration of NaN3. During ICU admission and HV-CVVH, serum concentration of NaN3 rapidly decreased from 41.5 to 5.03 mg/L after 7 h, becoming undetectable approximately 16 h post-ingestion. Concurrently, ultrafiltrate concentrations diminished from 6.2 mg/L to undetectable after 16 h. Urine concentrations consistently remained at low to undetectable in each measurement.

We calculated a NaN3 plasma half-life of 1.7 h during HV-CVVH. Due to the absence of two consecutive samples without HV-CVVH the half-life without HV-CVVH could not be determined. When the patient received HV-CVVH for only part of the measured period (3.25 h before initiation and 3.5 h during HV-CVVH), the half-life was 2.3 h.

To analyze the metabolic state of patient during the NaN3 intoxication and potential effect of levocarnitine, we measured amino acids, patterns of free carnitine (FC) and acylcarnitine (AC) in serum, and organic acids in ultrafiltrate and urine (Tables S4, S5, S6 and S7 in Supplementary Appendix). The most notable observations are increased concentrations of pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, and beta-hydroxybutyrate, consistent with impaired oxidative phosphorylation. No evident patterns were discovered in AC or organic acid concentration after administration of levocarnitine, aside from a decrease in lactate. Amino acid concentrations quickly decreased within a day. The most marked decrease was observed in valine, leucine, citrulline, arginine, alanine and isoleucine serum concentrations.

Virtually all deliberate ingestions of > 700 mg NaN3 have been described as lethal, despite extensive treatment [ 1 , 8– 11 , 17 ]. Here, we describe a unique case of a patient who survived ingestion of a supralethal dose of 3000 mg of NaN3 and provide a potential treatment strategy including levocarnitine.

NaN3 is highly water soluble and has a low molecular weight (65 g/mol) [ 18 ]. Yet detailed physicochemical data on NaN3 remains very limited and only assumptions on volume of distribution and protein binding can be made [ 11 , 19 ]. Regarding clearance, NaN3 is primarily metabolized in the liver to nitric oxide, which is then excreted by the kidneys. Given its water solubility and low molecular weight, both hemofiltration and hemodialysis could theoretically enhance NaN3 elimination. However, the effectiveness of these methods was uncertain due to the lack of information on its volume of distribution and protein binding, which may influence their efficiency. Until now, no information was available on the effect of renal replacement therapy on NaN3 elimination. One case report has been described, detailing a successful treatment with hemodialysis [ 20 ]. However, they did not measure NaN3 concentration in dialysate making definitive conclusions about the effectiveness of dialysis impossible. We observed elevated NaN3 concentrations in the ultrafiltrate (6.2 mg/L), confirming that HV-CVVH can effectively eliminate NaN3 from circulation. Unfortunately, real-time clearance could not be calculated due to the method of sample collection. As a result, a direct comparison between NaN3 clearance with HV-CVVH and renal clearance is not feasible due to the lack of sufficient data. It is, however, reasonable to assume that the profound shock commonly associated with NaN3 intoxication can significantly impair renal function, thereby reducing renal clearance and affecting overall plasma clearance. Based on these considerations, we believe that immediate initiation of HV-CVVH in all suspected NaN3 intoxications should be strongly considered. As the use of HV-CVVH is not universally feasible, CVVH or (continuous) hemodialysis will likely serve as a viable alternative to HV-CVVH based on the compound’s chemical characteristics. However, initiating intermittent hemodialysis in NaN3 intoxication can pose challenges due to the severe hemodynamic shock typically observed. Our decision to initiate HV-CVVH was primarily driven by the need for rapid correction of severe metabolic acidosis and restoration of buffer balance in a hemodynamically unstable patient, a scenario in which HV-CVVH is particularly effective.

We calculated a plasma half-life of NaN3 of 1.7 h during HV-CVVH and 2.3 h when HV-CVVH was used for only part of the measured period. Due to insufficient sampling, the half-life without HV-CVVH could not be determined. While we cannot definitively conclude that HV-CVVH shortens NaN3’s half-life, our data suggest it was longer when HV-CVVH was only partially used. The only available comparative data comes from a Japanese case report, which documented a half-life of 2.5 h in a patient without dialysis or hemofiltration [ 21 ].

After ingestion, NaN3 quickly binds to cytochrome C oxidase complex of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. Consequently, NaN3 remains in plasma for a relatively short time [ 22 ].﻿ This means that (HV-)CVVH (or hemodialysis) should be initiated as soon as possible after NaN3 ingestion to enhance elimination during its short plasma phase. Interestingly, in our patient the concentration of NaN3 in the urine remained very low, being undetectable in the first two measurements, especially when compared to the ultrafiltrate concentrations. This observation suggests a limited renal elimination of NaN3 in our patient, possibly due to profound hemodynamic shock. Furthermore, as the intoxication progressed, HV-CVVH likely assumed the primary role in NaN3 clearance.

Throughout the patient’s ICU and psychiatric ward admission, we utilized lactate concentration as a surrogate marker for impaired oxidative phosphorylation activity. Inhibition of complex IV in the oxidative phosphorylation system halts ATP production, causing cells to rely on glycolysis [ 4 , 5 ], which produces excess pyruvate. Normally, pyruvate is converted into acetyl-CoA for the Krebs cycle, generating NADH and FADH2 to fuel ATP production. However, when NAD + is depleted, the Krebs cycle halts, and pyruvate is diverted to lactate via lactate dehydrogenase. Lactate concentration rises when acetyl-CoA accumulation impedes pyruvate conversion. Although lactate is not a very specific biomarker, by following lactate levels in a single patient in time, the patient becomes its own reference, and this makes lactate a reliable marker for impaired oxidative phosphorylation activity. Benefiting from the well-established clinical utility of lactate and its widespread availability in hospitals.

The irreversible binding of NaN3 to cytochrome c oxidase may help explain the persistent hyperlactatemia, even after the resolution of hemodynamic shock. We hypothesize that the metabolic dysfunction continues as long as NaN3-bound cytochrome c oxidase remains present. Although the exact turnover rate of cytochrome c oxidase in humans is not well-defined, ongoing mitochondrial dysfunction could contribute to the patient's symptoms. These symptoms likely improved gradually as mitochondrial function and cytochrome c oxidase turnover were restored over time.

As an adjunctive potential therapeutic measure, we administered levocarnitine to our patient at a dosage of 1 g four times daily. The dosing regimen was based on the pathophysiological principles underlying primary carnitine deficiency, where recommended doses range from 20 to 40 mg/kg. Given the severity of the patient’s condition and the continuous elimination of levocarnitine via HV-CVVH, we opted for a slightly higher dose to ensure adequate therapeutic concentrations. The rationale for employing levocarnitine centered on the aim to optimize acetyl-CoA concentration within the mitochondria through the transportation of fatty acyl carnitine and the subsequent production of acetyl-CoA via beta-oxidation. This strategy aimed to stimulate the Krebs cycle, facilitating the maximal generation of NADH for utilization by the remaining cytochrome C oxidase unbound to NaN3 (Fig. 2). Notably, the administration of levocarnitine had an unexpectedly substantial impact on lactate levels. Initially, the decrease in lactate concentration was attributed to HV-CVVH, but a review of the literature suggests that the effect of HV-CVVH may be limited [ 23 , 24 ]. And the effect of levocarnitine persisted as lactate concentration increased upon levocarnitine discontinuation and subsequently normalized upon reintroduction, also in absence of HV-CVVH (Fig. 1). Fig. 2 illustrates NaN3 site of action within the oxidative phosphorylation pathway. The proposed biochemical mechanism of levocarnitine is highlighted in green

To try and elucidate this recurrent observation, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of amino acids, organic acids, and profiles of free carnitine and acylcarnitine in consecutive blood measurements. Our findings primarily revealed patterns indicative of oxidative phosphorylation blockade, characterized by elevated concentrations of pyruvate and acetyl-CoA (Tables S4, S5, S6 and S7 in Supplementary Appendix). Unfortunately, the identification of a clear metabolic pathway explaining the observed impact of levocarnitine on decreased lactate concentration remained elusive. Despite the absence of a definitive pathway, we assert that levocarnitine holds promise for application in NaN3 intoxications. This assertion is supported by three key factors: (1) the unique favorable clinical outcome observed in our patient, (2) the recurrent nature of the observed impact, and (3) the minimal harmful side effects associated with levocarnitine—particularly crucial in the context of NaN3 intoxication, where mortality rates are exceedingly high and therapeutic options are extremely limited.

In conclusion, we describe the full recovery of a patient ingesting a supralethal 3-g dose of NaN3. This was accomplished by applying a combination of supportive ICU care, ensuring plasma clearance by HV-CVVH and administering intravenous levocarnitine. Further investigation is required to elucidate the precise cellular metabolic role of levocarnitine in NaN3 intoxication and validate its efficacy in future cases.
