# Mosaic pathogenic variants in AKT3 cause capillary malformation and undergrowth.
**Authors:** Bolli A, Nriagu B, Britt AD, Toole AD, Treat J, Srinivasan A, Sheppard SE
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet A (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmg.a.63121](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmg.a.63121)

## Content

Capillary malformations are slow-flow vascular malformations that affect the microcirculation including capillaries and post capillary venules and can be associated with growth differences. Specifically, the association of capillary malformations with undergrowth is a vastly understudied vascular syndrome with few reports of genetic causes including PIK3CA, GNAQ, and GNA11. Recently, a somatic pathogenic variant in AKT3 was identified in one child with a cutaneous vascular syndrome similar to cutis marmorata telangiectatica congenita, undergrowth, and no neurodevelopmental features. Here, we present a male patient with a capillary malformation and undergrowth due to a somatic pathogenic variant in AKT3 to confirm this association. It is essential to consider that mosaic pathogenic variants in AKT3 can cause a wide spectrum of disease. There is a need for future studies focusing on capillary malformations with undergrowth to understand the underlying mechanism.

Capillary malformations (CMs) are slow-flow vascular malformations that affect the microcirculation including capillaries and post capillary venules (Happle, 2015). They typically present as a pink to red or purple patch depending on the skin phototype (Nriagu et al., 2021). CMs may be isolated or syndromic. CMs are commonly associated with hypertrophy or overgrowth and more rarely associated with undergrowth of the affected area (Lee et al., 2013). It is unclear whether this overgrowth is due to growth phenotypes due to the mosaic genetic cause or due to the increased blood flow to the area. The pathophysiology of the undergrowth associated with CMs is unknown.

Few studies have evaluated genetic causes of capillary malformations with undergrowth. Somatic pathogenic variants associated with undergrowth in vascular lesions have been previously reported in PIK3CA, TEK, GNAQ, and GNA11 (Martinez-Glez et al., 2022). The association of mosaic variants in PIK3CA with capillary malformations with undergrowth was confirmed by another group (Cubiró et al., 2020).

Recently, a single patient with a capillary malformation “reminiscent of cutis marmorata telangiectatica congenita” with undergrowth without neurodevelopmental features due to somatic pathogenic variant in AKT was reported (Davies et al., 2021). AKT3 is a serine/threonine protein-kinase that is involved in metabolism, angiogenesis, cell survival and growth. Abnormal regulation of the AKT3 gene can lead to cancer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and neurovascular disease (Hers et al., 2011). Here, we present the second a case of a male patient with a somatic pathogenic variant in AKT3 with features consistent with capillary malformation with undergrowth to confirm this association.

The parents provided informed consent for publication.

Clinical information was extracted from the medical record. A standard dysmorphology exam was performed as part of clinical care and included leg girth measurements. The thigh was measured 3 cm from the top of the patella. Calves were measured at the area of largest girth, 3 cm below the patella.

Somatic Overgrowth and Vascular Malformation Panel from the University of Pennsylvania Genomic Diagnostic Laboratory was performed from genomic DNA isolated from the capillary malformation and leukocytes. The panel contained the following 34 genes: AKT1, AKT2, AKT3, BRAF, CCM2, CCND2, CDKN1C, FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3, FLT4, GLMN, GNA11, GNA14, GNAQ, HRAS, IDH1, IDH2, KDR, KRAS, KRIT1, MAP2K1, MAP3K3, MTOR, NRAS, PDCD10, PIK3CA, PIK3R1, PIK3R2, PTEN, RASA1, SMO, STAMBP, and TEK.

A 5-year-old boy born at a gestational age of 37 weeks was referred to the multidisciplinary Comprehensive Vascular Anomalies Clinic at our institution for diagnosis and treatment of knee pain and a CM encompassing most of the left leg. He was seen by our multidisciplinary team of Medical Genetics, Radiology, Dermatology, Occupational Therapy, and Physical Therapy.

The patient was spontaneously conceived. He was born at a gestational age of 37 weeks by forceps-assisted vaginal delivery at 2.46 kg (22nd percentile) following an uncomplicated pregnancy. A fetal echocardiogram was performed due to his mother’s history of a right aortic arch. No fetal cardiac anomalies were identified. A CM was noted on the left leg shortly after birth. The legs appeared to be approximately the same size. There were no immediate postdelivery complications, and the patient was discharged home on the second day of life.

Around 1 month of age, the family noticed the girth difference of the legs, with the left leg smaller than the right (Figure 1a,b). There were purple-brown patches that extended from the ankle to the hip. Due to the CM, at 2 months of age, he was evaluated by a dermatologist and a vascular surgeon who initially thought the affected limb was suggestive of Klippel Trenaunay Syndrome (KTS). An MRI of the left lower extremity with and without contrast was performed at 2 months of age and showed normal vasculature. The imaging demonstrated undergrowth of the left lower extremity, involving the bones, musculature, and subcutaneous fat (Figure 2). A biopsy was recommended for further diagnosis but was not completed at that time. A hip ultrasound performed at 1 month of age for family history of hip dysplasia was negative. Previous orthopedic evaluations occurred at 4 months, 18 months, 25 months, and 5 years. Leg length was noted to be equal clinically. Radiographs of the knees due to knee pain were normal at 25 months of age.

The patient had a normal developmental history. He rolled at 4 months, sat at 6 months, and walked at 13 months of age. His first words were at about 9 months, and he spoke in sentences at 2 years of age. A full three-generation pedigree showed no family history of capillary malformation. His mother had a history of right aortic arch and left hip dysplasia. There was no family history of developmental delays, multiple miscarriages, or stillbirths.

On physical exam, he had similar facial features to his parents, capillary malformation, and undergrowth. Growth parameters showed length at the 28th percentile, weight at the 15th percentile, and head circumference at the 52nd percentile. He had no frontal bossing and no hypertelorism. He had slightly hypoplastic alae nasi and a normal nasal bridge and tip. The hands and feet had no clinodactyly, macrodactyly, nor polydactyly. There was slight partial 2–3 syndactyly of the left foot. The left lower extremity had purple-brown patches extending from the left foot to the left hip (Figure 1c,d). The smallest patch was on the dorsal side of the foot while the largest was on the lateral side of the left thigh extending distally to the top of the knee and down the shin. The affected left thigh measured 21.5 cm around compared to the unaffected right thigh, which measured 23.8 cm around. The left calf measured 18.5 cm around and the right measured 19.9 cm around. The left extremity was smaller than the right—10.7% and 7.6% for the thighs and calves, respectively. Foot length and width were equal bilaterally.

Genetic testing was recommended and identified a somatic pathogenic variant in AKT3 c.49G > A (p.Glu17Lys) from genomic DNA isolated from capillary malformation skin at a variant allele frequency between 4.3% and 4.8%. The variant was absent from genomic DNA isolated from blood.

The patient was referred to physical therapy since his mother noted a change in his gait during running or following increased physical activity, along with occasional leg pain. His physical therapy examination found a subtle strength asymmetry between legs, limited single leg balance on the left side, and preferential use of the right leg during running, jumping, and stair negotiation. Following six sessions of physical therapy over 6 weeks to address these impairments, his symmetry and strength were improved, but he continued to experience leg pain with increased physical activity.

In our patient, his initial dermatologist and our team recognized the purple-brown patches as a CM. It is important to note that given the patient’s darker skin phenotype the CM is harder to recognize. CMs in individuals with darker skin phototypes can be mistaken for hyperpigmentation, delaying diagnosis and appropriate medical care (Nriagu et al., 2021). Clinicians should be aware of how to recognize CMs in Black or Indigenous and People of Color and how they differ from the majority of CMs in the literature.

Pathogenic variants in several genes have been implicated in CM with undergrowth. Undergrowth with vascular lesions was observed in patients who had somatic pathogenic variants in PIK3CA, TEK, GNAQ, and GNA11 (Cubiró et al., 2020; Martinez-Glez et al., 2022). Interestingly, somatic pathogenic variants in PIK3CA more commonly cause overgrowth (Cubiró et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2013).

These genes do not completely encompass the genomic spectrum for CMs with undergrowth. In our patient, genetic testing revealed a somatic pathogenic variant c.49G > A (p.Glu17Lys) in AKT3. This variant has been reported in three other individuals with CM with undergrowth. However, only one of three patients with the AKT3 variant presented with a similar undergrowth phenotype described as having an “atrophy of the underlying subcutaneous dermis and fat on the left side of the lateral aspect of the torso … buttock … thigh” (Davies et al., 2021). This patient also presented with normal neurological development and head circumference and thus there was no need for brain imaging. Similarly, our patient had a normal neurological development and head circumference, and we did not recommend brain MRI. The remaining two patients presented with hemimegalencephaly or megalencephaly, CM, and undergrowth (Davies et al., 2021). The individuals in the previous article were described to have a reticulated capillary malformation “reminiscent of cutis marmorata telangiectatica congenita,” whereas our patient had a confluent capillary malformation. In our patient, the patches were broad in most areas, which is more consistent with capillary malformation compared to the thin livedo pattern as would be seen in cutis marmorata telangiectatica congenita.

Atrophy, hypotrophy, or undergrowth has been associated both with cutis marmorata telangiectatica congenita and with capillary malformations. Cutis marmorata telangiectatica congenita is a subtype of capillary malformation, as defined by the 2018 International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies Classification (Wassef et al., 2015), which can be confused with a reticulated capillary malformation. Cutis marmorata telangiectatica congenita can be characterized by congenital presentation of reticulate erythema, which does not respond to local warming and absence of venectasia. Pink or red color is also typical of a capillary malformation compared to the thin, purple livedo pattern of cutis marmorata telangiectatica congenita, though the coloration helps to distinguish one from the other in darker skin phototypes. About a quarter of patients have body asymmetry and about 5% of patients have atrophy (Kienast & Hoeger, 2009). Additional associated features seen in subset of patients include telangiectasia, ulceration and fading during the first 2 years of life (Kienast & Hoeger, 2009). In contrast, capillary malformations may be associated with undergrowth of the musculoskeletal system and dermal atrophy. In the largest retrospective study of 37 participants with vascular malformations with undergrowth, almost all participants had muscle undergrowth (92%) (Martinez-Glez et al., 2022). Bone undergrowth was more common than subcutaneous hypoplasia (68% compared to 24%) (Martinez-Glez et al., 2022). In our patient, MRI at 2 years of age demonstrated undergrowth of bones, musculature, and subcutaneous fat. Imaging has not been repeated to evaluate the specific tissue currently contributing to the asymmetry in our patient.

Interestingly, megalencephaly-capillary malformation syndrome, characterized by CM, megalencephaly, and overgrowth, is less commonly caused by AKT3 (p.Arg465Trp and p.Asn229Ser) (Rivière et al., 2012). As shown in these studies, megalencephaly is strongly associated with AKT3-related syndromes (Alcantara et al., 2017; Davies et al., 2021; Dobyns & Mirzaa, 2019; Rivière et al., 2012). Our patient’s presentation of CM with undergrowth demonstrates the spectrum of AKT3-related syndromes. It is possible that the manifestations are due to the tissue distribution of the variant; however, further research is needed to understand the how the same variant may cause undergrowth and overgrowth. Given that AKT3 is downstream of PIK3CA, is likely that there are similar mechanisms of disease caused by mosaic variants in either.

Although the genetic testing panel used in this case is labeled as an overgrowth panel, it does contain genes to assess the undergrowth phenotype. The somatic overgrowth and vascular malformation panel used in this patient is limited to 34 genes accounting for most of the common genes associated with vascular malformations. It is important to realize that AKT3 is not included on all vascular panels for genetic testing. Physicians should be aware of the CM with undergrowth and its association with AKT3 before ordering tests to determine whether a somatic variant in this gene is the cause.

In summary, this case supports the previous association that activating mosaic variants in AKT3 cause localized CM with undergrowth. It is essential for clinicians to recognize CMs in patients with darker skin phototypes and to choose a somatic panel that includes sequencing of AKT3 when assessing patients with capillary malformation and undergrowth. Further studies are needed to evaluate the incidence of AKT3 as a cause for capillary malformation and undergrowth. Further research is needed to elucidate the mechanism of undergrowth and overgrowth caused by recurrent variants in PI3K-AKT signaling.
