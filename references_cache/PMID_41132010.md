# A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis on the Prevalence of Brucella melitensis in Sheep and Goat.
**Authors:** Das S, Das P, Das A, Hasib FMY, Chowdhury S
**Journal:** Vet Med Sci (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/vms3.70667](https://doi.org/10.1002/vms3.70667)

## Content

Brucellosis is one of the neglected zoonotic diseases (NZD) in the world that causes disease globally. Brucella melitensis, one of the organisms responsible for causing the disease in sheep and goat, is responsible for the disease in humans. The disease is transmitted mainly from sheep and goat to humans via ingestion (typically through milk), inhalation, abrasion and so on. Despite the zoonotic importance of the disease, there is lack of comprehensive data such as overall prevalence around the world is yet to be measured. The aim of this study is to review all the available articles on the prevalence of the infection in sheep and goats systematically and determine the overall prevalence around the world. Therefore, a literature search was conducted in PubMed for articles published from January 2010 to December 2023 on the relevant topic. The inclusion criteria were set to extract relevant data from the selected articles. A random effect meta-analysis model was used in analysing the data. The derived prevalence was presented in percentage along with 95% confidence interval (CI). The pooled prevalence of B. melitensis infection globally in goat was 2.46% (95% CI: 1.70%-3.21%) and in sheep was 8.07% (95% CI: 6.36%-9.78%); the combined pooled prevalence in both sheep and goat was 5.54% (95% CI: 4.63%-6.45%). Overall, on the basis of regions, the pooled prevalence in goat ranged from 0.07% (95% CI: -0.08% to 0.22%) to 16.82% (95% CI: 12.69%-20.95%). In sheep, the regional pooled prevalence ranged from 0.03% (95% CI: -0.03% to 0.08%) to 60.39% (95% CI: 58.50%-62.27%). This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated the global burden of B. melitensis infection in sheep and goat that would help better understanding the global distribution of the infection and the potential areas of zoonotic transmission. The data have been retrieved from various types of journals with different types of study designs and were analysed to highlight the variation of prevalence across the countries, among different species, diagnostic methods and sample sources. This knowledge would help in planning effective control measures targeting high risk species, areas and so on.

Brucellosis, despite worldwide efforts of continuous surveillance and screening, remains a major health concern worldwide. In both human and animal health sectors, it has been called in multiple names worldwide, to name a few: Malta fever, Mediterranean fever, Undulant fever, Contagious abortion, Bang's disease, Neapolitan fever, Crimean fever, and Corps disease (Moreno 2014). Each year, people suffering from Brucellosis enrol in different hospitals, especially those living near livestock. There is an occupational risk to veterinarians, abattoir workers and farmers who handle infected animals/carcasses and aborted foetuses or placentas (Pereira et al. 2020). Yet there is still much to be known about the disease patterns worldwide.


Brucella was first isolated in 1887 by David Bruce, a microbiologist, from the spleens of four soldiers in Malta Island (Rossetti et al. 2022). It was later demonstrated by Thmistokles Zammit, a Greek physician who was working with Bruce, that the soldiers were being affected by the organism as they were drinking unpasteurized goat milk that transmitted the organism from the affected goats (Wyatt 2005). At the same time, a physician–veterinarian named Bernard Bang studied a disease in cattle in Denmark, then known as ‘contagious abortion’, and isolated the causal agent, naming it Bacillus abortus (Bang 1897). Alice Evans, a microbiologist, later showed in 1918 that it was the causal agent of brucellosis in humans and was very much similar, both morphologically and biochemically, to the organism that Bruce discovered (Evans 1918). Later, this group of organisms was brought under the genus ‘Brucella’ by a veterinary scientist, Karl Meyer, settling the issue with the nomenclature (Steele 2008).

Brucellosis is caused by a genus of intracellular bacteria that are Gram‐negative, non‐motile, facultative anaerobic coccobacilli (Alton and Forsyth 1996). The bacteria target a wide spectrum of mammals, including humans, cattle, sheep and goat (Cutler et al. 2005). There are 12 identified species within Brucella genus, of which three species, B. melitensis, B. abortus and B. suis, are observed to be associated with diseases in humans and animals in most cases (Dahouk et al. 2017). Among those, B. abortus affects cattle; B. melitensis affects small ruminants such as sheep and goats; and pigs are the host of B. suis (Spickler 2018).


B. melitensis is transmitted generally from small ruminants to humans through ingestion of the organism, direct contact with contaminated mucous membranes, and abraded skin (Spickler 2018). In small ruminants, the organism causes middle to late‐term gestation abortion along with stillbirths and birth of weakened offspring, often followed by a retention of placenta (Rossetti et al. 2022). These symptoms are about the only clinical signs shown by the affected animals. In male animals, some rare cases of unilateral or bilateral epididymo‐orchitis may be observed (Ahmad and Niaz 1998; Ali et al. 2019; Chand et al. 2002). In humans, the effect of the organism may range from being asymptomatic to developing multiple syndromes. In acute form, patients may exhibit diverse symptoms such as fever, chills, headache, myalgia and lymphadenopathy (Corbel et al. 2006). It can affect the genital system of male animals, causing epididymo‐orchitis, prostatitis, and seminal vesiculitis. In pregnant women, abortion and premature birth are observed (Corbel et al. 2006). Additionally, among congenitally infected infants, sepsis, pneumonia and other syndromes have been observed (Corbel et al. 2006).

Over half a million new cases occur in humans annually, and prevalence rates in some countries exceed 10 cases per 100,000 population (Norouzinezhad et al. 2021). The estimated annual global incidence of brucellosis is nearly 2.1 million. Although Africa and Asia are most at risk of the disease, it is still a concern for the Americas and Europe (Laine et al. 2023). The worldwide prevalence of brucellosis in men is 15.27% and in women 15.33% (Khoshnood et al. 2022).

The baseline prevalence of brucellosis in livestock worldwide has been measured, ranging from 1.4% to 20% (Kiiza et al. 2023). In another study, it was observed that the prevalence of the disease in both Asia and Africa was 8% in the livestock population (Suresh et al. 2022). Brucellosis is evidently endemic in humans and livestock in Bangladesh (Rahman et al. 2017). In a study published in 1983, it was found that the prevalence of brucellosis in dairy and agricultural workers in Bangladesh was 12.8% and in goat farmers was 21.6% (Rahman 1983). Another study conducted in the Mymensingh district found 11.1%, 6.5% and 4.7% of sero‐prevalence among veterinary personnel, dairy workers and animal farmers, respectively (Muhammad et al. 2010). Prevalence was higher in individuals drinking raw milk and farmers handling goats.

Brucellosis is one of the most economically significant zoonotic diseases impacting humans, livestock and wildlife (Shekhar 2018). It was observed that although vaccination was cost‐effective in controlling brucellosis, test‐and‐slaughter was not (Kiiza et al. 2023). Although most of the data on economic impact are available for high‐ and middle‐income countries, the low‐income countries suffer the most financially (McDermott et al. 2013). In Africa, the production loss caused by bovine brucellosis is consistently estimated at 6%–10% of the income per animal (Suresh et al. 2022). In Nigeria, in the last century, the yearly loss was estimated at $575,605, whereas in Argentina it was $60 million per year (McDermott et al. 2013). Among the south‐east Asian countries, India has suffered a median loss of $3.4 billion due to brucellosis (Singh et al. 2015), whereas another study suggests a loss of $58.8 million every year (Kollannur et al. 2007).

To understand the disease further and to prevent any future incidence, a holistic approach is paramount. A systematic review and meta‐analysis on the topic will paint a global picture that will help us understand the disease better. Despite being discovered more than a century ago, there is still a lack of comprehensive methods on how to control and prevent the disease successfully and economically. To make that possible, the prevalence of the disease worldwide, under different conditions, needs to be understood. Although there are many scientific journals reporting the prevalence in small ruminants, there is still none with a pooled prevalence demonstrating the status quo worldwide. Furthermore, the reported prevalence widely varies around the world, due to different factors such as methodology of the study, varying geographic conditions, sample source, strategy of sampling, duration of study and diagnostic approaches. This study aims to provide clarity on the varying nature of the prevalence through a systematic review and meta‐analysis, demonstrating the differentiation in different countries, methods and so on. The study was conducted according to the guidelines provided by PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) for systemic review and meta‐analysis.

The literature search was performed systematically to identify all published articles on prevalence of B. melitensis in sheep and goats. The search was performed only in PubMed for articles published between 2010 and 2023. A number of keywords were selected and combined using Boolean operator ‘AND’, ‘OR’ etc.

The search algorithm targeted (sero‐prevalence OR sero‐prevalence OR prevalence OR incidence OR occurrence OR survey OR case OR frequency OR detection OR identification OR isolation OR characterization OR rate OR investigation OR diagnosis) and (B. melitensis OR Brucellosis OR Brucella) AND (goat OR goats OR buck OR bucks OR doe OR caprine OR capra OR ovine OR small ruminant OR small ruminants OR sheep OR ram OR rams OR ewe OR ewes) in Title/Abstract. Additionally, manual screening of reference lists of published articles was done to minimize the chance of missing relevant articles. Restrictions were placed on the article type (research articles only) and the timeframe of publication (2010–2023) and language (English only).

The result generated in PubMed was imported in Microsoft Excel 2013, and duplicate records were identified and removed. Two separate authors (S.D. and P.D.) then screened the remaining records by title and abstract, followed by full‐text screening to meet the inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved by the third author (Y.H.).

Each eligible article was thoroughly studied, and the following information were extracted: author, year of publication, country, host, sample source, sample size, method of detection/diagnosis, positive cases and prevalence.

We prepared two separate worksheets for sheep and goat for the analysis. Data were sorted, cleaned, coded and recoded in Microsoft Excel 2013. Using a summarized Excel datasheet with the selected articles for the meta‐analysis, descriptive summary statistics with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p values were generated using the software package STATA/IC‐17.0 (Stata Corp, 4905 Lakeway Drive, College Station, TX, USA). Random effect meta‐analyses (REM) were performed using ‘metan’ command with the datasets separately for goats and sheep. REM was used as it considers the fact that there is heterogeneity in the effect estimate; in contrast, a fixed‐effect model (FEM) assumes the presence of no heterogeneity between studies, making it a less appropriate choice for this study. The study variables were evaluated using the chi‐square test on Cochran's Q statistics (with p values), followed by I
2 statistics to determine the degree of heterogeneity (I
2 > 75%). To identify the qualitative bias in the articles funnel‐plots were done. The asymmetrical funnel plot and points falling outside the funnel indicated publication bias. Egger's test was performed to evaluate small‐study effects. A p value of less than 0.05 indicated the presence of a small study effect. Data visualizations were conducted using Microsoft Excel 2013 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA), R software (version 4.4.2; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) via RStudio (version 2024.12.0; RStudio PBC, Boston, MA, USA) and QGIS (version 3.22.10; QGIS Development Team).

Selected studies were evaluated for quality of reporting and selection for bias using a quality appraisal checklist. The quality assessment score was assigned according to the objectives of the study. Eleven questions (Supporting Information Appendix A) were arranged to assess the quality of the studies with appropriate ‘yes’ and ‘no’ options. A ‘yes’ to a question received a score of 1, whereas a ‘no’ received a 0. Articles scoring 0–3 were counted as low quality, scores of 4–7 were counted as moderate quality, and 8–11 were counted as high quality (Figure 1).

In the case of sheep however, the highest prevalence was found at 30.34% when ELISA was used and the lowest at 1.18% when PCR method was used. Combined prevalence was the highest at 30.34% and lowest at 0.3% for ELISA and Multiplex PCR, respectively (Table 1).

From the six types of sample sources used in these studies, the highest prevalence for goat was observed at 9.93% when blood sample was used and the lowest prevalence at 0.24% when tissue samples were used. A similar pattern is observed in the case of sheep as well where the highest was at 14.10%, and the lowest was at 0.28% for blood and tissue samples, respectively. To the best of the author's knowledge, no other meta‐analysis has been done on the prevalence of B. melitensis in sheep and goat based on the literature published between 2010 and 2023.

Most of the studies were from Asia, especially the Middle Eastern region and Africa, with a few countries from Europe, South America and North America. No studies from Oceania matched the inclusion criteria of this study. The reason could be the selection of only one database as the source of data. There is also a probability that a lack of publication on this subject is present in certain regions, such as South America. The lack of publication from the developed countries could be because of the complete or partial eradication of the disease, rendering it relatively irrelevant.

The meta‐analysis found the pooled prevalence of goat to be lesser at 2.46% (95% CI: 1.70%–3.21%), whereas the pooled prevalence of sheep is much higher at 8.07% (95% CI: 6.36%–9.78%). Additionally, the combined pooled prevalence of both species is 5.54% (95% CI: 4.63%–6.45%). This demonstrates the uneven distribution of the organism between both species around the world.

Our systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrate the widespread prevalence of B. melitensis around the world. The organism was most prevalent in Syria at 60.39% (95% CI: 58.50%–62.27%, I
2 = 100%) and least prevalent in Georgia at 0.03% (95% CI: −0.03% to 0.08%, I
2 = 0.0%) in both goat and sheep. Brucellosis is considered to be endemic in Georgia, with the possibility of underreporting of the disease (Akhvlediani et al. 2017). Historically, Syria is considered to be the country that had the highest human brucellosis burden, reporting a total of 137,609 cases between 2007 and 2019 (Liu et al. 2024). The pooled prevalence was 4.37% (95% CI: 1.21%–7.53%) and 3.68% (95% CI: 0.95%–6.42%) in goats and sheep, respectively, in Iran. This resonates to the fact that most part of the country is considered endemic for brucellosis, with the annual incidence in both humans and animals at a high level (Golshani and Buozari 2017). Pooled prevalence of the organism in goats in Oman and the Arabian Gulf region was 0.3% (95% CI: −0.3% to 0.9%) and 0.24% (95% CI: 0.03%–0.45%), respectively. Human brucellosis is endemic in most of the Middle Eastern countries, and the highest incidence of brucellosis has been reported in that region, including countries, such as Syria, Turkey, Iraq and Oman. A similar endemic scenario can be seen in the case of brucellosis in small ruminants in the middle‐eastern countries as well (Liu et al. 2024).

In Africa, another systematic review and meta‐analysis found an 8% prevalence of Brucellosis among the livestock of Africa (Suresh et al. 2022). In our study, a larger range of prevalence can be observed in the subjects, with the values for pooled prevalence in goat and sheep ranging from 3.84% to 16.82% and 0.3% to 24.05%, respectively.

Among the South Asian countries, Pakistan showed 8.94% (95% CI: −5.6% to 23.48%) and 3.45% (95% CI: 0.94%–5.96%) pooled prevalence in goats and sheep, respectively, whereas the combined pooled prevalence stood at 6.26% (95% CI: 0.99%–11.52%). Brucellosis is considered endemic, and prevalence is often high in both ruminants and humans in Pakistan (Jamil et al. 2021). India had 1.81% (95% CI: 0.79%–2.83%) prevalence in goats, whereas a previously conducted meta‐analysis showed a 12% prevalence of Brucellosis in Indian livestock population (Suresh et al. 2022).

Additionally, China showed a prevalence of 4.44% (95% CI: 1.43%–7.45%) in goats. The disease remains a major public health concern in the country, with outbreaks in humans reported in 25 out of 32 provinces and autonomous regions (Wen et al. 2024). The country reported 684,293 cases of human brucellosis from 2004 to 2021.

The meta‐analysis demonstrated considerable heterogeneity in all cases of the study, indicating significant variability between studies. The extent of the bias in the selected studies was demonstrated by the funnel plot. Two funnel plots were done for both goats and sheep. The funnel plots were clearly asymmetrical, with most of the points falling outside of the funnel indicating publication bias. The sources of the asymmetry were tested by the Egger test, which shows bias towards studies with larger effect sizes. However, there are many different possible factors for funnel plot asymmetry, namely, selection bias, true heterogeneity, data irregularities, artefact, as well as by chance (Egger et al. 1997).

The majority of the sample source was from blood and serum, whereas other sources included milk, reproductive tissues, other tissues and visceral organs. Automated systems have been reported to detect more than 95% of B. melitensis‐positive cultures within 7 days of incubation. The ‘gold standard’ for laboratory diagnosis of brucellosis is known to be PCR. However, most of the studies selected used that method as a final diagnostic tool, preceded by other diagnostic techniques. Studies using serological tests were included only when using B. melitensis–specific antigen. The majority of the studies adopted PCR as the preferred diagnostic method; others included ELISA, c‐ELISA, RBPT, SPAT, STAT, SAT, multiplex PCR and RT‐PCR. Most of the studies using PCR as the diagnostic method of choice had the samples screened with more economical tests, such as RBPT and CFT at first. Although being expensive for least developed and developing countries, the PCR test is considered more sensitive than blood culture and more specific than serological tests in detecting Brucellosis (Dahouk et al. 2013). In the case of sensitivity, the ELISA test has been shown to be highly sensitive for the detection of brucellosis compared to SAT and PCR (Hajia et al. 2013). Additionally, although highly sensitive, RBPT often produces false positive results, rendering the test fit for screening process only (Legesse et al. 2023). The SAT test has proven to be less efficient than other tests such as ELISA and PCR (Hajia et al. 2013). Compared to conventional PCR, RT‐PCR is considered to have more sensitivity and specificity for detecting Brucella spp. in blood and serum samples (Navarro et al. 2002). Another study found the sensitivity of SPAT test to be greater than STAT test and less than RBPT (Stemshorn et al. 1985). In the case of specificity, c‐ELISA shows higher specificity than the RBPT test (Perrett et al. 2010).

For diagnostic purposes, the selection of sample source (e.g., blood, serum and tissue) depends on the context of the research, observed clinical signs, accessibility, and so on. For clinical brucellosis, different organs of the aborted foetus (stomach, spleen and lung), placenta, vaginal secretions, milk, sperm, epididymis and others make valid samples. During slaughter, to confirm suspected cases of brucellosis, tissues from different organs, such as genital and oropharyngeal lymph nodes, spleen, mammary gland and surrounding lymph nodes, are collected. Most of the studies used blood and serum as their sample source. The second most collected type of sample is milk. Consumption of raw milk and milk‐based products produced under questionable control measures has been identified as the leading cause of brucellosis in humans; incident rates vary from 33.9% to 100% (Islam et al. 2023). Furthermore, the most important organisms causing brucellosis through milk have been proven to be B. abortus and B. melitensis (Frank and Hassan 2002). A few of the studies used tissues, including visceral organs and reproductive tissues as well. The organism has shown tropism, the phenomenon where a certain organism shows tendency to behave in a certain way for example, moving to a certain part or organ of the host such as liver, lungs and so on, for organs containing large population of phagocytes such as lymph nodes, spleen and liver, also, for organs of the genital system, such as the uterus, epididymis, testis and placenta (De Carvalho et al. 2023). Each study collected samples befitting their aims and objectives. In the case of liquid samples, compared to blood samples, serum samples are more optimum as specimen for diagnosing brucellosis via PCR in the case of diagnosis in humans (Zerva et al. 2001).

There were a number of studies where the authors did not report important information such as study duration, sampling strategy and site of study/location. Moreover, in some cases, the authors had to read between the lines to extract necessary information. And so, the authors of such studies should take utmost care in mentioning the necessary information clearly and concisely to make the studies more impactful.

The key limitation of the study is that the search was limited to only one database—PubMed. The lack of coverage of any other databases makes the study less universal and homogenous. As no database alone covers all relevant studies, the study misses out on some of the most scientifically wide‐scoped databases such as Web of Science and Scopus. The reason behind not including other databases was the inability of getting access as the authors did not have the necessary subscription. Alternative databases that used to provide free access have recently stopped doing so such as the CABI digital library, ultimately leaving us with only PubMed, Consequently, the study may have been exposed to publication bias as different databases show affinity towards different types of results and methods of a study. And so, due to a lack of databases covered, the results have a probability of being less generalizable. Some other limitations are worth mentioning to consider while interpreting the result. First, the review only included English literature, which has likely resulted in the exclusion of a few non‐English studies that were relevant. The restriction was primarily due to resource and language constraints, which limited our ability to reliably extract and interpret non‐English data. Second, some of the data were excluded as the prevalence in sheep and goat or other species was merged and could not be extracted properly. Third, some of the relevant studies were excluded as they discussed data from aborted population, such as aborted foetuses, populations that proved positive in a previous survey or study, and so on. If merged with other studies, the prevalence rate might have become unusually high, leading to a misinterpretation of the existing condition. Fourth, a few of the included studies have a very low sample population that were included, which may not represent the current condition of brucellosis in that particular country accurately. This might lead to an overestimation or underestimation of the data. Additionally, although the study focused only on B. melitensis, brucellosis in small ruminants may involve more than one species. It has been documented that in mixed herds, there is a crossover of B. melitensis with B. abortus and occasionally with B. ovis. Our focus on only one organism is due to its status as a predominantly etiological agent and to minimize heterogeneity in the analysis of the data. Finally, the majority of the studies implemented multiple diagnostic tests to determine the prevalence. Even though only the final specific diagnostic test is mentioned, other screening tests were also implemented on the same samples. The authors selected only the tests that had more sensitivity and specificity than the other tests in the same study.

This systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrated the prevalence of B. melitensis globally that would help in understanding the global burden of the organism in sheep and goat. The data have been retrieved from various types of journals with different types of study designs and were analysed to highlight the variation of prevalence across the countries, among different species, diagnostic methods and sample sources. This knowledge would help in planning effective control measures to effectively prevent and control the disease by adopting appropriate policy measures.

The findings highlight the variation in prevalence of B. melitensis around the world that points to a high prevalence in the middle‐eastern region and some countries in Africa as well. Reasons for the causes need to be explored and stringent health regulations need to be undertaken to tackle the disease in these regions. There is an urgent need for an upgrade in surveillance system, diagnostic facilities, awareness programs, vaccination campaign and so forth. Furthermore, the study discusses the currently used methods of diagnosis of the disease along with the best options for diagnosis recognized internationally and emphasizes the need for better diagnostic tools with improved sensitivity and specificity to get more accurate results. It further elaborates on the importance of choosing appropriate sample source based on the aim of the study. However, the study also shows a significant level of heterogeneity and publication bias and urges the readers to interpret the data with caution.
