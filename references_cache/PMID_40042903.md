# BIN1 reduction ameliorates DNM2-related Charcot-Marie-Tooth neuropathy.
**Authors:** Goret M, Thomas M, Edelweiss E, Messaddeq N, Laporte J
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2419244122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2419244122)

## Content

Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) disease, the most common inherited neuromuscular disorder, manifests as progressive muscle weakness and peripheral nerve defects. Dominant mutations in DNM2, encoding the large GTPase dynamin 2, result in CMT without any suggested therapeutic strategy. Different dominant mutations in DNM2 also cause centronuclear myopathy (CNM), and increasing BIN1 (amphiphysin 2), an endogenous modulator of DNM2, rescued CNM in mice. Here, we found that increasing BIN1 level exacerbated the phenotypes of the Dnm2K562E/+ mouse carrying the most common DNM2-CMT mutation. Conversely, whole-body reduction of Bin1 expression level, through the generation of Dnm2K562E/+ mice with heterozygous loss of BIN1, restored motor performance and ameliorated muscle organization and structural defects of peripheral nerves. The rescue of motor defects was maintained at least up to 1 y of age. BIN1 inhibited the GTPase activity of DNM2, and the rescue was driven by an increased activity of the K562E DNM2-CMT mutant, and a normalization of integrin localization in muscle. Overall, this study highlights BIN1 as a modifier of DNM2-CMT, and its reduction as a potential therapeutic strategy. It also revealed an opposite pathological mechanism and inverse therapeutic concepts for DNM2-CMT peripheral neuropathy versus DNM2-CNM myopathy.

Charcot–Marie–Tooth (CMT) disease, also known as hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy, stands as one of the most prevalent inherited neurological disorders, affecting approximately one in 2,500 individuals ( 1 , 2 ). This condition is characterized by peripheral nerve impairments, correlating with progressive muscle weakness starting in lower extremities, often accompanied by sensory deficits. One of the defining features of CMT is its genetic complexity, with over 100 associated genes. Classification of CMT cases is based on nerve conduction velocity (NCV) and results in three distinct types: demyelinating (NCV<35 m/s), axonal (NCV>45 m/s), or intermediate (NCV between 35 to 45 m/s), with intermediate forms exhibiting both axon and myelin defects. Further subtypes are identified based on inheritance patterns and specific genetic causes. Despite the prevalence of CMT, no therapy is currently approved for this disease ( 3 , 4 ).

Dominant mutations in the DNM2 gene, which encodes the dynamin 2 protein, have been reported to cause an intermediate form of the disease (DI-CMTB; MIM#606482) ( 5 , 6 ), as well as a purely axonal (CMT2M) ( 7 ). DNM2 is a ubiquitously expressed GTPase, playing critical roles in various cellular processes, including endocytosis, membrane trafficking, and cytoskeleton remodeling ( 8 – 10 ). A dozen of different DNM2-CMT mutations have been documented ( 11 ). Most of them localize to the PH (Pleckstrin Homology) domain ( 10 – 12 ), disrupting interactions with membrane lipids, and consequently reducing GTPase and membrane fission activities ( 13 , 14 ). Consistently, studies in mice have shown that deleting DNM2 in Schwann cells results in severe peripheral nervous system defects, with impaired axonal organization and myelination, likely due to increased apoptosis, cell cycle dysregulation, cytoskeletal abnormalities, and endocytic deficits ( 15 ). Patients carrying DNM2-CMT mutations exhibit reduced NCV, nerve fiber structural defects, progressive muscle weakness and atrophy, diminished coordination, skeletal deformities, and may display additional symptoms such as ptosis, ophthalmoparesis, cataracts, or neutropenia ( 16 – 18 ). The K562E heterozygous missense is the most prevalent mutation ( 5 ). In accordance, the Dnm2K562E/+ mouse is viable and displays motor deficit and muscle atrophy together with mild peripheral nerve defects ( 19 ).

Noteworthy, different dominant mutations in DNM2 are associated with centronuclear myopathy (CNM) (MIM#160150), a congenital myopathy with muscle weakness and myofiber structural disorganization ( 20 ). While most CNM mutations are also located in the PH domain, they are predicted to disturb the interaction between the PH and stalk domains, promoting an active conformation, and are associated with increased GTPase and membrane fission activities ( 11 , 13 , 14 , 21 , 22 ). Consistently, studies have shown that hyperactive DNM2 mutations induce excessive membrane fission in muscle cells, and proposed that the fragmentation of T-tubules impairs excitation–contraction coupling, thereby resulting in muscle weakness ( 14 , 23 ). Importantly, previous preclinical studies have shown that DNM2 downregulation cures DNM2-CNM in mice ( 24 – 26 ). In addition, DNM2 is negatively regulated in skeletal muscle by Bridging Interactor 1 (BIN1) ( 27 ), involved in membrane curvature and tubulation. Other studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of increasing BIN1 levels to rescue a Dnm2-CNM mouse model ( 28 ), likely by mitigating excessive DNM2 activity, thereby improving T-tubule maintenance.

While preclinical therapeutic trials have addressed DNM2-CNM, investigations into DNM2-CMT remain unexplored. Here, we first tested whether increasing BIN1 levels can also rescue DNM2-CMT and strikingly found it exacerbated the Dnm2K562E/+ mouse phenotypes. Conversely, decreasing BIN1 levels rescued most neuromuscular defects, highlighting an opposite therapeutic concept for the DNM2-CMT neuropathy versus the DNM2-CNM myopathy.

To assess whether increasing BIN1 levels, that was shown to rescue a DNM2-CNM mouse model, can also rescue DNM2-CMT, we crossed Dnm2K562E/+ CMT mice with BIN1 transgenic mice (TgBIN1), the later presenting a 3.3-fold BIN1 overexpression compared to wild-type (WT) in the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle (Fig. 1A). None of the TgBIN1 Dnm2K562E/+ fetuses, which appeared healthy and viable at E18.5, survived the first days after birth (Fig. 1B). Among the 33 mice genotyped at P8, none were TgBIN1 Dnm2K562E/+, indicating that BIN1 overexpression in Dnm2K562E/+ mice is lethal (Fig. 1C). These results highlight the opposite pathomechanisms underlying CNM and CMT diseases linked to DNM2 mutations. Consequently, we evaluated the benefit of BIN1 downregulation for DNM2-CMT.

To investigate the benefit of decreasing BIN1 in DNM2-CMT, we crossed the Bin1ex20+/- mouse with a constitutive heterozygous Bin1 knockout, with the Dnm2K562E/+ CMT mouse and compared their behavioral and locomotor activities with Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ littermates at 8 wk (Fig. 2A). RT-qPCR and western blot analyses confirmed an approximate 50 % reduction of Bin1 RNA and protein expression in both groups with Bin1 heterozygous knockout (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B).

Dnm2K562E/+ mice presented no difference of body weight compared to WT, but a 5 mm decreased body length (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Dnm2K562E/+ mice also showed impaired motor performance, as evidenced by a decreased hanging time and a two to threefold reduction in locomotor and rear activities (Fig. 2 C–E). By contrast, Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ mice had a normal body length, and restored motor performance, as indicated by normalized hanging time and activity. Overall, Bin1ex20+/- Dnm2K562E/+ exhibited a rescued motor function.

To better understand the motor improvement observed in Bin1ex20+/- Dnm2K562E/+ mice, we analyzed muscle mass and histology. The mass of the TA muscle, normalized to body weight, exhibited a significant 21% reduction in Dnm2K562E/+ mice compared to WT. Reduction of BIN1 in the Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ mice showed a tendency to rescue muscle mass (no significant difference compared to both Dnm2K562E/+ and WT mice) (Fig. 3A). The mass of other muscles was not particularly affected in Dnm2K562E/+ mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–C).

Analysis of TA transversal sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) revealed a myofiber hypotrophy in Dnm2K562E/+ mice, with a 1.5-fold higher proportion of small fibers compared to WT (Fig. 3 B and C). BIN1 decrease showed a tendency to improve fiber size. HE images revealed no further obvious defects, with no centralized nuclei and no defects in fiber circularity (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D–F).

As BIN1 decrease normalized Dnm2K562E/+ motor function but not muscle atrophy, we further looked at myofiber general ultrastructure and localization of proteins regulating myofiber organization. Electron microscopy analysis of Dnm2K562E/+ longitudinal TA sections revealed no notable myofiber disorganization. As in WT, sarcomeres and Z-lines appeared aligned and triads intact (SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). Similar results were described for Bin1ex20+/- ( 29 ) and observed in Bin1ex20+/- Dnm2K562E/+ sections.

Immunofluorescence analyses revealed an abnormal accumulation of desmin, a key regulator of intracellular organization, and β1-integrin, an adhesion molecule trafficking in a BIN1- and dynamin-dependent pathway in Dnm2K562E/+ TA muscle sections (Fig. 3D, white arrows) ( 30 , 31 ). Desmin was abnormally accumulated inside 37 % of fibers in Dnm2K562E/+ mice, against 4 % for WT, suggesting degradation defects (Fig. 3E). This defect was lowered to 18 % in Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ mice. β1-integrin was abnormally accumulated inside 2 % of fibers in Dnm2K562E/+ mice, against 0.5 % for WT, suggesting recycling defects (Fig. 3F). This was normalized to 0.5 % in Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+.

As prior transcriptomic and histological analyses of the Soleus muscle revealed increased extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition in Dnm2K562E/+ mice ( 19 ), similarly as in DNM2-CMT patient ( 32 ), this parameter was assessed. Masson Trichrome staining confirmed the presence of fibrosis in Soleus muscle of Dnm2K562E/+ mice (Fig. 3G, white arrows). BIN1 decrease provided a strong improvement, with less ECM deposition between fibers.

Upon Soleus muscle examination, Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA) immunofluorescence revealed abnormal internal staining in a few Dnm2K562E/+ and Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ fibers (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A, white arrows). Given that WGA stains membrane components and integrin previously showed abnormal localization in Dnm2K562E/+ fibers (Fig. 3 D and F), we further investigated the endosomal pathway in the Soleus. In Dnm2K562E/+ Soleus fibers, the abnormal internal WGA dots colocalized with β1-integrin and endosomal markers EEA1 (early endosomes), RAB11 (recycling endosomes), and LAMP1 (lysosomes), confirming defects in the recycling process (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). These findings confirmed β1-integrin recycling defects in Dnm2K562E/+ Soleus.

To further evaluate muscle organization, TA transversal sections were stained with succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), a marker for oxidative activity and mitochondrial distribution. No anomalies were observed in any mouse groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). However, electron microscopy unveiled several morphological defects in both subsarcolemmal and intermyofibrillar mitochondria in Dnm2K562E/+ mice compared to WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B) ( 33 ). Some mitochondria presented a smaller size (asterisks), a rounder shape, fragmentation (black arrowheads), unusual fusion patterns, the presence of internal aggregates, or condensed cristae. Mitochondria defects were also observed in Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ muscle sections, with the most notable manifestation being cristae dilatation (black arrows). Muscle sections from both Dnm2K562E/+ and Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ mice revealed abnormal invaginations of the sarcolemma, with mitochondria clustering (red arrows). Given prior transcriptomic analysis indicating mitochondrial dysfunction in the Soleus of Dnm2K562E/+ mice at 8 wk ( 19 ), further investigations were conducted. Western blot analysis of key proteins associated with mitochondrial dynamics revealed that while FIS1 expression, linked to mitochondrial fission, remained unaltered, MFN2 levels associated with mitochondrial fusion were slightly elevated in Dnm2K562E/+ mice, which may suggest mitochondria hyperfusion (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D). Additionally, cytochrome C (cytC), a crucial component of the electron transport chain, exhibited a 76 % reduction in the Dnm2K562E/+ compared to WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). Both MFN2 and cytC levels were not normalized in Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ group.

Overall, BIN1 reduction in Dnm2K562E/+ mice induced a limited improvement of TA muscle atrophy while it did not improve mitochondrial defects. More strikingly, it normalized desmin and β1-integrin localization in TA myofibers and fibrosis in Soleus. Since Dnm2K562E/+ motor defects were fully rescued by BIN1 decrease, the positive effect of BIN1 modulation may also impact the peripheral nervous system.

We then investigated the neuromuscular system, by first quantifying acetylcholine receptors expression levels in TA. Our results showed a 3.3-fold increase in the expression level of Chrna1, encoding for the alpha subunit, in Dnm2K562E/+ mice and no relevant changes for Chrng and Chrne RNA levels, encoding respectively gamma and epsilon subunits (Fig. 4 A–C). The increased expression of Chrna1 is a known marker of denervation ( 34 ). Chrna1 increase might reveal a muscle denervation, rescued in Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ mice.

As DNM2-CMT is defined by nerve fiber structural defects, the sciatic nerve was assessed by electron microscopy. At 8 wk, a reduction in the g-ratio was observed in Dnm2K562E/+ mice (Fig. 4 D and E). This reduction was exclusively due to a decrease in axon size, as myelin thickness remained unaffected (Fig. 4 F and G). BIN1 downregulation in Dnm2K562E/+ mice provided a partial rescue of the g-ratio (different from Dnm2K562E/+ and WT mice), that can be attributed to myelin sheet thinning while axon size was not improved. These alterations might be a compensatory mechanism to keep the ratio axon diameter/myelin thickness constant, albeit no strong correlation was found between these parameters (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). To better understand this thinning of myelin sheet, the level of early growth response protein 2 (EGR2), a transcriptional regulator of myelin genes ( 35 ), was quantified by western blot in the sciatic nerve and no major modulation was found in any of the groups (Fig. 4H).

Overall, BIN1 downregulation in Dnm2K562E/+ mice improved muscle innervation and nerve fibers g-ratio.

Next, we investigated the molecular mechanism that underlies the observed neuromuscular improvement in the Dnm2K562E/+ CMT mice with BIN1 downregulation. To investigate whether the pathology and the BIN1-mediated rescue effect were associated with the localization of DNM2 and BIN1 proteins, immunofluorescence analyses were performed on longitudinal Gastrocnemius sections. In both WT and Dnm2K562E/+ muscles, DNM2 displayed the expected colocalization with α-actinin at the Z-disc ( 36 , 37 ) and BIN1 colocalized with DHPR at the triad ( 27 , 38 ), suggesting that protein mislocalization is unlikely to be a major cause of the Dnm2K562E/+ myofiber defects (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

Then, we quantified DNM2 levels. Dnm2 mRNA levels were unchanged in Dnm2K562E/+ TA muscle compared to WT (Fig. 5A). However, at the protein level, DNM2 was 2.8- and 2.5-fold higher in Dnm2K562E/+ TA and Soleus muscles, respectively, compared to WT, likely compensating for its partial loss of function (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Since Dnm2 RNA levels were not elevated, the increase in protein may result from enhanced protein stability, improved translation efficiency, or reduced degradation. In the Bin1ex20+/-Dnm2K562E/+ group, reducing BIN1 tended to lower the elevated DNM2 protein levels in Dnm2K562E/+, supporting BIN1’s role in regulating DNM2 dynamics. No major differences in DNM2 levels were observed in the sciatic nerve of the Dnm2K562E/+ CMT mouse (Fig. 5C).

To investigate the interplay between DNM2 and BIN1, in vitro tests were performed using recombinant proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). We tested the muscle-specific (mBIN1) BIN1 isoform and the ubiquitous (uBIN1) isoform, the latter being the most abundant in peripheral nerves ( 38 ). Pull-down assays showed that BIN1 binds similarly to recombinant (r)DNM2-WT and K562E (Fig. 5 D and E). uBIN1 exhibited stronger binding to DNM2 compared to mBIN1, confirming previous data showing mBIN1 can adopt a closed conformation decreasing DNM2 binding ( 39 ). The interaction was specific, as neither rDNM2-WT nor K562E bound to GSH resin alone or GSH resin with GST protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C and D).

Then, we assessed the GTPase activity of DNM2. The K562E mutant demonstrated a lipid-uncoupled nature, as its basal GTPase activity was not increased by phosphatidylserine (PS) supplementation, unlike the rDNM2-WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). With PS, the K562E mutant showed reduced GTPase activity compared to WT. Increasing uBIN1 concentrations inhibited the PS-induced GTPase activity of both rDNM2-WT and -K562E (Fig. 5F). To achieve the same level of GTPase activity with the K562E mutant as observed with rDNM2-WT, a fourfold reduction in BIN1 concentration was required (e.g., Y = 0.25 in Fig. 5F, red dashed line). This suggests that reducing BIN1 level by 50 % in Dnm2K562E/+ mice contributed to increase the DNM2 GTPase activity, thereby underlying the phenotypic improvements observed.

To investigate whether BIN1 downregulation was effective on the long term, we analyzed the main phenotypes of Dnm2K562E/+ mice at 1 y of age. The improvement observed at 8 wk in body length and rear activity upon BIN1 decrease in the Dnm2K562E/+ CMT mice was maintained (Fig. 6 A and B). The gait analysis revealed a decreased paw placement angle in Dnm2K562E/+ mice compared to WT, that was not seen at 8 wk (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). BIN1 decrease tended to improve this coordination defect. Concerning muscle mass, although the lower TA mass was not rescued, we observed a strong atrophy of the Soleus muscle which was fully rescued through BIN1 decrease in Dnm2K562E/+ mice (Fig. 6 D and E). Concerning peripheral nerves, axonal atrophy observed at 8 wk was still observed at 1 y in Dnm2K562E/+ mice, and this parameter was not rescued through BIN1 decrease at both ages (Figs. 4F and 6 F–H). The g-ratio in Dnm2K562E/+ nerves was unchanged at 1 y compared to WT, precluding the investigation of a potential rescue through BIN1 decrease. Overall, the rescue observed in the Dnm2K562E/+ mice with BIN1 decrease was maintained over time in skeletal muscle.

In this study, decreasing BIN1 in the Dnm2K562E/+ CMT mouse model rescued most of the muscular and neurological phenotypes, including motor function, muscle organization, and peripheral nerve defects (overview in SI Appendix, Fig. S8). BIN1 inhibits the DNM2 GTPase activity, and reducing BIN1 increased the DNM2 activity of the K562E CMT mutant, correlating with the phenotypes rescue. These ameliorations were mostly maintained over time.

DNM2 role in CMT pathogenesis and peripheral nerves is barely known. Dnm2 tissue-specific knockout in Schwann cells revealed that DNM2 is essential for peripheral nerve development and myelin maintenance, potentially through its implication in clathrin-mediated endocytosis and cytokinesis ( 15 , 40 ). Of note, BIN1 was also implicated in endocytosis and cytokinesis in different cell types ( 41 , 42 ).

In vitro and in cellulo studies convincingly showed that BIN1 remodels membrane curvature, interacts with DNM2, while DNM2 is a membrane fissioning protein ( 28 , 43 – 45 ). In skeletal muscle, several in vivo investigations suggested BIN1 elongates and maintains T-tubules while DNM2 fragments them ( 14 , 27 , 46 , 47 ). Of note, BIN1 was found concentrated at T-tubules in muscle and at the nodes of Ranvier in the nervous system ( 48 ).

Here, we confirmed BIN1 is a negative regulator of DNM2. BIN1 inhibits the GTPase activity of DNM2 (Fig. 5). Moreover, increasing BIN1 level in a mouse model of CNM with a gain-of-function mutation in DNM2 (Dnm2RW/+) rescued most disease phenotypes ( 28 ). In addition, reducing DNM2 level in a mouse model of CNM lacking BIN1 also rescued the disease phenotypes ( 27 ). In the present study, we found that BIN1 should be decreased to rescue the Dnm2K562E/+ mice, revealing an opposite pathomechanism for DNM2-CMT neuropathy versus DNM2-CNM myopathy. Thus, DNM2-CMT mutations are rather loss-of-function conversely to the DNM2-CNM mutations. It is probable that heterozygous DNM2-CMT mutations exert a dominant negative effect on the WT DNM2 protein also present in patients, as heterozygous knockout Dnm2+/- mice are healthy ( 19 , 49 , 50 ).

Our data support the hypothesis that the rescue of Dnm2K562E/+ phenotypes in both skeletal muscle and peripheral nerves is due to an enhanced DNM2 GTPase activity following the decrease of its regulator, BIN1. At the cellular level, at least in muscle, the reduction of BIN1 level rescued integrin localization defects and alterations of the ECM. Indeed, the integrin pathway controls the link between focal adhesions and the ECM in both skeletal muscle and the nervous system ( 51 ). BIN1 was also shown to regulate endocytic recycling in vivo ( 52 ). Noteworthy, integrin recycling defects were found in a CNM mouse model and were rescued following BIN1 overexpression ( 31 ). Therefore, both loss- and gain-of-function mutations in DNM2 associated with CMT or CNM result in integrin alterations, highlighting the importance of balanced DNM2 activity for tissue homeostasis. These integrin alterations are rescued through opposite modulation of BIN1 in the two diseases [this study and ( 28 )].

This study reveals a link between BIN1 and CMT neuropathies. The role of BIN1 in peripheral nerves or in myelination is unknown. In the central nervous system, a few studies suggested that BIN1 is implicated in synaptic transmission like amphiphysin 1, and is found in the oligodendrocytes myelinating cells ( 53 ).

Our study provides a proof of concept to ameliorate DNM2-CMT, suggesting BIN1 downregulation as a promising strategy to prevent the disease. Additional studies are necessary to explore translational approaches that can be applied to patients. In particular, a better understanding of the disease’s cell-autonomous mechanisms and the identification of the primary tissue to target is a prerequisite to define the best approach and vectors to modulate BIN1 level, for example, via shRNA, siRNA, or antisense oligonucleotides.

In addition to the treatment of DNM2-CMT, BIN1 downregulation has demonstrated efficacy in other contexts, such as the administration of a BIN1 monoclonal antibody for targeted immunotherapy in ulcerative colitis ( 54 ), or BIN1 knockdown using adeno-associated virus serotype 9-packaged shRNA-mBIN1 to rescue systolic dysfunction in the aging heart ( 55 ).

Nevertheless, modulation of BIN1 should be tightly regulated as reducing BIN1 might lead to unintended secondary effects. In our Bin1ex20+/- group, we observed decreased rear activity, g-ratio, and axon diameter but no life-threatening defects. However, muscle-specific ablation of Bin1 in adult mice did not impact on motor function nor muscle structure ( 29 ). Additionally, BIN1 reduction in the brains of individuals with multiple sclerosis correlates with demyelination ( 53 ) and neuronal Bin1 knockout mice showed deficits in excitatory synaptic transmission along with impaired spatial memory consolidation ( 56 ). BIN1 is a main genetic locus associated with late-onset Alzheimer’s disease, though the pathomechanism remains unclear ( 57 , 58 ). BIN1 downregulation may also promote tumor growth, as evidenced by microRNA-211-mediated BIN1 downregulation in oral squamous cell carcinoma ( 59 ), and decreased BIN1 levels correlated with different cancer types ( 60 – 62 ).

Conversely, BIN1 overexpression was also proposed as a therapeutic strategy for different diseases. Viral-mediated exogenous expression of BIN1 limited heart failure progression and improved survival in a mouse model of pressure overload-induced heart failure ( 63 , 64 ). And, as discussed above, exogenous expression of BIN1 rescued CNM linked to DNM2 mutations ( 28 ).

In conclusion, this study validates the inverse pathomechanism between DNM2-CNM and DNM2-CMT, as an opposite modulation of BIN1 is necessary to cure these diseases.

Four genotypes were generated: WT, Bin1ex20+/-, Dnm2K562E/+, and Bin1ex20+/- Dnm2K562E/+. All mice used were on a 25% C57BL/6N and 75% C57BL/6J background. Both males and females were used for experiments at either 8 wk or 1 y of age.

Finger biopsies were used for genotyping. The floxed Dnm2 allele was identified using the primers “6115 Er KE” and “6116 Ef KE” for the K562E mutation. The human Bin1 was detected with the primers “BinTg F” and “BinTg R” for TgBIN1, and Bin1 exon 20 excision was assessed using the primers “3418 Lf” and “3423 Er” for Bin1ex20+/- (primer sequences in SI Appendix, Table S2).

Mice were hosted in ventilated cages with free access to food and water in temperature-controlled rooms with 12 h daylight/dark cycles.

For BIN1 downregulation, two different cohorts of mice were used, one at 8 wk of age and one at 1 y of age (mice aged from 50 to 56 wk). Body length, stride, and paw angle were assessed by gait analysis on a motorized treadmill with a camera, at a constant speed of 14 cm/s. Body length was measured nose to tail base, three measures were averaged; and paw angle as the angle between body line and paw center when in contact with the surface, six measures were performed (3/side). The actimetry test measured spontaneous locomotor activity in mice using a closed cage equipped with infrared light beams. It tracked the number of rears and locomotor activity, recorded during the night from 7 pm to 7 am, after a period of acclimatization from 10 am to 7 pm. Activity counts were averaged over the night. Hanging tests measured latency to fall on an inverted grid (maximum time = 60 s). This test was repeated three times, with a 5 min delay between trials and the average of the two best results was used.

Mice were killed at 8 wk or 1 y by carbon dioxide suffocation and muscles were dissected and weighted. TA, Soleus, and Gastrocnemius were frozen in liquid nitrogen-cooled isopentane and stored at −80 °C for later histology, RNA, and protein extraction. Sciatic nerves used for molecular analyses were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. TA and sciatic nerve used for electron microscopy were fixed in electron microscopy buffer and stored at 4 °C.

8 µm transversal muscle sections were stained with HE, SDH, or Masson Trichrome and imaged using the NanoZoomer 2.0-HT slide scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics). 20 X images were used for analysis. Fiber size distribution and circularity were determined on TA sections stained with HE, using CellPose ( 65 ) software to segment individual fibers and Fiji ( 66 ) to calculate fibers MinFeret diameter and circularity. For the circularity, fibers were classified from 0 to 1, with 0 corresponding to a line and 1 to a circle. One transversal section of the whole muscle was quantified for each animal.

The fixation and permeabilization conditions, as well as the incubation protocols and imaging methods employed, are detailed in SI Appendix, Table S1, according to the specific muscle analyzed. A section with no primary antibody incubation was used for each animal as negative control.

The TA and sciatic nerve samples were fixed by immersion in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2.5% paraformaldehyde in cacodylate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4), and washed in cacodylate buffer for further 30 min. The samples were postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer for 1 h at 4 °C and dehydrated through graded alcohol (50, 70, 90, and 100%) and propylene oxide for 30 min each. Samples were oriented (TA longitudinal, nerve transversal) and embedded in Epon 812. Semithin sections were cut at 2 µm and ultrathin sections were cut at 70 nm (Leica Ultracut UCT) and contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined at 70 kv with a Morgagni 268D electron microscope (FEI Electron Optics, Eindhoven, the Netherlands) equipped with a Mega View III camera (Soft Imaging System).

RNA was isolated from TA muscle samples using TRI Reagent (#TR118 Molecular Research Center) and the Precellys® Evolution Touch tissue homogenizer (Bertin technologies) (two 15 s cycles at 5,500 rpm). cDNA synthesis was achieved using SuperScriptIV Reverse Transcriptase (#18090010 Invitrogen). qPCR was done with cDNA amplified and SYBER Green Master Mix I (#04887352001 Roche Diagnostics) together with 0.5 μM of forward and reverse primers (SI Appendix, Table S2). qRT-PCR was performed using technical triplicates. Amplicons were analyzed using a Lightcycler 480 (Roche Diagnostics). The expression level of each transcript was expressed relative to that of the housekeeping gene Rsp11 using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method and expressed as the fold change from the average of the WT control group.

g-ratio, axon diameter, and myelin thickness measurements were performed using for each nerve a set of 10 electron microscopy images, covering different regions of the nerve, captured at a scale of 10 µm and the AxonDeepSeg segmentation software ( 67 ).

TA muscles and sciatic nerve samples were placed in RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF 1 mM, Sodium Orthovanadate 1 mM, Sodium Fluoride 5 mM, and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail. Tissue samples were lysed using Precellys® Evolution Touch tissue homogenizer (Bertin technologies) (two 20 s cycles at 6,000 rpm) and cleared by centrifugation. Protein concentrations were quantified using a DC Protein Assay Kit (#5000116 BioRad).

Extracted protein were separated using a polyacrylamide gel, prepared with the TGX™ FastCast™ Acrylamide Kit (#1610173 BioRad). A 10% gel was used for most antigen detection, except FIS1 and cytC for which a 15% gel was manually prepared.

10 µg of protein in a volume of 12 µL was loaded for TA muscle samples, while 5 µg of protein in a volume of 20 µL was loaded for sciatic nerve samples. Electrophoretic migration was conducted at 200 V. Protein transfer to nitrocellulose membranes was carried out at 2.5 A using the Trans-Blot Turbo RTA Mini Nitrocellulose Transfer Kit (#170-4270 BioRad). Normalization of protein loading was determined by Ponceau S staining. Membranes were blocked for 1 h in TBS containing 5% nonfat dry milk and 0.1% Tween-20 (#P2287-500ML Merck) before overnight 4 °C incubation with a specific primary antibody (SI Appendix, Table S2) diluted in 5% nonfat dry milk in TBST. Membranes were washed and incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (#32209 ThermoFisher Scientific) were employed to generate chemiluminescent signals, captured using Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare Life 6 Sciences). Fiji was used to quantify band intensity. Data were normalized to Ponceau S staining and the mean value of the control group.

For the Bin1ex20+/- line, the homemade anti-BIN1 antibody was used, while for the TgBIN1 line, the commercial BIN1 antibody was used, as it also recognizes the human isoform.

All unedited gels used for protein quantification, including those not shown in the figures and their loading controls, are provided in supplementary materials.

The cDNAs of human ubiquitous isoform of dynamin 2 (rDNM2-WT and rDNM2-K562E) were cloned into pVL1392 transfer vector. The resulting plasmids were cotransfected in insect cells with AcMNPV viral DNA (Bac10:KO1629,Δv-cath/chiA-LoxP:DsRed) linearized by Bsu36I to generate the recombinant baculoviruses as described previously ( 68 ). The virus generation, amplification, and protein expression were performed using standardized procedures ( 69 ).

For large scale production of recombinant dynamins, Sf21 insect cells (IGBMC baculovirus facility, Strasbourg, France) cultivated in the serum free Sf-900TMII medium (Gibco) were infected at a density of 1.0 × 106 cells/mL and a multiplicity of infection of 5 with the virus stocks. 2 d after infection, cells were pelleted by centrifugation (2,500×g for 10 min) and snap-frozen for storage at −80 °C.

rDNM2-WT and rDNM2-K562E proteins were purified with Human SH3 domain of BIN1 as the SH3 domain captures full-length dynamin 2 through a high affinity interaction with its PRD domain ( 37 ).

Human SH3 domain of BIN1, muscle-specific (mBIN1) BIN1 isoform and the ubiquitous (uBIN1) BIN1 isoform genes were cloned in pGEX6p1 expression vector. This vector introduces an N-terminal Glutathione S-transferase (GST) tag followed by a PreScission protease recognition site. These proteins were produced in Escherichia coli BL21 using pGEX6P1 plasmid after induction with IPTG (1 mM) for 3 h at 37 °C. Then, cells were centrifuged at 7.500 g, lysed, applied on Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (GSH-resin, GE Healthcare) and washed as described in the protocol ( 70 ). GSH-resin with GST-SH3 was used for rDNM2-WT and rDNM2-K562E protein purifications. GST-resins with mBIN1 and uBIN1 were used for pull-down assay (see below).

Insect cell pellets with rDNM2-WT and rDNM2-K562E proteins were resuspended in Dynamin-buffer A: 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 5% (v/v) glycerol, Protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche) and lysed with glass dounce tissue homogenizer. The lysates were cleared with ultracentrifugation at 20,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C and applied on GSH-resin with GST-SH3 overnight. After washing with Dynamin-buffer B: 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 5% (v/v) glycerol, the elution of rDNM2-WT and rDNM2-K562E was done with Dynamin-buffer C: 1.2 M NaCl, 20 mM PIPES pH 6.8, 1 mM DTT, and dialyzed with Dynamin-buffer B.

The interaction between human DNM2 and human BIN1 was tested by pull-down assay. Purified Dynamins in Dynamin-buffer B were loaded on GSH-resin cross-linked with GST-mBIN1 or GST-uBIN1. After 3 h of incubation at 4 °C, the beads were washed 3 times with Dynamin-buffer B, separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Coomassie staining. The experiments were performed in technical triplicates.

GTPase activity of rDNM2-WT and rDNM2-KE proteins was measured with malachite green colorimetric assay ( 71 ). These dynamins at 8 nM were mixed with GST-uBIN1 in different ratios. Then the proteins were incubated in the buffer 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 2.5 mM Mg2+, with or without 2-Diacyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-L-serine (PS, 4 µg/mL) for 90 min at 37 °C. The concentration of GTP in the reaction mix was 0.5 mM in basal conditions (without PS) and 0.3 mM in PS-supplemented condition. Reactions were terminated with Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, pH 7.4), and phosphate was detected by the addition of malachite green solution (2% w/v ammonium molybdate tetrahydrate, 0.15% w/v malachite green and 4 M HCl). GTPase activity results were confirmed with at least two different protein productions and purifications. Absorbance at 650 nm was measured with a Synergy HTX Multimode Reader (Agilent BioTek) using software Gen5 version 2.07.

Statistical analyses and graphs were made using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1. Initially, a two-way ANOVA was performed for most experiments to assess the effects of sex and genotype. This analysis revealed either a significant effect of the genotypes alone or, in some cases, a significant effect of both factors. However, there was no significant interaction between these factors, except for body weight, where males and females were plotted separately. Subsequently, to consolidate the findings, data from males and females were combined. The number of males and females used in each test is available (SI Appendix, Table S3). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, except when the sample size was large, in which case the D’Agostino & Pearson test was used. If all data followed a normal distribution, a one-way ANOVA was performed. Alternatively, a Brown–Forsythe and Welch ANOVA was employed if the assumption of equal variances was violated. When data were lognormal rather than normal, a log transformation was performed before ANOVA. For data not following a normal or lognormal distribution, a Kruskal–Wallis test was performed. Multiple comparisons of normally distributed data with homoscedastic or heteroscedastic variances were conducted using uncorrected Fischer’s LSD test and unpaired t test with Welch’s correction, respectively. For nonnormally distributed data, an uncorrected Dunn’s test was used. When a data point visually deviated from a dataset, the ROUT outlier test was conducted (with the strictest threshold of Q = 0.1%), and if an outlier was detected it was subsequently removed from the dataset and the graph. Details of each test performed are summarized SI Appendix, Table S3. For all graphs, all pairwise comparisons were performed, and only significant differences are plotted. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs show individual data points, and mean ± SD.
