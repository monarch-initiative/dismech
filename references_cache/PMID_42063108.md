# Surgical management of catamenial pneumothorax: a systematic review.
**Authors:** Mekhail A, Kilby J, Macalister S, Manivannan PC, Seitz M
**Journal:** J Cardiothorac Surg (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13019-026-04227-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04227-1)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Catamenial pneumothorax is a rare manifestation of thoracic endometriosis syndrome and is associated with a high risk of recurrence despite surgical intervention. Increasingly, multimodal strategies combining thoracic surgery with adjunctive hormonal therapy have been put in place as standard practice however, optimal management remains uncertain.

METHODS: A systematic review of the literature was conducted using MEDLINE/PubMed, Scopus, CENTRAL, Google Scholar, SciSpace, and the Cochrane Library for studies published between January 2000 and December 2025. Studies reporting outcomes of surgical management for catamenial pneumothorax were included. Data was extracted on patient characteristics, surgical techniques (diaphragmatic and pleural interventions), use of postoperative hormonal therapy, and recurrence rates.  RESULTS: Analysis of 37 studies encompassing over 1,800 patients suggests that treatment approach is a major determinant of outcomes. Meta-analysis demonstrates a pooled recurrence rate of 17.3% with postoperative hormonal therapy compared to 54.2% without (p < 0.01) [1], with younger age independently associated with higher recurrence risk. Diaphragmatic intervention is critical to reducing recurrence, with studies reporting 12.5% recurrence when diaphragmatic surgery is combined with pleurodesis versus 100% with pleurodesis alone [15]. Diaphragm resection has been identified as an independent protective factor against recurrence (HR 0.16; 95% CI: 0.03-0.77; p = 0.022) [5].

CONCLUSIONS: The available evidence supports a multimodal approach to the management of catamenial pneumothorax. Comprehensive thoracoscopic surgery addressing diaphragmatic pathology, combined with pleurodesis and postoperative hormonal therapy, is consistently associated with reduced recurrence. Given the observational nature of the available data, these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution and highlight the need for prospective, collaborative studies.

Catamenial pneumothorax (catamenial pneumothorax) is a rare but clinically challenging manifestation of thoracic endometriosis syndrome (TES), characterised by recurrent spontaneous pneumothoraces occurring at the time of menstruation [1–3]. The term “catamenial,” from the Greek word for “monthly,” was first used by Lillington in 1972, although the phenomenon was described previously by Maurer et al. in 1958 [4, 5]. Diagnostic criteria commonly include at least two episodes of spontaneous pneumothorax occurring within 72 h before or after the onset of menses in women of reproductive age, although some authors have proposed extending this window [3, 6, 7].

Catamenial pneumothorax is the most common manifestation of TES, which encompasses a spectrum of conditions resulting from ectopic endometrial tissue within the thorax. Other manifestations include catamenial haemothorax (cyclical pleural effusion), catamenial haemoptysis (cyclical coughing of blood), and pulmonary endometriotic nodules [8, 9].

The management of catamenial pneumothorax presents unique challenges that distinguish it from primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP). Surgical intervention for PSP achieves recurrence rates of less than 5% whereas catamenial pneumothorax demonstrates substantially higher recurrence rates ranging from 8% to 40% even after surgical treatment [3, 7, 9]. This persistently high recurrence rate has driven the evolution of treatment strategies toward more aggressive, multimodal approaches that address both mechanical air leak and underlying hormonal drivers of disease. The high recurrence rate in catamenial pneumothorax has significant implications for patient quality of life, healthcare resource utilisation, and the need for repeated interventions. Many patients experience multiple episodes before the correct diagnosis is established, as the temporal relationship with menstruation may not be immediately apparent or may not be elicited during initial history or examination [3]. The average delay from symptom onset to diagnosis ranges from 3 to 5 years in many series, during which patients may undergo multiple chest tube insertions and even surgical procedures without addressing the underlying ectopic endometrial tissue in the thoracic cavity [11].

Catamenial pneumothorax is an uncommon cause of spontaneous pneumothorax in women, accounting for approximately 2.8% to 5.6% of all cases of spontaneous pneumothorax in women of reproductive age [12, 13]. The true incidence may be underestimated due to under recognition of the temporal relationship with menstruation and the requirement for a high index of clinical suspicion [3]. catamenial pneumothorax predominantly affects women in their third and fourth decades of life, with a mean age at presentation ranging from 33 to 38 years across published series [5, 14, 15].

The overwhelming right-sided predominance (90–95% of cases) is one of the most striking epidemiological features of catamenial pneumothorax and provides important clues to its pathogenesis [1, 2, 10]. Left-sided and bilateral cases have been reported but are uncommon [16]. Many patients with catamenial pneumothorax have concurrent pelvic endometriosis, with reported prevalence ranging from 40% to 80% depending on whether laparoscopic evaluation is routinely performed [6, 17, 18]. This association highlights the systemic nature of endometriosis and the importance of multidisciplinary treatment from both the thoracic surgery and gynaecological teams.

The pathogenesis of catamenial pneumothorax remains incompletely understood, and multiple non-exclusive mechanisms have been proposed to explain the development of pneumothorax in association with menstruation [2, 3, 19].

This theory has gained the most support from histopathological evidence, and proposes that endometrial tissue implants on the visceral pleura, parietal pleura, or diaphragm through retrograde menstruation and transperitoneal- transdiaphragmatic migration [2, 20]. During menstruation, these ectopic endometrial implants undergo cyclical bleeding and necrosis, leading to pleural inflammation, disruption of pleural integrity, and air leak from the lung parenchyma or through diaphragmatic fenestrations. Histological confirmation of endometrial tissue on pleural or diaphragmatic specimens has been documented in 30–50% of surgical cases, providing histopathological support for this mechanism [6, 21].

This theory suggests that air (and potentially endometrial tissue) migrates from the peritoneal cavity through congenital or acquired diaphragmatic fenestrations into the pleural space [2, 22]. Diaphragmatic defects are identified in 20–40% of patients undergoing thoracoscopy for catamenial pneumothorax, with defects ranging from small microscopic pores to fenestrations several centimetres in diameter [7, 23, 24]. The predominance of right-sided disease may be explained by the unique anatomy of the right hemidiaphragm, which has thinner muscular components and more frequent congenital defects, particularly in the tendinous portion [25].

This theory proposes that cyclical hormonal fluctuations, particularly the surge in prostaglandin F2α during menstruation, leads to vasospasm of pulmonary vessels and bronchiolar constriction, leading to alveolar rupture and air leak [2]. Additionally, high levels of prostaglandins may increase pleural permeability and reduce surfactant production, predisposing to pneumothorax [26]. While this mechanism is more difficult to prove directly, the clinical efficacy of hormonal suppression therapy provides indirect support.

Present understanding suggests that catamenial pneumothorax likely results as a combination of these mechanisms, with the relative contribution of each varying among individuals [2, 3]. This multifactorial pathogenesis helps explain the variability in surgical findings and why no single treatment approach is consistently successful.

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

A comprehensive systematic literature search was conducted to identify studies evaluating the surgical management and adjunctive hormonal therapy for catamenial pneumothorax. Searches were completed across multiple electronic databases, including MEDLINE/PubMed, SciSpace (Deep Search, Basic Search, and Full-Text Search), Google Scholar, Scopus, CENTRAL (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials), and the Cochrane Library.

The search included publications from January 2000 to December 2025 in order to capture up to date management strategies, particularly the widespread adoption of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and current hormonal therapies.

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords relating to catamenial pneumothorax, thoracic endometriosis, surgical management, diaphragmatic intervention, hormonal therapy, and treatment outcomes. Key terms included “catamenial pneumothorax,” “thoracic endometriosis” or “thoracic endometriosis syndrome,” “endometriosis-related pneumothorax,” and surgical descriptors such as “VATS,” “thoracoscopy,” “thoracotomy,” and “diaphragmatic repair or resection.” Terms relating to adjuvant therapy and outcomes, including “hormonal therapy,” “GnRH agonist,” “dienogest,” “progestin,” “recurrence,” and “recurrence-free survival,” were also incorporated. Reference lists of relevant articles and reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Eligible studies included those reporting outcomes of surgical treatment for catamenial pneumothorax, including observational studies (retrospective or prospective cohort studies), case series comprising more than five patients, and systematic reviews or meta- analyses. Studies were required to report recurrence rates, surgical techniques, and the use and outcomes of hormonal therapy.

Studies were excluded if they were case reports or small case series (≤ 5 patients), did not report outcomes specific to catamenial pneumothorax (for example, studies combining catamenial and non-catamenial spontaneous pneumothorax without separate analysis), were conference abstracts without full-text availability, or represented duplicate publications. In the instance of overlapping cohorts, the most recent or most comprehensive study was included. Studies without a verified English translation were also excluded.

Data were extracted from each included study using a standardised approach. Extracted study characteristics included first author, year of publication, country, study design, study period, sample size, and duration of follow-up. Patient demographic data included age, number of patients, laterality of pneumothorax (right-sided, left-sided, or bilateral), presence of pelvic endometriosis, and history of previous pneumothoraces.

Details of surgical management was recorded, including the operative approach (VATS or open thoracotomy), conversion rates, diaphragmatic interventions (repair, resection, or mesh reinforcement), pleural procedures (mechanical abrasion, chemical pleurodesis, or pleurectomy), and pulmonary resections such as wedge resection or bullectomy. The use of concurrent laparoscopy for pelvic endometriosis and the administration of peri- operative or post-operative hormonal therapy was also documented.

Outcome measures included pneumothorax recurrence rates, time to recurrence, recurrence-free survival, and reported hazard ratios or odds ratios for identified risk or protective factors. Surgical and hormonal therapy-related complications were extracted where reported.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise study characteristics, patient demographics, and surgical techniques. Recurrence rates calculated as proportions with 95% confidence intervals when sufficient data was available. For meta-analysis of time- to-event outcomes (recurrence-free survival), hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals were extracted or calculated from available data. When studies reported sufficient data, we performed meta-analysis using random-effects models to account for expected heterogeneity and differences across studies. Subgroup analyses were planned based on surgical procedure, type of hormonal therapy, and duration of follow-up. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Our search found 40 publications that were analysed to help address our clinical question of how to manage catamenial pneumothorax with the lowest recurrence rates. Following application of our inclusion and exclusion criteria, 37 studies were included in the final analysis. Two studies were excluded due to small sample size (≤ 5 patients), and one duplicate publication was removed. The included studies comprised 27 primary retrospective cohort studies, 4 systematic reviews or meta-analyses, 1 prospective cross-sectional study, and 5 case series with more than five patients. The studies spanned publication years from 2003 to 2025, with the majority published after 2015.

The geographic distribution of studies was diverse, with significant contributions from Asia, the Middle East, Europe, and North America. This geographic diversity supports the generalisability of findings across different healthcare systems and patient populations.

The patient population across studies demonstrated consistent demographic patterns characteristic of catamenial pneumothorax. The mean age at presentation ranged from 24 years to 41 years, with most studies reporting mean ages between 33 and 38 years. Patients presented with recurrent pneumothorax episodes prior to diagnosis, with some experiencing more than ten episodes before definitive treatment.

Diaphragmatic fenestrations were identified in 30% [43] to 100% [15] of patients undergoing surgical exploration, with most studies reporting rates between 50% and 90%. These defects were typically located on the central tendon of the right hemidiaphragm and ranged from microscopic pores to larger fenestrations several centimetres in diameter. Histopathological confirmation of endometriosis was achieved in 40% [6] to 85% [42] of cases where tissue specimens were obtained.

Apical blebs/bullae were identified in 25% [19] to 62.5% [37] of patients.

Surgical management of catamenial pneumothorax has evolved considerably over the study period, with video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) becoming the predominant approach. It should be noted that the included studies did not consistently differentiate between uniportal and multiportal VATS, and this distinction was therefore not captured in the analysis; this represents a limitation given the increasing adoption of uniportal techniques in contemporary practice.

VATS was employed in the majority of contemporary series, with utilisation rates exceeding 85% in recent studies [7]. Pleurodesis techniques varied substantially across studies. Chemical pleurodesis, predominantly using talc, was employed in many centres, while mechanical pleurodesis through pleural abrasion was preferred by others. Some studies employed combined mechanical and chemical approaches. Pleurectomy was performed in a subset of patients and was associated with reduced recurrence in one study [11].

Diaphragmatic interventions included primary suture repair, resection of affected diaphragm, and mesh coverage or reinforcement. The choice of prosthetic material varied, with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) mesh [38], polyglycolic acid (PGA) sheets [35, 47], polypropylene mesh [48], and biological meshes all reported. The European multicentre study by Gatteschi et al. [9] compared prosthetic replacement with direct surgical repair and found a trend toward lower recurrence with prosthetic replacement (26.3% versus 56.8%), though this did not reach statistical significance.

Hormonal therapy was used as an adjunct to surgical management in the majority of studies, with considerable variation in the specific agents, timing, and duration of treatment.

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists were the most commonly employed hormonal agents, used in the majority of studies reporting specific agents. Combined oral contraceptives or oestrogen-progestin preparations were used in some centres, though several studies reported inferior outcomes with these agents compared to GnRH agonists [18, 19].

The timing of hormonal therapy was predominantly postoperative, though one study advocated for preoperative administration 1.5 to 2 months before planned surgery to suppress menstruation during the early postoperative period when pleurodesis is forming [7]. Treatment duration ranged from 3 months to 18 months, with most protocols recommending 6 months of therapy.

Side effects of hormonal therapy were reported in one study, including depression (6.8%), excessive sweating (3.4%), and headache (3.4%) [42]. The necessity to limit GnRH agonist treatment duration due to menopausal side effects was noted by several authors.

Several studies demonstrated markedly different recurrence rates based on treatment approach. Campisi et al. [15] reported 100% recurrence with pleurodesis alone compared to 12.5% when diaphragmatic surgery was combined with pleurodesis and hormonal therapy. Similarly, Pishchik et al. [48] documented zero recurrences among patients receiving combined surgery with diaphragmatic resection, mesh reinforcement, and postoperative hormonal therapy, compared to 100% recurrence with drainage alone and 60% with surgery without hormonal therapy. The importance of addressing diaphragmatic pathology was reinforced by multiple studies. Bagan et al. [21] reported no recurrences among five patients undergoing diaphragmatic repair compared to three recurrences among five patients with pleurodesis alone. Kim et al. [5] identified diaphragm resection as a protective factor against recurrence (hazard ratio 0.16; 95% CI: 0.03–0.77; p = 0.022). Charoensup et al. [11] found that pleurectomy was significantly associated with reduced recurrence (p = 0.029). The meta-analysis by Elsayed et al. This systematic review identified several patient, surgical, and hormonal factors associated with recurrence following treatment for catamenial pneumothorax.

The meta-analysis by Elsayed et al. [1] demonstrated that each additional year of age reduced recurrence risk by 6%, a finding that probably reflects more biologically active endometriosis, a longer reproductive lifespan with continued hormonal cycling, and potentially distinct disease biology in younger women. Failure to address diaphragmatic pathology was the strongest surgical predictor of recurrence. Studies comparing pleurodesis alone to pleurodesis with diaphragmatic intervention consistently demonstrated lower recurrence rates with comprehensive management [15, 21], and the presence of intraoperative thoracic endometriosis lesions was an independent predictor of postoperative recurrence [8]. The co-occurrence of diaphragmatic lesions and apical pathology in a sizeable proportion of patients underscores the importance of thorough thoracoscopic inspection, since multiple pathological processes frequently contribute simultaneously to air leak. Regarding hormonal treatment, GnRH agonists consistently outperformed oestrogen-progestin preparations; Ciriaco et al. [18] reported that recurrence was significantly correlated with oestrogen-progesterone use (p < 0.005), and discontinuation of any hormonal therapy was associated with recurrence in several series [37]. Early recurrence — occurring within days to weeks of surgery — was reported in some centres and attributed to failure to achieve adequate pleurodesis before the first postoperative menstruation. Voskresensky et al. [7] reported that 46.1% of their patients experienced recurrence at a median of only 6 days postoperatively, coinciding precisely with the first postoperative menses, which has led to interest in preoperative hormonal suppression to protect the critical early pleurodesis period.

Minimally invasive VATS has become the standard operative approach, offering reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery, but above all — in the specific context of catamenial pneumothorax — superior visualisation of the entire pleural cavity and diaphragm. It should be noted that the included studies did not consistently differentiate between uniportal and multiportal VATS, and this distinction was not captured in the analysis, which is an acknowledged limitation given the increasing adoption of uniportal techniques. Addressing diaphragmatic pathology through repair or resection emerged as one of the most important modifiable determinants of recurrence. The optimal technique remains debated, and the choice between primary suture repair, segmental resection, and mesh reinforcement is best guided by defect size and tissue quality: small fenestrations of less than 1 cm are generally suitable for primary repair with non-absorbable sutures and pledgets [10, 28], moderate defects of 1–3 cm may benefit from mesh reinforcement depending on tissue quality [29], while large defects or extensive endometriotic involvement of the diaphragm typically require segmental resection with prosthetic reconstruction [7, 30]. Mesh was used in 12–46% of cases across studies, with higher rates in series reporting more advanced diaphragmatic disease, though the potential for long-term mesh-related complications — including migration, infection, and chronic pain — must be weighed against the benefits [31]. Pleurodesis was performed in 81.7–96.1% of cases, reflecting near-universal acceptance of this adjunctive procedure [4, 7, 32]. Mechanical abrasion has become the most commonly reported technique in recent series, largely for its technical simplicity under VATS and the avoidance of talc-related complications, though one study found that pleurectomy was independently associated with reduced recurrence (p = 0.029) [11], suggesting that more aggressive pleural interventions may confer additional benefit in selected patients. Regarding pleurodesis technique, an important limitation of the current evidence base is that studies frequently omitted detail on whether talc was insufflated under direct thoracoscopic vision or via a chest drain, whether coverage was applied to the entire pleural cavity or the apex alone, and whether pleurectomy was partial or complete. These distinctions carry clinical relevance and their absence limits the conclusions that can be drawn about optimal technique. Lung parenchymal resection was performed selectively in 15–35% of cases, targeting visible blebs, bullae, or endometriotic nodules. It should be emphasised that the macroscopic absence of identifiable lung lesions does not equate to histologically normal parenchyma: wedge resection has revealed occult pathology, including millimetric surface defects and endometrial deposits, even in macroscopically unremarkable lung, and histopathological examination of any resected specimen is therefore strongly encouraged.

The striking heterogeneity in reported recurrence rates — from 0% to 100% — across the included studies largely reflects differences in treatment intensity rather than inherent variation in disease biology. Studies reporting recurrence below 20% consistently combined comprehensive diaphragmatic intervention, pleurodesis, and postoperative GnRH agonist therapy, and generally had longer follow-up periods [15, 48]. Studies reporting recurrence above 40% typically employed less aggressive surgical approaches, often pleurodesis alone without diaphragmatic intervention, and made inconsistent use of hormonal therapy, or included populations with particularly severe or refractory disease. The 0% recurrence achieved with combined diaphragmatic surgery, pleurodesis, and hormonal suppression in some series [48] compared with 62–100% with less comprehensive strategies strongly supports the primacy of treatment intensity as the key outcome determinant. The critical role of diaphragmatic surgery is further underscored by the independent protective effect of diaphragm resection demonstrated by Kim et al. on multivariate analysis (HR 0.16; 95% CI: 0.03–0.77; p = 0.022) [5], and the clinical observation that occult diaphragmatic defects — not visible macroscopically at surgery — may only become apparent during menstruation, meaning that comprehensive diaphragmatic assessment is warranted regardless of intraoperative appearances. Pooled data from Elsayed et al. [1] provide the most robust evidence for adjuvant hormonal therapy, with a recurrence rate of 17.3% compared to 54.2% without treatment. Individual study results were more variable, and some patients experienced recurrence despite therapy, while others achieved near-zero rates. The optimal duration of hormonal suppression remains undefined: most protocols recommend six months, but some authors advocate 12–18 months, and the observation that recurrence frequently follows discontinuation raises the question of whether long-term or indefinite suppression may be necessary in certain patients. The practical constraints of prolonged GnRH agonist use — including menopausal side effects and bone density loss — represent a genuine clinical challenge that requires individualised discussion with patients.

For clinicians managing patients with catamenial pneumothorax, a high index of suspicion is essential. In any woman of reproductive age presenting with recurrent right-sided pneumothorax, a detailed menstrual history should be elicited, and chest CT may usefully identify diaphragmatic defects, pleural nodularity, or other features of thoracic endometriosis. Where surgical management is indicated, VATS allows thorough inspection of the pleural cavity and diaphragm with limited morbidity. Diaphragmatic fenestrations or endometriotic lesions should be addressed surgically where identified, pleurodesis should be performed routinely, and visible parenchymal lesions resected. Concurrent laparoscopy may be considered in selected patients to assess and treat pelvic endometriosis. All resected tissue should be submitted for histopathological analysis. Postoperative hormonal therapy should be offered to most patients unless contraindicated, with agent selection and duration tailored to individual circumstances including fertility intentions and tolerability. Short-term preoperative suppression to prevent menstruation during the early pleurodesis period may be considered in cases where the first postoperative menses is a concern. Follow-up should be particularly attentive during the first postoperative year, with symptoms assessed in relation to the menstrual cycle, and longer-term surveillance maintained to identify late recurrences and coordinate management of associated pelvic endometriosis.

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. The majority of included studies were retrospective observational cohorts, which are inherently prone to selection bias, information bias, and confounding, and no randomised controlled trials were identified comparing surgical techniques or hormonal regimens. The rarity of catamenial pneumothorax makes large-scale prospective trials extremely difficult, though multicentre registries would represent a meaningful step forward. Substantial heterogeneity existed across studies in the definitions of recurrence (symptomatic versus radiographic), follow-up duration (6 months to over 10 years), hormonal therapy agents, doses and durations, and patient disease severity. This heterogeneity limits the precision of pooled estimates. Publication bias is a further concern: smaller series with negative outcomes are less likely to reach publication, potentially inflating apparent treatment effects. Many studies did not report sufficient data to allow extraction of hazard ratios or time-to-event data for formal meta-analysis. Although forest plots were planned to visualise pooled effect sizes for diaphragmatic intervention and hormonal therapy, the heterogeneity in outcome definitions, follow-up duration, and the limited number of studies providing extractable time-to-event data precluded their meaningful generation; where meta-analytic data were available, as in Elsayed et al. [1], these are cited directly rather than re-pooled. Standardisation was also lacking in the reporting of surgical details, particularly pleurodesis: studies rarely specified whether talc was delivered under direct vision or via chest drain, whether coverage was partial or complete, or whether pleurectomy was partial or total. Pathological analysis of resected specimens was similarly inconsistent, limiting conclusions about the true spectrum of microscopic disease. Follow-up duration varied widely across series, and shorter follow-up periods may underestimate true long-term recurrence rates. Finally, the simultaneous use of multiple interventions in most patients makes it difficult to isolate the independent contribution of any single treatment component. Despite these limitations, this review synthesises the largest body of evidence currently available on the surgical management of catamenial pneumothorax and provides a practical framework for clinical decision-making.

Looking ahead, the development of prospective multicentre registries — ideally at an international level — would represent a significant step forward, enabling systematic collection of surgical strategies, hormonal therapy regimens, and long-term outcomes with a rigour that single-centre retrospective studies cannot achieve. Further comparative work on diaphragmatic repair techniques, pleurodesis strategies, and the timing and duration of hormonal therapy would help standardise practice. Collaborative efforts between thoracic surgery, gynaecology, and respiratory medicine societies could facilitate the development of consensus-based guidance on diagnostic criteria, surgical decision-making, hormonal therapy selection, and follow-up, with particular attention to special populations such as adolescents and women wishing to preserve fertility.

For clinicians managing catamenial pneumothorax, the available evidence supports a comprehensive strategy combining meticulous thoracoscopic resection of all identifiable thoracic endometriotic lesions, diaphragmatic repair or replacement, and adjuvant hormonal suppression. Patients should be counselled about the chronic nature of endometriosis, the possibility of recurrence despite optimal treatment, and the importance of sustained follow-up. With appropriately selected multimodal therapy, many patients can achieve durable remission, though long-term surveillance remains essential.

Catamenial pneumothorax is a rare but clinically important manifestation of thoracic endometriosis syndrome that demands a comprehensive, multimodal approach. The evidence consistently favours thoracoscopic surgery addressing diaphragmatic pathology, combined with pleurodesis and postoperative hormonal therapy, as the strategy most likely to achieve durable remission. Given the predominantly observational nature of the available data, these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution and reinforce the need for prospective, collaborative studies to define optimal treatment protocols.
