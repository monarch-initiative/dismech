# Successful Control of Late-Onset Nephrotic Syndrome in FN1-Associated Fibronectin Glomerulopathy: A 17-Year Clinical Course.
**Authors:** Kanazawa N, Iyoda M, Abe K, Suzuki T, Shirai Y, Miura K, Honda K, Honda H
**Journal:** Nephrology (Carlton) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/nep.70273](https://doi.org/10.1111/nep.70273)

## Content

Fibronectin glomerulopathy (FNG) is a rare renal disorder characterized by excessive glomerular fibronectin deposition, often associated with variants in the fibronectin 1 (FN1) gene. Clinically, FNG presents with proteinuria, hematuria, and hypertension, and may progress to end-stage kidney disease. However, its long-term clinical course and optimal management remain incompletely defined. We report a sporadic case of FNG in a 26-year-old woman carrying an FN1 variant, c.2918A > G (NM_212482.4). Seventeen years after initial detection of proteinuria, she developed nephrotic syndrome with generalized edema. Supportive therapy, including renin-angiotensin system inhibitors and diuretics, was insufficient, and prednisolone and cyclosporine A were initiated for management of nephrotic syndrome. The patient subsequently achieved partial remission, with urinary protein decreasing to < 1 g/gCr and no major adverse events. During tapering of immunosuppression, a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor was added as adjunct therapy. This case suggests a potential role for immunosuppressive therapy in management of late-onset nephrotic syndrome associated with FNG, with SGLT2 inhibition potentially contributing to maintenance of remission.

Fibronectin glomerulopathy (FNG) is a rare hereditary glomerular disease characterized by excessive deposition of fibronectin within the glomeruli. Pathogenic mutations in the fibronectin 1 (FN1) gene have been reported in FNG patients, and they are detected in approximately 40% of patients [ 1 , 2 ]. Although the mechanisms underlying glomerular accumulation of fibronectin fibrils remain unclear, impaired clearance of variant fibronectin and binding of circulating factors to the molecule may contribute [ 1 , 2 ].

Clinically, FNG commonly presents with proteinuria, hematuria, and hypertension [ 3 ]. While earlier reports described mild proteinuria [ 4 ], more recent cohorts have shown that nephrotic‐range proteinuria is more common [ 5 , 6 ]. Kidney function typically declines over 15–20 years [ 7 ], and patients may progress to end‐stage kidney disease between the ages of 20 and 60 [ 4 , 8 ]. No standard treatment has been established [ 1 , 3 ], and published reports on therapeutic strategies and longitudinal outcomes remain limited. Supportive therapy, including renin–angiotensin system (RAS) inhibition, is the mainstay of treatment [ 5 ], whereas the efficacy of immunosuppressive therapy remains uncertain. Here, we report a sporadic case of FNG with an FN1 mutation who developed late‐onset nephrotic syndrome. Partial remission was achieved following treatment with prednisolone and cyclosporine A, and a sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor was subsequently added as adjunctive therapy for maintenance of remission.

A Japanese woman was first noted to have proteinuria during a school medical screening at 9 years of age. As proteinuria gradually worsened, she underwent a renal biopsy at age 11. Light microscopy demonstrated a diffuse membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN)–like pattern with lobulated glomeruli, abundant periodic acid–Schiff (PAS)–positive subendothelial and mesangial deposits, and mesangial cell and matrix proliferation (Figure 1a,b). Electron microscopy revealed subendothelial and mesangial fibrillary deposits, establishing the diagnosis of FNG. Weak immunoglobulin and C3 staining were observed. Genetic testing identified a missense FN1 variant, c.2918A > G (NM_212482.4). No variant was detected in either parent, and the case was considered sporadic. She was managed conservatively with supportive therapy, including telmisartan.

Approximately 15.5 years (186 months) after the initial diagnosis, she was referred to our hospital because of progressive hypoalbuminemia and generalized edema despite ongoing supportive therapy (telmisartan 40 mg/day and furosemide 80 mg/day). Her blood pressure was 125/87 mmHg, and marked peripheral edema was present. Laboratory tests showed nephrotic‐range hypoalbuminemia and dyslipidemia with preserved kidney function (serum creatinine 0.79 mg/dL, albumin 1.5 g/dL, total protein 3.5 g/dL, LDL cholesterol 439 mg/dL). Inflammatory markers were negative. Complement levels were normal, and autoimmune and viral serologies were unremarkable. Urinalysis showed microscopic hematuria (10–19 red blood cells/high‐power field), and 24‐h urinary protein excretion was 4.8 g/day. Chest radiography showed bilateral pleural effusions. Based on these findings, nephrotic syndrome was diagnosed.

A repeat renal biopsy was performed 187 months after the initial diagnosis. Light microscopy showed diffuse glomerulomegaly with lobulated architecture and abundant PAS‐positive subendothelial and mesangial deposits, resulting in narrowing to occlusion of capillary lumina. Focal mesangial proliferation and foam‐cell infiltration within the capillary loops were noted (Figure 1c,d). Interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy were mild (< 10%). Patchy interstitial foam‐cell infiltration was present (Figure 1e), and no significant arteriosclerosis or arteriolosclerosis was observed. Whole‐slide images are provided in Figures S1–S7. Immunofluorescence showed staining for IgG, IgM, kappa/lambda light chains, C1q, and fibrinogen in the capillary loops and mesangial areas, whereas C3 showed segmental staining (Figure 2). Electron microscopy demonstrated subendothelial and mesangial electron‐dense deposits with heterogeneous density and fibrillary structures measuring 10–20 nm in diameter, consistent with fibronectin glomerulopathy (Figure 1g). Diffuse foot‐process effacement and focal podocyte detachment from the glomerular basement membrane were observed (Figure 1f). Immunofluorescence on frozen sections for fibronectin using IST‐4 and IST‐9 showed IST‐4–dominant staining in the capillary loops and mesangial areas (Figure 1h,i), consistent with the accumulation of plasma‐derived fibronectin. These findings were consistent with the original diagnosis of fibronectin glomerulopathy. To further investigate the possibility of a superimposed autoimmune‐mediated podocytopathy, serum anti‐nephrin, anti‐podocin, and anti‐Kirrel1 autoantibodies were measured using previously described methods [ 9 ]. All three autoantibodies were below their respective cutoff values. In addition, tissue analyses for IgG colocalization with nephrin, podocin, and Kirrel1 showed no findings consistent with the currently recognized autoimmune podocytopathies (Figure 2a–c).

Immunosuppressive therapy was initiated 188 months after the initial diagnosis because of persistent nephrotic‐range proteinuria and uncontrolled systemic edema despite supportive treatment. The patient received methylprednisolone 500 mg/day for 3 days, followed by oral prednisolone 30 mg/day (0.6 mg/kg/day). Cyclosporine A (1.5 mg/kg/day) was added concurrently. Following treatment initiation, urinary protein excretion gradually decreased, serum albumin levels increased, and the prednisolone dose was progressively tapered. Empagliflozin was introduced at 206 months as additional supportive therapy. Partial remission had already been achieved by 204 months and has been maintained to date. FNG is a rare hereditary glomerular disease characterized by excessive deposition of fibronectin within the glomeruli. Several pathogenic variants in the FN1 gene on chromosome 2q32 have been identified as causative variants [ 1 ]. The c.2918A > G (NM_212482.4) variant, resulting in the p.Tyr973Cys substitution within the heparin‐binding domain III, is the most frequently reported mutation. This recurrent mutation has been described in multiple familial cases [ 10 , 11 , 12 ]. However, there have also been several reports of patients without a known family history, as in our case [ 10 , 13 ].

Light microscopy in the initial biopsy 15.5 years earlier demonstrated massive, diffuse PAS‐positive deposits. Similar findings were observed in the second biopsy; however, foam cell infiltration was also observed in both glomerular and tubulointerstitial areas. Although such findings are not typical of FNG, they may reflect long‐standing heavy proteinuria and lipid leakage associated with nephrotic syndrome [ 14 ]. In the second biopsy, immunofluorescence showed positive staining for IgG, IgM, C3, C1q, and fibrinogen. Although these immunoreactants are typically negative in FNG [ 2 ], similar findings have occasionally been reported in previous cases. Their clinical significance remains uncertain, and the nature of these deposits in this case cannot be definitively determined, including whether they represent nonspecific trapping of plasma proteins or a superimposed immune‐complex‐mediated process [ 1 , 15 ]. Electron microscopy revealed fibrillary deposits consistent with fibronectin fibrils [ 1 , 2 , 3 ], together with moderate foot process effacement, indicating podocyte injury. Dominant IST‐4 staining compared with IST‐9, indicating plasma fibronectin deposition, supported the diagnosis of FNG [ 1 , 16 ].

The clinical course of FNG is variable, even among individuals with the same mutation [ 5 ]. In a review of 76 cases, the mean age at diagnosis was 33.7 years, with a wide range from childhood to late adulthood [ 6 ]. In our patient, proteinuria began at 9 years of age and progressed to nephrotic‐range levels. Although renal function remained preserved, nephrotic‐range proteinuria has been associated with an increased risk of progression to end‐stage kidney disease [ 1 , 5 ].

There is currently no established treatment for FNG. Most patients are managed with supportive therapy, including angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, and recurrence after kidney transplantation has been reported [ 17 ]. Although systemic immunosuppressive therapy for FNG has not been established, prednisolone, calcineurin inhibitors, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, mizoribine, and Tripterygium wilfordii Hook F (thunder god vine) have been described in several cases [ 5 , 15 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ]. In the present case, prednisolone and cyclosporine A were administered because of persistent nephrotic‐range proteinuria and uncontrolled edema despite supportive therapy. Proteinuria decreased and serum albumin levels improved after the initiation of these treatments. Although various mechanisms for these agents have been proposed [ 15 , 19 , 20 ], their clinical relevance remains uncertain. It is unlikely that immunosuppressive therapy directly influenced fibronectin deposition. Cyclosporine A may have reduced proteinuria through its known effects on renal hemodynamics, which should be considered when interpreting the therapeutic response [ 22 ]. Beyond these hemodynamic effects, the therapeutic response may also be related to stabilization of podocyte function, a key determinant of proteinuria in many glomerular diseases. To further investigate the possibility of a superimposed autoimmune‐mediated podocytopathy, serum anti‐nephrin, anti‐podocin, and anti‐Kirrel1 autoantibodies were measured, and tissue analyses for IgG colocalization with nephrin, podocin, and Kirrel1 were performed [ 9 ]. Neither serum nor tissue analyses supported the currently recognized autoimmune podocytopathies. These findings suggest that the favourable therapeutic response may have involved mechanisms of podocyte injury beyond those currently recognized. In this context, the second biopsy demonstrated foam cell infiltration and weak immune deposits that were not evident in the initial biopsy. Although the clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain, they may reflect secondary inflammatory and podocyte‐associated changes superimposed on the underlying FNG [ 23 ].

During tapering of prednisolone, an SGLT2 inhibitor was added as adjunct supportive therapy for proteinuric chronic kidney disease (CKD) without major adverse effects. Although partial remission had already been achieved prior to its introduction, the SGLT2 inhibitor may have contributed to the maintenance of remission through hemodynamic and metabolic effects, including restoration of tubuloglomerular feedback and reduction of intraglomerular pressure, as reported in other forms of proteinuric CKD [ 24 ].

Overall, the clinicopathological features and long‐term clinical course of this case add to the limited literature on FNG and suggest that immunosuppressive therapy may be a potential therapeutic option for managing nephrotic syndrome associated with FNG, while SGLT2 inhibition may contribute to maintenance of remission.
