# Genetic analysis and intracytoplasmic sperm injection outcomes of Chinese patients with congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens.
**Authors:** Cheng H, Yang S, Meng Q, Zheng B, Gu Y, Wang L, Song T, Xu C, Wang G, Han M, Shen L, Ding J, Li H, Ouyang J
**Journal:** J Assist Reprod Genet (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10815-022-02417-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10815-022-02417-z)

## Content

PURPOSE: Congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD) is a major cause of obstructive azoospermia and male factor infertility. CBAVD is mainly caused by mutations in the genes encoding CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) and ADGRG2 (adhesion G protein-coupled receptor G2). This study aimed to describe CFTR and ADGRG2 variations in 46 Chinese CBAVD patients and evaluated sperm retrieval and assisted reproductive technology outcomes.

METHODS: The CFTR and ADGRG2 genes were sequenced and analyzed by whole-exome sequencing (WES), and variations were identified by Sanger sequencing. Bioinformatic analysis was performed. We retrospectively reviewed the outcomes of patients undergoing sperm retrieval surgery and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI).

RESULTS: In total, 35 of 46 (76.09%) patients carried at least one variation in CFTR, but no copy number variants or ADGRG2 variations were found. In addition to the IVS9-5 T allele, there were 27 CFTR variations, of which 4 variations were novel and predicted to be damaging by bioinformatics. Spermatozoa were successfully retrachieved in 46 patients, and 39 of the patients had their own offspring through ICSI.

CONCLUSION: There are no obvious hotspot CFTR mutations in Chinese CBAVD patients besides the IVS9-5 T allele. Therefore, WES might be the best detection method, and genetic counseling should be different from that provided to Caucasian populations. After proper counseling, all patients can undergo sperm retrieval from their epididymis or testis, and most of them can have their own children through ICSI.

Congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD) is an important cause of male factor infertility and obstructive azoospermia, which roughly accounts for 1–2% of male infertility and at least 6% of male obstructive azoospermia [1–3]. CBAVD exists as a simplex phenotypic trait or as an accompanied phenotype of the autosomal recessive inherited disease cystic fibrosis (CF). Most CBAVD patients have abnormalities of theCFTR(cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator, OMIM 602,421) gene [4,5], while some have variations in theADGRG2(adhesion G protein-coupled receptor G2) gene [6,7] or other genes such asSLC9A3[8],PANK2,SLC26A3[9],SCNN1B, andCA12[10].

CFTRhas an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern; the gene is located on chromosome 7q31.2 containing 27 exons, and it encodes the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator, which belongs to the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter superfamily that plays a critical role in chloride and bicarbonate homeostasis during sperm epididymal maturation and capacitation [11].ADGRG2is another CBAVD causative gene and has an X-linked inheritance pattern [6]. The gene encodes the adhesion G-protein-coupled receptor G2, which belongs to the adhesion-class G-protein-coupled receptors superfamily that is specifically expressed within the efferent ducts. Its mutation may result in obstructive azoospermia due to fluid accumulation in the testes [12]. The other genes linked to CBAVD were only described in sporadic cases.

According to previous reports,CFTRgene variations are responsible for most cases of CBAVD in patients; about 78 to 80% of CBAVD patients carry at least one variation in theCFTRgene, but compoundCFTRmutations are expected to cause pathogenic defects [11,13]. Compound heterozygote variations (at least two different variations) in theCFTRgene can be found in 63 to 83% [14–16] CBAVD patients. Genetic analysis of the entire coding and neighboring regions has become cost-effective with the growing application of whole-exome sequencing (WES). Novel gene variations continue to be discovered [8–10,17–19], including non-codingCFTRvariants with large rearrangements and deletions [16,20–22] and polymorphisms in the untranslated region (UTR) [23].

Asian CBAVD patients exhibit a differentCFTRmutation spectrum from Caucasians; the most common mutations in that population are F508del, R117H, G542X, and G551D, while the 5 T mutation is commonly reported with high frequency in Chinese (44.4%) [24] and Egyptian (43.7%) subjects [25].

Most couples with a CBAVD-affected partner can have biological offspring through assisted reproductive technology (ART). Considering that CBAVD is a disorder that impacts fertility, genetic testing and counseling are strongly recommended for affected couples [26,27]. However, noCFTRhotspot mutations have been identified in the East Asian population [26], so the European Association of Urology guideline forCFTRscreening [27] may be not appropriate for the Chinese population.

The aim of this work is to describe the phenomenon, the spectrum of pathological gene variations, and the sperm retrieval and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) outcomes of Chinese CBAVD patients to help guide genetic counseling, with the ultimate goal of using ART and/or preimplantation genetic screening (PGS) if needed to help them to have a healthy baby.

A total of 46 sporadic CBAVD patients seen at Suzhou Municipal Hospital’s outpatient clinic were enrolled from November 2018 to November 2019. All patients were diagnosed as CBAVD with impalpable scrotal vas confirmed by scrotum ultrasound, transrectal ultrasound (Table1). Semen parameters (no sperm detected after centrifugation in triplicate analyses, semen pH < 7.0, decreased concentration nor absence of fructose, lack of neutral alpha-glucosidase), hormone profile (normal concentrations of plasma follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, and plasma inhibin B), and testicle volume (a normal or larger testicular volume) were assessed for all CBAVD patients. No patients had any other symptom of CF or CF-related disorders. Karyotype analysis was normal in all men except one individual with a 46, XY, Yqh-karyotype. We excluded subjects with diseases related to male infertility such as Y chromosome microdeletion or with renal abnormalities or those with congenital unilateral absence of vas deferens.

Blood samples from CBAVD patients, their parents, and female partners (if needed) for further confirmation of compound heterozygous status orCFTRcarrier screening were preserved in 2-mL ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulant tubes (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and stored in a − 80 °C freezer.

The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Nanjing Medical University Affiliated Suzhou Hospital and was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All study participants signed informed consent forms. We analyzed and followed all patients who underwent sperm retrieval surgery for ART and their ICSI and/or PGS outcomes.

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA was fragmented with a Covaris-focused ultrasonicator (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA). The 5′- and 3′-UTRs, all exons and exon–intron boundary sequences (± 10 bp), and pathogenic deep introns ofCFTRandADGRG2were captured using the xGen Exome Research Panel (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA).

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, DNA libraries were prepared, and sequencing was performed on a HiSeq X10 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) with the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner [28]. Single nucleotide variants and indels were called using GATK, Platypus, VarScan, LoFreq, FreeBayes, SNVer, SAMtools, and VarDict [29] and filtered and annotated using the ANNOVAR software [30]. According to the 1000 Genomes Project and ExAC Browser, genetic variants with allele frequencies > 1% were excluded, while nonsense, essential splice-site, frameshift, and potentially deleterious missense (SIFT, MutationTaster, and PolyPhen-2) variants were retained for further analysis. According to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines, variants were classified into five levels: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of unknown significance, likely benign, and benign [31]. Copy number variation (CNV) based on sequencing depth was analyzed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer [32]. Genes with two or more alleles with potentially deleterious missense or loss-of-function mutations were retained because X-linked and autosomal recessive inheritance were assumed for CBAVD. We also compared candidate genes with pathogenic genes known to cause azoospermia in mice (http://www.informatics.jax.org/mgihome/homepages/) and enriched testis genes in the database (http://www.proteinatlas.org/). The sequencing and following bioinformatic analyses were conducted by the NuProbe company (Shanghai, China). The datasets used and analyzed will be provided by the corresponding author on reasonable request.

WES results were verified by Sanger sequencing to avoid false positives. The primer sequences will be given on request. Polymerase chain reaction products were bidirectionally sequenced by Sanger sequencing using a 3130 DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).

Spermatozoa were surgically extracted from the epididymis and/or testis of 46 patients. Percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration (PESA) and/or testicular sperm aspiration (TESA) was performed under local anesthesia. PESA was performed on the epididymis caput at the distal part using a syringe containing 0.5 mL of G-IVF fertilization medium (Vitrolife, Göteborg, Sweden); TESA was performed when spermatozoa were insufficient for ICSI or when the epididymis was not sufficiently distended. The epididymal fluid was diluted and washed twice in G-IVF, then sperm concentration, motility, and vitality were evaluated under an inverted phase contrast microscope. Sperm motility was categorized into four grades (A, B, C, and D) for practical use according to the 4th World Health Organization Laboratory Manual [33]. Then, the sperm was injected into the egg.

Participants’ hormonal parameters were normal. The clinical diagnosis of CBAVD was confirmed by ultrasound in all 46 patients. Almost all had acidic semen pH, relatively lower seminal fructose, and zero neutral alpha-glucosidase content. No CBAVD patient had a remarkable medical history.

All 46 samples sequenced had a mean depth of 123.56 ± 11.36 × , yielding roughly 11.67 ± 0.15 Gb of data per sample. Twenty-seven differentCFTRheterozygote variations were identified in 46 CBAVD patients, besides the IVS9-5 T allele (c.1210-7_1210-6delTT) (Table2). No variants were detected in 11 patients, 1 variant was detected in 13 patients, 2 variants (including 5 T) were detected in 20 patients, and 3 variants were detected in 2 patients. Two frameshift indels, four splicing mutations, and four missense mutations were classified as pathogenic. One missense mutation was classified as likely pathogenic. Five missense mutations had uncertain significance, and six missense had conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity. Most (76.09%, 35/46) patients had at least oneCFTRmutation including a c.1210-12 T (5 T,NM_000492.3) variant (29.35%, 27/92).

The 5 T variation showed the highest allelic frequency of the variants identified in our study. The second most common mutation (3.26%, 3/92) was c.650A > G (p.E217G), and the third highest allele frequency (2.17%, 2/92) was for c.2936A > C (p.D979A), c.2988 + 2 T > C, and c.4056G > C (p.Q1352H). Almost half (47.83%, 22/46) of CBAVD patients had at least two mutations in theCFTRgene; 28.26% (13/46) had only one, and 23.91% (11/46) had no mutations (Table2). NoADGRG2variations were identified in CBAVD patients. No CNV was found in theCFTRorADGRG2gene (Table1).

All variations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Compound heterozygote patterns (carrying at least twoCFTRmutations including the IVS9-5 T allele) were confirmed by Trio WES analysis of blood samples from the parents of the CBAVD patients. No pathogenic or likely pathogenicCFTRmutations were found in the spouses of the CBAVD patients, except one wife (No. CB12) with c.592G > C, who later selected PGS to have a healthy male.

Of 46 CBAVD patients, 24 (52.17%) carried 7 T/7 T alleles, 15 (32.61%) carried 5 T/7 T alleles, 6 (13.04%) carried 5 T/5 T alleles, and 1 (2.17%) carried 6 T/7 T alleles. No CBAVD patients carried the 9 T allele (Table1).

Among the 27 variants detected in 46 patients, 4 were novel (p.L32R (NM_000492:c.95 T > G), p.D110fs (NM_000492:c.330delC),NM_000492:c.579 + 7A > G, p.I444F (NM_000492:c.1330A > T)) (Table3). They were not previously recorded in the ClinVar,CFTRmutation, dbSNP, 1000G, or ExAC databases. These novel variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (data not shown). Spermatozoa were successfully retrieved through TESA/PESA from all 46 CBAVD patients. Of these, 34 samples had low motility (grade C or D) and a high abnormality rate, while 12 samples had high motility and relative high sperm concentrations (Table1). PESA was carried out on 37 samples, while TESA was performed for 4 of the 46 patients due to insufficient quantities of sperm for cryopreservation. Five patients underwent TESA as the first procedure because the epididymis was not distended. Overall, 39 (ages 20–36, mean age 27.31 ± 3.34) of the 46 couples gave birth to 29 males and 22 females, while the remaining seven couples (ages 24–34, mean age 28.43 ± 3.46) did not reproduce because of female infertility (three for oocyte maturation arrest, two for premature ovarian failure, and two for polycystic ovary syndrome and ovulation disorder).

In the present study, 46 Chinese CBAVD patients without any CF phenotypes were treated for infertility and azoospermia. The coding regions, UTRs, and partial introns of theCFTRandADGRG2genes were sequenced with WES. We investigated CBAVD pathogenesis based on bioinformatics analysis and validated the effects of the identified variations. The variant spectrum may provide a reference for clinical diagnosis and genetic counseling for Chinese CBAVD patients seeking to reproduce.

The results show that 35 of the 46 (76.09%) patients had at least oneCFTRvariant, which is slightly lower than 78 to 80% in previous reports [11,13]. However,CFTRgene mutations vary considerably depending on ethnic/geographic origins [34]. The Caucasian hotspotCFTRvariants p.M470V and p.F508del were not found in this study. The variant spectra differ between Asian and non-Asian populations [35–37]; according to studies carried out in Chinese populations,CFTRvariations are thought to be due to a founder effect [38]. Our results provide further support for the hypothesis that there are no so-called hotspot variations in China, indicating that this population might require different screening and counseling strategies.

The variations identified in this work are largely similar to previous studies, but there are some differences. The 5 T variant at the splice acceptor site of intron 8 plays a vital role in CBAVD; it was detected in 21 men and was the most common variant in Chinese CBAVD patients. The IVS9-5 T allele rates (29.35% overall, 32.61% with 5 T/7 T, and 13.04% with 5 T/5 T) were lower than those in other Chinese cohorts, which were 44.5% [39], 32.02% [40], and 55.26% [11]. Previous studies [41,42] reported that the 5 T allele could reduce exon 10 splicing efficiency and reduce the levels of full-length mRNA transcripts below a specific threshold, which leads to CBAVD or CF phenotypes. The vas deferens might be sensitive toCFTRdeficiency, so patients with the 5 T allele may have reduced transcription and symptoms such as azoospermia and infertility [26].

The other two variation rates for p.I556V (NM_000492:c.1666A > G) and p.Q1352H (NM_000492:c.4056G > C) were also high (4.48% and 1.85%, respectively) in the Eastern Asian population and the most common variants in Chinese CBAVD patients as previously reported [11,18,43]. However, we found only one and two patients carrying the variations, respectively (2.17% for p.I556V and 4.35% for p.Q1352H). This may be due to our limited sample size. The p.I556V variant is located in the NBD1 domain of theCFTRgene. A previous study [43] and the 1000 Genomes Database showed that c.1666A > G had a high frequency in the general East Asian population. Given what is known about this variant, it is hypothesized that the variant is a specific polymorphism in East Asians or has a weak pathogenic effect. The p.Q1352H variant is located in the NBD2 domain of theCFTRgene, and it can decrease the ion channel function ofCFTRprotein in vitro [44]. It is likely to be pathogenic, but several studies [45–47] showed that this variant was found as part of a predisposition screen in an ostensibly healthy population. In combination with allele frequency data from public databases, this variant is considered unlikely to cause CF or CBAVD and is therefore likely benign. Two patients in our study possessed this variant, suggesting that it may be the most common missense variant in Chinese CBAVD patients.

CFTRp.G970D is another frequently reported variation; one study detected it in 9 of 56 (16.07%) Chinese CF patients [38] and in 5 of 73 (6.84%) CBAVD patients [18], but it was only detected in 1 of 46 (2.17%) patients in the present study. However, c.650A > G was detected in 3 of 46 (6.52%), c.2936A > C, c.2988 + 2 T > C, and c.4056G > C were each detected in 2 of 46 (4.35%). The c.2988 + 2 T > C variation was pathogenic, and the other three had conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity. The remaining 22 mutations were detected in only single cases in current study, and 4 have not been described in the literature. This and previously reported findings indicate that the hotspot-basedCFTRscreening strategy may not be suitable for Chinese CBAVD patients. Given that the variations are distributed throughout the whole gene, a panel for azoospermia including 67 genes or WES may be more appropriate for screening.

To date, 2105 variants have been detected in theCFTRgene. According to their deleterious effects, these variants can be classified into mild (classes IV, V, and VI) and severe (classes I, II, and III) phenotypes [48,49]. In this study, four splicing and two frameshift mutations (c.2988 + 2 T > C, c.3874-4A > G, c.579 + 7A > G, c.3469-3C > A, p.D110fs, and p.T682fs) could be classified as class I because they result in defective synthesis of the full-length protein. The 5 T variant is class V because it reduces protein expression but does not inhibit its function [41]. Almost half (47.83%) of the CBAVD patients in this study had at least two mutations in theCFTRgene, and the presence of two or more mildCFTRalleles results in atypical CF-related diseases [50]; in combination, these “mild” mutations do not lead to upper airway disease, only vasal absence as CBAVD.

Previous reports have demonstrated that theCFTRprotein participates in spermatogenesis, and mutations in the gene may result in azoospermia [51,52]. Llabador et al. [53] assessed the sperm retrieval and ICSI outcomes of 108 CBAVD patients and showed that only 30.5% had hypospermatogenesis and 19.4% had normal spermatogenesis. The results from their CBAVD patients revealed that spermatogenesis was slightly impaired, which is similar to our findings. Spermatozoa were retrieved from all 46 patients undergoing PESA or TESA (100% retrieval rate), but most of them had only C or D grade sperm motility (Table3). We speculate that spermatogenesis may be affected inCFTR-related CBAVD patients, but further studies are needed to confirm our conclusion because of the small sample size.

In summary, 46 CBAVD patients received a definitive genetic diagnosis after screening the entireCFTRandADGRG2genes, then we assessed their risk of passing down a deleterious mutation. We found that rareCFTRvariants play a major role in the Chinese CBAVD population, but hotspot mutations do not. This study also revealed thatADGRG2variants do not play a major role in CBAVD in this population. We speculate that mild or heterozygousCFTRgene mutations carried by CBAVD patients may contribute to lower sperm quality, but this is unclear because of the low sperm concentrations in samples from our cohort.

The absence of bilateral vas deferens renders CBAVD patients prevent them from conceiving naturally, but their testes usually have normal spermatogenesis. Most can have biological children by obtaining sperm through TESA or PESA and using ART. In our study, 39 of 46 CBAVD couples were able to have their own children by PESA/TESA and ICSI; they achieved a pregnancy rate of 84.8% and a live delivery rate of 100% after 48 ICSI cycles, which was higher than previous reports [53,54]. The age of female partners ranged from 20 to 36, and the mean age was 27.31 ± 3.34 years. Spermatozoa origin did not affect ICSI outcomes, similar to Kamal’s study [55]. The other seven couples (ages 24–34, mean age 28.43 ± 3.46) were unable to reproduce due to female fertility issues. Three females were later diagnosed with oocyte maturation arrest caused byTUBB8orZP3gene mutations, two for premature ovarian failure, and two for polycystic ovary syndrome and ovulation disorder; none of these seven females had useful oocytes after various ovarian stimulation methods. Our high pregnancy and live birth rates may be due to the young age and strong fertility of the female partners. Our small sample size may also have introduced bias, so we need to collect more samples for further analysis.

Although most Chinese CBAVD patients can have their own children with assistance, there is a relatively high risk of these offspring developing CBAVD [56], underscoring the importance of detectingCFTRandADGRG2gene mutations in men with CBAVD. If a pathogenic mutation is detected, the spouse’s entireCFTRgene should be sequenced, genetic counseling should be conducted based on the results, and ICSI and PGS should be considered to prevent hereditary risk to the offspring. Prenatal screening can also be considered to reduce the birthrate of children with CBAVD [57]. However, only one couple (No. CB12) employed PGS as the wife was found to be a c.592G > C mutation carrier. Of five embryos, two did not carry mutations, and one was transplanted that resulted in the birth of a healthy male. We will further trace the clinical phenotypes and genotypes of the 39 families who successfully reproduced and provide clinical genetic counseling when their children enter adulthood.

In conclusion, we suggest that Chinese CBAVD couples be screened forCFTRandADGRG2mutations prior to conception. WES or a 67-gene panel (includingCFTRandADGRG2) linked to male infertility is a cost-effective method to screen mutations in promoter regions, exons, and their flanking sites (especially the 5 T mutation). Based on the screening results, we can suggest if a couple should seek reproduction assistance using PGS or ICSI. Pathological variations could not be detected in some CBAVD patients, indicating that the condition has genetic heterogeneity and there are still other unknown genetic factors to explore. Patients without identified causative genes should undergo additional tests to determine the possible pathogenesis.
