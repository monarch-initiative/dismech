Sleep is evolutionarily conserved across all species, and impaired sleep is a 
common trait of the diseased brain. Sleep quality decreases as we age, and 
disruption of the regular sleep architecture is a frequent antecedent to the 
onset of dementia in neurodegenerative diseases. The glymphatic system, which 
clears the brain of protein waste products, is mostly active during sleep. Yet 
the glymphatic system degrades with age, suggesting a causal relationship 
between sleep disturbance and symptomatic progression in the neurodegenerative 
dementias. The ties that bind sleep, aging, glymphatic clearance, and protein 
aggregation have shed new light on the pathogenesis of a broad range of 
neurodegenerative diseases, for which glymphatic failure may constitute a 
therapeutically targetable final common pathway.
