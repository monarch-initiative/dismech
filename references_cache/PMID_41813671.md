# CD8(+) T cell loss induces autoinflammation in inborn errors of cell death.
**Authors:** Dai J, Jin T, Su G, Han X, Wang J, Liu C, Zheng W, Zhang Q, Zhang R, Kuehn HS, Yang J, Guo L, Zhang D, Li M, Xu Y, Yue T, Wen M, Zhu J, Kang M, Lai J, Wu F, Wang S, Zhang J, Aksentijevich I, Rosenzweig SD, Lee PY, Yuan J, Yu X, Zhou Q
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-026-70317-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-70317-2)

## Content

Systemic autoinflammatory diseases (SAID) with inborn errors of cell death (IECD) are caused by overactivation of programmed cell death (PCD). However, the pathogenesis by which PCD leads to autoinflammation remains unclear. Here, we identified IECD patients carrying compound heterozygous RIPK1 variants K377E/R390G with autoinflammatory manifestations. Mechanistically, K377E and R390G mutations suppress NF-κB signaling and activate RIPK1 to promote cell death. CD8+ T cells of the patients displayed overactivated RIPK1 and excessive cell death, leading to an elevated CD4/CD8 ratio, which could also be detected in patients with cleavage-resistant mutation of RIPK1 or SHARPIN deficiency. We show that the increased cell death of CD8+ T cells promotes TNF and IFN-γ secretion to activate monocytes/macrophages, which triggers overproduction of proinflammatory cytokines. In addition, disruption of the communication between T cells and monocytes/macrophages through pharmacologic blockade of TNF and IFN attenuates proinflammatory cytokine production in macrophages and relieves all the symptoms in patients. This study further clarifies the mechanism for a group of IECD with SAID. Increased CD4/CD8 ratio and augmented RIPK1 activation in T cells provide potentially additional criteria for diagnosis of RIPK1-dependent IECD and a combination of TNF/JAK inhibitor could be an effective therapy for the diagnosed patients.

Systemic autoinflammatory diseases (SAIDs) are a group of disorders induced by overactivation of the innate immune system characterized by recurrent fever and multi-organ inflammation, but typically without high-titer antibodies or antigen-specific T cells1,2. To date, more than 50 genes responsible for monogenic SAIDs have been identified. The list of genes whose mutations lead to SAIDs is enriched with key regulators of programmed cell death (PCD). Enhanced PCD is one of the characteristics in the SAID patients caused by the cleavage resistance mutations in RIPK13–5 or deficiencies of RIPK16,7, TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1)8, OTU deubiquitinase with linear linkage specificity (OTULIN), HOIL1-interacting protein (HOIP)9, Heme-oxidized IRP2 ubiquitin ligase-1 (HOIL1)10, and Shank-associated RH domain-interacting protein (SHARPIN)11. This clinical entity is designated as ‘inborn errors of cell death (IECD)’11. Pathogenic variants of IECD have been shown to activate apoptosis and necroptosis in cellular or murine models12. Therapies targeting inflammatory cytokines, such as anti-IL6, anti-TNF, or anti-IL-1 antibodies, were applied to treat IECD patients, but refractory cases still showed unresponsiveness or tolerance after long-term treatment3,5,8,11. Therefore, investigation of therapies targeting upstream molecules is urgently needed to offer more effective treatment on the refractory SAID with IECD. The excessive cell death is strongly linked to autoinflammatory diseases in humans, while the pathogenesis by which cell death causes spontaneous inflammation in these conditions is unknown. The study of the pathogenesis aids the improvement of targeted therapy for refractory IECD.

RIPK1 is a key mediator of cell death and inflammation13. Overactivation of RIPK1 leads to enhanced apoptosis, necroptosis, and inflammatory response in mice and humans14. Whereas RIPK1 deficiency and heterozygous non-cleavable variants in RIPK1 cause immunodeficiency and dominantly inherited autoinflammatory diseases, respectively, a recessive autoinflammatory disease caused by RIPK1 variants has not been reported. K63-linked ubiquitination of RIPK1 on K377 is essential for NF-κB pathway activation and negative regulation of cell death. Mice bearing Ripk1K376R/K376R knockin mutation display embryonic lethality due to excessive apoptosis and necroptosis caused by RIPK1 activation15–17. Nevertheless, the function of RIPK1 ubiquitination in human disease has not been defined or studied. In this study, we identified two autoinflammatory patients carrying recessively inherited compound heterozygous RIPK1 variants K377E/R390G. We demonstrate that RIPK1 K377E/R390G variants lead to autoinflammatory disease by promoting RIPK1 activation-mediated death of CD8+ T cells and identify TNF and IFN-γ as molecules linking programmed cell death and autoinflammation.

We identified two children from one family with autoinflammatory disease characterized by recurrent fevers, lymphadenopathy, and skin rashes (Fig. 1a–d). They also developed colitis characterized by diarrhea, abdominal pain and perianal abscess. At the age of 7 months and 4 years, respectively, patient 1 (P1) and patient 2 (P2) displayed fever episodes that lasted 3-4 days and recurred every 8–9 days. Their fever episodes were accompanied by elevated serum C-reactive protein (CRP), serum amyloid A (SAA), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), D-dimer, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (Fig. 1e, and Supplementary Fig. 1a–c). Neither patient exhibited evidence of immunodeficiency. In addition, the levels of proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF were strongly increased in the serum of both patients during flares (Fig. 1f). Serum CRP levels and adenosine deaminase (ADA) activity in patients were higher than those of unaffected controls and parents during disease flares and even in remission (Fig. 1f). By performing whole exome sequencing (WES) and Sanger sequencing on the family, we identified that both P1 and P2 carried the RIPK1 compound heterozygous mutations (c. 1129 A > G / p. K377E and c. 1168 C > G / p. R390G), whereas their unaffected mother (and maternal grandmother) and father (and paternal grandmother) were heterozygous carriers of RIPK1 R390G and K377E, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). All heterozygous family members were healthy. Therefore, this autoinflammatory disease in the siblings is recessively inherited and segregated with the inheritance of both RIPK1 variants. The lysine residue at amino acid position 377 is the site for K63-linked ubiquitination of RIPK118, while the function of residue R390 has not been characterized. Both K377 and R390 residues are conserved in RIPK1 across species (Supplementary Fig. 1f). The two RIPK1 variants were predicted to be deleterious with their CADD scores well above that of the non-cleavable RIPK1 mutations D324V, D324H, and D324N (Supplementary Fig. 1g). No other candidate variants were identified by WES.

Given that RIPK1 is a widely expressed regulator of programmed cell death, we utilized SV40-immortalized human dermal fibroblasts (SV40-HDFs) and primary T cells derived from unaffected controls and P2 to assess the sensitivity to apoptosis induced by TNF (T) with cIAP1/2 degrader SM-164 (S) as well as necroptosis induced by TNF (T) with pan-caspase inhibitor z-VAD-FMK (Z) or TSZ. HDFs from P2 showed increased susceptibility to both TZ/TSZ-induced necroptosis, which was fully rescued by RIPK1 kinase inhibitor Necrostatin-1s (Nec-1s), and to TS-induced apoptosis, which was only partially inhibited by Nec-1s (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Fig. 2a). Primary T cells isolated from patients, but not heterozygous carriers and unaffected controls, were more sensitive to TS-induced apoptosis or TSZ-induced necroptosis, which was in accordance with the recessive inheritance pattern (Fig. 1h). P2’s SV40-HDFs and P1’s primary T cells displayed higher levels of RIPK1 phosphorylation at S166 and MLKL phosphorylation at S358 under cell death stimulation, and these patterns could be inhibited by Nec-1s (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c), indicating that SV40-HDFs and T cells bearing K377E/R390G mutations were prone to RIPK1 activation-induced cell death. Thus, compound R390G and K377E mutations in RIPK1 sensitized patients’ cells to programmed cell death.

To further investigate the function of K377E and R390G mutations in RIPK1 under isogenic conditions, we established HT-29 cell models with either the K377E or R390G knockin mutation. Both mutant cell lines showed increased sensitivity to TZ-induced cell death (Supplementary Fig. 2d). The elevated cell death levels, along with enhanced RIPK1 and MLKL phosphorylation in K377E and R390G knockin HT-29 cells, could be reversed by Nec-1s (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f). Thus, both K377E and R390G mutations in RIPK1 could promote cell death. We also determined the effect of RIPK1 K377E and R390G mutations on the TNFR1 downstream NF-κB and MΑPK pathways. The activation of both NF-κB and ERK signaling was partially impaired in P2 HDFs compared to unaffected controls (Supplementary Fig. 2g). Consistently, NF-κB activation and p38 phosphorylation were also suppressed in K377E or R390G knockin HT-29 cells upon TNF stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 2h). Consequently, the transcription of TNF, IL1B, and IL8 was reduced in K377E or R390G knockin HT-29 cells compared to WT control (Supplementary Fig. 2i). The TNFR signaling complex forms upon TNF stimulation is crucial for NF-κB activation19. Notably, both P2 HDFs and K377E or R390G knockin HT-29 cells showed reduced RIPK1 ubiquitination shifts within the TNFR signaling complex (Supplementary Fig. 2j, k).

K377 is a ubiquitination site in the RIPK1 intermediate domain (ID domain)18. Consistently, the K377E mutation, but not R390G, directly abolished the ubiquitination of the ID domain (Supplementary Fig. 2l). Since R390 is not expected to be a direct site for ubiquitination, we considered the possibility that R390G might affect the recruitment of E3 ubiquitination modifiers. We screened key reported key regulators of K63-linked ubiquitination within the TNFR signaling complex, including the E3 ubiquitin ligases TNF Receptor-Associated Factor 2 (TRAF2), cellular Inhibitor of Apoptosis 1 (cIAP-1), cellular Inhibitor of Apoptosis 2 (cIAP-2), Mind Bomb-2 (MIB2), Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha-Induced Protein 3 (A20), the deubiquitinase cylindromatosis (CYLD), as well as the key adaptor protein TRADD (TNFR1-associated death domain protein). We found that the interaction between MIB2 and the RIPK1 R390G mutation was significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 2m, n). Furthermore, MIB2 has been reported to mediate the ubiquitination of RIPK1 at Lys377 and Lys 63420. The reduced interaction between MIB2 and R390G therefore could explain the impaired ubiquitination of the R390G RIPK1 mutant. Collectively, these data demonstrate that both K377E and R390G mutations promote RIPK1 activation-dependent cell death and impair pro-survival NF-κB signaling.

We next studied the inflammatory signaling pathways by performing single-cell RNA sequencing on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from P1, P2, and unaffected controls. The myeloid compartment in patients was significantly expanded (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Fig. 3a). Similar to the previously reported RIPK1 D324V mutant4, transcriptional up-regulation of proinflammatory cytokines (IL1B and CXCL8) was detected in patient cells, especially in monocyte subsets (Fig. 2b). Likewise, differential gene expression analysis revealed the greatest up-regulation of genes governed by NF-κB and type-I IFN pathways in myeloid cells from the patients (Fig. 2c, d, and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). The up-regulated genes in the patients’ monocytes were also enriched for pathways of cytokine production, cytokine stimulation, and negative regulation of cell death in the gene ontology (GO) analysis (Fig. 2e, and Supplementary Fig. 3d), suggesting that myeloid cells play a crucial role in increased cytokine production and proinflammatory signal transduction in the patients. Increased basal expression of IL-1β, IL-6 as well as IFNγ in monocytes from P1 was confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2f). However, monocytes isolated from the patients exhibited reduced transcription of NF-κB regulated genes including IL1B, IL6, CXCL8 and TNF in response to TNF stimulation (Fig. 2g), in accordance with partially suppressed NF-κB activation in SV40-HDFs as demonstrated earlier (Supplementary Fig. 2g). Thus, we assessed the cell death response of monocytes to TNF stimulation. The monocytes from the patients were more sensitive to TS-induced apoptosis than that of unaffected controls, whereas they showed similar sensitivity to necroptosis induced by TSZ (Fig. 2h). In addition, no differences of apoptosis and necroptosis were observed between monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) from patients and that from unaffected controls (Fig. 2i, and Supplementary Fig. 3e). On the other hand, although the expression of cell death suppressor genes, including TNFAIP3, BCL2A1, BCL6, BCL3, RNF144B, BIRC2 and BIRC3, was elevated across various patient immune cell types, their expression levels were markedly higher in myeloid lineage cells compared to lymphocyte subsets (Fig. 2j, k and Supplementary Fig. 3f). Therefore, the elevated inflammatory response of monocytes in patients may be regulated non-autonomously by other cell types that were more susceptible to cell death.

Group 2 contained 24 cytokines (e.g., sCD95) that were persistently elevated, while Group 3 consisted of 32 cytokines that showed a continuous decrease (Fig. 3c, and Supplementary Fig. 4c–f). In order to further define the source of the cytokines in group 1 and 2, we compare the expression scores of the two groups between patients and unaffected controls by analyzing single-cell RNA-seq data. The cytokines in group 1 were most significantly up-regulated in patients’ monocytes, low-density granulocyte (LDG), plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC), and plasma cells (Fig. 3d), consistent with the strong inflammatory signature in myeloid cells during fever. Thus, amplification of inflammation by myeloid cells is an important step for fever episode. Notably, the genes encoding cytokines in group 2 were up-regulated most significantly in the patients’ lymphocyte subsets, especially in CD8+ T cells, implying a pathogenic role of K377E/R390G variants in T lymphocytes (Fig. 3e, and Supplementary Fig. 4g). In addition, the expression levels of cell death suppressor genes in T lymphocytes were also lower compared with myeloid cells (Supplementary Fig. 3f), suggesting that T lymphocytes may be sensitized to undergo programmed cell death. These results supported a critical role of monocytes in promoting inflammation and implied that T lymphocyte subsets may also contribute to the pathophysiology of this autoinflammatory disease.

To better understand the impact of RIPK1 mutations on the immune profile, we performed cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) on PBMCs isolated from unaffected controls and patients. CyTOF result revealed that CD4+ T, B, and γδ T cell proportions in patients were elevated, whereas CD8+ T and NK cell proportions were reduced compared to unaffected controls (Supplementary Figs. 5a, b). Apoptosis, indicated by cleaved caspase-3, was increased in CD8+ TEMRA, γδT, NK, and B cell subsets of patients (Fig. 3f). Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that patients’ CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, not CD19+ B cells and CD14+ monocytes, displayed augmented activation of RIPK1 and cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 3g–j and Supplementary Fig. 5c–g). Therefore, T cells were the most sensitive compartment for RIPK1 activation and PCD in vivo.

The CyTOF results also revealed a global activation of patients’ immune cells as indicated by CD69 expression, while elevated CD38 expression appeared to be restricted to B cells and T cells (Supplementary Fig. 5h, i). Increased expression of CD69 in T cells was further validated by flow cytometry (Fig. 3k). Concomitantly, higher levels of T cell activation-related proinflammatory mediators, including sCD25, TNF, and IFNγ, were observed in the serum of P1 and P2 as well as (cleavage-resistant RIPK1-induced autoinflammatory) CRIA patients (Fig. 3l), indicating that patients’ T cells were overactivated.

Activated lymphocytes undergo cell expansion and indeed we observed an elevation in the number of total lymphocytes, as well as that of CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells and B cells, except for that of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and NKT cells in the immunophenotyping results (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Fig. 6a). As a consequence of the loss of CD8+ T cells, CD4/CD8 ratio was significantly increased in patients. Of note, CD4/CD8 T cell ratio in heterozygous parents remained normal, indicating that alteration of this ratio requires biallelic K377/R390 variants in RIPK1 (Supplementary Fig. 6b). In vitro expansion of T cells isolated from P1 upon anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation was abnormal, but the proliferation ability was undampened (Fig. 4b, c), reflecting the occurrence of T cell death. Specifically, CD8+ T cells went through a contraction phase after activation, presumably resulted from activation-induced cell death (AICD)22. To further characterize the abnormalities in the patient’s T cells, we analyzed T cell subsets from in vitro cultured PBMCs. We observed that CD8 expression was decreased in approximate half of the CD8+ T cells after culturing PBMCs isolated from P2 in vitro for 24 h, while the expression on CD8+ T cells of unaffected controls remained normal (Fig. 4e). Through gating on CD8hi (CD8+ T cells with normal CD8 expression) or CD8lo (CD8+ T cells with lower CD8 expression) T cells, we found that in contrast to CD8hi T cells, most of the CD8lo T cells exhibited augmented cleaved caspase-3, indicative of activated apoptosis. In addition, total RIPK1 level in CD8lo T cells decreased, which may be caused by caspase-3 cleavage (Fig. 4e).

The decreased CD8 expression in patients’ T cells and the increases in CD4/CD8 ratio were more prominent after prolonged in vitro culture or after TS or TSZ stimulation compared to that of control (Fig. 4f, and Supplementary Fig. 6c–e). Therefore, we hypothesized that CD8+ T cells were more prone to undergo PCD than CD4+ T cells. Then we isolated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from patients and heterozygous carriers and stimulated with TS or TSZ. We found that CD8+ T cells were more sensitive to PCD than CD4+ T cells, which explained the increase of the CD4/CD8 ratio under PCD stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 6f, g). Moreover, we also performed in situ knock-in of WT RIPK1 in patients’ T cells, which successfully reversed the CD4/CD8 ratio induced by TS or TSZ, further confirming the activated RIPK1 resulting in increased cell death and CD4/CD8 ratio in T cells (Fig. 4g, h and Supplementary Fig. 6h, i). In contrast, neither ferroptosis induced by RSL3 nor RIPK1-independent apoptosis induced by staurosporine (STS) can differentiate cell death of CD8+ T cells from the patients compared to that of unaffected controls (Supplementary Fig. 6j). In addition, the activation of patients’ T cells by anti-CD3 antibody stimulation increased CD4/CD8 ratio, which could be reversed by ponatinib or partially reversed by Nec-1s (Fig. 4i, j and Supplementary Fig. 6k, l). Since ponatinib inhibits the kinase activity of RIPK1 and RIPK323, these results suggest that they might play important roles in regulating AICD in the patient T cells bearing K377E/R390G compound RIPK1 mutations.

To investigate the effect of RIPK1 K377E and R390G mutations on the CD4/CD8 ratio of T cells in mice, we crossed Ripk1K376R/+ with Ripk1+/– mice to generate Ripk1K376R/− mice, which could be weaned at Mendelian ratio but develop severe systemic inflammation (Supplementary Fig. 6m)15,16. Interestingly, no difference of CD4/CD8 ratio in peripheral T cells was observed between Ripk1K376R/– and Ripk1+/– mice at 3 weeks old (Fig. 4k). In contrast, the CD4/CD8 ratio in peripheral T cells from 8-week-old Ripk1K376R/− mice was significantly higher than that in the other three genotypes (Fig. 4l, and Supplementary Fig. 6n), which is consistent with that of K377E/R390G patients. Together, these results suggested that T cells, especially CD8+ T cells, were the most susceptible hematopoietic lineage to RIPK1 activation-induced cell death, as reflected by the elevated CD4/CD8 in patients with the RIPK1 K377E/R390G variants.

The results showed that patients’ T cells induced higher cytokine expression in monocytes derived macrophages (MDMs) comparing to that of T cells isolated from unaffected controls (Fig. 5b, c). The impaired inflammatory response by patients’ MDMs may be explained by the suppressed NF-κB activation caused by RIPK1 K377E/R390G mutations. Interestingly, patients’ T cells could still trigger massive cytokine production in autologous MDMs (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Previous studies have proposed TNF and IFNγ secreted by chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells as key contributors to the activation of macrophages during cytokine release syndrome (CRS)24. TNF combined with IFNγ was also reported to be a key inducer of inflammation in patients with severe COVID-1925. IFNγ and TNF were specifically upregulated in patients’ T lymphocytes and NK cells, whereas receptors of these two cytokines were highly expressed in myeloid cells (Fig. 5d, and Supplementary Fig. 7b, c), providing additional support for T cell-monocyte/macrophage communication through these two receptor-ligand pairs. In comparison to unaffected controls, T cells, especially CD8+ T cells from patients, exhibited greater production of IFNγ and TNF. The production of these cytokines was further enhanced upon TS or TSZ stimulation (Fig. 5e, and Supplementary Fig. 7d). These results indicated that IFNγ and TNF produced by activated T cells may be the key factors to initiate the inflammatory response of MDMs. Consistently, increased production of TNF, IL1B, and IL6 in MDMs generated from unaffected controls stimulated by either patient T cells or anti-CD3/CD28-activated donor-matched T cells could be partially inhibited by neutralization of IFNγ or TNF alone. The cytokine production was completely blocked by combined blockade of IFNγ and TNF (Fig. 5f, and Supplementary Fig. 7e). Collectively, these data demonstrated that RIPK1 overactivation in CD8+ T cells promoted TNF and IFNγ secretion to trigger inflammatory cytokine production in MDMs.

In order to validate this model and assess whether the function of RIPK1 K377E and R390G mutations in CD8+ T cells was sufficient to induce inflammation, we exploited the Cre-loxp system combined with bone marrow transplantation to conditionally express WT or mutated human RIPK1 in specific hematopoietic compartments (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Fig. 8a). Mouse lineage negative bone marrow cells (Lin- BMs, including hematopoietic stem cells and marrow stromal cells) isolated from donor Cd8a-creERT2 mice or Lyz2-creERT2 mice were transduced with lentivirus encoding EF1α-promoted loxp-GFP-stop-loxp-TagBFP2-P2A-human RIPK1 expression cassettes (LSL-hRIPK1). Afterwards, transduced Line- BMs were transplanted into lethally irradiated donor mice followed by tamoxifen induction. Transplantation efficiency and the specificities of Cd8a and Lyz2 promoters were validated (Supplementary Fig. 8b–h). GFP expression in Line- BMs reflected transduction efficiency, and TagBFP2 confirmed WT/mutated RIPK1 expression in specific cell types upon tamoxifen induction (Supplementary Fig. 9). We then evaluated the serum levels of inflammatory cytokines in the recipients. Under basal conditions, serum Tnf and Il-6 levels were higher in mice with CD8+ T cells expressing mutated RIPK1 (K377E, R390G) than WT RIPK1 and empty vector (EV) controls (Fig. 5h). No significant differences were observed between mice bearing myeloid cells overexpressing EV/WT RIPK1 controls and mutated RIPK1 (Fig. 5i). The result demonstrated the pathogenic function of K377E and R390G as well as the initiator role of T cells in the autoinflammation.

Since TNF and IFNγ were considered to mediate the inflammatory cytokine production of myeloid cells from patients, we assumed that disruption of communication between T cells and myeloid cells by pharmacologic blockade of TNF and IFNγ could be a promising therapeutic strategy. Therefore, we compared the treatment effectiveness of TNF inhibitor or IL-6 inhibitor alone with combined blockade of TNF and IFNγ pathway (Fig. 6a, b). Both patients showed alleviation of fever frequencies and the inflammatory indexes. However, the P1 developed skin rashes during the treatment. Moreover, the increased serum levels of TNF in both patients could not be reversed by tocilizumab. The symptoms of colitis presented in P1 could not be relieved by tocilizumab (Fig. 6a). TNF-targeting therapy, adalimumab, was also administered, and both P1 and P2 responded with reduced severity of skin rashes and decreased frequency of fevers (Fig. 6a, b). Nevertheless, P2 still developed colitis (Fig. 6b). Moreover, adalimumab had limited effects on WBC and lymphocyte counts (Fig. 6c). At the same time, adalimumab alone was not enough to suppress serum levels of IL-6, in accordance with the partial inhibitory effect of adalimumab on IL-6 produced by MDMs in the co-culture assay (Figs. 5f and 6c). We next trailed the combined therapy blocking both TNF and IFNγ signaling in P1 and P2. Since the IFNγ-neutralizing antibody emapalumab was only approved for hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis locally, we used ruxolitinib, which selectively targets IFNγ downstream JAK1/JAK2 kinase as an alternative in combination with adalimumab (Fig. 6a–c). Strikingly, after treated with combined therapy, both P1 and P2 no longer exhibited symptoms of fever and colitis and remained clinically asymptomatic for over one year. Concurrently, all tested inflammatory profiles, including increased counts and frequencies of WBC and lymphocyte, elevated serum levels of IL-6 and TNF, were recovered after the treatment (Fig. 6c). This is a report of biallelic mutations K377E/R390G in RIPK1 causing early-onset SAIDs. Mechanistically, K377E/R390G mutations lead to defects in NF-κB and activation of RIPK1, which promote cell death and systemic inflammation. The condition in our study is inherited in an autosomal recessive (AR) pattern, which is different from the autosomal dominantly inherited CRIA syndrome, even if they share similar autoinflammatory manifestations. In mouse models, Ripk1D325A/D325A mice died at embryonic day 10.53. In contrast, Ripk1K376R/K376R mice died at embryonic day 12.515,16. The differential lethality of these two mutations indicate that the D325A mutation may be more deleterious than the K376R mutation. Furthermore, mutation in RIPK1 D324 does not impair NF-κB pathway3–5, while compound heterozygous mutations in RIPK1 K377/R390 suppressed NF-κB activation. This mechanism may account for the distinct pathogenicity between D324 and K377/R390.

Mutations of genes such as TBK1, HOIP, HOIL1, and SHARPIN, have been shown to cause several SAIDs8–11. Variants of these genes have been studied in murine models and have been revealed to cause embryonic lethality attributed to enhanced cell death19,26–28. In contrast, patients with RIPK1-dependent programmed cell death-driven SAIDs (PCD-driven SAIDs) only manifest periodic inflammation with excessive production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF. Overactivation of cell death has been observed in the cells derived from these patients, supporting an important role of cell death in the pathogenesis of SAIDs. However, the distinct phenotypes between patient and mouse indicate that dysregulated cell death may exerts distinct effects on human and mouse cell. Therefore, the cell types considered as drivers of autoinflammation in mouse require further validation using patient-derived samples.

Our study provides a mechanism that K377E/R390G variants of RIPK1 induce programmed cell death in T cells to cause SAIDs. CD8+ T cells bearing K377E/R390G have the propensity to be activated and expanded and were prone to undergo programmed cell death at the same time. During this process, CD8+ T cells secrete massive TNF and IFNγ, which prime monocytes or macrophages to produce proinflammatory cytokines. The accumulation of the inflammatory cytokines, such as group 2 cytokines secreted from monocytes, would induce the onset of fever. At the same time, inflammation accelerates the proliferation of T lymphocytes and autoinflammation with a positive feedback loop that amplifies the inflammatory reaction (Fig. 7g). Eventually, inflammation may be terminated with the decrease of activated CD8+ T cells.

This model provides a new insight that not only the innate immune system, but also adaptive immune cells can play a causative role in the onset of SAIDs. We demonstrate that the death of T cells promotes TNF and IFNγ secretion and acts as the trigger of inflammation, while myeloid cells, such as monocytes and macrophages, serve as executors of PCD-driven SAIDs in that they take responsibility to produce excessive cytokines to induce autoinflammation. Hence, cellular communication is essential for the pathogenesis of PCD-driven SAIDs, and the molecules contributing to this process could be the therapeutic targets.

The elevation of CD4/CD8 ratio was detected in peripheral lymphocytes of the patients with CRIA syndrome and Sharpenia (Fig. 7e, f), implying that the pathogenesis of CD8+ T cell death driven autoinflammation is also applicable to these two conditions. We proposed that increased CD4/CD8 ratio, augmented RIPK1 activation in T cells, combined with inflammatory indexes, are the potential markers for PCD-driven SAIDs. These biomarkers may aid the diagnosis of PCD-driven SAIDs with unidentified pathogenic factors.

Anti-TNF and anti-IL-6 therapies offered partial improvement on symptoms of both P1 and P2. Previous studies also reported that some refractory patients with PCD-driven SAIDs displayed tolerance to anti-TNF or anti-IL-6 antibodies3,5,8,11. Colitis of P1 could not be relieved using a combination of JAK1/JAK2 kinase activity inhibitor ruxolitinib and anti-IL-6 tocilizumab (Fig. 6a). By contrast, combined therapy with dual-inhibition of TNF and JAK in two patients showed successful amelioration, supporting that blockade of communication between T cells and myeloid cells is effective therapy for PCD-driven SAIDs. Based on the finding that the emapalumab/adalimumab combination reduced cytokine levels to baseline in our co-culture system, we propose that this combination therapy may translate to clinical benefit (Fig. 5f). Besides, inhibition of RIPK1 activation with RIPK1 kinase inhibitors or RIPK1 proteolysis targeting chimera degraders could be a promising therapeutic strategy as they could directly inhibit RIPK1 activation-induced cell death and inflammation, but more efforts are needed in clinical trials13,29.

Taken together, we reported biallelic mutations K377E/R390G in RIPK1 leading to early-onset SAIDs and demonstrated the mechanism that both K377E and R390G mutations impair RIPK1 ubiquitination, which suppresses NF-κB and activates RIPK1 to induce cell death. Our findings also revealed the essential role of T cells in PCD-driven SAIDs, as they are the most sensitive hematopoietic compartments to cell death and have the predisposition to undergo AICD, which motivates the inflammatory response of myeloid cells. This finding indicates the importance of cell-cell communication in the pathogenesis of PCD-driven SAIDs. What is more, increased CD4/CD8 ratio, augmented RIPK1 activation, as well as TNF and IFNγ secretion of T cells could provide evidence for diagnosis of PCD-driven SAIDs, and pharmacologic blockade of TNF and IFNγ pathways would often be effective therapy for diagnosed patients.
