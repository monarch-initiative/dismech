# Monoallelic and biallelic KDM5A variants identified in patients with autism spectrum disorder.
**Authors:** El Hayek L, Gogate A, Chen WC, Kaur K, Zaki MS, De Wachter M, Van Schil K, Dublin-Ryan L, Zamani M, Bartos MN, Hiatt SM, Courdier C, Michaud V, Kenny J, Day M, Pang L, Nasab ME, Madani Manshadi SA, Eslahi A, Rasoul MA, Sanchez-Mendoza EH, DeLuca C, Marafi D, Stevens SJC, Ivanovski I, Frey T, Steindl K, Rauch A, O'Connor K, Velinov M, Shen X, Janssen EJM, Sedighzadeh S, Kordi-Tamandani DM, Khajeh A, Elshafie RM, Bastaki L, Misra VK, Firoozfar Z, Goldenberg PC, Toosi MB, Mojarrad M, Kavanagh K, Koboldt DC, Margot H, Hurst ACE, Weber A, Bergmann C, Houlden H, Maroofian R, Weis D, Ceulemans B, Chahrour MH

## Content

Chromatin regulation is critical for neurodevelopment, and its disruption has emerged as a key pathogenic mechanism in neurodevelopmental disease, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), a condition known for genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity. We previously identified an ASD gene, KDM5A, encoding a histone H3 lysine 4 demethylase, and reported de novo and inherited variants in nine individuals with severe ASD and other neurodevelopmental phenotypes. Here, we expand the genetic and phenotypic spectrum of KDM5A-related neurodevelopmental disorders and investigate the functional impact of identified variants. Through international collaborations, we assembled a cohort of 24 additional individuals from 21 families with rare, protein-altering KDM5A variants. All individuals presented with severe speech impairment and intellectual disability, often alongside ASD and other neurodevelopmental features. The variants include missense, nonsense, frameshift, and splice site, distributed across nearly all functional domains of the protein. Structural modeling revealed localized conformational disruptions, particularly at conserved residues in enzymatic or chromatin-interacting domains. For a subset of variants, we demonstrated reduced KDM5A protein levels in cell lines derived from affected individuals. Transcriptomic profiling revealed variant-specific gene expression changes, most pronounced in variants affecting the PLU1 chromatin-binding motif and the Jumonji C domain of the enzymatic core. American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics-guided reclassification supported pathogenicity for the majority of variants, including multiple upgrades from uncertain significance to pathogenic or likely pathogenic. Together, these findings implicate diverse KDM5A alleles in a rare but recurrent form of ASD and establish KDM5A as a key regulator of neurodevelopment and chromatin-mediated ASD pathogenesis.

Epigenetic chromatin remodeling is essential for regulating gene expression, guiding cellular differentiation, and maintaining cellular identity.1 During development, undifferentiated cells exhibit a broadly permissive chromatin landscape2,3 that is progressively remodeled as cells differentiate and acquire specialized identities.4 Chromatin regulators, such as histone demethylases, help fine-tune gene expression programs by modulating histone marks at key developmental loci and time points. In the brain, chromatin regulation plays critical roles in multiple aspects of neurodevelopment,5 including neuronal lineage specification, synaptic development, and the establishment of neuronal circuits.1,6 Disruption of chromatin regulatory mechanisms has emerged as a key pathogenic driver in neurodevelopmental disorders, particularly autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Mutations in several genes encoding chromatin regulators have been causally linked to ASD and related neurodevelopmental conditions (e.g., ARID1B, CHD8, MECP2, and SETD5).7,8,9,10,11,12 Among these, genes encoding histone lysine demethylases, including KDM5B and KDM5C, are recurrently disrupted in a host of neurodevelopmental disorders, including ASD and intellectual disability (MIM: 618109 and 300534),9,13,14,15,16,17,18,19 highlighting the importance of histone methylation dynamics in brain development.

ASD is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by deficits in communication, diminished social skills, and stereotyped behaviors. The condition is often accompanied by comorbidities, including intellectual disability, developmental delay, motor impairments, and seizures.20,21 ASD affects 1 in 31 children (∼3.2%) and is ∼3.4 times more common in males.22 In addition to phenotypic heterogeneity, ASD is marked by extreme genetic heterogeneity. While hundreds of genes have been implicated in ASD risk,23,24,25 each typically accounts for only a small fraction of cases (∼0.08%–0.21%).24,26 This genetic architecture suggests that ASD comprises a collection of individually rare disorders, each potentially disrupting distinct neurodevelopmental pathways.

We previously identified KDM5A, which encodes a chromatin regulator in the KDM5 family of histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4) demethylases,5,27 as an ASD gene28 (MIM: 620820). KDM5A represses transcription by demethylating the H3K4me3 mark typically found at promoters of actively transcribed genes.29 The gene is highly intolerant to loss-of-function variation (pLI = 1.00).30 In 2020, we reported pathogenic KDM5A variants in nine individuals with severe ASD.28 The identified variants were private, either de novo or inherited, and distributed across multiple functional domains of the protein, with no single mutational hotspot, suggesting that multiple domains are functionally important. Most of the individuals exhibited complete lack of speech, along with intellectual disability and other neurodevelopmental features.28 We further showed in a constitutive Kdm5a knockout mouse model (Kdm5a−/−) that loss of KDM5A disrupts vocalization, sociability, learning and memory, and neuronal morphogenesis.28 More recently, we demonstrated that KDM5A plays a critical role in establishing cell identity in the hippocampus.31

Here, we report 24 additional individuals with ASD harboring KDM5A variants, expanding the allelic and phenotypic spectrum associated with this gene. Through integrative analyses, we investigate the functional impact of these variants and their disruption of downstream biological pathways, advancing our understanding of this rare genetic subtype of ASD.

LCLs were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% v/v penicillin-streptomycin solution, and 1% v/v Fungizone. For immunoblotting, cells were lysed in SDS lysis buffer, and western blot analysis was performed as described below.

To detect endogenous proteins, cell pellets from LCLs were collected, washed with PBS, and frozen at −80°C. Pellets were lysed in SDS lysis buffer (100 mM Tris pH 6.8, 4% SDS, 20% glycerol, 10 mM NEM, 1 mM PMSF), supplemented with 2 mM OPT, and cOmplete Mini EDTA-free protease inhibitor and phosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor tablets (Roche). Samples were heated at 70°C for 10 min or boiled for 5 min, then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the DC Protein Assay (Bio-Rad). A total of 25 μg of protein, mixed with β-mercaptoethanol and bromophenol blue, per sample per lane, was loaded onto 6% or 8% polyacrylamide gels. Gels were run and proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore) for 2 h on ice. Membranes were blocked in 5% milk in 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature then incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Membranes were washed in 5% TBS-T followed by a 1-h incubation with secondary antibody (donkey anti-rabbit, Jackson ImmunoResearch, 711-035-152, 1:10,000 for KDM5A, 1:2,500 for vinculin, and 1:20,000 for actin) at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primary antibodies used were anti-KDM5A (Abcam, ab70892, 1:1,000), anti-vinculin (Cell Signaling, 4650S, 1:500), and anti-actin (Cell Signaling, 4970S, 1:10,000). Control (Ctrl) samples were obtained from individuals without neurodevelopmental diagnoses who were age and sex matched to KD-19-3 and KD-20-3.

RNA was extracted from LCL pellets derived from affected individuals, parents, and siblings, when available, using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and purified with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Genomic DNA was removed using the RNase-free DNase kit (Qiagen). RNA quality was assessed using the Agilent TapeStation 4200, and all samples had a RIN score of ≥8. RNA concentrations were measured using a Qubit fluorometer, and 1 μg of DNase-treated total RNA was used for library preparation with the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). Poly(A) RNA was purified and fragmented, and strand-specific libraries were amplified and purified using AmpureXP beads. Samples were sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500 using V2.5 reagents.

Raw sequencing data were de-multiplexed and converted to FASTQ files using bcl2fastq (v2.17, Illumina). FASTQ files were quality checked using FastQC (v0.11.2). Quality-control metrics are presented in Figure S2. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using STAR (v2.5.3a).33 Read counts were then generated using featureCounts (Subread v1.4.6).34 Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2.35 Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in affected individuals compared to unaffected individuals using two approaches: (1) by comparing all affected individuals across families, and (2) by analyzing differences within each family. Sex was included as a covariate in the model used for analyses across all families (design formula: ∼sex + affected). In contrast, for analyses performed within individual families, small sample sizes (n = 3–5) precluded reliable inclusion of sex as a covariate. Therefore, these analyses used affected status alone (design formula: ∼affected). DEGs located on the X or Y chromosome are annotated in Table S5 and excluded from the volcano plots. Genes with a |log2 fold change in expression| ≥ 0.3 and an adjusted p ≤ 0.05 were considered significant DEGs. The list of known ASD genes was obtained from the Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI) Gene database36 (2026 Q1 release), and DEGs were overlapped with this list. The functional annotation tool DAVID37 was used to find biological process terms enriched in the DEG sets. Redundant Gene Ontology terms were merged using REVIGO.38 The SimRel semantic similarity measure was used to calculate dispensability. Terms with dispensability < 0.15 were plotted.

To investigate the impact of identified mutations on protein structure, the wild type (WT) protein sequence of KDM5A (UniProt: P29375) was downloaded from the UniProt database.39 For each missense mutation identified in affected individuals, the corresponding FASTA file was manually edited at the relevant amino acid residue to reflect the observed mutation. Both the WT and mutant protein sequences were then subjected to three-dimensional structure prediction using AlphaFold,40 employing default parameters. The resulting relaxed model PDB files were visualized using ChimeraX.41 The specific mutated amino acid residues were highlighted in green within the visualized protein structures. Per-residue conservation scores were obtained by submitting the WT KDM5A FASTA file to the ConSurf42 web server using default parameters. Disordered regions were annotated based on UniProt.39

Genetic variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG)/Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) guidelines43 using the bioinformatics tool InterVar.44 InterVar provided an initial automated interpretation of each variant using inputs that included the gene name and specific mutation. The ACMG/AMP guidelines categorize variants into pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) and benign/likely benign (B/LB) based on 28 distinct criteria. For P/LP classification, there are 16 criteria (very strong, PVS1; strong, PS1–4; moderate, PM1–6; supporting: PP1–5). For B/LB classification, there are 12 criteria (stand-alone, BA1; strong, BS1–4; supporting, BP1–7). InterVar automatically generated predictions for 18 of these criteria based on existing annotation datasets: PVS1, PS1, PS4, PM1, PM2, PM4, PM5, PP2, PP3, PP5, BA1, BS1, BS2, BP1, BP3, BP4, BP6, and BP7. The remaining criteria required manual adjustment and user input, specifically (1) PS2 and PM6, adjusted based on whether the variant was de novo; (2) PM3 and BP2, adjusted based on whether the variant was inherited in compound heterozygous/trans mode with a pathogenic variant; (3) PS3 and BS3, assessed using data from functional studies, including our results from western blot analysis and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq); (4) PP1 and BS4, assessed based on variant segregation with disease in additional affected family members when available; and (5) PP4 and BP5, not subject to any manual adjustments. The criteria application and variant classification presented in this paper do not necessarily reflect the criteria and classification of the clinical testing laboratories involved in this study.

Following our initial study in 2020 reporting KDM5A as an ASD gene28 (MIM: 620820), we established additional collaborations with clinicians and researchers, leading to the identification of additional KDM5A variants in patients with neurodevelopmental phenotypes. In addition to the nine patients from seven families described in our initial report (KD-1 through KD-7),28 we identified 24 additional patients (14 males, 10 females) from 21 families (nine consanguineous) harboring KDM5A variants (KD-8 through KD-29) (Figure 1A; Table S1). The patients primarily presented with ASD and speech abnormalities, ranging from complete lack of speech (16/23 patients assessed) to very limited speech of a few words or other speech issues (7/23 patients assessed). Additional phenotypes included autistic features, developmental delay, intellectual disability, muscle hypotonia, seizures, and cardiac abnormalities (Figure 1B; Tables 1 and S2). Regardless of the variant type or the impacted KDM5A protein domain, the patients consistently exhibited severe developmental phenotypes and, in most cases, required daily care. KDM5A variants were identified through whole exome sequencing, whole genome sequencing, panel testing, or chromosomal microarray analysis. None of the patients harbored pathogenic variants in other known disease genes, and the identified KDM5A variants segregated with the clinical phenotypes in the families. In total, we identified de novo variants in seven individuals, inherited variants in 13 individuals, and variants in four individuals for whom the inheritance pattern could not be confirmed (Figure 1C; Tables 1 and S3). There were 12 individuals with biallelic variants and 12 individuals with monoallelic variants (Figure 1C; Tables 1 and S3). The majority of the variants were either homozygous inherited or heterozygous de novo, consistent with our earlier findings,28 although there were notable exceptions. Three patients from three different families (KD-9, KD-13, and KD-14) had biallelic compound heterozygous KDM5A variants, representing the only cases with inherited variants from non-consanguineous families. In KD-19, a heterozygous KDM5A variant was inherited from the mother, who had formal diagnoses of bipolar disorder, depression, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), marking a case of dominant inheritance. All homozygous inherited KDM5A variants occurred in consanguineous families: KD-15, KD-16, KD-17, KD-24, KD-25, KD-28, and KD-29. Interestingly, in KD-20 and KD-27, the KDM5A variants were heterozygous de novo (assumed de novo in KD-20), representing two cases of non-homozygous variants in consanguineous families (Figure 1; Table 1).

We identified a spectrum of unique KDM5A variants distributed across most of the protein’s domains, including Jumonji C (JmjC) (one), JmjN (one), plant homeodomain (PHD) 1 (one), PHD3 (one), putative DNA/chromatin-binding motif (PLU1) (seven), and inter-domain regions (13) (Figures 2A and 2B; Table S3). The variants included 13 missense mutations (KD-8, KD-9, KD-11, KD-13, KD-16, KD-18, KD-20, KD-21, KD-23, KD-25, and KD-29), seven nonsense mutations (KD-10, KD-14, KD-15, KD-17, KD-22, KD-24, and KD-26), two frameshift mutations (KD-19 and KD-27), and two splice site mutations (KD-14 and KD-28) (Figures 2A and 2B; Tables 1 and S3). Consistent with our earlier report,28 these variants span nearly every domain of the KDM5A protein, with many located in inter-domain regions (Figures 2A and 2B). For a subset of families, we obtained blood samples and generated LCLs. Western blot analyses demonstrated reduced KDM5A protein levels in affected individuals from KD-8, KD-19, KD-20, KD-21, and KD-26 (Figure 2C). Affected individuals from KD-8, KD-20, and KD-21 harbor de novo missense variants (assumed de novo in KD-20), suggesting that one allele produces functional protein while the mutant allele appears to result in a degraded product. In KD-19, the heterozygous frameshift mutation appears to lead to degradation of the product from the mutant allele, resulting in decreased protein levels in both the affected individual (KD-19-3) and his affected mother (KD-19-2), who harbors the same variant. Similarly, in KD-26, the heterozygous nonsense mutation appears to lead to degradation of the product from the mutant allele, resulting in decreased protein levels in the affected individual. In KD-14, we performed an mRNA assay on a blood sample from the affected individual to assess the functional impact of the intronic maternal allele (c.2427-17 A>G [p.Arg809Serfs∗10]). We found that the intronic variant activates a cryptic splice site, introducing an additional 16 nucleotides into exon 18 of KDM5A and creating a frameshift with a premature termination codon (Figure 2D).

We visualized the domain organization of KDM5A, highlighting the key domains48,49,53,54: JmjN (residues 18–59), ARID (residues 85–170), PHD1 (residues 295–343), JmjC (residues 470–586), Zn (residues 676–728), PLU1 (residues 741–1,072), PHD2 (residues 1,163–1,215), and PHD3 (residues 1,608–1,659) (Figure 3A). Using AlphaFold, we modeled the three-dimensional structures of WT KDM5A (Figures 3A and 3B) and the missense variants (Figure 3C). Nonsense, frameshift, multi-exon deletion, and splice site variants were not modeled. Visual inspection of these predicted structures revealed discernible conformational differences between the WT and mutant proteins. While the overall global fold of KDM5A was generally preserved in some variants, we consistently observed localized structural alterations, particularly in regions proximal to the mutation sites. These changes included shifts in backbone conformation, altered side-chain packing, and increased flexibility within specific loops or secondary structural elements. Notably, the most pronounced structural deviations from the WT frequently correlated with mutations in critical functional domains, such as the PLU1 domain (KD-8), the PHD3 domain (KD-16), and key inter-domain linker regions (KD-11) (Figure 3C). To quantify the evolutionary significance of the mutated residues, we performed conservation analysis of the WT KDM5A structure using ConSurf42 (Figure S3; Table S4) and colored the amino acid residues by conservation scores (1–9, with 9 being the most conserved). We found that the KDM5A variants were distributed across a range of evolutionary contexts (Figures 3B and S3). Among the missense variants, KD-5 (JmjC) had the highest conservation score of 9, suggesting that the position is under strong negative selection and likely essential for KDM5A function (Figures 3C and S3; Table S4). A second group of missense variants with above-average conservation scores (5–8) included KD-1, KD-3, KD-8, KD-13 (P), KD-16, KD-18, KD-20, KD-23, KD-25, and KD-29 (Figures 3C and S3; Table S4). The remaining missense variants had lower conservation scores (1–4), all located in inter-domain regions. ConSurf analysis provided further insights into the biochemical nature of the mutated residues. For each structure in Figure 3C, we included a 10-residue sequence containing the mutated residue, annotated with conservation score, residue accessibility (buried “b” or exposed “e”), and predicted role (functional “f” or structural “s”). We identified two highly conserved, exposed, functional residues (KD-13 [P] and KD-23) and one highly conserved, buried, structural residue (KD-5) (Figure 3C). Additionally, four variants were in intrinsically disordered regions (KD-3, KD-11, KD-16, and KD-21) (Table S4). Most missense variants were located in exposed residues, except for KD-5 and KD-16, which were buried (Table S4). Given that KDM5A is a histone lysine demethylase that represses target gene expression, we profiled the transcriptomes of LCLs from affected individuals and their unaffected family members to better assess the functional impact of KDM5A variants. We performed RNA-seq on LCLs from KD-128 (de novo missense variant in an inter-domain, N terminus), KD-528 (homozygous missense variant in JmjC), KD-628 (de novo nonsense variant in an inter-domain, C terminus), KD-8 (de novo missense variant in PLU1), and KD-21 (de novo missense variant in an inter-domain, C terminus). Samples from other families were either unavailable or too limited in number to support analysis. We first analyzed differential gene expression using DESeq2,35 comparing all affected individuals with ASD to all the unaffected family member controls. This analysis identified only seven DEGs (|log2 fold change| ≥ 0.3, adjusted p ≤ 0.05): ADAM23, AFF3, EMILIN1, EPB41L3, FCER1G, IGFBP5, and L1TD1, all significantly upregulated in the affected group (Table S5). This limited number of DEGs in the combined analysis was expected, given the heterogeneity across unrelated individuals affected with ASD, who harbor diverse genetic variants impacting different domains of KDM5A. These domain-specific disruptions likely impact distinct downstream biological pathways and may lack strong converging transcriptomic signatures. To investigate potential variant-specific effects on gene expression, we performed differential gene expression analysis within individual families (Table S5). The number of identified DEGs varied considerably across families. KD-1 had six DEGs, KD-5 had 597, KD-6 had 121, and KD-21 had 15 DEGs. KD-8 exhibited the largest number of DEGs, with 1,955 upregulated and 2,178 downregulated genes (Figure 4A). DEGs identified in KD-5 and KD-8 were significantly enriched for ASD-associated genes from the SFARI Gene database (p = 0.05 and 0.0015, respectively) (Table S5). Four genes were recurrently identified across DEG sets: CAPG, EEF1A2, SLC16A3, and ZBTB38. Mutations in EEF1A2, which encodes eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1 alpha-2, have been reported in developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (MIM: 616409) and intellectual disability (MIM: 616393).55 Gene Ontology analysis was performed for the DEGs in KD-5 and KD-8, the families with the highest number of DEGs, and revealed enrichment in several key biological processes, including signal transduction, cell-cell adhesion, translation, chromatin organization, neuron differentiation, neuron development, and neurogenesis (Figure 4B; Table S6). Prior to reclassification, 23 affected individuals had variants that were previously categorized as either variants of uncertain significance (VUSs) or unclassified (presumed VUSs) (Table 2). One individual harbored a variant initially classified as likely pathogenic (KD-27). Through the integration of these multiple lines of evidence, we reclassified variants in 16 individuals. This included reclassification from VUS to pathogenic in 11 individuals (KD-8, KD-10, KD-14, KD-15, KD-17, KD-19, KD-21, KD-22, KD-24, KD-26, and KD-28), from VUS to likely pathogenic in two individuals (KD-11 and KD-20), from likely pathogenic to pathogenic in one individual (KD-27), and from VUS to likely benign for one of the two compound heterozygous variants in KD-9. For the remaining six affected individuals (KD-13, KD-16, KD-18, KD-23, KD-25, and KD-29), the initial classifications remained unchanged (Table 2).

The KDM5 family of proteins, KDM5A, KDM5B, KDM5C, and KDM5D, are multi-domain epigenetic regulators that function as transcriptional repressors or activators in a context-dependent manner.56 Dysregulation of these proteins contributes to a range of neurodevelopmental disorders.9,13,14,15,16,17,18,19 In this study, through international clinical collaborations, we expanded the genetic landscape of KDM5A-related neurodevelopmental disorders by identifying unique variants in a cohort of 24 individuals with ASD from 21 families. This work builds upon our previous publication, which described nine individuals with ASD,28 significantly increasing the number of known cases to a total of 33. All the identified variants are rare, with very low minor allele frequencies in population databases. Importantly, all 31 unique variants identified to date are private, with no recurrent alleles observed across unrelated individuals. The newly identified cohort included 14 males and 10 females. Of the 21 families, nine were consanguineous. Additionally, 12 affected individuals harbored biallelic inherited variants, while seven harbored de novo variants. Clinical and molecular characterization, combined with in silico predictions and functional validation, confirmed that these variants are deleterious, disrupting KDM5A protein structure and function. Our findings demonstrate that both monoallelic and biallelic variants in KDM5A are associated with neurodevelopmental phenotypes, most notably severe speech impairment, ASD, developmental delay, and intellectual disability. These results are consistent with the intolerance of KDM5A to genetic variation, reflected by its high pLI score (1.00) and elevated missense Z score (3.61). In addition to our cohort, three independent studies have reported KDM5A variants in individuals with ASD and developmental delay.57,58,59

In this study, we identified distinct pathogenic variants in KDM5A that affect different domains and regions of the protein, with transcriptomic data hinting at specific disrupted downstream mechanisms for each allele. ADAM23, AFF3, EMILIN1, EPB41L3, FCER1G, IGFBP5, and L1TD1 were all significantly upregulated DEGs in the cohort of individuals affected with ASD. ADAM23 promotes human neural progenitor differentiation,60
AFF3 is associated with intellectual disability and encodes a protein integral to cortical neuronal migration in mice,61 and EMILIN1 has been linked to motor neuropathy.62 In addition, CAPG, EEF1A2, SLC16A3, and ZBTB38 emerged as recurrent DEGs across individuals affected with ASD, although their direction of regulation varied. These findings highlight that ASD is characterized not only by phenotypic and genetic heterogeneity but also by allelic heterogeneity among affected individuals harboring mutations in the same gene. This underscores the complexity of the disorder and emphasizes the need to investigate it at molecular resolution.

While the identified variants were distributed across the entire protein, including multiple domains and inter-domain regions, the PLU1 domain was most frequently affected, with seven unique variants identified in eight individuals. These variants included one frameshift, two nonsense, three missense, and one splicing variant. Despite the molecular heterogeneity, affected individuals with PLU1 variants showed strikingly similar clinical features. All eight individuals presented with profound speech abnormalities, ranging from absent or markedly delayed speech to vocabulary restricted to only a few words. Additional shared phenotypes included developmental delay (five individuals), feeding difficulties (five individuals), intellectual disability (four individuals), and sleep disturbances (four individuals). The PLU1 domain, a cysteine-rich region, is conserved across the KDM5 family of proteins.48 Although its specific role in KDM5A is not fully defined, work on KDM5B has shown that PLU1 mediates interactions with the transcription factors PAX9, FOXC2, and FOXG1,63 supporting its importance in transcriptional regulation. This is further supported by our findings that the KD-8 variant, located in PLU1, caused the most pronounced disruption to the transcriptome, with the largest number of DEGs. Interestingly, no variants were identified in the Zn domain in either this cohort or our previously reported cases.28 Although this domain is also highly conserved, its specific function in KDM5A remains unknown. Moreover, no pathogenic variants, likely pathogenic variants, or VUSs in gnomAD are located within this region, suggesting possible selective constraint. A key insight from our findings is that the pathogenicity of KDM5A loss of function cannot be explained solely by its enzymatic activity. While variants in the JmjC domain can impair its demethylase function, variants in other domains may disrupt non-enzymatic functions, such as chromatin positioning or interactions with regulatory complexes, leading to equally severe phenotypes. Thus, the pathogenic mechanism likely reflects a combination of catalytic and non-catalytic disruptions.

In conclusion, both monoallelic and biallelic KDM5A variants cause a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by ASD, severe speech abnormalities, developmental delay, and intellectual disability. Functional studies confirm that these KDM5A variants reduce protein levels, disrupt protein structure, and significantly alter the transcriptome. Together, our findings establish KDM5A as a critical regulator of neurodevelopment and strengthen its role in ASD pathogenesis.
