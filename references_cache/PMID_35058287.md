# Diagnosis of a floppy neonate with misleading clues: unraveled as congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy.
**Authors:** Ravikumar S, Devi U, Balakrishnan U, Chandrasekaran A
**Journal:** BMJ Case Rep (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1136/bcr-2021-247555](https://doi.org/10.1136/bcr-2021-247555)

## Content

Contactin-associated protein 1 (CNTNAP1)-related congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy (CHN) is a rare type of peripheral neuropathy and has a clinically heterogeneous presentation. We report a neonate with an atypical presentation in the form of global hypotonia, facial diparesis and partial response to neostigmine challenge test. There was no clinical improvement on initiation of anticholinesterase drug for suspected congenital myasthenia and hence stopped. Detection of a pathogenic variant in CNTNAP1 gene by clinical exome sequencing and subsequent reverse phenotyping confirmed CHN as the aetiology for this floppy neonate, which is known to have high mortality. The baby was given supportive care and she succumbed secondary to complications of prolonged ventilation.

Keywords:neuromuscular disease, neonatal and paediatric intensive care, paediatrics (drugs and medicines)

Hypotonia in a neonate can be either central (88%–90%) or peripheral (9%–10%).1Sensorium is affected in central hypotonia, whereas in peripheral hypotonia, sensorium is often intact and the lesion can be at any level starting from the anterior horn cell. In the neonatal period, hypotonia observed in diseases involving anterior horn cell, neuromuscular junction (NMJ), muscle and peripheral nerves is clinically similar. Hence, the demarcation of the anatomical level in a lower motor neuron or peripheral hypotonia is very challenging in neonates. Before the advent of clinical exome sequencing (CES), differentiating between these causes was possible only by invasive diagnostic tests. In congenital myasthenia, additional facial and ocular involvement is present and screening tests can guide us in the management while awaiting final diagnosis. Here we report a neonate with atypical clinical features misleading us towards different management while awaiting CES report.

A preterm girl baby was born at 33 weeks of gestation with a birth weight of 1660 g to a 22-year-old mother by normal delivery out of third-degree consanguineous marriage. An antenatal scan at 32 weeks of gestation revealed polyhydramnios and a small-calibre stomach. There was no history of reduced fetal movements or fetal distress. The baby was floppy at birth with no breathing efforts. Hence, positive pressure ventilation was initiated followed by intubation. Apgar score was 3/10 and 6/10 at 1 and 5 min of life, respectively. On reassessment after 2 hours, the baby had reduced quantity of movements, axial weakness, facial diparesis, myopathic facies and no spontaneous eye movements. The baby was able to lift legs transiently against gravity when painful stimuli were given. She had an extension deformity of the right index finger probably due to contracture of joints. Deep tendon reflexes were not elicitable. There were no abnormal movements or seizures. Dolls eye movements were present. The baby had global hypotonia. She did not have any dysmorphism or tongue fasciculation. Head circumference was normal. Parents had no evidence of myotonia or myasthenia. Handgrip test was normal in them. The baby reached full tube feeds by day 5. The respiratory efforts, activity and response to pain did not improve throughout the hospital stay and ventilator requirements progressively increased due to ventilator-associated pneumonia.

MRI brain with spectroscopy revealed subdural haemorrhage and enlarged extra-axial spaces (figure 1). Electrolytes, workup for sepsis, metabolic workup and CSF analysis were normal. Serum acetylcholine receptor antibody was reported to be negative. Nerve conduction study (NCS) was deferred since the baby was unstable. Electromyography (EMG) was not done due to the unavailability of appropriate disposal concentric needle for preterm (30G). The baby showed partial and transient response to neostigmine challenge test (0.02 mg/kg s/c) in the form of spontaneous eye-opening after approximately 30 min of administration and lasting for around 3 hours but did not have an improvement in body movements or respiratory efforts (figure 2).

Defects causing lower motor hypotonia were considered in our baby. Given the floppy neonate with facial diparesis, defect at the level of the NMJ was considered more likely than other causes of lower motor hypotonia like peripheral neuropathies, congenital myopathies and spinal muscular atrophy. Congenital myasthenia was considered as working diagnosis because of the early facial and bulbar involvement and partial response to the anticholinesterase (AChE) drug.

While awaiting CES, the baby was empirically started on oral pyridostigmine at a dose of 2 mg/kg/day in two divided doses but was stopped after 1 week since there was no positive response in terms of respiratory efforts or activity. The baby was given intravenous fluids on day 1 of life. Expressed breast milk was started from day 2 through the nasogastric tube. The feeds were gradually increased, and parenteral fluids were stopped by day 5 of life. The infant failed two trials of extubation to non-invasive support. The baby was changed to high-frequency ventilation because of the high mean airway pressure requirement on conventional ventilation. Antibiotics were hiked to piperacillin-tazobactam and then to meropenem and amikacin.

CES (Illumina platform; 6440 gene panel; Interpretation-American college of medical genetics recommendations) revealed a pathogenic homozygous 5’ splice site variation c.715+2T>G in intron 5 of contactin-associated protein 1 (CNTNAP1gene (mRNA accession number: ENST00000264638.4) causing an autosomal recessive hereditary neuropathy called congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy (CHN). The baby’s condition progressively worsened and she expired at 35 days of life.

Lower motor neuron hypotonia is less frequent among neonates. It occurs due to lesions at various levels of the nervous system, such as the NMJ involvement in 8% of cases, isolated nerve involvement in 8% of cases, anterior horn cell involvement in about 24% of cases and muscle involvement in 60% of cases.1

Neonates with defects in NMJ have poor respiratory efforts and often require resuscitation at birth. These infants have frequent episodes of apnoea, feeding difficulties and generalised weakness. Facial weakness is prominent, but eye movements are often normal. Ptosis may be present but is not remarkable. Infants who are treated with anti-cholinesterase agents show drastic improvement, and spontaneous remission often in the first few months of life. For congenital myasthenic syndromes refractory to AChE drug as in postsynaptic slow channel defect, drugs such as di-aminopyridine and albuterol are found to be effective.2In our baby, respiratory efforts and spontaneous eye-opening were absent and there was progressive worsening with no response to medication.

Early infantile-onset hereditary demyelinating neuropathies are commonly Dejerine–Sottas disease (DJS) and CHN. Although DJS represents hereditary demyelinating neuropathy presenting before 2 years of age, CHN reflects a defect in myelin synthesis rather than degeneration of pre-existing myelin manifesting at birth.3Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies (HSAN) types 2, 3, 4 and 5 can also present at birth. However, only the features of type 3 also known as familial dysautonomia or Riley–Day syndrome are well studied when compared with other types. It occurs exclusively in the Ashkenazi Jewish population and is characterised by feeding difficulty, jerky limb movements, hypotonia, respiratory dysfunction, blood pressure lability, retrocollis and opisthotonos.4

CHN is characterised by non-progressive weakness, areflexia, hypotonia and respiratory failure.5Phrenic nerve and vocal cord paralyses also have been rarely reported.6CHN can be of either neonatal or infantile onset. Neonatal variety has a poor prognosis and high mortality. The distal involvement characteristic of peripheral nerve disease is difficult to demonstrate in the neonatal period as in our case. Treatment for CHN is symptomatic and supportive. It is critical to prevent contractures, scoliosis and recurrent pulmonary infection and to optimise motor function.

Nerve biopsy reveals evidence of demyelination like onion bulb formation in DJS while complete absence of peripheral myelin in CHN. Nerve biopsy in type 3 HSAN reveals a reduction in unmyelinated fibres with preservation of myelinated fibres. A nerve biopsy was not planned for our baby.3In the era of genomic diagnostics, invasive tests are not preferred and are not routinely done. Single fibre EMG has a sensitivity of >95% in detecting a neuromuscular transmission defect. Decremental response in repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS) test is seen in disorders of NMJ.7 8Inherited neuropathies are characterised by extremely slow nerve conduction velocities on NCS.4EMG and RNS were not done in our baby.

CES in our baby was suggestive ofCNTNAP1-related CHN. CES testing was outsourced and a major limitation was the long turn-around time (TAT) of around a month. Definitive diagnosis was pending and prognostication could not be done awaiting CES report. Though expedited testing can yield results within 10 days, the cost involved is high. Unlike other forms of CHN,CNTNAP1-related CHN affects myelination of both the central and peripheral nervous systems and presents with global hypotonia, facial diplegia, and multiple cranial nerve palsies (table 1).9Thus, it does not manifest with features of peripheral neuropathy alone. Facial diparesis misled us to consider congenital myasthenia syndromes in our child.

Types of congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy—clinical features and differences

Confirmed mutations inCNTNAP1have been reported in 17 cases from 9 unrelated families. Arthrogryposis multiplex congenita was reported in 13 out of 18 (72%) cases.9Six of 19 (32%) patients survived beyond infancy, with the oldest reported patient being 13 years old. In our case, an extension contracture of the right index finger alone was present. Seizures were reported in about half of the patients who survived. Our baby did not have any seizure activity. MRI in babies withCNTNAP1-related CHN showed prominent extra-axial spaces similar to our case and also ventricular enlargement and delayed myelination. All survivors required tracheostomy and mechanical ventilation. Our baby succumbed secondary to complications of prolonged ventilation.

To conclude, unless there is a good and consistent response, the sensitivity of the neostigmine challenge test is low and treatment cannot be initiated. CES can help to arrive at a diagnosis in atypical clinical presentation and reverse phenotyping as in our case. In the unusual setting of a floppy neonate with features of global hypotonia and facial diparesis,CNTNAP1-related CHN should be considered as a possibility.

CNTNAP1-related congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy does not have a classical clinical presentation as that of other peripheral neuropathy and is difficult to diagnose based on clinical features alone.

Partial response to neostigmine challenge test cannot guide in definitive diagnosis and management.

Clinical exome sequencing is helpful in heterogeneous, complex, or atypical clinical presentations.
