Summary
5-amino-4-imidazolecarboxamide-ribosiduria (AICA)-ribosiduria is an exceedingly rare
autosomal recessive condition resulting from the disruption of the bifunctional purine
biosynthesis protein PURH (ATIC), which catalyzes the last two steps of de novo purine
synthesis. It is characterized biochemically by the accumulation of AICA-riboside in urine.
AICA-ribosiduria had been reported in only one individual, 15 years ago. In this article, we
report three novel cases of AICA-ribosiduria from two independent families, with two novel
pathogenic variants in ATIC. We also provide a clinical update on the first patient. Based on
the phenotypic features shared by these four patients, we define AICA-ribosiduria as the
syndromic association of severe-to-profound global neurodevelopemental impairment,
severe visual impairment due to chorioretinal atrophy, ante-postnatal growth impairment,
and severe scoliosis. Dysmorphic features were observed in all four cases, especially
neonatal/infancy coarse facies with upturned nose. Early-onset epilepsy is frequent and can
be pharmacoresistant. Less frequently observed features are aortic coarctation, chronic
hepatic cytolysis, minor genital malformations and nephrocalcinosis. Alteration of the
transformylase activity of ATIC might result in a more severe impairment than the alteration
of the cyclohydrolase activity. Data from literature points towards a cytotoxic mechanism of
the accumulated AICA-riboside.

Take-home message
This first cohort of individuals affected by AICA-ribosiduria reveals a clinically homogenous
and severe disorder affecting neurodevelopment, vision, bone, and growth.

Introduction

Purines are a wide class of biomolecules possessing essential and ubiquitous functions for
cells and organisms. Notably, they are building units of RNA and DNA (adenine and guanine)
and are involved in energy transfer (ATP, GTP, cyclic AMP, NADH, and coenzyme A). The
normal catabolism of purines results in the excretion of uric acid in urine, and its anabolism
relies on two alternative pathways: salvage (recycling), or de novo purine biosynthesis
(DNPS).
1,2
In human, DNPS relies on a multienzyme complex called purinosome1, which catalyzes the
conversion of phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP) to inoside monophosphate (IMP)
through ten successive steps involving a total of six enzymes (Figure 1). The purinosome
probably protects the cell from the toxicity of the intermediates of DNPS, through substrate
channeling.
3  DNPS is a folate-dependent biological process and a target for drugs used for
cancer chemotherapy as well as for autoimmune disease therapy.1,2

A functional DNPS pathway appears decisive for organisms since the deficiency of its
enzymatic components leads to severe developmental defects. Adenylosuccinate lyase
(ADSL) catalyzes two non-sequential steps in DNPS
4 and its deficiency (ADSL deficiency,
OMIM #103050) results in a spectrum of neurological diseases ranging from fatal neonatal
encephalopathy to moderate or severe psychomotor retardation, depending on the amount
of residual enzymatic activity. Trifunctional Purine Biosynthetic Protein Adenosine-3 (GART)
and multifunctional protein ADE2 (PAICS) catalyze five other steps of DNPS, and their
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.




deficiency in a zebrafish experimental model leads to embryonic lethality, or microphthalmia
and pigmentation defects in adult survivors.5 Moreover, PAICS deficiency has been recently
reported for the first time in human, in two siblings affected by multiple congenital
malformations with fatal neonatal outcome.
6 Finally, bifunctional purine biosynthesis
protein PURH (ATIC) is a homodimer which catalyzes the penultimate and final steps of DNPS
through its phosphoribosylaminoimidazolecarboxamide formyltransferase (EC 2.1.2.3) and
IMP cyclohydrolase (EC 3.5.4.10) enzymatic activities respectively.7,8 ATIC deficiency results
in 5-amino-4-imidazolecarboxamide-ribosiduria(AICA) -ribosiduria (OMIM #608688). It is an
autosomal recessive disorder described so far in only one patient, presenting with a
devastating neurological picture, involving profound intellectual disability, epilepsy,
congenital blindness and dysmorphic features. AICA-ribosiduria is detectable biochemically
by the excessive accumulation of AICA-riboside along with SAICA-riboside and
succinyladenosine, in urine and cerebrospinal fluid.
9

In this article we report three novel cases of AICA-ribosiduria, along with a clinical update on
the first case, 15 years after the initial publication.
9 We report two novel pathogenic variants
in ATIC. We compare the clinical features of the four patients, to define the AICA-ribosiduria
syndrome.

 Clinical findings

Case 1
This male proband is the first and only child of healthy unrelated parents (Figure 2), with no
relevant familial history. The pregnancy was marked by late intrauterine growth retardation
(IUGR) of unknown etiology, confirmed at birth after 39 weeks of gestation (weight -2 SD;
height -3 SD; normal head circumference). Neonatal examination noted moderate
generalized hypotonia with distal limb hyperlaxity and bilateral hip dislocation.
Ultrasonography of the heart, abdomen, and brain showed no abnormality. A severe delay in
motor and cognitive skills was noted thereafter. The boy sat at age 2.5 and did not achieve
independent walking at age 7. He acquired a basic spoken language at age 5, consisting of a
dozen words at age 7. He was still learning new skills. Routine brain MRI and EEG are normal,
with no clinical seizures. Postnatal growth is impaired, with a current height and weight
around -3 SD, and preserved head circumference. Several dysmorphic features were noted

bone age. Repeated radiographs of the skeleton did not show any specific sign of bone

 Case 4: follow-up of the historical case
The first case of AICA-ribosiduria was reported in 2004 in a 4 years-old girl.9 She is the
second child of unrelated parents. She presented with a severe global developmental delay,
congenital blindness due to chorioretinal dystrophy, epilepsy, and dysmorphic features
(Table 1). She also had a minor cardiac malformation (ostium secundum atrial septal defect)
and a normal brain MRI. This patient is now 18 years-old and displays a profound
developmental delay. She can neither walk nor stand independently, in the context of major
hypotonia, but can sit. She did not acquire a spoken language or even isolated words.
Epilepsy is drug-resistant, with 1 to 2 seizures of less than one minute per day, under a triple
anti-epileptic therapy (vigabatrin, valproate and clonazepam). She takes folic acid for folate
deficiency. She suffers from severe irreducible scoliosis, and from major feeding issues
resulting in a body weight of less than 30 kg. However, her family denies enteral nutrition
and surgical intervention for scoliosis, and rarely attends to hospital. There is no clinical
feature suggestive of a hepatic disorder, but no specific investigation of the hepatic function
or morphology could be performed due to the denial by the family. She lives at home.

Discussion
 Clinical features of AICA-Ribosiduria
All four patients described in this article share the following phenotypic features: severe-to-
profound global neurodevelopemental impairment, severe visual impairment due to
chorioretinal atrophy, severe scoliosis, and antenatal and postnatal growth impairment. We
thus propose that these should be considered as the core diagnostic features of the
syndrome. However, due to the very limited number of patients, we cannot assert that they
are obligatory features. Epilepsy of infancy-to-childhood onset is present in three of the four
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.




patients, and thus should be considered as a major diagnostic feature of the syndrome,
although unspecific. Epilepsy varied in severity and did not seem to follow a specific clinical
pattern. Response to antiepileptic drugs also varied between patients: epilepsy was
controlled under valproate for one but was drug-resistant for the two others. Facial
dysmorphism was also present in all four cases, notably during the neonatal period and
infancy. Some dysmorphic features were similar in cases 1 to 3, especially the coarse facies
and upturned nose. However, these features were not noted for case 4. Facial dysmorphism
should thus be considered as a feature of the syndrome but lacks specificity.  Less frequently
observed features are aortic coarctation, chronic hepatic cytolysis, minor genital
malformations and nephrocalcinosis. These should be considered as potential features of
the syndrome. Aortic coarctation was observed in only one patient with confirmed ATIC
deficiency (case 2) but was also present in her deceased monozygotic twin. There was no
available DNA sample to confirm the diagnosis in this twin.  
 Genotype-phenotype correspondence

The disease presentation is similar in cases 2, 3, and 4, while it is less severe in case 1. Unlike
the other three patients, case 1 has a basic spoken language, has a better visual function,
can stand, and is still learning new skills. He is also the only patient who developed a chronic
hepatic disease. Since all 4 cases are compound heterozygotes for a null variant and a
missense variant, we suspect that the phenotypic differences could relate to the nature of
the missense variation. Cases 2-4 carry the same p.(Lys426Arg) missense variant, located in
the transformylase domain. Accordingly, enzymatic studies showed that p.(Lys426Arg)
completely lacks transformylase activity
9, but retains some cyclohydrolase activity. Studies
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.




thereafter conducted on cultured fibroblasts revealed a significantly reduced expression of
the ATIC protein, and a disruption of the purinosome assembly.13 By contrast, p.(Ala136Thr)
from case 1 is located in the cyclohydrolase domain, just next to Lysine 137, an amino acid
crucial for the cyclohydrolase activity of dimeric ATIC.14 Hypothetically, p.(Ala136Thr) could
thus decrease the cyclohydrolase activity of ATIC, rather than the transformylase activity,
possibly accounting for the phenotypic variations between case 1 and the other cases.

 Possible European founder effect of c.1277A>G and c.131delinsGCA
The pathogenic variant c.1277A>G p.(Lys426Arg) was found in families 2 and 3. These
families might be remotely related since they originate from the same region of France.
However, we cannot rule out a more distant founder effect since this variant is carried by
three heterozygous individuals from European ancestry in gnomAD (allele frequency in
European population: 1/38 000).  
Moreover, a study found by exome sequencing conducted on radiosensitive cell line derived
from patients suspected of having ataxia-telangiectasia and Lesch-Nyhan spectrum
disorders, a heterozygous complex frameshift mutation c.[130dup;131_132insA] in the ATIC
gene, of unknown significance.
15 This variation is also carried by 8 heterozygous Europeans
in gnomAD, while neither c.130dup nor c.131_132insA alone is reported. This indicates that
c.[130dup;131_132insA] is a unique rare variant present in the European population rather
than a random association of two unreported variants and should thus be written
c.131delinsGCA.  Interestingly, c.131delinsGCA overlaps with c.131delinsGGA found in case

 Pathological mechanism of AICA-ribosiduria
The pathological mechanism of AICA-ribosiduria has yet to be determined. It probably
involves a cytotoxic effect of an accumulated intermediate compound of the DNPS pathway
or may be related to the disruption of the purinosome13 resulting in a deficiency in purine
synthesis. A cytotoxic effect is more convincing since the salvage pathway is theoretically
capable of compensating the dysfunction of DNPS, and since AICA-riboside and AICAR/ZMP,
the major accumulating molecules in ATIC-deficiency, possess patent cytotoxic effects. Their
cytotoxicity has been subjected to intensive research in the last few years, notably for its
potential for cancer treatment. AICAR/ZMP is a recognized activator of the intracellular
energy sensor protein kinase AMPK16,17 and also possesses AMPK-independent anti-tumoral
properties. For instance, it is able to induce programmed necrosis in prostate cancer cells
through a cytotoxic mechanism involving reactive oxygen species.18 AICAR/ZMP is also
capable of inducing cellular apoptosis in human osteosarcoma cells19, or in gallbladder
cancer cells through a cytotoxic mechanism involving endoplasmic reticulum-stress
activation.20 AICA-riboside has also demonstrated pro-apoptotic abilities in a cellular model
of von Hippel-Lindau renal carcinoma and been able to reduce the size of renal tumors in a
mouse model.21 Its AMPK-independent anti-tumoral properties could result from the
activation of the tumor suppressor genes LATS1 and LATS2.22 AICA-riboside has been
subjected to phase I/II clinical trials for treating chronic lymphocytic leukemia23 and seems to
be relatively well tolerated in doses probably vastly inferior to those in AICA-ribosiduria.

Taken together, the recent literature shows a toxicity of AICA-riboside and AICAR/ZMP in
multiple experimental setups and tissues, strongly suggestive of a cytotoxic mechanism in
AICA-ribosiduria. Any mean to prevent this AICAR-mediated cytotoxicity might be beneficial
for patients with AICA-ribosiduria.
 Role of ATIC in hepatic metabolism
Because case 1 displays chronic and acute hepatic abnormalities of undetermined etiology,
we have searched the literature for a link between ATIC and hepatic metabolism. ATIC is
involved in glucose and lipids metabolism. In hepatocytes, ATIC inhibition increases insulin-
mediated endocytosis of the insulin receptor.24 AICA-riboside, after its intracellular
phosphorylation to AICAR/ZMP, inhibits gluconeogenesis in rats hepatocytes25, induces
hypoglycemia in mice26 and prevents olanzapine-induced hyperglycemia in mice.27 We have
observed multiple episodes of apparently idiopathic transitory hypoglycemia in case 1, but
only a transient neonatal hypoglycemia has been described in case 4. These events might
thus be related to an increased activation of the Insulin pathway in hepatocytes, in the
context of ATIC deficiency. AICA-riboside treatment in a mouse model modifies the liver
proteome28, and ATIC expression is upregulated in and is involved in human hepatocellular
carcinoma.29 Addition of AICA-riboside to isolated rat liver cells causes an inhibitory effect on
the synthesis of fatty acids and cholesterol through the stimulatory effect of AICAR/ZMP on
AMP-kinase.16 These findings suggest that the decrease in serum cholesterol observed in
cases 1, 2 and 4 might be mediated by the accumulated intracellular AICAR/ZMP.
Conversely, since fatty acids and cholesterol counterbalance the anti-proliferative properties

of AICA-riboside in glioblastoma cells,18 one could wonder if a lipid-rich diet could have
beneficial effects in patients suffering from AICA-ribosiduria.  
Role of ATIC in other human pathologies
In human pathology, ATIC has been involved in several other processes. Gene fusion of ATIC
to the proto-oncongene ALK and the phosphorylation of the wild-type ATIC protein have
both been involved in neoplastic processes in anaplastic large cell lymphoma and in
inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors.30–33 The c.347C>G (p.Thr116Ser) polymorphism
possibly modulates the pharmacological response to methotrexate in rheumatoid arthritis,
however controversially
34–38, in pediatric osteosarcoma38, in juvenile idiopathic arthritis39
and in psoriasis34. The allelic frequency of this polymorphism is high, between 0.25 and 0.3,
with approximately 10% homozygotes in the general population (gnomAD), and the clinical
utility of the c.347C>G polymorphism seems debatable at least for now. Other
polymorphisms of ATIC have been proposed to be associated with congenital heart diseases
.
40 Finally, ATIC inhibition and knockdown have been shown to induce chemo-radio-
sensitization through impaired double-strand break repair in cell culture.15

Conclusion
About 15 years after the publication of the first and only case of AICA-ribosiduria, we report
three additional individuals (one male and two females) affected by this seemingly
exceedingly rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by a deficiency in the bifunctional
enzyme ATIC. We also provide a clinical update on the first case. On this basis, we propose
that the following should be considered as the core features of this syndrome:  severe-to-
profound global neurodevelopemental impairment, severe visual impairment due to
chorioretinal atrophy, antenatal and postnatal growth impairment, and severe scoliosis.
Early-onset epilepsy and facial dysmorphic features should be considered as major features.
The biallelic disruption of ATIC leads to an abnormal accumulation of AICA-riboside in the
urine of all four patients, along with SAICA-riboside and succinyladenosine. The pathological
mechanism probably involves a cytotoxic effect, which would constitute an interesting target
for therapy.
