# Trametinib Decreased Intracerebral Hemorrhages and Endothelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition in KRAS(G12V)-Induced Brain Arteriovenous Malformations in Mice.
**Authors:** Myint O, Freeman BH, Jeong J, Park H, Wilfur SM, Huang S, Park JE, Körbelin J, Aronowski J, Park ES, Chen PR, Kim E
**Journal:** Stroke (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1161/STROKEAHA.125.052418](https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.125.052418)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs) are a major risk factor for intracerebral hemorrhages in young patients. Recent clinical studies have reported that the majority of human bAVMs harbor somatic KRAS (Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog) mutations. In our previous study, we confirmed the causal role of endothelial KRASG12V mutation in bAVM development in mice through hyperactivation of the MEK/ERK (mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase) pathway. However, the treatment efficacy of targeting MEK/ERK signaling in bAVMs, regarding clinical and biological outcomes, remains unclear.

METHODS: In this study, we used the US Food and Drug Administration-approved MEK inhibitor, trametinib, to treat mice with bAVMs induced by brain endothelial cell (EC)-specific KRASG12V overexpression (KRASG12V/bEC mice). We treated KRASG12V/bEC mice with either vehicle or trametinib and evaluated outcomes on mortality and the alteration of bAVMs, and the hemorrhages. We also determined the effect of trametinib on the endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition, which has recently been explored in human bAVMs and implicated in bAVM pathology, in bAVMs of KRASG12V/bEC mice and cultured mouse ECs transfected with KRASG12V (ECG12V).

RESULTS: We observed that trametinib significantly improved survivability, potentially through improved vessel integrity and reduction in the severity of bAVM ruptures. Our longitudinal observation of mice bAVMs using noninvasive magnetic resonance imaging revealed that trametinib decreased the total hemorrhagic volume at 8 weeks of treatment when administered at 1 mg/kg compared with the vehicle group, which was confirmed by histological analyses. In addition, trametinib altered endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition by reducing the expression levels of mesenchymal markers (N-Cad [N-cadherin], Slug, CD44 [cluster of differentiation 44]) and increasing an EC marker (VE-Cad [vascular endothelial cadherin]) in bAVMs of KRASG12V/bEC mice. Trametinib also decreased proliferation (Ki-67) and angiogenic (CD34 and pVEGFR2 [phosphor-vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2]) markers in KRASG12V/bEC mice's bAVMs. We also confirmed that trametinib normalized the endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition-associated marker expression and functions in ECG12V.

CONCLUSIONS: Our data demonstrate that trametinib improves bAVM pathology by reducing hemorrhagic risk and normalizing endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition. These findings suggest that trametinib is a promising agent to treat patients with bAVM.

Brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs) are nebulous dysplastic blood vessels within the brain that result in abnormal shunting of blood between the arterial and venous systems. With an estimated prevalence between 10 and 18 per 100,000 adults1, bAVMs represent the most common cause of primary intracerebral hemorrhages (ICHs) in young adults2. It has been estimated that nearly half of bAVM patients may experience ICHs, with a resultant mortality risk exceeding 20%3. Patients with unruptured bAVMs may still suffer from debilitating symptoms, including seizures, headaches, and focal neurological deficits4. Despite these devastating consequences, current treatment modalities are limited to surgical, radiotherapy, or endovascular interventions, with conservative management utilized in a subset of cases5. Currently, there are no pharmacologic agents proven to treat bAVMs. This is largely due to a poor understanding of the molecular mechanisms responsible for bAVM pathogenesis.

Recently, somatic activating mutations in the KRAS gene have been found to be present in the ECs of more than half of human bAVM tissues, with the KRASG12V and KRASG12D variants having the highest prevalence6,7. We previously confirmed that the KRAS mutation is sufficient to induce bAVMs by brain EC-specific overexpression of KRASG12V in mice (KRASG12V/bEC mice) using an adeno-associated viral (AAV) system, called AAV-BR18,9. These bAVMs in KRASG12V/bEC mice effectively model key features of the human disease, including the characteristic tangled niduses and spontaneous ICHs8. Therefore, this model serves as a valuable tool for preclinical studies exploring bAVM pathophysiology and testing therapeutic strategies.

KRAS is a member of the RAS oncogene family, encoding for a protein that cycles between the active GTP-bound state and the inactive GDP-bound state10. Active KRAS-GTP subsequently initiates a variety of intracellular signaling cascades through pathways that include MEK/ERK, leading to enhanced transcription of genes associated with cellular survival, migration, proliferation, and differentiation10,11. Evidence overwhelmingly implicates MEK/ERK signaling in the pathogenesis of fast-flow AVMs8,12. Accordingly, preclinical studies, including ours, have shown that MEK/ERK signaling mediated bAVM development induced by KRAS mutations8,12. The evidence suggests that targeting MEK/ERK signaling could be beneficial for bAVM treatment.

Numerous inhibitors targeting the MEK/ERK pathways are currently under clinical evaluation13. Among them, trametinib is a highly selective MEK1/2 inhibitor that is FDA-approved to treat mutated BRAF-associated melanoma. The persistent inhibition of MEK 1/2 and ERK1/2 has been shown in multiple cancer cell lines and xenograft tumor models14. In our previous study, we showed that trametinib successfully inhibits bAVM growth in KRASG12V/bEC mice8. Nguyen et al. also showed that trametinib improved the survival rate, accompanied by normalized vessels in organs, including the brain, of a postnatal mouse model with EC-specific KRASG12D activation15. It has been shown that trametinib could reduce the size of extracranial AVMs16,17. However, it is not clear if trametinib is beneficial in mature cerebral AVMs.

Endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) is a process by which mature endothelial cells (ECs) acquire mesenchymal cell characteristics. Recently, increased expression of mesenchymal markers, as well as EndMT-associated transcription factors were explored in human7,18, and mouse bAVMs19,20. In addition, KRAS mutations increased EndMT makers in cultured human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs)21. Therefore, as evidence builds in support of the major role of EndMT in bAVM pathogenesis, it is crucial to investigate the processes by which KRAS mutations drive EndMT and test if targeting the MEK/ERK pathway by trametinib can normalize the EndMT process in bAVM formation.

In this study, we evaluated the effects of targeting the MAPK/ERK pathway with trametinib on bAVMs in KRASG12V/bEC mice. We found that trametinib treatment significantly elongated the survival of KRASG12V/bEC mice, which is accompanied by reduced hemorrhagic risk and EndMT. The results demonstrate that trametinib could be considered as a promising treatment for bAVM patients.

All animal experiments were approved by the Center for Laboratory Animal Medicine and Care (CLAMC) of the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston and followed the ARRIVE guideline (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments). C57BL/6 mice were provided by the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Mice were kept in a room with a light-dark cycle of 12:12 hours and a temperature of 22°C. Animals were provided with standard water and mouse chow ad libitum. We previously found that the bAVM formation was not different between male and female mice8. Therefore, we only used male mice for this study. Animals were randomly grouped for each treatment. Outcome analyses were performed by researchers who were blinded to the treatment conditions. Details on study design, sample size determination, randomization, and blinding are provided in the Supplemental Materials. The Major Resources Table was also attached in the Supplemental Materials.

AAV-BR1-CAG-hKRASG12V-WPRE and -eGFP-WPRE were purchased from a custom AAV production service (Vector Biolabs, Malvern, PA). At five to six weeks of age, 2.5 or 5.0 × 1010 genome copies (GCs)/mouse of AAV-BR1-CAG-hKRASG12V-WPRE (KRASG12V/bEC mice) or -eGFP-WPRE (control mice) in 100 μl of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were injected into the retro-orbital sinuses of mice8,9.

Trametinib (Selleckchem, Houston, TX; 1 or 2 mg/kg)22 or vehicle (0.5% [hydroxypropyl]methyl cellulose and 0.2% Tween-80) was prepared for each administration to KRASG12V/bEC mice and daily administered by oral gavage for up to 31 weeks (only weekdays, but not weekend) after the injection of AAV-BR1-KRASG12V until mice experienced spontaneous termination. For histological analyses (immunostaining and iron staining), a separate cohort of mice was sacrificed after 10 weeks of the trametinib treatment.

Mice were anesthetized under 1–1.5% isoflurane in a gas mixture of 30% oxygen and 70% medical air during imaging. A physiological monitoring system (Small Animal Instruments, Stony Brook, NY, USA) was used to monitor the respiratory rate (80–120 breaths/min) and to maintain body temperature (36.5±0.5°C). At the UTHealth imaging core facility, MRI was performed with a 7 Tesla Bruker BioSpec system (Bruker Biospin, Billerica, MA) equipped with a B-GA12 gradient set. Images were collected in the following order: 1) T2-weighted imaging: 2D RARE with 3 echo times, TR/TE: 5000/(20, 60, 100) ms, RARE factor: 4, in-plane resolution: 90 × 90 μm2, slice thickness: 500 μm, total slices: 30, number of average: 4.2) T2*-weighted imaging: 2D MGE with 4 echo times, TR/TE: 1000/(3, 7, 11, 15) ms, in-plane resolution: 90 × 90 μm2, slice thickness: 500 μm, total slices: 30, number of average: 4. 3) MRA: 3D FLASH, TR/TE: 20/2.5 ms, flip angle: 30°, resolution: 60 × 60 × 60 μm3, number of averages: 2. After the image acquisition, mice were returned to the recovery cage and monitored until they awoke.

Segmentation masks of hemorrhagic regions of interest (ROIs) displayed on T2*-weighted images were generated via manual segmentation by a blinded researcher using the local thresholding technique with the software MedSeg (AI A/S, Oslo, Norway). The following post-image processing criteria were standardized across all T2*-weighted images measured in MedSeg: windowing: min=0, max = 300,000, mask opacity: 1.0, pointer opacity: 0.3, pointer size: 5–20, threshold: min = 0, max = adjusted to match ROI border. Volumetric calculations to 6 significant figures were obtained using the open-source software 3D Slicer (www.Slicer.org), measuring voxels from the segmentation masks containing only hemorrhagic ROIs previously generated with MedSeg.

Ferric iron in brain sections was detected by Prussian blue staining using a kit (ab150674, abcam). Working iron stain solution was prepared by 1:1 ratio of hydrochloric acid (2%) and potassium ferrocyanide solution. Brain sections were incubated with the mixture, washed in distilled water, and then counterstained with nuclear fast red solution. The blue-stained area was quantified using ImageJ.

Mouse brain sections and cultured ECs were fixed with 10% formalin and blocked with blocking buffer (1% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100, 0.02% Tween 20, 10% donkey serum in PBS). The fixed samples were incubated with primary antibodies: CD31 (AF3628, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN), Ter119 (MAB1125, R&D systems), VE-Cad (361900, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), p-ERK1/2 (9101S), N-Cad (13116S), Slug (9585S), CD44 (37259S), and Ki-67 (9129S) from Cell Signaling Technology, CD34 (NB6001071, Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO), or pVEGFR2 (SAB4504567, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), followed by secondary antibodies: 488-conjugated donkey anti-goat (705-545-147) or anti-rabbit IgG (711-545-152), Rhodamine Red-X-AffiniPure donkey anti-mouse (715-295-151), anti-rabbit (711-295-152), or anti-goat IgG (705-295-147), 647-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit (711-605-152), anti-mouse (715-605-150), or anti-rat IgG (712-605-153). All secondary antibodies were purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch (West Grove, PA). Slides were mounted with DAPI Fluoromount-G (0100-20, SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, AL) and observed under a fluorescence (Leica Thunder Imager) or confocal (Nikon) microscopy, which were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

Mouse brain microvascular ECs (C57–6023, Cell Biologics, Chicago, IL, USA) were cultured in DMEM (CM002–050, GenDEPOT, Katy, TX) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (CA005), 10 % fetal bovine serum (F0901–050) from GenDEPOT, and EC growth supplement (ECGS) (356006, Corning, NY, USA). 105 cells/well were transfected with 10 × 1010 GC/well of pAAV-CAG-eGFP-WPRE or -hKRASG12V-WPRE plasmid using Lipofectamine™ 2000 Transfection Reagent (11668019, Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h, the cells were treated with DMSO (Veh control) or trametinib (100 nM) (Selleckchem, Houston, TX).

ECs were lysed in protein lysis buffer (R4100, GenDEPOT) with sonication. The proteins were loaded in 4–20% ExpressPlus™ Page Gel (M42015, GenScript, Piscataway, NJ) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (1704273, Bio-Rad Laboratories) using Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (1704150, Bio-Rad Laboratories). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: phosphor (p)-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) (9101S), total (t)-ERK (9102S), GFP (2956S), N-Cad (13116S), Slug (9585S), CD44 (37259S) from Cell Signaling Technology, KRAS (sc-30, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), VE-Cad (361900, Thermo Fisher Scientific), or β-actin (A1978, Sigma-Aldrich) followed by secondary antibodies: rabbit IgG horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated antibody (1:1,000, HAF008, R&D Systems) or mouse IgG HRP-conjugated antibody (1:1,000, HAF007, R&D Systems).

After 24 h transfection, ECs were treated with DMSO or trametinib (100 nM) (Selleckchem, Houston, TX). A wound-healing migration assay was conducted 3 days after transfection. A 200 μl pipette tip was used to scratch the plate. Images were taken at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours (h) with the microscope (magnification x4). The wound closure rate (%) within the scratches was measured using ImageJ.

ECs were seeded in the upper chambers of transwell inserts (pore size, 0.4 μm; diameter, 24 mm; Corning Transwell; Sigma-Aldrich). After 24 h, the cells were transfected with the plasmid using Lipofectamine™ 2000 Transfection Reagent and treated with Veh or trametinib 24 h of transfection. The outer chamber was also filled with DMEM and replaced with fresh medium every two days. TEER values of each well were measured every day for 5 days using an EVOM2 endothelial volt ohmmeter (WPI, Sarasota, FL) equipped with an STX2 chopsticks electrode (WPI, Sarasota, FL). The blank transwell insert filled with media only was measured every time at the start of the experiment and subtracted from the average resistance across the cell layer. Each measurement was repeated at least 3 times. The TEER was determined by multiplying the resulting net resistance (Ω) by the semi-permeable membrane insert’s surface area (Marea, cm2) (TEERreported = Resistancetissue (Ω) × Marea (cm2)).

All data are expressed as mean ± SEM. The normal (Gaussian) distribution (two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test for two groups, and one-way ANOVA for more than two groups, followed by pairwise multiple comparisons using Tukey’s post-hoc test) was used. For MRI volumetric analysis, the data were evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was determined using an F-test. n-values indicate the number of mice or region of interest (ROI, randomly selected intact or bAVM vessels) for in vivo and the number of wells for in vitro studies. The number of replicates and sample sizes for each experiment is shown in each figure legend. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Software (San Diego, CA). If the p-value is less than 0.05, the results are considered statistically significant.

To confirm if trametinib inhibits the MEK/ERK signaling pathway, we measured p-ERK protein levels in cultured ECs overexpressing KRASG12V (ECG12V, ECs transfected with pAAV-CAG-hKRASG12V-WPRE), treated with either vehicle (Veh) or trametinib. p-ERK levels were significantly increased in ECG12V compared to control ECs (ECGFP, ECs transfected with pAAV-CAG-eGFP-WPRE). The KRASG12V-enhanced p-ERK expression was reversed by trametinib treatment (Fig. 1A). We also confirmed the trametinib efficacy on p-ERK inhibition in brain sections from KRASG12V/bEC mice treated with vehicle or trametinib (1 or 2 mg/kg) for two months (treatment started at 10 weeks post-AAV-BR1-KRASG12V injection). Immunostaining data demonstrated that trametinib treatment significantly reduced p-ERK expression in bAVM vessels of KRASG12V/bEC mice compared to vehicle-treated control mice (Fig. 1B). These data demonstrate that trametinib successfully inhibited the pERK signaling in vitro and in vivo.

In the first cohort, we induced bAVMs in mice by injecting 5 × 1010 GC/mouse of AAV-BR1-KRASG12V and treated the mice with either Veh or 1 mg/kg of trametinib. Because we did not observe significant changes in vascular size and hemorrhages through MRI/MRA following 1 mg/kg of trametinib, we increased the dose to 2 mg/kg from 15 weeks after starting the treatment. We observed that KRASG12V/bEC mice treated with Veh gradually died; however, the mortality of KRASG12V/bEC mice was significantly delayed with trametinib treatment (Fig. 2A). In the second cohort, we reduced AAV-BR1 injection doses by 2.5 × 1010 GC/mouse to delay the mortality in KRASG12V/bEC mice. The trametinib treatment for this second cohort was split into 1 and 2 mg/kg groups. While the reduction of AAVs did not significantly improve the mortality in KRASG12V/bEC mice, we consistently observed that trametinib treatments significantly delayed the animal mortality in both dosage groups compared to the vehicle-treated group. There was no better effect of the higher dose of trametinib (2 mg/kg) on the mortality of KRASG12V/bEC mice compared to a lower dose (1 mg/kg) (Fig. 2B). These results suggest that trametinib prolongs the survival of KRASG12V/bEC mice.

To see the effects of trametinib on bAVMs in vivo, MRI was carried out starting 3 weeks after vehicle or trametinib treatment. MRA images were taken serially at specific intervals until the animals were sacrificed. Overall, MRI/MRA imaging showed that the number of KRASG12V-induced bAVMs was not significantly altered in trametinib-treated mice (1 or 2 mg/kg) compared to vehicle-treated mice (Figure 3A and B). We further performed volumetric analysis using T2*-weighted MRI images scanned at 3 and 8 weeks after the treatments by manual segmentation with MedSeg and 3D Slicer (Fig. 3C). At 3 weeks of treatment, lesion volumes were slightly decreased in mice treated with 1 mg/kg of trametinib, but there were no significant differences in trametinib-treated groups compared to Veh group (Fig. 3D). However, at 8 weeks of treatment, the mice treated with 1 mg/kg of trametinib demonstrated significantly lower hemorrhagic volumes than the vehicle treated mice (Fig. 3D). The 2 mg/kg trametinib treatment group trended towards a lower hemorrhagic volume compared to vehicle treatment at this time point, although the differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3D). We further confirmed hemorrhages with Prussian blue staining, which detects ferric-irons (Fe3+) in tissues. We found that the intensity of Prussian blue-positive deposition was significantly decreased in 1mg/kg of trametinib treatment. However, 2mg/kg of trametinib treatment did not show a significant difference in iron deposit intensity or numbers compared to the Veh-treated group (Fig. 4A and B). Furthermore, our previous study showed that infiltrated Ter-119+ erythroid cells were found in KRASG12V-induced bAVMs8. We found that both low and high doses of trametinib attenuated Ter-119+ cells in the endothelial and parenchymal areas around bAVMs in KRASG12V/bEC mice. There was no difference between the two doses of trametinib treatment groups (Fig. 4C and D).

EndMT has recently been implicated in bAVM pathology7,18–20. To confirm if trametinib altered EndMT in bAVMs, we measured EndMT markers in brain sections of KRASG12V/bEC mice treated with Veh or trametinib (1 or 2 mg/kg) by immunostaining. Mean fluorescence intensities of mesenchymal markers such as N-Cad, Slug, and CD44 were extensively higher in bAVM vessels of KRASG12V/bEC mice compared to those in the vessels of the intact area. Trametinib treatment significantly reduced their expression in bAVMs compared to vehicle-treated mice. An EC marker, VE-Cad expression levels were also increased in bAVMs compared to intact vessels; however, its intensity was slightly higher in bAVMs treated with trametinib (Fig. 5A). Our previous study showed that Ki-67 proliferation marker was increased in bAVM of KRASG12V/bEC mice8. In this study, trametinib significantly reduced proliferating Ki-67+ cells and angiogenic CD34+ and pVEGFR2+ cells in CD31+ bAVMs in KRASG12V/bEC mice compared to Veh-treated mice (Fig. 5B), suggesting reduced proliferation and angiogenesis in KRASG12V-induced bAVMs by trametinib. We also evaluated the effect of trametinib on EndMT in cultured mouse brain ECs overexpressing KRASG12V (ECG12V). N-Cad, Slug, and CD44 were significantly increased, but VE-Cad was decreased, in ECG12V compared to control (ECGFP). However, the expression of these EndMT markers was reversed by trametinib treatment, with a stronger efficacy at 5 days of treatment (Fig. 6). These data suggest that trametinib normalizes KRASG12V-induced EndMT.

Increased EC proliferation and migration are hallmarks of aberrant development of vessels and abnormal angiogenesis. In wound healing scratch assay, ECG12V showed a faster migration than ECGFP, while trametinib treatment significantly reduced the KRASG12V-increased migration (Fig. 7A). Ki-67 is only detectable during the active phases of the cell cycle and is only expressed by proliferating cells23. ECG12V showed a higher percentage of Ki-67+ cells compared to ECGFP, whereas Ki-67+ cells were significantly decreased in ECG12V with trametinib treatment (Fig. 7B). We also examined the functional output of cell junction integrity by measuring the transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER). While the TEER values were overall consistent and stable in ECGFP, the values were significantly reduced in ECG12V. Notably, trametinib increased TEER values, eventually closer to the levels of ECGFP (Fig. 7C). Overall, these data demonstrate that trametinib restored KRASG12V-dysregulated EC functions and barrier strength.

Brain AVMs are fast-flowing, abnormal vascular lesions that can recur even after surgical resection. Due to numerous complications associated with invasive bAVM treatments, operative procedures may actually worsen the condition of the patient. There are currently no known pharmaceutical agents able to treat bAVMs. Therefore, treatment options for bAVMs are still challenging and rely largely on invasive procedures. Upon confirmation of the role of MEK/ERK signaling in mediating vascular abnormalities, including mutant KRAS-induced bAVM development6,12,24,25, our previous study focused on the preventative effect of trametinib, an FDA-approved MEK1/2 inhibitor14, and reported that trametinib attenuated bAVM growth in our established bAVM mouse model (KRASG12V/bEC mice)8. Here, we used trametinib to further understand its treatment effects in controlling the grown bAVMs using our established KRASG12V/bEC mice, whether the treatment can reduce the size of matured bAVMs or bAVM-associated ICHs. Overall, we found that trametinib treatment initiated at 10 weeks after KRASG12V overexpression could elongate animal survival (Fig. 2) and reduce hemorrhagic risk (Fig. 3), which were accompanied by normalized EndMT (Fig. 5). These results demonstrate the treatment efficacy of trametinib on bAVM pathology.

It has been shown that trametinib could reduce the size of extracranial AVMs16,17. Volumetric analyses of MRI images showed that the volume of lesions (including bAVMs/ICHs) was significantly smaller in mice treated with trametinib (Fig. 3C). However, the size and number of bAVMs were not noticeably changed in trametinib-treated mice based on our MRA imaging (Fig. 3A). Even though the vascular abnormalities remained unresolved, trametinib did substantially increase the lifespan of mice compared to vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 2). Spontaneous hemorrhages from bAVMs are a main feature of our KRASG12V/bEC mice8. We observed the bAVM lesions in many locations throughout the brain of each KRASG12V/bEC mouse8. However, human patients with sporadic bAVMs typically possess a single nidus in their brain, which typically occurs in the forebrain area, as in our mouse model26. We suspect that the presence of multiple bAVMs and abundance of bAVM-associated hemorrhages in our mouse model is a consequence of the widespread overexpression of KRASG12V mutation induced by AAV-BR1-KRASG12V injection8. Thus, our model exaggerates bAVM pathology, and the outcomes from this animal model may not directly translate to humans. However, this bAVM model offers many advantages, facilitating research to identify the pathogenic mechanisms and test pharmacological effects, as the molecular and functional changes could be more dramatic.

With the reduced lesion volume in MRI imaging (including hemorrhagic signals) (Fig. 3), decreased iron deposition and erythrocyte extravasation in bAVMs of mice treated with trametinib (Fig. 4) suggest that reduced hemorrhages are relevant to the longer survival of mice by trametinib treatment. However, the bAVMs in our KRASG12V/bEC mice could be continuously evolved, and mice treated with trametinib also eventually died (Fig. 2). This may suggest that our treatment could not show the full efficacy of trametinib to reduce the bAVMs size due to failing to overcome the exaggerated bAVM progression by KRASG12V overexpression. In addition, the hemorrhagic burden could be complex in the mice with numerous hemorrhagic lesions scattered throughout the brain, as it encompasses many factors, including hemorrhage volume, location, mass effect, and intraventricular extension. Indeed, a single small hemorrhage in a critical brain region, such as the brainstem, may be sufficient to induce premature demise, whereas numerous hemorrhages scattered in less eloquent regions may be silent. This randomized pattern of hemorrhages makes it difficult to truly quantify the hemorrhagic burden or isolate the individual lesion responsible for mortality in our model. Nevertheless, our findings demonstrate a beneficial efficacy of trametinib on bAVMs and provide an additional rationale for the clinical trials currently evaluating the use of trametinib in extracranial and intracranial AVMs27,28. However, further investigation is required to clarify how other characteristics defining the hemorrhage (e.g., location) can dramatically impact the burden from the lesion, with the relationship between bAVM instability and survival time in this model.

EndMT, a key process involved in vascular abnormalities, has recently been implicated in bAVM pathology. Previous studies showed that EndMT markers were increased in bAVMs of human patients7,18–20. It is also reported that KRASG12V or KRASG12D mutation enhanced EndMT in cultured HUVECs6,21. One of the novel findings in our study is that trametinib normalized EndMT in bAVMs (Fig. 5). Our in vitro studies using mouse ECs also showed that trametinib reversed KRASG12V-induced EndMT marker expression and EC functions (Fig. 6 and 7). Therefore, our results suggest that EndMT is a critical process in KRASG12V-induced bAVM development and maintenance, and normalization of EndMT may be a key mechanism by which trametinib improves bAVM pathology. While TGFβ signaling is crucial in promoting EndMT, the crosstalk between MEK/ERK and TGFβ has been shown. For example, ERK activation is required for TGFβ-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition29,30. In our previous study, we showed that TGFβ gene expression was higher in KRASG12V-induced bAVMs8. However, there is also a controversial study. Ricard et al. have shown that the loss of ERK1/2 in adult mice induced widespread EndMT and activated endothelial TGFβ signaling31. Therefore, the exact mechanisms by which targeting MEK/ERK inhibition reduced EndMT in the context of bAVMs harboring KRAS mutation should be further studied.

EndMT is the process whereby ECs acquire higher plasticity with increased proliferation, migratory, invasiveness, and loss of cell-to-cell adhesion30,32. EndMT is considered a critical process giving rise to mesenchymal cells necessary for new aberrant vessel formation in developmental and pathologic angiogenesis32,33. EndMT is also associated with blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability via the loss of junctional proteins34,35. Among junction proteins, reduced VE-Cad is one of the hallmarks in ECs undergoing EndMT19,20, and the reduced VE-Cad has been involved in bAVM microhemorrhages36. We also observed that VE-Cad levels were decreased in the mouse bAVMs and ECG12V along with higher EndMT markers and reduced EC-barrier integrity, which were all normalized by trametinib treatment (Fig. 5–7). As endothelial barrier disruption contributes to the vessel weakness and permeability, the improved EC integrity by trametinib could be relevant to the reduced hemorrhages in the mouse bAVMs (Fig. 3 and 4). Trametinib’s effects on EndMT may be involved in reducing the bAVM-associated hemorrhages in KRASG12V/bEC mice.

While our data demonstrates an association between EndMT and trametinib-improved hemorrhages in bAVMs, it would be critical to test the direct role of EndMT in the trametinib effects on BBB integrity. However, EndMT is a dynamic process orchestrated by multiple factors, and attempting to influence this process by targeting individual components of EndMT may not be sufficient to test its role. In addition, ECs are interacting with other vascular constituents, such as mural cells and astrocytes37. Immune cells also interact with ECs and can influence their response to the treatment38. Accordingly, the role of other brain vascular constituents was implicated in bAVM vessel rupture. Our previous study observed dysregulated and ambiguous mural cell coverages in bAVMs of KRASG12V mice, which have spontaneous hemorrhages in bAVMs8. Another study using an Alk1-deficient bAVM model reported that the bAVM vessels had a reduction in mural cell coverage, which was accompanied by brain parenchymal erythrocyte extravasation39. Inflammatory cells, such as microglia and macrophages, and astrocytes are also considered to play a role in vessel integrity and bAVM rupture38. Therefore, the effect of trametinib on EC molecular and functional regulations should be far more complex in vivo. Ultimately, for a more accurate representation of in vivo effects, compatible conditions between in vitro and in vivo systems should be established (e.g., co-culture system comprised of ECs with other vascular constituents) to identify how these vascular components orchestrate bAVM pathology.

Several studies have additionally reported the effectiveness of targeting MEK/ERK signaling in AVMs. In a KRASG12V-induced AVM zebrafish model, MEK inhibition showed a partial decrease in brain hemorrhages and AV shunt resolution, whereas PI3K inhibition did not12. In addition, a case report of a Cobb syndrome patient with a KRAS mutant metameric AVM showed decreased blood flow to the AVM with trametinib treatment40. Likewise, a patient with an EPHB4 mutation having a capillary malformation-arteriovenous malformation (CM-AVM) lesion in her thigh also showed a positive response to trametinib treatment41. Based on numerous studies like these, there are also several ongoing clinical trials using trametinib in extracranial and intracranial AVMs27,28. Our findings in this study demonstrate that trametinib improves KRASG12V-induced bAVM pathological outcomes by normalizing the EndMT process, leading to reduced migration and proliferation of mutant ECs, rectified junction protein expression, improved vascular integrity, and potentially a decreased risk for hemorrhagic conversion. Thus, the findings we presented here suggest that MEK inhibition may be a promising non-invasive treatment strategy for bAVM patients.

Studies have reported trametinib’s effect on peripheral immune response. Low doses (0.1 or 0.3 mg/kg/d for 10 or 28 days) of trametinib down-regulated circulating T cells and splenic CD8+ T cell expansion. High-dose (2.0 mg/kg) of trametinib treatment for 30 days reduced myeloid cells engraftment in bone marrow42. Another study using a rat lung transplantation model showed that trametinib (0.1 and 0.3 mg/kg for 28 days) suppressed functional differentiation of peripheral T and B cells43. Also, an in vitro study showed that trametinib directly suppressed both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell proliferation and cytokine secretion44. Although the previous trials for extracranial AVM patients with a trametinib treatment with a dose of 0.5 mg daily, then increased to 0.5 mg twice daily after one month, for 6 months16 and another trial with 1mg of trametinib daily for 2.5 years17. Although these case reports did not report any significant adverse effects regarding immunosuppression or immune cell populations, this does not preclude the possibility that some degree of effect may still occur. Therefore, the effects of long-term systemic and local immune responses, as well as other adverse effects from trametinib treatment, should be carefully considered when translating our findings to human patients.

In this study, we used only male mice to confirm the efficacy of trametinib in our bAVM mouse models because there was no significant difference in bAVM development between male and female mice in our previous study8. However, the long-term course of disease and effectiveness of treatment may vary between males and females. Differential pharmacokinetics of anti-cancer drugs (including trametinib) between males and females have been reported45,46. It was also shown that a combination treatment using trametinib with rapamycin was more effective at reducing spleen tumors in male mice compared to females47. In addition, a study using Drosophila showed that trametinib consistently and robustly extends female lifespan and reduces intestinal stem cell proliferation, tumor formation, tissue dysplasia, and barrier disruption in the guts, but the effects were not significant in male flies48. Therefore, further investigation regarding a potential differential effect of trametinib on bAVMs of males versus females is needed.

Therapeutic approaches to reduce the risk of bAVM rupture would be critical for bAVM patients, besides targeting bAVM regression. Trametinib improves mortality and bAVM hemorrhagic risk in KRASG12V/bEC mice. These beneficial effects of trametinib in bAVM pathology are involved in normalized EndMT and vascular integrity. Trametinib possesses a long half-life (~ 5 days)49 and has been safe for long-term treatment in human16,17. We also did not observe a significant change in mouse body weights or any specific side effects (data not shown) during the treatments. Taken all together, our study suggests that trametinib could be a safe and effective treatment option for bAVM patients.
