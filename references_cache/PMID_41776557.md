BACKGROUND: The non-breast non-ovarian cancers associated with BRCA1 and BRCA2
pathogenic variants (PVs) are controversial. We aimed to examine this using a
prospective cohort design.
METHODS: This study included 1260 BRCA1 and 1058 BRCA2 PV carriers (91% were
females) from two consortia: the Breast Cancer Family Registry (BCFR) and the
Kathleen Cuningham Foundation Consortium for Research into Familial Breast
Cancer Follow-Up Study (kConFab-FUS). The carriers were free of cancer other
than breast or ovarian cancer at baseline and had a median baseline age of
45.5 years. For 16 types of non-breast, non-ovarian cancers, standardized
incidence ratios (SIRs) relative to population incidence, the probabilities of
relative risk effect size > 2 (i.e., moderate risk) and cumulative risks to age
80 years were estimated.
RESULTS: During a median follow-up time of 11.4 years, 161 non-breast,
non-ovarian cancers were observed. For BRCA1 PV carriers, little evidence of
increased risk was observed. The prostate, pancreatic, and all non-pancreatic
cancer SIRs were 1.7 (95% CI 0.7-4.2), 1.1 (95% CI 0.3-4.6) and 0.85 (95% CI
0.68-1.06), respectively; the probabilities of relative risk > 2 were 0 and 67%
for prostate and pancreatic cancers, respectively. For BRCA2 PV carriers,
increased risks of pancreatic (SIR = 6.6, 95% CI 3.8-11.6), prostate (SIR = 3.6,
95% CI 1.9-6.8) and stomach (SIR = 3.1, 95% CI 1.01-9.8) cancer were observed,
with a cumulative risk to age 80 years of 8.3, 82.0, and 1.6%, respectively. For
all the other non-breast, non-ovarian cancers combined, the SIR was 0.85 (95% CI
0.66-1.10).
CONCLUSIONS: Apart from pancreatic, prostate, and possibly stomach cancers for
BRCA2 PV carriers, and possibly pancreatic cancer for BRCA1 PV carriers, there
is no evidence that BRCA1 and BRCA2 PV carriers have substantially increased
risks of other non-breast, non-ovarian cancers. Our prospective risk estimates
are informative for cancer risk assessment for people with BRCA1 and BRCA2 PVs.

BRCA1 and BRCA2 pathogenic variants (PVs) are known to be associated with high risks of cancers of the breast and ovary, and BRCA2 PVs are also associated with increased pancreatic and prostate cancer risks [1–4]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommends breast, ovarian, and prostate cancer risk management for BRCA1 and BRCA2 PV carriers, and pancreatic cancer risk management for BRCA2 PV carriers, and BRCA1 PV carriers with a family history of pancreatic cancer [5]. Elevated risks of other cancers, such as biliary tract, blood, bone, brain, cervical, colorectal, endometrial, esophageal, gastric, liver, lung, renal, testicular cancers, and melanoma, have also been reported [2, 3, 6–15]; however, these cancers are not reported consistently. Understanding the spectrum of cancers associated with the PVs is critical for understanding the role of BRCA1 and BRCA2 PVs in carcinogenesis, and is an essential first step in improving risk management for PV carriers.

Most of the current cancer risk estimates for PV carriers are from cross-sectional or retrospective data, especially those from family-based studies. These estimates are vulnerable to biases arising from data collection accuracy or data analysis approaches, e.g., whether family ascertainment is correctly accounted for. These biases could be mitigated through prospective cohort study designs. Efforts have been made to prospectively estimate the risks of breast, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic cancers [1, 4, 16], but not of other suggested cancers.


To provide more evidence about the cancer spectrum for PV carriers, we used a prospective cohort design to investigate the risks of cancers other than breast or ovarian cancers.

Our prospective cohort included BRCA1 and BRCA2 PV carriers recruited from two consortia: the Breast Cancer Family Registry (BCFR) and the Kathleen Cuningham Foundation Consortium for Research into Familial Breast Cancer Follow-Up Study (kConFab-FUS).

The BCFR includes six sites, one in Australia, one in Canada, and four in the USA (California, New York, Philadelphia, and Utah) [17]. The Australian, Canadian, and Northern Californian sites recruited population-based breast cancer case families through cancer registries, and the New York, Philadelphia, and Utah sites recruited multiple-case families through family cancer clinics and community outreach. All six sites followed the individuals together as an enriched cohort with a range of absolute cancer risks [18]. Participants have been recruited since 1996 and followed regularly, through annual contact with probands and systematic registry-wide follow-ups [18]; the most recent follow-up was completed in December 2023.

The kConFab has recruited multiple-case breast cancer families ascertained through family cancer clinics in Australia and New Zealand since 1997 [19, 20]. The same BCFR questionnaires were used for collecting epidemiological and family history data. Through the kConFab-FUS, a subgroup of participants has been systematically followed for cancer outcomes, epidemiological risk factors, and risk management behaviors using a mailed follow-up questionnaire administered every 3 years [18, 21] until May 2023.

Carriers from the two consortia were identified by a combination of genetic testing methods over time; see the previous publications for more details [19, 22]. There were 2863 carriers with follow-up data available: 1537 (854 BRCA1, 683 BRCA2) from the BCFR and 1326 (713 BRCA1, 613 BRCA2) from the kConFab-FUS. Baseline age was defined as age at study recruitment or age at genetic testing, whichever came last. Thirty-four carriers (16 BRCA1, 18 BRCA2) who were over 80 years of age at baseline, and 513 carriers (291 BRCA1, 222 BRCA2) who had a cancer diagnosis other than breast, ovarian or non-melanoma skin cancers before or at baseline (Additional file 1: Table S1) were excluded, resulting in 2318 carriers being included in analysis. All participants provided written informed consent, and all centers in the consortia obtained local ethics approvals.

Carriers were followed from baseline until the first of the following: age 80 years, death, completion of the last follow-up or record linkage, or diagnosis of any primary cancer other than breast, ovarian, or non-melanoma skin cancers. For endometrial cancer analysis, carriers with a hysterectomy at baseline were excluded, and hysterectomy was included in follow-up censoring. For a cancer of interest, carriers diagnosed with that cancer during the follow-up time were considered affected, and the other carriers, regardless of their other cancer diagnoses, were considered unaffected.

Based on the cancer diagnoses of the participants and the availability of population incidence data, a total of 16 cancers, grouped by anatomical sites using ICD-10 codes (Additional file 1: Table S2), were analyzed. For each cancer, standardized incidence ratio (SIR) was estimated via Poisson regression to compare the observed incidence with the country-, sex-, calendar year-, and age-specific population incidence in the Cancer Incidence Rates in Five Continents. Robust standard error of the SIR was estimated to account for the familial clustering of the carriers. SIRs stratified by age 60 years were also estimated and compared using a likelihood ratio test. Cancer annual incidence was estimated using standard cohort analysis, and cumulative risk was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier analysis.

We used simulation to estimate the smallest SIRs that our sample size could detect with 80% statistical power. Assuming the number of cancer cases follows a Poisson distribution, we conducted 10,000 simulations, with the number of observed cases in each simulation drawn based on the sample size, expected cancer incidence and a target SIR. The smallest SIR is the target SIR for which the null hypothesis was rejected using a two-sided test with a Type I error rate of 5% in at least 80% of simulations.

The posterior probability of the cancer relative risk true effect size > 2 (i.e., moderate or high risk) was calculated using the Bayesian approach, based on the prior risk estimate from the largest family-based study [2] and the observed SIR in this study, as well as their precisions.

Cox regression with age as the time scale was conducted to investigate whether cancer risks differed by gene, country, family history of any cancer in first-degree relatives, and personal history of breast or ovarian cancer. The regression analyses were stratified by country, except for the one investigating the country difference. Robust standard errors for the hazard ratios were estimated to account for familial clustering of carriers. The proportional hazard assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, which was met for all analyses.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted: carriers with breast or ovarian cancer at baseline were excluded, and follow-up was also censored at breast or ovarian cancer diagnosis.

The analyses were performed using StataMP 14 and R. All statistical tests were two-sided, and results with a P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

A total of 2318 PV carriers were included in the analysis, including 1260 BRCA1 PV carriers and 1058 BRCA2 PV carriers; of these, 2105 were females and 213 were males (Table 1). At baseline, 40.1% of the carriers had a personal history of breast cancer, 4.5% had a personal history of ovarian cancer, and 36.7% had a first-degree relative diagnosed with any cancer. The carriers had a median baseline age of 45.5 years. During a median follow-up period of 11.4 years (interquartile range: 5.9–16.2 years), 161 (6.9%) carriers had a primary cancer diagnosis other than breast or ovarian cancer, and 69.9% of these diagnoses were verified via clinical records, cancer registry linkage or pathology reports.

Across the investigated cancers, the smallest SIR that our sample size could detect with 80% statistical power ranged from 1.3 to 18.6, with a median of ~ 3 (Additional file 1: Table S3). Specifically, there was 80% power to detect an SIR ≥ 4.1, ≥ 3.2, and ≥ 1.3 for the cancer of the pancreas, prostate, and all the other sites combined, respectively.

For BRCA1 PV carriers, no evidence of increased cancer risk was found (Table 2). The SIR was 1.1 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.3–4.6) for pancreatic cancer and 1.7 (95% CI 0.7–4.2) for prostate cancer (Table 2); no evidence of increased risk at ages < 60 years was found for these two cancers (Table 3). The SIR for all non-pancreatic cancers combined was 0.85 (95% CI 0.68–1.06). Increased risks at ages < 60 years were observed for leukemia and lymphoma; however, there was no statistical evidence of a difference by age (Table 3).

For BRCA2 PV carriers, increased risks were observed for pancreatic (SIR = 6.6, 95% CI 3.8–11.6), prostate (SIR = 3.6, 95% CI 1.9–6.8), stomach (SIR = 3.1, 95% CI 1.01–9.8), and thyroid (SIR = 2.2, 95% CI 1.01–4.6) cancers (Table 2); the risks for pancreatic and prostate cancers were higher at ages < 60 years (Table 3). The SIR was 0.85 (95% CI 0.66–1.10) for all cancers other than pancreatic, prostate, and stomach. Although increased risks at ages < 60 years were observed for stomach, bladder, and endometrial cancers, there was no evidence of an age interaction for these cancers (Table 3). All lymphoma and multiple myeloma cases were diagnosed < 60 years, and increased risks for these cancers were observed in this age group.

We found no evidence that the observed increased cancer risks differed by country, family history of any cancer, or personal history of breast or ovarian cancer (Additional file 1: Table S4). We did not find evidence that the cancer risks differed between the two types of PV carriers, except for pancreatic cancer which had a higher risk for BRCA2 PV carriers (Additional file 1: Table S5).

The posterior probability that the pancreatic cancer relative risk effect size was > 2 was 66.5% for BRCA1 PV carriers and 100% for BRCA2 PV carriers (Table 2 & Additional file 1: Figure S1). For prostate cancer, the probabilities were 0.02% for BRCA1 PV carriers and 91.7% for BRCA2 PV carriers. The probability that the stomach cancer effect size was > 2 was 99.8% for BRCA2 PV carriers. Although an elevated SIR of thyroid cancer was found for BRCA2 PV carriers, the probability that the effect size was > 2 was < 40% (Table 2 & Additional file 1: Figure S1).

For BRCA2 PV carriers, the incidences of pancreatic, prostate, and stomach cancers were 93 (95% CI 52–169), 1262 (95% CI 656–2425) and 25 (95% CI 8–79) per 100,000 person-years, and the cumulative risks to age 80 years were 8.3% (95% CI 3.9–17.7%), 82.0% (95% CI 41.2–162.9%), and 1.6% (95% CI 0.4–5.8%), respectively (Fig. 1).

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective study that comprehensively investigated the risks of 16 types of non-breast, non-ovarian cancers for BRCA1 and BRCA2 PV carriers. Although previous prospective studies of prostate and pancreatic cancers [4, 16] had larger sample sizes (42 prostate cancer cases from 823 male carriers, and 34 pancreatic cancer cases from 8295 female carriers), our study has the following strengths: (1) a longer follow-up time—median 11.4 years for all carriers and 6.3 years for male carriers, c.f., 8.6 years in Katona et al. for pancreatic cancer [16] and ~ 5.5 years in Nyberg et al. for prostate cancer [4]; and (2) a 3–6 years younger average baseline age than the carriers in Katona et al. and Nyberg et al..

Our study confirmed higher pancreatic, prostate, and possibly stomach cancer risks for BRCA2 PV carriers, and possibly higher pancreatic cancer risk (the probability of relative risk > 2 was 67%) for BRCA1 PV carriers. More importantly, our study provided evidence that there are unlikely to be substantial risks of other cancers, including prostate cancer for BRCA1 PV carriers. These findings are consistent with those from the largest comprehensive retrospective family-based study [2]. Although statistical power to detect a relatively small risk for some of the investigated cancers was limited, the study had sufficient statistical power to detect a moderate risk for all non-pancreatic, non-prostate cancers combined. Furthermore, the conclusions are not based on the results of our study alone; findings from the largest family-based study [2] were also considered through the posterior probabilities. Because PV carriers in our study were from families ascertained via population-based or clinic-based breast cancer patients, our cancer risk estimates apply to BRCA1 and BRCA2 PV carriers in general, under the assumption that the risks of non-breast cancers are independent of personal or familial history of breast cancer, conditional on the PV status. Notably, if the PVs carried by the study sample are specific to breast cancer patients and their families, our findings might not be applicable to other carriers in the population.

Our study prospectively estimated that the pancreatic risk for BRCA2 PV carriers relative to non-carriers was ~ 6, with a higher risk at younger ages, and the cumulative risk by age 80 years was ~ 8%. These risk estimates are consistent with those from retrospective family-based studies and case–control studies [2, 7, 10, 11, 23, 24]. A case–control study using the Biobank Japan resource reported higher-risk estimates: relative risk > 10 and lifetime cumulative risk > 13% [3]; such differences might be due to ethnicity differences. Although our cumulative risk estimate is higher than the 2.7% estimated by Katona et al. [16] using the prospective data for > 2400 BRCA2 PV carriers, the confidence intervals of the two estimates largely overlapped. For BRCA1 PV carriers, inconsistent results have been reported [2, 3, 7–9, 12, 16, 23, 24]. A retrospective analysis of BCFR families reported a fourfold relative risk [24]. Although our prospective analysis did not observe a statistically significant SIR and the SIR estimate was significantly lower than the estimate for BRCA2 PV carriers, we found evidence for a high probability of a relative risk > 2, suggesting that BRCA1 PV carriers could have a moderate risk of pancreatic cancer; more studies of this finding are warranted.

In terms of prostate cancer risk for BRCA2 PV carriers, previous retrospective studies reported relative risks of 2–6 and lifetime risks of 17–33% [2, 7, 10–12, 14, 15, 25–28]; the prospective study of 447 carriers by Nyberg et al. [4] reported a relative risk of 4.5 and a lifetime risk of 60%, and these estimates were reduced to 2.3 and 41%, respectively, after adjusting for possible prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening effects. The lifetime risk estimate of 82% in our study is higher than the estimate of Nyberg et al., which could be due to potential screening effects for known carriers. The risk may be lower for unscreened carriers. Although PSA rates have also increased in the general population, our estimate is still much higher than the population lifetime prostate cancer risk of 17% (based on 2020 Australian cancer incidence [29]). The higher risk estimate could also be due to the sample size of 90 carriers that is insufficient to provide a precise estimate—this is reflected by the wide confidence interval of the estimate, with a lower bound of 41%, the same as what Nyberg et al. estimated after adjusting for PSA screening. Our study suggests that BRCA1 PV carriers do not have an increased risk for prostate cancer overall, consistent with previous findings [4, 7–9, 12, 25–27].

Whether PV carriers have an increased risk of stomach cancer is uncertain. Previous retrospective family-based studies reported relative risks of 2–7 for BRCA1 PV carriers and 2–4 for BRCA2 PV carriers [2, 7, 8, 11, 12]; however, stomach cancer family history data in those studies were self-reported with few verifications and subject to misclassification of some ovarian cancers as stomach cancers, especially given that female carriers were found to have a higher risk than male carriers [2, 11]. Based on three stomach cancers, all in female carriers, our study suggests BRCA2 PV carriers possibly have an increased risk: a threefold relative risk and a lifetime risk of 1.6%. The three cases included two adenocarcinomas and one squamous cell carcinoma. Although the pathology report of the squamous cell carcinoma was based on a biopsy from the stomach, we cannot rule out the possibility that it was an esophageal primary cancer extending into the stomach. The stomach cancer association could be dependent on ethnicity, as a relative risk of ~ 5 for both BRCA1 and BRCA2 PV carriers was reported by studies from Biobank Japan [3, 30], in which stomach cancer cases were ascertained from hospitals and 74% of the cases were males. Notably, the studies have also found a substantial interaction between H.
pylori infection and homologous-recombination genes on stomach cancer risk [30], while our study samples are from countries with lower H.
pylori prevalences, which may explain our lower risk estimate than those from the Japanese population.

Our study also found an association with thyroid cancer; however, no prior study has reported an association for thyroid cancer, and considering our result together with previous findings [2], the probability of BRCA2 PV carriers having a moderate risk for thyroid cancer is not high. Our result was based on six cases, three of which were with known histology and all of them were papillary carcinoma.

Our findings support the NCCN guidelines on pancreatic and prostate cancer management for BRCA2 PV carriers, and our pancreatic cancer risk estimate could be used for risk assessment, especially given that the pancreatic cancer risk estimates used by the guidelines were from retrospective data [5]. More evidence is needed to refine the risk estimates for pancreatic cancer for BRCA1 PV carriers and stomach cancer for both PV carriers. More importantly, our study did not find evidence of an increased risk of other cancers, which does not support these cancers to be considered in risk counselling.

Our study has several strengths: (1) it used prospectively collected data and a large proportion of cancer diagnoses was verified; (2) it investigated 16 types of non-breast non-ovarian cancers; and (3) the longer follow-up time and younger baseline age as mentioned above. Nevertheless, our study also has several limitations: (1) the study sample included carriers who were alive and cancer-free at baseline; thus high-risk carriers who died or were diagnosed with cancer at an earlier age were not included, potentially resulting in cancer risk underestimation; (2) due to data collection, we could not investigate whether cancer-specific family history modifies the cancer risks, but only the family history of any cancer; (3) the carriers were from Australia, New Zealand, and North America with most being of European ancestry, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other races and ethnicities; (4) most of the carriers were females, which might limit the generalizability of the findings on non-sex-specific cancers to male carriers, though the largest family-based study did not find evidence of a risk difference between male and female carriers [2]; (5) approximately half of the carriers had a breast and/or ovarian cancer diagnosis before baseline, and the diagnosis and relevant treatment might influence the risks of subsequent cancers after baseline, although our sensitivity analysis of excluding these carriers showed similar results.

Apart from pancreatic, prostate, and possibly stomach cancers for BRCA2 PV carriers, and possibly pancreatic cancer for BRCA1 PV carriers, there is no evidence that BRCA1 and BRCA2 PV carriers are likely to have substantially increased risks of other non-breast non-ovarian cancers. Our risk estimates are informative for cancer assessment for people with a BRCA1
or BRCA2 PV.
