# Phenotypic and genotypic description of GMPPA-congenital disorder of glycosylation: A review of 26 cases.
**Authors:** Altassan R, Aldhahri SK, Macdonald G, Shah R, Morava E
**Journal:** Mol Genet Metab (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymgme.2025.109196](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymgme.2025.109196)

## Content

GMPPA-Congenital Disorder of Glycosylation (CDG) is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive CDG caused by pathogenic variants in GMPPA that affects the N-linked glycosylation pathway. Affected individuals present with three major symptoms: achalasia, alacrima, and impaired intellectual development during infancy. Current management of GMPPA-CDG is targeted to address patients' symptoms. To date, 23 individuals have been reported with GMPPA-CDG. This paper reviews the clinical, biochemical and genetic characteristics of the reported 23 patients and adds 3 patients with GMPPA-CDG. We also describe the effect of oral N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) supplementation in 3 patients. Besides alacrima, achalasia and developmental/ intellectual disability, we noted in these patients also variable growth impairment, facial dysmorphism, hyperkeratosis, hypohidrosis, anodontia, and hearing deficit. Under treatment with GlcNAc (4-6 g/day), we noted improved tear production in our 3 patients. Given its effect on different developmental pathways, we emphasize the need for multidisciplinary care for this multisystem disorder. We did not find a genotype/phenotype correlation in our cohort of 26 patients.

GDP-mannose pyrophosphorylase A (GMPPA) is a regulatory subunit of the GDP-mannose pyrophosphorylase complex, which catalyzes the conversion of mannose-1-phosphate and GTP into GDP-mannose, a key precursor in N-linked glycosylation. Defects in GMPPA lead to disruption in N-linked glycosylation pathway especially mannose-rich glycoproteins [1].

Pathogenic variants in GDP-mannose pyrophosphorylase A (GMPPA) (OMIM 615495) cause GMPPA-Congenital Disorder of Glycosylation (GMPPA-CDG), also known as alacrima, achalasia, and impaired intellectual development syndrome (AAMR) (OMIM 615510). This syndrome typically presents at birth or during early infancy with its hallmark features: alacrima, achalasia, and developmental disability [2]. Additional variable manifestations include hypotonia, gait abnormalities, anisocoria, and sensory/visual deficits. Although GMPPA-CDG shares some clinical features with triple A syndrome, adrenal insufficiency, characteristic of the latter, is notably absent in GMPPA-CDG [3].

In this review, we analyzed a total of 26 individuals diagnosed with GMPPA-CDG, including three unreported cases from our Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation (CDG) natural history study. Our goal is to expand the clinical and genetic spectrum of GMPPA-CDG and propose strategies to improve its diagnosis and management.

We evaluated three patients with confirmed pathogenic GMPPA variants from among 238 CDG patients enrolled in the Frontiers of Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation Consortium (FCDGC) natural history study (institutional review board [IRB] approval: 19–005187; NCT04199000). Prospective and retrospective data were available, with regular patient follow-ups forming part of the standard care at each participating site.

Additionally, we conducted a comprehensive review of all published cases of patients with GMPPA-CDG confirmed pathogenic variants. This review focused on the clinical, metabolic, and molecular characteristics of these patients. We searched the PubMed database from the date of the first clinical description until March 2025 using the following terms: GMPPA-CDG, GMPPA-related disorders, Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation, and alacrima, achalasia, and impaired intellectual development syndrome, AAMR. Relevant articles were identified based on title and abstract screening, followed by full-text review to determine the eligibility for inclusion in the study.

Once the relevant cases were identified, data extraction was performed to collect detailed information on each patient’s demographics, clinical features including growth parameters, neurological symptoms, developmental assessment, systems involvement, and other relevant phenotypic traits, genetic variants, and treatments. We summarized the demographic and clinical characteristics of the included cases. The findings were synthesized to provide a comprehensive overview of the phenotypic spectrum of GMPPA-CDG, highlighting commonalities, variabilities, and trends observed across the cases. Limitations of the study, such as potential publication bias and heterogeneity in reporting, were also acknowledged and addressed in the discussion section.

Three patients from the FCDGC natural history study with known pathogenic GMPPA variants were recruited at Mayo Clinic (Rochester, MN) and The Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (New York, NY). The protocol was approved by the institutional review board (IRB: 18–007276). Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients or their legal guardians. Participants in this nonblinded observational pilot study received oral GlcNAc supplementation over 6–18 months. To minimize gastrointestinal and metabolic side effects, GlcNAc intake was increased in a stepwise fashion over the study period as follows: months 0–3: 1.0g twice a day, months 3–6: 2.0g twice a day, months 6–18: 3.0g twice a day. Participants were instructed to continue their regular diet in combination with daily oral GlcNAc supplementation. This GlcNAc dose is comparable to the dose used in a current clinical trial in NGLY1-CDDG. Prior studies investigating irritable bowel syndrome in children have demonstrated this amount of GlcNAc to be safe and tolerable by patients. Eye involvement was assessed by regular ophthalmology visits.

This review included twenty-six molecularly confirmed GMPPA-CDG patients (9 males and 17 females). Data from 23 patients were collected from previously published articles (n = 7), including case cohorts (n = 1) [2], and case reports (n = 6) ([4-8], and [9]). Data for the so far unreported additional three patients were collected from the shared natural history database (supplementary files 1, 2).

The youngest and oldest patients were 9-month and 47 year-old, respectively. Patients’ ethnicities were diverse, including Europeans, Central Americans, East Asians, Africans, and Middle Easterns.

Three phenotypic features were universally present, marking hallmark characteristics of this syndrome. Alacrima (absence of tear production), achalasia (abnormal esophageal motility), and developmental delay were noted in all individuals (26/26 = 100 %). Intellectual disability was mentioned in 22 individuals (data were missing in four reports), indicating that cognition is almost always affected in GMPPA-CDG (Fig. 1).

There was impaired growth, called “Failure to Thrive or short stature” in 11 individuals (11/12, 91 %). Short stature was stated in 10 individuals, and was associated with failure to thrive in only six of them. One patient had failure to thrive without short stature. Data on growth were not reported in 15 individuals.

Other symptoms were more variable and less frequently reported in GMPPA-CDG. For example, the facial dysmorphism was found in more than half of the patients (8/12) and showed no typical features [4-7].

In addition to the developmental/ intellectual disability, hypotonia was reported in 10/21 patients, gait abnormalities (due to ataxia or spasticity) in 7/17, and hyperreflexia in 2/18. Two individuals had an unspecified seizure disorder. Only four patients had brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Of these, three showed normal results, two reported by [5], and one identified later from the natural history database on the patient reported by [8]. One patient exhibited only bilateral absence of the lacrimal glands, as reported by [9].

Gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms like poor feeding, recurrent vomiting, dysphagia, and regurgitation were the first presenting manifestations in almost all individuals due to the achalasia. Achalasia diagnosis was delayed or misdiagnosed as feeding intolerance in a few patients. Feeding tolerance improved following the therapeutic interventions for the achalasia. However, no records on the impact of achalasia management on the growth parameters. In our new cohort, two had growth measurements below normal percentiles, while one was at the lower end of the normal range.

All patients underwent upper GI studies for the diagnosis. Interventions were mainly by the supportive nasogastric or gastrostomy tube-feeding, in addition to surgical correction by Heller myotomy.

Less reported findings include anodontia (2/5), hypohidrosis (8/24), microcephaly (3/10), hearing loss (6/17), postural hypotension (5/17), and hyperkeratosis (4/17). Variable eye abnormalities, including nystagmus, anisocoria, esotropia, strabismus, and astigmatism were reported in seven individuals.

All individuals had molecular confirmation for GMPPA-CDG. Exome sequencing, either as a solo, trio, or quad, was the main molecular test used for diagnosis, performed in 20 individuals. The rest were diagnosed by either microarray and homozygosity mapping, gene panel, or GMPPA gene sequencing. Genetic analysis of the cohort revealed 17 different pathogenic variants in GMPPA. Homozygous variants were detected in most of the reported individuals who were born to consanguineous families. All type of mutations were reported in this cohort, including nonsense, missense, deletion, duplication, and frameshift (Fig. 2). There was no genotype/phenotype correlation found in this review. Although there were no reports of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) or cortisol levels, none of the individuals exhibited adrenal insufficiency, and none reported abnormalities in the serum transferrin profiles or other glycosylated proteins evaluated by mass spectrometry of transferrin isoelectric focusing.

The three individuals who received oral supplementation with GlcNAc, two from the new cohort and one previously reported by [8], all tolerated dose escalation to 6 g/day, except for one patient who had abdominal discomfort and had to keep the daily dose at 4 g/day. All three patients reported improvement of their eye symptoms, confirmed by their ophthalmologist. One patient had less vascularization on eye exam after a year of treatment. One patient reported less gastrointestinal discomfort and subjective improvement in swallowing. No diarrhea was reported. No change was observed in growth or development (Table 1).

GMPPA-CDG or previously called AAMR, is an autosomal recessive N-linked CDG, due to biallelic pathogenic variants in GMPPA. The disease symptoms appear either at birth or during early infancy and are marked by cerebral involvement as well as autonomic dysfunction, which includes alacrima and achalasia.

Patients with GMPPA-CDG experience a range of cognitive challenges, including developmental/ intellectual disability affecting all patients. Most of the affected individuals were either non-verbal or only spoke a few words. Such findings suggest that early cognitive and developmental support should be prioritized in the clinical management of these patients.

The combination of achalasia and alacrima, together with neurological involvement, is a strong indicator of GMPPA-CDG. Although the pathomechanism of these autonomic symptoms is not understood, the presence of these features signifies a strong association with GMPPA-CDG, where the autonomic dysfunction phenotype aligns with the typical presentation of this disorder [10].

Like other CDGs, GMPPA-CDG can affect patients’ growth. This might be overlooked in this retrospective review, as the systemic involvement assessment was lacking in some reports. Linear growth appeared to be significantly affected in patients with GMPPA-CDG, suggesting that GMPPA has a substantial impact on skeletal or possible hormonal development.

Patients with GMPPA-CDG showed notably reduced linear growth, indicating that GMPPA likely plays an important role in skeletal or hormonal development. Growth hormone levels were measured and found to be decreased in two children. One patient was successfully started on growth hormone therapy without side effects. The presence of impaired growth reflects the metabolic implications of glycosylation disorders, which can affect cellular and systemic growth pathways. This growth impairment was closely associated with the presence of other developmental issues, pointing to the disorder’s impact on physical development and supporting its classification within congenital disorders with systemic manifestations [2]. Accurate evaluation of growth in GMPPA-CDG may have been limited in this retrospective review, as several reports lacked comprehensive assessment of systemic involvement in this disease.

Achalasia by itself is a rare motility disorder due to the absence of esophageal peristalsis and impaired relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter. It manifested with refractory gastric reflux, recurrent vomiting, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, and failure to thrive [11]. The achalasia diagnosis can be missed or delayed in some patients [7]. An underlying genetic diagnosis should be considered in congenital achalasia, especially that there are so far only three genetic disorders linked to it, including Achalasia-addisonianism-alacrimia syndrome (AAAS), Moyamoya 6 with achalasia (GUCY1A1) and GMPPA-CDG. Achalasia management approach is per the standards of care highlighted in [11].

Congenital alacrima is characterized by absence or hyposecretion of tears due to agenesis or improper development of lacrimal gland ducts and associated structures [12]. There are thirteen different genetic syndromes associated with congenital alacrima, including GMPPA-CDG [12]. Topical lubricants and artificial tears are the main therapies used to reduce the risk of complications like severe scarring, perforation, or blindness [13]. There are surgical options that have shown some benefit [14]. In GMPPA-CDG, the lack of tearing is linked to autonomic nervous system dysfunction and secondary inflammation. N-acetyl Glucosamine monosaccharide (GlcNAc) has an anti-inflammatory function and has been used in certain autoimmune diseases [15]. Currently, a clinical trial is investigating the effects of oral GlcNAc on tear production in patients with N-glycanase 1 deficiency (NGLY1-CDDG), which is associated with alacrima [https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05402345]. Based on our hypothesis that elevated GlcNAc levels may have a potential benefit in the mannosylation phenotype observed in GMPPA-CDG by reducing inflammation, we have administered oral GlcNAc supplementation to three of our patients. Following treatment, these patients demonstrated reduced corneal inflammation upon eye examination, decreased reliance on artificial tears, and no occurrences of eye infections. Further evaluation is required to determine the effect of this treatment.

Koehler et al. suggested that mannose-depleted diet might offer therapeutic benefits for GMPPA-CDG, given the elevated GDP-mannose levels observed in GMPPA-deficient cells and the role of GMPPA in regulating GMPPB activity [2]. Supporting this, experimental data from GMPPA-deficient mice demonstrated that a mannose-restricted diet normalized the serum mannose levels and skeletal muscle morphology, and improved hyperglycosylation, preventing neuronal degeneration and motor skill loss [1]. Some experts recommended limiting dietary intake of mannose-rich foods in GMPPA-CDG patients. However, further research is necessary to evaluate the clinical efficacy of this approach.

While hyperkeratosis, anodontia, and hypohydrosis were not present in all cases, these findings in several GMPPA-CDG cases suggest potential ectodermal involvement in GMPPA-CDG [2,6].

Unlike other CDGs, none of the reported GMPPA-CDG patients had an abnormal transferrin, or other glycoproteins. Normal CDT pattern could be related to the indirect impact of the GMPPA defect on the overall N-glycosylation pathway, and not a specific step that CDT testing focuses on. Utilizing tools like N-glycomics or N-glycoproteomics studies might give a better understanding of the impacts of GMPPA defects. A precise evaluation of glycoproteins in GMPPA-CDG may have been limited in this retrospective review.

In this review, we identified alacrima, achalasia, impaired growth, intellectual disability, and developmental delays as the most consistent features of GMPPA-CDG. Additionally, facial dysmorphism, hyperkeratosis, hypohidrosis, anodontia, and hearing deficits were noted as variable but characteristic presentations of the disorder. Given its systemic effects on autonomic, neurodevelopmental, and physical growth pathways, these findings underscore the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to diagnosing and managing GMPPA-CDG. As a potential therapeutic intervention, we propose the use of oral GlcNAc (N-acetylglucosamine) supplements to address ocular manifestations of the disease. Currently, management of AAMR remains primarily symptomatic, adhering to established standards of care. Emerging therapeutic options, such as a mannose-depleted diet and GlcNAc supplementation, warrant further investigation to establish their safety and efficacy.
