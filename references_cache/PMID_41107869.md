# Tumour spectrum, distinguishing features and management recommendations for NTHL1-associated tumour syndrome: a systematic review.
**Authors:** Gao W, Liao C, Buchanan DD, Macrae F, de Voer RM
**Journal:** Hered Cancer Clin Pract (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13053-025-00323-w](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13053-025-00323-w)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND AIM: To systematically describe the genotypes and phenotypes of NTHL1-associated tumour syndrome (NATS) cases reported in the literature. METHODS: A systematic review of literature across Medline, Embase and Web of Science was carried out by two independent reviewers. Studies reporting individuals with germline biallelic NTHL1 likely pathogenic/pathogenic variants (PVs) were identified and collected for statistical analysis. RESULTS: In total, 24 studies reported 77 individuals with germline biallelic NTHL1 PVs (51.9% female) from 54 families with 81.8% (63/77) of cases developing at least one cancer [median age at first cancer diagnosis 47 years; range 19–68 years]. The c.244 C > T, p.(Gln82*) PV occurred in 50/54 (92.6%) probands with 31 of these being homozygous and 19 having this PV in compound heterozygosity with another PV. Colorectal cancer occurred in 39/77; (50.6%), with the median age at diagnosis 50 years; range 31–73 years followed by breast cancer 25/77; (32.5%), with median age at diagnosis 47 years; range 36–68 years. Other phenotypes were central nervous system neoplasia, skin, gynecological, urothelial and hematological cancers. Colonic polyposis (≥ 10 polyps of any histology) was observed in 39/70 (55.7%) cases reported to have colonoscopy. In probands, colorectal cancer was the most common indication for genetic testing (38/54; 70.4%) followed by colorectal and breast cancer (8/54; 14.8%). The presence of COSMIC mutational signature SBS30 in a cancer genome may indicate the presence of biallelic NTHL1 deficiency. CONCLUSION: The prevalence of colorectal-, breast, endometrial cancers and meningiomas in this series highlights the importance of surveillance. Findings of this systematic review should inform guidelines for screening and diagnosis. COSMIC mutational signature 30 may assist in defining the tumour spectrum and assessing the pathogenicity of variants of unknown significance. Prospective studies on cancer development in NATS cases, with broader ascertainment, are recommended to further characterize of the cancer spectrum and penetrance.

Biallelic germline pathogenic variants (PVs) in the base excision repair DNA glycosylase gene NTHL1 underlie the autosomal recessive tumour syndrome NTHL1-associated tumour syndrome (NATS; OMIM: #616415). NTHL1 was first described as a novel polyposis and colorectal (CRC)-predisposition gene in 2015 by Weren et al. (2015) [1]. Soon after, a case with biallelic NTHL1 PVs and multiple cancers was reported [2]. Grolleman et al. (2019), reported a broader cancer phenotype beyond CRC and polyposis in 19 individuals with biallelic NTHL1 PVs, where the syndrome was referred to as NTHL1-associated tumour syndrome or NATS [3].

NATS is a rare cancer syndrome with an estimated occurrence in the European population of one in 114,770 individuals [4]. Currently, surveillance advice is in place only for CRC, breast cancer and in some centres, endometrial cancer. Differences in practices for endometrial cancer screening are noted, given the limited sensitivity and specificity of available screening techniques [5, 6]. However, recent literature suggests that individuals with NATS are also at increased risk of developing other extra-colonic cancers, including neoplasia of the nervous system and urinary tract cancers [1, 2, 6–9].


NTHL1 encodes a bifunctional DNA glycosylase that recognises and excises damaged nucleotides, specifically oxidised pyrimidines [5, 10]. As a result of biallelic inactivation of NTHL1 (NTHL1-deficiency), cancers present with C >T transitions at non-CpG sites in their genomes, which can be identified as a specific single base substitution (SBS) tumour mutational signature defined as SBS30 in COSMIC [11]. Grolleman et al. (2019) confirmed that extracolonic cancers in individuals with biallelic NTHL1 PVs demonstrate SBS30, linking NTHL1-deficiency to the development of CRC, breast cancer, endometrial cancers and meningiomas [3].

Here, we systematically reviewed the current literature on individuals with NATS, to provide a complete overview of all reported clinical characteristics, including associated (pre)malignancies and thus far reported benign tumours. This collation of phenotypes may inform appropriate cancer surveillance strategies to optimize the care for individuals with NATS.

The databases EMBASE, SCOPUS and Web of Science were searched on 10th March 2025. The key search terms were generated via online Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) generators with human troubleshooting and subsequently combined with Boolean operators. We used the following search terms: NTHL1, “NTHL1” or “Nth Like DNA Glycosylase 1”; genotype, “genotype*” or “genetic*” or “genes” or “genome” or “DNA”; phenotype, “phenotype*” or “syndrome*” or “tumour syndrome*” or “cancer*” or “symptom*” or “trait*” or “disease*”. All literature was subsequently reviewed following the PRISMA guidelines for new systematic reviews [12]. Two reviewers (WG and CL) independently screened all titles and abstracts. Only papers in English involving human subjects were included. The inclusion criteria for this review were restricted to literature that describes individuals with germline biallelic likely pathogenic or pathogenic variants (abbreviated as PVs) in NTHL1 with detailed information on gender, age at cancer and/or colonic polyposis diagnosis and clinical phenotypes. A broad range of studies was incorporated for detailed review including case reports, original studies, and conference abstracts.

A consensus approach was used in selecting full text for screening. Where there was disagreement, a third reviewer (FM or RDV) was consulted to make a final decision. Thereafter, full text screening was independently carried out by WG and CL. Any disagreements in data collection were resolved through consultation with FM and/or RDV. Moreover, we also performed a critical review of the references of each included study and added studies that were not identified through our initial literature search. Given the broad variety of studies, all included studies were scored using a modified JBI critical appraisal tool to evaluate the bias and quality of evidence in each study (Supplementary Table 1) [13]. A data extraction template was designed to collect data in a standardised way from included studies. The following characteristics were included in the data collection: Pathogenic variant and genotype (homozygous or compound heterozygous), protein change, gender, age of genetic testing, age at diagnosis for each reported condition, colonoscopy results, histopathology of polyps and clinical information for each subsequent extraintestinal neoplasm. The collected data was then analysed through descriptive statistics and one-way ANOVA for the age of cancer diagnosis to determine statistically significant differences by gender and between index cases and non-index cases, P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Furthermore, we sought whether each study used the standardised ACMG criteria to classify pathogenic variants. The authors of the study by Enaskula et al. [14]. were contacted to clarify homozygosity state, to request further genetic information on the reported variants and information on segregation analyses in the family.

In total, 565 studies were identified in the literature search, including 296 duplicate studies. We screened 269 reports and based on title and abstract reading, 264 studies were considered irrelevant and a further 19 studies were excluded based on full text screening. Two studies were added to the final list of relevant literature through citation screening of the included studies [15, 16]. In total, 24 studies were included in the full text extraction [1–3, 6, 8, 9, 14–31] (Fig. 1).

We identified 54 unique families and 77 individuals (37 males, 40 females) diagnosed with NATS (Supplementary Table 2). Overall, 24 males 24/37; 65.7%) and 30 females (30/40; 75%) were index cases. On average, 1.43 biallelic carriers [range 1–4] were identified per family. In total, 13 different PVs in NTHL1 were identified in the 77 individuals. All PVs reported to date were either nonsense, frameshift or splice site variants (Table 1). The most frequently observed PV was NTHL1 (NM_002528.7) c.244 C > T (p.Gln82*). Thirty out of 54 families (30/54; 55.6%) presented with NTHL1 c.244 C > T (p.Gln82*) in a homozygous state. A further 19 families (19/54; 35.2%) presented with NTHL1 c.244 C > T (p.Gln82*) in a compound heterozygous genotype with another PV. Four families presented with PVs other than NTHL1 c.244 C > T (p.Gln82*) (4/54; 7.4%; Table 1). While there is a predominance of NTHL1 c.244 C > T (p.Gln82*), the PVs are not restricted to one domain or region of the NTHL1 gene (Fig. 2).

In the total cohort, 18 types of malignancy and five types of premalignancy/benign tumours were reported in 63/77 (81.8%) cases [median age at first cancer diagnosis 47; range 19–68 years]. Fourteen (18.2%) biallelic PV individuals did not report malignant or pre-malignant tumours at the time of their publication. The median age of genetic testing was 58 years [range 24–79] for the 77 individuals with this information available. The median age of first cancer diagnosis was 47 years [range 19–68] in 63 cases. The various tumour types were split into nine categories: colorectal cancer (CRC)/polyposis, breast cancer, duodenal polyps/cancer, gynaecological cancers (ovarian, cervical, endometrial), central nervous system (CNS) tumours, dermatological cancers (squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), basal cell carcinoma (BCC), melanoma), urinary tract cancers, haematological cancers, and endocrine cancers (Table 2). We did not observe any statistically significant differences between the age of tumour onset between males and females, nor between index cases and non-index cases (P >.05). To inform understanding of ascertainment, we explored the reasons for genetic testing. For 49/77 (63.6%) biallelic PV individuals, genetic testing for NTHL1 PVs was indicated based on the presence of CRC and/or polyposis (Table 3).

For 71 biallelic PV cases (92.2%), at least one colonoscopy was reported with the median age of last colonoscopy being 54 years [range 33–79]. Cumulative counts of polyps were recorded incorporating multiple colonoscopies. For 64 out of 71 individuals (90.1%) at least one polyp was confirmed on colonoscopy. Forty-three individuals (43/71; 60.6%) were identified with adenomas, 17 individuals (17/71; 23.9%) reported hyperplastic polyps and for six individuals (6/71; 8.5%) serrated polyps were reported (Table 1). For two NATS cases, F48P1 (age 36 years) and F17P1 (age 37 years), neither polyps nor CRC were identified on colonoscopy. (Supplementary Table 2) [8, 15]. For four cases a gastroscopy identified polyps, with two of these individuals diagnosed with duodenal cancer (F8P1, F11P1, F18P1 and F53P1). Nine individuals (14.1%) had 100 or more cumulative polyps on colonoscopies reported between age 47 to 67 years (Supplementary Table 2). Overall, the distribution of the reported histopathology of colonic polyps indicates that most polyps were adenomatous (67.2%), followed by hyperplastic polyps (26.6%) and serrated polyps (9.4%) (Fig. 3).

In total, 39 out of 77 NATS cases (50.6%) were diagnosed with CRC. The median age at CRC diagnosis was 50 years [range 31–73 years]. The most common location of CRC was the rectosigmoid colon (9/39; 23.1%), and ascending colon and caecum (8/39;20.5%), followed by transverse and descending colon (3/39; 7.7%). CRC was diagnosed in 29 out of 54 index individuals (37.7%) and 10 out of 23 non index cases (43.5%). nine of the 39 (23.1%) individuals had more than one occurrence of a primary colorectal cancer, including five metachronous and four synchronous colorectal cancers. Four patients demonstrated duodenal polyps of which 2 were also diagnosed with duodenal cancer (one patient with synchronous finding of duodenal polyps and duodenal cancer (F53P1) and one patient with metachronous duodenal polyp and cancer (F8P1). In addition, two small intestinal desmoid tumours were also reported [19].

Breast cancer was diagnosed in 25 cases (25/77; 32.5%) of which 24 were females (24/50; 48%). Most breast cancer cases were NATS index cases (21/30; 70%). Four out of 23 non-index cases (17.4%) were diagnosed with breast cancer, including the one male with breast cancer. This male was diagnosed with an invasive ductal carcinoma grade 3 breast cancer at age 58 years (F18P1, Supplementary Table 2 with compound heterozygous NTHL1 c.391_392del, p.(Ser131Glnfs*30) and NTHL1 c.244 C >T, p.(Gln82*) variants [14]. The median age of first diagnosis of breast cancer was 47 years [range 36–68]. Nine out of 25 individuals (36%) presented with an invasive ductal carcinoma. One female index case (F30P1) presented with lobular breast carcinoma. Four index cases presented with bilateral breast cancer and two cases had unspecified triple negative breast cancer (F32P2 and F27P1, Supplementary Table 2).

Endometrial cancer was reported in eight biallelic PV cases (8/77; 10.4%), including two in non-index family members (2/10 non-index females; 20%). The median age at endometrial cancer diagnosis was 57 years [range 45 to 74 years]. A serous subtype was diagnosed in a woman at 53 (F30P1) and mixed endometrioid/mucinous subtype diagnosed at 57 in another woman (F32P2) [3]. Additionally, two index patients aged 58 (F42P1) and 74 (F54P1, Supplementary Table 2) were reported as a uterine carcinosarcoma malignant mixed Mullerian tumour of the uterus, which is a rare cancer [1, 6].

Two cases of ovarian cancer (mixed endometroid/mucinous subtype) was diagnosed at age 57 years in a non-index individual (F32P2) and at age 41 in index individual (F37P1) [3]. In addition, two cases of cervical cancer were reported (F29P4 and F30P1, Supplementary Table 2) [3].

In total, 16 cases of CNS neoplasia were reported in the total cohort (20.1%). Fourteen cases were meningiomas, of which ten were from index cases (10/54; 18.5%) and four from non-index cases (4/22; 20.8%). The median age at meningioma diagnosis was 47 years [range 37–69]. In addition, several other tumours in the nervous system were reported. One male index case was diagnosed with a multifocal dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumour at age 19 years (F19P1, Supplementary Table 2) [14]. A neuroma (site not specified), diagnosed at age 71, was reported in a female index patient with concurrent meningiomas diagnosed at ages 63 and 76 (F47P1, Supplementary Table 2) [8]. Furthermore, one case of an unspecified type of brain tumour was diagnosed in a non-index case at age 27 years (F29P3, Table 1) [3].

In the total cohort, 14 cases of non-melanoma skin cancer, including nine cases (9/77; 11.7%) of BCC and five cases (5/77; 6.5%) of SCC as well as two cases of melanoma were reported. The median age of non-melanoma skin cancer diagnosis was 44 years [range 24–63]. Among the non-index patients, five (5/23; 21.7% non-index) reported non-melanoma skin cancers including four BCC and one SCC. Two cases of melanoma were reported, one patient with scalp melanoma at 68 years (F10P1, Supplementary Table 2) [19] and another index case with metastatic melanoma at 41 years (F50P1, Supplementary Table 2) [8]. One female index case (F47P1, Supplementary Table 2) had unknown type of skin cancers reported at ages 40 and 45 [8].

Nine cases of urinary system malignancies were reported among the total cohort of 77 NATS cases (9/77; 11.7%), with a median age of diagnosis for urinary system malignancy at 60.5 years [range 47–66]. Three subtypes were described with locations and histology extracted wherever available. Urothelial cancer (UC) was the most common urinary cancer reported (n = 6, all index cases), followed by renal cell carcinoma (RCC; n = 2; one index and one non-index case) and prostate cancer (n = 1; index case, Supplementary Table 2).

Seven cases of haematological cancers were identified in the total cohort of 77 individuals with NATS with a median age of diagnosis at 62 years [range 33–76]. Three patients were diagnosed with non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, a female index diagnosed at age 74 (F39P1, Supplementary Table 2) [25], a non-index male diagnosed at age 70 (F54P2) [1] and a non-index female diagnosed at 65 years (F52P2) [1]. Two individuals were diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia (AML), one index female diagnosed at age 59 years (F25P1, Supplementary Table 2) and a male diagnosed at 62 years (F28P1) [3]. One index male was diagnosed with myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) at age 33 years (F29P1) [3] and chronic myelomonocytic leukemia was diagnosed in a female non index case at 76 years old (F47P1, Supplementary Table 2) [8].

One case of follicular thyroid cancer was diagnosed at age 70 years in a male index case who was homozygous for NTHL1 c.244 C >T, p.(Gln82*) (F22P1, Supplementary Table 2) [17].

The presence of single base substitution mutational signature 30 (SBS30) can be used as a genomic tumour biomarker of biallelic inactivation of NTHL1 (NTHL1 deficiency). Drost et al. showed that, by generating an NTHL1-deficient colorectal organoid, SBS30 can arise from cases with germline NTHL1 PVs [11]. In 2019, Grolleman et al. showed that NTHL1 deficiency underlies the main mutational process in most tumours (13/14 tumours analysed; 93%) that develop in individuals with NATS, which provided molecular evidence that deficiency of NTHL1 results in the development of tumours in various tissues [3]. Of the breast tumours in Grolleman et al., those tested for mutational signatures predominantly exhibited SBS30 (4/4 tumours, 100%); however, the number tested was small, and signature presence should not be conflated with cohort-level risk. Furthermore, mutational signature analyses can also be used to investigate if variants of unknown (VUS) significance cause NTHL1 deficiency, as was done in the recent study by Pinto et al. [6]. By sequencing of the CRC of F41P3 (Supplementary Table 1) they showed that this tumour presented with a high level of SBS30, suggesting that NTHL1 was fully deficient in this tumour. Together with the predicted ACMG pathogenicity and co-segregation of the NTHL1 VUS in the family, they concluded that this variant is likely pathogenic, providing the first evidence of an in-frame deletion to be involved in NATS. For two further single probands with an NTHL1 missense variant in trans with a known NTHL1 PV, mutational signature analysis suggested a deficiency of NTHL1, but unfortunately co-segregation analysis could not be performed, leaving these two NTHL1 missense variants as VUS but highly suspicious for pathogenicity based on the ACMG criteria [6].

Here, we analysed the genotypes and phenotypes of 77 individuals with NATS from 54 families, representing the largest overview of published NATS cases to date. This series highlights that NATS is an adult-onset cancer syndrome, with all 77 cases having the first diagnosis of a tumour above the age of 18 years. Cancers in colorectum, breast, endometrium and skin were the most frequently occurring. An interesting observation is the occurrence of meningiomas in a substantial number of NATS cases. No statistically significant differences were observed in age of onset of cancer amongst the index versus non-index cases or different sexes. However, in this series, we did observe that non-index NATS family members tend to present with cancer later in life, contrary to the usual pattern of earlier diagnosis in relatives due to early screening. Thus far, all but one, reported PVs in NTHL1 are protein truncating variants, suggesting that pathogenic NTHL1 missense variants are a rare cause of NATS [6]. Due to the rarity of the syndrome and the ascertainment bias in identification of NATS cases, proper cancer risk assessment beyond CRC remains challenging. However, next to surveillance for colorectal tumours, surveillance for breast cancer, endometrial cancer and meningiomas may be considered given the observed occurrences of these tumours in this cohort.

In total, thirteen different PVs in NTHL1 were identified. All but one PV were protein truncating and thus far, no pathogenic missense variants in NTHL1 have been reported. The truncating variant NTHL1 p.(Gln82*), located in the second exon, was the most commonly observed variant. The high frequency of NTHL1 p.(Gln82*) variant in NATS cases to date is likely not the result of a founder allele, as it is identified in NATS cases of different ancestries and reported in various populations in gnomAD [32]. To date, the majority of NATS probands are Caucasian, although it is not clear whether this is due to limited genetic testing outside Europe and North America, or if there is an intrinsic scarcity of NATS cases in non-Caucasian populations. As the NTHL1 p.(Gln82*) variant was identified in homozygosity in the first studies that report on NATS it could be that by only screening for the presence of this PV other cases are missed, especially, as in approximately half of the currently known NATS probands another PV than NTHL1 p.(Gln82*) was identified in homozygosity or compound-heterozygosity. This observation emphasizes the need to screen the full coding sequence of NTHL1 in diagnostics. Further research should be performed in other populations, especially in East Asia and Africa where no NATS families have been reported to date.

The presence of mutational signature SBS30 in a tumour genome may suggest deficiency of NTHL1 [3, 11]. However, as genomic sequencing of tumours is not standard of care in most countries, it is unlikely that the presence of SBS30 in a tumour genome will become one of the diagnostic testing criteria for NATS in the near future. Yet, analysis of tumour genomes may be informative to establish the full spectrum of tumours that develop as a result of NTHL1 deficiency, as mutational signature analysis is informative to establish the mutational processes that have contributed to the development of a certain (rare) cancer, as was previously shown by Grolleman et al. for NATS [3]. Recent data has shown that germline heterozygote carriers of NTHL1 PVs are not at increased risk to develop tumours, however, occasionally loss of heterozygosity of the wildtype allele occurs resulting in tumours with the SBS30 mutational signature [15, 33, 34]. The studies by Pinto using mutational signature analysis opens new approaches to determining pathogenicity of constitutional NTHL1 variants [6]. The presence of SBS30 in a tumour may contribute to understanding the functional effect of variants that are more challenging to classify including in-frame insertions and -deletions or missense variants.

Overall, the most common reported cancer in the 77 NATS cases was CRC, followed by breast cancer. However, in females with NATS, breast cancer was more frequently reported than CRC. These cancer frequency observations need to be considered in context of how the index cases were ascertained and selected for genetic testing. Compared to 70.4% of index cases who were tested because of prior CRC/polyposis, only 13% of non-index relatives underwent testing because of CRC or polyposis (Table 3). Data specific to unselected CRC cohorts shows extreme rarity; many universal CRC panel studies either did not include NTHL1 initially or identified no biallelic carriers. The largest matched tumor–normal pan-cancer series (n = 11,081) [15] detected 1 biallelic NTHL1 (0.009%)—a breast case, not CRC—underscoring low prevalence in unselected populations. Risk of ascertainment bias is considerable in reported cases of NATS and, therefore, hampers proper cancer risk assessment. Nevertheless, when considering only non-index NATS cases, the co-occurrence of colorectal and breast cancer in the current experience suggests this is higher than in the general population and also the age of onset is younger than expected in the general population [35, 36]. The presence of many other cancer types in NATS cases occurring likely more frequently and at a younger age than in the general population, confirms that NATS manifests as a multi-tumour syndrome with a heterogenous manifestation of cancer types. Clinicians need to be alert that the cancer spectrum extends beyond colorectal- and breast cancer, but that complete cancer risk assessment is still lacking. Multi-disciplinary risk management teams should also be based on family history for cancer and recent information in literature, leading to personal and family based guidance for surveillance for NATS cases.

The collective data from 77 NATS cases underline the intestinal manifestation of NATS being mostly an adenomatous polyposis syndrome. Serrated- and hyperplastic polyps were also reported, but these were in the minority. This mixed histopathology is comparable to what is observed in MUTYH-associated polyposis (MAP) [37]. However, phenotyping of the polyposis in the reported NATS cases is limited by a lack of information on the histopathological polyp type as well as the polyp burden and reporting of ages at colonoscopy and the location of polyps. Amongst the NATS cases with polyp counts reported at colonoscopy(n = 58), the median number of polyps was approximately 21 [range 1-200 polyps]. This number is likely an underestimate for life-time number of polyps, given the young and variable age group of NATS cases reported to date (the median age of last reported colonoscopy was 54 years [range 33–79 years]). However, this observation does suggest that the burden of colorectal polyp development is comparable to what is observed for MAP [38]. The distribution of cancers throughout the colorectum in NATS cases is similar to the location of CRC found in the general population and in the polyposis syndromes FAP and MAP: mostly rectosigmoid cancers, caecal and ascending colon cancers, followed by transverse and descending colon cancers [39, 40]. The current recommendation for colonoscopy screening for NATS cases is every two years starting 18–20 years [5]. The age range at which CRC was identified in NATS cases in this review was 31–79 years, supporting the recommended age of 18–20 years for the start of screening by colonoscopy being 10 years before the earliest observed CRC diagnosis. The current guideline on frequency of colonoscopy is based on risk of cancer, rather than any understanding of the biological speed of carcinogenesis [41], which is poorly understood for NATS and other syndromes.

Reports of gastroduodenoscopies are sparse in NATS cases and, therefore, potentially the polyp burden in the stomach and duodenum are underestimated. Only four NATS cases with duodenal polyps have been reported, including two who developed duodenal cancer [1, 19],. The lifetime risk of duodenal cancer and presence of duodenal polyps is elevated in FAP, but is reported less frequently for MAP [42, 43]. More reports on systematic gastrointestinal endoscopy in NATS cases are needed to define the incidence of adenomas and cancers in the duodenum.

Although the histology of the observed breast cancers was not provided for all cases, for those that did report histology the predominant subtype was invasive ductal carcinoma. Interestingly, one male with NATS was described to be diagnosed with invasive ductal carcinoma, although speculative, this may be suggestive of a possible elevated risk of breast cancer in both genders [14]. The range of ages of breast cancer diagnosis of NTHL1 cases aligns with the current recommendation for surveillance of breast cancer with either MRI from 30 to 60 years and mammography annually from age 40 to 75 years old [5]. To this extent the current breast screening guidelines seem to cover all ages of diagnoses of the breast cancers in the reported population. Given uncertain breast cancer penetrance in NTS and rarity in unselected cohorts, we do not recommend routine population-level MRI on the basis of NTHL1 alone. Instead, individualize breast screening by absolute risk modelling and family history; consider annual MRI only when lifetime breast risk exceeds standard high-risk thresholds or within research protocols. Non-detection of NTHL1 in large breast cohorts to date does not refute an association in confirmed biallelic NTS; it reflects rarity and the requirement for tumor-level confirmation. The proportion of triple negative breast cancer seems consistent with the 12–17% average proportion of triple negative breast cancer in the breast cancer population, but more data is needed to allow comparisons [44], as not all cancers were tested and/or reported for hormone status. Seven NATS cases were diagnosed with contralateral breast cancer and in most (92%) of these cases the second breast cancer developed years after treatment of the first breast cancer. While no evidence for prophylaxis for NATS associated breast cancers has been described, the development of contralateral breast cancer in a proportion of NATS cases may have implications for surveillance and/or treatment choices in the future. Furthermore, to our knowledge breast cancer is not a feature of MAP and hence, the presence of breast cancer may provide clinical guidance in discerning the NATS phenotype.

The third most common cancer identified in NATS cases is endometrial cancer. One-sixth of the female NATS cases developed endometrial cancer, while the estimated lifetime risk of developing endometrial cancer in the general population is 2.6%, suggesting a possible increased risk for endometrial cancers in NATS cases [45]. Endometrial cancers have also been associated with MAP, with a recent study reporting endometrial cancers in approximately 3% of the MAP population [38]. Only one ovarian cancer was identified in the current series of NATS cases in the literature. Most guidelines for gynaecological surveillance in cancer syndromes do not advocate for gynaecological surveillance due to low sensitivity and specificity of currently available techniques, together with the generally good prognosis of endometrial cancer. However, for centres that accept the limited sensitivity and specificity of available gynaecological screening options, surveillance of gynaecological cancer starting in postmenopausal women from age 45 years via transvaginal ultrasound and endometrial biopsy could be considered. For pre-menopausal women affected by NATS surveillance could be considered in case of abnormal uterine bleeding or exposure to unopposed estrogen in line with guidelines of the American Academy of Family Physicians [46].

The development of meningiomas is also likely increased in NATS cases compared to the general population. In line with meningiomas being a tumour mostly developing in females, about one-third of index and non-index NATS females were diagnosed with a meningioma. In the general population about 1% of individuals above the age of 45 years have a (asymptomatic) meningioma [47], suggesting an elevated risk for the development of meningiomas in individuals with NATS. We believe the ascertainment risk is small given most meningiomas present asymptomatically and only one case in our series was tested due to having meningioma. Although meningiomas are benign and the clinical benefit is to be determined, in line with the recommendation by Weatherill et al. and in the absence of appropriate risk estimates, brain and spinal MRI every two years beginning in the third decade of life could be considered for females given the prevalence of meningiomas in the current published series of NATS cases [8, 48].

Individuals with NATS may have a potential elevated risk to develop cancers in the urinary tract (8.5% of cases developed a urinary tract cancer), as has been described for other genetic tumour risk syndromes like Lynch syndrome (lifetime risk 4%) and MAP (6–8% lifetime risk in females and 6–25% lifetime risk in males) [49–52]. However, due to the limited number of cases and families with NATS, further research is needed before appropriate advice on surveillance for urinary tract cancers can be given.

A variety of dermatological tumour types (BCCs, SCCs and melanomas) were reported in individuals with NATS, but so far no clear difference in age of onset or prevalence of skin cancers compared to the general population is observed [53]. However, as for any sun exposed population, individuals with NATS should have regular skin checks.

We encountered several limitations in review of the current available genotypic and phenotypic information of the 77 NATS cases. First, follow-up time was limited or undisclosed in most of the reports thereby hampering estimates of cancer risk. Second, not all studies had uniform reporting of colonoscopies, biopsy details and further diagnostic information on reported cancers. We addressed this by contacting the authors of individual studies to confirm further variant information that were originally absent in data we retrieved from abstracts [14]. Several studies have provided updates on previous reported NATS index families where new diagnostic and treatment information has been obtained and this should be encouraged to help further characterise the phenotypic spectrum of this rare syndrome [3, 8, 15]. Third, ascertainment bias was high, especially with respect to CRC and/or polyposis. Up to two-third of NATS cases (49/77 cases; 64%) were referred to familial cancer clinics based on established diagnosis of CRC and/or polyposis. To account for this, we analysed data on relatives of NATS probands whose tumours were not subject to such ascertainment bias. However, the low number of biallelic NTHL1 PV carrier family members reported to date hampers risk estimate for individual cancer types.

In conclusion, in this systematic review we analysed the genotypes and phenotypes of 77 individuals with NATS from 54 families, representing the largest overview of published NATS cases to date. Due to the location of PVs across NTHL1, we recommend screening of at least the complete coding sequence of NTHL1 when testing for NATS. In the currently described cases of NATS, a high frequency of colorectal-, breast-, endometrial- and skin cancer, as well as meningiomas in both probands and their family members is observed. In line with current surveillance advice, screening for colorectal adenomas and cancers should be performed biennially, starting at the age of 18–20 years. Breast cancer screening should start at age 30 years. Furthermore, based on local guidelines or family information screening for other tumours, such as endometrial cancer and meningiomas may be considered. Prospective studies in cancer development in NATS cases are recommended to further characterise the spectrum and risk of cancers in individuals with NTHL1-associated tumour syndrome.
