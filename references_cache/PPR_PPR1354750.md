# Comprehensive review of the genotype-phenotype spectrum and treatment for biallelic insulin receptoropathy based on 259 affected people

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>  Background  Biallelic deleterious variants in the insulin receptor gene (  <italic>INSR</italic>  ) cause severe insulin resistance (IR), historically partitioned into Donohue syndrome, Rabson–Mendenhall syndrome and type A IR. Case literature is scattered, and clinical management rests on uncontrolled case series. Genotype–phenotype relationships, natural history, prognosis and optimal management are only partially established. Methods  We reviewed reports of biallelic INSR variants published over 40 years and supplemented these with cases from international referral centres, abstracting clinical, biochemical and genetic data from 259 affected individuals (226 unrelated probands; 210 distinct INSR variants). Variant burden was compared with the general population using gnomAD, and a Random Survival Forest-based prognostic model was developed, with cross-validation of feature importance. Results  Complete loss-of-function (KO) variants affected 33.3% of patients vs 12.6% in gnomAD (p = 2.5×10⁻¹²), with no domain clustering. All biallelic KO patients presented in the first month of life. Disease was multi-system, encompassing profound IR with fasting hypoglycaemia, hyperglycaemia without dyslipidaemia, pre-/postnatal growth failure with soft-tissue overgrowth, cholestatic liver disease, nephrocalcinosis, cardiac hypertrophy, ovarian enlargement, coagulopathy and colonic overgrowth. Seventy-two individuals (28%) died at a median 0.36 years, most commonly from sepsis; all KO patients were deceased or censored by 36 months. The prognostic model discriminated survival beyond 2 years, stratifying the cohort into tertiles with 2-year survivals of 89.8%, 83.3% and 39.5%. Biallelic KO variants and low birthweight standard deviation score were the strongest negative predictors, with male sex also adverse. Evidence guiding therapy is weak: insulin and metformin remain pragmatic mainstays, SGLT2 inhibitors show encouraging benefit, and rhIGF-1, used in a third of cases, yielded mixed outcomes. Conclusions  Biallelic insulin receptoropathy forms a clinical continuum. Routinely available presentation features permit data-driven prognostication, facilitating individualised risk assessment. Several stereotyped tissue-specific features remain poorly understood mechanistically. Strengthening of the therapeutic evidence base is urgently required.  </p>

INTRODUCTION
Insulin is essential for human life due to its pre-eminent role in transducing nutritional cues into metabolic responses. It exerts actions on nearly all tissuesby binding to a dimeric membrane-associated tyrosine kinase receptor encoded by the INSR gene [1]. Human disease-causing variants in INSR were identiﬁedin human severe insulin resistance (IR) in 1988 [2, 3], swiftly following description of the nucleic acid sequence of the INSR gene in 1985 [4, 5]. In ensuingyears a rush of mutation discovery led to rapid delineation of the genotype and phenotype spectra of INSR-related disorders, and the view summarised in theearly 1990s [6, 7] still applies today [8].
Human INSR-related severe IR encompasses autosomal recessive insulin receptoropathies caused by pairs of loss-of-function variants in any part of thereceptor, and monoallelic IR caused by heterozygous mutations [6, 7]. Variants causing monogenic INSR-related IR have most widely been viewed as lying inthe intracellular region of the receptor and exerting dominant negative action on co-expressed wild-type half receptors [9–13], however increasing evidencesuggests that heterozygosity for simple loss-of-function (LoF) mutations also causes severe IR, often in conjunction with stressors such as puberty(companion monoallelic manuscript already submitted to Genome Medicine).
The phenotypic spectrum of INSR defects is wide, ranging from neonatal extreme IR, growth retardation in utero and beyond, and death in the ﬁrst year of life,to insulin resistant diabetes or occasionally hypoglycaemia presenting in mid-life. The clinical nomenclature used to capture this spectrum is variablyapplied. It includes terms such as “leprechaunism”, coined in early clinical descriptions of the severe end of the spectrum [14], and later sometimes usedeven for adult patients with INSR mutations. This term is now largely eschewed due to lack of scientiﬁc utility and pejorative connotations in favour ofDonohue Syndrome, based on the same index clinical description of the lethal recessive syndrome [14]. Rabson Mendenhall syndrome, also based on earlydescription of a condition with intermediate severity identiﬁed in childhood [15], and Type A IR, denoting peri- or post-pubertal severe IR andhyperandrogenism in women [16], complete the spectrum. No settled terminology is used for post-pubertal men with INSR-related severe IR. We focus hereon biallelic insulin receptoropathy exclusively.
Current knowledge of the optimal treatment of people with recessive INSR defects is based on uncontrolled case reports and case series [17]. However, thewide range of genotypes and phenotypes, and the imprecisely delineated correlation between them, pose a particular problem when evaluating such reports.Speciﬁcally, uncertainty regarding the natural history of different biallelic variant combinations means that it is usually not possible to ascribe an observedrelatively good outcome with certainty to therapy. In some, and perhaps many, cases better outcomes may instead reﬂect a lesser degree of dysfunction ofsome variant combinations.

In this study we draw on the largest available number of cases with biallelic INSR defects, with two goals: ﬁrst, to offer the most comprehensive summary ofthe complex, multi-organ pathology of recessive receptoropathy to date, thereby extending understanding of the role of the INSR in normal humandevelopment and physiology. Second, to use machine learning to improve prognostic stratiﬁcation of patients with biallelic INSR variants, based on clinical,biochemical and genetic features. This stratiﬁcation may guide future therapeutic studies.
METHODS
Ascertainment, ethics
Ascertainment and ethical approvals are described in detail in (companion monoallelic manuscript already submitted to Genome Medicine). In brief, wesearched PubMed with terms including “monogenic insulin resistance”, “Type A insulin resistance”, “Donohue Syndrome”, “Rabson Mendenhall Syndrome”,“leprechaunism”, “insulin receptor mutation”, or “INSR” and “insulin resistance”, and selected all case reports, case series and trials as well as relevantreviews, before secondary review of cited reports not captured by the primary search. We augmented published probands with unpublished patients withbiallelic predicted pathogenic and/or loss-of-function (LoF) INSR variants identiﬁed through genetic testing at referral centres in Cambridge, Paris, the NIH,Exeter, Rome, or Chennai. Reports were grouped into three eras demarcated by therapy licensing landmarks: before 1999, 2000–2012, and 2013 to present,as described in (companion monoallelic manuscript already submitted to Genome Medicine).
Data curation/cleaning
Data curation and cleaning methods are described in (companion monoallelic manuscript already submitted to Genome Medicine). In brief, amino acidnumbering followed the INSR MANE transcript NM_000208.4 (B isoform). Population frequencies were from gnomAD [18] (accessed July 2026). NMDprobability was assessed using NMDetective [19], splice-site variants using SpliceAI [20], and variant deleteriousness using CADD v1.7 [21, 22]. As CADD isknown to under-score indels [23], the artefactually low CADD score for the c.591delC frameshift variant was replaced with 30. Variants were classiﬁed ashigh-conﬁdence complete LoF if they were: (1) nonsense or frameshift variants predicted to undergo NMD or lying in the EC domain; (2) essential splice-sitevariants without predicted alternative splice site unmasking by SpliceAI; or (3) deletions of one or more EC exons causing frameshift. EC missense variantswere additionally annotated with results from a massively multiplexed assay of variant effect [24]. Patients with biallelic conﬁdent complete LoF variantswere annotated as functional INSR knockouts (KO).
Allele-speciﬁc CADD scores were summed for biallelic combinations to generate a combined CADD score. For unscorable variants meeting LoF criteria, thehighest observed CADD score (52.0) was assigned. We derived a uniﬁed experimental functional score from our recently reported MAVEs [24], using thecomposite sum of cell surface expression, insulin binding, and maximal signalling. Gaps in the EC missense score matrix were imputed using MAVE-Polish(https://mavepolish.onrender.com/); variants robustly predicted to cause LoF received the lowest experimental score (-3.1); and IC missense variants (at orbeyond residue 974) received − 4.6 to account for dominant negative effects [24]. Scores for each allele were summed to generate a uniﬁed function scoreper biallelic combination.
Birthweight SDS were computed using INTERGROWTH-21st [25] and BMI SDS using CDC reference data [26], without accounting for ethnicity. Organ-speciﬁcclinical features and treatment use were recorded dichotomously, and longitudinal biochemical measures as single values. Full genetic and clinical data areaggregated in Additional File 1.
Analysis and statistical methods
In variant distribution analyses, individuals were the counting unit. For IC/EC distribution and mutation type frequencies, the denominator was all 259probands; for gnomAD comparisons, only probands with CADD-scoreable or imputable variants. A CADD PHRED > = 16 threshold was applied to gnomADvariants. IC (407 aa) vs EC (975 aa) variant density ratios were compared using a two-sample Poisson rate ratio test (conditional exact binomial, Clopper-Pearson 95% CI) as described previously (companion monoallelic manuscript already submitted to Genome Medicine). Variant-type proportions and IC:ECratio-of-ratios between cohort and gnomAD were compared using Fisher's exact test with Bonferroni correction; sensitivity analysis across CADD thresholds15–30.
Categorical comparisons used Fisher's exact test, continuous variables Mann-Whitney test, SDS comparisons the one sample Wilcoxon signed rank test, andtime-to-mortality Mantel-Cox log-rank testing, using GraphPad Prism 11.0.0.
Random Survival Forest Analysis
A clinical prognostic model was built from quality-controlled, collated data. Expanded details of methods are in Additional File 2. More brieﬂy, censoring timewas age at last contact; survival time was time from presentation to death or censoring. Candidate predictors, selected for clinical plausibility and datacompleteness, included summed CADD scores and the uniﬁed variant function score. Imputation was used to avoid selection bias: probabilistic recodingmapped a feature-speciﬁc proportion of "unknown" values to "No" according to clinical expert priors that unreported ﬁndings likely indicate true absence, thenremaining unknowns were stochastically imputed from within-fold observed distributions. Continuous missing values were median-imputed within eachtraining partition and categorical predictors one-hot encoded with infrequent-category handling; predictors with > 50% missingness were excluded.
Page 5/25

Continuous candidate predictors were gestational age, birthweight SDS, summed CADD scores, and summed experimental function scores [24]. Categoricalpredictors included reporting era, sex, mutation group, diabetes, cardiac/liver abnormalities, coagulopathy, nephromegaly, hypoglycaemia, nephrocalcinosis,and hypothyroidism. Volatile biochemical indices often documented under non-standardised conditions such as plasma insulin concentrations were omitted.Neurocognitive impairment and frequent infections were excluded to avoid survival bias, as these require signiﬁcant follow up to manifest, meaning theirinclusion as baseline predictors would artiﬁcially inﬂate survival estimates.
A Random Survival Forest was trained by dividing the group into ﬁve subgroups or “folds”. This enabled cross-validation, with each fold withheld in turn as anindependent test set. Calibration was veriﬁed by comparing mean predicted versus Kaplan–Meier-observed 2-year survival across the ﬁve subgroups, usingpooled out-of-fold predictions to prevent optimistic bias. Patients were stratiﬁed into Low-, Medium-, and High-risk tertiles. Discrimination was assessed byHarrell's and Uno's C-index, 2-year accuracy by time-dependent AUC and Brier score, and calibration by the Integrated Brier Score (0.1-2.0 years) andpredicted versus Kaplan-Meier-observed survival across subgroups. Feature importance was estimated by permutation (30 repeats per fold). Analyses usedPython (pandas, NumPy, scikit-survival, scikit-learn, lifelines, matplotlib, seaborn).
Large Language Model Use
Large Language Models were used only for verifying internal consistency of data presented, and to aid text editing to reduce word count.
RESULTS
General characteristics of population studied
Data from 259 affected people were accessible, including 226 probands and 33 relatives. There was a rise in rate of ascertainment since roll out of nextgeneration sequencing from 2009 (Figure 1A). There was an equal distribution of males (n=117) and females (n=129), with 13 people of undocumented sex.Sixty-four patients were reported from UK centres, 40 from the US, 26 from France, 21 from Japan, 15 from Italy, 13 from Saudi Arabia, 11 from China, and 10or fewer from other countries. Ethnicity was inconsistently documented. Most patients presented in the neonatal period or infancy, however, there was a tail
extending to 30 years. Median [25th,75th centile] age at last follow up was in infancy for patients with biallelic complete LoF variants (designated “KO”) (0.30[0.17,0.33], maximum 1.25 years) and ranged from infancy through late adulthood for non-KO patients (9.5 [1.2,17.0], maximum 62 years).
Genetic architecture of biallelic insulin receptoropathy
210 different INSR variants were identiﬁed among 226 unrelated probands, 118 homozygous and 108 compound heterozygous (Figure 1B). Counting singleprobands from independent families only, the most common variants were S350L (16 occurrences in 9 probands (e.g. [27-32])); P220L (14 occurrences in 7probands); I146M (11 occurrences in 6 probands, all with ancestry traced to Somalia or Yemen (e.g. [33-35])); T937M (9 occurrences in 7 probands [36-40]);and R141W (6 occurrences in 3 probands, all in the Gulf states [41, 42]). 158 variants were seen in only a single proband. Including non-unique variants, therewere 297 missense, 54 nonsense, 26 frameshift, 25 essential splice-site and 6 other splice-site variants, 11 in-frame deletions, 25 large deletions orduplications, 4 indels, 1 insertion and 1 lost stop codon (Figure 2A).
The biallelic cohort was signiﬁcantly enriched for complete LoF variants relative to gnomAD (33.3% vs 12.6%, OR = 3.47, p = 2.5x10-12), unlike in monoallelicINSR-related IR, where LoF variants were depleted (companion monoallelic manuscript already submitted to Genome Medicine). Also strikingly unlikemonoallelic disease, which features 14-fold enrichment in IC missense variants, neither missense nor LoF variants in biallelic disease were differentiallydistributed between IC and EC regions: domain-length-normalised IC:EC density ratios (DR) showed no signiﬁcant difference: for missense variants, cohortDR was 0.85 (95% CI 0.56-1.26) vs gnomAD 1.14 (1.01-1.27); for LoF variants, cohort DR was 1.25 (0.72-2.12) vs gnomAD 0.86 (0.61-1.18). Both remainednon-signiﬁcant across all CADD thresholds. This proportionate distribution is consistent with absence of a position-speciﬁc dominant-negative mechanism,unlike monoallelic disease.
Homozygous variants allow in vivo comparison of variant deleteriousness unclouded by uncertainty inherent in functional predictions or limited in vitrofunctional assays. We thus plotted birthweight SDS (a robust and stable readout), against age at last assessment (the most clinically important endpoint) forall homozygous LoF and missense variants or in-frame indels (Figure 2B,C). Homozygous LoF variants were generally associated with very low birthweightSDS and early death. Three splice-site variants were exceptions; two were associated with higher birthweight and prolonged survival, and one with a very lowbirthweight SDS but surprisingly long survival. One of these variants affects a non-essential splice site, with some residual normal splicing predicted, whileone of the essential splice-site variants is predicted to induce an in-frame exon deletion. These variants can thus plausibly be regarded ashypomorphic rather than true LoF. The third variant is conﬁdently predicted to confer complete LoF, but, notably, that patient was treated with recombinanthuman IGF-1 (rhIGF-1) from the age of 42 months (Additional File 1). Missense variants and in-frame indels showed a much wider range of birthweights andsurvival; around 40% had birthweight SDS above -2.5, early demise was rare, and survival beyond 10 years was common, with most individuals still alive whenlast evaluated. Most recurring variants (P220L, I146M, R141W, S350L) fell in this group.
Presenting features
All individuals with functional INSR KO presented in the ﬁrst month of life, while median age at presentation among individuals with other allele combinationswas ~3 months (Figure 3A). Median age at last follow up (whether to death or last review) among KO individuals was less than 4 months (range: neonatal toalmost 3 years), compared to 10.0 years (range: neonatal to 60 years) in non-KO individuals (Figure 3B). Presenting features are shown in Table 1. In theentire cohort, the most common presenting features were growth failure (44.3%), dysmorphic features (34.7%), hyperglycaemia (30.6%), acanthosis nigricansor laboratory evidence of hyperinsulinaemia (22.8%), signs or symptoms of hyperandrogenism (13.1% of females), and hypoglycaemia (12.3%). KO patients

were more likely than others to present with growth failure (81 vs 40.4%, p = 0.0004) and/or dysmorphic features (67 vs 32%, p=0.0028), with no difference inpercentages reported with hyperglycaemia, hypoglycaemia, hyperinsulinaemia, or hyperandrogenism at presentation.
Metabolic phenotype
Frequencies of clinical features observed are reported in Table 2. Diabetes or hyperglycaemia was documented in >90% of individuals; this usually presentedin the ﬁrst decade of life, but onset ranged from birth to adulthood. Hypoglycaemia was described in 103 individuals (72%) and was frequently neonatal, withblood glucose nadirs as low as 0.7 mmol/L. It was predominantly fasting, though postprandial/postabsorptive hypoglycaemia was also described. It wasasymptomatic in some cases despite very low glucose concentrations (1.1–1.6 mmol/L), consistent with chronic adaptation. Several patients showedprogression from severe infantile hypoglycaemia towards diabetes. Many exhibited alternating hypo- and hyperglycaemia, or hypoglycaemia observed onlyafter insulin or rhIGF-1 initiation.
In longer-term survivors, diabetic microvascular complications occurred at young ages (Table 2). Eye complications were documented in 20 instances, withproliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) most common (n=7); blindness occurred in 5 patients (12-35 years). One patient developed PDR precipitated by sharpHbA1c improvement on rhIGF-1 [43]. Renal complications were documented in 30 instances, ranging from microalbuminuria to end-stage renal failure (at 24and 39 years), appearing from 13.5 years. Neuropathy was rarely reported.
Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) was documented in only 16 cases (Table 2), with ﬁrst episodes from 6 months [44] to 18 years, despite near-complete INSR lossin many. DKA recurred in at least 3 patients. Infection was a common trigger. Insulin requirements during DKA were extreme, up to 50,000 units/day [45]. DKAalso occurred in single individuals during growth hormone therapy [46] or SGLT2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) treatment (unpublished).
Median fasting insulin (Figure 3C) was extremely high (1729 [712, 3575] pmol/L) with normal median fasting glucose (4.7 [3.0, 9.3] mmol/L), althoughindividual values ranged from hypoglycaemia to severe hyperglycaemia (Figure 3D). Fasting insulin was higher in KO patients than others, despite lowerfasting glucose. Non-fasting or post challenge insulin was likewise strikingly elevated (3972 [1860, 6368] pmol/L, Figure 3E), with concomitantly elevatedglucose (13.6 [8.7, 19.4] mmol/L, Figure 3F); there were no differences between KO individuals and others, though documentation of differences betweenfasting and non-fasting values was sparse in many severely affected infants. Median HbA1c was elevated (77 [48, 107] mmol/mol) and did not differ bygenotype; however, only 2 KO patients had HbA1c reported (Figure 3G).
Lipid abnormalities, including dyslipidaemia and Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) (Table 2) were extremely rare, sharply atodds with monogenic lipodystrophic forms of severe IR [47]. Median serum triglyceride concentration was 0.9 [0.7, 1.2] mmol/L; only 5 patients (14.7%) hadtriglycerides >1.7 mmol/L, with the highest 5.9 mmol/L, documented transiently on days 3-4 of life [48]. Median HDL-cholesterol (HDL-C) was 1.6 [1.3, 1.9]mmol/L; only 4 patients (12.9%) had HDL-C <1.0 mmol/L, and two of these also had mild hypertriglyceridaemia (Additional File 3). Increased serum LDL-cholesterol was noted in 3 patients.
Growth
Birthweight SDS (Figure 3H) was available in 136 patients (52%). Median [25th, 75th centile] birthweight was -2.8 [-3.3, -1.8], signiﬁcantly lower than thepopulation average (p<0.0001). Birthweight SDS was lower in KO patients than non-KO patients (-3.6 [-4.2, -2.9] vs -2.7 [-3.3, -1.7]; p=0.0002). Impairedpostnatal growth (Table 2) was present in all KO patients, with postnatal weights usually 5-7 SD below the mean. 74.3% of non-KO patients also showedreduced postnatal growth, though a wide range of height outcomes was seen. Many documented adult heights fell 2-4 SD below normal, although a minorityreached the normal range. 
Skeletal abnormalities included brachydactyly, scoliosis, rhizomelic limb shortening, rickets, and osteoporosis. At odds with linear growth impairment, avariety of features reminiscent of excess GH/IGF1 action were also seen, including prognathism, macroglossia, and other features of pseudoacromegaly.Body composition was also commonly reported as abnormal. Reduced adipose tissue and/or muscle mass were commented on in the majority (71%) ofpatients, including all but one KO patient, but in those surviving to older ages a range of different patterns of body composition were seen. Several reportsdescribe relatively preserved or frankly increased adiposity in the lower limbs ([49, 50], follow up of patient IV-7 from [34], two further unpublished cases)(Additional File 1). 
Organ/system-speciﬁc phenotypic features
Reproductive: Hyperplasia of sex-hormone dependent tissues including genitalia and nipples was noted in 73% of the cohort, including all KO patients (Table2). A wide range of age at pubertal onset and trajectories were reported. Four individuals reportedly showed precocious puberty, in two of whom breastdevelopment was observed at 3-4 years old, despite prepubertal hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal activity. Frankly delayed puberty and/or hypogonadotrophichypogonadism was reported in 4 individuals, and delayed menarche or primary amenorrhoea after normal onset of puberty in 11. Among 8 individuals inwhom age at menarche was reported, the median age was delayed at 15.5 [14.3, 16.0] years. Postpubertally, severe hirsutism was frequent, sometimessuﬃcient to warrant ovariectomy.
Enlarged, polycystic ovaries were reported in 90% of females (Table 2), often detectable neonatally. Two patients had ovarian tumours: one a 1.2 kg juvenilegranulosa cell tumour (JGCT) with desmoplastic implants after rhIGF-1 [51], another bilateral JGCT treated by ovariectomy at 7 weeks, leading to post-operative death [52-54]. Mechanical complications from enlarged ovaries included respiratory compromise [55] and colonic compression [56]. Ovariantorsion occurred in 2 cases. Oophorectomy was undertaken in at least 7 cases. Tumours also occurred in oestrogen-responsive tissues: two endometrialcancers (at 24 and 28 years old) and one breast cancer at 24 years [57]. In males, the only reproductive complication beyond infantile macrogenitalia was atesticular seminoma at 23 years [57].

Most had ventricular or septal hypertrophy (n=28), with 13 havinghypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, usually diagnosed neonatally and occasionally antenatally [65, 66]. Hepatobiliary: Liver disease was reported in 56 patients (55%), more prevalent in KO than non-KO (89 vs 52%, p=0.04, Table 2). A cholestatic pattern wasmost common (29 individuals), with bilirubin as high as 262 mmol/L. One liver was described as 'almost black' at laparotomy at 7 weeks [68] and others asgreen at autopsy. Autopsy ﬁndings included cholestasis with bile duct casts [60], canalicular cholestasis with portal/periportal ﬁbrosis [65], cholangiolarproliferation (follow up from [69]), bile thrombi in pericentral sinusoids [70], and hepatitis [71]. Colonic hypertrophy and/orrectal prolapse occurred in 12 patients, including two with diffuse colonic polyposis and benign histology [73], [33], and one who had a single polyp (follow upfrom [55]). Three had colonic dilation [74, 75], one also with pancreatic exocrine insuﬃciency [74].

Skin infections, including candidiasis and hidradenitis suppurativa, were reported in 9 patients, with ill-deﬁned, recurrent psoriasiformlesions in 2 of them [64, 77].

Those with frequent infections had higherHbA1c compared to those who did not (98 [80, 131] vs 50 [34, 80], p=0.007). Enamel hypoplasia and poorly calciﬁed canines werereported in several cases.

Longevity and causes of death
Seventy-two patients (28%) died, at median age 0.36 [0.25 - 1.36] years. Causes of death (39 reported) included infection or sepsis (64%) andcardiorespiratory failure (41%). Deaths were more frequent in KO than non-KO individuals (73 vs 24%, p<0.0001). Kaplan-Meier analysis (Figure 4A) showedall KO patients deceased or censored by 36 months (p<0.0001). Inspection of other mutational subgroups revealed no obvious differences in outcome,though an approximately similar subset of individuals in each group died in the ﬁrst 2 years, as for the KO patients. Random survival forest analysis
Given limitations of prognostication, we set out to evaluate predictors of outcome at presentation using Random Survival Forest (RSF) machine learning, anapproach that makes no prior assumptions about which variables matter or how they relate to outcome. The primary outcome evaluated was the time frompresentation to death or censoring, which was taken as survival time. Candidate prognostic variables were selected based on clinical plausibility, datacompleteness, and robustness of the reported phenotype or measure. A categorical imputation strategy ﬁlled in undocumented categorical phenotypes,drawing on clinical judgement of the meaning of lack of documentation, as detailed in Additional File 2.
The RSF model was applied to 155 individuals, among whom 51 died, while 104 were censored during follow up, indicating a well-balanced sample forforecasting. A cross-validation protocol was used wherein the dataset was split into ﬁve distinct subgroups, with each group maintaining the sameproportion of deaths. Each group was withheld in turn to serve as an independent test set, while the remaining groups were pooled as the training set. Foreach independent test set, the RSF model generated a prognostic risk score, with higher scores indicating worse predicted outcomes. A target prognostichorizon was set at 2.0 years following presentation, given preliminary survival analysis (Figure 4A) and the suggested survival cut off of 2 years as adiscriminator of Donohue Syndrome. 
The RSF model demonstrated high accuracy in discriminating clinical trajectories. The pooled out-of-fold Harrell’s concordance index was 0.79, while incross-validation, the model demonstrated solid prognostic stability, yielding a mean Harrell’s C-index of 0.80 (SD = 0.08; 0.68 - 0.90 across folds). Whenaccounting for censoring patterns during follow up, the mean Uno's C-index remained strong at 0.72 (SD = 0.16). Short-term prognostic accuracy at 2 yearspost-presentation was evaluated using a dynamic Area Under the Curve (AUC), yielding a mean value of 0.84 (SD = 0.11) and a maximum of 0.97 acrossfolds, indicating reliable capacity to predict absolute mortality during the early post-presentation phase. Overall calibration was excellent, with a mean 2-yearBrier score of 0.14 (SD = 0.01; range 0.13-0.15). Further performance metrics, their meaning and interpretation are summarised in Additional File 2.
Dividing prognostic risk scores into tertiles segregated the cohort into three distinct clinical survival proﬁles (Figure 4B,C). The Low-risk group (n = 52; meanbaseline risk score 2.78) experienced only 6 deaths, maintaining an observed Kaplan-Meier 2-year survival probability of 89.8%. All 3 of these 6 low riskindividuals for whom causes of death were documented died of fulminant infections. The Medium-risk cohort (n = 51; mean baseline risk score 6.88)experienced 11 deaths, giving a 2-year survival probability of 83.3%. In contrast, the High-risk cohort (n = 52; mean baseline risk score 15.11) demonstratedpoor prognosis, with 34 deaths and a 2-year survival probability of only 39.5%.
Shuﬄing input variables identiﬁed key drivers of mortality among the candidate predictors (Figure 4D). KO versus non-KO status was the single mostpowerful predictor, in keeping with prior Kaplan Meier analysis (Figure 4A), with a mean permutation importance score of 0.049 (SD 0.034). This wasfollowed by birthweight SDS (0.032 (SD 0.026), early-onset coagulopathy (0.026 (SD 0.021)), sum of CADD scores (0.01 (SD 0.011)), sex (0.013 (SD 0.012)),and liver pathology (0.012 (SD 0.005)), and the reporting era (0.011 (SD 0.01)). The experimentally determined Uniﬁed Function Score, baselinehypoglycaemia, and thyroid dysfunction provided negligible or negative predictive value.
In view of several severe documented ovarian complications, we expected that the sex disadvantage would lie with females, however the opposite provedtrue: sex-based out-of-fold survival analysis showed male infants to have more than double the raw mortality rate of females (45.3% [34/75] vs 21.3%[17/80]). Moreover, the low-risk group from the RSF was heavily female-skewed (44 females vs 8 males; 85% female), with the high-risk group reciprocallymale-dominated (36 males vs 16 females; 69% male). 
Treatment
Among 259 patients, 142 had reports of medication use for diabetes, IR, and/or hypoglycaemia. For the 117 patients with no reported medication use, it wasnot possible to determine if medications were not used or not reported. Among the 142 with known medication use, 44% used a single drug, 25% used twodrugs, 15% used three drugs, 9% used 4 drugs, and 7% used 5 or more drugs. The most commonly reported drug classes were insulin (N=92; 65%), metformin(N=82; 58%), rhIGF-1 (N=49; 34.5%), SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i; N=18; 13%), and thiazolidinediones (N=17; 12%). Fewer than 10% used incretin agonists,metreleptin, or other classes. 5% of patients received medical or surgical therapy for hyperinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia, including diazoxide (5 cases (e.g. [34,82]; one later undergoing subtotal pancreatectomy on two occasions [34]) and octreotide (1 case [72]). 
Medication use followed expected temporal patterns (Figure 5), with SGLT2i use increasing since 2013 (17% in that period). Insulin doses ranged from high(1.5 units/kg/day) to extreme (>750 units/kg/day; maximum 48,000 units/day). Dietary measures, including continuous enteral feeding for fastinghypoglycaemia and carbohydrate restriction for postprandial hyperglycaemia, were documented in 39 individuals (25%).
Most classes of common anti-diabetic drugs had little or no rigorous documentation of eﬃcacy, with the limited evidence presented often confounded bycombination therapy. The most promising preliminary evidence relates to SGLT2i, whose mechanism of glucose lowering is INSR-independent. SGLT2i usewas reported in 18 individuals. Among 12 cases where comment on eﬃcacy was offered, glycaemic beneﬁt was reported in 10 (e.g. [44, 62, 83, 84]), with 2discontinuations for polyuria and urinary tract infection [85] or diabetic ketoacidosis (in the context of temporary lack of access to adequate insulin supply).Ketonaemia was reported stable in several reports when tracked on initiation of therapy [44, 62].  Reported effects on urinary calcium excretion wereinconsistent, with both increase and decrease described on treatment [62, 86].
Beyond conventional anti-diabetic therapy, several more specialised pharmacotherapies have been used. Most prominent is rhIGF-1, used since the early1990s [50]. rhIGF-1 was given to 34.5% of patients, sometimes combined with IGFBP3 [55, 87], or administered as continuous infusion [36, 51, 65, 67] in view

of evidence of short IGF-1 half-life in biallelic insulin receptoropathy [88]. Dosing varied from 50 to >1,000 mg/kg/day. Evidence supporting rhIGF-1 use hasbeen reviewed elsewhere [17, 77, 79, 89] and is of relatively low quality despite its frequent use. Outcomes were mixed: clear beneﬁt in 7, partial or temporarybeneﬁt in 11, non-response in 4, and 6 discontinuations for adverse events. Illustrative responders showed marked improvements in acanthosis nigricans,insulin, and HbA1c [78, 90]. Adverse events reported during rhIGF-1 therapy include DKA and endometrial carcinoma [91], worsening or development ofcardiomyopathy (n³4), hypoglycaemia [77], infection, accelerated microvascular complications on abrupt fall in HbA1c [43], airway-compromising tonsillarhypertrophy [36], papillary thyroid cancer (follow-up of [55]) and ovarian enlargement, torsion or tumour (e.g. [51]). Importantly, however, all thesecomplications also occurred in multiple, reportedly rhIGF-1-naïve patients, so attribution to therapy is not established from these data, and indeedcardiomyopathy also reportedly improved during continuous infusion of rhIGF-1 in at least 2 individuals [65, 67].
Another specialised therapy reported was metreleptin, reported in 11 individuals. Modest HbA1c improvement but also non-responses were noted [77, 92],and in a phenotype characterised by leanness the weight loss induced may be undesirable; some patients discontinued metreleptin for this reason [77]. Otheradjunctive or exploratory therapies reported included growth hormone with or without concomitant rhIGF-1, which appeared to worsen glycaemia,ursodeoxycholic acid for cholestasis, propranolol for cardiac hypertrophy, and antihypertensives. Suppressive doses of levothyroxine for thyroid carcinomaled to a dramatic improvement in diabetes control with discontinuation of high-dose insulin in one patient [93], but a subsequent small clinical trial ofliothyronine did not demonstrate beneﬁt [94].
DISCUSSION
This study assembles the largest group of individuals with biallelic INSR-related IR to date, including 226 unrelated probands carrying 210 distinct INSRvariants, unifying and extending 40 years of reports. We clarify the mutational spectrum, offer the deﬁnitive description of the associated multi-systempathology, and use the scale of the aggregated outcome data to support data-driven prognostication. We also address management questions, includinghow to conceptualise the complex roles of IGF-1 in pathogenesis and therapy.
The mutational spectrum of biallelic INSR disease is broad and largely private, with even recurrent alleles — e.g. S350L, P220L, I146M, T937M, R141W —accounting for only a small minority of cases. This means that genotype-based prediction usually rests on the functional class of novel variants rather thanprior experience of speciﬁc alleles. Biallelic disease was strongly skewed towards severely hypomorphic or null alleles, distributed in proportion to IC and ECdomain length, as expected of a recessive LoF disorder. This is distinct from monoallelic INSR-related IR, which features enrichment for IC dominant negativemissense variants (companion monoallelic manuscript already submitted to Genome Medicine).
The complete “KO” genotype – which we deﬁne as the presence of biallelic convincing complete LoF alleles - deﬁnes the upper phenotypic severity bound ofthe disorder. KO individuals cluster at the lowest birthweight SDS and die in infancy, with no KO individual surviving beyond 36 months. In contrast, manyhomozygotes for missense variants with residual activity are born less growth-restricted and survive into second and third decades, with a continuum ofseverity seen.
The historical division of INSRopathy into Donohue and Rabson Mendenhall syndromes, and Type A IR was imprecise. Our RSF model shows that continuousrisk-based prognostication is feasible, with good discrimination and reasonable calibration. Stratiﬁcation into risk tertiles suggests potential correlates forthese eponymous labels. Before this is formalised, however, prospective data are needed. The model is probabilistic and provisional, trained on retrospectivedata with wide conﬁdence intervals, and will require external validation and reﬁnement as prospective data accrue. We propose making the RSF modelaccessible to clinicians for prognostic counselling and to allow adjustment for expected baseline severity when interpreting treatment effects.
The variables driving the RSF model reinforce that the strongest single predictor is KO status. Birthweight SDS ranked next, in keeping with foetal growthrestriction being an integrated readout of receptor function, followed by early-onset coagulopathy (likely a marker of severe hepatic involvement), summedCADD score as a continuous index of allelic severity, sex, and liver pathology. The uniﬁed experimental function score did not contribute to the variablesdriving the model, presumably outcompeted by the summed CADD score. This likely reﬂects that fact that the experimental score was only partiallyexperimental, imputing gaps in the extracellular domain, and making simplistic assumptions about intracellular missense mutation function scores.Rerunning the RSF process when a full set of truly experimental function scores will be of great interest.
Sex appears as a driver due to excess male mortality. This was a surprise given the severe, hormone-responsive ovarian pathology (enlargement, torsion,JGCT, oestrogen-responsive malignancy) seen in numerous affected females. However beyond insulin receptoropathy, male neonates have a generalmortality disadvantage of approximately 1.3:1 across virtually all neonatal conditions, including prematurity, sepsis, and undernutrition [95, 96]. They alsohave higher metabolic rates, faster growth velocity and smaller fat reserves than females [97]. This baseline metabolic and energetic difference may rendermale infants more susceptible to physiological decompensation in the face of defective INSR function. Another non-mutually-exclusive possibility is thatovary-derived oestrogen has a cytoprotective effect. Oestrogen preserves mitochondrial integrity during metabolic stress, localising to mitochondria andimproving skeletal muscle bioenergetics independent of ERα signalling [98]. It also extends survival in mice with cardiac-speciﬁc deletion of IRS1 and IRS2,and protects pancreatic β-cells from oxidative-stress-induced apoptosis via Erα. These speculative explanations require formal testing in future, however.
This series conﬁrms the extreme IR of biallelic INSRopathy, with early life fasting hypoglycaemia and postprandial hyperglycaemia evolving to sustainedhyperglycaemia and early microvascular complications. The conﬁrmed rarity of ketoacidosis, even in KO individuals and in the face of profoundhyperglycaemia, suggests that residual insulin-like action mediated by extreme hyperinsulinaemia is suﬃcient to restrain ketogenesis even when it cannotnormalise glucose. Ketoacidosis, when it does occur, tends to accompany intercurrent illness. Despite extreme IR, overt dyslipidaemia was rare and mild ortransitory, and MASLD was documented in only 2 patients. This is in sharp contrast to recessive lipodystrophic severe IR, in which IR is accompanied byhypertriglyceridaemia and near-universal MASLD, but consistent with ﬁndings in monoallelic defects in either the insulin receptor [99] or downstreamsignalling gene PIK3R1 [100].

Neurodevelopmental impairment, reported in around 30% of the cohort, was strongly associated with a history of neonatal hypoglycaemia. The pattern ofdeﬁcit and its timecourse was moreover consistent with observations of children with other causes of neonatal hypoglycaemia [101–103], which appears tobe the most parsimonious explanation. A contribution from disrupted brain insulin signalling cannot be excluded however, and indeed murine neuronal InsrKO has behavioural and metabolic consequences [104].
Susceptibility to infection is a common feature of the disorder, and sepsis was the single commonest contributor to death. Susceptibility is likelymultifactorial, with chronic hyperglycaemia, leptin deﬁciency [105, 106], undernutrition and possible defective immune cell insulin/IGF signalling [107, 108], alllikely interacting with stressors such as repeated instrumentation. The practical message is that infection is a leading and partly preventable cause of death,and that glycaemic control, nutritional support and scrupulous infection control are important.
Growth failure, both antenatal and postnatal, was the presenting feature in almost half the cohort, and was more extreme in KO individuals. Intrauterinegrowth restriction is consistent with insulin, acting through INSR, being a major foetal growth factor. Importantly, patients had co-existence of both lineargrowth impairment and pseudoacromegaloid overgrowth. Where growth is INSR-dependent (especially of bones, muscle and adipose tissue), loss of INSRcauses growth impairment; where growth can be driven through IGF1R by paracrine/autocrine IGF-1 or extreme hyperinsulinaemia, in contrast, high GH mayproduce local overgrowth. The admixed undergrowth/overgrowth phenotype may thus be a reﬂection of differential tissue-speciﬁc insulin/GH/IGF1Rcrosstalk.
The ovary is a ﬂorid example of this overgrowth, giving rise to major morbidity and mortality, including two ovarian torsions, two histologically conﬁrmedJGCT (one bilateral in a neonate), at least seven oophorectomies, and secondary oestrogen-responsive malignancies (two endometrial and one breastcarcinoma before 30 years). Marked ovarian enlargement occurs even in KO patients, arguing that much of the ovarian response is IGF1R-mediated. Ovarianstimulation requires intact gonadotrophins, as GnRH suppression abolishes extreme hyperandrogenaemia [109, 110]. Whether early GnRH agonist treatmentin severely affected female infants will mitigate ovarian enlargement is worthy of testing, although potential cytoprotective effects of ovarian oestrogen alsoneed be evaluated.
The range of other organ-speciﬁc pathology seen in biallelic insulin receptoropathy is well described in this study. This includes nephrocalcinosis andsometimes renal tubulopathy, cardiac hypertrophy and/or left to right shunts, and liver disease, which was present in around half of all patients with data,being far more common in KO individuals. The pattern of INSR-related liver pathology — intrahepatic canalicular involvement with inspissated bile and agrossly normal extrahepatic tree — was in keeping with a pattern documented in the index report in 1954 [14], which described inspissated canalicular bileplugs. Bile duct proliferation, and, in longer-surviving cases, portal and periportal ﬁbrosis are added to the recognised trajectory by the current report. Thisliver pathology phenocopies inherited bile-secretory cholestasis, best exempliﬁed by progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis [111]. This circumstantiallyimplicates a defect of bile-secretory or ductal machinery in pathogenesis of this characteristic feature of severe biallelic INSRopathy.
The cohort's two cases of diffuse colonic polyposis, together with the marked colonic hypertrophy and prolapse seen almost exclusively in KO individuals,invite a parallel with the epidemiological association between hyperinsulinaemia and colorectal neoplasia [112, 113]. The rare disease association suggeststhat hyperinsulinaemia is a trophic stimulus to the colonic epithelium, and that this may not be mediated by the INSR. A similar effect may be at play, milderbut sustained through adulthood, in common IR, potentially accelerating cancer development and/or growth.
Whether renal and cardiac features of human INSR KO or near KO reﬂect tissue-autonomous developmental and trophic roles of the INSR, the associatedseverely hyperinsulinaemic endocrine milieu, or grossly perturbed metabolic dialogue among metabolic organs, or a combination of these, is unknown. Therelevant tissue-speciﬁc Insr KOs in mice, which replicate the tissue autonomous signalling defect but not the systemic perturbation, do not replicate thehuman pathology [114–117].
Recommendations for clinical care of patients with recessive insulin receptoropathy are limited by low-quality evidence from case reports and series.Moreover, insuﬃcient knowledge of natural history makes distinguishing treatment beneﬁts or effects from spontaneous changes diﬃcult. Given theselimitations, all conclusions presented here regarding therapies should be taken as observations and not the product of controlled comparisons.
Insulin, sometimes at extreme doses, and metformin remain the backbone of glycaemic management, aimed at reducing hyperglycaemia and itsmicrovascular and infective consequences rather than at correcting the underlying resistance, which no available drug does. Of the newer options, SGLT2i aremechanistically attractive because they lower glucose by a route that is independent of the insulin receptor and so should retain eﬃcacy however severe thereceptor defect. The cohort experience, though small, is encouraging: beneﬁt was commented on in 10 of 12 treated individuals. These drugs promoteketogenesis, however, and could precipitate ketoacidosis in a population with limited insulin reserve (indeed one discontinuation followed DKA). Moreover,the renal and cardiovascular beneﬁts established for this class in type 2 diabetes cannot be assumed to transfer to a disorder with distinct renal and cardiacpathology. Nevertheless, on balance SGLT2i are the most promising insulin-independent pharmacological option at present and warrant systematic,prospective evaluation with careful ketone monitoring, rather than uncritical adoption.
Recombinant human IGF-1 is the treatment that most sharply exposes current incomplete understanding of recessive receptoropathy. It was used in a thirdof the cohort at widely varying doses, and outcomes were mixed. Thus, while rhIGF-1 is worth trialling, it is not reliably effective, and its beneﬁts, where theyoccur, are often incomplete and offset by possible harm. Reports of tumour growth (including a JGCT) and of worsening cardiac hypertrophy (fourindividuals on rhIGF-1) are consistent with agonism at IGF1R, the receptor that plausibly mediates several of the disease's proliferative complications,although these could also reﬂect the natural history of insulin receptoropathy. A recent series supports the notion that rhIGF-1 beneﬁt is limited at the mostsevere end of the spectrum [77], though on the other hand the two “KO” patients who survived longest both received infusion of rhIGF-1.
The overarching therapeutic message of the current work is that the ﬁeld has reached the limit of what aggregated case reports can tell it. Progress nowrequires prospective, multicentre data collection with predeﬁned outcomes, ideally through an international registry and consensus core outcome set, so that
Page 11/25

the effect of interventions can be judged against the disorder's variable natural history. The prognostic framework developed is one step towards that end,permitting adjustment for expected severity when comparing treated and untreated patients.
Important limitations apply to this study. It is a retrospective aggregation of published and unpublished cases spanning many decades and centres, withattendant ascertainment and publication bias towards the most severe and instructive cases. Phenotyping is inconsistent across sources and eras, assaysdiffer, and ethnicity is unevenly recorded. Outcome analysis is affected by censoring and by improvements in supportive care, which the reporting-era term inthe prognostic model only partly captures. Functional consequences were largely inferred from variant class and in silico prediction, though the KO deﬁnitionis stringent.
CONCLUSIONS
This series deﬁnes, at unprecedented scale, the clinical and genetic spectrum of biallelic insulin-receptor disease. The mutational burden is spread evenlyacross the receptor and in homozygotes forms a graded allelic series with complete KO as the upper severity bound. It is a multi-system disease: beyondprofound IR, hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia without dyslipidaemia and steatosis, it features severe pre- and postnatal growth failure admixed withwidespread soft tissue overgrowth, as well as cholestatic liver disease, renal tubulopathy and nephrocalcinosis, cardiac hypertrophy, ovarian enlargementand colonic overgrowth, with early ovarian or oestrogen-responsive cancers reported. No current agent corrects the receptor defect; insulin and metforminremain the pragmatic mainstay of therapy, with SGLT2 inhibitors an emerging promising insulin-independent option. rhIGF-1 is worth trialling but is ofuncertain risk and beneﬁt balance. We present a new prognostic model showing that severity can be quantiﬁed from routinely available features, offering apath away from eponymous categories towards individualised, mechanism-anchored risk assessment. The priorities now are prospective, registry-baseddata with predeﬁned outcomes, external validation of the prognostic framework, so that counselling and evaluation of therapies can rest on ﬁrmer ground,and investigation of the basis of increased male vulnerability, and preclinical investigation of novel advanced therapies.
