# Melanotic Neuroectodermal Tumor of Infancy: Clinicopathological Evaluation of a 10-Year Consecutive Case Series from a Tertiary Cancer Center.
**Authors:** Sharma D, Qureshi S, Khanna N, Manjali J, Laskar S, Baheti A, Patil V, Panjwani P, Ramadwar M
**Journal:** Head Neck Pathol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s12105-025-01789-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12105-025-01789-9)

## Content

PURPOSE: Melanotic Neuroectodermal Tumor of Infancy (MNTI) is a rare, locally aggressive tumor with distinct pathological features and treatment paradigms commonly occurring in the head and neck region. Microscopically, it consists of a biphasic population of small neuroblast-like cells and larger melanin-containing epithelioid cells. The main purpose of this study is to characterize clinicopathological and immunohistochemical features of MNTI at a single institution and discuss challenges in the differential diagnosis.

METHODS: We performed a retrospective analysis of MNTI cases diagnosed at our center during a 10-year period and discussed the differential diagnoses.

RESULTS: Eleven MNTI cases were identified. Median patient age was 5 months. Male to Female ratio was 1.75:1. Tumor distribution was in the Maxilla (n = 8), Mandible (n = 1) greater wing of Sphenoid (n = 1), and Temporal bone (n = 1). All tumors revealed classic biphasic morphology in the resection specimens. By immunohistochemistry, 9/9 (100%) cases were positive for both AE1/AE3 and HMB45 in the larger epithelioid cells and 6/6 (100%) were positive for Synaptophysin in the smaller neuroblast-like cells. One patient had unique nested areas composed of mature glial tissue. One patient who had incomplete resection was given adjuvant radiotherapy. One patient developed a solitary ipsilateral lymph nodal metastasis. Follow-up period ranged from 1 to 93 months. All the patients were alive with no evidence of disease at the last follow-up (median: 16 months).

CONCLUSIONS: Lack of consideration of MNTI in the differential diagnosis can lead to misdiagnosis and undue exposure to cytotoxic therapies. Awareness of the classic biphasic morphology and distinct immunoprofile of MNTI is essential.

Melanotic Neuroectodermal Tumor of Infancy (MNTI) is a rare, locally aggressive tumor consisting of a characteristic biphasic population of small neuroblast-like cells and larger melanin-containing epithelioid cells [1]. It is recognized now as a locally aggressive and rarely metastasizing entity [1–2]. Most cases of MNTI described to date have been reported in the head and neck region of infants where they can mimic a wide range of tumors [3]. We performed a retrospective analysis of the clinicopathological and immunohistochemical features of MNTI from 11 patients and herein discuss the differential diagnoses that allow for improved identification of this entity.

The objective of our study is to project clinical, histopathological, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) characterization of MNTI cases from a single tertiary care center in a decade. Discussion on the differential diagnoses, management, and molecular updates of MNTI ensues.

We identified 11 patients diagnosed with MNTI from the surgical pathology archives of the department of pathology covering a 10-year period (2013–2022). We gathered the available clinical and demographic information of the cases, including treatment and follow-up details from the electronic medical records system of our hospital.

The following clinical parameters were recorded: patient’s age, sex, clinical presentation, site, radiological details, treatment, outcome, and follow-up details.

Following morphological features were recorded: tumor size, tumor border/infiltration into bony tissue, tumor architecture/pattern, type of tumor cells, stroma, mitotic activity per 2 mm2, mucosal ulceration, necrosis, perineurial invasion, lymphovascular space invasion, and status of surgical cut margins. In all cases, pathology reports and all available Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) stained sections and immunohistochemistry results were reviewed.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed, after antigen retrieval, and the following antibodies were used: AE1/AE3 (clone AE1/AE3; 1:300 dilution; Biocare Medical, Concord, CA, USA), HMB45 (clone HMB45; 1:300 dilution; Dako, Glostrup, Denmark), Synaptophysin (clone MRQ-40; 1:250 dilution; Cell Marque, Rocklin, CA, USA), NeuN (clone A60; 1:800; Millipore), S-100 (clone 4C4.9; 1:1000 dilution; Cell Marque), Desmin (clone D33; 1:400 dilution, Dako), MyoD1 (clone EP212; 1:100 dilution; Cell Marque), MIC2 (clone 12E7; 1:200 dilution, Dako), FLI1 (clone MRQ-1; 1:100 dilution; Cell Marque), Chromogranin A (clone DAK-A3; 1:250; Dako), CD56 (clone BC56CO4; 1:400 dilution; Cell Marque), GFAP (clone 6F2; 1:400; Dako), LCA (clones 2B11 and PD7/26; 1:300 Dilution; Dako) and Ki67 (clone MIB-1, 1:400 dilution, Dako). For all cases, IHC was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions with appropriate positive and negative controls.

For photomicrographs, H&E and IHC slides were scanned using a 3DHistech Pannoramic MIDI II slide scanner (3DHistech, Budapest, Hungary), and images were captured via screenshots. Data analyses were performed by Microsoft Excel software v16.44 (Microsoft, Washington, USA).

Demographic, clinical characteristics, treatment details, and follow-up of all 11 patients are summarized in Table 1. The MNTI cohort consisted of 7 male and 4 female patients (ratio 1.75:1), with ages ranging from 2 to 12 months (median; 5 months, mean; 5.6 months). All 11 tumors arose in the bones of the head and neck region; out of which 8 arose in the maxilla, 1 in the mandible, 1 in the sphenoid bone, and 1 in the temporal bone. All patients presented with painless swelling, gradually increasing in size, in an otherwise healthy infant.

All 11 patients underwent a biopsy of the tumor; two also had fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) performed elsewhere. Nine of the 11 patients were referred from other hospitals, 8 of which were referred with diagnoses that included MNTI (n = 4), metastatic poorly differentiated neuroblastoma (n = 1), primitive neuroectodermal tumor (PNET) (n = 1), small round cell tumor (n = 1) and malignant spindle cell tumor (on FNAC) (n = 1). Thus, misdiagnosis or non-specific diagnosis was made in 4/8 cases (50%).

Eight patients had their definitive surgery performed at our institution. Two of these 8 patients had been operated elsewhere earlier with incomplete excision. Parents of one patient refused surgical treatment of the child despite adequate counseling while one patient was referred only for histopathology consultation. Complete surgical resection was achieved in 8 out of 9 patients. One patient out of these 9 (case 7) had a grossly incomplete resection wherein, the tumor was seen extending medially into the cavernous sinus and overlying the petrous segment of the carotid artery.

Microscopically, infiltrative borders were noted in all 9 cases. Extensive permeation into the bone was present in all cases (case 2; Fig. 2b). All resected tumors showed a characteristic biphasic cell population consisting of smaller round neuroblast-like cells and larger epithelioid melanin-containing cells (Fig. 2c). Stroma was fibrotic and cellularity was variable for all the cases. Mitotic activity was infrequent, with all cases having ≤ 1 mitosis per 2 mm2. Focal mucosal ulceration was noted in one case (case 6). Necrosis, perineurial invasion, and lymphovascular space invasion were not present in any of the cases. Mucosal or bony cut margins were free of tumors in 8 of 9 patients. Lymph node metastasis was noted in one of the patients (case 11; Fig. 2d).

One case revealed an unusual finding in the form of prominent and distinct nested areas composed of mature glial tissue consisting of admixed glioneuronal cells within a neurofibrillary matrix (case 10; Fig. 2e, f). These nests were seen dissecting the otherwise typical neuroblast-like and epithelioid areas. Ganglion cells were not identified in this case. None of the other 10 cases displayed any glial differentiation.

IHC studies were performed as a part of the diagnostic workup of 10 out of 11 cases and are summarized in Table 2. One case was diagnosed as MNTI purely based on morphology (case 7). Other MNTI cases had a distinct immunophenotype. 9 out of 9 (100%) of MNTI were positive for AE1/AE3 in the larger epithelioid cells (Fig. 3a). 9 out of 9 (100%) cases were positive for HMB45 in the larger epithelioid cells (Fig. 3b). Both of these markers were negative in the smaller neuroblast-like cells. Synaptophysin was positive in the smaller neuroblast-like cells in 6 out of 6 (100%) cases (Fig. 3c) with two cases out of these displaying weak to moderate positivity (cases 8 and 10). Ki67 IHC was performed in only 5 cases and ranged from < 1–20% (Fig. 3d). GFAP and NeuN were performed for case 10 and both were positive in the glioneuronal components respectively (GFAP; Fig. 3e). Additionally, weak positivity for synaptophysin was noted in the neurofibrillary matrix (Fig. 3f). Expression of other IHC markers were as follows: MIC2 (1/4 cases, in a dot-like pattern, 25%), CD56 (2/2 cases, 100%), chromogranin A (1/2 cases, 50%), S-100 protein (1/8 cases, 12.5%), desmin (0/3 cases, 0%), LCA (0/3, 0%), NKX2-2 (0/2, 0%), FLI1 (0/2, 0%), and MyoD1 (0/2, 0%).

Adjuvant radiotherapy was given to the patient with incomplete resection (case 7) with a dose of 41.4 Gy over 23 cycles. Of 8 patients with available follow-up, no recurrences were seen and all were alive with no evidence of disease. One of these 8 patients (case 11) developed an ipsilateral neck swelling after 6 months of disease-free interval; selective neck dissection was performed and a single level 1B lymph nodal metastasis was noted (Fig. 2d). Time till the last follow-up ranged from 1 to 93 months (median: 16 months; mean: 28.9 months).

MNTI is characterized as a rare benign tumor with primitive morphology that behaves in an indolent yet locally aggressive manner [4]. However, the recent 5th edition of the WHO classification of pediatric tumors considers MNTI of the head and neck as a malignant entity [2]. More than 10 decades since the original description, approximately 500 cases of MNTI have been described in the literature [1]. However, this entity remains of indeterminate histogenesis despite advances in immunohistochemistry and molecular pathology. Clinically, MNTI presents as a painless, sessile growth with invariable blue or black pigmentation. A subset of patients present with elevated VMA [5].

We report our single institutional experience of clinicopathological and immunohistochemical analyses of 11 patients diagnosed with MNTI. Most of the MNTI cases in the literature have been published as case reports and there are only 5 studies that have reported 10 or more cases [6–10]. To our knowledge, this is the largest documented series of MNTI in the Head and Neck region from a single institution reported for a period of 10 years or less. The mean age in our cohort was 5.6 months. A sequential increase in age portending a better prognosis has been suggested [11–12]. Concordant with previous reports, our series also showed a male predilection and maxillary preponderance [11, 13].

Histologically, the tumor shows a classical biphasic population of cells in a fibrous vascularized stroma composed of smaller round neuroblast-like cells and larger epithelioid melanin-containing cells [4]. Alveolar or nesting architecture is usually noted, however, sheeting and pseudo-glandular patterns are also seen. Infiltrating growth patterns with bony destruction is also commonly noted.

Typical biphasic morphology and usual immunohistochemistry profile (AE1/AE3 and HMB45 positivity in larger epithelioid melanin-containing cells; synaptophysin positivity in smaller neuroblast-like cells) are sufficient for the diagnosis of MNTI in a given clinical context. More than half of the cases in our series were misdiagnosed before they were referred to our center highlighting the requisite awareness of this entity and knowledge of differential diagnoses.

Differential diagnoses of MNTI include tumors with embryonal ‘small round’ cell morphology, biphasic phenotype, and tumors with melanocytic differentiation. Neuroblastoma (especially metastatic) can be confused with MNTI when only the small round component predominates and the melanotic, epithelioid cell component is inconspicuous. Neuroblastoma typically shows characteristic neuropil and rosette formation (unless dealing with an undifferentiated variant) and can rarely show pigmentation [14–15].

We experienced diagnostic difficulty in one of our cases which had an unusual feature of the presence of extensive glial differentiation with glioneuronal nests in addition to neuroblast-like and melanin-containing epithelioid cells. (Fig. 3c). This deviation from typical morphology has been rarely reported in the literature [16–17]. This finding may be projected as an exception and a potential source of incorrect diagnosis. Raised levels of urinary VMA seen in some patients can be a further confounding factor in an infant wherein the biopsy from a tumor involving facial bones and showing primitive round cells can lead to a misdiagnosis of metastatic neuroblastoma.

Undifferentiated round cell sarcoma prototype Ewing sarcoma usually shows nests and sheets of small round cells typically with cytoplasmic vacuolations and focal to extensive necrosis. Diffuse membranous MIC2 positivity along with FLI1 and NKX2-2 positivity is the characteristic IHC profile for Ewing sarcoma. Desmoplastic small round cell tumor (DSRCT) is known to occur rarely in the head and neck bones, however, is vanishingly rare in infants [18]. DSRCT shows a multi-phenotypic immunoprofile and expresses epithelial, muscular, and neural markers that are distinctive to DSRCT along with EWSR1::WT1 fusions [19–20]. Whilst RMS can easily be diagnosed with the help of positive immunohistochemistry for desmin, myoD1, and myogenin, it should be noted that cases of MNTI with myogenic differentiation have also been reported [6, 21–22].

Tumors with melanotic differentiation include mucosal melanoma and clear cell sarcoma of soft tissue, both of which are exceeding rare in infancy and do not share morphologic similarities with MNTI [23–26]. Teratomas containing ectodermal retinal pigment epithelium layer predominance could also be a rare differential diagnosis [27–28]. However, extensive sampling would generally reveal structures from other germ cell layers as well. Interestingly, two case reports have been published wherein MNTI was reported as a component of the Teratoma [29–30].

Non-Hodgkin lymphomas of the head and neck are fairly common with incidence ranging from 11 to 33% amongst extra-nodal lymphomas [31]. Burkitt’s lymphomas and lymphoblastic lymphomas can be morphologic differential diagnoses of neuroblast-like cell predominant MNTI. Positivity for lymphoid IHC markers and negativity for synaptophysin, AE1/AE3, and HMB45 would help to differentiate them from MNTI. Odontogenic tumors of children rarely occur in infancy, however, pigmented variants of, and melanotic epithelial rests in odontogenic tumors need to be differentiated from MNTI in an appropriate clinical context [32].

The clinical behavior of MNTI is indolent despite being composed of ‘malignant small round cells’ which in other instances are a hallmark of aggressive clinical course, albeit in diverse tumor types. We wish to highlight the impact of incorrect diagnoses on patients with MNTI through the above discussion. Neuroblastoma, Ewing sarcoma, lymphoma, and other differential diagnoses as discussed above are treated with aggressive chemotherapy regimens combined with radiotherapy and morbid surgical resections whereas MNTI is treated with surgery alone in most of the patients.

Regarding the treatment of MNTI, complete surgical resection is considered a gold standard and incomplete resection has been purported to be one of the main reasons for frequent recurrences [3, 12] Very late recurrences have also been reported [33]. Lymph node metastases have been reported in only 1.35% of MNTI cases [3]. Malignant and metastatic MNTI constitute only 1.4–2.1% in the reported case series [12]. One of our patients developed a solitary regional lymph nodal metastasis. All 8 of our patients for whom follow-up details were available are alive with no evidence of disease. Adjuvant and neo-adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiation therapy have been utilized to treat certain patients with MNTI who are less likely to undergo complete surgical resection of the tumor [34–35].

For a better understanding of MNTI pathobiology, molecular characterization has been attempted, however, in very few cases. BRAFV600E mutation was seen in one out of 3 cases analyzed in a study by Gomez et al. in 2015 and BRAF testing was therefore suggested as a potential predictive biomarker for vemurafenib therapy [36]. Recently however, Xia et al. demonstrated that none of their 11 MNTI patients had any evidence of BRAFV600E mutation confirmed via Amplification-refractory mutation system-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ARMS-qPCR) and Sanger sequencing [10]. Another case report documented a novel RPLP1::C19MC fusion along with a germline mutation in the CDKN2A gene in a case of fibular MNTI [37]. Epigenetic evaluation of MNTI has also been performed. In a series of 7 MNTI cases, DNA methylation profiling demonstrated a similar methylation profile to Group 3 Medulloblastoma suggesting a possibility of a common progenitor cranial neural crest cell [38]. A limitation of our study is that molecular characterization could not be performed due to budgetary constraints. Future studies incorporating detailed molecular elucidation could offer deeper insights into etiopathogenesis. Nevertheless, for resource-limited settings, accurate pathological diagnosis and complete surgical resection remain the mainstay of MNTI management.

MNTI, albeit a rare tumor, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of pediatric head and neck tumors. Identification of a biphasic population of larger melanin-containing epithelioid cells and smaller neuroblast-like cells along with a distinct immunohistochemical profile is helpful for accurate diagnosis. In conclusion, we show that the distinction of MNTI from other small round cell tumors is essential in challenging cases for avoiding misdiagnosis. Infiltrative growth, high recurrence rates, and even rarer metastatic instances demand early diagnosis, complete excision, and careful follow-up for MNTI cases.
