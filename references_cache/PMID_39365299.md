# First Brazilian Case Report of Unrelated Patients with Identical ISG15 Mutation.
**Authors:** Napoleao SMDS, Salgado RC, Ferreira JFS, de Barros Dorna M, de Moura TCL, França TT, Barreiros LA, Gomes LN, Condino-Neto A
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-024-01811-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-024-01811-9)

## Content

BACKGROUND: ISG15 deficiency is a mixed syndrome of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial infections (MSMD), a rare inherited condition characterized primarily by recurrent infections from low-virulence mycobacteria and monogenic type I interferonopathy.

OBJECTIVE: To characterize the laboratory and molecular features of two patients from different families affected by the same ISG15 variant.

METHODS: We began with clinical characterization and investigation, assessed IL-12/IFN-γ production, performed genetic characterization through WES and Sanger sequencing, conducted an in silico molecular analysis of the genetic ISG15 variant's protein impact, and utilized RNAseq for transcriptome analysis to understand pathway impacts on ISG15-deficient subjects from unrelated families.

RESULTS: A mutation in the ISG15 gene was identified, affecting two patients treated in different hospitals and cities in Brazil (Fortaleza and Sao Paulo), who are also members of unrelated families. Both patients showed low IFN-γ production when stimulated with BCG or BCG + IL-12. ISG15 deficiency presented with two distinct clinical phenotypes: infectious and neurological. It was identified that both patients are homozygous for the variant (c.83 T > A). Furthermore, it was observed that the mutant protein p.L28Q results in an unstable protein with increased flexibility (ΔΔG: -2.400 kcal/mol). Transcriptome analysis revealed 1321 differentially expressed genes, with significant upregulation in interferon pathways, showing higher expression in patients compared to controls.

CONCLUSION: This study describes the first reported cases in Brazil of two unrelated patients with the same ISG15 mutation c.83 T > A, exhibiting infectious features such as mycobacterial infections and systemic candidiasis, neurological findings, and skin lesions, without adverse reactions to the BCG vaccine.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS: Reporting ISG15 gene mutations in Brazilian patients enhances understanding of genetic susceptibilities, guiding effective diagnostics and treatment. Identifying high-risk individuals aids clinical practices, genetic counseling, and influences public health policies. We have identified the first case in Brazil of the same ISG15 variant c.83 T > A that was identified in two unrelated patients with distinct clinical phenotypes, infectious and neurological.

Introduction
Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial infections 
(MSMD) is a rare inherited condition characterized primar-
ily by recurrent infections from low-virulence mycobacteria, 
such as the attenuated vaccine component of Mycobacte-
rium bovis, bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), and environ-
mental mycobacteria. These infections occur in apparently 
healthy patients without evident immunological abnormali-
ties who have an effective immune response against other 
microorganisms [1]. Patients with MSMD are susceptible to 
developing atypical or extrapulmonary tuberculosis caused 
by either M. tuberculosis or other non-tuberculous myco-
bacteria (NTM), as well as other infections from intracellu-
lar pathogens such as Salmonella spp., and mucocutaneous 
and fungal infections including Candida spp., Paracoccidi-
oides spp., and Histoplasma spp. [1 –3].
Different inborn errors of immunity (IEI) can lead to the 
early, disseminated, and persistent onset of life-threatening 
mycobacterial infections seen in MSMD. Therefore, the dif-
ferential diagnosis between MSMD and other IEIs is cru-
cial [1, 4, 5]. The mildest form of MSMD may have a late 
onset, be well-delimited, present milder clinical manifesta-
tions, and show apparent improvement as the individual 
ages [1]. Epidemiologically, MSMD affects approximately 
1 in 50,000 individuals, with one of the main clinical signs 
being local (BCGitis) or disseminated (BCGosis) adverse 
reactions to the BCG vaccine [6 , 7]. In countries such as 
Brazil, BCG vaccination is part of the established pediatric 
vaccination calendar and is provided by the public health 
system. To date, 21 genes have been identified (IFNG, 
IFNGR1, IFNGR2, STAT1, JAK1, IRF8, SPPL2A, IL12B, 
IL12RB1, IL12RB2, IL23R, ISG15, TYK2, RORC, CYBB, 
NEMO, TBX21, ZNFX1, USP18 andIRF1) that character -
ize 35 different genetic forms of MSMD [6 , 8–14], The 
majority of these genes are associated with autosomal 
recessive (AR) inheritance, but they can also exhibit auto-
somal dominant (AD) or X-linked (XR) inheritance [ 4, 
8]. Furthermore, these genetic forms can be classified as 
partial (P) or complete (C) functional deficiency, including 
deficiency in protein production and mechanism underlying 
protein dysfunction [1 , 6, 15].
Type I interferons (IFN-Is) play a crucial role in protec-
tive immunity against viral infections. However, an over -
abundance or prolonged IFN-I signaling can be pathogenic, 
leading to the development of type I interferonopathies [8]. 
ISG15 is a ubiquitin-like molecule induced by IFN-Is that 
functions both as a soluble protein and a protein modifier 
[8, 15, 16]. Soluble ISG15 can induce IFN-γ and stabi-
lize ubiquitin-specific peptidase 18 (USP18) in humans, 
thereby acting as a negative regulator of IFN signaling [8 , 
10, 15, 16].
To date, ISG15 deficiency has presented with two dis-
tinct clinical phenotypes: infectious and neurologic. ISG15 
deficiency is a mixed syndrome of MSMD and monogenic 
type I interferonopathy. Initial reports indicated that MSMD 
was the primary phenotype in a family from Turkey and 
a family from Iran with ISG15 deficiencies [17]. The lack 
of extracellular secreted ISG15, which normally engages 
the LFA-1 receptor on T and natural killer (NK) cells [18] 
leads to a hypomorphic induction of IFN-γ, similar to other 
genetic deficiencies that result in a complete or partial loss 
of response to or production of IFN-γ [17].Conversely, in 
a family from China with complete ISG15 deficiency, the 
primary clinical presentation included intermittent seizures 
due to intracranial calcifications [19].
Genetic research is currently a valuable tool for the accu-
rate diagnosis of patients with inborn errors of immunity and 
has a significant impact on the effective therapeutic manage-
ment of these patients. Our paper presents a case report of 
patients, for the first time in Brazil, from unrelated families 
with the same mutation (c.83 T > A) in ISG15 gene.
Methods
Patients’ Family and Clinical History
Both patients, P1 and P2, are female and born to consan-
guineous parents (Fig. 1A). P1, born in 2008, was the fourth 
pregnancy of her mother and has three male siblings, one 
passed away at six months due to seizures and congenital 
heart disease. P2, born in 2018, was the third child in her 
family. Her older siblings, a six-year-old female, and a thir-
teen-year-old male, are healthy with no recurrent infections 
reported. Both patients were fully vaccinated according to 
the Brazilian Ministry of Health’s early childhood vacci-
nation schedules facilitated by the Unified Health System 
(SUS). Neither P1 nor P2 experienced adverse reactions to 
the BCG vaccine.
Patient P1’s initial medical challenges began within the 
first six months of life, presenting with seizure episodes 
and cardio-respiratory failure, requiring a ten-day stay in 
the intensive care unit (ICU). During infancy, she suffered 
from various episodes of otitis and sinusitis, as well as 
cases of pneumonia, some of which were treated empiri-
cally as fungal in origin. By the age of three, arthral-
gia was evident, and by eight years old, she experienced 
tonsillitis and a urinary tract infection. At ten years old, 
P1 was diagnosed with cervical and axillary granulomas 
exhibiting caseous necrosis, with isolates suggestive of 
Histoplasma spp. The patient also presented with vulvo-
vaginitis and dermatophytosis, along with a case of tuber -
culous lymphadenitis without mycobacterial isolation but

with a positive PPD, treated empirically for MAC. Further 
complications included neurological issues such as epi-

the presence of small intracranial calcifications (Fig.  1B).
Patient P2 first presented with medical symptoms at 
two months of age, including fever and cervical lymphad-

She developed encephalitis characterized by convulsive

men was initially unresponsive; only when the regimen 
was expanded to include ethambutol after genetic test 
results (suggesting Mycobacterium bovis ) did she start to 
respond. Her most recent medical reviews noted systemic 
candidiasis. P2 also developed chronic liver disease. She 
has hypogammaglobulinemia and underwent immuno-
globulin replacement therapy.
Both patients tested negative for HIV/EBV/CMV/HSV 
and re-testing of the viral panel was conducted during 
each hospitalization cycle.
Analysis of IL‑12 and IFN‑y Production
Peripheral blood was collected from patients and healthy 
individuals (controls) in a sodium heparin tube (BD Vacu-
tainer). The collected blood was diluted in RPMI 1640 cul-
ture medium (Sigma), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine 
serum in a 1:2 ratio. Then, 200µL of the diluted blood 
were placed in two round-bottom 96-well 3595 cell culture 
plates (Corning®), one for IL-12, and the other for IFN-y. 
In each plate the rows with the Control and patient wells 
were named, and along with the diluted blood the stimuli 
and cytokines were added. In the IL-12 plate, two stimulus 
groups of mycobacterial lysate (ML) and BCG were used. 
2µL of ML in duplicate and 4.5 µL of BCG were added, then 
2µL of the recombinant IFN-y cytokine (BD) was added. In 
the IFN-y plate, the two stimulus groups of mycobacterial 
lysate (ML) and BCG were also used. 10µL of LM were 
added in duplicate and 4.5 µL of BCG, then 4µL of recom-
binant human IL12p40 (BD) was added. The plates were 
stored in a cell culture oven at 37 °C in 5%CO2, after 24 h 
(IL-12 plate) and 48 h (IFN-y plate) the plates were centri-
fuged at 600 g conditions for 10 min at room temperature.

Supernatants were collected, stored at -20 °C. Quantification 
of the cytokines IFN-y and IL12p40 were performed by the

Genetic Investigation
Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from peripheral blood 
collected in an EDTA tube using the Wizard® Genomic 
DNA purification extraction kit (Promega) following the 
manufacturer's instructions. The samples were stored at -4ºC 
overnight. Sample concentration and quality were measured 
using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher 
Scientific) and gDNA integrity evaluated by electrophoresis 
in a 1.5% agarose gel (Sigma-Aldrich) stained with SYBR® 
Green-II stain RNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After quan-
tification, gDNA integrity was analyzed by gel electrophore-
sis with 1.5% agarose in Tris–Acetate-EDTA (TAE) buffer 
stained with SYBR® Green II RNA stain (ThermoFisher) 
and compared to a 1 Kb Ladder molecular weight standard. 
The samples were stored at -20ºC until the date of use.
Whole Exome Sequencing (WES)
Exome sequencing was performed from gDNA at the Insti-
tute of Bioscience at University of São Paulo (São Paulo, 
Brazil) and Invitrogen facility. An adapter-bound library 
was prepared with the TruSeq DNA Sample Preparation Kit 
(Illumina). Exome capture was performed with the Sure-
Select Human All Exon Kit (Agilent). Paired-end sequenc-
ing was performed with the Illumina HiSeq2000, enabling 
100 bp reads. For sequence alignment, variant calling and 
annotation, sequences were aligned to the human genome 
reference sequence (GRCh37 Build). Variants were pro-
cessed by genomic character analysis tools, such as GATK 
and SAMtools, and the variants were annotated and stored.
DNA Sequencing (Sanger)
Genes of interest were amplified by the Polymerase chain 
reaction (PCR) technique. For the PCR reaction a mix was 
prepared containing solution with Milli-Q water, Buffer 
(10x), dNTP (Deoxyribonucleotide Phosphatase), MgCl 
(250 mM), Taq-Polymerase (5U/µl), 1 µg of gDNA and the 
respective primers (foward/reverse). The PCR was processed 
in a SimpliAmp thermal cycler (ThermoFisher) with the fol-
lowing programming: The primers used for the ISG15 gene 
EXON 2 were Foward: CGG GAT GTA GAG GAC AGA CA, and 
Reverse: CGC TCA CTT GCT GCT TCA GG. The cycles started 
at an initial temperature of 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 
30 to 35 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, ringing temperature of 
60–62 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The 
final extension temperature was 72 °C for 5 min, ending with 
4º until the moment of removal from the equipment.
Mutant Protein Modeling
To predict the change in protein stability due to the mutation, 
in silico analysis was conducted using Dynamut software 
(http:// biosig. unime lb. edu. au/ dynam ut/) with the developer’s 
parameters.
Differential Expression Analysis of Bulk RNA‑seq 
and Data Visualization
To characterize the immunological signature from global 
transcriptional profiles in ISG15-deficient patients, RNA-
seq read counts were transformed into log2 counts per mil-
lion. Differential expression analysis was then performed 
using the EdgeR pipeline via the NetworkAnalyst 3.0 webt-
ool (https:// www. netwo rkana lyst. ca/). Genes were consid-
ered upregulated with a log2-fold change > 1 and down-
regulated with a log2-fold change <  − 1, using an adjusted 
p-value < 0.05 to identify significant differential expression 
between ISG15-deficient patients and controls.
Enrichment analysis was also conducted using the default 
parameters in NetworkAnalyst. Differentially expressed 
genes were utilized to identify enriched pathways terms via 
the Reactome database. Significantly enriched immunologi-
cal pathways were determined based on an p-value of less 
than 0.05. A hierarchical clustering heatmap was employed 
to depict the gene expression patterns associated with the 
Interferon-signaling pathway. This visualization was gener-
ated using the `heatmap` function in R.
Study Approval
The ethics committee of the Institute of Biomedical Sciences 
of the University of São Paulo approved the experimental 
protocols (CAAE 69005017.2.0000.5467) and an informed 
consent was signed by either the patient or a family member.
Results
Cellular Evaluation
Patient P1 displayed elevated levels of IgG, accompanied by 
low leukocyte counts, particularly in the CD19 lymphocyte 
and NK cell populations. This immunological profile sug-
gests a specific alteration in both B-cell and natural killer 
cell functions. On the other hand, Patient P2 exhibited nor -
mal total leukocyte counts but presented with decreased IgA 
levels and reduced NK cell populations, indicating selective

immunodeficiencies, particularly affecting mucosal immu-
nity and natural killer cell-mediated responses.
Both patients demonstrated low production of IFN-γ 
when their whole blood leukocytes were stimulated with 
BCG or BCG combined with IL-12 (Figure1 C). This 
response mirrors what is typically observed in patients with 
deficiencies in IL-12p40 and IL-12Rβ1, suggesting a poten-
tial disruption in the IL-12 signaling pathway, which is cru-
cial for effective immune response against mycobacterial 
infections. However, the production of IL-12p40 and IL-
12p70 appeared normal under the same stimulations, indi-
cating that the disruption may specifically affect the IFN-γ 
axis rather than the entire IL-12 pathway.
Genetic Findings
To differentiate common population polymorphisms from 
rare variants, we used allele frequency, selecting variants 
based on the Minor Allele Frequency (MAF). For inborn 
errors of immunity (IEI), we applied a filter for rare alleles 
with MAF ≤ 1%, along with a reference table of IEI genes 
documented in the literature. Additionally, we assessed 
the pathogenicity of the identified variants according to 
the Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) 
score, filtering those with a score > 15. The evaluation also 
included the location of the variants (exonic, intronic, UTR, 
or splicing), zygosity, and read quality.
Confirmation of the whole exome sequencing (WES) 
findings was achieved through Sanger DNA sequencing 
(Fig. 1D). The homozygous variant in the ISG15 gene (c. 
83 T > A p. L28Q) reported in both patients resulted in 
the substitution of thymine (T) with adenine (A), causing 
the replacement of the amino acid leucine (L) with glu-
tamine (Q) in exon 2. This variant was previously reported 
in the literature as compound heterozygosis [14] and 
described by in silico analyses as pathogenic/disease caus-
ing (Polyphen2 = 1, Sift = 0, MutationTaster = 0.99, CAD-
Dphred = 26.2) but has not been previously reported in 
Brazilian patients. Additionally, we confirmed the origin of 
the mutated alleles through sequencing of the parents, who 
exhibited a heterozygous pattern for the variant. Alterations 
in the ISG15 gene follow an autosomal recessive inherit-
ance pattern.
In silicoPrediction Analysis Related to the Mutant 
p.L28Q ISG15 Protein and Transcriptome Analysis
Furthermore, interatomic predictions of the mutated and 
wild-type (WT) amino acid residues demonstrated that this 
amino acid exchange produces an unstable mutated protein, 
with ΔΔG: -2.400 kcal/mol, indicating that the protein in its 
final conformational state has gained flexibility, as indicated 
by the red coloration in the structural model. (Fig.  1E).
To understand the transcriptome-level deficiency of 
ISG15 found in the patients of the study, a transcriptome 
analysis was conducted comparing control and patient sam-
ples. A total of 1321 differentially expressed genes were 
identified, of which 927 were to be upregulated and 394 
were downregulated. Subsequent enrichment analyses of 
the differentially expressed genes revealed that, among the 
enriched pathways, the most significant are the interferon 
pathways, where the majority of the genes are upregulated 
(Fig.  1F). Next, to identify the expression pattern of this 
set of genes, an expression and clustering analysis was per-
formed using a heatmap. This analysis allowed the obser -
vation of the expression similarity of these genes in the 
two groups, demonstrating that they are considerably more 
expressed in the patient group compared to the control group 
(Fig. 1G).
Discussion
The ISG15 gene encodes a ubiquitin-like interferon protein 
that modifies substrates through a process similar to ubiq-
uitination, referred to as ISGylation [6 , 19]. ISG15 can be 
secreted by various cells, including myeloid cells, and acts as 
a potent inducer of IFN-γ production in T lymphocytes, syn-
ergistically with IL-12 [20]. The inheritance of the ISG15 
defect follows a complete autosomal recessive pattern [6 , 
19]. Individuals with ISG15 variants exhibit susceptibility 
similar to patients with IL12RB1 and IL12 gene deficiencies, 
which is reflected in the low IFN-γ levels observed in the 
patients in this case report. An effective immune response 
against mycobacterial infections requires the integrity of the 
IL12/23/ISG15-IFN-γ circuit. Mutations in any of the 21 
genes involved in this circuit can lead to either an impaired 
response or production of IFN-γ [8 , 9, 21]. Some patients 
may have low intracellular but not extracellular ISG15 and 
exhibit increased IFNα/β immunity with high circulating 
IFNα levels [6, 17, 19]. Although these cytokines were not 
measured in the patients in this study, their clinical mani-
festations suggest they follow the standard pattern described 
in the literature.
Both patients, P1 and P2, were born to consanguineous 
parents from different families, each set of parents being 
first-degree cousins, a feature relevant for Mendelian inher-
itance analysis [6, 21]. The detailed family and clinical his-
tories of P1 and P2 underscore the complex interplay of 
genetic, immunological, and infectious factors in their health 
trajectories. Their cases highlight the critical importance 
of comprehensive vaccination programs and the need for 
targeted medical interventions in managing and mitigating 
severe health conditions in pediatric patients with consan-
guineous parentage. They both have a clinical history of 
difficult-to-treat mycobacterial infection, along with multiple

Despite these commonalities, there are notable differ -

The literature presents evidence that mutations in the ISG15

Based on reports in the literature, recognizing the syndro-
mic features of ISG15 deficiency is crucial for diagnosis [14, 
17]. ISG15-deficient patients have been identified with vari-
ous presentations, including reactions to BCG vaccination, 
intermittent seizures, and dermatological features (Table 1) 
[14, 19]. In our study, it is noteworthy that both patients 
did not present adverse reactions to the BCG vaccine alone 
but exhibited a broader range of clinical characteristics [19, 
21]. Therefore, specialists in pediatric infectious diseases, 
neurology, and dermatology must be aware of the pheno-
typic diversity with which ISG15 deficiency and other type 
I interferonopathies can present. Another interesting point 
to note is that, to date, intracranial calcifications have been 
identified only in P1, very similar to what has been reported 
in Chinese patients [19]. However, both patients had expe-
rienced seizures or epileptic episodes at some point in their 
lives, which deserve to be highlighted.
The mutation identified in the patients from our study 
has been previously reported in the literature but in a com-
pound heterozygosity (c.83 T > A and c.284del) case, result-
ing in the p.L28Q substitution and a frame shift generation 
a premature stop codon respectively [17]. In contrast, our 
patients were identified as homozygous for only the mutation 
c.83 T > A, maintaining the expected autosomal recessive 
inheritance pattern. In the same study [17], a prominent pat-
tern of skin lesions was observed. In our study, patient P2 
was the closest to the phenotype described. Even in homozy-
gosis, the skin lesions did not worsen more than expected.
In the literature, studies analyzing mRNA levels for the 
five standard ISGs (IFIT1, MX1, IFI27, RSAD2, and IFI44I) 
in whole blood have shown that patients exhibit higher 
steady-state levels of ISG expression than healthy controls, 
suggesting a classical type I interferonopathy [17]. In our 
study, PBMCs from patients were used, although without 
individual analysis of each specific population. In our heat-
map analysis, it is evident that in the latter half of the graph, 
there is a set of genes with very similar expression levels 
between the two patients. Within this gene set, there is a 
specific group that shares greater similarity in expression 
values between both patients and controls, which aligns with 
the findings described in the literature. However, what stands 
out is the slight difference in some gene expression patterns 
between the patients, which may reflect the differences in the 
clinical phenotypic presentation of P1 and P2.
Conclusion
Multiple genes contribute to Mendelian Susceptibility to 
Mycobacterial Disease (MSMD), causing clinical overlap 
and diagnostic delays. Timely diagnosis requires com-
prehensive microbiological, immunological, and genetic 
approaches. Reporting ISG15 gene mutations linked to 
mycobacterial infections in Brazil enhances understand-
ing of genetic susceptibilities, aiding in effective diagnostic 
and therapeutic strategies. Identifying high-risk individuals 
guides clinical practices and genetic counseling, impact-
ing public health policies and epidemiological research. 
Advanced genetic studies highlight Brazil's unique genetic 
diversity, emphasizing the necessity for population-specific 
data to accurately portray clinical manifestations and ulti-
mately improve patient care and outcomes. In summary, 
our study describes the case of two unrelated patients with 
the same homozygous mutation in the ISG15 gene, the first 
reported in Brazil. Both had infectious and neurological fea-
tures, as well as skin lesions, and showed no adverse reaction 
to the BCG vaccine.
