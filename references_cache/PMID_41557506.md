# Metabolic reprogramming during human neuron differentiation indicates glutaminase as a key determinant in Fragile X syndrome.
**Authors:** Shah S, Barnes D, Liu B, Tseyang T, Do T, Bubenik JL, Jung S, Anadolu MN, Ivshina MP, Martínez-Cerdeño V, Berry-Kravis E, Swanson MS, Spinelli JB, Richter JD
**Journal:** Cell Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.celrep.2025.116857](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.celrep.2025.116857)

## Content

Metabolic homeostasis gone awry is a contributor to, if not an underlying cause of, several neurologic disorders. Fragile X syndrome (FXS) is a neurodevelopmental disorder caused by a trinucleotide repeat expansion in FMR1 and consequent loss of the encoded protein FMRP, which results in downstream molecular, neurologic, and mitochondrial deficits that are linked to cognitive impairment. In the human postmortem brain, many metabolites and solute carrier proteins are coordinately dysregulated, which also occurs during the differentiation of human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) into excitatory neurons. Metabolic tracing in FXS neurons demonstrates a dearth of glutamine deamidation to glutamate, which reduces anaplerosis into the TCA cycle, potentially hindering the bioenergetic and biosynthetic functions of mitochondria. Mechanistically, aberrant expression of glutaminase isoforms in FXS is responsible for reduced glutaminolysis, thereby altering glutamate levels, which may contribute to FXS.

The human brain undergoes significant metabolic changes during development and aging; for example, oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial function are reduced in the aging brain.1 When metabolic homeostasis is perturbed, it can exacerbate neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders and may broadly contribute to intellectual disabilities such as autism spectrum disorders (ASDs).2,3 Fragile X syndrome (FXS) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that is the most common single-gene cause of autism. Metabolic characteristics include small and fragmented mitochondria,4–7 ineffective ATP generation by the TCA cycle,8 and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS).9 These and other observations suggest that the FXS brain is likely to display profound metabolic defects that may contribute to the etiology of the syndrome.

In FXS, an expansion of 200 or more CGG repeats in the FMR1 5′ untranslated region (UTR) can lead to cytosine methylation in the promoter and expansion regions that cause transcriptional silencing and loss of its encoded protein FMRP, resulting in several pathological conditions, including speech and developmental delays, hyperactivity, anxiety, other behavioral disturbances, and intellectual disability.10 FMRP is an RNA-binding protein that, at least in the mouse brain, binds ~1,000 or more mRNAs.11,12 A primary function of FMRP is to regulate translation; although earlier studies using radiolabeled amino acid incorporation into protein suggested that FMRP primarily repressed translation,13 high-resolution techniques such as ribosome profiling show that FMRP can both repress and activate translation.14–16 In the Fmr1-deficient mouse brain, dysregulated translation leads to secondary molecular and neural defects, including dysregulated splicing,17 altered epigenetic modifications,15,18 synapse dysfunction,19 and impaired neural circuit formation.20 Mouse Fmr1, however, does not undergo a CGG expansion or expansion-dependent gene methylation and transcriptional silencing. In addition, some studies indicate that bulk protein synthesis is either unaltered or reduced in the human FXS brain,21,22 the opposite of what is observed in the Fmr1-deficient mouse brain.13 These or other molecular differences may be a root cause of why agents or procedures that reverse FXS-like phenotypes in mice23 have been disappointing in human FXS clinical trials.24

Recent studies have suggested a direct connection between metabolism and FMRP-regulated translation.3 Shen et al. identified RACK1 (receptor for activated C kinase 1) mRNA, which encodes a 40S rRNA subunit-interacting protein, as a direct target of FMRP.3 RACK1 in turn controls the expression of other mRNAs that mediate mitochondrial integrity and function. In a similar vein, Fenton et al. showed that FMRP regulates translation of a mitochondrial fusion factor (MFF), which, as the name indicates, controls mitochondrial morphology, organization of this organelle’s DNA, and localization in neurons.25

To study fundamental aspects of human FXS, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of postmortem FXS and typically developing (TD) brain tissues. Gene Ontology molecular function analysis of the human FXS cortex showed that among the misregulated RNAs were those encoding amino acids and other solute transporters, including the primary excitatory neurotransmitter, glutamate. Similarly, in human FXS induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neurons, expression of glutamate transporter molecules that are responsible for delivering the neurotransmitter to the synaptic cleft and for clearing it is dysregulated, suggesting a mechanism whereby prolonged extracellular glutamate induces neuron hyperexcitability. These data suggested that the metabolome in the human FXS brain and in neuronal cultures might be dysregulated. Indeed, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis of both the human cortex and cerebellum showed that many metabolites were dramatically altered, including amino acids, sugars, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates, energy and oxidative phosphorylation-related compounds, glycolytic pathway molecules, and many others. Similarly, FXS excitatory neurons also showed altered metabolite abundance both intracellularly and extracellularly, with a prominent increase in glutamine and a decrease in glutamate. To test whether deamidation of glutamine to glutamate is compromised in FXS neurons, we used stable isotope tracing of 13C-labeled glutamine and demonstrated impaired conversion to glutamate as well as downstream metabolites such as a-ketoglutarate, aspartate, and other TCA cycle intermediates. Glutamine deamidation is catalyzed by glutaminase (GLS1), whose RNA is alternatively spliced to produce two primary isoforms, KGA and GAC (higher catalytic activity). In mature FXS neurons, the high activity isoform of the enzyme (GAC) is reduced, which impairs glutamate production and downstream metabolite generation and TCA cycle function. Neither these metabolic disturbances nor the glutamate transporter alterations are reflected in the mouse Fmr1-deficient brain. Our studies demonstrate that the coordinate interplay among glutamate synthesis, secretion, and anaplerosis into bioenergetic pathways is disrupted in and contributes to FXS.

Our recent studies revealed widespread RNA expression changes in Fmr1-knockout (KO) mouse brain and peripheral tissues15,17 and human FXS blood samples.26 To determine whether changes in RNA expression are prevalent in the human FXS postmortem brain, we sequenced RNA from five male FXS brains and five age-matched TD cortices (Figure 1A). Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis identified 74 upregulated and 189 downregulated RNAs in the FXS brain (p.adj < 0.05) (Figure 1B; Table S1). Gene Ontology highlighted enrichment in genes of the SLC (solute carrier) superfamily encoding transporters for amino acids, organic anions/acids, and ions (e.g., SLC17A6, SLC7A11, SLC1A2, and SLC6A11) and neurotransmitter-receptor genes such as GRIN2C for glutamate and GABRG1 for GABA (Figures 1C and 1D; highlighted in Figure 1B). Consistent with our bulk RNA-seq findings, a prior single-nucleus RNA-seq study of postmortem FXS brain tissue also demonstrated cell-type-specific dysregulation of SLC RNAs27 (Figures 1B and S1A). To examine solute changes that may be linked to these transporters, we performed LC-MS on polar metabolites from human patient-derived cortex and cerebellum samples (6 samples per genotype) (Figure 1A). The FXS cortex showed a significant depletion of amino acids (13 of 20) and significant changes in central carbon metabolites in glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, fatty acid oxidation, and nucleotide metabolism (Figure 1E; Table S2). A joint pathway analysis of DEGs and dysregulated metabolites in the FXS cortex revealed altered metabolite networks and functionally related genes (e.g., decreased SLC6A15 with corresponding Leu, Ile, Met, Phe, and Pro reductions) (Figure S1B). The FXS cerebellum showed similar metabolite changes, including a dearth in free amino acids (15 of 20), energy metabolism intermediates (TCA cycle and glycolysis), ATP, GTP, nucleotide metabolism intermediates, and neurotransmitters (choline, GABA, Glu, taurine, and Ser) (Figure 1F; Table S2). Amino acid changes were most prominent in both the cortex and cerebellum (similar reductions, Figure S1C), followed by energy and nucleotide metabolism intermediates (Figure 1G). A decreased ratio of reduced to oxidized glutathione (GSH:GSSG) was observed in the FXS cortex (Figures 1E and S1D), with a similar trend in the cerebellum, suggesting increased oxidative stress (Figure S1D). In the cerebellum, lactate was decreased (Figure S1E) as was the lactate:pyruvate ratio in the cortex (Figures 1E and S1E). There were very few or no polar metabolites that were altered in the mouse Fmr1-KO cortex or hippocampus (Figure S1F), indicating that profound metabolic imbalance is particularly prevalent in human FXS. Thus, in analyzing human FXS brain tissue, we identify a robust and unexpected reprogramming of amino acid metabolites and their associated transporters, inspiring us to elucidate their connection with this neurodevelopmental disorder.

To determine if these metabolic changes could also be observed in peripheral tissues of patients with FXS, we analyzed polar metabolites in plasma. Consistent with results in the brain tissue, FXS plasma displayed reductions in amino acids and nucleotide/energy metabolism intermediates (Figures 2A–2C; Table S2). To determine whether the metabolic changes track with the amount of FMRP, we used three FXS patient-derived lymphoblast cell lines (LCLs) with varying amounts: FXS1, no FMRP; FXS2, partial FMRP; and TD cells with a normal amount of FMRP (Figure 2D). LC-MS analysis of the cells and extracellular culture media (Figures 2E and S2A; Table S2) reflected wide-spread metabolite imbalances, including amino acids and energy/nucleotide metabolism intermediates in FXS cells. Principal-component analysis (PCA) of both compartments shows a variance in metabolite levels reflecting the amount of FMRP, suggesting that FMRP reprograms metabolic pathways in this neurodevelopmental disorder (Figures 2D and S2A). Heatmap clustering highlights distinct metabolite differences in both compartments (e.g., cluster 1) (Figure 2E). An integration of intracellular cluster 1 metabolites, which include amino acids such as methionine and serine, showed a reduction from TD to FXS2 to FXS1 (Figure 2F, bottom) that mirrored FMRP levels. Extracellular cluster 1 metabolites showed similar reductions in both FXS cell lines (Figures 2E–2G and S2A), suggesting excess uptake of metabolites in FXS cells. LC-MS experiments, including additional patient-derived LCLs, FXS3, and TD6, showed persistent reductions in a number of metabolites, including serine (Figure S2B).

Glutamine (Gln), the most abundant free amino acid, is a precursor for other amino acids, nucleotides, and lipids via deamidation to glutamate (Glu); it also replenishes the TCA cycle and contributes to redox homeostasis. We observed decreased Gln and Glu in FXS LCLs (Figure 3A; Table S2), implying increased glutamine utilization. Despite lower intracellular Glu, extracellular levels increased, suggesting increased Glu secretion into the culture media (Figure 3A). To investigate Gln utilization in FXS cells, we performed [13C5, 15N2]-glutamine stable isotope tracing in TD, FXS2 (partial FMRP), and FXS1 (no FMRP) LCLs for 8 h, after which the isotopologs reached a steady state (Figure 3B). Gln enters the TCA cycle via oxidative or reductive carboxylation (blue intermediates in Figure 3C). In FXS LCLs, we found elevated glutaminolysis by both oxidative (increased C4-labeled Asp) and reductive (increased C3-labeled Asp and C5-labeled citrate) pathways (Figures 3D and 3E). Notably, FXS cells show higher conversion of Gln to Glu, which is readily secreted into the media (Figures 3D and 3E) and could result in excitotoxicity. Taken together, the increased Glu synthesis (i.e., Gln tracing) combined with decreased Glu levels (i.e., steady state LC-MS) indicates elevated Glu consumption in FXS LCLs.

We employed multiple FXS patient-derived iPSC lines (FXS)28–31 along with their CRISPR-generated CGG-excised isogenic controls (iControl),28,31 as well as independent iPSCs from TD individuals,30 to investigate metabolomic changes during in vitro neuronal differentiation. These iPSCs were differentiated into neural progenitor cells (NPCs) by dual-SMAD (mothers against decapentaplegic/Sma) inhibition (Figure 4A). LC-MS profiling of polar metabolites in NPCs generated from two FXS iPSC lines,29,30 compared with those from two TD lines, each analyzed in triplicate (Figure 4B), revealed genotype-dependent clustering by heatmap and PCA (Figures 4C and 4D), with significant changes (log2 fold change [log2FC] ≥ ±2) shown in bar plots (Figure 4D; see also Table S2). Western blot analysis confirmed FMRP expression patterns consistent with genotype (Figure 4B, bottom).

To test the FMRP dependence of metabolite changes, we examined NPCs generated from two independent FXS lines and their CRISPR-edited isogenic rescue counterparts obtained from the N. Benvenisty28 and S. Warren31 laboratories (Figure 4E). LC-MS profiling of polar metabolites (triplicates per line) showed enrichment of increased metabolites in both rescue lines relative to their FXS counterparts (cluster 2, Figure 4F; see also Table S2). PCA of intracellular metabolites again demonstrated clear genotypic segregation (Figure 4F, top right), and metabolites with log2FC ≥ ±2 are shown in Figure 4F (bottom right). Extracellular metabolite analysis also showed genotype-specific differences (Figure 4G; see also Table S2).

Finally, to assess whether FMRP influences metabolism during neuron maturation, we differentiated isogenic lines into forebrain excitatory neurons and profiled intra- and extracellular metabolites at weeks 1 and 4 (Figure 5A).28 Western blots for the synaptic markers PSD-95 and synaptophysin indicate that although neuron maturation is delayed in FXS cells (i.e., reduced synaptophysin at week 1), by week 4, it is similar to that of iControl (Figure 5B). GFAP expression indicates that astrocytes are present at comparable levels in FXS and iControl cultures by week 4 (Figure 5B). LC-MS for polar metabolites during neuron differentiation revealed that amino acids, energy/nucleotide metabolism, and neurotransmitters were adversely affected in FXS cells and in the culture media at all stages (Figures 5C and 5D). These data are reminiscent of the metabolite dysregulation in the FXS brain (Figure 1) and plasma (Figure 2).

Across our FXS metabolomics datasets, we noted intracellular amino acid imbalances and a dearth of extracellular free amino acids, which we hypothesized could be due to increased protein synthesis by mTOR signaling or by impaired recycling due to a block in autophagy. However, while PI3Kβ levels increased in FXS NPCs, phosphorylated mTOR (Ser2481) or rpS6 (Ser240/244) did not, suggesting that mTOR hyperactivation does not contribute to reduced free amino acids (Figure S5A). We detected elevated expression of P62, an autophagy marker, as noted previously in FXS cells,32,33 without corresponding changes in LC3B levels in FXS NPCs, suggesting a potential disruption in amino acid recycling (Figure S5B).

Several metabolites were similarly dysregulated during neuronal differentiation at weeks 1 and 4 in the FXS neurons compared to the isogenic rescue neurons (Figure S5C; also see Table S2). S-adenosyl methionine (SAM) levels in all FXS neuron maturation stages were reduced, potentially resulting in epigenetic changes (Figure S5C). Classification of the metabolite abundance differences in FXS showed that they were involved in energy, nucleotide, and amino acid metabolism (Figure 5D). Several amino acid-derived neurotransmitters/neuromodulators were differentially expressed in FXS neurons at week 1/4, suggesting impaired neurotransmission. We found altered levels of both precursor metabolites and neurotransmitter products in neurons (e.g., Asp and NMDA, Figure S4F; Met/Cys and Taurine, Gln and Glu, and Glu and GABA, Figures 5E and S5C). During differentiation, Glu levels steadily increase, influencing neuronal maturation.34 However, in FXS neurons, Glu levels decreased as neurons matured (Figures 5C, 5D, and S5C). Additionally, improper redox buffering in FXS neurons could result in increased oxidative stress (GSSG) throughout differentiation (Figure S5C). These data suggest that imbalances in multiple metabolites during neuronal differentiation in FXS likely impair cell function, energy homeostasis, and neurotransmission.

Maintaining proper Glu levels is crucial for neuronal maturation, neurotransmission, and survival. Glu synthesis from Gln and its recycling between neurons and astrocytes is orchestrated by several Glu transporters (SLC superfamily genes). Based on changes in the RNA levels of Glu transporters in the FXS cortex (Figure 1), we examined whether these changes also occurred in FXS neurons (FMRP levels validated in Figures 6A and S6A). Vesicular glutamate transporters (VGLUTs) package Glu for synaptic release, with three forms (VGLUT1–3) encoded by distinct SLC genes (SLC17A7, SLC17A6, and SLC17A8). In the FXS cortex, we found an increase in SLC17A6 and SLC17A8 RNA expression (Figure 1). In FXS cells, we find an initial decrease in SLC17A6 RNA levels and its encoded protein, VGLUT2, at the NPC stage, which is reversed in FXS neurons at week 4 (Figures 6A and 6B). VGLUT1 levels are lower in FXS NPCs, increase in week 1 neurons, and are restored in week 4 FXS neurons (Figures 6A and 6C, top). We also observed significant dysregulation of SLC6A15 (B(0)AT2), SLC7A10 (ASC1), and SLC17A8 (VGLUT3) in human FXS iPSC-derived neurons, similar to that in the FXS cortex (Figures S6B–S6D and 1). During neuronal maturation, a transition from VGLUT2/3 to VGLUT1 typically occurs; however, FXS neurons show impairment of this neurodevelopmental switch, resulting in increased VGLUT2/3 encoding RNA/protein levels in neurons at week 4 (Figures 6B–6E and S6D). This elevated VGLUT2/3 could result in improper synaptic Glu release and alter neuronal firing in the FXS brain.35

Astrocytic Glu uptake is performed by the predominant EAAT2 (SLC1A2) or the EAAT1 (SLC1A1) transporters, which rapidly clear synaptic Glu. In FXS neurons, SLC1A2 RNA levels were reduced in NPCs and week 1 neurons, with a decrease in the encoded EAAT2 protein levels at week 4 (Figures 6A and 6D). EAAT1 protein levels increased at the week 1 and 4 neuronal stage in FXS, compensating for diminished EAAT2 expression (Figures 6A and 6C, bottom). The switch in the principal Glu release transporter, VGLUT1, to the less efficient VGLUT2/3, combined with potentially impaired synaptic clearance of Glu (EAAT1 instead of EAAT2) in FXS, likely results in excessive Glu at synapses and hyperexcitability (Figures 6E and 6F). In the FXS cortex, we found reduced SLC7A11 RNA levels (Figure 1) encoding the xCT transporter, a cystine/glutamate antiporter critical for redox balance via glutathione synthesis in astrocytes. Although the SLC7A11 RNA levels were reduced in FXS week 1 neurons, its encoded protein, xCT, was elevated in NPC and week 1 neurons (Figure 6G). Increased oxidized glutathione (GSSG) during FXS neuronal differentiation (Figure S5C) suggests heightened demand for xCT expression to sustain redox homeostasis. Consistently, the xCT levels in independent FXS NPC lines (FXS848-3 and SC128) were elevated compared to control lines (CTR2-20 and SC173). Notably, this increase was reversed in the CRISPR-edited rescue line (iControl) (Figure S6E).

In the Fmr1-KO mouse cortex, we found no changes in VGLUT2, xCT, or EAAT2 (Figure S6F) and no changes in Glu/Gln levels (Figure S1E), demonstrating the importance of using human material to assess a human disorder. We also showed that in human NPCs, SLC17A6 expression but not SLC6A15 or SLC7A11 tracked with FMRP levels (Figure 6H). In conclusion, our data indicate an impaired Glu-Gln cycle in FXS cells characterized by dysregulated Glu release (VGLUT1, VGLUT2, VGLUT3, and xCT) and uptake (EAAT1 and EAAT2) transporters, which may either promote or result from abnormal Glu/Gln levels during neuronal differentiation (Figure 6I).

To test whether the reduced steady-state Glu levels in FXS neurons were due to impaired synthesis, we traced [13C5,15N2]-glutamine in week 4 FXS neurons. LC-MS data showed that Gln was not efficiently converted into Glu and, consequently, fewer labeled carbons enter the oxidative and reductive TCA cycle (reflected by reduced levels of labeled α-KG, fumarate, malate, and aspartate) (Figure 7A). However, the percentage of labeled Glu secreted by FXS neurons was increased (Figure 7A), suggesting altered neurotransmission, which is consistent with changes in Glu transporter expression in week 4 neurons (Figure 6). Indeed, in most FXS tissues and cells studied thus far, Glu levels are reduced, and Gln levels are increased (Figure 7B, dotted lines represent control levels of respective metabolites). These findings demonstrate that in FXS, glutaminolysis is deficient, resulting in dysregulation of Glu production and secretion.

Glutaminase (GLS) deamidates Gln to generate Glu, which is reversed by glutamine synthetase (GLUL) (Figure 7C). While GLUL levels are unchanged in FXS neurons (Figure 7C), GLS1 isoforms are dysregulated at both the RNA and protein levels during differentiation. In FXS NPCs, GLS1 isoforms KGA and GAC are increased, but in week 1 neurons, both isoforms are diminished. By week 4, KGA levels were unchanged, but GAC (higher catalytic activity) was nearly undetectable in FXS neurons, which limits Gln-to-Glu conversion (Figures 7A–7C). The two GLS1 isoforms arise by differential splicing of GLS1 RNA, which, while altered in FXS NPCs and early neurons, is normal in week 4 neurons. These data suggest that GLS1/GAC may be regulated post-transcriptionally, either by FMRP-mediated translational control or via reduced protein stability. The reduction in Gln-to-Glu conversion when combined with increased Glu release and reduced clearance would elevate hyperexcitability and oxidative stress.

Quantitative analysis of MitoTracker-stained NPCs revealed a marked reduction in mitochondrial number, size, and network complexity in FXS-derived NPCs compared to their CRISPR-corrected iControl NPCs (Figures S7A and S7B). Decreases in mitochondrial count, area, perimeter, aspect ratio, form factor, and branching parameters point to smaller, fragmented organelles with diminished interconnectivity (Figures S7A and S7B). Such a shift is consistent with previous studies demonstrating a fragmented and simplified mitochondrial network in FXS.3,36 In FXS NPCs, we find decreased levels of TOM20, an outer membrane protein indicative of total mitochondrial quantity (Figure S7C). Functionally, this suggests that disrupted mitochondrial dynamics and reduced capacity sustain neuronal demands. In addition, reduced mitochondrial content may underlie the observed impairment in TCA cycle anaplerosis, further compromising energy production and contributing to cascading deficits in neural function in FXS.

This study highlights that GLS dysregulation is a probable underlying cause of impaired neurotransmission and cognitive function in human FXS. GLS is essential for Gln deamidation, which, when reduced, is responsible for the decrement of neurotransmitters, including Glu, GABA, and Asp, as well as TCA cycle intermediates that influence bioenergetics. Moreover, disruptions in SLCs, particularly several that transport glutamate, further compromise neurotransmission by increasing Glu secretion into, but diminishing removal from, the synaptic space. This likely results in increased firing of action potentials and the sensory hyperactivity and other symptoms that are characteristic of FXS. In contrast to post-mitotic neurons, GLS is elevated in proliferating cells, which increases glutamate synthesis and production of TCA cycle intermediates.

By combining RNA-seq of the human FXS postmortem brain cortex with metabolomic analysis, we find a remarkably coordinated dysregulation of SLC protein mRNAs and the solutes that their encoded proteins transport. Furthermore, LC-MS of FXS brain tissues (cortex and cerebellum) and plasma primarily demonstrates a 43%–58% reduction of polar metabolites, mostly amino acids or their derivatives, TCA cycle, and nucleotide metabolism intermediates. A few of these metabolite changes were previously detected in patients with FXS using magnetic resonance spectroscopy.37–40 Autistic phenotypes, often co-occurring with FXS, have been linked to dysregulated SLC expression.41,42 Although metabolic distress is not a frequently encountered co-morbidity of these neurodevelopmental disorders, metabolite deficits could influence brain function. In contrast, Fmr1-KO mice display little to no change in SLC transporter RNAs, their encoded proteins, or polar metabolites, which is surprising since compromised mitochondrial function has been observed in these animals or in cells derived from them.14,43 These fundamental metabolic differences may contribute to the severity of human FXS brain pathology that is not phenocopied in the Fmr1-KO mouse brain.

FXS brain, blood, and iPSC-derived neurons, which are FMRP deficient, show altered metabolite levels, suggesting that the metabolome could serve as a biomarker for the disorder. Indeed, in LCLs, the extent of steady-state metabolite dysregulation tracks with the amount of FMRP. Thus, therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring FMRP in the brain (difficulty of delivery notwithstanding), such as antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) rescue of FMR1 mis-splicing,26 demethylation of CGG repeats, or CGG editing,28,31 could be scored by LC-MS metabolic profiling of cerebrospinal fluid before and after treatment.

Neurotransmission by Glu is controlled by multiple transporters, and enzymes that generate, deposit, and clear the metabolite from the synaptic cleft are mostly dysregulated in differentiating FXS neurons. VGLUT2, a vesicular Glu transporter that has a higher synaptic Glu release probability than the canonical VGLUT1 transporter and is responsible for higher Glu release.44 In FXS neurons, VGLUT2 expression is increased and may underlie the increased Glu release observed in vitro. xCT, which releases Glu in the synaptic cleft, is also upregulated in FXS neurons. EAAT1 and −2, which clear glutamate, are up- and downregulated, respectively, in FXS. Downregulation of EAAT2 (higher Glu affinity and lower KM for transport) may cause a glutamate surplus. Thus, increased release of Glu and impaired clearance may result in excessive Glu signaling in FXS, inducing hyperexcitability via elevated calcium influx and oxidative stress.

Glu synthesis from Gln is controlled by two isoforms of GLS (GLS1), KGA (less active) and GAC (more active), the latter of which oligomerizes to increase glutamine affinity.45 In FXS, both GLS1 RNA isoforms are dysregulated during neural differentiation; NPCs show predominant GAC-encoding RNA, but in week 4 neurons, KGA-encoding RNA is more prevalent. Most importantly, FXS week 4 neurons lack the GAC isoform, which is responsible for reduced glutamine deamidation and, consequently, TCA cycle metabolite generation. Thus, the dearth of several metabolites in FXS is largely due to GLS dysregulation. GLS1 RNA is also reduced in human ASD brains, and a mouse model of GLS1 deficiency results in ASD-like phenotypes.46 These data reinforce GLS1 as a regulator of higher brain function.

Metabolite and RNA analysis in human FXS reveals a holistic view of molecular dysregulation at multiple levels, from neurotransmitter generation (especially Glu) to transport and mitochondrial function. GLS is centrally important for these events, but FMRP’s role in its regulation is unclear. In this regard, the high activity GAC isoform, at least in week 4 neurons, may be regulated at the level of direct or indirect FMRP-mediated mRNA translation or protein stability. Mitochondrial staining demonstrated decreased mitochondrial number, size, and branching parameters in FXS-derived neural cells compared to controls, which is further exemplified by decreased levels of the mitochondrial membrane protein TOM20. These alterations reflect a fragmented and simplified mitochondrial network, consistent with impaired mitochondrial dynamics and reduced functional capacity. In any event, the fact that FMRP deficiency results in a cascade of metabolite dysregulation in humans suggests that amelioration of the disorder would require strategies focusing on the restoration of FMRP in the brain.

We demonstrate extensive dysregulation of polar metabolites in the human FXS postmortem brain, FXS patient-derived plasma, lymphoblastoid cells, NPCs, and forebrain excitatory neurons in vitro. Some of this metabolic reprogramming can be explained by altered SLC protein expression, impaired mitochondrial function, and reduced GLS expression. However, one limitation is that this study focused on polar metabolites, which excluded other metabolites, such as lipids, that would have given greater clarity as to the extent of metabolic dysfunction. In addition, although our iPSC-derived neuronal cultures contained astrocytes (15%), we did not titrate the neuron-to-astrocyte ratio, which may have revealed additional alterations in SLC proteins and/or metabolite dysfunction. We also limited our tracing analysis to isotopically labeled glutamine, which, by exclusion of other tracing experiments, such as with labeled glucose, a more comprehensive picture of the metabolic deficits of FXS cells was not revealed. Finally, although we noted that the Fmr1-KO mouse brain does not reflect the metabolic or SLC protein dysregulation that we observed in human FXS, we did not explore these differences further. Because Fmr1-KO animals are commonly used as proxies for human FXS, it would be worth-while to perform a more thorough metabolic analysis of these animals.

Briefly cells were plated on Matrigel coated AggreWell800 wells in STEMdiff Neural Induction Medium + SMADi and embryoid bodies were allowed to form for 13 days followed by Rosette selection as per manufactures instructions. The rosettes were plated on Poly-L-Ornithine (PLO)/laminin coated plates for 3 days and then dissociated into single cells and expanded as NPCs in STEMdiff Neural Progenitor Medium. To generate neuron precursors, NPCs were cultured in STEMdiff Forebrain Neuron Differentiation media (Catalog #08600) for 3 days on PLO/laminin plates followed by replating at a density of 135,000 cells/cm2 in PLO/laminin plates in STEMdiff Forebrain Neuron Maturation media (Catalog #08605) with Compound E for 4 weeks. For Immunofluorescence staining, cells were cultured on glass coverslips and fixed using cold 4%PFA for 15 min. After washes with 1XPBST, coverslips were blocked in 5% normal donkey serum in PBST for 1 h. Coverslips were incubated in appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and then with secondary antibodies after PBST washes. Coverslips were mounted on slides using the ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Catalog#P36941, ThermoFisher Scientific).

Frozen brain tissues were powdered in liquid nitrogen and RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific # 15596026). The RNA obtained was treated with TURBO DNase (Invitrogen # AM2238) and was further purified using the RNA clean and concentrator kit (ZymoResearch # 11–325). RNA quality and abundance were assessed using fragment analysis (Agilent/Advanced Analytical). The quality scores ranged from 3.3 to 6.1 across the samples. Strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were constructed with 500ng of total RNA using ribosomal RNA depletion and the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA library prep kit for Illumina. Samples were PCR amplified for 9 cycles and sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq 2000 P4 XLEAP-SBS kit with 2 x 100 nucleotide paired-end reads. The FASTQ files were analyzed using the RNA-seq analysis pipeline on Foundry.47 Briefly, the reads were filtered and trimmed using trimmomatic and cutadapt used for adapter removal. Genome alignment to hg38 Gencode v43 was performed using STAR (v2.6.1). Salmon (v1.5.2) was used to align RNA-Seq reads to reference transcripts and estimate gene and isoform expression levels. Differential expression analysis was done using DESeq2 with a threshold for significance set at p.adj <0.05.

The postmortem brain tissues were powdered under liquid nitrogen and samples were normalized based on tissue weight. The metabolites were extracted using 80% methanol. For plasma and other liquid samples, 10ul of sample was added to 90ul of 80% methanol and vortexed for 10mins before spinning to collect the supernatant containing extracted metabolites and vacuum dried. The extract was then resuspended in 100ul LC/MS grade water and appropriate amounts were loaded for analysis based on cell counts or protein levels quantified using a BCA assay where possible. For powdered brain tissues and cell pellets from in-vitro cultures, samples were incubated on dry ice with cold 80% methanol. The extracts were spun down and the supernatant was vacuum dried before resuspending in LC/MS grade water.

A Q-Exactive orbitrap mass spectrometer with an Ion Max source and HESI II probe attached to a Vanquish Horizon UHLPC system was used to measure polar metabolites. 2 μL of sample was injected into the machine and ran through a SeQuant ZIC-pHILIC 5um 150 × 2.1 mm analytical column (Sigma) with a 2.1 × 20 mm guard column (Sigma) attached to the front end. The column oven was set to 25°C and autosampler was set to 4°C. Buffer A was comprised of 20 mM ammonium carbonate (Sigma), 0.1% ammonium hydroxide (Sigma) in HPLC-grade water (Sigma) and Buffer B was compromised of 100% acetonitrile (Sigma). The liquid chromatography was set to a flow rate of 0.15 mL/min. The mass spectrometer was set to full scan (70–1000 m/z), polarity switching mode, with the spray voltage set to 4.0 kV, heated capillary to 350°C, and the HESI probe at 30°C. The sheath gas flow was set at 10 units, auxiliary gas at 1 unit, and sweep gas flow at 1 unit. The resolution of scan was set to 70,000, AGC target to 1x106, and maximum injection time at 20 msec. An additional scan between 220 and 700 m/z was used to enhance nucleotide detection in the negative mode as well with the maximum injection time set to 80 msec. For glutamine flux tracing experiments, cells were cultured for 8hrs in glutamine free media supplemented with 2mM L-Glutamine-13C5,15N2 (Catalog#607983, Millipore Sigma). After treatment, cell pellet and media from cultures was collected and processed for LC/MS as above. A media blank with heavy glutamine was used as a reference for normalization. Fold changes were calculated, and statistical testing was performed using Graphpad Prism.

A total of 500 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the QuantiTect cDNA synthesis kit (Qiagen, #205311) with oligo(dT)20 primers and random hexamers. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted on a QuantStudio 3 system with iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (BIO-RAD, #1725122), performing technical duplicates for each sample across three biological replicates (N = 3).

Cells were lysed for 10 min on ice at 4°C using RIPA buffer supplemented with cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Catalog# 11836170001, Millipore Sigma) and PhosSTOP (Catalog# 4906837001, Millipore Sigma), followed by a 10-min incubation on ice and dissociation via pipetting. The lysates were centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant collected. Protein concentrations measured using the BCA assay. A total of 10 μg of protein was mixed with SDS-bromophenol blue reducing buffer containing 40 mM DTT and subjected to western blot analysis on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel. PVDF membranes used for transfer, and blocking done in 5% nonfat milk for 30 min. Membranes were washed three times for 5 min each with 1X PBST and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies used are listed in the Key Resources table. Membranes were washed three times for 10 min each with 1X TBST and incubated with anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 1:10000) at room temperature for 1 h. After three additional washes with 1X TBST (10 min each), membranes were developed using ECL-Plus (Pierce) and visualized using a GE Amersham Imager. Images analyzed using Fiji.48

Coverslips with 200,000 NPCs per genotype were stained with 50nM MitoTracker Green FM dye in DMSO (Cat#M7514, ThermoFisher Scientific) for 30mins at 37degC. After fixing, the coverslips were washed in pre-warmed DMEM:F12 and fixed in 4%PFA for 15 min. Coverslips were mounted on slides using the ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Catalog#P36941, ThermoFisher Scientific). 3D confocal images were obtained using LeicaDMi8 with 63× objective and 3um z stack sections. Maximum projection cell images were used to threshold and perform 2D mitochondrial analysis using open source MitoAnalyser49 plugin for ImageJ/Fiji application. Data was plotted using Graphpad Prism.
