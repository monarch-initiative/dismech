Abstract
Cancer is a multifaceted disease characterized by the gradual accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations within 
cells, leading to uncontrolled cell growth and invasive behavior. The intricate interplay between environmental factors, 
such as exposure to carcinogens, and the molecular cascades governing cell growth, differentiation, and survival con-
tributes to cancer’s development and progression. This review offers a comprehensive overview of key molecular targets 
and their roles in cancer development. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors are implicated in various cancers due 
to their role in regulating lipid metabolism, inflammation, and cell proliferation. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related fac -
tor 2 protects cells from oxidative damage but can also promote tumor cell survival. Cytochrome P450 1B1 metabolizes 
exogenous and endogenous substances, and its increased expression is observed in several cancers. The constitutive 
androstane receptor regulates gene expression, and its dysregulation can lead to liver cancer. Transforming growth factor-
beta 2 is involved in the development and progression of various cancers by dysregulating cell proliferation, differentia-
tion, and migration. Chelation treatment has been investigated for removing heavy metals, while genetically altered 
immune cells show promise in treating specific cancers. Metal–organic frameworks and fibronectin targeting represent 
new directions in cancer treatment. While some heavy metals, such as arsenic, chromium, nickel, and cadmium, are known 
to have carcinogenic properties, others, like zinc, Copper, gold, bismuth, and silver, have many uses that highlight their 
potential as effective cancer control tactics. There are a variety of heavy metal-based technologies that show potential 
for improving cancer treatment methods, including targeted drug delivery, improved radiation, and diagnostic tools.

1 Introduction
Cancer is a complicated disease characterized by uncontrolled cell proliferation and tissue infection [1 ]. Research-
ers have discovered many molecular mechanisms and signaling cascades that cause cancer in recent years. This has 
revealed biomarkers and key molecular players in cancer formation. Cancer is a significant worldwide public health 
issue, with a considerable influence on mortality rates. In the year 2020, cancer accounted for about 10 million 
deaths, representing approximately one-sixth of the total documented fatalities [2 ]. According to available data, 
the global mortality rate attributed to diverse types of cancer was around 9.6 million individuals in the year 2017 
[3]. During the period of 2013–2017, the cancer mortality rate, which is determined by the number of cancer-related 
deaths per 100,000 individuals yearly, was recorded as 158.3 for both males and females [4 ]. In 2020, the prevalent 
types of cancer in terms of incidence encompassed breast cancer, lung cancer, colon and rectal cancer, prostate 
cancer, non-melanoma skin cancer, and stomach cancer [5 ]. In 2024, the American Cancer Society projects approxi-
mately 2,001,140 new cancer cases and 611,720 cancer deaths in the United States [6 ]. Research conducted in the

Xenobiotics refer to exogenous substances that infiltrate the human body as a result of contact with many envi-
ronmental origins, including but not limited to pollutants, poisons, and toxins [10]. Certain xenobiotics have been 
associated with the development of cancer, and their modes of action entail interactions with distinct biological 
cascades. Several important cascades are involved in cellular processes, including Peroxisome proliferator-activated 
receptors (PPARs), Cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily B member 1 (CYP1B1), Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR), 
Tumor growth factor β (TGF-β), and Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) [11]. The induction of oxidative 
assault by xenobiotics is a crucial factor in the development of cancer. Several types of heavy metals, including arse -
nic, chromium, nickel, and cadmium, together with substances like aflatoxin, have been found to play a role in causing 
oxidative damage, DNA alterations, and the development of tumors. Various forms of cancer, such as skin cancer, lung 
cancer, urethral carcinoma, kidney carcinoma, and gastric cancer, have been linked to exposure to xenobiotics [12].
The comprehension of the molecular mechanisms that underlie the initiation and advancement of cancer holds 
notable significance, as it provides opportunities for the implementation of preventative measures and the devel-
opment of groundbreaking therapeutic approaches. In response to the detrimental impacts of xenobiotics, various 
therapeutic approaches have been developed, including Chelation therapy [13], CAR-T Cell Therapy [14], immu-
notherapy utilizing genetically modified T cells to selectively eliminate cancer cells, and the MOFs Technique [15]. 
The therapeutic applications of Fibronectin, including medication transport and the suppression of cell adhesion, 
exhibit significant potential for the field of cancer therapy. Hence, this review focuses on the molecular mechanism 
implicated in the development of cancer produced by heavy metals, as well as the current techniques employed to 
mitigate their harmful effects.
2  Xenobiotics in cancer promotion
Heavy metals are well recognized as one of the most perilous categories of environmental xenobiotics [16]. Heavy metals 
have a high level of resistance to environmental degradation and have the ability to accumulate within living organisms. 
This accumulation can result in the generation of free radicals and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to toxicity in both 
animals and humans. Elevated quantities of heavy metals, including Cu, zinc, lead, cadmium, and chromium, have been 
detected in the soil of agricultural areas [17]. The presence of heavy metal pollution in terrestrial ecosystems has the 
potential to directly impact plants by offering an elemental defense mechanism. Identifying molecular biomarkers that 
are targeted by xenobiotics can provide insight into the mechanisms underlying their carcinogenic effects. One example 
of a xenobiotic target molecular biomarker in cancer is the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [18]. Heavy metals, such as 
arsenite, can induce the activation of the AhR, resulting in its translocation into the nucleus and subsequent binding 
to the promoter region of the Cyp1a1 gene [19]. This activation may be attributed to the potential disruption of the 
connection between AhR and heat shock protein 90 (HSP90). Furthermore, it has been documented that the molecu-
lar chaperone HSP90 is capable of activating AhR. The activation of the AhR by heavy metal xenobiotics is mediated 
through either direct binding to AhR or by interfering with the interaction between AhR and HSP90 [20]. This elucidates 
the intricate relationship between environmental contaminants and cellular regulatory mechanisms. Abnormal expres-
sion and activation of the AhR have been observed in a range of malignancies, encompassing leukemia, lymphoma, 
glioblastoma, ovarian, lung, liver, and head and neck cancers [21]. The inhibition of AhR represents a potentially fruitful 
approach for cancer treatment, as it has been shown to enhance the sensitivity of tumors to chemotherapy, as seen by 
the effects observed with doxorubicin [22].
Xenobiotics such as benzo[a]pyrene can directly cause DNA assault, which triggers the activation of checkpoint kinases 
and DNA repair cascades. However, if the impairment is too severe, it can lead to genetic mutations and chromosomal 
abnormalities that promote cancer development [23]. Finally, the epigenetic machinery is also a target for xenobiotics in 
cancer development [24]. Xenobiotics such as bisphenol A and phthalates can alter DNA methylation and histone modifi-
cation patterns, which can result in the dysregulation of gene expression and the promotion of cancer development [25].

3  Molecular cascades of xenobiotics‑induced cancer
Xenobiotics have been shown to cause cancer by interfering with normal cellular processes. The activation of recep -
tors like PPARs, aAhR, and CAR are part of the molecular processes that are implicated in xenobiotic-induced cancer 
[26]. These receptors can increase tumor growth by stimulating cell proliferation. Cyclin D1 and other genes involved 
in cell cycle progression are upregulated in response to PPAR activation. By stimulating the aAhR cascade, CYP1B1 
aids tumor development [27]. Chemotherapy medications may be metabolized and eliminated more quickly if CAR 
is used to stimulate the production of drug-metabolizing enzymes like cytochrome P450 and uridine nucleotide 
transferases [28].
3.1  PPARs
PPARs are a group of nuclear receptors that have significant regulatory functions in the biotransformation of lipids 
and glucose. However, recent studies have also implicated PPARs in the pathogenesis of cancer, particularly in tumor 
progression [29]. The molecular mechanism underlying PPAR-mediated tumor progression involves several key pro -
cesses. One mechanism by which PPARs promote tumor progression is through the expression of the epithelial-
mesenchymal transition (EMT) cascade. EMT refers to the phenotypic transformation of epithelial cells, wherein they 
acquire mesenchymal traits such as enhanced motility and invasiveness. The activation of PPAR has been observed 
to initiate the process of EMT through the regulation of gene expression related to cell adhesion, extracellular matrix 
remodeling, and cytoskeletal architecture [30].
PPARs can also promote tumor progression by regulating angiogenesis. Angiogenesis is a critical process for tumor 
growth and metastasis, as it supplies the tumor with nutrients and oxygen. By increasing the synthesis of VEGF and 
other substances that encourage angiogenesis, PPAR has been proven to induce vascular development [29]. Another 
mechanism by which PPARs promote tumor progression is through modulation of the immune response. PPARs pos-
sess the ability to modulate the transcriptional activity of genes associated with the activation and differentiation of 
immune cells, including T-helper 1 (Th1) and T-helper 2 (Th2) cytokines [31]. PPARs possess the ability to modulate 
the production of anti-apoptotic genes, including Bcl-2, hence exerting regulatory control over cell survival [ 32]. 
Finally, PPARs can also regulate tumor progression by modulating the activity of other signaling cascades, such as 
the Wnt/β-catenin cascade. As an illustration, it has been observed that di-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate exhibits PPAR 
activation properties in rodent models, but such effects have not been observed in human cell lines [33].
3.2  CYP1B1
It is a member of the cytochrome P450 superfamily of enzymes and plays a crucial role in the biotransformation of 
xenobiotics and endogenous compounds. Overexpressed CYP1B1 has been linked to tumor progression in several 
investigations conducted in recent years. The molecular mechanism underlying CYP1B1-mediated tumor progres-
sion involves several key processes [34]. One mechanism by which CYP1B1 promotes tumor progression is through 
the activation of the aAhR cascade. CYP1B1 can metabolize PAHs found in tobacco smoke and other environmental 
pollutants to generate reactive intermediates that can activate the AhR cascade. AhR cascade has been associated 
with various cellular processes that are implicated in the growth of tumors. These processes include enhanced cell 
proliferation, angiogenesis, and immunological suppression [35].
CYP1B1 can also promote tumor progression by regulating the metabolism of steroid hormones, such as estrogens. 
CYP1B1 can metabolize estradiol to generate 4-hydroxyestradiol, which can promote cell proliferation and angiogen-
esis by the expression of PI3K/AKT signaling cascades. CYP1B1 can also metabolize androgen to generate metabolites 
that activate the androgen receptor, which plays a crucial role in prostate cancer progression [36]. Another mechanism 
by which CYP1B1 promotes tumor progression is through the regulation of the cell cycle. CYP1B1 can regulate the 
expression of genes involved in cell cycle progression, such as cyclin D1, cyclin E, and CDK2, leading to increased 
cell proliferation [37]. CYP1B1 can also inhibit apoptosis by regulating the expression of anti-apoptotic genes, such 
as Bcl-2, and surviving. The expression of genes associated with the Wnt/β-catenin cascade can be regulated by 
CYP1B1, resulting in enhanced tumor development and metastasis. Finally, CYP1B1 can promote tumor progression

3.3  CAR 
CAR, alternatively referred to as NR1I3, is a constituent of the nuclear receptor superfamily, which encompasses transcrip-
tion factors responsible for modulating the expression of genes implicated in drug metabolism and the maintenance 
of energy balance [39]. Recent research findings have indicated that CAR executes a significant role in the development 
of cancer, specifically in facilitating tumor proliferation and conferring resistance to chemotherapy treatments. The 
molecular mechanism underlying CAR-mediated tumor progression involves several key processes. One mechanism by 
which CAR promotes tumor progression is through the regulation of drug metabolism. CAR can activate the expression 
of drug-metabolizing enzymes, such as CYPs and UGTs, leading to increased metabolism and elimination of chemo -
therapeutic drugs. This can result in reduced efficacy of chemotherapy and the development of drug resistance [40].
The role of CAR in tumor growth can be facilitated through its regulation of endogenous chemical metabolism. As an 
illustration, the CAR can induce the transcription of genes associated with the metabolic cascades of bile acids. These 
molecules have been linked to the etiology and advancement of colon and liver malignancies [41]. CAR can also regulate 
the biotransformation of fatty acids and glucose, which are important for energy metabolism in cancer cells. Another 
mechanism by which CAR promotes tumor progression is through the regulation of cell proliferation and apoptosis. CAR 
can activate the expression of genes involved in cell cycle progressions, such as cyclin D1 and CDK4, leading to increased 
cell proliferation [42]. By upregulating anti-apoptotic genes like Bcl-2 and Bcl-Xl levels of anti-apoptotic genes like Bcl-2 
and Bcl-xL, CAR can also prevent cells from committing suicide. CAR can also promote tumor progression by modulat -

Wnt/-catenin cascade. The Akt/mTOR cascade, which controls cell growth and metabolism, can be activated by CAR as 
well [43]. CAR has the ability to modulate the immune response within the tumor microenvironment. It can induce the

3.4  TGF‑β
TGF-β is a cytokine that plays a crucial role in the regulation of cell growth, differentiation, and apoptosis. Dysregula-
tion of TGF-β2 signaling has been implicated in the development and progression of cancer [45]. In this portion, we will 
discuss the molecular mechanism of TGF-β2-mediated SAB-β-Gal and SM22 in tumor progression in the pathogenesis of 
cancer. Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) serves as a biomarker for cellular senescence, a phenomenon 
characterized by permanent growth cessation in response to diverse assault stimuli [46]. The induction of senescence 
in both normal and cancer cells has been demonstrated through the overexpression of CDKIs, p16INK4a, and p21CIP1/
WAF1 by TGF-β [47]. In addition, TGF-β2 can induce the expression of SA-β-Gal by upregulating lysosomal biogenesis 
and secretion [48]. Senescence induced by TGF-β2 can have both tumor-suppressive and tumor-promoting effects, 
depending on the context and timing of TGF-β2 signaling. SM22 is a marker of smooth muscle cells, which are important 
components of blood vessels and other tissues. TGF-β2 has been shown to promote the differentiation of smooth muscle 
cells and the formation of blood vessels [49].
In addition, TGF-β2 can elicit the upregulation of SM22 gene expression in several cellular populations, including 
malignant cells. The involvement of SM22 has been identified in the modulation of cellular migration and invasion, 
as well as in the pathogenesis and advancement of diverse malignancies, such as breast, lung, and pancreatic cancers 
[50]. The molecular mechanism underlying TGF-β2-mediated SAB-β-Gal and SM22 in tumor progression involves

3.5  Nrf2
The transcription factor known as Nrf2 is of significant importance in the control of cellular defense mechanisms 
against oxidative and xenobiotic assaults. Dysregulation of Nrf2 signaling has been implicated in the development 
and progression of cancer. In this portion, we will discuss the molecular mechanism of Nrf2-mediated tumor pro -
gression in the pathogenesis of cancer. Nrf2 exerts a crucial role in the regulation of several genes associated with 
the antioxidant response, detoxification processes, and anti-inflammatory cascades [54]. In typical physiological 
circumstances, the activity of Nrf2 is suppressed by its cytosolic inhibitor, known as Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 
1 (Keap1), which promotes the destruction of Nrf2 via the ubiquitin–proteasome system. Upon encountering oxida-
tive assault or exposure to electrophilic chemicals, the Nrf2 undergoes dissociation from Keap1 and subsequently 
relocates to the nucleus. Within the nucleus, Nrf2 binds to specific areas known as antioxidant response elements 
(AREs) located in the promoter regions of target genes. Consequently, leads to the expression of transcription for 
these target genes [55].
In cancer, Nrf2 can be overexpressed or constitutively activated, leading to the upregulation of genes involved in cell 
proliferation, survival, and drug resistance [56]. Nrf2 has been shown to promote tumor growth and metastasis in various 
types of cancer, including lung, breast, liver, and pancreatic cancer. Initially, it ought to be clarified that Nrf2 possesses the 
ability to modulate the transcriptional activity of genes implicated in the control of cellular cycle advancement, including 
cyclin D1, cyclin E1, and CDK4/6. Nrf2 can suppress the transcription of genes associated with programmed cell death, 
including Bax and caspase-3, while simultaneously enhancing the transcription of genes that promote cell survival, such 
as Bcl-2 and Mcl-1 [57]. Furthermore, the transcription factor Nrf2 can modulate the transcription of genes that play a 
crucial role in drug metabolism and detoxification processes. These genes include glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), 
UGTs, and multidrug resistance-associated proteins (MRPs). The overexpression of these genes may contribute to the 
emergence of drug resistance in cancer cells. Third, Nrf2 may influence the production of genes including IL-6, IL-8, and 
programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) that are implicated in inflammation and immune evasion [58]. The overexpression 
of these genes has the potential to facilitate the progression of tumors and the spread of cancer cells by augmenting pro-
cesses such as angiogenesis, invasion, and immune evasion. Understanding the molecular mechanism underlying Nrf2-
mediated tumor progression is important for identifying potential targets for cancer prevention and treatment (Fig.  3).
Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytoplasm, bound to its repressor Keap1. Upon oxidative stress or exposure to electrophiles, 
this interaction is disrupted, leading to Nrf2 stabilization and accumulation [59]. Nrf2 then translocates to the nucleus, 
where it forms heterodimers with small musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma proteins. This complex binds to AREs in the 
promoters of target genes, initiating their transcription. Once activated, Nrf2 induces a range of cytoprotective genes, 
including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), which degrades heme into biliverdin and other byproducts that mitigate oxidative 
damage [60]. It also regulates NAD(P)H oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), essential for detoxifying reactive quinones, and GSTs, 
which aid in detoxifying harmful substances by conjugating glutathione. By upregulating these antioxidants, Nrf2 helps 
cells neutralize ROS, reducing the oxidative damage caused by platinum-based chemotherapeutics, such as cisplatin. 
This antioxidant response allows cancer cells to resist chemotherapy. Nrf2 also promotes metabolic reprogramming, 
favoring glycolysis and fatty acid synthesis, which provides energy for cell proliferation during stress. Additionally, Nrf2 
inhibits apoptosis by regulating anti-apoptotic factors, allowing cancer cells to evade chemotherapy-induced cell death 
and contributing to chemoresistance [61].

4  Xenobiotic‑induced oxidative assault and carcinogenesis
Lead, arsenic, and cadmium are heavy metals known for their toxicity, which can result in the initiation of oxidative 
assault and subsequent manifestation of many health complications. Exposure to lead has been found to be corre -
lated with impairments in several physiological systems, such as the neurological system, cardiovascular system, and 
reproductive systems. The influence of lead on oxidative assault is evident in both the brain and liver, as evidenced 
by a positive correlation between greater lead concentrations and elevated levels of lipid peroxidation [62]. The 
heme synthesis cascade is impeded by the presence of lead, which interferes with the functioning of enzymes such 
as δ-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase and ferrochelatase [ 63]. Consequently, this disruption results in the production 
of ROS. Arsenic, an extensively researched foreign substance metal, induces oxidative assault through the generation 
of ROS, resulting in cellular harm and the initiation of oxidative-responsive signaling cascades [64].
Several investigations have identified the presence of superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals in 
several cell types, including HEL30 cells, NB4 cells, and CHOK1 cells when exposed to arsenic [65]. Superoxide is 
observed as the principal species that is generated by arsenic, initiating a cascade phenomenon that leads to the 
production of other ROS through dismutation and Fenton reactions. In contrast, cadmium does not directly produce 
free radicals that are linked to the occurrence of oxidative assault. However, it can be inferred that it indirectly results 
in the generation of superoxide, hydroxyl radicals, and nitric oxide radicals. Cadmium has the ability to stimulate the 
generation of hydrogen peroxide, a non-radical compound [66]. This hydrogen peroxide then acts as a reservoir of 
free radicals via a process known as Fenton chemistry. The indirect mechanism of cadmium involves the displace -
ment of iron and Cu ions within cellular proteins, resulting in the liberation of unbound iron or Cu ions. These ions 
play an active role in the process of oxidative assault through Fenton reactions, hence adding to the carcinogenic 
consequences of cadmium [67].

molecular mechanism underlying ROS-mediated pathogenesis of cell proliferation and impaired DNA repair involves 
several key processes [68]. One mechanism by which ROS promotes cell proliferation is through the expression of the

factor-1α (HIF-1α) protein, activated in response to hypoxia [71]. HIF-1α is responsible for the regulation of gene expres-
sion related to crucial biological processes such as angiogenesis, glucose biotransformation, and cell survival. ROS-
induced oxidative assault can stabilize HIF-1α, leading to increased cell proliferation and tumor growth. ROS can also 
impair DNA repair mechanisms by damaging DNA and interfering with the function of key repair enzymes. ROS can 
instigate damage to DNA, encompassing single-strand breaks, double-strand breaks, and base alterations. These detri-
mental effects have the potential to create genetic mutations and chromosomal abnormalities [72]. Furthermore, it has 
been observed that ROS can directly hinder the activity of DNA repair enzymes, specifically those involved in the base 
excision repair (BER) cascade. This hindrance occurs by oxidizing crucial cysteine residues in these enzymes [73].
ROS can also promote DNA methylation, which can lead to epigenetic changes that impair DNA repair. ROS-induced 
oxidative assault can promote the formation of 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), which can then be converted to 
5-methylcytosine (5mC) by the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) enzyme [74]. Increased DNA methylation can impair 
DNA repair by inhibiting the binding of repair enzymes to assaulted DNA. In summary, ROS-induced oxidative assault can 
promote cell proliferation and impair DNA repair mechanisms by activating MAPK signaling cascades, stabilizing HIF-1α, 
damaging DNA interfering with the function of key repair enzymes, and promoting DNA methylation. These mechanisms 
contribute to the pathogenesis of cancer and other diseases associated with ROS-induced oxidative assault (Fig.  4).
The effect of xenobiotics including arsenic, chromium, nickel, cadmium, and aflatoxin on oxidative assault and car -
cinogenesis at the molecular level. These toxic chemicals can cause an increase in ROS, which then attack cells via oxi-
dative assault. The xenobiotics cause DNA damage, mutations, and genomic instability by upsetting the cellular redox 
equilibrium and weakening the antioxidant defense mechanisms [75]. To reduce cancer risk from occupational and 
environmental carcinogens, proper safety measures and laws are required.
5  Heavy metal xenobiotics induced cancer: ROS mediated mechanisms
Various heavy metal xenobiotics and their ROS-mediated mechanisms in cancer pathogenesis are explained below;
5.1  Arsenic
Arsenic, a hazardous metalloid, exhibits widespread distribution in the environment. The act of being exposed to arsenic 
has been linked to an elevated susceptibility to many forms of cancers. The molecular mechanism underlying arsenic-
induced carcinogenesis is complex and involves multiple cellular processes. One of the key mechanisms by which arsenic 
promotes carcinogenesis is through the generation of oxidative assault. Arsenic can stimulate the expression of ROS 
and RNS, which can assault cellular macromolecules, including DNA, proteins, and lipids [76]. Arsenic-induced oxida-
tive assault can lead to DNA assault and mutations, consequently the development of cancer. In addition to its role in 
generating oxidative assault, arsenic can also disrupt cellular signaling cascades that are critical for maintaining cellular 
homeostasis [77].
Arsenic can activate various signaling cascades, including the MAPK, the PI3K/Akt, and the NF-κB cascades. Arsenic 
can also interfere with DNA repair mechanisms, which can increase the accumulation of DNA assault and mutations [78]. 
The inhibitory effects of arsenic on the repair of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) have been demonstrated through its 
interference with the recruitment of repair proteins, including ATM and DNA-PK, to the specific locations of DSBs [79]. 
Moreover, arsenic can induce epigenetic changes that can alter gene expression and contribute to the development 
of cancer. The induction of DNA methylation and histone changes by arsenic has been demonstrated, resulting in the 
suppression of tumor suppressor genes and the stimulation of oncogenes.

development of cancer. Furthermore, cadmium exposure has been shown to interfere with the function of the

7  Therapeutic strategies
The efficient management of cancer necessitates a comprehensive comprehension of therapeutic approaches to address 
the intricate nature of the disease. This discussion explores many methodologies, including Chelation treatment, CAR-T 
cell therapy, Metal–Organic Frameworks (MOFs), and the therapeutic significance of fibronectin (Table 2). The investiga-
tion of these tactics sheds light on their potential to tackle the complex obstacles presented by cancer, hence highlighting 
the continuous pursuit of novel and efficient therapies.
7.1  Chelation treatment
It is a significant therapeutic approach utilized to manage the adverse consequences of heavy metal-induced toxicity, 
providing a method to alleviate the detrimental impacts caused by these toxic compounds. Various heavy metal ions 
exhibit different affinities for specific donor atoms present in ligands [127]. The variations in affinity observed play a 
fundamental role in the process of selecting chelating agents.
Numerous chelating agents have been discovered and implemented in the realm of clinical practise. The compound 
known as 2,3-Dimercapto-Propanesulphonate (DMPS) is being discussed. The aforementioned substance, which is soluble 
in water, is employed for the purpose of mitigating the toxic effects of arsenic and mercury [128]. Dimercaptosuccinic 
acid (DMSA) is a chemical compound commonly used in academic and scientific research. DMSA, often referred to as suc-
cimer, is composed of two carboxylic and two thiol groups, rendering it efficacious in the management of lead, mercury, 
and arsenic toxicity [129]. The chelating agent under consideration is a lipophilic C5-branched chain alkyl monoester 
of DMSA, which possesses the ability to traverse cell membranes and reach intracellular compartments. Recent studies 
have shown evidence of the effectiveness of this method in the process of chelation for the removal of arsenic, lead, 
cadmium, and mercury [130]. Both MchDMSA and MmDMSA are lipophilic compounds. MchDMSA is characterized by a 
cyclic carbon chain that is analogous to DMSA, while MmDMSA features a straight and branched chain methyl group that 
is analogous to DMSA. These chemicals have been shown to reduce cadmium and arsenic levels in living creatures [131].

7.3  Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) technique
MOFs, which belong to a category of porous materials, are utilized for the purpose of drug delivery in the context of

7.4  Therapeutic potential of fibronectin
Fibronectin (FN) has garnered considerable attention in the field of cancer research owing to its possible multifaceted 
involvement in cancer pathobiology [138]. Several research has put forth the notion that FN may serve as a tumor sup -
pressor, hindering the first stages of tumor formation. However, contrasting evidence suggests that FN is associated with 
cancer spread and a worse prognosis when found within tumor cells. In particular, the suppression of α5 and β1 integ-
rins, which play a crucial role in facilitating cell adhesion mediated by FN, has been shown to decrease the adherence of 
ovarian tumor cells in a three-dimensional model that incorporates primary cells and extracellular matrix constituents 
[139]. Additionally, the findings from various search results indicate a plausible correlation between protease inhibitors 
and FN in the context of cancer spread [138].
8  Defending against cancer with the symphony of heavy metals
The convoluted connection between heavy metals, especially Zn and Cu, has recently come under scrutiny in the field 
of cancer detection and prevention, particularly in relation to prostate cancer. Deficits in zinc, an important trace metal 
that is involved in many physiological processes, have been linked to negative health outcomes [151]. Nevertheless, zinc’s 
association with prostate cancer is multifaceted. A number of studies have shown that zinc may help prevent prostate

families are essential for maintaining zinc homeostasis within cells. Two proteins, ZIP (SLC39A) and ZnT (SLC30A), are

functions in zinc control are performed by these transporters, which are expressed either ubiquitously or selectively in 
different tissues. Specifically, a clinical trial that included nearly 700 men with prostate cancer found that adequate zinc 
absorption was associated with a decreased risk of prostate cancer [153]. Multiple studies have explored the link between 
zinc supplements and prostate cancer. A US case–control study found zinc reduced cancer risk, showing a dose–response

autophagy, cancer treatment, and Cu. However, due to the cytotoxic effects of excess Cu, Cu ionophores like disulfiram

development [157].
There is evidence that gold complexes, such as gold nanoparticles, can cause cancer cells to die by apoptotic and 
non-apoptotic means. The p53 cascade is one of the important signaling molecules and cascades for gold-induced cell 
death [158]. In this cascade, p53 is activated by gold complexes, which in turn increase ROS levels and p21 expression, 
which ultimately leads to cell death. Further, gold complexes are very effective TrxR inhibitors, which means they slow 
the proliferation of cancer cells by interfering with the thioredoxin redox cascade and, in the end, killing the cells [159]. 
Another channel that gold complexes use to regulate cellular reactions to oxidative assault and cause apoptosis in cancer 
cells is the ASK1-p38-MAPK signaling axis [160]. Further, mitochondrial malfunction, including free radical production and 
mitochondrial membrane potential disturbance, is linked to gold-induced cell death. Researchers Coulter et al. investi-
gated the potential of maytansine analogue DM1 (MTC-100038) in combination with ultrasmall gold core nanoparticles 
(2 nm) to treat hepatocellular cancer in morin [161].
In the field of biomedicine, Silver (Ag) nanoparticles have been combined with photoacoustic imaging to create a new 
method for identifying and validating the nanoparticles’ intended role in cancerous tissues. In vivo tumor assessment 
using foliate-modified Ag nanoparticles, which improved photoacoustic contrast with normal tissues, showed promise. A 
composite substrate of high-sensitivity thermally annealed silver nanoparticles and porous silicon bragg mirror (AgNPs/
PSB) was used to improve serum Raman spectroscopy (SERS) by Yang et al. (2023) [162]. With more pronounced spectral 
peaks than conventional Raman spectroscopy, this method shows promise for identifying new breast cancer biomarkers. 
A machine learning algorithm and improved SERS substrate distinguished breast cancer patients from healthy controls. 
Noorbazargan et al. (2021) found that silver nanoparticles (9.87 µg/mL) induced nuclear fragmentation, apoptosis, and 
elevated p53 expression in lung cancer cells, suggesting therapeutic potential [163].
9  Conclusion
Lead, arsenic, and cadmium are examples of heavy metals that are particularly carcinogenic because of the oxidative 
assault they cause and the cellular cascades they can disrupt. Heavy metals can have deleterious effects on the human 
body, although chelating agents like DMSA, DMPS, MiADMSA, MchDMSA, and MmDMSA have shown they can remove 
these metals from the body. CAR-T cell modification to specifically target heavy metal-related malignancies is a cutting-
edge approach to cancer treatment that shows promise for improving outcomes. In the case of heavy metal-induced 
cancers, MOFs have shown great promise as efficient drug delivery vehicles, opening the door to potentially game-
changing advances in treatment strategies. Finding FN to be a potential treatment for cancer opens up new doors. Heavy 
metal-induced cancers may be combatted with strategies that particularly target FN, such as preventing tumor cells from 
adhering to FN or reducing FN production in the extracellular matrix. Cancer pathogenesis research has also focused 
on several other biomarkers, such as ROS, PPARs, CYP1B1, CAR, TGFβ2, Nrf2, ALK, and EZH2. Early warning symptoms 
of cancer can be found in alterations to these processes, which may be genetic, epigenetic, proteomic, or metabolic

CAR-T cell immunotherapy, which is beneficial, faces toxicity issues such as cytokine release syndrome and off-target 
effects. MOFs, which could be drugs carriers, become unstable in physiological conditions and produce harmful metal 
ions. Fibronectin, an essential extracellular matrix component, has limitations that promote cancer development and 
inflammation. These limitations highlight the need to develop and optimize these therapeutic techniques to increase 
clinical safety and efficacy. The development of effective cancer treatments that can target cancer caused by heavy 
metals relies on a thorough understanding of these biological cascades. Last but not least, heavy metals such as zinc, 
Cu, gold, bismuth, and silver have several uses that show they could be good ways to manage cancer. The plethora of 
applications that these heavy metals provide, from improved diagnostic tools to tailored drug administration, bode well 
for the future of cancer treatment.
