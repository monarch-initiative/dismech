# Two novel cases of biallelic SMPD4 variants with brain structural abnormalities.
**Authors:** Aoki S, Watanabe K, Kato M, Konishi Y, Kubota K, Kobayashi E, Nakashima M, Saitsu H
**Journal:** Neurogenetics (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10048-023-00737-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10048-023-00737-5)

## Content

Sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase 4 (SMPD4) encodes a member of the Mg2+-dependent, neutral sphingomyelinase family that catalyzes the hydrolysis of the phosphodiester bond of sphingomyelin to form phosphorylcholine and ceramide. Recent studies have revealed that biallelic loss-of-function variants of SMPD4 cause syndromic neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by microcephaly, congenital arthrogryposis, and structural brain anomalies. In this study, three novel loss-of-function SMPD4 variants were identified using exome sequencing (ES) in two independent patients with developmental delays, microcephaly, seizures, and brain structural abnormalities. Patient 1 had a homozygous c.740_741del, p.(Val247Glufs*21) variant and showed profound intellectual disability, hepatomegaly, a simplified gyral pattern, and a thin corpus callosum without congenital dysmorphic features. Patient 2 had a compound heterozygous nonsense c.2124_2125del, p.(Phe709*) variant and splice site c.1188+2dup variant. RNA analysis revealed that the c.1188+2dup variant caused exon 13 skipping, leading to a frameshift (p.Ala406Ser*6). In vitro transcription analysis using minigene system suggested that mRNA transcribed from mutant allele may be degraded by nonsense-mediated mRNA decay system. He exhibited diverse manifestations, including growth defects, muscle hypotonia, respiratory distress, arthrogryposis, insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus, sensorineural hearing loss, facial dysmorphism, and various brain abnormalities, including cerebral atrophy, hypomyelination, and cerebellar hypoplasia. Here, we review previous literatures and discuss the phenotypic diversity of SMPD4-related disorders.

Introduction
Sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase 4  (SMPD4) encodes  neutral sphingomyelinase s-3
(nSMase3), which is a member of the Mg2+-dependent, neutral sphingomyelinase family
that mediates  the hydrolysis of membrane sphingomyelin to form phosphorylcholine
and ceramide  [1, 2] . nSMase3 is localized to  the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi
apparatus, and nuclear pore s [2-5], and it has been suggested that nSMase3 is activated
by tumor necrosis factor and is likely to be involved in cell stress-induced growth arrest
and apoptosis [2, 6] . However, the detailed protein functions of nSMase3 remain
elusive.
Recent studies have reported that b iallelic SMPD4 variants cause
neurodevelopmental disorder s with microcephaly, arthrogryposis, and structural br ain
anomalies (OMIM #618622) , which are characterized by progressive microcephaly,
facial dysmorphism, simplified gyration, hypomyelination, a thin corpus callosum , and
congenital arthrogryposis  [7-14]. To date,  35 individuals from 22 families have been
reported. They show various additional phenotypes, including  skeletal abnormalities,
congenital heart defect s, diabetes mellitus, cerebellar or brainstem hypoplasia, severe
encephalopathy, intrauterine growth retardation, and neonatal respiratory distress, often
leading to death at a young age . Genetic analyses revealed five missense variants, four
splice sites, three splice region variants, f ive nonsense variants, six frameshift variants,
and four chromosomal microdeletions encompassing the SMPD4 gene.
Here, we report the cases of two unrelated individuals with biallelic SMPD4 variants
showing congenital microcephaly, abnormal gyration , corpus callosum  hypoplasia,
seizures, and severe global developmental delay.  Both patients had novel

3

loss-of-function variants but presented different phenotypes. Here, w e compare these
cases with previous cases and discuss the clinical features of SMPD4-related
syndromes.

Subjects and Methods
Exome sequencing (ES)
After obtaining written informed consent,  detailed clinical information and peripheral
blood samples from the two patients and their parents were obtained by the physicians
overseeing them. The experimental protocols were approved by the institutional review
boards of Showa University School of Medicine and Hamamatsu University School of
Medicine. The genomic DNA extracted from the blood leukocytes of the patient s was
captured using an xGen Exome Research Panel kit (IDT, Coralville , IA) and sequenced
using NextSeq  500 (Illumina) with 75-bp paired -end reads . Exome data processing,
variant calling, and variant annotation were performed as described  previously [15].
Candidate variants were confirmed using Sanger sequencing  with an ABI 3 500xL
Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from  the peripheral whole blood of family 2 and a healthy
control individual  using a Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit (New ENGLAND
BioLabs) and subjected to reverse transcription using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit
(TAKARA BIO), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. We designed target-specific

4

primers for SMPD4 in exons 12 and 15 to confirm aberrant splicing . TA cloning of the
PCR products was performed as described previously [16].

Minigene splicing assay
A m inigene-splicing assay was performed to assess  the effect of the c.1188+2dup
variant on RNA splicing. A 2187-bp DNA fragment of SMPD4 spanning exons 12 to 15
was amplified from genomic DNA derived from patient 2 with target-specific primers
carrying restriction sites for EcoRI and KpnI; forward primer:5’-
CTGAGAATTCATGAAGCCAGAGCAGGCCTCA-3’, and reverse primer: 5’-
CGCGGTACCAGACTTTGGCCACTCGGAACA-3’. Amplified DNA fragments were
cloned into the EGFP-N2 vector using EcoRI and KpnI sites. We selected both
wild-type (WT) and variant (VT) plasmid vectors using colony PCR and confirmed the
sequences using Sanger sequencing. HEK293T cells were grown to 80% confluence in
6-well plates and transiently transfected with 2 μg of WT or VT plasmids using the PEI
MAX reagent (Polysciences, PA, USA) in triplicate.  After 48 h of incubation, total
RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and
cDNAs were synthesized as described above.

Results
Case report
Patient 1 was the first child born to unrelated , healthy parents. The pregnancy was
uneventful and she delivered spontaneously at 40 weeks of gestation with no asphyxia.
Her body weight was 2,385 g ( -2.2 standard deviation [SD]), height was 48.5 cm ( -0.7

5

SD), and head circumference was 30.6 cm ( -2.3 SD). She experienced generalized tonic
convulsions at 4 days of age. The seizures temporarily disappeared with phenobarbital;
however, epileptic spasms with hypsarrhythmia on electroencephalography (EEG)
occurred at 6 months of age. The administration of pyridoxal phosphate, valproic acid ,
and zonisamide was ineffective. Intramuscular injections of adrenocorticotrophic
hormone improved the EEG findings and resolved epileptic spasms ; however , focal
seizures persisted. She obtained eye pursuit s for the object at 5 months and a social
smile at 6 months. She had central sleep hypopnea and required mechanical ventilation
with synchronized intermittent  mandatory ventilation mode during nighttime at three
years and 10 months  of age.  Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed
simplified gyral pattern , thin corpus callosum , prominently delayed myelination , but
neither cerebellar hypoplasia  nor progressive changes  (Fig. 1A-D). A t 4 years and  7
months of age, her height was 96.5 cm ( -1.7 SD), body weight was 12.3 kg ( -1.9 SD),
and head circumference was 42.5 cm ( -5.3 SD). The patient had hepatomegaly and a
vertical talus. Interictal EEG revealed no epileptic discharges. However, she
experienced daily focal seizures while receiving levetiracetam, clonazepam, and
perampanel. She had spastic quadriplegia and profound intellectual disability, with
neither head control nor meaningful words.
Patient 2 was an 11-year-old boy. He was born through induced vaginal delivery at
41 weeks and 1 day of gestation after fetal growth restriction. The measurement of birth
weight, le ngth, and head circumference was 2370  g ( -3.06 SD), 46.5  cm ( -1.92 SD),
31.0 cm ( -2.1 SD), respectively. He showed micrognathia, blepharophimosis, and
arthrogryposis with hypotonia in all four limbs,  but no  cardiac defects. A neonatal

6

screening test  using automated auditory brainstem response suggested hearing
impairment, and no response was observed at 100 dB in the bilateral auditory brainstem
response. Owing to respiratory distress and dyspha gia, tracheostomy and gastrostomy
were performed at 6 months  and 3 years of age , respectively. His respiration  was
assisted by a ventilator  in the synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation mode.
The patient showed severe developmental delay and multiple joint contractures with
limited spontaneous movements. Hypermotor seizure s such as  crawling movements of
both arms occurred at the age of 4 weeks. E EG revealed paroxysmal discharges in the
frontal lobe. The antiepileptic drugs valproate and clobazam were initially found to be
effective against seizures. Intractable fever-induced status epilepticus started at 1 years
of age. Supplementary motor area seizure s occurred at 8 years of age  and diminished
with perampanel administration. He was diagnosed with i nsulin-dependent diabetes
mellitus (IDDM) at 6 years of age, which led  to hospitalization due to ketoacidosis .
Glucose, HbA1c, and glycated albumin levels were highly elevated (631  mg/dL, 9.6%,
and 29.1%, respectively). I slet-associated autoantibodies including glutamic acid
decarboxylase, islet antigen -2, and insulin were absent. However, both oral
hypoglycemic agents (metformin hydrochloride and sitagliptin phosphate hydrate ) and
subcutaneous injection of a rapid-acting insulin analog  are needed for  blood sugar
control. Brain MRI showed diffuse brain atrophy, simplified gyral pattern , thin corpus
callosum, prominently delayed myelination , and mild cerebellar hypoplasia  (Fig. 1
E-H).

Genetic analyses

7

We performed case -only ES for the two patients and found three novel SMPD4
(NM_017951.5) variants: a h omozygous frameshift variant  (c.740_741del,
p.(Val247Glufs*21)) in  patient 1 and compound heterozygous variants (c.1188+2dup
and c.2124_2125del, p.(Phe709*)) in patient 2. Sanger sequencing confirmed that each
pathogenic allele segregated from its health y carrier parents to the probands (Fig. 2A
and B). These variants were absent in our 218 in-house Japanese control exome datasets
and public databases, including the Genome Aggregation Database v3.1.1
(http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) and 38KJPN Allele Frequency Panel
(https://jmorp.megabank.tohoku.ac.jp/). The c.1188+2dup variant is a duplication of the
T nucleotide in the canonical splice donor site and  was predicted  to cause abnormal
mRNA splicing using the SpliceAI program  (loss [ acceptor] 0. 70, loss [donor] 0.98,
gain [donor] 0.46). RT-PCR with cDNAs derived from patient 2 and his parents showed
that two differently sized products  were amplified in patient 2 and his carrier father
(Figure 2C). Sequence analysis of the cloned PCR products revealed that  the
c.1188+2dup variant caused exon 13 skipping, generating a premature stop codon at the
372nd residue (p.Ala367Ser*6) (Figure 2D). Based on the American College of Medical
Genetics Standards and Guidelines , all three variants (PVS1, PM2, and PM3) are
classified as pathogenic . These findings suggest that SMPD4 variants were likely
causative in our cases.

In vitro splicing assays
Gel images of the RT-PCR products indicated that the band intensity of VT was  weaker
than that of the WT (Fig. 2C). We hypothesized that this difference may be caused by

8

mutant mRNA  decomposition via nonsense -mediated mRNA decay  (NMD) or partial
aberrant transcription from the mutated allele. To test th is hypothesis, we performed  in
vitro splicing assays using SMPD4 minigene construct (Fig. 3A ). To specifically
amplify cDNAs derived from the mini gene vector, we designed PCR primers for
SMPD4 exon 12 and GFP sequences (Fig. 3A). RT-PCR with WT cDNAs showed only
a single band, whereas VT showed two different -sized amplification products, in which
shorter product was dominantly amplified  (Fig. 3B ). Sanger sequencing revealed that
sequence of longer and shorter products  matched the canonical spliced mRNA
sequence, including exons 1 2 to 15, and the abnormally spliced mRNA sequence
skipped exon 13 (Fig. 3B). Considering these results, we assumed that exon 13 skipped
mRNA was dominantly transcribed from mutant allele but that it may be degraded by
the NMD system in vivo, leading to low expression in the patient’s blood leukocytes.

Discussion
Including our two patients, 35 affected patients from 22 unrelated families with biallelic
pathogenic SMPD4 variants have been described  [7-14]. We summarized the clinical
manifestations of our two patients and previously reported cases are summarized  in
Table 1.  Our patient s shared clinical features similar to those of previous patients,
including developmental delay, congenital microcephaly , seizures, skeletal
abnormalities, and brain structural abnormalities , including progressive microcephaly,
delayed myelination, simplified gyration, and a t hin corpus callosum. In this study,
patient 2 showed additional some SMPD4-related syndrome characteristic features
including facial dysmorphisms, congenital arthrogryposis, intrauterine growth

9

retardation, and persistent respiratory distress. IDDM was a characteristic finding
observed in patients who survived beyond infancy  [7, 12] and patient 2 also developed
IDDM at 6 years of age . Conversely, patient 1 exhibited none of the aforementioned
characteristic features.
About half of the individuals with SMPD4 variants died during the prenatal period
(including terminated pregnancy, 6/3 5) or under one year of age (11/3 3), and the other
patients survived to school age or adulthood. Patients with homozygous missense and
compound heterozygous missense and splice region variants tended to survive longer
and showed relatively mild phenotypes  [7, 12]. However, the opposite trend was also
observed; some patients with homozygous missense variants died during the prenatal or
neonatal period [10], whereas patients with homozygous truncating variants survived to
school age  [7, 12] . O ur two patients with truncating variants also showed severe
neurological phenotypes, but  survived to school age. These findings suggest that the
phenotypes of  SMPD4-related syndromes are highly variable, and the
genotype-phenotype correlation remains unpredictable.
SMPD4 is ubiquitously expressed in human tissues , but predominantly in cardiac and
skeletal muscles [2]. SMPD4 is also expressed in the fetal brain ; however, its protein
expression gradually declines during brain development  [7]. Sphingolipids are highly
enriched in the nervous system and are important for proper brain development and
function; therefore, SMPD4 deficiency may impair nervous system development ,
leading to brain structural abnormalities and various neurological problems [17, 18].
Previous studies have implicated elevated nSMase3 activity  in skeletal muscle
atrophy [19-22] and insulin resistance  [23, 24] , leading to exercise intolerance,

10

respiratory disorders, chronic heart failure, and diabetes mellitus, some of which were
observed in patients with SMPD4 variants. In addition , intraperitoneal injection of a
neutral sphingomyelinase  inhibitor or adeno -associated virus serotype 9 containing
small hairpin RNA targeting SMPD4 mRNA in heart failure rats with reduced ejection
fraction revealed that the inhibition of nSMase3 activity or suppression of SMPD4
mRNA with small hairpin RNA prevented skeletal muscle weakness and relieved the
phenotypes [25]. Injection of a hypoglycemic compound in to diabetic mice improved
insulin resistance and downregulated Smpd4 expression [26]. Although p atients with
loss-of-function (LoF) SMPD4 variants are generally considered to lack nSMase3,  the
reason for these conflicting findings remain unclear.  Evaluation of nSMase3 activity
using cultured fibroblasts derived from individuals with SMPD4 LoF variants also
demonstrated no significant alteration of  sphingomyelin and ceramide levels despite of
the neutral sphingomyelinase activity depletion  [12]. Furthermore, acid
sphingomyelinase (ASM) knockout mice , a mouse model of the eponym ous
Niemann-Pick disease  caused by biallelic LoF SMPD1 variants, showed elevated
ceramide levels despite the deficit of ASM a ctivity [27]. Considering these findings, it
is hypothesized that compensatory activation of other sphingomyelinases  may occur .
However, each other neutral sphingomyelinase, nSMase1, nSMase2 and
mitochondrial-associated nSMase, is localized to different cell compartment, the
endoplasmic reticulum, the Golgi apparatus and plasma membrane, and mitochondria,
respectively [28-31]. Therefore, further analysis is required to elucidate this supposition.

Conclusion

11

We identified three novel loss-of-function SMPD4 variants in two Japanese patients
with congenital microcephaly, abnormal gyration, corpus callosum hypoplasia, seizures,
and severe global developmental delay. Our patients supported the phenotypic diversity
of SMPD4-related disorders with biallelic truncating SMPD4 variants; however, further
studies are required to fully elucidate the phenotype–genotype correlation.
