# Serum Amyloid A Binding to Glycosaminoglycans is Synergistic with Amyloid Formation: Therapeutic Targeting in the Inflammation-linked Amyloidosis.

Serum amyloid A (SAA), a small lipophilic plasma protein elevated in 
inflammation, is a precursor of amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis, the major 
life-threatening complication of chronic inflammation in animals and humans. 
Although heparan sulfate (HS) is a potent amyloid agonist, particularly in AA 
amyloidosis, therapeutic targeting of SAA-HS interactions using a small-molecule 
HS/heparin decoy was unsuccessful. To understand molecular underpinnings, we 
used recombinant lipid-free human and murine SAA1 and human SAA2 to explore 
their interactions with various glycosaminoglycans at pH 5.5-7.4 during amyloid 
formation, from native protein to amyloid oligomers and fibrils. Effects of pH 
and glycosaminoglycan sulfation/charge supported by prior computational studies 
indicate electrostatic origin of SAA-glycosaminoglycan interactions. HS/heparin 
can promote amyloidogenesis by inducing non-native β-sheet and apparently 
causing liquid droplet formation in SAA in solution. Structural and binding 
studies by spectroscopy and ELISA reveal previously unknown synergy between 
amyloid formation and heparin/HS binding by SAA. We propose that this synergy 
potentially extends to other protein amyloids and stems from longitudinal 
binding of HS polyanions to basic residue arrays on amyloid oligomers or 
fibrils. This binding mode explains our finding that a minimal heparin chain 
length exceeding 20 monosaccharides is necessary to compete with HS for binding 
to amyloid oligomers. The results help explain prior failure of a small-molecule 
drug in targeting of SAA-HS interactions and consider alternative HS-targeting 
approaches for AA and, potentially, other amyloid diseases.

Serum amyloid A (SAA, ~12 kDa) is a family of evolutionally conserved lipophilic proteins that are elevated in inflammation and act as acute-phase reactants [1–3]. In response to inflammatory stimuli, SAA is secreted systemically by the liver and locally by various tissues in vertebrates [4–6]. Upon injury, infection or acute inflammation, SAA levels in blood increase rapidly and dramatically (up to 1,000-fold within 24–48 hours) but transiently and are proposed to modulate the onset and the resolution of inflammation [3,5,7,8]. In chronic inflammatory diseases including autoimmune disorders, cardiovascular disease, chronic infections, and obesity-associated inflammation, blood levels of SAA are moderately but persistently elevated (up to 5-fold) [6,9]. Numerous beneficial functions have been attributed to this small protein in host defense and inflammation control, particularly in lipid transport and signaling during inflammation [3,8]. We proposed that SAA can act as a detergent to solubilize cell membrane debris and clear them from the sites of injury [10], while SAA complexes with lipids may act as lipoprotein hubs in inflammation control [7]. However, persistently elevated SAA levels can cause amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis in a fraction of patients with chronic inflammatory diseases [11–13]. AA amyloidosis is the major complication of chronic inflammation in humans and the major amyloid disease in animals [11–15]. In this life-threatening disorder, C-terminally truncated fragments of SAA, termed AA, form extracellular amyloid deposits mainly in the spleen, kidney and liver, causing organ failure if untreated. Current treatments include anti-bacterial and anti-inflammatory therapies to suppress SAA level, as well as the late-stage kidney transplantation [14]. These treatments are effective for most but not all patients, necessitating the development of additional therapeutic approaches.

Over 90% of circulating SAA is bound to lipoproteins in a highly α-helical conformation that is protected from misfolding; a small fraction of this exchangeable (water-soluble) protein is in a transient labile lipid-poor or lipid-free form that is prone to aggregation and forms the protein precursor of amyloid [8,16,17]. Besides lipids, other major factors influencing amyloid formation include pH [8,10] and interactions with the extracellular matrix components, which have specific effects on SAA aggregation and/or amyloid formation [18,19]. In particular, SAA interactions with heparan sulfate (HS), a highly sulfated glycosaminoglycan (GAG) in the extracellular matrix and in various amyloid deposits, modulate AA amyloidosis and provide its therapeutic target [20–23]. The causal role of HS in AA amyloidosis was first proposed by Kisilevsky and colleagues reporting temporal co-occurrence of HS/heparin and AA amyloid deposition at early stages of inflammation-linked amyloidosis [24,25] and was confirmed in subsequent studies [5,26,27]. Animal model studies showed that overexpression of heparinase, which cuts long chains of HS/heparin into shorter fragments, renders the mice resistant to AA amyloidosis [28]. In vitro studies showed that HS/heparin can dissociate SAA from protective complexes with lipids to generate the protein precursor of amyloid, promote a non-native β-sheet, and drive amyloid aggregation in vitro [27,29]. Taken together, these and other studies have strongly implicated SAA-HS interactions as a causative factor in the development of AA amyloidosis and its therapeutic target [20]. Blocking these interactions using small-molecule heparin analogues was suggested as a general strategy against AA and other amyloid diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease [23]. A small-molecule drug eprodisate (1,3-propanedisulfonic acid) was designed as a heparin decoy to bind SAA and block these interactions [30,31] but failed to show efficacy in phase-3 clinical trials (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01215747). The reasons for this failure were unclear, highlighting the need to better understand SAA-HS interactions during amyloid formation.

The pro-amyloidogenic effect of HS/heparin extends beyond SAA to many unrelated proteins that do not necessarily bind HS/heparin in their native states and are involved in systemic or neurodegenerative diseases [20,22,32–40]. HS co-deposits with diverse amyloid proteins in vivo and accelerates amyloid formation in vitro, apparently via scaffolding [41], suggesting that HS/heparin interacts with specific sites that are present on amyloid oligomers and fibrils but are absent from the native proteins [42]. Such heparin binding sites have been proposed based on numerous atomic structures of ex-vivo amyloid fibrils determined in recent years by cryogenic electron microscopy (cryoEM) [43,44], including AA fibrils [45–47]. All these fibrils contain parallel in-registry intermolecular β-sheet (PRIBS) motives wherein periodic arrays of identical residues from different molecules run along the fibril spine. HS/heparin interactions with PRIBS motives have been predicted [42] and observed by cryoEM [48] for a wide range of amyloid fibrils, wherein long polyanionic heparin chains bind laterally to periodic arrays of uncompensated basic residues along the fibril surface.

Such uncompensated basic arrays are present in cryoEM structures of tissue-derived amyloid fibrils of murine and human SAA isoform 1 (mSAA1 and hSAA1), a major amyloidogenic isoform in mice and men [45]. In silico studies by our team suggest that some extra densities seen in the cryoEM maps of mSAA1 and hSAA1 likely represent HS chains bound to these basic arrays via electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonds [42,49]. This longitudinal binding raises a question whether small-molecule drugs such as eprodisate can block such extensive electrostatic interactions. Another unresolved question is GAG-SAA interactions at early stages of amyloid formation as the protein converts from it native α-helix/random coil conformation to intermolecular amyloid β-sheets. Although two basic heparin-binding sites have been proposed based on the x-ray crystal structure of native lipid-free hSAA [50], the binding details are unknown. Of particular interest are GAG interactions with amyloid oligomers, which are metastable biologically active species with unknown atomic structures on pathway to fibrils [35,51].

To address these questions, here we explore SAA -GAG interactions at various stages of amyloid formation, from native proteins to amyloid oligomers and fibrils. Since AA amyloidosis was proposed to have lysosomal origin [10,52,53] while amyloid deposits extracellularly, we focus on pH 5.5 and pH 7.4 representing these compartments; moreover, kidney and spleen, the major sites of AA amyloid deposition, can undergo acidification during inflammation [54]. Three families of GAGs varying in charge/sulfation are found in AA amyloid deposits [53] and are explored: heparin that represents highly sulfated regions of HS, mono-sulfated chondroitin sulfate A (CSA), and non-sulfated hyaluronic acid (HA); we also explore the chain length effects of heparin (Supplemental Table S1). Prior studies have addressed aspects of GAG sulfation and chain length effects on amyloid formation by SAA or its fragments [18,19]; our comprehensive study couples a recently developed ELISA approach for quantitative GAG binding [55] with spectroscopic and electron microscopic analyses of mSAA1, hSAA1 and hSAA2 conformation and aggregation. Furthermore, we use this approach to test whether eprodisate and heparin-binding peptides can block heparin-SAA interactions at various stages of amyloid formation. The results help explain the failure of eprodisate in clinical trials and consider alternative approaches to inhibit heparin-amyloid interactions in SAA and, potentially, other amyloid-forming proteins.

Heparin binding by soluble mSAA1 (henceforth termed mSAA) was explored at pH 5.0–7.5. Major changes were observed between pH 5.5 and 7.0. The results at pH 7.4 and pH 5.5 representing pH ranges 7.0–7.5 and 5.0–5.5, respectively, are shown. For recombinant mSAA, heparin affinity chromatography showed greatly increased binding affinity and binding capacity at acidic vs. neutral pH (Fig. 1A). ELISA using heparin-coated plates independently verified increased mSAA binding at acidic vs. neutral pH (Fig. 1B). Similarly, recombinant hSAA1 from two different commercial sources, Millipore Sigma and Peprotech, showed increased heparin binding at pH 5.5 vs. pH 7.4 (Fig. 1C).

To determine the structural underpinnings for these pH effects, we explored the conformation and stability of mSAA in solution by far-UV circular dichroism (CD), Fourier transform infrared (FT IR) and fluorescence spectroscopy, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). At pH 7.4, mSAA is intrinsically disordered at temperatures T>25 °C but becomes partially α-helical below 25 °C with melting temperature Tm~18 °C, as detected in heating experiments monitored by CD [56] (Fig. 2A, B, blue circles). Murine and human SAA proteins form oligomers under these conditions ([57,58] and references therein). Interestingly, heating data of mSAA recorded by DSC and Trp fluorescence anisotropy showed a large endothermic order-to-disorder transition between 25 and 50 °C centered near 37 °C [56,59] (Fig. 2C, D, blue open circles). Since mSAA1 is in a random coil conformation at these temperatures and shows no secondary structural changes by far-UV CD, this transition did not represent protein unfolding and probably reflected melting of protein oligomers.

In the presence of heparin at pH 7.4 (blue solid lines), mSAA showed a better-ordered secondary structure of α+β type suggested by far-UV CD spectra (Fig. 2A, blue solid line). Furthermore, heparin induced small shifts in the heat-induced structural transitions detected by DSC, far-UV CD and fluorescence anisotropy (Fig. 2B–D). Moreover, ~50 nm droplets were observed by TEM at 22 °C in mSAA+heparin samples at pH 7.4 (Fig. 2E). Upon addition of heparin at pH 5.5, major changes in the structure and stability of mSAA were observed. CD spectra showed that the ordered secondary structure increased and was predominantly β-sheet, as evident form a single negative CD peak centered near 118–120 nm and a positive peak near 198 nm (Fig. 2A). This β-sheet was highly thermostable: a large increase in Tm, from 18 °C in mSAA alone to 82 °C in mSAA+heparin, was observed in the heating data recorded by CD, DSC and Trp fluorescence anisotropy (Fig. 2A–D, pink solid lines). TEM detected no large droplets in mSAA at pH 5.5 either with or without heparin but suggested smaller 8–10 nm oligomers in the presence of heparin (mSAA+hep, Fig. 2E).

FT IR spectra of mSAA were consistent with far-UV CD and showed that heparin induced ordered secondary structure rich in β-sheet, especially at acidic pH: upon addition of heparin, the β-sheet content increased from 12% to 24% at pH 7.4 and from 13% to 48% at pH 5.5 (Fig. 3A). Tables S1 and S2 summarize heparin-induced secondary structural changes in mSAA1 at pH 7.4 and 5.5. Additionally, heparin induced increase in Trp emission intensity at pH 5.5 suggesting decreased solvent quenching (Fig. 3B, pink lines), which is consistent with formation of well-ordered protein structure observed by CD and FT IR. Fluorescent dye anilino-naphthalene sulphonate (ANS) showed a large decrease in emission intensity indicating a decreased solvent-accessible hydrophobic surface area upon addition of heparin at either pH (Fig. 3C); moreover, ANS emission intensity was lower at pH 5.5 vs. 7.4 either with or without heparin, suggesting a more compact protein structure at pH 5.5. Heparin-induced changes in protein aggregation could contribute to these effects.

Taken together, the results in Figures 2 and 3 showed that heparin induces non-native β-sheet in the intrinsically disordered lipid-free mSAA in solution near 25 °C. Such a binding-induced folding is typical of intrinsically disordered proteins [60]. Notably, this β-sheet contrasts with the predominantly α-helical conformation observed in the X-ray crystal structures of mSAA and hSAA1 (~80% α-helix) [50,61], in mSAA1 in solution in the presence of a protein folding osmolyte trimethylamine-N-oxide (~80% α-helix), and in mSAA1-lipid complexes (45–55% α-helix) [62]. The heparin-induced β-sheet content was particularly high at pH 5.5 (48% in mSAA+heparin, Fig. 3A) and showed unusually high thermostability, with Tm of 82 °C (Fig. 2B–D). Notably, CD analysis of mSAA+heparin in solution showed that most of this β-sheet rapidly unfolded upon increasing pH from 5.5 to 7.4 (Fig. 2A, solid pink and blue lines). Therefore, despite its high thermostability, this β-sheet structure was distinct from amyloid. To our knowledge, such a heparin-induced highly thermostable non-native structure has not been reported previously. Furthermore, despite large variations in protein concentration (from 0.05 mg/ml in fluorescence to 0.1 mg/ml in CD, 1 mg/ml in DSC and ~2 mg/ml in FT IR), the results of different experiments showed similar heparin- and pH-dependent trends, suggesting that these trends did not stem from concentration-dependent changes in protein oligomerization and extended to a wide range of protein concentrations including those found in vivo (1 μg/ml – 10 mg/ml) [11,13].

Unlike mSAA that was intrinsically disordered at pH 5.5–7.4 (Fig. 2A), CD spectra of hSAA1 showed a partially ordered structure at pH 5.5 that became highly α-helical at pH 7.4 (Fig. S1). Heparin induced significant CD changes in hSAA1 at pH 5.5 but not at pH 7.4 (Fig. S1). The heparin-induced secondary structure observed at acidic pH in mSAA and hSAA1 could potentially contribute to stronger binding of these proteins to heparin at pH 5.5 vs. pH 7.4 (Fig. 1).

To determine how the major types of GAGs found in the extracellular matrix influence the solution conformation of mSAA and hSAA1, we added heparan sulfate (HS), chondroitin sulfate A (CSA), or hyaluronic acid (HA) to protein solutions at either pH 7.4 or pH 5.5 and assessed the secondary structure at 37 °C using CD spectroscopy. Supplemental Table S1 shows the chemical structures of these GAGs. Sulfation and negative charge increased in order HA < CSA < HS < heparin. In mSAA, all sulfated GAGs (heparin, HS and CSA) induced secondary structural ordering that was greater at pH 5.5 than pH 7.4. At either pH, ordered structure in mSAA increased as HA < CSA < heparin < HS (Fig. 4A). At pH 7.4 in hSAA1, which was highly α-helical, only HS induced significant CD changes suggesting decreased α-helical content (Fig. S2A). At pH 5.5 when hSAA1 was less α-helical, all GAGs induced distinct conformational changes: HS induced β-sheet folding, heparin induced mixed structure, while CSA and HA induced predominantly α-helix (Fig. S2A). These results suggest that the secondary structure in mSAA and hSAA1 induced by GAGs at pH 5.5 varied from β-sheet to α-helix depending upon GAG’s sulfation and perhaps other factors (e.g. GAG conformation in solution, the length of the sulfated regions, the sulfation pattern, etc.) The maximal β-sheet content in both proteins was induced by HS at pH 5.5 (Fig. 4A; Fig. S2A).

Next, the binding of mSAA and hSAA1 to various GAGs at pH 7.4 and pH 5.5 was examined by ELISA (Fig. 4B; Fig. S2B). Both proteins showed stronger binding to all GAGs at pH 5.5 vs. 7.4. At either pH, the binding strength increased in order HA, CSA < heparin < HS (Fig. 4A, B; Fig. S2A, B). The observation of strongest binding to highly sulfated GAGs at pH 5.5 suggested that protonation of histidines and perhaps the N-terminal α-amino group in mSAA and hSAA1 contributed to protein-GAG interactions. Taken together, large effects of GAG charge/sulfation and pH point to electrostatic interactions as a major contributor to the SAA-GAG binding and the GAG-induced non-native secondary structure in mSAA and hSAA1.

To test whether the GAG-induced non-native β-sheet bolsters amyloid formation, mSAA was incubated alone or with heparin for up to 72 h under amyloid-promoting conditions (37 °C with shaking) as described in Methods. Protein aggregation and amyloid formation were monitored by fluorescence, far-UV CD and FT IR spectroscopy, TEM (Fig. 5) and binding of amyloid-specific antibodies (Fig. S3A, B). Amyloid fibrils were detected by TEM and antibody binding after 72 h incubation under any conditions explored (72 h in Fig. 5D and Fig. S3A, B). The kinetics of amyloid formation, which was monitored by binding of a fluorescent dye thioflavin T (ThT) and by antibody binding, and the final protein conformation, which was assessed by CD and FT IR, depended upon both heparin and pH as described below.

For mSAA alone, ThT kinetic data indicated that formation of amyloid-like structure was faster at pH 5.5 than pH 7.4 (Fig. 5A). After 72 h of incubation, mSAA alone showed pH-dependent spectral changes such as a low-wavenumber IR peak characteristic of amyloid cross-β-sheet [62], which was observed at 1630 cm−1 at pH 7.4 and at 1624 cm−1 at pH 5.5. These differences suggested distinct protein conformations at pH 5.5 and 7.4, with greater cross-β-sheet content at acidic pH (Fig. 5B–C). Heparin greatly accelerated formation of amyloid-like structure in mSAA at pH 7.4 by eliminating the lag (nucleation) phase and accelerating the growth phase in the ThT kinetic data. Dot blot using antibodies that bind to pre-amyloid oligomers (A11) or amyloid fibrils (OC) confirmed that heparin accelerated amyloid formation at pH 7.4 (Fig. 5A, B). Heparin also promoted amyloid formation at pH 5.5, but the effect was limited to increased amplitude of the growth phase in ThT kinetic data (Fig. 5E, blue and pink curves). After 72 h incubation of mSAA with heparin, CD and FT IR spectra at both pH were similar (Fig. 5F, G); at both pH the amyloid-specific IR peak was observed at 1620 cm−1 in mSAA incubated with heparin (Fig. 5G). Differences in this peak position for mSAA+heparin vs. mSAA alone, which were observed at both pH, suggested possible structural differences between amyloids grown in the presence and in the absence of heparin.

To test whether heparin incorporated into amyloid, mSAA fibrils grown at pH 7.4 under conditions described in Figure 5A in the presence of fluorescein-labeled heparin were pelleted, and fluorescein emission spectra of the pellet, the supernatant, and the total sample were recorded at similar protein concentrations (Fig. S3C). Strong emission from the pellet, weak emission from the supernatant, and intermediate emission from the total sample suggested that most heparin incorporated into fibrils.

Compared to mSAA, amyloid formation by hSAA1 was relatively slow, taking over 200 hours to reach a plateau in the ThT kinetics curve (Fig. S4A). TEM showed amyloid fibrils formed after 7 days of protein incubation either alone or with heparin at pH 7.4 or 5.5 (Fig. S4C, D, 7 days). Notably, like mSAA+heparin, TEM showed that prior to incubation, hSAA1+heparin formed ~50 nm droplets at pH 7.4, while no droplets formed at pH 5.5 and protein oligomers under 10 nm in size were apparent (Fig. S4D, 0 hours). Oligomers 7–10 nm in size were also observed by non-denaturing gel electrophoresis of lipid-free mSAA and hSAA1 in our prior studies [63].

Several conjectures emerge from these results. First, at pH 7.4 heparin induces an apparent liquid-liquid phase separation in mSAA and hSAA1: both proteins form ~50 nm droplets in solution at pH 7.4 but not at pH 5.5 (Fig. 2E, Fig. 5H; Fig. S4D). Second, at pH 7.4 heparin induces relatively little β-sheet in mSAA in solution but greatly accelerates amyloid formation; conversely, at pH 5.5 heparin induces high β-sheet content in mSAA but has a smaller effect on the rate of amyloid formation (Figs. 2A, 5E). Therefore, heparin-accelerated amyloid formation, which was observed at pH 7.4 in mSAA and hSAA1 (Fig. 5E, Fig. S3A), probably stems from factors other than the heparin-induced β-sheet. We posit that these amyloid-promoting factors may include the protein-rich ~50 nm droplets observed in the presence of heparin at pH 7.4 but not pH 5.5 (Figs. 2E, 5H, S4D). Third, heparin alters not only the kinetics of aggregation (Fig. 5E; Fig. S3B) but also the IR spectrum of amyloid at both pH (Fig. 5C, G), suggesting direct incorporation of heparin into amyloid fibrils grown in its presence. In fact, heparin co-deposition with such fibrils was verified using fluorescein-labeled heparin (Fig. S3C); this finding is relevant to co-deposition of heparin with amyloid in vivo.

To examine how GAG sulfation influences formation of SAA droplets and amyloid, mSAA was incubated with various GAGs at pH 7.4 under amyloid-promoting conditions (Fig. 6). Before incubation, liquid droplets were observed by TEM in mSAA samples containing heparin, fewer droplets were seen with HS, and no droplets were seen with CSA or HA (Fig. 6B–F, top). After incubation, amyloid fibrils were observed by TEM in all samples with and without GAGs (Fig. 6B–F, bottom). ThT fluorescence suggested that the rate of amyloid formation increased with all GAGs in order free SAA < HA, CSA < HS < heparin (Fig. 6A), which correlated directly with the GAG sulfation and generally agreed with prior studies [18,19]. In summary, all GAGs explored accelerated amyloid formation by mSAA at pH 7.4, yet the greatest effect was observed for heparin and HS, which also induced protein condensation into droplets (Figs. 2E, 5H, S4D, 6C, D). Therefore, droplet formation induced by highly sulfated GAGs in mSAA at pH 7.4 may be a contributing factor to amyloid formation.

Taken together, these results suggest that electrostatic interactions between heparin or HS and mSAA or hSAA1 promote formation of ~50 nm droplets at pH 7.4. We hypothesize that these protein-rich droplets, which probably give rise to amyloid [64], contain GAGs that can directly incorporate into amyloid fibrils and influence their structure. This hypothetical mechanism may be relevant to the extracellular co-deposition of SAA amyloid and HS in vivo.

To test whether heparin can bind to preformed amyloid fibrils, fibrils grown from mSAA or hSAA1 alone at pH 7.4 as described in Fig. 5A–D and Fig. S4 A and C were incubated with heparin for 1 h at 37 °C. FT IR spectra of mSAA fibrils showed a peak shift from 1625 cm−1 before incubation to 1620 cm−1 after incubation with heparin (Fig. 7A). FT IR spectra of hSAA1 fibrils also showed a blue shift in the peak by about −4 cm−1 upon addition of heparin (Fig. 7D). Interestingly, in the presence of heparin bundles of aligned fibrils of mSAA and hSAA1 were invariably seen by TEM, suggesting heparin-induced fibril clustering (Fig. 7B, E).

Next, the fibrils of mSAA or hSAA1 grown in the absence of heparin were incubated with fluorescein-labeled heparin, the unbound heparin was removed, the pellet was resuspended, and the emission spectra were recorded as described in Methods (Fig. 7C, F). Strong fluorescein emission detected in the pellet verified heparin binding to pre-formed fibrils of mSAA and hSAA1. Taken together, our results in Fig. S4 and Fig. 7C, F reveal that heparin incorporates into SAA amyloid fibrils grown in the presence of heparin and binds to amyloid fibrils grown in its absence.

So far, we reported GAG interactions with either native or fibrillar proteins. Next, we tested the interactions of GAGs with protein oligomers formed early during the amyloidogenic pathway. mSAA or hSAA1 were incubated at pH 7.4 in the absence of GAGs under amyloid-promoting conditions for 10 to 20 h for mSAA and 3 to 5 days for hSAA1; these incubation times correspond to the end of the nucleation phase or the early growth phase in ThT kinetics when A11-positive amyloid oligomers were formed (Figs. 5A, S3A, S4A). Sample aliquots taken at these times were probed by ELISA for binding to GAG-coated plates. Both proteins showed binding to HS and heparin which progressively increased during incubation; both proteins showed much less binding to CSA and HA, and the binding showed little or no increase over time (Fig. 8A, C). Heparin affinity chromatography of mSAA confirmed that its binding affinity and binding capacity increased upon formation of amyloid oligomers (Fig. 8B). Two time-dependent peaks in the chromatogram suggested two distinct protein populations, low-affinity native mSAA (0 h, back line) and high-affinity amyloid oligomers whose population increased from 10 and 20 h (gray lines in Fig 8B). The results consistently show that SAA preferentially binds to highly sulfated GAGs, and the binding affinity increases upon formation of soluble amyloid oligomers.

We conclude that SAA binding to heparin/HS is synergistic with amyloid formation: addition of heparin to native SAA promotes amyloid formation, especially at pH 7.4 (Fig. 5E, Fig. S3A, B, Fig. S4A), while amyloid formation by SAA increases its binding to heparin/HS (Fig. 8). Again, both effects depend critically upon pH and GAG sulfation, suggesting key role of electrostatic interactions between GAGs and amyloid. These results complement prior studies showing the binding of heparin/HS or other polyanions to mature amyloid fibrils of various proteins in vivo and in vitro [42,48], as well as the ability of heparin/HS to accelerate amyloid formation by many unrelated proteins, including those that do not bind heparin/HS in their native state [32,39,42].

To explore the effects of GAGs on a very similar amyloidogenic protein that does not bind GAGs in its native state, we used hSAA2, one of two major human isoforms that, like hSAA1, deposits as pathologic amyloid in vivo. Protein amino acid sequences are listed in Supplemental Materials; hSAA2 differs from hSAA1 in two positions, D60N and H71R. The protein secondary structure, amyloid formation kinetics, and GAG binding were explored at pH 7.4 by far-UV CD, ThT fluorescence, TEM and ELISA. In its native state in solution at T≥25 °C, hSAA2 was intrinsically disordered; addition of heparin caused no conformational changes, suggesting lack of binding (Fig. S5A). ELISA confirmed that native hSAA2 did not bind heparin or any other GAGs explored (Fig. S5D, black). Nevertheless, ThT data showed that heparin greatly accelerated amyloid formation kinetics of hSAA2, essentially eliminating the lag (nucleation) phase and speeding up the growth phase (Fig. S5B). TEM showed heparin-induced droplet formation in native hSAA2 in solution (Fig. S5C, 0 h + heparin), which probably contributed to faster amyloid nucleation observed by ThT fluorescence despite lack of direct binding at 0 hours. After 5 days of incubation, TEM showed hSAA2 fibrils formed with and without heparin; like with hSAA1 and mSAA1, bundles of fibrils were observed in hSAA2+heparin samples (Fig. S5C, 5 days). Finally, hSAA2 oligomers that formed in the absence of GAGs at early stages of amyloid formation (10 or 20 h of incubation as shown in Fig. S5A, black line) were explored by ELISA for GAG binding (Fig. S5D). The results showed that, despite lack of binding by native hSAA2 at 0 hours, amyloid oligomers formed after 10 or 20 h of incubation showed progressively increased binding to heparin and HS; no such binding was detected for CSA or HA (Fig. S5D). The latter finding echoes similar results for hSAA1 and mSAA1 (Fig. 8C, A) and suggests that electrostatic interactions between amyloid oligomers and the highly sulfated GAGs contribute to greatly accelerated amyloid growth in all tree proteins. Moreover, the observation that hSAA2 in its native state does not bind heparin/HS but shows progressively increased binding during amyloid formation (Fig. S5D) strongly supports the synergy between amyloid formation and heparin/HS binding.

In the next series of experiments, we explored the binding of heparin/HS derivatives mimetics to various forms of mSAA. First, to determine the minimal chain length necessary for strong binding to native mSAA in solution, we examined four synthetic short-chain heparin derivatives, dp4 to dp20, containing 4 to 20 monosaccharide units (Supplemental Table S1). The protein was incubated for 1 hour at 37 °C, pH 7.4 with either dp4, dp8, dp16, dp20 or heparin (as a positive control) at 1:1 or 1:10 protein to oligosaccharide weight ratio, whereupon mSAA binding to heparin-covered plates was measured by competitive ELISA. Binding by mSAA alone was considered 100% (Fig. 9, control); decreased absorbance indicated decreased binding. In the presence of heparin in solution, the absorbance decreased from 100% to ~10%; with dp20 the absorbance decreased to ~50%; and dp4-dp16 caused no significant decrease (Fig. 9A). Therefore, at least 20 monosaccharides units are necessary for binding to native soluble SAA and partially blocking heparin binding, in good agreement with prior studies of SAA fragments [19]. Moreover, unlike heparin, dp20 and shorter molecules failed to induce ordered secondary structure in mSAA in solution at pH 7.4 (Fig. 9B).

Second, since SAA binding to heparin/HS progressively increased during amyloid formation, we tested whether dp20 interfered with this process. To this end, mSAA was incubated at pH 7.4 under amyloid-promoting conditions for 10 or 20 hours to form pre-amyloid and amyloid oligomers detected by A11 antibody binding and by heparin affinity chromatography (Fig. S3A, Fig. 8B). Next, the samples were incubated at pH 7.4 for 1 h with either dp20 or heparin and were examined by competitive ELISA for binding to heparin-coated plates (Fig. 9C). ThT fluorescence measured before and after incubation remained invariant, suggesting that no significant amyloid-like structure formed during 1 h incubation in the plates. In the presence of dp20, the signal decreased to 50% at time 0 (native SAA) and to ~70% after 10 hours of incubation, while little if any decrease was observed after 20 hours upon formation of A11-positive amyloid oligomers (Fig. S3A). In stark contrast, in the presence of soluble heparin as a positive control, all samples showed a large decrease in signal from 100% to ~10%, indicating that soluble heparin effectively blocked protein binding to heparin-coated plates during amyloid formation. Taken together, the results in Figure 9 indicate that dp20 has the minimal chain length necessary to interfere with heparin binding to native SAA in solution; however, during formation of amyloid oligomers, dp20 progressively lost its ability to block heparin binding. This implies that dp20 is too short to block heparin binding to mSAA amyloid.

A similar strategy was used to explore eprodisate™ (1,3-propanedisulfonic acid, Table S1), a small-molecule drug that was proposed to block the heparin binding sites on SAA but failed to show efficiency in clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT01215747, “Efficacy and Safety Study of KIACTA in Preventing Renal Function Decline in AA Amyloidosis”).

To elucidate molecular basis for this failure, soluble mSAA at either pH 7.4 or pH 5.5 was incubated under amyloid-promoting conditions for 10 to 20 h, followed by incubation with 0–1 mM eprodisate at 37 °C for 1 hour (drug:protein molar ratio ranging from 0:1 to 25:1) . Next, the samples were examined by competitive ELISA to determine if the drug interfered with heparin binding to native mSAA and to amyloid oligomers. As expected, in the absence of eprodisate at pH 5.5 and 7.4, amyloid formation during incubation of mSAA progressively increased its binding to heparin-coated plates (Fig. 10A, B open bars at 0, 10 and 20 h). At pH 7.4, eprodisate decreased heparin binding to native mSAA (0 h) and to early amyloid oligomers (10 h) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 10A). However, heparin binding to amyloid oligomers at a later stage (20 h) remained high and eprodisate decreased it only slightly. Compared to pH 7.4, at pH 5.5 heparin binding to SAA was nearly two-fold higher; eprodisate slightly decreased heparin binding to native SAA (0 h), while little (10 h) or no significant decrease (20 h) was seen for amyloid oligomers (Fig. 10B).

Next, eprodisate and/or heparin were added in different order (first eprodisate then heparin or vice versa) to native SAA, and far-UV CD spectra were recorded. In contrast to heparin, eprodisate did not induce any ordered secondary structure in mSAA at pH 5.5–7.4 (Fig. 10C, D, blue lines); therefore, increase in the ordered structure detected by CD provided a readout for heparin binding to mSAA. At pH 7.4 heparin induced large CD changes in mSAA suggesting formation of α+β structure both in the absence and in the presence of eprodisate; CD varied slightly depending on the order in which the two compound have been added (Fig. 10C). At pH 5.5, heparin-induced CD changes were similar under any conditions explored, indicating formation of similar β-sheet rich structure (Fig. 10D). These results verify that even at a drug-to-protein molar excess of 50 used in our studies (up to 1 mM eprodisate and ~ 20 μM SAA), eprodisate was unable to block heparin binding to native soluble mSAA at pH 5.5–7.4.

Finally, we examined the effect of eprodisate on heparin binding to hSAA1 at various stages of amyloid formation. The protein alone was incubated at either pH 7.4 or pH 5.5 under amyloid-promoting conditions for 0 (native) or 3 to 5 days to generate amyloid oligomers detected by ThT fluorescence (Fig. S4A). Next, hSAA1 samples were incubated with 0–1 mM eprodisate at 37 °C for 1 hour, followed by competitive ELISA to test the effect of eprodisate on heparin binding (Fig. 10E, F). The binding of hSAA1 to heparin-coated plates, which progressively increased upon amyloid formation (3 and 5 days), was only partially blocked by eprodisate, echoing similar results for mSAA (Fig. 10 E, F).

Taken together, the results in Figure 10 show that eprodisate caused a dose-dependent decrease in heparin binding by native mSAA or hSAA1. However, even at a high drug-to-protein ratios (up to 25:1 mol:mol) used in our studies, eprodisate only partially blocked heparin binding. Moreover, the blocking effect progressively decreased upon amyloid formation by mSAA or hSAA1 at pH 5.5 and 7.4 (Fig. 10A, B, E, F). These findings help explain why eprodisate failed to show efficiency in clinical trials for AA amyloidosis. Amyloid-promoting factors other than HS could have also contributed to this failure.

To-date, the efforts to interfere with the pro-amyloidogenic SAA-HS interactions have been focused on using heparin mimetics to block the binding sites on SAA. As an alternative strategy, we attempted to block the binding sites on HS/heparin by using protamine, a small Arg-rich nuclear protein that binds heparin and DNA [65]. Again, mSAA alone was incubated under amyloid-promoting conditions for 10 or 20 hours to form amyloid oligomers, followed by 1 h incubation of either mSAA alone or with eprodisate (1 mM) or protamine (10 μg/ml); protein binding to heparin-coated plates was measured by ELISA. Unlike eprodisate, protamine fully blocked heparin binding by mSAA at various stages of amyloid formation (Fig. 10G). When Arg (6–60 μg/ml) was used instead of protamine in similar studies, no blocking was observed (data not shown), suggesting key role for the polycationic nature of protamine. This opens a possibility to explore heparin-binding polycationic peptides as potential therapeutics for AA amyloidosis.

Heparin-SAA interactions are a major amyloid-promoting factor and a therapeutic target in AA amyloidosis [20,21]. Eprodisate was proposed to act as a heparin decoy to block these interactions [30,31]. The failure of this drug to show efficiency in clinical trials was particularly disappointing, as no other amyloid-specific therapeutics are in the pipeline for AA amyloidosis. The molecular basis for this failure and alternative strategies are suggested in the current study. It reveals that although this small-molecule drug can decrease heparin binding to native lipid-free mSAA1 and hSAA1, the effect is progressively diminished during amyloid formation (Fig. 10). This finding reflects the previously unknown synergy between the heparin binding and amyloid formation: Not only heparin/HS binding promotes amyloid formation by SAA, particularly at pH 7.4 representing the extracellular site of amyloid deposition (Fig. 5E; Fig. S3A, B; Fig. S4A; Fig. 6A), but also amyloid formation by mSAA1 and hSAA1 increases their binding to heparin (Fig. 8; Fig. S5). Notably, this synergy extends to hSAA2 that in its native state does not bind heparin but shows a progressive increase in binding during amyloid formation; conversely, heparin promotes amyloid formation by hSAA2 (Fig. S5B, D). We speculate that this synergy extends to other proteins that may or may not bind GAGs in their native state. In fact, amyloid formation by diverse protein generates PRIBS motives that form extended GAG binding sites, while GAG binding to these sites balances their charge and stabilizes the PRIBS motives promoting their propagation. If so, the impact of the current study extends beyond AA to other amyloid diseases.

Notably, SAA binding and amyloid formation depend strongly on pH and GAG sulfation: stronger binding is observed upon increased protein protonation at pH 5.5 vs. pH 7.4 and with increasing GAG sulfation (Figs. 1–4, S1, S2). These findings are consistent with prior reports on the importance of GAG sulfation [18,23] and point to the key role of electrostatic interactions for GAG-amyloid binding; this conjecture is supported by our in-silico studies using molecular and Brownian dynamics simulations of ex vivo fibril structures of mSAA1 and hSAA1 with heparin [49]. Taken together, these studies suggest that the synergy between heparin/HS binding and amyloid formation by SAA stems from electrostatic interactions between polyanionic chains of heparin/HS with uncompensated charges in basic residue arrays on the amyloid surface. Such uncompensated arrays are energetically unfavorable; binding of heparin/HS polyanions helps balance the charge and reduces the energy barrier to amyloid nucleation and propagation. Conversely, formation of PRIBS motives by SAA and other amyloid proteins generates extended periodic basic arrays in amyloid which form the polyanion binding sites. The resulting synergy probably extends to other polyanions (polyphosphate, RNA) that can also act as amyloid agonists in vivo and in vitro [39,66,67].

Despite this mechanistic similarity, the binding affinity for individual polyanions is expected to differ for different amyloid structures, since the basic arrays in these structures are located in regions with different amino acid composition and geometry. Furthermore, the extracellular matrices of various organs and tissues differ in their GAG composition. This suggests that GAG-specific effects on amyloid formation, such as those revealed in the current study (Fig. 4B; Fig. S2B; Fig. 6), potentially influence the site of amyloid deposition in vivo [21].

Lateral binding of heparin/HS chains to amyloids helps explain why an oligosaccharide chain length exceeding dp20 is required to compete with heparin for binding to SAA amyloid oligomers (Fig. 9A, C), which is consistent with chain length effects on amyloid formation by SAA fragments [19]. We hypothesize that the minimal chain length is commensurate with the linear size of amyloid oligomers. If so, chains longer than dp20 correspond to amyloid oligomers containing more than 20 protein molecules and exceeding 10 nm in length, assuming oligosaccharides bind to amyloid PRIBS motives at an approximate 1:1 ratio of monosaccharide per protein rung and the rungs are spaced at 4.8 Å [42].

In addition to direct binding, heparin/HS (but not the less sulfated GAGs) can also induce formation of ~100 nm droplets observed by TEM in mSAA, hSAA1 and hSAA2 at pH 7.4 (Figs. 2E, 5H, 6C, D; Figs. S4D, S5C). Such protein-rich droplets can be induced extracellularly by HS [68] and are expected to promote amyloid formation in various proteins by condensing and perhaps aligning protein molecules [64]. This effect of heparin/HS has not been previously reported for SAA and may potentially contribute to its co-deposition with HS.

Another interesting finding is that heparin and HS induce non-native β-sheet in mSAA and hSAA1 in solution at pH 5.5 (Figs. 2A, 3A, 4A). This β-sheet unfolds rapidly upon pH increase from 5.5 to 7.4, suggesting that it is distinct from amyloid, although it converts into amyloid over time. This finding may be relevant to the extracellular deposition of SAA amyloid in the kidney and spleen during inflammation, which can cause a large drop in pH in these organs up to pH 5.7 [54]. Remarkably, non-native β-sheet is also induced in mSAA and hSAA1 in the presence of liposomes with high content of anionic phospholipids or unesterified fatty acids; this β-sheet is metastable and readily converts into amyloid [62]. Taken together, these findings suggest that SAA interactions with 1D or 2D arrays of negative charges promote formation of the non-native β-sheet that can convert into amyloid.

Importantly, the current study suggests potential strategies to block the pathologic interactions between SAA and HS/heparin by using longer poly-sulfonated molecules that might be more efficient inhibitors as compared to disulfonate (Fig. 10A–F), a concept that necessitates in vivo testing. One potential drawback is that such long polyanions might themselves promote amyloid formation. Alternatively, heparin ligands such as protamine, a basic Arg-rich peptide clinically used to block excess heparin, can also block heparin-amyloid interactions (Fig. 10G). The effects of such peptides on amyloid formation and cellular uptake via the HS proteoglycan receptors are unknown and are subjects of future studies.

Notably, factors other than highly sulfated GAGs can also augment amyloid formation. One example is GAGs with little or no sulfation, such as CSA or HA (Fig. 6). Another example is collagens, which co-deposit with amyloids and can form superhelical complexes with amyloid fibrils in vivo [69]. Complexes of amyloids with other biopolymers such as collagens and GAGs can influence the mechanical and biological properties of amyloids, affect secondary nucleation on the fibril surface, mask amyloids from clearance by microphages, or hamper the binding of diagnostic dyes to amyloid deposits [69,70 and references therein]. The role of these and other factors in AA amyloid formation and disease pathology is subject of future studies.
