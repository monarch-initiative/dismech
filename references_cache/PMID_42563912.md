# Pyridoxal phosphate binding protein (PLPBP) deficiency mimicking opsoclonus-myoclonus-ataxia syndrome.
**Authors:** Takle M, Sahjwani D, Bharucha-Goebel D, Rapp T, Bouska C, Kornbluh A, Sen K
**Journal:** Ann Child Neurol Soc (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/cns3.20098](https://doi.org/10.1002/cns3.20098)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Genetic and metabolic conditions can mimic diagnoses such as hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, meningoencephalitis, epilepsy, and opsoclonus-myoclonus-ataxia syndrome (OMAS). Without a high index of suspicion and proper testing, diagnoses can be missed, and treatment delayed.

METHODS: A 3-year-old girl with a history of neonatal seizures and previous nondiagnostic epilepsy gene panel presented with seizures, behavioral changes, and discrete episodes of myoclonus, tremors, and abnormal eye movements following a viral illness.

OBJECTIVE AND INTERPRETATION: Initial evaluation was concerning for OMAS, though metabolic causes remained on the differential. Metabolic testing revealed elevated glycine and glutamine, suggestive of a possible inborn error of metabolism. Whole exome sequencing demonstrated compound heterozygous variants in the PLPBP gene associated with pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy (PDE), consistent with her clinical presentation and leading to her diagnosis of PLPBP deficiency.

DISCUSSION: Clinicians should gauge the indications, advantages, and limitations of targeted sequencing panels versus whole exome sequencing. Continued evaluation is recommended in patients with a history of neonatal and infantile epilepsy, especially if they present with episodic crises related to viral illness even if prior genetic and metabolic investigations have been nondiagnostic. This report also highlights the clinical overlap between PLPBP deficiency and OMAS, and the differences in pathophysiology, treatment pathways, and implications.

This 14‐day‐old girl born at term presented to the emergency department with new‐onset seizures described as episodes of full‐body stiffness, rhythmic shaking, and right eye deviation (Figure 1). On neurological examination, she was diffusely hypertonic with superimposed generalized myoclonic jerks. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, including white blood cell count and meningitis/encephalitis panel, was unrevealing. Electroencephalogram (EEG) background showed occasional multifocal sharp waves, periods of attenuation lasting 2–5 s, and multiple clusters of myoclonic and tonic seizures. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and spectroscopy were unrevealing (Figure 2). Seizures abated with phenobarbital, levetiracetam, and oxcarbazepine, and she was discharged on this antiseizure medication (ASM) regimen. A multigene epilepsy panel revealed a variant of uncertain significance in the GRIN2B gene, which was later confirmed to be paternally inherited and downgraded to benign. Interval EEGs showed normal background without recurrent seizures, and phenobarbital was stopped. Her working diagnosis was self‐limited nonfamilial neonatal seizures. Development remained appropriate for her age, and at 2 years of age she spoke approximately 30 words, was running and dancing, was feeding herself, and was able to help with dressing and undressing.

At 27 months of age she presented with breakthrough seizures along with episodic agitation, screaming, and tremor (Figure 1). MRI with and without gadolinium administration showed mild T2 hyperintensities in the posterior pons and dentate nuclei (Figure 2); basic CSF studies were unremarkable. Given her recurrent seizures, behavioral changes, and hyperintensities on MRI, autoimmune encephalitis was suspected, and she was empirically treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) 2 g/kg and a 5‐day course of intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) 30 mg/kg daily. Her seizures and episodes of agitation resolved within a week. Serum and CSF oligoclonal bands and autoimmune encephalopathy autoantibody panel resulted negative, and full diagnostic criteria for autoimmune encephalitis were not met.
1
 Human metapneumovirus testing resulted positive, and this clinical presentation was attributed to suspected functional decline and breakthrough seizures associated with viral illness.

She remained clinically stable without recurrent seizures until 33 months of age, when she presented with breakthrough seizures, behavioral changes, and episodes of myoclonic movements, tremors, multidirectional rapid eye movements suspected to be opsoclonus, and agitation (Figure 1). MRI was repeated and stable (Figure 2), and CSF studies were normal. Differential diagnosis remained broad, including neurometabolic disorders and opsoclonus‐myoclonus‐ataxia syndrome (OMAS), as seizures, hyperkinetic movements, and behavioral changes can present in both. Screening metabolic labs and plasma amino acids resulted in elevated glutamine (1036 mcmol/L, reference range 330.0–726.0) and glycine (556 mcmol/L, reference range 129.1–429.7).
2
 She was again empirically treated with IVIG 2 g/kg and a 5‐day course of IVMP due to suspicion for OMAS, and ASMs were adjusted. Whole exome sequencing (WES) revealed one maternally inherited likely pathogenic variant (c.199 G>A) and one paternally inherited variant of uncertain significance (c.322G>C) in the PLPBP gene associated with pyridoxal 5′‐phosphate binding protein (PLPBP) deficiency. Given the clinical fit with the patient's phenotype, she was diagnosed with PLPBP deficiency. Ongoing planned outpatient immunotherapy was discontinued, and she was started on pyridoxine supplementation of 20 mg/kg/day. Topiramate was discontinued, and she has been clinically stable without seizures and has been undergoing physical and speech therapies.

The patient was admitted three times with three different working diagnoses: self‐limited nonfamilial neonatal infantile seizures, autoimmune encephalitis, and OMAS. Ultimately, the unifying diagnosis was determined to be PLPBP deficiency. Her initial multigene panel in the neonatal period did not show a pathogenic variant, and she was diagnosed later via WES. Multigene panels are not all‐encompassing, as they are curated by clinicians and molecular geneticists based on contemporaneous knowledge and literature. They are modified and expanded over time as novel monogenic disorders are discovered.

4
 In this patient, the PLPBP gene, formerly known as PROSC, was added to the epilepsy gene panel between the patient's initial testing and time of diagnosis at 3 years of age. When a diagnosis is not made via multigene panel testing and a patient continues to have episodes of encephalopathy or seizures of unknown etiology, it is important to consider WES, whole genome sequencing, or expanded metabolic testing.

Many genetic conditions present with neonatal seizures and can be classified into structural brain malformations, inborn errors of metabolism, and syndromic or nonsyndromic single‐gene disorders.
5
 Inborn errors of metabolism can present in newborns and infants with nonspecific symptoms such as vomiting, encephalopathy, and seizures. These are rare monogenic disorders caused by defects in biochemical pathways leading to accumulation of toxic moieties or failure of cell energy machinery. These disorders can mimic other more common diagnoses such as hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy, sepsis, meningitis, or cardiorespiratory illnesses. Therefore, a high index of suspicion is required for timely diagnosis and treatment. These conditions should be considered in neonates who do not respond well to standard ASM management and have periodic crises triggered by physiologic stressors such as fasting, febrile illnesses, or surgeries. Our patient experienced periods of seizure freedom but decompensated with exposure to viral illnesses. The differential diagnosis for inborn errors of metabolism that present with neonatal seizures and encephalopathy is broad and includes organic acidurias, peroxisomal disorders, pyridoxamine 5’‐phosphate oxidase (PNPO) deficiency, nonketotic hyperglycinemia (NKH), urea cycle disorders, and biotinidase deficiency. Genetic confirmation of a specific diagnosis provides important prognostic information for families and informs recurrence risk for future pregnancies.
6
 Definitive genetic diagnosis can also guide clinical management since many of these conditions have disease‐specific treatments in the form of specific diets or vitamins/supplements. This patient was started on pyridoxine for treatment of PLPBP deficiency and has markedly improved.

The patient's seizures, behavioral changes, and abnormal eye movements during her third admission were concerning for OMAS (Figure 1). OMAS can be a challenging diagnosis as it is based on fulfilling three of the four following clinical criteria: (1) opsoclonus, (2) ataxia or myoclonus, (3) behavior change or sleep disturbance, and (4) neuroblastoma.
7
 During this presentation, the patient had myoclonic movements, irritability, tremors, insomnia, and abnormal eye movements suspected to be opsoclonus. Because these symptoms were concerning for OMAS, she was treated empirically with IVIG and IVMP that resulted in clinical improvement. However, her history of neonatal seizures, repeated presentations, and abnormal metabolic evaluation were inconsistent with OMAS. Abnormal eye movements that mimic opsoclonus can occur in many conditions, including disorders associated with GLUT‐1 deficiency and ATP1A3‐ and CACNA1A‐related disorders, among others.

Variants in several genes have been documented to cause pyridoxine‐dependent epilepsy (PDE), including mutations in ALDH7A1, PNPO, and PLPBP.
12
 Inheritance is autosomal recessive in all of these entities. ALDH7A1 was included in the first genetic panel but PLPBP was not. PLPBP codes for pyridoxal phosphate‐binding protein (PLPBP).
13
 Pyridoxal 5′‐phosphate (PLP) is the active form of vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) and functions as a cofactor in enzymes involved in neurotransmitter synthesis. Dysfunction of PLP can lead to seizures.
14
 Seizure types can be variable and include generalized tonic‐clonic, clonic, myoclonic, and epileptic spasms.
15
 Burst suppression, multifocal spikes, focal discharges, and a slow background can be seen on EEGs of patients with PDE, similar to our patient.
15
 Typical MRI findings can include periventricular cysts, thinning of the corpus callosum, cortical atrophy, and simplified cortical gyral pattern, as well as nonspecific white matter and mild T2 hyperintensities. Neurodevelopmental outcomes vary; behavioral problems, developmental delay, mild to severe intellectual disability, and autism spectrum disorder occur in approximately 75% of patients with PDE.
16
 Biochemical testing can also offer diagnostic clues: nonspecific and nondiagnostic elevations in glutamine, glycine, and lactate have been described and were present in this patient.
17



This patient was ultimately diagnosed with PLPBP deficiency via WES following multiple presentations concerning for self‐limited nonfamilial neonatal infantile seizures, autoimmune encephalitis, and OMAS. Seizure freedom was achieved after initiation of supraphysiologic doses of pyridoxine.
