# Trends in keratinocyte skin cancer incidence, mortality and burden of disease in 33 countries between 1990 and 2017
**Authors:** Dorothy D Yang, Kim Borsky, Chinmay Jani, Conor Crowley, Jeremy N Rodrigues, Rubeta N Matin, Dominic C Marshall, Justin D Salciccioli, Joseph Shalhoub, Richard Goodall
**Journal:** British Journal of Dermatology (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1093/bjd/ljac064](https://doi.org/10.1093/bjd/ljac064)

## Content

Abstract

Background
Keratinocyte cancers (KCs) are the most common type of cancer in the White population worldwide, with associated high healthcare costs. Understanding the epidemiological trends for KCs, namely basal cell carcinomas (BCCs) and cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs), is required to assess burden of disease, project future trends and identify strategies for addressing this pressing global health issue.


Objectives
To report trends in BCC and SCC incidence, and SCC mortality and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs).


Methods
An observational study of the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database between 1990 and 2017 was performed. European Union countries and other selected high-income countries, including the UK and the USA, classified as having high-quality mortality data, were included. Annual age-standardized incidence rates (ASIRs), age-standardized death rates (ASDRs) and DALYs for each country were obtained from the GBD database. Trends were described using joinpoint regression analysis.


Results
Overall, 33 countries were included. For both BCC and SCC in 2015–2017, the highest ASIRs were observed in the USA and Australia. Males had higher ASIRs than females at the end of the observation period in all countries for SCC, and in all countries but two for BCC. In contrast, the highest ASDRs for SCC were observed in Australia and Latvia for males, and in Romania and Croatia for females. The highest DALYs for SCC for both sexes were seen in Australia and Romania. Over the observation period, there were more countries demonstrating decreasing trends in mortality than in incidence, and disparities were observed between which countries had comparatively high mortality rates and which had high incidence rates. Overall reductions in SCC DALYs were observed in 24 of 33 countries for males, and 25 countries for females.


Conclusions
Over the past 27 years, although trends in SCC incidence have risen in most countries, there is evidence that mortality rates have been decreasing. Burden of disease as assessed using DALYs has decreased in the majority of countries. Future work will explore potential explanatory factors for the observed disparity in trends in SCC incidence and mortality.

Introduction 
Skin cancer can largely be grouped into melanoma and keratinocyte cancers (KC), the latter 
comprising basal cell carcinomas (BCC) and cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas (SCC). KC 
are the commonest type of cancer in the white population worldwide.1,2  
Recent studies have shown that incidence and prevalence of KC have increased in many 
countries. This is partially explained by improvements in disease registration leading to cases 
being captured more accurately, but other contributory factors likely include early detection, 
increased ultraviolet radiation (UVR) exposure, use of immunosuppressive agents, and 
increasing life expectancy. This has been described as “The Skin Cancer Epidemic”, with 
associated increasing cost of treatment.3-5 In the United States (US), the annual cost of 
treating KC has recently been estimated to be $4.8 billion.6 Understanding past 
epidemiological trends can inform prediction of future trends and identify strategies to curb 
this epidemic. 
The objective of this study was to perform the most up-to-date analysis of the incidence and 
mortality of KC across countries with good-quality reporting of nation-level epidemiological 
data. We compare trends in BCC and SCC incidence, as well as SCC mortality and disability-
adjusted life-years (DALYs), between 1990-2017, based on an observational study of the 
Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database. Through Joinpoint regression analyses we 
identified time periods within this overall study period in which there were notable changes 
in disease burden. 
 
Methods 
 

 
Data source 
Epidemiological data on KC between 1990-2017 were obtained from the GBD database using 
methods we have described previously.7,8 In brief, this publicly available database contains 
data collected for the GBD study,9,10 an annually reported study which utilises data from 
multiple sources to report internally consistent estimates for health loss related to specific 
diseases. These sources include administrative records, censuses, clinical trials, demographic 
surveillance, disease registries, environmental monitoring, epidemiological surveillance, 
financial records, surveys, and vital registration.11  
For this study, we extracted mortality, prevalence, incidence and DALY data using the GBD 
results tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool) for the following causes: “non-
melanoma skin cancer (basal-cell carcinoma)” and “non-melanoma skin cancer (squamous-
cell carcinoma)”. 
For mortality rates, only data from vital registries (VR) were used. This is classified as the 
highest-quality data source according to the GBD. Due to the great variability in registration 
practices among national cancer registries, mortality data from cancer registries were not 
used. All 33 countries have VR data for the observed period from 1990-2017. The mortality 
rates together with covariates with well-documented associations are then used in a cause of 
death ensemble model (CODEm) to model the death rates of cutaneous SCCs. The selected 
covariates included: smoking status, healthcare access and quality index, average latitude, 
socio-demographic index, and lag distributed income per capita.9 
The input data for incidence and prevalence estimates (and subsequent DALY calculations) 
were derived from disease registries, as well as primary literature and clinical informatics 
data (such as Marketscan). For cancer registry data reported as “other and unspecified 
malignant neoplasm of skin”, proportions from literature were used to split the data into SCC 

 
and BCC. All available data were then modelled in DisMod MR 2.1 (Disease Model, 
Bayesian Meta-regression) to compute final incidence and prevalence rates.10 Of the included 
countries, no regional or national cancer registry data were available for Latvia, Luxembourg 
and Malta. Population survey data only were used instead to compute incidence rates. 
A complete list of all data inputs can be reviewed by country on the GBD tool 
(https://ghdx.healthdata.org/keyword/skin-cancers). Only countries with high quality 
mortality estimates were included in this analysis. A more detailed description of the data 
quality can be found in the Supplementary Material. 
 
Data handling 
Age-standardised incidence rates (ASIRs), age-standardised death rates (ASDRs) and 
DALYs, each reported per 100,000 population per year, were extracted for SCC, with ASIRs 
extracted for BCC, using the GBD results tool. The GBD standard population was used for 
age-standardisation. Mortality from BCC is extremely low, and no BCC mortality data are 
available from the GBD study.13-16 The GBD study quantifies health losses with DALYs. This 
summary measure combines years of life lost (YLL), and years lived with disease (YLDs), 
which describes time lived in states worse than full health. From the prevalence estimates, 
YLDs were calculated. Panel surveys then assigned three disability weights to the condition, 
mainly focusing on physical deformity, pain, itching, trouble sleeping, and, in the highest 
weight class, the avoidance of social contacts or thoughts about suicide. The weighted YLDs 
then underwent a comorbidity correction, accounting for disease burden related to other 
comorbidities generally present within each population. This data together with YLLs then 
result in the estimate of DALYs.10 

 
Sex-specific absolute and percentage changes in BCC and SCC ASIRs, SCC ASDRs, and 
SCC DALYs for each country were calculated as the difference between the start (1990-
1992) and end (2015-2017) mean values for these parameters. Three-year mean values were 
used to diminish the impact of any inter-year variability. 
Mortality-to-incidence ratios (MIRs) for each year for both sexes were obtained by dividing 
the SCC ASDR by SCC ASIR. 
 
Statistical analysis 
Trends were modelled using the Joinpoint regression analysis method, as we have described 
previously.7,8,17 This involves modelling based on changes in the linear slope for trends over 
time. Analysis begins with no Joinpoints, and tests for significant changes in the model with 
the sequential addition of Joinpoints where there is a significant change in the slope of the 
line. The model computes an estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) for each trend by 
fitting a regression line to the natural logarithm of the rates being studied. All analyses were 
performed using Joinpoint (command-line version 4.5.0.1; 
https://surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/). 
 
Results 
Based on the inclusion criteria, 33 countries were included in this study. Incidentally these all 
fell into two of the GBD super-regions (countries grouped on the basis of cause-of-death 
patterns)18: “Central Europe, Eastern Europe and Central Asia” (Bulgaria, Croatia, Czech 
Republic, Hungary, Poland, Romania, Slovenia, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania), and “High-
Income” (Australia, Japan, the US, Canada, Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, 

 
Germany, Greece, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, Malta, Netherlands, Norway, 
Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, the UK). 
 
Basal cell carcinoma 
 
Most recent data for BCC incidence 
Supplementary Figures 1A and 1B show the mean BCC ASIRs for 2015-2017. The highest 
ASIRs were seen in the US (males: 865.4, females: 552.1) and Australia (males: 288.6, 
females: 261.4). Japan (males: 9.6, females: 8.1) had the lowest ASIRs. Males had higher 
ASIRs than females in all countries except Canada and Lithuania. 
 
Trends in BCC incidence between 1990-2017 
Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 1 show BCC incidence rates between 1990 and 2017. In 
each country, directions of trends, whether increasing or decreasing, were similar for both 
sexes. The largest percentage increases in incidence were seen in Canada (males: +684.9%, 
females: +762.7%), and the largest percentage decreases were seen in Sweden (males: -
54.9%, females: -57.0%). 
 
Joinpoint regression analysis for BCC incidence trends between 1990-2017 
Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 display the results of Joinpoint analysis. Countries 
demonstrating continuously increasing BCC ASIRs across the observation period for both 
sexes were Canada, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Italy and Slovenia. Australia 
demonstrated continuously decreasing ASIRs for males, while ASIRs initially remained 
stable for females and then started decreasing. 

 
Of specific note, the US had unusually shaped Joinpoint curves, with both sexes 
demonstrating a steep decrease in incidence between 1995 and 2000, followed by an abrupt 
steep increase up until 2005, after which incidence rates stabilised. Another country of note 
was Sweden, where incidence rates fell quite steeply from the beginning of the observation 
period until 1994-1995 and then abruptly stabilised. 
 
Squamous cell carcinoma 
 
Most recent data for SCC incidence 
Supplementary Figures 1C and 1D show the mean SCC ASIRs for 2015-2017. The highest 
ASIRs were seen in the US (males: 326.8, females: 171.2) and Australia (males: 283.7, 
females: 152.4). The lowest ASIRs were seen in Japan (males: 1.5, females: 1.2). Males had 
higher ASIRs than females in all countries. 
 
Trends in SCC incidence between 1990-2017 
Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 4 show SCC incidence rates between 1990 and 2017. 
Male ASIRs had increased in 2017 compared to 1990 in 27 of the 33 countries, while female 
ASIRs had increased in 28 countries. The largest percentage increases in incidence were seen 
in Poland (males: +636.0%, females: +204.0%) and Canada (males: +545.8%, females: 
+503.3%). The largest percentage decreases in incidence for males were seen in Luxembourg 
(-34.7%), and for females in Romania (-15.0%) and Greece (-15.0%).  
 
Joinpoint regression analysis for SCC incidence trends between 1990-2017 
Supplementary Tables 5 and 6 display the results of Joinpoint analysis. Countries 
demonstrating continuously increasing SCC ASIRs for both sexes were Canada, Czech 

 
Republic, Estonia, Italy, Latvia, Slovenia and the US. No countries demonstrated 
continuously decreasing SCC ASIRs across the observation period. 
Several countries demonstrated periods when male incidence increased at a notably greater 
rate than female incidence, even if this did not persist until the end of the observation period. 
Countries demonstrating this most dramatically include Canada, France, Greece, Hungary, 
Poland and Romania. 
 
Most recent data for SCC mortality 
Supplementary Figures 2A and 2B show the mean SCC ASDRs for 2015-2017. The highest 
ASDRs for males were observed in Australia (2.77), while the highest ASDRs for females 
were observed in Romania (0.95). The lowest ASDRs were seen in Japan (males: 0.51, 
females: 0.19) and Iceland (males: 0.66, females: 0.13). Males had higher SCC ASDRs than 
females in all countries. 
 
Trends in SCC mortality between 1990-2017 
Male ASDRs had decreased in 2017 compared to 1990 in 24 of the 33 countries, while 
female ASDRs had decreased in 22 countries (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 7). The largest 
percentage increases in ASDRs were seen in Latvia (males: +66.34%, females: +70.77%). 
The largest percentage decreases were seen in Spain (males: -39.63%, females: -54.64%). 
 
Joinpoint regression analysis for SCC mortality between 1990-2017 
Supplementary Tables 8 and 9 display the results of Joinpoint analysis. No countries 
demonstrated continuously increasing SCC mortality across the observation period. Spain 
showed continuously decreasing SCC mortality for both sexes, while Finland and 
Switzerland showed continuously decreasing SCC mortality for males. 

 
 
SCC MIRs and DALYs

Material. 
 
Discussion 
In this study, the US and Australia had by far the highest incidence rates for both BCC and 
SCC. In addition , there  is a clearer association  between high BCC incidence rates and 
geographical variation – in particular, Northern European countries including Norway, Iceland, 
Finland, Ireland and the UK , had among the highest incidence rates . In contrast, association 
with geography appeared to be  less strong for SCC – Norway, Ireland and the UK  also had 
high incidence rates for SCC, but Iceland and Finland had among the lowest. These high 
incidence rates among Northern European countries could be due to a combination of the 
predominantly fair-skinned populations in these countries, and increased popularity of artificial 
sun-tanning activities and holidays abroad at sunny destinations (although these are not unique 
to Northern European countries) leading to increased exposure to UVR.19,20 However, this does 
not explain the relative differences between BCC and SCC incidence among Northern 
European countries, to which o ther known risk factors for SCC such as immunosuppression 
and HPV infection may be contributory. 
For both BCC and SCC, males had higher ASIRs than females in 2015-2017 in all countries, 
except for Canada and Lithuania for BCC. However, we noted that for BCC, direction of 
trends in male and female incidence rates matched well on a temporal scale in most countries 
(Figure 1). In contrast, trends in male and female SCC incidence rates showed divergence at 
different points in quite a few countries, whether in direction or degree of change – for 
example, some countries experienced steeper increases in SCC incidence for males than 

 
females. Again, this may suggest differences in the risk factors and underlying aetiology of 
BCC and SCC. Previous studies have predominantly shown a higher incidence of KC among 
males than females, which has often been attributed to greater UVR exposure among men.20-
24 There has also been evidence to suggest that risk of BCC or SCC is not solely related to the 
quantity of UVR exposure, but that pattern of exposure is also important. Several studies 
suggest that SCC may predominantly be associated with long-term or cumulative UVR 
exposure, whereas BCC’s relationship to UVR is thought to result more from early life, 
intense sun exposure with a strong genetic component.15,25-28 This is an area of ongoing study, 
and it is unclear whether differences in pattern of UVR exposure could be a factor underlying 
the wider sex disparities in incidence seen for SCC than BCC. 
We also noted significant differences in trends between SCC incidence and mortality rates 
over the observation period. Australia had among the highest incidence and mortality rates 
for both sexes. However, other countries with high ASDRs, e.g. the Eastern European 
countries Romania, Poland, Hungary and Czech Republic, had lower ASIRs. In contrast, the 
US, which had by far the highest SCC incidence rates, had among the lowest SCC mortality 
rates and MIRs for both sexes. Indeed, the ranking of countries by MIR in 2015-2017 is 
almost the inverse of that for ASIRs (Supplementary Figures 1 and 2). This is likely due, in 
part, to the variation in incidence rates between countries being much wider than that of 
mortality rates. While ASDR only vary between 1-2 digits from the highest to the lowest 
countries, ASIR variability lies in the hundreds. However, this inverse relationship between 
incidence and MIR could also suggest that some countries with a higher incidence of SCC 
tend to have lower mortality from SCC. Although it is assumed that the increase in SCC 
incidence over the past decades is partly due to a real increase in cases, it is likely that 
increase in screening and improved diagnostic pathways have contributed, leading to 
identification of cases that may have escaped diagnosis in the past.5,29,30 Within Germany, 

 
implementation of whole-population skin cancer screening led to increased incidence rates 
for KC.29 In other countries, efforts have focused more on raising awareness about skin 
cancer, and encouraging self-examination and seeking medical attention for suspicious 
lesions.31 For example, in Australia and the UK, efforts have focused on educational 
interventions promoting sun-protective behaviours and early detection, such as the UK Sun 
Awareness national campaign.32-35 It could be speculated that countries that have 
implemented more successful strategies aimed at promoting public awareness and early 
detection are identifying more cases of early SCC, when they are curable, thus resulting in 
lower mortality. Importantly, there is limited evidence currently available to suggest that 
either educational interventions or screening lead to overall reductions in mortality from KC, 
with most studies thus far focussing on the impact on melanoma.36-40  
In this study, we used trends in DALYs to assess the burden of disease from SCC. We found 
Australia, Romania, Latvia and the US to have the highest DALYs at the end of the 
observation period for both sexes. There appeared to be less association between geography 
and DALYs in this study, suggesting that a complex combination of other factors contribute 
to ultimate burden of disease. Of note, while our study found incidence rates to have 
continued increasing over the past 27 years in the majority of countries, more countries have 
started to show decreasing trends in mortality rates and DALYs. In this retrospective 
observational study, we cannot conclusively attribute causality to the trends. There have 
certainly been advancements in the management of SCC, including optimisation of surgical 
modalities and radiotherapy16; use of immunotherapeutic agents has also become standard of 
care for advanced SCC in countries including the UK.28,41,42 However, it may be that a 
combination of primary prevention, early detection, and treatment strategies implemented 
over the past decades have led to reductions in mortality secondary to SCC.  

 
Interestingly, a recent study published by Urban et al. looked at trends in KC across the 7 
GBD super-regions, and found that globally, the prevalence of SCCs increased by 310% 
between 1990-2017, while the percentage of total DALYs due to KC increased from 0.03% 
in 1990 to 0.05% in 2017. In comparison, we examined epidemiological trends among 
individual countries within the Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and High-Income super-
regions, and found that mortality and DALYs had decreased in most countries by 2017 
compared to 1990. This again highlights the disparity in trends among countries globally, for 
which underlying factors are likely to include health expenditure, public health strategies, and 
availability of different treatments.43 
There are several limitations to consider when interpreting the results of this study. Firstly, 
this was a retrospective observational study looking at data limited to the identification and 
comparison of trends. It is therefore not possible to draw any causal relationships between 
proposed exposures and the KC outcomes. Secondly, recording of incidence and mortality 
secondary to KC is fraught with difficulties. KC are common with comparatively low 
mortality and are diagnosed in a variety of specialist and non-specialist settings, leading to 
difficulty in ensuring consistent recording of all cases. Furthermore, multiple tumours 
frequently occur or recur within one individual, with different cancer registries adopting 
different methods for recording these – one method is to record only the first case of KC 
within one individual, and not any multiple or subsequent tumours.5,44-47 Cancer registries 
often exclude KC altogether due to these difficulties. Considering that cancer registries were 
a major data source for the GBD and therefore for this study for deriving incidence estimates, 
these variations in KC case registration worldwide may affect the reliability of data and the 
comparability of trends between different countries. Third, differences in healthcare systems, 
both in terms of funding and management strategies, may lead to variations that decrease the 
comparability of trends both between countries. Finally, changes in coding practices and 

 
systems (including the change from International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 to ICD-
10 during the study period) may compromise data robustness. However, the mapping of ICD 
codes to causes within the GBD cause list attempts to adjust for differing coding systems.48  
Difficulties in obtaining accurate incidence estimates have been cited in many publications 
relating to these diseases, and with the above-mentioned differences in practices when 
registering these cancers, there is no single reliable data source that perfectly reflects the 
status quo. Most publications rely on disease registries in combination with statistical 
modelling to adjust for missing data. Only a very limited number of countries globally have 
detailed cancer registries that include KC. But even for these countries, the selection of the 
standard population when using age-standardised rates can greatly influence the final 
estimates. All the above can lead to significant differences when reporting on incidence rates 
in literature. For example, Kwiatkowska et al. reported on KC incidence in the UK between 
2013-2018 and noted significantly higher rates than reported by the GBD.49 They used 
national cancer registries and pathology reports as input data and standardised rates using the 
European standard population. This, as well as differences in coding and registration 
practices between this study and the GBD dataset, limit the direct comparability. Similarly, 
Adalsteinsson et al. used national cancer registry data from Iceland, standardised by the world 
population, to estimate national BCC incidence rates between 1980-2017.50 In this instance, 
their estimates lie below the GBD data, but they predominantly show an increase over time, 
while the GBD estimates largely stable rates. The GBD study models estimate for BCC 
incidence in Iceland using data derived from multiple data sources, including the Icelandic 
Cancer Registry, Icelandic Statistical Yearbook, Health Statistics in Nordic Countries report, 
and the NORDCAN database. The variety of data sources used per country for different years 
likely accounts for some of these observed differences.  

 
While acknowledging these limitations, the main strength of this study is the 
comprehensiveness of the data analysed. We utilised national statistics for all countries 
classified by the GBD as having high-quality data over a long observation period, allowing 
representation of many countries with quality-controlled data in our analysis. By using sex-
specific, age-standardised rates to make comparisons, we aimed to minimise the effects of 
any confounding variables to analyse the most robust global data available. 
While the GBD estimates may vary from other reports in literature, and the different 
methodology somewhat limits direct comparison of rates between these studies, the uniform 
application of the GBD modelling to all countries over the entire time frame still allows 
important time trend observations within a country. Moreover, the GBD modelling allows 
accounting and partially correcting for inter-country differences in coding and registration 
practices, and comparison between countries is therefore likely more feasible than individual 
national-level studies applying entirely different methodological practices. Lastly, the GBD 
modelling undergoes several iterations and continuous comparison to the most robust data 
available until best model fit is achieved, with well performing fit statistics for non-
melanoma skin cancers.9 
In conclusion, this study ﬁnds generally increasing incidence rates in both BCC and SCC 
over the past 27 years. However, mortality rates have started decreasing in a significant 
number of countries, especially towards the end of the observation period. Overall, burden of 
disease as assessed using DALYs has decreased in most countries. Future work should 
explore factors underlying the observed disparity in trends in incidence, mortality, and burden 
of disease.
