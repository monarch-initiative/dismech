# Adult-Onset Bilateral Optic Neuropathy in a Patient with Non-Familial Childhood-Onset Generalized Dystonia Associated with Mitochondrial DNA 14459G>A Mutation: A Case Report and Review of Literature.
**Authors:** Thaller M, Samra AP, Chaudhary UJ, Roque M, Pall H, Mollan SP, Srinivasan V
**Journal:** Neuroophthalmology (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1080/01658107.2024.2405697](https://doi.org/10.1080/01658107.2024.2405697)

## Content

The occurrence of Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy in association with dystonia is exceedingly rare. There have been only a few previously reported cases describing this clinical phenotype with the mitochondrial DNA 14459 G>A/ND6 mutation. This mutation has been described to also manifest as isolated Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy or Leigh Syndrome/Leigh-like Syndrome in a very small number of patients. We report the case of a 27-year-old female who presented with bilateral sequential optic neuropathy on a background of non-familial generalized dystonia. Magnetic resonance imaging performed during childhood had shown bilateral high signal changes in the basal ganglia. Extensive testing for a possible autoimmune etiology was unrevealing. Her vision did not improve with aggressive steroid and plasma exchange treatment. Targeted genetic testing revealed a mitochondrial DNA 14459 G>A/ND6 mutation. Genetic analysis for the mitochondrial DNA 14459 G>A/ND6 mutation should be tested in a patient presenting with bilateral sequential optic neuropathy with co-morbid dystonia.

The association of Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON) with spastic dystonia (OMIM 500001), which is characterized by optic atrophy, spastic dystonia, and bilateral basal ganglia lesions, is rare. This unique disorder was first reported by Marsden et al. in seven members of two families in whom dystonia was variably associated with subacute visual loss or asymptomatic optic atrophy, and striking bilateral symmetric lucencies, especially in the putamen, were found on computerized tomography.1 A family with similar clinical characteristics reported by Novotny et al. was subsequently found by Jun et al. to harbor a mitochondrial (m.) DNA 14459 G>A/ND6 mutation.2,3

The combination of spastic dystonia and LHON has since been reported in only a few unconnected families with a m.14459G>A/ND6 mutation, combined m.14459G>A and m.6064A>T, m.14596T>A/ND6 mutation, m.3697G>A/ND1 mutation, and families without a mitochondrial DNA mutation.4–13 Kurt et al. recently reported heteroplasmic m.14459G>A mutations in two unrelated children with non-maternally inherited generalized dystonia and showing bilateral magnetic resonance imaging lesions in nucleus pallidus and putamen.14 Both children had reached their teenage years, and they were intellectually active, despite their motor problems.

This right-handed female of British Indian ethnicity symptoms initially started at around the age of 3 years with tip toe walking and clenching of hands. Prior to this, she had a normal gestation and motor milestones. This dystonic posturing started initially on the right side and over the next 3 years became generalized affecting her speech, face, arms, and both legs. Her mental function remained well preserved. A diagnosis of idiopathic torsion dystonia was suggested at age 4 years. She developed fixed flexion deformity of the right leg with contractures as early as age 14. There was no family history of dystonia or consanguinity.

Extensive investigations were performed throughout her childhood and adolescent years. Tests for blood copper, ceruloplasmin, vacuolated lymphocytes, cholesterol, triglyceride, lysosomal enzymes, carnitine, acetyl L-carnitine, and urine organic acid were all normal. Her cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination showed a CSF lactate level of 1.8 mmol/l (normal range in an adolescent and adult 1.2–2.2 mmol/l). CSF neurotransmitter metabolites and amino acid levels were normal. MRI scan of brain showed bilateral high signal changes in basal ganglia. DNA testing for mitochondrial DNA and DYT1 gene mutations were negative.

Her generalized dystonia was non-responsive to L-dopa, baclofen, and tizanidine treatment trials. For her dystonia, she was implanted with a Bilateral Globus Pallidus Internal Deep Brain Stimulator (DBS) at age 15 years, and for lower leg pains, a dorsal root ganglion stimulator was inserted at age 25 years. She uses a wheelchair. She remains intellectually active despite her motor problems and has achieved a postgraduate university degree.

This 27-year-old female presented to the Neurology Department with sudden onset blurring of vision on waking up, which initially affected the right eye but rapidly also involved the left. There was no pain on eye movement, nor was there any history of head injury, recent travel, infections, hearing problems, new onset focal weakness, or sensory symptoms.

Her neurological examination at this time revealed generalized dystonia affecting her face, speech, arms, and legs. Best-corrected visual acuity was 6/36 OD (right eye) and 6/36 OS (left eye). She could only see the Ishihara color plate on the right and 2/17 plates on the left. Pupils are both 2–3 mm, normally reactive to light with a right relative afferent pupillary defect. Goldman visual field examination showed central scotomas (Figure 1a,b), dilated fundoscopy showed bilateral temporal pallor, with the optic nerve heads being worse on the right, and retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) imaging with optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Heidelberg Spectralis™ platform SD-OCT) on the right eye revealed central and temporal thinning and temporal thinning on the left eye (Figure 2). Cranial nerves examination was otherwise normal. Her cerebellar and sensory examinations were normal.

Computed tomography (CT) scan of brain showed the presence of bilateral implantation of DBS leads in correct position with expected accompanying artifact. No intracranial or retro-orbital abnormalities were seen. Magnetic resonance imaging was not possible as an adult as this was precluded by the presence of a dorsal root ganglion stimulator. Visual evoked potentials revealed no definite reproducible cortical response bilaterally. Blood tests including B12 and folate levels, thyroid function tests, urea and electrolytes, full blood count, calcium levels, creatine kinase, angiotensin-converting enzyme, and C-reactive protein were all normal. Serum immuno-electrophoresis for IgA, IgG, and IgM was normal. CSF examination showed two polymorphs and four mononuclear cells, protein: 0.96 g/l (normal range 0.15–0.45 g/l), glucose: 5.2 mmol/l (serum 6.2 mmol/l) and IgG oligoclonal bands in CSF only. CSF microscopy showed reactive lymphohistiocytic pleocytosis: mononuclear cells consistent with activated lymphocytes and reactive monocytes/macrophages. A single mitosis was noted, and there was no evidence of malignant cells. Immunologic testing for antibodies GABA1, AMPA1, AMPA2, NMDA, LGI1, Mitochondrial (M2), ANA, dsDNA, Neutrophil Cytoplasmic Ab, Anti MPO, and Anti PR3 were all negative or normal except CASPR2, which was equivocal. Serum Aquaporin4 and MOG antibodies were negative. There was no evidence of generalized sensorimotor neuropathy on nerve conduction studies.

While awaiting results of her mitochondrial DNA tests, in view of the possibility of an autoimmune basis for her bilateral subacute optic neuropathy, she was given a course of high-dose intravenous steroids for three days and five sessions of plasmapheresis with no significant improvement of visual acuity or visual field in either eye (Figure 1c,d). Whilst visual problems were present, she reported significant red and green discoloration of her vision. The visual fields first deteriorated (Figure 1c,d) and then partially recovered over time (Figure 1e,f). The RNFL imaging with OCT showed progressive thinning (atrophy) in both eyes over the course of the review (Figure 2).

Mitochondrial DNA analysis for MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like episodes), MERRF (Myoclonic Epilepsy and Ragged Red Muscle Fibres), and NARP (Neurogenic muscle weakness, Ataxia, and Retinitis Pigmentosa) were negative. Common LHON mutations: m.3460G>A, m.11778G>A, and m.14484T>C were negative. Further targeted testing detected a LHON and dystonia-associated mutation: m.14459G>A with 97% heteroplasmy.

Leber’s Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON) is caused by mutations in one of three mitochondrial DNA-encoded complex I genes (m.3460G>A, m.11778G>A, and m.14484T>C) in 95% of cases; however, where these are negative, then whole mitochondrial genome testing for other mutations is recommended where the clinical suspicion remains. This is especially important for people with LHON plus syndrome, where there is evidence of bilateral optic neuropathy and additional neurological problems, for example dystonia or a Leigh-like syndrome.

In this case, our patient had a preceding spastic dystonia, 24 years prior to the development of ocular symptoms, which warranted further targeted testing. This confirmed the mitochondrial mutation m.14459G>A in the ND6 region. The specific association of the m.14459G>A variant with LHON and dystonia has its own disease name of Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy and dystonia (LDYT, OMIM #500001).

In the literature, including on the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/variation/9689/?oq=14459G&m=m.14459G%3EA), there is marked variation reported of the m.14459G>A presentation, and it has been shown to present with any combination of LHON, dystonia, and Leigh syndrome. There are inter-generational differences in presentation of this mutation, with LHON predominating in earlier generations and dystonia in the later ones.2,3 Within siblings, there is varying symptomatology with the same mutation noted.5,6,10,11 Leigh syndrome, a subacute necrotizing encephalomyelopathy usually fatal by approximately 5 years, has been shown at the more typical onset of infancy to early childhood,15,16 presenting most commonly with seizures and non-prominent dystonia but without visual impairment.15 However, a Leigh-like syndrome has been demonstrated in young adults with LHON.17

The reason for the striking difference in the clinical outcome of the m.14459G>A mutation is a matter of ongoing research. The pathological manifestation of the m.14459G>A mutation is thought to be highly tissue-specific, depending also on the levels of heteroplasmy present in each organ or tissue involved. This was seen in a Japanese series with homoplasmic mutations resulting in infantile dystonia and heteroplasmic variant showing a later juvenile onset truncal ataxia and parkinsonism.18 The existence of secondary mtDNA variants able to modify the disease presentation of a primary mutation has been suggested before.19,20 The involvement of visual pathways in 14459G>A-mutated patients is generally a later event in contrast to dystonia that develops in the early childhood.

People with LHON will often have had courses of corticosteroids, especially where there is a rapid deterioration in vision. This is due to the differential diagnosis of inflammatory optic neuropathy, which includes multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease. In inflammatory optic neuropathy, corticosteroids can help preserve vision or speed up recovery, although in LHON, there is no evidence of benefit.21,22

The occurrence of LHON in association with dystonia is rare. It is important to consider the possibility of mitochondrial DNA mutation m.14459 G>A in cases of bilateral sequential subacute optic neuropathy suspected to be LHON if the common LHON mutations m.3460G>A, m.11778G>A, and m.14484T>C are absent, particularly if associated clinical features of dystonia are present.
