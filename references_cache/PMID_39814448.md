OBJECTIVE: Population-based epidemiology studies about antisynthetase syndrome 
(ASS) are lacking. Our aims were to determine the incidence and prevalence of 
ASS and assess malignancy risk among patients following ASS diagnosis.
METHODS: A retrospective, population-based cohort of adults with incident ASS 
residing in Olmsted County, Minnesota, in 1998-2019, was assembled. Fulfillment 
of Solomon classification criteria for ASS and clinical data were collected by 
manual chart review. Patients were followed until death, migration from the 
area, or December 31, 2019. Malignancy was defined by physician diagnosis in the 
medical record. Incidence rate was age- and sex-adjusted to the 2010 US White 
population. Point prevalence rate was obtained on January 1, 2015.
RESULTS: Thirteen patients with ASS were identified (7 [54%] female, 13 [100%] 
White, median age 44.9 [IQR 41.9-58.3] years). The age- and sex-adjusted 
incidence of ASS was 0.56 (95% CI 0.25-0.87) per 100,000 population. Incidence 
was highest in the 50-59 age group. Age- and sex-adjusted prevalence was 9.21 
per 100,000 (95% CI 3.44-14.98). Two of 13 (15%) were diagnosed with malignancy 
within the follow-up interval and none within 3 years of ASS diagnosis. At 
median 11.9 (IQR 7.0-13.4) years of follow-up, 12/13 (92%) of patients were 
alive.
CONCLUSION: ASS is rare, with an incidence of 0.56 per 100,000 population and 
prevalence of 9.21 per 100,000. In this cohort, incidence was similar between 
male and female individuals, and was highest in persons aged 50-59 years. None 
of the patients developed malignancy within 3 years of ASS diagnosis.

The antisynthetase syndrome (ASSD) is a distinct subgroup of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies characterized by myositis, interstitial lung disease, inflammatory arthritis, Raynaud phenomenon and an aminoacyl tRNA synthetase autoantibody. The condition was defined in 1990 (1,2). The Jo-1 antibody, present in 10–40% of patients with ASSD, and directed against the histidyl-tRNA synthetase, is the most common associated antibody (3,4). Other more recently associated antibodies include PL-7, PL-12, EJ, OJ, Ha, Ks, and Zo (3,4).

Population-based epidemiology studies of ASSD are lacking. The estimated prevalence of the Jo-1 antibody is 1.5/100,000, yet incidence and prevalence of the disease are unknown (1,3,4). Epidemiological studies have been challenging due to the heterogeneity of the disease and lack of consensus-driven, validated criteria for ASSD (5,6). The discovery of additional autoantibodies which define ASSD, as well as developments in imaging and other diagnostic testing, may be contributing to an increase in diagnosed cases.

Dermatomyositis, an autoimmune condition characterized by muscle inflammation and characteristic skin rashes which shares some features of ASSD, has been associated with increased risk of malignancy, particularly in the first three years of disease (7,8). Despite many cases of malignancy in patients with ASSD reported in the literature, estimates of frequency vary widely from 1.7 to 19.4% (9,10). To date there is no consensus on association between ASSD and malignancy, particularly in the early stages of the disease. The need for specific cancer screening recommendations for patients with newly diagnosed ASSD is largely unknown, although recently published, expert-driven consensus guidelines for cancer screening in newly diagnosed idiopathic inflammatory myopathies has classified patients with ASSD to be at low-risk for occult cancer (11).

Our aims were to determine the incidence and prevalence of ASSD in a well-defined geographic area over a 22-year period, and to assess malignancy risk among incident ASSD patients within 3 years of diagnosis.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Mayo Clinic (20–001744) and Olmsted Medical Center (014-OMC-20). A waiver of informed consent was granted for this minimal risk study.

A retrospective, population-based cohort was assembled, comprised of adults with incident ASSD residing in Olmsted County, Minnesota, between Jan 1, 1998, and Dec 31, 2019. Patients were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes corresponding to relevant conditions. The electronic medical charts of patients who had a positive Jo-1 antibody, and of all patients with ICD codes corresponding to inflammatory myopathy (G72.41) or myositis (710.3, 710.4, D89.89, G72.41, G72.49, M33.10, M33.11, M33.19, MDD.20, M33.21, M33.29, M33.90, M33.91, M33.99), were reviewed manually by study personnel (CMC, WL). A random sampling of 50 patients with ICD codes corresponding to other myopathy (359.9, G72.89, G72.9, G73.7) were reviewed manually for inclusion as well, to ensure no missed cases of ASSD. Fulfillment of Solomon et al. criteria (12) and clinical data were collected by manual chart review (CMC4), via a validated medical records linkage system (13). Cases of “incomplete ASSD” were not included for the purposes of this study (10). Patients who met criteria but were diagnosed with an alternative disease were excluded from study. Interstitial lung disease was defined by physician diagnosis in the medical record, inclusive of findings on pulmonary function testing, radiography, and/or computed tomography (CT) scan. Malignancy was defined by physician diagnosis in the medical record.

Patients were followed until death, migration from the area, or December 31, 2019. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with ASSD. Age-specific and sex-specific incidence rates were calculated using the number of incident cases as the numerator and population counts from the REP census as the denominator (14). Age-specific and sex-specific prevalence rates were calculated using the number of prevalent cases on Jan 1, 2015, as the numerator and population counts from the REP census as the denominator (14). Incidence and prevalence rates were age- and sex-adjusted to the 2010 U.S. White population. In order to compute 95% confidence intervals for incidence and prevalence rates, it was assumed that the number of cases followed a Poisson distribution. Time-to-event analysis methods were not used to summarize characteristics that developed during follow-up due to the small sample size. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

13 patients with ASSD who fulfilled Solomon et al. criteria in the geographic area were identified in the 22-year study period. 7 (54%) were female, 13 (100%) Caucasian, median age 44.9 years (IQR: 41.9–58.3) at time of meeting criteria.

Thirteen patients (100%) had positive anti-nuclear antibody; 12 (92%) had positive Jo-1 antibody (Figure 1). A myositis-specific panel was positive in 1 out of 4 patients showing PL-7 antibody. At time of meeting criteria, 4/13 patients (31%) had fever, 11 (85%) had interstitial lung disease (ILD) diagnosed by a physician, 11 (85%) had myositis, and 6 (46%) required oxygen supplementation. Of the 11 patients who ever developed ILD, 8 patients (73%) had a nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) pattern (Figure 1). Of the 11 patients with myositis, 6 (55%) had a positive electromyogram (EMG), 1 (9%) had positive muscle magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and 5 (45%) had muscle biopsy performed, all of which demonstrated inflammatory myopathy.

At any point during the disease course, all 13 patients were treated with corticosteroids (100%), 9 (69%) with mycophenolate mofetil, 8 (62%) with azathioprine, 6 (46%) with methotrexate, 3 (23%) with rituximab, 2 (15%) with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and 1 (8%) with cyclophosphamide.

The age- and sex-adjusted incidence of ASSD was 0.56 (95% CI: 0.25–0.87) per 100,000 population. The age-adjusted incidence was 0.59 (95% CI: 0.15–1.03) per 100,000 for females, and 0.52 (95% CI: 0.10–0.94) per 100,000 for males. Incidence rate was highest in the 50–59 age group, at 1.01 per 100,000 population. Age- and sex-adjusted prevalence of ASSD on Jan 1, 2015, was 9.2 per 100,000 (95% CI: 3.4–15.0).

Within the follow-up interval, 2/13 (15%) were diagnosed with a malignancy (Hodgkin’s lymphoma, leiomyosarcoma) , neither of which was within 3 years of ASSD diagnosis. Mean time to malignancy diagnosis from ASSD criteria was 52 (range: 40–64) months. At a median 11.9 (IQR: 7.0–13.4) years of follow-up, 12/13 (92%) of patients were alive.

The antisynthetase syndrome, characterized by an aminoacyl tRNA synthetase autoantibody, myositis, interstitial lung disease, inflammatory arthritis, and Raynaud’s phenomenon, is a distinct disease entity initially defined in 1990 (1,2). Since then, tRNA synthetase antibodies other than the Jo-1 have been identified and described (3,4), yet the incidence and prevalence of ASSD itself has been unknown. Epidemiologic studies have been challenging due to rarity of the disease, lack of consensus classification criteria, overlapping features with other rheumatologic conditions, and lack of ICD code specific to ASSD.

This study identified 13 patients with antisynthetase syndrome who fulfilled Solomon et al. criteria (12) during a 22-year study interval between 1998 and 2019. Ours is the first population-based epidemiological study demonstrating that the incidence of ASSD in a geographically based population is 0.56 per 100,000 population, after adjusting for age and sex. The prevalence of ASSD in this cohort on Jan 1, 2015, was 9.2 per 100,000.

The results of this study are in line with estimates of incidence of all idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) ranging from 11 to 660 per one million person-years (15), as ASSD represents a subset of these conditions. Similarly, prevalence estimates for IIM range from 25 to 34 per 100,000 individuals (15) in other North American cohorts, with prevalence of ASSD expected to be lower than IIM conditions as whole. Highest incidence of ASSD was observed in the 5th and 6th decades of life, as observed in IIM conditions as a whole (15). A slight predominance of female (54%) versus male patients with incident ASSD in this cohort differs from prior research reporting a 2:1 female/male ratio (12, 15). The majority of patients in this cohort (92%) had a positive Jo-1 autoantibody, and only 1 of the 13 patients had non-Jo-1 tRNA synthetase antibody detected (PL-7). The other known autoantibodies associated with ASSD were not detected in this population; this may be a reflection of the study’s small sample size and low prevalence overall (6,15), as well as lack of commercially available testing for these antibodies until the later years of this study interval.

An important finding of this study was the fact that in this cohort, there were no cases of associated malignancy within the first 3 years after fulfilling criteria for ASSD. While a malignancy risk in dermatomyositis has been clearly established, with approximately 25% of patients with dermatomyositis being diagnosed with a malignancy within 3 years (5,6), data supporting a potential relationship between ASSD and malignancy has been mixed. Reports exist of patients with ASSD who have malignancies concurrently diagnosed, including lung, breast, ovarian, renal, liver, colon cancer and melanoma (9,10, 16–18). However, a large cohort of 233 patients with ASSD by Hervier et al. found cancer association no higher than that of the general population (10). Furthermore, a large meta-analysis by Oldroyd et.al involving 69 myositis studies found that the presence of interstitial lung disease and any anti-t RNA synthetase antibody—in particular Jo-1—was significantly associated with a lower risk of cancer (19). In the current study, 2/13 (15%) patients with ASSD were diagnosed with a malignancy (Hodgkin’s lymphoma, leiomyosarcoma) within the follow-up interval, with mean time of 52 (range: 40–64) months to malignancy diagnosis from meeting ASSD criteria. The data from this cohort does not suggest an association between development of ASSD and malignancy.

Strengths of this study include a novel investigation into the epidemiology of ASSD. Other strengths include a geographically-based cohort of patients, with access to records from nearly all medical facilities in the area (13) to allow for comprehensive review despite a retrospective design, a 22-year study interval with median 11.9 years of follow-up for patients, and manual medical record review by physicians.

The primary limitations of this study are its retrospective nature and small cohort size of 13 patients, which reflects the rarity of the disease. ASSD was identified in 1990, and therefore a longer study interval would not be feasible. Additionally, there is potential for missed cases due to developments in testing for anti-synthetase antibodies, which did not become commercially available until the later years of this study’s interval, as well as ASSD’s heterogeneous nature with no consensus definition (5). This risk has been minimized by the use of Solomon et al. criteria for all included cases and by use of manual medical chart review (12). Finally, a mortality analysis was not feasible due to only one death observed in the study cohort.

In summary, antisynthetase syndrome is rare, with incidence of 0.56 per 100,000 population and prevalence of 9 per 100,000. Incidence appears similar in our cohort between males and females, differing from previous estimates of higher incidence in females, and highest in persons ages 50–59 years. None of the patients in this cohort developed malignancy within 3 years of ASSD diagnosis.
