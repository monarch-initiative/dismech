# Limited benefit of liver transplantation in a boy with biallelic severe deficiency of hydroxymethylbilane synthase and review of prior reported cases.
**Authors:** Araque M, Ma CD, Jordan A, Faust D, McGuire L, Bonkovsky HL
**Journal:** Mol Genet Metab Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymgmr.2026.101334](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymgmr.2026.101334)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: A male infant presented at three months of age with generalized ataxia, hypotonia, aspiration of liquids and recurrent generalized seizures. He was treated with levetiracetam and phenobarbital. Methods: Extensive testing, including whole genome sequencing was done.

RESULTS: He had two known pathogenic variants in the HMBS gene: p.R167Q [maternal] and p.T35M [paternal]. Plasma and urine exhibited high concentrations of 5-aminolevulinic acid, porphobilinogen, and uroporphyrin 1. Activity of hydroxymethylbilane synthase in red blood cells of the child was markedly reduced [18 nmol uroporphyrin/ g hemoglobin /h; reference range: 60-335]; it was ∼50% of normal in parents. The findings supported the diagnosis of biallelic severe HMBS deficiency with severe disease phenotype. A 5-day course of intravenous heme led to no observable clinical improvement. Orthotopic liver transplantation at the age of 15 months led to only mild transient improvement. Developmental delays and seizures persisted. MRI scans of the brain showed progressive white matter volume loss, cystic changes, and multifocal supratentorial signal abnormalities. He died at 88 months of age. Findings at autopsy of the brain showed patchy gliosis and leukodystrophy. We also review 11 previously reported cases.

CONCLUSION: No effective disease-modifying therapy currently exists for biallelic HMBS deficiency. Substantial neurological injury is present by the time of diagnosis and seems irreversible.

Acute intermittent porphyria [AIP] is an autosomal dominant disorder resulting from pathological variants in the HMBS gene, which produce defects in hydroxymethylbilane synthase [HMBS] activity in the heme biosynthesis pathway and may lead to an accumulation of 5-aminolevulinic acid [ALA] and porphobilinogen [PBG] [Fig. 1]. [1], [2], [3], [4] AIP classically presents in post-menarchal women in adolescence or young adulthood with episodic neurovisceral crises. [5] In rare circumstances, biallelic homozygous or compound heterozygous pathogenic HMBS variants result in profoundly reduced enzyme activity, producing a disease entity distinct from classical autosomal dominant AIP. These biallelic forms have been associated with variably but generally severe neurodevelopmental disorders, known as HMBS-related leukoencephalopathy, characterized by developmental delay, hypotonia, epilepsy, and poor neurological outcomes. [6], [7], [8] To the best of our knowledge, fewer than 15 persons with biallelic HMBS mutations have been described heretofore [Table 1], and the mechanisms underlying early, persistent central nervous system [CNS] dysfunction remain incompletely understood. Orthotopic liver transplantation [OLT] has been consistently curative for classical hepatic AIP, as replacing the native liver restores normal hepatic HMBS activity and corrects the overproduction of ALA and PBG; however, its efficacy in congenital AIP with predominantly neurological symptoms is uncertain. [5], [11], [12] In previously reported patients with biallelic heme pathway defects, including a woman with biallelic mutations in HMBS, OLT failed to normalize porphyrin precursor levels or halt neurological progression, suggesting extrahepatic overproduction of ALA and PBG. [6], [9], [13], [14], [15] These cases raise important mechanistic questions regarding tissue-specific heme requirements, blood-brain barrier permeability, and the role of toxic intermediates in neurodevelopment.

In this report, we describe a child with biallelic, compound heterozygous congenital AIP who exhibited profound neurological impairment and developmental delays from infancy, with persistent elevations in ALA, PBG, and porphyrins with only partial biochemical response and no sustained clinical response to intravenous heme or OLT. This case provides more insight into the pathophysiology of compound heterozygous AIP, the limitations of liver-directed therapies, and the possible contribution of extrahepatic heme biosynthetic dysfunction and poor blood-brain barrier permeability to heme or other potential therapies, such as givosiran.

A 12-month-old boy with a history of hypogammaglobulinemia treated with IVIG, severe developmental delays, hypotonia, systemic arterial hypertension, and refractory seizures presented to our clinics with his parents for management of newly diagnosed compound heterozygous AIP. He had been delivered at 37 weeks by Caesarean section because of breech presentation following an otherwise uncomplicated pregnancy. His parents were not consanguineous. His early neonatal period was notable for mild jaundice not requiring phototherapy, along with hypotonia and feeding difficulties. Very early in life, his mother noted a lack of visual tracking by the infant. Over the ensuing months, he demonstrated subtle developmental delays, recurrent episodes of apnea, and generalized tonic seizures with oxygen desaturations, prompting hospitalization and initiation of levetiracetam and phenobarbital. By 4 months of age, his generalized hypotonia had worsened, with poor head control and increasingly prominent global developmental delays.

Lumbar puncture was not performed for analysis of levels of ALA, PBG, or porphyrins due to the risks of sedation in an infant with refractory seizures and declination of the procedure by his parents. Comprehensive epilepsy and mitochondrial genetic panels were unremarkable apart from multiple heterozygous variants of unknown significance, which prompted whole exome sequencing. This extended sequencing identified compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in HMBS, namely, c. 500 G > A, p.R167Q [maternal] and c. 104C > T, p.T35M [paternal], establishing the diagnosis of congenital biallelic AIP. [The maternal mutation is a common and well-known pathogenic mutation, whereas the paternal mutation is less common, but, based upon our experience, is also pathogenic and associated with ∼50% reduction in activity of HMBS]. [4] Following this diagnosis, the parents established care with The Porphyrias Center of Excellence at Wake Forest University, as well as continuing care at academic centers in Cincinnati, OH and Indianapolis, IN. Because of the known risks of barbiturates in AIP, his medication regimen was adjusted, including tapering off phenobarbital, which is well-known to increase hepatic ALA synthase-1 and over-production of ALA and PBG. His initial porphyria labs at age 14 months revealed markedly elevated urinary ALA [105 mg/g creatinine (Cr); reference 0–7 mg/g Cr], PBG [289 mg/g Cr; reference 0–4 mg/g Cr], and total porphyrins [4269 nmol/g Cr, predominantly uroporphyrin 1; reference 0–300 nmol/g Cr], with severely reduced erythrocyte HMBS activity [18 nmol uroporphyrin/g Hb/h; reference: 60–152 nmol uroporphyrin/g Hb /h]. His parents were tested as well; both had normal urinary ALA/creatinine, whereas his mother had mildly increased urinary PBG/creatinine and total porphyrin/creatinine levels. The erythrocyte HMBS activities in his mother and father were 29 and 42.4 nmol uroporphyrin/g Hb/h, respectively, ∼50% decreased activities, consistent with classical AIP.

At the age of 14 months, he was admitted to the inpatient service at Brenner's Children Hospital of Wake Forest NC Baptist for a 5-day course of intravenous hematin [Panhematin, Recordati Rare Chemicals], which led to a partial ∼50% biochemical improvement [urinary ALA 22 mg/g Cr, urinary PBG 68 mg/g Cr, urinary porphyrins ∼2000 nmol/g Cr]. Weekly infusions of IV hematin were continued until liver transplantation.

Due to the persistence of his clinical, biochemical, and genetic findings, and despite concerns that liver transplantation might not prove effective, he underwent an OLT at 15 months of age at Indiana University/Riley Children's Hospital. The liver donor was unrelated to the infant and had no history to suggest AIP or any other form of porphyria. No genetic or biochemical testing of the organ donor for AIP, or any other types of porphyria, was carried out.

His post-transplantation course was complicated by multiple episodes of rejection, adenovirus and metapneumovirus infections, and C. difficile colitis. Despite adequate graft function with adherence to immunosuppression, his neurologic status showed only mild and transient improvement, and he continued to exhibit frequent seizures, including gelastic seizure activity, global developmental delay, and severe hypotonia. A permanent gastrostomy tube was placed after a swallowing study demonstrated aspiration. Because severe adenoid hyperplasia and obstructive sleep apnea had been noted, he underwent an adenoidectomy with improvement in sleep apnea. A follow-up MRI of the brain showed mild cerebral volume loss [Fig. 2]. Repeat NMR spectroscopy did not reveal a lactate peak in the brain.

We carried out studies of mitochondrial functions and energetics in the child and his parents, as part of a pilot study of these variables in patients with AIP. [16] The child was more severely affected than his parents, and there were significant correlations between the severity of the energetic defects and the over-production of ALA and PBG. [16]

Repeat testing for ALA, PBG, and porphyrins was obtained 2 years after his OLT and continued to reveal elevated urinary ALA, 25.6 mg/g Cr, urinary PBG, 79.5 mg/g Cr, and urinary porphyrins, 1815 nmol/g Cr, which were about 50% reduced from his pre-transplant measurements.

The child continued to receive monthly infusions of IVIG for hypogammaglobulinemia, which persisted. His seizures continued to worsen, and repeat EEGs showed a Lennox-Gastaut pattern, prompting the implantation of a vagal nerve stimulator [VNS] when he was 5 years old. Despite multiple medications and VNS setting adjustments, seizures continued. Sleep apnea recurred.

His mother felt that, in weeks prior to death, her son was able to ‘make decisions with eye gaze’, able to take thickened foods, and able to move extremities independently with prompted direction, albeit with ataxia.

Unfortunately, despite extensive multidisciplinary care, including multiple swallowing studies, bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage, and esophagogastroduodenoscopy, and ongoing efforts at enrichment of his environment and mechanical supports of his posture and lower extremities, he continued to experience recurrent seizures, terminal status epilepticus, pulmonary aspiration, and progressive weakness. In view of his overall course, his parents elected to stop continuous artificial ventilation and to make organs available for transplantation to others. The patient ultimately died at 88 months, not long after mechanical ventilation had been withdrawn, from respiratory failure and sepsis related to status epilepticus with aspiration pneumonia.

Initial electroencephalogram [EEG] completed at 6 months of age showed intermittent left occipital focal slowing and left centro-temporal polyspike and slow wave discharges. By 5 years of age the EEG had evolved to show generalized slow spike and wave complexes with atonic, tonic and tonic-clonic seizures captured on a prolonged study.

Brain MRI at 7 months of age showed enlargement of the bifrontal extra-axial fluid spaces without any signal abnormality [Fig. 2]. Follow-up MRI imaging at 20 months of age, after liver transplant, was stable showing prominence of the supratentorial subarachnoid spaces. MRI imaging at 4 years of age showed moderate white matter volume loss and T2 hyperintensities in the frontoparietal periventricular white matter. MRI imaging at 7 years of age demonstrated extensive signal abnormality in the supratentorial bilateral white matter with cavitation and cystic changes most notable in the left frontal white matter. Also noted was extensive volume loss in the supratentorial white matter and less severe volume loss in the cerebellum [Fig. 2].

Post-mortem examination was carried out on the child shortly after death. His weight was 20.4 kg [25–30 percentile]; height was 121 cm [40–50 percentile. Most organs and tissues appeared unremarkable at both gross and microscopic examination. The weight of the brain was 1110 g [∼35 percentile]. Sections from the brain showed terminal global ischemic injury with multiple areas demonstrating pericellular edema, neuropil vacuolization, and numerous hypereosinophilic and pyknotic “red dead” neurons.

The frontal lobes, basal ganglia, temporal lobes, midbrain, and hippocampus revealed multiple red neurons consistent with subacute to chronic ischemic injury. The basal ganglia showed reactive axonal swelling and degeneration along with few perivascular hemosiderin-laden macrophages highlighted on stains for CD68 and CD163. There were no significant areas of demyelination. Sections of the cortical band throughout the sampled areas showed pericellular retraction with maturing neurons and no obvious migrational abnormalities or focal lesions. Sections of the hippocampal area showed intact architecture with mild ischemic injury with hypereosinophilic and pyknotic neurons within CA1 and CA2.

Sections of the periventricular white matter showed reactive astrocytes with prominent eccentrically placed eosinophilic cytoplasm and increased numbers of CD68 positive cells. Also present were cavitary lesions of white matter necrosis within the periventricular white matter with surrounding gliosis containing numerous macrophages, highlighted on both GFAP and CD68 stains. There were a few foci of dystrophic calcification and ependymal nests. The junction between gray and white matter showed clear demarcation as highlighted on Luxol Fast Blue stain. Sections of the cerebellum showed appropriately developing folia with some vascular congestion. The cerebellar dentate nucleus showed a few mildly hypereosinophilic neurons with slightly smudged nuclei, favored to be agonal changes. GFAP immunostain highlighted minimal to mild gliosis in zone 2 and zone 3 neurons.

Iron stain of the brain was negative. The NeuN immunostain for neurons was unremarkable. A section from the pituitary gland revealed a Rathke's cleft cyst with otherwise unremarkable histology of the pituitary gland parenchyma.

In summary, the most significant findings at autopsy of the CNS were ischemia and agonal changes, with patchy minimal to mild gliosis, in the frontal lobe, basal ganglia, temporal lobe, midbrain, and hippocampus. There were reactive axonal swelling and degeneration in the basal ganglia and cystic cavitation in the periventricular white matter. In addition, he also had demonstrated progressive leukodystrophy on imaging [Fig. 2].

A handful of severe biallelic deficiencies of HMBS have been reported worldwide, mostly in infants and children, with varying symptoms and signs, including presentations with recurrent severe abdominal pain, vomiting, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome [PRES], syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone [SIADH] and hyponatremia, muscle weakness or hypotonia, seizures, and in more severe cases, leukodystrophy, intellectual disability, and developmental delay or regression [Table 1]. [7], [9], [15], [17], [18]

We here describe perhaps the youngest patient to be diagnosed with congenital biallelic AIP resulting in severe progressive leukoencephalopathy within the first year of life. Additionally, we found only two other cases of reported liver transplantation as an attempt to treat the disease. [9], [15] Two years after his OLT, our patient's excretions of ALA, PBG, and porphyrins remained significantly elevated with only about a 50% reduction in the marked overproduction of ALA, PBG, and uroporphyrin, compared with his pre-transplant status. Similarly, prior to OLT, at age 14 months, intravenous hematin, given daily for 5 days, had led to transient ∼50% reductions in urinary ALA, PBG, and porphyrins. Extended intravenous heme therapy was not done. Attempts were made to try to secure givosiran, then under study for adult classical AIP, but Alnylam Pharma, the developer of givosiran, declined to provide the drug for trial prior to FDA approval. In view of the known highly selective targeting of givosiran to hepatocytes and the only ∼50% decrease in ALA and PBG overproduction produced by OLT, it is probably unlikely that givosiran would have affected the overall clinical course in a positive way.

Unfortunately, but not surprisingly, neither heme therapy, albeit administered daily for only 5 days, followed by once weekly for a few weeks, nor liver transplant led to any meaningful improvement, nor stabilization of the overall clinical course of the child.

Despite confirmation of good graft function and appropriate immunosuppressive therapy post-transplant, the patient's clinical course did not show sustained or meaningful improvement, with continued seizures, pre-terminal status epilepticus, and progressive weakness leading to inability to swallow and recurrent aspiration that ultimately led to his death in the setting of sepsis and respiratory failure. This case is consistent with another case recently reported of liver transplantations in a less severely affected patient with progressive neurodegenerative conditions secondary to biallelic mutations in the HMBS gene. [15] The latter case of a less severely impaired 48-year-old woman in Australia with late childhood onset ataxia, neuropathy, and pyramidal symptoms, also demonstrated lack of improvement in ALA, PBG, and porphyrin overproduction and continued progression of leukodystrophy despite OLT. [15] Among six other individuals with biallelic HMBS variants, one was reported to undergo OLT. [9] As in our patient, this patient seemed to have modest clinical improvement after the transplant with improved neurologic function; however, this was short-lived and followed by decline and progression of the leukoencephalopathy 12 months later. [9]

Additionally, in Sweden, a 7-year-old child with ADP porphyria who underwent OLT did not experience improvement, [19] nor did a child with ADP treated with OLT in the USA. [17] Also worthy of note is that another patient with ADP, treated for several years with IV heme with benefit, did not show any favorable effect from subcutaneous givosiran. [18] Taken together, the results indicate that, unfortunately, liver transplantation does not have an overall favorable impact upon the clinical disease and course of patients with biallelic severe deficiencies of HMBS, nor of ALA dehydratase.

Several mechanisms have been postulated to cause the neurological manifestations observed in AIP and ADP, including both reversible and irreversible neurological dysfunction. [20] ALA, which is significantly elevated in ADP and in AIP that is biochemically active, is disseminated throughout the body with high concentrations exhibiting neurotoxicity, perhaps, due in part to structural similarities to GABA, leading to partial agonist or antagonist effects at GABA receptors, which can lead to neuronal cell damage and apoptosis. [20] Additionally, it is believed that elevations in ALA impair glutamate uptake, increasing oxidative stress in neuronal cells. [20] The toxic effects of ALA also include increased oxidative stress and, perhaps, defective mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production. [16]

Prolonged heme deficiency caused by mutations in the HMBS gene is also suspected to contribute to the leukoencephalopathy described in our case and prior case reports. [20] Insufficient heme is thought to impair neuronal axonal transport and to disrupt tryptophan metabolism, which hinders neurotransmission. [20] Heme deficiency in the body during an acute episode of AIP has been suggested to decrease nitric oxide production, increasing blood pressure and cerebral vasoconstriction, which can lead to both reversible and irreversible ischemic changes in the brain, including PRES. [20] These increases in blood pressure can lead to secondary hyperperfusion during an acute episode, which results in the compromise of the blood-brain barrier and causes fluid to leak from the vascular lumen into the brain interstitium, causing vasogenic edema. [20] The excess of ALA, which is a known neurotoxin, and deficiency in heme and hemoprotein synthesis are thought to be the main causes of leukoencephalopathy in patients with HMBS mutations, although additional, still undefined, other contributory factors are probably also important.

Seizures were an ongoing difficulty for the child herein described, and they have been described as well in other infants and children with biallelic severe deficiencies of HMBS or of ALAD [Table 1]. Seizures also occur in approximately 20% of patients with symptomatic classical AIP, in whom they are often associated with electrolyte imbalances, particularly hypomagnesemia and hyponatremia due to SIADH, and/or GI and renal losses. [2] It has been noted that there is a higher incidence of seizures reported in the pediatric population with AIP compared to adults. [10] Therefore, in combination with the neurotoxic porphyrin precursors, recurrent seizures may contribute to the irreversible cognitive and behavioral deficits seen in some pediatric cases reported. [10] As in our case, treatment of seizures with barbiturates, hydantoins, carbamazepines, lamotrigine, valproate, and others, may exacerbate the induction of hepatic ALA synthase-1 and overproduction of ALA, PBG, and porphyrins, thereby magnifying the clinical disease. Earlier studies recommended bromides as anti-seizure medications for patients with AIP despite their well-known toxicity profile. [21] Levetiracetam, which does not appear to exacerbate AIP, has been a welcome recent addition to the pharmacopoeia.

The clinical course of the child reported herein highlights that successful OLT, which is curative for classical monoallelic AIP, is of little, if any, long-term benefit in the congenital biallelic disease. The continued progression of neurologic disease [Fig. 2] with only modest improvements in excretion of ALA, PBG, and porphyrins indicates that the marked excess in ALA and PBG production arises not only from the liver, but likely from many tissues, especially from the bone marrow, which is the major site of normal heme synthesis. Given the continued progression of neurologic dysfunction and the significant risks of OLT and post-transplant immunosuppression, it now seems clear that OLT is not curative management for biallelic HMBS-related leukoencephalopathy, nor for ADP. In another recent case report, alternative therapies were attempted post-transplant, including IV heme administration, which did not provide meaningful clinical improvement, and a trial of givosiran therapy, which selectively targets the liver ALA synthase-1, was sought but declined by the drug's supplier, given the patient's status post liver transplant. [15] One would not expect givosiran to be of much biochemical or clinical benefit after OLT, which should have already corrected the very low activities of hepatic HMBS and the secondary up-regulation of hepatic ALA synthase-1.

Biallelic HMBS-related encephalopathy is a debilitating, progressive neurologic condition associated with mutations in the HMBS gene. Fortunately, it is very rare, with fewer than 10 cases reported thus far [Table 1]. Because the clinical penetrance of classical AIP is relatively low, live births of infants with biallelic severe deficiencies will probably continue to occur. [1] As described here and elsewhere [Table 1], effective treatment for such patients remains unknown. Liver transplantation should not be done, based upon our and other recent experience and in light of the shortage of organs for OLT and the substantial costs and risks of OLT. Additional research and investigation are warranted to seek appropriate management for these patients and provide meaningful improvements in their clinical courses. It seems that new treatment approaches will need to focus on trying to improve the overproduction of ALA and PBG within the nervous system. Unfortunately, even by the time of birth, the overproduction of neurotoxic factors in utero may already have led to severe compromise, as seemed to be the case for the child reported herein. Because of the rarity of the disease, it will be a stretch to envision prenatal diagnosis and/or therapy directed at the CNS, such as prenatal or neonatal intrathecal heme, givosiran, or gene or mRNA therapies.

In conclusion, this case expands the limited literature on congenital, biallelic HMBS related disease and underscores a severe neurodevelopmental phenotype distinct from classical AIP. Despite partial, ∼50% biochemical responses to intravenous heme and OLT, the persistence of markedly elevated heme precursors and the unfortunate clinical decline of the patient highlight the central role of extrahepatic heme biosynthetic dysfunction, especially in the nervous system and bone marrow. This and other cases indicate that liver-directed therapies, including OLT, are insufficient to halt disease progression and may expose patients to greater morbidity without meaningful improvement in symptoms. Early and irreversible neurotoxicity from excess ALA and prolonged heme deficiency are likely the prime suspects contributing to poor outcomes.
