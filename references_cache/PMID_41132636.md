# A Case of Cerebral Folate Deficiency due to FOLR1 Mutation in a 10-Year-Old Girl: Clinical Presentation and Treatment Outcomes.
**Authors:** Ahmadabadi F, Heidari-Foroozan M, Jazayeri SA, Karimzadeh P, Hatami E
**Journal:** Clin Case Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ccr3.71316](https://doi.org/10.1002/ccr3.71316)

## Content

Cerebral folate deficiency (CFD) is characterized by reduced levels of folate, particularly 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5-MTHF), in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). This case report describes a 10-year-old girl diagnosed with CFD due to a pathogenic mutation in the FOLR1 gene, which impairs folate transport across the blood-brain barrier. The patient, who exhibited normal early development, experienced developmental regression and refractory seizures after the age of four. Despite treatment with multiple antiepileptic medications, her seizures remained uncontrolled. Genetic testing via whole exome sequencing confirmed the FOLR1 mutation, leading to the initiation of folinic acid supplementation. Following treatment, the patient demonstrated significant clinical improvement. This case underscores the critical role of genetic testing in diagnosing CFD and guiding effective treatment strategies, including the timely use of folinic acid to mitigate neurological deficits associated with folate transport impairments.

Cerebral folate deficiency (CFD) is characterized by low levels of folate in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), particularly a significant reduction in the active metabolite 5‐methyltetrahydrofolate (5‐MTHF). Folate absorption occurs in the gastrointestinal tract via two primary mechanisms: the reduced folate carrier (RFC1) encoded by the SLC19A1 gene and the proton‐coupled folate transporter (PCFT), encoded by the SLC46A1 gene [1]. Once in the bloodstream, folate is transported into the CSF by the folate receptor alpha (FRα), which is encoded by the FOLR1 gene. FRα facilitates its entry across the blood–brain barrier through endocytosis and transcytosis. CFD may result from pathogenic mutations in FOLR1 that impair FRα function or from circulating autoantibodies directed against FRα, both of which block folate transport into the central nervous system [2]. Within the body, folate is metabolized into various active forms, including dihydrofolate, tetrahydrofolate, and folinic acid [3, 4], which are essential for numerous biochemical processes, such as DNA repair, gene expression, and myelination.

CFD can result from both primary congenital disorders, for instance deficiencies in methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase or folate receptor alpha, and secondary conditions, including mitochondrial disorders and serine deficiency [5]. Notably, inadequate folic acid intake during the preconception period has long been associated with an increased risk of neural tube defects [5], while the link between folate deficiency and neurological complications was first documented in 1973 [6]. The condition manifests with various neurological symptoms, including hypotonia, microcephaly, ataxia, seizures, and, in severe cases, para‐ or quadriplegia. The first report of CFD in children emerged in 2002, highlighting a cohort with normal serum folate but low CSF 5‐MTHF, who experienced developmental regression after an initial period of normal growth [7].

In this case report, we detail the clinical presentation, molecular findings, and neuroimaging results of a patient diagnosed with CFD, contributing to the limited literature on this condition.

The patient is a 10‐year‐old girl, the second of three children, born to second cousins, following an uneventful pregnancy and natural vaginal delivery. There is no family history of seizures or other neurological disorders. She has had anemia from infancy, but the data is not available. Her early neurological development was normal, with no delays in motor skills, speech, or cognition until the age of 4. However, she subsequently began experiencing episodes of staring, followed by developmental regression across all three domains. An electroencephalogram (EEG) revealed epileptiform discharges, indicating seizure activity. She was treated with multiple medications, including primidone, levetiracetam, sodium valproate, and acetazolamide; however, she did not respond to these medications, and her seizures remained uncontrolled.

The patient presented to the hospital with tonic seizures characterized by jaw clenching and upward gaze and tonic posture of the upper extremities. Repeated EEG showed a slow, irregular background, suggestive of an encephalopathic pattern (Appendix S1). Due to the refractory nature of her seizures and developmental regression, genetic testing was performed. Whole exome sequencing identified a pathogenic mutation in exon 3 of the FOLR1 gene with variant: chr11:71906478 C>G, c.332C>G (p.pro111Arg) (Table 1), which is known to cause CFD.

Following this diagnosis, the patient was started on Folinic acid 15 mg BD and levetiracetam, which led to a rapid improvement in her symptoms and control of seizures by 50%. She has since been discharged with appropriate follow‐up care.

CFD has been associated with five principal mechanisms. The first involves impaired transport of folate across the blood–brain barrier, often due to defects in the folate receptor alpha (FRα). These defects can arise from the presence of autoantibodies targeting FRα, genetic mutations causing functional loss or dysregulation of FRα expression, defects in FRα endocytosis, or membrane pathologies such as those observed in Smith‐Lemli‐Opitz syndrome [8]. A second mechanism is the reduction of intracellular folate stores, leading to a diminished folate pool in the brain. Increased folic acid utilization, secondary to conditions such as aromatic amino acid decarboxylase deficiency, reactivated herpes infections, or immune‐mediated diseases like subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, is another potential mechanism [9]. Additionally, increased folate catabolism, often associated with oxidative stress or inflammation, may contribute to CFD. Finally, disruptions in folic acid metabolism within the brain, either through inherited enzyme deficiencies (e.g., dihydrofolate reductase deficiency) or acquired conditions such as hyperhomocysteinemia linked to deficiencies in vitamins B2, B6, B9, and B12, are recognized mechanisms [9].

This case presents a 10‐year‐old female diagnosed with CFD due to a pathogenic mutation in exon 3 of the FOLR1 gene. The patient's seizures were refractory to multiple antiepileptic drugs, and her clinical status progressively deteriorated despite extensive treatment. Genetic testing revealed the FOLR1 mutation, consistent with prior reports associating FOLR1 variants with CFD and developmental regression. Following diagnosis, folinic acid supplementation led to marked clinical improvement.

Since the initial description of FOLR1 variants by Steinfield et al. in 2009 [10], 42 patients have been identified with various FOLR1 mutations, including missense, splice‐site, nonsense, and duplication variants.

Clinically, most patients with FOLR1 variants exhibit normal early development, with symptoms such as motor retardation, developmental regression, and unsteady gait typically emerging between the ages of 2 and 3 years. Many patients also present with refractory seizures, including myoclonic, partial, tonic, and tonic–clonic seizures, along with muscle spasms, decreased muscle tone, and tremors. Behavioral abnormalities, including irritability, hyperactivity, and autistic features, have been reported in some cases [10, 11, 12]. MRI findings frequently demonstrate delayed myelination of cerebral white matter and cerebellar atrophy, while Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS) indicates low choline and inositol levels. EEG abnormalities, such as a slow background and multifocal epileptiform discharges, are commonly observed [13].

CFD due to FOLR1 mutations follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, with consanguinity representing a significant risk factor. In this case, both parents were first‐degree cousins and confirmed carriers of the FOLR1 variant. Genetic testing plays a crucial role not only in confirming the diagnosis but also in preventing recurrence through preimplantation genetic testing (PGT). PGT enables the selection of unaffected embryos during in vitro fertilization, offering a means of reducing the transmission of inherited conditions.

Folinic acid supplementation remains the cornerstone of treatment for CFD, with earlier initiation associated with improved clinical outcomes. Dosing typically begins at < 1 mg/kg/day, escalating to 1–3 mg/kg/day, with both oral and intravenous routes of administration demonstrating favorable responses. Additionally, ketogenic diet therapy has shown benefits in cases of drug‐resistant epilepsy, providing another therapeutic avenue for CFD management [14].

In summary, we report the case of a 10‐year‐old girl with cerebral folate deficiency due to a pathogenic FOLR1 mutation. The patient's seizures were refractory to standard treatments, and genetic testing facilitated the diagnosis of CFD. Following folinic acid supplementation, her condition improved markedly, highlighting the importance of genetic testing in guiding treatment for CFD.
