# Real- World Use of Complement Inhibitor Therapy in Critically Ill Children With Thrombotic Microangiopathy: Results From the Spanish Multicenter Matucip Registry.
**Authors:** González-Río E, Bermúdez-Barrezueta L, Bobillo-Pérez S, Espinosa-Pereiro C, Rodríguez-Núñez A
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-10174386/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10174386/v1)

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>Background  Complement inhibitor therapy is the first-line therapy for suspected complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), but is also used off-label in other thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) subtypes. This study aimed to characterize complement inhibitor therapy use in pediatric patients with TMA admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU). Methods  Retrospective observational study of patients older than 1 month admitted to the PICU with TMA who received complement inhibitor therapy between 2017 and 2025, using data from the MATUCIP registry (20 Spanish PICUs). Results  A total of 40 children were analyzed. Half were diagnosed with CM-HUS, 40% with infection-associated TMA, and 10% with secondary TMA. Eculizumab was used in 82.5% of cases. The median time from diagnosis to treatment initiation was 3 days (IQR 1–5). Compared to CM-HUS, non-CM-HUS patients exhibited a higher rate of extrarenal manifestations (95% vs. 50%; p = 0.003), later treatment initiation (4.5 [IQR 3–6] vs. 2 [IQR 1–3] days; p = 0.003), longer PICU stays (21 [IQR 11.5–27.5] vs. 9 [IQR 3.7–14.2] days; p = 0.003), and a higher incidence of hypertension at discharge (95% vs. 58%, p = 0.019). Kidney replacement therapy was required in 72.5%, with continuous kidney replacement therapy as the predominant modality. Mortality was 5%. Conclusions  Among children with TMA admitted to the PICU and treated with complement inhibitor therapy, half had suspected CM-HUS, while the remaining half received off-label therapy. Patients with suspected CM-HUS initiated treatment significantly earlier than patients with other TMA subtypes.</p>

Abstract

INTRODUCTION
Thrombotic microangiopathies (TMA) constitute a heterogeneous group of diseases characterized by
vascular endothelial injury that leads to microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and
ischemic damage in different organs[1, 2]. TMAs are classiﬁed into different subgroups based on the
pathophysiological mechanism responsible for vascular injury: severe deﬁciency in ADAMTS13 activity
in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway in
complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), and infectious processes such as
Page 2/16

hemolytic uremic syndrome secondary to Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli infections (STEC-HUS),
among others [2–4].
Humanized anti-C5 monoclonal antibodies (eculizumab and ravulizumab) are drugs that bind to
complement protein C5, thereby blocking its cleavage into C5a and C5b and consequently preventing the
formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) [5]. These agents constitute the treatment of choice
in patients with suspected CM-HUS, as they act directly on its underlying pathophysiological mechanism.
In clinical practice, they are also used off-label in other forms of TMA with severe and/or refractory
involvement, since it is assumed that complement activation, although not the initiating mechanism,
frequently contributes to the progression and ampliﬁcation of endothelial damage [6–9].
Published evidence on the use of these agents in the pediatric population has focused primarily on
speciﬁc subgroups of TMA, particularly CM-HUS [10–13] and STEC-HUS [14–18]. Recently, Lax et al.
reported a cohort of 24 pediatric patients with CM-HUS, STEC-HUS, or transplant-associated TMA (TA-
TMA) treated with eculizumab [19]. However, the available evidence mainly evaluates medium- and long-
term outcomes, primarily in terms of disease control. Data on the impact of these drugs during the acute
phase of the disease, particularly in the acute intensive care setting, are limited in both adult and
pediatric patients. Understanding patient characteristics and the effects of these agents during this
critical phase is essential to optimize initial therapeutic decision-making, establish prognostic factors,
and improve the acute management of these patients in pediatric intensive care units (PICU).
Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to characterize the use of complement inhibitor agents in
children diagnosed with TMA while admitted to the PICU, including indications, timing, and treatment
patterns. As a secondary objective, we aimed to describe and analyze the clinical course during PICU
admission in this population.
METHODS
Ethical aspects  
The study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee (REC) of Galicia (code
2018/228). 
Study design and study population 
A retrospective descriptive observational study was conducted using data from the MATUCIP registry, a
multicenter database involving 20 PICUs in Spain. Data collection comprised a retrospective phase from
January 2017 to June 2018, followed by a prospective phase from July 2018 to December 2025. All
pediatric patients older than 1 month of age with a diagnosis of TMA who received treatment with
complement-inhibiting agents during their PICU admission were included and their data were
pseudonymized according to REC indications.   
Deﬁnitions and Measurements 
Page 3/16

Demographic and clinical variables: age (months), sex, weight (kilograms), reason for admission,
initial clinical manifestations (gastrointestinal, respiratory, neurological, or others), and relevant
personal and family history. Chronic pharmacological treatment as well as recent exposure to drugs
or vaccines were also recorded. 
TMA diagnosis. Blood analytical parameters at the time of suspected TMA were collected.
Suspected TMA diagnosis was established in the presence of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia
(hemoglobin < 10 g/dL together with > 1% schistocytes in peripheral blood and/or elevated LDH
and/or decreased haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 150,000/µL or > 25% decrease
from baseline value), and acute kidney injury according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global
Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 criteria[20]. Additionally, cases with clinical suspicion of TMA that did not
meet the complete triad were also collected. Patients were classiﬁed into different TMA subtypes,
as shown in Figure 1.
Management and treatment. Administration of maintenance ﬂuid therapy, intravenous ﬂuid bolus,
intravenous albumin, antibiotic therapy, antihypertensive drugs, need for kidney replacement therapy,
plasmapheresis, or transfusion of red blood cells, platelets, and/or fresh frozen plasma were
recorded. In cases where intravenous ﬂuids or transfusions were administered, the volume in mL/kg
was quantiﬁed. For all pharmacological treatments, the name of the drug name was recorded.
Regarding complement inhibitor drugs, the number of doses, start date, and time in days from
suspected TMA diagnosis to administration of the ﬁrst dose were recorded. Early treatment
initiation was deﬁned as administration of the ﬁrst dose within the ﬁrst 48 hours after suspected
diagnosis. 
Clinical evolution. Neurological, cardiac, and gastrointestinal involvement during admission were
recorded. Blood laboratory tests were collected on days 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 after TMA diagnosis, as
well as at PICU discharge. Likewise, sequelae present at PICU discharge (kidney injury, hypertension,
neurological, cardiac, or gastrointestinal involvement), mortality, and PICU length of stay (days) were
documented.
Statistical analysis 
Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA)
and JASP version 0.96.0 (JASP Team, University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Figures were created
using JASP version 0.96.0 and Microsoft PowerPoint version 16.16.27 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond,
WA, USA). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous
variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Univariate analyses were
performed using the χ² test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test
or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Statistical signiﬁcance was set at p <
0.05. Given the descriptive nature of the study and the limited sample size, between-group comparisons
were considered exploratory. No formal primary outcome was deﬁned for hypothesis testing.
RESULTS
Page 4/16

Study population
Between 2017 and 2025, 160 patients were enrolled in the MATUCIP registry. Of these, 40 patients (25%)
received treatment with complement inhibitors and constituted the study sample for the present
analysis. Half of those  patients (20/40) were diagnosed with CM-HUS, 40% (16/40) with infection-
associated TMA — the majority in the context of STEC-HUS (13/16) — and the remaining 10% with
secondary TMA, occurring in the setting of systemic lupus erythematosus, hematopoietic stem cell
transplantation, and ciclosporin use in a patient with inﬂammatory bowel disease.
Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics 
Of the 40 patients treated with complement inhibitors, 47% (19/40) were male, with a median age of 4.2
years (IQR 1.8–8.8) and a median weight of 17 kg (IQR 12–29). Relevant medical history was present in
22.5% of patients (9/40), with four cases involving signiﬁcant underlying conditions: two patients with
prior hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, one with inﬂammatory bowel disease, and one with
suspected autoimmune hepatitis. At the time of diagnosis, hemolytic anemia was present in 72% of
cases (29/40), and schistocytes were identiﬁed on peripheral blood smear in 87% (35/40). The classical
clinical triad of anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury was present in 52% of cases (21/40).
Baseline clinical and demographic characteristics, along with laboratory values at admission, are
summarized in Table 1 for the overall cohort and stratiﬁed by TMA subtype. 
Complement inhibitor therapy 
Of the 40 patients treated with complement inhibitors, 82.5% (33/40) received eculizumab, 15% (6/40)
received ravulizumab, and one patient (2.5%) was initiated on eculizumab and subsequently switched to
ravulizumab. The median time from diagnosis to treatment initiation was 3 days (IQR 1–5). Figure 2
shows the time from diagnosis to complement inhibitor administration according to TMA subtype. 
Early treatment administration (within the ﬁrst 48 hours of diagnosis) was carried out in 40% of cases
(16/40). When analyzing by subgroups, CM-HUS patients received early treatment in 60% of cases
(12/20), whereas in secondary TMA this percentage was 25% (1/4) and in infection-associated TMA
patients 19% (3/16). No serious adverse effects related to complement inhibitor therapy were reported in
any patient. 
Clinical course during PICU stay
Regarding therapeutic management, 72.5% of patients (29/40) required kidney replacement therapy at
some point during PICU admission. Some patients required more than one modality; continuous kidney
replacement therapy (CKRT) was the most frequently used, accounting for 79% of cases (23/29),
followed by intermittent hemodialysis in 21% (6/29) and peritoneal dialysis in 3.4% (1/29). Figure 3
illustrates the evolution of the main laboratory parameters and urine output throughout the admission.

The median length of stay in the PICU was 14 days (IQR 8–22.5). At discharge, hypertension and kidney
injury were the most frequent sequelae, occurring in 74.3% of patients (29/39), while neurological
sequelae were recorded in 10% of cases (4/40). Two patients (5%) progressed to brain death and died
prior to PICU discharge. Table 2 summarizes the clinical course during admission and supportive
therapies, both for the overall cohort and stratiﬁed by TMA subgroup. 
Comparative analysis between patients with CM-HUS and patients receiving off-label complement
inhibitor therapy. 
When comparing patients with CM-HUS against those with other TMA subtypes, hereafter referred to as
non-CM-HUS, both groups were comparable in terms of demographic variables and laboratory
parameters at admission. Extra-renal manifestations were more frequent in the non-CM-HUS group
compared to CM-HUS (19/20 [95%] vs. 10/20 [50%], p = 0.003), driven particularly by a higher rate of
encephalopathy (9/20 [45%] vs. 2/20 [10%], p = 0.031) and respiratory involvement (8/20 [40%] vs. 1/20
[5%], p = 0.02). Patients with CM-HUS received complement inhibitor therapy signiﬁcantly earlier than
those with other TMA subtypes (2 [IQR 1–3] vs. 4.5 [IQR 3–6] days, p = 0.003) and required a shorter
duration of CKRT (5 [IQR 1.5–8.2] vs. 14 [IQR 8–18.5] days, p = 0.002). At PICU discharge, the non-CM-
HUS group exhibited a signiﬁcantly higher prevalence of hypertension (18/19 [95%] vs. 11/19 [58%], p =
0.019). The median PICU length of stay was signiﬁcantly longer in the non-CM-HUS group (21 [IQR 11.5–
27.5] vs. 9 [IQR 3.7–14.25] days, p = 0.003). Detailed clinical characteristics, management, and clinical
course for both groups are summarized in Table 3.
DISCUSSION
To our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst multicenter study to speciﬁcally evaluate the use of complement
inhibitors in pediatric patients with TMA admitted to the PICU, as well as their short-term clinical course.
Eculizumab was the most commonly used agent, administered in 82.5% of cases. Among all the
patients, 50% had a suspected diagnosis of CM-HUS, while the remaining 50% received these agents in
other TMA subtypes on an off-label basis. Notably, initiation of complement inhibitor therapy occurred
signiﬁcantly earlier in patients with CM-HUS compared to those with other TMA subtypes. 
Off-label use of complement inhibitor therapy 
Off-label use of complement inhibitor therapy was highly prevalent in our cohort, accounting for half of
the treated patients. This frequent use beyond approved indications likely reﬂects the severity and
complexity of clinical presentation at the time of treatment initiation, as well as the challenges in
establishing a deﬁnitive etiological diagnosis in the acute phase of TMA. A substantial proportion of
patients exhibited signiﬁcant organ involvement, including neurological complications—such as seizures
(40%) and encephalopathy (45%)—and respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (30%).
In this context, and given the potential role of complement dysregulation across different TMA subtypes,
clinicians may have opted for early complement blockade as a therapeutic strategy in the absence of
equally effective alternatives. These ﬁndings are consistent with previous reports describing increasing
Page 6/16

off-label use of complement inhibitors in severe or unclear cases of TMA, particularly in critically ill
patients, where early treatment is often prioritized despite limited high-quality evidence. 
The most frequent off-label indication was STEC-HUS. The role of complement inhibitors in this
condition has been extensively studied, although current evidence does not support their systematic
administration. Multiple case series[14, 21–23] and case reports[24–28] have described their use, but
systematic reviews [15, 18] did not demonstrate consistent clinical beneﬁt. The two available
randomized controlled trials in pediatric patients have not fully clariﬁed the role of complement inhibitor
therapy in STEC-HUS. The ECULISHU trial (Garnier et al., 2023)[16] did not demonstrate beneﬁt during
the acute phase of the disease but reported a lower incidence of kidney sequelae at one year in patients
treated with eculizumab, although patients with severe extrarenal involvement were excluded from this
study. The ECUSTEC trial (Ives et al., 2024)[17], speciﬁcally designed to evaluate severe forms of STEC-
HUS, did not demonstrate neurological beneﬁt at 60 days. Evidence for complement inhibition therapy in
Sp-HUS is even more limited, being restricted to small case series and case reports in patients with
severe disease [29–33]. Another off-label indication with increasing use in practice is TA-TMA [34–36].
Jodele et al. [37]published the ﬁrst prospective multi-institutional study in children and young adults with
high-risk TA-TMA, demonstrating signiﬁcantly superior survival at 6 months and at 1 year post-transplant
compared with untreated historical controls. However, the safety proﬁle of complement inhibitors in this
setting remains controversial. Schoettler et al. (2025)[38], in a single-center retrospective case-control
study, observed that pediatric patients with TA-TMA treated with eculizumab had a signiﬁcant increase in
bacterial and viral infections, as well as infection-related mortality.  
Our ﬁndings reﬂect real-world clinical practice, where complement inhibitors are frequently used off-label
in patients with severe TMA [39]. This observation aligns with Muff-Luett et al.[40], who reported that
44% of eculizumab treatments in a cohort of 152 pediatric patients were for non-approved indications.
Similarly, studies examining off-label drug use in pediatrics have identiﬁed eculizumab as one of the
most frequently prescribed medications outside approved indications [41, 42]. This practice pattern
reﬂects the challenges of real-world clinical care of critically ill pediatric patients. When managing
patients with severe, rapidly progressive TMA, clinicians are frequently confronted with diagnostic
uncertainty and the urgency of the clinical situation, often having to initiate multiple empirical therapies
simultaneously before a deﬁnitive etiologic diagnosis can be established. Moreover, etiologic diagnosis
may be unclear in the early days of presentation, and even when a compatible etiology is identiﬁed (for
example, through microbiological isolation of STEC), this does not exclude the possibility of underlying
concomitant complement dysregulation [43]. As Kim et al. [44] noted, despite numerous case reports,
case series, and descriptive studies of eculizumab use in off-label indications, there remains a striking
paucity of well-designed clinical trials to rigorously evaluate its eﬃcacy and safety. These challenges
underscore the need for continued research into the potential role of complement inhibitors in different
TMA subtypes, particularly those with severe organ involvement.
Differences between complement inhibitor therapy in CM-HUS and other TMA subtypes
Page 7/16

In patients with suspected CM-HUS, the median time from clinical suspicion to treatment initiation was 2
days, consistent with previously reported intervals of 2–2.5 days [12, 13, 19]. This timing is similar to that
reported in non-critically ill cohorts, suggesting that treatment is driven mainly by diagnostic suspicion
rather than by disease severity, with prompt initiation once alternative etiologies are reasonably excluded
in line with current guidelines. This highlights the importance of a rapid diagnostic evaluation,
particularly since initial laboratory values and clinical presentation, with diarrhea as the predominant
symptom, were similar across both groups. By contrast, initiation was signiﬁcantly delayed in non-CM-
HUS patients, likely reﬂecting off-label use as a rescue strategy in cases of clinical deterioration or lack
of response to conventional therapy.
Limitations
Our study has several limitations. First, the absence of systematic follow-up data after PICU discharge
limits the assessment of long-term outcomes, including chronic kidney disease, persistent neurological
sequelae, and disease recurrence. It also restricts the evaluation of response to complement inhibitor
therapy, given that complete response is generally deﬁned in the literature as sustained hematological
and kidney recovery persisting beyond four weeks. Second, the limited sample size reduces the
statistical power of between-group comparisons and may limit the detection of clinically meaningful
differences. This is further compounded by the etiological heterogeneity of the non-CM-HUS group,
which additionally limits the interpretation of these comparisons. Finally, treatment indication was not
standardized and was likely inﬂuenced by disease severity, clinical deterioration, and local practice
patterns, introducing potential confounding by indication. 
Strengths and clinical implications 
This study provides relevant data to characterize the use of complement inhibitor therapy in pediatric
patients with TMA admitted to the PICU, a setting in which available evidence remains limited. Its
multicenter design, with the participation of 20 PICUs, strengthens the validity of the results and reﬂects
real-world clinical practice in a heterogeneous and representative setting. The high proportion of off-
label use of these agents observed in our cohort highlights that, in daily clinical practice, complement
inhibitors are frequently employed beyond their approved indications, underscoring the need for further
research into their role in TMA subtypes other than CM-HUS.
CONCLUSION
In this national multicenter registry, among pediatric patients admitted to the PICU with suspected TMA
who received complement inhibitor therapy, half were treated off-label, while the remaining half were
treated for suspected CM-HUS. Patients with suspected CM-HUS initiated complement inhibitor therapy
signiﬁcantly earlier than those with other TMA subtypes.
