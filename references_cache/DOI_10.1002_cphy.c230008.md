Abstract
The exocrine and endocrine are functionally distinct compartments of the pancreas that have 
traditionally been studied as separate entities. However, studies of embryonic development, adult 
physiology, and disease pathogenesis suggest there may be critical communication between 
exocrine and endocrine cells. In fact, the incidence of the endocrine disease diabetes secondary to 
exocrine disease/dysfunction ranges from 25 to 80%, depending on the type and severity of the 
exocrine pathology. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate how exocrine-endocrine “crosstalk” 
may impact pancreatic function. In this review, we discuss common exocrine diseases, including 
cystic fibrosis, acute, hereditary, and chronic pancreatitis, and the impact of these exocrine 
diseases on endocrine function. Additionally, we review how obesity and fatty pancreas influence 
exocrine function and the impact on cellular communication between the exocrine and endocrine 
compartments. Interestingly, in all pathologies, there is evidence that signals from the exocrine 
disease contribute to endocrine dysfunction and the progression to diabetes. Continued research 
efforts to identify the mechanisms that underlie the crosstalk between various cell types in the 
pancreas are critical to understanding normal pancreatic physiology as well as disease states.
Introduction
The pancreas is composed of two cellular compartments: the exocrine, which makes and 
secretes enzymes that facilitate digestion, and the endocrine, which makes and secretes 
hormones that regulate glucose homeostasis (Langerhans 1869; Lebenthal and Lee 1980; 
Pipeleers et al. 1982; Pandol 2010; Barker et al. 2013; Baskin 2015). Although functionally 
distinct, exocrine and endocrine pancreas cells originate from a common progenitor cell 
population present in the earliest pancreatic bud domains that evaginate from the foregut 
endoderm in the embryo. Subsequent pancreas organ development results in a robust 
structural connection between the two compartments.

Pancreatic organogenesis occurs in two stages, known as primary and secondary transitions 
(Pan and Wright 2011). During the primary transition (embryonic (E) day 9.5 to 12.5), 
progenitor cells compose the ventral and dorsal pancreatic buds. These progenitor cells co-
express the transcription factors Pancreatic and duodenal homeobox 1 (Pdx1) and Pancreas 
associated transcription factor 1a (Ptf1a), which are essential for pancreas development as 
evidenced by the pancreatic agenesis in mouse models with genetic deletion of either Pdx1 
or Ptf1a (Offield et al. 1996; Sellick et al. 2004; Burlison et al. 2008; Fukuda et al. 2008). 
Once these pancreatic progenitors have proliferated, the cells in the bud will stratify into an 
epithelium that then undergoes branching morphogenesis (Burlison et al. 2008).
The subsequent secondary transition (E13.5 to E16.5) is characterized by the cellular 
differentiation of progenitor cells residing in specific locations within the branching 
epithelium. Specifically, the differentiation of acinar cells occurs from Ptf1a-expressing 
exocrine progenitor cells located at the ductal “tips” and the differentiation of hormone-
expressing islet cells (insulin-expressing beta cells, glucagon-expressing alpha cells, 
somatostatin-expressing delta cells, pancreatic polypeptide-expressing PP cells and ghrelin-
expressing epsilon cells) occurs from Pdx1 and Neurogenin3 (Ngn3)-expressing endocrine 
progenitor cells located in the ductal “trunk” (Sznurkowska et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2019). 
Live imaging has revealed that as islet cell types differentiate, they migrate away from 
the branching ducts and form endocrine clusters that will subsequently coalesce into 
the structures known as the islets of Langerhans (Puri and Hebrok 2007; Miller et al. 
2009). Although the exact mechanism behind the clustering and final islet structure is 
still not fully understood, recent evidence suggests that the clusters undergo aggregation 
and fission to form individual islets dispersed throughout the lobes of the pancreas, 
which are primarily composed of acinar cells (Sznurkowska et al., 2020). The vascular 
system develops alongside the pancreatic ducts, providing further structural connection 
between the endocrine and exocrine compartments (Azizoglu et al. 2016). Studies using 
two-photon confocal microscopy and 3D rendering have discovered that the integrated 
vascular network within the pancreas shows a bidirectional microcirculation flow, which 
supports the paracrine regulation from acinar to endocrine cells observed in vivo (Dybala et 
al. 2020b, a).
The functional and structural interaction between the exocrine and endocrine pancreas is 
also highlighted in clinical observations from exocrine diseases, which have documented 
deleterious outcomes on endocrine function. In general, the incidence of diabetes onset 
following exocrine dysfunction ranges from 25 to 80%, depending on the type and severity 
of the exocrine pathology. Specifically, chronic pancreatitis is an exocrine disease wherein 
approximately 70–80% of patients subsequently develop diabetes (Larsen et al. 1987; Ewald 
et al. 2012; Ewald and Hardt 2013; Bhattamisra et al. 2019). Cystic fibrosis (CF) is another 
exocrine disease where subsequent beta cell dysfunction in the form of Cystic Fibrosis 
Related Diabetes (CFRD) is reported in 3.5% of individuals (Adler et al. 2008; Norris et 
al. 2019). The clinical connection between pancreatic cancer and diabetes development has 
also been studied (Andersen et al. 2017; Roy et al. 2021; Wu 2021; Ruze et al. 2023), 
and it is estimated that 8% of patients with pancreatic cancer present with recent onset of 
diabetes (Ewald et al. 2012). In addition to these examples of exocrine disease that impact 
endocrine function, obesity can contribute to exocrine dysfunction due to the deposition of

The Endocrine Pancreas
A. Structure
As mentioned above, the hormone-expressing cells that compose the islets of Langerhans 
originate from progenitor cells in the trunk of the ductal epithelium during the secondary 
transition (Sznurkowska et al. 2018). These progenitor cells within the trunk are bipotent 
and give rise to ductal and endocrine cells. The upregulation of the transcription factor Ngn3 
in a subset of the bipotent trunk progenitor cells specifies their endocrine fate (Gradwohl et 
al. 2000). Hormone-expressing islet cells then differentiate from the endocrine progenitors, 
delaminate from the ductal epithelium, expand in number and coalesce in the early postnatal 
period into the islets of Langerhans (Mastracci and Sussel 2012; Sznurkowska et al. 
2020). These islets comprise five cell types, including beta, alpha, delta, PP, and epsilon 
cells, which secrete insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, pancreatic polypeptide, and ghrelin, 
respectively (de Hoyos-Guevara 1969; Pelletier 1977; Yu et al. 2019). Whereas all islet cell 
types are present during embryonic development, the epsilon cell population is reduced after 
birth (Wierup et al. 2002). Ultimately, these multicellular islet structures become dispersed 
throughout the exocrine tissue after organogenesis.
Greater access to human donor tissue for research purposes has assisted in the identification 
of similarities and differences between the rodent and human pancreas. Most prominently, 
the different endocrine cell types are distributed within the islets in a species-specific 
manner (Kim et al. 2009). Rodent islets are organized with a mantle of alpha, delta, and PP 
cells surrounded by a central core of beta cells (Dolenšek et al. 2015). Interestingly, human 
islets have the rodent spatial organization in the early postnatal stage. However, individual 
islets will cluster with age, resulting in a less uniform islet structure where alpha cells 
and beta cells are found in the core (Kim et al. 2009). Bonner-Weir et al. identified that 
the cellular distribution of smaller islets in the human pancreas appears similar to those of 
rodents, whereas larger human islets are composed of multiple subunits with core-mantle 
structure (Bonner-Weir et al. 2015). Furthermore, Ionescu-Tirgoviste et al. demonstrated that 
islets cluster near the vasculature in the human pancreas (Ionescu-Tirgoviste et al. 2015), 
which is in line with the requirement for islets to have adequate vascularization to facilitate 
bidirectional blood flow and proper pancreatic function (Lammert et al. 2003). Interestingly, 
the rodent pancreas has an uneven distribution of islets, with a higher density in the tail 
(dorsal bud-derived) portion (Aguayo-Mazzucato et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2013). In both 
rodents and humans, the islets located in the ventral bud-derived portion of the pancreas 
show a greater proportion of PP cells (Ekblad and Sundler 2002; Wang et al. 2013). Clearly, 
despite subtle species-specific differences, the mammalian pancreatic islet is organized to

B. Endocrine Function
The pancreatic islet cells are responsible for maintaining whole-body glucose homeostasis 
by secreting hormones in response to metabolic cues (Röder et al. 2016). A key player 
is the hormone insulin, secreted by the beta cells in response to increased blood glucose 
(Banting et al. 1922). This process is known as glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS). 
It involves several signaling pathways, including increased intracellular calcium, which 
stimulates insulin granule fusion with the membrane and hormone secretion (Yang and 
Berggren 2005; Komatsu et al. 2013; Sabatini et al. 2018). Increased insulin production 
is also a key component of GSIS. The increase in insulin protein synthesis leads to a 
higher demand for protein folding in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), which then requires 
activation of the unfolded protein response pathways (UPR) to increase ER functional 
capacity and ensure adequate insulin production (Eizirik and Cnop 2010; Chan et al. 2015).
The islet microenvironment is also important in beta cell function (Brereton et al. 2015). 
Glucagon, the hyperglycemic hormone secreted by the pancreatic alpha cells, influences 
beta cell insulin release (Samols et al. 1965). Studies have demonstrated that activating 
glucagon receptors in beta cells ensures adequate cAMP concentration, which is required for 
first-phase insulin secretion (Schuit and Pipeleers 1985; Gelling et al. 2009; Cabrera et al. 
2022). In addition to glucagon, somatostatin (SST) secreted by the delta cells affects both 
alpha and beta cell secretion (Strowski et al. 2000). In particular, Briant et al. demonstrated 
that delta and beta cells are electrically coupled, and through this mechanism, delta cells 
contribute to insulin secretion regulation (Briant et al. 2018). In addition to this role, beta 
cell gap junctions are essential to maintain intracellular calcium levels in all beta cells to 
ensure basal insulin secretion (Benninger et al. 2011).
In addition to the hormone producing cells, there are other cells and components of the islet 
microenvironment that affect endocrine pancreas function. Of note, resident macrophages 
within the endocrine pancreas influence beta cell function and are essential for the 
maintenance of beta cell mass (Banaei-Bouchareb et al. 2004; Jensen et al. 2020). These 
macrophages are capable of sensing the ATP increase associated with glucose stimulated 
insulin secretion (Weitz et al. 2018), and Thai et al demonstrated that the absence of 
islet resident macrophages resulted in altered in vitro high glucose stimulated secretion 
suggesting a regulatory role for macrophages in beta cell function (Thai et al. 2023). 
Interestingly, resident macrophages have also been implicated in beta cell proliferation under 
obesogenic conditions (Ying et al. 2019). Ying et al showed that in the setting of obesity, 
macrophages increase expression of platelet-derived growth factor a (Pdgfa) and activate 
the PDGF/PDGFR pathway, which stimulates beta cell proliferation thus contributing to 
the compensatory increase in beta cell mass (Ying et al. 2019). Additionally, this study 
demonstrated that cell-cell contact is needed for the macrophage effect on the beta cell (Ying 
et al. 2019).
The vasculature also plays a significant role in endocrine function. Moldovan et al 
demonstrated the dynamic nature of this interaction by showing that insulin secretion

are contractile cells found on all capillaries, including those within the islets, are essential 
for endocrine function (Almaça et al. 2020). Almaça et al demonstrated that contracted 
pericytes result in reduced blood flow to the islet whereas relaxed pericytes lead to increased 
blood flow (Almaça et al. 2018). In addition to regulating blood flow, pericytes are key 
for beta cell maturation. For example, Sasson et al depleted pericytes in the islets using a 
transgenic diphtheria toxin-based mouse model and observed a reduction in beta cell identity 
markers including insulin, Pdx1 and MafA (Sasson et al. 2016). Moreover, Sakhneny et al 
demonstrated that pericytes secrete bone morphogenic protein 4 (BMP4) and depletion of 
BMP4 signaling from the pericytes leads to reduced insulin secretion (Sakhneny et al. 2021). 
Altogether, these studies investigating islet microcirculation and the role of vascular cells in 
the islet highlight the influence that non-endocrine cells have on islet function.
Beta cell dysfunction is a crucial step in several pathologies, and one of the mechanisms 
mediating this dysfunction, as observed in diabetes, is oxidative stress (Dinić et al. 
2022). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) cause beta cell damage and impair beta cell 
secretory capacity by suppressing insulin gene transcription, mitochondrial function, and 
the expression of the critical beta cell transcription factors MafA and Pdx1 (Maechler et al. 
1999; Kaneto et al. 2002; Kawamori et al. 2003; Harmon et al. 2005). It has been proposed 
that beta cells are particularly susceptible to dysfunction and apoptosis in response to ROS 
due to the reduced presence of antioxidant enzymes (Grankvist et al. 1981; Tiedge et al. 
1997). It is interesting to note that context is crucial for understanding beta cell response 
to stimuli. For example, interleukin 6 (IL-6) has been shown to protect beta cells from 
apoptosis by inducing autophagy and introducing cell death through the activation of nitric 
oxide production (Oh et al. 2011; Linnemann et al. 2017).
More recently, research has focused on understanding beta cell failure from a single cell 
analysis point of view (Carrano et al. 2017). Single cell transcriptomics of beta cells 
revealed subpopulations with different transcriptional profiles associated with ER function 
and overall cellular maturation (Baron et al. 2016). Additional “omics” studies highlight 
how disease states can alter regulation of gene expression and have deciphered how 
mutations in distant loci influence beta cell function. In particular, Chiou et al used single 
nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (snATAC-seq) and 
determined that chromatin accessibility in islet cells varies in different metabolic states 
(Chiou et al. 2021b). This study also explored type 2 diabetes (T2D) risk variants and 
proposed that exploring the accessibility and influence of risk variants on the insulin 
promoter can increase our understanding of enhancer regions in endocrine function and 
dysfunction (Chiou et al. 2021b).

The Exocrine pancreas
A. Structure
As described in the introduction, the ductal epithelium becomes organized during the 
primary transition and the progenitor cells at the tips of the ducts give rise to the acinar 
cells. Extensive gene expression cascades instruct acinar cell differentiation, which includes 
the upregulation of the transcription factor Ptf1a to initiate restriction to the acinar cell 
fate (Zhou et al. 2007; Solar et al. 2009). In addition to Ptf1a, other transcription factors 
are essential for the correct fate determination of acinar cells, including the basic helix-loop-
helix family member a15 (Mist1) (Jia et al. 2008). Whole-body genetic deletion of Mist1 in 
mice revealed its role in acinar cell organization and the maintenance of acinar cell identity 
(Pin et al. 2001; Jia et al. 2008).
The structural arrangement of the developing pancreas in the embryo dictates the postnatal 
organization of the exocrine. In particular, the differentiating acinar cells are located at 
the ends of the branching ducts, which leads to a multi-lobular pancreas structure due to 
continued ductal expansion (Zhou et al. 2007; Sznurkowska et al. 2018). In addition to 
its role in maintaining acinar differentiation, Mist1 has also been implicated in acinar cell 
proliferation and exocrine expansion (Jia et al. 2008). A study by Jia et al. showed that Mist1 
induced p21 expression, inhibiting cyclin activity and reducing proliferation (Jia et al. 2008). 
Interestingly, the mechanism for postnatal exocrine pancreas growth differs between species, 
with human pancreas expansion occurring due to hyperplasia, whereas the rodent exocrine 
pancreas expands by hypertrophy (Anzi et al. 2018). In general this leads to a pancreas 
wherein the exocrine represents greater than 90% of the organ by mass, arranged into four 
lobes that can be further segmented into lobules, with each one associated with a duct to 
facilitate pancreatic secretion drainage to the duodenum (Rahier et al. 1981; Watanabe et al. 
1997; Yasuda et al. 2006; Walkowska et al. 2022).
The pancreas structure highlights the connections within the exocrine and the essential 
role of the ducts in overall exocrine function. During development, the branching ductal 
epithelium presents as finger-like protrusions (Villasenor et al. 2010). Work by Dahl-
Jensen et al., using wholemount imaging at three different time points of development, 
demonstrated that at earlier stages, these ductal protrusions result in redundant duct 
branching; however, as the exocrine pancreas develops and the acinar and ductal cells 
secrete fluid into the ducts, these undergo pruning, and only those ducts with the higher 
flow will be maintained in the exocrine (Dahl-Jensen et al. 2018, p.). Altogether the 
structural determinants of acinar cell expansion and ductal organization contribute to 
exocrine pancreas function.
B. Exocrine Function
Acinar cells make pro-enzymes (zymogens) that are secreted into the pancreatic ducts 
and flow to the duodenum to be processed into active enzymes that aid digestion (Beck 
1973; Scheele et al. 1981; Ishiguro et al. 2012). The epithelial cells that compose the 
ducts produce bicarbonate that is transported into the ductal lumen via multiple channels, 
including the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) (Steward et

al. 2005; Novak et al. 2011; Gustafsson et al. 2012). Under physiological conditions, 
acinar cell secretion of zymogens into the ductal lumen will acidify the environment and 
cause ATP release from the zymogen granules into the ducts (Haanes and Novak 2010; 
Behrendorff et al. 2010). Increased ATP regulates the purinergic ATP receptors (P2) in 
the ductal epithelial cells, which block sodium reabsorption and create a chloride gradient 
that causes bicarbonate release by CTFR (Ishiguro et al. 2002; Shirley et al. 2005). This 
secreted bicarbonate facilitates the appropriate pH within the ducts to maintain the secreted 
pro-enzymes in a zymogen state (Steward et al. 2005). In addition to bicarbonate channels, 
ductal epithelial cells express Aquaporin1 in their membrane (Hurley et al. 2001), which 
permits adequate fluid secretion volume and influences bicarbonate secretion from CTFR 
channels (Venglovecz et al. 2018). Other aquaporin family members may also influence 
pro-enzyme secretion and activation (Arsenijevic et al. 2019). In general, the pancreatic 
ductal epithelial cells secrete on average 2.5 liters of fluid that can have a bicarbonate 
concentration of up to 150 mM, which maintains the zymogens in an inactive state within 
the ductal lumen (Whitcomb and Ermentrout 2004; Pandol 2010).
The acinar cell zymogen secretion is triggered by cholecystokinin (CCK), which, in rodents, 
is produced by gut L-cells in response to food (Polak et al. 1975). CCK can trigger zymogen 
secretion by either directly binding to its receptor in acinar cells or indirectly through 
activation of a cholinergic pathway in which the pancreatic innervation stimulates acinar 
cell secretion (Soudah et al. 1992; Li et al. 2004). It has been suggested that the indirect 
signaling pathway is responsible for the majority of CCK effects on acinar cells in humans 
(Funakoshi et al. 1999; Ji et al. 2002).
From a functional perspective, the pro-enzymes produced by the acinar cells are categorized 
by the type of molecule upon which they act including amylases, lipases, and proteases, 
which break down carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins, respectively (Keller and Allan 1967; 
Rinderknecht 1986). Of note, trypsinogen is critically important as it will be processed 
into trypsin in the duodenum and then functions to cleave/activate other digestive enzymes 
including pro-elastase (into elastase) and pro-lipase (lipase) (Williams 2004; Zheng et al. 
2009). Interestingly, pathological conditions such as an acidic environment within the acinar 
cell can lead to the activation of trypsin in the pancreas instead of the duodenum (Niederau 
and Grendell 1988). This requirement of a lower acidic environment to maintain zymogens 
in their inactive forms highlights the need for adequate pH buffering within the ducts 
to avoid cellular damage and how communication between different components of the 
pancreas is essential for adequate function.
Exocrine Diseases and the Impact on Endocrine Function
A. The Concept of Exocrine-Endocrine “Crosstalk”
Intra-islet and acinar-duct cell interactions are critical regulatory components of endocrine 
and exocrine function, respectively. Whereas there is significant evidence that interaction 
between these pancreatic compartments is critical during organogenesis, more recent 
studies suggest that communication between the exocrine and endocrine (so-called exocrine-
endocrine “crosstalk”) also contributes to both normal physiology and disease pathogenesis 
(Pandol 2010; Price et al. 2010; Pan and Wright 2011; Overton and Mastracci 2022;

Mastracci et al. 2023). The pancreas in endocrine disease has been noted to show 
altered overall pancreas size and exocrine function. In particular, patients with Type 1 
Diabetes (T1D) have reduced pancreas size, which could suggest that insulin signaling is 
important for pancreas growth (Yagihashi 2017). Recently it has been demonstrated that 
the hyperinsulinemia in Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) can signal insulin receptors on acinar cells 
and result in excessive digestive enzyme production and local inflammation (Zhang et 
al. 2022). The effects of insulin dysregulation on pancreatic function support the role of 
exocrine-endocrine crosstalk. Studies using single cell analyses and imaging mass cytometry 
(IMC) have also revealed previously unappreciated cellular changes that also support the 
idea of crosstalk. For example, the analysis of human pancreatic and peripheral blood 
samples from individuals with T1D (and controls) by snATAC-seq identified a significant 
number of T1D risk variants in cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) that were active in T cells 
and islets cells (Chiou et al. 2021a). However, the authors also found variants associated 
with increased risk for T1D in cCREs expressed in acinar and ductal cells including a T1D 
risk variant in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) locus (Chiou 
et al. 2021a). Using IMC, Fasolino et al demonstrated that MHC class II-expressing ductal 
cells are enriched in T1D donor tissue suggesting there may exist an interaction between 
exocrine cells and the immune system that influences T1D progression (Fasolino et al. 
2022). Taken together, these studies demonstrate that analyzing the pancreatic compartments 
simultaneously can significantly increase our understanding of how different cell types 
influence each other as well as disease.
Clinical correlative data has also contributed to our appreciation that diseases first affecting 
exocrine function can impact endocrine function, often resulting in the onset of diabetes 
(Figure 1; Table 1). In particular, Cystic fibrosis and Pancreatitis share common exocrine 
disease features, such as acinar cell loss, tissue fibrosis, and immune infiltration. In contrast, 
some exocrine diseases feature unique characteristics such as the beta cell autoantibodies 
observed in Chronic Pancreatitis. Altogether, both unique and shared exocrine features are 
thought to drive the crosstalk that contributes to endocrine dysfunction; however, it should 
be noted that further study is needed to thoroughly evaluate all the cellular alterations 
that contribute to endocrine dysfunction in the setting of exocrine disease. Additionally, 
we propose that investigating the replacement of acinar cells with adipocytes/fat tissue in 
some patients with obesity may present an opportunity to further inform the influence of 
exocrine-endocrine communication in the disease settings. In the following subsections, we 
review the evidence that suggests that crosstalk from exocrine disease can impact endocrine 
function.
B. Cystic fibrosis
Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disease caused by mutations in the CFTR protein 
(Shteinberg et al. 2021). The clinical features of CF include dysfunction of the exocrine 
pancreas, lungs, and gastrointestinal system due to pathologic mucus production (Sheppard 
and Nicholson 2002). Specifically with respect to the pancreas, impaired exocrine secretion, 
acinar atrophy, tissue fibrosis and ductal obstruction by mucus contribute to the exocrine 
failure observed in CF (Durie and Forstner 1989).

The gene mutations associated with CF were initially described by Riordan et al., who 
identified that CFTR encoded a protein involved in chloride transport (Riordan et al. 1989). 
It was later demonstrated that CFTR itself is an apical membrane chloride channel (Bear 
et al. 1992) that can also transport bicarbonate (Choi et al. 2001). These two functions of 
CFTR implicate it in the functional regulation of mucus osmotic balance, viscosity, and pH 
in the pancreatic ducts, which are required for proper exocrine function. In a multicenter 
study with over 500 individuals with CF, less than one-third retained normal exocrine 
pancreatic function, as measured by the abundance of fecal pancreatic elastase. Of note, 
those individuals who retained exocrine function harbored less severe CFTR mutations, 
which would provide sufficient CFTR transcript production to maintain normal pancreatic 
function (Augarten et al. 2008).
Crawford et al. first described the presence of CFTR in the pancreas by demonstrating 
its expression in the epithelium of pancreatic ducts (Crawford et al. 1991). It was further 
demonstrated that both chloride and bicarbonate levels were altered in patients with CF 
(Kopelman et al. 1989). Mechanistically, a decrease in intracellular chloride in ductal cells 
activates a kinase-dependent regulation of bicarbonate permeability in CFTR, leading to 
higher efflux of bicarbonate into the ductal epithelium (Park et al. 2010). The correct amount 
of bicarbonate ensures the appropriate pH of the pancreatic chyme, which maintains the 
pro-enzymes in their zymogen state until activation in the duodenum (Park and Lee 2012). 
In addition, CFTR-originated bicarbonate is essential for solubilizing the mucus in different 
epithelial tissues; in its absence, a highly adherent mucus forms (Gustafsson et al. 2012).
In response to the altered composition of the pancreatic chyme, improper zymogen secretion 
is observed in humans harboring CFTR mutations, leading to blockage in the small ducts 
of the pancreas (Imrie et al. 1979). Accumulation of the highly adherent mucus contributes 
to this ductal blockade resulting in pancreatic inflammation that progresses to acinar cell 
atrophy (Tucker et al. 2003). There is little observed impact on the endocrine pancreas at 
birth; however, CF-related diabetes (CFRD) increases from 2% in children to 40–50% in 
adults (Moran et al. 2009). Of note, a family history of diabetes significantly increased the 
risk of developing CFRD, suggesting that the susceptibility to development of CFRD may 
have a genetic component (Blackman et al. 2009).
Although mouse models have been used to study the effect of CF in other organs, rodents 
have an alternative pathway to CFTR in the pancreas thus preventing the development of 
pancreatic insufficiency (Scholte et al. 2004). Using a porcine model, Uc et al. demonstrated 
that certain digestive enzymes are reduced in piglets with CF (mutations) (Uc et al. 2012). 
As a result, the response to stimuli that induce pancreatic fluid volume and pH is altered 
suggesting that this animal model better replicates the pancreatic phenotype of the human 
disease (Uc et al. 2012). Moreover, Rogers et al. showed that the porcine model of CF also 
presents with pancreatic tissue fibrosis, which is a feature observed in the human disease 
(Rogers et al. 2008). Similar to human pathology, the islets in these CF piglets are also 
mostly intact at birth (Meyerholz et al. 2010). However with age, hyperglycemia is observed 
in adult pigs with CF and thus this model can be used to study exocrine-endocrine crosstalk 
in the disease setting (Uc et al. 2015).

The exact mechanism underlying the exocrine-endocrine crosstalk that contributes to 
pancreatic dysfunction in CF remains largely unknown. In humans, it has been suggested 
that the islets suffer secondary damage from exocrine tissue fibrosis and resultantly become 
deformed or destroyed (Kelly and Moran 2013). From a pathways perspective, O’Malley et 
al. hypothesized that the chronic damage in the pancreas due to elevated pancreatic oxidative 
stress and reduced antioxidant activity contributes to impaired insulin secretion in the islets 
of CF pigs, leading to beta cell failure and CFRD (O’Malley et al. 2022). This aligns with 
the imbalanced redox signaling previously described in human islets from patients with 
CFRD. Moreover, there is an increase in oxidative stress in the exocrine of CF patients 
due to increased lipids oxidation and the pro-inflammatory environment of CF, which 
then activates the deleterious redox pathways within the beta cell (Ntimbane et al. 2009; 
Hasan et al. 2021). The imbalanced redox signaling in the acinar cells further generates 
a pro-inflammatory environment that is deleterious for beta cell survival, the effects of 
which have been noted by the expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin 1 
beta (IL-1 beta) in post-mortem analysis of islets from the pancreata of individuals with 
CF (Hull et al. 2018). Although this data is preliminary, it is interesting that oxidative 
stress could play a role in endocrine dysfunction in CFRD as this is considered a major 
contributor to beta cell dysfunction in diabetes, thus possibly connecting the progression to 
diabetes in the setting of CF (Dinić et al. 2022). The loss of beta cells, and therefore insulin 
secretion, in CFRD may also result in an increased loss of CFTR in ductal epithelial cells 
(Marunaka 2017). Sun et al demonstrated that activation of the insulin receptor results in 
increased synthesis of Cftr mRNA in epithelial cells and CFTR localization to the membrane 
(Sun et al. 2014a). Given this data showing how insulin signaling from the endocrine 
pancreas contributes to the maintenance of ductal cell CFTR expression, it is reasonable 
to connect endocrine dysfunction with altered CFTR. Altogether, there is growing evidence 
that endocrine-exocrine crosstalk can contribute to disease progression.
In addition to the malfunctioning exocrine tissue, exocrine replacement with adipose tissue 
has been observed in the pancreata of individuals with CFRD (Iannucci et al. 1984). During 
this adipogenesis, the islets are remodeled, and this remodeling was found to correlate with 
the transient recovery of beta cell function and euglycemia (Sun et al. 2014b; Yi et al. 2016). 
However, long-term exposure to the fatty tissue that replaced the exocrine is associated with 
altered islet cytoarchitecture and decreased beta cell mass, suggesting that changes in the 
tissue surrounding the islets influence islet form and function (Löhr et al. 1989). More recent 
studies compared fibrotic replacement of the exocrine with fatty replacement and found that 
islets had altered morphology only when the exocrine was replaced with fat (Bogdani et 
al. 2017). Taken together, these data suggest that an intact exocrine pancreas with proper 
structural organization is needed for endocrine cell survival and function.
C. Acute Pancreatitis
Acute pancreatitis (AP) is an exocrine disease that is subdivided into “mild” or “severe” AP 
depending on the clinical presentation. Mild AP first presents local pancreas inflammation 
with no organ failure and a short period from onset to resolution (approximately one 
week). However, severe AP is characterized by pancreatic failure for over two days, fluid 
accumulation, and tissue necrosis (Banks et al. 2013). The condition affects approximately

Author Manuscript Author Manuscript Author Manuscript Author Manuscript

130 out of every 1 million individuals in the US, with 15% of cases developing diabetes 
within one year of exocrine disease onset (Sellers et al. 2018; Richardson and Park 2021).
Over a century ago, Hans Chiari first proposed that AP initiates with the intra-acinar 
activation of zymogens, leading to pancreatic autodigestion (Chiari 1896). It was later 
demonstrated that vesicle membrane disruption was key to initiating inflammation. Vesicle 
disruption then caused intracellular activation of trypsinogen and its release into the 
extracellular space, which initiated exocrine tissue necrosis (Willemer et al. 1989; Mithöfer 
et al. 1998; Hofbauer et al. 1998). In addition to trypsinogen, Gukovsky et al. discovered 
activated NFκB in acinar cells in early-stage AP; the activated NFκB led to cytokine release, 
neutrophil infiltration, and the activation of a local inflammatory response (Gukovsky et 
al. 1998). Interestingly AP outcome has been correlated with acinar cell expression of 
Mist1, a protein critical for maintaining acinar cell organization and identity. Karki et al. 
determined that the constitutive expression of Mist1 in the mouse pancreas is associated 
with increased acinar cell death and exocrine loss (Karki et al. 2015). Based on the elevated 
zymogen production in the pancreas of these Mist1-overexpressing mice, it is suggested that 
temporary loss of acinar cell secretory capacity is critical for exocrine repair and prevents 
tissue inflammation (Karki et al. 2015).
Multiple mechanisms have been implicated in how AP is initiated, all of which contribute 
to improper trypsinogen activation. One mechanism identified the mis-sorting of cathepsin B 
(CatB), a lysosomal hydrolase associated with organelle autophagy, into zymogen granules 
causing trypsinogen activation and the initiation of the inflammatory pathway (Van Acker 
et al. 2007). Another study suggested that an imbalance in autophagic flux is associated 
with impaired vesicle trafficking, contributing to the inflammation associated with AP 
(Mareninova et al. 2009). Hientarant et al. demonstrated that trypsinogen and NFκB 
activation, an early step of AP, can occur due to pathological caerulein signaling (Hietaranta 
et al. 2001). Caerulein is a CCK analog that activates the CCK receptor (Dockray 1972). 
Therefore, when caerulein is inhibited there is a significant reduction in intracellular 
activation of zymogens (Hietaranta et al. 2001; Husain et al. 2007).
Reed et al. proposed another mechanism for AP establishment wherein reducing 
extracellular pH results in the accumulation of calcium in the acinar cell that causes 
precocious activation of zymogens (Reed et al. 2011). This points to a role for ductal cells 
in AP pathogenesis, given that the bicarbonate secreted by these cells is essential to regulate 
acinar extracellular pH (previously reviewed in (Hegyi et al. 2011). Therefore, although 
initially postulated to be a disease solely affecting the pancreatic acini, there is mounting 
evidence that other pancreatic cells, i.e. duct cells, are involved in the pathology.
The role of the ductal system in the etiology of AP highlights how the interconnection 
of the pancreatic compartments can contribute to disease progression. Therefore, it should 
be expected that AP progression impacts the endocrine system. In line, the prevalence of 
diabetes following AP presentation is approximately 15% after the first year and increases 
to 20–50% after five years with AP (Das et al. 2014; Zhi et al. 2019). One significant 
predictor of diabetes development after AP is the presence of exocrine necrosis, which has 
been correlated with endocrine dysfunction (Garip et al. 2013). Mild inflammation of the

exocrine has also been shown to increase cytokine presence in the pancreas, particularly 
the expression of the cytokine interleukin-6 (IL6) (Gillies et al. 2016). The presence of IL6 
correlates with diabetes development after AP; studies suggest that IL6 increases hepatocyte 
insulin resistance, contributing to diabetes onset (Senn et al. 2002; Gillies et al. 2016). 
Moreover, preliminary studies have implicated excessive nitric oxide production, oxidative 
stress, and loss of ER function in the beta cell dysfunction of AP-associated diabetes (Qader 
et al. 2003; Hong et al. 2016). More recently, it has been suggested that the presence of 
islet autoantibodies in a subset of patients may lead to diabetes secondary to AP, as the 
consistent inflammation could stimulate an autoimmune attack on the beta cells leading to 
their dysfunction (Yadav et al. 2023). The proinflammatory environment observed in AP has 
been associated with acinar cell extracellular vesicles (EVs). Recent in vitro data suggests 
that acinar EVs can exacerbate the proinflammatory environment (Tang et al. 2022). Tang 
et al induced AP-EVs production in vitro by treating cells with caerulein. These AP-EVs 
contained microRNAs capable of downregulating the FoxO1 pathway in macrophages, 
inducing M1 polarization, which enhanced the proinflammatory environment (Tang et al. 
2022). Altogether, current data suggests that the immune system contributes to AP-derived 
diabetes both via proinflammatory signals that lead to beta cell dysfunction as well as the 
presence of autoantibodies that enhance susceptibility to immune attack.
Interestingly, Bruce et al demonstrated that loss of insulin signaling during AP can 
exacerbate acinar cell loss (Bruce et al. 2021). This study described a loss of ATP supply 
to the membrane calcium channels of acinar cells in the absence of insulin signaling. This 
results in cytotoxic accumulation of calcium in the acinar cell, which in turn contributes to 
cell death and exocrine inflammation (Bruce et al. 2021). This recent work suggests that 
the presence of insulin may assist in reducing acinar inflammation, which also provides 
evidence to support how exocrine-endocrine crosstalk may participate in disease progression 
or severity. In short, endocrine derived signals affect inflammation, which is a hallmark of 
pancreatitis.
D. Hereditary Pancreatitis
Hereditary pancreatitis (HP) is characterized by mutations in genes encoding proteins that 
when dysfunctional cause early-onset pancreatitis, usually within the first two decades 
of life (Panchoo et al. 2022). The most common gene mutations are found in serine 
protease 1 (PRSS1) and account for 68–80% of HP cases (Howes et al. 2004; Rebours 
et al. 2009). Mutations in the genes encoding serine protease inhibitor Kazal type 1 
(SPINK1), chymotrypsin C (CTRCT), carboxyl ester lipase (CEL), and CFTR have also 
been associated with HP development (Panchoo et al. 2022; El Jellas et al. 2022).
The connection between PRSS1 and the induction of exocrine dysfunction in HP was 
discovered with the first identified PRSS1 mutation, which altered the cleavage of 
trypsinogen into trypsin leading to excessive mis-localized activated trypsin and acinar 
cell autodigestion (Whitcomb et al. 1996). Subsequently, identified mutations in PRSS1 
have shown similar effects (Sahin-Tóth 2000). The trypsin activation pathway relies on the 
activity of inhibitors to maintain trypsinogen in its inactive state, which also explains the 
presentation of HP in individuals with mutations in SPINK1 (Kume et al. 2012). SPINK1

mutations lead to acinar destruction, fibrotic tissue, and thickened acinar secretions in the 
ducts, highlighting the impairment of the pancreas as a whole in HP (Felicelli and Pezhouh 
2021). Moreover, in humans, excessive amounts of trypsin combined with intra-acinar 
activation results in a similar exocrine inflammation and acinar cell destruction profile as 
observed in AP (Patel et al. 2013).
Although diagnoses of HP are often underreported due to a lack of diagnostic genetic 
testing, current estimates place the incidence of the disease at 0.57 per 100,000 individuals, 
with approximately 80% developing diabetes as a secondary effect of HP (Ramalho and 
Dytz 2020). Archer et al. generated an HP mouse model to determine the mechanisms 
that drive diabetes onset in HP and found that the early stages of HP involve immune cell 
infiltration and acute injury of exocrine pancreatic tissue. In contrast, the main characteristic 
observed in later stages of HP was fibrosis in the exocrine tissue suggesting that the 
mechanisms of beta cell dysfunction are similar to those described in AP (Archer et al. 
2006). Significant islet cell loss has been described as well as accompanying hyperglycemia 
in 20–30% of HP patients (Dytz et al. 2017; Ramalho and Dytz 2020).
Ræder et al. described a mutation in CEL as another cause for HP; CEL mutations are also 
known as the underlying cause of Mature Onset Diabetes of the Young type 8 (MODY8) 
(Ræder et al. 2006). In this case report, a family with hereditary diabetes also presented 
with symptoms of exocrine deficiency such as abdominal pain and stool alteration. The 
identification of a mutation in CEL provided a connection to the exocrine origin of the 
disease. Clinical analyses showed a significant reduction of bicarbonate secretion, loss 
of acinar and endocrine tissue at later stages of life and fat infiltration in the pancreas 
(Ræder et al. 2006). More recent in vitro co-culture experiments suggest that mutant CEL 
can be transferred from the exocrine to the endocrine thus mediating exocrine-endocrine 
crosstalk (Kahraman et al. 2022). Mutant CEL protein was shown to be internalized by 
the beta cells and formed aggregates that impaired secretion (Kahraman et al. 2022). This 
communication between the exocrine and endocrine in a disease setting demonstrates how a 
genetic mutation in an exocrine-related gene can directly influence endocrine function.
E. Chronic Pancreatitis
Chronic pancreatitis (CP) is characterized by fibrosis and inflammation in the exocrine 
pancreas that leads to pancreatic atrophy, ductal distortion, exocrine and endocrine 
insufficiency (Whitcomb et al. 2016). As the name suggests, chronic pancreatitis is 
associated with a prolonged inflammation of the organ, leading to irreversible structural 
damage (Ashraf et al. 2021). Both genetic and environmental factors including alcohol 
consumption and tobacco smoking, can contribute to CP development and influence the 
disease classification and prognosis (Schneider et al. 2007).
Depending on the type of injury, ductal and/or acinar cells may be affected. Acinar 
and duct cell apoptosis releases proinflammatory cytokines into environment and attracts 
immune cells (Brock et al. 2013). Ultimately, the proinflammatory environment leads to the 
activation of pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), which develop a myofibroblast-like phenotype 
that makes them capable of producing and secreting ECM components (Apte et al. 1999). 
An increase in collagen I/III and fibronectin within the intercellular space of the exocrine

tissue leading to fibrosis (Brock et al. 2013). It has also been proposed that alcohol and 
tobacco consumption can directly stimulate the activation of PSCs, directly causing fibrosis 
(Klöppel et al. 2004). Active PSCs express TGFβ, which causes autocrine inhibition of 
collagen degradation, thus creating a positive loop of inflammatory signaling and fibrosis in 
an exocrine destructive manner (Shek et al. 2002).
The onset of diabetes after CP has 15% incidence within 3 years of CP diagnosis; this 
incidence increases to greater than 30% by 5 years post diagnosis (Zhu et al. 2019). 
Long-term retrospective studies showed that by 50 years post CP diagnosis, 90% of 
patients developed diabetes (Pan et al. 2016). Interestingly, risk factors for CP onset did 
not contribute to CP-associated diabetes development (Zhu et al. 2019). The islet cell loss 
and hyperglycemia in CP is commonly associated with a more established (later stage of the 
disease) fibrotic exocrine pancreas (Blondet et al. 2007; Kobayashi et al. 2010). However, 
beta cell dysfunction has since been reported in earlier stages of CP and it has been proposed 
that genetic susceptibility to diabetes plays a critical role in determining whether a patient 
with CP subsequently develops diabetes (Olesen et al. 2020). Interestingly, a reduction in 
PDX1 expression correlated with diabetes development in patients with CP, suggesting that a 
loss of beta cell differentiation contributes to diabetes onset (Mitnala et al. 2010). However, 
the most significant component of endocrine cell loss in CP is inflammation, mediated by 
the proinflammatory environment created by acinar cell apoptosis and fibrosis (Sasikala et 
al. 2012). The presence of proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1 beta, tumor necrosis 
factor alpha (TNF-alpha), and interferon-gamma (IFN-gamma), have been implicated in 
apoptosis initiation and reduced cAMP signaling in beta cells during CP associated diabetes, 
as occurs in other diseases associated with beta cell dysfunction (Ciochina et al. 2022). 
Of note, T cells contribute significantly to the immune islet infiltrate responsible for the 
majority of the beta cell loss in CP (Talukdar et al. 2016). Similar to AP, development of 
diabetes secondary to CP has been associated with the presence of islet autoantibodies and 
an increased chance of autoimmune attack due to local inflammation (Yadav et al. 2023).
Although beta cell loss is an important component of diabetes onset in CP, it is worth 
noting that alpha cell functional alterations have been reported in select studies (Linde et 
al. 1977; Kannan et al. 1979; Larsen et al. 1988). More recent work has shown that in 
diabetes secondary to CP there is a loss of glucose-induced inhibition of glucagon secretion, 
similar to that observed in T2D, suggesting that this population of cells could play a role 
in the pathogenesis of diabetes secondary to CP (Mumme et al. 2017). Kumar et al saw 
the same elevation of glucagon due to loss of inhibition by glucose and highlighted that 
Freýs procedure, a common intervention for CP, possibly increased the rate of alpha cell 
dysfunction (Kumar et al., 2018)
Pancreatic Fat Accumulation and Exocrine-Endocrine Crosstalk
As reviewed above, many cell types in the pancreatic microenvironment contribute to 
function and dysfunction. Significantly, adipocyte accumulation in the exocrine pancreas 
can contribute to beta cell failure. Obesity is defined as abnormally increased body fat, 
which can be generally inferred by body-mass index (BMI), with overweight defined as 
BMI greater than 25 kg/m2 and obesity defined as BMI greater than 30 kg/m2 (WHO

2021). Meta-analyses of clinical studies identified that the effect of adipose tissue gain 
is cumulative, with each additional 5 kg/m2 gained above 25 kg/m2 representing a 40% 
increase in mortality from ischemic heart disease (Prospective Studies Collaboration 2009; 
Global BMI Mortality Collaboration et al. 2016). Obesity is a risk factor for vascular 
disease, neoplasia, and diabetes (Prospective Studies Collaboration 2009). Furthermore, 
increased BMI and waist circumference are correlated with increased visceral fat in the 
liver, muscle, and pancreas (Pieńkowska et al. 2019). In particular, the accumulation of fat 
in the exocrine pancreas, also known as “fatty pancreas” (FP) disease, impairs pancreas 
function, including effects on insulin resistance and secretion (Lu et al. 2019; Altinmakas et 
al. 2021). Recently, Skudder-Hill demonstrated that fat accumulation in the body and tail of 
the pancreas positively correlated with insulin resistance, while overall fat accumulation was 
associated with loss of beta cell function (Skudder-Hill et al. 2022). The prevalence of FP in 
the general population is estimated to be 25–30% and in individuals using obesity therapy 
this number increases to approximately 60% (Lee et al. 2009; Sepe et al. 2011; Lesmana 
et al. 2015). It has been proposed that genetic susceptibility as well as environmental 
factors could influence the development of FP; however, the current understanding of FP 
pathophysiology is limited due to insufficient testing (Romana et al. 2018)
Obesity has whole body effects including endocrine dysfunction, and patients with obesity 
having a 7 fold increased risk of developing diabetes (Abdullah et al. 2010). Interestingly, 
beta cell dysfunction secondary to obesity has been proposed to be due in part to a genetic 
susceptibility to beta cell failure exacerbated by excessive adiposity (Manning et al. 2012). 
As the replacement of acinar cells with adipocytes is a commonly observed phenotype in 
exocrine pathologies, it would be interesting to investigate how fatty replacement influences 
cellular crosstalk in the pancreas. Thus better understanding of FP could shed light on the 
mechanisms that facilitate exocrine-endocrine crosstalk in the disease setting.
The effect of FP on exocrine pancreas function has been reported in case studies with data 
showing that elevated BMI is correlated with reduced fecal elastase (Kiker et al. 2018). A 
study using MRI analysis of fat deposition in the pancreas identified that pancreatic function 
was inversely correlated with the accumulation of fat in the pancreas and identified that at 
least 35% of patients with pancreatic steatosis present with exocrine pancreas insufficiency 
(EPI) (Tahtaci et al. 2018). Whereas human EPI correlates with FP in some studies, other 
studies have found no association between these parameters (Terzin et al. 2014).
Concerning the impact of FP/exocrine dysfunction on endocrine function, clinical studies 
show that approximately 30% of individuals with FP present with impaired endocrine 
function (Wang et al. 2014; Miyake et al. 2018). Of note, patients with T2D do not 
show an increase in FP when compared with weight matched controls (Saisho et al. 
2007). However, the published studies evaluating FP leading to glucose intolerance show 
conflicting results, and it has been suggested that both extent of fat infiltration and genetic 
risk may contribute to the effect of FP on endocrine function (Gaborit et al. 2015; Saisho 
2016; Steven et al. 2016; Murakami et al. 2017). A bioinformatics model proposed that 
increased lipid peroxidation due to increased free fatty acids (FFAs) leads to the production 
of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, which subsequently stimulates deleterious 
pathways in the beta cells (Andrare et al. 2021). However, the mechanisms that underlie

this beta cell failure remain unclear due to methodological limitations of human clinical 
studies such as tissue collection for molecular analysis. More recently, mouse models were 
established to further advance the understanding of how adipose tissue replacement of 
exocrine impacts endocrine function.
Work by Mathur et al. showed that in genetically obese mice, fat accumulation in the 
pancreas was present and these cells secreted the adipokines IL-1beta and TNF-alpha 
(Mathur et al. 2007). This same study identified increased FFAs in the pancreatic tissue 
(Mathur et al. 2007) thus demonstrating that rodents can be used as a model to understand 
the effect of FFAs on beta cell function. In fact, the increase in circulating FFAs that occurs 
in FP and obesity may have a short-term negative effect on endocrine function. For example, 
genetic predisposition to diabetes and prolonged FFAs elevation can cause Toll-like receptor 
4 (TLR4) activation in the adipocytes. The activation of TLR4 drives the adipocyte secretory 
profile to one that is pro-inflammatory, increasing macrophage infiltration and IL-1beta 
secretion (Gerst et al. 2019; Wagner et al. 2022). This pro-inflammatory cytokine release 
is an important mediator of the immune cell-beta cell crosstalk and activates ER stress 
pathways and subsequent beta cell apoptosis (Eizirik et al. 2009; Gerst et al. 2019). Taken 
together, these mouse models highlight how exocrine replacement with fat in FP and obesity 
influence the interaction of the exocrine and endocrine and how the cells in these different 
pancreatic compartments can drive disease-causing cellular communication.
Conclusion
Deciphering the mechanisms that permit communication between cells in the exocrine 
and endocrine compartments of the pancreas can shed light on how exocrine disease 
can drive endocrine dysfunction (Scotet et al. 2020; Iannuzzi et al. 2022; Overton and 
Mastracci 2022). Herein, we have reviewed the exocrine phenotypes associated with CF, 
AP, HP, CP, and obesity/FP. Whereas each exocrine-related condition has a different clinical 
presentation including ductal obstruction by mucus, acinar ablation and inflammation or 
exocrine replacement with fat, the subsequent impact on endocrine/beta cell function is 
similar. This would suggest that the beta cell loss, beta cell dysfunction, and disturbed islet 
cytoarchitecture that develops secondary to exocrine disease may be driven by crosstalk 
from other damaged cells in the pancreas. That said, given that not all individuals with 
CF, AP, HP, and CP and obesity/FP progress to diabetes, further investigation is needed 
to understand those signals that may preserve the beta cells in the setting of exocrine 
dysfunction.

Figure 1. Exocrine disease “crosstalk” can contribute to endocrine dysfunction.
Cellular “crosstalk” may contribute to the secondary endocrine dysfunction observed in 
exocrine diseases. Cystic fibrosis, acute, hereditary and chronic pancreatitis are exocrine 
disease with described secondary endocrine dysfunction and diabetes incidence. Features of 
each disease are denoted with colour-coded circle labels to diagram common and unique 
features associated with each disease.
(PINK circle) In Cystic fibrosis (CF), ductal obstruction and acinar atrophy drives 
inflammation of the exocrine pancreas, which can lead to an immune infiltration of the 
pancreatic islets, increased redox signaling and impaired endocrine function.
(BLUE circle) In Acute Pancreatitis (AP), premature intra-acinar activation of zymogens 
leads to exocrine tissue inflammation and necrosis, which attracts immune cells that 
can interact with the islets and contribute to beta cell dysfunction via increased pro-
inflammatory cytokine secretion.
(GREEN circle) In Hereditary Pancreatitis (HP), genetic mutations in PRSS1, SPINK, 
CTRCT, CFTR, and CEL cause inappropriate zymogen activation, acinar cell autodigestion 
and islet cell death. Mutations in CEL specifically cause protein aggregation within the beta 
cells leading to dysfunction.
(PURPLE circle) In Chronic Pancreatitis (CP), immune infiltration and inflammation lead 
to stellate cells activation and excessive ECM deposition, resulting in acinar cell loss. This 
inflammatory exocrine environment leads to immune infiltration of the endocrine pancreas, 
which combined with genetic susceptibility can result in beta cell failure and death. Alpha 
cells are also dysfunctional in chronic pancreatitis.
