# The Incidence and Risk Factors of Gout in Non-dialysis Chronic Kidney Disease Patients With Asymptomatic Hyperuricemia.
**Authors:** Beberashvili I, Marsa HA, Doenyas-Barak K, Maslakov I, Efrati S
**Journal:** Kidney Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xkme.2025.101197](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xkme.2025.101197)

## Content

RATIONALE & OBJECTIVE: Gout frequently complicates chronic kidney disease (CKD), yet the incidence and specific risk factors for gout in patients with CKD and asymptomatic hyperuricemia remain unclear. We aimed to determine gout incidence and risk factors in nondialysis patients with CKD and asymptomatic hyperuricemia.

STUDY DESIGN: Retrospective cohort study.

SETTING & PARTICIPANTS: Analysis of 771 ambulatory nondialysis patients with CKD (stages 3-5) with asymptomatic hyperuricemia (serum urate level, ≥7.0 mg/dL), without prior gout, followed from 2010 to 2023 at a single center.

PREDICTORS: Multiple clinical and laboratory factors were evaluated, including demographics, comorbidities, medications, and biochemical markers.

OUTCOMES: Incident gout diagnosis confirmed by rheumatology specialists.

ANALYTICAL APPROACH: Cox proportional hazards models and receiver operating characteristic curve analyses. A composite weighted index was derived from regression-based predictors.

RESULTS: Over a median follow-up of 47 months, gout developed in 140 (18.2%) patients (incidence rate, 41.9 cases per 1,000 person-years). Independent predictors of gout included younger age (per year; hazard ratio [HR], 0.97; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.96-0.99), higher serum urate (per 1 mg/dL; HR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.14-1.46), lower serum creatinine levels (per 1 mg/dL; HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.65-0.99), and diuretic use (HR, 1.86; 95% CI, 1.00-3.45). Higher composite weighted index (per 1 unit; HR, 2.19; 95% CI, 1.70-2.83) was associated with increased gout risk.

LIMITATIONS: Single-center, retrospective study design limits generalizability and causal inference.

CONCLUSIONS: Incident gout is common among patients with CKD and asymptomatic hyperuricemia. Younger age, elevated serum urate levels, lower creatinine levels, and diuretic use significantly predict gout onset. Identifying high-risk patients using these predictors could guide preventive therapeutic decisions.

Asymptomatic hyperuricemia, as defined by the 2020 American College of Rheumatology Guideline for the Management of Gout, refers to urate levels ≥6.8 mg/dL in the absence of inflammation episodes triggered by urate crystals, such as gout flares.1 Asymptomatic hyperuricemia is frequently linked with lifestyle-related diseases, including hypertension,2 diabetes,3 metabolic syndrome,4 nonalcoholic fatty liver disease,5 chronic kidney disease (CKD),6 and cardiovascular disease.7 Previous clinical studies suggest that hyperuricemia is a modifiable risk factor for CKD development and progression,8,9 though the impact of lowering serum urate on kidney disease or cardiovascular events remains controversial.10,11 On the other hand, the use of serum urate-lowering therapy has shown potential in preventing the onset of gout in patients with CKD. A secondary analysis of the FEATHER (Febuxostat Versus Placebo Randomized Controlled Trial Regarding Reduced Renal Function in Patients With Hyperuricemia Complicated by Chronic Kidney Disease Stage 3) study revealed that febuxostat treatment effectively prevents the development of gouty arthritis in Japanese patients with CKD and asymptomatic hyperuricemia.11 Johnson et al10 highlighted the potential renal benefits of lowering serum urate levels in patients with gout, emphasizing evidence that urate crystals may contribute to both local and systemic inflammation, with the latter possibly increasing the risk of cardiovascular events and mortality. A time-dependent Cox regression analysis from a large epidemiological study indicated that a gout episode was independently linked to a 1.5-fold increase in mortality risk among patients with kidney failure undergoing hemodialysis.12 These findings highlight the importance of identifying high-risk populations among patients with CKD with asymptomatic hyperuricemia who are at an increased risk of developing gout.

The German Chronic Kidney Disease study, a prospective multicenter study from Germany involving 5,085 patients with CKD, reported an asymptomatic hyperuricemia prevalence of 61% and a gout prevalence of 24.3%.13 Therefore, the majority of patients with CKD with asymptomatic hyperuricemia do not develop gout. However, its prevalence increases as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) declines, ranging from 16.0% in those with an eGFR >60 mL/min/1.73 m2 to 35.6% in those with an eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2.13 The 2020 American College of Rheumatology guidelines for gout management1 suggest that, unlike the Japanese guidelines recommending treatment for serum urate levels above 9 mg/dL,14 most asymptomatic patients with hyperuricemia (including those with CKD) may not benefit from lowering serum urate levels because the risks and costs may outweigh the potential benefits, given the low likelihood of progressing to gout.1

We hypothesized that specific subgroups of patients with CKD with asymptomatic hyperuricemia, particularly those characterized using a combination of risk factors for developing gout, represent a distinct high-risk phenotype. This study aimed to evaluate the incidence of gout and identify the key risk factors associated with its development in this population.

This retrospective study was conducted using a clinical database of 8,000 ambulatory patients with CKD stages 3-5, not dependent on dialysis, who were managed in the outpatient clinics of our institution between 2010 and 2023.

The local ethics committee at our center waived the requirement for informed consent because of the study’s retrospective design. We excluded patients who had not undergone serum urate testing or who had been diagnosed with gout before 2010. Additionally, patients who did not meet the criteria for asymptomatic hyperuricemia were excluded. Asymptomatic hyperuricemia was defined as a serum urate level >7.0 mg/dL14 in 3 consecutive measurements, without a diagnosis of gout or nephrolithiasis. Subsequently, we excluded patients who had received serum urate-lowering treatment (including allopurinol and febuxostat) for any reason, those who transitioned to care at another institution, and those with incomplete data. As the use of urate-lowering therapy in patients with CKD often reflects off-label treatment of asymptomatic hyperuricemia and is not always initiated even in patients with confirmed gout, we chose not to use antihyperuricemic medication as a surrogate indicator for gout diagnosis. This left us with 771 patients with CKD who met the criteria for asymptomatic hyperuricemia and had all the necessary data available for analysis. The severity of comorbidities was evaluated using the Charlson comorbidity index.15 The primary aim of our study was to identify gout diagnoses made at our institution’s Rheumatology Institute from 2010 until the end of the study period. Initially, the diagnosis of gout was based on the Rome criteria.16 However, since 2015, diagnoses have been made using the updated Jansen criteria,17 which incorporate clinical, laboratory, and imaging domains. These new criteria offer high sensitivity (92%) and specificity (89%) compared with the gold standard for gout diagnosis. Of the 140 patients diagnosed with gout during the follow-up period, 46 patients with CKD (32.9%) required synovial fluid aspiration for confirmation of the diagnosis.

Patients were censored at the time of their first gout diagnosis (incidence) or upon death.

The laboratory tests conducted on patients upon their admission to our institution's outpatient clinics were recorded, with all analyses performed in our center's laboratory. Blood samples were processed using automated methods to measure complete blood count, creatinine, serum urate, and ferritin levels. Albumin levels were quantified using the bromocresol green method. The eGFR was calculated using the CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) 2021 creatinine-based formula.18

The study analyzed both quantitative and categorical data. Quantitative variables with a normal distribution were presented as means and standard deviations, whereas nonnormally distributed variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages. To compare normally distributed continuous variables between the 2 groups, a 2-sided t test was used, whereas nonnormally distributed continuous variables were compared using theMann-Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests.

Survival analyses were carried out using the Kaplan-Meier survival curve and the Cox proportional hazard model. The results of the univariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Based on the multivariable Cox regression analysis with backward elimination, we constructed a composite weighted index to represent the cumulative risk of developing gout. Cutoff values for continuous predictors (age, serum urate levels, and serum creatinine levels) were selected using receiver operating characteristic analysis to optimize sensitivity and specificity. The resulting binary variables were assigned weights proportional to their logistic regression coefficients, following a commonly used approach for risk index construction.19 The final index ranged from −2.39 to 0.74, and its association with incident gout was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models.

To avoid multicollinearity and model overfitting, we examined the variance inflation factor for all covariates. Variables showing high collinearity (variance inflation factor, > 3) were excluded from the final regression models. Specifically, the geriatric nutritional risk index, eGFR, and CKD stage were removed because of strong correlations with body mass index (BMI), albumin levels, and serum creatinine levels, respectively. Because the geriatric nutritional risk index and eGFR are derived indices incorporating variables already in the model, we prioritized the inclusion of raw variables (eg, albumin levels, BMI, and creatinine levels) to enhance model transparency and interpretability. Sex, BMI, and Charlson comorbidity index were selected a priori for inclusion in the multivariable model based on their established associations with gout incidence and their potential role as confounders in patients with CKD. Specifically, sex and BMI are well-recognized contributors to gout risk,13 and the Charlson comorbidity index was included to account for the overall burden of chronic illness, which may influence the likelihood of developing gout. The final multivariable Cox proportional hazards models included the composite weighted index, age, sex, BMI, Charlson comorbidity index, and CKD stages (G3-G5).

As a sensitivity analysis accounting for the competing risk of death, we applied the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model using the crr function from the cmprsk package in R (R Core Team). This model estimated the subdistribution HRs for gout incidence while treating death as a competing event.

All analyses were performed using SPSS, version 29 (IBM SPSS), and R (version 4.4.0). A linear term for composite weighted index was included in the Cox model to simplify the relationship, enhancing stability and interpretability.

A total of 771 patients with CKD in stages 3-5, not undergoing dialysis, with an average age of 72 years, and comprising 337 (44%) women, were included in the statistical analysis. Over half of the participants (65.6%) had type 2 diabetes, and the majority (625 [81.1%]) were using diuretics, including thiazides or loop diuretics.

Over a median follow-up period of 47 months (25th and 75th percentiles: 18-78 months), 140 (18.2%) patients developed gout. The incidence rate of gout in this study population was 41.9 cases per 1,000 person-years. The incidence of gout by CKD stage was as follows: 112 of 527 (21.3%) in stage 3, 25 of 168 (14.9%) in stage 4, and 3 of 76 (3.9%) in stage 5.

A threshold of −0.65 was associated with 61% sensitivity and 60% specificity in predicting incident gout.

In the Fine-Gray competing risk analysis, the composite weighted index remained an independent predictor of gout incidence (subdistribution HR, 2.11; 95% CI, 1.65-2.70; P < 0.001), even after adjusting for death as a competing event and for sex, BMI, comorbidity burden, and CKD stage. Notably, a higher Charlson comorbidity index and more advanced CKD stage were associated with a lower subdistribution risk of gout (HR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.78-0.90; P < 0.001; and HR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.48-0.89; P = 0.006; respectively), suggesting that increased mortality may limit the likelihood of experiencing a gout event. Sex and BMI were not significantly associated with gout incidence in this model.

The study identified a high incidence of gout among patients with CKD. Those at the greatest risk are typically in stages 3-4 of CKD, with creatinine levels below 1.51 mg/dL, serum urate levels above 8.3 mg/dL, a younger age around 71 years, and regular diuretic use. These findings highlight the importance of considering preventive treatment to lower serum urate levels in high-risk patients with CKD.

Despite numerous studies showing a high incidence of kidney dysfunction in gout patients, data on gout incidence among patients with CKD are surprisingly scarce. Previous research found a 6-fold increase in gout incidence and a 20-fold increase in hyperuricemia among patients with severe kidney dysfunction.20 Additionally, CKD prevalence in patients with gout is higher than previously reported, at around 40%, according to data from health systems and gout databases.21 Effective control of serum urate levels is generally low among patients with gout and even lower in those with CKD.21 The Framingham Heart Study identified CKD as an independent risk factor for gout but did not assess CKD severity or report eGFR values, only categorizing participants as with or without CKD.22 The gout occurrence rate in our population was 41.9 per 1,000 person-years, compared with 27.2 per 1,000 person-years in a large multicenter US study of patients with CKD23 and 18.8 per 1,000 person-years in men in a retrospective cohort study using linked health care databases from Ontario, Canada.24 This discrepancy is likely due to differences in the demographic and clinical characteristics of the populations studied. Notably, all patients with CKD in our study had asymptomatic hyperuricemia, whereas the US study included patients with varying baseline serum urate levels,23 and the Canadian study did not report serum urate levels at all.24 Importantly, our study is the first to report on the risk of developing gout among patients with CKD with asymptomatic hyperuricemia.

Our findings align with previous research showing that the incidence of gout in patients with CKD stage 5, including those on dialysis, is comparable with the general population, with a 5% incidence after 1 year of dialysis and 15.4% after 5 years.12 The relatively low gout incidence in CKD stage 5, particularly in patients receiving hemodialysis, compared with CKD stages 3-4, may be due to reduced inflammatory cytokine production.25 Schreiner et al25 found that monocytes from patients with end-stage renal disease secreted lower levels of inflammatory cytokines like interleukin 6 in response to monosodium urate crystals, which might help prevent acute gout despite high serum urate levels.

Our finding of increased gout risk in patients with CKD with asymptomatic hyperuricemia and less severe CKD likely reflects the interplay of uric acid handling, systemic inflammation, and triggers such as dietary habits or medications (eg, thiazide or loop diuretics). Gout risk generally rises with CKD severity because of proportional increases in serum urate levels.23,24 However, in less severe CKD, disproportionately high urate levels and greater fluctuations—a key trigger for gout flares26—may outweigh this trend. These fluctuations are more likely driven by variable dietary intake in early CKD27 and become less pronounced as CKD progresses.28 Furthermore, in advanced CKD, higher mortality rates may prevent gout development, skewing the observed risk toward patients with milder CKD. Indeed, the gout risk was attenuated in our multivariable model after accounting for all-cause mortality. Future large-scale longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the higher gout incidence observed in less severe CKD among patients with asymptomatic hyperuricemia.

Our study identified specific characteristics in patients with CKD that elevate the risk of gout. Typically, the first gout attack occurs after years of asymptomatic hyperuricemia. In the Normative Aging Study, gout incidence was 0.1% annually for serum urate levels <7.0 mg/dL, 0.5% for levels between 7.0 and 8.9 mg/dL, and 4.9% for levels >9.0 mg/dL.29 Another study reported a 12% gout incidence over 14 years in patients with serum urate levels between 7.0 and 7.9 mg/dL.30 The cutoff values for high gout risk in our study align with those observed in the general population.29,31

The impact of age on gout incidence remains unclear, with studies showing mixed results. Although a study from Ireland reported a linear increase in gout incidence,20 other research, including ours, observed a decrease in gout onset after age 70-80 years in patients with CKD.32 Similarly, the Framingham Heart Study found an inverse relationship between age and gout onset in patients with CKD.22 These findings emphasized the need for careful interpretation of data on age and gout, considering study limitations and population-specific factors.

Our study’s findings on the association between diuretics and an increased risk of gout align with existing research. Our results confirm that diuretics are an independent risk factor for gout, both in the general population33 and among patients with CKD.13 This association is largely explained by the ability of thiazide and loop diuretics to reduce renal uric acid excretion, thereby increasing serum urate levels and gout risk.13,33

First, as a retrospective study, our research is susceptible to biases, making it impossible to establish a causal link between new gout cases and the analyzed risk factors. Second, our data were derived from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population with CKD. Third, because of the inherent limitations of the database, we cannot be certain that all existing gout cases were fully accounted for, and some cases may have been missed despite clinical follow-up and documentation. Despite these limitations, the findings are significant, highlighting the importance of identifying high-risk subpopulations among patients with CKD because gout significantly affects their morbidity, mortality, and quality of life.13 As a retrospective study using real-world data, the findings may be highly relevant to clinical practice. Notably, our cohort included a relatively high proportion of female participants, which is uncommon in gout-related studies that typically focus on male-dominant populations.24,26

In conclusion, gout incidence is high among CKD patients with asymptomatic hyperuricemia. Younger patients with CKD (under the age of 71 years) with low creatinine levels <1.5 mg/dL) and elevated serum urate levels (>8.3 mg/dL), who frequently use diuretics, are at a heightened risk of developing gout. Preventive treatment to lower serum urate levels might reduce this risk in such patients. However, further research is needed to assess the effectiveness and safety of this preventive approach.
