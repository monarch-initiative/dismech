# Mice carrying the homologous human shelterin POT1-L259S mutation linked to pulmonary fibrosis show a telomerase deficiency-like phenotype with telomere shortening with increasing mouse generations.
**Authors:** Sánchez-Vázquez R, Burgaz García-Oteyza S, Serrano R, Flores JM, Martínez P, Blasco MA
**Journal:** Genes Dev (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1101/gad.352855.125](https://doi.org/10.1101/gad.352855.125)

## Content

Pulmonary fibrosis is a lethal disease associated with damaging insults to the lung and with organismal aging. The presence of short and dysfunctional telomeres has been placed at the origin of this disease in a percentage of both familial and sporadic cases. Recently, a mutation in the telomere-binding protein protection of telomeres 1 in humans (hPOT1), the hPOT1-L259S mutation, was found in families with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Here, we generated a Pot1a L261S knock-in mouse harboring the murine homologous hPOT1-L259S mutation. We found that the homozygous Pot1a L261S mice show shorter telomeres and degenerative pathologies in the intestine, testes, and lungs at old ages, a phenotype that is aggravated with increasing mouse generations, in striking analogy to the telomerase-deficient mouse models. Furthermore, we found that the POT1a-L261S mutant protein binds more strongly to TPP1 and to telomerase and impedes telomerase-dependent telomere lengthening in vivo. We show that telomerase activity at telomeres is reduced in the presence of POT1a-L261S, which behaves as a dominant negative mutant, thus providing a potential mechanism by which Pot1a L261S knock-in mice phenocopy the short telomere phenotype of the telomerase knockout model.

Telomeres are protective structures at chromosome ends that are essential to prevent end-to-end chromosome fusions and chromosome end degradation. Mammalian telomeres consist of tandem repeats of the TTAGGG DNA sequence bound by a six protein complex known as shelterin, which encompasses TRF1, TRF1, POT1, TPP1, RAP1, and TIN2 (de Lange 2005; Blasco 2007). The shelterin complex is essential to ensure chromosome stability and to regulate telomere length. TRF1 and TRF2 bind to the double-stranded region of telomeric DNA, whereas TIN2 bridges TRF1 and TRF2 and binds TPP1, which recruits POT1 (protection of telomere 1) to the telomere (de Lange 2005; Blasco 2007).

Telomerase is a reverse transcriptase, TERT, that adds de novo TTAGGG repeats onto chromosome ends by copying an associated RNA molecule that acts as a template: the telomerase RNA component (TERC) (Blackburn et al. 2006). Although telomerase is active at early embryonic developmental stages, its expression is widely silenced after birth, thus leading to progressive telomere shortening in all cell types, including the stem cell compartments (Flores et al. 2008; Xie et al. 2015). In particular, telomeres gradually shorten throughout life span, which is associated with cell turnover and the “end replication problem” of linear chromosomes (Harley et al. 1990). This telomere attrition within stem cell compartments reduces their ability to renew tissues and self-renew and is proposed as a primary molecular cause of aging and the development of age-related diseases (López-Otín et al. 2013, 2023). The initial evidence that telomere shortening underlies age-related diseases and influences longevity came from studies on telomerase-deficient mice, which exhibit signs of premature aging in both low-proliferating (heart and brain) and high-proliferating (bone marrow, gut, skin, and testis) tissues that are aggravated with increasing mouse generations (Blasco et al. 1997; Lee et al. 1998; Herrera et al. 1999; Leri et al. 2003; Ferrón et al. 2004). Aging phenotypes in telomerase-deficient mice are anticipated across mouse generations due to inherited shorter telomeres, with the later generations showing an earlier onset of aging phenotypes and dying prematurely before reaching reproductive age (Lee et al. 1998; Herrera et al. 1999). The telomerase-deficient mouse model was the first to demonstrate genetic anticipation associated with telomerase deficiency and short telomeres.

In agreement with an essential role of telomerase and telomere maintenance in sustaining limitless cell division, telomere maintenance is a hallmark of cancer cells, as it allows for their indefinite cell proliferation capability (Hanahan and Weinberg 2000; Blackburn 2001; Cesare and Reddel 2010). Thus, telomerase is a highly mutated gene in many different cancer types (Allory et al. 2014; Huang et al. 2015; Barthel et al. 2017).

POT1 is the only shelterin component that binds to the single-strand (ss) region of telomere, the so-called G-strand overhang (Baumann and Cech 2001; Lei et al. 2004; Liu et al. 2004). POT1 binds telomeric ssDNA via both of the oligonucleotide/oligosaccharide-binding (OB) fold domains (OB1 and OB2) in its N terminus (Baumann and Cech 2001; Lei et al. 2004). Although human cells contain only one POT1 gene, mouse cells have two POT1 paralogs: Pot1a and Pot1b (Hockemeyer et al. 2006; Wu et al. 2006). The two mouse POT1 proteins are highly conserved and can associate with telomeric DNA, but whereas the Pot1b knockout (KO) mouse is viable, Pot1a deletion in mice is embryonic-lethal (Hockemeyer et al. 2006, 2007; He et al. 2009).

Several mechanisms have been proposed by which hPOT1 regulates telomere length. POT1 and its interacting partner, TPP1, have been shown to have roles in both recruiting and inhibiting telomerase access to telomeres (Loayza and De Lange 2003; Wang et al. 2007; He et al. 2009; Tejera et al. 2010; Nandakumar and Cech 2013). POT1 also has been proposed to regulate telomere length by recruitment of the CST complex that binds to telomeric DNA and regulates the fill-in synthesis of the telomeric C strand (Chen et al. 2012; Pinzaru et al. 2016, 2020).

In mice, POT1a and POT1b seem to have divergent roles in telomere maintenance. POT1b has been proposed to interact with TPP1 to promote telomerase recruitment to telomeres to elongate the telomeric G strand as well as C-strand fill-in through CST complex recruitment, whereas POT1a has been proposed to negatively regulate telomerase access to telomeres (Gu et al. 2021).

In agreement with a role for shelterin in regulating telomere length, components of the shelterin telomere protective complex have also been found mutated in several types of human sporadic or familial cancers as well as in the so-called telomeropathies or telomere syndromes (Shen et al. 2020b; Zade and Khattar 2023). In particular, mutations in POT1 have been found in mantle cell lymphoma, adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, parathyroid adenoma, colorectal cancer, pulmonary sarcomatoid carcinoma, sporadic and familial chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), familial melanoma, glioma, thyroid cancer, and Li–Fraumeni-like (LFL) syndrome families (Newey et al. 2012; Ramsay et al. 2013; Robles-Espinoza et al. 2014; Shi et al. 2014; Speedy et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2014; Bainbridge et al. 2015; Calvete et al. 2015; Kataoka et al. 2015; Chubb et al. 2016; Calvete et al. 2017; McMaster et al. 2018; Wong et al. 2019; Richard et al. 2020; Shen et al. 2020a,b). Mutations in POT1 found in CLL, melanoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, T-cell lymphoma, and LFL syndrome result in longer telomeres and are associated with higher chromosomal instability (Ramsay et al. 2013; Shi et al. 2014; Calvete et al. 2015; Pinzaru et al. 2016; Gu et al. 2017; McMaster et al. 2018), another hallmark of cancer (Hanahan and Weinberg 2000).

The telomeropathies or telomere syndromes include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), bone marrow failure, and dyskeratosis congenita, among others, which are characterized by presenting with very short telomeres (Blasco 2005; Armanios and Blackburn 2012; Holohan et al. 2014). Genetic anticipation in these syndromes results from progressive shortening of telomeres as the consequence of inheritance of both short telomeres and the allele carrying the causative mutation, analogous to the telomerase-deficient mouse model (Armanios and Blackburn 2012). Some of the shelterin components have also been found mutated in these telomere disorders, highlighting their importance in maintaining a proper telomere structure. In particular, TPP1 and TIN2 mutations have been found in patients with bone marrow failure, dyskeratosis congenita, Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndrome, and Revesz syndrome (Savage et al. 2008; Walne et al. 2008; Armanios and Blackburn 2012; Guo et al. 2014; Kocak et al. 2014). More recently, mutations in POT1 have been associated with IPF and dyskeratosis congenita (Kelich et al. 2022; Tummala et al. 2024).

However, the pathobiology of POT1 mutations in cancer and aging syndromes is largely unknown owing to the lack of in vivo animal models. To our knowledge, we generated the first and only humanized mouse model harboring a homologous POT1 mutation found in a cancer syndrome; namely, the Pot1aR117C knock-in mouse carrying the hPOT1R117C mutation associated with Li–Fraumeni like syndrome (Martínez et al. 2022). These Pot1aR117C mice carrying the p.R117C mutation in heterozygosis had longer telomeres and showed increased incidence of angiosarcomas, similar to the human carriers (Martínez et al. 2022).

Little is known about the pathological consequences of the human POT1 variants found in telomere aging-prone syndromes. In this work, we have generated the Pot1aL261S knock-in mouse harboring the homologous hPOT1-L259S mutation found in families with IPF (Kelich et al. 2022). We found that the POT1a-L261S mutant protein localizes to telomeres and binds to telomeric ssDNA with the same efficiency as the wild-type POT1a protein. Interestingly, the Pot1aL261S mutation in homozygosis results in shorter telomeres. In a manner similar to that of telomerase-deficient mice, the Pot1aL261S/L261S mice show several degenerative pathologies in the testes, intestines, and lungs, as well as a lower body weight and progressively shorter telomeres with successive mouse generations, indicating a role of telomerase in the short telomere phenotype of Pot1a mutant mice. At the mechanistic level, we found a stronger interaction between POT1a-L261S and both TPP1 and telomerase compared with the wild-type POT1a. Importantly, overexpression of telomerase in adult Pot1aL261S/L261S mice did not lead to telomere lengthening, suggesting that the POT1a-L261S mutant protein is blocking the activity of telomerase-elongating telomeres and acts as a dominant negative. Together, our findings support a model in which the POT1a-L261S mutant protein exerts a higher telomerase inhibitory effect at telomeres than wild-type POT1a; thus, the Pot1aL261S knock-in mouse phenocopies the telomerase knockout model.

To identify the potential amino acid homolog of the human shelterin POT1-L259S mutation associated with familial cases of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in mice, we performed homology alignments of the mouse POT1a and POT1b and the human POT1 protein sequences using the Align tool of UniProt. We found that human POT1-L259S is a conserved amino acid in mouse POT1a at position L261 and a conservative replacement for valine at position 260 in the case of POT1b (Fig. 1A). Thus, we set out to generate a knock-in mouse in the Pot1a locus carrying the homologous human POT1 mutation. To this end, we used CRISPR–Cas9 genome engineering (Adli 2018) in the presence of a single-strand oligo donor (ssODN) containing the L261S mutation as a template for homologous recombination in order to change the POT1a leucine at the 261 position to a serine amino acid and generate a mouse heterozygous for Pot1a-L261S (Fig. 1B,C). We then intercrossed the heterozygous Pot1a+/L261S mice to obtain first-generation (G1) Pot1aL261S/L261S homozygous mice.

To assess the potential consequences of the presence of the POT1a-L261S mutant protein, we generated mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) from Pot1a+/L261S mouse intercrosses to generate Pot1a+/+, Pot1a+/L261S, and G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S MEFs. Using Western blot protein analysis, we did not observe differences in the levels of the wild-type or POT1a-L261S proteins between Pot1a+/+ and Pot1aL261S/L261S MEFs, respectively, indicating a similar protein stability of wild-type and mutant POT1a (Fig. 1D).

POT1a has been shown previously to have a role in the regulation of telomere length (Gu et al. 2021). Thus, we measured telomere length in Pot1a+/+, Pot1a+/L261S, and G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S MEFs originated from two independent founders. To this end, we performed telomeric quantitative fluorescence in situ hybridization (Q-FISH) on interphase cells in which the amount of telomere fluorescence detected in individual telomere spots is proportional to telomere length. Interestingly, the mean telomere fluorescence intensity per nucleus did not differ between Pot1a+/+ and Pot1a+/L261S cells but was significantly decreased in Pot1aL261S/L261S compared with the Pot1a+/+ and Pot1a+/L261S MEFs (Fig. 1E). These results indicate that the Pot1aL261S mutation in homozygosis causes telomere shortening in the first mouse generation. We did not find significant differences between both independent founders (Fig. 1E). We confirmed this finding by using telomere restriction fragment (TRF) analysis, an independent technique to measure telomeres based on Southern blotting. We found a 30% decrease in the mean size of telomere fragments in G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S mutant MEFs (25–28 kb) compared with Pot1a+/+ wild-type controls (36–38 kb) (Fig. 1F), also demonstrating the presence of shorter telomeres. We did not observe any changes in the amount of single-stranded G overhangs between genotypes (Fig. 1F). These results clearly demonstrate that the Pot1aL261S mutation in homozygosis results in shorter telomeres compared with wild-type MEFs.

We next measured mean telomere length in white blood cells of Pot1a+/+, Pot1a+/L261S, and G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S young mice (10 weeks of age) originated from two independent founders. The homozygous Pot1aL261S/L261S mice from both founders showed significantly shorter telomeres compared with their wild-type counterparts (Fig. 1G). Of note, heterozygous Pot1a+/L261S mice also showed a trend to have shorter telomeres compared with wild-type mice, though the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1G). No differences in telomere length between male and female mice of the same genotype were detected (Fig. 1G).

Critically short and/or unprotected telomeres are known to induce a persistent DNA damage response at chromosome ends (Blasco et al. 1997; d'Adda di Fagagna et al. 2003). Thus, we next analyzed global genomic DNA damage by performing immunofluorescence of the γH2AX DNA damage marker. In agreement with the presence of critically short telomeres, we observed higher DNA damage amounts in G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S compared with Pot1a+/+ MEFs (Fig. 1H). We also analyzed DNA damage in Pot1a+/+ G1 Tert−/− MEFs and found similar global DNA damage levels in G1 Pot1aL261S/L261STert+/+ and Pot1a+/+ G1 Tert−/− MEFs, suggesting that the elevated γH2AX DNA damage in Pot1a knock-in MEFs could be attributed to shortened telomeres rather than dysfunctional unprotected telomeres associated with the Pot1aL261S/L261S mutation (Fig. 1H).

First-generation G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S mice were born at normal Mendelian ratios, indicating the absence of embryonic lethality (Fig. 2A). Using a breeding scheme previously reported by us (Blasco et al. 1997), we intercrossed G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S mice from founder 1 to generate successive generations until the fifth generation of these mutant mice (G2, G3, G4, and G5). No differences in litter size were detected between intercrosses of G1 and G2 Pot1L261S/L261S and heterozygous Pot1a+/L261S mice (Fig. 2B). However, there was a significant reduction in litter size when G3 and G4 Pot1L261S/L261S were intercrossed to generate G4 and G5, respectively, compared with intercrosses between heterozygous Pot1a+/L261S mice (Fig. 2B). We also analyzed the litter size from intercrosses of Pot1a+/+ G1–G2–G3 Tert−/− and heterozygous Pot1a+/+Tert+/− mice from an independent mouse colony and observed a significant reduction in the size of the litter originated from telomerase-deficient mice (Fig. 2B).

Interestingly, both Pot1aL261S/L261S males and females showed a decreased body weight compared with Pot1a+/+ mice under the same feeding conditions, though in females the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2C,D). This decreased body weight was not aggravated with increasing mouse generations (Fig. 2C–F). At 35 weeks of age, we observed a similar body weight reduction (10%) in the four generations of G1–G2–G3–G4 Pot1aL261S/L261S mice compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 2E–G). Of note, heterozygous Pot1a+/L261S male mice also showed a reduction in body weight compared with wild type (P = 0.06), suggesting that this Pot1a mutation might be acting as a dominant negative, therefore modelling the heterozygous condition of human POT1-L259S carriers (Fig. 2C,E).

Thus, we next set out to quantify chromosomal aberrations in Pot1a+/+
Tert+/+, G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S
Tert+/+, and G4 Pot1aL261S/L261S
Tert+/+. To this end, we performed telomere Q-FISH on metaphase spreads to visualize telomeres (Fig. 3D). In agreement with shorter telomeres in G1–G4 Pot1aL261S/L261S, we observed a significant increase in the incidence of signal-free ends (SFEs) and end-to-end chromosome fusions compared with wild-type MEFs (Fig. 3E,F). Interestingly, we detected the presence of diplochromosomes in Pot1aL261S carriers (Fig. 3G). This type of chromosome aberration has been observed previously upon deletion of Pot1a (Hockemeyer et al. 2006).

As we found shorter telomeres in Pot1aL261S/L261S MEFs compared with wild-type MEFs, we next set out to address whether Pot1aL261S/L261S mice also showed short telomeres, in analogy to the short telomeres found in IPF patients carrying the homologous human POT1-L259S mutation (Kelich et al. 2022). To this end, we measured telomere length by using telomeric Q-FISH in several mouse tissues including the lung, liver, and intestine of Pot1a+/+ and for increasing mouse generations (G1–G4) of Pot1aL261S/L261S mice at 10–12 weeks of age. We found progressively shorter telomeres in the lung, liver, and intestine of successive generations (G1–G4) of Pot1aL261S/L261S mice compared with age-matched Pot1a+/+ controls (Fig. 4A–D). We also measured telomere length in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from Pot1a+/+ and Pot1a+/L261S mice and in increasing mouse generations (G1–G3) of Pot1aL261S/L261S mice at 70–100 weeks of age by Q-FISH of interphase nuclei (Canela et al. 2007). We also found progressively shorter telomeres in successive generations of Pot1aL261S/L261S aged mice (Fig. 4E). Again, we observed a trend of shorter telomeres in heterozygous Pot1a+/L261S compared with Pot1a+/+ mice, pointing to the dominant negative nature of this mutation.

To address a potential role for telomerase in this short telomere phenotype, we generated double-mutant mice harboring the Pot1aL261S knock-in mutation and the Tert knockout mutations. We crossed G1 Pot1L261S/L261S
Tert+/+ and Pot1a+/+ G1 Tert−/− to generate double-heterozygous Pot1+/L261S
Tert+/− mice. These heterozygous mice were intercrossed to obtain the double-mutant mice—the Pot1a-KI Tert-KO mouse model. We bred up to generations 2 and 3 (G2 and G3) of Tert−/− mice harboring either the Pot1a wild-type or mutant allele in homozygosis. We analyzed telomere length in the lungs, liver, and intestines of Pot1a+/+G2 Tert−/− and G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S G2 Tert−/− mice to address whether the short telomere phenotype observed in Pot1aL261S carriers was telomerase-mediated. Indeed, no additive telomere shortening was observed when comparing Pot1a+/+ G2 Tert−/− mice with G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S G2 Tert−/− mice (Fig. 4F), supporting the notion that the POT1a-L261S mutant protein is defective in telomerase recruitment to telomeres and/or telomerase activity regulation at telomeres.

Tert+/+, Pot1a+/+ G3 Tert−/−, and G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S G3 Tert−/− mice at 30 weeks of age and measured telomere fluorescence. We found that the mean telomere fluorescence was significantly decreased in all the genotypes compared with wild-type cells (Fig. 4G). No significant differences were detected between males and females of the same genotype. Of note, no significant differences were detected among G3 Pot1aL261S/L261S

The presence of short and dysfunctional telomeres leads to a persistent DNA damage response at telomeres and increased DNA damage and senescence markers (d'Adda di Fagagna et al. 2003). Thus, we next analyzed telomeric DNA damage in Pot1a+/+
Tert+/+, G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S
Tert+/+, Pot1a+/+ G3 Tert−/−, and G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S G3 Tert−/− mice at 30 weeks of age by quantification of the so-called telomere damage-induced foci (TIFs), which were detected by immunocolocalization of the DNA damage marker 53BP1 and the TRF1 telomere-binding protein. We observed a significant increase of cells with TIFs in G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S
Tert+/+, Pot1a+/+ G3 Tert−/−, and G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S G3 Tert−/− compared with wild-type PBMCs (Fig. 4H). Of note, no differences in the amount of cells with TIFs were observed between Pot1a+/+ G3 Tert−/− and G1 Pot1aL261S/L261S G3 Tert−/−, indicating that the POT1a-L261 mutant protein does not confer additional telomere damage other than that generated from telomere shortening, thus supporting the epistatic relationship between Pot1aL261S mutation and telomerase deficiency.

Next, we sacrificed a group of—four to six healthy old (65–75 weeks of age) Pot1a+/+, G2 Pot1aL261S/L261S, G3 Pot1aL261S/L261S, and G3 Pot1a+/+ G3 Tert−/− mice to analyze the potential pathologies associated with expression of the POT1a-L261S mutant protein. We performed a full histopathological study and found an increased frequency of intestinal, lung, and testis degenerative pathologies in G3 Pot1a-L261S knock-in mice that was comparable with the pathologies observed in the G3 Tert knockout mice (Fig. 5A,B). As a control, none of the wild-type mice analyzed showed any pathological signs in these tissues. Degenerative intestinal pathologies were characterized by epithelial and glandular atrophy with shortened villi and/or a decreased number of glands and cystic hyperplasia as well as diffuse inflammatory reaction. The lungs presented BALT hyperplasia with hemosiderosis, inflammatory infiltration, and focal thickening of the alveolar septa. Testes showed low cellularity and maturation arrest in seminiferous tubules and in some instances focal testicular atrophy with the absence of germinal cells and an increase in Leydig or interstitial cells (Fig. 5B). No differences in age-associated pathologies were detected in the ovaries, liver, kidney, or spleen between G3 Pot1a-L261S knock-in and G3 Tert knockout mice compared with wild-type mice at this age. Atrophies of the intestinal epithelia, lung profibrotic pathologies, and defective spermatogenesis are frequent and life-threatening degenerative lesions associated with short telomeres in telomerase-deficient mice (Lee et al. 1998; Herrera et al. 1999; Piñeiro-Hermida et al. 2020). The fact that we found similar incidences of these pathologies in G3 Pot1a-L261S knock-in and G3 Tert knockout mice strongly supports a genetic interaction between the Pot1a-L261S mutation and telomerase deficiency.

We also analyzed global DNA damage by using the DNA damage marker γH2AX in the intestines and lungs of both genotypes (Fig. 5C,D). We found significantly higher DNA damage burden in the intestines of G3 Pot1a-L261S knock-in and G3 Tert knockout mice compared with wild-type mice and G2 Pot1a-L261S knock-in mice. The different outcomes in DNA damage burden between intestines and lungs likely reflect the proliferative nature of the former. We also examined the ability of the POT1a-L261S mutant protein to bind single-strand (ss) telomeric DNA in vitro. To this end, we incubated a biotinylated ss(TTAGGG)6 oligonucleotide with nuclear extracts of HEK293T cells expressing either HA-Pot1a or HA-Pot1aL261S alleles. We used streptavidin magnetic beads to pull down POT1a bound to the biotinylated oligonucleotide. The results show that POT1a-L261S binds telomeric ssDNA with the same affinity as the wild-type POT1a protein (Fig. 7D). Thus, the POT1a-L261S mutant protein binds with a normal affinity to both ds-telomeric and ss-telomeric DNA but with a higher affinity to the TPP1 shelterin protein.

POT1 has been proposed to associate with telomerase through TPP1 and to enhance telomerase processivity by strengthening its binding affinity to telomeric ssDNA (Wang et al. 2007; Zaug et al. 2010). However, recent high-resolution structures of telomerase bound to TPP1–POT1 have revealed that POT1 binds not only the DNA substrate but also TERT through its OB1 and OB2 domains, forming a gate in front of the telomerase active site and promoting telomeric repeat addition processivity (Sekne et al. 2022). To address whether the POT1a-L261S mutant protein is affected in telomerase binding, HEK293T were transfected with pBabe-MYC-mTert-U3-hTR-500, pACEM-mTpp1-FLAG, and pBabe harboring either wild-type Pot1a or mutant Pot1aL261S. The Terc–TERT–TPP1–POT1a complex was immunoprecipitated with anti-FLAG antibody, and the eluate was used to synthesize cDNA to assay for Terc RNA abundance. The eluate was also subjected to Western blot analysis to analyze the immunoprecipitated TPP1 as a control (Fig. 7E). We observed a 2.5-fold higher abundance of Terc in cells expressing the POT1a-L261S mutant protein, indicating a higher affinity for telomerase binding of the mutant POT1a protein (Fig. 7E). We then addressed telomerase activity in vitro by TRAP in Pot1aL261S/L261S and wild-type MEFs either overexpressing Tert or in the absence of overexpression. As a control, we also used telomerase-deficient cells. In vitro telomerase activity using nuclear extracts from Pot1aL261S/L261s cells was similar to that of wild-type cells in both conditions: with endogenous TERT levels and when TERT was overexpressed (Fig. 7F). These observations suggest a model in which the L261S missense substitution is affecting telomerase activity at telomeres in vivo.

Telomere shortening is one of the hallmarks of aging and has been proposed to be at the origin of different age-related diseases (López-Otín et al. 2023). In support of this notion, defects in telomere maintenance genes cause a group of premature aging diseases known as telomeropathies or telomere syndromes, including dyskeratosis congenita, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), aplastic anemia, Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndrome, Coats plus syndrome, and Revesz syndrome, which are characterized by presenting with very short telomeres (Blasco 2005; Armanios and Blackburn 2012; Holohan et al. 2014). These telomere syndromes are also characterized by presenting with genetic anticipation (Mitchell et al. 1999; Revy et al. 2023), which is when the onset of phenotypes associated with the genetic defect is anticipated with each generation of individuals. Progressive anticipation of phenotypes was also observed with increasing generations of the telomerase-deficient mouse model generated previously, leading to the discovery of these human diseases (Blasco et al. 1997). Indeed, mutations in telomerase and other telomere maintenance genes have been found to be associated with these diseases (Mitchell et al. 1999; Vulliamy et al. 2001; Yamaguchi et al. 2005; Tummala et al. 2022a,b, 2024; Revy et al. 2023; Kochman et al. 2024). Among these genes, mutations in the shelterin protein POT1 have been identified in Coats plus syndrome, IPF, and dyskeratosis congenita to date (Kelich et al. 2022; Tummala et al. 2024).

IPF is a lethal lung disease originated by damaging agents in the lungs, including smoking and pollution, among others. Its prevalence increases with age, suggesting that cellular and organismal aging is also at the origin of the disease (Martinez et al. 2017). Current pharmacological treatments are based on antifibrotic pathways but are not curative (Hunninghake 2014; King et al. 2014; Karimi-Shah and Chowdhury 2015). Among the telomere syndromes, IPF is the most common condition associated with telomere dysfunction in humans, and 8%–15% of familial cases have been linked to telomerase mutations in either TERT or TERC (Armanios et al. 2007; Alder et al. 2008). Recently, a mutation in human POT1 was described as associated with familial cases of IPF showing short telomeres; namely, the hPOT1-L259S mutation (Kelich et al. 2022). To understand the mechanism by which this mutation contributes to pulmonary fibrosis, we have generated the homozygous Pot1aL261S knock-in mouse harboring the homologous hPOT1-L259S mutation found in humans (Kelich et al. 2022). Of relevance, the Pot1aL261S mutation results in shorter telomeres in different mouse tissues, thus demonstrating that the short telomere phenotype found in human carriers is directly caused by the mutation in POT1. The Pot1a-L261S mutation also leads to shorter telomeres in heterozygosis though to a lesser extent than in homozygosis, indicating a dominant negative nature of the Pot1aL261S allele, as in the case of hPOT1-L259S mutation-carrying family members, where the disease shows genetic anticipation (Kelich et al. 2022). We studied this mutation in homozygosis because it leads to faster telomere shortening than could be expected in heterozygosis, where several more generations would be needed to reach the short telomere length. We also observed genetic anticipation in the Pot1a-L261S knock-in mouse model that shows progressively shorter telomeres and higher incidence of intestinal, lung, and testis degenerative pathologies in successive generations of mice. Progressive telomere shortening and earlier onset of associated aging phenotypes in successive generations of the telomerase-deficient mice have also been described (Blasco et al. 1997; Lee et al. 1998; Herrera et al. 1999; Leri et al. 2003; Ferrón et al. 2004). Indeed, the Pot1a-L261S knock-in mouse model generated in this work phenocopies the telomerase-deficient mouse, underscoring the telomerase-dependent telomere shortening observed in Pot1aL261S/L261S cells.

POT1 recruitment to telomeres requires its interaction with the TPP1 shelterin protein, and this is thought to enhance telomerase processivity by strengthening its affinity to single-stranded telomeric repeats (Baumann and Cech 2001; Lei et al. 2004; Ye et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2007; Zaug et al. 2010). However, recent high-resolution structures of telomerase bound to TPP1–POT1 have revealed that POT1 binds not only the DNA substrate but also TERT through its OB1 and OB2 domains, forming a gate in front of the telomerase active site promoting telomeric repeat addition processivity (Sekne et al. 2022). Mutations in TERT that disrupts its interaction with POT1 impair telomere elongation in vivo but not in vitro, indicating that interactions between POT1 and TERT are essential for effectively recruiting telomerase to telomeres in living cells (Sekne et al. 2022). We also found that in vitro telomerase activity using nuclear extracts from Pot1aL261S/L261s cells was unaffected in vitro. These observations suggest a model in which the L261S missense substitution is affecting telomerase recruitment and/or activity at telomeres in vivo. In mice, POT1b is involved in telomerase recruitment to telomeres, whereas POT1a acts as a negative regulator by limiting telomerase access to telomeres (Gu et al. 2021). Because the Pot1aL261S knock-in mouse model has an intact POT1b, it is likely that the mechanism underlying POT1a-L261S-mediated telomere shortening is an enhanced inhibition of telomerase activity at telomeres. Indeed, we found here that POT1a-L261S binds to TPP1 and to telomerase with higher affinity than POT1a wild type while showing similar ssDNA binding capacity. Furthermore, we showed that telomerase overexpression cannot compensate the short telomere phenotype of Pot1a mutants. These results strongly indicate that the POT1a-L261S mutant protein impedes telomerase activity at telomeres and suggest that this mutation might be acting as a dominant negative. Further biochemical and functional assays are needed to further understand the effects of the Pot1a-L261S mutation on telomere shortening. Importantly, our findings suggest that IPF patients carrying the POT1-L259S mutation would not benefit from telomerase activation therapies, thus highlighting the importance of functional studies in models of the disease to develop effective treatments.

Understanding how POT1 mutations cooperate to regulate telomerase and telomere maintenance is instrumental to elucidate how POT1 mutations lead to cancer and telomere syndromes and to develop tailored therapies for these human diseases.

The knock-in for Pot1aL261S was generated by CRISPR–Cas9 genome engineering (Adli 2018) in the presence of a single-strand oligo donor (ssODN) containing the L261S mutation as a template for homologous recombination by Charles River (https://www.criver.com). Briefly, cells were lysed with CHAPS buffer, and the soluble fractions were freshly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Cell extracts were incubated with telomeric primers for a 60 min initial extension step at 30°C. The extended reaction was subjected to PCR amplification (25 cycles of 30 sec at 94°C, 30 sec at 59°C, and 30 sec at 72°C) in the presence of 32P end-labeled telomeric primer. The PCR reactions were resolved in 8% polyacrylamide and 7 M urea gel electrophoresis in 0.5× TBE buffer. The gel was dried, exposed to a phosphorimager, and scanned with a Typhoon scanner.

Tissue samples were fixed in 10% buffered formalin, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin wax, and sectioned at 2.5 µm. Tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a series of graded ethanol until water and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin for pathological examination. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on deparaffinized tissue sections processed with 10 mM sodium citrate (pH 6.5) and cooked under pressure for 2 min. IHC staining of tissue sections was performed with phospho-Histone H2AX (ser139) (Millipore 05-636), Ki67 (Cell Signaling Technology 12202), and smooth muscle actin (SMA) (DAKO IR611). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and analyzed by light microscopy. The quantitative analysis of the images was performed blindly using QuPath (Bankhead et al. 2017).

MEFs and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were plated in poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips, fixed for 10 min in 4% buffered formaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% PBS–Triton for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h. Samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with mouse anti-γH2A.X-Ser139 (1:2000; Millipore 05-636) or with rabbit anti-53BP1 (Novus Biologicals NB100-304). Cells were then washed and incubated with goat antimouse Alexa 555 (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific A21424) for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were stained with DAPI. Fluorescent signals were visualized in an Opera Phenix Plus high-content screening system. Fluorescence signal was quantified using Harmony software.

HEK293T cells were cotransfected with either pBabe-HA-Pot1a-6xHis, pBabe-HA-Pot1a-L261S-6xHis, or empty vector. Cells were treated for 1 min with pre-extraction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8, 50 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.5% Triton, 300 mM sucrose) and then fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min. Slides were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with rat anti-HA (Roche 11867423001) and mouse anti-TRF1 (Abcam ab10579). Cells were washed with PBS–Tween 0.2% and incubated with donkey antirat Alexa 555 (Invitrogen A48270) or goat antimouse Alexa 488 (Invitrogen A11029), respectively, for 1 h at room temperature in a humid chamber. Samples were mounted in ProLong Gold with DAPI (Invitrogen). Fluorescent signals were visualized in a SP8-STED superresolution confocal microscope (Leica). HA-POT1a and TRF1 signals were quantified using LAS X(Leica).

For quantitative telomere fluorescence in situ hybridization (Q-FISH), paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and fixed with 4% formaldehyde, followed by digestion with pepsin/HCl and a second fixation with 4% formaldehyde. Slides were dehydrated with increasing concentrations of EtOH (70%, 90%, and 100%) and incubated with the telomeric (TTAGGG) probe labeled with Cy3 for 3 min at 85°C followed by 1 h at room temperature in a wet chamber. The slides were washed extensively with 50% formamide and 0.08% TBS–Tween 20. Confocal microscopy was performed at room temperature with a laser-scanning microscope (Leica TSC SP8) using a plan apo 63 Å, 1.40 NA oil immersion objective (HCX). Maximal projection of z-stack images generated using advanced fluorescence software (LAS) was analyzed with Leica application suite X (LAS X). The DAPI images were used to detect telomeric signals inside each nucleus. When indicated, GFP RNAscope in situ hybridization was performed before telomeric Q-FISH analysis according to the manufacturer's instructions (ACD Bio, https://acdbio.com).

High-throughput (HT)-Q-FISH on MEFs and on PBMCs was done as described previously (Canela et al. 2007). Confocal microscopy was performed with an Opera Phenix Plus high-content screening system. Telomere signal was quantified using Harmony software. The DAPI images were used to detect telomeric signals inside each nucleus.

MEF cells were incubated with 0.1 µg/mL colcemide for 4 h. After hypotonic swelling in 0.03 M sodium citrate for 30 min at 37°C, cells were fixed in methanol:acetic acid (3:1). Quantitative telomere fluorescence in situ hybridization (Q-FISH) was performed as described previously (Samper et al. 2001). Images were captured using a Leica DM6B microscope with a 100× oil objective. Telomere length was analyzed using Leica application suite X. The incidence of chromosomal aberrations per metaphase was determined by eye. The images were analyzed blindly.
