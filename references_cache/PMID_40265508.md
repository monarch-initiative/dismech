# Mirtazapine Is Associated With Shorter Hospital Stay in Pediatric Avoidant Restrictive Food Intake Disorder-A Retrospective Chart Review.
**Authors:** Chishti A, Groh RE, Maginot TR, Adler L, Shott ME, Kaye WH, Frank GKW
**Journal:** Int J Eat Disord (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/eat.24449](https://doi.org/10.1002/eat.24449)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Avoidant restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is a relatively new diagnostic entity, and no medication has been approved. Recent data suggest that mirtazapine, a tetracyclic antidepressant, could improve outcomes in ARFID. We conducted a retrospective chart review to examine the effects of mirtazapine on ARFID treatment in youth.

METHODS: Hospital records from 87 children and adolescents with ARFID treated in an inpatient medical stabilization program were analyzed. Treatment outcome was tested for the effects of mirtazapine on weight gain, length of hospitalization, and duration of nasogastric feeding tube versus orally delivered nutrition. Other medications prescribed (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, dopamine D2 receptor partial agonist aripiprazole, and dopamine D2 receptor antagonists) and comorbid disorders (depression and anxiety) were included in the analysis.

RESULTS: Age was similar between individuals treated with or without mirtazapine (12.7 ± 1.0 years vs. 13.1 ± 1.0 years). Thirty-eight (44%) patients with ARFID were treated with mirtazapine. MANOVA indicated significant effects of mirtazapine (Wilks' λ = 0.741, F = 3.322, p = 0.007, partial η 2 = 0.26, power = 0.908) and being associated with shorter hospital stays (F = 10.041, p = 0.002, partial η 2 = 0.14) and a lower number of nasogastric feeding tube days (F = 6.202, p = 0.015, partial η 2 = 0.09). Mirtazapine treatment was associated with a faster rate of weight gain (F = 4.121, p = 0.046, partial η 2 = 0.05).

DISCUSSION: In our highly controlled environment, all patients reached a comparable target weight, but patients treated with mirtazapine did so faster. Further research should investigate the clinical effects and underlying mechanisms of mirtazapine in patients with ARFID.

Avoidant restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is a relatively new diagnosis within the Feeding and Eating Disorders category of the DSM‐5 (American Psychiatric Association 2013). ARFID is a complex and multifaceted syndrome characterized by an apparent lack of interest in eating or food, avoidance based on the sensory characteristics of food, or concern about the aversive consequences of eating. ARFID is associated with significant weight loss or failure to achieve expected weight gain, nutritional deficiency, dependence on enteral feeding or oral nutritional supplements, marked interference with psychosocial functioning, or a combination of those. The DSM‐5 and follow‐up research have suggested subtypes characterized by fear or concern about aversive consequences of eating, lack of interest in eating or food, avoidance based on the sensory characteristics of food, and a mixed subtype (Sanchez‐Cerezo et al. 2024). Reported prevalence of ARFID varies widely from 1.5% to 64% within eating disorder populations, while comorbidity with anxiety and depressive disorders is high (Eddy et al. 2019; Zickgraf et al. 2019; Kambanis et al. 2020). Furthermore, studies in adults who are gastroenterology patients or seeking gastroenterological care have shown that ARFID symptoms are present in between 5% and 30% of patients (Burton Murray et al. 2022; Murray et al. 2020). This overlap applies to the pediatric population as well; one retrospective study found that ARFID symptoms were present in 23% of pediatric neuro‐gastroenterology patients (Murray et al. 2022), and another recent study reported ARFID symptoms in 42% of youth who were treated in a gastroenterology clinic for disorders of gut‐brain interaction (Matherne et al. 2025). The recent description of disorders of gut‐brain interaction (DGBIs), previously known as functional gastrointestinal disorders, highlights the possibility of a shared pathophysiology across different organ systems that could be targeted with medications (Barbara et al. 2025).

Current therapeutic interventions include cognitive‐behavioral and family‐based psychotherapy, including desensitization to avoided foods in the outpatient setting (Kambanis et al. 2020). However, when the illness is severe, treatment may include inpatient medical stabilization and nutritional rehabilitation using a nasogastric feeding tube. Especially, a multidisciplinary approach shows promise in treating individuals with ARFID (Crone et al. 2023).

The neurobiology of ARFID remains unclear, and no medication has been approved for its treatment. Despite this lack of approved medications, patients with ARFID are frequently treated with psychoactive medications, while research on psychopharmacological treatments in this population is lacking (Lin et al. 2021; Brewerton and D'Agostino 2017; Muratore and Attia 2022). One medication of interest is the tetracyclic antidepressant mirtazapine, which improves low appetite, decreases nausea and vomiting, and supports gastric emptying, typical associated symptoms of ARFID (Bhattacharjee et al. 2019; Iglesias‐Escabi et al. 2022; Lalani et al. 2023). A hypothesis‐driven article comparing psychoactive medications and their potential use in ARFID suggested that mirtazapine could be an effective treatment in this population (Gray et al. 2018). Mirtazapine is an antagonist at serotonin 2A, 2C, and 3, alpha‐1 and 2A adrenergic, and histamine H1 receptors, and is an agonist at kappa opioid receptors (Wishart et al. 2018). Mirtazapine leads to the release of norepinephrine and serotonin, neurotransmitters that affect mood and anxiety, while its kappa opioid receptor agonism has analgesic effects (Wishart et al. 2018; Anttila and Leinonen 2001). Mirtazapine is FDA‐approved for adults with major depressive disorder. It is often used off‐label for insomnia, anxiety, post‐traumatic stress, or obsessive‐compulsive disorders due to its sedative, antiemetic, anxiolytic, and appetite‐stimulant effects (Jilani et al. 2024). A few case reports have been published that suggested that mirtazapine could be beneficial for ARFID treatment (Naguy et al. 2023; Naviaux 2019). Other small studies have investigated the second‐generation antipsychotic olanzapine (Wishart et al. 2018), reporting benefits for weight restoration and improvement in anxiety and depression (Brewerton and D'Agostino 2017; Dolman et al. 2021). A retrospective chart review on serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) with and without added hydroxyzine showed both approaches had comparable outcomes at the end of treatment (Mahr et al. 2022). Taken together, research on psychopharmacological interventions in ARFID is largely lacking.

Based on the promise of the theoretical model and our positive clinical experience with mirtazapine in the ARFID population, we have started using this medication as a first‐line intervention, while informing patients and their families that the FDA has not approved the medication for ARFID and that it would be used off‐label based on clinical experience (Gray et al. 2018). In this study, we conducted a retrospective chart review on pediatric patients with ARFID who were treated in our specialized eating disorder medical stabilization unit to further test the hypothesis that mirtazapine would improve treatment outcomes in ARFID, consistent with the previous case reports.

The University of California San Diego Institutional Review Board and Rady Children's Hospital approved the search of electronic patient records from 2017 to 2022 to identify pediatric patients who had been treated for ARFID in our specialized eating disorder inpatient Medical Behavior Unit. This multidisciplinary inpatient program that delivers services by adolescent medicine, dietary services, psychiatry, and psychology provides medical stabilization for children, adolescents, and young adults with eating disorders. Patients are carefully assessed and treated for general medical and psychiatric diagnoses and attend individual and group therapy sessions. The clinical assessment by a psychologist, psychiatrist, or both determines eating disorder diagnosis such as ARFID and likely subtype. On admission to the program, the dietician determines a treatment target weight based on age and the previous BMI percentile that the patient followed over time. The meal plan and total calories are calculated to target an approximately 200‐g daily weight gain. Patients who cannot complete their nutritional intake by mouth with solid food or liquid supplement for 24 h are placed a nasogastric tube for liquid nutrition. The amount of solid food versus food taken as a liquid supplement is calculated daily based on the required total caloric intake. Criteria for inpatient admission are significantly reduced food intake leading to medical instability, including low heart rate below normal values for age, severe orthostatic hypotension, or laboratory abnormalities that require inpatient admission to prevent harm. Patients are discharged when they meet the criteria for medical stability and do not require tube feeding.

Charts were selected for children and adolescents if patients were under 20 years of age per WHO definition (Canadian Paediatric Society 2003) and had a stay of at least 10 days. This time frame was chosen to allow for measurable behavior change after the establishment of steady‐state blood level condition for medication prescribed (four to five half‐lives).

Chart extraction included the following information: age, length of inpatient stay, height, weight trajectory over the course of the hospital stay, total and percent of nasogastric feeding tube (NG) days throughout the stay, percent solid food intake at discharge, and medications prescribed. Height and weight data were transformed into an age‐adjusted Z‐score of BMI based on the CDC calculator (CDC 2016). Diagnoses were also extracted based on psychiatric and psychological diagnostic assessments on admission. A clinical psychologist reviewed consults completed by psychology staff on the unit. The patients were categorized into three subtypes: sensory sensitivity, lack of interest in eating, and fear of aversive consequences of eating. Since many participants exhibited overlapping features, the primary subtype was determined based on the factor that led to hospitalization. For example, if a patient had a history of picky eating but later developed a fear of eating, the latter was designated as the primary subtype since it was the main cause of hospitalization.

Age was assessed using ANOVA comparing individuals on or off medication. A mixed model group‐byby‐condition analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on the Z‐score of age‐adjusted BMI pre‐ and post‐treatment for the effects of mirtazapine and other medication and comorbid conditions. The chi‐square test was used to assess group differences in comorbidity.

The effects of mirtazapine on the remaining outcome variables were first analyzed using multivariate ANOVA in individuals treated with mirtazapine only compared to no‐medication controls, including comorbid conditions as factors in the model. Second, we conducted a multivariate ANOVA that included all individuals and medications prescribed and comorbid condition effects as factors in the model. Outcome variables were the length of stay, total NG days, % NG days of total hospitalization days, and percent solid foods consumed on discharge. Independent variables of interest aside from mirtazapine were other medications prescribed (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs], the partial dopamine D2 receptor agonist aripiprazole, and dopamine D2 receptor antagonist antipsychotics). Comorbid conditions, depression, and anxiety were also included as factors in the model. Partial eta squared was calculated to indicate effect size (0.01 small, 0.06 medium, 0.14 large effect).

The chart review selection process yielded 87 youth with ARFID (Figure S1). Of these, 58 (67%) identified as female gender. Thirty‐six (41%) patients were non‐Hispanic/White, 41 (47%) Hispanic/Latinx, 2 (2%) Asian, 4 (5%) Black, 1 was of American Indian/Alaskan Native race, and for 3 individuals, race was not recorded. Sociodemographic variables were not recorded.

With respect to the ARFID subtype, 51 (59%) patients were of the primarily fear of aversive consequences subtype, 25 (29%) of the lack of interest in food or eating subtype, 7 (8%) of the sensory sensitivity subtype, and for 4 (4%) no specific subtype was determined due to lack of predominance of one subtype (mixed subtype).

Twenty‐two (25%) patients were on no medication, 38 (44%) were treated with and discharged on mirtazapine 29 [77%] of which were started on mirtazapine in the hospital), 8 (9%) with aripiprazole (2 [25%] started in the hospital), 17 (20%) with other antipsychotics olanzapine or risperidone (2 [12%] started in the hospital), and 28 (32%) with the SSRIs escitalopram, fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, or sertraline (9 [32%] started in the hospital). Of patients on SSRIs, three were on mirtazapine and another antipsychotic, one on mirtazapine and an SSRI, two patients were on mirtazapine and an SSRI and aripiprazole, and nine patients were on an SSRI and another antipsychotic. Ten patients were prescribed hydroxyzine and three lorazepam on an as‐needed basis.

Comorbid conditions observed were major depressive and anxiety (generalized and social) disorders, which were distributed similarly across medication groups. See Table 1 and supplemental material for a detailed prescription.

Age was similar between individuals treated with versus without mirtazapine (12.7 ± 1.0 years vs. 13.1 ± 1.0 years, F = 0.047, p = 0.829), SSRIs (13.0 ± 1.1 years vs. 12.7 ± 0.9 years, F = 0.045, p = 0.832), aripiprazole (12.6 ± 1.5 years vs. 13.1 ± 0.7 years, F = 0.096, p = 0.794), or other antipsychotic (12.8 ± 1.1 years vs. 13.0 ± 1.0 years, F = 0.020, p = 0.889), and was not included in the model for medication or comorbidity effects. All individuals were of cis‐gender sex, and sex distribution was similar across individuals treated with (25/13 female/male ratio) versus without (33/16 female/male ratio) mirtazapine (χ
2 = 0.023, p = 0.879). Subtype distribution was also similar between individuals treated with (sensory sensitivity subtype n = 3, fear of negative consequences subtype n = 23, lack of interest in food or eating subtype n = 10, mixed subtype n = 2) versus without (sensory sensitivity subtype n = 4, fear of negative consequences subtype n = 28, lack of interest in food or eating subtype n = 15, mixed subtype n = 2) mirtazapine (χ
2 = 1.302, p = 0.729).

The mean dose administered for mirtazapine was 11.25 ± 4.4 mg, ranging from 3.75 mg to 15 mg. The dose was not associated with discharge BMI percentile or other outcome measures.

The mixed model ANOVA comparing age‐adjusted BMI Z‐score before and after treatment was not significant by medication intervention (mirtazapine F = 1.078. p = 0.303; SSRI F = 0.014, p = 0.908; aripiprazole F = 1.555, p = 0.287, other antipsychotic F = 0.783, p = 0.380), or for the effects of comorbidity (major depression F = 0.142, p = 0.708; anxiety disorder F = 0.371, p = 0.545).

MANOVA in individuals treated with mirtazapine only (n = 32) compared to patients on no medication (major depression and anxiety included in the model) showed a significant effect of drug (Wilks' λ = 0.501, F = 1.598, p = 0.005, partial η
2 = 0.13, power = 0.947). Post hoc analysis indicated a lower length of stay (p = 0.027) and number of total NG tube feeding days (p = 0.002) for mirtazapine treatment. Major depressive disorder in the no‐medication group versus the mirtazapine group was associated with longer hospitalization (p < 0.001) and total (p < 0.001) and percent (p = 0.017) NG tube feeding days.

The multivariate ANOVA on all individuals with ARFID testing the effects of mirtazapine on length of stay, age‐adjusted BMI Z‐score difference, or NG tube feeding days, and including other medications and comorbidity in the model yielded the following results. Treatment with mirtazapine showed a statistically significant multivariate result (Wilks' λ = 0.741, F = 3.322, p = 0.007, partial η
2 = 0.26, power = 0.908). Treatment with aripiprazole also yielded a statistically significant multivariate result (Wilks' λ = 0.702, F = 4.039, p = 0.002, partial η
2 = 0.30, power = 0.958). SSRI (Wilks' λ = 0.836, F = 1.862, p = 0.103, η
2 = 0.16) or other antipsychotic (Wilks' λ = 0.943, F = 0.574, p = 0.749, η
2 = 0.06) medication, and comorbid major depressive (Wilks' λ = 0.879, F = 1.105, p = 0.373, η
2 = 0.12) or anxiety disorder (Wilks' λ = 0.962, F = 0.316, p = 0.944, η
2 = 0.04) were not significantly associated with the outcome measures in the multivariate analysis.

Estimated marginal means analysis (Table 1) indicated that mirtazapine was associated with shorter length of stay and lower number of total NG tube feeding days; aripiprazole was associated with both higher total and percentage of NG tube feeding days. A post hoc analysis on the rate of weight gain indicated significantly higher rate of weight gain in the mirtazapine treated group (0.12 kgday) versus patients not on mirtazapine (0.08 kgday, F = 4.121, p = 0.046, partial η
2 = 0.05, power = 0.518).

The results of this retrospective chart review suggest that treatment with mirtazapine is associated with reduced length of inpatient stay and total NG tube feeding days throughout a patient's stay. While the data are derived from a retrospective chart review and need replication in a randomized controlled trial, the study suggests that mirtazapine's mechanism of action targets pathophysiology that is relevant to ARFID and could be beneficial for treating the disorder.

Individuals with ARFID are frequently treated with psychoactive medication despite uncertain effects (Lin et al. 2021). Similarly, it is unclear to what extent comorbid conditions affect short‐term outcomes (Kambanis et al. 2020). In our sample, most patients were treated with psychoactive medication. At the same time, only a minority had a comorbid major depressive or anxiety disorder, indicating that medication was prescribed to treat ARFID behaviors.

Patients in all groups reached comparable discharge BMI Z‐scores gain, and there were no significant differences in the change in BMI Z‐scores between individuals who were taking mirtazapine and those who did not. We expect that this is due to a ceiling effect of weight gain in a highly structured program that includes NG feeding support. However, patients who were administered mirtazapine reached their target weight faster. All patients in our program go through the same standard program that includes multidisciplinary assessments and behavioral treatment, and we also standardize medication treatment as much as possible with mirtazapine as a first‐line agent for ARFID that could be added to other medications. Most patients on mirtazapine were started on the medication when starting treatment in our program, whereas other medications most often had been prescribed already before admission. We do not prescribe mirtazapine based on specific ARFID presentations, such as subtype. To avoid reporting or rater bias, we used objective outcome criteria, including age‐adjusted BMI and days on NG tube, as primary outcome measures.

The lower number of hospital days for the mirtazapine group suggests that the medication facilitated appetite increase and weight gain over untreated individuals. Research indicates that mirtazapine has metabolic effects that promote eating and weight gain, and we expect that those effects have played a role in this study (Laimer et al. 2006). Total NG tube feeding days were fewer in the mirtazapine group, while the percentage of NG tube feeding days was similar to that of the no‐mirtazapine‐treated individuals. Mirtazapine is a serotonin, alpha‐adrenergic, and histamine receptor antagonist and leads to the release of norepinephrine and serotonin, neurotransmitters that affect mood and anxiety, while its kappa opioid receptor agonism has analgesic effects (Wishart et al. 2018; Anttila and Leinonen 2001). An overall faster transition to food intake by mouth might be related to the anxiolytic effects of mirtazapine. Anecdotally, our patients reported reduced anxiety and improved mood, but whether those effects contributed to the normalization of food intake and weight gain requires further study. Mirtazapine has also alleviated functional dyspepsia and reduced gastrointestinal symptoms (Jiang et al. 2016; Carbone et al. 2017). It is, therefore, possible that mirtazapine could have reduced gastrointestinal discomfort in our sample and supported food intake by reducing anxiety and pain and the sensation of fullness in response to eating (Matherne et al. 2025).

Most patients on medication other than mirtazapine were already on those on admission, and mirtazapine was added due to the ineffectiveness of those other medications. Starting an SSRI or antipsychotic in the hospital was due to comorbid major depressive disorder or anxiety interfering with daily life but unrelated to the ARFID diagnosis. Added antipsychotic medication was prescribed for mood dysregulation‐associated behavior problems.

The comparison of the subsample of patients treated with mirtazapine only versus no medication supported the results in the larger sample and further highlighted the importance of untreated major depressive disorder on ARFID outcome. The results indicate that clinicians should diagnose and treat comorbid depression in this population to facilitate recovery.

In the full sample, neither SSRIs, other antipsychotics, nor depression or anxiety disorder were associated with the ARFID outcome measures. Patients treated with aripiprazole, on the other hand, had more total NG days and % NG days, while the length of stay was not different compared to the untreated group. That result was unexpected, given that the partial dopamine receptor agonist aripiprazole has shown some promise in anorexia nervosa (Frank et al. 2017). While the aripiprazole‐treated group had more NG days, this group was less underweight compared to the no‐aripiprazole comparison individuals. Aripiprazole was used in patients who had higher emotion dysregulation and more oppositional behaviors. Thus, this sample was anecdotally more severe for externalizing behaviors. Aripiprazole is associated with increased nausea and other gastrointestinal symptoms as part of possible adverse drug effects, and we expect that higher oppositionality, together with a lack of metabolic and appetite‐stimulating factors, contributed to the result.

Retrospective chart reviews highlight associations but are not as rigorous as randomized controlled trials and thus cannot allow for conclusions to be drawn about causation. Neither the effects of SSRI or other antipsychotic medications nor comorbid major depression or anxiety disorder were significant in the full sample multivariate ANOVA, which may be due to the small sample sizes of subgroups. The decision to start mirtazapine was based on the patient and parent decision and their perception of whether this medication could be beneficial, while the retrospective chart review could not reliably determine reasons for accepting or declining the medication. Due to the overlap in medication and comorbid conditions across the sample, we analyzed medication and comorbidity effects as individual factors in the model. Some patients took as‐needed medication, and their effects could not be determined in this analysis. While the literature describes frequent co‐occurrence of gastroenterological disorders in the ARFID population, our patients tend to be admitted after the exclusion of such comorbid conditions that would explain weight loss and malnutrition, and chart data extraction did not provide reliable data. Patients' charts indicated anecdotally increased tiredness on mirtazapine or, at times, more vivid dreams on mirtazapine, but the retrospective study design did not allow for a systematic side effects analysis. While there were significant results for aripiprazole, indicating more total and percent NG tube feeding days when on the medication, the sample was too small to draw firm conclusions as to the underlying mechanisms. While it is possible that aripiprazole has less beneficial metabolic effects important for ARFID treatment, it is further possible that the medication was prescribed to particularly severe patients who had failed other interventions. We included in the statistical model also the effects of anxiety and depression. Depression was associated with worse outcomes in the unmedicated ARFID group. The result could indicate that depression interferes with ARFID treatments and requires treatment, but such a link needs further study. Additionally, this retrospective chart review was conducted on patients treated in an inpatient unit. Given such patients' added complexity and comorbidities, these findings may not be generalizable to less severely ill patients with ARFID. Four individuals with ARFID were diagnosed with ADHD on discharge (previously diagnosed), and one individual was diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder together with previously diagnosed ADHD. Distribution of ADHD was not significantly different between groups (χ
2 = 0.595, p = 0.441) and whether ADHD or autism spectrum disorder affects mirtazapine effectiveness cannot be determined from this study. All patients were medically cleared before admission to our program to rule out organic reasons that would explain the eating problem, and none of the patients in this sample was diagnosed with a gastrointestinal disorder on discharge. However, it is possible that secondary medical factors could have played a role in patients' symptom presentation. ARFID subtypes have not been formally recognized, and the retrospective chart review did not allow for a more structured assessment such as using the Nine Item ARFID Screen (Burton Murray et al. 2021). While we included potential confounds in our analysis, there could have been variables not measured that could have contributed to the observed findings.

In conclusion, this retrospective chart review supports the hypothesis that adolescent patients with ARFID who are prescribed mirtazapine during an inpatient stay show improved short‐term outcomes. These outcomes include shorter lengths of stay and higher rates of weight gain compared to patients who do not receive this medication. Further controlled research is needed to better understand the mechanism and potential benefits of mirtazapine as a valuable treatment option for pediatric patients diagnosed with ARFID.
