Infantile spasms are age-specific seizures of infantile epileptic 
encephalopathies that are usually associated with poor epilepsy and 
neurodevelopmental outcomes. The current treatments are not always effective and 
may be associated with significant side effects. Various mechanisms have been 
proposed as pathogenic for infantile spasms, including cortical or brainstem 
dysfunction, disruption of normal cortical-subcortical communications, genetic 
defects, inflammation, stress, developmental abnormalities. Many of these have 
been recently tested experimentally, resulting into the emergence of several 
animal models of infantile spasms. The stress theory of spasms yielded the 
corticotropin releasing hormone (CRH)-induced model, which showed the higher 
proconvulsant potency of CRH in developing rats, although only limbic seizures 
were observed. Models of acute induction of infantile spasms in rodents include 
the N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) model of emprosthotonic seizures, the prenatal 
betamethasone and prenatal stress variants of the NMDA model, and the 
γ-butyrolactone induced spasms in a Down's syndrome mouse model. Chronic rodent 
models of infantile spasms include the tetrodotoxin model and the multiple-hit 
models in rats, as well as two genetic mouse models of interneuronopathies with 
infantile spasms due to loss of function of the aristaless X-linked 
homeobox-related gene (ARX). This review discusses the emerging mechanisms for 
generation of infantile spasms and their associated chronic epileptic and 
dyscognitive phenotype as well as the recent progress in identifying pathways to 
better treat this epileptic encephalopathy.

West syndrome is defined by the trial of clusters of axial epileptic flexion or extension tonic spasms first appearing in infants (infantile spasms (IS)), psychomotor impairment, and a classical interictal electroencephalographic pattern called hypsarrhythmia [1, 2]. Electrographically, IS often demonstrate an ictal electrodecremental response (EDR), showing sudden attenuation of the background activities. The classical interictal pattern of hypsarrhythmia consists of high amplitude, chaotic background with multifocal epileptic activities. However, in many infants with IS, modified hypsarrhythmic patterns or simply multifocally epileptic background may be seen instead [2, 3]. This raises the possibility that certain pathogenetic elements of IS and hypsarrhythmia may not be completely identical. IS can co-exist or evolve into other types of seizures and epilepsies, including Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, which may be difficult to treat. Most importantly, the cognitive and neurodevelopmental outcomes can be significant and greatly affect the quality of life of these patients and their families. A majority of patients with IS have intellectual disabilities whereas the risk for autism may be increased, especially if IS are due to underlying structural/metabolic pathologies [4].

The first line treatments for IS include adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and high dose steroids, as well as vigabatrin, a GABA aminotransferase inhibitor, which has a special indication for patients with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) [5–7]. Treatment response is better in patients with IS of unknown etiology especially if treated early with these drugs [5, 7–9]. However, a significant percentage of the responders are still affected by considerable residual cognitive and neurodevelopmental deficits whereas in others spasms and seizures may recur.

There are currently more than 200 possible etiologies associated with human IS, ranging from structural, metabolic, unknown etiologies or genetic causes [10, 11]. Among the commonest causes are perinatal hypoxic-ischemic injury, TSC and cortical malformations. The underlying pathologies range from grossly normal for age neuroimaging studies to significant brain pathologies, including hydranencephaly, in which both cerebral hemispheres can be absent [12]. These suggest that the basic network required for the expression of IS is preserved across these pathologies and can be pathologically activated by each of the associated underlying etiologies. Several theories have therefore emerged on the underlying pathogenesis of IS, implicating brainstem structures, cortical regions, dysregulation of cortical-subcortical connectivity, specific genetic deficits, stress mechanisms, autoimmune or infectious processes, specific signaling pathways, or asynchronous pathologic development of the brain (developmental desynchronization). Many of these have been tested in the experimental arena leading to the recent development of animal models of IS. Here we will review the existing models of IS and discuss what they reveal about the mechanisms triggering IS, their associated epilepsy and dyscognitive features, their electrographic characteristics and target pathways that could be useful in the development of new therapies.

According to an NINDS/NIH-sponsored workshop on Models of Pediatric Epilepsies (Bethesda, 2004), the criteria for an ideal model of West syndrome include (a) unprovoked spasms or myoclonic seizures early in postnatal life; (b) ictal decremental responses associated with spasms; (c) interictal hypsarrhythmia pattern; (d) response to drugs that are used in human IS (ACTH and/or vigabatrin); (e) behavioral and/or cognitive sequelae [13]. Although these criteria summarize the main features of West syndrome, it is not certain that all are required features of an animal model of West syndrome. For instance, accepting only animal models with IS that are responsive to current therapies may hinder us from identifying new therapies for drug-refractory IS. Also, hypsarrhythmia is a dramatic and bad prognostic feature of human West syndrome, but many infants with IS may have abnormal and epileptiform EEG without meeting the criteria for hypsarrhythmia [2, 3].

Attempts to generate such models in rats and mice produced models that demonstrate the characteristic seizures, epileptic spasms, either only in the immediate post-induction period (acute models) or chronically for a sustained period after induction (chronic models) (Table 1). Since all of the existing animal models demonstrate epileptic spasms, although at various age groups and with variable penetrance of the other syndromic features of West syndrome, models will be discussed using the following terminology. Epileptic spasms or IS will refer to the axial spasms as seizures. Although in the description of the models these terms have been used interchangeably, as proposed in the relevant original publications, the discussion distinguishes the models with age-appropriate expression of spasms (i.e. IS) from those that exhibit epileptic spasms also in adulthood. To avoid the term West syndrome, which has been specifically linked with the triad cited above, the term IS syndrome will be used instead, to indicate the coexistence of epileptic spasms with any of the chronic and evolving features of the syndrome: psychomotor impairment, other epileptic seizures, or hypsarrhythmia. The models will then be discussed as to whether they recapitulate the age-specific and chronic nature of epileptic spasms, the psychomotor and epilepsy outcomes, and the presence of hypsarrhythmia.

The observation that ACTH and high glucocorticoid doses may suppress IS in a significant subgroup of patients with IS, and preferentially those of unknown origin, led to the development of the stress hypothesis of IS [14]. Stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis leading to CRH-induced release of ACTH and subsequently of adrenal glucocorticoids. CRH is a known proconvulsant compound [15]. Baram proposed the hypothesis that IS of unknown origin are due to exaggerated CRH stress response. According to the proposed hypothesis, this may occur either because of prior unusual stressful conditions that lead to hyperfunctional CRH-sensitive pathways or because genetic or other predisposing factors prime the brain to respond with exaggerated CRH activation in the face of normal stress [14]. Based on the known developmental patterns of CRH and CRH receptor expression in the rat brain, it was proposed that the age of highest vulnerability to stressful events (and therefore to stress-released CRH) would be the first two postnatal weeks of a rat’s life [16]. Indeed, intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) infusions of CRH, during the first week of life, induced seizures at considerably lower doses compared to those needed to induce seizures in adult rats [15]. However, CRH-induced seizures show focal onset (jaw myoclonus, focal limb extension and clonus), resemble limbic-type seizures, and are responsive to phenytoin [15] but not to porcine ACTH1–39 [17]. CRH induced neither spasms nor EDRs in the EEG, failing to provide the proof of concept evidence that IS are indeed due to excess CRH. The possibility that CRH might trigger IS via specific regional effects in selected target brain regions cannot be excluded however by the available data.

Intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of NMDA, an agonist of the NMDA-type glutamatergic receptors, elicits a number of behaviors, including hyperactivity, emprosthotonic seizures (tonic flexion), tail twisting, followed by clonic and tonic seizures, which have also been described as NMDA-induced status epilepticus [18–21]. The tonic flexion seizures (emprosthotonus) are age-specific, appearing only during the first 3 weeks of life and are dose-dependent [21]. Low doses of NMDA trigger emprosthotonic seizures in the pups but a preponderance of other motor seizures appear with higher doses. The generators of NMDA-induced seizures, and specifically of emprosthotonus, are not known. [14C]2-deoxyglucose uptake studies indicated increased uptake in limbic areas, hypothalamus, and brainstem structures during the period when emprosthotonic seizures and the other NMDA-induced behaviors occurred [22]. In the original description of the model, it was noted that electroclinical correlation was poor and that the EEG background showed reduced amplitude, rare spikes and late motor limbic-type seizures [21]. In subsequent studies, using the lower doses of NMDA, EDRs were reported with the flexion spasms [22]. However, no recurrent spontaneous seizures have been reported in the post-induction days or months. Deficits in learning and coordination and stereotypies have been reported in older ages [19, 20].

Preclinical drug testing in the NMDA model has been done administering the drugs prior to the NMDA-induction protocol (Table 2). Based on these, induction of spasms by NMDA is inhibited by porcine ACTH1–39 [23], although rat ACTH1–39 had no effect in a previous study [22]. Vigabatrin also prevents the induction of NMDA spasms [24]. Pyridoxine decreased the incidence of NMDA spasms but induced epileptiform activity [20]. Pre-treatment with valproate had no or modest effect on NMDA-induced spasms [20, 24]. Clonazepam was ineffective [25] whereas hydrocortisone paradoxically increased the number of NMDA-induced spasms [20]. Overall, this model provides evidence for NMDA-sensitive and age-specific mechanisms implicated in the generation of emprosthotonic seizures, and mechanisms implicated in their induction. However, to this date, there is no evidence that NMDA administration is sufficient to also generate the chronic phenotype of the human epileptic encephalopathies with IS.

The first model consisted of prenatal betamethasone administration at gestational day 15 (G15) followed by NMDA induction of spasms at PN12–15 [22, 26]. Prenatal betamethasone accelerated the onset and increased the incidence of NMDA spasms [26] but also rendered them ACTH-sensitive, given that the same group had demonstrated that rat synthetic ACTH1–39 was not effective in their experimental setting [22] (Table 2). A second model of prenatal restraint stress performed twice at G15 also accelerated the onset and increased the frequency of NMDA spasms at PN15 [27]. NMDA-induced spasms in this prenatal stress model required repetitive rather than single dose administration of high doses of ACTH1–39 to inhibit NMDA spasms [27]. A reference to a similar unpublished model consisting of prenatal exposure of pregnant rats to forced cold swimming and postnatal NMDA induction of spasms was included in a recent publication, reporting that it led to decreased cortisol and ACTH levels in the offspring [28]. These studies lent support to the idea that prior prenatal stress may aggravate the severity of NMDA spasms, yet it may have a beneficial effect by rendering spasms ACTH sensitive [22, 28]. One possibility is that this priming effect may be due to long-lasting disruption of the HPA axis [29].

The third variation of the NMDA model examined the impact of postnatal adrenalectomy (PN11) on NMDA spasms, showing that it accelerates and exacerbates the NMDA spasms at PN15 [23]. Yet it is still unclear how adrenalectomy or hydrocortisone may each exacerbate NMDA spasms [20, 23]. Interestingly, the same group demonstrated that adrenalectomy did not abolish the ACTH effects, since porcine ACTH1–39 still suppressed spasms in both adrenalectomized and non-adrenalectomized NMDA-injected rats. These point towards additional glucocorticoid-independent effects of ACTH.

Although these studies demonstrate that perinatal stress and HPA axis disruption may facilitate the generation of NMDA-induced spasms and enhance their sensitivity to ACTH, they have not yet provided evidence that they suffice on their own to induce IS. An interesting study used a questionnaire-based retrospective approach to probe whether recollection of significant prenatal significant memorable events (positive or negative) might pose a risk factor for IS in the offspring of the mothers included in the study [30]. Higher prenatal stress levels were found in the mothers of infants with IS compared to those who delivered unaffected babies or infants with other seizures. Although this is well fitting to the animal studies of IS, it is surprising that prenatal stress had no effect on the epilepsy cohort, since other types of seizures are also known to be modulated by prior stress [31].

A third of infants with Down syndrome and epilepsy are reported to have IS [32]. Cortez et al investigated methods of inducing spasms in a mouse model of Down syndrome (Ts65Dn mouse) and reported that systemic administration of γ-butyrolactone (GBL), a prodrug of the GABAB receptor agonist γ-hydroxybutyrate, in 1 week to 2 month old mice, triggered epileptic extensor spasms [33]. EDRs were reported as ictal correlates. GBL-induced spasms occurred however only during the immediate post-injection period without recurrence during the subsequent days. GBL-induced spasms were sensitive to rodent ACTH1–24 but not to porcine ACTH1–39, responded to vigabatrin, valproate, and a GABAB receptor antagonist but worsened with baclofen (GABAB receptor agonist) and 5-hydroxytryptophan, a precursor of serotonin (Table 2). The 5-hydroxytryptophan effects are reminiscent to the old observation that 15% of infants with Down syndrome receiving this drug for hypotonia manifested IS [34]. However GBL spasms in the Ts65Dn model also responded to ethosuximide, which does not affect human IS. The authors propose that spasms in this model, and possibly in Down syndrome, may implicate GABAB receptor mechanisms.

Frost and Hrachovy proposed the theory of developmental desynchronization as one of the underlying mechanisms of IS [11]. They hypothesize that temporal desynchronization can be produced by genetic or acquired factors that interfere with genes, transcriptional factors or other maturational processes of the developing brain. The result is “a situation incompatible with normal integrated brain function” which may lead to IS [11]. To induce this desynchronization in brain development experimentally, chronic infusions of a sodium channel blocker, TTX, were done in the right hippocampus or central cortex of PN10–12 rats, for 4 weeks [35]. Seizures that resembled spasms with EDRs appeared after PN21 through adulthood, and were also associated with other types of seizures. The older age of appearance of spasms gave the advantage of placing multiple electrodes that captured electrographic interictal patterns that resembled to hypsarrhythmia [35]. High frequency EEG activities have been reported ictally and interictally in this model, over multiple sites of a wide field, and particularly at the contralateral to the infusion cortex [36, 37]. There is no published data on drug testing yet with drugs that are used for human IS in this model.

Clinical association studies have implicated ARX in many patients with IS presenting either with identifiable structural lesions (i.e. lissencephaly) or with no detectable neuroimaging abnormality [10]. Two independent research groups developed mouse models with loss of function of ARX and described epileptic seizures resembling spasms.

The mouse model of conditional ARX knockout (ARX CKO mouse) entails targeted knock down of the ARX gene in ganglionic eminence neurons that give rise to cortical interneurons [38]. ARX CKO mice manifest both early life seizures shown in acute, brief PN14–17 recordings (Racine stage 5 but not spasms), but also seizures in adulthood which resembled epileptic spasms. The interesting feature of this model was the pronounced loss of calbindin and to a lesser degree calretinin-positive interneurons from the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, although parvalbumin interneurons were intact. This histological finding is anticipated since loss of function of ARX interferes with the migration and differentiation of interneurons, introducing the concept of “interneuronopathies” as an underlying pathology of IS [39].

The second model is a knockin ARX mouse model (ARX KI mouse) generated by a triplet repeat expansion of the first polyalanine tract of ARX from 16 to 23 alanine codons [40]. This polyalanine expansion favors the nuclear aggregation of ARX in the mutant cells, creating a condition of partial loss of function of this transcription factor with implications for the differentiation and migration of the affected interneurons. Again, reduced cortical, hippocampal and striatal interneurons were found, preferentially affecting the calbindin, Neuropeptide Y (NPY), and cholinergic interneurons and relatively sparing the calretinin and parvalbumin positive cells. Phenotypically, ARX KI mice exhibited clusters of spasms between PN7–20, with declining frequencies after PN11, later appearance of other seizure types (3.5 – 10 weeks of life), reduced anxiety, impaired associative learning and sociability.

In combination, these two models demonstrate that interneuronopathies due to ARX loss of function may recapitulate the chronicity of the phenotype of IS syndrome, but also underline that the type and cellular selectivity of the harbored mutations may define the phenotype. The heterogeneity of phenotype of ARX gene defects is also well known from the human cohorts.

IS in the setting of a structural lesion are a considerable concern as they are associated with poorer prognosis and response to treatments. Prior studies implicated different scenarios, including dysregulation of cortical-subcortical interactions and abnormal serotonin metabolism [34, 41, 42]. Etiologies may range from cytotoxic injuries (i.e. ischemic, hypoxic) or inflammatory processes [43–46]. Our group implemented therefore a multiple-hit approach to target many of these processes and intentionally recreate a model of IS due to structural lesion [47]. PN3 rats are stereotactically injected with doxorubicin (right intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.)) and lipopolysaccharide (right parietal cortex) and then they receive p-chlorophenylalanine (PCPA; serotonin depleter) i.p. at PN5. Although these ages are usually considered as neonatal rat ages, they correspond to the infantile stage of motor milestones, during which IS, a motor seizure, occurs. Pups are in the process of developing ambulatory capability as per open field activity testing during the first 2 weeks, similar to human infants [47]. Indeed, this treatment induces a chronic phenotype of an early stage with clusters of flexion and extension spasms with EDRs (PN4–13), suggesting that PCPA is not necessary for spasms induction. In addition, other seizure types emerge starting at PN9, learning deficits that persist after the resolution of spasms as well as sociability deficits occur [47]. Although such deficits may be partially anticipated as a result of the underlying lesion, they are partially reversible, as they can be partially improved by effective treatments [48]. The underlying pathology involves the right cortical, hippocampal and periventricular subcortical regions. In reminiscence of the described concept of “interneuronopathy” fewer and malformed GABAergic interneurons can be seen in cortical regions, including contralaterally to the lesion, in the multiple-hit model (unpublished data).

The chronic phenotype in this model allowed us to test a number of treatments using the same treatment approach as done in clinical practice, with drugs given after the onset of spasms. Spasms in the multiple-hit model are not affected by daily administration of a synthetic ACTH1–24 fragment, and were transiently responsive to vigabatrin (Table 2) [47]. This relative refractoriness of spasms in this model is reminiscent of the more refractory course of IS due to underlying structural lesions [5, 8, 9, 49]. Furthermore spasms did not respond to phenytoin that has no effect on human IS [50]. One of the promising candidate treatments is carisbamate, which showed acute efficacy in suppressing spasms, within 2 hours [50]. The mechanism of carisbamate-mediated suppression of spasms is unknown but different than sodium channel blockade, as phenytoin has no effect [50] (Table 3).

A third of patients with epilepsy due to TSC, a genetic disorder with overactive mTOR signaling, demonstrate IS [51]. Dysregulation of the mTOR pathway is also seen in cortical dysplasias and malformations which are common etiologies for IS [6, 52–54]. We therefore tested in the multiple-hit model of IS the role of the mTOR pathway in the pathogenesis and treatment of spasms [48]. The mTOR inhibitor rapamycin, given after the onset of spasms, suppressed spasms in a dose-dependent manner: low doses (1–3 mg/kg/day i.p.) had delayed but transient benefit on spasms whereas higher doses (6 mg/kg i.p.) were needed to suppress spasms acutely. Interestingly, implementation of a pulse 3-day course of rapamycin (6 mg/kg at PN4; 3mg/kg/day at PN5 and PN6) resulted in early spasms cessation, partial improvement of learning deficits at PN16–19 Barnes maze testing, with good tolerability [48]. It is worth noting that the effects of rapamycin in naïve pups were quite different, because it tended to worsen, rather than improve learning on Barnes maze [48]. This emphasizes the importance of testing treatments in the appropriate context, in the presence of the targeted disease, rather than in disease-naïve subjects. The implementation of the pulse treatment protocols with rapid-onset efficacy is therefore a promising strategy for this age-specific epileptic syndrome, which may allow us to reap the benefits but minimize side effects.

Following our study, pre-treatment with low rapamycin doses was given in the prenatal betamethasone/NMDA model without any acute effect on NMDA spasms [26]. The lack of efficacy of low rapamycin doses on the immediate post-induction period of spasms corroborates our results with low rapamycin doses in the multiple-hit model. However, further comparisons of the two studies are hindered by the different ages and dose-requirements in the two models, the different treatment protocols (pre-vs post-treatment) and the lack of evidence for target engagement by rapamycin in the betamethasone / NMDA model.

Overactivation of the mTOR pathway was detected at the cortical regions of pups during the period of spasms in the multiple-hit model [48]. Pups with spasms had increased expression of the phosphorylated ribosomal protein S6 (pS6; downstream target of TORC1 complex). In addition, effective doses for spasms correlated with those needed to restore pS6 expression to normal levels, confirming target engagement in the multiple-hit model [48]. The mTOR pathway is a central pathway receiving converging signals from a number of sources, including energy and metabolism related processes, inflammatory signaling pathways, growth factors, hormones, neurotransmitters. It also controls neuronal differentiation and function. It appears therefore as a likely candidate target for many of the known etiologies of IS (i.e. hypoxia-ischemia, inflammation) and possible effector for some of the observed underlying pathologies (i.e. dysplasias).

Through the studies in animal models of IS, it is clear that the seizures seen in IS syndrome, i.e. epileptic spasms, can be induced with different approaches, including NMDA receptor activation in naïve pups, GABAB receptor activation in a genetic substrate of Down syndrome, genetic defects in the ARX gene, chronic blockade of cortical or hippocampal activity with TTX, or with structural and inflammatory cortical-subcortical lesions. The existing evidence suggests that prenatal stress and adrenal insufficiency are risk factors, rather than triggers, of IS. It is yet unclear however whether they are selective risk factors for IS rather than for other epileptic seizures as well. Prenatal stress may also render IS more responsive to ACTH treatment. The concept of interneuronopathy in IS is supported by the ARX models but also by our observations in the multiple-hit model. The possible pathogenic role of overactivated mTOR pathway in IS and associated cognitive decline is supported by the multiple-hit model and clinicopathological and genetic studies in patients with brain malformations and TSC.

The chronicity of the phenotype, a key feature of IS syndrome, has only been recapitulated by the TTX, ARX, and multiple-hit models. The chronicity of the phenotype cannot be simply explained by the long-standing presence of the initial hit (functional, genetic or structural). It is more likely that the initial hits trigger a cascade of other plastic changes that cumulate to the emerging epilepsy, cognitive and neurodevelopmental deficits. This is exemplified by the role of the mTOR pathway in the multiple-hit model, in which inhibition of the mTOR overactivation stops spasms and improves cognition, even if the underlying lesion is still present. In the TTX model, spasms by far outlast the period when TTX infusion takes place. In the ARX models, also, the symptoms are age-specific and evolving.

At present, hypsarrhythmia has been reported only in the TTX model of IS, with EEG recordings done in adult rats. It is currently particularly difficult to establish or disprove a diagnosis of hypsarrhythmia in developing rodents. The technical limitations in the number of electrodes that can be placed in the small and fragile skull of developing pups do not allow the determination of multifocality, high amplitude, or disorganization with the same confidence as we have in human EEG studies. Further, it is uncertain whether a high amplitude period in the EEG of a rodent, on its own, has the specificity to detect hypsarrhythmia, rather than other epileptic or nonepileptic background abnormalities, in the absence of its associated features of multifocality and disorganization.

Few of these models share the age-specificity of human IS, including the multiple-hit, the ARX KI, and the NMDA models. Interestingly, occasional flexion spasm-like behaviors can be seen in other models of seizures (i.e. kainic acid status epilepticus) in immature rodents supporting the idea that the immaturity of the developing brain may be a more suitable substrate to manifest epileptic spasms. However what renders a model of IS different from a model of status epilepticus with occasional spasm-like behaviors is the spectrum of seizure and electrographic manifestations and the syndromic features that accompany and specify each condition. It is therefore important to maintain the same syndromic diagnostic approach in our view of the animal models, as we do for human patients, if we are to pursue mechanisms and treatments specific for the IS syndrome. Maintaining clinically relevant and translatable phenotypes in the animal models will be essential in improving the predictive value of preclinical findings, as these models are being optimized for the identification of new candidate therapies.
