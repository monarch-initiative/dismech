# Extended functional rescue following AAV gene therapy in a canine model of LRIT3-congenital stationary night blindness.
**Authors:** Takahashi K, Kwok JC, Sato Y, Aguirre GD, Miyadera K
**Journal:** Vision Res (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.visres.2023.108260](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2023.108260)

## Content

Congenital stationary night blindness (CSNB) is a group of inherited retinal diseases in which either rod-to-ON-bipolar cell (ON-BC) signaling, or rod function is affected leading to impaired vision under low light conditions. One type of CSNB is associated with defects in genes (NYX, GRM6, TRPM1, GPR179, and LRIT3) involved in the mGluR6 signaling cascade at the ON-BC dendritic tips. We have previously characterized a canine model of LRIT3-CSNB and demonstrated short-term safety and efficacy of an ON-BC targeting AAV-LRIT3 (AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE) gene therapy. Herein, we demonstrate long-term functional recovery and molecular restoration following subretinal injection of the ON-BC targeting AAV-LRIT3 vector in all eight treated eyes for up to 32 months. Following subretinal administration of the therapeutic vector, expression of the LRIT3 transgene, as well as restoration of mGluR6 signaling cascade member TRPM1, were confirmed in the outer plexiform layer (OPL) of the treated area. However, further investigation of the transgene LRIT3 transcript expression by RNA in situ hybridization (RNA-ISH) revealed off-target expression in non-BCs including the photoreceptors, inner nuclear, and ganglion cell layers, despite the use of a mutant AAVK9#4 capsid and an improved mGluR6 promoter designed to specifically transduce and promote expression in ON-BCs. While the long-term therapeutic potential of AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE is promising, we highlight the necessity for further optimization of AAV-LRIT3 therapy in the canine CSNB model prior to its clinical application.

Congenital stationary night blindness (CSNB) is a heterogeneous group of non-progressive inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) primarily affecting retinal bipolar cells and photoreceptors (PRs) (Zeitz et al., 2015). Affected individuals suffer from non-progressive, dark or dim-light visual difficulties starting from birth. The clinical presentation of CSNB is broadly classified into two main forms, Riggs (Riggs, 1954) and Schubert-Bornstein (Schubert & Bornschein, 1952), based on the electroretinogram (ERG) pattern. Riggs-type CSNB is associated with PR dysfunction and presents with selective loss of rod function. In contrast, the Schubert-Bornstein-type results from inaccurate signal transmission from PRs to retinal bipolar cells, and can be further subdivided into complete (cCSNB) and incomplete (iCSNB) subtypes, depending on whether the recordable rod ERG response under scotopic conditions is absent or diminished, respectively (Miyake et al., 1986). The cCSNB form is associated with genetic variants primarily affecting one of five known genes involved in the metabotropic glutamate receptor 6 (mGluR6) signaling cascade that takes place at the ON-bipolar cell (ON-BC) dendritic tips: NYX (Bech-Hansen et al., 2000; Pusch et al., 2000), GRM6 (Dryja et al., 2005; Zeitz et al., 2005), TRPM1 (Li et al., 2009; van Genderen et al., 2009), GPR179 (Audo et al., 2012; Peachey et al., 2012), and LRIT3 (Zeitz et al., 2013).

Leucine-rich repeat immunoglobulin-like transmembrane domain 3 (LRIT3) is a member of the leucine-rich repeat family and localizes to the outer plexiform layer (OPL), playing an important role in PR to ON-BC signal transmission. Mouse models with genetically impaired LRIT3 have similar scotopic ERG b-wave abnormalities to human cCSNB patients with LRIT3 variants (Charette et al., 2016; Neuillé et al., 2014). Further studies in these mouse models indicated that LRIT3 is essential for the localization of transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily M member 1 (TRPM1) and nyctalopin, components of the mGluR6 signalplex, to the OPL (Hasan et al., 2020; Neuillé et al., 2015). We have previously characterized a naturally-occurring strain of beagle dogs with predominant loss of scotopic ERG b-waves associated with a LRIT3 variant (Das et al., 2019; Kondo et al., 2015). In this canine model, we identified an exonic 1-bp insertion in canine LRIT3 (cLRIT3) that generated a truncated cLRIT3 protein that no longer localizes to the OPL (Das et al., 2019). This large animal CSNB model is a valuable platform for testing the therapeutic efficacy and safety of novel gene therapies aimed at correcting ON-BC dysfunctions associated with defective LRIT3.

Recently, we reported the therapeutic efficacy of an AAV-based gene therapy designed to target ON-BCs in the canine CSNB model (Miyadera et al., 2022). In this study, we assessed various combinations of AAV capsid variants, promoters, and injection routes to determine the most efficient strategy to specifically express the transgene in ON-BCs. Two AAV capsid variants previously screened in wild type (WT) dogs (Öztürk et al., 2021) were selected based on their ability to effectively target ON-BCs following either intravitreal (AAVK9#12) or subretinal (AAVK9#4) administration. Each capsid variant was combined with two different modified mGluR6 promoters, the original full-length promoter (lgGRM6) and the shortened promoter (shGRM6) (Lu et al., 2016). Using delivery routes optimized for each capsid, four unique vector constructs (AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE; AAVK9#4-lgGRM6-cLRIT3; AAVK9#12-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE, and AAVK9#12-lgGRM6-cLRIT3) were injected in the eyes of LRIT3-CSNB affected dogs. Notably, subretinal injection of the AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE was the only therapeutic strategy that promoted functional recovery and transgene expression for at least one year in all six injected LRIT3-CSNB canine eyes and up to 16 months post-injection (Miyadera et al., 2022). Herein, we report the long-term follow-up in eyes following AAV-based gene therapy with this therapeutic vector construct in the canine CSNB model. We also examine the specificity of cLRIT3 transgene transduction in ON-BCs as well as the association between the injection route and the degree of therapeutic efficacy.

This research was conducted in strict accordance with the recommendations of the NIH (Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, 2011), and in full compliance with the US Department of Agriculture’s Animal Welfare Act, Animal Welfare Regulations, and the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Pennsylvania (IACUC no. 803269).

The LRIT3-CSNB model originated from a research colony that was used for disease characterization (Kondo et al., 2015) and identification of the genetic variant (Das et al., 2019). The dogs in the current study are part of a newly established LRIT3-CSNB research line developed at the Retinal Disease Studies Facility (RDSF) of the University of Pennsylvania that was formed by outcrossing Beagle dogs from the original colony (Kondo et al., 2015) to WT dogs, and to Beagle dogs not known to be directly related to the original Japanese colony but those that were identified to harbor the LRIT3 variant through molecular screening of dogs bred in the United States. Both control (WT) and affected dogs were bred at the RDSF or by a commercial laboratory animal supplier, and they were maintained and housed at the RDSF under identical conditions of diet, medications, vaccinations, and ambient illumination with cyclic 12h ON-12h OFF. CSNB-affected dogs were identified based on genotyping of the LRIT3 genetic variant (Das et al., 2019) as well as the characteristic loss of scotopic ERG b-wave (Kondo et al., 2015; Miyadera et al., 2022).

Identification of the optimal expression cassette for robust and stable therapeutic gene expression was demonstrated in our previous study (Miyadera et al., 2022). The design and construction of the AAV vectors harboring the cLRIT3 transgene have been described previously (Miyadera et al., 2022). To validate the ON-BC-specific expression of the two versions of modified GRM6 promoters (i.e., lgGRM6 and shGRM6) in combination with the AAVK9#4 mutant capsid, two control vectors were generated. Each control vector contained one of the modified GRM6 promoters upstream of one of the reporter transgenes and were packaged into AAVK9#4 capsids (AAVK9#4-lgGRM6-GFP-WRPE and AAVK9#4-shGRM6-mCherry-WRPE). AAV vectors were generated by triple-plasmid co-transfection purified and titered as previously described (Flannery & Visel, 2013).

Under general anesthesia, viral vectors were delivered by subretinal or intravitreal injections in eyes of CSNB affected or WT dogs as previously described (Miyadera et al., 2022) (Table 1 and Table 2 show injection details for CSNB and WT dogs, respectively). Viral vectors were diluted in sterile balanced salt solution (BSS) and 100 to 200 μL was delivered per eye. A transvitreal approach was used without vitrectomy via pars plana by using a custom-modified subretinal injector with a 39-gauge polyimide cannula (Retinaject; Surmodics, Inc., Eden Prairie, MN, USA) (W A Beltran et al., 2010; Komáromy et al., 2006) under direct visualization through a Machemer magnifying lens (OMVI; Ocular Instruments Inc., Bellevue, WA, USA) using an operating microscope. Subretinal delivery was directed at the superotemporal quadrant to form a large uniform bleb, encompassing ~20 to 40% of the retina, which was immediately visualized and documented by fundus photography (RetCam Shuttle; Natus Medical, Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA). Intravitreal delivery was performed by deposition of viral solution in the posterior vitreous pre-retinally at four sites horizontally across the visual streak as previously described (Miyadera et al., 2022). Anterior chamber paracentesis was performed immediately post-injection to prevent an increase in intraocular pressure. The post-injection antibiotic and anti-inflammatory routine treatment plan was described previously (Aguirre et al., 2021).

Ophthalmic examinations included slit-lamp biomicroscopy, tonometry, indirect ophthalmoscopy, and fundus photography. Any abnormalities, including development of inflammation or lesions, were documented. The ophthalmic examinations were conducted pre-injection, 1- and 2-d post-injection, weekly for the following 4 weeks, biweekly for an additional 8 weeks, and then, every 12 to 16 weeks up to 32 months post-injection.

ERG recordings were performed as previously described (Miyadera et al., 2022) at pre-injection and at the following post-injection time points; 0.9–1, 3, 6–8, 11–14, 15–18, 21–22, 23–24, 26–28, and 32 months after treatment. Full-field flash ERGs were conducted on both eyes using a custom-built Ganzfeld dome fitted with light-emitting diode stimuli from a ColorDome stimulator (Diagnosys LLC, Lowell, MA, USA). After 20 min of dark adaptation, rod and mixed rod–cone-mediated responses to single 4-ms white flash stimuli of increasing intensities (−3.7 to 0.5 log cd·s·m−2) were recorded. After 5 min of white light adaptation (1.03 log cd·m−2), cone-mediated responses to a series of single white flashes (−2.7 to 0.5 log cd·s·m−2) and to 29.4-Hz flicker stimuli (−2.7 to 0.3 log cd·s·m−2) were recorded. While the increasing light intensity series are routinely recorded, for the purpose of assessing maximal and isolated rod- and cone-specific responses, light intensities that best represent each rod (−1.7 log cd·s·m−2) and cone (single flash at 0.5 log cd·s·m−2, flicker at 0.3 log cd·s·m−2) response were used for analysis. Multiple comparisons were performed using Dunn’s multiple comparisons test following a significant Kruskal-Wallis test. Two data sets were compared using two-tailed Mann-Whitney test. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.4.1 (GraphPad software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Expression of cLRIT3 mRNA in the retina was visualized by RNA-ISH using the RNAscope assay (Advanced Cell Diagnostics (ACDBio), BioTechne, Newark, CA, USA) following the previously described protocol (Sudharsan et al., 2021). Canine LRiT3-specific RNAscope probes spanning nucleotides 189 to 678 (XM_848057.5) were designed and custom made by ACDBio (catalog no. 1029561-C1). Polr2a and DapB probes (Supplemental Table 1) were used as positive and negative control. Ten micrometer frozen sections were cut from PFA-fixed OCT-embedded retinas with a cryostat, and the sections were mounted on glass slides (Globe Scientific Inc., Mahwah, NJ, USA). Target retrieval was performed on fixed frozen sections by heating the slides in Target Retrieval buffer at 86 - 90 °C for 10 min followed by protease digestion. RNA-ISH was performed using the RNAscope 2.5 HD Assay-Red following the guidelines in the product manual. After counterstaining with Hoechst 33342 (1:1000; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, catalog no. 62249), the retinal sections were examined and images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica SP5-II; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, German) with a 63× objective or a fluorescence microscope (Axioplan, Carl Zeiss Meditec GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany) with a 40× objective.

Preparation and processing of tissues for retinal morphology and IHC have been detailed previously (Miyadera et al., 2022). Cryosections were incubated overnight with appropriate primary antibodies (Supplemental Table 1) in dilution buffer (4.5% fish gelatin, 0.1% sodium azide 5% BSA, and 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS). In all cases, antigen-antibody complexes were visualized with fluorochrome-labelled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor Dyes, Molecular Probes, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with 1:1000 dilution and then counterstained using Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Scientific) with 1:1000 dilution. The stained sections were photographed using SP5-II confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems) under 100× objectives or a fluorescence microscope (Axioplan, Carl Zeiss Meditec GmbH) with a 40× objective. Digital images were processed using the Leica Application Suite X (Leica Microsystems).

In our previous study, we found that subretinal injection of the therapeutic vector AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE apparently had superior efficacy in eyes in which there was obvious intravitreal leakage compared to eyes that had uncomplicated subretinal injections (Miyadera et al., 2022). We therefore hypothesized that the route of injection could affect the efficacy of the therapeutic vector. To test this hypothesis, we compared three different routes of injection: subretinal, intravitreal, and subretinal + intravitreal combined (Table 1). Injection of the therapeutic vector either subretinally or through combined subretinal + intravitreal injection resulted in significantly improved scotopic ERG b-wave amplitudes compared to pre-treatment or injection of ‘non-therapeutic’ vectors (Fig. 1A, B, D). Although the subretinal + intravitreal combined injections resulted in higher rod b-wave amplitudes than the intravitreal injections alone, the difference in ERG b-wave amplitudes between these two injection routes was not significant (Fig. 1D). Notably, there was no improvement in the rod ERG b-wave amplitude in the eye injected with the therapeutic vector solely via the intravitreal route (Fig. 1C, D). These data indicate that subretinal delivery of the therapeutic vector is required for recovery of visual function and that intravitreal injection, either alone or in addition to subretinal deposition, does not significantly contribute to recovery of visual function.

To evaluate the long-term efficacy of subretinal AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE injection, ERGs were recorded for up to 32 months after treatment. As shown in Fig 1, no significant difference in therapeutic efficacy was found between the eyes that received the therapeutic vector subretinally or via a combined subretinal + intravitreal injections. We therefore included both groups (i.e. subretinal ± intravitreal route) in the therapeutic group. However, as there was no functional restoration in the eye injected intravitreally with the therapeutic vector, it was assigned to the non-therapeutic group. Compared to LRIT3 heterozygous canines, the rod-derived ERG b-wave amplitude in LRIT3 homozygous affected canines was notably decreased (Fig. 2A and Supplemental data 1A). The scotopic b-wave amplitude in the therapeutic group showed statistically significant recovery from 0.9-1 months to 11-14 months post-injection compared to the non-therapeutic group (Fig. 2A). Even after 14 months post-injection, there was a consistent effect on scotopic b-wave in the therapeutic group up to 32 months after treatment (Fig. 2A and Supplemental data 1C). As expected, there was no significant difference in the cone-derived ERG amplitude between control, therapeutic, and non-therapeutic groups (Fig. 2B and Supplemental data 1B).

In our previous study, a modified mGluR6 promoter (lgGRM6) was used with different AAV capsid variants to validate ON-BC-specific expression of reporter genes in WT canine retina (Miyadera et al., 2022). However, the vector ultimately identified as the therapeutic vector utilized a shorter version (shGRM6) of the modified mGluR6 promoter. Although the shGRM6 promoter was functionally effective, ON-BC-specific gene expression in canine retina using this promoter had yet to be demonstrated. To confirm the regulation of ON-BC-specific expression by shGRM6 and lgGRM6 promoters, reporter constructs AAVK9#4-lgGRM6-mCherry-WPRE and AAVK9#4-shGRM6-GFP-WPRE were injected subretinally in two WT canines. One canine received one vector in each eye, while the other received both vectors in each eye, either in a single bleb or as two separate blebs (Table 2). Retinal histology 2 months after administration showed that both vectors expressed reporter proteins specifically in the secondary neuronal layer without off-target expression in other layers throughout the treated area (Fig. 3A-C).

To determine if molecular recovery was maintained long-term following AAV gene therapy, co-immunostaining was performed using anti-LRIT3 antibodies and antibodies against postsynaptic markers. Prominent LRIT3 signal intensity was observed around second-order neuron markers (protein kinase C alpha [PKCα] and the alpha subunit of Go [Goα]) in WT canine retina (Fig. 4A). Untreated areas in the canine CSNB eye (outside of vector bleb, dog K11-OD) had no punctate LRIT3 signals in the OPL and showed decreased expression of PKCα and overexpression of Goα in the postsynaptic region (Fig. 4B). In contrast, in the eye with the best therapeutic response (dog CHACUG-OS), LRIT3 expression in the OPL was restored substantially and persisted up to 32 months after treatment (Fig. 4C). The long-term expression of transduced LRIT3 in the OPL was detected in all eyes with more than two years of scotopic b-wave recovery (Supplemental data 2; four eyes in three animals, CHACUG-OS, K16-OD, K16-OS, and K31-OS). In these eyes, LRIT3 signals were found in the cell bodies and dendritic terminals of secondary neurons as well as in the OPL, indicating transgene expression in bipolar cells (Fig. 4C). Moreover, in the regions where LRIT3 signals were restored, the expression level of PKCα in the OPL was also restored, but the mislocalization of Goα remained unchanged (Fig. 4C).

We next performed RNA-ISH using canine LRIT3-specific RNAscope probes to verify ON-BC-specific transduction of LRIT3 by the ON-BC-targeted AAV vector construct. While undertaking our initial studies of these therapeutic vectors, Hasan et al. reported that endogenous LRIT3 transcripts are expressed in the PR-containing outer nuclear layer (ONL) of mouse retina (Hasan et al., 2019). While surprising, we also find that endogenous LRIT3 mRNA can be detected specifically in the ONL of WT canine retina (Fig. 4D). The mRNA expression level was markedly reduced in the untreated area of the affected retina (outside of bleb, dog K16-OD) compared to that of WT retina (Fig. 4E). Strikingly, in the AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE-treated region (dog CHACUG-OS), overexpression of LRIT3 mRNA was observed across all layers of the retina, indicating that LRIT3 gene transduction by AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE was not strictly ON-BC-specific, even though the reporter gene assay supported the intended specificity (Figs. 3, 4F, and Supplemental data 3). These findings suggest that LRIT3 gene transduction by the therapeutic vector injection is not restricted to ON-BCs, and that off-target LRIT3 gene expression in the PRs might be partly if not predominantly responsible for the restoration of LRIT3 expression in the OPL and the resulting functional rescue.

LRIT3 is essential for the correct localization of its interacting protein, TRPM1, in the OPL. In the retina of the mouse CSNB model, postsynaptic localization of these proteins is impaired by the absence of LRIT3, but can be restored with AAV-based LRIT3 expression (Hasan et al., 2019). To assess the expression pattern of TRPM1 in the OPL of the canine CSNB model, IHC was performed using commercially available antibody targeting TRPM1. The signals of TRPM1 were detected in the OPL of WT canine retina but were undetectable in the untreated CSNB retina (Fig. 5A, B). In the AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE-treated canine CSNB retina, TRPM1 expression was partially restored in the OPL (Fig. 5C). Labeling of GPR179 puncta was unaffected by CSNB or therapy and localized to the OPL in the retinas of all groups (Fig. 5D, E, F). These findings support that recovery of visual function following AAV-LRIT3 therapy is mediated by normalization of the mGluR6 signaling cascade as a result of LRIT3 restoration in the OPL.

In this study, we have demonstrated long-term efficacy of an AAV-based gene therapy in the canine LRIT3-CSNB model using an AAV capsid and a promoter designed to improve targeting of ON-BCs. We confirmed sustained functional recovery based on scotopic ERG as well as localization of the LRIT3 therapeutic transgene in the OPL for up to 32 months after subretinal injection of AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE. Compared to other preclinical studies of AAV-based therapy for IRD in canine models, the duration of therapeutic efficacy in the current study is very encouraging, and it is reasonable to expect sustained functional recovery beyond the endpoint of the current study (Acland et al., 2005; William A Beltran et al., 2017; Cideciyan et al., 2018).

In our previous report, we posited that combined subretinal + intravitreal injection of AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLR/73-WPRE might exhibit greater efficacy than subretinal injection alone. To examine this possibility, an LRIT3-affected canine eye was treated with the therapeutic vector via the intravitreal route only, but no recovery of scotopic ERG b-wave was observed. While some individual eyes that received the AAV vector via both injection routes showed apparently better recovery of scotopic ERG amplitudes than eyes treated by subretinal delivery alone, there was no significant difference in the mean b-wave amplitude between each group. In addition, regardless of additional intravitreal deposition of vectors, subretinal delivery of the therapeutic vector was required for the long-term restoration of b-waves. Considering that AAVK9#4 is an AAV capsid designed for subretinal injection, these results suggest that subretinal delivery is suitable as an injection route for AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE. Studies in LRIT3-deficient mice have revealed markedly altered cone-mediated signals (Neuillé et al., 2014). Previously, we have shown moderately reduced cone-mediated b-waves in the LRIT3-CSNB affected dogs (Kondo et al., 2015) albeit with no detectable clinical consequences. Human LRIT3-CSNB patients show moderate cone-mediate ERG abnormalities (Zeitz et al., 2013) similar to affected dogs. Future studies to address the effect of deficient and restored LRIT3 in the cone pathway could elucidate a more complete role and action of LRIT3.

Altered subcellular localization of PKCα and Goα in the bipolar cells is a unique characteristic phenotype of the LRIT3-CSNB canine model (Das et al., 2019; Kondo et al., 2015), as it has not been reported in LRIT3-affected mouse models. In the affected retina, the expression level of PKCα, a serine/threonine protein kinase that modulates the mGluR6 signaling cascade, is decreased across the dendrites and somata of the rod bipolar cells whereas Goα, an essential component of signal transmission, shows increased labeling in the ON-BC dendritic terminals. Restoration of PKCα to the OPL in the treated area indicates it may contribute to functional recovery. Furthermore, detailed examination using confocal microscopy revealed loss of TRPM1 in the OPL of LRIT3-CSNB canine retina, as previously reported in LRIT3nob mice (Neuillé et al., 2015). Restoration of TRPM1 to ON-BC dendritic terminals following AAV-LRIT3 therapy has also been observed in the mouse models, further supporting the usefulness of this therapeutic vector (Hasan et al., 2019; Varin et al., 2021).

We originally designed the therapeutic vector, AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE to achieve ON-BC specific transgene expression. Indeed, utilization of the selected AAV-capsid and promoter in the reporter construct AAVK9#4-shGRM6-GFP-WPRE demonstrated strict GFP signal localization in the bipolar cells when injected subretinally. Therefore, it was unexpected that subretinal injection of the ON-BC-targeted AAV-LRIT3 vector led to extensive LRIT3 mRNA expression in all retinal layers within the treated bleb area. Indeed, although LRIT3 mRNA signals were observed in the INL (the original target), the predominant signals were observed in the ONL. Consistent with such findings, promoter-independent transgene expression has been previously reported in studies assessing AAV-based Müller glia or astrocyte-specific gene transduction using a modified GFAP promoter (Le et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021). Therefore, we postulate that insert-dependent sequences impact gene transduction, permitting escape from promoter regulation through as yet unclear mechanisms.

The current study was designed prior to a report (Hasan et al., 2019) proposing that the expression of punctate LRIT3 in the OPL resulted from PR-expressed LRIT3 localized to the presynaptic membrane. Consistent with the report by Hasan et al., our RNA-ISH results in WT canine retina showed that endogenous LRIT3 mRNA was expressed predominantly in the ONL rather than in the INL. In addition, previous studies in the mouse LRIT3nob model suggest that LRIT3 gene transduction targeting PRs may have a greater therapeutic effect than targeting ON-BCs (Hasan et al., 2019; Varin et al., 2021). Taking these into account, it appears likely that the therapeutic effect of AAVK9#4-shGRM6-GFP-WPRE demonstrated in our study is affected by off-target transgene expression in the PRs. However, it remains possible that ectopic LRIT3 expression in ON-BCs may also contribute to either short-term or long-term functional effects, and thus warrants further study. Further examination of AAV-LRIT3 therapy, including PR-targeted AAV constructs in the canine CSNB model as well as a dose de-escalation study to identify the lowest effective titer is warranted prior to their development for clinical application.

The current study demonstrated long-term functional and molecular recovery following subretinal injection of an ON-BC-targeted AAV-based LRIT3 gene therapy in a canine LRIT3-CSNB model. In the retinal area treated with the therapeutic vector, the introduced LRIT3 protein was expressed and TRPM1, involved in the mGulR6 signaling cascade, were also restored in the OPL. However, further investigation at the transcript level revealed that this AAV vector construct lacked the strict specificity to ON-BCs expected from the selected mutant AAVK9#4 capsid and the improved mGluR6 promoter. While the long-term therapeutic potential of AAVK9#4-shGRM6-cLRIT3-WPRE is promising, further optimization of AAV-LRIT3 therapy, including PR-targeted AAV constructs in the canine CSNB model, is needed to optimize its clinical application.
