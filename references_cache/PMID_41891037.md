Congenital heart disease (CHD) occurs in over half of individuals with 22q11.2
deletion syndrome (22q11.2DS), but lesion type varies widely. We analyzed 3,016
unrelated postnatal individuals with 22q11.2DS from specialized centers in the
United States, Canada, Europe, South America, Israel, and Australia, including
1,868 with whole-genome sequencing. The typical 3 Mb LCR22 A-D deletion was
present in 2,788 individuals (92.4%), with smaller A-B (n=172, 5.7%) and A-C
(n=56, 1.9%) deletions comprising the remaining cohort. In multivariable
mixed-effects logistic regression adjusting for sex, deletion group, genome-wide
principal components (PCs), and recruitment site, four non-intercept
associations, to test relationships, met study-wide FDR statistical
significance. Compared with the A-B deletion, the A-D deletion was associated
with lower odds of persistent truncus arteriosus (OR=0.37, 95% CI 0.18-0.75) and
higher odds of isolated septal defects (OR=4.68, 95% CI 1.71-12.83), although
precision was limited by the smaller A-B group. PC2 was associated with lower
odds of pulmonary stenosis/atresia with additional lesions (OR=0.73, 95% CI
0.61-0.87), and PC4 with higher odds of abnormal origin of the subclavian
arteries (OR=2.59, 95% CI 1.37-4.89). ADMIXTURE-derived continental ancestry
proportions did not show independent associations with these two PC-associated
outcomes. These lesion-specific findings suggest the hypothesis that deletion
interval and broader genetic background may contribute to CHD variability in
22q11.2DS, pending future replication.
KEY MESSAGES: What is already known on this topic: Chromosome 22q11.2DS is a
rare genetic disorder associated with serious medical challenges. Most affected
individuals have congenital heart disease but with variable phenotypic
expression. One of the main questions still an open topic in the field is why
individuals with 22q11.2DS show extensive phenotypic heterogeneity.What this
study adds: Analysis of retrospective data on 3,016 individuals with 22q11.2DS
and 1,868 with sequence data suggest that deletion type and genetic variation
influence phenotypic expression of congenital heart disease.How this study might
affect research, practice or policy: This work informs the clinician as to the
importance of testing for deletion type and obtaining accurate cardiac
phenotypes to diagnose 22q11.2DS that will improve the prognosis and disease
progression. It also implicates the complexity of sex and ancestry as
confounding factors that will help guide future research studies to identify
genetic modifiers of congenital heart disease.

ABSTRACT
Congenital heart disease (CHD) occurs in over half of individuals with 22q11.2 deletion
syndrome (22q11.2DS) and the types of lesions range from mild to severe.  To determine the
basis of variation in cardiac phenotypes we analyzed demographic data from 3,016 unrelated
individuals with 22q11.2DS from centers in the Northeast US, Canada, Europe, South America,
Israel and Australia.  Most individuals in this cohort had a 3 million base pair hemizygous
deletion between low copy repeat, LCR22 A-D (87.2%), while a minority had nested proximal
deletions (1.5 Mb, A-B, 5.4%; 1.8 Mb, A-C, 1.8%).  By univariable analysis methods, we found
several demographic features contributed to phenotypic variation, which may in part be due to
collection site heterogeneity. To disentangle these effects, we performed multivariable mixed-
effects logistic regression and uncovered significant differences between CHD phenotypes and
these features. Individuals with the A-D deletion had a lower risk of persistent truncus arteriosus
(OR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.18-0.75) but a higher risk of septal defects (OR = 4.7, 95% CI 1.7-12.8)
compared to those with the smaller A-B deletion, suggesting distinct developmental pathways
sensitive to 22q11.2 gene dosage. In addition, genome-wide genetic principal components
(PCs) were associated with specific CHD subtypes, including reduced risk of pulmonary
stenosis or atresia with other heart lesions with increasing PC2 (OR = 0.73, 95% CI 0.61-0.87)
and increased risk of abnormal origin of the subclavian arteries with PC4 (OR = 2.6, 95% CI
1.4-4.9), indicating that background genetic variation may modify lesion-specific susceptibility.
Together, these results suggest that both deletion size and background genetic variation shape
the highly variable cardiac phenotypes in 22q11.2DS.

INTRODUCTION
The chromosome 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (22q11.2DS; DiGeorge/velo-cardio-
facial/conotruncal anomaly face syndrome) is a congenital anomaly disorder that occurs in
1/1,000 fetuses 1;2 and 1/4,000 live births 3,4 constituting the most frequent genomic disorder in
humans.  This syndrome results from non-allelic homologous recombination events between
flanking low copy repeats on chromosome 22q11.2 termed LCR22 5-8 resulting in de novo
deletions of different sizes (Figure 1).  Molecular genetic testing such as multiplex-ligation
dependent probe amplification (MLPA) or chromosomal microarray is typically used for
diagnostic testing of patients with clinical features reminiscent of 22q11.2DS.  The most
frequent deletion is a 3 million base pair (Mb) hemizygous deletion flanked by LCR22 A-D (A-D).  
Nested A-B (1.5 Mb) and A-C (1.8 Mb) deletions occur with lower frequency but with similar
overall phenotypes 7-10.  
The clinical presentation of this syndrome ranges from mild to severe including
characteristic facial differences, immune deficits, and hypocalcemia as well as neurocognitive or
psychiatric disorders 11,12.  One of the most frequent and serious medical findings is congenital
heart disease (CHD) that occurs in 50-80% of individuals with 22q11.2DS 13. The types of CHD
that occur range from isolated, mild aortic arch or arterial branching defects (right sided aortic
arch, RAA; abnormal origin of the right or left subclavian arteries, ASA) with little clinical
symptoms, ventricular and/or atrial septal defects (septal defects, SD; VSD, ASD) to severe,
complex and lethal conotruncal heart defects (CTDs) including tetralogy of Fallot (TOF),
interrupted aortic arch type B (IAAB) and persistent truncus arteriosus (PTA) 14,15. These severe
CTDs require surgery for survival beyond the neonatal or early infancy period.  Overall, there is
extensive phenotypic heterogeneity in the prevalence and types of CHD in 22q11.2DS. The role
of demographic features on phenotypic heterogeneity of CHD is not well established due to the
rarity of the syndrome and lack of a comprehensive and systematic assessment of basic
medical data.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed basic demographic information and detailed
cardiac phenotypes from 3,016 unrelated individuals with 22q11.2DS. They were ascertained at
clinical centers that specialize in care of individuals of 22q11.2DS in the US, Canada, Europe,
South America and Australia.  This large cohort size makes it possible to identify significant but
subtle risks or protection for CHD types.  Univariable analysis identified different points of
heterogeneity of the data. This necessitated multivariable analyses to adjust for confounding
factors in the analysis. This multivariable approach revealed that both deletion size and
genome-wide genetic principal components are associated with specific CHD phenotypes.

Study population
De-identified demographic data were evaluated from 3,746 unrelated individuals with
22q11.2DS recruited from clinical centers for 22q11.2DS in the US, Canada, Northern and
Southern Europe, Chile and Australia, including and in addition to the International 22q11.2
Brain and Behavior Consortium 16 (Full de-identified dataset is presented in Supplementary
Table S1).  Each had a clinical diagnosis of 22q11.2DS (velo-cardio-facial syndrome-
MIM#192430, DiGeorge syndrome-MIM#188400, conotruncal anomaly face syndrome).  The
type of the 22q11.2 deletion was determined using molecular diagnostic testing including
chromosomal microarray, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA; (SALSA
MLPA kit P250 DiGeorge; MRC Holland, The Netherlands 17) or research based whole
exome/genome sequencing 18-20.  We included deletions between LCR22 A-B, A-C and A-D in
this study.  Rare subjects with nested distal deletions such as B-D and C-D deletions were
excluded.  Subjects with other deletion types on 22q11.2, or those extending centromeric or
telomeric to this region, were extremely rare and were also excluded.  Both types of deletions
(B-D, C-D, or atypical deletions) were excluded due to the paucity of these samples.
Most individuals with 22q11.2DS were unrelated probands and had a de novo deletion.  
Transmitting parents and relatives with 22q11.2DS were excluded from this study for
consistency.  A total of 3,016 subjects fulfilled the criteria for the study and had cardiology and
echocardiography reports as infants and young children.  Babies and children were included,

Association between sex or self-reported race/ethnicity and deletion type  
Whole genome sequence (WGS) was available for 1,868 of the 3,016 individuals included in the
study (below). Exclusion of first-degree degree kinship among these 1,868 subjects was
confirmed using KING software21. For those with WGS, we could identify the genetic sex and
those with mismatches were already excluded.  For the 1,148 without WGS data, we used
patient identified sex for the analysis.  Proband sex (male, female) was compared across
22q11.2DS subjects using a 2×3 contingency table. Each proband was classified into one of
three 22q11.2 deletion types: A-B, A-C, and A-D.  Independence between sex and deletion-type
group was evaluated using the Pearson’s c2 test. Statistical significance was defined as
adjusted P < 0.05 for multiple comparisons.  Self-reported race/ethnicity was defined as White,
Black or African American, Asian, American Indian or Alaska Native, Natie Hawaiian or Other
Pacific Islander, Mixed race or Other (not belonging to any of the other categories).  Mixed
ethnicity is considered a combination of more than one race/ethnicity such as White with Black
or Asian. We were missing self-reported race/ethnicity for 15% of the cohort.  Self-reported
race/ethnicity analyses were restricted to the two largest self-reported race/ethnicity categories
in the cohort, “White” and “Mixed”. A 2×3 contingency table of deletion size by race/ethnicity
was constructed and data were analyzed as described for sex and deletion size.

Deletion size and genetic background contribute to heterogeneity in CHD risk
Preliminary univariable screening analyses indicated that the prevalence of CHD classes and
phenotype subgroups varied across recruitment sites and showed associations with sex,
deletion size, and genetic principal components for selected lesions (Supplementary Tables
S6-S10). In particular, strong site-to-site heterogeneity for CTD and other lesions suggested that
simple unadjusted models were insufficient to disentangle correlated demographic and genetic
effects. These observations motivated the use of a multivariable mixed-effects framework to
jointly model covariates while accounting for recruitment-site heterogeneity.

To understand how different factors jointly influence the occurrence of CHD types in
22q11.2DS, we applied the multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression method. This
approach models each class and phenotype subgroup as the outcome while simultaneously
adjusting for deletion size, sex, and population structure (PC1-PC5), with recruitment site
included as a random effect. This approach accounted for both measured covariates and
unmeasured differences across sites, such as diagnostic practices or ascertainment patterns,
and provided more reliable estimates of independent effects than univariable models.

DISCUSSION
In this report, we demonstrate that deletion size at chromosome 22q11.2 , but not sex,
influences risk for specific cardiac lesions in 22q11.2DS.  Individuals carrying the common 3Mb
A-D deletion had a reduced risk of PTA but an increased risk of SDs compared with individuals
carrying the smaller nested 1.5Mb A-B deletion.  These opposing effects suggest that
haploinsufficiency of genes within the B-D region differentially modifies susceptibility to distinct
cardiac phenotypes.  In addition, principal components reflecting genome-wide variation were

Genes that contribute to CHD types in 22q11.2DS.  The most frequent class of defects in
22q11.2DS are CTDs; they occurred in 40% of this cohort.  There are over 47 protein coding
genes that are hemizygously deleted in the 3Mb A-D region, and there also are additional non-
coding genes in this region.  A subset of the coding genes is required for cardiac development
based upon studies of mouse models, particularly for CTDs.  These include TBX1 (T-box

Heterozygous mutations of TBX1 in the absence of a deletion result in a phenocopy of

demonstrate its importance in cardiac development.  Inactivation of one copy of the gene in the
mouse results in mild lethality after birth with aortic arch anomalies (ASA and IAAB) at reduced
penetrance 44.  However, inactivation of both copies results in a perinatal lethal PTA at full
penetrance 44.  Mice with an A-B equivalent hemizygous deletion have increased lethality
compared to Tbx1 heterozygous mice and have aortic arch anomalies at higher penetrance 45.

and intracardiac muscle defects 63,64.  This sheds new light onto the fact that placental defects
can secondarily affect embryonic development including heart formation and remodeling, that
when defective causes of CHD in 22q11.2DS.  The DGCR8 gene encodes an essential micro-
RNA processing enzyme that is ubiquitously expressed in all cells.  Inactivation of mouse Dgcr8
in the mesoderm results in early embryonic lethality but inactivation in the second heart field
resulted in mid-gestational lethality with a PTA and thin ventricular walls with hemorrhage 65,66.  
Inactivation of Dgcr8 in neural crest cells results in perinatal lethality with a PTA with VSD 67.  
Therefore, Dgcr8 and perhaps micro-RNAs are required also for formation of the outflow
tract/conotruncal region of the heart.  
HIRA (Histone cell cycle regulator) encodes a multifunctional protein functioning as a
chaperone to bring histones to DNA independent of replication.  Inactivation of Hira in mice
results in pre-implantation lethality and studies in chick showed that knockdown results in a PTA
by affecting neural crest function 68.  Since its not deleted in most 22q11.2DS patients, its direct
role in human patients is yet to be established.  There are other genes in the 22q11.2 region
that may contribute to CHD when hemizygously deleted, but they have not been directly
implicated in cardiac development as of yet.

in the LCR22 A-B region, while CRKL is in the C-D region.  It is possible that haploinsufficiency
of these genes, together with those affecting basic homeostatic functions such as those
involved in mitochondrial functions 69,70 affecting genetic networks of other genes in rest of the

Limitations of this study.  Despite the large cohort size, several limitations should be
considered.  Participants were recruited at multiple clinical sites across the US, Europe, South
America, and Australia.  Although we observed ancestral heterogeneity within the cohort, the
majority of individuals were of European ancestry. This imbalance may limit the generalizability
of findings to more diverse populations. Broader representation will be important for evaluating
potential population-specific modifiers in 22q11.2DS.  Further, clinical ascertainment also varied
across sites 15.  Some centers were pediatric tertiary care hospitals, whereas others were adult
psychiatry outpatient services 14, resulting in differences in age at evaluation and referral
patterns.  Mild cardiac lesions, such as isolated aortic arch branching defects, might therefore
have been under-detected at certain sites14.  Nonetheless, all subjects were recruited from
centers of clinical excellence for 22q11.2DS and awareness of the importance of cardiac
evaluation.  Finally, bicuspid aortic valve defect was not included in this study due to its high
prevalence in the general population, although it has been reported in association with 22q11.2
deletions 71.  

Conclusions: These findings demonstrate that deletion size and genome-wide genetic
background contribute to variation in cardiac phenotypes in 22q11.2DS.  The largest A–D
deletion was associated with reduced risk of persistent truncus arteriosus but increased risk of
septal defects, indicating that deletion size influences the spectrum of cardiac outcomes in
divergent ways.  In addition, principal components capturing genome-wide population structure
were associated with selected CHD subtypes, although direct ancestry proportion estimates did
not show independent effects. Together, these results suggest that both regional gene dosage
within 22q11.2 and broader genetic background may influence lesion-specific susceptibility.
