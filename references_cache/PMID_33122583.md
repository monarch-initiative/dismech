# Common Variable Immunodeficiency, Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia, and Pancytopenia Associated With a Defect in IKAROS.
**Authors:** Yilmaz E, Kuehn HS, Odakir E, Niemela JE, Ozcan A, Eken A, Rohlfs M, Cansever M, Gok V, Aydin F, Karakukcu M, Hauck F, Klein C, Unal E, Rosenzweig SD, Patiroglu T
**Journal:** J Pediatr Hematol Oncol (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1097/MPH.0000000000001976](https://doi.org/10.1097/MPH.0000000000001976)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Mutations in IKZF1, which encodes Ikaros family zinc finger 1 (IKAROS) transcription factor, are associated with recurrent infections, cytopenia, autoimmune diseases, and hematologic malignancies. Diverse clinical phenotypes resulting from IKZF1 mutations include pulmonary fungal infections, cytopenia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), and malignancies. In this study, we aimed to assess the DNA-binding ability and pericentromeric (PC) localization of a variant of IKZF discovered in a patient.

MATERIALS AND METHODS: DNA-binding ability of a pathogenic IKZF variant was tested using electrophoretic mobility shift assay and PC localization of the variant was assessed by immunofluorescent microscopy in NIH3T3 cells.

RESULTS: Clinical features of a 3-month-old male infant who underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation because of an IKZF1 mutation-associated common variable immunodeficiency, AIHA, and pancytopenia are described. DNA studies revealed a heterozygous missense variant (IKZF1 NM_006060 c.427C>T; p.R143W). Cotransfection studies revealed that mutant R143W has a partial dominant-negative effect over PC targeting and DNA binding.

CONCLUSIONS: IKZF1 mutation must be kept in mind if neonatal AIHA, common variable immunodeficiency, and pancytopenia are observed.

Ikaros family zinc finger (ZF) 1 (IKAROS) is a transcription factor encoded by IKZF1 and regulates the expression of several target genes by binding to their promoter regions.1,2 IKAROS is a member of the IKAROS protein family, which also includes AIOLOS, HELIOS, EOS, and PEGASUS, all of which are expressed in lymphocytes. In the early 1990s, IKAROS was defined as the regulator of lymphocyte-specific genes.3 Members of the IKAROS family play critical roles in hematopoiesis, particularly in the differentiation, proliferation, and function of lymphoid cells.4,5 In this regard, IKAROS regulates both T and B cell development. Importantly, IKAROS is also involved in the activation of B cells.4 Additionally, IKAROS was shown to be expressed in and regulates the development and functions of various innate immune cells including NK cells, ILC subsets as well as plasmacytoid dendritic cells.6,7 Such diverse actions of IKAROS in distinct lineages have been attributed partly to functions of distinct domains of the protein in different lineages.6–14 The presence of various isoforms and hetero- or homo-dimeric or multimeric actions of IKAROS also allows it to act as a repressor in some and activator in other cells.4,6 Somatic and germline mutations in the IKZF1 have been associated with various hematological malignancies (especially precursor B cell leukemia) and immunodeficiency as well as T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia.1 IKAROS is essential for normal hematopoiesis, autoimmune, tumor suppression. Therefore, mutations in IKAROS were found to be related not only to hematological malignancies but also to many autoimmune diseases (Systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjogren’s) and solid tumors (lung, ovary, colorectal, liver).2 Herein, we share the clinical and molecular biological data for a patient with heterozygous missense IKZF1 variant (NM_006060 c.427C> T; p.R143W) and our experience with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).

Modeling of the IKAROS ZF1–3 amino acid sequence was performed using the iTasser server (https://zhanglab.ccmb.med.umich.edu/I-TASSER/) and the Protein Data Bank template 1mey. Model visualization, structure editing, and rotamer analysis were performed using UCSC Chimera (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimera/).

NIH3T3 cells (1×105) grown on coverslips in 6-well plates were transfected with pCMV6-AC-Myc-DDK WT or R143W, and pcDNA3-HA-IKAROS WT using Effectene (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Immunofluorescence assay was performed as previously described.15

HEK293T cells were transfected with pCMV6-AC-Myc-DDK-WT and/or mutant R143W, gel mobility shift assays were performed with LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as previously described.15 Sequences for human probes were as follows: γ satellite from human chromosome 8 (γ-Sat8) (forward: 5′-BIOTIN-GCGAGACCGCAGGGAATGCTGGGAGCCTCCC; reverse: 5′-BIOTIN- GGGAGGCTCCCAGCATTCCCTGCGGTCTCGC); IKAROS consensus-binding sequence 1 (IK-bs1) (forward: 5′-BIOTIN-TCAGCTTTTGGGAATACCCTGTCA; reverse: 5′-BIOTIN-TGACAGGGTATTCCCAAAAGCTGA).

The patient was a 3-month-old male, born to unrelated healthy parents. He was previously a healthy child. Bruises began to form on his body two weeks prior to the consultation. The family thought that these bruises might have been due to trauma. He started to pale at the same time. The patient was referred to our hospital after pancytopenia was detected in an outside center. Physical examination revealed petechia in the body and mouth mucosa, ecchymosis in the lower extremities. Abdominal examination showed no hepatosplenomegaly, no pathological lymphadenopathy. Other system examination findings were normal. He was hospitalized in our pediatric hematology-oncology clinic to investigate the etiology of pancytopenia. Laboratory tests revealed the following results: white blood cells, 1420/μL (absolute neutrophil number: 140/μL, absolute lymphocyte number: 1240/μL); hemoglobin, 5.3 g/L; and platelets, 1 000/μL. CMV/EBV antibody and DNA were negative. Tests for antinuclear antibody were negative. Direct Coombs’ test was 3+ positive. Serum IgG level (395 mg/dL) and serum IgM level (31,9 mg/dL) were detected low. Lymphocyte subsets were as follows: CD3: 1137 cells/μL, CD4: 761cells/μL, CD8: 261cells/μL, CD19: 17 cells/μL, NK: 38 cells/μL. Erythrocyte and platelet suspensions were given. Bone marrow aspiration was performed. Bone marrow was hypocellular and displayed hemophagocytosis. The patient was given intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and steroid. Fluconazole and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis was initiated. Genetic tests were requested due to bone marrow failure, familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, and immunodeficiency. Thrombocyte and erythrocyte suspension support was provided to the patient, who did not respond to IVIG and steroid treatment.

Magnetic resonance imaging was performed due to persistent vomiting in the patient. Subdural hematoma and microhemorrhages were detected. Platelet suspensions support continued. Abdominal ultrasonography was performed due to the presence of elevated liver enzymes. Thirteen mm of gallstones were detected, and ursodeoxycholic acid was started. Thorax computed tomography (CT) was performed for ongoing fever despite empirical treatment, and galactomannan was studied. Consecutive galactomannan levels were found to be high, and thorax CT showed multiple fungal balls but no cavitated lesions in the lung parenchyma. Voriconazole was started upon the diagnosis of invasive pulmonary aspergillus.

The whole-exome sequencing was performed at Ludwig-Maximilians-University, Munich, and identified a heterozygous IKZF1 mutation in the patient (Fig.1A). Three-dimensional (3D) modeling predicts that the IKAROS R143W variant results in conformational changes that destabilize the ZF2 β-hairpin containing the C-x-x-C sequence necessary to coordinate the Zn+2 ion and the loop between the β-hairpin and α-helix containing N159, the site of previously described dominant-negative IKAROS mutations (Fig.1B).15

To test the pericentromeric (PC)-heterochromatin (HC) localization and DNA binding functions of IKAROS, immunofluorescence (Fig.2A) and the EMSA experiments (Fig. 2B) were performed. IKAROS R143W mutant showed impaired DNA-binding to targets containing the core consensus sequence, and immunofluorescence microscopy revealed failure of IKAROS pericentromeric localization. Interestingly, co-transfection experiments mimicking the heterozygous condition demonstrated that the mutant R143W elicited a partial dominant-negative effect over wild type IKAROS’s DNA binding and PC-HC targeting.

The patient has an 8-year-old healthy sibling and no relatives with a similar disease history. Unrelated donor screening was initiated for the patient after confirming the absence of HLA compatible sibling and family donor. An HLA compatible (12/12) unrelated donor was found from national bone marrow banking of TURKKOK, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) was performed following the preparation regimen consisting of busulfan, fludarabine, and rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin. Methotrexate and cyclosporine were given for graft versus host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis. Engraftment day for myeloid was the 12th day of HSCT. Transplant-associated microangiopathy was considered in the patient after detection of hypertension, LDH elevation, schistocytosis in the peripheral blood smear, low haptoglobin level, increased platelet, and erythrocyte requirement in the post-transplant follow-up. Cyclosporine was discontinued and mycophenolate mofetil was started. Plasma exchange was performed. First, eltrombopag, and then romiplostim were started due to the absence of platelet engraftment. The patient’s clinical and laboratory findings improved. Platelet engraftment was observed on day one hundred and one of the HSCT with romiplostim. Steroid was started for stage IIII skin and stage II gastrointestinal system GVHD. Methylprednisolone was given at a dose of 2 mg/kg/ day for grade II skin and gastrointestinal system GVHD. The steroid was stopped after GVHD control with methylprednisolone and mycophenolate mofetil. Foscarnet was given due to CMV PCR positivity (8175 copies/ml). After the CMV PCR result of the patient came negative he was discharged on the 104th day and followed up in the outpatient clinic of HSCT. Although the patient was fully chimeric, he died because of respiratory infection and pulmonary hemorrhage on the 194th day of HSCT.

IKAROS plays an important role in the differentiation and specialization of lymphocytes including B and T cells.2 IKAROS mutations have been linked to different diseases such as autoimmune disease, leukemia, solid tumor, and common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) and combined immunodeficiency (CID) such as primary immunodeficiency.4,5 In a cohort study of 29 patients by Kuehn et al.10, clinical findings have been described as recurrent respiratory tract infection, autoimmune diseases, sepsis, B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, central nervous system infection. A decrease in total serum immunoglobulins, the absence of peripheral B lymphocyte/NK cells, and CD4/CD8 ratio reversal were found in the reported cases.4 Patients with de novo heterozygous dominant-negative mutations (N159S or N159T) have severe clinical and immunological phenotype compared to the patients with IKAROS haploinsufficiency.10,15 A total of 8 patients were reported, 7 with N159S and one with N159T, all have profound combined immunodeficiency phenotype including opportunistic infections, hypogammaglobulinemia, abnormal T cell phenotypes, and virtually absence of B cells.1,15 Our patient was admitted to our clinic with signs of bone marrow failure, common variable immunodeficiency (CVID), autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). Immunoglobulin G and M levels and B cell numbers at admission were low similar to previous findings. CD4/CD8 ratio was 2.9. We found a heterozygous missense variant mutation in the IKZF1 gene (c.427C> T; p.R143W) in our patient. The same mutation in IKZF1 was previously reported in 4 members of a family with a CVID-like clinical picture presenting with sinopulmonary infection (4/4), autoimmune disease (2/4), low B cells (3/4), and hypogammaglobulinemia (4/4).12 Our detailed in-vitro analysis of the R143W mutation confirmed that it behaves in a partial dominant-negative manner on DNA binding and PC-HC localization, and opposite to the haploinsufficiency effect originally described.12 This discrepancy raises an important and concerning point regarding functional testing and interpretation in relation to IKAROS human mutations. As an example, mutation N159S was originally reported as a haploinsufficiency defect13, but exhaustive reanalysis confirmed it was acting through a strong dominant-negative effect and exerting a severe and unique phenotype.15 Moreover, the family reported by Eskandarian et. al.12 also carried a heterozygous missense TNFSFS10 mutation (TRAIL, g.172241096, c.79G>T, p.Gln27Lys [Q27K]) that co-segregated with IKZF1 R143W variant, likely determining a blended phenotype. A partial dominant-negative effect on DNA binding and PC-HC localization, as the one determined for IKAROS R143W, may represent a novel allelic variant in the spectrum of IKAROS-associated diseases.

The ages of the patients with IKAROS gene defects are variable. Patients with gene defects from the neonatal period to 6th decade have been reported.11–14 Clinical manifestations and laboratory findings of our patient and the patients with the same IKZF1 variants are presented in Table 1.

Due to bone marrow failure symptoms, our patient was diagnosed at a very early age compared to other cases with the same mutation. However, two patients with different variants presenting with CVID and pancytopenia in the literature were diagnosed at comparable ages to our patient.5,13 The presence of both immunodeficiency and pancytopenia in all three cases indicates that wild-type IKAROS acts in both the immune system function and hematopoiesis.9

As in other cases of primary immunodeficiency, the main treatment in patients with IKZF1 defects is periodic immunoglobulin replacement therapy.9 However, this is not curative. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation was performed in some patients because of the early onset and rapid course of the disease. But there is little experience in transplantation.14 Allogeneic HSCT for IKZF1 mutations has been reported in the literature.11,15 The data of patients who underwent HSCT due to IKZF1 gene defect and the data of our patient are presented in Table 2.

In the literature, we found reports of six transplanted patients1,9,11,15,17 with IKZF1 gene defects, and p.R143W was not among them. Risk factors for the survival of patients who underwent HSCT were multifactorial.16 Additionally, early and recurrent Pneumocystis jirovecii infection was observed in four reported cases but not in our case.1

Acute GVHD has been shown to be more severe after allogeneic HSCT in mouse models with IKZF1 mutation. These findings suggest that IKZF1 mutant hosts may be under increased GVHD risk.9 Skin and gastrointestinal system GVHD developed in our patient and patient A1. GVHD was taken under control by using steroids in a short time in our patient. The post-transplant follow-up data of the patients are presented in Table 2.

In the other five cases, the myeloid engraftment was observed between +12th and + 51st days, the platelet engraftment was observed between +8th and +167th days.1, Myeloid engraftment was at +17th day in our case. Platelet engraftment was at +101st day with eltrombopag and romiplostim. Platelet count did not reach normal levels in our patient even at +188th day after transplantation similar to patient A3 and our patient received eltrombopag treatment.

There were no complications such as hepatic veno-occlusive disease, chronic GVHD after transplantation neither in our patient nor the other patients.1,9 Patient A3 died from liver failure secondary to cholangitis in the first year of transplantation. Patient A5 died from renal and pulmonary failure 40 days after transplantation. Our patient died from a respiratory infection and pulmonary hemorrhage on the +194th day of post-HSCT. In our case, the average chimerism after transplantation (between days 12 and 51) was found to be greater than 99% as in the other cases.

Three surviving patients are still under follow-up after transplantation. Intravenous immunoglobulin and antimicrobial prophylaxis were discontinued, and the routine vaccination was initiated. T, B, myeloid series are stable and in the normal range (except the absolute neutrophil count of patient A1, platelet count of patient A2). Lansky/Karnofsky performance score of the patients was evaluated as 100. The immunoglobulin and antimicrobial prophylaxis of our patient were continued on +194th day after transplantation.

In conclusion, IKZF1 mutations may lead to recurrent infections, hematological malignancies, many autoimmune diseases, solid tumors, and immune thrombocytopenia. Management of post-transplant IKZF1 mutant patients may be complicated with GVHD or infections. Early transplantation to patients with IKZF1 gene-related conditions may increase survival.
