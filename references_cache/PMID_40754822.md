# AAV-based gene therapy ameliorates neurological deficits in a mouse model of childhood-onset neurodegeneration with cerebellar atrophy.
**Authors:** Wada H, Hama N, Hasegawa K, Takamuku C, Yoshida M, Omura M, Araki A, Kuwako KI
**Journal:** Mol Ther (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymthe.2025.07.055](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymthe.2025.07.055)

## Content

Childhood-onset neurodegeneration with cerebellar atrophy (CONDCA) is an autosomal recessive pediatric disorder caused by biallelic mutations in the ATP/GTP-binding protein 1 gene, which encodes cytosolic carboxypeptidase 1 (CCP1) with deglutamylase activity. These patients typically exhibit progressive motor and cognitive impairment, often leading to childhood mortality. Despite its severe clinical course, no effective treatments have been developed. In this study, we demonstrate the efficacy of gene therapy using adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors to deliver a functional mouse Ccp1 mutant in a Ccp1-deficient mouse model of CONDCA. Expression of the N-terminally truncated form of Ccp1 (Ccp1Δ1), which retains deglutamylase activity, in neurons of Ccp1-deficient neonatal mice via a blood-brain barrier-penetrating AAV vector strongly prevented Purkinje cell degeneration during postnatal development. Furthermore, gene therapy using Ccp1Δ1 partially, albeit significantly, improved motor functions in these mice, notably enhancing voluntary gait stability. This study provides robust preclinical evidence supporting the potential of postnatal AAV gene therapy as a treatment strategy for CONDCA.

In 2018, Shashi et al., reported on 13 patients with a novel neurodevelopmental disorder currently known as childhood-onset neurodegeneration with cerebellar atrophy (CONDCA, the OMIM #618276; https://www.omim.org/entry/618276).1 Affected individuals typically present with global developmental delay, cerebellar atrophy, and muscular hypotonia early in infancy. These symptoms progressively worsen, often resulting in death.1,2,3,4,5,6 Other common features include ataxia, cognitive delay, feeding difficulties, tendon reflex abnormalities, eye movement disorders, respiratory insufficiency, spasticity, and dystonia.7 To date, an effective therapeutic strategy for CONDCA has not been established.

The ATP/GTP-binding protein 1 (AGTPBP1) gene, located on chromosome 9q21, encodes the cytosolic carboxypeptidase 1 (CCP1) protein (also known as the nervous system nuclear protein induced by axotomy 1 [Nna1]),8 which is associated with CONDCA.1 Pathogenic variants in the AGTPBP1 gene in CONDCA include missense and nonsense mutations, as well as exon deletions. Since multiple nonsense mutations in the AGTPBP1 gene lead to the loss of the CCP1/Nna1 protein (hereafter referred to as CCP1) and several missense mutations cause increased protein instability, it is strongly suggested that CONDCA arises from a loss of function of CCP1.1

In humans, CCP1 is most strongly expressed in the testis, with prominent expression in various brain regions, including the cerebellum and cerebral cortex, and in muscle, lung, and kidney (The Human Protein Atlas; https://www.proteinatlas.org). Both human CCP1 and mouse Ccp1 contain two functional domains: the cytosolic carboxypeptidase (CP) N-terminal domain, which is highly conserved in certain members of the M14 metallocarboxypeptidase family, and the catalytic zinc CP domain (Figures 1A and 3A).9,10,11 Functional analyses have shown that Ccp1 is involved in post-translational modifications of tubulin. Specifically, Ccp1 acts as a tubulin deglutamylase that removes glutamate residues from the C terminus of tubulin peptides.12,13,14,15 Through this activity, Ccp1 plays a pivotal role in the tyrosination/detyrosination and polyglutamylation/deglutamylation cycles of tubulin. Long before the first report of CONDCA, the Purkinje cell degeneration (pcd) mouse, a spontaneous mutant exhibiting cerebellar ataxia, was discovered,17 and subsequent study identified the Agtpbp1 as the causative gene.18 To date, several pcd mice with presumably distinct Agtpbp1 mutations, including pcd1J, pcd2J, pcd3J, pcd5J, pcd7J, pcd8J, and pcd9J, have been identified.7,19 Although pathological severity varies among these strains, most display ataxic symptoms. Certain pcd mice also show cognitive impairment.20,21,22

The most characteristic cellular pathology in pcd mice is severe Purkinje cell degeneration, which progresses rapidly and severely during postnatal development, with most Purkinje cells being lost within 1 month after birth, and is thought to underlie the ataxic symptoms.17,18,19 Ccp1 dysfunction leads to excessive tubulin polyglutamylation in various brain regions, including the cerebellum and cerebral cortex, of pcd- and Ccp1-deficient mice, and in the skeletal muscle of patients with CONDCA carrying the nonsense mutation in the AGTPBP1 gene.1,23 Abnormal accumulation of polyglutamylated tubulin caused by Ccp1 dysfunction is known to impair axonal vesicle transport and induce endoplasmic reticulum stress in neurons; this has been proposed as the primary mechanism of neurodegeneration in pcd mice and may also apply to patients with CONDCA.23,24,25,26

In this study, we aimed to conduct the first gene therapy investigation for CONDCA using a mouse model. Transvascular delivery of a blood-brain barrier (BBB)-penetrating adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector enabled widespread neuronal expression of a truncated mouse Ccp1 mutant with preserved enzymatic activity, significantly preventing Purkinje cell degeneration and mitigating motor deficits. These results highlight the potential of AAV-based gene therapy as a treatment for CONDCA.

A study using the mouse ortholog of CCP1 demonstrated that four CONDCA-related CCP1 missense mutations within and near the CP N-terminal domain and the CP domain (Y694D, T851M, R918W, and H990L) impaired the expression and deglutamylase activity of CCP1 (Figure 1A).1 We first characterized two additional CONDCA-related CCP1 mutations, missense mutation R799C in the CP N-terminal domain and nonsense mutation Q856∗, which causes the deletion of the CP domain (Figure 1A). To this end, mouse Ccp1 R791C and Q848∗, which align with human CCP1 R799 and Q856, respectively, were expressed in HEK293 cells. Compared to wild-type Ccp1, both Ccp1 R791C and Q848∗ showed reduced protein expression, which was notably restored by treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Figure 1B). This result suggests that these mutations destabilize Ccp1, making it more prone to proteolytic degradation. We next examined the neuronal expression of Ccp1 Q848∗ in vivo. A BBB-penetrating AAV vector expressing Ccp1 Q848∗ or Venus under the synapsin1 promoter, a small pan-neuronal promoter, was administered intravenously to wild-type mouse neonates on postnatal day 1 (P1). At 3 weeks of age, both Ccp1 Q848∗ and Venus were strongly expressed in Purkinje cells; however, by 6 weeks of age, only Venus was observed, and Ccp1 Q848∗ was barely detectable (Figures 1C and 1D). This observation further supports the instability of Ccp1 Q848∗. Our current findings, taken together with those of the previous report,1 indicate that CONDCA mutations may lead to a significant reduction in Ccp1 expression.

Since CONDCA is likely caused by the loss of CCP1 function, we generated Ccp1-deficient mice (Ccp1−/−) as a model for CONDCA to assess the efficacy of gene therapy (Figures 2A and S1; Table S1). Genome editing using the improved genome editing via oviductal nucleic acid delivery (iGONAD) method, which introduced a stop codon in exon 18 of the Agtpbp1 gene, resulted in the loss of Ccp1 expression (Figures 2A and 2B). Similar to previously reported Ccp1-deficient mice in which exons 21–22 are deleted,27 the Ccp1−/− mice generated in this study exhibited a smaller body size compared to wild-type mice (Figure S2). Importantly, Ccp1−/− mice displayed rapid Purkinje cell degeneration; it began at 4 weeks of age, and most Purkinje cells were lost by 6 weeks of age (Figures 2C and 2D). At 6 weeks of age, the Ccp1−/− mice also showed noticeable cerebellar atrophy (Figure 2E). In line with the timing of Purkinje cell loss, Ccp1−/− mice began exhibiting tremors at 4 weeks of age and displayed marked gait deficits, such as staggering and tumbling, by 6 weeks of age (Figures 2F). We next aimed to generate an AAV vector to validate CONDCA gene therapy in Ccp1−/− mice. To express functional Ccp1 in neurons throughout the brain, including Purkinje cells in the cerebellum, we utilized a transvenous administration system with a BBB-penetrating AAV vector carrying the PHP.eB capsid28 and the synapsin1 promoter employed in the experiments shown in Figures 1C and 1D. Since the full-length mouse Ccp1 exceeds the DNA packaging capacity of AAVs (∼4.7 kb, including gene expression regulatory elements), we generated three deletion mutants of Ccp1 (Ccp1Δ1-Δ3) by progressively truncating the protein from the N terminus while retaining the CP domain (Figure 3A). All Ccp1Δ1-Δ3 proteins were stably expressed in HEK293 cells; however, only Ccp1Δ1, which lacks the N-terminal 300 amino acids, retained deglutamylase activity comparable to that of full-length Ccp1, as assessed by Δ2-tubulin levels (Figures 3B and 3C). We then generated an AAV vector expressing Ccp1Δ1 (AAV-Ccp1Δ1) and evaluated its in vivo expression by intravenously administering AAV-Ccp1Δ1 to wild-type neonates at P1. At 3 weeks of age, Ccp1Δ1 was strongly expressed in neurons throughout the brain, including cortical neurons and Purkinje cells (Figures 3D–3F). Furthermore, in contrast to Ccp1 Q848∗ (Figure 1C), Ccp1Δ1 showed a stable expression in neurons, including Purkinje cells, even at 6 weeks of age (Figure 3F). To evaluate the efficacy of gene therapy for CONDCA by expressing Ccp1Δ1 protein, AAV-Ccp1Δ1 was administered to Ccp1−/− mice, and the degeneration of Purkinje cells, which is a hallmark tissue alteration in Ccp1 dysfunction, was analyzed. Since Purkinje cell loss and tremor typically begin as early as 4 weeks of age in Ccp1−/− mice (Figures 2C and 2D), AAV-Ccp1Δ1 was administered to Ccp1−/− neonates at P1. At 6 weeks of age, when most Purkinje cells were lost in Ccp1−/− mice, AAV-Ccp1Δ1-treated Ccp1−/− (Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/−) mice retained a large number of Purkinje cells throughout the cerebellum, particularly in the nodular, posterior, and central regions (Figure 4A). In contrast, AAV-Venus-treated (Venus-Ccp1−/−) mice displayed no protective effect on Purkinje cells (Figure 4A). In the cerebellar lobe VI of Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/− mice at 6 weeks of age, Purkinje cells exhibited normal dendritic morphology, and the cell density in the Purkinje cell layer was comparable to that in wild-type mice (Figures 4B and 4C). Furthermore, Purkinje cells in Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/− mice remained intact even at 4 months of age, suggesting that Ccp1Δ1 expression contributes to Purkinje cell survival rather than merely delaying cell death (Figure 4D). Notably, in Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/− mice, the preserved Purkinje cells exclusively expressed Ccp1Δ1, and Purkinje cells that did not express Ccp1Δ1 appeared to have been lost from the Purkinje cell layer (Figure 4E). These findings suggest that Ccp1Δ1 protects Purkinje cells in a cell-autonomous manner. We next investigated whether AAV-Ccp1Δ1 administration could improve motor deficits in Ccp1−/− mice using behavioral analysis. At 4 months of age, Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/− mice gained slightly but significantly more weight than did Ccp1−/− mice (Figure S2). At both 6 weeks and 4 months of age, Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/− mice did not show significant improvements in footprint patterns or forced walking performance (Figure S3). However, the incidence of tumbling and staggering during free movement was dramatically reduced in Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/− mice compared to that of Ccp1−/− mice and Venus-Ccp1−/− mice (Figures 5A and 5B; Videos S1, S2, S3, and S4). Additionally, the wire-hang test demonstrated that neuromuscular function was significantly better in Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/− mice compared to that of Ccp1−/− mice and Venus-Ccp1−/− mice (Figures 5C and 5D). In this study, we demonstrated that AAV-based gene therapy, which delivers a functional Ccp1 mutant to the brain via a non-invasive intravenous injection, significantly improved the pathology in a CONDCA mouse model. Ccp1Δ1, which retains deglutamylase activity, was delivered via AAV and stably expressed in neurons in the entire brain (Figure 3). AAV administration into neonatal mice in this study did not cause an increase in mortality or any apparent tissue abnormalities, showing no safety concerns. The delivery of Ccp1Δ1 markedly prevented Purkinje cell degeneration and partially restored motor functions, including voluntary gait stability, in Ccp1−/− mice (Figures 4 and 5). Although further advances will be necessary to enhance therapeutic efficacy for future clinical use, these findings highlight the potential utility of AAV-based gene therapy as a promising approach for the first-line treatment of CONDCA.

Since CONDCA is believed to result from a loss-of-function mutation in Ccp1, the most direct therapeutic approach would involve supplementing functional Ccp1 protein or inhibiting the abnormal accumulation of polyglutamylated tubulin caused by Ccp1 dysfunction. The former approach demonstrated that crossing pcd mice with mice expressing normal Ccp1 led to a dramatic recovery from Purkinje cell degeneration and motor deficits in pcd mice.29,30 In the latter approach, genetic deletion of tubulin-tyrosine ligase-like 1 or 4, polyglutamylases, in pcd mice strongly suppressed Purkinje cell degeneration.23,31 Building on the concept of the former approach, the present study demonstrated that AAV-based gene therapy, designed to replenish functional Ccp1, is highly effective in improving the pathology in Ccp1−/− mice and holds significant potential for clinical application.

In AAV gene therapy targeting the central nervous system, the delivery route of AAV is critical.32 For example, direct intracranial injection of AAV poses significant challenges, including high invasiveness and insufficient delivery to broad target areas. Consequently, intravenous administration, which allows non-invasive delivery of transgenes to extensive brain regions, is considered the optimal approach; however, it faces a strong challenge of penetrating the BBB. For this reason, in this study, we explored CONDCA gene therapy via intravenous administration using the AAV-PHP.eB capsid, which exhibits exceptionally high BBB penetration in the C57BL/6 mouse strain,28,33 and successfully achieved Ccp1Δ1 delivery to the entire brain, including the cerebellum (Figures 3D–3F). However, AAV-PHP.eB is not applicable to preclinical studies in non-human primates or subsequent human clinical trials, as the receptor for PHP.eB, the glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored protein LY6A, is absent in primates.34,35,36 Nevertheless, remarkable progress in capsid engineering in recent years has led to the development of promising tools, such as the modified AAV-9 with cell-penetrating peptide and AAV-MaCPNS capsids, which can penetrate the BBB in non-human primates.37,38 As capsid engineering techniques, such as in silico design and directed evolution, continue to evolve in tandem with innovations in human organoid technologies, the emergence of AAV tools capable of BBB penetration for human clinical applications is expected to be a reality in the near future.39

This study, the first to explore gene therapy for CONDCA in an animal model, marks a substantial advancement; however, unresolved challenges related to functional recovery persist, warranting further investigation. Although Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/− mice showed a significant reduction in tumbling and staggering during free locomotion, along with improved performance in the wire-hang test, no significant improvements were observed in the footprint and rotarod tests (Figures 5 and S3). In contrast, a previous study demonstrated that crossing pcd3J mice with the mice expressing wild-type Ccp1 under the control of the Purkinje cell-specific L7 promoter not only suppressed Purkinje cell degeneration but also significantly improved performance in both the footprint and rotarod tests.29 This study strongly suggests that the motor deficits in pcd mice are primarily driven by the detrimental effects of the Agtpbp1 gene mutations in Purkinje cells, while the direct impact of these mutations on other tissues, including the skeletal muscles, appears to be minimal. A possible explanation for the discrepancy in the results between the previous study on pcd3J mice29 and the present study is that, unlike mouse genetic approaches, AAV-Ccp1Δ1 administration does not induce Ccp1Δ1 expression in all Purkinje cells, particularly in the anterior region of the cerebellum, where viral transduction efficiency is lower than that in other regions (Figure 4A). Consequently, complete functional recovery of the cerebellar circuit may not have been achieved in Ccp1Δ1-Ccp1−/− mice. Another possibility is that Ccp1Δ1 is unable to fully recapitulate the functions of Ccp1. Although Ccp1Δ1 possesses deglutamylase activity sufficient to prevent Purkinje cell degeneration in Ccp1−/− mice, the absence of the N-terminal 300 amino acids may disrupt some of Ccp1’s functions, potentially affecting cellular processes in Purkinje cells that are crucial for cerebellar circuit-mediated locomotor control. In this context, circumventing the size limitation of AAV to enable the expression of full-length Ccp1 instead of Ccp1Δ1 is crucial for acquiring the full function of Ccp1. The use of a hybrid dual-vector system, which splits a single gene into two fragments and expresses them from separate vectors to reconstruct a large protein through strategies such as splicing recombination,40,41,42 is expected to reveal whether the introduction of full-length Ccp1 can further restore locomotor function in Ccp1−/− mice.

Although we evaluated the therapeutic potential of gene therapy for CONDCA using cerebellar phenotypes that have been well established in previous research, its clinical applicability is ultimately dependent on the efficacy of this approach in restoring the broad range of neurological deficits observed in patients with CONDCA. Accordingly, future studies should also aim to determine whether cognitive impairments, a further major symptom of CONDCA, can be ameliorated by the administration of gene therapy. Given that, to date, the specific brain regions and cell types associated with the cognitive deficits in patients with CONDCA have yet to be sufficiently clarified, their identification will be important for defining targets for gene therapy in CONDCA. Despite these challenges, we established that the BBB-penetrating AAV vector used in this study, driven by the synapsin1 promoter, can successfully deliver Ccp1Δ1 to a wide range of neuronal types in the brain, including cortical neurons (Figures 3D and 3E). Consequently, this approach may also have effects on transgene expression in, as yet to be identified, cell populations implicated in the cognitive impairments associated with CONDCA. Further investigations of the effects of AAV-Ccp1Δ1 administration on cognitive impairments in pcd mice or Ccp1−/− mice will provide valuable insight into the therapeutic potential of gene therapy for the treatment of CONDCA.

The open reading frame (ORF) of mouse Agtpbp1, which encodes the Ccp1 protein, was amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from a cDNA library derived from the P12 mouse pontine nucleus and subsequently cloned into the pCAG-neo vector (Wako). Truncated Ccp1 variants, including Q848∗ (1–847 amino acids), Δ1 (301–1,218 amino acids), Δ2 (494–1,218 amino acids), and Δ3 (768–1,218 amino acids), were PCR amplified from pCAG-neo-Ccp1 (1–1,218 amino acids) and cloned into the SalI and NotI restriction sites of pCAG-neo. The pCAG-neo-Ccp1 R791C plasmid was constructed using the KOD -Plus- Mutagenesis Kit (TOYOBO). The hemagglutinin (HA) tag was inserted at the 3′-end of the Ccp1 ORFs. The following plasmids were purchased: pENN.AAV.hSyn.HI.eGFP-Cre.WPRE.SV40 (Addgene plasmid #105540), pUCmini-iCAP-PHP.eB (Addgene plasmid #103005),28 and pHelper (Agilent Technologies). To construct pENN.AAV.hSyn.HI.Ccp1 Q848∗/Ccp1Δ1/Venus.WPRE.SV40, the sequence encoding eGFP-Cre was excised from pENN.AAV.hSyn.HI.eGFP-Cre.WPRE.SV40 using NcoI and HindIII restriction enzymes, and the PCR-amplified sequences of Ccp1 Q848∗ (HA-tagged), Ccp1Δ1 (HA-tagged), or Venus were cloned into these sites.

Ccp1−/− mice were generated using the iGONAD method.43,44,45 The CRISPR RNA (crRNA, 5′-GTGCCCATACGGATCCACCA-3′), trans-activating crRNA (tracrRNA, #1072533), and Alt-R S.p.Cas9 Nuclease V3 (Cas9 protein, 10 μg/μL, #1081059) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). The crRNA and tracrRNA were dissolved in autoclaved water to a concentration of 200 μM and stored at −80°C until just before use. To prepare the chimeric guide RNA (gRNA), crRNA and tracrRNA were mixed in a 1:1 ratio, incubated at 94°C for 2 min, and then annealed at room temperature (RT) for 10 min. The electroporation solution was prepared by mixing the gRNA, Cas9 protein, 1% Fast Green (Waldeck GmbH & Co. KG), and Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a volume ratio of 3:1:1:5.

One day prior to the iGONAD procedure, wild-type female mice (C57BL/6J) were mated with male mice between 17:00 and 19:00. The following morning, between 9:00 and 10:00, the presence of a vaginal plug was visually confirmed. On the same day, between 16:00 and 19:00, the pregnant females were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane (Viatris) and their body surfaces were sterilized with 70% ethanol. The oviducts were then exposed, and the electroporation solution was injected into the ampulla of the oviduct using a glass capillary with an outer diameter of approximately 100 μm. After the solution had diffused by gentle rubbing of the surface of the oviduct, a small piece of paper soaked in warmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was placed over the ampulla. The oviduct was held with a forceps-type electrode (CUY650P3, Nepagene) and was electroporated using NEPA21 electroporator (Nepagene) under the following conditions: ±40 V, 5 ms pulse duration, 50 ms interval; 3 pulses with 10% decay for the poring pulse and ±10 V, 50 ms pulse duration, 50 ms interval, 3 pulses with 40% decay for the transfer pulse. After electroporation, the oviduct was returned to the abdominal cavity and the abdominal wall and skin closed.

To generate a Ccp1−/− mouse, the genotypes of offspring born via the iGONAD method were confirmed by DNA sequencing using the primer 5′-ACGGAGATTACAGCCAGGTGCA-3’. The founder mouse carrying both alleles, one with the intact sequence and the other with a 2-bp deletion in exon 18, was selected (Figures 2A and S1). To assess potential off-target effects, the top ten predicted off-target sites in the founder mouse, identified using the CRISPR-Cas9 guide RNA design checker (IDT, https://sg.idtdna.com/site/order/designtool/index/CRISPR_SEQUENCE), were sequenced, and no off-target activity was detected (Table S1). After crossing Ccp1+/− male and female mice, the genotypes of their offspring were determined at P1 by PCR using the following primers: 5′-CTGCTGCTTTGGCCAGCTCACT-3’ (common forward), 5′-ACGGATCCACCATGGT-3’ (wild-type allele-specific reverse), and 5′-TACGGATCCCATGGTC-3’ (knockout allele-specific reverse). PCR was performed with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 min, followed by a 3-step PCR program: 98°C for 10 s, 59.9°C for 5 s, and 72°C for 5 s (26 cycles).

C57BL/6J mice were bred in the animal facility of Shimane University School of Medicine, Japan. All animal care and experimental procedures were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines approved by the Experimental Animal Care Committee of Shimane University School of Medicine (approval number: IZ3-126, IZ3-129, IZ6-52, and IZ6-53). The day of birth was designated as P0. Mice of both sexes were used for analysis.

Recombinant AAV vectors were generated by transfecting HEK293 cells with the following plasmids: pENN.AAV.hSyn.HI.Ccp1 Q848∗/Ccp1Δ1/Venus.WPRE.SV40, pUCmini-iCAP-PHP.eB, and pHelper, as described previously.46,47 Briefly, HEK293 cells were cultured in 10 cm dishes with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and transfected using the polyethylenimine method with PEI MAX (Polysciences). Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the culture medium containing the transfection reagent was replaced with serum-free DMEM. Six days after the medium exchange, the AAV-containing supernatant was harvested and concentrated using Vivaspin 20 with a 100,000 MWCO PES membrane (Sartorius). The final volume of the AAV solution was approximately 200 μL. The viral genomic titer was determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR). Genomic DNA was extracted from the concentrated viral preparation, and qPCR was performed using THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO) and primers targeting the WPRE sequence 5′-CTGTTGGGCACTGACAATTC-3′ and 5′-GAAGGGACGTAGCAGAAGGA-3'. The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 min, followed by a 2-step PCR program, 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s (40 cycles). For viral transduction, wild-type and Ccp1−/− mice at P1 were intravenously injected with 30 μL of the AAV solution (AAV-hSyn-Ccp1 Q848∗/Ccp1Δ1/Venus, 1.5 × 1013 viral genome) in the retro-orbital sinus. Injections were performed using a 0.5 mL syringe with a 30-gauge needle (NIPRO).

HEK293 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For expression analysis of Ccp1 and its mutants, cells were transfected with 5.0 or 10.0 μg of pCAG-neo-Ccp1 full-length, R791C, Q848∗, Δ1, Δ2, or Δ3 plasmids using NEPA21 electroporator and harvested 41 h post-transfection. For proteasome inhibition, cells were treated with 5 μM MG132 (MedChemExpress) or dimethyl sulfoxide as a vehicle control at 24 h after transfection and incubated for an additional 17 h.

Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described.48 Briefly, mice were transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS (pH 7.4), and their brains were dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA in PBS at 4°C overnight. Tissue sections (50–60 μm thick) were prepared using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica). The sagittal floating sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS at RT for 15 min, followed by incubation in TNB blocking buffer (PerkinElmer) at RT for 1 h. Sections were then incubated at 4°C overnight with primary antibodies diluted in TNB blocking buffer, followed by a 1.5 h incubation at RT with secondary antibodies in TNB blocking buffer. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-HA (3F10, Roche, 1:2,000), anti-calbindin (214 004, Synaptic Systems, 1:1,000), anti-GFP (GFP-1010, Aves, 1:1,000), and anti-calbindin (CB955, Sigma-Aldrich, 1:40,000). The following secondary antibodies were used at 1:1,000: Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated donkey anti-rat IgG (712-605-153, Jackson ImmunoResearch; A-48272, Invitrogen), Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-guinea pig immunoglobulin (Ig)G (706-585-148, Jackson ImmunoResearch), and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-chicken IgY (703-545-155, Jackson ImmunoResearch). Nuclear DNA was stained with 10 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 (DOJINDO). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X810, Keyence).

Tissue sections (50–60 μm thick) were incubated in a 0.2% cresyl violet solution at RT for 2 min, followed by a brief rinse in distilled water. The sections underwent dehydration in a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%) at RT for 1–5 min, were subsequently cleared with xylene at RT for 10 min, and mounted using Entellan new (Merck Millipore). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X810, Keyence).

To quantify Purkinje cell density, Purkinje cells labeled with a calbindin antibody along a 500 μm segment of the Purkinje cell layer in lobe VI of the cerebellum were counted using image analysis software (BZ-H4M, Keyence).

Western blot analysis was performed as previously described.49 Briefly, cells or mouse brain tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Nacalai Tesque), incubated on ice for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm at 4°C for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a TaKaRa BCA protein Assay Kit (TAKARA). Extracts were separated by 7%, 9%, or 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, electroblotted to a polyvinylidene fluoride microporous membrane (Merck Millipore), and subsequently incubated with the following antibodies: anti-CCP1 (14067-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1,000), anti-Δ2-tubulin (AB3203, Millipore, 1:1,000), anti-HA (clone 3F10, Sigma-Aldrich, 1:5,000), and anti-β-actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:1,000). After incubation with peroxidase conjugated IgGs (Rockland, Jackson ImmunoResearch), the proteins were visualized using an ECL kit (Bio-Rad) with a luminescence image system (ImageQuant 800, Amersham). Signal intensities were quantified using ImageQuant TL (Amersham).

Behavioral analyses, including the free movement, wire-hang, footprint, and rotarod tests, were conducted at 6 weeks and 4 months of age, as previously described, with minor modifications.50,51,52 For the free movement test, the spontaneous behavior of the mice was recorded on a video for 5 min in a translucent plastic box (29 × 18 × 12 cm). Tumbling or staggering events were counted during the observation period. In the wire-hang test, mice were placed on a wire mesh (10 × 10 cm), which was then inverted and positioned above a container (28 cm in height). The time until the mouse fell was recorded, with a maximum duration of 8 min. Each trial was performed five times, with an 8-min interval between trials. The highest and lowest scores were excluded, and remaining three scores averaged. For the footprint test, the soles of the forelimbs and hindlimbs were painted with red and black ink, respectively. Mice were placed on a white paper (15 cm wide, 42 cm long) and allowed to walk toward a shelter. The stride lengths of the forelimbs and hindlimbs were measured. In the rotarod test, mice were tested using a rotarod apparatus (RTR-M5, MELQUEST). On the first 2 days, mice underwent a 300-s training session on the apparatus, rotating at a constant speed of 10 rpm. On the third day, the latency to fall was measured during a 300-s trial, in which the rotarod accelerated from 0 to 60 rpm. The data were obtained by averaging the scores from three trials, with a 5-min interval between each.
