BACKGROUND: Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a significant 
postoperative complication following femoral neck fractures (FNFs) in 
adolescents, that has garnered considerable attention from researchers. Despite 
this interest, the incidence of ONFH in adolescents post-FNF surgery has not 
been extensively evaluated. To contribute to the body of research, we performed 
a meta-analysis utilizing articles sourced from multiple databases, with an 
emphasis on the rate of ONFH occurrence in young adolescents following FNF 
surgery.
OBJECTIVE: The purpose of this study was to investigate the incidence of ONFH 
after FNFs in adolescents worldwide and analyse potential risk factors affecting 
its occurrence to provide guidance for the treatment and rehabilitation after 
FNFs in adolescents and reduce the incidence of ONFH.
METHODS: A comprehensive search of medical literature databases, including 
MEDLINE, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Embase, was conducted to 
identify relevant studies on ONFH and its associated risk factors after surgical 
treatment for FNFs in adolescents, covering the period from the establishment of 
the database to April 2024. Studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria 
were excluded. The study was conducted based on the preferred reporting items 
for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA). Literature extraction, 
management, and data entry were performed using Zotero 6.0 and Excel 2016. The 
meta-analysis was conducted using R Studio 4.2.2. Subgroup analysis, sensitivity 
analysis, and assessment of publication bias were performed to explore sources 
of heterogeneity and evaluate the reliability of the results. This study has 
been registered on Prospero. (registration number: CRD4202452794; date of 
registration: 03/04/2024) RESULTS: A total of 17 publications involving 862 
patients with FNFs were included in this meta-analysis. The findings revealed 
that the incidence of ONFH after FNF surgery in adolescents was 24.02% [95% CI 
(0.2118, 0.2712)]. The included studies demonstrated good consistency, and no 
publication bias was observed.
CONCLUSION: This study revealed that the incidence of ONFH after FNFs in 
adolescents is high (24.02%). Early screening and effective treatment of 
postoperative patients are crucial.

Femoral neck fracture (FNF) is a common type of proximal femur fracture, accounting for 53% of all proximal femur fractures [1], usually occurring in middle-aged and elderly people. The incidence of femoral neck fractures in adolescents is lower than in adults. In adult patients, the primary cause of osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is trauma, usually following a proximal hip fracture or dislocation [2]. The prevalence of hip fracture in children is < 1% than in adults, with a reported child/adult ratio of 1/130 [3]. It has been reported that the incidence of FNFs in adolescents is less than 1% [4]. They are characterized by severe pain and swelling, shortening of the legs, and deformity. These injuries may also trigger soft tissue injury, shock, internal bleeding, etc. ONFH, non-union, and premature epiphyseal closure are major complications after injury [5, 6], with ONFH being the most severe and frequent complication. Traumatic ONFH can result from damage to and disruption of the vascular network surrounding the femoral head [6]. The occurrence of ONFH is also influenced by the patient’s baseline condition at the time of fracture and the treatment employed. The causes of FNFs in adolescents are typically significant trauma. Fractures are often complex, with notable displacement, and most are unstable fractures, resulting in substantial vascular destruction around the femoral head [7], insufficient blood supply to the bone, and death of osteocytes and bone marrow cells, leading to progressive structural damage and collapse of the femoral head [8]. The structural characteristics of adolescents include limited blood supply, a small diameter of the epiphysis and femoral neck, high demands for joint function, and high expectations for recovery [9, 10]. Therefore, it is relatively difficult to treat osteonecrosis of the femoral head after femoral neck fractures in adolescents, with most of them having to undergo total hip arthroplasty (THA) at middle and old ages if not treated in time [11].

At present, no treatment method for ONFH that occurs after FNF in teenagers yields satisfactory long-term outcomes [12]. How to predict the incidence of ONFH is extremely important due to the shortage of relevant treatments. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to systematically review and analyse the literature worldwide regarding the incidence of ONFH following FNFs in adolescents to provide strong evidence to support early clinical screening and prevention of ONFH after FNFs in adolescents.

A comprehensive search was conducted in major medical literature databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Embase, to identify relevant studies in the field of ONFH and FNF. The search ranged from the establishment of the databases until January 1, 2024. The search terms used included terms such as “Osteonecrosis of the femoral head”, “Femur Head Necrosis”, “Avascular Necrosis of the Femoral Head”, “Prevalence”, “Epidemiology”, “Incidence”, “Secondary Attack Rate”, “Incidence Proportion”, “Cumulative Incidence”, “Person-time Rate”, “Femoral neck fracture” and others. (For more details regarding the search strategy, please refer to the Appendix.)

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) original studies, including cross-sectional, case‒control, or cohort design observational studies with a sample size exceeding eight cases, (2) adolescent patients between nine and 19 years old who suffered traumatic FNFs, regardless of race, sex, region, or other demographic factors, (3)treatment involving closed or open reduction internal fixation, (4) ONFH was detected through imaging during follow-up, and the total number of cases and relevant information were recorded, and (5) studies within the period from database establishment to April 2024.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) study subjects with concurrent ipsilateral intertrochanteric fractures, subtrochanteric fractures, femoral stem fractures, or other hip periarticular fractures, (2) study subjects with concurrent pathological fractures or a history of long-term high-dose glucocorticoid use or alcohol abuse, (3) case reports, animal studies, literature reviews, conference papers, and studies lacking full-text availability, and (4) studies viewed as low quality by literature evaluation.

Two researchers independently conducted the literature search and screening process, adhering to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. EndNote X9 was employed for efficient management of the collected literature. Initially, the screening involved a comprehensive examination of titles and abstracts. The full texts of the selected articles were subsequently carefully reviewed to exclude those that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Finally, a quality assessment of the included literature was performed, and any articles rated as low quality were excluded. Any uncertainties or inconsistencies were resolved through a central discussion between the researchers. If needed, a third researcher was consulted to resolve any remaining conflicts.

The quality assessment was conducted under the Agency for Health care Research and Quality (AHRQ) rating scale (Schedule 1). The AHRQ consists of 11 items, each of which is evaluated as “Yes,” “No,” or “Unclear.” A score of 1 was assigned for a response of “Yes,” whereas a score of zero was given for “No” or “Unclear.” The total score for the 11 items ranged from zero to 11. The literature was categorized according to the total score as follows: zero-three for low-quality studies, four-seven for medium-quality studies, and eight-11 for high-quality studies (Table 1).

A systematic search was conducted in multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The initial literature search yielded a total of 191 articles, among which 92 duplicates were excluded, and 66 articles were further eliminated according to the evaluation of titles and abstracts. Through subsequent rounds of rescreening and quality assessment, an additional 16 articles were screened out because of their inconsistency with this study and the inability to obtain the full text. Ultimately, 17 literature sources meeting the predetermined criteria were selected for inclusion in this study (Fig. 1).

The meta-analysis included a total of 17 articles, consisting of 15 cross-sectional studies, one prospective randomized controlled study, and one longitudinal study. There was a total of 862 patients with FNFs. Detailed information regarding the characteristics of the patients can be found in Table 2.

The extracted information encompassed two main categories. First, general information, which included details such as the first author’s name, country, year of publication, source of cases, and study type, was collected. Second, various study characteristics, including sample size, number of ONFH cases, gender distribution, cause of trauma, fracture displacement status, time interval between injury and treatment, modality of reduction, quality of reduction, type of internal fixation, initiation time of postoperative weight-bearing, duration of follow-up, and age, were recorded. To ensure accuracy, the extracted information was processed in Excel 2016. Prior to data entry, the data were checked by two individuals to maintain consistency.

Statistical analysis was conducted using R Studio 4.2.2. The metaprop function was applied to combine effect sizes of single-group rates. Prior to combination, data transformation of individual ONFH rates and normality tests of the transformed rates were carried out. The selection of appropriate estimation methods depended on the results of the normality tests. Heterogeneity between the results of the included studies was assessed by combining the χ2 test and I2. I2 values of zero, 25, 50, and 75% were used to categorize the magnitude of heterogeneity as none, low, medium, and high, respectively. In accordance with the Cochrane review guidelines, an I2 value ≥ 50% indicated the use of a random-effects model for combining effect sizes, whereas a common-effects model was employed in cases where heterogeneity was lower. For the meta-analysis of risk factors associated with ONFH, the effect sizes were represented by the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Funnel plots were generated using the funnel function to visually assess publication bias in the literature. Additionally, the metabias() function, combined with the Egger linear regression method, was used to perform statistical testing for publication bias. Sensitivity analysis of the included studies was conducted using the metainf() function. This analysis involved calculating the combined effect size after excluding the included studies to assess the quality and consistency of the study results. The significance level for all tests was set at α = 0.05.

The meta-analysis of ONFH rates in the 17 included papers revealed no significant heterogeneity among the studies (P = 0.02, I2 = 45%). As a result, a common effect model was employed for the analysis. The combined estimate for the ONFH rate was 24.02% [95% CI (0.2118, 0.2712)], as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Table 3 presents the key findings from the subgroup analysis, encompassing seven distinct subgroups: continent, year of publication, follow-up time, trauma-surgery waiting time, gender, modality of reduction and classification of fracture. The results of these seven subgroup analyses were all based on a fixed effects model.

Most of the 17 population-based studies were from Asia (n = 682), while two were from Europe (n = 36), two were from Africa (n = 74), and one was from America (n = 70). The incidence of ONFH was 20.97% (95% CI, 17.98-24.31%) in Asia (with low heterogeneity [I2 = 25%; P = 0.19]), 28.57% (95% CI, 18.40-40.62%) in America, 36.42% (95% CI, 22.40-53.20%) in Europe (with no heterogeneity [I2 = 0; P = 0.46]), and 36.67% (95% CI, 26.46-48.24%) in Africa (with no heterogeneity [I2 = 0; P = 0,38]). The meta-analysis results demonstrated that continents are not heterogeneous sources of the incidence rate of ONFH after FNFs in adolescents, but it seems that the incidence rate in Africa is higher than that in other regions (Table 3).

A sex difference in the prevalence of ONFH was observed in five out of the 17 included clinical studies. These five studies involved a total of 136 women (41.2%) and 194 men (58.8%). The incidence of ONFH was 27.72% (95% CI, 20.87-35.81%) for women (with no heterogeneity [I2 = 0; P < 0.57]) and 21.79% (95% CI, 16.44-28.29%) for men (with no heterogeneity [I2 = 0; P = 0.46]) (Table 3). The incidence of ONFH appeared to be lower among men than among women (Table 3).

By subgroup analysis of fracture types, we found differences in the incidence of femoral head necrosis in adolescents with different types of femoral neck fractures. Adolescent femoral neck fractures were classified according to a system described by Delbet and modified by Colonna (Fig 4). The incidence rates of femoral head necrosis after type I femoral neck fracture were 50.00% (95% CI, 16.79-83.21%) (with no heterogeneity [I2 = 0; P = 1.00]) and 30.43% (95% CI, 20.93-41.95%) (with medium heterogeneity [I2 = 51%; P = 0.04]) for type II femoral neck fracture, 20.63% (95% CI, 14.75-28.09%) (with no heterogeneity [I2 = 0%; P = 0.64]) for type III femoral neck fracture and 31.85% (95% CI, 3.00-87.58%) (with medium heterogeneity [I2 = 66%; P = 0.09]) for type IV femoral neck fracture. There appears to be a greater probability of postoperative necrosis of the femoral head in young adults with type I femoral neck fractures (Table 3).

To understand whether there was a link between treatment modality and the incidence of ONFH, we performed a subgroup analysis of different treatment modalities. The common treatment methods for FNFs include closed reduction internal fixation (CRIF) and open reduction internal fixation (ORIF). The incidence of ONFH after CRIF was 23.78% (95% CI, 16.93-32.31%) (with no heterogeneity [I2 = 0%; P = 0.72]), and 23.69% (95% CI, 17.10-31.85%) (with low heterogeneity [I2 = 39%; P = 0.18]) after ORIF (Table 3).

To understand whether there was an association between time and the incidence of ONFH, we conducted subgroup analysis according to publication year. The publication years of the 17 articles were divided into five subgroups, each spanning a five-year interval, as a common effects model (Table 2). The subgroup analysis revealed that the incidence of ONFH also varied across publication time periods. The incidence of ONFH was 39.62% (95% CI, 26.45-54.0%) between 1999 and 2004, 15.7% (95% CI, 10.12-23.55%) between 2004 and 2009, 19.6% (95% CI, 12.11-30.15%) between 2009 and 2014, 21.8% (95% CI, 10.18-40.67%) between 2014 and 2019, and 23.71% (95% CI, 19.84-28.06%) between 2019 and 2024. The meta-analysis indicated that the year of publication did not contribute to the source of heterogeneity in the combined incidence rate. However, when the combined incidence of the four subgroups was compared (since only one related article was published between 1999 and 2014, the incidence of ONFH over five years may have been skewed by the small number of articles published), the incidence of ONFH after FNFs in adolescents showed an overall increasing trend over time (Table 3).

In this study, the term “follow-up time” refers to the period from FNF surgery to the diagnosis of ONFH. To analyse the effect of study follow-up time on the incidence of ONFH, we divided patients into < 24 months, 24–48 months, and > 48 months and performed subgroup analysis. The results revealed that the incidence of ONFH in patients with less than 24 months of follow-up was 24.18% (95% CI, 19.78-29.2%), 22.13% (95% CI, 17.58-27.46%) at 2448 months, and 28.35% (16.9-43.49%) at more than 48 months (Table 3).

Through the meta-analysis mentioned above, we also reported that the incidence was greater in the African region, the time of onset was between 1999 and 2004, the follow-up time was greater than 48 months, the waiting time for treatment was less than 24 h, the fracture type was Delbet type I, the treatment mode was closed reduction and internal fixation, and the patients were male. The results of heterogeneity analysis revealed that the fracture classification subgroup had the greatest influence on the degree of heterogeneity, whereas other factors had less of an impact, suggesting that the different fracture classifications were the source of heterogeneity (Table 3).

Although FNFs represent less than 1% of all fractures in children, ONFH is more likely to occur after fracture and treatment [31]. The main reasons may be as follows. (1) Compared with those of adults, the femoral neck in children is made up of strong bone and has a thick periosteal cover, and they often suffer from strong violent injury that can cause fracture, which is the main reason for the high incidence of ONFH [28, 32]. (2) Due to the strong external force of the injury, there is obvious displacement of the fracture of the femur. Moreover, the blood supply of the fracture end is severely damaged, and reduction and internal fixation are more difficult than in adults. Therefore, the operation may further aggravate local vascular injury. (3) The blood circulation of the femoral head of children is not well developed, and establishing effective collateral circulation after injury is difficult. (4) Due to the barrier effect of the epiphyseal plate, the intramedullary femoral nutrient artery cannot enter the femoral head to support the blood supply of the femoral head [28]. (5) Intraoperative plants (such as screws) pass through the unclosed epiphyseal plate, damaging the epiphysis [33, 34]. This meta-analysis systematically included studies on postoperative complications of FNFs in adolescents and reached a preliminary conclusion: the global incidence of femoral head necrosis after femoral neck fractures in adolescents was 24.02% [95% CI (0.2118, 0.2712)]. This conclusion is in general agreement with the studies of Kofoed H., Mont MA and Panteli M [35–37].

In this study, subgroup analyses were conducted on seven factors, including the fracture classification, in adolescent patients with FNFs to identify potential factors influencing the combined rate of ONFH. However, no significant factors resulted in fluctuations in the necrosis rate. The heterogeneity observed may be attributed to other factors not included in the statistical analysis, such as surgery duration, intraoperative bleeding, surgical technique and approach, internal fixation method, and surgeon experience. To analyse these factors thoroughly, more detailed clinical study data are needed.

According to the subgroup analysis, the incidence rate in Africa is much higher than that in Asia, which may be related to the differences in economic development, medical level, main labour mode and race between the two regions. Only one paper from the Americas region was included, and the results were not representative, so we performed only a descriptive analysis.

According to the subgroup analysis, the combined morbidity rate was 25.00% [95% CI (3.19-65.09%)] in the < 24 h group, which was significantly greater than that in the > 24 h group. Previous studies have shown that prolonged preoperative waiting time is often associated with a greater incidence of postoperative complications, such as lower extremity venous thrombosis and periprosthetic infection, which are independent risk factors for the aforementioned complications [38, 39]. However, the relationship between the preoperative waiting time and ONFH remains unclear in earlier studies, as the traction status and position of the affected limb during the waiting period are important confounding factors [40]. Unfortunately, in our study, these particular data could not be effectively extracted. As a result, the accuracy of the subgroup analysis results may be compromised.

In the subgroup analysis focusing on the classification of fractures, the incidence of femoral head necrosis after Delbet type I fracture was much greater than that after the other three fracture types. This finding is similar to the results of Akkari M et al. [41]. In their study, the incidence of femoral head necrosis after type I fracture was approximately 60%. Type I fractures are intraarticular fractures. In a meta-analysis, AlKhatib N et al. [42] proposed that when the fracture end is located in the articular capsule, the formation of a large number of haematomas leads to increased pressure in the articular capsule, which can further compress the remaining blood vessels, affecting the blood supply of the femoral head. This can subsequently lead to avascular necrosis of the femoral head. Therefore, after the occurrence of a type I fracture, immediate treatment should be employed to reduce the occurrence of complications.

In the subgroup analysis focusing on the modality of reduction, the combined morbidity rate was 23.69% [95% CI (17.10-31.85%)] in the ORIF group, which was slightly lower than the combined morbidity rate of 23.78% [95% CI (16.93-32.31%)] in the CRIF group. Although available studies do not explicitly indicate that CRIF leads to a higher rate of ONFH than ORIF, it is known that different surgical approaches have varying effects on the blood supply near the femoral neck. For example, Steffen et al. [43] conducted a study on the impact of different approaches for hip surface replacement and reported that, compared with the posterior approach, the anterolateral approach could cause higher oxygenation levels near the femoral neck and relatively lower postoperative ONFH rates. Researchers have noted that the posterior approach, which involves separating the short external rotator muscle group, potentially damages the medial rotator femoral artery, thereby affecting the blood supply to the femoral head [44]. Although ORIF may be more invasive, it enables precise anatomic repositioning under direct vision, facilitating the recovery of blood flow after displaced FNFs. Acceptable reduction criteria for displaced FNFs include a neck-shaft angle between 130° and 150° and an anteversion angle between 0° and 15°, with a valgus angle of up to 15°. In contrast, the varus angle and posterior tilt may lead to postoperative complications such as osteonecrosis [45]. A meta-analysis of FNFs in children revealed no significant difference in the rate of ONFH between the ORIF and CRIF groups [46]. These findings suggest that ORIF may be more beneficial for fracture recovery and reduce the incidence of ONFH when the fracture is more displaced and difficult to correct with CRIF.

Our research revealed that the global incidence of osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) following femoral neck fractures (FNFs) in adolescents is 24.02%. Among the classifications of femoral neck fractures, the incidence of ONFH is notably greater for Delbet type I fractures, at approximately 60%. Although there is little difference in the impact of various reduction methods on the incidence of ONFH, ORIF may be beneficial for reducing ONFH rates in complex fractures. However, this study has certain limitations. First, at present, international studies on femoral head necrosis after femoral neck fractures in adolescents are still insufficient, resulting in fewer studies included in meta-analyses and unbalanced regional distributions, which may affect the junction accuracy of fruit. Second, few studies were included in the individual subgroup analysis, and the value of the combined results was limited. Third, confined by the research design of the included studies, multiple biases are inevitable, which may affect the reliability of the results. Further studies are needed to collect more comprehensive samples and data to address these limitations and better understand treatment outcomes for this disease.

In summary, current evidence illustrates that the incidence of femoral head necrosis after femoral neck fractures in adolescents is high. In addition, it is necessary to further optimize the surgical plan for femoral neck fracture and implement sufficient postoperative rehabilitation to ensure early identification and effective treatment of patients at risk. Moreover, clinicians must implement rigorous follow-up protocols for adolescents who have undergone surgical treatment for femoral neck fractures. This should include regular monitoring and imaging studies to detect early signs of osteonecrosis of the femoral head and carry out prompt intervention. When osteonecrosis of the femoral head occurs, early intervention and joint preservation treatment (JPT) should be performed, and total hip arthroplasty should be delayed as much as possible. For example, core decompression (CD) combined with bone marrow-derived cell therapy (BMCT) was used to treat early ONFH [47, 48]. When osteonecrosis of the femoral head progresses to the advanced stage, total hip arthroplasty, which is a mature orthopaedic surgery that can improve the joint function and symptoms of patients and restore the length of the lower limbs, should be selected [49]. In conclusion, prioritizing early screening and effective treatment can significantly improve patient outcomes and enhance the overall quality of life for these young individuals.
