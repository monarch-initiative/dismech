</div></section></section><section><section><section><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>α-Tectorin (TECTA), β-tectorin (TECTB), and carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule 16 (CEACAM) are secreted glycoproteins that are present in the tectorial membrane (TM), an extracellular structure overlying the hearing organ of the inner ear, the organ of Corti. Previous studies have shown that TECTA and TECTB are both required for formation of the striated-sheet matrix within which collagen fibrils of the TM are imbedded and that CEACAM16 interacts with TECTA. To learn more about the structural and functional significance of CEACAM16, we created a <em>Ceacam16</em>-null mutant mouse. In the absence of CEACAM16, TECTB levels are reduced, a clearly defined striated-sheet matrix does not develop, and Hensen's stripe, a prominent feature in the basal two-thirds of the TM in WT mice, is absent. CEACAM16 is also shown to interact with TECTB, indicating that it may stabilize interactions between TECTA and TECTB. Although brain-stem evoked responses and distortion product otoacoustic emissions are, for most frequencies, normal in young mice lacking CEACAM16, stimulus-frequency and transiently evoked emissions are larger. We also observed spontaneous otoacoustic emissions (SOAEs) in 70% of the homozygous mice. This incidence is remarkable considering that &lt;3% of WT controls have SOAEs. The predominance of SOAEs &gt;15 kHz correlates with the loss of Hensen's stripe. Results from mice lacking CEACAM16 are consistent with the idea that the organ of Corti evolved to maximize the gain of the cochlear amplifier while preventing large oscillations. Changes in TM structure appear to influence the balance between energy generation and dissipation such that the system becomes unstable.</p>
<section><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> CEACAM16, cochlea, Hensen's stripe, mouse, otoacoustic emissions, tectorial membrane</p></section></section><section><h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>The carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule (CEACAM) family of proteins contains 22 members thought to be involved in cell-to-cell interactions (<a>Zid and Drouin, 2013</a>). The secreted glycoprotein CEACAM16 is present in the cochlea, where it is observed in supporting cells, interdental cells of the limbus, and, principally, in the tectorial membrane (TM), an accessory structure overlying the organ of Corti (<a>Zheng et al., 2011</a>; <a>Kammerer et al., 2012</a>). We showed recently (<a>Zheng et al., 2011</a>) that the <em>Ceacam16</em> gene is mutated in American Family 1070, a family with a hearing loss that begins in adolescence and progresses with age to an ∼50 dB threshold shift, a decrease in sensitivity consistent with a loss of cochlear amplification (<a>Chen et al., 1995</a>). These individuals do not have a mutation in myosin heavy-chain 14 nonmuscle (MYH14), unlike other individuals classified as having nonsyndromic autosomal dominant hearing loss DFNA4 (<a>Yang et al., 2005</a>). Instead, a missense mutation (c.481&gt;C, p.T140P) is present in the gene encoding CEACAM16 that is predicted to disrupt a glycosylation site and interfere with protein stability (<a>Zheng et al., 2011</a>). Individuals with mutations in <em>MYH14</em> are now designated DFNA4A and those with a mutation in <em>Ceacam16</em> are DFNA4B.</p>
<p>The TM is an acellular structure that contains collagens (<a>Thalmann et al., 1986</a>; <a>Richardson et al., 1987</a>) and several noncollagenous proteins, including α-tectorin (TECTA) and β-tectorin (TECTB) (<a>Legan et al., 1997</a>), CEACAM16 (<a>Zheng et al., 2011</a>), otogelin (<a>Cohen-Salmon et al., 1997</a>), otogelin-like (<a>Yariz et al., 2012</a>), and otolin (<a>Deans et al., 2010</a>). Two additional proteins may mediate attachment of the TM to the cochlear epithelium: otoancorin on the surface of the limbus (<a>Lukashkin et al., 2012</a>) and stereocilin at the tips of the outer hair cell (OHC) stereocilia (<a>Verpy et al., 2011</a>). Of the noncollagenous proteins, TECTA and TECTB dominate the proteinaceous content, but their mRNA is not expressed after about postnatal day 22 (P22; <a>Rau et al., 1999</a>), making it difficult to conceive how the TM is maintained throughout life. In contrast, <em>Ceacam16</em> mRNA expression is observed by P10 and the protein is distributed throughout the TM by P18 (<a>Kammerer et al., 2012</a>). <em>Ceacam16</em> mRNA expression is maintained in older animals (<a>Kammerer et al., 2012</a>), and the continuous production of CEACAM16 may stabilize TECTA in the TM of adults because the two proteins are known to interact (<a>Zheng et al., 2011</a>).</p>
<p>Transgenic mouse models with mutations in <em>Tecta</em> and <em>Tectb</em>, as well as long-standing theoretical considerations (<a>Békésy, 1960</a>), indicate that the TM is essential for normal hearing (<a>Richardson et al., 2008</a>; <a>Richardson et al., 2011</a>). When <em>Tecta</em> is mutated (<a>Legan et al., 2000</a>; <a>Legan et al., 2005</a>; <a>Legan et al., 2014</a>), threshold shifts and changes in tuning are observed. Because of the close association between TECTA and CEACAM16, we investigated anatomical and physiological changes in mice lacking CEACAM16. Loss of CEACAM16 alters TM structure and enhances certain types of otoacoustic emissions that can be recorded in the ear canal and reflect OHC activity. In young mice, these alterations occur without significant changes in cochlear output.</p></section><section><h2>Materials and Methods</h2>
<section><h3></h3>
<section><h4></h4>
<section><h5>Creation of the Ceacam16-null mutant mouse.</h5>
<p>The mouse strain used for this project was created from an ES cell clone obtained from the National Center for Research Resources–National Institutes of Health (NIH)-supported Knockout Mouse Project (KOMP) Repository (<a>www.komp.org</a>) and generated by the CSD (CHORI, Sanger Institute, and UC Davis) consortium for the NIH-funded KOMP. In ES cell line EPD0319_6_B07, a splice acceptor site, followed by a lacZ expression cassette encoding β-galactosidase (<a>Testa et al., 2004</a>), is inserted into intron 1 of <em>Ceacam16</em> to create the <em>Ceacam16</em>-null allele, <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>tm1(KOMP)Wtsi</sup>, in which β-galactosidase is a reporter of <em>Ceacam16</em> gene expression. Chimeras were produced at the University of Sussex by injecting the ES cells into C57BL/6BrdCrHsd-<em>Tyr</em><sup>c</sup> (Charles River Laboratories) 3.5-d-old blastocysts and transferring into 2.5 d pseudopregnant C57BL/6J × CBA/Ca F1 hybrid females using standard methods (<a>Papaioannou and Johnson, 1993</a>). The parental ES cell line, JM8A1.N3, is C57BL/6N-A<sup>tm1Brd</sup> and the heterozygous A<sup>tm1Brd</sup> allele confers an agouti coat color in chimeras (<a>Pettitt et al., 2009</a>). Chimeric mice (6–8 weeks of age) were test mated with C57BL/6N-A<sup>tm1Brd</sup> females. Tail snips were taken from pups at 3 weeks of age to identify germline transmission by PCR. One germline transmitting chimera was bred to C57BL/6J (Harlan Laboratories) mice to establish the line <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>tm1(KOMP)Wtsi</sup>. The auditory sensitivity of the C57BL/6J and C57BL/6N strains is the same (<a>Kane et al., 2012</a>). Herein, the <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>tm1(KOMP)Wtsi</sup> allele is referred to as <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal</sup>.</p>
<p>Sperm from homozygous <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mutants was cryopreserved (<a>Ostermeier et al., 2008</a>) and rederived on a C57BL/6J (Jackson Laboratories) background at Northwestern University's Transgenic and Targeted Mutagenesis Laboratory (TTML). Data were acquired from mice between 3 and 7 weeks of age. Although subjects were obtained using both heterotypic and homotypic breeding, most of the null mutant data were obtained from the latter.</p></section><section><h5>Genotyping.</h5>
<p>Tail snips were either used directly for PCR (<a>Malumbres et al., 1997</a>) or genomic DNA was purified from tail samples using genomic DNA purification kits obtained from either Promega or Sigma-Aldrich. Standard PCR was used to genotype mice. The PCR band for the transgene is 308 bp in size and was created using the following primer pair: CeaGen F1, 5′ GGG GCA TAC CTA GCA AGA GTA AGG 3′ (forward primer), and Lar 3.1, 5′ CAC AAC GGG TTC TTC TGT TAG TCC 3′ (reverse primer). The PCR band for WT is 266 bp and was created using the following primer pair: CeaGen F2, 5′ CAA GGA CCT GCT AGT CTA TGC CTG 3′ (forward primer), and CeaGen R2, 5′ TCC CAT CCC ACA CTG CTC ACT CAC 3′ (reverse primer).</p></section><section><h5>X-Gal staining of Ceacam16-driven β-galactosidase expression.</h5>
<p>Mouse cochleae were dissected in PBS and transferred to cold X-Gal fixative (1% formaldehyde, 0.25% glutaraldehyde, 2 m<span>m</span> MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 5 m<span>m</span> EGTA, 0.02% NP-40 in 1× PBS) and fixed for 2 h on ice. After fixation, cochleae were washed 3× in PBS at room temperature. The bone between the round and oval windows was broken and a small hole was made at the apex. Cochleae were then transferred into 35 mm Petri dishes, each containing 3 ml of X-Gal solution (5 m<span>m</span> potassium ferricyanide, 5 m<span>m</span> potassium ferrocyanide, 0.01% sodium deoxycholate, 0.02% NP-40, 2 m<span>m</span> MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 2.5 m<span>m</span> X-Gal in PBS) and stored in the dark at 4°C for 5 d, with the X-Gal solution replaced with fresh solution on day 3. After 5 d, cochleae were washed 3× in PBS containing 0.02% NP-40, refixed in cold X-Gal fixative for 2 h on ice, and washed a further 3× in PBS containing 0.02% NP-40. Bony cochleae were decalcified in 0.5 <span>m</span> EDTA, pH 8.0, for 2–4 d, depending on the age of the animal, and cryosectioned as described previously (<a>Legan et al., 2014</a>).</p></section><section><h5>Antibody labeling.</h5>
<p>Cryosections were preblocked for 1 h in PBS containing 10% horse serum (PBS/HS) and stained overnight at room temperature in a humid chamber with a 1:200 dilution of mouse anti-β-galactosidase IgG<sub>2a</sub> antibody (Promega). Slides were then washed 3× in PBS and labeled for 2 h with 1:500 Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG<sub>2a</sub> (Invitrogen) and 1:200 FITC phalloidin (Invitrogen) diluted in PBS/HS. Slides were then washed a further 3× in PBS and mounted in Vectashield (Vector Laboratories). Stained slides were imaged on a Zeiss LSM510 confocal microscope, using a 63×, 1.2 numerical aperture water-immersion lens.</p></section><section><h5>Light and electron microscopy.</h5>
<p>Mice were killed by a lethal overdose of anesthetic, the cochleae removed, and the oval and round windows opened in PBS. A small hole was made through the bone at the apical end of each cochlea and 20 μl of fixative (2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 <span>m</span> sodium cacodylate, pH 7.2, containing 1% tannic acid) was slowly perfused through the oval window, followed by a further 20 μl of fixative through the hole made in the apical end. The cochleae were then immersed in fixative for ∼4 h at room temperature, followed by ∼12 h at 4°C. After 3 washes in 0.1 <span>m</span> sodium cacodylate, pH 7.2, cochleae were postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 <span>m</span> sodium cacodylate, pH 7.2, for 2–4 h at room temperature. Samples were rotated in 1.5 ml tubes during fixation. After osmication, cochleae were washed 3× in 0.1 <span>m</span> sodium cacodylate and then decalcified in 0.5 <span>m</span> EDTA, pH 8.0, for 2–4 d as required. Once decalcified, cochleae were washed once and then dehydrated through an ascending series of ethanol concentrations, equilibrated in propylene oxide, and infiltrated and embedded in epoxy resin (812; TAAB Laboratories). Cochleae were positioned in molds with the oval and round windows facing upward and polymerized at 60°C for 24 h. Using a Reichert Ultracut E ultramicrotome and a diamond knife, 1-μm-thick sections were obtained in a plane parallel with the base of each cochlear block such that profiles of the ∼4, 8, 20, and 40 kHz regions could be obtained in a single section. Sections were stained with 1% toluidine blue, viewed with a Zeiss Axioplan 2 microscope, and images were captured with a Spot RT slider camera at a resolution of 1600 × 1200 pixels. For transmission electron microscopy, sections were cut at a thickness of 80–100 nm, mounted on copper mesh grids, stained with uranyl acetate followed by lead citrate, and viewed with an Hitachi 7100 microscope operating at 100 kV. Images were captured with a Gatan Ultrascan 1000 CCD camera at 2048 × 2048 pixel resolution. Minor adjustments in brightness, contrast and sharpness were made in Adobe Photoshop CS5.</p></section><section><h5>Quantification of holes in the TM matrix.</h5>
<p>Images of toluidine-blue-stained semithin sections photographed with a 40× objective were imported into Photoshop CS5 and the tectorial membrane selected with the polygonal lasso. <p>Brief descriptions of the different types of otoacoustic emissions (OAEs) are provided at the appropriate locations in the Results section. Because OAEs are thought to arise in the process of hair cell transduction (<a>Jaramillo et al., 1993</a>; <a>Manley et al., 2001</a>; <a>Mom et al., 2001</a>; <a>Lukashkin and Russell, 2002</a>; <a>Siegel et al., 2005</a>; <a>Siegel, 2006</a>), they were chosen to study changes in physiology early in the signal-coding pathway and before inner hair cell (IHC) transduction. Distortion product emissions (DPOAEs) were recorded from the closed ear canal using a custom probe that incorporates a sensitive Knowles FG-3652-CX microphone. EMAV (<a>Neely and Liu, 1994</a>) and additional computer programs written in Visual Basic 6.0 were used to collect and analyze the emission responses. Because the probe can be placed close to the eardrum, sound calibrations in the ear canal of each individual mouse were performed using SysRes (<a>Neely and Stevenson, 1992</a>). The transfer function of the emission probe's microphone was measured (<a>Siegel, 2007</a>) and used to compensate canal pressures. DPOAEs were measured as iso-input functions obtained by varying the frequency of F2 while leaving the frequency ratio (F2/F1 = 1.2) constant. We also collected input–output functions for F2 = 12 and F2 = 27 kHz. To obtain a measure of F2-F1, in addition to the more commonly recorded 2F1-F2, we acquired an input–output function for F2 = 23 kHz and F2/F1 = 1.4. A change in parameters was required to produce F2-F1 within the mouse audiogram for stimulus tones that were not especially high in frequency, where even WT responses are inherently variable.</p>
<p>We also recorded spontaneous (SOAE), stimulus frequency (SFOAE), and transiently evoked (TEOAE) OAEs. To measure SOAEs, the noise floor in quiet was measured using SysRes by performing a spectral average of 40 samples of the canal pressure into a 524,288 point buffer. A fast Fourier transform (FFT) was performed on the time waveform of each sample and the magnitudes averaged. A moving rectangular window served to smooth the spectrum such that energy was integrated into nonoverlapping windows with a width of 93 Hz. Spontaneous emissions were identified as peaks in the noise spectrum. Because energy in SOAEs is not concentrated into one spectral line, the total power in each SOAE was calculated by integrating the energy in all spectral lines near the peak where the emission deviated from the background noise. Because this value contains the emission signal plus noise in each line, an estimate of the power in the noise was subtracted from the total. This calculation is similar to that described previously (<a>Talmadge et al., 1993</a>).</p>
<p>SFOAEs were measured for probe tones between 4 and 36 kHz using the suppression technique (<a>Siegel et al., 2005</a>). Probe and suppressor were presented using different speakers at 30 and 65 dB, respectively. Suppressor frequency was 86 Hz below the probe frequency and is assumed to eliminate the SFOAE produced by the probe. Vector subtraction was used to calculate the change in pressure induced by the suppressor and referred to as the residual. If suppression is complete, the residual provides an accurate estimate of the SFOAE. Input–output functions were collected for the probe frequency producing the strongest SFOAE in each individual animal. Threshold was designated as the level that generated a 10 dB SPL SFOAE.</p>
<p>The same stimulus frequency used to obtain the SFOAE input–output function was also selected for the TEOAEs recorded using a 0.5 ms tone burst with rise/fall times of 0.25 ms. Because our sound sources (modified Radio Shack Realistic #40-1310B Super Tweeters) produce negligible distortion &lt;80 dB, the pressure measured in the ear canal is dominated by the stimulus at high levels. Because the TEOAE is presumably saturated and contributes little to the canal pressure at high levels, the scale and subtract (compression) method was used to estimate the emission evoked by the stimulus (<a>Kemp and Chum, 1980a</a>, <a>1980b</a>; <a>Siegel et al., 2011</a>). When acquiring TEOAEs, a sequence of four stimuli were presented as a group. The probe was presented three times and the reference high-level tone was presented once. Responses to the probe tones were averaged separately from the responses to the reference, allowing the latter to be scaled down and subtracted from the averaged probe response. A total of 2048 samples were collected. This procedure effectively cancels the contribution of the stimulus to the measured pressure in the canal. For our recordings, the reference was always presented at 70 dB peak-equivalent SPL (peSPL) and the probe was introduced at 50 dB peSPL and then decreased in level to obtain a TEOAE threshold. A recursive-exponential window (duration 4 ms) was positioned near the center of the transient-response waveform, which served to minimize the influence of noise outside the windowed time segment. The TEOAE emission spectrum was extracted by performing an FFT on these windowed time waveforms. The TEOAE level reference was equivalent sound exposure level (<a>Goodman et al., 2009</a>).</p>
<p>All of the physiological measurements were obtained on anesthetized mice of both sexes (ketamine 100 mg/kg; xylazine 10 mg/kg, i.p.) and body temperature was maintained at ∼38°C using a heating blanket. Supplements of anesthesia at 20% of the dose for induction were administered as needed. At the end of an experiment, the animals were killed with Euthasol (200 mg/kg, i.p.). All procedures were approved by The University of Sussex and by Northwestern University, as well as by the Home Office in the United Kingdom and the National Institutes of Health in the United States.</p></section><section><h5>Whole-cell patch-clamp recording.</h5>

<p>X-Gal staining of <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> cochleae was used to estimate the onset of <em>Ceacam16</em> expression in the organ of Corti (<a>Fig. 5</a><em>A–F</em>). Labeling of the border cells, together with staining of Deiters' and epithelial cells of the spiral limbus and inner sulcus, is seen at P12, but only in the basal region of the cochlea (<a>Fig. 5</a><em>A</em>). Staining is first seen in the apical coil at P14, where labeling is predominantly in the spiral limbus (<a>Fig. 5</a><em>E</em>). However, by P16, clear labeling of border cells and Deiters' cells is also seen in apical coil sections (<a>Fig. 5</a><em>F</em>). Ultrastructural analysis of the central core of the TM in the apex of WT animals during these developmental stages reveals that, at P12 and earlier, the matrix of the apical coil TM consists of a disorganized meshwork of filaments in addition to rows of parallel collagen fibrils (<a>Fig. 5</a><em>G</em>). This appearance is similar to that seen at mature stages in mutant mice (<a>Fig. 4</a><em>D</em>). At P14, this noncollagenous matrix becomes condensed and more organized (<a>Fig. 5</a><em>H</em>) and, by P16, the striated-sheet matrix characteristic of the mature cochlea is clearly apparent (<a>Fig. 5</a><em>I</em>). These results suggest that there is a base to apex developmental progression in the expression of <em>Ceacam16</em> that correlates with maturation of the TM and that CEACAM16 is required for the formation of the striated-sheet matrix.</p>

<p>Before measuring any physiological responses, we constructed cytocochleograms for <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice at P43 to rule out the possibility that hair cell death might influence the recordings. <p>Data in <a>Table 1</a> show that ABR thresholds in homozygous mutants at 4 kHz are modestly elevated and statistically different from WT (Student's <em>t</em> test). The threshold shift at the lowest (for the mouse) frequency is consistent with that described previously (<a>Kammerer et al., 2012</a>). <p>In addition to the ABR recordings that document summed neural responses at the output of the cochlea, we collected OAEs to study changes occurring earlier in the signal-coding pathway. Otoacoustic emissions are sounds recorded in the ear canal that are not present in the incoming sound stimuli (<a>Avan et al., 2013</a>). Although their existence was predicted by <a>Gold (1948)</a>, it was <a>Kemp (1978)</a> who provided experimental verification of their existence. Although OAEs are not involved directly in signal coding, their observation does require hair cells with functional transducer currents and the driving forces required to amplify these currents. Therefore, emissions are extremely useful in evaluating cochlear function, especially in very small animals. One class, DPOAEs, is produced using a two-tone input, F1 and F2, with the most prominent intermodulation frequency being 2F1-F2.</p>
<p>Average distortion product otoacoustic emission or DP-grams (± 1 SD) are shown in <a>Figure 6</a><em>A</em> for L1 = 50 dB, L2 = 35 dB and in <a>Figure 6</a><em>B</em> for L1 = L2 = 70 dB. At the lower sound level, small changes in magnitude, albeit of apparent statistical significance, are obtained for F2 = 13.5 kHz (DP = 9 kHz) and 15.5 kHz (DP = 10.3 kHz). A statistical difference was also obtained at F2 = 40.9 kHz. At 70 dB, the only difference associated with the absence of CEACAM16 occurs at F2 = 47 kHz, the highest frequency that we tested. Input–output functions were also collected for F2 at 12 kHz (DP = 8 kHz) and for F2 at 27 kHz (DP = 18 kHz) (<a>Fig. 6</a><em>C</em>,<em>D</em>, respectively). In neither case was a statistically significant difference seen, even at moderate input levels. Because the 8 kHz DP measured by the input–output function (<a>Fig. 6</a><em>C</em>) showed no difference between mice lacking CEACAM16 versus WT, one might question the observation that the 9 kHz DP had an apparently significant magnitude reduction in the DP-gram (<a>Fig. 6</a><em>B</em>).</p>

Notably, no dramatic changes were seen in cochlear function in young <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice, except for the threshold elevation in the ABRs at frequencies approaching the lower limit of the murine audiogram. However, older mice lacking CEACAM16 show a progressive loss of sensitivity (<a>Kammerer et al., 2012</a>), as do DFNA4B patients with the T140P missense mutation that is thought to destabilize CEACAM16.</p></section><section><h3>Enhanced SOAEs, SFOAEs, and TEOAEs in <em>Ceacam16</em> mutant mice</h3>
<p>Although DPOAEs dominate measurements described in the literature, OAEs can also be generated using single-tone inputs: SFOAEs and TEOAEs. This latter classification is a somewhat arbitrary parsing of the same phenomenon obtained with either continuous tones or acoustic transients, respectively. In other words, SFOAEs coincide with stimulus frequency, whereas TEOAEs are transients generated in response to very short duration broadband inputs. Finally, SOAEs appear as peaks in the Fourier spectrum of the microphone signal recorded from the ear canal in the absence of sound stimulation. Because the phenotype of <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice was mild or unchanged for DPOAEs and ABRs, and because some models (<a>Shera and Guinan, 1999</a>) suggest a difference in mechanism for this latter group of OAEs, we recorded SOAEs, SFOAEs, and TEOAEs. In our experience, the incidence of SOAEs in WT mice is &lt;3%. In fact, we have recorded only 1 in 51 WT/heterozygous <em>Ceacam16</em>
<sup>βgal</sup> mice and only 2 before working with this mutant and using a large number (<em>N</em> = 78) of WT animals. In stark contrast, 70% of <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice (67 of 95 homozygotes) have SOAEs and some of these are large and numerous. Examples appear in <a>Figure 7</a><em>A</em>. The largest SOAE recorded from a <em>Ceacam16</em>
<sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mouse is plotted at the bottom in red. The sole SOAE recorded in a WT mouse is in black. Distribution of 150 SOAE frequencies recorded in 67 homozygous <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice is provided in <a>Figure 7</a><em>B</em>. They range between 14.9 and 32.1 kHz. This bar graph represents the number of SOAEs present in each kHz bin, such that the largest number of SOAEs falls in the bin ranging from 23.000 to 23.999 Hz. When multiple SOAEs are present, their average separation is 2470 Hz; that is, ∼10% of the most common SOAE frequency. The distribution of SOAEs based on their overall SPL is provided in <a>Figure 7</a><em>C</em>. The average SPL for mice lacking CEACAM16 was 17.0 ± 4.7 dB. The sole WT control had an SOAE of 7.6 dB (black bar).</p>

In fact, the average threshold (0 dB) for <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice was 37.3 ± 3.9 dB, whereas that for WT was 37.2 ± 2.7 dB (<em>p</em> = 0.833). Although thresholds for the cubic distortion product generated by F2 = 27 kHz were raised in <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice, the threshold differences were not statistically significant. When we compared SFOAE thresholds, however, the average threshold for homozygotes was 16.0 ± 6.3 dB and the WT/heterozygote threshold was 27.8 ± 7.8 dB (<em>p</em> = 0.00042). A similar improvement in TEOAE thresholds was also observed for <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice (21.6 ± 6.2 dB) compared with their WT controls (32.4 ± 6.1 dB). The more sensitive SFOAE and TEOAE thresholds for <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice, however, did not translate into better ABR thresholds. The latter were obtained at the same frequency as the SFOAE thresholds and for several nearby frequencies. Even when we chose the best ABR threshold in the vicinity of the SFOAE, <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> thresholds were 15.5 ± 3.8 dB and WT/heterozygous were 17.8 ± 7.0 dB (<em>p</em> = 0.97). It is, however, acknowledged that ABR thresholds were obtained using 5 dB changes in stimulus level, so any obvious fine structure may therefore have been missed. In addition, the ABR probably reflects responses generated by a fairly large group of hair cells such that the microstructure is no longer prominent. Because of the complicated nature of emission generation, understanding how these various phenomena are related is not straightforward. Because SFOAE fine structure in mice lacking CEACAM16 likely reflects a synchronization of SOAEs with the probe tone (<a>Burns et al., 1998</a>), it is difficult to interpret interactions between SOAEs and stimulus-related responses.</p>

<p>Our previous study (<a>Zheng et al., 2011</a>) announced the discovery of a mutation in <em>Ceacam16</em> as a cause of a certain type of human hereditary hearing loss and demonstrated that this secreted glycoprotein localizes to the TM and coimmunoprecipitates with TECTA. Because these results suggested a role in maintaining the integrity of the TM, <em>Ceacam16</em>-null mutant mice were produced to study the relationship between the structure of the TM and cochlear function. Although TECTA levels are not reduced in the TMs of mice lacking CEACAM16, TECTB levels decrease and evidence is presented indicating that CEACAM16 and TECTB interact. Furthermore, clearly defined striated-sheet matrix never materializes in the <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mouse, revealing that CEACAM16 is required for the final maturation of this structural feature. This maturation occurs between P14 and P16, coincident with the onset of hearing. With Ig-variable domains at its N- and C-termini (<a>Kuespert et al., 2006</a>), CEACAM16 may cross-link TECTB to TECTA, thereby defining the final structure of the matrix. Because CEACAM16 is expressed in older animals after mRNAs for the two tectorins can no longer be detected, its continual production may be required for the long-term maintenance and stability of the matrix within which the collagen fibrils of the TM are embedded.</p>
<p>It is interesting that, over most of the mouse audiogram, sensitivity is normal in young mice lacking CEACAM16; that is, thresholds for both DPOAEs and ABRs are WT like at most frequencies. The fact that NLC is also normal in <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice suggests that all components of the amplifier are working correctly, at least for young <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice on the C57BL/6 background. The WT-like input–output functions for DPOAEs (<a>Fig. 6</a><em>C</em>,<em>D</em>) also suggest that removal of CEACAM16 does not dramatically alter the contact between Kimura's membrane and the tallest OHC stereocilia. In addition, Kimura's membrane is not partially detached as it is in <em>Tecta</em><sup>Y1870C</sup> heterozygotes, in which there is a decrease in DPOAEs and CAPs (<a>Legan et al., 2005</a>). Relative comparisons between cubic and quadratic difference tones (<a>Fig. 6</a><em>E</em>,<em>F</em>) are also normal, suggesting that the TM attachment crown is secure (<a>Tsuprun and Santi, 2002</a>; <a>Goodyear et al., 2005</a>; <a>Verpy et al., 2011</a>) and that the set point of the OHC transducer is WT like.</p>
<p>Although not statistically significant, ABR thresholds in <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice show minimally reduced sensitivity at all frequencies, consistent with the idea that spontaneously oscillating ears may be less sensitive than those that do not oscillate. When feedback is excessive and the amplifier becomes oscillatory, gain for external stimuli can decrease (<a>Gold, 1948</a>). In contrast, at the lowest frequency measured, the ABR threshold is statistically different (Student's <em>t</em> test) from controls, similar to the increases in threshold reported by <a>Kammerer et al. (2012)</a>. This low-frequency threshold shift may relate to changes in the striated-sheet matrix, which are pronounced in apical regions of the cochlea (<a>Figs. 3</a>, <a>4</a>). An increase in porosity/decrease in solid fraction of the TM can reduce shear force (<a>Masaki et al., 2010</a>) and may decrease radial fluid flow driven by transverse TM movements (<a>Nowotny and Gummer, 2006</a>). Quantification of the holes observed in the TM indicates, however, that an ABR threshold shift is not observed until the holes account for more than ∼20% of the membrane's cross-sectional area. The observation that ABR thresholds are near normal for most frequencies coded in the basal 60% of the cochlea, where Hensen's stripe is absent, is not consistent with the suggestion that IHC bundle deflection is mediated by this TM structure (<a>Masaki et al., 2010</a>). It is conceivable, however, that the presence of Hensen's stripe can influence the phase of IHC excitation (<a>Steele et al., 2009</a>).</p>
<p>In contrast to normal ABRs and DPOAEs, SFOAEs and TEOAEs are larger and SOAEs are dramatically more frequent in <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>β gal/β gal</sup> mice, findings that are potentially significant. When discussing these results, it is useful to remember that DPOAEs originate in the region where overlapping excitation patterns associated with the stimulating primaries are large, with the result that DPOAEs can reflect the active process expressed around CF. Some of this energy is distributed in an apically traveling wave to the DPOAE frequency place such that components in the ear canal can be associated with the generation region and/or with the DP place location. In mice, however, DPOAEs generally appear to be dominated by generators in the region of overlap (<a>Heitmann et al., 1997</a>; <a>Banakis et al., 2008</a>; <a>Cheatham et al., 2011</a>). Compared with DPOAEs, SOAEs/SFOAEs/TEOAEs are commonly assumed to form a distinct and separate class of emissions (<a>Shera and Guinan, 1999</a>) and our data are consistent with this taxonomy. It is conceivable, however, that all emissions can be explained by distributed hair cell generators and a single generating mechanism (<a>Kemp and Chum, 1980b</a>; <a>Wilson, 1980</a>; <a>Siegel et al., 2005</a>).</p>
<p>Although the mechanisms of emission generation and back propagation are not completely understood, an explanation for the dichotomy between SOAEs/SFOAEs/TEOAEs and DPOAEs can be sought in anatomical changes seen in the <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice. The most conspicuous alteration due to loss of CEACAM16 in the basal part of the cochlea is the lack of Hensen's stripe. This finding suggests a possible explanation for the OAE results. An underlying assumption for most discussions on emissions is that an increase in cochlear irregularities could increase the opportunity for reflections and generation of SOAEs (<a>Zweig and Shera, 1995</a>). In addition, modeling suggests that longitudinal coupling within the TM may serve to stabilize cochlear function, thereby enabling high sensitivity (<a>Meaud and Grosh, 2010</a>). A comparative approach in several species of lizards also implies that the TM may influence longitudinal energy distribution, thereby playing an important role in determining emission properties in vertebrate ears (<a>Bergevin et al., 2010</a>). Changes in porosity of the TM can also influence TM traveling waves (<a>Sellon et al., 2014</a>).</p>
<p>Consistent with these ideas, data in this report imply that increases in the SOAE class of emissions in <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>β gal/β gal</sup> mice might be related to the loss of Hensen's stripe. Because SOAEs predominate in the octave above ∼15 kHz, the loss of Hensen's stripe in this same location may underlie their increase, possibly due to changes in the hydrodynamics of subtectorial fluid flow. It is therefore possible that fluid flow within the inner sulcus and subtectorial space changes from a stabilizing energy storage element (<a>de Boer, 1993</a>) to one that may alter the propensity of the cochlea either to produce or retransmit energy in the form of emissions in the absence of Hensen's stripe. Although a static model of shear motion has been entertained since ter Kuile's original descriptions &gt;100 years ago (<a>Békésy, 1960</a>), this model does not allow for fluid/structural interactions in the subtectorial space (<a>Nowotny and Gummer, 2006</a>). Shear motion simply results from the translation of transverse BM movements into a differential motion between the TM and reticular lamina, with the result that fluid flow due to these shearing motions stimulates IHCs (<a>Dallos et al., 1972</a>). The potential for more complex interactions requires further characterization of the TM's physical properties to understand how the anatomical changes favor feedback of energy associated with hair cell transduction but without reducing input to IHCs, except for frequencies at the margins of the cochlear frequency/place map. The fact that SOAEs, SFOAEs, and TEOAEs are clustered in the octave between ∼16 and 32 kHz suggests that it is loss of Hensen's stripe, possibly coupled with the holes seen in the TM, that allows for the enhancement of this family of emission components. Because production of numerous SOAEs is wasteful, in that energy is broadcast into the environment with no obvious advantage to hearing, the structure of the TM and the existence of Hensen's stripe may have evolved to suppress these spontaneous activities.</p>
<p>Our data indicate that CEACAM16 is required for striated-sheet matrix formation. Its continual expression may stabilize the noncollagenous TM matrix, the principal proteins of which are only produced during a short window of time early in development (<a>Rau et al., 1999</a>). In the absence of CEACAM16, changes in the striated-sheet matrix, which are dramatic in the apex, may correlate with increased ABR thresholds at the very lowest frequencies in young <em>Ceacam16</em><sup>βgal/βgal</sup> mice. In addition, Hensen's stripe, in the basal regions of the cochlea, may influence hydrodynamic conditions in the subtectorial space that normally suppress SOAEs, SFOAEs, and TEOAEs</p>
<p>It is now generally accepted that OHCs housed within the mammalian organ of Corti produce active mechanical gain to improve both sensitivity and frequency selectivity. Because spontaneous oscillation would compromise these outcomes, the system has evolved to minimize excessive instability. Only a small change in net power generation is required to transform a cochlea with optimized sensitivity and frequency selectivity into an oscillator (<a>Neely and Kim, 1986</a>). Data in this report indicate that changes in the TM due to loss of CEACAM16 alter the coupling between structural elements within the organ of Corti, thereby enhancing self-sustaining oscillations. In this context, SOAEs, SFOAEs, and TEOAEs appear to be sensitive indicators of instability.</p></section><section><h2>Footnotes</h2>
