BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex deficiency is in up to
90% caused by pathogenic variants in the X-linked PDHA1 gene. We aimed to
investigate female relatives of index patients with PDHA1-related disease to (i)
describe the prevalence of female PDHA1 carriers, (ii) determine whether they
had symptoms and signs, and (iii) delineate the associated phenotype.
METHODS: In a national population-based study, we identified 37 patients with
pathogenic variants in PDHA1. Sanger sequencing for the presence of the
pathogenic variant was performed in their mothers and female relatives. The
identified female carriers were clinically assessed, and their medical records
were reviewed.
RESULTS: The proportion carrying a de novo variant was 86%. We identified seven
female PDHA1 carriers from five families. Five of them exhibited clinical
features of the disease and were previously undiagnosed; all had signs of
peripheral axonal neuropathy, four presented with strokelike episodes including
two with Leigh-like lesions, and three had facial stigmata.
CONCLUSIONS: PDHA1-related disease is underrecognized in heterozygous female
carriers. Peripheral axonal neuropathy, strokelike and Leigh-like changes, and
facial dysmorphism should raise suspicion of the disorder. Genetic analysis and
clinical examination of potential female carriers are important for genetic
counseling and have implications for treatment.

The mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHc) is fundamental for aerobic energy production, linking glycolysis to the Krebs cycle by the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl‐CoA [1]. PDHc is a multienzyme complex consisting of three catalytic components, pyruvate decarboxylase (E1), dihydrolipoamide acetyltransferase (E2), dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (E3), and two regulatory subunits, E1 kinase and phospho‐E1 phosphatase [2]. In the first reaction step of PDHc, catalyzed by E1, pyruvate is decarboxylated and an acetyl group is transferred to E2 using thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) as a cofactor [3].

PDHc deficiency is one of the most common causes of primary lactic acidosis in young children [2]. The cause, although genetically heterogeneous, is in 75%–90% of reported cases due to pathogenic variants in the X‐linked PDHA1 gene (HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee: 8806), which encodes the E1α, a subunit of the first component [4, 5]. Ketogenic diet has been shown to be both safe and effective for most patients and could prevent further degenerative damage to the brain caused by the disease [6, 7]. In addition, certain pathogenic variants in relation to the TPP site of the E1 subunit are associated with a good clinical response to treatment with thiamine [8, 9, 10]. Recognition of the phenotype is important to enable earlier diagnosis and initiation of treatment.


PDHA1‐related disease (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man: 300502) in hemizygous males is characterized by a very variable phenotype and severity ranging from prenatal onset with cerebral dysgenesis and neonatal lactic acidosis, to infantile onset with Leigh syndrome, to a later onset with intermittent ataxia or dystonia. In contrast to males with the disease, the phenotype in reported female patients has been more uniform, typically with prenatal onset, dysmorphic features, microcephaly, psychomotor retardation, and epilepsy [11, 12, 13]. The phenotypic spectrum in females with PDHA1‐related disease and the proportion of de novo variants is still unclear. In approximately 25% of reported female patients, the mother was a heterozygote for the same disease‐causing variant [5, 14]. Most of the heterozygote mothers were referred to as asymptomatic, with limited information on whether they were clinically evaluated [13, 15, 16, 17].

As PDHA1‐related disease is X‐linked, one would expect a significantly higher ratio of male to female patients, but in most of previous studies an approximately equal number of male and female patients has been identified [5, 18]. In a recent population‐based neuroimaging study, the male:female ratio of PDHA1‐related disease was 1:2, suggestive of X‐linked dominant inheritance [19]. Also, the females more frequently presented with milder postnatal phenotypes than previously reported. This indicates a possible underdiagnosis of the disease in females and that better recognition of the phenotype is important to enable earlier diagnosis and initiation of treatment.

We hypothesized that female relatives of patients with PDHA1 pathogenic variants could be symptomatic carriers with undiagnosed symptoms and signs of disease. The study aim was to investigate female relatives of index patients with PDHA1‐related disease detected in an ongoing national Swedish population‐based study of PDHc deficiency to identify the proportion of genetic heterozygotes among mothers and female siblings, to determine whether they had symptoms and signs, and to describe the associated phenotype.

From an ongoing population‐based study of PDHc deficiency in Sweden, we identified 37 index patients with pathogenic variants in PDHA1. Further information about the studied population can be found in a previously published study [19].

All the mothers and when relevant, the first grade female relatives, were investigated with Sanger sequencing of DNA from peripheral blood leukocytes for the pathogenic variant present in the family. Genetic mosaicism was detected by Sanger sequencing.

All patients and female heterozygotes in the study were clinically assessed by a pediatric neurologist (A.S.) for manifestations of the disease. All medical records were reviewed. A standardized questionary was used to collect the historical data for the individuals regarding gender, age at symptom onset, pre‐ and perinatal symptoms, neuroradiology, epilepsy, psychomotor delay, organ involvement, laboratory test results, treatment, social situation, education, and occupation. A standardized clinical assessment form was used to record signs from the cranial nerves, sensory system, and upper and lower motor neurons, movement disorders, coordination, and dysmorphia. The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain of four individuals (11 MRI scans in total) and computed tomography (CT) of the brain of one individual (two CT scans in total), were re‐evaluated by a pediatric neuroradiologist and a pediatric neurologist [19].

The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (#289‐17, 2021‐04327, 106‐04). Oral and written consent were obtained by the study individuals before undertaking any study‐related procedures. All procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical committee responsible for human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2000. This article does not contain any studies with animal subjects performed by any of the authors.

A strokelike episode was defined as a paroxysmal neurological manifestation with insidious onset and progressive course, characterized by abrupt onset and a duration of >24 h of altered consciousness (encephalopathy) and/or focal neurological impairment (hemiparesis, convulsions, visual impairment, dysphasia, etc.). A strokelike lesion was defined as an acute or subacute cortical or subcortical lesion on MRI that did not conform to conventional vascular territories and was typically characterized by T1 hypointensity, T2/T2 fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery hyperintensity, diffusion‐weighted imaging hyperintensity, and variable apparent diffusion coefficient 20, 21]. A Leigh‐like lesion was defined as a bilateral, symmetric lesion similar to the above, affecting the deep gray matter (basal ganglia, midbrain, or brainstem) [22]. Microcephaly was defined as occipitofrontal head circumference more than 2 SD below the median for age and sex in the current Swedish growth chart.

We identified seven female heterozygous carriers of the same pathogenic PDHA1 variant as the index patients. They were from five different families; five mothers, one grandmother, and one sibling. All but one were assessed clinically, and five were found to have manifestations of the disease. The heterozygous mother who was unavailable for clinical assessment was described in the medical records as healthy. The pedigrees of the remaining four families are depicted in Figure 1. Median age at clinical onset and diagnosis of the symptomatic female carriers in the study, including the two index cases, were 5 and 33 years, respectively. All the symptomatic female carriers had signs of peripheral neuropathy presenting with pes cavus and/or loss of vibration sense. Neurophysiological investigations were performed in two of them (A.III.2, C.III.3), showing predominantly axonal polyneuropathy (Table 1). Four of them presented with strokelike episodes, two of them with strokelike lesions on MRI (A.IV.2 and D.IV.2) and two without (A.II.2 and C.III.3). Leigh‐like lesions were seen in two individuals (A.IV.2, A.III.2), one with both Leigh‐like and strokelike lesions (A.IV.2). Facial stigmata with smooth philtrum and thin upper lip were seen in three female carriers.

A.IV.2, the proband, was 20 years of age. She went to a special needs high school and was currently unemployed. She had clinical onset at approximately 1 year of age with psychomotor delay. At 4 years of age, she developed strokelike episodic exacerbations of psychomotor deterioration with ataxia and dysarthria and had increased lactate levels in blood and cerebrospinal fluid. Repeated MRI of the brain showed widespread strokelike and Leigh‐like lesions. Ketogenic diet was initiated at the age of 8 years, leading to stabilization of the disease without further relapses and improvement of the dysarthria, ataxia, and cognition. The patient had moderate intellectual disability. At last follow‐up at 20 years of age, clinical examination showed a thin upper lip and long philtrum, dysarthria, spasticity in the lower limbs, and pes cavus.

A.III.2 was the 54‐year‐old mother of the proband. She attended regular elementary school and worked in cleaning services. The early psychomotor development was normal. At the age of 8 years, she had onset of focal epilepsy with secondary generalization. Treatment with carbamazepine led to good seizure control, and the treatment was terminated at 18 years of age. At the same age, she experienced fatigue and was noted to have pes cavus. At the age of 27 years, seizures recurred and treatment with lamotrigine was initiated. MRI at the same age showed Leigh‐like lesions. At last follow‐up, at the age of 54 years, clinical examination showed a thin upper lip and smooth philtrum, dysarthria, ataxia, areflexia of the Achilles tendons, and pes cavus.

A.II.2 was the 80‐year‐old maternal grandmother of the proband. She attended regular elementary school and worked as a kitchen assistant. The early psychomotor development was normal. At the age of 24 years, 5 days after a normal vaginal childbirth, she presented with a strokelike episode characterized by sudden onset of expressive dysphasia, hemiparesis, and focal seizures of the right arm. The clinical examination showed hemiparesis and hyperreflexia on the right side. CT of the brain revealed no signs of ischemic stroke or hemorrhage. The symptoms of hemiparesis and dysphasia gradually disappeared over the following months. At the age of 40 years, she abruptly developed diffuse numbness of the skull and face, which lasted several months. At the age of 76 years, she had an episode with cognitive problems, loss of balance, and exercise‐induced muscle weakness that lasted for 1 month. Clinical examination showed paresis and dysdiadochokinesis of the left arm. At the age of 78 years, she experienced an episode of paresis and paresthesia of the left arm, lasting for 1 month. CT of the brain showed no signs of ischemic stroke or hemorrhage. At last follow‐up, at the age of 80 years, clinical examination showed thin upper lip, smooth philtrum, and pes cavus.

B.III.1 was the 45‐year‐old mother of a boy diagnosed with PDHc deficiency. She studied at a university and worked in project administration. The early psychomotor development was normal. Since the age of 12 years, she experienced a mild progressive, exercise‐induced foot pain, loss of balance, and difficulties walking up stairs. She had normal intellectual development. At last follow‐up, at the age of 45 years, clinical examination showed mild muscle weakness and sensory ataxia, with loss of vibration sense in the lower limbs, and pes cavus.

This family has previously been partly described [15].

C.III.3, the 53‐year‐old mother of two boys diagnosed with PDHc deficiency, had vocational education and worked as an assistant nurse. At the age of 5 years, during an infection, she developed muscle weakness. She was then only able to crawl for about 4 months but recovered completely thereafter. At the age of 10 years, she developed bilateral hand tremor that became more prominent in the morning and during fasting. At the age of 34 years, she experienced an acute strokelike episode with paresthesia of the tongue and slurred speech that lasted for 2 weeks. A few months later, during an infection, she lost sensation in the lower lip that lasted several weeks. Since childhood, she had experienced exercise‐induced muscle weakness. At the last follow‐up, at the age of 51 years, clinical examination showed ataxia with intention tremor, hyporeflexia of the upper and lower limbs, and pes cavus.

D.IV.2, the proband, attended a special needs elementary school. At the age of 1 month, she presented with sudden onset of left‐sided hemiparesis. MRI of the brain revealed a strokelike lesion. Treatment with ketogenic diet and thiamine supplementation was initiated at the age of 1 year, and she had had no further relapses since then. During childhood, she was diagnosed with severe intellectual impairment. At the last follow‐up, at the age of 9 years, clinical examination showed cerebellar ataxia with intention tremor, spasticity of the left upper and lower limbs, and pes cavus.

D.IV.1 was the 16‐year‐old older sister of the proband. She attended a special needs elementary school. At the age of 6 months, she came to medical attention due to psychomotor delay. The clinical examination showed spasticity, and she was suspected to have cerebral palsy. Treatment with ketogenic diet and thiamine supplementation was initiated at the age of 6 years and 4 months. During childhood, she was diagnosed with mild intellectual disability. At the last follow‐up, at the age of 15 years, clinical examination showed cerebellar ataxia with dysarthria, dystonia, spasticity, and pes cavus.

D.III.1 was the 43‐year‐old mother of the proband. She had received university education and worked in education. The PDHA1 variant was detected in 10% in blood, implying mosaicism. She had had no symptoms or signs associated with PDHc deficiency.

We performed targeted carrier screening of the female first‐degree relatives of patients with known PDHA1‐related PDHc deficiency in Sweden and studied the expression of the disease in the identified heterozygotes. De novo variants were found in 86% of the probands; the rest inherited the disease from their mother. All but one investigated female heterozygote showed clinical manifestations of PDHc deficiency upon assessment, with a median diagnostic delay of 28 years. The lack of symptoms and signs in the one female could be related to genetic mosaicism. Despite a relatively wide inter‐ and intrafamilial phenotypic variability, we found distinctive phenotypic features among the patients, namely signs of peripheral neuropathy, strokelike episodes with or without strokelike lesions, Leigh‐like lesions, and facial dysmorphism.

All affected female carriers had symptoms suggestive of sensorimotor peripheral neuropathy, such as sensory ataxia, hypoesthesia with loss of vibration sense, diminished deep tendon reflexes, limb weakness, and pes cavus. Chronic sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy is the most common pattern among patients with peripheral neuropathy due to mitochondrial disorders, presenting with a Charcot–Marie–Tooth phenotype [23]. Although considered a minor feature in many mitochondrial disorders, peripheral neuropathy is a predominant feature in mitochondrial disorders with abnormal axonal transport, such as in MFN2‐ and GDAP1‐related disorders, mtDNA replication and maintenance disorders, that is, POLG1‐, TWNK‐, TYMP‐, and MPV17‐related disorders, and in respiratory chain complex V disorders due to MTATP6 mutations [23, 24]. Our study suggests that X‐linked PDHc deficiency should be included among the mitochondrial disorders where peripheral axonal neuropathy is a key feature.

Strokelike episodes and their morphological equivalent, strokelike lesions, have been described in mitochondrial disorders mainly of mtDNA origin, most commonly reported in mitochondrial encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and strokelike episodes [25]. Strokelike episodes are less frequently described in mitochondrial disorders of the nuclear genome, except for the POLG‐ and TWNK‐associated encephalopathy syndromes [26, 27]. The strokelike episodes represent an acute focal neurological dysfunction that is presumed to occur as a result of energy insufficiency due to reduced mitochondrial function, and although clinically mimicking stroke, there is no neuropathological evidence of ischemia in the related lesions [28]. Wong argued that strokelike episodes are not a part of the phenotypic spectrum of PDHc deficiency [29]. In our recently published MRI study of 34 patients with PDHc deficiency, three of 23 patients presented with strokelike lesions, all with mutations in the PDHA1 gene [19]. As previously shown, the strokelike lesions may evolve over time, reflecting the clinical course of the disease [19, 26].

Leigh syndrome, characterized by bilateral symmetric necrotic lesions extending from the thalamus to the brainstem and the posterior columns of the spinal cord, presents in genetically heterogenous disorders linked to mitochondrial disorders in children [22]. Onset of disease typically occurs between 3 and 24 months of age, when these parts of the brain are especially vulnerable to disruption in energy supply. Later onset forms have been described in up to 20% of patients [30]. We identified Leigh‐like lesions with predominant involvement of globus pallidus in two of the female carriers. This pattern of involvement could be a diagnostic clue for PDHc deficiency and differs from Leigh syndrome caused by other mitochondrial disorders, in which striatum is more frequently affected [19, 31].

Another distinctive feature was the presence of dysmorphism, namely a thin upper lip and a deviant philtrum, found in all affected females of Family A. Facial dysmorphism such as narrow head, frontal bossing, prominent philtrum, and wide nasal bridge, has previously been described among mainly female individuals with PDHc deficiency, with an occurrence of 11% [4]. Prenatal involvement in females with PDHA1‐related disease may also include dysgenesis of the corpus callosum, asymmetrical periventricular leukomalacia, and subependymal cysts [19, 32]. The dysmorphic features, together with corpus callosum dysgenesis, have a striking resemblance to fetal alcohol syndrome. The latter is being attributed to inhibition of PDHc induced by acetaldehyde, the first product of alcohol oxidation [33, 34].

By providing an alternate source of acetyl‐CoA, derived from ketone bodies, to the developing brain, ketogenic diet is considered the treatment of choice in PDHc deficiency. It effectively reduces epilepsy and ataxia and promotes motor and neurocognitive development [6]. There is evidence suggesting its initiation as early as possible after birth to prevent further metabolic deterioration and irreversible cerebral damage [6, 35]. Despite a clear thiamine‐dependent mechanism, there is limited evidence on the response of PDHc deficiency to thiamine treatment. Occasional patients with mutations both within and outside the TPP binding region of the E1α subunit have shown clinical improvement and normalization of blood lactate upon thiamine treatment [9, 36]. In our study, three patients had been treated with thiamine supplementation at a dose of 300 mg/day. Although the treatment with thiamine was considered safe and well tolerable, its efficacy was difficult to assess during concomitant treatment with ketogenic diet.

In PDHA1‐related disease, it has been hypothesized that males with severe variants die in utero, and diagnosed females with severe variants survive [12, 37]. All our patients had missense variants in PDHA1, which likely contributed to their relatively mild phenotype compared to previously described female patients. Skewed X‐inactivation favoring the expression of the mutant allele to a varying degree has also been proposed as a mechanism behind the phenotypic variability in heterozygous females, even within the same family [13, 38]. This is demonstrated in the three‐generation Family A, with phenotypic features of Leigh syndrome, where the proband had earlier onset and more severe disease manifestations compared to the other two affected relatives. This is also the case in Family D, where the proband presented with signs of metabolic stroke, whereas the older sister developed a less severe phenotype, mainly characterized by psychomotor delay and a generalized movement disorder. The proportion of cells expressing the mutant allele varies from tissue to tissue [39]. Therefore, the residual enzyme activity measured in one tissue sample does not necessarily reflect the X‐inactivation pattern in other tissues, particularly the brain [39]. It can be used complementarily to genetic testing in the event of diagnostic dilemmas in presumed asymptomatic carriers.

There are a few limitations worth mentioning in this study. First, not all female relatives were available for clinical examination and genetic testing. Second, this was a cross‐sectional study. Although the individuals underwent physical examination by the same investigator (A.S.), this was performed at different time points from disease onset, making it difficult to study phenotypic changes over time and compare between patients. Third, the effects of treatment with thiamine and/or ketogenic diet were beyond the scope of this study and were not systematically assessed.

The results of this study provide support for the notion that PDHA1‐related PDHc deficiency is an underdiagnosed disorder in heterozygous female carriers. Peripheral axonal neuropathy is a key feature, and strokelike and Leigh‐like lesions and facial dysmorphism should raise the suspicion of underlying PDHc deficiency (Figure 2). Genetic analysis and clinical examination of potential female carriers are important for early diagnosis and genetic counseling, and have implications for treatment and family support.
