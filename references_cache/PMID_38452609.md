Cerebral creatine deficiency syndromes (CCDS) are inherited metabolic phenotypes
of creatine synthesis and transport. There are two enzyme deficiencies,
guanidinoacetate methyltransferase (GAMT), encoded by GAMT and arginine-glycine
amidinotransferase (AGAT), encoded by GATM, which are involved in the synthesis
of creatine. After synthesis, creatine is taken up by a sodium-dependent
membrane bound creatine transporter (CRTR), encoded by SLC6A8, into all organs.
Creatine uptake is very important especially in high energy demanding organs
such as the brain, and muscle. To classify the pathogenicity of variants in
GAMT, GATM, and SLC6A8, we developed the CCDS Variant Curation Expert Panel
(VCEP) in 2018, supported by The Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen), a National
Institutes of Health (NIH)-funded resource. We developed disease-specific
variant classification guidelines for GAMT-, GATM-, and SLC6A8-related CCDS,
adapted from the American College of Medical Genetics/Association of Molecular
Pathology (ACMG/AMP) variant interpretation guidelines. We applied specific
variant classification guidelines to 30 pilot variants in each of the three
genes that have variants associated with CCDS. Our CCDS VCEP was approved by the
ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group (SVI WG) and Clinical
Domain Oversight Committee in July 2022. We curated 181 variants including 72
variants in GAMT, 45 variants in GATM, and 64 variants in SLC6A8 and submitted
these classifications to ClinVar, a public variant database supported by the
National Center for Biotechnology Information. Missense variants were the most
common variant type in all three genes. We submitted 32 new variants and
reclassified 34 variants with conflicting interpretations. We report specific
phenotype (PP4) using a points system based on the urine and plasma
guanidinoacetate and creatine levels, brain magnetic resonance spectroscopy
(MRS) creatine level, and enzyme activity or creatine uptake in fibroblasts
ranging from PP4, PP4_Moderate and PP4_Strong. Our CCDS VCEP is one of the first
panels applying disease specific variant classification algorithms for an
X-linked disease. The availability of these guidelines and classifications can
guide molecular genetics and genomic laboratories and health care providers to
assess the molecular diagnosis of individuals with a CCDS phenotype.

Cerebral creatine deficiency syndromes (CCDS) are inherited metabolic phenotypes of creatine synthesis and transport. There are two enzyme deficiencies, guanidinoacetate methyltransferase (GAMT) (EC#2.1.1.2)[1], encoded by GAMT (MIM#602360), and arginine-glycine amidinotransferase (AGAT) (EC 2.1.4.1)[2], encoded by GATM (MIM#602360), which are involved in the synthesis of creatine. GAMT-related CCDS (MIM#612736) and GATM-related CCDS (MIM#612718) are inherited autosomal recessively. After synthesis, creatine is taken up by a sodium-dependent membrane bound creatine transporter (CRTR), encoded by SLC6A8 (MIM#300036), into all organs including the kidney, brain, and muscle. SLC6A8-related CCDS (MIM#300352)[3] is an X-linked disorder[4]. There are <150 individuals with GAMT-related CCDS, <25 individuals with GATM-related CCDS, and <200 patients with SLC6A8-related CCDS reported in the literature[5].

Global developmental delay, intellectual disability, or cognitive dysfunction, hypotonia, seizures, movement disorder and behavioral problems have been reported in individuals with CCDS, which are not specific to the CCDS phenotype, but can be observed as part of phenotypes in other genetic disorders. Urine, plasma, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) guanidinoacetate and creatine measurements are helpful to differentiate three disorders as well as biochemically confirm CCDS. The combination of elevated guanidinoacetate and low or low normal creatine are the specific biomarkers for GAMT-related CCDS and the combination of very low guanidinoacetate and low or low normal creatine are the specific biomarkers for GATM-related CCDS. Elevated urine creatine to creatinine ratio is the specific biomarker for SLC6A8-related CCDS in males. Symptomatic or asymptomatic females with SLC6A8-related CCDS can have normal or mildly elevated urine creatine to creatinine ratio. Males and females with GAMT- and GATM-related CCDS and males with SLC6A8-related CCDS have absent or markedly reduced creatine in brain magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS). The diagnosis is confirmed by the identification of pathogenic variants in GAMT, GATM and SLC6A8 using direct Sanger sequencing, if CCDS specific biomarkers are abnormal[5]. In recent years, targeted next generation sequencing panels (e.g., epilepsy, intellectual disability) and exome and genome sequencing identify individuals with variants in these genes. Biochemical investigations and enzyme activity measurements are helpful to assess variant pathogenicity.

Identification of individuals in the neonatal or early infantile period and initiation of the treatments result in a normal neurodevelopmental outcome in GAMT-[6] and GATM-related CCDS[7,8]. Recently the Recommended Uniform Screening Panel (RUSP) has been expanded to include GAMT-related CCDS among the conditions recommended for newborn screening in The United States[9].

The Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen), a National Institutes of Health (NIH)-funded resource, was established in 2013 to provide central resources for the definition and clinical relevance of genes and variants[10]. In 2015, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) (ACMG/AMP) published variant classification guidelines[11]. ClinGen supports more than 60 variant curation expert panels (VCEP) to develop standards to improve genomic knowledge (https://clinicalgenome.org/affiliation/vcep/#ep_table_heading). In 2018, we established the CCDS VCEP to develop standards for the curation of variants in GAMT, GATM and SLC6A8 at the 3-star review status and with the “FDA Recognized Database” tag. Using the ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines[12] and the ClinGen VCEP protocol (https://clinicalgenome.org/site/assets/files/3635/clingen_vcep_protocol_version_11_november2023.pdf)[13] we developed gene-specific variant classification guidelines for GAMT, GATM, and SLC6A8 and applied those to 30 pilot variants in each of the three CCDS genes. We received final approval from the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group (SVI WG) and Clinical Domain Oversight Committee in July 2022 to allow us to submit our classifications and supporting data to ClinVar and the ClinGen Evidence Repository (ERepo). To date, we have curated more than 180 variants using our gene specific guidelines for GAMT, GATM and SLC6A8. In this study, we described the development of ACMG/AMP criteria specifications for GAMT, GATM and SLC6A8 variants, and report our ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines for three genes associated with CCDS phenotypes.

The ClinGen CCDS VCEP members include clinical biochemical geneticists, medical geneticists, laboratory biochemical geneticists, molecular geneticists and genomic scientists, genetic counselors, research scientists with expertise in these disorders, a patient advocate and a nonprofit organization representative. The members held conference calls up to twice a month to review and discuss each of the ACMG/AMP criteria to develop gene and disease specific guidance on the classification of variants in GAMT, GATM, and SLC6A8. The CCDS VCEP members were divided into two groups, 1) creatine synthesis disorders, autosomal recessively inherited AGAT and GAMT deficiencies; 2) creatine transport disorder, X-linked SLC6A8-related CCDS. Our ClinGen CCDS VCEP followed the ClinGen VCEP protocol (ClinGen variant_curation_expert_panel_vcep_protocol_version_11 (clinicalgenome.org))[13] to fulfill the requirements to obtain approval from the ClinGen SVI WG and Clinical Domain Oversight Committee to submit our classifications and supporting data to ClinVar and the ClinGen Evidence Repository.

The 28 ACMG/AMP criteria were reviewed, and the applicable codes were specified based on gene-disease characteristics and/or following guidance from the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (SVI) working group[14]. Pathogenic criteria are weighted as very strong (PVS1)[15], strong (PS1–4), moderate (PM1–6), and supporting (PP1–5). Benign criteria are weighted as stand-alone (BA1), strong (BS1–4) or supporting (BP1–6)[12].

As loss of function is the molecular mechanism of CCDS, PVS1 was applied to all three genes[15]. Types of variants such as frameshift, nonsense, canonical ±1 or 2 splice site, and initiation codon variants or single or multiple exon deletions can lead to absence of gene product due to nonsense-mediated decay if a premature termination codon is generated upstream of the last 50 base pairs of the penultimate exon. To facilitate the interpretation of splice site variants, the in-frame and out-of-frame consequences for exon skipping in GAMT and GATM were determined using following assumptions: 1) disruption of a donor splice site will result in skipping the upstream exon and 2) disruption of an acceptor splice site will result in skipping of the downstream exon. However, all variants were first analyzed by SpliceAI to rule out the possibility of generation and use of a nearby cryptic splice site, which could result in a different molecular consequence, and any prediction could be over-ruled by the availability of RT-PCR or RNA-Seq data (Supplemental Table 1)[16–20].

A REVEL score was applied to predict the impact of missense variants[21]. PP3 was applied to missense variants with a REVEL score ≥0.7 for all three genes. BP4 was applied for a REVEL score ≤0.15 (GATM), ≤0.5 (GAMT), and ≤0.2 (SLC6A8) based on analysis of REVEL scores for limited number of that were clearly benign based on population frequency. These thresholds may be updated in future iterations of the specifications based on new guidance[22].

SpliceAI was applied to predict the impact of all variants on splicing[23]. PP3 was applied if a SpliceAI score was ≥0.5. No PP3 or BP4 criterion was applied, if a SpliceAI score was <0.2 (no impact on splicing) [23].

The allele frequency calculator (http://cardiodb.org/allelefrequencyapp) was used to derive the minor allele frequency (MAF) thresholds for BA1 and BS1 for GAMT, GATM, and SLC6A8 (Supplemental Document 1) [24–30]. The MAF threshold is calculated using maximum allelic contribution, prevalence, maximum genetic contribution, and penetrance. For the genes causing autosomal recessive creatine synthesis disorders, GAMT and GATM, the maximum genetic contribution and penetrance were set at 100%, assuming that biallelic pathogenic variants are fully penetrant in these genes and solely cause GAMT and AGAT deficiencies, respectively. Estimated prevalence of GAMT-related CCDS of 1 in 114,000 for GAMT-related CCDS (chosen the highest prevalence in general population to ensure that no potentially pathogenic variants exceeded these thresholds)[30] and estimated prevalence of 1 in 3,450,000 for GATM-related CCDS[29] were used in calculations for BA1 and BS1. Maximum allelic contribution of 100% was used to calculate BA1 and 40% for GAMT and 25% for GATM was used for BS1 based on the frequency of the most common variant reported in individuals with GAMT-related CCDS (GAMT c.327G>A variant)[31] and GATM-related CCDS (GATM c.484+1G>T variant)[29]. The MAF thresholds for PM2 were derived based on the highest population MAF for these variants in gnomAD v2.1.1. PM2 was applied at the supporting level based on the recommendations of the ClinGen SVI.

Prevalence of SLC6A8-related CCDS has most often been assessed in cohorts of individuals with neurodevelopmental phenotypes[5], where variants in SLC6A8 have been identified in up to 2% of the cohort[32]. The maximum genetic contribution was set at 100% for SLC6A8, assuming that pathogenic variants are fully penetrant in hemizygous males, and that this gene solely causes SLC6A8-related CCDS.

An estimated prevalence of 1:100,000 was used to estimate BA1, BS1, and PM2. Penetrance was set at 0.5 to account for female carriers who may be asymptomatic or mildly affected and escape clinical attention. Given the true penetrance in males is 100% for a pediatric onset condition, we specified that to meet the PM2 criterion, no homozygotes or hemizygous individuals may be present in any population database. Once population thresholds were set for BA1, BS1 and PM2, for all three genes, gnomAD v2 was assessed for the allele frequencies of known pathogenic variants following SVI WG guidance (https://clinicalgenome.org/site/assets/files/5182/pm2_-_svi_recommendation_-_approved_sept2020.pdf)[14]. In addition to being used to account for results of case-control studies, PS4 may also be used to count individual cases for rare autosomal dominant and X-linked disorders for which case-control studies are not available. PS4 was used in this manner to count unrelated individuals with sufficient evidence to support a diagnosis of creatine transporter deficiency (elevated urine creatine/creatinine ratio on one occasion at a minimum). With an increasing number of unrelated individuals with the variant, PS4 was applied at increasing strength, ranging from PS4_Supporting for one individual to PS4_Very Strong for four unrelated individuals in addition to any cases counted for PP4. PS4 was determined to not be applicable for the genes causing autosomal recessive creatine synthesis disorders which, instead, utilize PM3 as a case counting mechanism.

As per ACMG/AMP definition of functional assays, PS3 (deleterious) and BS3 (benign) were applied if variants were functionally characterized using site-directed mutagenesis and overexpression in heterologous cell types (in GAMT-deficient fibroblasts and HeLa cells for GAMT variants; in HeLa cells for GATM variants and CRTR-deficient fibroblasts for SLC6A8 variants) (Supplemental Table 2) [26,27,29,33–35]. Based on further guidance from the SVI WG, evidence for PS3 and BS3 was applied at the supporting level[36].

For SLC6A8, PS2 (de novo) was applied if there was an affected male with a variant and a non-carrier mother and a confirmed maternity. For an affected female paternity and maternity confirmation was required. For an apparently de novo variant in a male, without confirmation of maternity, or in a female without confirmation of maternity and paternity, PM6 was applied.

PM3 (in trans evidence) was applied to GAMT and GATM variants identified in compound heterozygous or homozygous individuals, using the points system developed by the ClinGen SVI. As recommended, the classification of the variant on the other allele did not use evidence from the variant under analysis to avoid circular logic.

PP4 (specific phenotype) was applied using a points system developed by the CCDS VCEP, based on the urine and plasma guanidinoacetate and creatine levels, brain MRS creatine level, and enzyme activity levels or creatine uptake in fibroblasts ranging from PP4, PP4_Moderate and PP4_Strong (Table 2). The weight of evidence assigned for PP4 is dependent upon the specificity of features for a specific gene; the higher the specificity, the higher the weight of evidence for PP4. As the phenotypes of CCDS (e.g., developmental delay, seizures, hypotonia and movement disorder) are nonspecific, we did not use the phenotypic information for the application of PP4. However, each of the CCDS has disease specific biomarkers that, when present, increase the likelihood of finding pathogenic variants in the gene including creatine and guanidinoacetate in body fluids, absence of creatine in brain MRS, and enzyme deficiency in lymphocytes or fibroblasts or decreased creatine uptake in fibroblasts. Based on expert opinion, we created a points system to reflect the specificity of different features, and combinations of features for each gene, and to assign the weight of PP4 accordingly. We reviewed the medical literature for all published individuals with CCDS (up to 2020, at the time of development of point system) and applied the point system to help us for our decision making.

Specific recommendations for the use of PP4 include: 1) Two or more data types are required for PP4_Strong; 2) For SLC6A8 variants, if PP4 is assigned, at any weight, the same patient cannot be included in the PS4 count (to avoid double-counting evidence; 3) If multiple unrelated probands with the variant have been identified, the case with the highest PP4 points is assigned the appropriate weight for PP4, and the other cases are used for PS4; 4) The variant must meet PM2_Supporting for PP4 to apply at any strength; 5) Full gene sequencing (gene and biomarker specific) has been carried out for the CCDS genes if PP4_Strong is applied, if not, consider downgrading.

Segregation data (PP1, BS4) were specified for SLC6A8 based on the principle that a family with multiple affected members with a pathogenic variant and unaffected members with no pathogenic variant increases the likelihood that the variant of interest is the cause for disease in that family. Likewise, the strength of this observation should correlate with the number of individuals observed in those families with expected segregation of a pathogenic variant. Affected males or mildly affected variant positive females should be counted towards segregation. Segregation of a variant in an unaffected female can be counted, if that female has an affected brother or son. However, given that many females are asymptomatic, variant positive unaffected females should not be counted as a non-segregation event[37].

Thirty pilot variants were selected for each of the three genes (for GAMT, GATM, and SLC6A8) from ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/intro/), the medical literature and the Leiden Open Variation Database (LOVD v.3.0). These included variants with a ClinVar classification of pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) (n=10), variant of unknown significance (VUS) (n=10) and likely benign/benign (LB/B) (n=10). Pilot variants were annotated using NM_000156.6 [The Matched Annotation from the NCBI and EMBL-EBI (MANE) Select] for GAMT-related CCDS (MONDO:0012999), NM_001482.3 (MANE Select) for GATM-related CCDS (MONDO:0012996), and NM_005629.4 (MANE Select) (MONDO:0010305) for SLC6A8-related CCDS. Biocurators collected data for each variant from published literature, and public databases such as Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/)[38] and entered the data into the ClinGen Variant Curation Interface (VCI, https://curation.clinicalgenome.org/)[39] to generate a provisional classification for each variant. The applied criteria were discussed on conference calls to reach a consensus on the classification of each variant for each gene. Questions from expert-reviewers and biocurators were discussed during bi-weekly meetings, to reach a consensus. Approved variants are published in ClinVar and the ERepo.

After approval of the CCDS VCEP’s ACMG/AMP specifications for each gene, the members continued to classify variants within GAMT, GATM, and SLC6A8, focusing on variants published in the medical literature.

The ClinGen CCDS VCEP was approved in July 2022. Currently, there are 15 members of the CCDS VCEP from the United States, and Canada (https://clinicalgenome.org/affiliation/50047/). The CCDS ACMG classification rules are listed in the ClinGen Criteria Specification registry for GAMT
https://cspec.genome.network/cspec/ui/svi/doc/GN026/versions (Supplemental Document 2)[40]; for GATM
https://cspec.genome.network/cspec/ui/svi/doc/GN025/versions (Supplemental Document 3)[41] and for SLC6A8
https://cspec.genome.network/cspec/ui/svi/doc/GN027/versions) (Supplemental Document 4)[42].

To date, the CCDS VCEP has submitted 181 variant classifications to ClinVar and the ClinGen ERepo including 72 variants in GAMT, 45 variants in GATM, and 64 variants in SLC6A8. A summary of the variant types and classifications are depicted in Figure 1, 2, and 3, respectively for GAMT, GATM and SLC6A8. Of these 181 variants, 32 variants (17.7%) were submitted to ClinVar for the first time by our CCDS VCEP, including 13 variants in GAMT, two variants in GATM, and 17 variants in SLC6A8. Seven variants in GATM classified by our VCEP (five submitted by OMIM, and two by laboratories) were previously listed in ClinVar without any classification.

There were 34 variants for which classifications from different ClinVar submitters were conflicting (P/LP/VUS n=14; VUS/LB/B n=19; and LP/VUS/LB/B n=1) prior to classification by the VCEP. This included 18 variants in GAMT, five in GATM, and 11 in SLC6A8. The CCDS VCEP classified 21 (61.8%) of those variants as P/LP (n=10) or B (n=11) thus reducing the uncertainty of these variant classifications. The remaining 13 variants were classified as VUS. A further six variants, which were previously submitted as VUS by a single submitter, were classified as LP (two in GAMT, and one in GATM) or B (three in GATM), while the VCEP classified 5 variants as VUS, conflicting with previous submitters (Supplemental Table 3). All other VCEP classifications were consistent with other ClinVar submitters.

Two of the CCDS, GAMT- and GATM-related CCDS have disease specific treatments. For this reason, early diagnosis is crucial to improve neurodevelopmental outcomes in these CCDS. To date it is not known if individuals with SLC6A8-related CCDS, who are identified and treated early with arginine, glycine, and creatine supplements, may or may not have improved neurodevelopmental outcomes. If individuals with CRTR can be diagnosed early, this may be a good opportunity to investigate the effectiveness of these supplements. Unfortunately, the phenotypes of the CCDS are non-specific and the CCDS are not included into the differential diagnosis of neurodevelopmental disorders widely. In countries with public funding or insurance services, untargeted molecular genomic investigations can help diagnose individuals with CCDS. The VUS is an important concern from untargeted molecular genomic investigations. CCDS are one of the few genetic diseases that guanidinoacetate and creatine levels in body fluids, and creatine peak in brain MRS can help with the re-classification of VUS and confirm the diagnosis of CCDS. To help health care providers, our CCDS VCEP developed specific variant classification algorithms for GAMT, GATM, and SLC6A8 specific variant classification algorithms to reduce the uncertainty of variant classifications and to confirm diagnosis of CCDS. Since our specifications were first approved, guidance from the SVI has been published to assist in the application of various types of evidence including the use of in silico tools for prediction of the impact of missense variants, assessment of the pathogenicity of splice variants, and segregation [37]. Future iterations of our guidelines will take these recommendations into account and previous classifications will be updated accordingly. Of note, our CCDS VCEP is one of the first panels developing and applying disease specific variant classification recommendations for an X-linked disease at the time of drafting our guidelines. In 2022 Rett/Angelman-like VCEP published their specifications for an X-linked gene, SLC9A6 [43] Re-curation of variants that have been previously classified by a VCEP is an important part of the ClinGen variant classification process. According to ClinGen guidance, any variant that has been classified as likely pathogenic or VUS must be reassessed within two years to evaluate any new data that could change the classification. Variants must also be reclassified if a conflicting classification (e.g., P/LP vs VUS, or LB/B vs VUS, or P/LP vs LB/B) has been submitted since the CCDS VCEP submitted their classification. This guidance serves to ensure that any conflicts or outdated classifications are addressed appropriately. Furthermore, any variants that will change classification based on updated guidance will be reclassified as resubmitted to ClinVar. There are more than 60 ClinGen VCEP to develop standards for variant classification to improve genomic knowledge. In the majority of those VCEP, there are no disease specific biomarkers to confirm pathogenicity of variants. To the best of our knowledge, only two VCEP, ACADVL VCEP and Lysosomal Diseases VCEP, applied PP4 using biomarker and enzyme levels including PP4_Supporting and PP4_Moderate. To the best of our knowledge, our VCEP is the only one using biochemical data for variant classification using a range of points system for PP4 including urine and plasma guanidinoacetate and creatine levels and creatine peak in brain MRS ranging from PP4_Supporting to PP4_Strong.

A review of ClinVar data (accessed December 3, 2023) revealed that a relatively small number of variants in the three genes had conflicting interpretations including 18 out of 601(3%) variants in GAMT, eight out of 447 (1.8%) variants in GATM, and 29 out of 1,113 (2.6%) variants in SLC6A8. Most of those variants with conflicting interpretations (46/55, 83.6%) were classified as VUS versus LB/B while the remaining variants were classified P/LP versus VUS including three variants in GAMT and five variants in SLC6A8. Up to 50% of variants in these three CCDS genes were classified as VUS in ClinVar including 223 out of 447 (50%) variants in GATM, 237 out of 601 variants (39%) in GAMT, and 221 out of 1113 (20%) variants in SLC6A8. To resolve VUS and conflicting interpretations, the CCDS VCEP has been collaborating with the Association for Creatine Deficiencies (ACD), an international nonprofit organization dedicated to the CCDS (https://creatineinfo.org/). The ACD has developed a patient registry and shares data with Genome Connect[44,45], a ClinGen effort that submits variant classifications and anonymized clinical data to ClinVar and shares data from the ACD registry with the CCDS VCEP. These collaborations will likely provide data to allow for the resolution of VUS and conflicting interpretations, as well as increase the number of variants in ClinVar for the three CCDS genes. In summary, we report specific variant classification algorithms for GAMT, GATM, and SLC6A8 to guide health care providers and molecular genetics and genomic laboratories for the confirmation of CCDS diagnosis for the first time. Our CCDS VCEP will continue curating variants in these three CCDS genes. Our group plans to update the ACMG/AMP specifications for each gene to incorporate new recommendations and guidance including the use of in silico predictors for missense variants[22] and interpretation of splicing variants[46] in the future.
