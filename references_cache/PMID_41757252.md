# Early pacing in a child with Lodder-Merla syndrome and progressive sinus node dysfunction: a case report.
**Authors:** AlAraibi AJ, Shakeeb FN, Bhat A, Husain F, Kalis NN
**Journal:** Eur Heart J Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/ehjcr/ytag109](https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjcr/ytag109)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Lodder-Merla syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive condition occasionally resulting in cardiac manifestations, including progressive sinus node dysfunction (SND).

CASE SUMMARY: A 1-year-and-10-month-old boy presented with seizures, severe global developmental delay, and hypotonia. Sinus pauses were noted on ECG. Genetic testing confirmed a homozygous splice-site mutation in GNB5, consistent with Lodder-Merla syndrome, and a heterozygous SPTBN4 variant. Holter monitoring initially revealed intermittent sinus pauses; subsequent testing showed marked progression. Following multidisciplinary evaluation, an epicardial VVI pacemaker was implanted, after which cardiac rhythm stabilized with reliable suppression of prolonged pauses.

DISCUSSION: Cardiac involvement in GNB5-related disorders may be overshadowed by the dominant neurodevelopmental presentation. This case highlights an important cardiovascular dimension to the syndrome. SND can be progressive and life-threatening, requiring early recognition and individualized pacing strategies. In young children with significant comorbidities, classic bradyarrhythmia symptoms may be absent, underscoring the need for a high index of suspicion and proactive rhythm surveillance to enable timely intervention.

SND in LMS can progress rapidly, underscoring the need for early rhythm surveillance and individualized pacing strategies.

The presence of additional genetic variants may modify disease expression and highlight the importance of comprehensive genetic evaluation in multisystem disorders.

Lodder–Merla syndrome (LMS) is a rare autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in GNB5. Fewer than 50 cases have been described, typically presenting with profound hypotonia, global developmental delay, epilepsy, sensory deficits, and autonomic dysfunction. Cardiac manifestations particularly sinus node dysfunction (SND) appear to be the most prevalent feature. In the largest cohort reported to date, SND was documented in 27 of 28 affected individuals.1 Despite this, cardiac manifestations may remain under-recognized due to the dominant neurological presentation, yet they carry substantial morbidity and risk of sudden cardiac death.

A 1-year-and-10-month-old boy with a history of seizure episodes was referred to our cardiology clinic after his second seizure led to an emergency department visit, where monitoring revealed significant bradycardia and sinus pauses consistent with SND.

The patient was born at term with a birth weight of 2.5 kg. His first seizure occurred at 12 months of age. Early infancy was otherwise marked by developmental delays and significant hypotonia. Family history revealed consanguineous parents (first degree relatives).

Examination revealed profound global developmental delay, including absence of speech. Severe generalized hypotonia was noted. Visual impairment was evident, with sensitivity limited to light and motion. He had bilateral convergent strabismus and horizontal nystagmus. Hearing appeared partially intact; he responded to environmental sounds but not to his name. Additional findings included features suggestive of obstructive sleep apnoea, chronic otitis media, and adenoid hypertrophy.

Initial concerns surrounding seizures led to comprehensive neurological and metabolic evaluations, including EEG, brain MRI, and metabolic screening, all of which returned normal results, excluding common epileptic or metabolic causes. An incidental finding of bradycardia and sinus pauses with normal atrioventricular (AV) conduction was noted during cardiac evaluations post-tonsillectomy. Echocardiography was normal, with only a minor patent foramen ovale.

Genetic testing via whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous splice-site variant in GNB5 (c.239-2A > C), classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG criteria (PVS1, PM2). Confirming the diagnosis of Lodder–Merla syndrome Type 1. An additional heterozygous likely pathogenic nonsense variant in SPTBN4 (c.958G > T; p.Glu320Ter) was detected which was also classified as likely pathogenic (PVS1, PM2).

Initial Holter monitoring documented sinus bradycardia with an average heart rate of 127 b.p.m. (minimum 77 b.p.m., maximum 177 b.p.m.) and 47 sinus pauses, the longest lasting 3.56 s. AV conduction was preserved, consistent with isolated SND (Figure 1).

Initial 24-h Holter recording. Minimum HR 77 b.p.m.; 47 sinus pauses detected, longest 3.56 s, with preserved AV conduction.

However, repeat monitoring showed further progression, with an average heart rate of 113 b.p.m. (minimum 65 b.p.m., maximum 166 b.p.m.) and 274 sinus pauses, the longest lasting 7.13 s (Figure 2).

Follow-up 22-h Holter recording. Minimum HR 65 b.p.m.; dramatic increase to 274 sinus pauses, longest 7.13 s, indicating rapid progression of SND.

The progression of SND prompted further extensive MDT consultations. After comprehensive discussions considering the potential risks and benefits, epicardial pacemaker implantation (Medtronic Azure™ S SR MRI SureScan™, Model W3SR01; VVI 60 b.p.m.) carried out surgically. During the procedure, the lead was screwed onto the right ventricle, and the pulse generator was placed beneath the rectus abdominis muscle (Figure 3).

Post-operative CXR. Anteroposterior view showing the epicardial ventricular lead on the right ventricle and the pacemaker generator situated beneath the rectus abdominis muscle.

Implantation parameters were stable at the time of surgery, with an acute pacing threshold of 1.5 V at 0.4 ms, R-wave sensing of 13.5 mV, and lead impedance of approximately 323 Ω, all within expected ranges for an epicardial system.

Following pacemaker implantation, ventricular pacing reliably prevented the prolonged sinus pauses, resulting in a stabilized heart rate profile even though the underlying SND remained unchanged. A post-operative ECG demonstrating appropriate ventricular pacing is provided (Figure 4).

Post-implantation ECG demonstrating sinus rhythm with ventricular-paced complexes. Ventricular rate is 96 b.p.m., PR interval 136 ms, QRS duration 72 ms, with pacing morphology consistent with appropriate function of the epicardial RV lead.

At the 6-month post-operative follow-up, the pacemaker demonstrated stable function. The ventricular pacing burden was approximately 12%, with RV sensing of 11.8 mV, a pacing threshold of 1.25 V at 0.4 ms, and a lead impedance of 304 Ω. No arrhythmic events or device-related complications were recorded.

Cessation of the previously reported seizure episodes was observed after implantation, which supported the decision to withhold antiepileptic medication. No chronic medications were required for ongoing management.

LMS encompasses a spectrum of neurodevelopmental and autonomic abnormalities driven by biallelic loss-of-function variants in GNB5. The severe Type 1 phenotype, typically associated with null alleles, is characterized by profound developmental impairment and clinically significant bradyarrhythmias.2 Our patient was homozygous for the splice-site variant c.239-2A > C, a likely pathogenic null allele. This genotype aligns with the rapid progression of SND observed and is consistent with the severe end of the LMS cardiac phenotype.1 To contextualize our patient within previously reported cases, Table 1 summarizes key cardiac and genetic features from the published literature, including the largest cohort reported by Lodder et al.1

The genetic testing also identified a heterozygous SPTBN4 likely pathogenic variant. The potential interaction between GNB5 and SPTBN4 has not been described previously. While we cannot exclude the possibility that this SPTBN4 variant may modify the neurodevelopmental presentation in the context of a complex genetic architecture, current evidence remains insufficient to support a definitive oligogenic mechanism. Based on available literature, the cardiac phenotype in our patient is best explained by the homozygous pathogenic GNB5 variant, consistent with the established spectrum of LMS. The potential contribution of SPTBN4 to neurodevelopmental severity therefore remains speculative; however, this case is, to our knowledge, the first to document the co-occurrence of GNB5 and SPTBN4 variants, raising a novel hypothesis regarding possible neurodevelopmental modification in LMS.3,4

Holter rhythm evaluation demonstrated a rapid progression of SND. This isolated nodal failure supports a phenotype of primary SND rather than generalized conduction disease. Mechanistically, GNB5 loss-of-function disrupts Gβ5-mediated parasympathetic modulation of the sinoatrial node through the RGS–GIRK pathway, resulting in heightened cholinergic sensitivity and impaired pacemaker activity. This pathogenic mechanism aligns with findings in gnb5 knockout zebrafish, which exhibit comparable bradycardia and sinoatrial node dysfunction.5

Management was complicated by the patient’s profound developmental and sensory impairments, which limited the ability to assess classic symptoms of bradyarrhythmia. Seizure activity remained the only potential clinical correlate and resolved following pacemaker implantation. Although a causal link cannot be definitively established, the temporal association suggests that prolonged pauses with resulting haemodynamic instability may have contributed. However, alternative explanations including the natural history of febrile seizures in children with GNB5-related neurodevelopmental impairment cannot be excluded, particularly in the absence of post-implantation EEG data.

Although atrial-based pacing is generally preferred in SND with intact AV conduction, several paediatric-specific considerations supported the selection of an epicardial VVI system in this case. In infants and small children, atrial lead stability is markedly lower than ventricular lead stability, particularly in neuromuscular or genetically fragile myocardium. These technical limitations often make reliable atrial capture difficult in the epicardial position. Moreover, the patient demonstrated rapidly progressive SND, and importantly, AV block has been reported in several individuals with LMS,1 indicating that conduction disease in GNB5-related disorders may not remain confined to the sinus node. Given the potential for progression to multi-level conduction disease, providing stable ventricular support was considered essential to prevent sudden deterioration.

Paediatric pacing guidelines also support this individualized approach. The 2021 PACES Expert Consensus emphasizes that pacing modality in children should not follow a single algorithm, but instead be tailored to age, size, venous access, growth considerations, and expected lead performance.6 Similarly, the 2022 HRS consensus on arrhythmia management in neuromuscular disorders endorses early pacing in progressive conduction disease to avoid sudden clinical decline, as demonstrated in disorders such as Kearns–Sayre syndrome.7 Although LMS is not specifically addressed in existing guidelines, the overarching principles clearly support early, individualized intervention when the risk of progression is high.

The timely pacemaker implantation eliminated prolonged sinus pauses, stabilized cardiac rhythm, and coincided with cessation of seizure activity. These outcomes underscore the critical haemodynamic benefit and life-saving potential of early intervention in high-risk paediatric neuromuscular disorders.
