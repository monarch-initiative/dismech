# Perinatal death in pig models of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy carrying sarcomere pathogenic variants.
**Authors:** Flisikowska T, Petersen B, Mearini G, Huber D, Kurome M, Stoff M, Schlossarek S, Lucas-Hahn A, Wolf E, Montag J, Schnieke A, Carrier L
**Journal:** J Mol Cell Cardiol Plus (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jmccpl.2025.100457](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmccpl.2025.100457)

## Content

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is an autosomal-dominant disease caused by genetic variants in sarcomeric proteins, particularly in myosin binding protein C3 (MYBPC3) and myosin heavy chain 7 (MYH7). Less known is that neonatal forms of HCM rapidly evolve into systolic heart failure and death within the first year of life. Although myosin inhibitors are now used to treat obstructive forms of adult HCM, there is still a need for novel therapeutic options and predictive animal models to assess them. Our aim was to model in pigs severe forms of human HCM carrying bi-allelic truncating MYBPC3 mutations or heterozygous missense MYH7 variants. Pigs were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 genome or cytosine-base editing in porcine fibroblasts combined with somatic cell nuclear transfer. Several pregnancies were established but piglets were non-viable. The MYBPC3-edited piglet exhibited a compound heterozygous mutation leading to a markedly low level of mutant MYBPC3 protein and cardiac hypertrophy, reflecting the situation in infants. The MYH7-edited piglets carried the heterozygous p.Arg453Cys variant and exhibited ventricular hypertrophy. In conclusion, MYBPC3 and MYH7 cloned piglets developed cardiac hypertrophy and died around birth, indicating that pigs are particularly sensitive to sarcomeric gene mutations.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most prevalent inherited cardiac disease with an estimated frequency of 1:200 to 1:500 [1,2]. HCM is transmitted in an autosomal dominant fashion, i.e. one affected allele is sufficient to trigger the disease and is caused by genetic variants in components of the cardiac sarcomere. Of these, myosin binding protein C3 (MYBPC3) and myosin heavy chain 7 (MYH7) are the most frequently mutated genes [3]. In contrast to MYH7 in which most variants are missense, >75 % of MYBPC3 variants lead to a truncated and unstable protein (for review, see [4]). Findings in patients and in mouse models with HCM indicate that the most prevalent disease mechanisms associated with MYBPC3 and MYH7 genetic variants are protein haploinsufficiency and poison peptide, respectively (for reviews, see [[5], [6], [7]]). HCM is characterized by left ventricular hypertrophy with or without left ventricular outflow tract obstruction and consequent congestion, diastolic dysfunction, myocardial disarray and increased interstitial fibrosis. HCM exhibits a large phenotypic variability. Although mean life expectancy of patients, who survive into young adulthood does not markedly differ from the normal population, sudden cardiac death is common in young adults with HCM, particularly in athletes [8]. About 5–15 % of adult HCM patients can progress to a hypokinetic, dilated form of the disease, typically referred to as end-stage or dilated-hypokinetic HCM [9,10]. Less known is that (rare) neonatal forms of HCM rapidly evolve into end-stage HCM with severe systolic dysfunction and death within the first year of life (for a review, see [4]). Some of these infants have bi-allelic truncating MYBPC3 mutations, expected to result in low level or absence of mutant MYBPC3 protein in the heart. In the absence of treatment options, gene therapy might provide a realistic alternative to heart transplantation. We previously provided evidence of long-term disease prevention by adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9)-mediated Mybpc3 gene therapy in homozygous Mybpc3-targeted knock-in mice, which genetically mimic human neonatal cardiomyopathies [11].

With the recent development of myosin inhibitors for patients with obstructive HCM [12,13] and a current phase 1b clinical trial with AAV9-MYBPC3 gene therapy (TN-201 [Tenaya Therapeutics]) for patients with non-obstructive MYBPC3-associated HCM a new era has started. However, not all patients with HCM may profit from myosin inhibitors [14], specifically mavacamten failed to meet the dual primary endpoint in phase 3 trial for non-obstructive HCM (ODYSSEY-HCM), and MYBPC3 gene therapy, and other approaches tested in rodents hold promise [15]. The objective towards clinical application is to develop larger animal models to evaluate the pathophysiology and test therapies. Pigs are a suitable species because they share many similarities with humans in anatomy and their physiological and pathophysiological responses (for a review, see [16]). In addition, pig models are widely accepted by regulatory bodies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency on the path to clinical trials. Two Yucatan minipig models of HCM created by conventional gene-targeted knock-in in fetal porcine fibroblasts were presented during scientific meetings previously [17,18]. Whereas the heterozygous p.Arg403Gly MYH7 variant resulted in a hypercontractile phenotype [17,19,20], the heterozygous p.Glu330X truncating variant in MYBPC3 led to systolic dysfunction [18]. These data point to unexpected opposite cardiac phenotypes in MYBPC3 and MYH7 mutant pigs. Another study showed successful TALEN-mediated insertion of the p.Arg723Gly MYH7 variant into porcine fibroblasts, but the cloned pigs died perinatally or early after birth [21].

In this study, we aimed at generating pig models with CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing or base editing in porcine fibroblasts in combination with somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) carrying either a bi-allelic MYBPC3 variant or a mono-allelic p.Arg453Cys MYH7 variant, both associated with a severe phenotype in humans ([[22], [23], [24]], and for a review [4]). We show that the generated compound heterozygous MYBPC3-edited and heterozygous MYH7-edited pigs both exhibited early perinatal death. While no mechanism could be deciphered, our findings suggest that pigs are particularly sensitive to mutations in sarcomeric proteins.

Porcine fetal and kidney fibroblasts (PFF and PKF, respectively) were isolated from male German Landrace pigs, and PKF from male and/or female Black Minipigs (BM-PKF), or F1 hybrid pigs (German Landrace x Black Minipigs, Hyb-PKF) by standard method [25]. PKFs and PFFs were cultured with high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Sigma, D5796) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM non-essential amino acids (NEAA), and 2 mM l-glutamine. Cells were passaged every 3–5 days and maintained at 50–90 % confluency in an incubator at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). For single cell selection, we used a limiting dilution approach in a 96-well format and cultivated in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 30 % FBS, 2 mM NEAA, 2 mM l-glutamine and CloneR (StemCell technologies, 05888, 10 μg/ml). When cells were expanded to 12 well-plates, media was changed to 10 % FBS without CloneR.

For CRISPR/Cas9-mediated cleavage of exons 6 and 23 of MYBPC3, the following single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were selected: Ex6 5’–GCTGTGAGGTATCCACCA–3′ and Ex23 5’–GACTCCTGCACGGTGCAGT–3′ that were cloned into pX330-Cas9 vector (Addgene, 42230) carrying a puromycin resistance. Transfections of PKF were performed by nucleofection (program C-17) of 1 × 106 cells using Nucleofector™ Solution (Lonza) and 5 μg plasmid. One day after transfection, the cells were selected for 48 h with 1.5 μg/ml puromycin and then expanded and analysed as single cell clones.

For CRISPR/Cas9-Base-Editor-mediated c.1357C>T transition in MYH7, sgRNA 5′- AGCCACGCCAGTACTTCATA-3′ was cloned into vector BPK1520 and co-transfected with pCMV-BE3 vector (Addgene, 73021) via nucleofection (Neon™ transfection system; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 100 μl Kit) with 20 μg BPK1520, and 20 μg pCMV-BE3, 1650 V, 3 pulses, 10 ms). Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were seeded in a limiting dilution and expanded as single clones.

For analysis of MYBPC3-edited clones, genomic DNA was isolated from cell clones or ear clip biopsy using GenElute Mammalian Genomic DNA Kit (Sigma, G1N350-1KT). PCR amplification was performed across the sgRNA target sites for exons 6 and 23 using specific primers. For exon 6, the primers used were Int5_F (5’–CTGCTCCTTCCACTCTTCCA–3′) and Int6_R (5’–TCACCTATTCACCTGTGCCC–3′), resulting in an amplicon of 723 bp. For exon 23, the primers were Ex23_F (5’–GCAGAGTCCGAGTAGAGACC–3′) and Ex23_R (5’–CAATGGGCATGAAGGGCTGG–3′), resulting in an amplicon of 812 bp. All PCRs were carried out using GoTaq polymerase (Promega, M3005) with the following thermal cycling parameters: 2 min, 95 °C then 35 cycles of: 30 s, 95 °C; 30 s, 60 °C; 60 s, 72 °C; followed by 5 min at 72 °C. Amplicons were verified by Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics).

For analysis of the MYH7-edited clones, cells or ear clip biopsies were lysed in lysis buffer (0.05 % SDS, 0.05 mg/ml proteinase K, 1× Buffer GoTaq reaction buffer) at 37 °C, 50 min and 95 °C, 12 min, and lysates were subjected to PCR analysis using primers 5’-TGGGTGGAGGAGTGTCCAGTA-3′ and 5’-GATGCAGAGCTGCTCAAAGCTGTT-3′ resulting in an amplicon of 492 bp. PCRs were carried out using GoTaq polymerase (Promega, M7841) with the following thermal cycling parameters: 2 min, 95 °C then 45 cycles of: 30 s, 95 °C; 30 s, 60 °C; 30 s, 72 °C; followed by 2 min at 72 °C. To detect cell clones with the C>T transition we subjected the PCR products to restriction analysis with NlaIII for 1 h. The wild-type (WT) alleles are expected to give two amplicons of 427 bp and 65 bp, whereas the R453C-gene-edited alleles should exhibit 3 amplicons of 246 bp, 181 bp and 65 bp. Cell clones with R453C-specific band profiles were subjected to Sanger sequencing (GATC, Germany) for verification.

Three to five selected MYBPC3-mutated cell clones were pooled and used for SCNT. SCNT was performed as described previously [26]. Two single clones of MYH7-mutated cells were used for SCNT as described previously [27].

RNA was isolated from heart tissue using innuSPEED Tissue RNA Kit or InviTrap® Spin Universal RNA Mini Kit (Invitek, 1060100300) according to the manufacturers' protocols. Five hundred ng of RNA was used for the cDNA synthesis using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Invitrogen, 12,574,026) or Tetro reverse transcriptase (Bioline, BIO-65050) according to the manufacturers' protocols. MYH7 mRNA for R453C-pigs was reverse transcribed into cDNA with a gene-specific primer (5’-GCTGAGCAGCTTGAGAGAGGACTT-3′).

SCNT experiments were carried out with pools of about five cell clones with bi-allelic MYBPC3 mutations in exon 6 or 23. Reconstituted embryos were transferred to seven sows. Two pregnancies (> 28 days) were established and confirmed by ultrasound. One was lost during late state, while the other went to term and resulted in one stillborn and eight mummified piglets. The stillborn female piglet had a birth body weight (BW) of 992 g and showed no obvious abnormalities. The BW was >2-fold higher than in newborn control female Göttingen minipigs (372 ± 101 g) [33]. A necropsy was carried out and tissue and organ samples obtained for molecular analysis.

DNA was isolated from the ear clip tissue and cultured kidney fibroblasts of the stillborn piglet and from a control WT piglet (Fig. 2A). PCR analysis between exons 22 and 24 produced the expected 812-bp fragment in both the WT and MYBPC3 mutant piglets. However, two additional longer fragments (~1.2 kb) were amplified in the MYBPC3 mutant piglet. Sequencing PCR products revealed that the stillborn piglet originated from a compound heterozygous mutant clone in exon 23. The 812-bp fragment revealed a 1-bp deletion in exon 23, leading to a PTC in exon 24 (allele 1; Fig. 2B). The predicted protein contains 832 amino acids, including 30 new amino acids. However, based on similar mutation, allele 1 is expected to result in unstable nonsense mRNA and therefore absence of MYBPC3 protein. Sequencing of the two longer bands indicated that one fragment was unspecific, while the other showed a 361-bp duplication inserted into exon 23 at the sgRNA recognition site. This duplication included the last 10 bp of exon 22, the full intron 22 (289 bp) and the first 62 bp of exon 23 (allele 2; Fig. 2B). Allele 2 was expected to result in a stable mutant mRNA containing 72 additional nucleotides. The resulting protein would be larger than WT MYBPC3 protein, and includes 24 duplicated amino acids, corresponding to positions 779 to 802 of the WT MYBPC3 protein. The heart weight of the MYBPC3-mutated piglet was 13 g (vs. 3.26 ± 0.67 g in control newborn female minipigs; [33]), and the heart weight-to-body weight ratio was 13 mg/g, i.e. 47 % higher than the calculated mean value of 8.8 mg/g in newborn female Göttingen minipigs [33], indicating significant cardiac hypertrophy. Total MYBPC3 mRNA level was lower than in WT control myocardial tissues, particularly in the right ventricle and septum of the mutant heart (Fig. 2C). This suggests that the gene-edited allele 2 resulted in a stable mRNA, whereas the gene-edited allele 1 resulted in a nonsense mRNA, which is likely at least partially degraded by the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay [37]. Genes such as natriuretic peptide A and B (NPPA and NPPB), skeletal muscle α-actin (ACTA1) and MYH7 are highly expressed in the fetal stage, downregulated after birth and reactivated upon cardiac stress in rodents [38,39]. RT-qPCR was performed on RNA from mutant and WT piglet myocardial tissues to assess the expression levels of these genes. NPPA and NPPB mRNA levels were 10-fold and 300-fold higher in mutant ventricular and septal tissues compared to WT myocardial tissues (Fig. 2C), supporting heart abnormality in the MYBPC3-mutated piglet. ACTA1 and MYH7 mRNA levels did not differ between mutant and WT pig myocardial tissues (data not shown).

Protein levels were determined by Western blot with specific antibodies directed against MYBPC3 and calsequestrin-2 (CASQ2) on myocardial protein extracts. The level of CASQ2 did not markedly differ between the mutant and WT myocardial tissues and was thus used as a loading control. In both WT and mutant myocardial protein extracts, 2 bands were detected for WT MYBPC3, of which the lower band (~120 kDa) was not specific (Fig. 2D). In the mutant piglet, no protein corresponding to allele 1 (calculated molecular weight of 91.5 kDa) was detected, again indicating degradation of the mRNA. The larger mutant MYBPC3 protein encoded by allele 2 was detected, and its level was only 6 % (left ventricle), 8 % (right ventricle) and 3 % (septum) of the WT MYBPC3 protein (Fig. 2D). This finding was reproduced with another anti-MYBPC3 antibody from Abcam (data not shown).

In the second part of this study, we aimed at developing a German Landrace pig model of HCM bearing a heterozygous genetic variant in exon 12 of MYH7 (c.1357C>T; p.Arg453Cys). We took advantage of the cytosine base editor (CBE), in which a cytidine deaminase was fused to a catalytically dead Cas9 to induce the C>T transition [40]. This study aimed at generating novel pig models for severe, neonatal and adult HCM with CRISPR/Cas9 genetic tools.

We first induced a double-strand cleavage in exon 6 or 23 of MYBPC3 with CRISPR/Cas9 in primary kidney fibroblasts and validated the clones by PCR-sequencing. Positive clones were subjected to SCNT and embryo transfer. Two late pregnancies were established; one went to term but gave rise to only mummified fetuses and 1 stillborn piglet. This gene-edited minipig exhibited a compound heterozygous MYBPC3 mutation in exon 23, significant increase in HW/BW ratio and lower levels of total mutant mRNAs. As seen previously in septal myectomies of patients with HCM [42] and in HCM mice [37] the truncating mutation resulted in nonsense mRNA degraded by the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay and the absence of truncated proteins in the porcine heart. In contrast, the larger MYBPC3 mutant protein including amino acid duplications was present but 3D protein structure prediction indicated altered folding likely to interfere with its interactions with titin and myosin (for a review, see [36]). The very low level of this mutant protein, despite the presence of mRNA, suggests protein instability, which can be due to increased ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation as shown previously in HCM mice [37]. The stillborn piglet also exhibited a marked elevation of NPPA and NPPB mRNA levels in the myocardium, indicating signs of heart failure. Interestingly, ACTA1 and MYH7 mRNA levels did not change in the diseased piglet myocardium, presumably because these isoforms are normally abundant in pig heart [43] such as in human heart [44,45]. It was unclear whether the premature death of this MYBPC3-edited minipig was due to i) the primary cells used, ii) the introduced MYBPC3 mutations, or iii) a combination of SCNT, mutational stress and suboptimal cells. However, the same cells were used for a separate nuclear transfer experiment and gave rise to healthy piglets, indicating that it was not the cell quality per se. On the other hand, other mutant pigs bearing heterozygous MYBPC3 variant have been created by conventional knock-in or CBE in porcine fibroblasts and SCNT and showed a severe phenotype or death [18,46]. The heterozygous MYBPC3 p.Glu330* mutant minipig developed end-stage HCM, with ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction [18] and the heterozygous MYBPC3 p.Arg495Gln variant did not result in viable pigs [46], indicating that a heterozygous MYBPC3 variant has more severe consequences in pigs than in patients with HCM. Importantly, our data also support previous findings in both human infants carrying bi-allelic MYBPC3 genetic variants that led to heart failure and death within 1 year (for reviews, see [4,36]) and in the Maine Coon cats with homozygous c.91G>C (p.Arg31Pro) MYBPC3 genetic variant that exhibited a severe phenotype with prenatal deaths [47,48].

In the second part of this study, we aimed at generating a viable pig model of adult HCM with the goal to better elucidate the molecular mechanisms and test therapies, since our previously TALEN-mediated MYH7-edited pig model was not viable [21]. We chose the human heterozygous c.1357C>T (p.Arg453Cys) genetic variant in MYH7, which is associated with a severe disease phenotype in humans, often associated with sudden cardiac death [[22], [23], [24]]. In this case, we used the CBE approach with a cytidine deaminase fused to a catalytically dead Cas9 [40] to introduce the genetic variant in porcine fibroblasts. All animals were stillborn, two piglets born via cesarian section died within the first 4 h after birth. Comparable to what we found in the compound heterozygous MYBPC3-edited piglet MYH7-edited developed significant ventricular hypertrophy. We furthermore detected signs for aspiration of amnionic fluid in the stillborn animals, suggesting fetal hypoxia, and minor alveolar edema. These findings hint to heart failure as possible cause of death. Our previously developed MYH7-edited pigs bearing the heterozygous c.2223C>G (p.Arg723Gly) genetic variant also died perinatally or within 24 h post-partum [21]. Even though the c.2223C>G MYH7 variant was shown to cause a severe form of HCM with early disease onset and high incidence of heart failure and sudden cardiac death in humans [49], perinatal death was not reported. There was no evidence that genome editing, or the cloning protocol itself may have caused the observed mortality of the Landrace piglet. These data contrast in part with the longer viability of a Yucatan minipig model of HCM bearing the heterozygous p.Arg403Gly MYH7 genetic variant that was presented in scientific meetings [17,20]. The authors also used CRISPR/Cas9-edited knock-in and SCNT in porcine fetal fibroblasts. The minipigs presented initially with hypercontractility and diastolic dysfunction, later progressing to normal or reduced contractility and increased risk of sudden death [20]. Even though these pigs were viable, they showed a much faster progression to sudden cardiac death as compared to humans with the same mutation.

The high sensitivity of the pigs to sarcomeric gene mutations contrasts to what is observed in mouse models of HCM. There are fundamental developmental differences in the postnatal period between pigs and mice, including myosin isoform switch, cardiomyocyte nucleation, hypertrophic growth and mitotic arrest [50]. First, the expression of cardiac genes differs between mice and pigs. In mice, Myh7 is the predominant fetal myosin isoform, whereas Myh6 (α-myosin heavy chain) expression decreases during embryonic development and starts to increase gradually after 16 days p.c. to be very abundant after birth [51]. In pigs such as in humans, the expression of the mutation-bearing MYH7 already starts in the first half of pregnancy [50], and thereby functional and structural alterations most likely already start in the fetus. Second, the cardiomyocyte cycling differs between mice and pigs [50]. In mice, cardiomyocytes undergo mitotic arrest and decline in mononucleated-diploid cell population at postnatal day 7 [52]. On the other hand, the cardiac regenerative potential in pigs is limited to postnatal day 3, and the lack of concentric growth until postnatal day 30 renders pig myocyte particularly sensitive to any major decrease in sarcomere function [50]. Furthermore, it has been shown that Mybpc3-deficient mice undergo an additional round of cell division within 10 days post birth compared to their WT littermates, leading to an increased number of mononuclear myocytes [53]. This finding suggests that MYBPC3 regulates postnatal myocyte cytokinesis. If MYBPC3 plays a role, the low level of MYBPC3 in MYBPC3-edited piglet could trigger an additional round of cardiomyocyte division, which could lead to increased HW/BW ratio due to hyperplasia. Although speculative, this hypothesis merits more in-depth analysis. Finally, due to its epitheliochorial structure, the placenta of pigs shows a more limited oxygen transport. In the mutated pigs, this limited oxygen transfer from maternal to fetal blood may become critical due to the lower cardiac capacity [54]. This assumption is supported by signs of fetal hypoxia in the stillborn MYH7-edited piglets, namely aspired amniotic fluid in the lungs, which could be explained by early cardiac dysfunction. While early alterations may still be tolerated in the haemochorial placenta of humans, forms of HCM with severe consequences may prove perinatally lethal in pigs.

In summary, we show that gene editing of two unrelated HCM mutations, a compound heterozygous MYBPC3 and a heterozygous MYH7 missense mutation led to non-viable piglets. The pre- and perinatal mortality appeared independent of the disease gene or variant type, the respective onset of the disease in humans – early (compound heterozygous MYBPC3) vs late onset (heterozygous MYH7), the pig genetic background – Landrace pigs or Black minipigs, and also the genome editing method – CRISPR/Cas9-mediated cleavage or CBE or TALEN-based gene editing. Finally, we believe that the technology is not a problem per se since several other viable gene-edited pig models have been created by us and others [[55], [56], [57], [58]].

We acknowledge some limitations in our collaborative study. First, the bi-allelic MYBPC3 mutation does not reproduce exactly the genetic situation in human infants; however, the consequence on protein level is similar, showing a very low level of mutant protein. Second, we could not analyze some parameters because of the premature death of the mutant piglets; specifically, the cardiac function by echocardiography was not analysed in both models and the quantification of the fetal gene program in the MYH7-edited piglet was not performed because we did not RNA fresh enough for quantification of gene expression.

Our data indicate that HCM genetic variants induce a severe phenotype in pigs. This would be the opposite of mice which are notorious insensitive to HCM variants. Thus, despite its general value as a large animal model for cardiac research, the pig might be not best suited as a genetic model of HCM.
