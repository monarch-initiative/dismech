Mutations in the microRNA processing genes DROSHA and DICER1 drive several
cancers that resemble embryonic progenitors. To understand how microRNAs
regulate tumorigenesis, we ablated Drosha or Dicer1 in the developing pineal
gland to emulate the pathogenesis of pineoblastoma, a brain tumor that resembles
undifferentiated precursors of the pineal gland. Accordingly, these mice develop
pineal tumors marked by loss of microRNAs, particularly the let-7/miR-98-5p
family, and derepression of microRNA target genes. Pineal tumors driven by loss
of Drosha or Dicer1 mimic tumors driven by Rb1 loss, as they exhibit
upregulation of S-phase genes and homeobox transcription factors that regulate
pineal development. Blocking proliferation of these tumors facilitates
expression of pinealocyte maturation markers, with a concomitant reduction in
embryonic markers. Select embryonic markers remain elevated, however, as the
microRNAs that normally repress these target genes remain absent. One such
microRNA target gene is the oncofetal transcription factor Plagl2, which
regulates expression of progrowth genes, and inhibiting their signaling impairs
tumor growth. Thus, we demonstrate that tumors driven by loss of microRNA
processing may be therapeutically targeted by inhibiting downstream drivers of
proliferation.

Mutations in microRNA processing genes, such as DROSHA, DICER1, and DGCR8, drive ∼2% of childhood cancers (Gröbner et al. 2018; Ma et al. 2018). These mutations arise in a family of cancers that resemble embryonic progenitors, including Wilms tumor, pleuropulmonary blastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and the brain tumor pineoblastoma (Hill et al. 2009; Rakheja et al. 2014; Torrezan et al. 2014; Brenneman et al. 2015; Walz et al. 2015; Wegert et al. 2015; Gadd et al. 2017; Snuderl et al. 2018; de Kock et al. 2019). It is thought that loss of microRNA production leads to derepression of the genes that are normally repressed by these microRNAs, leading to tumor formation (Bartel 2004; Flynt and Lai 2008). However, microRNA loss leads to transcriptome-wide changes with different effects in each cell type, and it is challenging to separate genes upregulated through loss of direct microRNA targeting from secondary effects. In addition, little is known about the specific microRNAs or microRNA target genes that regulate the balance between proliferation and differentiation in each cancer type. As a result, there is currently no way to therapeutically target these mutations.

Pineoblastoma is a rare brain tumor that resembles embryonic neurogenic progenitors of the pineal gland, a neuroendocrine organ in the brain that regulates circadian rhythms by producing melatonin (Maronde and Stehle 2007; Rath et al. 2013; Ibañez Rodriguez et al. 2016; Voiculescu et al. 2020). Pineoblastoma is treated with chemotherapy, surgery, and radiation (Hansford et al. 2022). However, the morbidities associated with each of these treatment modalities limits their utility; cranial radiation therapy, for instance, is generally avoided in young children. Most other DICER1/DROSHA-related tumors exhibit point mutations in these genes that incompletely impair microRNA production, but pineoblastoma can uniquely exhibit complete loss of DROSHA or DICER1, leading to complete loss of microRNA production (Snuderl et al. 2018; de Kock et al. 2020). Specifically, recent molecular studies have identified three mutational patterns in pineoblastoma: (1) biallelic loss of DROSHA, DICER1, or DGCR8; (2) biallelic loss of RB1; or (3) activation of MYC or FOXR2 (Li et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2020, 2021; Pfaff et al. 2020). The RB1 tumor suppressor normally regulates E2F transcription factors to allow progression through the G1/S cell cycle checkpoint (Goel et al. 2022), and MYC orchestrates tumorigenesis by binding “E-box” motifs in promoters or enhancers of tumor-promoting genes (Dang 2012). However, the microRNAs and microRNA target genes that mediate tumorigenesis in tumors driven by loss of DROSHA or DICER1 remain poorly understood.

Previous studies have used Rb1 loss, ectopic cyclin D1 expression, and Dicer1 loss to model the development of pineoblastoma in mice (Vooijs et al. 2002; Saab et al. 2006; Saab et al. 2009; Harajly et al. 2016; Chung et al. 2020; Idriss et al. 2023). To dissect how microRNA loss drives formation of pineoblastoma, we generated mice with loss of microRNAs in the developing pineal gland. Here we show that loss of Drosha or Dicer1 mimics loss of the Rb1 tumor suppressor by driving cell cycle progression through derepression of cyclin D2 (Ccnd2). Uncontrolled proliferation itself restrains tumor cells in an undifferentiated state, and restraining proliferation leads them to upregulate differentiation markers. In addition to being directly targeted by microRNAs, we found that Ccnd2 is also indirectly regulated by microRNAs through the transcription factor Plagl2, and blocking other Plagl2 target genes also slows tumor growth. Last, we show that human pineoblastoma tumors exhibit parallel expression patterns. Together, our results demonstrate the potential for therapeutic targeting of the downstream effects of microRNA loss in DROSHA or DICER1 mutant cancers.

Prior studies had demonstrated that mice develop pineoblastoma upon pineal-directed ablation of Rb1 and p53 (IrbpCre;p53flox/flox;Rb1flox/flox, referred to here as IPRb1) (Vooijs et al. 2002). These studies used a Cre recombinase line driven by the promoter of interphotoreceptor retinoid binding protein (Irbp; also known as Rbp3), which is expressed in retinal and pineal progenitors (Rodrigues et al. 1986; Marszalek et al. 2000). To understand how microRNA loss regulates pineal tumor formation, we developed mice with analogous loss of Drosha and p53 (IrbpCre;p53flox/flox;Droshaflox/flox; abbreviated as IPDrosha) or Dicer1 and p53 (IrbpCre;p53flox/flox;Dicer1flox/flox; abbreviated as IPDicer1) (Fig. 1A,B). These mice were healthy at birth, but at 5–10 months of age they began to exhibit signs of brain tumor formation. Within days of developing an enlarged skull, they become moribund.

By microscopic evaluation, IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors resemble human pineoblastoma. They contain large, pleomorphic, primitive cells with a high nucleus to cytoplasm ratio and rare inconspicuous nucleoli that resemble embryonic pineal progenitors (Fig. 1C). These cells are arranged as sheets in a vaguely nodular pattern with nuclear molding, frequent mitosis, and areas of necrosis. To confirm that these tumors resemble human pineoblastoma, we performed immunostains commonly used for clinical diagnosis (Verma et al. 2019). Consistent with pineoblastoma, these tumor cells stain positive for markers of proliferation and neuronal lineage (Ki-67 and synaptophysin, respectively) and negative for a glial lineage marker (glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP]) (Supplemental Fig. S1A). Furthermore, just as DROSHA/DICER1-related pineoblastomas arise later in childhood than RB1-driven pineoblastoma, IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors appeared to arise at later ages than IPRb1 tumors (median age of onset 269, 239, and 182 days, respectively), though we observed tumors as early as ∼5 months of age in all three groups. We did not observe any histological abnormalities in the retina, where IrbpCre is also active (data not shown).

To understand how these tumors arise, we next profiled their gene expression patterns by whole-transcriptome sequencing. Unsupervised clustering of their transcriptomes shows that IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors cluster together, which is consistent with the expectation that loss of Drosha or Dicer1 will have similar effects on the transcriptome, driven by loss of microRNAs (Fig. 1D). These tumors exhibit some similarities to IPRb1 tumors, but as a group they cluster apart from IPRb1 tumors, normal adult brains, or normal adult pineal glands. We also examined whether these models resemble a previously described model of pineoblastoma driven by WAP-Cre-mediated loss of Dicer1 or Rb1 (Chung et al. 2020). The relative expression patterns in Dicer1-driven versus Rb1-driven WAP-Cre tumors largely correlated with the relative expression patterns seen in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 versus IPRb1 tumors (Supplemental Fig. S1B).

The pineal homeobox transcription factors Otx2, Crx, and Pax6 are expressed at high levels in IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors, reflecting their shared pineal identity (Fig. 1E; Supplemental Fig. S2A; Santagata et al. 2009; Voiculescu et al. 2020). We also examined histone modification patterns in these tumors, specifically acetylation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27ac), which marks active promoters and enhancers, and trimethylation of histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4me3), which marks active promoters. Accordingly, the genomic regions surrounding Otx2, Crx, and Pax6 are marked by broad H3K27ac signal, sometimes termed “superenhancers” (Supplemental Fig. S2B; Whyte et al. 2013). Broad H3K27ac signal at these homeobox transcription factors indicates the importance of these genes in maintaining embryonic identity in these pineal tumors.

Next, we examined which stage of pineal gland development these tumors resemble. Pineal progenitors arise from neural progenitor cells early in embryogenesis (Maronde and Stehle 2007; Rath et al. 2013; Ibañez Rodriguez et al. 2016; Voiculescu et al. 2020). Although they are specified prenatally, they remain undeveloped until light exposure after birth and mature as day–night cycles are established at ∼2 months postnatally in mice. To generate gene expression patterns for these stages of development, we extrapolated marker genes from published RNA-seq from rat pineal glands (Supplemental Fig. S3A). Specifically, we identified 200 marker genes at each stage of rat pineal gland development: embryonic (E21 in rats, before light exposure), postnatal (P5, before day–night cycles were established), and adult (P20 and P40, after day–night cycles were established). Using these 200 gene signatures, we found that IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors retain the gene expression patterns of embryonic pineal progenitors, with enrichment for embryonic pineal markers and depletion for adult markers (Fig. 1F–H; Supplemental Fig. S3B–G).

Next, we examined whether this expression signature was unique to our tumors or whether other murine cancer models might also exhibit a similar signature. Specifically, because many of the embryonic pineal markers correlated with proliferation, we examined whether enrichment for proliferation-independent embryonic markers was specific to our tumors. To do this, we identified publicly available RNA-seq data sets of mouse models of cancer with paired noncancer control tissue. Specifically, we examined data from embryonic neuronal brain tumors (SHH-driven medulloblastoma and group 3 [MYC/SMARCA4-driven] medulloblastoma), glial brain tumors (MYCN-driven high-grade glioma, glioblastoma allografts, and histone H3.3 K27M diffuse midline glioma), and embryonic neuroectodermal extracranial tumors (TH-MYCN neuroblastoma). Combined with our comparisons of IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors and IPRb1 tumors with normal adult pineal glands, this constituted a total of eight comparisons between a murine cancer model and paired noncancer control tissue.

Having identified other murine tumor models, we next identified proliferation-independent embryonic signatures. Specifically, we generated four composite gene sets: proliferation markers, MYC targets, proliferation-independent embryonic pineal markers, and proliferation-independent adult pineal markers. To define a proliferation signature, we combined four proliferation-related Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) hallmark gene sets (Liberzon et al. 2015). For all eight tumor–normal comparisons, this proliferation signature was significantly enriched in tumors compared with normal tissues (Supplemental Fig. S4A). Similarly, we combined two hallmark gene sets of MYC target genes to derive a MYC gene signature. As expected, the three MYC/MYCN-driven tumor types were most enriched for Myc targets (group 3 medulloblastoma, MYCN glioma, and TH-MYCN neuroblastoma) but were enriched to a lesser extent in all eight tumor–normal comparisons (Supplemental Fig. S4B). We then removed proliferation markers from our developmental pineal marker sets to identify proliferation-independent embryonic and adult pineal markers. IPDrosha/IPDicer1 and IPRb1 tumors were the only tumors to show a significant enrichment for proliferation-independent embryonic pineal markers (Supplemental Fig. S4C). Similarly, IPDrosha/IPDicer1 and IPRb1 tumors showed the most significant depletion of proliferation-independent adult pineal markers (Supplemental Fig. S4D). Together, these results indicate that IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors all resemble a similar proliferative pineal gland progenitor.

To understand how IPRb1 tumors form with intact microRNA processing and, conversely, how IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors form with intact Rb1 function, we next investigated differences between IPRb1 and IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors. We first performed small RNA sequencing in IPDrosha tumors, IPRb1 tumors, matched normal brains, and age-matched normal adult pineal glands to identify the microRNAs regulating normal pineal gland development. As expected, IPDrosha tumors are devoid of canonical microRNAs (Fig. 2A,B; Supplemental Fig. S5A; Supplemental Data S1). Some microRNAs can be produced without Drosha through a noncanonical pathway if they are processed by the splicing machinery (called “mirtrons”) or are transcribed as a capped microRNA (Wen et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2016). These “Drosha-independent” microRNAs remain expressed in IPDrosha tumors at levels similar to matched normal brains, age-matched adult pineal glands, or IPRb1 tumors (Supplemental Fig. S5B–D). Similarly, we were also able to observe the effects of Drosha loss as we compared RNA-seq of IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors. Specifically, because Drosha cleaves pri-microRNA transcripts, we observed upregulation of these transcripts in IPDrosha but not IPDicer1 tumors (Supplemental Fig. S5E). Similarly, consistent with the importance of Drosha cleaving the Dgcr8 transcript (Han et al. 2009), we observed upregulation of Dgcr8 in IPDrosha tumors only (Supplemental Fig. S5E). Together, these findings are consistent with Drosha loss.

Using small RNA sequencing, we identified the most abundant microRNAs in age-matched adult pineal glands and in IPRb1 tumors, which resemble embryonic pineal progenitors. Members of the let-7/miR-98-5p, miR-99/100-5p, and miR-183-5p families are among the most abundant microRNA families in both IPRb1 tumors and adult pineal glands (Fig. 2A,B; Supplemental Fig. S5F,G). Like IPDrosha tumors, IPDicer1 tumors expressed undetectable levels of these microRNAs (Fig. 2C). We also noted that microRNAs of the let-7/miR-98-5p and miR-99/100-5p families are lower in IPRb1 tumors than in normal brains or pineal glands. Although Rb1 itself may regulate expression of let-7/miR-98-5p family members (Mu et al. 2010), this could also be a reflection of the embryonic state of IPRb1 tumors, as let-7 was first discovered as a marker of differentiation (Roush and Slack 2008). The let-7/miR-98-5p and miR-99/100-5p families are cotranscribed from the same genomic loci in an evolutionarily conserved manner (Roush and Slack 2008). They are not expressed in neural progenitors but are induced late in neural or retinal development to downregulate progenitor genes (Wu et al. 2012; La Torre et al. 2013; Chawla et al. 2016). We detected nearly all let-7/miR-98-5p family members in IPRb1 tumors and adult pineal glands (Supplemental Fig. S5H,I). The depletion of mature let-7/miR-98-5p microRNAs was seen in IPDrosha tumors despite broad H3K27ac and H3K4me3 at the host genes of let-7/miR-98-5p transcripts (Supplemental Fig. S5J). In contrast, although let-7/miR-98-5p and miR-99/100-5p microRNAs are abundant across many developed tissues, miR-183-5p is specifically abundant in the adult retina and pineal gland, where it regulates genes involved in the circadian rhythm (Clokie et al. 2012; Lumayag et al. 2013; Ben-Moshe et al. 2014). Germline mutations in the region encoding this microRNA produce retinal degeneration in humans (Lumayag et al. 2013). As expected, we only detected miR-183-5p in normal pineal and IPRb1 tumors, which resemble pineal progenitors (Fig. 2C). The high levels of miR-183-5p in IPRb1 tumors likely simply reflect their differentiation status. However, miR-183-5p has also been implicated as oncogenic in some contexts (Cao et al. 2020), and its expression may contribute to IPRb1 tumorigenesis.

To understand which microRNAs have the greatest effect on gene expression in IPDrosha tumors, we next examined the expression of genes predicted to be targeted by the 50 microRNAs expressed in IPRb1 tumors but not in IPDrosha tumors. Based on TargetScan calculations of sequence context and conservation, we found 781 genes predicted to be targeted by these 50 microRNAs (Agarwal et al. 2015). These predicted microRNA targets are expressed at higher levels in IPDrosha tumors than in IPRb1 tumors, consistent with a loss of microRNA repression (Fig. 2D).

However, observing the loss of a microRNA and upregulation of a predicted target transcript cannot fully distinguish between the direct and indirect effects of microRNA targeting. Some genes may be upregulated as a secondary effect of another derepressed microRNA target gene. Thus, we next used microRNA enhanced cross-linking immunoprecipitation (miR-eCLIP) sequencing to confirm which genes are directly targeted by microRNAs in IPRb1 tumors (Manakov et al. 2022). This technique directly identifies microRNA–mRNA interactions through immunoprecipitation of Argonaute 2 (AGO2) and ligation of microRNA–mRNA “chimeras.” We performed miR-eCLIP sequencing in two independent IPRb1 tumors and examined the nonchimeric peaks and chimeric peaks that were reproduced between the two samples (Supplemental Fig. S6A; Supplemental Data S2). As expected, reproduced reads clustered primarily in the 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of coding genes, confirming enrichment for regions captured by microRNA targeting (Supplemental Fig. S6B; Bartel 2004). In nonchimeric transcripts immunoprecipitated with AGO2, two of the three most significantly enriched motifs were let‐7/miR-98-5p recognition sequences (Supplemental Fig. S6C). Furthermore, let-7/miR-98-5p family members were commonly identified in the microRNA side of chimeric reads (Supplemental Fig. S6D). Altogether, the results from our integrated sequencing analyses highlight let-7/miR-98-5p microRNAs as key drivers of the gene expression differences between IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors and IPRb1 tumors.

Next, we examined whether the genes identified as direct microRNA targets in miR‐eCLIP sequencing of IPRb1 tumors are repressed compared with IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors. Genes represented in microRNA–mRNA chimeric reads were significantly upregulated in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors and were more likely to be upregulated than downregulated (Supplemental Fig. S6E,F). These confirmed microRNA target genes were even more upregulated in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors than the microRNA targets predicted by TargetScan (Fig. 2D,F; Supplemental Fig. S7A,B). Similarly, the confirmed top targets of the let-7/miR-98-5p family were more enriched than the set of predicted let-7/miR-98-5p targets (Fig. 2E,G; Supplemental Fig. S7C,D). In other words, much of the transcriptomic signature in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors can be directly attributed to loss of let-7/miR-98-5p.

Together, these results demonstrate that derepression of direct microRNA targets explains many of the differences between IPDrosha and IPRb1 tumors. Nevertheless, genes represented in microRNA–mRNA chimeras only constituted 23.1% of all genes overexpressed in IPDrosha tumors (Supplemental Fig. S6F). In other words, although microRNAs directly drive many of the transcriptomic differences between IPDrosha and IPRb1 tumors, up to 77% of the genes overexpressed in IPDrosha tumors may be attributable to indirect effects.

To understand how derepression of microRNA target genes causes cancer, we next examined other gene sets upregulated by loss of microRNAs, whether directly or indirectly. Specifically, we identified “hallmark,” Wikipathway, and chromosomal location gene sets differentially enriched in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors, IPRb1 tumors, and normal adult pineal glands (Supplemental Data S3–S5). When compared with normal adult pineal glands, we noted that the four most enriched hallmark gene sets overlapped between IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors, demonstrating their overlapping paths to tumor formation (Fig. 3A–C; Supplemental Data S3). These similarities in overall transcriptomes, despite independent molecular mechanisms, suggest that Drosha/Dicer1 loss and Rb1 loss converge to drive cancer formation through overlapping downstream effects.

As expected, because Rb1 normally regulates E2F transcription factors, the most enriched “hallmark” gene set in IPRb1 tumors is “E2F target genes” (Fig. 3C; Supplemental Fig. S8C). However, E2F target genes were also the most enriched gene set in IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors (Fig. 3A,B; Supplemental Fig. S8A,B). In cells with intact Rb1, D-type cyclins (cyclin D1, D2, or D3) partner with cyclin-dependent kinases 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) to phosphorylate Rb1 and release it from inhibiting E2Fs (Fassl et al. 2022; Goel et al. 2022). It had previously been shown that cyclin D1 (Ccnd1) overexpression was sufficient to replace Rb1 loss and drive pineal tumor formation (Saab et al. 2009; Zalzali et al. 2012). Thus, we examined the expression of D-type cyclins in IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors and found that cyclin D2 and D3 (Ccnd2 and Ccnd3) were significantly overexpressed compared with IPRb1 tumors, normal brains, and normal pineal glands (Fig. 3D; Supplemental Fig. S8D,E). Among these, Ccnd2 was also the most abundant transcript and is a known target of several microRNAs; for example, it is thought to cause proliferation of leukemias driven by loss of miR-16 (Kawano et al. 2015; Lovat et al. 2015). We confirmed that Ccnd2 is regulated by several microRNAs in our miR-eCLIP sequencing, including the miR-15/16-5p family (Fig. 3E). It was also overexpressed in WAP-Cre Dicer1-driven tumors (Supplemental Fig. S1B). Using immunohistochemistry, we also confirmed that IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors exhibit high levels of CDK4/6 activity, as nearly every cell stained positive for phosphorylated Rb1 (Fig. 3F). (As expected, the tumor cells in IPRb1 tumors lacked Rb1, though some entrapped nontumor cells stained positive.) In other words, Drosha loss and Rb1 loss converge on the same proliferation-driving pathway to drive cancer cells through the G1/S cell cycle checkpoint.

Another common downstream effect in IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors was enrichment for Myc target genes (Fig. 3A–C). MYC drives many cancers by activating transcription of proliferation drivers (Cappellen et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2008; Dang 2012), and gain of MYC or FOXR2 is known to drive a third subset of human pineoblastoma (Liu et al. 2021). In our RNA-seq, Myc is expressed at much higher levels in IPRb1 tumors than in IPDrosha or IPDicer1 tumors, suggesting that Myc upregulation could be a downstream effect of Rb1 loss. However, in all three tumor types, Mycn is the most abundant Myc family member (Supplemental Fig. S9A). It was overexpressed in IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors compared with normal brains or pineal glands. Furthermore, Mycn was one of the most abundant mRNA transcripts in miR-eCLIP chimeras and was targeted by a large number of microRNAs (Supplemental Fig. S9B). Although we did not observe an increase in Mycn transcript levels in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors compared with IPRb1 tumors, microRNAs could still regulate Mycn translation post-transcriptionally. In other words, the absence of these microRNAs could be connected to the enrichment for MYC target genes that we observed in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors.

In the developing brain, proliferation maintains progenitors in an undifferentiated state, and differentiation is allowed to occur once proliferation slows down (Liu et al. 2019). Accordingly, IPRb1 tumors may remain undifferentiated solely because of unrestrained proliferation of these embryonic progenitors. To understand whether IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors with high levels of Ccnd2 are dependent on CDK4/6 activity to remain undifferentiated, we treated them with the clinically approved CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib (Fry et al. 2004). Specifically, we implanted tumor cells as subcutaneous allografts and administered either vehicle or palbociclib to recipient mice. Over 2 weeks, palbociclib suppressed proliferation in IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors (Fig. 4A,B). Palbociclib-treated tumors exhibited less staining for phosphorylated Rb1 and Ki-67, consistent with a loss of CDK4/6 activity (Fig. 4C,D). Histologically, the palbociclib-treated IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors exhibit increased karyorrhexis, a sign of cell death, and increased background neuropil, a sign of neuronal differentiation (Fig. 4C). Palbociclib also extended survival of these mice, though three of the 16 mice treated with palbociclib suffered weight loss and other toxicities that led to their deaths after >2 weeks of therapy (Supplemental Fig. S10A,B).

Next, we used RNA-seq to understand how palbociclib affected IPDrosha tumor cells. Consistent with inhibition of CDK4/6, E2F target genes were the most downregulated hallmark gene set in palbociclib-treated tumors (Fig. 4E; Supplemental Fig. S10C). In contrast, interferon α and γ response pathways were enriched in palbociclib-treated tumors, consistent with reports that CDK4/6 inhibitors can induce antitumor immunity (Supplemental Fig. S10D; Goel et al. 2017; Deng et al. 2018; Schaer et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2018). When we examined pineal differentiation markers, we found that palbociclib-treated tumors were enriched for adult pineal markers such as Chrna3 and Nefh (Fig. 4F,G). On the other hand, embryonic markers were mildly depleted as a whole (Fig. 4F). Many embryonic genes remain highly expressed upon palbociclib treatment, such as the embryonic transcription factor Plagl2 (Fig. 4G). In other words, blocking the effect of Ccnd2 partially restores differentiation in IPDrosha tumors, but microRNAs remain absent, allowing some embryonic pineal markers to remain highly expressed.

Because CDK4/6 inhibition could induce expression of adult genes but could not suppress the embryonic gene signature, we next identified the proliferation-independent microRNA target genes that contribute to maintaining an embryonic expression program. Specifically, we identified embryonic pineal markers that were overexpressed in IPDrosha tumors, confirmed “top” let-7/miR-98-5p target genes by miR-eCLIP and independent of palbociclib. Three genes met these criteria: Bach1, Tgfbr1, and Plagl2. We focused on Plagl2 (PLAG1-like gene 2) because of previous evidence that it is an oncogenic, developmental transcription factor and because its most well-characterized target gene, Igf2, was the most overexpressed protein-coding gene in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors compared with IPRb1 tumors (Fig. 5A; Supplemental Fig. S11A,B; Alam et al. 2005; Shenoy et al. 2015; Strubberg et al. 2018). Notably, Plagl2 and Igf2 were also overexpressed in WAP-Cre Dicer1-driven tumors to a similar degree (Supplemental Fig. S1B). Our miR-eCLIP sequencing demonstrated that the 3′ UTR of Plagl2 interacted with several microRNAs (Fig. 5B). In fact, let-7/miR-98-5p microRNAs interacted with Plagl2 at three sites, including one highly conserved site with a 7-mer perfect match (Fig. 5B).

Histone marks at the Plagl2 locus were similar in IPDrosha and IPRb1 tumors, consistent with a gene whose expression is primarily regulated by microRNAs post-transcriptionally (Supplemental Fig. S11C). At the Ccnd2 locus, however, IPDrosha tumors had much higher levels of H3K27ac and H3K4me3, suggesting that Ccnd2 upregulation is due to a combination of transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation (Fig. 5C). Because the Ccnd2 promoter contains several predicted Plagl2 binding sites, we examined whether Plagl2 resides at the Ccnd2 promoter in IPDrosha tumors (Fig. 5C). Using ChIP-qPCR, we verified Plagl2 binding at a region of the Ccnd2 promoter near Plagl2 binding motifs (Fig. 5D). Last, we noted that levels of Plagl2, Igf2, and Ccnd2 are high in embryonic pineal glands and fall in parallel during pineal development, suggesting coordinated regulation (Supplemental Fig. S11D).

To verify that Plagl2 binding can activate this region of the Ccnd2 promoter, we next cloned two fragments of the Ccnd2 promoter into a luciferase reporter plasmid (Fig. 5C, regions 1 and 2). The first region was a 1.2 kb fragment that extended nearly to the Ccnd2 transcriptional start site, and the second region was a 0.5 kb fragment centered around the cluster of Plagl2 binding sites that we assayed by ChIP-qPCR. We used a minimal promoter as a negative control and a 0.9 kb fragment from the Igf2 promoter as a positive control. We cotransfected these reporters into HEK293 cells with an expression construct encoding either Plagl2 or GFP as a negative control and found that Plagl2 activated both Ccnd2 reporters (Fig. 5E; Supplemental Fig. S11E). Together, these results suggest that Ccnd2 is upregulated by both increased transcription and decreased microRNA repression and that the microRNA target gene Plagl2 contributes to the maintenance of the embryonic expression program, in part by regulating Igf2 and Ccnd2.

Igf2, whose overexpression drives many other childhood cancers, signals through the insulin signaling pathway primarily by binding the insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (Igf1r), leading to activation of mTORC1 and other kinase pathways (Werner and LeRoith 2022). Accordingly, we observed that the “insulin signaling” gene set was significantly enriched in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors compared with IPRb1 tumors (Fig. 6A; Supplemental Fig. S12A). Because Igf1r can signal through the Akt/mTOR or mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, we next used Western blots and immunostaining to examine the relative activities of these signaling pathways in IPDrosha and IPRb1 tumors. Our Western blots showed somewhat higher Akt/mTOR activity (phosphorylation of Akt and S6) in the IPDrosha tumors but similar levels of MAPK activity (Erk1/2 phosphorylation) (Supplemental Fig. S12B). Our immunostains showed patchy S6 phosphorylation in IPDrosha tumors, with positive staining in only a subset of tumor cells (Supplemental Fig. S12C).

We next tested whether IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors are dependent on Igf1r signaling. We chose to target Igf2/Igf1r itself rather than mTORC1 due to the heterogeneity of phospho-S6 staining and because mTORC1 inhibitors have shown a limited ability to kill cancer cells on their own (Meric-Bernstam and Gonzalez-Angulo 2009). Specifically, we tested whether IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors were sensitive to ceritinib, an Igf1r inhibitor that is clinically used in both adults and children and has favorable pharmacokinetics in rodents (Marsilje et al. 2013; Mossé et al. 2013). Ceritinib also has activity against anaplastic lymphoma kinase (Alk), but this gene was essentially undetectable in IPDrosha, IPDicer1, or IPRb1 tumors by RNA-seq (Supplemental Fig. S12D). In both IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors, ceritinib significantly impaired tumor growth (Fig. 6B,C). In contrast, IPRb1 tumors did not respond to ceritinib, which confirms that the expression differences in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors renders them specifically sensitive to ceritinib (Fig. 6D). Ceritinib led to improved survival in IPDicer1 tumors without weight loss or other toxicities (Supplemental Fig. S12E,F).

Vehicle- and ceritinib-treated IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors were histologically similar, though ceritinib-treated tumors appeared to have slightly more background neuropil (Fig. 6E). In immunostains, ceritinib-treated tumors exhibited a decrease in Rb1 phosphorylation and slightly less phosphorylation of S6, as expected for an Igf1r inhibitor (Fig. 6E,F; Supplemental Fig. S12G,H). Similarly, RNA sequencing revealed depletion of the insulin signaling genes in ceritinib-treated IPDrosha tumors, in accordance with inhibition of Igf1r activity (Fig. 6G).

Because we observed that ceritinib downregulated Rb1 phosphorylation, we next tested whether the combination of ceritinib and palbociclib would be additive. To achieve tolerability of the combination in vivo, we reduced the doses of both drugs. At these lower doses, ceritinib alone did not significantly reduce tumor size, but palbociclib still effectively suppressed tumor growth effectively and prolonged survival (Fig. 6H; Supplemental Fig. S13A). Furthermore, the combination of palbociclib and ceritinib significantly suppressed tumor growth even further compared with the palbociclib-treated tumors (Fig. 6H). At these doses, palbociclib-treated mice still exhibited some toxicity. Two of the 14 mice were euthanized after ≥2 weeks of therapy, and palbociclib-treated mice exhibited slightly less weight gain than their peers (Supplemental Fig. S13A,B). Histologically, ceritinib-treated tumors resembled vehicle-treated tumors, whereas palbociclib- and combination-treated tumors exhibited more karyorrhexis and neuropil (Fig. 6I). As in the single-drug experiments, ceritinib, palbociclib, and combination treatment reduced Rb1 phosphorylation (Fig. 6I,J). However, in contrast to our earlier single-drug study, we did not observe a significant decrease in Ki-67 in the palbociclib-treated tumors, perhaps because the time point when we harvested tumors was not ideal for gauging steady-state effects on cell cycle progression at this lower dose of palbociclib (Supplemental Fig. S13C,D). Similarly, we also did not observe a difference in phosphorylation of S6, suggesting that Igf1r inhibition was less effective at this lower dose of ceritinib (Supplemental Fig. S13C,D). Nevertheless, together, these studies demonstrate the importance of Igf1r signaling in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors and the therapeutic potential of signaling pathway inhibitors.

Last, we examined RNA-seq data from human pineoblastoma tumors to assess whether IPDrosha/IPDicer1 pineal tumors resemble the human disease. Although these tumors lack genomic annotation or methylation classification, we noted that DROSHA expression was essentially undetectable in two of nine tumors (Fig. 7A). Other pineoblastoma driver genes (DICER1, DGCR8, RB1, and MYC) were expressed at comparable levels across these nine tumors (Supplemental Fig. S14A). We then compared expression patterns in these two “DROSHA-low” tumors with the other seven “DROSHA-high” tumors. Consistent with impairment in microRNA processing, we found that DROSHA-low tumors are enriched for genes predicted to be targeted by let-7/miR-98-5p and other microRNAs (Supplemental Fig. S14B,C). Furthermore, the top three most enriched “hallmark pathways” in these DROSHA-low tumors overlapped with the most enriched hallmark pathways in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors (Figs. 3A,B, 7B). Specifically, the single most enriched hallmark gene set in DROSHA-low tumors is “E2F targets” (Supplemental Fig. S14D; Supplemental Data S6). Last, although they did not reach statistical significance, the two DROSHA-low tumors expressed slightly higher levels of CCND1, CCND2, PLAG1, and PLAGL2 (Fig. 7A). (IGF2 was not consistently detected in these tumors.) In sum, although this represents a small number of tumors, these data in human tumors are consistent with findings from our mouse model that impaired microRNA processing dysregulates the G1/S checkpoint.

Drosha or Dicer1 loss leads to derepression of microRNA target genes, which has cascading effects on the expression of other genes. In this study, we dissected Drosha/Dicer1-dependent gene networks using a combination of microRNA sequencing, RNA sequencing, miR-eCLIP sequencing, and histone profiling. Our study shows that loss of Drosha or Dicer1 drives pineal tumors by mimicking Rb1 loss. Together, these studies revealed that derepression of direct microRNA targets (such as Plagl2 and Ccnd2) produces secondary effects that drive tumor proliferation (overexpression of Igf2 and E2F target genes). Small-molecule inhibitors of these indirect effects can block tumor growth (Supplemental Fig. S14E). In other words, although mutations in DROSHA and DICER1 cannot be therapeutically targeted currently, the activation of oncogenic pathways driven by microRNA target genes could be blocked with available drugs.

Our results shed light on the cells from which pineoblastoma can arise. IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors express high levels of Pax6, Otx2, and Crx—homeobox transcription factors that cooperate to regulate pinealocyte development. In particular, Pax6 and Otx2 are expressed in rapidly dividing cells at the earliest stages of pineal development (Rath et al. 2013; Ibañez Rodriguez et al. 2016). Otx2 regulates the expression of Crx, a specific marker for retina and the pineal gland that may even be used as a diagnostic marker for retinoblastoma and pineoblastoma (Chen et al. 1997; Nishida et al. 2003; Santagata et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2021). Otx2 and Crx recognize the same binding motif and thus activate the same target genes, including Irbp (Kimura et al. 2000; Boatright et al. 2001; Rath et al. 2013). In fact, OTX2 and CRX are key oncogenic drivers in group 3 medulloblastoma and are likely to play similar roles in pineoblastoma (Adamson et al. 2010; Garancher et al. 2018). Together, these results suggest that pineoblastomas driven by loss of Drosha, Dicer1, or Rb1 arise from an embryonic pineal progenitor marked by expression of Pax6, Otx2, Crx, and Irbp.

Using a different Cre driver initially developed for the mammary gland, Chung et al. (2020) previously showed that Dicer1 ablation also drives development of pineal tumors; to our knowledge, our study is the first report of pineal tumors arising in mice through loss of Drosha (Wagner et al. 1997; Chung et al. 2020). Our findings recapitulate many of their findings, including overlapping expression patterns, incomplete penetrance, and delayed onset compared with Rb1-driven tumors, suggesting that tumors from the two different Cre drivers represent the same cell of origin. Incomplete penetrance is also observed in humans with germline pathogenic variants in DICER1. Only ∼19% of these patients develop any kind of cancer, and pineoblastoma is rare even in this population (Stewart et al. 2019). It is thought that the rate-limiting step in development of DICER1-related cancers in this population is the somatic “second hit” in certain cell types during a vulnerable stage of development (Brenneman et al. 2015). In our model, based on expression of embryonic pineal markers, we surmise that the window of vulnerability is a restricted period of prenatal development, and the number of cells at risk in a developing mouse pineal gland is small. Loss of Rb1 may cause unrestrained proliferation in the next cell division, but loss of Drosha or Dicer1 may require multiple steps that need to occur within a narrow window before they can impact cell proliferation: depletion of residual microRNAs, derepression of microRNA target genes, and deployment of secondary effects. Future studies will be needed to identify whether overexpression of any individual microRNA target genes such as Plagl2 is necessary for pineal tumor development.

Like previous studies of murine pineoblastoma, p53 loss was required for tumor development in our model (Vooijs et al. 2002; Saab et al. 2009; Zalzali et al. 2012; Idriss et al. 2023). These previous studies showed that when pineal cells are forced to proliferate, p53-induced senescence restrained progression into a pineal tumor (Saab et al. 2009; Zalzali et al. 2012). Loss of p53 allowed tumor formation by circumventing this senescence. However, although TP53 is commonly mutated in other DICER1-driven human cancers (Pugh et al. 2014; Kommoss et al. 2023), the role of such mechanisms is unclear in pineoblastoma, which rarely exhibits loss or mutation of TP53. Human pineoblastoma may use other mechanisms to bypass oncogene-induced senescence, such as loss or silencing of CDKN2A (which encodes p16INK4A and p14ARF) or amplification of MDM2 (Sharpless and Sherr 2015). Larger genomic studies in patient biospecimens, combined with existing conditional mouse models, could be used to study the role of other senescence mechanisms in pineoblastoma formation.

Although we focused on D-type cyclins, other aspects of cell cycle progression may also be important in pineoblastoma development. Recent studies have noted that CDK4/6 monophosphorylates Rb1, but this is usually insufficient for G1/S-phase progression without Rb1 hyperphosphorylation by cyclin E/CDK2 complexes (Narasimha et al. 2014; Kaulich et al. 2021). Previous studies showed that pineal tumors in mice can arise through dysregulation of the G1/S checkpoint by either ablating Rb1 or ectopically expressing Ccnd1 (Saab et al. 2009; Zalzali et al. 2012). Furthermore, the G2/M checkpoint gene set was upregulated in IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors. Together, these data suggest that D-type cyclins are rate-limiting for pineal tumor proliferation. When D-type cyclins are abundant, pineal tumors progress through the G1/S and G2/M checkpoints. On the other hand, when CDK4/6 is inhibited with palbociclib, cell cycle progression is inhibited, and G2/M checkpoint markers fall along with E2F targets. Understanding how loss of Drosha or Dicer1 dysregulates these checkpoints warrants further investigation. Nevertheless, by demonstrating that IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors upregulate D-type cyclins, we built on these studies and showed similarities between loss of Drosha/Dicer1 and loss of Rb1.

CDK4/6 inhibitors are clinically approved for use in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer and under investigation for treatment of other undifferentiated pediatric and adult tumors that retain intact RB1-E2F responses (Fassl et al. 2022; Goel et al. 2022; Schubert et al. 2022; Pu et al. 2024). Specifically, CDK4/6 inhibitors have been found to induce differentiation in neuroblastoma (Ferguson et al. 2023), rhabdomyosarcoma (Saab et al. 2006), medulloblastoma (Vo et al. 2023), and prostate cancer (Wu et al. 2021). To date, the only known genomic predictors of sensitivity to CDK4/6 inhibition directly drive overexpression of CCND1, CCND2, or CCND3 (Gong et al. 2017). These may arise through translocation, amplification, or variants that remove or alter microRNA binding sites in the 3′ UTR. Our results suggest that removal of the microRNAs themselves could also contribute to overexpression of D-type cyclins and thus sensitivity to CDK4/6 inhibitors.

When we compared IPDrosha and IPDicer1 tumors, we found that they largely resemble each other and cluster together, and we attribute the overlapping effects to loss of microRNAs. By identifying direct microRNA targets with miR-eCLIP, we showed that proliferation of IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors depends on a combination of the direct and indirect effects of microRNA loss. Our study also shows the limitations of microRNA target prediction. TargetScan has been shown to outperform other microRNA prediction algorithms (Agarwal et al. 2015; Kern et al. 2021), but identifying direct targets by miR-eCLIP was more predictive of gene expression changes than computational predictions alone. Further refinements to these prediction algorithms may be needed to account for long-term in vivo effects.

In this study, we focused on Plagl2 and Ccnd2, but the roles of other microRNA target genes should also be considered. For instance, we also found that MYC target genes were enriched in the IPDrosha/IPDicer and IPRb1 tumors, and many microRNAs regulate Mycn. Human pineoblastomas not driven by loss of RB1, DROSHA, or DICER1 are commonly driven by MYC activation (Liu et al. 2021). Similarly, a recently published mouse model driven by oncogenic Dicer1 mutations led to upregulation of E2F and Myc target genes (Wang et al. 2023). In other words, MYC activation may also be a common downstream effect of these mutations, and future strategies to therapeutically block MYC activity might also be effective in DROSHA/DICER1 mutant cancers. Our correlation with human tumors was limited by a small number of tumors with publicly available data and will need to be correlated in future studies encompassing more samples. These tumor samples are rare and usually derived from small biopsies in children, which limits the number of analyses that can be done. We used low DROSHA expression as a proxy for genomic loss, as this data set lacked genomic or methylation subtyping. Future studies with larger patient populations, correlating gene expression signatures with genomic or methylation analysis, will be needed to corroborate these findings. The mouse models that we describe here could be used to corroborate findings from patient samples, inform mechanistic questions that arise, and preclinically test new therapeutic possibilities.

We have also previously reported overlapping results in Wilms tumor, a pediatric cancer of embryonic renal progenitors frequently driven by mutations in microRNA processing genes (Rakheja et al. 2014; Torrezan et al. 2014; Walz et al. 2015; Wegert et al. 2015; Gadd et al. 2017). Unlike the complete loss of microRNA production seen in pineoblastoma, Wilms tumors most commonly exhibit point mutations in DROSHA that lead to incomplete impairment of microRNA processing. In two separate studies, we previously found that DROSHA/DICER1 regulate both PLAG1/IGF2 and CCND2 in Wilms tumor (Chen et al. 2018; Tiburcio et al. 2024). In the first study, we showed that PLAG1 was overexpressed in Wilms tumors with mutations in DROSHA or DICER1 and that DICER1 knockout Wilms tumor cells overexpress PLAG1 and IGF2 in a microRNA-dependent manner. In the second study, we found that CCND2 was the single most overexpressed gene in Wilms tumor cells when DROSHA was silenced and that mutated microRNA processing enzymes led to upregulation of E2F target genes. In the tumors that we examined in this study, although both Plagl2 and Igf2 were upregulated in IPDrosha/IPDicer1 tumors, we also noted some intertumor variability. IGF2 and CCND2 are important oncogenes in childhood cancers, and their expression can be modified through transcription factor binding, DNA methylation, chromatin modeling, microRNAs, copy number changes, and other mechanisms (Stroup et al. 2019; Pawlonka et al. 2021; Scalia et al. 2023). Our study highlights another potential mechanism by which these genes may be coordinately upregulated. Future studies will be needed to identify other the roles of other Plagl2 target genes or Plagl2-interacting proteins. Nevertheless, our observations suggest that despite arising from different cell types and through different effects on microRNA processing, DROSHA mutant Wilms tumors and pineoblastoma harness similar pathways to drive proliferation of embryonic progenitors.

It remains unclear why pineoblastomas tolerate complete loss of DROSHA or DICER1, while other tumors, including pleuropulmonary blastomas, Sertoli–Leydig cell tumors, and rhabdomyosarcomas, arise through point mutations in DICER1 (Brenneman et al. 2015; de Kock et al. 2019). These point mutations affect the metal-binding pocket of the RNase IIIb domain, which cleaves the 5p arm of pre-microRNA hairpins, resulting in loss of 5p-derived microRNAs but retention of 3p-derived microRNAs. Many of these DICER1-related tumors also resemble highly proliferative, undifferentiated progenitors. Furthermore, many of the microRNAs that we identified as key regulators of gene expression in pineoblastoma are 5p-derived and thus are lost in tumors with DICER1 missense variants, including members of the let-7/miR-98-5p, miR-99/100-5p, and miR-125/352-5p families. Our results suggest that the loss of let-7/miR-98-5p microRNAs is a key driving force in Drosha/Dicer1-driven pineoblastoma formation, and it is unclear whether loss of 3p-derived microRNAs is also required.

Recently, a subset of embryonal brain tumors was found to be defined by amplification and overexpression of PLAGL2, and our study expands on its oncogenic importance (Keck et al. 2023). PLAGL2 was initially described as a homolog of the PLAG1 transcription factor, whose translocation-driven overexpression defines pleomorphic adenoma of the salivary glands (Hensen et al. 2002). In the embryonic brain and in brain tumors, Plagl2 regulates the balance between differentiation and proliferation, and its overexpression shifts neural progenitors toward proliferation (Zheng et al. 2010; Adnani et al. 2018). Outside the brain, PLAGL2 also contributes to oncogenesis in lung cancer (Yang et al. 2011; Lin et al. 2023), colorectal cancer (Liu et al. 2018; Li et al. 2019a,b; Fischer et al. 2023), gastric cancer (Chen et al. 2023), breast cancer (Xu et al. 2018; Li et al. 2023), and acute myeloid leukemia (Landrette et al. 2005). In these cancers, PLAGL2 has been shown to contribute to proliferation, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and “stemness” by regulating target genes such as Igf2 and regulators of Wnt signaling (Zheng et al. 2010; Liu et al. 2018; Li et al. 2019b, 2023; Fischer et al. 2023; Keck et al. 2023). To our knowledge, however, this is the first description of PLAGL2 upregulation in DROSHA- or DICER1-related pediatric cancers.

There were three technical limitations that, if overcome, could expand the utility of this model and the extensibility of our findings. First, we were unable to culture our tumors ex vivo. Future in vitro studies could be used to functionally test our findings through genetic and pharmacological manipulations. Second, our transcriptomic analyses of developmental state were based on RNA-seq generated from three discrete stages of rat development. With the emergence of single-cell technologies, transcriptomic developmental atlases of the murine pineal gland may soon arise and may even describe microRNA expression at these developmental stages. A more detailed understanding of pineal gland development could fine-tune the developmental stages reflected by IPDrosha, IPDicer1, and IPRb1 tumors. Last, our studies used a subcutaneous allograft model over an orthotopic model for practical reasons. Subcutaneous patient-derived xenografts of pediatric cancer have been used to test dozens of drugs in preclinical studies, and response rates in these studies commonly correlate with clinical response (Peterson and Houghton 2004; Houghton et al. 2007). In our study, using a subcutaneous model allowed us to test the principle that these small molecules are capable of blocking growth of Drosha/Dicer1-driven cancers. CDK4/6 inhibitors and Igf1r inhibitors have been shown to cross the blood–brain barrier (Raub et al. 2015; Losonczy 2016; Russo et al. 2019; Braal et al. 2021), but differences between the subcutaneous and intracranial tumor microenvironments could affect how these tumors respond to drugs. Furthermore, although we only tested the combination of palbociclib and ceritinib in IPDrosha tumors, our results suggest that IPDicer1 tumors are likely to respond in a similar manner. Together with the small sample size of our human tumor correlation studies, these limitations warrant further investigation before such drugs are tested in a clinical setting.

In this study, we identified two small molecules that impaired tumor proliferation by blocking signaling pathways normally regulated by microRNAs but also exposed potential limitations of these drugs. Although they are both clinically approved, it remains to be seen whether they will be effective in children with these cancers. Although both drugs reduced proliferation, tumor cells still appeared histologically viable at the end of the experiment. Neither drug was cytotoxic on its own, and they may need to be combined with other therapies to harness clinical benefit. For instance, previous studies have found synergy between CDK4/6 inhibitors and immune checkpoint blockade, and we also found that palbociclib upregulated immune gene signatures (Supplemental Fig. S10D; Goel et al. 2017; Deng et al. 2018; Schaer et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2018). Alternatively, human tumors may harness other oncogenic signaling pathways or growth factors, such as RAS/MAP kinase; indeed, RAS pathway genes are the most frequently mutated genes in DICER1-related tumors after TP53 (Pugh et al. 2014; Bae et al. 2021; Kommoss et al. 2023). Future studies will be needed to identify the specific mitogenic signaling pathways used in human pineoblastomas. Furthermore, we also observed toxicities, particularly in mice who continued to receive prolonged palbociclib. Across three experiments at two dose levels, we observed significant toxicity in five of 30 mice. Previous reports had shown that mice tolerate palbociclib at similar or higher doses up to 4 weeks without overt toxicity (Fry et al. 2004; Parrish et al. 2015; Bollard et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2019; Salvador-Barbero et al. 2020; Callegari et al. 2022; Passeri et al. 2022; Saleh et al. 2023; Nave et al. 2024), and further studies into the therapeutic promise of palbociclib would need to balance its efficacy against long-term toxicity. Whether other inhibitors of proliferation or mitogenic signaling would provide a better risk to benefit ratio needs to be studied. Nevertheless, our results demonstrate the potential for the signals upregulated by microRNA loss to be therapeutic targets in cancers driven by impairment of DROSHA or DICER1.

Reads from each sample were mapped to the reference genome using STAR (v2.5.3a) (Dobin et al. 2013), and read counts were generated using featureCounts (Liao et al. 2014). Differential gene expression calculations were then computed from raw counts using the DESeq2 package (v1.42.0) (Love et al. 2014). The Wald statistic output for protein-coding genes from DESeq2 was used as input for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the fgsea package (v1.28.0) (Korotkevich et al. 2021). Hallmark gene sets were derived from MSigDB v2023.1.Mm or v2023.1.Hs for mice or humans, respectively (Liberzon et al. 2015). Predicted microRNA target genes were defined as genes with aggregate PCT ≥ 0.9 and total context++ score < −0.4 based on TargetScan (v8.0) (Agarwal et al. 2015).

For external data, RNA-seq FPKM tables from rat pineal glands across different stages of development were downloaded from GEO data set GSE46127 (Yamazaki et al. 2015). Differential expression was calculated using t-tests after log2 transformation. Marker genes at each time point were defined as the 200 most overexpressed protein-coding genes with mouse homologs compared with other time points. For correlations with the WAP-Cre model (Chung et al. 2020), microarray data from the Affymetrix mouse gene 2.0 ST array platform were downloaded from GEO data set GSE124537 and processed with the limma package to compute log2 fold change (Ritchie et al. 2015). These values were then correlated with log2 fold change computed from IPDrosha/IPDicer1 and IPRb1 tumors using linear regression.

Pineal tumors from IPDrosha/IPDicer1 mice were dissociated into single-cell suspension using the brain tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec 130-095-942), and then “cleavage under targets and release using nuclease” (CUT&RUN) (Skene and Henikoff 2017; Meers et al. 2019a) libraries were prepared from dissociated tumor cells using the ChIC/CUT&RUN kit (EpiCypher 14-1048) according to manufacturer's recommendations. Two frozen IPRb1 tumor fragments underwent miR-eCLIP sequencing at EclipseBio, as described previously (Manakov et al. 2022). Briefly, Ago2 was immunoprecipitated from cross-linked RNA. After ligation of miRNA–mRNA chimeras, input and immunoprecipitated RNA were analyzed by sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Chimeric and nonchimeric eCLIP sequences were processed using the pipeline described (Manakov et al. 2022) and compared with input in each sample using the CLIPper algorithm (v2.0) (Lovci et al. 2013). Chimeric and nonchimeric peaks were compared between the two samples to identify reproducible peaks. Reproducible nonchimeric peaks were those enriched in each IP with a log2 fold change of ≥3 and P-value ≤ 0.001. HOMER de novo motif analysis (Heinz et al. 2010) was used to identify enriched motifs in these peaks. Reproducible microRNA–miRNA chimeras were those with three or more chimeric reads and a reproducibility index of ≥0.5. Seed matches were then limited to perfect match, canonical 7-mers or 8-mers (based on TargetScan definitions) (Agarwal et al. 2015) in the 3′ UTRs of their target genes.
