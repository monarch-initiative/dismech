# Exploring the impact of brucellosis on maternal and child health: transmission mechanisms, patient effects, and current trends in drug use and resistance: a scoping review
**Authors:** Tran Xuan Ngoc Huy
**Journal:** Beni-Suef University Journal of Basic and Applied Sciences (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s43088-024-00569-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s43088-024-00569-8)

## Content

Abstract
Background
While often unrecognized, brucellosis, a significant zoonotic disease, silently endangers the health of mothers and children worldwide. This scoping review sheds light on transmission pathways, maternal–fetal consequences, and treatment hurdles, specifically considering maternal and child health concerns.

Method
To comprehensively grasp brucellosis in mothers and children, we systematically scoured electronic databases (DOAJ, Google Scholar, PubMed, and Semantic Scholar) for studies published after 2005. Our search included experimental studies (both randomized controlled clinical trials and quasi-experimental), analytical observations, descriptive reports, qualitative papers, and existing systematic reviews. All retrieved data were then charted and processed following Arksey and O'Malley's established framework for scoping reviews.

Result
Twenty-five studies spanning varied regions and methodologies met inclusion criteria. Key findings demonstrate that zoonotic brucellosis acquisition from livestock exposures among vulnerable maternal groups accounts for up to 70% of cases. Vertical transmission from mother to child during pregnancy, delivery, or breastfeeding was reported in 15–20% of cases. Substantial risks of miscarriage (25%), preterm birth (20%), hepatosplenomegaly (10%), febrile illness (30%), and possible long-term complications were documented. Treatment success rates using combination antibiotic therapy were reported to be as high as 98%, though emerging antibiotic resistance patterns challenge effective treatment, with 25% of Brucella isolates resistant to rifampin and 51% resistant to both trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and streptomycin.

Conclusion
This review reveals the alarming yet hidden toll brucellosis takes on maternal–fetal pairs and breastfeeding. In regions battling this endemic disease, tailored education, upgraded diagnostic tools, prompt antibiotic therapy, responsible antimicrobial stewardship, and One Health collaborations offer crucial pathways to shield mothers and children from its harmful consequences. Continued research will pave the way for even better solutions to alleviate this complex zoonosis, particularly for vulnerable populations.

infection from this represents a formidable global pub -
lic health threat with over 500.000 new reported human

Regarding pathophysiology, individuals affected fre -

Common symptoms include pyrexia, rigors, cephalal -

tendency to affect numerous organ systems, patients

brucellosis affecting expecting mothers proves uniquely 
concerning as such infections cultivate adverse maternal 
and fetal outcomes. Specifically, research shows a strong 
correlation between brucellosis and miscarriage, preterm 
birth, stunted prenatal growth, and even fetal demise [3]. 
Even more troubling, brucellosis passes vertically from 
mother to child during the prenatal and postpartum peri-

Brucellosis epidemiology and management practices 
exhibit significant regional variations across the globe. 
Africa and Asia bear the highest risk and incidence 
of human brucellosis, with major hotspots in equato -
rial and southern Africa, as well as the Middle East [5]. 
These regions often lack adequate public and animal 
health programs, perpetuating the disease’s prevalence 
[5]. The Americas show a more uniform spread of risk, 
with Central America having the highest risk, followed 
by northern and southern South America [5]. Europe, 
benefiting from advanced surveillance and control pro -
grams, has the lowest estimated case counts, though hot -
spots persist in the Mediterranean area [5]. Management 
challenges vary by region, with Africa and parts of Asia 
struggling with inadequate health programs and rapid 
population growth, while the Middle East grapples with 
close human–animal contact and raw milk consumption 
[5]. The Americas face issues related to farming practices 
and cross-border trade of raw milk products [5]. Glob -
ally, underreporting remains a significant problem, as 
exemplified by a study in Greece where official reports 
vastly underestimated actual case numbers [6]. Occupa -
tional exposure and consumption of unpasteurized dairy 
products are major risk factors across regions [5, 6]. To 
improve brucellosis management worldwide, there is a 
pressing need for well-designed observational studies, 
enhanced surveillance systems, international collabora -
tion, and multi-sectorial control policies informed by 
accurate disease burden assessments[5]. Addressing 
these regional differences and implementing targeted 
strategies is crucial for controlling brucellosis on a global 
scale [5].
Henceforth, notwithstanding the designation of brucel-
losis as a pivotal worldwide health affliction, the scientific 
community has historically disregarded this prevailing 
zoonotic disease. This scoping review strives to amal -
gamate modern evidence regarding Brucella transmission 
through a maternal/child health lens while summarizing 
brucellosis’ impact on maternal well-being, breastfeeding 
infants, and problems like treatment resistance. The con -
solidation of knowledge gaps and identification of future 
directions is expected to serve as a catalyst for further 
research on the interface between brucellosis, livestock, 
and humans in the foreseeable future.
1.2  Rationale
The rationale for conducting this scoping review on 
brucellosis in the context of maternal and child health 
is multifold. Brucellosis remains an understudied yet 
highly prevalent disease globally that disproportionately 
impacts vulnerable populations [1]. Pregnant women and 
young children are particularly susceptible to brucellosis 
and its potential complications. However, research spe -
cifically focused on brucellosis transmission, diagnosis, 
treatment, and prevention in maternal and child cohorts 
has been limited.
The selection of a scoping review approach was based 
on multiple factors. Scoping reviews enable the incor -
poration of several types of evidence, including non-
intervention research [7]. This will simplify the process 
of identifying and understanding important ideas from 
observational studies, case reports, and qualitative 
research that are specifically applicable to the setting of 
brucellosis in mother and child health. Furthermore, 
scoping reviews place emphasis on the comprehen -
sive identification and categorization of existing evi -
dence, rather than evaluating the caliber of the research 
that are included [8]. This facilitates the recognition of 
general patterns and areas of knowledge deficiency to 
guide future study. Finally, the extensive inclusion crite -
ria of scoping reviews will enable the retrieval of mate -
rial pertaining to the transmission dynamics, impact on 
patients, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and control of 
brucellosis in many geographical situations. This holistic 
approach is in line with the goal of improving the under -
standing of brucellosis in relation to animal, human, and 
environmental health.
The information compiled from this scoping review 
will identify crucial areas of focus for healthcare policy, 
research, and practice concerning brucellosis in mothers 
and children. It will also inform integrated interventions 
encompassing human health, livestock management, and 
food safety domains to mitigate brucellosis transmission 
while safeguarding maternal and child well-being.

To keep the scoping review focused on current evi -

1.3  Objectives
In order to better understand brucellosis and its impact 
on maternal and child health (MCH), this scoping review 
will look at the existing research on the topic.
The scoping review aims to accomplish the following:
1. To explore and discuss the mechanism of transmis -
sion for Brucellosis in MCH.
2. To understand the effect of brucellosis on maternal 
health.
3. To understand and discuss the effect of brucellosis on 
child health (breastfeeding infants below 2 years).
4. To examine current treatment, drug use, and drug 
resistance issues of brucellosis in maternal and child 
patients.
1.4  Definition of key terms
Brucellosis: Bacterial zoonotic disease caused by organ -
isms of the Brucella genus that can be transmitted from 
animals to humans.
Maternal health: Health and well-being of women dur -
ing pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period.
Child health: Health and well-being of infants and 
young children below 5 years of age.
Breastfeeding: Feeding an infant or young child milk 
from the breast/nipples.
Vertical transmission: Passing of an infection from a 
mother to fetus during pregnancy or an infant around 
childbirth.
Congenital: Present from birth.
Treatment: Medications and care plans used to manage 
brucellosis.
Drug use: Consumption of pharmaceuticals to treat, 
cure, or prevent disease.
Drug resistance: Reduction in effectiveness of drugs/
antimicrobials to inhibit or kill pathogens.
2  Main text
2.1  Review question
The primary inquiry for this scoping review, utilizing the 
PCC (Population, Concept, Context) framework, is as 
follows: What evidence is available concerning the trans -
mission, effects, treatment, and prevention of brucellosis 
(Concept) in the specific context of maternal and child 
health (Context), focusing on pregnant women, mothers, 
infants, and young children (Population)?
Additional sub-questions as per the scoping review 
objectives are:
1. What evidence exists regarding possible mechanisms 
or routes of brucellosis transmission to pregnant 
women and young children in the context of mater -
nal and child health?
2. What evidence exists regarding the effects or out -
comes of brucellosis infection among pregnant 
women and mothers in the context of maternal 
health?
3. What evidence exists regarding the effects or out -
comes of brucellosis infection in infants and young 
children below two years, including from potential 
vertical or breastfeeding transmission?
4. What evidence exists regarding current treatment 
approaches, drug use patterns, and drug resistance 
trends related to brucellosis infection in pregnant 
women, mothers, infants, and young children in the 
context of maternal and child health?

3.2  Types of sources
The following types of sources will be included in this 
scoping review:
1. Analytical observational studies (cross-sectional 
studies, case–control studies, and cohort studies)
2. Experimental study designs (randomized controlled 
trials and quasi-experimental studies)
3. Descriptive observational studies (case series and 
case reports)
4. Qualitative studies (ethnographies, phenomenologi -
cal studies, and focus group discussions)
5. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses
6. Text and opinion papers (comments, editorials, posi -
tion papers)
Experimental study designs such as randomized con -
trolled trials and quasi-experimental studies will be 
excluded as the interventions assessed in such stud -
ies do not align with the objectives of this descriptive 
scoping review. The inclusion of analytical studies will 
facilitate the mapping of quantitative evidence regard -
ing brucellosis transmission factors, effects on patients, 
treatments used, and drug resistance trends pertinent to 
maternal and child health contexts. Descriptive studies 
such as case reports will provide additional insights into 
real-world diagnosis, manifestation, severity, treatment 
challenges, and outcomes associated with brucellosis 
infection among mothers and children.
Qualitative evidence on the other hand will help high -
light patient experiences, cultural contexts of disease 
burden, health-seeking behaviors, and community per -
spectives influencing risks and outcomes related to 
maternal and child brucellosis. Text and opinion arti -
cles will capture contemporary discussion points and 
aid identification of current knowledge gaps necessi -
tating further original research on the topic. The wide 
range of evidence types to be synthesized will provide a 
rich understanding of brucellosis at the nexus of animal 
health, human health, and social contexts, which is cru -
cial for enhancing maternal and child well-being globally.

the Brucella genus. Table  1 gives the summary of stud -

and camels. Transmission occurs through direct con -

working closely with animals particularly susceptible 
[2].
In the context of maternal and child health, brucel -
losis transmission occurs via two main routes which 
include zoonotic transmission from animals and human 
transmission from mother to child vertically during 
pregnancy or through breastfeeding [10].
Several studies in this review described zoonotic bru -
cellosis transmission to pregnant women with close live -
stock exposures. For instance, Kledmanee et al. reported 
over 13% brucellosis seropositivity among Thai women 
with livestock contact [11]. This occupational exposure 
to potentially infected goat births or aborted fetuses 
facilitates direct inoculation and entry of Brucella bac -
teria into the body [12]. Consuming unpasteurized milk 
or dairy products also poses risks, though fewer cases 
stem from ingestion compared to direct inoculation of 
bacteria into the mucosa, conjunctiva, or abraded skin 
[13]. Therefore, while brucellosis transmission via inges -
tion warrants caution around consuming high-risk foods 
during pregnancy, direct contact with livestock through 
farming, veterinary work or animal birthing assistance 
confers particularly high zoonotic transmission risks that 
should be minimized.
Several cases demonstrate vertical transmission of 
brucellosis from mother to child during pregnancy or 
delivery. Esfehani and Glocwicz et  al. reported congru -
ent brucellosis serology between mothers and newborns, 
confirming prenatal transmission [14, 15]. Moreover, 
Al-Faifi and Ibrahim described neonatal brucellosis in a 
premature baby, likely from exposure to infected amni -
otic fluids in utero or during delivery from a mother with 
recurrent abortions from brucellosis [10]. Congenital and 
neonatal cases reaffirm that inadequately treated mater -
nal brucellosis can prompt placental seeding and fetal 
infection [16]. Therefore, prompt maternal diagnosis and 
antibiotic treatment is imperative.
Brucellosis transmission via breastmilk, while less com-
mon, remains a concern. Studies by other researchers 
also reported neonatal brucellosis from breastfeeding 
[17]. Even with antibiotic therapy, detectable Brucella 
bacteria may persist in breast milk [18]. Consequently, 
clinicians should be cognizant of potential brucello -
sis transmission to neonates during breastfeeding from 
mothers with confirmed/suspected brucellosis. Careful 
case-by-case consideration of temporary avoidance of 
breastfeeding may be prudent until maternal antibiotic 
treatment is completed.
The two main modes of brucellosis transmission are 
direct and indirect contact with the Brucella bacteria 
[2]. Several studies highlight the direct transmission of 
brucellosis through contact with infected animals and 
their secretions. Kansiime et al. noted that assisting cat -
tle births or handling aborted fetuses directly inoculates 
Brucella bacteria via mucous membranes or abraded 
skin [19]. Consumption of unpasteurized dairy products, 
particularly soft cheeses, also confer infection risks [13]. 
These studies caution against direct livestock exposures 
during pregnancy and emphasize avoiding unpasteurized 
dairy to mitigate transmission risks.
Notably, a case report by Carrera et  al. traced neona -
tal brucellosis to breastmilk consumption, representing 
direct person-to-person transmission from the infected 
mother [20]. Therefore, while less common than zoonotic 
transmission, direct exposure to Brucella bacteria via 
infected humans or breastmilk remains possible and war-
rants clinical consideration when managing brucellosis in 
mothers and children.
The reviewed evidence also highlights indirect modes 
of brucellosis transmission through environmental con -
tact. Kansiime et  al. described infections in individu -
als never directly exposed to aborting livestock but who 
had contact with contaminated pastures or water sources 
instead [19]. Brucella bacteria can persist in the envi -
ronment long enough to serve as a secondary infection 
source. Furthermore, abattoir and laboratory workers 
may acquire brucellosis through exposure to contami -
nated equipment, surfaces, or samples [13].
Therefore, in summary, brucellosis transmission to 
maternal and child cohorts occurs chiefly through direct 
zoonotic contact with infected livestock and secondar -
ily through vertical transmission from mother to child 
during pregnancy, delivery, or breastfeeding in inad -
equately treated cases. Minimizing direct contact with 
potentially infected livestock and prompt maternal diag -
nosis and antibiotic therapy is key to mitigating risks of 
adverse health outcomes [21]. The studies collated in 
this review provide consistent evidence supporting these 
zoonotic and vertical transmission routes that shape bru-
cellosis acquisition risks and guide targeted preventive

4.2  Effects on maternal health
Several studies demonstrate associations between brucel-
losis and increased miscarriage or stillbirth risks. Table  2 
gives the summary of studies about effects of brucello -
sis infection during pregnancy. A case–control study by 
Elshamy and Ahmed found a 19-fold increased likelihood 
of miscarriage among pregnant women seropositive for 
brucellosis compared to the uninfected control group 
[22]. This aligns with meta-analysis findings indicating 
average miscarriage rates of around 30% among preg -
nant women with brucellosis [23]. Proposed mechanisms 
include direct infection and inflammation of the placenta 
and fetus or maternal fever triggering uterine contrac -
tions [22]. Based on consistent evidence across multi -
ple studies, pregnant women diagnosed with brucellosis 
should receive prompt counseling regarding substantially 
elevated risks of miscarriage or stillbirth. Early detection 
and antibiotic treatment provide the best prospects for 
preventing such adverse outcomes.
4.3  Effects on child health
Several cases demonstrate vertical mother-to-child trans-
mission of brucellosis during pregnancy resulting in con -
genital infections. A case report by Al Anazi described 
a 5-day-old newborn delivered at 36 weeks gestation 
who presented with respiratory distress and was diag -
nosed with congenital brucellosis after the mother 
reported consumption of unpasteurized goat cheese dur -
ing pregnancy [24]. Zhao et  al. reported a similar case 
of a 33-week preterm infant with congenital brucellosis 
exhibiting extensive petechiae, thrombocytopenia, and 
other hematologic dysfunction requiring hospitaliza -
tion and antibiotic therapy [25]. Both cases exemplify 
severe manifestations of congenital brucellosis acquired 
from inadequately treated maternal infections. Though 
rare, such cases warrant clinicians maintaining vigilance 
for the possibility of transplacental brucellosis transmis -
sion if pregnant women report exposures or suggestive 
symptoms.
Beyond congenital acquisition, brucellosis exposure 
during delivery also risks neonatal infections and ensu -
ing complications. Akçakuş et al. described two neonates 
infected with Brucella melitensis meningitis attributed to 
contaminated blood products used in exchange transfu -
sions [26]. Fortunately, the cases responded to combina -
tion antibiotic therapy without disability upon follow-up. 
However, a review of neonatal brucellosis case reports 
found 13–15% mortality rates and observed long-term 
neurologic sequelae in over half of the survivors [27]. 
Therefore, while not extensively prevalent, vertical and 
perinatal brucellosis transmission events pose substantial 
morbidity and mortality risks to newborns and warrant 
increased awareness and prevention efforts. Screening 
mothers and avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures 
in exposed neonates are imperative.
While neonatal cases manifest most acutely, brucel -
losis exposures in infancy through unpasteurized dairy 
consumption or environmental contact raise concerns 
for subtle developmental impacts. A study by Kansiime 
et al. modeled associations between childhood brucello -
sis and cognitive delays in mathematical skills acquisition 
[19]. Proposed mechanisms include neuroinflammation 
and neural damage resulting from meningoencephalitis 
complications in a subset of pediatric cases. However, 
even mild infections may impact attention, learning, and 
memory through effects on developing neural networks. 
Therefore, clinicians should maintain vigilance regarding 
neurocognitive development milestones among children 
seropositive for brucellosis and be prepared to provide 
supportive interventions.

health consequences of childhood brucellosis. A case

over five years after neonatal infections and documented 
recurring joint pain and fatigability consistent with 
chronic brucellosis [10]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis 
found that 6.5% of pediatric brucellosis cases involved 
osteoarticular complications and 4.5% progressed to

4.4  Current drug use and drug resistance
The foremost antibiotics used to treat brucellosis include 
tetracyclines (doxycycline), aminoglycosides (strepto -
mycin, gentamicin), rifampin, fluoroquinolones (cipro -
floxacin), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole [30–32]. 
Table 3 gives the summary of studies about treatment 
for brucellosis. These agents act against Brucella species 
through different mechanisms interfering with protein, 
DNA, or cell wall synthesis required for bacterial replica -
tion. Combination regimens utilizing agents with diver -
gent actions decrease risks of resistance emergence and 
improve treatment success rates compared to individual 
drugs.
For pregnant patients, doxycycline, rifampin, and 
aminoglycosides at adjusted first-trimester doses effec -
tively clear infections while minimizing fetal hazards 
from toxicity [32]. After childbirth, more intensive com -
bination therapy helps prevent postpartum relapses. 
Optimal regimens and durations specific to maternal 
brucellosis require further research, but existing evidence 
and extrapolations provide a framework for treatment.
Available studies endorse current brucellosis antibiotic 
regimens during pregnancy, though comparative effi -
cacy data are lacking. A case series from Turkey reported 
universal treatment success combining doxycycline, 
rifampin, and an aminoglycoside, modified after the first 
trimester, for acute brucellosis in 12 pregnant women 
[32]. Another multicenter study described 98% cure rates 
using similar combination therapy adjusted for preg -
nancy stage and duration [33]. While second-line agents 
like quinolones may treat some rifampin-resistant strains, 
teratogenicity concerns constrain use during pregnancy 
[23]. Therefore, when selected and dosed appropriately 
under guidance, typical anti-Brucella antibiotics effi -
caciously and safely treat pregnant cases without usual 
regimens like quinolones. More research should further 
optimize maternal treatment protocols. However, cur -
rently available antibiotics remain largely efficacious.
Despite generally favorable treatment outcomes, the 
threat of antibiotic resistance poses growing concerns for 
managing brucellosis, especially amid vulnerable mater -
nal and child populations.
Several studies demonstrate rising resistance rates 
compromising options for effective treatment regi -
mens. A study found 25% of Brucella isolates resistant 
to rifampin, limiting a crucial first-line oral agent used 
even in pregnancy [34]. Additionally, 51% of strains are 
resistant to both trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and 
streptomycin, restricting two other typical treatment 
alternatives. Combined resistance to multiple agent 
classes gravely impairs constructing optimal combina -
tion regimens needed to improve outcomes and prevent 
relapse.
Proposed contributors likely include overreliance on 
limited antibiotics like rifampin monotherapy applying 
selective pressure on Brucella species to develop resist -
ance-conferring mutations [32]. Treatment non-adher -
ence and incomplete courses also foster incremental 
resistance amplification by exposing pathogens to sub-
therapeutic drug levels. Environmental reservoirs in live -
stock may further harbor resistant strains transmittable 
to naïve human hosts.
Ultimately as highlighted by several reviewed studies, 
clinicians must maintain vigilance for local resistance 
trends when designing anti-Brucella regimens, especially 
for maternal cases where therapeutic choices require 
complex risk–benefit balancing. Continued microbio -
logical surveillance alongside controlled clinical trials 
expanding available treatment options and optimizing 
combinations for resistant cases represent top priori -
ties for preserving efficacy [35]. Protecting vulnerable 
pregnant women and children also warrants coordinated 
One Health efforts limiting the propagation of resist -
ant Brucella strains across livestock–environment inter -
faces. The importance of One Health collaborations 
and responsible antimicrobial stewardship in manag -
ing brucellosis cannot be overstated. One Health, which 
integrates human, animal, and environmental health dis -
ciplines, is crucial for addressing the complex nature of 
brucellosis, a zoonotic disease affecting multiple species

5  Conclusions
This scoping review comprehensively investigated the 
existing information on the transmission mechanisms, 
health effects, and treatment strategies of brucellosis, 
specifically in the context of mother and child health. 
The evaluation encompassed a total of 25 papers, which 
consisted of analytical observational studies, descriptive 
analyses, qualitative evidence syntheses, and systematic 
reviews.
The primary goals of this scoping review were diverse 
and comprehensive. The researchers aimed to investigate 
the many transmission channels of brucellosis that may 
disproportionately expose pregnant women, mothers, 
and newborns. In addition, the authors sought to clarify 
the following impacts of these Brucella infections on both 
maternal health and child health outcomes. Ultimately, 
the analysis analyzed current brucellosis treatment pro -
tocols and observed patterns of medication resistance 
to provide guidance for medical practice and future 
research endeavors.
The evidence assessment emphasizes that Brucella can 
be transmitted to humans through various channels, with 
livestock contact, animal slaughter, and consumption of 
unpasteurized dairy products being common dangers 
that disproportionately affect pregnant women. Further -
more, the process of transmitting from mother to kid in a 
vertical manner was observed and recorded during preg -
nancy, childbirth, and breastfeeding in multiple research 
studies.
Concerning the effects on maternal health, there is 
clear quantitative evidence indicating an increased risk 
of miscarriage, stillbirth, and premature birth, ranging 
from 20 to 30%. Furthermore, around 30–40% of preg -
nant individuals experienced clinical febrile infection 
symptoms, including fever, rigors, and malaise. More 
than 33% of the mothers who were infected also experi -
enced hepatosplenomegaly and stomach discomfort. The 
scoping review revealed conclusive evidence that vertical 
congenital Brucella infection leads to neonatal respira -
tory distress, apparent petechial rashes, and hematologic 
abnormalities immediately after birth, specifically affect -
ing the health of the kid. Furthermore, the consequences 
of invasive transmission can encompass meningitis, 
persistent impairment, and, in severe instances, fatality. 
Continued monitoring is necessary to assess the possible 
cognitive, developmental, or long-term effects.
Presently, the primary components of most treatment 
plans for brucellosis during pregnancy consist of tetra -
cycline antibiotics, rifampin, and aminoglycosides. How -
ever, these medications are modified to ensure safety. The 
existing research confirms that these current antibiotic 
treatments are generally effective and safe during preg -
nancy in the majority of cases.
Nevertheless, the increasing rates of resistance, reach -
ing up to 50%, are significantly undermining the effec -
tiveness of numerous previously preferred antibiotics for 
treating Brucella infections. Probable contributing vari -
ables include an excessive dependence on a narrow range 
of antibiotics, insufficient adherence to treatment, and 
the dynamics of livestock reservoirs. This highlights the 
urgent requirement for ongoing monitoring, advance -
ment of novel medicinal medicines, and comprehensive 
One Health control initiatives in future.
To summarize, this scoping review analyzed 25 papers 
to create a comprehensive overview of the evidence 
on the significant impact of brucellosis on the health of 
mothers and children in locations where the disease is 
prevalent. The main areas of focus for policy and practice 
are preventing the transmission of diseases from animals 
to humans and from mother to child, closely monitoring 
and promptly treating cases of pregnancy to reduce com -
plications, developing new treatment options to address 
resistance, and promoting collaborative efforts between 
different fields of study to limit the spread of diseases 
between animals and humans. Additional rigorous study 
can further clarify the dangers, consequences, and inter -
ventions of brucellosis in order to protect vulnerable 
women and children worldwide.
This analysis yields several crucial suggestions to direct 
health policy, clinical practice, and research priorities 
in tackling brucellosis within the context of mother and 
child health. Creating specific instructions on avoiding 
high-risk foods and taking measures with livestock could 
provide communities the ability to control the spread of 
diseases that can be transmitted between animals and
