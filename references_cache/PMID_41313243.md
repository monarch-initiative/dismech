# Evidence-based classification of genes implicated in skeletal disorders using the ClinGen curation framework.
**Authors:** Webb RF, McCurry H, Girod A, Hughes M, Wilcox E, Patel M, Broeren EC, Tshering KC, DiStefano M, Botto LD, Burrage LC, Cormier-Daire V, Dong J, Ehmke N, Krakow D, Moosa S, Mortier G, Nagamani S, Pena L, Sanchez-Lara PA, Superti-Furga A, Unger S, Velasco D, Warman ML, Brown K, D'Cunha Burkardt D, Ferreira CR, ClinGen Skeletal Disorders Gene Curation Expert Panel
**Journal:** J Bone Miner Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/jbmr/zjaf183](https://doi.org/10.1093/jbmr/zjaf183)

## Content

More than 770 genetic skeletal disorders have been described, most with disease-causing variants reported in 1 of over 550 different genes. The ClinGen Skeletal Disorders Gene Curation Expert Panel was established to determine the strength of the evidence that supports specific gene-disease relationships (GDRs). Such information can assist clinical testing laboratories in choosing genes that should be included on diagnostic panels. Nine genes accounting for the most frequently encountered skeletal dysplasias (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL2A1, FGFR3, SLC26A2, TRPV4, COMP, ALPL, and SOX9) associated in the medical literature with 26 different skeletal disorders were reviewed using a semi-quantitative scoring framework. This framework is utilized by ClinGen to assess the clinical validity of GDRs. All 9 genes were "definitively" associated with at least 1 skeletal disorder and several were associated with multiple clinically or radiographically distinct skeletal conditions. Among these 26 GDRs, the ClinGen Skeletal Disorders Gene Curation Expert Panel determined that 22 (84.6%) had definitive relationships, 2 (7.7%) had moderate relationships, and 2 (7.7%) had limited relationships. None of the 26 GDRs were disputed or refuted. For moderate and limited GDRs, clinical and genetic reports from additional probands and their families are needed to upgrade these GDRs to definitive. Up-to-date assessments about the strength of the relationship between genes and phenotypes should improve the sensitivity and specificity of genetic testing in individuals with skeletal disease. The expert curations for the 9 aforementioned genes are published on the ClinGen website.

The Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) is an NIH-funded project dedicated to building a central resource that defines the clinical relevance of genes and variants for use in precision medicine and research.1 ClinGen realized a critical need for standardized evaluation of the literature claiming gene-disease relationships (GDRs) and developed a semi-quantitative framework to address this need. That framework evaluates the strength of the relationship between gene-disease pairs by thorough investigation of the literature for genetic or experimental evidence to classify a relationship as Definitive, Strong, Moderate, Limited, Disputed, Refuted, or No Known Disease Relationship.2 Furthermore, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) released a technical standard in 2020 stipulating that only genes with a gene-disease validity of Moderate strength or higher based on the ClinGen framework should be included on diagnostic panels, underscoring the importance of ClinGen’s approach.3 ClinGen assembles expert panels in various disease areas to curate critical questions surrounding the validity of GDRs and variant pathogenicity.4 As of June 2025, ClinGen has assessed 3,129 GDRs, about 23% of which lack sufficient evidence to support Moderate or stronger clinical validity and should therefore not be included on diagnostic panels.

The nosology committee for the International Skeletal Dysplasia Society (ISDS) recognizes >770 genetic skeletal disorders associated with variants in >550 genes.5,6 To evaluate the clinical validity of these GDRs, ClinGen formed the Skeletal (Disorders) Gene Curation Expert Panel (GCEP). The Skeletal GCEP started by evaluating nine genes that account for the most frequently encountered skeletal diseases. This paper summarizes findings for these nine genes and the 26 related skeletal disorders that have been attributed to variants in these genes. Skeletal GCEP-approved gene curations are available at the ClinGen website (https://search.clinicalgenome.org/kb/affiliate/10065?page=1&size=25&search=).

The Skeletal GCEP was formed in 2020. Member recruitment was conducted via email invitation. Members include clinicians experienced in diagnosing and caring for individuals with skeletal disorders, diagnostic laboratory experts with experience in genetic testing for genes within the group’s scope, ClinGen framework experts, and volunteer biocurators trained in gene-disease validity curation. The Skeletal GCEP currently comprises 32 members (https://www.clinicalgenome.org/affiliation/40065/).

The disease scope of the Skeletal GCEP includes monogenic disorders primarily affecting cartilage and bone. Complex, common skeletal diseases such as aging-related osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, or syndromic presentations where cartilage or bone phenotypes are not cardinal features were not considered for this initial round of curation. For conditions with additional cardinal phenotypes, experts from the relevant field were consulted to determine the best panel(s) to curate the gene.

The Skeletal GCEP initiated curation of the nine genes associated with frequently encountered skeletal diseases per expert consensus. These genes, in alphabetical order, are ALPL, COL1A1, COL1A2, COL2A1, COMP, FGFR3, SLC26A2, SOX9, and TRPV4. Gene-disease validity curation was performed by Skeletal GCEP curators using ClinGen’s Gene Curation Interface (GCI).7

Due to the allelic heterogeneity of genetic skeletal disorders, all genes were precurated using the ClinGen Lumping and Splitting guidelines.8 The 2019 version of the International Skeletal Dysplasia Society’s (ISDS) nosology of genetic skeletal disorders was considered when originally lumping and splitting skeletal disorders,5 followed by their 2023 revision.6 Genes associated with more than one skeletal phenotype had each GDR evaluated by considering molecular mechanism, phenotypic variability, and mode of inheritance.8 The expert panel was then tasked with lumping or splitting these disorders using published, replicated information on these topics. Two or more disorders caused by variants in the same gene were kept separate, or split, when the differences between the disorders were likely explained by differences in the mechanism of the variant’s effect, differences in the phenotypic spectrum of disease, or differences in inheritance pattern. Clinical severity alone was not used as justification for splitting two diseases, identified by the ISDS nosology, which share a potentially causal gene.

Following precuration, each GDR was evaluated using ClinGen’s semi-quantitative framework.2 This process involved a literature search using sources including PubMed (National Institutes of Health), GoogleScholar (Google LLC), and Web of Science (Clarivate). Other sources crucial to the curation process were: Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM),9 the Ontology Lookup Service (OLS; CORBEL grant 654248), and Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI).10 Points were given to data from these sources according to the strength of the evidence provided. This was done in accordance with ClinGen’s gene-disease validity standard operating procedures (https://clinicalgenome.org/docs/gene-disease-validity-standard-operating-procedure/).

Definitive curations, in addition to reaching 12 total points scored, required replication over time (more than two publications with convincing evidence over three years after the initial publication). A Strong classification is reached when a gene-disease pair results in a score greater than or equal to 12 points, but has not been replicated over time.

Evidence supporting a GDR is divided into two categories: genetic evidence and experimental evidence. Genetic evidence involves case-level data and considers phenotypic specificity and variant causality presented in each reported case. Segregation information is also considered for diseases that follow Mendelian inheritance. Experimental evidence involves functional assays and animal models that support the role of the gene in the related disorder or in phenotypic features associated with the GDR of interest.

Genetic evidence was scored following ClinGen’s standard operating procedure for gene curation (v7-v10; each curation is labeled with the SOP version used). Variants were upgraded by 0.5 points from the default based on evidence such as de novo status of the variant or functional evidence supporting impact of the variant. The Skeletal GCEP also awarded point upgrades of 0.5 points for cases with radiographs that showed phenotypic specificity supporting the variant’s impact. A scoring upgrade based on radiographic evidence is done following review of strong evidence presenting features highly consistent with disease, and differentiating the GDR from others in the differential diagnoses. This is especially important given the overlapping features that can be observed between separate disease entities. For missense and other non-null variants (default 0.1 points), the first of these upgrades was given 0.4 points, for a total of 0.5 points. Subsequent point upgrades for that variant were awarded 0.5 points (Table 1).

Point upgrades were also given in cases presenting with collagenopathies caused by variants that affect glycine residues within the triple helical domain of COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL2A1 (Table 1). Glycine residues at every 3rd position within the triple helical domain (i.e., Gly-X-Y) of alpha chains are important for collagen polypeptide assembly into triple helices.11,12 Therefore, in addition to upgrades due to de novo status and functional evidence, variant scores were upgraded by 0.4 if they altered a glycine residue in the triple helix.

Gene curations were presented to the Skeletal GCEP via monthly video conference. Scoring and classifications were discussed and modified, as needed. Reasons for adjusting scores included the Skeletal GCEP reviewing the consistency of published radiographs with the proposed condition, the variant detection methodology, the strength of a cell-based assay, and/or the phenotype match of an animal model.

Evidence summaries were written after each video conference for each GDR based on assessment by the Skeletal GCEP. These evidence summaries, along with GDR strength classifications, were published to the ClinGen website. ClinGen curations are updated periodically per ClinGen’s gene recuration policy (https://clinicalgenome.org/site/assets/files/2164/clingen_standard_gene-disease_validity_recuration_procedures_v1.pdf). To find the most current information please visit https://search.clinicalgenome.org/kb/affiliate/10065?page=1&size=25&search=.

The Skeletal GCEP considered the ISDS nosology with the ClinGen Lumping and Splitting guidelines to determine the number of GDRs per gene that were within the Skeletal GCEP’s scope (Figure 1).5,6,8

Published data indicated many skeletal disorders have locus and allelic heterogeneity. For example, osteogenesis imperfecta has been associated with variants in >20 different genes and for one of these genes (COL1A1),12 more than 300 different disease-causing variants have been observed.13 Moreover, since patients with the same variant can exhibit a range of signs and symptoms, categorically assigning a gene-phenotype relationship can be challenging. Yet categorical diagnoses are helpful for clinicians, patients, and families. SLC26A2 is associated with achondrogenesis type 1B, a fetal/perinatal lethal condition, and multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED), a non-lethal condition. Despite being caused by variants in the same gene, these disorders’ morphologic features and clinical prognosis are distinctly different, as defined by the ISDS nosology. Homozygosity for null SLC26A2 variants cause achondrogenesis type 1B, homozygosity for hypomorphic variants (e.g., p.Arg279Trp) cause MED, and compound heterozygosity for hypomorphic and null alleles is seen in the intermediate severity disorders diastrophic dysplasia and atelosteogenesis.14,15 While diplotype combination may be indicative of phenotypic presentation and/or severity, there is an overlapping phenotypic spectrum with histological and radiographic similarities between clinical presentations. Based on these similarities, SLC26A2-related achondrogenesis, SLC26A2-related atelosteogenesis, SLC26A2-related diastrophic dysplasia, and SLC26A2-related multiple epiphyseal dysplasia were lumped as a single GDR, autosomal recessive SLC26A2-related skeletal dysplasia.

Another example of variants in the same gene causing clinically distinct phenotypes, per the ISDS nosology, that were lumped into a single GDR is COMP-related pseudoachondroplasia and COMP-related multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (COMP-MED). While some evidence suggests genotype-phenotype correlations,16, there is no clear locus specificity of variation within the gene to determine a clear genotype-phenotype correlation. Additionally, there is substantial phenotypic overlap between the two disease entities. Thus, during precuration, literature representing all phenotypes associated with this gene were compiled for the curation of COMP-related skeletal dysplasia.

Some variants do not affect the gene product, but the pattern of expression of the gene product. Loss-of-function coding variants in SOX9 are common in campomelic dysplasia, while non-coding deletions that reduce expression are seen in Pierre Robin sequence17–19, and non-coding rearrangements that co-opt SOX9 regulatory elements to drive nearby gene expression have been reported in Cooks syndrome (anonychia with hypoplasia of distal phalanges).20,21 For these distinctly recognized skeletal disorders with identifiable mechanistic differences, the expert panel chose to split these disorders as separate GDRs.

The Skeletal GCEP chose to lump brachyolmia type 3, Spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, Kozlowski type (SMD-K), spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia, Maroteaux type (SEMD-M), and metatropic dysplasia into a single curation, termed TRPV4-related skeletal dysplasia. This was done on the basis of shared inheritance patterns and molecular mechanisms of disease, namely gain-of-function of the calcium channel leading to a dysregulated response to mechanical loading the altered expression of genes involved in endochondral ossification.22
TRPV4 was also curated by the Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) GCEP, resulting in a Definitive curation of TRPV4-related neuromuscular disease (2021; publicly available on the ClinGen website).

ALPL-related hypophosphatasia was split based on mode of inheritance, mechanism of disease, and clinical severity. ALPL-related AD hypophosphatasia (dominant-negative effect or haploinsufficiency) involves mild to moderate hypophosphatasia as well as odontohypophosphatasia. ALPL-related AR hypophosphatasia (loss-of-function) involves perinatal lethal, moderate, and severe hypophosphatasia as well as odontohypophosphatasia.

Lumping-splitting decisions for COL1A1 were made based on phenotypic spectrums, combined with shared modes of inheritance. Specifically, COL1A1-related osteogenesis imperfecta types 1, 2, 3, and 4 were lumped under a single GDR, COL1A1-related osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), due to clinical overlap. The only difference in molecular mechanism is that OI type 1 is caused by haploinsufficiency leading to reduced type 1 collagen, whereas types 2, 3, and 4 are caused by a dominant-negative effect altering type 1 collagen. Based primarily on phenotypic overlap and mode of inheritance, classic type Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS), arthrochalasia type EDS, and combined OI and EDS were all lumped as COL1A1-related Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. Finally, COL1A1-related Caffey disease was kept split as its own GDR due to distinct clinical features and specific variants causing disease.

Decisions for COL1A2 were made similarly. OI types 1, 2, 3, and 4 were lumped under COL1A2-related osteogenesis imperfecta, with the same mechanistic distinctions as described above. COL1A2-related Ehlers-Danlos syndrome includes arthrochalasia type as well as combined OI-EDS, although COL1A2-related Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, cardiac valvular type was split separately due to autosomal recessive inheritance and a distinct mechanism: heterozygous variants leading to skipping of exon 6, eliminating the N-proteinase cleavage site necessary for proper collagen processing in the arthrochalasia type, and biallelic loss-of-function variants in the cardiac valvular type.23

COL2A1 had the most split GDRs of any gene reviewed in this round of curation based on distinct clinical and radiographic features.24
COL2A1-related achondrogenesis, type II, was split as its own GDR, noting primarily de novo missense variants causing disease. Kniest dysplasia was split as a unique GDR caused by exon skipping. Platyspondylic dysplasia, Torrance type, another GDR, is lethal and caused by variants in the C-propeptide of COL2A1. Variants in the C-propeptide of COL2A1 can also cause a clinically distinct, spondyloperipheral dysplasia which is non-lethal. COL2A1-related non-lethal spondyloepiphyseal dysplasias are associated with missense variants that cause dominant-negative effects. These include spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia congenita, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia with metatarsal shortening, and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia with metaphyseal changes. COL2A1-related dysplasia of the proximal femoral epiphyses is also associated with missense variants causing a dominant-negative effect, but with distinct clinical presentations including Legg-Calve-Perthes disease, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, and Namaqualand hip dysplasia (osteoarthritis with mild chondrodysplasia). The seventh and final GDR distinguished for curation by this GCEP in relation to COL2A1 was Stickler syndrome type 1, caused by variants leading to haploinsufficiency.

Two GDRs involving FGFR3 (Muenke syndrome and Crouzon syndrome-acanthosis nigricans syndrome) were reviewed separately by ClinGen’s Craniofacial Malformations GCEP. Thanatophoric dysplasia types 1 and 2 were lumped as a single GDR, FGFR3-related thanatophoric dysplasia based on a shared inheritance pattern, gain-of-function mechanism of disease, and phenotypic spectrum. The rest of the curated GDRs related to FGFR3 were split from each other. Semidominant (SD) camptodactyly-tall stature-scoliosis-hearing loss (CATSHL) syndrome is caused by variants in FGFR3 with a dominant-negative effect. Coupled with distinct clinical features, this GDR was kept separate from others. Hypochondroplasia, achondroplasia, and severe achondroplasia-developmental delay-acanthosis nigricans (SADDAN) syndrome were also all split and kept separate based on distinct differences in clinical presentation. In SADDAN, not only are the specific variants different than in achondroplasia, but the clinical phenotype is accompanied by moderate to severe intellectual disability,25 while cognitive skills are normal in patients with achondroplasia.26 Hypochondroplasia is also not only caused by distinct variants, but despite a milder skeletal phenotype, it is thought to be associated with a higher incidence of intellectual disability and epilepsy than in achondroplasia.27,28

In summary, the expert panel reviewed published data and considered the patients’ clinical and radiographic features, the likely mechanisms of variants’ effects of the associated gene, and inheritance pattern when deciding whether to lump reported phenotypes into one or split them and keep them separate.

The summary of each curation is publicly available on the ClinGen website (https://clinicalgenome.org/affiliation/40065/). Of the 26 GDRs, 22 (84.6%) were Definitive, 2 (7.7%) were Moderate, and 2 (7.7%) were Limited (Table 2). As of this publication, the Skeletal GCEP has not classified any GDRs as Strong, Disputed, Refuted, or No Known Disease Relationship. The 22 GDRs with Definitive evidence are largely driven by well-documented cases in the literature. GDRs with Moderate or Limited evidence were due to there being fewer or less well-documented cases. Animal and/or cell models with similar variants or types of variation were available for 19 (73.1%) GDRs. Both Moderate curations (FGFR3-related SADDAN and FGFR3-related CATSHL) had available models.29–32 Of the two Limited curations, SOX9-related Pierre Robin sequence had a scorable model while SOX9-related Cooks syndrome did not have a scorable model.17

The three assessed autosomal recessive GDRs were Definitive while the one assessed semidominant GDR, Camptodactyly-tall stature-scoliosis-hearing loss syndrome, was Moderate. The aggregate classification scores used to assess the clinical validity of these GDRs are shown in Figure 2. Of note, FGFR3 and SD camptodactyly-tall stature-scoliosis-hearing loss syndrome reached 6.1 points, which falls between the limited (1-6) and moderate (7-11) cutoffs. The classification was rounded up per expert consensus to be Moderate.

The Skeletal GCEP awarded upgraded genetic evidence scores in cases of de novo inheritance, functional evidence, radiographic evidence, and/or variants occurring in the tripeptide repeats of COL1A1, COL1A2, COL2A1 (Table 1). For example, curation of COL2A1 and spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia includes a missense variant in COL2A1, c.2974A>G (p.Arg992Gly). This variant has a default score of 0.1 points and is initially upgraded by 0.4 points due to de novo occurrence with confirmed paternity/maternity.11 The variant, now worth 0.5 points, receives a subsequent upgrade of 0.5 points for published radiographs that are consistent with the disease.11 This missense variant, originally 0.1 points, is now scored for 1 point. Similar variants with functional data supporting the mechanism of disease or occurring at a Gly-X-Y tripeptide repeat in COL1A1, COL1A2, or COL2A1 could have warranted an additional upgrade of 0.5 points. Variants that are not predicted or proven to produce a null protein cannot be upgraded beyond 1.5 points.

The Skeletal GCEP curated a total of 22 Definitive GDRs (Table 2), each reaching a score greater than or equal to 12 points using the ClinGen framework. Definitive curations in the Skeletal GCEP included many of the well-recognized skeletal dysplasias, such as ALPL-related hypophosphatasia (AR severe, AR moderate, AD moderate, and AD mild), FGFR3-related thanatophoric dysplasia (type I and type II), FGFR3-related achondroplasia, FGFR3-related hypochondroplasia, COMP-related skeletal dysplasia (COMP-related pseudoachondroplasia and COMP-MED), SLC26A2-related skeletal dysplasia (SLC26A2-related atelosteogenesis type 2, SLC26A2-related diastrophic dysplasia, and SLC26A2-related achondrogenesis type 1B), SOX9-related campomelic dysplasia, and many skeletal collagenopathies. Definitive skeletal collagenopathies included osteogenesis imperfecta (types I-IV), COL2A1-related spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia congenita (part of the lumped entity COL2A1-related spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia), COL2A1-related achondrogenesis type II, COL2A1-related Kniest dysplasia, COL2A1-related Torrance-type platyspondylic dysplasia, COL2A1-related spondyloperipheral dysplasia, COL2A1-related spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia (spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia with marked metaphyseal changes), and COL2A1-related Stickler syndrome. All of these skeletal collagenopathies were attributed to causative variants in COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL2A1.

The Skeletal GCEP curated a total of 2 Moderate GDRs, with scores ranging from 6.1-11 points of genetic and experimental evidence (Table 2). Moderate GDRs include FGFR3-related SADDAN syndrome and FGFR3-related CATSHL syndrome. FGFR3-related SADDAN and CATSHL syndromes, with 4 and 2.6 points of genetic evidence respectively, had Moderate GDRs primarily from too few scorable case reports. The curations for both FGFR3-related SADDAN syndrome and FGFR3-related CATSHL syndrome included relevant animal models.

Two gene-disease pairs were found to have Limited evidence (0-6 points) to support the relationship by the Skeletal GCEP (Table 2). These Limited curations, SOX9-related isolated Pierre Robin sequence (PRS) as well as SOX9-related Cooks syndrome, were classified as such due to too few cases reported in the current literature. For example, deletions in the non-coding region of SOX9, causing reduced expression of SOX9 and KCNJ2,17,18 have been identified in patients with PRS and segregated with disease in affected family members, first reported in relation to SOX9 in 2009.17,33,34 Without additional clinical cases associated with independently arising SOX9 non-coding variants scored by the GCEP, this GDR could not be upgraded past Limited. Similarly, for Cooks syndrome, large duplications at the 17q24.3 locus involving the coding regions of KCNJ2 and KCNJ16, as well as the regulatory region of SOX9, have been reported in patients and were reported to segregate with disease in affected family members.21,35–37 Due to the multigenic involvement of these reports, scorable evidence for this GDR was scarce. SOX9-related Cooks syndrome also lacked an animal model and substantial experimental evidence to support the GDR. Importantly, failure to achieve Moderate, Strong, or Definitive classification does not mean there is no GDR; instead, it means that the strength of the GDR is not currently sufficient to recommend that GDR’s inclusion in a diagnostic testing panel. It is important to note that SOX9-related campomelic dysplasia reached a Definitive classification and should not be excluded from diagnostic testing panels.

After evaluating ALPL, COL1A1, COL1A2, COL2A1, COMP, FGFR3, SLC26A2, SOX9, and TRPV4, the Skeletal GCEP identified 22 Definitive, 2 Moderate, and 2 Limited GDRs. These classifications support inclusion of ~92% of these GDRs in diagnostic testing panels, which is consistent with these being some of the most common and well-studied genetic causes of skeletal dysplasia. The two Limited GDRs, SOX9-related isolated Pierre Robin sequence and SOX9-related Cooks syndrome have less published case literature and were further restrained because their variation occurred in non-coding regions of SOX9,which are difficult to interpret. The two moderate GDRs can be attributed to incidence of disease being less common than the Definitive skeletal dysplasias.

At least one Definitive GDR association was seen for each of the first nine genes chosen, with several of these genes being Definitively associated with multiple diseases. However, there were four GDRs for which only a Limited or Moderate score was achieved. These latter conditions were uncommon in comparison to the rest of the disorders reviewed in the round of curation. Since the ClinGen gene recuration policy is to revisit curations every 2 or 3 years, it is important that clinicians and scientists continue publishing and sharing data, especially for GDRs scored as Limited or Moderate.

Some of the COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL2A1 curations were at risk of Moderate classifications, despite the well-characterized nature of these GDRs. Hence, it was critical to upgrade the scores of the Gly-X-Y variants rather than counting them as “typical” missense variants resulting in lower cumulative scores. However, with the modified scoring, 12 points of genetic evidence were awarded in most cases, resulting in Definitive curations. This discrepancy highlights the importance of the flexibility of ClinGen’s curation framework to remain adaptable to specific clinical domains, and again the need for continued published cases and/or functional assays of well-known GDRs.

The debate between lumping or splitting skeletal disorders is long-standing. Dr. Victor McKusick’s presentation at the First Conference on the Clinical Delineation of Birth Defects (1968) and his subsequent manuscript advocated for considering lumping and splitting in genetic nosology: lumping in connection with pleiotropism; and splitting in connection with genetic heterogeneity.38 This argument is reflected in the well-reputed and long-standing nosology of hereditary skeletal disorders.5,6 This classification has been regularly updated by experts in the field of skeletal dysplasia since its inception more than half a century ago (the original Nosology meeting took place in 1969). This nosology is widely followed by clinicians and researchers around the world and remains critical for the diagnosis and management of clinically distinct entities. The body of this work examined the need to provide clinical laboratories with guidance on the nine genes in which variants cause the most frequently observed skeletal dysplasias. The results of this study are aimed at indicating the spectrum of disease that variation in these genes may cause while still allowing for clinically distinct diagnoses to be made. Based primarily on overlapping phenotypic variability, molecular mechanisms, and modes of inheritance, the Skeletal GCEP chose to lump more GDRs per gene than is described in the ISDS nosology. The ISDS Nosology has tried to address similar questions as the current Skeletal GCEP but not in such a systematic way. Many members of the Skeletal GCEP are also members of the Nosology committee. They largely agree that the Skeletal GCEP constitutes a logical and welcome evolution in the establishment of gene curations that shall provide a solid basis for a clinical working framework. On the other hand, such meticulous and agnostic curation work is laborious; the present first paper of the Skeletal GCEP includes nine genes, while the Nosology includes over 500 genes.

An updated version of the skeletal dysplasias nosology was released in May 2023.6 Many members of the Skeletal GCEP led the effort to write the updated nosology, which details the dyadic approach to disease naming (i.e., COL1A1-related Osteogenesis Imperfecta).39,40 Future work in the ClinGen Skeletal GCEP will involve updating curations with the context provided by the updated nosology and following the dyadic approach to disease names, which will be reflected in published curations.

The Skeletal GCEP will continue curation with the next series of genes focusing on treatable conditions. Round two includes 35 genes for genetic causes of abnormal stature (e.g., ACAN-related skeletal disorders and overgrowth syndromes), bone fragility (e.g., osteopetroses), and highly locus heterogeneous disorders (e.g., ciliopathies with skeletal phenotypes).

The Skeletal GCEP evaluated nine genes for which variants have been associated with commonly encountered skeletal dysplasias. Among 26 GDRs involving these nine genes, 22 GDRs were Definitive, 2 Moderate, and 2 Limited. No GDR was classified as Strong, Disputed, Refuted, or No Known Disease Relationship. The recuration of non-Definitive GDRs, consistent with ClinGen’s policy, can strengthen such relationships as additional cases and experimental evidence is published. With over 550 genes implicated in causing skeletal disorders, ongoing work by the Skeletal GCEP will clarify which genes and diseases belong on diagnostic panels, which is critical when caring for patients with skeletal disorders.
