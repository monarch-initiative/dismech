# Computational modeling of ATM signaling: a predictive framework for drug repurposing in ataxia-telangiectasia.
**Authors:** Merulla AE, Di Salvatore V, Gullotta GS, Maleki A, Russo G, Caraci F, Copani A, Pappalardo F
**Journal:** NPJ Syst Biol Appl (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41540-025-00629-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41540-025-00629-4)

## Content

Ataxia-Telangiectasia (A-T) is a rare genetic disorder caused by ATM mutations, leading to impaired DNA repair, oxidative stress, and neurodegeneration. We developed a computational model of ATM-mediated signaling using ordinary differential equations in COPASI, capturing key processes including DNA damage sensing, cell cycle regulation, autophagy, and oxidative stress response. The model simulates physiological, ATM-deficient, and drug-treated conditions to explore repurposing strategies. We evaluated the effects of spermidine, omaveloxolone, and HDAC4 inhibition, revealing mechanisms by which these compounds modulate dysfunctional signaling. Sensitivity and stability analyses confirmed the model's robustness, while enrichment analysis validated involvement of key pathways. Our results highlight the synergistic potential of combining autophagy activation and epigenetic modulation to partially restore homeostasis in ATM-deficient cells. This work introduces a generalizable modeling framework for simulating disease-specific signaling dysfunction and identifying therapeutic interventions, illustrating the value of computational systems biology in rare disease drug repurposing.

Ataxia-Telangiectasia (A-T) is a genetic disorder with an autosomal recessive manner, typically manifesting in early childhood with a severe prognosis. Symptoms often emerge by the age of two, primarily presenting as progressive cerebellar ataxia, which leads to difficulties in walking, balance, speech, and eye movement coordination. In addition, individuals with A-T may also experience several clinical manifestations other than neurological that aggravate over time, such as immunodeficiency, resulting in frequent respiratory infections, increased cancer susceptibility, growth impairments, skin and corneal telangiectasias, and insulin-resistant diabetes. These complications significantly reduce life expectancy, typically to the second or third decade of life1–3. Despite a worldwide prevalence between 1:40,000 and 1:100,000 newborns, A-T is the most common genetic ataxia with onset in the first decade of life4.

A-T is due to different pathological variants of the ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) gene, which encodes a ubiquitously expressed serine/threonine kinase belonging to the superfamily of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-related kinases (PIKKs). ATM regulates several essential pathways for cellular homeostasis, including DNA double-strand break (DSB) repair, cell cycle control and chromatin structure modification. It phosphorylates more than 1000 substrates5,6. As a key DNA damage sensor, ATM activates a complex network of downstream effectors involved in cell cycle checkpoints and DNA repair in response to DSBs6.

Beyond its nuclear functions, ATM also regulates critical cytoplasmic activities, particularly in postmitotic cells such as neurons7–10. Degeneration of cerebellar Purkinje cells underlies the motor dysfunctions observed in A-T patients and constitutes one of the first clinical signs of the disease2. Although the precise molecular mechanisms leading to neurodegeneration remain unclear, the cytoplasmic roles of ATM provide new insights into neurodegenerative processes7. Cytoplasmic ATM is associated with mitochondria and peroxisomes, the primary cellular sources of reactive oxygen species (ROS)8,11, where it regulates their activity and homeostasis. ROS can activate ATM12, which in turn triggers protective pathways by increasing pexophagy and autophagy, primarily through the inhibition of mTOR Complex 1 (mTORC1)13. Consequently, ATM deficiency elevates mitochondrial respiration rates and increases hydrogen peroxide levels in the cerebella of ATM-deficient (atm−/−) mice6,14,15. Similarly to ROS, ATM-mediated mTORC1 inhibition is also promoted under hypoxic conditions12,16. In addition, loss of ATM leads to the aberrant expression of cell cycle proteins in Purkinje cells and striatal neurons of both A-T subjects and ATM-deficient mice17. Neurons attempting to re-enter the cell cycle are significantly more susceptible to death when challenged with inflammatory or hypoxic stimuli18. Another mechanism leading to neurodegeneration involves HDAC4, a histone deacetylase with key roles in brain development and neuron survival, abundantly expressed in neurons where it is predominantly found in the cytoplasm in a phosphorylated form. In physiological conditions, the HDAC4 phosphatase 2A (PP2A) is downregulated via ATM-mediated phosphorylation. ATM deficiency then results in the enhanced activity of PP2A that leads to the dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation of HDAC4 where, through histones H3 and H4 deacetylation, suppresses neuronal gene expression. Amelioration of neurodegeneration and behavioral abnormalities of ATM-deficient mice is obtained by blocking either HDAC4 activity or its nuclear translocation; however, the full rescue requires the presence of phosphorylated HDAC4 in the cytoplasm, highlighting the crucial aspect of HDAC4 localization in neurons19. Recent studies suggest a key role of microglia in the pathophysiology of neurodegeneration in A-T20–22. Astrocytes from cerebella of ATM-deficient mice promote neurotoxicity by increasing oxidative stress20, whereas microglia show increased migration, impaired phagocytosis, and secretion of neurotrophic factors22. Data from A-T patients using pseudotime analysis on single nuclei RNA-sequencing confirmed activation of an intrinsic neurotoxic program in microglia contributing to neurodegeneration in A-T.

The extensive molecular network regulated by ATM, combined with its widespread expression, underscores the complexity of A-T. Despite significant research efforts, an approved treatment for this disorder remains unavailable.

Modeling and simulation of biochemical networks is a powerful resource for a better understanding of these systems. The interactions and dynamics of biochemical networks contribute to the complexity of cellular responses. Given that the kinetics of most networks are too complex to be described analytically, the need for efficient simulation algorithms becomes paramount. These algorithms are instrumental in understanding and predicting how these networks behave under various external stimuli23,24. The exponential growth of biological, chemical, and clinical data is transforming drug development. Computational pharmacology, with its in silico techniques, is poised to better comprehend and predict how drugs affect biological systems, to optimize the clinical use, reduce side effects, and aid in developing more effective and safer therapies. One of the most promising applications of computational pharmacology is drug repurposing, which involves discovering new therapeutic applications for drugs that have already been approved for the treatment of other diseases25,26. This approach represents a powerful, time and cost-saving strategy to find new pharmacological targets and effective drugs to alleviate A-T symptoms.

Among computational tools suitable for addressing these challenges, COmplex PAthway SImulator (COPASI) emerges as a versatile and accessible software, providing robust capabilities for modeling and simulating biological systems, time-course analysis, and parameter sensitivity evaluations, thereby facilitating the development of predictive models to support drug repurposing strategies27,28. Additionally, its compatibility with standard formats 29–31. such as SBML (systems biology markup language), an XML-based language for communicating and storing computational models of biological processes32, facilitates seamless integration with other research platforms, making it a powerful, effective and accessible resource for systems biology scientists. Other approaches, such as stochastic models, capture molecular noise, particularly relevant in systems with low molecule counts33. Agent-based models simulate individual components and their spatial interactions, revealing emergent behaviors at the cellular or tissue level34. In parallel, machine learning approaches analyse high-dimensional data to uncover patterns without relying on predefined mechanistic assumptions35.

Leveraging these capabilities, this study aims to build a comprehensive computational model of the ATM-mediated signaling network, initially reconstructed under physiological conditions and subsequently adapted to simulate the pathological scenario of A-T. The goal is to accurately represent ATM signaling dynamics in both physiological and pathological contexts, enabling the identification and prediction of potential therapeutic interventions through drug repurposing. Specifically, we tested three distinct pharmacological strategies targeting key aspects of A-T pathogenesis: (i) spermidine, a casein-kinase 2 activator36, which is potentially able to enhance ATR-mediated compensatory signaling via the regulation of the ATR activation domain of TOPBP137; (ii) omaveloxolone, activating NRF2 to bolster cellular defences against oxidative stress; and (iii) a selective HDAC4 inhibitor approach aimed at counteracting neuronal gene dysregulation. By simulating these interventions within our COPASI model, this work provides a predictive framework for assessing drug efficacy, accelerating the identification of promising repurposed drugs for treating Ataxia-Telangiectasia.

Analysis of the ATM signaling pathway using KEGG PATHWAY, integrated with cell cycle regulation maps, revealed significant interconnected mechanisms, including p53-mediated pathways involved in apoptosis and cell cycle control. Given the pivotal role of p53 in cellular stress responses, these insights also suggest a potential link between ATM signaling and pathways associated with oxidative stress and autophagy. Specifically, p53 may influence genes implicated in autophagic degradation processes and the detoxification of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby contributing to cellular homeostasis under stress conditions.

Our computational model provides a comprehensive framework for exploring molecular interactions central to the pathogenesis of A-T. Employing ordinary differential equation (ODE)-based simulations, we quantitatively assessed cellular dynamics involved in DNA damage detection, repair mechanisms, and survival regulation. The simulations considered three distinct biological scenarios: normal physiological conditions, pathological conditions characterized by ATM deficiency, and the cellular response under potential pharmacological interventions able to rescue ATM activity. This modeling approach enables an integrative understanding of both disease mechanisms and therapeutic strategies.

Inhibition of HDAC4 resulted initially in a marked increase in DNA damage, subsequently stabilizing over time (Fig. 3a). This pattern suggests partial compensatory action by ATR signaling in the absence of ATM. HDAC4 inhibition notably enhanced p53 activation, accompanied by increased p21 levels, facilitating cell cycle arrest and promoting DNA repair. Moderate elevations in pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and PUMA were observed, indicating possible initiation of apoptosis under conditions of persistent or severe damage (Fig. 3b). Enhanced autophagy activation was evident, CK2 kinase levels remained consistently stable, and NRF2 showed mild fluctuations, supporting its regulatory involvement in cellular stress responses (Fig. 3c). Treatment with omaveloxolone led to sustained activation of ATR signaling, resulting in a gradual and consistent reduction of DNA damage (Fig. 3d). This observation highlights the role of ATR and related compensatory pathways in promoting DNA repair in the absence of ATM. Omaveloxolone strongly activated p53, subsequently inducing increased p21 expression, whereas BAX and PUMA showed moderate activation, reflecting a carefully regulated apoptotic response (Fig. 3e). Autophagy activation was slightly elevated, CK2 remained stable, and NRF2 exhibited moderate activation (Fig. 3f), further underscoring its protective role in managing oxidative stress and cellular damage.

Spermidine treatment markedly activated autophagy, driving a decrease in DNA damage levels (Fig. 3i, g). Additionally, p53 activation and p21 expression increased significantly, accompanied by moderate increases in BAX and PUMA (Fig. 3h). These results suggest a balanced cellular response between promoting repair via cell cycle arrest and initiating apoptosis when necessary. Importantly, TOPBP1 displayed pronounced activation, highlighting its critical role in facilitating the DNA damage response and checkpoint control. CK2 kinase remained stable, and NRF2 showed slight activation (Fig. 3g). Overall, these findings suggest that spermidine effectively promotes autophagy as an alternative and complementary mechanism to manage cellular damage and preserve cell viability.

Collectively, these findings highlight the central regulatory role of ATM in orchestrating the DNA damage response, while ATR functions as a secondary pathway capable of partially compensating in ATM-deficient conditions. The diminished activation of p53 and its downstream pro-apoptotic targets in the absence of ATM underscores a compromised ability to effectively balance cell survival and programmed cell death.

All three Lyapunov exponents calculated for the model are negative, which indicates that the system is dynamically stable. In such systems, small perturbations to initial conditions decay exponentially over time, rather than growing or oscillating unpredictably. This means that the system will naturally return to equilibrium following minor internal or external fluctuations, maintaining its overall behavior over time.

The average divergence, computed as the sum of all Lyapunov exponents, was −12.4979, a notably highly negative value. This reinforces the conclusion that the system possesses a strong damping capability: perturbations introduced during simulation are rapidly suppressed, ensuring robust convergence toward the model’s stable attractor. This property is especially important in biological models, where resilience to fluctuations ensures that the simulated processes remain physiologically realistic over time.

To further assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a comprehensive Latin hypercube sampling (LHS)–based analysis across six key model parameters (Autophagy_inactive, CHK1_inactive, CHK2_inactive, NRF2_inactive, p53_inactive, and TOPBP1_inactive) for each of the three pharmacological models. Using 300–1000 sampled parameter sets per model, we evaluated how these uncertainties affect the behavior of DNA_damage, our primary output phenotype.

For each treatment, we present two representative figures: a scatter plot illustrating the relationship between each parameter and DNA_damage, and a correlation heatmap summarizing global sensitivity trends. These visualizations confirm that the drug effects on DNA_damage are robust to parameter perturbations. Notably, CHK1 inhibition emerged as a dominant driver of DNA repair across all interventions, while other parameters exhibited more subtle, context-specific effects. This systematic analysis supports the biological plausibility and model-invariant nature of our conclusions.

We first developed a comprehensive computational model of ATM-mediated signaling to elucidate the molecular underpinnings of A-T; then, we systematically explored therapeutic strategies through drug repurposing. Our findings underscore ATM pivotal role in coordinating DNA damage responses and demonstrate that pharmacological interventions targeting specific molecular pathways can partially restore cellular homeostasis under ATM-deficient conditions.

Our model highlights ATM as the central sensor orchestrating cellular responses to DNA damage, effectively integrating DNA repair mechanisms, oxidative stress responses, autophagy activation, and apoptosis. In physiological conditions, ATM rapidly initiates damage detection and triggers efficient downstream repair processes, prominently activating p53 and its regulatory network. In contrast, pathological simulations representing ATM deficiency revealed compromised cellular responses characterized by sustained DNA damage accumulation, attenuated p53 activation, impaired apoptotic signaling, diminished autophagic processes, and ineffective oxidative stress management. These computational insights closely align with experimental observations in A-T models, where impaired ATM activity is associated with increased genome instability, defective DNA repair, and heightened susceptibility to oxidative damage and neurodegeneration14,15,17.

ATR signaling emerged from our model as a secondary compensatory pathway activated in the absence of ATM. However, consistent with previous experimental reports6, ATR only partially compensates for ATM’s regulatory functions. Reduced activation of p53-dependent apoptotic mediators, such as BAX and PUMA, remains a critical vulnerability in cells lacking functional ATM.

To systematically identify promising therapeutic interventions, our computational simulations explored three distinct pharmacological strategies: HDAC4 inhibition, NRF2 activation via omaveloxolone, and autophagy induction and control of the DNA damage response by using spermidine. Specifically, since there is no selective HDAC4 inhibitor approved for human use, we modeled HDAC4 inhibition. Omaveloxolone was recently approved for Friedreich’s ataxia. Spermidine is an endogenous polyamine with multiple functions currently sold as a supplement.

Each intervention demonstrated unique regulatory effects, collectively enhancing aspects of the DNA damage response compromised in ATM deficiency.

HDAC4 inhibition notably increased p53 activation, augmenting cell cycle arrest mediated by p21 and promoting more effective repair pathways. Moreover, moderate activation of pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and PUMA suggests a selective induction of apoptosis in irreparably damaged cells, aligning with recent literature demonstrating the neuroprotective potential of HDAC4 inhibition in ATM-deficient models19. The moderate yet consistent enhancement of autophagy under HDAC4 inhibition further supports its multifaceted therapeutic potential.

Spermidine treatment robustly activated autophagy, resulting in significant reductions in accumulated DNA damage. This finding reinforces emerging evidence supporting autophagy enhancement as a powerful strategy to counteract neurodegenerative processes, protein misfolding, and oxidative stress commonly associated with ATM-deficient states13,15. Spermidine’s capacity to induce balanced activation of both repair and apoptosis pathways, combined with increased TOPBP1 activation, underscores its potential utility in managing ATM-deficiency.

Omaveloxolone, acting primarily through NRF2 activation, effectively reduced oxidative stress markers in our simulations, consistent with experimental data in oxidative stress-related diseases38. However, compared to HDAC4 inhibition and spermidine, NRF2 activation alone appeared less capable of fully compensating for ATM loss. This partial efficacy may reflect NRF2’s targeted role predominantly in oxidative stress modulation rather than direct DNA repair activation, suggesting it may be more beneficial as an adjunctive rather than stand-alone therapy.

The complementary strengths and limitations of these interventions highlight the promising potential for combinational therapeutic strategies. Specifically, combining spermidine's cell stability-promoting effects with HDAC4 inhibition could synergistically restore a more robust DNA damage response, significantly improving therapeutic outcomes in A-T. Future studies are warranted to experimentally validate this computationally predicted synergy, particularly in neural culture models derived from ATM mutant mice.

The robustness and stability of our model were confirmed through extensive sensitivity and stability analyses. Sensitivity analyses identified ATM and ATR signaling pathways as critical regulatory nodes significantly influencing DNA damage sensing and response dynamics. Moderate sensitivity of NRF2 and HDAC4 further supports their potential as therapeutic targets. Stability analysis using Lyapunov exponents demonstrated robust and reliable model behavior, ensuring that predicted therapeutic responses are both reproducible and resilient to parameter fluctuations. This robustness reinforces the confidence in our model's predictive capability, making it an invaluable tool for further drug repurposing studies.

ORA results further corroborated our computational predictions, emphasizing DNA double-strand break repair, homologous recombination, and cell cycle checkpoint regulation pathways as critically disrupted in A-T. These findings provide additional biological validity to our computational model, further underscoring the clinical relevance of identified therapeutic strategies targeting DNA repair and cell cycle checkpoints.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. Our model predominantly focuses on ATM canonical nuclear signaling pathways and their immediate downstream effectors. Future iterations could incorporate additional cytoplasmic and mitochondrial roles of ATM, particularly those relevant to ROS regulation, metabolism, and mitochondrial integrity.

Our approach differs from the one described in a recent manuscript by ref. 39, who focused on screening a broad drug library to identify hits potentially capable of restoring DNA damage response in induced pluripotent stem cells derived from A-T patients. That work prioritized compounds solely based on phenotypic rescue without mechanistic insight, whereas our computational model adopts a mechanism-aware selection and, in the end, is able to identify and prioritize candidates through the lens of target engagement and pathway-specific modulation tied to ATM loss.

While this study focuses on a mechanistic, ODE-based modeling approach implemented in COPASI to capture the dynamic behavior of intracellular signaling pathways, alternative computational strategies also play important roles in the field. Stochastic models account for the probabilistic nature of molecular interactions, especially relevant in systems with low molecule counts, while agent-based models simulate individual cellular components and their spatial interactions, offering insight into emergent behaviors at the cellular or tissue level. In parallel, data-driven approaches, particularly those based on machine learning, are increasingly used to analyze high-dimensional omics and imaging data, often revealing predictive patterns without requiring explicit mechanistic detail. Together, these diverse modeling frameworks offer complementary perspectives for investigating complex signaling processes, depending on the biological context and available data.

Our computational framework reinforces ATM’s central role in DNA damage responses and offers actionable therapeutic insights through drug repurposing strategies. The proposed combination therapy utilizing spermidine and HDAC4 inhibition is particularly promising, meriting further experimental validation. These findings exemplify the transformative potential of computational systems biology to accelerate drug discovery and therapeutic innovation, especially in complex genetic disorders like A-T.

One of the key limitations of any mechanistic model lies in its dependence on available biological knowledge. Our model was constructed through manual curation of literature, the KEGG database, and Over-Representation Analysis (ORA), focusing on the core components of ATM/ATR signaling, oxidative stress response, and downstream effectors such as p53. While this approach ensured biological plausibility and interpretability, we acknowledge that it represents a simplified subset of the full signaling network. To evaluate the robustness of our results, we performed both Time-Course and Steady-State sensitivity analyses, which highlighted the most influential parameters and components. These analyses suggest that key conclusions—particularly regarding the roles of ATM, ATR, NRF2, and p53—are robust to parameter variations within the current network topology.

Nevertheless, we recognize that more comprehensive models could be built by integrating extensive protein–protein interaction and regulatory network databases (e.g., OmniPath, SIGNOR, NDEx). Inclusion or exclusion of specific biomolecules or regulatory links could impact model dynamics and outputs. As future work, we intend to explore the effect of extending the network using such resources, and to assess whether our conclusions hold under expanded topologies using functional and structural perturbation analyses.

The computational model developed provides a comprehensive representation of the ATM-mediated signaling network, capturing critical molecular events involved in DNA damage sensing, repair mechanisms, apoptosis, autophagy, and oxidative stress regulation under physiological and ATM-deficient pathological conditions. Simulations underscored ATM pivotal regulatory function, revealing how its deficiency significantly compromises cellular homeostasis, rendering cells vulnerable to persistent DNA damage and impaired stress responses.

Systematic evaluation of pharmacological interventions, including HDAC4 inhibition, spermidine-induced autophagy and facilitation of DNA damage response, and omaveloxolone-driven NRF2 activation, demonstrated their differential effectiveness in enhancing DNA repair processes, modulating apoptosis, and alleviating oxidative stress. Of particular note, the combination of spermidine and HDAC4 inhibition emerged as especially promising, offering synergistic benefits by simultaneously boosting autophagic activity, DNA repair efficiency, and apoptosis regulation.

Collectively, these results highlight the utility and predictive accuracy of computational modeling in identifying viable drug repurposing strategies for complex genetic diseases such as A-T. Future experimental validation of these combinational strategies will be critical, potentially guiding the development of innovative drugs to improve clinical outcomes and patient quality of life.

In summary, we present a robust and extensible computational framework that mechanistically simulates ATM signaling dysfunction and predicts effective therapeutic interventions. By combining dynamic pathway modeling, rigorous sensitivity/stability evaluation, and data-driven pathway enrichment, we demonstrate how computational systems biology can meaningfully accelerate rare disease research. The proposed combinational therapy targeting autophagy and chromatin remodeling offers an experimentally testable strategy that may guide future clinical development. More broadly, our approach showcases the power of mechanistic in silico tools in the rational design of personalized therapies.

The methodological approach employed in this study integrates computational modeling and bioinformatics analyses to systematically investigate ATM-mediated signaling pathways. Initially, pathway reconstruction and analysis were conducted through literature mining and database interrogation to identify key molecular interactions involved in ATM signaling. Subsequently, a conceptual model of DNA damage response was constructed to delineate critical functional modules. This conceptual framework served as the basis for implementing an in silico kinetic model using COPASI software, simulating ATM signaling under physiological, pathological, and pharmacologically treated conditions. Lastly, sensitivity and stability analyses were performed to evaluate model robustness, assess system behavior, and ensure predictive reliability for therapeutic intervention simulations.

A network analysis was conducted starting from the ATM gene to identify potential pharmacological targets for pathway-based drug repurposing. The first phase involved collecting and analysing data from scientific literature to characterize the ATM mechanism of action and its associated signaling pathways.

Relevant literature was retrieved from major scientific search engines, particularly PubMed, using keywords such as ataxia telangiectasia, ATM pathway, ATM and ATR, ATM and DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, ATM and autophagy, and ATM and neurodegeneration1,19,40–45.

To further investigate ATM-related signaling pathways, the KEGG PATHWAY Database (KEGG) was utilized46. Developed by Kyoto University, KEGG is one of the most comprehensive bioinformatics resources, providing curated information on metabolic and cellular pathways. It is widely used in omics research, systems biology modeling, and translational drug development.

For this analysis, a specific focus was placed on ATM pathways, leading to the selection of the signaling pathway related to the cell cycle, identified as “map04110”.

Starting with the ATM as the central node, a phosphorylation cascade involving multiple substrates, organized into distinct layers, was identified. The first layer consists of molecules that are directly phosphorylated by ATM kinase activity, while subsequent layers contain molecules phosphorylated by those above them, creating a cascading effect that ultimately activates various metabolic processes. Under normal physiological conditions, this cascade ensures the maintenance of key cellular pathways, including, DNA damage repair, apoptosis, and cell cycle checkpoint control within canonical signaling pathways. Outside the canonical ATM signaling pathway, the phosphorylation cascade leads to the mechanisms associated with oxidative stress response and genomic integrity preservation40.

In parallel with the KEGG pathway analysis, an over-representation analysis (ORA) was performed on key genes associated with the biological modules of interest in this model. This analysis was conducted using the R programming language47, more specifically the ReactomePA package48, to examine gene involvement in biological pathways, and the STRINGdb package49 to construct protein interaction networks. ORA is a statistical method used to assess whether a specific set of genes is significantly enriched in predefined biological categories, such as pathways, gene ontologies, or functional annotations. By comparing the proportion of genes in the selected set that belong to a given category with the proportion expected by chance in the background population—typically using statistical tests like Fisher’s exact test—ORA helps identify overrepresented biological processes or pathways, offering valuable insights into their functional significance50. In particular, this analysis focused on a list of specific genes identified in the literature as particularly relevant to the disease model: ATM, ATR, TP53, BRCA1, BRCA2, CHEK1, CHEK2, NRF2, KEAP1, TOPBP1, CK2, HDAC4, P21, BAX, PUMA, LC3, and BECN1. A functional enrichment analysis was performed to identify overrepresented biological processes, molecular functions, and pathways associated with these genes, offering insights into their potential roles in the disease context.

To validate the model predictions, an additional over-representation analysis (ORA) was conducted using the most influential species identified through the computational model’s sensitivity analysis.

This included variables such as DNA_damage, p53_active, ATR_active, NRF2, HDAC4, and BAX, selected based on either their high global sensitivity index or their dynamic response under perturbed ATM/ATR conditions.

These model variables were manually mapped to corresponding gene symbols and submitted as input to the ReactomePAR package. The enrichPathway() function was used with default parameters and the Reactome database as reference. Pathways with an adjusted p value <0.05 were considered significantly enriched. This validation step confirmed the involvement of key mechanisms such as DNA double-strand break repair, TP53 regulation, and cell cycle checkpoint control, thus supporting the predictive validity of the model (see Fig. 4).

Building upon this conceptual framework, an in silico model was developed to accurately represent the complex interaction network of the ATM pathway. This computational approach is crucial in the context of drug repurposing, as it enables detailed insights into molecular interactions and aids in the identification of promising therapeutic targets.

COPASI, an open-source computational tool derived from Gepasi51 simulation software originally developed by Pedro Mendes, was employed for this purpose. COPASI is specifically designed to facilitate the construction and analysis of mathematical models of diverse biological systems, such as metabolic networks, signaling pathways, gene regulatory circuits, and dynamics of infectious diseases.

The key features of COPASI, particularly valuable for this study, include: (i) Model definition: models are specified as chemical reactions between molecular species governed by reaction-specific rate laws (ordinary differential equations, ODE). Additionally, compartments, events, and global variables can be incorporated to enhance the model’s complexity and realism. (ii) Analytical tasks: COPASI supports multiple analytical functions, including simulations and sensitivity analyses, essential for understanding system behaviors. (iii) Import and export capabilities: COPASI supports the import and export of models in SBML and Gepasi formats, and it can also export models as source code in the C programming language.

For simulations, various algorithms can be selected based on the modeling objectives. In this study, we utilized LSODA (Livermore solver for ordinary differential equations with automatic method switching for stiff and non-stiff problems), an established algorithm widely employed in simulating biological systems modeled through ordinary differential equations (ODEs). The simulations conducted used a time-course approach, enabling the evaluation of temporal dynamics and transformations of molecular species over the simulated period.

The computational model was designed to simulate three biologically distinct scenarios: (i) a physiological condition, depicting ATM signaling under normal cellular circumstances; (ii) a disease condition, representing pathological changes resulting from ATM deficiencies; and (iii) a drug intervention condition, evaluating the effects of therapeutic compounds. To specifically investigate drug repurposing potential, we incorporated in silico simulations of three therapeutic interventions: a CK2 activation by spermidine, a selective inhibition of HDAC4, and an NRF2 activation by omaveloxolone.

The developed model comprises 32 molecular species and 41 biochemical reactions, carefully selected to reflect the critical components of the DNA damage response network, particularly emphasizing ATM/ATR signaling cascades and their downstream targets.

The DNA damage response in our model is delineated into two primary signaling branches: (i) the ATM/CHK2 pathway, primarily activated by severe DNA lesions such as double-strand breaks, and (ii) the ATR/CHK1 pathway, primarily responding to replication stress and single-stranded DNA breaks. Both signaling cascades converge onto the tumor suppressor protein p53, a central regulator orchestrating cell cycle arrest and apoptosis.

The simulation framework integrates essential regulatory proteins—including HDAC4, PP2A, I2PP2A, TOPBP1, CK2, NRF2, and p21—that modulate signaling cascade dynamics and DNA repair efficiency. Within the model, DNA damage and subsequent repair processes are represented explicitly through a species termed DNA damage, which directly initiates ATM and ATR signaling activation. ATM promotes damage response by activating p53 and CHK2, while ATR increases CHK1 activity, reinforcing repair processes significantly enhanced by activated CHK1.

These interactions unfold within a complex regulatory landscape. HDAC4, modeled across multiple states (active, inactive, and phosphorylated), downregulates p53-dependent gene expression through p53 protein deacetylation.

Phosphorylation of HDAC4 by CK2 results in its cytoplasmic retention, decreasing its nuclear inhibitory function and thus modulating p53 transcriptional activity. Furthermore, the phosphatase PP2A activates HDAC4, while its inhibitor I2PP2A—whose expression is stimulated following ATM activation—opposes PP2A, providing an additional regulatory layer influencing both p53 activity and ATR signaling.

TOPBP1 is integral for ATR activation, and CK2 amplifies DNA damage signaling by phosphorylating both HDAC4 and TOPBP1, thereby enhancing ATR function. Additionally, NRF2—a crucial mediator of the oxidative stress response—is tightly controlled by KEAP1, which targets NRF2 for degradation under normal conditions. Upon DNA damage or oxidative stress, KEAP1 inhibition allows NRF2 stabilization, initiating protective transcriptional responses that facilitate DNA repair and bolster cell viability.

p21, a central downstream effector of activated p53, inhibits cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), enforcing cell cycle arrest to permit damage repair. In cases where damage surpasses repair capabilities, the model incorporates pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and PUMA to mediate programmed cell death.

To elucidate potential therapeutic strategies, our model explicitly incorporates the following modulations: spermidine-driven autophagy activation and ATR-mediated DNA damage response, NRF2 upregulation by omaveloxolone, and targeted HDAC4 inhibition under pathological conditions. These computational interventions provide valuable mechanistic insights into therapeutic modulation of DNA damage responses in disease states such as ataxia-telangiectasia (A-T).

The implementation of this in silico model utilized COPASI software, with a structured process beginning by defining the system’s compartments—including dimensionality, simulation type, and initial volume. Subsequently, molecular species and biochemical reactions were defined in accordance with biological evidence. Following reaction establishment, time-course simulations were conducted by specifying simulation duration and intervals, enabling detailed visualization of species concentrations and dynamic changes within the modeled biological system.

Parameter values employed in the model were derived through comparison with published data from analogous modeling studies. Kinetic rates for critical signaling molecules such as ATM, p53, and CHK1/2 were assigned within the range of 10⁻² to 10⁻¹ s⁻¹, consistent with those reported by Hu et al. (2022)52, in order to accurately reproduce the characteristic p53 pulsing dynamics in response to DNA damage. Corresponding binding, dissociation, and degradation rates also exhibit similar magnitudes, thereby supporting the biological plausibility and relevance of these parameters within ODE-based frameworks of the DNA damage response.

With regard to molecular concentrations, it is important to provide a clarification: due to the lack of absolute quantitative data necessary to directly calibrate molecular concentrations, we adopted a qualitative modeling approach. The model was therefore constructed to reproduce the known qualitative behaviors of the biological system, rather than to predict exact concentration values. The primary aim was to assess whether the network architecture could generate biologically plausible dynamic trends in response to DNA damage in the absence of ATM activity.

This modeling strategy—employing relative concentrations and focusing on qualitative dynamics—is widely accepted in systems biology, particularly when dealing with complex regulatory networks for which quantitative measurements are limited or unavailable.

It is important to emphasize that, when available, experimental data on the effective concentrations of a given compound can certainly be used to refine or scale the model; however, in our case, such data were not strictly required, as the study was qualitative in nature.

Accordingly, we defined initial molecular concentrations at 100 mol/L for species in their inactive states and 0 mol/L for active forms. This convention reflects the assumption that inactive species initially exist at full availability, which progressively transitions to active forms as reactions proceed. Consequently, active species concentrations increase from zero, dynamically mirroring the biological activation process.

Regarding the concentrations of the computationally tested drugs, the same rationale was adopted, supported by relevant scientific evidence:

Omaveloxolone is a neuroprotective agent that has shown significant effects in in vitro cellular models at concentrations in the low micromolar range (typically 10–40 μM)53. Similarly, spermidine exhibits biological activity at concentrations of a few micromolar: an in vitro study on T lymphocytes identified approximately 5 μM as the optimal concentration to enhance cell proliferation and immunological memory, while in a model of neuronal oxidative stress, doses around 10 μM significantly protected cells, with efficacy lost below 10 μM and no further benefit observed beyond ~20 μM54,55. These experimental data provide a general reference that aligns with our choice to use 20 mol/L in the model for both Omaveloxolone and spermidine, in terms of order of magnitude and relative scaling.

Since no selective HDAC inhibitor has been approved for human use so far, to model the effect of a potential HDAC4 inhibitor, the initial state of HDAC4_inactive, the default species, was set to 0.

To assess the robustness and dynamic behavior of the computational model, sensitivity and stability analyses were performed using COPASI. Sensitivity analysis investigates how variations in model parameters affect the system’s behavior, identifying key regulatory parameters that significantly influence system dynamics. Two complementary approaches were employed:I.Time-course sensitivity analysis (TCSA) evaluates how parameter variations influence model behavior over time. TCSA identifies parameters that significantly affect the dynamics of the system at specific time points, revealing transient behaviors and critical periods during which particular parameters exert maximal regulatory effects56.II.Steady-state sensitivity analysis (SASS) examines the impact of parameter variations when the system reaches equilibrium, i.e., when variables exhibit minimal or no change over time. This analysis pinpoints parameters crucial for maintaining steady-state conditions, distinguishing influential factors from those with negligible long-term effects57.

Steady-state sensitivity analysis (SASS) examines the impact of parameter variations when the system reaches equilibrium, i.e., when variables exhibit minimal or no change over time. This analysis pinpoints parameters crucial for maintaining steady-state conditions, distinguishing influential factors from those with negligible long-term effects57.

Together, TCSA and SASS provide a detailed understanding of the influence of specific parameters throughout the temporal evolution of the system, as well as at equilibrium, allowing identification of key regulatory nodes and informing predictions about system behavior under various conditions.

Stability analysis was conducted using the Lyapunov exponent (LE) method within COPASI to further evaluate the model’s resilience to perturbations. The Lyapunov exponent quantifies the rate at which trajectories diverge or converge in the system’s phase space, providing critical insights into system stability. A negative LE indicates system stability, where perturbations diminish over time, driving the model back toward equilibrium. Conversely, a positive LE would indicate potential instability, with small perturbations amplifying and potentially leading to chaotic dynamics. Thus, analyzing the LE helps determine whether the model reliably returns to equilibrium following perturbations or exhibits instability58.

By combining sensitivity and stability analyses, we ensured that our model robustly captures the complex dynamic behavior of ATM-mediated signaling, supporting its reliability for predicting therapeutic effects in simulations.

To further evaluate the robustness of model predictions regarding drug-induced DNA damage, we performed a systematic parameter scan using Latin hypercube sampling (LHS). For each pharmacological scenario (HDAC4 inhibition, NRF2 activation via omaveloxolone, and CK2 activation via spermidine), we independently sampled 300 to 1000 parameter sets across six key regulatory nodes: Autophagy_inactive, CHK1_inactive, CHK2_inactive, NRF2_inactive, p53_inactive, and TOPBP1_inactive. These were previously identified as the most influential variables in local sensitivity analyses. Other parameters were kept fixed to preserve biologically realistic reaction kinetics and initial conditions. For each sampled set, the model was simulated and the resulting DNA_damage trajectory recorded. We then computed parameter-wise scatter plots and Pearson correlation matrices to assess the consistency of drug effects across plausible biological variability.
