BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVE: BRCA1 and BRCA2 pathogenic germline variants (PGVs)
are associated with higher risk of prostate cancer (PC). The IMPACT study
evaluated the utility of targeted prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening in
BRCA1/BRCA2 PGV carriers. Here we report outcomes after five rounds of PSA
screening in IMPACT.
METHODS: Between 2005 and 2015, 3063 participants aged 40-69 yr (median 54 yr)
were recruited from 65 centres in 20 countries in two cohorts: (1) BRCA1/BRCA2
PGV carriers (915 BRCA1, 901 BRCA2); and (2) age-matched noncarriers for a
familial PGV (727 BRCA1 and 520 BRCA2 noncarriers). Annual PSA screening was
performed, with PSA >3.0 ng/ml used as the indication for prostate biopsy. Our
aim was to identify differences by PGV status in (1) the incidence of PC and of
clinically significant PC (csPC; grade group ≥2) and (2) tumour stage and
characteristics after five screening rounds.
KEY FINDINGS AND LIMITATIONS: There was no statistically significant difference
in PC incidence between BRCA1/BRCA2 PGV carriers and noncarriers. csPC incidence
was significantly higher for BRCA2 PGV carriers than for noncarriers (3.1% vs
1.3%; p = 0.04). Among men with PC, the proportion of tumours with National
Comprehensive Cancer Network intermediate unfavourable/high risk was higher in
the BRCA1/BRCA2 PGV groups versus the corresponding group without PGVs (BRCA2:
65% vs 32%, p = 0.029; BRCA1: 56% vs 18%, p = 0.0017). There were no T4 or
metastatic PC cases. Pathology after radical prostatectomy revealed tumour
upgrading for 7/23 (26%) BRCA1 PGV carriers and 10/34 (26%) BRCA2 PGV carriers,
with no tumour upgrading for men without PGVs. Study limitations include the
biopsy compliance rate and changes in PC diagnostic pathways since 2005.
CONCLUSIONS AND CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS: Annual PSA screening in BRCA2 PGV
carriers confirmed a higher incidence of csPC and detection of clinically
relevant tumours in comparison to noncarriers. For the first time, we confirm
that PSA screening in BRCA1 PGV carriers results in early detection of NCCN
IR-U/HR PC. Systematic PSA screening is recommended for BRCA2 PGV carriers and
should be considered for BRCA1 PGV carriers.

There is no international consensus on a general population prostate cancer (PrCa) screening programme due to the well documented limitations of screening using the prostate-specific antigen (PSA) test and concerns around overdiagnosis. However, PSA remains the most effective PrCa biomarker available[1–3]. Targeting screening at higher-risk individuals with a known genetic predisposition, could alter the risk/benefit balance of PSA-screening.

Individuals carrying a pathogenic germline variant (PGV) in BRCA2 have a higher risk of PrCa, with an estimated relative-risk of 2.5–8.6-fold by 65yrs[4–6]. BRCA2 is associated with early-onset, clinically-significant (CS) (defined as Gleason score ≧7/ ≧Grade Group 2 (GG)) PrCa, higher rates of local and distant metastasis, higher mortality and is an independent prognostic factor for poorer outcome[7–11]. There are mixed reports about risk associated with PGV in BRCA1[12,13]. The estimated relative-risk is 1.8–3.8-fold by 65yrs with reports of an association with CS-PrCa[7,14–16].

Research has demonstrated that BRCA1/BRCA2 carriers managed with active surveillance have a higher rate of upgrade than non-carriers[17]. However, these data are based on retrospective reports of non-screen detected disease. Early detection through screening, if successful, could impact the disease outcome in BRCA1/BRCA2 carriers.

The IMPACT study (NCT00261456) is an international study, evaluating the utility of targeted PrCa-screening for BRCA1/BRCA2 carriers that has previously reported outcomes after three years of screening[18]. The study reported that PSA-screening detected a high proportion of early-stage CS-PrCa, particularly in BRCA2 PGV carriers, and PSA is more predictive for PrCa in BRCA1/2 carriers than non-carriers[19]. Here we report five rounds of screening in all participants, the primary outcome of IMPACT, including incidence of PrCa and CS-PrCa and PrCa characteristics between BRCA1/2 cohorts.

The IMPACT study has been reported previously[18–20] (Supplementary Figure 1). The protocol received approval from the West-Midlands Research and Ethics Committee (reference:05/MRE07/25), and each participating institution received the required local ethical approval. Written consent was provided by all participants.

The study was powered to detect a two-fold PrCa risk over five years of screening with 80% power at the p-value <0.05 level. The target sample was 500 BRCA1, 350 BRCA2 PGV carriers, and a control group of 850 individuals tested negative for a familial BRCA1/2 PGV. Participants were aged 40–69yrs, had not had a previous PrCa diagnosis nor undergone a previous PB.

Participants underwent annual PSA-screening for five screening rounds. If PSA was >3.0ng/mL, PB was recommended. PB referral was based on a single PSA level, PSA was not repeated unless clinically indicated. Centres were requested to follow a standard biopsy protocol taking 10–12 cores. Participants with a benign PB returned to annual PSA-screening. A repeat PB was recommended if PSA was >50% of the pre-PB PSA (Supplementary Figure 1). Where PB detected PrCa, participants were treated in accordance with local guidelines. Cancers were defined using the International Society of Urological Pathology 2014 Grade Group (GG) definitions and GG≥2 (Gleason score≥7) deemed “clinically significant (CS)”. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines were used to categorise tumours into risk groups based on risk of metastasis[21].

Sixteen centres offered an optional PB after the fifth screening round to all participants who had not yet undergone a PB, irrespective of PSA value. This was termed the ‘end-of-study biopsy’ and the purpose was to evaluate incidence of PrCa in PSA levels <3.0ng/mL.

Analyses were stratified by BRCA1/BRCA2 PGV status. Proportions of screen-detected disease and positive predictive value (PPV) of PSA>3.0ng/mL, and PB were compared between groups using the Chi-squared test.

PrCa prevalence for participants with PSA>3.0ng/mL at first PSA test was calculated. Cumulative incidence was calculated at the fifth screening round, compared across PGV status using Poisson regression, offset by person-years of follow-up, adjusted for age, ethnicity, and country, to calculate incidence rate ratios (IRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Follow-up started from the date of PSA taken on study entry, and ended at date of most recent screening round, diagnosis of PCa / csPCa, or death. Adjusted IRRs were calculated after 5 years of screening, with the reference being those of White British ethnicity, at median age at study entry. Sensitivity analyses were conducted excluding prevalent cancers (cancers diagnosed <12 months of enrolment). Mann-Whitney tests were used to compare median age and PSA at diagnosis; Fisher’s exact was used to compare proportions of tumour characteristics between groups.

Statistical analyses were undertaken using Stata14.2(StataCorp,2015) and GraphPad Prism10(GraphPad Software,USA). p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Recruitment took place at 65 centres in 20 countries between 2005–2015 (Supplementary table 1). The study population consisted of 915 BRCA1 carriers, 727 BRCA1 non-carriers; 901 BRCA2 carriers; 520 BRCA2 non-carriers. 134 BRCA2 non-carriers screened for PGVs in both BRCA1 and BRCA2 were included in both non-carrier controls cohorts, giving a total study population of 3,063 participants, and 2929 unique individuals.

Mean enrolment age was 54yrs (IQR:47;60) and 97% (2970/3063) participants were of White European ancestry. 35%(1037/2929) of participants had PSA screening prior to study entry (40%(356/901) BRCA2 carriers, 36%(189/520) BRCA2 non-carriers, 34%(316/915) BRCA1 carriers, 31%(229/727) BRCA1 non-carriers. Demographics, medical and family history of participants have been previously reported[19]. No statistically significant differences were observed between groups in respect of sociodemographic variables, symptoms, or prior screening behaviour.

After one screening round, 2,929 participants underwent PSA-screening, 227(7.8%) had a PSA>3.0ng/mL, and from 190 PB, 68(2.3%) PrCa were diagnosed. 53 participants declined PB. After five screening rounds, 572/2,929 participants(20%) had a PSA>3.0ng/mL requiring action. From 376 PB, overall PrCa incidence was 4.3%(126/2929). 230/594(39%) PSAs>3.0ng/mL did not fulfil PB, (reasons: personal choice, conflicting health reasons, clinicians’ advice). After five screening rounds, PrCa was diagnosed in 52/901(5.8%) BRCA2 carriers and 19/520(3.7%) non-carriers,(p=0.078) and 34/915 BRCA1 carriers(3.7%) and 22/727(3.0%) non-carriers,(p=0.4) (Table 1a; Figure 1a, Figure 1b; Supplementary Figure 2).

Cancer incidence was 16 for BRCA2 carriers compared with 11 in non-carriers(adjusted IRR=1.7,[95% CI 1.0, 2.9],p=0.059), and 11 for BRCA1 carriers compared with 8.8 in non-carriers(adjusted IRR=1.3,[95% CI 0.8, 2.3],p=0.3)(Table 1a).

Incidence of CS-PrCa (≥GG2) at diagnosis was statistically significantly higher in BRCA2 carriers compared with non-carriers (3.1%(28/901) to 1.3% (7/520),p=0.039). There was no statistically significant difference in BRCA1 carriers compared with non-carriers (risk difference 0.60(−0.41,1.6),p=0.3)(Table 1b, Figure 1c, Figure1d).

IMPACT is the only multi-centre international prospective PrCa-screening study in BRCA1 and BRCA2 PGV carriers. This report presents the complete five-year screening outcome data from IMPACT. These data confirm previous findings that BRCA2 carriers are diagnosed at a younger age and have a higher incidence of more aggressive disease. For the first time these data confirm that, whilst overall incidence may not be higher, the tumours that BRCA1 carriers develop have significantly higher-risk features compared with non-carriers. This was particularly apparent when comparing incident cancers within the BRCA1 cohort with a statistically significant difference observed between carriers and non-carriers (p=0.0017). One of the main criticisms of PSA screening in the general population is the over detection of indolent tumours, however these data highlight the importance of screening these high-risk carriers.

A strength of IMPACT is that most participants (35%) did not have PSA testing prior to study enrolment [19]. This led to a large proportion of cases being diagnosed at study entry. However, cancers were detected at every screening round in BRCA2 carriers and all except for one screening round (year 4) in BRCA1 carriers.

Based on previous reports from IMPACT, the European Association of Urology guidelines were initially updated in 2020 to include the recommendation (and further updated in 2024) for PrCa-screening for all BRCA2 carriers starting at age 40yrs[16,22]. Whilst debate continues about the use of PSA-based PrCa-screening at population-level, the benefits of targeting PrCa-screening at BRCA2 carriers to facilitate early-detection of CS-PrCa are clear.

We have demonstrated that using a PSA level of >3.0ng/mL, the PrCa detection rate is 4.3% (126/2929), tumours are detected at an early curable stage, and the biopsy rate is low at 12% (364/2929). After five screening rounds, there were no deaths from PrCa, and no metastatic disease at presentation. An annual screening interval, using a PSA threshold of 3.0ng/mL successfully detected CS-PrCa at every screening round in BRCA1/BRCA2 carriers, except for one screening round (year 4) in BRCA1 carriers. These data add support to recommend annual PSA-screening in BRCA1/2 carriers (Supplementary Figure 3).

For the first time, we report a statistically significant difference in the classification of screen-detected tumours diagnosed in BRCA1 PGV carriers and non-carriers (p=0.0017), as has been previously reported in BRCA2 carriers and confirmed again in this report. The NCCN criteria were used to classify tumours by risk of metastasis which includes increased detail on the pathological features, whereas past reports used the more simplistic NICE (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence) criteria[18,19]. As reported previously, we observed a trend in increased incidence of PrCa in BRCA2 carriers compared with non-carriers (p=0.078). Whilst PrCa incidence in BRCA1 carriers is not significantly increased when compared with non-carriers, there is a statistically significant difference in the aggressiveness of these screen-detected tumours.

An important finding is the high proportion of tumours with a GG increase at RP in comparison with the diagnostic PB. In total 17 cases were increased from GG1/2 to ≥GG2 whilst only four cases were reduced. All disease with an increase in GG was reported within BRCA1/2 carriers. No statistically significant difference was observed between diagnostic GG or carrier status for those recommended to undergo RP. Consequently, there was no suggestion of bias towards carriers preferentially being managed with surgery.

The post-RP results demonstrate that PrCas in BRCA1/2 carriers have worse pathological appearance on surgical resection and supports reports of aggressive genomic features in tumours of BRCA2 carriers[9,23,24]. This would argue against active surveillance in this group.

When the data were analysed to consider if the cutoff of PSA of >3.0ng/mL is appropriate for biopsy, the end of study biopsies and those undertaken off protocol were analysed. The 12.7%(14/110) PrCa incidence in the subgroup of biopsy naïve participants undergoing an end-of-study biopsy was detected with PSAs of 1.9–2.9ng/mL. However, it was reassuring to see that 86%(12/14) had GG1 localised disease and the incidence of CS-PrCa was low (1.8%;2/110). If we consider all PrCa diagnosed with a PSA<3.0ng/mL 4/22 (18%) were clinically significant. This suggests that PB is not indicated unless the PSA algorithm dictates this as per protocol and PSA is >3.0ng/mL

Furthermore, few interval cancers were detected between screening rounds supporting the safety of an annual PSA-based screening algorithm. Notably, a PSA of 2.0ng/mL corresponds to 90th centile at age 50 with a PSA of 4.0ng/mL corresponding to 90th centile at age 60.

Not all participants complied with the study protocol, and therefore some cancers may have been missed in those who declined, or were advised against PB by their local physicians. However, all participants will be followed-up for a further five years and it would be expected that CS-PrCa would be detected within that period.

We acknowledge that awareness of genetic status may determine protocol compliance as more non-carriers declined PB in the BRCA2 cohort (34%(60/177) versus 47%(45/96) carriers versus non-carriers), however PB decline was similar in the BRCA1 cohort at 40%(68/171) versus 38%(57/150) carriers versus noncarriers. This higher biopsy uptake in BRCA2 carriers may represent a counselling bias, with those known to carry a BRCA2 PGV and with raised PSA levels encouraged to undergo biopsy.

Another challenge in this longitudinal study is practice changes over time. IMPACT began in 2005, prior to multiparametric MRI featuring in the PrCa diagnostic pathway[25,26]. PB has also changed from transrectal to an MRI-guided targeted transperineal approach. Extrapolating data from general population studies to apply to this genetically high-risk cohort is challenging; the integration of MRI in high-risk individuals requires further research.

Furthermore PB pathology was reported in local screening centres, reflecting real-world outcome data, but limiting the data by the large number of pathologists classifying tumour characteristics. Central pathology review is a future planned outcome.

Further follow-up of these screen detected PrCas is required to analyse mortality and morbidity and clarify longer-term impact of screening for BRCA1/BRCA2 PGV carriers.

Following five PSA-screening rounds, these data confirm BRCA2 PGV carriers have a higher incidence of CS-PrCa, are diagnosed at a younger age and present with more high-risk disease when compared with non-carriers. For the first time, we confirm that annual PSA-screening results in the early diagnosis of high-risk screen-detected disease in BRCA1 PGV carriers.

These results further support the previously published recommendation of systematic PSA-screening in BRCA2 carriers and supports consideration of this recommendation for BRCA1 carriers.
