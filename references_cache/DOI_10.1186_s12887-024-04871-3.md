Abstract
Background Lead encephalopathy, while thankfully rare, is a devastating and potentially fatal consequence of lead 
intoxication. Owing to successful public health measures, severe lead toxicity is not often encountered by most 
practicing physicians in the United States, making both its recognition and management challenging.
Article summary A case study of a 4-year-old female presenting in refractory status epilepticus, found to have severe 
microcytic anemia and lead level > 100 mcg/dL.
Case presentation We report a case of a 4-year-old girl who presented with refractory seizures, severe microcytic 
anemia, and a history of developmental delay who was ultimately diagnosed with lead encephalopathy, requiring 
multiple courses of calcium disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and succimer for rebounding lead 
levels.
Conclusion Rapid recognition and appropriate management is essential to ensure neurologically intact survival. This 
case documents one of the first cases of successful lead chelation after multiple courses of calcium disodium EDTA 
and succimer dual therapy since the removal of dimercaprol from the United States market. This case also highlights 
the importance of using body surface area dosing for chelation therapy in order to prevent under-dosing in young 
children.

Case presentation
A previously healthy 4-year-old female presented to the 
emergency department (ED) in status epilepticus. The 
day prior, she had multiple episodes of non-bloody, non-
bilious emesis. On the morning of presentation, she had 
an episode of emesis upon awakening after which she 
became lethargic and began having generalized tonic-
clonic (GTC) seizure activity. Review of systems was neg-
ative for recent upper respiratory infection symptoms, 
fevers, diarrhea, changes in appetite or activity level, wit -
nessed trauma or ingestions.
Her seizures were refractory to three doses of 0.2 mil -
ligram/kilogram (mg/kg) intravenous (IV) midazolam by 
emergency medical services en route to the ED. She con -
tinued to have seizures on arrival to the ED and received 
levetiracetam 40 mg/kg IV. The ED team then elected to 
intubate for airway protection and hypoxia in the setting 
of status epilepticus. Rapid sequence intubation was per -
formed with IV ketamine, fentanyl and rocuronium, and 
the pediatric critical care team elected to use continuous 
IV midazolam 0.1 mg/kg/h as their primary sedative. Her 
vitals were notable for blood pressure of 113/76 mm Hg 
(> 95th percentile for height and age) and tachycardia to 
148 beats/min. Temperature, blood glucose, and serum 
sodium were within normal limits. Subsequent physical 
exams and computerized tomography head imaging were 
unremarkable. A mild respiratory acidosis was noted 
on venous blood gas; respiratory panel and serum drug 
screen were negative. Complete blood count (CBC) was 
remarkable for leukocytosis (23.3 10 9/L) with neutrophil 
predominance (77%) and microcytic anemia (Hemo -
globin 5.7  g/dL, MCV 68.1 fL). The patient was placed 
on video electroencephalogram (EEG) monitoring and 
admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit for further 
workup and management of status epilepticus.
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed an elevated 
protein (117.4 mg/dL) but was otherwise normal with a 
negative gram stain and meningitis/encephalitis panel. 
She required increasing anticonvulsant therapy when the 
EEG was reported to have continued “subclinical seizures 
with bilateral diffuse cerebral dysfunction and further 
worsening over the right hemisphere” . The midazolam 
infusion was gradually titrated to 1 mg/kg/h in addition 
to levetiracetam 10  mg/kg/dose every 8  h and a pento -
barbital 5  mg/kg bolus followed by a continuous infu -
sion titrated to 3.5 mg/kg/h to achieve burst suppression, 
which was recommended by neurology for refractory 
status epilepticus treatment. Repeat CBC on hospital day 
(HD) 2 showed worsened microcytic anemia (Hemoglo -
bin 4.7 mg/dL, MCV 70 fL) with high ferritin levels (230 
ng/mL). Urine and serum metabolic studies were all nor -
mal as well. She was transfused with 2 units of packed red 
blood cells at a total of 30 mL/kg. Her blood lead level 
(BLL) resulted on HD3 and was markedly elevated at 
116.2 mcg/dL, and a diagnosis of severe lead poisoning 
resulting in lead encephalopathy was made. Poison con -
trol was immediately notified. Based on recommenda -
tions and current availability, succimer (350 mg/m 2/dose 
every 8  h per Table  1) was started, and once obtained, 
calcium disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid 
(EDTA) (1500 mg/m 2/dose daily) was started on HD5 
via a continuous infusion. Patient experienced one addi -
tional seizure post initiation of calcium disodium EDTA 
and succimer. Succimer was changed to dimercaprol (75 
mg/m2/dose every 4 h) on HD6. The patient remained on 
telemetry monitoring during calcium disodium EDTA 
therapy, and was found to have no abnormal electro -
cardiography (ECG) findings. During calcium disodium 
EDTA therapy, the patient experienced mild transient 
hypercalcemia to a maximum serum calcium of 11.1 mg/
dL. No other adverse effects of lead chelation therapy was 
noted.
Upon further history, the patient did not have well 
child checkups since early infancy and was only updated 
to her two month vaccines. On presentation the patient 
weighed 22.7  kg ( > 95th percentile for age) and was 
112  cm in stature ( > 95th percentile for age). Mother 
reported the child had speech delay, with about 10–20 
word vocabulary and 1–2 word sentences, but played 
well with other children and made eye contact. Her only 
other developmental history documented by a medical 
professional notes about 2–3 word vocabulary around 
15 months of age. Her gross motor skills appeared intact, 
but fine motor skills were delayed as she could not use 
scissors or properly grasp a pencil. When asked about 
possible lead exposures, the mother stated the child was 
occasionally found eating paint chips in the home, which 
the mother would try to prevent when witnessed. The 
patient also preferred to chew on ice. The mother denied

were opened and mixed with applesauce then adminis -
tered via naso-gastric tube immediately after mixing.
The patient’s subsequent hospital course included 
additional courses of succimer with poor response 
to this agent (see figure 1), and a second course of cal -

of therapy. By day 7 of her third course of succimer, the 
patient had two BLLs below 25 mcg/dL (19.2 and 21.8 
mcg/dL) and was cleared for discharge. The patient was 
admitted to our institution for 89 days for continued lead 
chelation therapy. By this time, the patient was felt to be 
near her pre-morbid neuropsychiatric baseline as per her 
mother. A home inspection by the local health depart -
ment revealed the presence of lead on windowsills with 
chipped paint and bite marks, with > 10,000ppm of lead 
within the surrounding dust. Abatement was performed 
prior to discharge.
Discussion and conclusions
This case illustrates the importance of maintaining a 
high index of suspicion for lead poisoning. The patient 
exhibited severe microcytic anemia, seizures, and devel -
opmental delay, each of which has a somewhat broad dif -
ferential when considered independently. However, the 
combination led the clinicians to suspect lead poisoning 
and fortunately institute treatment rapidly.
This case, given availability of lead chelation therapies, 
is the first published to manage severe lead toxicity with 
encephalopathy with dual calcium disodium EDTA and 
succimer. Historically, succimer has been successfully 
utilized in severe lead toxicity in a resource limited set -
ting [2]. Thurtle et al. evaluated 148 succimer courses in 
patients with BLL > 120 mCg/dL, and found, similar to 
our case, that the first course of succimer was associated 
with the largest change in BLL compared to subsequent 
courses, and BLL rebound was seen in 82% succimer 
courses. Thurtle et al. also found monotherapy succimer 
elicited a decrease in BLL of approximately 25% over -
all and about 50% in hospitalized patients. In a similar 
case, published by Talbot et al., a 2 year old in the United 
Kingdom presented with febrile seizures and encepha -
lopathy found to have a BLL of 364 mCg/dL (more than 
three-fold more than our patient) and was managed 
with monotherapy sodium edetate 75  mg/kg once daily. 
Despite declining lead levels (last documented BLL day 
12 of admission or day 7 post sodium edetate in initiation 
between 40 and 80 mCg/dL), this patient ultimately did 
not make a neurological recovery and passed away [3].
Lead is a neurotoxic substance that can cause global 
damage affecting brain function and development. Chil -
dren exposed to elevated lead levels (≥ 3.5 mCg/dL) are at 
increased risk for cognitive and behavioral problems [ 4]. 
This may be due to a host of factors including increased 
gastrointestinal absorption, enhanced blood-brain bar -
rier permeability, and the vulnerability of the developing 
brain to the effects of lead. Additionally, iron deficient 
children have enhanced absorption of lead, likely due 
to both the increased ingestion of leaded non-nutritive 
substances from resultant pica and from decreased com -
petitive inhibition at the apical divalent metal transporter 
(DMT1) in the small intestine [5]. As the blood lead level 
rises, the severity of effects increases. Unfortunately, 
early symptoms of lead poisoning may be vague and go 
undetected.
Pediatric lead encephalopathy is a rare complication of 
lead poisoning, which has a historical mortality rate of 
28 to 45%, with four out of five surviving children hav -
ing neurologic sequelae [ 6]. Thankfully, lead intoxication 
rates among the US pediatric population have declined 
substantially owing to public health interventions in the 
70s and 80s; still, roughly 25% of US children live in hous-
ing with deteriorating lead-based paint and remain at risk 
of lead exposure [ 7]. Thus, while severe lead intoxication 
is now rarely encountered in the United States, the asso -
ciated high mortality and the propensity for severe neu -
rologic sequelae makes rapid recognition and treatment 
essential.
Lead encephalopathy tends to occur in the setting of 
blood lead levels in excess of 80–100 mcg/dL, and results 
from disruption of the cerebrovascular endothelium, 
leading to capillary leak and subsequent cerebral edema 
and increased intracranial pressure [ 8]. Along with 
removal from or of the source of exposure, lead encepha -
lopathy management centers around meticulous critical 
care, management of increased intracranial pressure if it 
occurs, and combined parenteral chelation therapy with 
dimercaprol and calcium disodium EDTA [ 9, 10]. Body 
surface area (BSA) based dosing of the chelating agents is 
recommended and was used in our patient’s case to avoid 
under-dosing.

Chelation therapy in our case was complicated by 
nationwide shortages of the two first line parenteral che -
lating agents, dimercaprol and calcium disodium EDTA, 
owing to the cessation of operations of Akorn pharma -
ceuticals and undisclosed reasons by Bausch Health, 
respectively. The poison center assisted the pharmacy in 
borrowing calcium disodium EDTA from a nearby hos -
pital. Succimer was initiated on HD4, and due to delays 
in transport, calcium disodium EDTA was initiated on 
HD5. Dimercaprol replaced succimer on HD6 once it 
became available. Because calcium disodium EDTA has 
variable penetration across the blood brain barrier [ 2, 11] 
and interim guidance for management of lead encepha -
lopathy or severe toxicity in the setting of dimercaprol 
unavailability has not been established, we propose it is 
reasonable to consider dual chelation therapy with cal -
cium disodium EDTA and succimer at this time. Barriers 
to this substitution that our group experienced include 
lack of literature to support comparable efficacy of suc -
cimer administration via enteral feeding tubes [2].
A rebound increase in blood lead concentration is often 
seen after chelation therapy as lead stores in the soft tis -
sue and bone are released and re-equilibration occurs, 
necessitating repeat treatments [ 11]. To ameliorate the 
potential deleterious effects of such rebound, our patient 
was transitioned immediately to oral succimer after com-
pletion of combination parenteral therapy with calcium 
disodium EDTA and dimercaprol. While we believe this 
succeeded in blunting the rate of rise, our patient ulti -
mately required several additional courses of chelation 
with succimer and calcium disodium EDTA for persis -
tent blood lead level elevations.

a patient who presented with severe microcytic anemia 
and status epilepticus. We hope that this case will serve 
as a reminder to clinicians to consider this diagnosis in 
compatible clinical scenarios, as rapid recognition and
