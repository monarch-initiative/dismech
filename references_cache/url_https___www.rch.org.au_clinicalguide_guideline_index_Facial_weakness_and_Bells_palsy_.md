<h2>See also</h2>
<p>
<a>Stroke</a>
<br/>
<a>Acute otitis media</a>
<br/>
<a>Hypertension in children and adolescents</a>
</p>
<h2>Key points</h2>
<ol>
<li>Bell palsy is an idiopathic unilateral lower motor neuron facial nerve palsy </li>
<li>Other causes of facial weakness should be excluded before making a diagnosis of Bell palsy </li>
<li>Almost all children recover within 12 months without treatment, however some children may have a prolonged period of functional impairment, facial asymmetry and emotional distress </li>
</ol>
<h2>Background</h2>
<ul>
<li>Facial nerve palsy in children may be idiopathic or caused by infection, inflammation, trauma, tumour or a vascular event</li>
<li>Bell palsy is an idiopathic lower motor neuron palsy of the facial nerve</li>
<li>It is important to rule out other causes before making a diagnosis of Bell palsy</li>
<li>Idiopathic or post-viral facial palsy is uncommon in children less than 2 years </li>
</ul>
<h2>Assessment</h2>
<p> Assessment of Bell palsy is aimed at confirming the diagnosis and excluding other important causes of facial weakness</p>
<h3>History</h3>
<p> Features typical of Bell palsy</p>
<ul>
<li>Onset: Bell palsy usually occurs very quickly (over hours or no more than 1-2 days)</li>
<li>Facial pain: usually mild pain in the face or behind the ear (common in Bell palsy)</li>
<li>Dry eyes and poor tear clearance from the eyes</li>
<li>Previous similar episodes </li>
</ul>
<p>Red flags for conditions other than Bell palsy</p>
<ul>
<li>Other neurological features such as severe headache, blurred vision, double vision, weakness/numbness in arms or extremities, ataxia (consider
        <a>Stroke</a>) </li>
<li>Hyperacusis (increased sensitivity to sound) and altered taste (both uncommon in Bell palsy)</li>
<li>Preceding viral infections or trauma to the head or face</li>
<li>Severe pain (may indicate mastoiditis, VZV)</li>
</ul>
<h3><strong>Examination</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li>Full neurological examination (cranial and peripheral) is essential </li>
<ul>
<li>Bell palsy involves all facial nerve branches on the affected side. Ptosis and forehead muscle weakness may be evident. These features are absent in upper motor neuron lesions</li>
</ul>
<li>Eye examination (to look for corneal abrasions)</li>
<li>Skin lesions or vesicles on the face or in the ear canal (Ramsay Hunt Syndrome)</li>
<li>Signs of malignancy (pallor, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy)</li>
<li>Signs of
        <a>otitis media</a>, mastoiditis or parotitis</li>
<li>Blood pressure and temperature (hypertension may rarely be associated with facial nerve palsy)</li>
</ul>
<h3>Assessment of severity</h3>
<p>Severity can be measured using the House Brackmann grading scale (see Additional notes). This can be useful to monitor progress</p>
<h2>Management</h2>
<p>
<img/>
<map>
<area/>
<area/>
</map>
<br/>
</p>
<h3><strong>Investigations</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li>The diagnosis of Bell palsy is made on clinical history and examination. Investigations are not usually required </li>
<li>FBE (to exclude haematological malignancy)</li>
<ul>
<li>If features of an alternative cause of facial weakness, particularly malignancy</li>
<li>If commencing steroids</li>
</ul>
<li>If features of an alternative cause of facial weakness, consider</li>
<ul>
<li>Swab for HSV and VZV (if vesicles are present)</li>
<li>Neuroimaging (in consultation with neurology)</li>
</ul>
</ul>
<h3><strong>Treatment</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li>Almost all children recover within 12 months without treatment (most within 6 weeks of onset). However some children may have a prolonged period of functional impairment, facial asymmetry and emotional distress. Provide
        <a>Bell palsy parent handout</a> </li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Eye care</strong>
</p>
<ul>
<li>All children with an inability to close their eye should have eye care to prevent corneal abrasions (dry eye syndrome)</li>
<li>Lubricating (Hypromellose) ocular drops at least three times during the day</li>
<li>Pad eye shut after application of lubricating ocular ointment at night</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Steroids</strong>
</p>
<ul>
<li>The vast majority of children with Bell palsy recover without treatment. There is not enough evidence that early treatment with prednisolone improves complete recovery in children</li>
<li>In adults there is good evidence that steroids (1 mg/kg/day (max 50 mg) oral daily for 10 days) improve recovery, particularly within 72 hours of onset. The age of transition to adult evidence is unknown </li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Antivirals</strong>
</p>
<ul>
<li>Aciclovir may be considered if vesicular rash present</li>
</ul>
<h2>Consider consultation with local paediatric/specialist team when</h2>
<ul>
<li>Child is
        &lt;2 years of age or atypical features on history or examination (consult Neurology) </li>
<li>Child with evidence of otitis media, mastoiditis or parotitis (consult ENT)</li>
</ul>
<h2>Consider transfer when</h2>
<p>Children requiring care beyond the level of comfort of the local hospital</p>
<p>For emergency advice and paediatric or neonatal ICU transfers, see
    <a>Retrieval Services</a> </p>
<h2>Consider discharge when</h2>
<ul>
<li>No signs of complications</li>
<li>Follow up with health professional (eg GP, paediatrician) within 1-2 weeks is arranged</li>
</ul>
