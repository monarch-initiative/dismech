# Cornelia de Lange Syndrome.
**Authors:** Adam MP, Bick S, Mirzaa GM, Pagon RA, Wallace SE, Amemiya A, Deardorff MA, Raible SE, Krantz ID

## Content

CLINICAL CHARACTERISTICS: Cornelia de Lange syndrome (CdLS) encompasses a spectrum of findings from mild to severe. Severe (classic) CdLS is characterized by distinctive facial features, growth failure (often prenatal onset; height and weight <5th centile throughout life), hypertrichosis, and limb abnormalities (e.g., complete absence of elbow and forearms, oligodactyly [missing digits], micromelia [small hands], subtle phalangeal abnormalities). Craniofacial features include synophrys, highly arched and/or thick eyebrows, long eyelashes, short nose with depressed nasal bridge and anteverted nares, small, widely spaced teeth, and microbrachycephaly. Individuals with a milder phenotype have less severe growth, cognitive, and limb involvement, but often have facial features consistent with CdLS. Across the CdLS spectrum, IQ ranges from below 30 to 102 (mean: 53). Many individuals demonstrate autistic and self-injurious behaviors. Other frequent findings include gastrointestinal dysfunction, cryptorchidism or hypoplastic genitalia, myopia, hearing impairment, and congenital heart disease.

DIAGNOSIS/TESTING: The diagnosis of CdLS is established in a proband with suggestive clinical features and/or a heterozygous pathogenic variant in BRD4, MAU2, NIPBL, RAD21, or SMC3 or a hemizygous or heterozygous pathogenic variant in HDAC8 or SMC1A identified by molecular genetic testing.

MANAGEMENT: Treatment of manifestations: Use of CdLS-specific growth charts with supplementary formulas and/or gastrostomy tube placement to meet nutritional needs as necessary. Aggressive management of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) with assessment of potential gastrointestinal malrotation; consideration of fundoplication if reflux is severe. Physical, occupational, and speech therapy to optimize psychomotor development and communication skills. Aggressive treatment of nasolacrimal duct obstruction. Standard treatment for epilepsy, limb defects, gastrointestinal malformations, ocular issues, hearing loss, cleft palate, dental anomalies, cryptorchidism/hypospadias, bicornuate uterus, vesicoureteral reflux, cardiac defects, immunodeficiency, anemia, and thrombocytopenia. If surgery is being considered, preoperative evaluation for thrombocytopenia and cardiac disease with careful monitoring of the airway during anesthesia are recommended. Family and social work support. Surveillance: At each visit, measure growth parameters and evaluate nutritional status and safety of oral intake; monitor for signs and symptoms of GERD and for evidence of aspiration with respiratory insufficiency; monitor developmental progress and educational needs; behavioral assessment for anxiety, attention deficits, and aggressive or self-injurious behaviors; assess for new manifestations such as seizures or signs of autonomic dysfunction; assess mobility and self-help skills; assess family needs. At least annual ophthalmology evaluation and dental evaluation with cleaning; audiology evaluation annually in childhood and adolescence.

GENETIC COUNSELING: BRD4-, MAU2-, NIPBL-, RAD21-, and SMC3-CdLS are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. HDAC8- and SMC1A-CdLS are inherited in an X-linked manner. Autosomal dominant inheritance: Most individuals have the disorder as the result of a de novo germline constitutional or postzygotic mosaic pathogenic variant. Rarely, individuals diagnosed with CdLS have an affected parent. Each child of an affected individual has a 50% chance of inheriting the CdLS-related pathogenic variant. While most familial recurrences of CdLS are the result of gonadal mosaicism in a phenotypically normal parent, rare instances of mildly affected individuals having children with CdLS have been reported. X-linked inheritance: Most individuals have the disorder as the result of a de novo pathogenic variant. If the mother of the proband has an SMC1A or HDAC8 pathogenic variant, the chance of transmitting it in each pregnancy is 50%. Male sibs who inherit the pathogenic variant will be affected. Female sibs who inherit the pathogenic variant will be heterozygous. Females with a heterozygous SMC1A pathogenic variant often have some features of CdLS. Females with a heterozygous HDAC8 pathogenic variant have clinical features overlapping those of hemizygous males, but the severity is greatly influenced by the pattern of X-chromosome inactivation. If the father of the proband has an SMC1A or HDAC8 pathogenic variant, he will transmit it to all his daughters and none of his sons. If the CdLS-causative pathogenic variant has been identified in an affected family member, prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing are possible.
