Solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) is an exceedingly rare mesenchymal neoplasm 
classified as a soft tissue sarcoma (STS). While historically considered 
indolent, up to 50% of cases can recur or metastasize after long-term follow-up. 
Complete surgical resection constitutes the cornerstone of treatment for 
localized disease, while the management of locally advanced or metastatic SFT 
poses a significant clinical challenge underscoring the need for effective 
systemic therapies. Antiangiogenic agents have shown promising outcomes, 
particularly in non-dedifferentiated SFT, and are increasingly favored by some 
as first-line options. Regarding chemotherapy, traditional anthracycline-based 
STS regimens have demonstrated limited efficacy, shifting the current focus 
towards combinations with alkylating agents, such as dacarbazine, and other 
agents such as microtubule inhibitors, like trabectedin. This review summarizes 
current systemic therapeutic strategies for SFT, highlighting the central role 
of antiangiogenic therapy as the standard backbone for managing advanced 
disease. It also discusses current data on chemotherapy, as well as molecularly 
targeted and immunomodulatory approaches, which are emerging options that 
require further validation. In addition, we discuss evolving concepts in 
systemic treatment sequencing, predictive biomarkers, advanced therapeutic 
combinations under clinical evaluation, and potential future therapeutic agents 
and targets.

Solitary fibrous tumor (SFT), first described in 1931, represents a rare histological spectrum of soft tissue neoplasms of mesenchymal origin, accounting for approximately 2–3.7% of all soft tissue sarcoma (STS) cases [1–4]. These tumors are often slow-growing and present with delayed onset of symptoms. While it was believed that most SFTs behave indolently and do not recur following gross-total surgical resection (GTR), reported local or distant recurrence rates have varied from approximately 10% up to 50% (with long-term follow-up) [5–10]. In cases where surgical management is not feasible, systemic therapies pose a unique clinical challenge. Despite growing clinical experience, optimal systemic management for SFT remains poorly defined, particularly given the biological differences between anaplastic/dedifferentiated SFT (DDSFT) and non-DDSFT subtypes. This review aims to summarize data on systemic therapy selection across SFT subgroups and evaluate emerging treatment strategies for refractory disease.



Epidemiology
SFT preferentially arise from the serosal membranes, meninges, and deep soft tissues. Although historically linked to a pleural origin, more than two-thirds of SFTs occur outside the thoracic cavity [11, 12]. Approximately one-third of patients present with thoracic tumors (28–30%), while common extra-pleural locations include the head and neck, particularly involving the meninges (25–27%), the intra-abdominal region (18–20%), the trunk (7–10%), and the extremities (8–22%) [11, 12].Tumors arising in the retroperitoneum, peritoneum, and mediastinum are generally associated with a more aggressive clinical course compared to those in other anatomical sites [10, 13]. In contrast, head and neck SFT typically exhibits more indolent behavior, with a longer time-to-metastasis relative to trunk tumors [11].SFTs occur with approximately equal frequency in men and women and can develop at any age, although incidence peaks between 50 and 55 years [14]. Notably, recent studies suggest that male sex is associated with shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and a higher risk of metastasis [15]. Due to the often slow-growing nature of this disease, local and metastatic recurrences may occur in up to 50% of patients even a decade after initial diagnosis, which underscores the importance of long-term clinical surveillance [10].

Cytogenetics and Histological Classification
Historically, SFT posed a significant diagnostic challenge. However, over the past decade, the identification of the intrachromosomal NGFI-A binding protein 2 (NAB2)–signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) gene fusion has substantially improved diagnostic precision with this fusion now being considered a pathognomonic hallmark of SFT [16–19]. This fusion not only facilitates diagnosis but also suggests a potential molecular therapeutic target. The rearrangement, caused by the inversion of the long arm of chromosome 12, results in the loss of NAB2-mediated suppression of early growth response (EGR) genes, thereby promoting constitutive cellular proliferation [12, 17].The near-universal presence of the NAB2–STAT6 fusion across SFT cases indicates a fundamental role in tumorigenesis, contributing to uncontrolled cell growth [9, 12, 17]. Additionally, several other receptor tyrosine kinases—including vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFR) 1–3, platelet-derived growth factor receptors (PDGFR-α and -β), insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1), discoidin domain receptor 1 (DDR1), and HER2—have been found to be overexpressed in SFTs. These alterations may correlate with activation of downstream signaling cascades promoting tumor growth, particularly the Akt-mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK1/2) pathways [9, 12, 20–24].Other genetic aberrations, such as mutations in the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter and dysregulation of p53, have been observed in a subset of cases. However, their prognostic significance has not been completely elucidated yet [25–29].Advances in the molecular characterization of SFT have also prompted significant changes in classification. A pivotal change came with the 2013 WHO classification, which integrated hemangiopericytoma under the SFT category[30]. The current WHO system classifies SFTs based on their biological behavior, moving away from the traditional benign/malignant dichotomy, which often failed to reliably predict clinical outcomes [31]. According to the 2020 WHO classification, SFT is recognized as either intermediate (rarely metastasizing) or malignant, with DDSFT variants considered within the malignant category [9, 31]. The latter variant is characterized by an abrupt transition from conventional low-grade SFT to high-grade sarcoma and is associated with a distinct clinical course and treatment response [32]. Due to its unique histopathological and clinical features, this subtype is sometimes discussed as a separate entity in clinical practice and clinical trials [33].

Predictive Algorithms
Given the limited prognostic accuracy of histological features alone, clinicians increasingly rely on integrated risk assessment models to guide management decisions. Consequently, multivariate risk stratification models have been developed to enhance prognostication. Among existing prognostic frameworks, the Demicco model remains widely employed due to its simplicity and reproducibility in stratifying metastatic risk for extra-meningeal SFTs [34, 35]. This model incorporates patient age, tumor size, and mitotic count to stratify tumors into low, intermediate, or high metastatic risk categories [34, 35]. In routine clinical use, the Demicco model provides a structured framework for estimating metastatic risk at the time of diagnosis, allowing clinicians to tailor surveillance schedules, discuss prognosis more accurately with patients, and determine whether additional therapeutic strategies beyond surgery should be considered. Later versions of this model incorporated additional histologic markers, such as tumor necrosis, to enhance predictive accuracy [36].Alternative approaches, such as the Salas model, consider factors like anatomical location and history of radiotherapy (RT), omitting tumor size while maintaining other histological and clinical variables [10]. In a more recent stratification framework, Georgiesh and colleagues highlighted mitotic activity, presence of necrosis, and male sex as significant negative prognostic indicators [37].Dedicated scoring systems have also been designed for thoracic SFT [38–41] with some of them incorporating features such as pedunculation and pleural origin (parietal or visceral) [38–40].Lastly, more advanced prediction models, such as the one developed by Zhang et al. include the mitotic count, the density of Ki-67+, CD163 + cells (marker of M2-macrophages), and mTOR mutation [42]. Even though this model was shown to perform more favorably compared to the Demicco and Georgiesh model [42], its status remains investigational and external evaluation of real-world feasibility and efficacy is necessary.

The management of SFTs should be individualized and discussed in a multidisciplinary tumor board with rare STS expertise [9, 29]. Initial Management: Surgery and Adjuvant Treatment


The management of SFTs should be individualized and discussed in a multidisciplinary tumor board with rare STS expertise [9, 29]. GTR is still the primary therapeutic modality for all localized SFTs and has been shown to significantly improve PFS and overall survival (OS) [9, 43, 44]. Therefore, prognosis for primary or recurrent SFT can be favorable if the patient is amenable to tumor (re-)resection [45–47].

While randomized trials are lacking, retrospective studies suggest a reduction in local relapse with adjuvant RT [48–57]. Nonetheless, its impact on survival outcomes remains inconsistent across series [48–58], with the largest meta-analysis finding only a short-term benefit with no 10-year OS improvement [43].

Given the variability in survival benefit, in clinical practice RT is generally reserved for cases with positive surgical margins, where (re-)resection is not feasible, or for tumors displaying high-risk features. This practice is supported by studies showing that adjuvant RT benefits patients undergoing subtotal resection but not those achieving GTR [44].

Although underexplored, perioperative systemic interventions, such as chemotherapy [59] and antiangiogenics [60], have been reported in cases of anatomically challenging locations such as the spine.

Even though patients with metastatic SFT often demonstrate somewhat prolonged survival relative to other metastatic STS patients, outcomes remain suboptimal. In some cohorts, metastasectomy has been the only variable linked with improved survival [11], yet many individuals—especially those with locally inoperable or multifocal disease (multiple metastases can be found in up to 70% of patients as first metastasis occurrence [11])—lack curative options and face uncertain prognoses [8].

Systemic chemotherapy continues to be utilized in unresectable cases, though traditional STS regimens such as anthracycline-based and ifosfamide-based combinations are often less effective in SFT [61]. Additionally, antiangiogenic agents have shown significant promise in non-DDSFT and may outperform conventional chemotherapy in terms of PFS based on indirect retrospective comparisons, leading some experts to recommend these agents as first-line therapy [29]. Treatment response in early studies was often evaluated using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria; however, there is a growing shift, particularly in tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) studies, toward the use of the Choi criteria. This set of criteria was originally defined to determine responses of gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) to imatinib. The Choi criteria better reflect changes in tumor size and density following contrast administration on the CT scan to account for vasculature changes [62] and were more capable of capturing treatment-related changes than RECIST in several SFT studies [63–65]. The higher response rates observed are presumed to largely reflect vascular modulation rather than substantial tumor shrinkage, an expected effect of antiangiogenic therapies. For central nervous system involvement, some studies have utilized the McDonald and Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) criteria [66, 67], though their application remains relatively uncommon. Treatment regimens, response rates, PFS and OS from key retrospective series, case reports, prospective sarcoma trials enrolling SFT patients, and SFT-specific trials are summarized below. The following section is organized by therapeutic class, beginning with antiangiogenic agents, followed by conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, immunotherapy, other targeted therapies and emerging investigational approaches.



Antiangiogenics
SFTs are highly vascular neoplasms characterized by overexpression of angiogenesis-related receptors, including PDGFR and VEGFR [9]. A range of antiangiogenic agents has been investigated for treatment, including pazopanib, sunitinib, regorafenib, axitinib, sorafenib, imatinib, and anlotinib [63–65, 68–75]. Pazopanib is a multi-TKI active against VEGFR1–3, PDGFR, KIT, and FGFR1–3 [87] that has shown preclinical and clinical activity against SFT [70]. In an initial study of eleven treatment-naïve patients with metastatic SFT, results included one partial response (PR) (9%) by RECIST, five PRs (46%) by Choi, a PFS of 4.7 months, and an OS of 13.3 months[71]. The GEIS-32 was the first clinical trial to evaluate pazopanib’s activity for SFT patients stratified into “typical SFT” and “malignant SFT” cohorts (based on the former WHO classification) [63, 65]. In the “malignant SFT” cohort (which excluded DDSFT following rapid progression in early enrollees), 35 patients yielded two (6%) PRs by RECIST and 18 (51%) by Choi. The PFS was 5.57 months, and the 2-year OS rate was 73% [63]. In the “typical SFT” cohort, among 31 RECIST-evaluable patients, there were 2 (6%) PRs. By Choi, there were 18 (58%) PRs with a PFS of 9.8 months [65]. To date, pazopanib remains one of the TKIs with the most encouraging prospective phase II data in SFT, whereas other TKI use is mainly supported by smaller retrospective or exploratory series. Regorafenib is multi-TKI targeting VEGFR1–3, PDGFRβ, KIT, BRAF, FGFR1 [88], which has shown clinical benefit in different types of progressive sarcomas [89]. Preclinically, regorafenib has demonstrated strong activity in DDSFT patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models compared to other antiangiogenics such as pazopanib, sunitinib, bevacizumab, and sorafenib [70]. Clinical data from a small exploratory phase II trial resulted in an objective response rate (ORR) of 37.5% by Choi, with all responses being PRs. Notably, five of the six Choi responses occurred in patients previously treated with antiangiogenics, supporting the potential utility of sequencing or rotating these agents [73]. The PFS was 4.7 months, which is lower compared to historical antiangiogenic data, but may reflect the advanced and heavy prior treatment history of the cohort [73].

Sunitinib is a multi-TKI targeting VEGFR 1–3, PDGFRβ, Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 (Flt3), and KIT [91], associated with the induction of long-lasting stable disease (SD) and some PRs in small retrospective cohorts and case reports [21, 74, 76, 81]. In a small SFT cohort, PRs occurred in 6.5% by RECIST and to 48% of patients by Choi [64]. Of note, in a phase I trial, the only PR (1/18) observed was in an SFT patient [134]. Furthermore, there is an additional report of a patient with SFT who developed resistance to antiangiogenic therapy and subsequently responded to figitumumab, suggesting that alternative targeted therapy strategies may be effective and warrant consideration in the post-progression setting [21]. Despite these encouraging signals, enthusiasm for IGF-1R-targeted therapies has declined in the broader sarcoma field [135], resulting in limited further exploration in SFT as well. Nonetheless, recent interest in pathway-based combination therapies may help revive this promising therapeutic avenue [135, 136].

Although SFTs are exceedingly rare, their unique genetic profile and the scarcity of effective treatment options have sparked a growing interest in identifying novel therapeutic targets and strategies. Advances in molecular characterization are revealing potential pathways for intervention.

The NAB2–STAT6 fusion is the defining molecular hallmark of SFT and offers a promising target for RNA-directed therapies [16–19]. Preclinical studies have shown that antisense oligonucleotides and CRISPR/CasRx approaches can selectively suppress NAB2–STAT6 fusion transcripts without affecting wild-type STAT6. This RNA-targeting technology significantly reduced cellular proliferation in vitro and tumor growth in an athymic nude mouse model, laying the foundation for a potentially novel and precise treatment strategy for SFT [137].

Beyond the NAB2–STAT6 fusion, comprehensive genomic analyses have identified a broader spectrum of actionable genomic alterations. In a large study, 17.6% of SFT cases harbored such mutations [42]. These included isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) mutations in 6.9% of cases and alterations predictive of response to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors in an additional 6.9%. Mutations within mTOR, which is also a potential target for DDSFT [80], were detected in 3.1% of tumors [42]. Less frequent potentially targetable alterations included cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6, MEK, AKT, PI3K, and BRAF. These findings remain preclinical and experimental, with no translation into clinical trials yet.

Other emerging targets that have shown promise and are under active investigation include ISG15 and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) [29, 63, 138, 139]. ISG15, a ubiquitin-like modifier implicated in tumor progression and poor prognosis in SFT, has been identified as a potential therapeutic target [29, 63]. Functional studies demonstrated that ISG15 regulates genes associated with cancer stem cell phenotypes. Its in vitro downregulation sensitized SFT cells to doxorubicin, pazopanib, and trabectedin within three-dimensional culture models, suggesting a possible role in overcoming therapeutic resistance [139]. PCNA is a key regulator of DNA damage tolerance and can be detected in up to 80% of aggressive SFTs [138]. Ongoing research led by Martín-Broto and colleagues seeks to further elucidate its involvement in SFT pathogenesis and its implications for chemotherapy resistance, prognosis, and therapeutic vulnerability [29].

Combination strategies have emerged as a logical progression in addressing the complex biology of SFT. Preclinical studies in patient-derived SFT cell lines demonstrated that the combination of trabectedin with multi-TKIs specifically ponatinib or dasatinib exerted synergistic effects in vitro [140]. These promising results warrant further investigation in vivo and in clinical settings, particularly for patients with treatment-refractory disease. Additional evidence from a recent meta-analysis indicated that sarcoma patients treated with anti-IGF-1R therapies in combination with mTOR inhibitors achieved improved survival compared to those receiving anti-IGF-1R monotherapy alone [136]. This finding is particularly relevant for SFT, as up to 50% of tumors exhibit activation of the Akt/mTOR pathway [141] and when coupled with preliminary clinical data on IGF-1R inhibition in SFT, it further supports this combined approach as a potentially effective treatment strategy.

Combination drug approaches should also be considered within the field of immunotherapy. Beyond the RAR-Immune trial, which serves as an umbrella study for rare sarcomas testing PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors, specific combination strategies tailored to the genomic features of SFT should be further explored. For instance, one SFT study identified a high concentration of CD47 on tumor cells, which acts as an antiphagocytic signal by binding to signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) on macrophages [127]. Notably, CD47 expression correlated with PRAME levels, suggesting that inhibition of the CD47–SIRPα axis could enhance macrophage-mediated immune responses and may hold therapeutic promise [127]. Intriguingly, another study identified two tumors that exhibited both the IDH1 p.R132S mutation and high PD-L1 expression [42]. This duality in the molecular profile might imply a potential synergistic benefit from combining PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint blockade with IDH1-targeted therapy. Collectively, these findings underscore the value of biomarker-guided immunotherapeutic strategies that could guide the design of future clinical trials for SFT.

Advances in molecular profiling have enabled a more precise characterization of SFT as a distinct clinical and biological entity; however, these insights have yet to translate into meaningful improvements in therapeutic outcomes or patient preselection for the most appropriate treatments. Current data suggest that, in non-DDSFT, antiangiogenic agents are often considered a preferred first-line option, while chemotherapy is more commonly used in later treatment lines. In DDSFT, cytotoxic chemotherapy is widely used as an initial approach, with antiangiogenic therapies frequently introduced afterward. Areas warranting further investigation include head-to-head comparisons of antiangiogenic agents such as pazopanib with chemotherapy regimens, particularly anthracycline- and dacarbazine-based combinations, to clearly establish the optimal first-line treatment for non-DDSFT. Furthermore, optimal sequencing strategies—both in terms of rotating different antiangiogenics and integrating antiangiogenics with immunotherapy or chemotherapy—should be defined to maximize treatment durability and improve long-term outcomes. Immunotherapy, while still investigational, warrants further exploration.

Additionally, emerging data showing that up to 50% of SFT patients relapse after initial treatment underscore the need to evaluate the role of neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapies. These efforts should be aligned with established risk stratification models to inform patient selection for clinical trials and to identify those most likely to benefit from perioperative interventions.

Ultimately, given the rarity and distinct biology of SFT compared to other STS, international collaboration and participation in SFT-specific clinical trials remain essential to advance treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes.
