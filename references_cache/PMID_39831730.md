# An X-Linked Ataxia Syndrome in a Family with Hearing Loss Associated with a Novel Variant in the BCAP31 Gene.
**Authors:** Paucar M, Li T, Bergendal Å, Savitcheva I, Pourhamidi K, Laffita-Mesa JM, Nordgren A, Engvall M, Uhlén P, Lagerstedt-Robinson K, Svenningsson P
**Journal:** Mov Disord (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mds.30116](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.30116)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Pathogenic variants in B-cell receptor-associated protein (BCAP31) are associated with X-linked, deafness, dystonia and cerebral hypomyelination (DDCH) syndrome. DDCH is congenital and non-progressive, featuring severe intellectual disability (ID), variable dysmorphism, and sometimes associated with shortened survival. BCAP31 encodes one of the most abundant chaperones, with several functions including acting as a negative regulator of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) calcium ion (Ca2+) concentration. Here, we characterize an X-linked syndrome, its underlying genotype, and a functional evaluation of the identified candidate genetic variant.

METHODS: Evaluation of motor features, neuroimaging studies, neurophysiological, and cognitive tests. Whole exome sequencing (WES) was applied, a plasmid encoding BCAP31 with and without a candidate variant was transfected into SH-SY5Y cells to assess subcellular location and to measure Ca2+ levels in the cytoplasm.

RESULTS: Adult-onset ataxia, cognitive impairment, and hearing loss leading to deafness are the predominant features. Reduced penetrance, slow progression with preserved ability to walk in advance age, and universal cerebellar atrophy are other features for this syndrome. This condition is associated with the new variant c.22G>A (V8I) in BCAP31 at Xq28. The subcellular location of the V8I BCAP31 protein was not altered but caused significant elevation of cytosolic Ca2+.

CONCLUSIONS: Our findings expand the spectrum of variants in BCAP31 from neurodevelopmental syndromes to include a progressive neurodegenerative disease with variable expressivity. This is the first time ataxia is described in association with a BCAP31 variant and functional evidence of pathogenicity is provided. Additional BCAP31 cases featuring ataxia are needed to establish an association. © 2025 The Author(s). Pathogenic variants in B‐cell receptor‐associated protein (BCAP31), previously known as BAP31 or DXS1357E, are associated with the severe X‐linked dystonia, deafness, and cerebral hypomyelination (DDCH, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man [OMIM]: 300475) syndrome. The impact of BCAP31 variants in calcium homeostasis has not been evaluated before. Our aim was to describe a family affected by a new and milder phenotype featuring predominant cerebellar ataxia with slow progression, universal cerebellar atrophy, long survival, hearing loss, and executive deficits associated with the novel variant c.22G>A (p.Val8Ile) in the BCAP31 gene. In addition, we investigated pathogenicity in SH‐SY5Y cells transfected with this variant to assess subcellular location and its impact on calcium homeostasis.

This study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (EPN dnr 2016/2538–32), patients provided oral and written consent. A comprehensive characterization that included clinical and psychometric evaluations, neuroimaging studies, neurophysiological tests, and genotyping of a family affected by X‐linked recessive ataxia was carried out. Cognitive tests were administered on four men (III:4, III:6, V:2, and V:3; see Supplementary Data S1).

This is a Swedish family whose index case (V:3) was initially investigated for nucleotide expansions associated with the most common ataxias. Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed as a duo study in the family analyzing the index case and his affected relative (III:6). Data processing was performed at Oxford Genome Technologies (http://www.ogt.co.uk/). Briefly, paired end sequencing libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer's instruction (TruSeq, Illumina, San Diego, CA), exonic sequences captured using Agilent SureSelect AllExon v4 (Agilent) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 instrument in high output mode (2 × 100 bp). Reads were mapped to the hg19 reference genome using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner, and variants were called using the Genome Analysis Toolkit. Variants were filtered for X‐linked variants. The detected variants were further filtered by selecting those found in genes associated with clinical symptoms in the OMIM database, and variants were discarded if the phenotype in OMIM was not relevant. Detected variants predicted to be causing the patients symptoms were confirmed using Sanger sequencing. Carrier testing in the family was performed using Sanger sequencing. The index case was investigated with a muscle biopsy, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) from the muscle sample was also analyzed. A lumbar puncture was carried out on the index case and in patient III:4.

Plasmid DNA, BCAP31‐tGFP (RG226633), was bought from Origene (Rockville, MD). BCAP31‐tGFP mutant was performed with the designed primer (Forward: GTGGACTGCAaTTGCCACCTT Reverse: TGCAGACTCATGGCGATC) and the Q5 Site‐Directed Mutagenesis Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, E0552S). The plasmids ER‐mRFP was a gift from Erik Snapp (Addgene plasmid 62236; RRID: Addgene_62,236).
22



SH‐SY5Y, obtained from ATCC (CCL‐2266 Homo sapiens neuroblastoma), were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium F12 (DMEM F12) supplemented with high glucose, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (final concentration 100 U/mL of penicillin and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin), 10 mM 4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM L‐GlutaMAX and 1 × non‐essential amino acids (NEAA) (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) at 37°C, in 5% CO2 and split 1:10 every 4 to 7 days. Three clonal cell lines derived from the same parent SH‐SY5Y cell line were used for transient expression experiment. Transfection and imaging were replicated three times with each clonal from different passage numbers.

For transient expression during live cell imaging, wild type SH‐SY5Y cells were seeded in eight‐well chambered (Nunc Lab‐Tek 155411 or Nunc Lab‐Tek 155409) at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well. Transfections were performed by adding 80 ng (BCAP31‐tGFP or its mutant together with ER‐mRFP) of each plasmid using 1 μL Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per well. Live cell imaging was performed at 18 to 24 hours after transfection.

For transient expression during calcium imaging, wild type SH‐SY5Y cells were seeded in 35 mm cell culture dish (VWR, 734–2814) at a density of 3 × 104 cells/mL. Transfections were performed by adding 2 mg of BCAP31‐tGFP or its mutant using 10 μL Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Calcium imaging was performed at 18 to 24 hours after transfection.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) imaging of live cells expressing BCAP31 genetically fused with tGFP, was performed using the Zeiss Airyscan 880 inverted microscope system equipped with an Ar‐ion laser (with three lines: 458 nm, 488 nm, and 514 nm) and a 633 nm He‐Ne laser, a Plan Apochromat 40/1.4 oil immersion objective, main dichroic beam splitter 488/561/633, emission filters BP 493 to 598 nm (turbo green fluorescent protein (tGFP)), and BP 580‐620 nm (monomeric red fluorescent protein (mRFP)), and GaAsP PMT detector. To minimalize signal cross‐talk, the multitrack acquisition mode was used. Image post‐processing was not applied, except for contrast and brightness adjustment applied to the image, using the ZEN 2 software (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Calcium imaging in cell cultures was performed by loading the cells with the Ca2+‐sensitive fluorochrome Fura‐2‐acetoxymethyl ester (Fura‐2/AM) (5 μM, Thermo Fisher) together with 0.1% Pluronic F‐127 (Thermo Fisher) at 37°C for 20 minutes in cell culture medium. Measurement of intracellular Ca2+ was carried out in a Krebs‐Ringer buffer containing 119.0 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 1.3 mM MgCl2, 1.0 mM NaH2PO4, 20.0 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), and 11.0 mM dextrose at 37°C using a heat‐controlled chamber (QE‐1, Warner Instruments, Hamden, CT) with a cooled EMCCD Cascade II:512 camera (Photometrics, Tucson, AZ) mounted on an upright microscope (Carl Zeiss) equipped with a 20×/1.0NA lens (Carl Zeiss). Excitation at 340 and 380 nm was assessed with a wavelength switcher (DG4, Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA) at sampling frequency 0.5 Hz. Emission light was recorded at 510 nm. MetaFluor (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) was used to control the whole equipment and to analyze the collected data. Cyclopiazonic acid (10 μM, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was applied to inhibit sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+‐ATPase (SERCA) activity. The cytosolic calcium content was measured as the difference between the baseline 340/380 ratio before cyclopiazonic acid (CPA) addition and the peak 340/380 ratio after CPA addition. This value was normalized to 1.0 for untreated cells. The recordings were analyzed using three parameters: the maximum fluorescence amplitude (the height of each peak minus baseline), area under the curve (AUC, time from 144 seconds to 750 seconds) and slope at half‐maximal amplitude (rate of signal increase during time to peak).

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Numbers of cells analyzed in each graph are given in the corresponding figure legend. Two‐tailed Student's t test, Welch's test, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test were used to compare two groups. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Differences were significant when P < 0.05.

The pedigree is shown in Figure 1, and clinical data presented in Table 1. Briefly, this is a six generations family with seven men affected by X‐linked ataxia, one of the affected men was likely misdiagnosed as affected with parkinsonism (III:3), three other men were assigned as symptomatic based on history provided by their relatives (I:1, III:1, and III:2), for details see Supplementary Data S1. We examined seven patients (five men and two women). Hearing loss was congenital in three affected men and diagnosed in a fourth man approximately age 20 years, therefore, hearing loss preceded ataxia in all cases. The oldest living man with ataxia in this family went through cochlea implant surgery at age 58 years. None of the two examined women had ataxia, but one of them has moderate SNHL and recommended hearing devices. Eye bottom examination on the index case (V:3) and in patient III:4 was normal and two patients have nystagmus (V:3 and III:6). All male patients harboring the same genetic variant but one (V:2) suffered from cerebellar ataxia. Onset of ataxia was during teen age or early adulthood (mean age of onset was 20 years). Cognitive deficits were also found in all the four tested men, both women declined neurophysiological tests and cognitive assessments. Ability to walk was preserved late into the course of disease and life‐expectancy was not affected. All men with ataxia had severe cerebellar atrophy (Fig. 2A–D), this atrophy was mild in the only three patients without ataxia, one man and both women (V:2, III:7, and IV:2) (Fig. S1A–C). FDG‐PET displayed cerebellar hypometabolism in two men with ataxia (patients V:3 and III:t6) (Fig. 2A,B,E,F). Strength and reflexes were preserved in the index case and patients V:2, IV: 2 and III:7. The two oldest patients with ataxia display areflexia, loss of vibration sense in the malleolus and have axonal and demyelinating neuropathy, both were affected by type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Additionally, neurophysiological tests in these patients revealed prolonged latencies in motor evoked potentials (MEP) and sensory evoked potentials (SEP). Mild myopathic alterations were found in three patients (Table 1). The mild abnormal quantitative sensory testing in in the index case may suggest incipient small fiber neuropathy (SFN). One patient displays mild posturing and impaired finger taps (III:4), muscle tone was normal in all cases. The main phenomenology findings are shown in video files S1 and S2. There is no documentation reporting delayed motor and language development in any of the tested patients. Neither did we find any dysmorphism, cholestatic liver disease, or hypomyelination. Only one of the patients has a thick skull and two fused cervical vertebrae (Table 1); none of the patients suffered either from recurrent fever episodes or were prone to infections.

The pattern of cognitive dysfunction in all the four men was slow information processing speed, deficits in different executive functions were identified in patients III:4, III:6 and V:3, details provided in Table S1 and in Supplementary Data S1.

After ruling out nucleotide expansions with spinocerebellar ataxia type 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, and dentatorubral‐pallidoluysian atrophy, analysis of the FMR1 gene and array comparative genomic hybridisation (aCGH) were also normal (see Supplementary Data S1). WES identified a novel variant in the BCAP31 gene; BCAP31 (NM_005745.8): c.22G>A, p.(Val8Ile). This variant segregates with disease in this family, a healthy man in this family (V:1) does not harbor the BCAP31 variant. This variant is absent in two databases (ClinVar and Human Gene Mutation Database), but found in one female in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) (see Supplementary Data S1). In the muscle specimen from the index case, the variant m.15639 T>C in cytochrome b (MT‐CYB) was found at a low level of heteroplasmy (10%), this variant was also found at the same low level in both blood and fibroblasts; no other variant in mtDNA were found. This MT‐CYB variant is reported in homoplasmy in 12 healthy individuals in gnomAD and is also found in two other databases (Mitomap and Helix). It is not present in the index case's mother. The fact that his maternal grandfather was also affected by ataxia, dysarthria, and hearing loss, also supports the notion that this mtDNA variant is indeed benign. Variants in AIFM1, also associated with hearing loss with or without ataxia, were not found. Other non‐synonymous variants identified in the genes located on the X chromosome are shown in the Supplementary Data S1.

In all patients, hemoglobin levels, white blood cell count, liver transaminases, alkaline phosphatase, gamma‐glutamyl transferase (CGT), albumin, creatine kinase (CK), myoglobin, alpha‐fetoprotein, lactate dehydrogenase (LD), bilirubin, α‐tocopherol, cobalamin, folic acid, and lactate were normal. Antibodies against transglutaminase, glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) and paraneoplastic antibodies were absent, a wide screening for neurometabolic diseases was also normal. A muscle biopsy in the index case demonstrated normal morphology and activity in all the mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analyses in two men with ataxia (V:3 and III.4) showed normal cell count and protein levels, biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease were normal in both cases. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), neurofilament light protein, chemokine (C‐X‐C motif) ligand 13 (CXCL13) in the CSF were also normal in both cases. Monoamine metabolites in the CSF were normal in III:4, but 5‐hydroxy indoleacetic acid (5‐HIAA) was slightly reduced in patient V:3 (see Supplementary Data S1).

Prior evidence suggested that cleavage of BCAP31 at the ER can trigger higher cytosolic Ca2+ concentration. Transfection efficiency and expression level were analyzed based on tGFP fluorescent intensity of more than 10 field of views and 60 cells separately, no significant difference was found between wild‐type and V8I BCAP31 (Fig. S2). We investigated whether this pathogenic BCAP31 variant altered Ca2+ homeostasis by loading transfected cells with Fura‐2/AM and measuring the increase in cytosolic Ca2+ that results from CPA‐induced depletion of ER stores (Fig. 4). CPA invokes a rapid emptying of ER Ca2+ stores to the cytosol by inhibiting SERCA pumps that normally maintain the concentration of ER Ca2+ several orders of magnitude above that of the cytosol. To quantify signal changes in live cells, CPA‐induced Ca2+ increase in wild‐type and mutant were compared using the following parameters: the maximum fluorescence amplitude, AUC and slope at half‐maximal amplitude.

The maximum fluorescence amplitude and AUC of mutant after addition of CPA (10 μM) revealed a significant increment in cytosolic Ca2+ when compared with the wild‐type, indicating the different ER Ca2+ capacity between wild‐type and mutant. The mutant has a slope greater than wild‐type after addition of CPA (10 μM), suggesting increased Ca2+ flux. These results suggest that the single amino acid mutation in BCAP31 interfere Ca2+ homeostasis.

Based on CLSM imaging results, the wild‐type and V8I BCAP31 display a comparable distribution pattern and were localized to the ER. However, we found V8I BCAP31 expressing cells have an increment in cytosolic Ca2+ concentration after CPA treatment. Caspase cleavage product of BCAP31 induces higher cytosolic Ca2+ signals and the release of ER Ca2+ affects the mitochondria. Inhibition of Ca2+ transmission between the ER and mitochondrial affect mitochondrial morphology and redistribution of cytochrome c.
21
 In this study, the single amino acid mutation changed Ca2+ homeostasis and has an increment in cytosolic Ca2+. We assume that the V8I BCAP31 variant will also affect mitochondria through Ca2+ release from the ER. Moreover, BCAP31 is involved in the ER‐mitochondrial crosstalk by interaction with Fission 1 homolog.
24
 In general, it might be reasonable to think that BCAP31 deficiency possibly results in mitochondrial dysfunction. This impact may occur at the central nervous system level because we did not find any evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction in the muscle. Our findings notably expand the spectrum of syndromes associated with variants in BCAP31 to include slowly progressive ataxia with preserved ability to walk, universal cerebellar atrophy, and hearing loss. We did not find evidence of ID among our patients as seen in DDCH, but a pattern of cognitive deficits emerges in our evaluation with reduced information processing speed and executive dysfunction. Absence of ataxia in one man with hearing loss (V:2) and mild cerebellar atrophy, constitutes a forme fruste of this new X‐linked syndrome. Our findings are also in notorious contrast with the early demise initially reported in some patients with DDCH.

Impaired calcium homeostasis in PC is one of the major pathogenesis themes for other genetic diseases such as spinocerebellar ataxia type 6, and type 15 for which the underlying mutations is in calcium channel genes CACNA1A and ITPR1.
30

31
 Other ataxia diseases affecting genes upstream the type 1 inositol 1,4,5‐triphosphate receptor (IP3P1), the gene product of ITPR1, display alterations in calcium homeostasis in PCs.
31
 Whether this is the case in patients with the novel X‐linked ataxia disease we report here remain to be studied. The main limitation of our work is absence of neuropathological data and that the female patients did not go through cognitive assessment and neurophysiological tests. Other limitations are absence of clinical documentation in patient from earlier generations and the inherent bias when age of onset is estimated in long retrospect.

This is the first time alterations in cytosolic Ca2+ are demonstrated in a missense BCAP31 variant. Taken together, variants in BCAP31 include a wide spectrum of disease raging from an early and severe non‐progressive neurodevelopmental syndrome associated with ID, dysmorphism, and hearing loss to a late‐onset ataxia syndrome with slow progression and variable degree of cognitive impairment. The reasons for this reduced penetrance and variable expressivity are, as for many other genetic syndromes, still unknown. The role of genetic modifiers needs to be explored for this growing spectrum; both loss of function (LOF) and missense BCAP31 variants are associated with DDCH, although LOF seem to confer a more severe phenotype.
7
 Other than that, clear‐cut genotype–phenotype correlations are not possible to establish yet for the conditions associated with BCAP31 variants. It is also difficult to discern any predictors of early death in DDCH, most of the patients who died early harbored LOF variants in BCAP31 and suffered from recurrent fever episodes, and/or infections.
