# Unveiling MYH2-related myopathy: Histological-genetic insights from a case series and systematic review.
**Authors:** Labella B, Brochier G, Beuvin M, Lacene E, Chanut A, Madelaine A, Labasse C, Méneret A, Roos A, Kölbel H, Levine A, Yoon G, Svahn J, Bouhour F, Streichenberger N, Nadaj-Pakleza A, Malfatti E, Bassez G, Behin A, Laforet P, Villar-Quiles RN, Leonard-Louis S, Maisonobe T, Stojkovic T, Eymard B, Romero NB, Padovani A, Filosto M, Cassandrini D, Biancalana V, Rendu J, Polavarapu K, Métay C, Evangelista T
**Journal:** J Neuromuscul Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1177/22143602251393910](https://doi.org/10.1177/22143602251393910)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in the MYH2 gene are associated with congenital myopathy. We report 13 unrelated patients and a systematic review of published cases through 30 October 2024, supplemented by HGMD Pro (2024.2) and LOVD (https://www.lovd.nl/) databases. In the case series, most patients (n = 11/13, 84.6%) exhibited clinical symptoms from early childhood to middle adulthood. The mean age of onset was 22.8 years (vs 7.73 years in literature review), likely because of absence of birth contractures, described in early reports. External ophthalmoparesis, though not a defining feature, should raise suspicion of MYH2-related myopathy in patients with a CPEO-like presentation and limb-girdle weakness. Notably, two patients presented with postural tremor of the upper limbs as their first symptom. Although rimmed vacuoles were a characteristic feature in initial reports of MYH2-related myopathy, they were absent in our case series. The most informative stain was ATPase, detecting the lack or reduction of type 2A fibres. Electron microscopy revealed additional features, such as the presence of cores associated with rods in one patient and pathological subsarcolemmal mitochondrial accumulations in 2 patients. Fifteen novel variants were identified amongst the 13 patients of this study, expanding the current genetic landscape for MYH2-related muscle diseases. The phenotypic and histological diversity observed can not be fully accounted for by genetic factors alone, suggesting the presence of additional contributing factors. This wide variability may lead to under-recognition of this disease, thus the importance of clinicians' awareness on the topic.

Myosins are a large family of more than 20 actin-dependent enzymes involved in muscle contraction by adenosine triphosphate (ATP) hydrolysis, cell mobility, cytokinesis, cell polarization and signal transduction.1,2 The head domain sequence similarity and domain organization differentiate the functions of myosins.2–4 The expression of the different myosin heavy chain (MyHCs) isoforms in muscle tissue affects the contractile activity of fibers.2,4 Myosin II is the prototype of skeletal muscle myosin.2,3,5,6 It is a hexamer composed of two MyHCs and two pairs of myosin light chains (MCLs) and two regulatory light chain subunits (MCL-2).1–3,5,6 The myosin head is located in the N-terminal part of the protein and is divided into an actin-binding motor head responsible for ATPase activity and a neck domain (or light chain binding domain) that acts as a lever responsible for filament sliding during contraction.1,3,5,6 The C-terminal sequence forms an α-helix that dimerises with a second MyHC to form a coiled coil tail, which is subdivided into subfragment-2 (S2, first third of the tail) and light meromyosin (LMM, two thirds).1,2,5,6
MYH2 encodes the MyHC-IIA isoform expressed in muscle fast type 2A fibers.
3
 In 1998, Darin et al. described a new autosomal dominant myopathy in a large family with 19 affected individuals.
7
 The clinical features were similar to multi-minicore disease, but the presence of ophthalmoplegia and joint contractures at birth suggested a novel entity.
7
 Although the gene was not initially identified, the presence of rimmed vacuoles with cytoplasmic and intranuclear inclusions in muscle biopsies suggested a link to the hereditary inclusion body myopathy spectrum.
7
 Subsequent research localized the gene to a 2-Mb region on chromosome 17p13, marking the first myopathy associated with a mutation in a fast MyHC isoform.
8
 Over the past twenty years, additional autosomal dominant and recessive pathogenic variants in the MYH2 gene have been identified, revealing a more complex genetic landscape.
9


In the present study, we describe a case series of 13 unrelated patients with different clinical, histological and genetic patterns that expand the spectrum of MYH2-related myopathy. Moreover, we performed a systematic literature review to summarize the current literature on the neuropathological features and the related genetic variants.

We report on thirteen patients referred to the Neuromuscular Morphology Unit of the Myology Institut and the Pitie-Salpetriere Hospital in Paris (France), Neuromuscular Morphology Unit of the Henri Mondor Hospital in Créteil (France), Neuromuscular reference center in Strasbourg (France), Neuromuscular Unit of the Hospital of Lyon (France), Paediatric Unit of Neurology in Essen (Germany) and the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto (Canada) for muscle biopsy. Data on clinical evaluations and ancillary studies performed during medical follow-up was collected retrospectively.

The patients gave informed consent for muscle biopsy, genetic analysis and reuse of biological samples for research. Open muscle biopsies were obtained from the deltoid muscle or vastus lateralis. The specimens were snap frozen in isopentane cooled in liquid nitrogen for histoenzymology and immunohistochemistry (IHC) and included in epon for electron microscopy (EM).10,11 Co-staining with antibodies against Myosin (DSHB BA-D5 AB_2235587, DSHB SC-71 AB_2147165, DSHB 6H1 AB_2314830) was performed at the Myology Institute in Paris for 7 patients.

Digital photographs of the biopsies were obtained with AxioCam HRc HIS-0043, ZEISS for the histoenzymology staining's and AxioCam 305 mono, ZEISS for the immunofluorescence. Electron microscopy was carried out on all patients except one. Ultra-thin sections (80 nm in thickness) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, observed using a JEOL 1400 Flash transmission electron microscope (120 kV, Japan) and were photo documented using a Xarosa digital camera (imaging software RADIUS).

MYH2 gene was analyzed on genomic DNA from peripheral blood using next-generation sequencing (NGS)–based gene panels or exome study. If a likely pathogenic or pathogenic MYH2 variant was identified, direct gene sequencing was performed subsequently to confirm the presence of the variant. Details of genetic analysis are provided in Supplementary data. Each variant was classified following the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) classification and described using the HGVS nomenclature.12,13

A systematic search was conducted using Pubmed, Scopus, Science Direct and Cochrane Library databases to identify MYH2-related myopathy reports published up to 30 October 2024. This search was completed by reviewing the MYH2 variants available in the HGMD Pro (HGMD® Professional 2024.2) and LOVD databases (https://www.lovd.nl/). The keywords “myopathy [MeshTerms] AND MYH2”, “myopathy [MeshTerms] AND myosin heavy chain IIa” “histology AND MYH2”, “myosin [MeshTerms] AND external ophthalmoplegia” and “mutation [MeshTerms] AND MYH2” were used to query each database (title-abstract-keywords). The process for record selection followed The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was applied for screening, inclusion and exclusion of articles.
14
 Two researchers independently screened all studies for eligibility based on title, abstract, and full text. Eligibility criteria included (1) description of clinical features of cases defined by the authors as MYH2-related myopathy, supported by (2) genetic test results. Articles that provided only genetic or muscle pathology data were excluded, except if they had been previously described in literature by the authors and clear reference was provided in the article. No quality threshold was applied to study selection, given the limited number of publications meeting the review criteria. Descriptive data were analysed using Microsoft Excel 2010 and SPSS v. 26.0. Due to heterogeneity between studies in terms of outcomes, outcome tool assessment, study design, and study setting, the results were analyzed qualitatively.

Main clinical findings and investigations performed are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of onset was 22.8 years with a wide range from 2 months to 54 years. Most patients became clinically affected from early childhood to middle adulthood (n = 11/13, 84.6%). A slight prevalence of male sex is noticed (males, n = 8/13, 61.5%).

The most frequently reported presenting symptom was slowly progressive proximal muscle weakness in 7/13 cases (53.8%). Two cases of infantile onset (Patient 8 and 10) presented with motor developmental delay and muscle hypotonia without respiratory involvement. Patient 10 also had feeding difficulties, requiring a G-tube for nutrition since 10 months of age. Two patients (Patients 3 and 5) presented with unusual clinical symptoms and are described in more detail. Patient 3 was almost asymptomatic at diagnosis and underwent clinical examination after increased creatine kinase (CK) levels were found while undergoing investigation for postural tremor. Neurological evaluation was normal except for a marked limitation of upward gaze. Patient 5 underwent neurological evaluation for cervical pain and postural/intentional tremor of upper limbs. Mild ptosis, ophthalmoparesis and proximal muscle weakness were noticed at that point. No other extrapyramidal signs were identified and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was normal.

In our cohort, respiratory and cardiac examinations were unremarkable, except for Patient 6. This patient had a more complex phenotype characterised by epilepsy, implantation of a pacemaker for atrioventricular block at age 45, and surgery for bilateral cataracts at age 42. This patient was previously tested for myotonic dystrophy type 1 and 2, centronuclear myopathies, desminopathy and mitochondrial diseases.

Mean delay from clinical symptoms onset to muscle biopsy was 12.8 years (range 0 to 35 years). The main findings of muscle biopsies are summarized in Table 2 and Figure 1. Increased variability of fibre size, with multiple atrophic fibres of mostly round shape, was the most frequent histological change (n = 10/13, 76.9%). In around one third of the cases (n = 5/13, 38.3%) a few hypertrophic fibres were also observed. Abnormal nuclear positioning (n = 10/13, 76.9%- Figure 1(a)) was a common feature, either as nuclear internalization (n = 4/10, 40%) or centralisation (n = 6/10, 60%). None of the samples showed rimmed vacuoles. Seven cases (53.8%) exhibited mildly increased endomysial connective tissue. Fatty replacement was only noticed in the oldest patient of our cohort (Patient 2 - Figure 1(a)) and mildly in Patient 12. No inflammation or significant necrosis was observed. Although the intermyofibrillar network was well preserved in most cases (n = 8/13, 61.5%), Patient 1 displayed mild focal disorganizations and Patient 4, 11 and 13 exhibited a mini-core-like pattern of type I fibres in standard oxidative staining (Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide - NADR) (Figure 1(b)).

The routine ATPase stain revealed a marked type I fibre predominance in ten of the 13 patients (76.9%, Patients 1,4-8,10-12). The immunohistochemistry allowed for a better characterization of the fibre types and additionally demonstrated a complete lack of type II fibres in Patient 3 and severely reduced type IIA fibres in Patient 2. In the case of Patient 9, no clear predominance of fibre type was identified. The immunohistochemical myosin triple staining carried out in Patients 1-7 evidenced a notable reduction in the number of type IIA fibres in all patients, as well as the complete absence of type II fibres in Patient 3 (Table 3). It was observed that patients who exhibited symptoms during middle adulthood (Patients 1 and 5) tended to have a higher number of type IIA fibres compared to those with an earlier age of onset. The presence of hybrid fibres co-expressing different types of myosin was observed in 6 patients. These hybrid fibres were either co-expressing myosin IIX and IIA (n = 3/7, 42.8% - Figure 1(d)) or myosin I and IIA (n = 3/7, 42.8%).

The EM studies revealed variability of fibre size and an increased number of nuclear internalization or centralisations in most of patients (n = 9/12, 75%). Patients that lacked these features (Patient 9, 10 and 11) had infantile or early childhood onset. In four cases (Patient 1, 2, 5, 9, 13) we have found scattered focal disorganization of the Z lines (Figure 2(a)). The latter may manifest as a focal streaming of the Z-line or involve the entirety of one or more sarcomeres, resulting in a multi-core/core pattern in Patient 2 and 13 (Figure 2(b)). However, more heterogeneous alterations were found in a subset of patients. Patient 1 disclosed not only focal disorganization but also areas of sarcomeric disorganisation that comprised the entire diameter of the muscle fibres (Figure 2(d)). Although rods were found in markedly atrophic fibres in several patients (2, 4-7), they were also identified in clusters in mildly atrophic fibres in Patient 1 (Figure 2(e)). Two fibres showed stacking and expansion of the cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum as well. EM for Patient 3 was characterized by the presence of swollen sarcoplasmic reticulum and a pathological accumulation of mitochondria within the subsarcolemmal region or within the fibres themselves (Figure 2(f)). In the regions surrounding the nuclei, the presence of small debris, in particular lipofuscin, and glycogen was noticed. These findings were similar to those noticed in EM for Patient 9. Patients 5-8 and 10 also exhibited subsarcolemmal mitochondrial accumulations, though they were typically less pronounced.

Genetic data describing the 18 variants detected in the 13 patients in the present study can be found in Table 4. They are mostly novel variants that have never been reported so far (n = 15/18, 83.3%, Table 4).

Only two patients had autosomal dominant variants (c.4052A > T, p.(Glu1351Val) in Patient 1 and c.5673 + 1G > C, p.(Thr1860_Ala1891del) in Patient 2), both located in the coiled coil region. The variant c.4052A > T is a novel missense variant (not reported in HGMD Pro and LOVD databases or published) with supportive in-silico predictions, which we now classify as a VUS under ACMG/AMP criteria due to the absence of segregation and functional data. Conversely, c.5673 + 1G > C has previously been documented in the literatureas a dominant pathogenic variant, with patients exhibiting a similar phenotype to Patient 2, namely the absence of ophthalmoparesis.15,16

Among recessively inherited MYH2-related myopathies (n patients = 11/13, 84.6%), variants were most frequently homozygous (n patients = 7/11, 63.6%). They were all novel variants except for c.533C > T, p.(Thr178Ile) in Patient 6 and c.2377C > T, p.(Arg793*) in Patient 11. The five patients showing heterozygous composite variants are described in detail. Patient 5 had three unreported variants, two located in the motor head domain (c.1306G > A, p.(Glu436Lys) and c.1381G > A, p.(Gly461Arg)) and the third (c.5404C > T, p.(Arg1802Cys)) in the coiled-coil tail domain. All variants have very low allelic frequency (GnomAD v2) and pathogenicity bioinformatics scores are in favor of the variants being pathogenic. Only the unaffected mother and sister were available for segregation studies. The mother was found to carry the variant c.1306G > A and the sister the other two variants, suggestive of an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. Unfortunately, the father's testing could not be performed as he was deceased and there were no other available relatives. We may assume that the other two variants were most probably inherited from the deceased father or less probably are de novo variants.

Patient 7 carried two variants, c.349-1G > T, p.? and c.2104_2111del, p.(Asn702Alafs*8) both located in motor head domain. In that domain, another study reported c.2400delG, p.(Phe801Serfs*28) a small deletion resulting in a premature stop codon associated with more severe phenotype and pediatric onset but in the homozygous state.
17


Patient 12 carried the c.2793_2794del, p.Arg931Serfs*2 and c.4510del, p.Leu1504* variants. He represents the first documented case in the literature of compound heterozygous deletions in the MYH2 gene.

In our two AD cases with coiled-coil tail variants (Patients 1–2), rimmed vacuoles were absent and pathology was dominated by type I fibre predominance with marked reduction of type IIA fibres on ATPase/IHC. As well, AR cases with motor-head variants, tail or mixed-domain variants all share a spectrum from severe IIA reduction to complete absence of type II fibres. EM seems to more likely show subsarcolemmal mitochondrial accumulations in AR cases. Comparing zygosity within AR inheritance, we did not observe systematic differences between homozygous and compound heterozygous cases in the hallmark type IIA fibre reduction or EM patterns.

Key words were used to query each database (title-abstract-keywords) according to PRISMA guidelines, resulting in a total of 347 records as illustrated in Figure 3. Duplicate records or articles outside of the scope of the study (n = 306) were excluded and 41 records were retrieved for screening. The abstracts of these records were manually screened to identify publications that included original studies (case report or case series) on human MYH2-related myopathy and 17 publications were excluded because of article type. The full text of the remaining 24 records were examined and all but one matched eligibility criteria.15–37 Review of MYH2 variant using HGMD Pro (HGMD® Professional 2024.2) and LOVD databases (https://www.lovd.nl/), allowed us to retrieve one additional article.

Overall, 86 patients affected by MYH2-related myopathy are described in literature. Where sex was indicated, the majority of patients were male (52 vs. 33). Age at onset data is available for 61 patients, while the type of onset symptoms is documented in 55 cases. The average age at which symptoms initially manifested was 7.73 years. This is consistent with the observation that, in the earliest reports, joint contractures at birth were classified as the initial symptom. The older patient in this systematic review experienced first symptoms in middle-adulthood (36 years). Overall, the most frequently reported presenting symptom was slowly progressive muscle weakness (n = 20/55, 36.6%). Neonatal onset (n = 26/61, 42.6%) was mainly associated with joint contractures (n = 18) and/or difficulty in feeding (n = 9). Neurological examination was mainly characterized by the presence of proximal muscle weakness (n = 74/86, 86%) and ophthalmoparesis (n = 75/86, 87.2%), together with facial weakness (n = 55/86, 63.9%). Ptosis was noticed in less than one third of patients (n = 17/86, 19.7%). Axial involvement, especially in neck flexor muscles, was also frequent (n = 49/86, 56.9%). Distal muscle involvement is unneglectable (n = 36/86, 41.8%) and is usually isolated in the upper limbs (n = 31/36, 86.1%). Dysphagia (n = 4/86, 4.6%) and respiratory involvement (n = 2/86, 2.3%) at the time of neurological examination were rare. CK levels mostly ranged from normal value to 5x upper limit value. EMG performed in 22 patients was consistent with a myopathic pattern with short and brief motor unit potentials. Only twelve patients underwent muscle MRI that disclosed mainly atrophy and fat substitution in the anterior and posterior thigh compartments (n = 11/12, 91.6%) and gastrocnemii (n = 8/12, 66%), followed by gluteal muscles involvement (n = 4/12, 33%). In Supplementary Table 1, we provide a detailed description of clinical features and histology of 45 patients. The morphology was observed in 55 muscle biopsies, given that a small number of patients underwent multiple biopsies over the course of the disease. Despite considerable heterogeneity across studies, the predominant histological features observed were variability in fibre size (n = 39/55, 70.9%), internalised nuclei (n = 23/55, 41.8%) and type I fibre predominance or uniformity (n = 36/55, 65.4%). Rimmed vacuoles were only observed in seven samples (12.7%). In nineteen samples (34.5%), various degrees of intermyofibrillar network disorganisation were observed. This finding is consistent with the results of EM studies (n = 20/55, 36.3%), which predominantly report the presence of focal or extensive areas of sarcomere disorganisation (n = 10/20, 50%). Nuclear or cytoplasmic inclusions are reported (n = 5/20, 25%) and rods have been identified in only one case (5%).

Overall, twenty-eight different genetic variants are described in the Supplementary Table 2. In terms of the nature of variants, the majority were missense (n = 10/28, 35.7%) followed by nonsense variants (n = 8/28, 28.6%) and splicing variants (n = 6/28, 21.4%). Only four small deletions were reported (n = 4/28, 14.3%). Most variants were located in the motor head domain (n = 15/28, 53.6%). The majority of variants are inherited in a recessive autosomal pattern (n = 23/28, 82.1%).

Clinical, histological and genetic characteristics of MYH2-related myopathy are currently expanding. The age of onset is variable ranging from severe neonatal presentation to the third decade of life,15,19,21,23,25 but it can be even older according to the present study (fifth decade in Patient 2).

In line with literature, slow feeding and dysphagia may be the presenting features of infantile or early childhood onset as seen for Patient 10 and 11.17–19,22 None of the patients of this study presented with joint contractures, which were observed in the first reported cohort of MYH2-related myopathy but have been less commonly reported in subsequent studies.7,20,22 After the perinatal and neonatal period, slowly progressive proximal limb-girdle weakness is indeed the main presenting symptom, frequently accompanied by weakness of the neck flexors.15,26,28,33–35

Tissue expression of MyHC IIA is thought to contribute to the clinical phenotype, explaining the involvement of the neck flexor muscles (e.g., sternocleidomastoid) and ophtalmoparesis.33,39 Nevertheless, sparing of ocular movements is reported both in the present study and in literature.15,16 Therefore, external ophthalmoparesis should be regarded as a supportive clue rather than a defining hallmark of MYH2-related myopathy. When ocular involvement is present, it is frequently associated with facial weakness and/or ptosis.18,26,27,29,33,34 Isolated ptosis as observed in Patient 4 appears to be a rare phenomenon, yet it has also been documented by Hedberg-Oldfors C. et al.
16
 Ocular involvement is typically observed in childhood or may manifest progressively in young adulthood, thereby further expanding the range of potential differential diagnoses in these patients.
27
 The absence of fluctuating characteristics and the presence of no extramuscular signs (such as diabetes, cerebellar and extrapyramidal signs) may assist in discerning this condition from mitochondrial diseases or myasthenia.
40
 On the other hand, the differential diagnosis with congenital myopathies and muscular dystrophies, including oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy, may be more clinically complex.
41
 Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of the predominant symptoms, combined with histological findings such as the absence of inclusions, is the most effective diagnostic approach.

Movement disorders such as tremor must not be overlooked, as they were the first reported symptom in two of our patients and also described recently by Liao X et al.
32
 Although it was not the primary presenting symptom, a few cases in the past literature describe the presence of hand tremor.7,15 The concept of myogenic tremor has only been recently recognized and linked to variants in sarcomeric genes, as the tremor pacemaker is supposed to be located at the level of the myofilaments.
42
 In MYH2-related myopathies, patients mostly presented with a medium to high frequency postural tremor of the distal joints (fingers).7,32 This may be a consequence of either clinical or subclinical distal muscle weakness, as there are usually no signs of peripheral neuropathy associated and a considerable prevalence of upper limbs distal weaknees has been unveiled by our systematic review. However, it appears also to be present without any sign of distal muscle involvement, thus suggesting that sarcomere contractile abnormalies related to MYH2 variants could induce oscillations within the reflex arc and generating tremors.32,42

In contrast to the well-established correlation between MYH7-related myopathies and cardiomyopathy, multisystem involvement is not observed in MYH2-related myopathy due to the distinct distribution of MyHC isoforms in tissues.
9
 Restrictive respiratory disease can rarely be present, yet patients don’t usually require ventilatory support with the exception of the severe neonatal presentation.17,18 For this reason, the case of Patient 6 could be consider as atypical, given that he is affected by epilepsy and cardiac arrhythmia. However, one patient described by Tajsharghi H. et al. in 2014 (Patient C) also developed unknown status epilepticus and died unexpectedly of supposed cardiac arrhythmia in the context of coronary disease.
33
 It is noteworthy that Patient 6 exhibits the same variant described in another subject from the aforementioned paper (Patient A), who presented only with external ophthalmoplegia and muscle weakness. The absence of systemic involvement in this patient might be explained by the young age at last observation. Due to the current lack of knowledge and evidence we believe that cardiac and respiratory evaluations should be considered in the follow-up of these patients. Early-onset cataract as in Patient 6 is uncommon, but also reported in two other patients by Hernandez-Lain et al.
28
 An association between MYH2-related myopathy and ocular involvement should be proven by further data, but we suggest to investigate this possibly underestimate feature in future cases.

Laboratory and imaging studies did not identify any specific characteristics that could help with the diagnosis. CK levels are usually normal or only mildly elevated (<1000 UI/L).15,16,18,23,24,31,33,35 Muscle MRI findings may vary according to clinical severity and are not influenced by the type of the inheritance. The predominant involvement of semitendinosus muscle with relative sparing of the semimembranosus and the anterior compartment muscles as described in Patient 2 has been consistently noted in previous reports,18,19,26,30,34 yet fat substitution of gluteal muscles was a more common finding in our cohort. Moreover, MRI findings do not strictly correlate with clinical and pathological involvement, as demonstrated in Cabrera M. et al. and D’Amico et al. studies in which discrepancy between mild MRI involvement and relevant pathological changes of the muscles from anterior compartment have been described.17,18

Therefore, muscle biopsy is still a crucial tool to establish the diagnosis. Histological findings may range from mild fibre size variability to marked type I fiber predominance with disorganization of myofilaments, making it necessary to highlight the most typical features and to point out the factors that may influence the histological appearance. In early reports of MYH2-related myopathy, rimmed vacuoles were noted as a defining feature, which led to the term inclusion body myopathy 3 (IBM3), thought to be specific to autosomal dominant (AD) inheritance.
7
 Although only two patients (Patient 1 and 2) in our study carried dominantly-inherited pathogenic variants, neither patient exhibited rimmed vacuoles at histological evaluation consistent with two recent reports of the same c.5673 + 1G > C variant.15,16 Moreover, cases of AR inheritance characterized by the presence of rimmed vacuoles have been reported in literature.27,30 Disease duration appears to cause a dystrophic-like pattern, with increase of fatty and connective tissue.16,18,20,24,33 Disarray of the myofibrillar network is usually reported either in older patients (> 45 y.o.) or in patients harboring severe pathological changes and rimmed vacuoles.16,18,27,29,34 The most common histological features in our study were fibre size variability and increased nuclear internalization, consistent with the literature. Few hypertrophic fibres can be observed, likely related to a compensatory mechanism. The most informative stains to guide the diagnosis were the ATPase reactions, as they were able to detect type I fibre predominance and the lack or reduction of type IIA fibres which are the only pathological feature in some cases.
33
 Complementary immunohistochemistry of myosin isoforms can be useful in case of poorly differentiated fibres. In those cases, it is the only method to confirm the reduced number of type IIA fibres. Nevertheless, the presence of hybrid fibres either co-expressing types IIA and embryonic MyHC or IIA MyHC fibres and slow/beta cardiac myosin has been highlighted in past studies.16,28,29 In fact, morphological changes in adult cases are not restricted to type IIA fibres but can be seen in all muscle fibre types.16,17,30 That might be explained by the increased proportion of hybrid muscle fibres, expressing more than one MyHC isoform, which also occurs normally in aging.
22


EM was less frequently reported in literature, usually revealing focal disorganizations of myofilaments.18,19,21,22,30 In our experience it was very useful as it was able to detect small pathological changes even in muscle biopsies that appeared normal, as in Patient 9. Recently, EM performed in the Madigan et al. study showed a novel histological pattern with the presence of filamentous tangles or rods in type IIA and hybrid fibres.
29
 Indeed, the presence of rods in only mildly atrophic fibres was noticed also in Patient 1, yet they were restricted to the sarcoplasma and not associated with filamentous tangles. Rods were also found in other patients but usually localised in the very atrophic fibres. Interestingly, accumulation of mitochondria within the subsarcolemmal region was noted in two young patients (Patient 1 and 9). This finding has been previously reported by Cabrera-Serrano M et al.
18
 Mitochondrial morphology was observed to be normal, with no evidence of inclusions. It is unclear whether different degrees of activity could have a role in these cases, since the subjects were significantly different regarding disease severity. For instance, Patient 3 was almost asymptomatic, whereas Patient 9 and Cabrera-Serrano M.'s study subject exhibited moderate to severe phenotypes. In addition, Patient 1 and 9 displayed the presence of swollen sarcoplasmic reticulum. Recently, Sonne A. et al. demonstrated an increased adenosine triphosphate (ATP) demand of relaxed myosin heads expressing type II MyHC in the patients with MYH2 truncating mutations.
43
 This might impact on the skeletal muscle metabolism and, therefore, explain the EM findings previously described.

Heterogeneity of histological features could be related to multiple reasons. As previously underlined, disease duration and age of patients might influence the histopathological findings. This variation is likely due to differences in MyHC IIA expression patterns from fetal development through to adulthood, as well as residual production of defective myosin.
30
 For a similar reason, given that most human muscles are mixed in their fibre type composition, the location of the biopsy may play a role.39,44 Moreover, physical training is an important factor in the regulation of MyHC isoform expression in normal skeletal muscle, inducing a shift from type IIB to IIA fibres when subjected to endurance training.
24
 However, these factors per se are not sufficient to explain the broad heterogeneous scenario, which raises interest in the influence of genetic variants or epigenetic factors in the phenotype and histological findings.

The site of the mutation differently affects the function of type II myosin. Pathogenic variants in MYH2 usually cluster either with functional domains of motor head or in the coiled-coil tail region.27,29 They are either missense or affecting splicing site, as shown in Figure 4.

Regarding inheritance pattern, AD pathogenic variants are more commonly located in the coiled-coil tail region, as seen in Patients 1 and 2 of our study, whereas AR variants have a more widespread distribution and may affect different domains of the MyHC-IIA isoform.27,29 The patients who displayed more severe infantile onset exhibited variants in the IQ motif or coiled-coil tail region. If reduced MyHC isoform expression was the main determinant of clinical severity, we would expect that truncating variants would be associated with severe infantile or young adult onset forms. However, Patient 3, who harbours a nonsense pathogenic variant, was almost asymptomatic as a young adult. Also, D’Amico A et al. described a severe neonatal onset form associated with a missense mutation in the coiled coil site.
19
 We did not perform a transcript analysis of MyH-IIA expression and the available data in the literature are limited. Complete loss has been found both in severe and milder phenotypes, raising the question of whether the primary determinant of variant pathogenicity is not the absence of MyH-IIA expression itself, but rather the effect on the enzyme's functional domain.
34
 In fact, c.2116G > A missense variant in the SH1-2 helix domain has shown to be deleterious in Drosophila models by impacting the chemomechanical cycle of myosin–actin interaction.45,46

According to our review, the presence of rimmed vacuoles seems to be likely related either to motor head domain variants or heterozygous compound variants within the catalytic head and tail domains.
8
22–24,27,29,30 Furthermore, we propose that this histological finding is more indicative of disease progression (in patients over 30 years old) rather than an unusual early sign. In fact, the patient described by Telese R et al. showed rimmed vacuoles only in the second muscle biopsy performed as an adult.
30


Although ours is only the second report in the literature, we can confirm that the presence of rods can be a feature of MYH2-myopathy. From our study and from the literature review we cannot define a correlation between the causative variant and the presence of rods. The patient reported by Madigan et al. was compound heterozygous for variants in opposite regions of the MYH2 gene.
29
 Excluding the patients where rods were present only in very atrophic fibres, our patient (Patient 1) had a pathogenic variant in the coiled-coil tail region very close to the variant reported by Madigan et al.
29
 We may then speculate that this histological feature might be related to variants in the coiled-coil tail region. Nevertheless, c.3331C > T results in a premature STOP codon, whereas c.4052A > T is a missense variant.

According to the literature review, patients carrying the same pathogenic variant usually share a similar phenotype and histology. The only exception is the c.2400delG variant, which seems to be an outlier as it is responsible for a severe neonatal onset in the study by Tsabari et al. and a mild to moderate phenotype in the study by Lossos et al.25,36 However, intrafamilial variability in patients carrying the same pathogenic variant has also been reported.15,35 Differences between subjects are more often related to disease severity rather than clinical characteristics.
35
 This suggests that factors other than MYH2 genotype influence the phenotype, yet no modifier genes or allelic variations have been clearly identified to date.
20
 Myosin post-translational modiﬁcations could play a role in the expression of the myopathic phenotype.
39


Clinical, histological and genetic characteristics of MYH2-myopathy are currently expanding, revealing a heterogeneous picture. Joint contractures and ophthalmoplegia are no longer essential hallmarks of the diagnosis. MYH2-related myopathy may join the group of myopathies that are associated with myogenic tremor. As ancillary studies are not specific, muscle biopsy still plays an important role in these cases. Among the considerable range of histological abnormalities, the most significant feature is the absence of type IIA fibres. The initial observation of rimmed vacuoles in MYH2-related myopathy was considered a defining feature. However, this finding is becoming increasingly rare, and it appears to be most prevalent in a specific subset of patients, predominantly older individuals. This study constitutes the second report of the presence of rods, thereby corroborating the hypothesis that MYH2 patients exhibiting extensive areas of disorganized myofibrils may also display this additional neuropathological feature. EM study also showed mitochondrial accumulations and the presence of swollen sarcoplasmic reticulum that might support the idea that post-translational myosin modifications can impact skeletal muscle metabolism.

In conclusion, we summarized the main neuropathological features and expanded the clinical spectrum of MYH2-related myopathy. Most pathogenic variants occur in motor head domain and are either missense or affect splicing. No clear correlations between the type of variant and the severity of the disease was found, although coiled coil region seems to be associated with infantile severe onset.

More studies are needed to define the factors that may influence clinical and histological heterogeneity. Nevertheless, it is important that clinicians are aware of this rare entity.
