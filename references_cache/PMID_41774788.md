# Collectin-11 regulates osteoclastogenesis and bone maintenance via a complement-dependent mechanism.
**Authors:** Howard MC, Farrar CA, Nauser CL, Jeon Y, Polycarpou A, Smolarek D, Greenlaw R, Foster M, Garred P, Vizitiu DA, Mukhopadhyay S, Sacks SH
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2511950123](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2511950123)

## Content

The human developmental disorder 3MC syndrome (Malpuech, Michels, Mingarelli, and Carnevale) features skeletal deformities associated with a deficiency of the pattern recognition molecule collectin-11 (CL-11), yet the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms remain uncertain. Here, we demonstrate that CL-11 deletion alone does not produce skeletal abnormalities in mice; however, combined deficiencies of CL-11 with complement components MASP-2 (lectin pathway), complement factors B, or C3 (alternative pathway amplification) result in marked vertebral bone loss and spinal curvature by 12 wk of age. Ex vivo osteoclast (OCL) differentiation from bone marrow-derived cells of these double-knockout mice was profoundly impaired but was substantially restored by CL-11 supplementation. This dependence on CL-11 and complement was recapitulated in human OCLs derived from induced pluripotent stem cell lines. CL-11 and the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) colocalized to OCLs and their precursors in normal bone from embryonic development through to adulthood. Together, these findings identify CL-11 as a key regulator of osteoclastogenesis and bone homeostasis acting in concert with complement-mediated signaling, and they nominate CL-11 as a potential therapeutic target in conditions involving dysregulated osteoclast function and bone remodeling.

Collectin-11 (CL-11) is a soluble C-type lectin that—like other collectins of the lectin pathway, such as Collectin-10 and mannan-binding lectin (MBL)—recognizes carbohydrate motifs on both self and nonself structures to activate the lectin complement pathway (LP) (1). It plays key roles in innate immunity, inflammatory disorders, and embryogenesis (2). The widespread tissue expression of CL-11 suggests its fundamental biological importance across different cell populations (3).

The basic structural unit of CL-11 is a triple-chain complex, with each chain comprising a carbohydrate-recognition domain (CRD) and a collagen-like domain (CLD), separated by a neck region. These CL-11 complexes self-combine into dimers and trimers (100 to 200 kDa), enhancing carbohydrate-binding avidity. MBL-associated serine proteases (MASPs 1 to 3) physically associate with the CLD of CL-11, facilitating the formation of the classical pathway C3 convertase (C4bC2b), which leads to C3 cleavage (4, 5). MASP-2, besides cleaving C4 and C2, can directly cleave C3 (5–7). The MASP1 gene encodes three splice variants MASP-1, MASP-3, and MAP-1 (also known as MAp44), which contribute indirectly to the alternative pathway (AP) by activating factor D and thereby converting C3bB into C3bBb, the alternative pathway C3 convertase (8). Thus, CL-11/MASP complexes can initiate both classical and alternative C3 convertases at the ligand-binding site for CL-11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

The subsequent cleavage of C3 and C5 generates anaphylatoxins (C3a and C5a), the opsonin C3b, and membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9), promoting cell activation, damage, and cell death. Given that CL-11 is expressed in cells of ectodermal, endodermal, and mesenchymal origin (1, 2), this locally produced pattern-recognition molecule (PRM) may exert regional functions. Notably, mice lacking tissue CL-11 resist postischemic kidney injury (9). In humans, mutations in COLEC11 (or the related COLEC10 gene) cause 3MC syndrome (10–12), a developmental disorder characterized by craniofacial dysmorphia, cleft palate, stunted growth, and multiorgan defects (13). Mutations in MASP1 making MASP-3 inactive have also been identified in 3MC patients (10, 14), suggesting that disruption of complement activation—rather than a direct effect of PRMs on developing tissues—may underlie the syndrome’s pathology.

A role for complement in bone formation is further supported by evidence that complement factors B (CFB), C3, C5, and C9 are expressed in the growth plate, a specialized cartilaginous region at the ends of long bones essential for longitudinal growth and endochondral ossification (15, 16). Chondrocytes also express complement components C4, C2, and C3 (17). Moreover, mice lacking C3 or C5 exhibit impaired fracture healing, with C5 deficiency having a greater impact than C3 deficiency, highlighting the role of complement activation—particularly its terminal effector pathway—in bone remodeling (18). Osteoclast (OCL) formation may also be directly influenced by anaphylatoxins, even in the absence of key differentiation factors receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL) and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (19). More recently, studies in animal models have underscored the contribution of complement to axial spondyloarthritis, revealing a complex relationship with both bone formation and bone loss at vulnerable skeletal sites (20, 21).

To further investigate the interplay between the LP and AP triggered by CL-11, we generated double knockout (DKO) mice with disruptions in Colec11 and either Masp-2 (LP), Cfb (Complement factor B; AP), or C3 (common to both pathways). A striking proportion of the adult DKOs developed spinal curvature (kyphoscoliosis), whereas most single knockout (SKO) mice lacking CL-11, MASP-2, CFB, or C3 in isolation displayed no such skeletal phenotype. The present study determined how, when, and where CL-11 contributes to this unexpected skeletal phenotype.

We generated four groups of DKO mice: Colec11−/−/C3−/−; Colec11−/−/Masp-2−/−; Colec11−/−/Cfb−/−; C3−/−/Masp-2−/−). By 12 wk of age, kyphosis and/or scoliosis occurred in 23 to 28% of the three DKO groups lacking CL-11 (Colec11−/−/C3−/−; Colec11−/−/Masp-2−/−; Colec11−/−/Cfb−/−), compared to only 0 to 1% in wild-type (WT) mice, SKO mice, and DKO mice with intact CL-11 (C3−/−/Masp-2−/−) (Table 1). Gross dissection and skeletal staining confirmed the spinal deformity, revealing a marked loss of distinction between bone and cartilage (Fig. 1 A and B). Thus, the absence of CL-11 predisposed mice to the skeletal phenotype, but only in combination with a second deletion (C3, CFB, or MASP-2).

Postnatal emergence (≥12 wk) of the skeletal phenotype suggested a defect in bone maintenance rather than development, and so we focused on the role of the osteoclast (OCL)—a cell specializing in bone remodeling and sensitive to complement effectors. Using bone marrow–derived stem cells (BMSCs) originating from different genotypes, we examined the capacity for ex vivo differentiation into OCLs. WT BMSCs efficiently generated mature, multinucleated giant OCLs, defined by expression of the OCL-associated receptor OSCAR and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) positivity, and associated with increased CL-11 detection (Fig. 6 A–C). In contrast, Colec11−/−/C3−/− BMSCs exhibited pronounced reduction in multinucleated OCL formation, quantified by the microscopic area occupied by such cells (Fig. 6 D and E). Similarly, Colec11−/−/Cfb−/− progenitors largely failed to differentiate into mature OCLs (Fig. 6E and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). By comparison, OCLs derived from Colec11−/− SKO mice exhibited only modest reductions in OCL number and size (Fig. 6 D–G and SI Appendix, Fig. S5B).

Prompted by earlier reports linking terminal complement pathway activation to increased bone formation (22), we specifically examined MAC formation during OCL differentiation. MAC deposition was markedly impaired in Colec11−/−/C3−/− myeloid progeny, as assessed by C9 staining (Fig. 6 F and G). OCLs derived from Colec11−/− SKO mice instead showed only minor reduction in MAC decoration (Fig. 6 D–G). Together, these data indicate that efficient OCL differentiation requires CL-11–dependent complement activation supported by either C3 or CFB, converging on MAC formation.

We briefly considered the contribution of C5a receptor signalling downstream of terminal pathway activation, given prior evidence implicating C5a in OCL differentiation under inflammatory conditions (19). However, under the non-inflammatory conditions studied here, pharmacological inhibition of C5aR1 using the antagonist PMX53—either alone or in combination with CL-11 deficiency—had no significant effect on murine OCL differentiation from myeloid precursors (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), in keeping with a predominant role for MAC rather than C5a, in homeostatic osteoclastogenesis.

To determine whether CL-11 directly supports OCL differentiation, we supplemented the differentiation medium with recombinant CL-11 (rCL-11, 0.9 µg/mL) in Colec11−/−/C3−/− and Colec11−/−/Cfb−/− BMSC cultures. This treatment partially restored CL-11 binding and significantly increased the presence of multinucleated giant cell OCLs (Fig. 6 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Consistent with this observation, the average number of nuclei per OCL in rCL-11–treated Colec11−/−/C3−/− BMSCs increased to 12.6, compared with 6.6 in untreated Colec11−/−/C3−/− cells (P < 0.01) and 16 in WT cells (P < 0.001 versus untreated Colec11−/−/C3−/− cells). Cell-surface MAC formation was also partially rescued (Fig. 6 F and G). Notably, since rCL-11 restored MAC formation even in the absence of added C3 (Fig. 6F), this suggests an alternative, C3-bypass activation mechanism downstream of CL-11 that leads to activation of the terminal complement pathway.

Based on these ex vivo data, we hypothesized that CL-11–OCL interactions could promote complement activation and OCL differentiation under physiological conditions. To test this, we analyzed unmanipulated WT mouse bone sections for CL-11 and MAC deposition. Large multinucleated TRAP+ OCLs were strongly positive for CL-11 and for polymeric C9 confirmed with a TCC marker (Fig. 7A and SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). Similarly, in normal embryonic bone at different pre-OCL developmental stages, CL-11 and C9 localized to regions of cellular condensation within presumptive vertebral bodies (Fig. 7B).

These findings strongly support a functional relationship between CL-11, MAC assembly, and OCL differentiation. Our data indicate that CL-11 and complement regulate osteoclastogenesis from embryonic bone development through adult bone maintenance. We propose that chronic disruption of this pathway leads to vertebral pathology by 12 to 13 wk of age, as observed in our mouse model.

To confirm relevance, we performed complementary studies with OCLs differentiated from human-induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). CL-11 deletion resulted in marked impairment of OCL differentiation, which was partially rescued by supplementation of the differentiation medium with recombinant CL-11. In addition, treatment with Mirococept—a truncated complement receptor-1–based inhibitor of C3 and C5 convertases (23)—significantly reduced OCL formation, demonstrating that normal human OCL differentiation is complement-dependent.

Together, these data indicate that both CL-11 and complement activation regulate the differentiation of OCLs from human myeloid cells. Notably, the human system appears more sensitive to the individual effects of CL-11 and C3 than the mouse system, in which only their combined activity was impactful.

Our results demonstrate that mice lacking CL-11 develop age-related vertebral bone loss, but only in the absence of at least one additional key component of the lectin or alternative complement pathways. This dual deficiency was associated with impaired OCL differentiation in bone marrow cultures, which was largely restored by supplementation with recombinant CL-11 at physiological concentration. The presence of CL-11 and the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on osteoclasts and precursor cells in normal bone further supports the role of CL-11 as a physiological regulator of OCL differentiation, contributing to bone remodeling throughout life (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

Not all mice with the susceptible DKO genotype developed overt disease, likely reflecting an interplay between genetic and environmental factors, with manifest bone pathology observed in up to 28% of adults. This proportion may underestimate the true prevalence, as only DKO mice exhibiting overt phenotypes underwent CT imaging or histopathological analysis. Lesions were most prominent in the lumbo-thoracic vertebral spine, suggesting that repetitive mechanical loading during routine activities such as feeding and grooming may contribute to disease expression.

Bone is a dynamic tissue maintained by bone-forming osteoblasts (OBLs) and bone-resorbing OCLs, both of which interact with the complement system. OBLs, derived from mesenchymal lineage, secrete bone matrix before differentiating into osteocytes (24). OBLs are potential targets for C5a, as C5aR1 is involved in osteogenic differentiation and migration (19, 25). In contrast, OCLs arise from the monocyte-macrophage lineage and they degrade bone matrix through proteolytic enzymes and acidification (24). Deletion of CD59, an inhibitor of MAC formation, results in increased bone resorption and cortical bone excess due to elevated OCL differentiation, with no impact on OBL differentiation (22). Our data suggest that the complement system plays a homeostatic role in bone maintenance, implicating CL-11 as a critical trigger in this mechanism. While MAC gain-of-function appears to promote excessive long bone growth (22), our study shows that its loss leads to vertebral spine disintegration. Although both gain and loss of MAC affect the skeletal system, CL-11 has multifunctional roles beyond MAC signaling, explaining the differing bone defects.

The bone abnormalities observed in our study required the loss of a second complement activation component alongside CL-11. Redundancy is a hallmark of the complement system, ensuring effective conversion of C3 and C5 into their active forms through multiple pathways. This complexity may explain why an additional activation-pathway defect (e.g., CFB, MASP-2, C3) was necessary for the bone repair mechanism to fail. Conversely, MAC formation was restored by CL-11 supplementation, even in the absence of C3. Like thrombin—a protease capable of cleaving C5 to bypass C3 (26)—CL-11-driven LP activation may directly cleave C5, initiating MAC formation independently of C3, albeit less efficiently.

The mouse phenotype in our model is more specific than that of most of the reported 3MC syndrome patients, who present with multiple developmental defects. Besides common features such as hypertelorism and craniosynostosis (12), skeletal abnormalities in 3MC include malformed ears (27), hypoplastic scapulae (14), skull asymmetry (28), and various cleft palate forms (12, 29). Mechanistic studies have focused on neural crest cell (NCC) patterning (3, 10), which gives rise to craniofacial mesenchyme and contributes to skull and pharyngeal bone development (30). The restricted phenotype observed in our mice, manifesting only around 12 wk of age, suggests distinct pathogenic mechanisms beyond embryonic NCC migration. Notably, no skull abnormalities were detected in affected mice by CT measurement. Our focus on OCLs reveals a different CL-11-regulated cellular mechanism. The observed trabecular bone loss, increased marrow adiposity, and nucleus pulposus degradation of intervertebral discs align with dysregulation in both mesenchymal (e.g., OBLs, chondrocytes, adipocytes) and hematopoietic (e.g., OCLs) lineages.

Complement activation in WT embryos was evident between E13.5 and E18.5, with MAC formation detected on chondrocytes from E13.5—coinciding with the cartilaginous anlagen formation before primary ossification at E18.5. This period marks the transition of proliferating chondrocytes to hypertrophic chondrocytes, followed by invasion by OCLs and endothelial cells, and subsequent bone matrix deposition by differentiating OBLs (31, 32). The reduced MAC formation observed in DKO embryos from E13.5 to E18.5 may have impaired endochondral ossification, predisposing them to mechanical stress-induced bone decay in adult life.

Bone marrow stromal cells and monocyte-macrophage lineages produce complement components (e.g., CL-11, C3, CFB) that regulate the local microenvironment. Our differentiation experiments incorporated no exogenous complement, except for rCL-11 in the reconstitution study. Given this CL-11 gene expression and detected complement activation products (C3d, MAC), we propose that complement proteins produced locally regulate OCL differentiation from myeloid precursors. Thus, OCLs and their progenitors likely regulate by secreting CL-11 and other complement factors, contributing to OCL renewal and bone maintenance.

Our proof-of-principle study using iPSC-derived human OCLs strengthens the clinical relevance of these findings. Moreover, our experiments suggest that human OCLs exhibit heightened sensitivity to the effects of CL-11 alone, consistent with the clinical phenotype of 3MC syndrome, in which defective bone formation typically arises from isolated deficiencies in CL-11 or MASP-1 (10, 14).

In summary, we identify a function of CL-11 in regulating OCL differentiation from myeloid precursors. This function depends on synergy with other lectin or alternative pathway components; without it, bone integrity deteriorates with age. Our model offers insight into how CL-11 mutations and lack of complement signalling contribute to human skeletal abnormalities and suggest involvement of 3MC syndrome mechanisms beyond embryonic NCC migration. Conversely, excessive OCL activity is a hallmark of diseases such as renal bone disease, osteoporosis, and erosive osteoarthritis, where monoclonal antibody drugs targeting the OCL differentiation factor RANKL are now in clinical use or being evaluated (33, 34). Our study suggests CL-11 too as a potential target in this context.

Mouse antihuman CL-11 staining employed a mouse-on-mouse kit (Vector Labs; BMK-2202). Spinal sections were stained with TRAP (Sigma-Aldrich; 387A) for osteoclast identification.

MAC detection was primarily performed using a rabbit anti-rat C9 monoclonal antibody (a gift from P. Morgan, Cardiff University, UK) known to cross-react with mouse C9 (21, 42). The subsequent availability of a mouse TCC specific-monoclonal antibody (clone 12C3; Hycult Biotech) (43) enabled independent verification of the staining pattern observed with the anti-C9 antibody (SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

Bone marrow (BM) was extracted from femurs and tibias of killed mice following Maridas et al. (44). Bones were cleared of soft tissue, washed in 70% ethanol for 1 min, and transferred to PBS. BM was flushed out using a 25G needle, filtered through a 70 µm strainer, centrifuged (10 min at 1,200 rpm), resuspended at 2 × 106 cells/mL in BM medium (10% FBS, 1% Pen/Strep in α-MEM [Gibco]), and preincubated for 2 h on 13 mm glass coverslips coated with 1% gelatin (w/v) (35). The medium was changed after 72 h and subsequently every 48 h until confluency.

Osteoclast differentiation was induced using BM medium supplemented with 0.05 µg/mL murine RANKL (PeproTech; 315-11C) and 0.015 µg/mL murine CSF (Life Technologies; RP8615), replaced every 48 h for 7 d. Rescue experiments included 2 mM CaCl2 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.9 µg/mL human rCL-11 (35, 45) at each medium replacement. Staining was performed for TRAP or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated phalloidin (Thermo-Fisher Scientific; A12379), and photographs were taken using an Olympus BX51 fluorescent microscope.

RNA was extracted from BM-derived cells using the miRNeasy kit (Qiagen; 217084), followed by cDNA synthesis (GoScript Reverse Transcription System, Promega; A500). Gene expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the reference gene. Results are expressed as fold-change in expression of test genes in differentiated versus undifferentiated cells.

ImageJ (NIH) software (9, 35) in conjunction with Ilastik 1.4.1rc2 software was used to generate pixel-based segmentation images of TRAP-stained OCLs and calculate the area of OCLs in microscopic images—see SI Appendix, Fig. S5C for details. Percent C9 staining was normalized to DAPI. Vertebral adiposity was estimated by counting circular white areas in at least three vertebrae across six sections per vertebral region. Nucleus pulposus area was measured using NDP Viewer (Hamamatsu Photonics). Osteoclast quantification was based on TRAP-stained cultures at 100× magnification.

Human KOLF2 iPSCs (obtained from the Human Induced Pluripotent Stem Cell Initiative, HIPSCI) were maintained on vitronectin-coated plates (Gibco, A14700) in Complete Essential 8 Basal Medium (Thermo Fisher, A1517001) supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen, 15140122). ROCK inhibitor (0.312 µM; Sigma, Y0503-1MG) was included at the time of plating to enhance cell adhesion.

Collectin-11 knockout iPSC lines were generated using the Synthego Gene Knock-Out v2 kit with a custom-designed sgRNA pool. The sgRNA sequences were CCUUACCCCACAGCUGUCGC, GCUUCUCCUCCUUCACCAGC, and GCAUGCUCAGCGUGCCCCCG. sgRNAs were reconstituted, complexed with spCas9-2NLS nuclease, and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. A total of 0.5 × 106 iPSCs were resuspended in 80 µL Opti-MEM (Gibco, 31985-062), combined with the sgRNA/Cas9 mixture, and electroporated using the NEPA21 system. Cells were immediately plated at 5 × 104 cells per 10-cm dish. Individual clones were isolated, expanded, and screened by targeted PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing to confirm disruption of the COLEC11 locus.

For validation, total RNA was extracted from WT and COLEC11−/− iPSCs using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, 74106) and converted to cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, 4374967). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR green method, with fold change calculated by 2–ΔΔCt method using TBP as housekeeping gene. The following primers were used for RT-PCR: human COLEC11: forward, 5’-CCCTGGTCCTAATGGAGAACC-3’ and reverse, 5’-CACCGGCGACAGCATTCTT; human TBP: forward, 5’-TGTATCCACAGTGAATCTTGGTTG-3’ and reverse, 5’-GGTTCGTGGCTCTCTTATCCTC-3’. Data are presented as fold-change in expression in differentiated versus undifferentiated cells.

Osteoclast differentiation from human iPSCs was performed following protocols adapted from published work (46, 47). KOLF2 iPSCs were maintained in Essential 8 medium on vitronectin-coated six-well plates. For embryoid body (EB) formation, iPSCs were seeded at 1.25 × 105 cells per well in ultralow attachment round-bottom 96-well plates (Corning, CLS7007) in Essential 8 medium containing 50 ng/mL human BMP-4 (PeproTech, 120-05) and 10 µM ROCK inhibitor (Tocris, 1254). After 48 h, the medium was replaced with fresh Essential 8 lacking ROCK inhibitor and cultured for a further 48 h.

EBs were transferred to 0.1% gelatin-coated dishes containing X-VIVO 15 medium (Lonza, LZBE02-060F) supplemented with 1% GlutaMAX, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 55 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 50 ng/mL human M-CSF (PeproTech, 300-25), and 25 ng/mL human IL-3 (PeproTech, 200-03) with medium change every 5 ~ 7 d. Myeloid precursor cells were harvested after 2 to 3 wk. For osteoclast differentiation, precursors were plated at 1 × 105 cells/cm2 in α-MEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 100 ng/mL human M-CSF, and 100 ng/mL human RANKL (PeproTech, 310-01C). Cultures were maintained for 7 to 14 d with medium changes every 2 to 3 d.

Rescue or inhibition assays included daily addition of 2 mM CaCl2 (Sigma-Aldrich, C5670) plus 1 µg/mL recombinant human CL-11, or 40 µg/mL Mirococept. Following differentiation, cells were fixed and processed for TRAP staining and imaged using a Nikon Ti2 wide-field imaging system at 40× magnification

TRAP-positive osteoclasts were quantified using ImageJ software as described for mouse BMSC-derived osteoclasts.

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test (P < 0.05 considered significant). One-way or two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons was used for three or more groups (P < 0.05 considered significant). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism v9.
