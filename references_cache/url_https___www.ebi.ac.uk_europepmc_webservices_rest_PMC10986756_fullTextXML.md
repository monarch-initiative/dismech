Disease-causing <italic>JAK1</italic> gain-of-function (GoF) mutations are considered exceedingly rare and have been identified in only four families. Here, we use forward and reverse genetics to identify 59 individuals harboring one of four heterozygous <italic>JAK1</italic> variants. In vitro and ex vivo analysis of these variants revealed hyperactive baseline and cytokine-induced STAT phosphorylation and interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) levels compared with wild-type <italic>JAK1</italic>. A systematic review of electronic health records from the Bio<italic>ME</italic> Biobank revealed increased likelihood of clinical presentation with autoimmunity, atopy, colitis, and/or dermatitis in <italic>JAK1</italic> variant-positive individuals. Finally, treatment of one affected patient with severe atopic dermatitis using the JAK1/JAK2-selective inhibitor, baricitinib, resulted in clinically significant improvement. These findings suggest that individually rare <italic>JAK1</italic> GoF variants may underlie an emerging syndrome with more common presentations of autoimmune and inflammatory disease (JAACD syndrome). They are caused by germline mutations in immune or immune-regulatory genes and can result in early-onset severe infections, autoimmunity, autoinflammation, allergy, and/or malignancy (<xref rid="bib27" ref-type="bibr">Tangye et al., 2022</xref>). The unique causative genetic variants are thought to range from exceedingly rare (private) to ultrarare (minor allele frequency [MAF] &lt; 0.1%); however, current studies estimate the aggregate frequency of these disorders to be between ultrarare and rare (0.1% &gt; MAF &lt; 1%), affecting nearly 1 in 1,000 individuals and accounting for a growing healthcare burden (<xref rid="bib19" ref-type="bibr">Picard et al., 2015</xref>; <xref rid="bib26" ref-type="bibr">Tangye et al., 2020</xref>, <xref rid="bib27" ref-type="bibr">2022</xref>).</p><p>The JAK–STAT pathway encompasses an evolutionarily conserved signaling cascade by which cells respond to exogenous ligands and transmit their signals into transcriptional regulation, thereby orchestrating various biological functions including cell growth, differentiation, innate immune sensing, and adaptive immune activation. In brief, the pathway starts with the engagement of a transmembrane receptor by an extracellular ligand, which triggers dimerization or oligomerization of partnering receptors. This induces the preassociated Janus kinases (JAK1-4, TYK2) to <italic>cis</italic>- and <italic>trans</italic>-phosphorylate tyrosine residues of their own and the partnering receptor–JAK complexes. This phosphorylation signature induces the recruitment of signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT1-4, STAT5a–b, STAT6) protein monomers, which are also phosphorylated, enabling the formation of homo- or heterodimers. These active STAT–STAT complexes then translocate to the nucleus where they initiate specific gene expression programs that carry out the desired cellular response.</p><p>Within the JAK–STAT pathway, JAK1 stands out as a master regulator of the cascade in that it mediates signals for at least 28 different cytokines, accounting for almost half of all ligands that signal through the JAK–STAT pathway (<xref rid="bib9" ref-type="bibr">Hammarén et al., 2019</xref>). The <italic>JAK1</italic> gene (chr1:64,833,223-65,067,754; GRCh38/hg38) encodes a broadly expressed mRNA transcript that is translated into an evolutionarily conserved tyrosine kinase consisting of four core domains—FERM, SH2, pseudokinase, and kinase (<xref rid="bib29" ref-type="bibr">Yamaoka et al., 2004</xref>; <xref rid="bib3" ref-type="bibr">Fagerberg et al., 2014</xref>). Once translated, JAK1 traffics to the cell membrane where it binds its cognate intracellular receptor tails, forming the readied complex necessary for cytokine signaling. We and others have previously used a forward genetics approach to identify and characterize four families with exceedingly rare IEIs (<xref rid="bib1" ref-type="bibr">Del Bel et al., 2017</xref>; <xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr">Gruber et al., 2020</xref>; <xref rid="bib25" ref-type="bibr">Takeichi et al., 2022</xref>; <xref rid="bib4" ref-type="bibr">Fayand et al., 2023</xref>) that are caused by GoF variants in the <italic>JAK1</italic> gene. In these cases, the affected individuals initially presented in infancy and experienced lifelong, treatment-refractory inflammatory disease with heterogeneous features ranging from atopic dermatitis and asthma to gastrointestinal (GI) inflammation, autoimmunity, and growth defects. The four families were found to each harbor a non-synonymous single-nucleotide variant (SNV) in the <italic>JAK1</italic> gene, resulting in the amino acid changes A634D (CADD = 32) (<xref rid="bib1" ref-type="bibr">Del Bel et al., 2017</xref>), S703I (CADD = 26.1) (<xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr">Gruber et al., 2020</xref>), H596D (CADD = 24.1) (<xref rid="bib25" ref-type="bibr">Takeichi et al., 2022</xref>), and C787F (CADD = 28) (<xref rid="bib4" ref-type="bibr">Fayand et al., 2023</xref>), all within the pseudokinase domain of the JAK1 protein. Of the four variants, A634D and S703I were tested biochemically in the human in vitro system, exhibiting remarkably high levels of basal and cytokine-induced JAK and STAT phosphorylation, as well as hyperactive induction of interferon (IFN)-stimulated genes (ISGs) compared with wild-type (WT) <italic>JAK1</italic>. This in vitro GoF phenotype was mirrored by detailed molecular characterization of patient samples ex vivo, in which hyperactive baseline and cytokine-induced JAK–STAT pathway activation was determined to be the likely cause of the severe disease phenotypes.</p><p>Given the four previous cases of exceedingly rare <italic>JAK1</italic> GoF variants leading to high levels of biochemical activity and ensuing severe inflammatory disease, we hypothesized that less rare <italic>JAK1</italic> variants with mild-to-moderate GoF activity could be associated with more common presentations of inflammatory, allergic, and/or autoimmune diseases. Here, we use both forward and reverse genetic approaches to describe four novel <italic>JAK1</italic> GoF variants with a spectrum of in vitro and ex vivo activity, all leading to a syndromic disease pattern encompassing autoimmunity, atopy, colitis, and/or dermatitis.</p></sec><sec id="s2" disp-level="1"><title>Results</title><sec id="s3" disp-level="2"><title>Six families with a spectrum of inflammatory disease were found to harbor heterozygous variants in <italic>JAK1</italic></title><p>Initially, six unrelated probands and seven affected family members (<xref rid="fig1" ref-type="fig">Fig. 1 a</xref>) presented to specialty clinicians in dermatology, gastroenterology, or clinical immunology with the spectrum of autoimmunity, atopy, colitis, and dermatitis (<xref rid="tbl1" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref> and <xref rid="sup1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Table S1</xref>). hypothesized that these candidate <italic>JAK1</italic> variants might be contributing, at least in part, to more common presentations of autoimmunity and autoinflammation. To test this, we first mined the EHR-linked Mount Sinai Bio<italic>ME</italic> Biobank, which includes over 30,000 individuals with exome sequencing data and identified 43 additional individuals harboring either of the prototype <italic>JAK1</italic> variants in the heterozygous state: E139K (<italic>n</italic> = 0), R506C (<italic>n</italic> = 31), S700N (<italic>n</italic> = 1), and V985I (<italic>n</italic> = 11). To determine whether these <italic>JAK1</italic> variants were associated with autoimmune or inflammatory diseases, we devised a blinded systematic review that employed a comprehensive search of EHRs for key terms representing signs and symptoms of autoimmune and inflammatory disease (<xref rid="sup3" ref-type="supplementary-material">Table S3</xref>)—comparing EHR findings from <italic>JAK1</italic> variant-positive individuals (<italic>n</italic> = 43) to variant-negative individuals (<italic>n</italic> = 129) who were age-, sex-, and self-reported race/ethnicity matched (<xref rid="figS1" ref-type="fig">Fig. S1, a–c</xref>). Given that these variants are not present in public genetic databases such as gnomAD, they are deemed private variants and therefore considered, by historical standards (<xref rid="bib27" ref-type="bibr">Tangye et al., 2022</xref>), to be exceedingly rare in the human population. However, the signs and symptoms of the 59 individuals we studied, all harboring one of four novel <italic>JAK1</italic> variants, comprise more clinically common presentations of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, which may explain the clinical features of 0.14% of all individuals in our healthcare system (<xref rid="tbl2" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>). These clinical phenotypes include both autoimmune and inflammatory features and can molecularly exhibit cellular immune JAK–STAT hyperactivity, although this hyperactivity may even predate the development of the disease. The spectrum of disease includes variable genotype-specific immune dysregulation ranging from intermittent peripheral eosinophilia, for which there remains no clear temporal or molecular link with JAK1 GOF, to myeloid and lymphoid hyperactivation, even in the context of immunosuppressive treatments. Despite these molecular phenotypes being mild, the identification and study of the patients herein demonstrates that even rare or ultrarare variants could be contributing to more common presentations of autoimmunity and inflammation than previously thought (<xref rid="figS1" ref-type="fig">Fig. S1 f</xref>). Indeed, this work expands the number of bona fide <italic>JAK1</italic> GOF-driven disease cases from four individuals (JAK1 A634D and S703I) to include 59 additional individuals. This demonstrates the importance and power of integrating long-standing forward genetics approaches used in IEI discovery with emerging technologies that enable reverse genetics-guided diagnosis and characterization of these diseases. Inspired by the marriage of these two approaches, we posit that these individually rare (&lt;1:1,000) or ultrarare (&lt;1:10,000) genotypes represent risk alleles of varying penetrance which, collectively, act as harbingers of a <italic>JAK1</italic> GoF-associated autoimmunity, atopy, colitis, and dermatitis (JAACD) syndrome, as indicated by the clearly enriched syndromic phenotypes in our systematic EHR review.</p><p>Akin to the previously described disease-causing <italic>JAK1</italic> variants, the four genotypes identified here are predicted to be deleterious by CADD assessment, with prediction scores for every variant (H596D = 24.1; A634D = 32; S703I = 26.1; C787F = 28; E139K = 22.6; R506C = 21.2; S700N = 25.1; V985I = 18.2) rising well above the <italic>JAK1</italic> MSC of 3.313. However, the directionality of protein damage, whether GoF or loss of function (LoF), is impossible to predict using traditional methods such as CADD. As such, we used a novel in silico framework, which utilized natural language processing to identify features of published disease-causing alleles, which could, in turn, be used to iteratively train a <italic>JAK1</italic>-specific algorithm that could predict whether a given <italic>JAK1</italic> allele is a neutral (no effect), LoF, or GoF (<xref rid="bib11" ref-type="bibr">Kircher et al., 2014</xref>; <xref rid="bib20" ref-type="bibr">Rentzsch et al., 2019</xref>; <xref rid="bib23" ref-type="bibr">Sevim Bayrak et al., 2021</xref>). This tool accurately identified JAK1 A634D and S703I as likely GoF, which was expected given that they are published variants. More importantly, this tool accurately labeled JAK1 E139K, R506C, and S700N as likely GoF, and V985I as neutral, thus suggesting these four genotypes are likely to represent only a sampling of potential <italic>JAK1</italic> GoF variants. Expanding this further, the identification of 15 additional <italic>JAK1</italic> variants that are predicted to be GoF by the same in silico approach suggests that many more individuals may harbor variants with disease-causing GoF activity, potentially explaining up to 0.5% of all individuals in our healthcare system (<xref rid="tbl2" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>). This hypothesis must, of course, be rigorously documented with masked case-control studies tested biochemically and evaluated immunologically. However, our identification of overrepresented syndromic features suggests that in patients presenting with these clinical phenotypes, especially when multiple family members are affected, <italic>JAK1</italic> genotyping could lead to a substantial increase in precision therapy for a variety of inflammatory disorders. This is especially true for clinical presentations that might not have prompted genetic testing using current guidelines.</p><p>The clinical and immunological success in treating severe <italic>JAK1</italic> GoF-mediated disease with JAK inhibitors in this study and previous ones (<xref rid="bib1" ref-type="bibr">Del Bel et al., 2017</xref>; <xref rid="bib7" ref-type="bibr">Gruber et al., 2020</xref>; <xref rid="bib25" ref-type="bibr">Takeichi et al., 2022</xref>; <xref rid="bib4" ref-type="bibr">Fayand et al., 2023</xref>) makes a strong case for a clinical trial where inclusion criteria could incorporate the presence of a JAK1 GoF variant. This could be timely given the growing use of next-generation genomics and EHR-linked genetic databases in broader and earlier genetic diagnosis of disease-causing JAK1 GOF cases.</p></sec><sec id="s9" disp-level="1"><title>Materials and methods</title><sec id="s10" disp-level="2"><title>Human experimental guidelines approval statement</title><p>This research was conducted in accordance with the Medical Research Guidelines (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.ukri.org/councils/mrc/" ext-link-type="uri">https://www.ukri.org/councils/mrc/</ext-link>) and with the principles expressed in the Helsinki Declaration. In silico damage prediction was carried out using either combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) or a natural language processing and machine learning–based GoF prediction tool (<xref rid="bib23" ref-type="bibr">Sevim Bayrak et al., 2021</xref>). The CADD framework functions by integrating sequence-derived context, gene annotations, epigenetic markers, and functional predictions into a single metric by contrasting evolutionarily stable variants with simulated variants (<xref rid="bib11" ref-type="bibr">Kircher et al., 2014</xref>; <xref rid="bib20" ref-type="bibr">Rentzsch et al., 2019</xref>). The resulting score for any given SNV is compared with the gene-specific mutation significance cutoff (MSC), which is considered the upper threshold of non-deleteriousness for that gene (<xref rid="bib11" ref-type="bibr">Kircher et al., 2014</xref>; <xref rid="bib20" ref-type="bibr">Rentzsch et al., 2019</xref>). The natural language processing and machine learning–based tool for GoF prediction functions by using natural language processing to identify discriminative gene- and protein-level features of published disease-causing SNVs to train a machine-learning algorithm that can predict whether a given SNV will be neutral (no effect), LoF or GoF in terms of protein activity (<xref rid="bib23" ref-type="bibr">Sevim Bayrak et al., 2021</xref>). To do this for JAK1 specifically, over 150,000 published abstracts were text-mined using natural language processing to extract the discriminative features of pathogenic JAK1 variants. The extracted features were then annotated at the variant, gene, and protein levels, and the annotations were integrated into an iteratively trained in silico model. The final trained tool was then used to test whether candidate SNVs would confer neutral, LoF, or GoF phenotypes on JAK1 activity.</p></sec><sec id="s13" disp-level="2"><title>Visualization of JAK1 crystal structure</title><p>The full crystal structure of murine JAK1 dimer complexed with intracellular tails of IFN-λR1/IL-10Rβ (PDB ID <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.rcsb.org/structure/7T6F" ext-link-type="uri">7T6F</ext-link>) (<xref rid="bib6" ref-type="bibr">Glassman et al., 2022</xref>) was visualized using UCSF ChimeraX (v1.6.1), developed by the Resource for Biocomputing, Visualization, and Informatics at the University of California, San Francisco, with support from National Institutes of Health R01-GM129325 and the Office of Cyber Infrastructure and Computational Biology, National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (<xref rid="bib17" ref-type="bibr">Pettersen et al., 2021</xref>).</p></sec><sec id="s14" disp-level="2"><title>Systematic EHR review</title><p>The Mount Sinai Bio<italic>ME</italic> Biobank is an EHR-linked biobank in New York City with exome sequence data available for over 30,000 subjects in the New York City metropolitan area (The Bio<italic>ME</italic> Biobank Program of the Charles Bronfman Institute for Personalized Medicine.
