# The association between juvenile xanthogranulomas in neurofibromatosis type 1 patients and the development of leukaemia: A systematic review.
**Authors:** Meyer SN, Vaughn A, Li Y, Studer AC, Rauen KA, Kiuru M
**Journal:** J Eur Acad Dermatol Venereol (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1111/jdv.19321](https://doi.org/10.1111/jdv.19321)

## Content

A subset of patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) develop juvenile xanthogranulomas (JXG), the most common non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and some patients also develop juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML), a rare, and frequently lethal myeloproliferative-myelodysplastic disorder of childhood. Yet, these associations are poorly delineated. We performed a systematic review to clarify the relationship between NF1, JXG, and JMML, and identify patients that may benefit from additional screening.

PubMed was searched for articles describing NF1 patients with JXG and/or leukemia. Eligibility criteria and screening methodology are described in Figure 1. Sixty-two articles (date range 1966–2017) describing 177 patients were included. Patients were grouped by their association: NF1+JXG (without leukemia), NF1+leukemia (without JXG), NF1+JXG +leukemia. Additionally, we compared these groups including JMML cases only.

There were 55 patients with NF1+JXG, 107 with NF1+leukemia (54 with JMML), and 15 with NF1+JXG+leukemia (13 with JMML) (Table 1). JMML was the most common leukemia subtype, and notably, more common in NF1 patients with JXG than those without (13/15, 86.7% vs 54/98, 55.1%, respectively; P=0.024). Family history of NF1 and male sex were more common in NF1 patients with JMML than those without, though did not reach statistical significance (Table 1). NF1 patients with JXG and JMML were younger at initial presentation (mean=1.6 years for NF1+JXG+JMML vs 2.9 for NF1+JMML vs 2.5 for NF1+JXG, P=.018), with a trend towards an earlier diagnosis of JXG (mean=0.6 years for NF1+JXG+JMML vs 3.0 for NF1+JXG, P=.099), and had larger JXG (mean=8.5mm for NF1+JXG+JMML vs 4.3 for NF1+JXG, P=.011). We did not find significant differences in the number of JXG between the groups (P=.370). Leukemic cells of 31/36 (86.1%) patients with JMML had evidence of biallelic inactivation of NF1, versus 1/8 (12.5%) in patients with non-JMML leukemias (P< .001).

The association between NF1, JXG, and JMML has been previously described, yet remains debatable in the literature. As expected, our systematic review showed that JMML is the most common subtype of leukemia in NF1. This is supported by prior reports of NF1 patients having a 300–350-fold increased risk of JMML and 10–15% of JMML occurring in children with NF1. 1 , 2 By contrast, JMML accounts for only 1% of all pediatric leukemias in the general population, with an estimated incidence of 1.2 cases per million among 0–14 year-old children. 3 , 4 Further, our study showed that biallelic inactivation of NF1 in the JMML cells of NF1 patients was very common, strengthening the association between NF1 and JMML.

JMML leukemia subtype was more common among NF1 patients with JXG than those without in our study, supporting the triple association, although a younger age in the former group may have been a confounding factor. Because most of the eligible articles included in our study were case reports or series and not population-based studies, we did not estimate the incidence of the triple association to limit the effects of a potential publication bias.

JMML was also associated with a positive family history of NF1 and male sex, corroborating prior studies. 5 , 6 Moreover, we found that NF1 patients with both JXG and JMML presented earlier and developed larger JXG at earlier ages, typically before one year of age. Current guidelines do not recommend screening for JMML in NF1 patients. 7 However, based on our results, increased surveillance of JMML may be advisable in NF1 patients that have familial NF1, are male, or develop large JXGs in infancy. Additional studies on the efficacy and timing of JMML screening is needed for consensus guidelines.

In summary, NF1 patients have an increased risk of JMML. The triple association remains very rare, but surveillance for JMML may be considered in children with NF1, particularly male patients with a family history of NF1, who have large or early-onset JXGs.
