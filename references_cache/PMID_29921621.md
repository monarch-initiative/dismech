# Outcomes of beta blocker use in cocaine-associated chest pain: a meta-analysis.
**Authors:** Pham D, Addison D, Kayani W, Misra A, Jneid H, Resar J, Lakkis N, Alam M
**Journal:** Emerg Med J (2018)
**DOI:** [10.1136/emermed-2017-207065](https://doi.org/10.1136/emermed-2017-207065)

## Content

OBJECTIVES: Beta blockers (β-blockers) remain a standard therapy in the early treatment of acute coronary syndromes. However, β-blocker therapy in patients with cocaine-associated chest pain (CACP) continues to be an area of debate due to the potential risk of unopposed α-adrenergic stimulation and coronary vasospasm. Therefore, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of available studies to compare outcomes of β-blocker versus no β-blocker use among patients with CACP.

METHODS: We searched the MEDLINE and EMBASE databases through September 2016 using the keywords 'beta blocker', 'cocaine' and commonly used β-blockers ('atenolol', 'bisoprolol', 'carvedilol', 'esmolol', 'metoprolol' and 'propranolol') to identify studies evaluating β-blocker use among patients with CACP. We specifically focused on studies comparing outcomes between β-blocker versus no β-blocker usage in patients with CACP. Studies without a comparison between β-blocker and no β-blocker use were excluded. Outcomes of interest included non-fatal myocardial infarction (MI) and all-cause mortality. Quantitative data synthesis was performed using a random-effects model and heterogeneity was assessed using Q and I2statistics.

RESULTS: A total of five studies evaluating 1794 subjects were included. Overall, there was no significant difference on MI in patients with CACP on β-blocker versus no β-blocker (OR 1.36, 95% CI 0.68 to 2.75; p=0.39). Similarly, there was no significant difference in all-cause mortality in patients on β-blocker versus no β-blocker (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.26 to 1.79; p=0.43).

CONCLUSIONS: In patients presenting with acute chest pain and underlying cocaine, β-blocker use does not appear to be associated with an increased risk of MI or all-cause mortality.

Cocaine is the most commonly used stimulant in Europe, with an estimated 9.4% of adults in the UK alone having used cocaine at least once.12 In 2011, the US Department of Health and Human Services reported that nearly half of the 1.3 million annual illicit drug related visits to the emergency department were linked to cocaine.3 It has been estimated that up to 22% of non-fatal myocardial infarctions in persons 18–45 years of age have been attributed to cocaine abuse underscoring the need for effective therapeutic strategies in this global population.45 Though oral beta-blockers (β-blockers) have been recognised as an established therapy in acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and remain a class I recommendation in those without impending cardiogenic shock in both American and European guidelines due to significant mortality benefits across multiple randomised trials, their role in cocaine-associated chest pain remains controversial due to the risk of unopposed α-adrenergic stimulation and coronary vasospasm.6–10 According to both the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) non ST-elevation myocardial infarction (non-STEMI) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the management of ACS in patients presenting without persistent STEMI guidelines, β-blockers should not be administered to patients with non-STEMI precipitated by cocaine use because ‘of the risk of potentiating coronary spasm.”89 However, these recommendations regarding the current management of cocaine-associated chest pain are anecdotally driven and were derived from animal data as well as controlled studies done in the catheterisation laboratory performed in a different clinical era.11 Interestingly, in clinical practice adherence to this statement does not always hold true as Gupta et al recently reported that up to 85.8% of cocaine-positive patients presenting with STEMI or non-STEMI in a multicenter registry still received β-blocker within 24 hours of presentation.12 Furthermore, new retrospective single-centre studies with patients presenting with cocaine-associated chest pain have been performed. However, conclusions have been limited by small sample size, short durations of follow-up and mixed results. Given the continued controversy and conflicting data, we conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate the safety of β-blocker therapy in patients presenting with cocaine-associated chest pain.

A systematic and time-unlimited literature search using MEDLINE and EMBASE was performed to identify relevant studies up to 1 September 2016. Using the search terms ‘beta blocker’ and ‘cocaine’ as well as commonly used β-blockers (‘atenolol’, ‘bisoprolol’, ‘carvedilol’, ‘esmolol’, ‘metoprolol’ and ‘propranolol’), reports were identified for the analysis. To further locate other potentially relevant studies that may have been missed by the electronic search, reference lists of the extracted studies and review articles were manually screened for relevant articles without language restrictions. We also searched journal supplements, online highlights and engaged in direct discussion with experts in cocaine-associated chest pain to identify potentially relevant studies, and attempts were made to contact authors where needed. Study abstracts were reviewed to confirm relevance, and full articles were extracted. Eligible studies selected for inclusion were based on the following criteria: (1) study design: retrospective or prospective trials, since no randomised control trials have been conducted to date, (2) type of participants: adults ≥18 years of age, (3) symptoms: chest pain suspicious for ACS on presentation as defined by the trials, (4) cocaine usage: positive urine drug screen or reported usage and (5) outcomes: non-fatal myocardial infarction or all-cause mortality (in hospital). Our intent was to assess the impacts of β-blocker therapy in chest pain, and thus we did not search for β-blocker use in cocaine-associated hypertension. Excluded were animal or non-human studies, case reports, observational studies, review articles, studies that did not focus on acute non-traumatic chest pain on presentation or studies that did not report the outcomes of interest, as well as studies that did not compare β-blocker therapy to non-β-blocker therapy among patients with cocaine-associated chest pain (figure 1). Since we wanted to perform an inclusive study, we did not exclude studies based on the follow-up period or the number of patients in the study. The study was performed in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses for Protocols statement for meta-analyses, and included studies were individually assessed for bias.13 A funnel plot was assessed to explore risk of publication bias (figure 2).

Study quality assessment and data extraction were then independently performed by three investigators (DP, DA and MA). Selected abstracts, titles and full-text articles were also reviewed by two investigators. Baseline characteristics including mean age, sex, race, hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), coronary artery disease (CAD), hyperlipidaemia (HLD), HIV, congestive heart failure (CHF), lung disease and serum creatinine and outcomes of interests including non-fatal myocardial infarction and all-cause mortality were extracted from each trial.

Review Manager V.5.1 (Cochrane Collaboration) was used to perform the meta-analysis. The baseline demographic data was analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS V.19.0) where a p value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Percentages and mean±SD were calculated to describe the distributions of categorical and continuous variables, respectively (two-tailed independent samples Student’s t-test and X2 test with Yates’ correction). Effect sizes for outcomes of interest were estimated using OR and 95% CI. Given the inherent heterogeneity in the data, results from the random effects model were reported. Measures of heterogeneity including Cochran’s Q-statistic, and I2 statistics were computed with an I2 ≥25% representing significant heterogeneity.

The quality of each non-randomised control trials used in this study were assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS).14 Each study was scored based on comparability, exposure and outcomes, as well as patient selection. We specifically assigned up to two stars for study group comparability (ie, study design and length of follow-up), up to three stars for adequacy of ascertainment of cocaine exposure and outcomes (ie, urine toxicology screens/assays, myocardial infarction and mortality) and a maximum of four stars based on study population selection (ie, chest pain, those with prior cardiac history, etc). The maximum score achievable for a study was nine with a score of ≥6 being considered as high quality. The results for the NOS plus a summary of each trial are listed in table 1.

From the initial search yielding 580 citations, the meta-analysis consisted of five studies enrolling 1756 patients based on the predetermined inclusion/exclusion criteria (figure 1).15–19 We did not find any randomised control trials meeting these criteria. Datillo et al evaluated 310 patients with available troponin measurements and was one of the earliest studies to assess for in-hospital mortality.15 In the Mohamad et al trial, 364 patients who presented to a Detroit emergency department with chest pain and positive urine drug screen for cocaine were included.16 Rangel et al was the first trial to incorporate both short-term and long-term mortality and included 328 patients.17 Ibrahim et al performed a retrospective analysis that reported 378 patients and included a subgroup analysis using selective versus non-selective β-blocker showing no significant difference in the mean peak troponin level.18 Fanari et al studied 376 patients with an added emphasis on adverse in-hospital outcomes including stroke and ventricular arrhythmia.19

Baseline characteristics between cocaine-associated chest pain patients who were given β-blocker versus no β-blocker are listed in table 2. The patients with cocaine-associated chest pain who were given β-blocker had a higher prevalence of comorbidities including HTN, DM, known CAD, HLD, history of CHF and more advance chronic kidney disease. The β-blocker group was also older and consisted of more African-Americans. The patients who were not treated with β-blocker were noted to have a higher prevalence of lung disease including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and asthma. Both groups were similar in terms of male sex and HIV status.

All five trials (n=1756) reported on non-fatal myocardial infarction in patients who presented with cocaine-associated chest pain as presented in figure 3. β-blocker use in the setting of cocaine-associated chest pain was not associated with a statistically significant difference in the rate of non-fatal myocardial infarction between the β-blocker group (94/610 (15.4%)) versus the no β-blocker group (162/1146 (14.1%)) (OR 1.36, 95% CI 0.68 to 2.75; p=0.39, I2=71%). One early study did show increased myocardial infarction with β-blocker use (statistically significant), despite the overall result of no harm.15

All-cause mortality was reported in three trials (n=1014) and is presented in figure 4. While the mortality rate was lower in the β-blocker group (6/348 (1.7%)) compared with the no β-blocker group (22/666 (3.3%)), this was not statistical significant (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.26 to 1.79; p=0.43, I2=0%). In addition, three studies also reported the incidence of cardiac arrhythmias (of varying type), while two mentioned the occurrence of stroke events. However, no differences were noted based on β-blocker use.

In this meta-analysis of 1794 patients evaluating the effects of β-blocker in cocaine-associated chest pain, there was no significant difference in the rate of non-fatal myocardial infarction or all-cause mortality. The results of this review highlight the lack of the evidence available (as exampled by the absence of randomised control trials) and support contemporary clinical practice. Furthermore, our findings suggest that we reconsider the guidelines regarding the usages of β-blocker in the setting of cocaine usage.6–9 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis assessing this imperative issue. These results suggest that β-blocker therapy may be a safe option in patients presenting with chest pain and suspected cocaine use.

Cocaine affects the cardiovascular system through increases in catecholamine levels leading to stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system via α and β adrenergic receptors.20 This can result in various deleterious effects such as accelerating atherosclerosis, altering platelet function, promoting endothelial dysfunction and inducing direct myocyte apoptosis. Given these risks, β-blockers have been used cautiously in the setting of cocaine given the inherent threat of unopposed α-adrenergic stimulation leading to coronary vasoconstriction and worsen blood pressure.620

The most recent ACC/AHA and ESC guidelines on the management of cocaine-associated chest pain and myocardial infarction label the use of β-blocker in the management of non-STEMI/unstable angina as a class III indication.8 Further, although not discussed in the last ACC/AHA STEMI guideline document, β-blocker use in cocaine positive subjects was also given a class III recommendation in the preceding document.7 Yet, the evidence supporting these recommendations are largely from animal studies, expert opinion and theoretical observations.20–22 In terms of clinical data, the basis for these guidelines were derived from two haemodynamic studies involving a total of 45 human subjects given intravenous β-blockers and referred to the cardiac catheterisation laboratory nearly three decades ago.2324 Additionally, at that time, no prospective outcome data existed examining this issue. Recent clinical studies have since challenged conventional wisdom with the suggestion that β-blocker use may be safe in patients with cocaine-associated chest pain since it has not been associated with worsening outcomes in terms of non-fatal myocardial infarction16–19 or all-cause mortality.151719

Many potential explanations may explain these findings. The most plausible reason may be linked to the modulatory effects of β-blocker on ischaemia.8 Specifically, these agents act by reducing myocardial oxygen demand through their effects on blood pressure, heart rate and contractility.411 β-blockers also act to alter the arrhythmogenic predisposition of patients presenting with ACS, and theoretically, protect against the direct myocyte toxicity resulting from the excessive catecholamine stimulation.22 As many patients presenting with chest pain may have ongoing ischaemia, increased arrhythmogenic potential and high sympathetic discharge, which could further potentiate ongoing ischaemia and cardiac deterioration, these benefits may outweigh any adverse risk associated with β-blocker use among patients presenting with cocaine-associated chest pain and may aide clinicians on the front lines. Taken together, these alterations may explain the lack of significant adverse events noted in our study.

Despite these results, limitations should be considered. The most prominent of which is the inclusion of data derived from retrospective, non-randomised studies subjecting this analysis to information and selection bias. Second, toxicology screening tests may stay positive for 48–72 hours after cocaine ingestion.25 Therefore, the timing and dosage of cocaine ingestion cannot be accurately extrapolated in our study and may potentially influence the outcomes of interest. Nevertheless, urine drug testing reflects routine real-world clinical practice and is thus a reasonable choice as an inclusion criteria. Most of the included studies focused on in-hospital events during the time in which cocaine would be expected to have interaction with the administered β-blocker therapy.15161819 However, in the analysis incorporating events over 2 years from presentation (Rangel et al), β-blocker use among cocaine users was not found to be associated with an increase in events suggesting relative safety of this therapy.17 Also, despite extensive search, there were relatively few studies available, meeting the parameters of this analysis. There was significant heterogeneity between studies included in this analysis. This is at least in part explained by the non-randomised nature of the data, variability in patient populations and the limited number of available studies. However, considering the need for guidance in this area among practising clinicians, we elected to proceed with the analysis. Due to the retrospective nature of the available data, it is difficult to exclude whether some patients were treated with β-blockers primarily for haemodynamic reasons, rather than chest pain alone. Additionally, there was a general lack of delineation across studies as to the dose and route of β-blocker used. Further, some overlap or switch of β-blockers used could not be excluded and outcomes based on β-blocker type (selective vs non-selective) were not available. Therefore, we could not delineate outcome differences based on β-blocker route and type. This may be important as some experts advocate improved outcomes with combined α and β blockade properties.23 Finally, as a systematic review our results may have been influenced by publication bias, heterogeneity among studies and lack of access to primary patient data.

In conclusion, chest pain related to cocaine use continues to be a significant public health concern. Based on the available studies among those presenting with acute chest pain related to cocaine, β-blocker therapy does not appear to be associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction or death. Additional studies evaluating β-blocker use among traditional low cardiovascular-risk subgroups with cocaine-associated chest pain are needed.
