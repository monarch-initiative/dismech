Polycythemia vera (PV) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm, characterized by trilineage hypercellularity, which increases the risk of thrombosis. Stiff-person syndrome (SPS) is a very rare autoimmune neuronal excitability disorder, in which antibodies disrupt the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters, leading to stiffness and spasms of agonist and antagonist muscles.

This report describes the first known case of PV and SPS occurring simultaneously in a middle-aged male patient. He presented with back stiffness and painful abdominal spasms and was found to have erythrocytosis incidentally. He had highJAK2V617F mutation burden and was positive for anti-GAD65 antibody. Progressively intensified therapy was given until his symptoms resolved.

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of these conditions as well as the need for further exploration of potential shared molecular mechanisms between PV and SPS.

Polycythemia vera (PV) is a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by clonal proliferation of myeloid cells, which leads to erythrocytosis in all patients and thrombocytosis and/or leukocytosis in half of patients [1]. Consequently, clinical manifestations include stomach discomfort caused by splenomegaly, minor or overt thrombosis and hemorrhage, vasomotor symptoms, and others. The median age at diagnosis is approximately 60 years, with no significant sex difference. More than 95% of PV patients have an activating Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) gene variation. Therefore, patients who present with characteristic clinical features should be tested forJAK2mutation, especially the most common V617F and exon12 mutations. The basic components of treatment are therapeutic phlebotomy and antiplatelet agents. Patients with history of thrombosis or who are over 60 years of age are classified as high-risk and should receive cytoreductive drugs, including hydroxyurea, interferon formulations, and ruxolitinib, a JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor [2,3]. Notably, the use of interferons should be avoided in patients with untreated autoimmune diseases. PV carries the risk of evolving into myelofibrosis or acute myeloid leukemia, and these risks are greater among patients with persistent leukocytosis and/or highJAK2mutation burden [3].

Stiff-person syndrome (SPS), a rare autoimmune neuronal excitability disorder, occurs more often in the middle-aged and among women. The onset is insidious, and patients gradually develop persistent and intense muscle spasms particularly of the proximal lower limbs and lumbar paraspinal muscles, along with stiffness, task-specific phobias, and other unusual manifestations [4]. The majority of patients with SPS are positive for autoantibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 (GAD65). Other associated antibodies are targeted at the glycine receptor (GlyR), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors, dipeptidyl peptidase-like protein 6 (DPPX), amphiphysin, and gephyrin [5]. Paraneoplastic varieties of SPS have been documented, usually in association with breast and lung cancer, and anti-amphiphysin and anti-gephyrin are the commonly responsible autoantibodies. However, research has indicated that SPS patients with anti-GAD65 are also 7 times more likely to have underlying cancer [6]. Treatments for SPS consist of GABA-enhancing drugs to relieve symptoms and immunotherapy to prevent disease progression. The most commonly applied immunotherapy is intravenous immunoglobulin, while studies have provided evidence that rituximab, plasmapheresis, and autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation also could be an option [4,5].

Here, we report possibly the first case of a patient suffering from PV and SPS simultaneously.

A 47-year-old male was hospitalized due to stiffness in the back, activity limitation, and erythrocytosis found incidentally. The patient had generally been in his usual state of health until early March 2024, when he developed stiffness in the back that became increasingly worse, making it difficult for him to bend and roll over. He also experienced paroxysmal stiffness and painful spasms in the abdominal muscles, which attacked at most 10 times a day and was more severe when stimulated by sound at night. The patient had been evaluated in the pain management department of another hospital. Laboratory data showed a red blood cell (RBC) count of 7.94 × 1012/L and a hemoglobin (HGB) level of 217 g/L (reference range, 120–160 g/L). His past blood count in August 2022 showed no abnormality. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine indicated mild degeneration of the intervertebral disks without cord or nerve-root compression and edema of paraspinal soft tissues at T6-7. Oral imrecoxib and baclofen did not relieve the patient’s symptoms. For further diagnosis and treatment, the patient came to the hematology center of our hospital. He denied any other accompanying symptoms or chronic diseases. His family history was negative for neurologic, autoimmune, or neoplastic diseases. His eating and sleeping habits, urination, and defecation were normal, but he had lost approximately 10 kg over the previous year.

Physical examination on admission was notable for a nearly violet face, red conjunctiva, and increased tone at both the back and abdomen, which aggravated when moving or touched. Mild lordosis of the lumbar spine was observed. No enlarged lymph nodes or sternal tenderness were found. The tone, strength, and deep reflexes of four extremities, higher cortical function, sensation, gait, and stance were normal; pathologic signs were negative. Repeated laboratory test results confirmed elevated RBC count, HGB level, and hematocrit level and a lowered erythropoietin (EPO) level of 1.46 mIU/mL (reference range, 2.59–18.50 mIU/mL), as shown in Table1. Abdominal ultrasound showed a slightly enlarged spleen. A bone marrow biopsy specimen was markedly hypercellular for his age (80–90% cells) with trilineage proliferation (Fig.1). A molecular genetic study revealed aJAK2V617F mutation burden of 60.36%. The rectus abdominis and paravertebral muscles exhibited action potentials in the resting state on electromyography. An SPS-relevant antibody profile was then evaluated, which provided a positive result for serum anti-GAD65 (cytometric bead array 1:1000+, Fig.2A). Positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT) further suggested SPS with elevated uptake values in the cervicodorsal and intercostal muscles (Fig.2B). No signs of malignancy were observed.

According to the diagnostic criteria for PV last updated by the World Health Organization (WHO) and International Consensus Criteria (ICC) in 2022 (Table2) [1,7], this patient met al.l diagnostic criteria and was classified as low risk. Intermittent phlebotomy was performed, and low-dose aspirin was given, which failed to lower the patient’s HGB level. After adding hydroxyurea, the HGB remained as high as 200 g/L. Therefore, ruxolitinib was prescribed, and the patient was closely monitored due to the risk of thrombosis. The patient’s HGB level has been maintained at normal range ever since. The diagnosis of probable SPS was made mainly based on clinical manifestations [4]. Most SPS patients are seropositive, and a high titer of anti-GAD65 is strongly indicative of SPS [4,5]. No evidence of other neurological diseases was found that could explain the patient’s symptoms, and brain MRI also eliminated the possibilities of antibody-related encephalitis or encephalomyelitis. Therefore, he was diagnosed with non-paraneoplastic SPS. After a neurologic consult, diazepam was administrated, and the dose was gradually increased until the spasms improved. The effects later wore off, and diazepam was replaced with clonazepam and baclofen combined with mycophenolate mofetil. However, the patient’s stiffness and spasms worsened, and he developed tingling pain in the back. In addition to increasing the medication dosages, prednisone was added and human immunoglobin was given intravenously for 5 consecutive days, after which the patient’s symptoms resolved.

PV is a clonal MPN that originates from hematopoietic stem cells, with an incidence rate of 0.4–2.8 cases per 100,000. It is pathologically characterized by bone marrow trilineage hypercellularity, which produces an excess of erythrocytes, leukocytes, and platelets. PV usually manifests as purple skin and mucosae, especially in the face and neck and extremities, splenomegaly, increased bleeding tendency, and atypical neurological symptoms, including tinnitus, dizziness, and blurred vision [2,3]. JAK2 is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase that participates in the signal transduction of growth factors such as erythropoietin and thrombopoietin. TheJAK2mutation that changes valine to phenylalanine at the 617 position renders it constitutively activate and increases the sensitivity of hematopoietic cells to growth factors [8]. Additionally,JAK2mutation can influence coagulation and thrombosis through upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines [9]. The main treatment goals are to relieve symptoms and control the risk of thrombosis. Antithrombotic drugs and periodic phlebotomy to lower the hematocrit level to below 45% during plethora constitute the foundation of therapy. Cytoreductive drugs, including hydroxyurea, interferons, andJAK1/JAK2inhibitor ruxolitinib, should be given in high-risk patients and those with uncontrolled symptoms or intolerance of phlebotomy [3]. The selection of these agents should be individualized based on patients’ symptoms, preferences, response to treatment, and adverse events. Approximately 12% and 6% of PV cases transform to myelofibrosis and acute myelocytic leukemia, respectively. The risk factors for fibrotic transformation include persistent leukocytosis, a highJAK2mutation burden, and the presence of additional gene mutations [2].

SPS is a rare autoimmune disease affecting the central nervous system, with an incidence rate of 1–2 per million. The symptoms vary, and stiffness and spasms of the affected muscles are nontypical in the early stage. SPS is commonly undiagnosed or misdiagnosed as Parkinson disease, myelopathy, dystonia, or spondylitis [4,5]. Therefore, clinical manifestations, abnormal electromyographic features, and serum or cerebrospinal fluid anti-GAD antibodies should be considered in combination to support accurate diagnosis [4]. GAD catalyzes the decarboxylation of glutamate to GABA and is the rate-limiting enzyme. Glutamate is the most abundant excitatory neurotransmitter, whereas GABA is the chief inhibitory neurotransmitter in the nervous system [10]. Anti-GAD antibodies disturb the balance between these two neurotransmitters and enhance neurological excitement, leading to simultaneous and constant contraction of agonist and antagonist muscles [6]. Nearly 80% of SPS patients are positive for high levels of anti-GAD65 antibodies. Because the pathogenesis of SPS remains unclear, treatments are mainly focused on symptom control. GABA receptor agonists are the most commonly used. Physical therapy and rehabilitation can also help maintain patients’ movement abilities. Immunotherapy has shown some effectiveness in slowing disease progression. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) and immunosuppressants like corticosteroids, azathioprine, and mycophenolate mofetil are first-line treatments, while the monoclonal antibody rituximab that depletes CD20 + B cells may be beneficial if IVIg is ineffective. Plasmapheresis is an adjunct therapy for patients with severe spasms, and when the above therapies fail, autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation or chimeric antigen receptor T cell infusion can be tried [5].

Very few cases of coexisting autoimmune disease and MPNs have been reported [11,12]. Through a literature review, we found only one case report of the co-occurrence of SPS positive for anti-GAD65 and multiple myeloma, and whether the two conditions were related in that patient remains unknown [12]. The present case is the first reported case of PV with concurrent SPS, and it is difficult to determine whether these conditions were related to happened incidentally. Because PV was found by chance, the chronological order of the occurrence of the two diseases is not definite. A previous study implied that autoimmune diseases are associated with an increased risk of MPNs, and the researchers proposed that autoimmune conditions cause inflammation-driven tumorigenesis [13]. Recent studies also suggested that the JAK/STAT pathway plays a role in rheumatic arthritis and cardiovascular events via its proinflammatory activity [14,15]. A genome-wide association study identified a possible link betweenJAK2and Crohn’s disease, an inflammatory bowel disease [16]. These collective findings suggest that constitutive activation ofJAK2may regulate autoimmunity through chronic inflammation, and further research is needed to determine the underlying molecular mechanism.

The initial treatment goals for the present case were to control the patient’s risk of thrombosis, relieve the patient’s muscle discomfort, and preserve the patient’s movement ability. Importantly, the use of interferons should be avoided in patients with autoimmune diseases. The present case highlights the importance of precision medicine. Multi-disciplinary treatment, close follow-up, prompt adjustment of medications, and supportive care are crucial to improving the quality of life of patients with complicated rare diseases.
