# Pathological IgE Production in Inborn Errors of Immunity and Beyond.
**Authors:** Ma CS, Hsu PS
**Journal:** Immunol Rev (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/imr.70053](https://doi.org/10.1111/imr.70053)

## Content

In this review, we examine different mechanisms for the generation of pathological IgE through the study of inborn errors of immunity (IEI). In particular, we focus on IEIs that are susceptible to atopic disease, such as severe eczema, eosinophilia, and/or food anaphylaxis; this group of approximately 50 genes has been coined primary atopic disorders (PADs). PADs link specific genes and pathways to atopic disease and the production of IgE and can provide insights into more common allergies experienced by the general population.

Allergy and atopic diseases are among the fastest growing chronic conditions in developed countries. The incidence of atopic diseases, especially food allergies and eczema, as well as the number of allergy‐related hospitalizations, has increased substantially in the last 20 years [1, 2]. An estimate of 1 in 5 individuals are affected by eczema and 1 in 10 infants has food allergies [3, 4]. In addition to representing major health issues, they impose a significant cost of $7.8 billion annually in Australia alone. The cellular basis of allergic disease involves distinct but co‐operating immune cell types: pathogenic T helper 2 (Th2) lymphocytes, which aberrantly produce excessive amounts of the cytokines interleukin (IL)‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13, that drive the production of pro‐allergenic immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies by activated B cells [5]. However, we know this is a very simplistic view of the pathogenesis of allergic disease as distinct subsets of pathogenic CD4+ Th2 cells have been identified in human allergy and mouse models of allergic disease [5, 6, 7] and there is likely to be a B cell intrinsic component for generating IgE. A thorough understanding of the pathways driving dysregulation of these lymphocyte populations, in addition to biomarkers to quantify disease burden and effects of allergen‐specific immunotherapy, is still lacking [8, 9].

Inborn errors of Immunity (IEI) are a heterogenous group of disorders caused by loss‐ or gain‐of‐function in germline genes that are essential for an intact immune system. As such, affected individuals are susceptible to severe, recurrent opportunistic bacterial, viral, and/or fungal infections. However, the spectrum of disease extends beyond infectious susceptibility to include autoimmune, malignant, and allergic manifestations. The latest update from the international union of immunological societies (IUIS) IEI committee reports on over 550 IEIs [10]. The term primary atopic disorders (PADs) has been used to reference the collection of IEIs that present with allergic manifestations such as early onset, treatment‐resistant eczema, eosinophilia, and food anaphylaxis. These conditions link specific genes and pathways to atopic disease and can provide insights into more common allergies experienced by the general population. In particular, the production of IgE is the Ig isotype underlying the majority of allergic diseases. Here we review some of the IEIs that present with increased serum IgE levels, and insights this may provide for the generation of IgE.

The downstream effects of cytokines binding to their respective cytokine receptors are mediated via Janus‐activating kinases (JAK) ‐ signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathways. The mammalian genome contains 4 JAKs, JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, TYK2, and seven STATs, STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5a, STAT5b, STAT6. IEIs resulting from defects in JAKs and STATs have provided invaluable insights into the cytokine regulation of IgE production.

To date, disease‐causing variants have been identified in JAK1, JAK3, and TYK2. Autosomal recessive (AR) loss‐of‐function variants in JAK1, JAK3, and TYK2 reduce cytokine signaling and result in defective immunity. Specifically, AR loss‐of‐function variants in JAK3 result in a T−B+NK− form of severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) [11, 12]. In the case of JAK1 and TYK2, affected individuals present with increased susceptibility to mycobacteria and viruses [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. It is worth noting that although the first report of TYK2 deficiency in an individual with recurrent viral, bacterial, and fungal infections in addition to atopic disease (atopic dermatitis, elevated serum IgE) [13] and allergic disease has been associated with another case of TYK2 deficiency [14], hyper IgE and atopic disease are not typically associated with TYK2 deficiency [15, 16]. This suggests that the atopic disease associated with cases of TYK2 deficiency is due to compounding factors such as variants in other genes. On the flip side, autosomal dominant (AD) gain‐of‐function variants in JAK1 result in early onset severe atopic disease [18, 19, 20, 21]. Treatment with JAK inhibitors such as ruxolitinib (JAK1/2 inhibitor) and tofacitinib (pan‐JAK inhibitor) resulted in clinical improvement including reduced eosinophilia and atopic dermatitis [18, 19, 21]. Indeed, this is one of the first indications that a JAK inhibitor could be used to treat atopic dermatitis by inhibiting STAT/cytokine signaling pathways.

Hyper IgE syndrome (HIES) is a primary immunodeficiency characterized by recurrent bacterial (Staph and Strep species) sinopulmonary infections, chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (
Candida albicans
), and, as the name indicates, extremely high levels of serum IgE, often underlying atopic dermatitis and eosinophilia. While a lot of cases of HIES are sporadic, it can also be inherited in an AR and AD manner, with the latter associated with a non‐immune connective tissue phenotype (characteristic facial appearance, recurrent fractures, scoliosis, osteoporosis, joint hyperextension, and retention of primary teeth). In 2007, two groups independently discovered that AD‐HIES was due to dominant negative heterozygous loss‐of‐function variants in STAT3 [22, 23], thereby linking the STAT3 pathway as a negative regulator of IgE. However, as STAT3 is abundantly expressed in many lymphocyte lineages and functions downstream of multiple cytokine receptors, it was not immediately clear how defects in STAT3, on the one hand, resulted in defects in humoral immunity and, at the same time, predisposed individuals to increased serum IgE and atopic disease. These defects have since been revealed to be at least two‐fold. In the case of defective memory B cells and Ag‐specific antibodies, this is attributed to a B cell intrinsic defect in responsiveness to the STAT3‐dependent cytokine IL‐21 [24, 25], and a B cell extrinsic defect in generating T follicular helper (Tfh) cells [26] in the absence of intact STAT3 signaling.

Similarly, the increase in IgE in AD‐STAT3 is likely to result from B cell intrinsic as well as B cell extrinsic defects. The former has been highlighted in mice by experiments whereby B cell specific knock‐out for Stat3 recapitulated the increased switching and secretion to IgE observed in human AD‐HIES patients [27]. A STAT3 dependent B cell intrinsic defect in generating IgE has also been shown in humans by a unique experiment of nature whereby an individual exhibited increased serum IgE, due to B cell–specific mosaic expression of a loss‐of‐function STAT3 variant [28]. However, the level increase in IgE was much lower than that observed in AD‐HIES patients with loss of STAT3 function in all immune cells, supporting the idea that STAT3 restrains IgE via B cell intrinsic and B cell extrinsic effects. The latter is supported by the Th2 bias in AD‐STAT3; specifically, memory CD4+ T cells from STAT3 deficient patients have been shown to produce excessive amounts of IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13 [29]. This result provided the rationale for treatment of STAT3 deficient patients with Dupilumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody to IL‐4R, which is a common component to IL‐4 and IL‐13 cytokine receptors. STAT3‐deficient patients saw an improvement in serum IgE levels and atopic dermatitis post‐Dupilumab treatment [30, 31], undeniable proof that excessive IL‐4 and IL‐13 production are driving IgE and underlying atopic dermatitis in STAT3 deficiency.

The pathway(s) whereby STAT3 negatively regulates IgE was not entirely clear. However, some light was shed in 2018, when bi‐allelic loss‐of‐function variants in ZNF341 were found to cause autosomal recessive HIES, and ZNF341 was shown to function as a transcription factor that augments the expression of STAT3 [32, 33]. Similar to STAT3 deficiency, ZNF341 patients have increased serum IgE, increased Th2 cytokine production, and associated atopic dermatitis, which rapidly improved following treatment with Dupilumab [31, 34]. Furthermore, as IEIs due to defects in cytokine/cytokine receptors with or without atopy and elevated serum IgE as syndromic features are reported, more pieces of the puzzle have been revealed. The most significant being atopic disease in patients due to bi‐allelic loss‐of‐function variants in IL‐6R (elevated serum IgE, severe eczema, eosinophilia) [35, 36], or IL6ST encoding for gp130 (eczema) [37]. However, it is worth noting that similar to STAT3 deficiency, which is embryonically lethal in mice, AR IL6ST resulting in complete absence of gp130 is neonatally lethal in humans due to thrombocytopenia, skeletal, pulmonary, neurological, and renal problems, with only one child reported to have survived past the first year of life [37]. Interestingly, AR loss‐of‐function and heterozygous dominant negative variants in IL6ST phenocopy STAT3 dominant negative and ZNF341‐deficient patients with regards to atopic (increased serum IgE, severe eczema, eosinophilia) as well as non‐atopic (recurrent lung infections, connective tissue abnormalities) symptoms [38, 39, 40]. AD‐HIES due to defects in IL6ST were found to have an increase in CCR4+ Th2 cells that expressed higher levels of the Th2 transcription factor GATA3, and memory CD4+ T cells had a propensity to make increased amounts of IL4, IL‐5, and IL‐13 [39, 40]. It is worth noting that autosomal recessive hypomorphic variants in the glycosylation enzyme phosphoglucomutase 3 (PGM3) lead to a hyper IgE type immunodeficiency that overlaps with IL6ST deficiency, including atopic disease, and increased serum IgE levels [41, 42, 43]. However, it is unclear how a defect in a glycosylation enzyme results in atopic disease, especially since glycosylation of IgE appears to be intact [44]. However, it has been suggested that in the absence of PGM3, there is insufficient N‐glycosylation of gp130 resulting in decreased surface expression and subsequent impaired signaling of gp130 dependent cytokines. Nevertheless, this is an elegant example of how an IEI has provided insights into the pathogenesis of atopic disease, in this instance highlighting the role for cellular metabolism in the form of glycosylation, in preventing atopic disease. Taken together, these observations in IEIs indicate IL‐6 signaling inhibits IgE secretion, and defects in IL‐6 pathway account for atopic disease in AR loss‐of‐function IL6R, IL6ST, ZNF341, and AD dominant negative IL6ST and STAT3. Consistent with this, IL‐6 has been shown to prevent Th2 cell polarization by promoting SOCS3‐dependent suppression of IL‐2 signaling [45]. Whether this is the mechanism of increased Th2 cytokines when IL‐6‐ZNF341‐STAT3 is disrupted remains to be determined.

Interestingly, while patients with loss‐of‐function variants in IL21 or IL21R are characterized as having a combined immunodeficiency with recurrent respiratory infections, impaired humoral immunity, and severe cryptosporidiosis, ~50% of IL‐21 deficient patients also present with mildly elevated serum IgE and ~20% develop asthma [46]. Consistent with this, memory CD4+ T cells from some, but not all, IL‐21/IL‐21R‐deficient patients were found to be skewed towards IL‐4/5/13 production [29]. Interestingly, increased serum IgE and IgE+ plasmablasts have been reported in Il21 and Il21r deficient mice, and IL‐21 has been shown to prevent IL‐4‐induced class switch recombination to IgE by murine B cells in vitro [47, 48, 49]. Further to this last point, the elevated serum IgE in Il21r‐deficient mice is driven by IL‐4; IgE levels returned to normal in mice deficient for both Il4 and Il21r [48]. However, the increase in serum IgE in IL‐21/IL‐21R deficient individuals is relatively small compared to that observed in the IL‐6/STAT3/ZNF341 pathway. Thus, the IL‐21/STAT3/ZNF341 pathway is likely to repress Th2 cytokines and IgE in some settings but not others. One possible explanation is that there is a threshold for the amount of IL‐21 produced by CD4+ T cells that is required to suppress IL‐4 induced IgE production by B cells. Deciphering this would provide more insights into the regulation of IgE.

In regard to other STAT3‐signaling cytokines, it is clear from IEI due to loss‐of‐function variants in IL10/IL10R [50, 51, 52], IL11RA [53], IL23R [54] and IL27RA [55], who do not present with increased serum IgE nor atopic disease, that IL‐10, IL‐11, IL‐23, and IL‐27, respectively, are unlikely to be involved in the negative regulation of Th2 cytokines and IgE.

AD loss‐of‐function variants in ERBB2IP, which encodes ERBB2‐interacting protein (ERBIN). ERBIN‐deficient patients were found to phenocopy AD STAT3 loss‐of‐function patients, with respect to atopic disease, with elevated serum IgE and eosinophilic esophagitis and a connective tissue phenotype, with scoliosis and joint hypermobility [56]. However, it is not known if the high IgE in ERBIN‐deficient patients is due to a B cell intrinsic defect in addition to the high expression of IL‐4Ra and GATA3 and production of IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13 by ERBIN‐deficient CD4+ T cells. Mechanistically, STAT3 induces ERBIN, which binds to SMAD2/3, keeping it in the cytoplasm and preventing transcriptional activity induced by TGFβ. Thus, in ERBIN loss‐of‐function individuals, the absence of ERBIN allows SMAD2/3 into the nucleus, where TGFβ signaling upregulates IL‐4Ra, establishing a feed‐forward loop of IL‐4/IL‐4R/GATA3 activation. Thus, the absence of ERBIN results in an increase in Th2 cytokines and subsequent atopic disease via gain‐of‐function/unchecked TGFβ signaling.

Further implicating the TGFβ pathway in regulating IgE and Th2 cytokines, heterozygous AD variants in TGFBR1, TGFBR2 [57], SMAD3 [58], TGFB2 [59] or TGFB3 [60, 61] have been shown to cause Loeys‐Dietz syndrome, which is characterized by arterial tortuosity and aneurysms, hypertelorism, and cleft palate in addition to severe atopic disease (asthma, food allergy, atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, eosinophilic gastrointestinal disease) [57, 62, 63]. Consistent with atopic disease in these individuals, they present with increased serum IgE levels, Th2 cytokines, and eosinophilia. However, there is controversy regarding whether these variants result in a gain or loss in TGFβ signaling, and accordingly whether TGFβ induces or restrains Th2 cytokines/IgE, as both have been reported depending on the assays used and/or cell type investigated. In favor of a model whereby variants in TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 are gain‐of‐function and TGFβ signaling enhances Th2 cytokines, TGFβ stimulation of CD4+ T cells from Loeys‐Dietz syndrome patients with variants in TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 resulted in an increase in phosphorylated Smad2/3 and Th2 cytokines [62]. Indeed, an increase in phosphorylated Smad2 has been observed in the aortic tissue of patients with Loeys‐Dietz syndrome [57, 62]. Consistent with the role of TGFβ in regulatory T cell (Treg) development and function, Loeys‐Dietz syndrome patients with variants in TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 had an increase in Tregs that secreted the cytokine IL‐13, suggesting the presence of pathogenic Th2 bias Tregs [62]. A gain‐of‐function role for TGFβ in Loeys‐Dietz syndrome patients with variants in TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 would support the mechanism of action of ERBIN.

However, paradoxically, although enhanced TGFβ signaling is observed, the missense and nonsense variants in TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 are predicted to truncate the kinase domain and inhibit signal transduction, i.e., to result in a loss of TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 function [57]. Similarly, in medial vascular smooth muscle cells of the aorta, pathogenetic variants in SMAD3 resulted in increased expression of SMAD3, phosphorylated SMAD1, and TGFB1 [58]. Murine T cells deficient for SMAD2 or SMAD4 had an increase in Th2 cytokine‐associated genes [64]. More recently, TGFβ signaling has been shown to be impaired in Loeys‐Dietz syndrome patients with variants in TGFBR1 and TGFBR2, suggesting the defect is due to loss‐of‐function [65]. This reduction in TGFβ is believed to result in an increase in type 2 Tfh cells, which drive IgE production by B cells [65]. In summary, it is still unclear whether the increase in serum IgE in Loeys‐Dietz syndrome is the result of increased or decreased TGFβ signaling. Furthermore, it is unclear whether this perturbation in TGFβ signaling is having a direct effect on class switching to IgE by B cells or just increased Th2 cytokine production by CD4+ T cells. Indeed, IL‐4‐induced IgE production by human B cells is inhibited by TGFβ [66].

More recently, AD heterozygous gain‐of‐function variants in STAT6 have been identified [67, 68, 69, 70]. Consistent with the role of STAT6 in promoting IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13 expression by CD4+ T cells and IL‐4 and IL‐13‐induced class switch recombination to IgE in B cells, STAT6 gain‐of‐function individuals primarily presented with severe early‐onset atopic disease characterized by treatment‐resistant atopic dermatitis often predisposing to skin infections, multiple allergies associated with anaphylaxis, hypereosinophilia and eosinophilic esophagitis, gastritis and duodenitis, and high serum IgE levels.

Loss‐of‐function variant in the IFNγ pathway results in an increase in Th2 cytokine production (IL12RB1, IFNGR1, IFNGR2, STAT1, TBX21), but not all affected individuals are predisposed to atopic disease [29, 71]. A patient with AR loss‐of‐function TBX21, which encodes Tbet, presented with persistent upper airway inflammation and eosinophilia, which are hallmarks of atopic disease; but interestingly, high serum IgE has never been detected, nor other forms of atopic disease such as food allergy and atopic dermatitis [71]. Loss‐of‐function variants in IFNGR1 result in a higher incidence of atopic disease such as atopic dermatitis, allergies, rhinitis, and asthma compared to the general population [72]. Although significantly lower than AD STAT3 loss‐of‐function individuals, increases in serum IgE have been observed in individuals with IFNGR1 [72]. This is consistent with the role of IFNγ in inhibiting IgE production and Th2 cell differentiation [73]. Taken together, extremely elevated IgE is not a common phenomenon in IEI of the IFNγ signaling pathway, and although IFNγ is involved in the regulation of IgE, it is not the main restraint in IgE production.

In addition to this, perturbations in the IL‐2 signaling pathway, as is the case in AR [74, 75, 76] or DN [77] loss‐of‐function variants in STAT5B and biallelic loss‐of‐function variants in IL2RA [78, 79], are likely to have an indirect effect on IgE production by B cells via regulation by Tregs, and this will be discussed in detail in the Tregopathies section. Taken together, many cytokines contribute to the regulation of IgE; however, the study of IEIs has suggested the predominant pathway responsible for repressing class switch to IgE by B cells is the IL‐6/IL‐6R‐gp130/STAT3/ZNF341 pathway. It would be tempting to speculate that individuals administered with the IL‐6R mAb, tocilizumab, which is used to treat autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, may see an increase in serum IgE.

Actinopathy refers to proteins associated with the regulation of the actin cytoskeleton. This includes proteins such as DOCK8, which forms a complex with WASP and WIP. DOCK8 is a guanine nucleotide exchange factor that converts Rho GTPases such as CDC42 from its inactive to active form. When DOCK8 converts CDC42 to its active GTP‐bound form, it binds and phosphorylates WASP, inducing a conformational change whereby WIP is released. WIP binds and activates the actin‐related protein 2/3 (ARP2/3) complex, resulting in actin polymerization and branching of F‐actin. Of note, actin polymerization mediated by the ARP2/3 complex is regulated by the WAVE‐regulatory complex, including one of its components, hematopoietic protein 1 (HEM1) [80].

Interestingly, loss‐of‐function variants in some, but not all, are associated with hyper IgE and allergic disease, thereby implicating the cytoskeleton in atopy. The most well documented is biallelic loss‐of‐function variants in DOCK8, which was initially classified as the AR form of HIES [81, 82]. Patients with DOCK8 deficiency develop a combined immunodeficiency characterized by severe and recurrent mucocutaneous viral (Molluscum contagiosum, HPV, HSV, VZV, CMV, EBV), fungal (
Candida albicans
) and bacterial (
Staphylococcus aureus
, 
Streptococcus pneumoniae
, 
Haemophilus influenzae
) infections, atopic dermatitis, food allergies, eosinophilia, and extremely elevated serum IgE levels. Memory CD4+ T cells from these patients are skewed towards a Th2 phenotype, often at the expense of Th1 and Th17 cells [83, 84], which is likely to contribute to the increased IgE and atopic symptoms. The mechanism whereby an absence of DOCK8 results in an increase in Th2 cytokines remains unknown. However, human and mouse studies indicate DOCK8 restrains a novel population of CXCR5+BCL6+GATA3+ IL‐4‐, IL‐5‐, and IL‐13‐producing Tfh cells that are responsible for driving allergen/food anaphylaxis in DOCK8 deficiency ([6]; unpublished results). Furthermore, studies in mice have revealed Dock8‐deficient CX3CR1‐expressing mononuclear phagocytes produce IL‐1β that induces GM‐CSF production by CD4+ T cells, and blocking either IL‐1β, GM‐CSF, or caspase activation was able to obliterate the excessive Th2 cytokine response in Dock8‐deficient mice [85]. In addition, although B cell intrinsic defects in activation and generation of memory B cells have been reported [86, 87], whether there is a B cell intrinsic mechanism to the generation of IgE requires further investigations.

IEIs due to loss‐of‐function variants in WASP [88], WIP [89], ARPC1B (a component of the ARP2/3 complex) [90, 91, 92] or NCKAP1L (encoding HEM1) [93] present with a range of clinical symptoms including atopic dermatitis and increased serum IgE levels, with or without thrombocytopenia and eosinophilia. Mechanistically, actinopathies such as WASP are associated with poor immune synapse formation and impaired T cell activation [80, 94], though there is no definitive evidence that this results in bias towards a Th2 phenotype in this context. Furthermore, more work needs to be done to determine if increased IgE production by B cells is due to an increase in Th2 cytokines, intrinsic B cell defects, and/or dysregulation of other cell types such as Tfh13 cells and Tregs, as has been shown for DOCK8 and/or WASP deficiency [6, 95, 96, 97]. Consistent with a B cell intrinsic role, DOCK8 has been shown to act as an adaptor protein linking the TLR/MyD88 pathway to STAT3 in B cells, suggesting increased IgE in DOCK8 deficiency may involve the same pathway as STAT3 loss‐of‐function. However, only ~50% of MyD88 deficient patients have been reported to present with increased serum IgE levels, and this was only moderately greater than that of age‐matched controls [98].

Serine–threonine kinase 4 (STK4), also known as mammalian sterile 20‐like 1 (MST1), is a kinase upstream of DOCK8, and biallelic variants in STK4/MST1 lead to a combined immunodeficiency similar to DOCK8 and WASP deficiency, i.e., susceptibility to viral infections, malignancy, autoimmunity, severe atopic dermatitis, eosinophilia, and elevated serum IgE [99, 100, 101]. Interestingly, STK4/MST1 has been linked to the IL‐2/STAT5 pathway, suggesting dysregulated IgE may be related to Treg and/or T cell dysfunction. Consistent with this, DOCK8 has recently been reported to be essential for intact Treg function via regulation of IL‐2. It is worth noting that although DOCK8 functions in the regulation of CDC42, heterozygous variants have been reported in CDC42, but atopic symptoms or elevated IgE have not been reported in these patients [102, 103, 104]. In summary, further work is required to determine if DOCK8, WASP, STK4/MST1, and STAT3 operate via a similar pathway to negatively regulate IgE production, and if so, mechanistically if this is B cell intrinsic or due to factors such as Treg function and/or increased Th2 differentiation/cytokine production. Nevertheless, this is likely to be complex and due to multiple factors.

The caspase recruitment domain (CARD) protein, B cell lymphoma/lymphoma 10 (BCL10) and mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma translocation protein 1 (MALT1) together make up the “CBM” complex [105]. CARD proteins may be activated by T cell receptor (TCR) activation or other exogenous/endogenous stimuli and recruit CARD domain‐containing protein BCL10 and the para‐caspase MALT1 to form the oligomeric CBM signalosome [106], which activates nuclear factor kappa B (NFκB), mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs) and other immune pathways. Germline mutations in CARD9, CARD11, CARD14, BCL10, and MALT1 are known to cause IEI with a wide range of phenotypes including immune deficiency, immune dysregulation, and allergies [107].

Pathogenic variants in CARD11, also known as CARD‐containing MAGUK protein 1 (CARMA1), result in a wide range of clinical phenotypes depending on the residual function and expression of the protein. Bi‐allelic loss‐of‐function mutations in CARD11 (with undetectable or truncated CARD11 protein expression) lead to a profound combined immune deficiency with pan‐hypogammaglobulinemia, impaired T cell proliferation and low/absent Treg cells [108]. IgE is not elevated, and atopy does not appear to be a significant feature despite reduced Treg cells, likely due to profoundly impaired T cell function and hypogammaglobulinemia. However, AD DN loss‐of‐function mutations in CARD11, resulting in protein dysfunction (but not absence), lead to a different clinical entity now referred to as “CARD11‐associated atopy with dominant interference of NF‐kB signalling (CADINS)” [109]. The clinical phenotype of CADINS is broad, ranging from features consistent with AD and AR HIES to IPEX‐like presentations [110]. In a large series of patients with AD DN CARD11 mutations, elevated IgE levels and atopic manifestations including eczema, food allergies, asthma, eosinophilic esophagitis and allergic rhinitis occurred in around 90% of cases [110]. Immune investigations revealed impaired T cell proliferation, hypogammaglobulinemia but often with elevated IgE, and Th2 skewing of T cell cytokine responses [109]. On the other end of the spectrum, heterozygous gain‐of‐function mutations in CARD11 cause a B‐cell lymphoproliferative disease with immunodeficiency called B‐cell expansion with NF‐kB and T‐cell anergy (BENTA) [111, 112]. Atopy or elevated IgE does not appear to be a feature of BENTA.

In a similar vein, AD DN mutations in CARD14, associated with impaired NF‐kB activation, lead to early onset severe atopic dermatitis with significantly elevated IgE and other atopic diseases [113]. Whereas AD gain‐of‐function mutations in CARD14 have been associated with the development of psoriasis, but not atopic diseases or elevated IgE [114]. CARD9 plays an important role in activating NF‐κB downstream of Dectin‐2, which acts as a fungal‐sensing pattern recognition receptor [115]. Therefore, biallelic loss‐of‐function mutations in CARD9 lead to a specific and significant susceptibility to invasive and superficial fungal disease [116]. However, about half of these patients also had high IgE levels and eosinophilia in a large case series, although clinical atopic diseases were not described except in one patient [117]. The reason for this discrepancy remains unclear. Biallelic loss‐of‐function mutations in BCL10, with absent BCL10 expression, cause a combined immunodeficiency with impaired T cell proliferation and hypogammaglobulinemia [118, 119], though atopic diseases and elevated IgE have not been reported. In contrast, biallelic loss‐of‐function mutations in MALT1, which lead to absent or severely reduced protein expression, result in a combined immunodeficiency with an atopic phenotype including elevated IgE levels (22%), eosinophilia (67%), and eczema (78%), though food allergy was not a prominent feature [120, 121].

Overall, CBM‐opathies present with a diverse clinical phenotype depending on the protein affected and the severity of the functional defect. It seems that hypomorphic mutations leading to some residual protein function and weakened, but not abrogated, NF‐κB signaling are more likely to be associated with atopic manifestations, whereas mutations resulting in complete absence of protein/function lead to a more severe combined immune deficiency without atopic phenotype. This suggests that perhaps weak NF‐κB signaling may favor Th2 development.

A similar phenomenon can be observed with monogenic defects leading to impaired TCR activation. Null mutations in many of the genes involved in T cell activation such as ZAP70 and LAT lead to SCID, whereas hypomorphic mutations (with residual protein function and therefore weak TCR signaling) in the same genes result in a combined immunodeficiency with atopic manifestations such as high IgE, eosinophilia, and atopic dermatitis [122, 123, 124]. Similarly, hypomorphic mutations in RAG1 and RAG2 (which are required for VDJ recombination and TCR and BCR repertoire generation) or other SCID genes (such as IL‐7RA, Artemis, DNA Ligase IV, adenosine deaminase) can result in Omenn syndrome that is characterized by eosinophilia, high IgE, and atopy, in addition to immune deficiency [125]. Two schools of thought exist regarding the reason for these observations. One is that weak TCR signaling events favor Th2 differentiation, which has been shown in various in vitro and murine models [126, 127]. The other is that relatively strong TCR signaling is required for Treg differentiation [128]; therefore, the reduction in Tregs in the setting of weak T cell activation results in loss of Th2 suppression. Indeed, it is clear that Tregs are important for the control of allergic disease, as described below.

Tregopathies is a term coined to describe a group of IEIs that affect Treg differentiation, homeostasis, or function. Importantly, many but not all tregopathies are associated with atopic predispositions and increased IgE production. The classic tregopathy, immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X‐linked (IPEX) is due to loss‐of‐function variants in the FOXP3 gene, a transcription factor essential for Treg differentiation, resulting in the absence of Tregs or their function [129]. It is an X‐linked disorder characterized by the development of both autoimmunity (autoimmune enteropathy, cytopenias, endocrinopathies) and atopy (high IgE, eczema, eosinophilic enteropathies and food allergies) [130], demonstrating the important role of Tregs in maintaining tolerance to both self and foreign antigens, thereby constraining both autoimmune and allergic pathologies, respectively.

Another important group of tregopathies are IEIs that impair the IL‐2/STAT5 signaling pathway, which is important for Treg homeostasis [131] and function [132]. Not surprisingly, AR loss‐of‐function and DN STAT5B variants present as variable phenocopies of IPEX, including eczema and moderately high IgE production [74, 77], though food allergies are not universally reported in these patients. Additional features in loss‐of‐function STAT5B defects include growth retardation due to growth hormone insensitivity and immunodeficiency due to impaired conventional T cell/NK cell activation/proliferation. AR loss‐of‐function variants in CD25 (IL‐2RA) resulting in impaired IL‐2 signaling present similarly to AR STAT5B deficiency, except for the lack of growth hormone insensitivity [78, 79]. Interestingly, patients with AD gain‐of‐function variants in STAT3 also present very similarly to STAT5B deficiency due to the suppressive effect of STAT3 hyperactivation on STAT5 signaling [133]. However, in contrast to STAT3 loss‐of‐function patients, the IgE level is only moderately elevated.

Somewhat paradoxically, somatic gain‐of‐function STAT5B variants have been associated with eosinophilia, atopic dermatitis, and food allergies [134, 135, 136]. Interestingly, this was not always associated with an increase in serum IgE, but an increase in CD4+ T cells expressing Th2 cytokines was observed. Furthermore, an increase in Tregs has also been detected, suggesting Tregs are not capable of restraining Th2 cytokine production and subsequent IgE production by B cells, although Treg function was not examined [135]. Treatment with the JAK inhibitor Ruxolitinib resulted in improved eosinophilia, IgE levels, and food allergy [136]. Thus, both loss‐of‐function and gain‐of‐function in STAT5B have been associated with an increase in IgE and atopic disease; however, the mechanisms by which these occur require further investigations.

Several IEIs that affect Treg suppressive function have been defined. Cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA‐4) is essential for Treg mediated suppression of T cell co‐stimulation [137]. CTLA‐4 haploinsufficiency results in a wide spectrum of clinical phenotypes (including lymphoproliferation, malignancy, autoimmunity and a CVID‐like phenotype) with variable penetrance. Skin involvement, mainly atopic dermatitis, is present in 56% of the patients [138], though there are no formal reports of elevated IgE levels or food allergies in these patients. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐responsive and beige‐like anchor protein (LRBA) plays an essential role in the trafficking and expression of CTLA‐4; LRBA deficiency therefore causes a similar (but fully penetrant) phenotype to CTLA‐4 haploinsufficiency [139]. Eczema, but not high IgE levels, has been reported in LRBA deficiency [140].

BACH2 is a transcription factor critical for T/B cell differentiation and maturation as well as Treg cell development and homeostasis. Patients with BACH2 haploinsufficiency present with reduced Foxp3 expression in Tregs and an IPEX‐like phenotype but with hypogammaglobulinemia (including low IgE) [141]. However, so far, a significant atopic phenotype has not been reported in these patients.

Overall, it is clear that Treg cells are instrumental for the control of both autoimmune and allergic responses. Although all IEIs that impact Treg cell survival or function lead to increased autoimmunity, the development of atopic disorders in these IEIs is not universal. In particular, atopic diseases (other than eczema) are not reported to be prevalent in CTLA‐4 haploinsufficiency, LRBA deficiency, and BACH2 haploinsufficiency. This may be because they are not documented, or because these IEIs are additionally associated with impaired antibody (and therefore IgE) production, or alternative pathways in Treg cell homeostasis or function exert control over allergic responses in these IEIs. Further investigations are required to clarify this.

Although a large focus of this review has been on perturbations to the adaptive immune system in the generation of IgE and atopic disease, it should be noted that other factors also come into play. Some of these have been highlighted in other reviews [142, 143, 144]. Briefly, this includes variants in genes associated with structural proteins (flaggrin), intracellular adhesion molecules [corneodesmosin (CDSN), desmoplakin (DSP), desmoglein 1 (DSG1)] and protease inhibitors (SPINK5) that subsequently disrupt the epithelial/skin barrier, resulting in severe atopic dermatitis/eczema. It has been suggested that once the barrier is disrupted, allergens that stimulate allergic inflammation can get through. The mechanisms whereby disruption of the skin barrier results in elevated IgE and atopic disease require further investigation. However, more recently, there has been a suggestion that disruption of the skin barrier leads to a systemic increase in IL‐33, which subsequently drives Th2 bias [145].

The cellular basis of the allergic reaction is due to degranulation of mast cells and the release of mediators such as histamine. Several IEI that result in increased mast cell degranulation and subsequent atopic symptoms such as urticaria have been identified: adhesion G protein‐coupled receptor E2 (ADGRE2), phospholipase C gamma 2 (PLCG2) and TPSAB1, which encodes alpha tryptase. However, these defects in granulocyte dysfunction are not usually associated with increased serum IgE.

As summarized above, atopic manifestations are prevalent in certain IEIs with defects in T cell activation, Treg cell homeostasis, specific cytokine signaling, or actin function. However, do these genes have an impact on the development of atopy and/or high IgE production in the general population who do not otherwise have monogenic disease? Several genetic approaches have been employed to study the genetic predispositions for atopic disorders, including atopic dermatitis/eczema, IgE‐mediated food allergies, asthma, and allergic rhinitis. Genomic approaches used include candidate gene association studies (CGAS), genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) and whole genome sequencing (WGS). However, significant heterogeneity exists between the studies due to study population definition and the co‐occurrence of multiple atopic disorders in the same individuals. Despite this, several important groups of genes or pathways have come to light (reviewed in [146]).

Looking at genes associated with IgE‐mediated food allergies, examples include the HLA genes, genes that affect the epithelial barrier (FLG, SPINK), genes that are involved in T cell activation (MALT1) and genes involved in cytokine signaling (IL‐2, IL‐4, IL‐21, STAT6) [146]. Some, but not all of these, overlap with the IEI genes. Specifically, the MALT1 gene variant was identified in GWAS of peanut‐allergic children in the LEAP study [147], which highlights the important role of the CBM complex and downstream NFkB signaling in regulating CD4+ T helper differentiation and immune outcomes. Similarly, STAT6 variants were also identified in several candidate gene association studies [148, 149] to be associated with food allergy, highlighting the important role of IL‐4 signaling in promoting Th2/IgE‐mediated disease.

It is important to note, however, that the significant rise in atopic diseases is quite rapid and recent (last 20–30 years), and therefore unlikely to be attributable to significant changes in the human genome in that short span of time. It is far more likely that new environmental factors lead to changes in the way that certain genes are expressed via epigenetic imprinting [150]. This then results in functional defects that may affect analogous T cell activation, Treg homeostatic, actin, or cytokine signaling pathways seen in PADs. Indeed, we and others have shown that in children with food allergies, naïve T cells demonstrate impaired T cell activation in response to TCR stimulation [151, 152]. In particular, impaired store‐operated calcium entry (SOCE) may reduce NFAT signaling and promote Th2 bias during T helper cell differentiation [152]. Whether this signaling issue is restricted to T cells is unclear, as very few studies have looked specifically at other cell types such as B cells in food allergic patients. There is, however, evidence that, in contrast to T cells, innate immune cells such as monocytes appear to be more inflammatory and mount an exaggerated inflammatory response to LPS stimulation [153] in food allergic children, though the reasons for this remain unclear. Additionally, whether these functional immune changes occur only in food allergic individuals or are present in other atopic diseases requires further investigations, as the co‐occurrence of atopic disorders is common and most studies do not distinguish specifically between these disorders.

In this review, we have highlighted several groups of IEIs that are associated with atopic manifestations and pathological IgE production. Broadly, these include IEIs that lead to dysregulated cytokine signaling which favor Th2 polarization, defects that lead to impaired but not absent T cell activation which concurrently promote Th2 differentiation and impair Treg development, tregopathies that directly impact Treg‐mediated control of allergic inflammation and actinopathies that may act on multiple pathways to promote Th2 differentiation and IgE production (summarized in Figure 1). However, there remain many questions regarding how certain IEIs predispose individuals to atopy and increased IgE production. For example, the mechanisms by which actinopathies promote atopy and IgE production in addition to associated Treg defects, specific mechanisms for Treg‐mediated control of allergy given not all Treg defects present with atopy, or the potential impact of varying strengths of NF‐kB signaling on T helper cell fates/Th2 development.

Defects in pathways that result in dysregulated IgE production. The study of inborn errors of immunity (IEI) have reviewed defects in pathways that subsequently result in increased IgE production. This includes IEIs that can be grouped as actinopathy, Tregopathies, impaired T cell activation, and dysregulated cytokine signaling. These pathways are reviewed in this manuscript.

Although PADs do inform important pathways in the control of allergic responses in humans, whether these pathways are affected in common allergic disorders such as IgE‐mediated food allergies is largely unknown. Although there is some overlap of genes highlighted through gene linkage analysis of common allergic disorders and PADs (such as MALT1, STAT6), this is not entirely consistent. This is likely because changes in immune pathways in common allergic disorders may partly be a result of environmentally induced epigenetic changes rather than genetic polymorphisms. This would be in keeping with the recency and rate of rise in allergic disorders, which implicate the important role of environmental influences. Future studies using a combination of epigenetic, transcriptomic, and functional analyses in common allergic disorders may reveal further insights into the mechanism of allergy and pathological IgE production in humans.
