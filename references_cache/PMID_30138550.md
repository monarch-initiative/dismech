# Distinct Clinical and Pathological Features of Melorheostosis Associated With Somatic MAP2K1 Mutations.
**Authors:** Jha S, Fratzl-Zelman N, Roschger P, Papadakis GZ, Cowen EW, Kang H, Lehky TJ, Alter K, Deng Z, Ivovic A, Flynn L, Reynolds JC, Dasgupta A, Miettinen M, Lange E, Katz J, Klaushofer K, Marini JC, Siegel RM, Bhattacharyya T
**Journal:** J Bone Miner Res (2019)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jbmr.3577](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.3577)

## Content

Melorheostosis is a rare hyperostotic disease of the long bones classically characterized by a "dripping candle-wax" radiographic appearance. We recently described somatic activating mutations in MAP2K1 as a cause of melorheostosis. Here, we report distinguishing characteristics of patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis. Fifteen unrelated patients with radiographic appearance of melorheostosis underwent paired biopsies of affected and unaffected bone for whole-exome sequencing, histology, and cell culture. Eight patients with mutations in MAP2K1 in affected bone were compared to the seven MAP2K1-negative patients to identify distinguishing characteristics. Patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis had a distinct phenotype with classic "dripping candle-wax" appearance on radiographs (p = 0.01), characteristic vascular lesions on skin overlying affected bone (p = 0.01), and higher prevalence of extraosseous mineralization and joint involvement (p = 0.04 for both). Melorheostotic bone from both MAP2K1-positive and MAP2K1-negative patients showed two zones of distinct morphology-an outer segment of parallel layers of primary lamellar bone and a deeper zone of intensely remodeled highly porous osteonal-like bone. Affected bone from MAP2K1-positive patients showed excessive osteoid (p = 0.0012), increased number of osteoblasts (p = 0.012) and osteoclasts (p = 0.04), and increased vascularity on histology in comparison to paired unaffected bone which was not seen in affected bone in most MAP2K1-negative patients. The identification of a distinct phenotype of patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis demonstrates clinical and genetic heterogeneity among patients with the disease. Further studies are needed to better understand the underlying pathophysiology and associated skin findings. © 2018 American Society for Bone and Mineral Research.

The diagnosis of sclerosing bone diseases can be challenging as multiple diseases can have a similar radiological and clinical phenotype. Over the past few decades, several clinically similar conditions have been separated after the demonstration of genetic heterogeneity whereas other clinically diverse conditions have been found to share a common genetic etiology.(1)

The term melorheostosis is derived from Greek—melos (part), rhein (flow), and osteon (bone)—because of its characteristic “dripping candle-wax” appearance on radiographs (Fig. 1A, B; Supporting Video 1). The disease was first described by Leri and Joanny at the Medical Society of Paris in July 1922, in a 39-year-old female with a 8-year history of left shoulder pain.(2) The earliest case of the disease is believed to be the fibula of an Alaskan skeleton from 5th century AD, which is now at the American Museum of Natural History in New York City.(3)

Melorheostosis is generally diagnosed in patients with islands of radiodensity in long bones, which show focal uptake on radionuclide imaging. There is no available confirmatory test and the diagnosis can be subjective. In 2001, Freyschmidt(4) retrospectively reviewed the radiological features in 23 patients with melorheostosis and, in addition to the classic “dripping candle-wax appearance,” described other radiologic patterns— osteoma-like, myositis ossificans–like, osteopathia striata–like, and mixed (Supporting Fig. 1). However, mechanistic understanding of how these different radiographic patterns relate to disease pathophysiology remains unexplored.

We recently identified somatic activating mutations in MAP2K1 as a cause of melorheostosis in eight of 15 patients who underwent paired bone biopsies of affected and unaffected bone.(5)
MAP2K1 somatic mutations with an allele frequency of 1% to 25% were found in affected, but not contralateral unaffected bone. All identified mutations affected one of two residues (p.Q56P, p.K57E, and p.K57N) in the negative regulatory domain of the MEK1 MAP2 kinase, which increased enzymatic activity and phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) in histological sections of bone and osteoblasts cultured from these biopsies. This manuscript defines distinguishing characteristics between MAP2K1-positive and MAP2K1-negative disease.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases (NIAMS; Clinicaltrials.gov
NCT02504879). Eligibility criteria for study enrollment were as follows: (i) adult suspected or diagnosed to have melorheostosis and (ii) radiographic appearance consistent with the disease.(4) Patients were recruited either through self-referral via the Melorheostosis Association or through referral by their local physicians. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient. The diagnosis of melorheostosis was confirmed by the anatomic correlation of radiographic abnormality with increased uptake on 18F-sodium fluoride (18F-NaF) PET/CT studies. Areas of abnormally increased 18F-NaF bone activity reflect increased bone surface exposed to blood flow resulting in a higher availability of binding sites for the radiotracer, indicating underlying increased bone turnover.(6,7) Paired biopsies of affected and unaffected bone were obtained from 15 patients who consented to this procedure. Bone samples were used for extraction of genomic DNA, culture of osteoblasts and histopathological evaluation. Genomic DNA samples underwent deep whole-exome sequencing (Otogenetics, Atlanta, GA) with 107× to 270× coverage for each sample and at least 50× coverage for most exome targets. Using two somatic mutation callers (muTectv1.1.734 and Strelka v1.0.1435), somatic variants were filtered for those not present in the unaffected bone, causing protein or non-coding RNA (ncRNA) sequence changes or splicing changes, <1% frequency in ExAC and 1000 Genomes Project databases, not in a duplicated genomic region and called by both muTect and Strelka. Exome sequencing was complemented by high-coverage amplicon sequencing with an average sequencing coverage >10,000× per sample. A targeted next generation sequencing amplicon library prep kit from Swift Biosciences (56G Oncology) was used to screen for mutations in addition to MAP2K1. The panel targets 56 clinically-relevant overgrowth and cancer-related genes (Supporting Table 1). Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) was used to validate and quantitate the allele frequency of somatic mutations in bone and skin samples.(5)

Patient-reported pain symptoms were collected from all subjects using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS). All subjects underwent medical history and physical examination, dermatologic and neurological evaluation, imaging studies and biochemical evaluation of markers of bone turnover. Five MAP2K1-postive and five MAP2K1-negative patients underwent 6-mm paired skin punch biopsies—one from skin overlying the affected bone, at the site of most prominent skin change, and a second skin biopsy from the contralateral unaffected extremity. Skin samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, routinely processed, and embedded in paraffin for histological evaluation.

Radiographs of all bones with increased uptake on 18F-NaF PET/CT were obtained. X-rays were classified based on the patterns of occurrences.(4) Whole-body skeletal disease burden was assessed by quantification of disease-related 18F-NaF activity using the MIM Vista workstation (version 6.5.9). The whole-body CT performed in conjunction with 18F-NaF PET/CT was used to evaluate for extraosseous mineralization.

A piece of the bone sample was fixed in 4% neutral buffered formaldehyde, decalcified and embedded in paraffin. Five-micrometer (5-μm) sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. A second piece of bone sample was fixed in 70% ethanol, embedded undecalcified in polymethylmethacrylate, and prepared for histomorphometric analyses using standard procedures.(8) Evaluation of Ki-67, a nuclear protein associated with cellular proliferation, was performed on two affected bone samples.(9)

Statistical evaluation for clinical characteristics was performed with R version 3.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org/). Differences between MAP2K1-positive and MAP2K1-negative patients were assessed by Mann-Whitney-tests for continuous outcomes and by Fisher’s exact test for dichotomous or categorical outcomes. Statistical evaluation of bone histomorphometric variables of bone formation and resorption was performed with GraphPad Prism 6.0h (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Results are reported as median values with interquartile range. Statistical significance was considered as p<0.05.

Additional methodological details are available in the Supporting Methods.

The clinical attributes of eight MAP2K1-positive patients were compared to the seven MAP2K1-negative patients (Table 1). Using limited range of motion on exam as a proxy for involvement of joint by the disease, a statistically significant association of joint involvement was noted in patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis (75% patients with MAP2K1-postive melorheostosis versus 14.3% patients with MAP2K1-negative melorheostosis; p value=0.04).

Patients from both groups reported pain, described most frequently as a constant dull aching pain with intermittent sharp pain. The median PROMIS pain interference score for MAP2K1-positive patients was 56 (range, 56 to 62.9), whereas MAP2K1-negative patients had a median PROMIS pain-interference score of 62.8 (range, 57.4 to 68.2), suggesting no significant difference in pain severity between the two groups. Median age at diagnosis of patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis was 11 years (range, 10 to 15.75 years), with a median disease duration of 33.5 years (range, 25.25 to 38 years), whereas median age at diagnosis of patients with MAP2K1-negative melorheostosis was 12 years (range, 7.5 to 16 years), with a median disease duration of 30 years (range, 19.5 to 40.5 years), showing no significant difference. There was no history of fragility fracture in patients from either group. No patient from either group reported a personal or family history of melorheostosis or other previously described co-occurring bone diseases such as osteopoikilosis or Buschke-Ollendorff syndrome. There was no statistically significant difference in gender preponderance (75% females in MAP2K1-positive versus 57.1% in MAP2K1-negative); prevalence of lower extremity disease (62.5% in MAP2K1-positive versus 71.4% in MAP2K1-negative); right-sided disease (75% in MAP2K1-positive versus 28.6% in MAP2K1-negative); and preponderance of sensory loss (75% patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis versus 85.7% patients with MAP2K1-negative disease).

To screen for low-frequency secondary mutations in other cancer-associated genes, we used amplicon-based sequencing for mutations in cancer associated hotspots in 56 genes. We did not find variants in any additional genes in the amplicon panel targeting the 56-common cancer-associated genes other than those already identified in MAP2K1 and KRAS in patients from either group.(5)

Six patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis had similar skin changes overlying the affected bone—erythematous patches—demonstrating a statistically significant association of this finding with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis (p=0.01) (Fig. 2A). Three patients with MAP2K1-negative melorheostosis had skin changes, although clinical skin findings varied among patients. Skin changes in both groups appeared to directly overlie the site of bone involvement. Histological review of skin samples showed thickened vascular wall and increased density of superficial vasculature in skin overlying the affected bone when compared to skin from the contralateral extremity (Fig. 2B). This change was noted in four of five samples in both MAP2K1-positive and MAP2K1-negative melorheostosis.

We found that patients who have a mutation in MAP2K1 were more likely to have a classic “dripping candle-wax” pattern on radiographs (p=0.01). Eight of nine (89%) MAP2K1-positive patients had classic “dripping candle-wax” appearance of X-ray. One MAP2K1-positive patient had a mixed pattern of classic and myositis ossificans-like pattern. None of the MAP2K1-negative patients had the classic “dripping candle-wax” X-ray appearance (Fig. 3A, B). Patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis were more likely to have extraosseous mineralization (p=0.041), commonly intramuscular and not palpable. Nearly all patients from both groups had polyostotic disease (100% in MAP2K1-positive group versus 85.7% patients in MAP2K1-negative group).

All skeletal and extraosseous melorheostotic abnormalities demonstrated intensely elevated 18F-NaF activity co-localizing with areas of increased attenuation on CT. In addition, areas with low-level increased hyperdensity on CT showed markedly increased uptake, enabling the diagnosis of early-stage melorheostotic lesions.

There was no significant difference in whole-body skeletal burden of the disease between the two groups as measured by total volume of all 18F-NaF avid lesions or total activity of all lesions. In addition, there was no association between the allele frequencies of the same mutation and the whole body skeletal burden of the disease (Supporting Table 2).

There was no significant difference in markers of bone formation (bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, osteocalcin, and amino-terminal pro-peptide) and resorption (carboxy-terminal pro-peptide, urinary N-terminal telopeptide [NTX], and deoxypyridinoline [DYD]) between the two groups after adjusting for age. Markers of bone turnover were within normal age-appropriate range in most patients in either group. Lack of marked abnormalities in markers of bone turnover may result from the focal nature of the disease, because bone turnover markers reflect the turnover of the entire skeleton and integrate the regional variations in turnover.(10)

Affected bone from patients with both MAP2K1-positive and MAP2K1-negative melorheostosis showed a high level of osseous differentiation and lacked any atypical cellular elements, such as stromal spindled or pleomorphic cells (Fig. 4A, B). Mitotic figures were not observed, and all cellular elements showed very low Ki-67 labeling (Fig. 4C, D, E, F). The majority of Ki-67 positive cells were observed in marrow regions filled with fibroblasts and fibrovascular stroma, often adjacent to regions of chondroid differentiation, and rarely in chondro-osseous matrix (<2%).

Typical melorheostotic bone lesions from both MAP2K1-positive and MAP2K1-negative patients showed two zones of distinct morphology—on the outer periosteal side (toward the soft tissue shell of the lesion), a segment formed by multiple layers of parallel lamellar bone and a deeper zone of intensely remodeled bone. Primary vascular canals could be distinguished within this extremely dense bone on the outer periosteal side. This indicates that lamellar apposition formed by bone modeling activity leads to radial outgrowth of the melorheostotic lesion (Fig. 5A). Within the deeper regions of the lesion, there was a zone of remodeled bone characterized by larger vascular channels with typical osteonal lamellae concentrically arranged around the surface of the channels (Fig. 5B). Affected bone from all MAP2K1-positive patients was characterized by excessive osteoid formation in comparison to the contralateral unaffected bone (Fig. 5C, D). Moreover, extensive seams of cuboidal osteoblasts were observed on osteonal surfaces in affected but not in unaffected bone tissue (Fig. 5E, F), along with increased number of osteoclasts (Fig. 5G). This is indicative of high bone remodeling activity in affected bone tissue of MAP2K1-positive patients. Affected bone tissue also often exhibited enlarged vascular spaces with multiple blood vessels (Fig. 5H). In contrast, affected bone specimens from MAP2K1-negative patients showed a rather broad histological spectrum. This ranged from normal-looking bone, like the contralateral unaffected cortical tissue (Fig. 5I, J) to a situation with enhanced cellular activity accompanied by focal mineralization defects and even endosteal fibrosis (not shown). Increased cellular elements and vascularity was not appreciated in most specimens of affected bone in MAP2K1-negative patients.

Further, comparison of bone histomorphometric variables revealed that all indices of bone formation and bone resorption were significantly increased in the MAP2K1-positive group compared to the MAP2K1-negative group, except osteoid thickness, which did not reach statistical significance (Table 2). In contrast, comparison of the histomorphometric variables of unaffected bone from both groups did not show any significant differences. This indicates that the differences in bone forming and bone resorbing parameters delineated by bone histomorphometric analyses are most likely due to the pathological alterations in melorheostotic bone and not by enhanced bone remodeling in the MAP2K1-positive group. Mineral apposition rate was calculated in two samples where fluorescence labeling was available and was in normal range.(8) Consistently, statistical evaluations showed that none of the histomorphometry parameters was significantly altered in affected versus unaffected bone in MAP2K1-negative patients.

Patients with melorheostosis caused by somatic activating mutations in MAP2K1 have a distinct clinical and pathologic phenotype. Among cases clinically labeled as melorheostosis, there is likely a mix of several disease patterns with potentially different underlying pathophysiology. MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis is characterized by classic “dripping candle-wax” appearance with frequent extraosseous mineralization and joint involvement and a distinct pathologic signature of increased osteoid formation, increased osteoblasts and osteoclasts, and increased vascularity of affected bone. A second subset of patients with germline mutations in LEMD3 and radiographic appearance of melorheostosis with coexisting familial osteopoikilosis has been described.(11-13) The common feature of all these patterns is the radiographic and histologic appearance of excess cortical bone in an asymmetrical focal pattern.

Some insight into the pathophysiology of melorheostosis can be gleaned from the histological findings. Bone overgrowth in MAP2K1-positive and MAP2K1-negative melorheostosis is primarily the result of an extensive focal periosteal bone apposition on the outer cortical envelope, leading to radial outgrowth of the lesion and the typical candle-wax appearance observed on radiographs, and by extensive osteonal bone remodeling. It should be noted that parallel lamellae of primary bone were found in all intact samples where a periosteal envelope was present. In samples where only endocortical bone was available, residual parallel lamellae were not always viewed, raising the possibility that either the primary bone was completely remodeled, or the outgrowth mechanism was different. Interestingly, affected bone from MAP2K1-positive patients exhibited many enlarged and often hypervascular channels. The prominent increase in osteoid in MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis suggests that the underlying disease process is associated with osteoblast dysfunction.

Mosaicism for MAP2K1 has been demonstrated in skin overlying the affected bone but not in skin from contralateral extremity. Skin changes are a well-recognized phenomenon in several mosaic disorders of bone overgrowth, including McCune-Albright syndrome (MAS), certain RASopathies, and Proteus syndrome.(14-16) Patients with MAS manifest large, geographic café-au-lait macules (CALM), which are often the first manifestation of disease; however, unlike the skin findings in melorheostosis, CALM in MAS do not correlate with the location of underlying fibrous dysplasia.(17) It appears likely that the cutaneous vascular changes noted in patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis results from increased density of thickened vessels. Mosaicism for MAP2K1 in skin overlying the affected bone was not seen in the one patient (Melo-16) with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis who did not have any cutaneous alterations. However, similar histological changes of thickened vessels were also reported in skin overlying the melorheostotic lesions in four of five patients with MAP2K1-negative melorheostosis. Patients with MAP2K1-negative melorheostosis, however, lacked a cutaneous vascular phenotype. Realistically, these histological changes noted in skin samples are quite subtle and limited by variation in vascular supply of different skin sites although the clinical phenotype clearly showed the vascular changes in patients with MAP2K1-positive patients, which was not seen in patients with MAP2K1-negative melorheostosis. Thus, the functional role of these histological changes remains to be explored. Evaluation by a dermatologist can provide a clue to a diagnosis of MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis, as only patients with somatic MAP2K1 mutations had overlying vascular skin changes.

Malignant transformation has been reported rarely in melorheostosis.(18-20) Our pathological and immunohistochemical findings confirm the nonmalignant nature of melorheostosis in both MAP2K1-positive and MAP2K1-negative patients. Absence of mutation in any additional overgrowth-causing gene beyond those originally identified in the 56G oncology panel further supports the nonmalignant nature of the disease. The natural history of the disease and lack of metastatic lesions outside of the anatomical area where the disease is found also supports a nonmalignant nature of melorheostosis. Although mutations in the negative regulatory domain in MAP2K1 have been found in a variety of tumors, when present in the germline in cardio-facio-cutaneous syndrome, these mutations do not appear to pose the same malignancy risk as found in other RASopathies like neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1).(21) Similar MAP2K1 mutations have been reported in variant hairy cell leukemia(22); however, to our knowledge, there are no reported cases of hematologic malignancy in patients with melorheostosis. Coupled with the absence of mutations in MAP2K1 in peripheral blood of patients with mosaic mutations in bone, this supports the premise that the mutation in melorheostosis occurs in a skeletal progenitor cell. Accumulation of additional mutations with aging and metabolic or environmental factors may lead to malignant transformation of melorheostosis. However, long-term follow-up is required to determine the risk of malignancy in these patients.

Treatment for melorheostosis remains symptomatic. The finding of somatic activating MAP2K1 mutations driving the disease in nearly 60% of our patients raises the possibility of use of inhibitors targeting the MAPK pathway in select patients with MAP2K1-positive melorheostosis. Of note, two inhibitors of MEK1,trametinib and cobimetinib, are approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of melanoma with mutations in BRAF, and another, binimetinib, has been studied for cardiac hypertrophy associated with the RASopathy, Noonan syndrome (Clinicaltrials.gov
NCT01556568).

Our study has limitations. First, the diagnosis of melorheostosis was ascertained based on radiographic appearance with corresponding increased uptake on 18F-NaF PET/CT imaging. This criterion is relatively subjective and may vary based on the experience of the clinician. Although we did not enroll any patient with a family history of occult bone diseases like osteopoikilosis, Buschke-Ollendorff syndrome, or melorheostosis, we did not perform radiographic or genetic evaluation to confirm family history.

As more melorheostosis patients undergo genetic testing of bone and especially of overlying skin, further knowledge of the genetic basis and genotype-phenotype correlations in melorheostosis and other diseases of high bone mass will emerge.
