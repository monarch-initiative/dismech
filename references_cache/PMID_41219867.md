# Multi-organ single-cell analysis reveals PLD4 as a biomarker and potential therapeutic target of blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm.
**Authors:** Wang M, Xue X, Wang Z, Wang C, Yu G, Mi Z, Sang X, Wang J, Liu Y, Chen X, Chen S, Zhou G, Liu H, Chen H, Sun Y, Zhang F
**Journal:** Cancer Cell Int (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12935-025-04038-9](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-025-04038-9)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm (BPDCN) is a rare hematological malignancy originating from precursors of plasmacytoid dendritic cells. This study aimed to understand the relationship between malignant clones in different tissues and determine new biomarkers to improve the management of BPDCN.

METHODS: We conducted a comprehensive analysis by integrating single-cell transcriptomic data from bone marrow (BM)-derived cells, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and skin-derived cells from one BPDCN patient and matched healthy controls (HCs). Validation was performed in two independent cohorts consisting of 12 BPDCN patients and control groups, including common skin tumors, cutaneous lymphomas, inflammatory skin diseases and HCs.

RESULTS: Our study revealed that malignant cells in the skin exhibited greater maturity and differentiation compared with those from BMs, suggesting that the BM is a more likely origin for BPDCN. Additionally, malignant cells across different tissues in the BPDCN patient displayed significant transcriptional heterogeneity. The multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) analysis revealed that the expression of PLD4 was significantly elevated in the dermis of BPDCN patients compared with the control groups. Furthermore, most PLD4-positive cells were found to co-localize with CD123 in BPDCN skin lesions.

CONCLUSIONS: This study suggested a potential clonal origin of tumor cells, and revealed the transcriptional heterogeneity among malignant cells across different tissues in BPDCN. PLD4 was identified as a specific marker of malignant cells, aiding in the diagnosis of BPDCN and showing potential as a therapeutic target.

Blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm (BPDCN) is a rare hematological malignancy originating from the precursors of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs). It can affect the bone marrow (BM), peripheral blood (PB), skin, and lymph nodes [1, 2]. Although BPDCN is classified as a hematological malignancy, 90% of patients present with skin involvement. Additionally, 64% exhibit skin-only diseases at diagnosis, suggesting a possible cutaneous origin [3, 4]. However, recent publication used integrated genomic and single-cell analysis suggest that BPDCN arises from clonal haematopoietic precursors in the BM, highlighting a potential BM origin [5]. Therefore, the exact origin of malignant clones remains unclear [6, 7].

The diagnosis of BPDCN is challenging because of the absence of straightforward diagnostic criteria based on immunohistochemistry (IHC), particularly concerning the expression of CD4, CD56, CD123 (IL3RA), and TCL1 markers, as well as the absence of lymphatic and myeloid markers [8]. Additionally, diagnostic challenges arise due to phenotypic overlap with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [9, 10], and the absence of typical markers or the presence of atypical phenotypes in some BPDCN patients [11]. Until recently, no consensus guidelines existed for managing BPDCN. Although novel therapies targeting CD123 have achieved response rates up to 90%, many patients still experience relapse with monotherapy. Treatment outcomes for recurrent/refractory BPDCN remain suboptimal [12, 13]. Therefore, elucidating the pathogenesis of BPDCN and developing new therapeutic strategies are crucial for improving clinical outcomes.

In this study, we analyzed the BM-derived cells, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and skin-derived cells of a BPDCN patient and matched healthy controls (HCs) using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). We sought to gain insights into the relationship between malignant clones in different tissues and identify novel biomarkers for improving BPDCN management.

Samples from six BPDCN patients, three Natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (NKTL) patients, three cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) patients, four basal cell carcinoma (BCC) patients, three melanoma patients, two diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) patients, two mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) patients, three langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) patients, six mycosis fungoides (MF) patients, three atopic dermatitis (AD) patients, and five psoriasis (PsO) patients, and eleven HCs were collected from the Shandong Provincial Hospital for Skin Disease, Shandong First Medical University. Samples from six BPDCN patients of the second validation cohort were collected from the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College. All patients were diagnosed based on clinical manifestations, histopathological examination, and other auxiliary examinations [13]. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of BPDCN patients are shown in Table S1. Three control BM samples (from GEO database: GSM3396161, GSM3396162 and GSM3396165) and three control PBMCs were provided by healthy donors (Table S2), and six control skin biopsies were obtained from fracture patients without tumor-related diseases. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The human research protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board, Shandong Provincial Hospital for Skin Diseases. This study was conducted in strict adherence to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This study has been approved by the ethics committees of Shandong Provincial Hospital for Skin Disease, Shandong First Medical University with reference ID of 2,021,000,614.

Density gradient centrifugation was used to isolate BM-derived cells and PBMCs using a lymphocyte separation solution (MD pacific). Skin biopsy samples were disassociated with Dispase II (Sigma) to effectively separate the epidermal and dermal layers. The minced epidermis was subjected to further enzymatic digestion with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) for a duration of 30 min, followed by filtration through a 70 µm cell strainer (Falcon). The dermis was digested with 1 mg/mL Collagenase P (Sigma-Aldrich) and 100 µg/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) for 50 min and filtered using a 70 µm cell strainer (Falcon).

Single-cell barcoding and library construction were executed using the 10x Chromium platform (10×Genomics). Subsequently, the prepared libraries were subjected to sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument. For each sample’s dataset, the initial sequencing reads were aligned and the expression levels were measured using the CellRanger software suite (version 6.5.0, 10×Genomics) in conjunction with the GRCh38 human reference genome. The resultant gene expression matrix underwent further processing and analysis through the Seurat R package (version 4.4.0) running on R (version 4.2.1). For quality control, we removed the cells with total unique molecular identifier fewer than 500 or genes fewer than 200 or higher than 10,000. We also removed all cells with more than 10% mitochondrial gene counts or those with more than 10% red blood cell reads [14]. Additionally, cells identified as potential doublets by the Scrublet Python package (version 0.2) were excluded from the analysis [15]. The copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed using the inferCNV R package (version 1.14.0), and the pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using the Monocle 2 R package. More detailed analysis methods are in supplementary materials.

Each specimen was thoroughly evaluated by the same investigator without prior knowledge of the disease history or laboratory findings. Skin biopsies were fixed in neutral buffered formalin, and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The embedded tissue was then sectioned into 2-μm thin slices. Color development was performed using a seven-color multiple fluorescent immunohistochemical staining kit (Akoya, DE, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The sections were imaged using the EVOS FL Auto 2 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For mIHC, anti-IL3RA/CD123 antibody (1:100, ab280355, Abcam), and phospholipase D4 (PLD4) polyclonal antibody (1:500, Product # PA5-98680, Invitrogen) were used.

The immunohistochemical staining in BPDCN and control samples was evaluated semiquantitatively based on four parameters: (1) intensity (negative = 0; weak = 1; moderate = 2; strong = 3), (2) extent (calculated by inForm 2.4 software), (3) variance (homogeneous or heterogeneous), and (4) relative localization (coexpression or non-coexpression). The H-score, defined as the product of the intensity score and the extent of staining, was derived for each case. An H-score of 20 was set as a cutoff for positivity.

A patient in his sixties developed scattered nodules over a period of three months. The dermatological examination revealed dark red nodules of varying sizes and without ulceration, itching, or tenderness (Fig. S1A). The histopathological evaluation of a skin biopsy showed a dense dermal infiltration of atypical cells highly suggestive of BPDCN. Immunohistochemistry revealed that the neoplastic cells were CD56+, CD123+, CD43+, weakly CD4+, and focally CD163+, but negative for CD3, CD20, and CD30 (Fig. S1B). The histologic examination of lymph node revealed the infiltration of conspicuous atypical mononuclear cells, whereas the bone marrow biopsy demonstrated an increased number of megakaryocytes and blasts, further confirming BPDCN (Fig. S1C and S1D). Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) scans showed lymphadenopathy at the base of the neck, mediastinum, axilla, and bilateral inguinal regions, splenomegaly, and mild pleural effusion (Fig. S1E). The patient received multi-agent chemotherapy, including vindesine, idarubicin, cyclophosphamide, pegaspargase and prednisone. Despite initial improvement, he relapsed after 4 months and died 6 months post-diagnosis.

We conducted scRNA-seq on BMs from one BPDCN patient and three HCs, PBMCs from the same patient and three HCs, as well as skin lesions from the patient alongside normal skin biopsies from six HCs to better understand the malignant cell characteristics in BPDCN. The flow chart for the study design is shown in Fig. 1. In addition, we collected five BPDCN patients from our institute as the first validation cohort and six BPDCN patients as the second validation cohort. Detailed clinical information on the twelve BPDCN patients is shown in Table S1. The mutational profiling of BPDCN using next-generation sequencing revealed that patients had TET2, ASXL1 and NRAS mutations (Table S4).

We performed an integrated analysis of single-cell transcriptomes of a total of 25,425 cells from BM, PB and skin tissue of this BPDCN patient to investigate the relationship between malignant clones across tissues in BPDCN (Fig. 2A). Tumor cells (cluster 1), identified based on the expression of canonical gene markers such as TCL1A, IRF8, TCF4, IL3RA and IRF7 [16], along with apparent copy number variation (CNV), were predominantly found in the BM (68.3%) and PB (31.3%), with minimal presence in the skin (0.4%) (Fig. S2A-D).

Next, we performed re-clustering and pseudotime trajectory analysis on all malignant cells (Fig. 2B-D). Their distribution along the trajectory within each sample source is shown in Fig. 2E. We found that BM-derived malignant cells appeared throughout the trajectory, whereas PB-derived malignant cells were distributed at both the start and end sites (Fig. 2E). In contrast, the malignant cells from skin tissue were predominantly distributed at the intermediate site of the trajectory (Fig. 2E). We then analyzed the gene expression patterns of all genes along the trajectory of BPDCN progression (Fig. 2F). The expression of MKI67 and HMGB2, genes involved in the cell cycle and cell proliferation, was significantly elevated at the starting point of the trajectory (Fig. 2G). Similar trends were observed for the expression of TUBA1B and TUBB (Fig. 2G). This suggested that malignant cells at the beginning of the pseudotime differentiation branch were in an early differentiation stage, and these malignant cells were primarily derived from the BM. However, skin-derived malignant cells showed low expression of cell proliferation-related genes MKI67 and HMGB2. Thus, skin-derived malignant cells might be in a more mature and differentiated state compared with those derived from BM. Consequently, BM was hypothesized to be a more plausible origin for BPDCN compared with the skin.

We performed scRNA-seq analysis on BMs, PBs, and skin tissues from the BPDCN patient and matched HCs to better understand the malignant cell characteristics across different tissues in BPDCN (Figs. 3A, 4A and 5A). The proportion of each sample and the cell numbers within each cluster were also further analyzed (Figs. 3B, 4B and 5B). In BMs, the largest differences in cell proportions between BPDCN patient and HCs were observed in cells from clusters 2, 6, and 7, whereas in PBs, the most significant differences were found in cells from clusters 8, 11, and 16 (Figs. 3B and 4B). Except for cluster 5, the cell proportions in other clusters of skin tissues from the BPDCN patient showed no significant difference compared with HCs (Fig. 5B). The malignant cells in BMs were identified in clusters 2 and 7 based on the expression of canonical gene markers [17] and apparent CNVs (Fig. 3C and D). Additionally, cluster 11 was identified as containing malignant cells in PBs (Fig. 4C and D). In the skin, T cells (cluster 12) and DCs (cluster 13) were subclustered, resulting in the identification of eight cell subtypes (Fig. 5D). Based on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we hypothesized that subtype 8 represented a population of highly malignant cells (Fig. 5E).

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of the top 20 upregulated DEGs (adjusted P value < 0.05, log2 fold change (log2FC) > 1, and pct.2 < 0.1) in malignant cells from BMs, PBs and skin tissues, compared to other benign cells, revealing the phenotypic heterogeneity of BPDCN across tissue compartments (Figs. 3E, 4E and 5E). Next, we performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis for each subgroup of malignant cells from different tissues, revealing that the common functions of malignant cells were related to antigen processing and presentation, similar to the role of pDCs (Figs. 3F, 4F and 5F). Additionally, the tumor progression-related signaling pathways, such as the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, sphingolipid metabolism and Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway, were also activated in malignant cells across different tissues (Figs. 3F, 4F and 5F).

In addition to transcriptional heterogeneity, a common gene expression signature was identified in malignant cells from different tissues, including PLD4, CD123, TCL1A, LAMP5 and FAM129C (Fig. 6A). As cutaneous involvement constitutes the predominant initial manifestation in most patients with BPDCN, we performed mIHC analysis on skin biopsies from the first validation cohort of six BPDCN patients, examining the expression of PLD4 and CD123. The expression of these genes was also analyzed in control groups, including common skin tumors (CSCC, BCC and melanoma), cutaneous lymphoma (NKTL, DLBCL, MCL, LCH and MF), inflammatory skin diseases (psoriasis and AD), and HCs. The mIHC analysis revealed that the expression of PLD4 and CD123 was significantly elevated in the dermis of BPDCN patients compared to common skin tumors, cutaneous lymphoma, inflammatory skin diseases and HCs (Fig. 6D and E). Furthermore, most PLD4-positive cells co-localized with CD123 in BPDCN skin lesions (Fig. 6D and S3B). We evaluated the diagnostic performance of PLD4 and CD123 immunostaining for BPDCN (Table S3). Both CD123 and PLD4 immunostain demonstrated 100% sensitivity. The specificity was 77.08% (37/48) for CD123 and 83.33% (40/48) for PLD4. Notably, the non-BPDCN samples that stained positive exhibited substantially lower H-scores (CD123: range 8–38, PLD4: range 8–65) compared with those from BPDCN patients (CD123: range 230–240, PLD4: range 245–265). To further assess the diagnostic accuracy, we performed IHC analysis on skin biopsies from the second validation cohort of six BPDCN patients, and the results showed that PLD4 was positive in the dermis of all patients (Fig. S3A). Given the higher sensitivity and specficity of PLD4, and their co-localization with CD123, we propose that PLD4 could serve as a potential specific marker for tumor cells, thereby facilitating the diagnosis of BPDCN. And targeting PLD4 inhibition might serve as a promising therapeutic approach for BPDCN treatment.

BPDCN is a rare, highly aggressive hematological malignancy that primarily affects the skin, BM, lymph nodes, and may also involve extranidal sites [18]. Currently, the evolutionary relationship among tissue-specific malignant clones in BPDCN is still unclear. The diagnostic criteria and formal treatment guidelines for BPDCN have not been fully established. In this study, we conducted a multi-organ analysis of the BM, PB, and skin samples from a BPDCN patient and their respective matched HCs using scRNA-seq technology, aiming to explore the intricate relationships among malignant clones across different tissues, and identify novel biomarkers for enhancing the diagnosis, prognosis, and management of BPDCN.

BPDCN can affect multiple tissues and organs, including the skin, BM and PB, yet there is a gap in the understanding of disease progression across different tissues. Previous studies have reported that most patients (90%) have skin involvement and 64% have only skin lesions, suggesting that BPDCN may originate in the skin [3, 4]. However, recent phylogenomic analysis indicated that BPDCN arise from clonal (pre-malignant) hematopoietic precursors in the BM [5]. Some studies thought that the evolutionary relationship among tissue-specific malignant clones could identify the tissue of origin of malignant cells and provide a map of tumor evolution [19]. Therefore, we employed scRNA-seq to examine cells from BM, PB and skin samples of one BPDCN patient, identifying malignant cells and conducting pseudotime trajectory analysis on all malignant cells. Our study found that malignant cells from the skin exhibited greater maturity and differentiation compared to those from BM, suggesting that the BM was hypothesized to be a more plausible origin for BPDCN, consistent with recent findings [5].

Due to its rarity and highly invasive nature, a global consensus on BPDCN treatment methods has yet to be established. Historically, various therapeutic strategies, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), have been attempted, but their outcomes have been deemed unsatisfactory [20–22]. Despite initial chemotherapy response rates of 40% to 90%, early relapse rates remain alarmingly high [23–25]. Moreover, with a median diagnosis age of approximately 70 years, HSCT and intensive chemotherapy are not suitable for many patients with BPDCN [20]. A major advancement in the field was the discovery of the near-ubiquitous expression of CD123 in BPDCN, paving the way for the development and approval of the novel treatment involving a recombinant human CD123 protein linked to diphtheria toxin. Although recent targeted CD123 therapies have changed the treatment landscape of BPDCN, significant challenges remain [26]. While the efficacy of first-line treatment is promising, relapse under monotherapy is common, and outcomes for recurrent/refractory BPDCN remains not optimistic [13, 27, 28]. Our study identified PLD4 as a more sensitive marker than CD123, and its co-localization with CD123 suggests that PLD4 could serve as a potential specific marker of tumor cells, enhancing BPDCN diagnosis. PLD4 is a transmembrane glycoprotein belonging to the phospholipase D family, primarily located in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus [29, 30]. Recent studies confirmed that PLD4 exhibits evolutionarily conserved expression in pDCs [31]. PLD4 also plays a crucial role in immune regulation, and had been implicated in the pathogenesis of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [32, 33] as well as other autoimmune diseases [34–37]. Malignant transformed cells in BPDCN might still retain part of the pDC gene expression program, which could account for the high PLD4 expression observed in BPDCN tumor cells. Although aberrant expression of PLDs, particularly PLD1 and PLD2, has been observed in various cancers, and inhibition or elimination of PLD activity has been shown to reduce tumor growth and metastasis [38], the role of PLD4 in tumorigenesis has yet to be elucidated. The regulatory mechanisms underlying the elevated PLD4 expression in BPDCN cells, as well as the molecular pathways through which PLD4 functions in BPDCN, remain to be clarified. At present, a phase I clinical trial using a PLD4 inhibitor for the treatment of SLE is ongoing in Japan (jRCT2071230085). These also indicated that PLD4 inhibitors could represent a promising therapeutic approach for BPDCN.

A limitation of this study was that scRNA-seq was performed on samples from only a single BPDCN patient, and cell numbers from the skin tissue of the BPDCN patient were limited. However, the single-cell transcriptional data derived from different tissues of a BPDCN patient were sufficient to answer the key scientific questions. Additionally, a large number of samples from various skin tumors, inflammatory skin diseases and HCs were used for mIHC experiments to further validate the BPDCN-specific marker PLD4.

In conclusion, the present study uncovered the potential clonal origin of tumor cells and highlighted the transcriptional heterogeneity of malignant cells across different tissues in BPDCN. PLD4 was identified as a specific marker of malignant cells to facilitate the diagnosis of BPDCN and offer new perspectives for precise targeted BPDCN therapy.
