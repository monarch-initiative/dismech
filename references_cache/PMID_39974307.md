# Reply.
**Authors:** Vincent D, Martin L, Cichon S, Drouet C, Ghannam A, other authors
**Journal:** J Allergy Clin Immunol Glob (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jacig.2025.100406](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacig.2025.100406)

## Content

To the Editor:

We appreciate the comments from Giavina-Bianchi et al1 on our article in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: Global.2 We fully support the message that bradykinin-mediated angioedema most often presents without urticaria and that patients with hereditary angioedema (HAE) can experience erythema marginatum, a prodromal manifestation that sometimes closely mimics urticaria.

HAE with carboxypeptidase N (CPN) deficiency, HAE-CPN, is the first HAE endotype with kinin accumulation to depend only on a defective bradykinin and anaphylatoxin catabolism. As also observed in HAE endotypes, angioedema and urticarial manifestations of HAE-CPN may or may not be concomitant. It actually breaks conventional paradigms, but its identification will be helpful to physicians and patients who are embarrassed by any observation of both bradykinin-angioedema and urticaria in a subset of patients.

As underlined by prior observations, a diagnosis of bradykinin-angioedema cannot be ruled out on the basis of a positive medical history of urticaria, and a misdiagnosis of erythema marginatum as urticaria may delay the diagnosis of HAE.3,4 There are few reports on the incidence of urticaria compared with the number of reports on other types of prodromal skin rash in patients with HAE. In this context, we have stated that bradykinin-angioedema cannot be ruled out solely on the basis of a positive medical history of urticaria.4 The lifetime incidence of urticaria might be higher in patients with HAE than in the general population.4

Indeed, in a nationwide retrospective analysis, Rasmussen et al showed that 22 of 87 patients with HAE (25%) reported at least 1 episode of urticaria, which is a higher prevalence than in the general population.3 The urticaria was acute, inducible (“physical”), or referred to as chronic spontaneous urticaria.3 Such clinical presentations of HAE are with erythematous and edematous pitting rather than with pruritic papules.5 On a basis similar to that of HAE-CPN, a lack of kinin catabolism likely accumulates ligand onto the receptor in iatrogenic angiotensin I converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi) angioedema, with observation of various types of rash, including urticaria. In contradiction to a clinical dogma that if urticaria is present the angioedema cannot be caused by ACEi, rash is not uncommon in a consistent number of patients with angioedema owing to ACEi or angiotensin II receptor–blocking agents.

Whether urticaria in some patients with bradykinin-angioedema is in some ways pathophysiologically different from common acute or chronic urticaria remains to be investigated. Some clues to its understanding have already been shown. Bradykinin contributes to anaphylaxis and allergic diseases with increased vascular permeability, and this reaction cascade is also initiated by allergen-stimulated mast cells. Swelling may be precipitated in patients with HAE by allergen exposure, emphasizing a role of mast cells in initiating bradykinin-angioedema.

Anaphylatoxins are mast cell nonimmune activators. Accumulation of both kinins and anaphylatoxins in a context of lack of their catabolism develops a synergistic effect on their receptors of endothelial and mast cells, driving their combined activation to subsequent angioedema and urticaria in patients with the HAE-CPN endotype. The HAE-CPN endotype actually adds complexity but also illustrates subtle and fascinating interactions between mast cell activation and bradykinin metabolism.
