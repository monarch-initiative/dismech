# Pathogenic variants in the human TONSL protein associated with SPONASTRIME dysplasia impair protein dimerization and DNA repair.
**Authors:** Karmakar A, Roy S
**Journal:** Sci Adv (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1126/sciadv.aee1129](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aee1129)

## Content

TONSL safeguards genome stability by facilitating replication-dependent DNA damage repair and protecting stalled replication forks through homologous recombination. Mutations in TONSL cause SPONASTRIME dysplasia, a rare skeletal disorder. We reveal that TONSL homo-dimerizes via its ubiquitin-like domain (UBL), and two recurrent SPONASTRIME dysplasia causative variants (R934W and G973R) abolish this dimerization. Crystal structures at 1.9 Å resolution show UBLWT forms domain-swapped dimers that assemble into ring-like octamers. The R934W variant eliminates critical hydrogen bonds and introduces steric clashes, forcing monomeric conformation. G973R destabilizes an evolutionarily conserved residue within a conformationally restricted β-turn. Biochemically, UBLWT exists as dimers while UBLR934W remains monomeric. Functionally, dimerization-deficient variants fail to suppress replication stress-induced DNA damage, show impaired RAD51 foci formation, and exhibit severely compromised survival following genotoxic stress. These findings establish TONSL dimerization as essential for genome maintenance and provide structural and mechanistic insights into SPONASTRIME dysplasia pathogenesis.

Maintenance of genome integrity during DNA replication is essential for cell survival and organismal development, as replication forks frequently encounter DNA lesions, topological stress, and protein-DNA complexes that can lead to fork stalling and collapse, generating cytotoxic DNA double-strand breaks (1). Eukaryotic cells counteract these threats through genome maintenance pathways, with the TONSL-MMS22L complex working as a critical regulator of replication fork stability and homologous recombination (2).

Loss of TONSL or MMS22L causes hypersensitivity to replication stress inducing agents, activation of ATR/ATM checkpoints, and accumulation of γH2AX and RPA foci (3–5). TONSL-MMS22L complex associates with multiple replication and chromatin-associated factors like MCM helicase, FACT complex, ASF1A/B histone chaperones, Ku70/Ku80, and core histones (6). The complex localizes to replication forks and DNA damage sites, where it binds to RPA-coated single-stranded DNA, and facilitates Rad51 mediated DNA strand exchange promoting homologous recombination-mediated fork restart (7).

TONSL specifically recognizes newly replicated chromatin via its ankyrin repeat domain, which binds histone H4 tails unmethylated at lysine 20 (H4K20me0), thereby targeting homologous recombination to post-replicative DNA (8). In contrast, in plants, during DNA replication, newly incorporated H3.1 recruits TONSOKU (Plant homologue of TONSL) via its TPR domain to facilitate the repair of replication-associated DNA break (9). TONSL-MMS22L complex also interacts with anti-recombinase FIGNL1 and is critical for homologous recombination in BRCA2/FIGNL1 double-deficient cells (10).

TONSL is an immortalizing oncogene (11) whose amplification and overexpression drive malignant phenotypes including replicative immortality, immune evasion, and chemoresistance across multiple cancer types such as triple-negative breast cancer (12), hepatocellular carcinoma (13, 14), and lung adenocarcinoma (15), while also maintaining cancer stem cell populations (16).

Bi-allelic variants in TONSL cause SPONASTRIME dysplasia (17–19), a rare autosomal-recessive skeletal dysplasia characterized by short stature, midface hypoplasia with a depressed nasal bridge, striation of the metaphyses, and other radiological abnormalities of the spine and long bones (20, 21). Among these variants, R934W and G973R, located in the ubiquitin-like (UBL) domain, are of special interest as embryos of knock-in mice with homozygous TonslArg924Trp/Arg924Trp (counterpart of human R934W variant) show early embryonic lethality (17) and the G973 residue is evolutionary conserved in eukaryotes. This indicates possible critical role of the UBL domain in modulating TONSL function.

Protein oligomerization represents a major regulatory mechanism in DNA repair pathways. Many double-strand break repair proteins including RAD51 (22–24), BRCA2 (25–27), CtIP (28), and the MRE11-RAD50 complex (29, 30) function as oligomers. MMS22L also undergoes phosphorylation-dependent homodimerization (31) raising the possibility that TONSL may similarly self-associate, given the presence of its UBL domain, similar to oligomerization-mediating PB1 domains (32).

Whether TONSL self-associates, which domains mediate this interaction, and how SPONASTRIME dysplasia disease-causing variants influence its quaternary structure remain unexplored. Here, we combine cellular, biochemical, and structural approaches to demonstrate that TONSL homo-dimerizes via its UBL domain, which adopts a domain-swapped dimeric architecture capable of forming higher-order ring-like octamers. The SPONASTRIME dysplasia-causative variants R934W and G973R disrupt this dimerization interface, with R934W stabilizing an obligate monomeric conformation incapable domain swapping. Functional analysis show that these dimerization-deficient variants abolish TONSL’s ability to repair DNA damage, prevent DNA breakage, and promote cellular survival during replication stress. As a whole, these findings establish dimerization as a critical regulatory mechanism for TONSL function and provide mechanistic insights into pathogenic effects of R934W and G973R disease-variants in SPONASTRIME dysplasia.

Chemical cross-linking with disuccinimidyl suberate (DSS) confirmed stable dimer formation. For endogenous TONSL, crosslinker addition generated dimeric and oligomeric species alongside a minor monomeric population in both HEK293T (Fig. 1D, left) and HepG2 cells (Fig. 1D, middle), whereas overexpressed Flag-TONSLFL formed exclusive dimers (Fig. 1D, right).

Native-PAGE followed by Western blot analysis showed that endogenous TONSL exists as both dimeric and higher order oligomeric species in both HEK293T (Fig. 1E, left) and HepG2 cells (Fig. 1E, middle), while overexpressed Flag-TONSLFL displayed both monomeric and dimeric populations (Fig. 1E, right), indicating that the dimer is the preferred native state.

To assess whether dimerization occurs in a cellular context, immunofluorescence-based colocalization experiments were performed in HepG2 cells co-expressing Flag-TONSLFL and HA-TONSLFL. The extent of colocalization between the differentially tagged proteins was quantified by confocal microscopy. Analysis of the fluorescent signals revealed extensive spatial overlap throughout cellular compartments, with a Pearson colocalization coefficient of 0.7395 ± 0.064 (Fig. 1, F and G), consistent with in-cell physical association.

The convergent evidence from these four independent experimental approaches establishes that TONSL possesses an intrinsic capacity for self-association and can form stable dimers ex vivo.

To map the specific domain responsible for mediating homo-dimerization ex vivo, we generated four Flag-tagged deletion constructs, each lacking a distinct domain: Flag-TONSLΔTPR, Flag-TONSLΔANK, Flag-TONSLΔUBL, and Flag-TONSLΔLRR (Fig. 2A). Each construct was co-expressed with HA-TONSLFL in HEK293T, followed by Flag-mediated co-immunoprecipitation analysis to assess their ability to interact with the full-length protein. Deletion of the TPR, ANK, or LRR domains had no effect on self-association, whereas UBL deletion markedly reduced co-immunoprecipitated HA-TONSLFL (Fig. 2B), identifying the UBL domain as the primary dimerization mediator.

To identify the specific residues within the UBL domain that sustain dimerization, we selected disease-associated missense variants rather than arbitrary substitutions, reasoning that pathogenic variants are more likely to disrupt functionally critical contacts. Among the reported variants in SPONASTRIME dysplasia, R934W and G973R were selected as representatives because both map directly to the UBL domain and have been confirmed as pathogenic in multiple unrelated families (17). Flag-TONSLR934W and Flag-TONSLG973R constructs were generated by site-directed mutagenesis (Fig. 2A), and their dimerization capacity was assessed by co-immunoprecipitation with HA-TONSLFL. Both variants substantially impaired TONSL self-association to a degree comparable to complete UBL deletion (Fig. 2, C and D), confirming that R934 and G973 are individually essential for the dimerization interface.

DSS-mediated cross-linking corroborated these findings. Unlike wild-type Flag-TONSLFL, which converted completely to dimers upon DSS treatment, Flag-TONSLΔUBL, Flag-TONSLR934W, and Flag-TONSLG973R all retained substantial monomeric populations after crosslinking, with only partial dimer formation (Fig. 2E), confirming their reduced dimerization propensity (Fig. 2F).

Immunofluorescence-based colocalization analysis in HepG2 cells provided cellular confirmation. Co-expression of HA-TONSLFL with Flag-TONSLΔUBL, Flag-TONSLR934W, or Flag-TONSLG973R yielded significantly reduced spatial overlap compared to wild-type Flag-TONSLFL (Fig. 2G). Quantitative image analysis revealed Pearson colocalization coefficients for Flag-TONSLΔUBL, Flag-TONSLR934W, and Flag-TONSLG973R were 0.4639 ± 0.11, 0.4641 ± 0.06, and 0.5394 ± 0.13, respectively, all significantly lower than wild-type (Fig. 2H).

Collectively, domain deletion mapping, disease-variant mutagenesis, DSS cross-linking, and immunofluorescence establish that TONSL homo-dimerization is mediated by its UBL domain, with R934 and G973 essential for the dimerization interface, and that their disruption by deletion or SPONASTRIME dysplasia-associated substitution severely impairs dimer formation.

To gain structural insights into the oligomeric properties of the UBL domain, we determined its crystal structure at 1.9 Å. Unexpectedly, the UBL domain crystallized as a domain-swapped dimer rather than a canonical monomeric fold (Fig. 3, A and B). Each monomer retains the characteristic ubiquitin-like topology featuring a mixed β sheet core flanked by α-helical elements, but the N-terminal β1 strand crosses over to stabilize the adjacent monomer, creating an intertwined domain-swapped dimeric architecture.

To determine whether the dimeric state observed in the crystal structure represents a biologically functional assembly or merely a crystallographic contact, we conducted a comprehensive interface analysis using the PISA server (33), which confirmed the dimer as a thermodynamically stable biological assembly. The structural analysis revealed that the two monomers adopt a head-to-tail arrangement with extensive intermolecular contacts. Each monomer buries ∼2040 Å2 (∼32% of its total accessible surface area of 6440 Å2) at the interface, with a solvation free energy gain (ΔGint) of −32.4 kcal/mol and a dissociation free energy (ΔGdiss) of 14 kcal/mol. The resulting dimer adopts an elongated, cylindrical shape (Fig. 3C) that contrasts with the compact globular monomer, potentially generating novel interaction surfaces for binding partners in vivo.

The dimer interface is stabilized by a cooperative network of hydrogen bonds and salt bridges (Fig. 3D). Main-chain atoms of β strand 1 from one monomer form an extensive hydrogen-bond network with β strands 2 and 4 of the other monomer. Additionally, Arg934, Arg936 and Gln938 form multiple salt bridge interactions, with Glu1002 and Asp941 enhancing overall stability. Electrostatic surface analysis further reveals complementary charge distribution across the dimeric interface (Fig. 3E). A positively charged patch contributed by Lys and Arg residues in one monomer precisely aligns with a negatively charged surface dominated by Asp and Glu residues from the other monomer, facilitating initial monomer docking.

Altogether, these data establish the TONSL UBL domain as a bona fide domain-swapped dimer, stabilized by an extensive cooperative network of hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic interactions.

Crystal packing analysis revealed that UBL domain-swapped dimers assemble into a ring-shaped oligomeric structure comprising eight dimeric units (Fig. 4A). The assembly has an outer diameter of ∼80 Å and a central cavity of ∼40 Å, forming a toroidal architecture with clear morphological distinction between the inner and outer surfaces. The octameric ring displays pseudo-symmetric organization when viewed from both top and side perspectives. Eight dimers occupy defined positions to create a continuous, closed ring.

Structural examination of the inter-dimer interface reveals the molecular basis for octamer stabilization. The inter-dimer interface is mediated by two loop regions (residues 983–987 and 993–997) that dock onto a hydrophobic surface patch of the adjacent dimer (Fig. 4, B and C). Specific contacts between Arg981, Gly984, Ala985, and Leu986 from one dimer and Gln940, His942, and Leu943 from the neighboring unit combine hydrogen bonding with hydrophobic packing, locking adjacent dimers into the angular orientation required for ring closure. This dual-mode recognition, hydrophobic docking supplemented by polar interactions, likely ensures both specificity and thermodynamic stability of the octamer.

Electrostatic surface potential mapping reveals charge asymmetry across the octameric assembly (Fig. 4D). The inner cavity presents a mixed electropositive and electronegative environment, while the outer surface is predominantly electronegative with neutral patches, a distribution that may define regulatory binding sites.

Multiple sequence alignment across evolutionarily distant species (human, rat, mouse, bovine, zebrafish, Drosophila, and Arabidopsis) reveals that Arg934 and Gly973 are invariant, indicating strong evolutionary conservation and essential functional roles (Fig. 5A). Mutations at both positions are causally linked to SPONASTRIME dysplasia, implicating each residue in maintaining the structural integrity of the UBL domain.

The absolute conservation of Gly973 is explained by its unusual backbone geometry (Fig. 5B). In the wild-type UBL domain, Gly973 adopts dihedral angles of φ = 76.9° and ψ = 12.9°, placing it in a region of the Ramachandran plot that is sterically forbidden for any residue bearing a β-carbon. The positive phi angle is particularly noteworthy, as it requires backbone geometry that would generate severe steric clashes between the carbonyl oxygen and side chain atoms in any residue other than glycine. Only glycine, lacking a side chain beyond the α-carbon, possesses sufficient conformational freedom to adopt this energetically strained backbone arrangement without incurring prohibitive steric penalties. Replacement of glycine with arginine introduces a bulky, charged side chain that cannot be accommodated in this restricted conformational space, inevitably leading to local structural distortion or misfolding. Consistent with this interpretation, in vitro protein purification demonstrates that the UBLG973R is severely destabilized and precipitates from solution, confirming that this substitution fundamentally compromises protein folding and stability.

At the wild-type dimer interface, Arg934 from Monomer 1 forms a salt bridge with Glu1002 from Monomer 2, providing an electrostatic anchor that stabilizes the domain-swapped configuration. The positions of both residues are unambiguously defined in 2Fo-Fc electron density maps contoured at 1.0 σ (Fig. 5C).

To determine the structural consequence of the disease-associated R934W substitution, we solved the crystal structure of the UBLR934W. In contrast to wild-type UBL, which assembles into domain-swapped dimers, the UBLR934W crystallizes exclusively as a stable monomer (Fig. 5, D and E). Side and top views of the structure reveal that UBLR934W retains the canonical ubiquitin-like β-grasp fold, with a four-stranded β sheet surrounding a central α helix. The preservation of overall tertiary structure indicates that the R934W substitution does not cause global destabilization but rather selectively disrupts quaternary assembly. High-resolution electron density maps provide unambiguous validation of the Trp-934 substitution (Fig. 5F). The 2Fo-Fc maps contoured at 1.0 σ show well-defined density for the tryptophan indole ring, validating the mutant residue incorporation. Replacement of Arg934 with tryptophan abolishes dimerization through two synergistic effects: loss of the Arg934/Glu1002 salt bridge eliminates the electrostatic driving force for dimer formation, while the bulky indole moiety sterically occludes the interface required for strand exchange. Overall, these changes lock the UBL domain in a closed, monomeric conformation incapable of higher-order assembly.

Together, these data establish Arg934 as a molecular switch governing UBL dimerization, and show that its substitution by tryptophan in SPONASTRIME dysplasia traps the domain in an obligate monomeric state.

To assess whether the structural differences between UBLWT and UBLR934W persist in solution, we performed biophysical characterization of both proteins. UBLG973R was excluded due to severe instability precluding in vitro purification. Size-exclusion chromatography showed that UBLWT eluted as two peaks (PWT1 at 92.65 ml; PWT2 at 101.87 ml), indicating equilibrium between two distinct oligomeric states, whereas UBLR934W exhibited a single peak (PR934W at 105.26 ml) (Fig. 6A). Molecular weight estimates from a gel filtration standard curve (Fig. 6B) revealed that PWT2 corresponds to 11.4 kDa, closely matching the theoretical monomeric mass of 9.9 kDa. The earlier-eluting PWT1 peak yielded a calculated molecular weight of 28.3 kDa, consistent with an elongated dimer. Notably, UBLR934W exhibited an apparent molecular weight of 8.2 kDa, slightly below the theoretical monomeric mass, suggesting a more compact globular conformation or anomalous migration behavior characteristic of tightly folded monomeric proteins on size-exclusion matrices.

Chemical cross-linking using 1,5-difluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (DFDNB) confirmed the difference in dimerization propensity. UBLWT generated prominent dimeric (∼20 kDa) and oligomeric (>35 kDa) bands with signal intensity increasing across protein concentrations (2–16 μg) (Fig. 6C), whereas UBLR934W showed substantially reduced dimeric and oligomeric band formation under identical conditions (Fig. 6D). Quantification of dimer-to-monomer ratios confirmed the superior dimerization capacity of UBLWT relative to UBLR934W (Fig. 6E).

Hydrodynamic radius (Rh), measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS), reflects the radius of a sphere with the same translational diffusion coefficient as the protein (34) and is commonly used to estimate effective protein size in solution. UBLWT exhibited a mean hydrodynamic radius (Rh) of 6.791 nm, ∼1.5-fold larger than UBLR934W (4.576 nm), consistent with a dimeric UBLWT and a predominantly monomeric UBLR934W (Fig. 6F).

Thermal stability analyses using differential scanning calorimetry revealed distinct unfolding behaviors for UBLWT and UBLR934W. UBLWT displayed a biphasic melting profile with transitions at Tm1 = 71.97°C and Tm2 = 93.03°C (Fig. 6G), consistent with sequential dimer dissociation followed by monomer unfolding (35, 36). In contrast, UBLR934W exhibited a single sharp melting transition at 106.81°C, substantially higher than either UBLWT transition, indicating the enhanced thermostability of UBLR934W (Fig. 6H). Temperature-dependent circular dichroism spectroscopy corroborated this finding. UBLWT showed progressive secondary-structure loss upon heating, as monitored by changes in CD signal at 208 nm, 218 nm and 222 nm (Fig. 6I), whereas UBLR934W exhibited superior resistance to temperature-induced denaturation, maintaining secondary structure at higher temperatures (Fig. 6J), confirming that the monomeric variant is intrinsically more stable than the wild-type protein.

Altogether, these solution-based biophysical analyses establish that the R934W variant fundamentally alters UBL dimerization dynamics, converting a protein that readily forms stable dimers and higher-order assemblies into an obligate monomer.

Western blot analysis revealed that TONSL depletion led to increased phosphorylation of key DDR proteins, including Chk1 (Ser296), Chk2 (Thr68), and RPA32 (Ser4/Ser8) which was even more exaggerated upon CPT treatment (Fig. 7B). Notably, while overexpression TONSLFL significantly reduced the phosphorylation events to near control levels, both the disease-associated variants, and TONSLΔUBL failed to do so. Quantitative analysis of the western blots (Fig. 7C) showed cells expressing TONSLR934W, TONSLG973R, or TONSLΔUBL variants exhibited significantly high persistent hyperphosphorylation of checkpoint kinases and RPA32, comparable to empty vector control, indicating inability to reverse the genotoxic stress due to CPT treatment. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated elevated γ-H2AX foci, a marker of DNA double-strand breaks, in TONSL-depleted cells treated with CPT. Cells overexpressing wild-type TONSL showed significantly reduced γ-H2AX foci formation, whereas disease-causative variants and TONSLΔUBL were unable to repair the DNA damage, showing markedly higher accumulation of γ-H2AX foci (Fig. 7, D and E). Phospho-CHK2 (T68) foci were similarly elevated in cells expressing the dimerization-deficient constructs relative to wild-type (Fig. 7, F and G), further confirming compromised checkpoint control. RAD51 foci formation, indicative of active repair via homologous recombination (HR) pathway, were markedly reduced in cells harboring TONSLΔUBL, TONSLR934W, and TONSLG973R relative to TONSLFL (Fig. 7, H and I), suggesting impaired HR functions.

Finally, to directly evaluate the extent of DNA damage, a comet assay was performed, in which fragmented DNA migrates further during electrophoresis, and the olive tail moment serves as a quantitative measure of DNA breaks. Expectedly, TONSL-depleted cells showed a marked increase in tail moments upon CPT treatment, reflecting extensive DNA damage. Reintroduction of wild-type TONSL significantly reduced the tail length, demonstrating effective rescue of the DNA damage. In contrast, cells expressing disease-associated TONSL variants or the TONSLΔUBL variant failed to prevent DNA damage accumulation and continued to display substantially longer comet tails. (Fig. 7, J and K). Overall, these findings demonstrate that SPONASTRIME dysplasia-associated variants (R934W and G973R) functionally inactivate TONSL by disrupting homo-dimerization, phenocopying complete UBL domain deletion. The mutant proteins fail to suppress DNA damage-induced checkpoint activation, cannot facilitate RAD51-mediated homologous recombination, and are unable to prevent accumulation of DNA double-strand breaks following genotoxic stress. These results establish that TONSL dimerization is essential for its DNA damage repair functions and provide molecular insight into the pathogenic mechanism underlying SPONASTRIME dysplasia.

To assess the functional consequences of impaired DNA damage response and homologous recombination observed with dimerization-deficient SPONASTRIME dysplasia variants, we examined cellular survival following replication stress. Given that TONSL plays a critical role in maintaining replication fork stability and promoting homologous recombination repair, we hypothesized that disease-associated variants would sensitize cells to replication stress-inducing agents. To assess this, we performed colony formation assays using cells depleted of endogenous TONSL and transfected with wild-type or disease-causative variant TONSL constructs, as well as UBL-deleted TONSL constructs, which were confirmed by Western blot. Under unperturbed conditions, cells with endogenous TONSL and overexpressed Flag-TONSLFL showed significantly higher colony formation and plating efficiency compared to disease-associated variants (R934W, G973R) or TONSLΔUBL (Fig. 8A). However, upon treatment with 3 μM CPT for 3 hours, colony formation and plating efficiency was reduced for all the cell types (even cells with endogenous TONSL), except cells overexpressing wild-type TONSL which fully rescued the survival defect of TONSL-depleted cells while both the disease-associated variants and TONSLΔUBL failed to provide significant protection (Fig. 8B). These findings establish a direct molecular link between SPONASTRIME dysplasia variants and cellular vulnerability to replication stress.

Mechanistic insights into how quaternary state of TONSL is linked to its DNA repair functions has remained elusive, particularly with respect to how disease-associated variants compromise its function. This study reveals that TONSL self-association mediated by the ubiquitin-like (UBL) domain, is essential for its DNA repair activity. The domain-swapped dimeric configuration represents a structurally unique adaptation that transforms the compact ubiquitin fold into an elongated cylindrical assembly. The large buried surface area and favorable solvation energetics collectively indicate that dimerization is thermodynamically stable and likely evolutionarily conserved. Crystallographic analyses further revealed that UBL dimers organize into octameric ring-shaped assemblies, whose precise cellular relevance remains to be established. Together, these observations establish TONSL dimerization as a functionally critical quaternary state rather than an incidental structural feature.

TONSL joins a growing class of DNA repair factors whose activity is governed by oligomeric assembly. Complexes such as MRE11–RAD50 (29, 30), RAD51 (22, 23), and BRCA2 (27) rely on higher-order organization to execute their functions, suggesting that dynamic oligomerization might be a conserved regulatory mechanism in genome maintenance. In this context, the equilibrium between monomeric, dimeric, and potentially higher-order TONSL assemblies may act as a molecular rheostat that tunes repair capacity to replication stress.

Building on this structural framework, we find that SPONASTRIME dysplasia-associated variants disrupt TONSL dimerization through distinct structural mechanisms that converge on loss of dimerization. The R934W substitution redirects protein folding without compromising overall thermodynamic stability, producing a hyper-stable monomer locked in a non-swapped conformation. Loss of the Arg934-Glu1002 electrostatic interaction destabilizes the domain-swapped interface, while the bulky tryptophan side chain sterically impedes strand exchange. The elevated thermal stability of R934W relative to wild-type protein reveals that the closed, non-swapped conformation represents a thermodynamic energy minimum that traps the protein in a functionally inactive state despite intact tertiary folding. In contrast, G973R impairs TONSL dimerization through a fundamentally different mechanism involving local structural destabilization. Gly973 occupies a backbone conformation that requires glycine-specific dihedral angles, and its replacement with arginine introduces steric clashes between the guanidinium side chain and adjacent backbone carbonyl groups, promoting misfolding and aggregation. The absolute evolutionary conservation of Gly973 across metazoans is fully consistent with its strict structural requirement and explains the severe functional loss upon substitution.

The functional consequences of R934W and G973R variants were assessed by complementing TONSL-depleted cells with each variant and assessing their capacity to restore genome stability. Under these conditions, both variants show persistent DNA damage signaling, reduced RAD51 foci formation, and replication stress-induced lethality, behaving indistinguishably from complete UBL deletion. These findings indicate that disruption of UBL-mediated dimerization renders TONSL functionally inactive and establishes the structural integrity of the domain-swapped assembly as essential for DNA repair.

These results should be interpreted in the context of SPONASTRIME dysplasia, an autosomal recessive disorder in which affected individuals harbor biallelic pathogenic TONSL variants, either in homozygous or compound heterozygous form, while heterozygous carriers remain clinically unaffected (17–19). This genetic framework indicates that wild-type TONSL is haplosufficient, and that substantial loss of TONSL dimerization is required to manifest the disease phenotype. Our experimental design, in which endogenous TONSL is fully depleted and cells are complemented with individual disease-associated variants, recapitulates this biallelic loss-of-function state present in affected individuals, thereby providing a physiologically relevant context for interpreting the observed functional defects.

While the dimerization-disruption mechanism described here provides a structurally and mechanistically validated pathway to SPONASTRIME dysplasia for UBL domain variants, it is important to recognize that R934W and G973R represent only a subset of the broader allelic spectrum of the disease. Survey of pathogenic and likely pathogenic TONSL variants reported in ClinVar and the literature reveals that many map outside the UBL domain, including within the tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR), ankyrin repeat (ANK), and leucine-rich repeat (LRR) regions. These variants are likely to impair TONSL function through mechanisms distinct from defective dimerization. Future systematic functional characterization of these non-UBL domain variants will be required to determine their relative contributions to disease pathogenesis.
