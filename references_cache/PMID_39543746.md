BACKGROUND: Cogan syndrome is a rare autoimmune systemic vasculitis presenting 
with interstitial keratitis and audiovestibular symptoms. The atypical form, 
characterized by more extensive ocular lesions with audiovestibular symptoms 
appearing with a longer delay and more frequent systemic features, is usually 
underdiagnosed, delaying treatment.
CASE PRESENTATION: We report the case of a 30-year-old Mediterranean female who 
presented recurrent left red and painful eye. The evolution in flare-ups during 
3 years, associated with a nonspecific biological inflammatory response, 
motivated ocular biopsies demonstrating diffuse inflammatory changes from the 
cornea to the sclera. Since Morocco is an endemic region for tuberculosis, the 
laboratory tests were expanded to identify the latent forms based on the 
tuberculin skin test and QuantiFERON TB Gold in tube assay, both positive for 
Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection. Thus, antibacillary chemotherapy was 
started for 6 months, with reappearance of the symptoms at the end of treatment. 
Three years later, the patient presented isolated and fluctuating 
audiovestibular Ménière-like symptoms, with progressive sensorineural hearing 
loss. The nonstabilization under usual medical treatment along with her diffuse 
inflammatory ocular lesions led to the diagnosis of atypical Cogan syndrome. The 
patient received long-term corticosteroid with notable improvement.
CONCLUSION: Cogan syndrome is a rare autoimmune disease that should be 
considered when faced with ocular and audiovestibular manifestations, even in 
its atypical form, to provide early and adequate treatment, which is the main 
prognosis factor in the control of irreversible lesions. Thus, interdisciplinary 
collaboration is fundamental along with screening for other infectious and 
systemic disorders that should include tuberculosis.

Cogan syndrome (CS) is a rare autoimmune systemic vasculitis presenting with interstitial keratitis and audiovestibular symptoms. It remains a diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out systemic and infectious diseases. The atypical form, characterized by more extensive ocular lesions with audiovestibular symptoms appearing with a longer delay of more than 2 years and more frequent systemic features, is usually underdiagnosed [1].

The treatment is based on corticosteroids and immunomodulatory therapy, with promising prognosis, particularly with an early start that can improve the odds of recovering hearing loss [2].

We report the case of a 30-year-old Mediterranean female, with no medical history, who presented recurrent left red and painful eye with tearing and photophobia evolving in flare-ups during 3 years. Slit-lamp examination showed stromal keratitis with scleral edema and tenderness along with dilation of the deep episcleral vessels.

To recognize any associated systemic disorders, targeted laboratory tests were carried out, summarized as follows (Table 1): besides positive antinuclear antibodies (Anti-Jo1) and a slight nonspecific inflammatory response with C-reactive protein (CRP) elevated to 36.39 mg/ml, the laboratory tests were negative. Also, ocular magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) returned normal with no scleral thickening or enhancement or focal cellulitis. Since Morocco is an endemic region for tuberculosis (TB), the laboratory tests were expanded to identify the latent forms based on the tuberculin skin test (TST) and QuantiFERON TB Gold in tube assay (QFT-GIT), both positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection. Thus, antibacillary chemotherapy (rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol) was started for 6 months, with reappearance of the symptoms at the end of treatment.

Three years later, the patient presented isolated and recurrent audiovestibular symptoms with Ménière-like spontaneous vertigo attacks (with left canal deficit in the caloric test at 47%) with left fluctuating and progressive sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus, refractory to usual medical treatment based on betahistine (24 mg twice a day) and thiazide diuretics (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Evolution of left sensorineural hearing loss

The nonstabilization of the patient under Ménière’s medical treatment, along with her diffuse inflammatory ocular lesions from the cornea to the sclera, led to the diagnosis of atypical Cogan syndrome. The patient received long-term corticosteroid (oral prednisone of 1 mg/kg/day) but refused immunosuppressive therapies. The overall evolution was marked by notable improvement with optimal visual prognosis with no long-term ocular symptoms nor vertigo attacks, however 40 dB hearing loss remained.

Cogan syndrome (CS) is a rare autoimmune disease that has been considered as a primary variable vessel vasculitis and characterized by inflammatory response of the eye and the audiovestibular system, with possible systemic involvement [1, 2]. In 1934, it was described by Baumgartner and Morgan as a nonsyphilitic interstitial keratitis with vestibular dysfunction resembling Ménière’s syndrome [3]. Later, in 1945, it was defined as a proper clinical entity by David Cogan after describing a series of four patients [4]. Even if CS can appear at any age, the most frequently affected population is young adults (30–40 years), with no gender predominance [1, 2].

The pathogenesis of CS is still unknown. It was first related to Chlamydia psittaci infection regarding the high titers of antibodies to Chlamydia trachomatis and the previous infection signs reported in a few patients [1, 5]. However, the effective role of Chlamydia or any other infectious agent was never truly established, and it is now considered to be an autoimmune disorder with humoral and cellular immune responses leading to the expression and upregulation of cytokines such as intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and interleukin (IL)-1β, but with no particular biomarker [1, 5], until 2002, when a specific antibody associated with the pathogenesis of CS was demonstrated: an immunoglobulin G antibody against DEP-1/CD148 autoantigen on endothelial and supporting cells of the sensory nerve epithelium of the inner ear [6]. In 2023, Chen et al. described bilateral atypical CS with a positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) RNA, reporting a triggering link between coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection and systemic vasculitis, possibly explained by the fact that SARS-CoV-2 may increase angiotensin II, decrease nitric oxide production in addition to the immune dysregulation and autoantibody reactions, resulting in vasoconstriction and endothelial damage, ultimately leading to vasculitis [7].

The histopathological examination of corneal and cochlear tissues shows chronic inflammatory process with plasma cells and lymphocyte infiltration and possible cochlear neuron loss, endolymphatic hydrops, new bone formation, degenerative changes in the organ of Corti, and atrophy of vestibulocochlear cranial nerve [1], as found in our patient.

In 1980, Haynes et al. [8] classified CS into two forms: the typical form, originally defined by Cogan as a nonsyphilitic interstitial keratitis rapidly progressing then resolving, with possible recurrences, associated with audiovestibular symptoms occurring within 2 years, usually leading to rapidly aggravating deafness; and the atypical form, in which more extended ocular lesions are present with audiovestibular dysfunction appearing with a longer delay and more frequent systemic features. Nevertheless, the typical form may develop to the atypical one later [1].

CS remains a diagnosis of exclusion that needs to rule out infectious and systemic diseases, including syphilis [1]. In our context, since tuberculosis is a major health problem in Morocco with an incidence of 82 per 100,000 people in 2022 according to the World Bank collection of development indicators, we can suppose that it can be associated with CS or at least is a differential diagnosis that needs to be explored and ruled out. Its diagnosis can be challenging specifically because of the paucibacillary nature of the disease and lack of laboratory facilities in resource-limited settings [9].

Management of CS depends on the severity of the disease and the involved organs [2]. The standard of care includes glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive medications, but no standard treatment plan has been established [10].

The topical form of steroids can be used for mild ocular symptoms [2]. The response on corticosteroids alone is up to 84% according to a study on 62 patients. Overall, the visual prognosis remains excellent with only 3% of long-term ocular symptoms [11]. On the other hand, it is observed that early and high-dose steroids (IV glucocorticoid or oral prednisone of at least 1 mg/kg/day or intratympanic injection as adjunct therapy) improve the odds of recovering hearing loss when given within 2 weeks of initial auditory symptoms. However, auditory symptoms still have a poor prognosis in the long term since the hearing loss is irreversible [1, 2].

Immunosuppressive therapies are considered in cases of nonresponse, intolerance, or contraindication to steroids. The molecules used in that indication are various, including cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, and azathioprine [2].

Also, the use of biologics in CS therapy is rising, including infliximab, rituximab, and tocilizumab (TCZ), proposing novel remedies for these patients. Indeed, infliximab is becoming a potential first-line treatment in association with corticosteroids, while studies have reported successful stabilization of CS hearing impairment with TCZ after successive failures of glucocorticoid, cellular immunosuppressant, and infliximab therapy [2, 10].

The aim of treatment remains the preservation of as much hearing and vestibular function as possible, avoiding ocular and systemic complications from vasculitis [2]. However, despite medical treatment, auditory prognosis still uncertain, with irreversible severe to profound hearing loss in 50–60% of patients who become candidates for cochlear implantation [12].

Boumghit et al. [12] reported 10 patients with CS operated for 14 cochlear implants between 2005 and 2020 with significant improvement in speech reception threshold and maximum intelligibility (P = 0.0002) after 1 year of followup, in line with results reported in literature of 82.5–94% of mean word recognition scores at a mean 1–9 years of follow-up. Indeed, literature data report good functional outcomes as long as the electrode array is properly inserted. However, adequate preimplant counseling is mandatory, to adjust patient expectations and evaluate the necessity for bilateral implantation [13].

Thus, cochlear implant should be considered in severe to profound hearing loss resistant to medical treatment, with no delay regarding the threat of cochlear fibrosis [12]. But, even with a cochlear implant, results may deteriorate owing to progressive cochlear ossification with worsening of speech perception abilities [13]. Also, Bovo et al. [13] reported a case with decreased speech perception ability to identification of words in closed set probably owing to auditory dyssynchrony, as an atypical consequence of the syndrome.

Cogan syndrome is a rare autoimmune disease that should be considered when faced with ocular and audiovestibular manifestations, even in its atypical form, to provide early and adequate treatment, which is the main prognosis factor in the control of irreversible lesions. Thus, interdisciplinary collaboration is fundamental as well as screening for other infectious and systemic disorders that should include tuberculosis.
