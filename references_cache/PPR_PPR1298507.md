# Prevalence and Treatment of Impulse Control Behaviours in Parkinson's Disease: A Meta- Analysis and Systematic Review
**Authors:** Reid G, Makukh M, Erukulla R, Sargent B, Koychev I
**DOI:** [10.21203/rs.3.rs-10554922/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10554922/v1)

## Content

<title>Abstract</title>  <p>Objective  Impulse control behaviours (ICBs) in Parkinson’s disease (PD) are common, but meta-analytical data on their prevalence and synthesised evidence for ICB treatments in PD are significantly outdated, necessitating an update that integrates newer studies. We systematically reviewed empirical studies to update the prevalence of ICBs in idiopathic PD and narratively synthesise the efficacy of existing therapeutic interventions. Methods  We searched PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE (Ovid), MEDLINE (Ovid), and the Cochrane Library from inception to June 1, 2023, including quantitative studies on human participants with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease. Risk of bias was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute tool. The primary outcome measures were ICB prevalence and ICB changes following psychological, pharmaceutical, or brain-based interventions (Registry: Open Science Framework, 10.17605/OSF.IO/ASG2N). Results  Thirty-seven studies were analysed. A random-effects meta-analysis of 27 studies (n = 17,906) found a mean event rate for at least one ICB in PD of 0.19 [95% CI = 0.15, 0.24], with evidence suggesting higher rates in patients taking dopamine agonists. A narrative synthesis of 10 studies (n = 283) found preliminary evidence for the effectiveness of cognitive behavioural therapy, levodopa infusion, atomoxetine, naltrexone, valproic acid, and deep brain stimulation in supporting ICB reduction. Conclusions  ICBs represent a substantial comorbidity in PD. While several intervention strategies show promise, the paucity of high-quality treatment studies requires findings to be interpreted with caution. Clinical screening and large-scale tracking of ICBs in PD are recommended, alongside rigorous future trials.</p>

Abstract
Objective
Impulse control behaviours (ICBs) in Parkinson’s disease (PD) are common, but meta-analytical data on their
prevalence and synthesised evidence for ICB treatments in PD are signiﬁcantly outdated, necessitating an update that
integrates newer studies. We systematically reviewed empirical studies to update the prevalence of ICBs in idiopathic
PD and narratively synthesise the eﬃcacy of existing therapeutic interventions.
Methods
We searched PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE (Ovid), MEDLINE (Ovid), and the Cochrane Library from inception to
June 1, 2023, including quantitative studies on human participants with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease. Risk of bias
was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute tool. The primary outcome measures were ICB prevalence and ICB
changes following psychological, pharmaceutical, or brain-based interventions (Registry: Open Science Framework,
10.17605/OSF.IO/ASG2N).
Results
Thirty-seven studies were analysed. A random-effects meta-analysis of 27 studies (n = 17,906) found a mean event rate
for at least one ICB in PD of 0.19 [95% CI = 0.15, 0.24], with evidence suggesting higher rates in patients taking
dopamine agonists. A narrative synthesis of 10 studies (n = 283) found preliminary evidence for the effectiveness of
cognitive behavioural therapy, levodopa infusion, atomoxetine, naltrexone, valproic acid, and deep brain stimulation in
supporting ICB reduction.
Conclusions
ICBs represent a substantial comorbidity in PD. While several intervention strategies show promise, the paucity of high-
quality treatment studies requires ﬁndings to be interpreted with caution. Clinical screening and large-scale tracking of
ICBs in PD are recommended, alongside rigorous future trials.
Introduction
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder affecting 1% of people over 60 (1).
While traditionally deﬁned by clinical motor symptoms such as bradykinesia and resting-state muscular rigidity, PD is
increasingly recognized as a complex neuropsychiatric condition due to the severe psychiatric symptoms that often
accompany the disease (2). Within this framework, impulse control behaviours (ICB) have garnered particular attention.
The purpose of this review was therefore to provide updated meta-analytical evidence of the prevalence of ICBs in PD
and a narrative synthesis of the evidence for ICB treatments in PD.
Prevalence of Impulse Control Behaviours
While Impulse Control Disorders (ICDs) refer to strict, categorical psychiatric diagnoses (such as pathological
gambling), Impulse Control Behaviours (ICBs) represent a broader clinical spectrum. This spectrum encompasses both
formal ICDs and subsyndromal, PD-speciﬁc phenomena, such as punding and hobbyism, that may not meet rigorous
Page 2/16

psychiatric criteria but still profoundly disrupt a patient's daily functioning and quality of life. ICBs cover pathological
gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive buying, compulsive eating, hobbyism, punding (i.e., the frequent repetition of non-
goal-oriented behaviours), compulsive medication use, hoarding, and walkabout. Prevalence rates of ICBs in PD, as
reported in existing literature, exhibit considerable variability, spanning from 14% to 40%, highlighting the importance of
better understanding ICBs in PD (3). The most recent meta-analysis conducted in 2018 of studies looking at the
prevalence of ICBs in PD included 14 case-control studies (4). This synthesis study reported that the estimated odds
ratio (OD) of having at least one ICB (of any kind) in PD compared to healthy controls was 2.07. Moderator analyses
showed that usage of PD medication and disease duration were signiﬁcant predictors of increased ORs for comorbid
ICBs. The authors noted that their results should be interpreted cautiously given the impact of potential publication bias
and the impact of the small study effect. Studies with smaller sample sizes have a relatively higher risk of
overestimating the reported effects with larger effects, in turn, being more likely to be published than smaller or null
effects. As the principles of open science continue to become more widespread, more recently published literature may
reﬂect more reliable ﬁndings, highlighting the potential utility of an updated meta-analysis. Furthermore, there is
evidence that ICB prevalence and most reported subtypes vary as a function of culture, socio-economical, and political
inﬂuences (5). Given behavioural norms across cultures shift over time, alongside recent evolutions in clinical practice,
such as shifts in dopamine agonist prescribing guidelines and the increased use of device-aided therapies, establishing
a modernized, highly powered prevalence baseline is critically needed to guide current clinical screening protocols.
Treating Impulse Control Behaviours
While traditional therapeutic interventions for PD have predominantly addressed the motor symptoms, the unmet need
pertaining to the non-motor aspects of PD remains considerable. First, those with PD and ICBs experience higher rates
of comorbid depression and report more compromised quality of life compared to those without ICBs (6). Second, the
administration of dopamine receptor agonists (DA) for PD motor symptoms has been linked to a 2–3.5-fold increased
risk of developing ICBs compared to patients without DA treatment (7). And third, the estimated incidence of ICBs in PD
patients treated with DA also appears to increase over time, with a 5-year cumulative incidence of nearly 50% (8). In
addition, it is also noteworthy that patients with PD may also often underestimate the presence and severity of ICB
symptoms, possibly inﬂuenced by a lack of insight, denial, and feelings of shame or guilt (9). That said, the last
systematic review of ICB treatment in PD was conducted over a decade ago, meaning that an updated synthesis would
have clear clinical value for helping people living with PD manage presentations of ICBs (10).
Objectives of the Current Review
In the current meta synthesis, we sought to review existing literature on ICBs in PD in order to achieve two main aims.
The driving rationale for this review is that PD-related dopaminergic therapy drives a spectrum of hyper-dopaminergic
behaviours; therefore, focusing exclusively on strict ICDs underestimates the true clinical burden. By assessing this
broader umbrella of ICBs, we ﬁrst aimed to update meta-analytical evidence of the prevalence of ICBs in individuals
diagnosed with PD. Second, we sought to narratively synthesise evidence of the eﬃcacy of pharmacological and non-
pharmacological interventions in managing ICBs in the PD population. By mapping the existing treatment landscape,
this secondary aim serves as a targeted scoping exercise to highlight the current paucity of high-quality clinical trial
data and identify critical gaps requiring future rigorous investigation.
Methods
Search Strategy and Data Sources
Page 3/16

A limited-scope search was initially conducted on Web of Science and PubMed to identify relevant index terms for the
ﬁnal systematic search strategy (see Table S1 in the online supplement). A single, uniﬁed systematic search was
performed to identify studies on two distinct clinical outcomes: (1) the prevalence of ICBs in PD, and (2) the eﬃcacy of
ICB treatments in PD. The systematic search was performed on PubMed, Web of Science Core Library, EMBASE (Ovid),
MEDLINE (Ovid), and the Cochrane Library from the databases’ inception to the 1st of June 2023. We also manually
searched the reference list of included studies to identify additional studies for inclusion. The systematic search and
reference-list review were conducted by two independent reviewers. The ﬁnalized methodology and search strategy
were retrospectively registered on the Open Science Framework to ensure methodological transparency (Registration
DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/ASG2N).
Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
Original studies, of any quantitative research design, on human participants, of any demographic, who had been
diagnosed with idiopathic PD were included. All durations and treatment statuses of participants with PD, with or
without any comorbidities, were considered eligible. The ICBs included, but not limited to, were pathological gambling,
compulsive shopping, compulsive eating, hypersexuality, and punding. Studies involving atypical Parkinsonism, or
which were not available as a full text written in English, were excluded.
Data Selection
Records were ﬁrst imported and deduplicated in Rayyan. Two independent reviewers screened the entries with our
inclusion criteria and disagreements were addressed with a third reviewer after the unblinding process. Data from the
included studies were summarised in a spreadsheet that captured study details, sample characteristics, PD features
(i.e., diagnostic tool, duration, cognitive status, and treatment plans), and details of any ICBs (i.e., prevalence,
treatments, comparators, and baseline-follow-up effectiveness measures). Consistent with our rationale to evaluate
these symptoms on a spectrum, we deliberately did not distinguish between studies that deﬁned cases as strict
impulse control disorders (e.g., diagnosed via psychiatric interview) and those that deﬁned them as impulse control
behaviours (e.g., identiﬁed via validated screening tools). Data from both assessment approaches were pooled under
the broad umbrella of ICBs to capture the complete clinical burden.
Data Analysis
Where appropriate, statistical computations were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis Version 4 (11), with
the remaining studies on ICB treatments being synthesised narratively. The effect size for our meta-analysis was the
event rate for the prevalence of (at least one) ICB in the included studies. A random-effects model was planned a priori
in order to capture between-study heterogeneity and allow for a distribution of true underlying effect sizes (compared
to a single underlying effect size assumed in a ﬁxed-effects model). Speciﬁcally, to calculate the pooled event rate, raw
prevalence proportions were logit-transformed prior to analysis to stabilize the variance and ensure appropriate
conﬁdence interval estimation. The between-study variance was estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird method. We
deliberately chose to pool all studies to yield a single, overarching prevalence estimate, rather than separating cohorts
by dopamine-treatment status. The scientiﬁc rationale for this approach was to establish the overall global burden of
ICBs across the broad, heterogeneous Parkinson’s disease population. Establishing this macro-level baseline is
essential for informing general, clinic-wide screening protocols, as it accurately reﬂects real-world clinical settings
where patient medication regimens are highly individualized, frequently adjusted, and often overlap.
Quality Assessment
Page 4/16

The Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal tool for Prevalence Studies was used by two independent reviewers to
assess the studies’ risk of bias (12). This appraisal tool assesses several aspects of a study’s quality, in which each
question can be answered yes, no, unclear, or not applicable. As the appraisal tool offers no guidance, it was decided a
priori that studies would be excluded from our review if they received two no responses or a combination of no and two
unclear responses.
Code availability
No custom computer code or algorithms were generated for this study. Statistical analyses were conducted using the
commercially available Comprehensive Meta-Analysis Version 4 software.

Treatment Effects
Drug Naïve Cohorts. The prevalence of ICBs in drug naïve PD cohorts differed across three studies, including 11.8%
(16), 17.5% (17), and 44.3% (18). In one study, a prevalence of 17.5% was detected with the MIDI assessment but not
during clinical interviewing (17). There was also evidence of a different prevalence for speciﬁc behaviours, including
compulsive buying (11%), hypersexuality (11%), intermittent explosive disorder (7%) (17), and for gambling (1.2%),
compulsive buying (3.0%), hypersexuality (4.2%), compulsive eating (7.1%), punding (4.8%), hobbyism (5.4%),
walkabout (0.6%), and a category for other (18.5%) (18).
Dopamine-Levodopa Recipients. In one study, the rate of at least one ICB in participants taking DA medication (DA+)
was 17.1% compared to drug naïve patients’ prevalence of 6.9% (19). According to a longitudinal study, at least one ICB
was seen in 26.5% (DA-/levodopa+), 26.7% (DA+/levodopa-), and 30.1% (DA+/ levodopa+) of patients: rates which
stayed stable over a 2-year period (6). In another study, at least one ICB was seen in 7.2% (DA-/levodopa+), 14.0%
(DA+/levodopa-), 17.7% (DA+/ levodopa+), and 1.7% (DA-/levodopa-) of patients (7).
Speciﬁc impulse control behaviours. One study reported different prevalence for speciﬁc impulse control behaviours
as a function of medication status in compulsive eating (DA + = 26.6%, DA- = 17.2%), compulsive buying (DA + = 20.3%,
DA- = 6.9%), hobbyism (DA + = 20%, DA- = 8%), compulsive medication use (DA + = 8.6%, DA- = 6.3%), hypersexuality
(DA + = 13.3%, DA- = 6.4%), and gambling (DA + = 6.3%), DA- = 0.6%) (20).

Treatment of ICBs
For a summary of the key data from studies focusing on ICB treatments, see Table S4 in the online supplement.
Psychological Interventions
In a randomised controlled trial, patients receiving cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for their PD-related ICBs showed
decreases in clinician-rated global symptom severity scores with small-to-medium effect sizes (η² = 0.21) compared to
patients receiving standard medical care (21). In another study, CBT, combined with standard medical care, resulted in
decreased severity of ICBs, compared to standard medical care alone at 6-months follow-up. These results held true for
patients with pathological gambling, both with and without comorbid PD (22).
Pharmacological Interventions
One study showed that levodopa infusion improved impulsivity and dopamine dysregulation syndrome (DDS) in PD
patients, achieving symptom reduction across various types of ICBs (23). Among the ﬁve patients showing punding
behaviour, improvements were observed in all cases, although complete remission of punding was achieved in only one
patient. Across a series of studies on atomoxetine, the drug has been shown to improve inhibition, leading to
enhancements in stopping accuracy in cognitive tasks, such as the stop signal task (Cohen’s d = 2.06), and reductions
in risk-taking behaviour (Cohen's d = 0.25). However, it should be noted that these studies measured proxies of
cognitive impulsivity rather than changes on formal ICD clinical scales (24). In addition, atomoxetine has also been
shown to restore the response inhibition network in patients with PD (25). Naltrexone has been shown to relate to a
reduction in patient-rated ICB symptoms compared to placebo (Cohen’s d = 0.40), but not in rating scales completed by
clinicians (26). In a small case-control study of 4 patients, valproic acid was shown to relate to a reduction in DDS
symptoms in patients with PD (27).
Other Interventions
In one study of subthalamic deep brain simulation, post-operative improvements in ICB questionnaire scores were
reported in 12 of 16 patients with an effect size of Cohen’s d = 4.2 (28). In another longitudinal study, subthalamic deep
brain simulation successfully treated all pre-operative ICBs, but some patients showed de novo ICBs between 1 and 7
years after surgery (29). In one study using low-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, it was found
that this treatment transiently suppressed punding behaviours in PD patients (30).

Discussion
The aim of the current study was to synthesise updated evidence on the prevalence of ICBs in PD, as well as evidence
for the eﬃcacy of therapeutic interventions for treating ICBs in the PD population. To our knowledge, this systematic
review and meta-analysis represents the largest study of this nature conducted in the ﬁeld to date, representing an
update since the last synthesis of ICB prevalence in PD in 2018.
The calculated mean effect size for the prevalence of ICBs in PD, across the 24 studies included in our meta-analysis,
was 19.2%. This substantial prevalence reinforces our driving hypothesis that ICBs exist on a broad clinical spectrum.
By deliberately pooling data across both strict psychiatric ICD diagnoses and subsyndromal ICB screening tools, our
results elucidate the true clinical burden of these hyper-dopaminergic behaviours and reveal a scope of impairment that
is signiﬁcantly larger than what is captured by rigid diagnostic criteria alone. The highest reported prevalence of an ICB
in PD in a single study was for punding. As for healthy controls, the reported rates of at least one ICB varied between
4.6% and 15.2% with the most prevalently reported ICB in healthy controls being hobbyism (16). Among individuals with
PD who had not yet been treated with medication for PD, the prevalence of ICBs ranged from 0% to 44.3% (16–18).
Subgroup analyses within the reviewed studies consistently pointed to a higher prevalence of ICBs among patients
treated with dopamine agonists (DA). However, the prevalence of ICBs in PD patients, whether on DA treatment or not,
tended to exceed that of the general population, suggesting a predisposing vulnerability to developing ICBs in patients
with PD.
While the primary driver of ICBs appears to be pharmacological, emerging evidence suggests that some patients may
also possess a baseline genetic vulnerability to ICBs in PD, which may involve genes associated with the signalling
pathways connected to G protein-coupled receptors (31). Given the role of dopamine in reward processing, the
dopamine dysregulation hypothesis would suggest that aberrant functioning of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia
nigra in PD plays a role in the emergence of ICBs (32). Exacerbated dysregulation of mesolimbic dopamine
transmission associated with the use of DA medication may affect reward processing that leads to the higher
prevalence of ICBs in PD patients who are DA+. It is possible that DA medication may sensitise the reward system in
the basal ganglia, which leads to exaggerated reward processing, which is seen symptomatically as ICBs. Alternatively,
insuﬃcient top-down regulation of dysregulated midbrain reward processing through the recruitment of frontostriatal
circuits may lead to a deﬁcit in inhibitory mechanisms or executive function that contribute to the emergence of ICBs in
PD (33).
The treatments evaluated in this review demonstrated a collective improvement in ICB symptoms, though the data
remain too sparse to establish a single gold-standard approach. Instead, our synthesis suggests that treatment eﬃcacy
depends heavily on the speciﬁc intervention and the targeted ICB subtype. Neuromodulation, speciﬁcally deep brain
stimulation (DBS), demonstrated the largest quantiﬁable beneﬁt on pre-operative ICBs (Cohen’s d = 4.2), though its
application is limited by invasive risks and the potential emergence of de novo ICBs post-surgery (28). For speciﬁc
motor-loop ICBs like punding, continuous levodopa infusion showed targeted eﬃcacy, achieving symptom reduction
and partial remission by stabilizing dopaminergic stimulation. Pharmacological interventions yielded mixed results:
while atomoxetine signiﬁcantly improved cognitive proxies of inhibition (Cohen’s d = 2.06), it was not directly assessed
against clinical ICB diagnoses (24). Naltrexone reduced patient-rated symptom severity (Cohen's d = 0.40) but failed to
improve clinician-rated impressions (26). Finally, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) emerged as the most consistently
evaluated and accessible non-pharmacological adjunct, reliably producing small-to-medium improvements (η² = 0.21)
in global symptom severity across broad ICB presentations, including pathological gambling (21). Although promising,
it is crucial to acknowledge the limited availability of high-quality studies in this ﬁeld on pharmacological,
psychological, and surgical interventions for ICBs. While case studies offer valuable descriptive insights, their
vulnerability to selection bias signiﬁcantly limits the extent to which their ﬁndings can be applied and generalised.

Moreover, an understanding of the speciﬁc ICB subtypes responding to intervention would be useful and perhaps an
important focus for future research. Identifying how PD biomarkers associate with ICBs, or establishing ICB-speciﬁc
biomarkers, as has been done in other literature, would help in the recruitment of patients for trials and in the
evaluation of treatment eﬃcacy for ICBs in PD (34). Consistent with previously conducted reviews on impulse control
disorders, the available data remain insuﬃcient to conﬁdently recommend one treatment approach over another in
addressing the ICBs seen in PD (4).
This meta-analysis has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, heterogeneity across studies was
notable. This variance is expected given the wide variety of assessment methods used in the literature, ranging from
formal clinical interviews to self-report questionnaires, as well as natural differences in the patient populations, such as
variations in disease duration and medication status. While our methodology deliberately pooled these disparate
approaches to capture the full spectrum of ICBs, this overarching 19.2% prevalence is best interpreted as a broad
indicator of overall clinical burden rather than a strict diagnostic rate. Second, it is possible that studies with non-
signiﬁcant or lower prevalence rates were underreported or unpublished in the ﬁeld. Third, the majority of studies were
cross-sectional, limiting our ability to make inferences about the temporal dynamics and progression of ICBs in PD.
Fourth, our systematic search concluded on June 1, 2023. While this represents a comprehensive synthesis of the
literature up to that point, any trials published after are not captured. Nevertheless, the massive scale of the prevalence
data synthesised here ensures the baseline estimates remain robust and highly clinically relevant. Longitudinal data
would be helpful for understanding this trajectory. Future research should prioritise standardised diagnostic criteria,
longitudinal designs, and more detailed reporting of clinical and treatment-related variables to improve the quality of
future syntheses.
Conclusions
In conclusion, beyond PD motor symptoms, the varying prevalence and diverse manifestations of ICBs within PD
emphasise the critical importance of early detection and comprehensive assessment of these neuropsychiatric
symptoms in PD. Clinicians should adopt a proactive approach, identifying potential ICB symptoms among PD cohorts,
and perhaps scaling efforts to identify and intervene in PD-ICBs as has been done with other neuropsychiatric
variables, such as cognitive impairment (35). First line management may include pharmacological intervention or deep
brain stimulation for patients with severe ICBs, coupled with adjunct psychological interventions, such as CBT. CBT
interventions are digitizable and can be administered by a variety of trained professionals, as well as through self-help
and psychoeducational resources, potentially yielding an attractive cost-beneﬁt balance in supporting people with PD
and comorbid ICBs. Given ICBs in PD are associated with more severe symptoms of depression, understanding which
cognitive mechanism relate depression, ICBs, and PD, as has been done in other neuropsychiatric diseases, would
facilitate the use of CBT in this population (36). Further large and prospective studies are needed to validate the utility
of such interventions in this context, and with increasing clinician recognition of ICBs in this patient cohort, such
research may lead to signiﬁcant patient beneﬁt in the future by establishing a robust evidence base for treatment
approaches.
