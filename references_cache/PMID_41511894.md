# Staphylococcus aureus periprosthetic joint infection and its pathogenesis in total hip and knee arthroplasty: a narrative review.
**Authors:** Diaz-Dilernia F, Campagna N, Vasarhelyi E
**Journal:** EFORT Open Rev (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1530/EOR-2025-0093](https://doi.org/10.1530/EOR-2025-0093)

## Content

Staphylococcus aureus is the most common pathogen in periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs), capable of biofilm formation and resistance mechanisms, complicating diagnosis and treatment. PJIs remain a leading cause of total joint arthroplasty failure and are associated with significant morbidity, mortality, and healthcare and economic burdens. Biofilm formation by S. aureus on prosthetic materials is central to PJI persistence and antibiotic resistance. Novel treatment strategies, including copper-coated implants, show promise as adjunct therapies but require further clinical validation.

Osteoarthritis is the most common joint disease worldwide, with knee involvement accounting for over 60% of cases (1). This degenerative disorder affects joint cartilage and subchondral bone, causing pain, inflammation, and reduced mobility, which significantly impairs quality of life. Total joint arthroplasty (TJA) relieves pain and restores motion in patients with end-stage osteoarthritis (2). Some TJAs require revision surgery due to infection, wear, loosening, instability, persistent pain, or fracture (3).

Primary TJAs are rising globally, with an estimated 5 million replacements in the US by 2040 (4). Consequently, revision procedures are increasing (4), primarily due to periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) (5), which occur in 1–2% of total hip (THA) and knee arthroplasties (TKAs) (6, 7, 8).

There has been limited progress in improving diagnoses, treatments, and outcomes of PJI. Despite prevention efforts, PJI remains a leading and rising cause of TJA failure (9). PJIs are associated with higher morbidity, mortality, and hospitalization, impacting patients and healthcare systems (5, 10). PJIs have a 5-year mortality rate of 21%, and revision surgery for PJI has a 2.5-fold higher mortality risk than aseptic revisions (11, 12).

Thus, we conducted a narrative review to comprehensively synthesize current research and knowledge on Staphylococcus aureus PJI in THA and TKA, including epidemiology, classification, pathogenesis, and treatment. We searched Embase and PubMed (inception to June 2025) using combinations of keywords: PJI, Staphylococcus aureus, total hip arthroplasty, and total knee arthroplasty, along with specific terms.

PJI rates vary based on definitions, study parameters, and regions. Table 1 summarizes epidemiological trends of PJI. Kurtz et al. reported 5-year cumulative PJI risks of 1.09% for THA and 1.38% for TKA in the US, reflecting patient risk and suggesting PJI cases will rise with increasing TJA volumes (13). The Nordic Arthroplasty Register Association demonstrated increased 5-year revision rates for PJI after THA, from 0.7 to 1.2%, in 2004–2018 (14). The McMaster Arthroplasty Collaborative reported a decrease in TKA PJI incidence from 2002 to 2016 in Ontario, with a 5-year cumulative incidence of 1.12%, and no change in THA PJI risk over this period, with a 5-year cumulative incidence of 0.89 (6, 7).

PJIs have significant economic impacts beyond patient morbidity and mortality. Table 2 summarizes the economic burden of PJI. The US cost to treat PJI was $566 million in 2009, projected to rise to $1.85 billion by 2030 (10, 15). Treatment costs vary by intervention. The gold standard, two-stage revision surgery, for PJI costs $55,000–$60,00 (16). Single-stage revision surgeries for PJI are more expensive than revisions for aseptic loosening due to longer surgery times, increased blood loss, and higher complication rates (17). Debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) triple the costs of a single PJI case compared to a primary TJA (18). Canadian data also highlight PJI’s economic impact, showing a five-fold increase in hospital expenditure compared to primary TJA, including longer hospital stays, more clinic visits, and higher readmission rates (19, 20, 21).

Different classification schemes for PJIs exist, with the most widely accepted one based on time to presentation and dissemination mechanisms, developed by the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) in 2011 and updated in 2018 (22). MSIS classification encompasses major criteria, such as sinus tract and pathogen isolation, and minor criteria, including serum markers, synovial fluid (SF) analysis, histopathology, and culture results.

Acute/early PJIs result from contamination during surgery or the initial postoperative period, usually caused by virulent microorganisms from normal cutaneous flora. They are often symptomatic and diagnosed within 90 days of implantation. Late chronic PJIs occur between 3- and 24-month post-surgery, typically due to less virulent microorganisms. The distinction between acute and late chronic PJIs depends on the organism’s virulence and inoculum needed for infection, leading to a more indolent presentation. Late hematogenous PJIs result from distant spread and can appear any time post-TJA, presenting acutely in a previously healthy joint with systemic symptoms, often indicative of a septic process (23).

Other diagnostic systems have also been proposed, including those by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) (24) and the European Bone and Joint Infections Society (EBJIS) (25). A comparative analysis by Streck et al. demonstrated that PJI diagnosis is significantly dependent on the diagnostic criteria used (26). Table 3 summarizes the comparison of several diagnostic systems.

Most PJIs diagnosed during the first postoperative year result from direct contact or aerosolized contamination of implants or surrounding soft tissues during surgery (27). A low inoculum can establish an infection, with less than 102 colony-forming units (CFUs) of S. aureus needed in an animal model with a hip hemiarthroplasty, compared to 104 CFU without a device (28). Similarly, a more recent study demonstrated that infection dose determines disease outcome, with 103 CFU of S. aureus sufficient to establish a chronic biofilm PJI (29). Both studies provide strong mechanistic evidence, although their animal-based nature limits direct extrapolation to human PJIs. Another initiation mechanism is contiguous dissemination from a nearby focus. Early postoperative superficial surgical site infections can progress to the prosthesis and deeper layers. Conversely, late PJIs can occur if old wounds are disrupted by trauma or adjacent surgery. Hematogenous dissemination from a distant site may occur throughout the patient’s follow-up.

Multiple authors reported a 12–40% risk of hematogenous PJI after confirmed S. aureus bacteremia (30, 31, 32). Recent evidence on hematogenous PJI secondary to S. aureus bacteremia demonstrates a substantial reduction in reported incidence compared to earlier studies. Murdoch et al. (30) prospectively reported a 34% infection rate, while Sendi et al. (31) retrospectively found a comparable 30–40% rate. Both studies were limited by small, single-center cohorts and pre-modern antimicrobial protocols, which may have led to an overestimation of risk. In contrast, and more recently, Blank et al. (32) performed a retrospective study, showing a 12% incidence and found younger age and multiple prostheses as key risk factors.

Biofilms are any syntrophic consortium of microorganisms (mono- or polymicrobial) embedded in an extracellular matrix, which enables cells to adhere to surfaces and to each other. These microorganisms often include bacteria, fungi, and protists.

Microorganisms typically settle around implants, affecting the sensitivity of culture samples. A diagnostic alternative is sonication techniques, which enhance biofilm detection by disrupting adherent bacteria (33). Silva et al. conducted a systematic review of recent protocols, providing moderate to high-quality evidence, but noted variable sensitivity and the need for standardized sonication methods. Normal flora microorganisms rarely cause infections but can become pathogenic, leading to PJIs with biofilm formation on medical devices (34). Biofilms play a crucial role in PJI pathogenesis, with viable S. aureus biofilms frequently found in infected prostheses, underscoring their significance. This was demonstrated by Stoodley et al. (35) through an in vivo study, offering strong mechanistic validity, despite limitations of a single case report. The extracellular matrix, composed of polysaccharides and proteins, protects biofilms against antibiotics and immune responses, making treatment challenging and often requiring implant removal via surgery. Antibiotic resistance is associated with ‘persister’ cells and protective biofilm structures (36).

Initially, the infection is localized within the intraarticular space, and histology may reveal large granulomas containing neutrophils and abscesses. The next stage involves dissemination to the proximal bone, with the upper third of the metaphysis becoming compromised by the third week. If the infection progresses, it eventually affects the remainder of the metaphysis and adjacent diaphysis (34). A recent study by Cai et al. (37) demonstrated that S. aureus small-colony variants were more prevalent within intraosseous bone samples from chronic than acute infections, suggesting a mechanistic role for the propagation of persistent PJI, although limited by animal model design.

Hematogenous infections exhibit distinct initiation processes. Crémieux et al. (38) reported that hematogenous long-bone osteomyelitis may begin at the metaphysis and progress to the joint space, affecting the prosthesis. The clinical applicability of this study is limited by its use of animal models; however, it provides valuable mechanistic insights. Further studies are needed to determine if these differences affect diagnosis or treatment.

In osteoarthritis, the concentrations of proteins and hyaluronic acid and the thermostability and osmolality of SF are often compromised (39).

The host’s immune system includes an innate response against pathogens in different body fluids, including SF. The literature suggests SF has defense factors beyond cell-mediated immunity, contributing to the joint’s natural protection against infection. Gruber et al. (40) showed SF’s bactericidal activity against Staphylococcus species, including S. aureus.

Metals interacting with SF may influence its antimicrobial properties. Iron, an essential element for microorganisms, plays a crucial role in enzymatic processes involved in DNA synthesis and electron transport. Free iron in living organisms is limited because it forms insoluble oxyhydroxides under aerobic and neutral pH conditions. In addition, iron is sequestered into proteins, such as transferrin and lactoferrin (41).

Iron is scarce in SF from osteoarthritic patients due to transferrin activity. Ahmadzadeh et al. (42) demonstrated higher transferrin concentration in SF from osteoarthritic patients than in those with rheumatoid arthritis, who exhibited higher free iron levels. Transferrin may reduce bacterial growth in SF by limiting iron availability (43). Watson et al. (44) demonstrated SF’s bactericidal effects against S. aureus, with iron restriction aiding the complement system in killing bacteria.

Identifying the infecting microorganism is crucial for successful PJI treatment. Studies indicate that gram-positive cocci, primarily S. aureus and CoNS, are most common, accounting for 50–60% of PJIs (27, 45, 46). Streptococci and Enterococci represent approximately 10%, while aerobic gram-negative bacilli are also responsible for about 10%. Culture-negative infections vary around 5–7%, influenced by factors, such as preoperative antibiotics, culture interpretation, potential contamination, and sample type and quality (27, 45).

Acute PJIs often involve S. aureus and aerobic gram-negative bacilli, comprising 60% of infections due to their high virulence (46). CoNS and polymicrobial infections are significant due to their low virulence and high inoculation. Late chronic PJIs are usually caused by low-virulence microorganisms, such as CoNS and Enterococci, with aerobic gram-negative bacilli being less common. Late hematogenous PJIs result from distant site spread, with S. aureus being the most frequently isolated microorganism (30, 31, 47).

S. aureus is a gram-positive, round-shaped commensal bacterium identified in the 1880s (48, 49). It is a part of the normal microbiota in the upper respiratory tract, skin, and the female lower reproductive tract. S. aureus can act as an opportunistic pathogen, expressing virulence factors, such as protein toxins and cell-surface proteins, which bind and inactivate antibodies (48). Approximately 20–30% of healthy patients carry S. aureus in their anterior nares, correlating with a higher PJI risk (48). S. aureus infections range from skin infections, osteoarticular, implant-related, to life-threatening diseases, such as pneumonia, meningitis, osteomyelitis, endocarditis, toxic shock syndrome, bacteremia, and sepsis (48, 49). Acute bacteremia and cutaneous infections often result from toxin and enzyme activity (50), while chronic infections are related to biofilm production, affecting bone, heart valves, and medical implants (51).

S. aureus is the most isolated microorganism in osteoarticular infections, including osteomyelitis (52), native joint septic arthritis (53), and PJI (54), accounting for 18–73% of PJI cases (34, 48). In TJA, S. aureus bacteremia leads to PJI in 29–39% of cases (31) and frequently causes invasive nosocomial bloodstream infections (55). Risk factors for S. aureus PJI include a body mass index >30, fracture as a cause for arthroplasty, skin disorders, and previous surgery in the affected joint (56).

S. aureus’ ability to develop antibiotic resistance complicates treatment. Penicillin resistance emerged in 1942, with the identification of beta-lactamase production in the mid-1940s. Methicillin was developed in 1959 to treat penicillin-resistant strains, but methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) appeared in 1960 (49, 57) due to the mecA gene encoding penicillin-binding protein 2a, which has a low affinity for beta-lactams (49, 58). The mecA gene is present in nearly all Staphylococcal species, making methicillin resistance easily transmissible between strains (49).

MRSA spread worldwide in the 1970s, acquiring antibiotic resistance, including gentamicin. By 2003, MRSA prevalence in US intensive care units (ICUs) was 60% (57). It has caused multiple cutaneous and soft tissue outbreaks in populations globally (59). MRSA is an endemic in the US, with 2% of the population as carriers and 43% of S. aureus cultures resistant to oxacillin (60).

Treatment of PJI after TJA is complicated by the rising prevalence of antibiotic-resistant strains (61). MRSA PJI rates are increasing, with Hays et al. (62) and Parvizi et al. (63) reporting a 20–50% prevalence in the US. PJIs lead to higher readmission rates, length of stay, more follow-up visits, and worse functional outcomes (19, 20, 21). The cost per case exceeds $100,000, creating a significant economic burden (19, 21). MRSA PJIs have higher costs than methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) infections due to longer stays and more ICU admissions (63), with annual costs approaching $450 million (63).

Due to its aggressive virulence, MRSA frequently causes acute PJI. With rising numbers of TJAs, MRSA-related PJIs threaten implant survival, resulting in prolonged management and leading to higher morbidity and mortality than MSSA (61).

Despite measures, such as skin decolonization, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and patient isolation, MRSA remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality (57). High surveillance and novel treatment options are needed (49).

S. aureus has glycopolymers recognized by the host immune system, including wall teichoic acid, peptidoglycan, lipoteichoic acid, and capsular polysaccharides, activating the complement system (64). S. aureus is adept at surviving under harsh conditions and evolving resistance to almost all available antibiotics (57). Resistance occurs through mutations increasing the antibiotic minimum inhibitory concentration, while tolerance involves reversible phenotypic adaptations (65). Factors contributing to antibiotic tolerance/resistance include biofilm thickening, metabolic dormancy, autolysis suppression, and genetic enzymatic modifications that prevent drug action (65).

Biofilm formation, a common phenomenon among bacteria, is crucial for antibiotic tolerance (66). Biofilms can form before PJI diagnosis and symptom onset, resulting from microbial, host, and environmental characteristics (67). Consequently, there are limited chances to intervene before biofilm formation occurs. S. aureus survival relies on biofilm formation (68, 69). Microbes enter the stationary phase during biofilm formation, which enhances antibiotic resistance and increases virulence (48, 70).

Orthopedic implants are susceptible to biofilm-related infections. S. aureus biofilms are found in infections of wires, pins, screws, plates, and prostheses (71). These infections can occur during surgery, from trauma, or through hematogenous dissemination (71). Biofilm formation around implants and surrounding soft tissues complicates eradication, and optimal management remains unclear (67, 72). Understanding biofilm formation is crucial for developing new treatments for these aggressive pathogens.

Traditionally, biofilm formation involves three stages: attachment, accumulation/maturation, and detachment/dispersal (73). Advanced technologies using time-lapse microscopy, such as BioFlux1000 systems, describe five stages: attachment, multiplication, exodus, maturation, and dispersal (Fig. 1) (69).

These microcolonies are formed from different groups of cells that have remained attached during the exodus stage, (E) dispersal: activated Agr-mediated quorum sensing initiates biofilm matrix modulation and dispersal of cells.

In 1986, Recsei et al. (82) identified the Agr quorum-sensing system as the master virulence regulator in S. aureus, depending on cell density and autoinducer accumulation. In S. aureus, an auto-inducing peptide (83) accumulates in culture medium and, upon reaching a threshold, binds to and activates AgrC histidine kinase. AgrC phosphorylates the response regulator, AgrA, initiating transcription from the P3 promoter of Agr operons. This produces RNAIII, a regulatory RNA controlling virulence factors and biofilm-associated genes (84). Due to auto-inducing peptide accumulation, the BioFlux1000 system demonstrated increased P3 promoter expression within microcolonies (85). P3 activation correlates with biofilm dispersal due to increased protease activity, degrading protein-based extracellular matrix (86).

S. aureus modulates virulence through complex global regulatory circuits, sensing environmental conditions and activating master regulators, adjusting gene expression to cell density via auto-induced quorum-sensing signals. These mechanisms demonstrate the resilience of S. aureus and highlight the need to target biofilm-mediated resistance for effective prevention and treatment.

Despite advances in surgical techniques, modern implant designs, and routine antibiotic prophylaxis, PJI remains a significant concern after TJA. Prevention is the primary objective, but treatment focuses on eradicating infection and preserving joint function (87). Determining the most effective treatment requires considering host immune status, comorbidities, PJI history, symptoms, wound characteristics, microorganism virulence, and patient expectations (23). A collaborative, multidisciplinary approach involving surgeons, microbiologists, and infectious disease specialists is critical (24, 87).

Treatment options vary based on PJI classification and include non-operative long-term antibiotic suppression, DAIR, one- or two-stage revision surgery (traditional gold standard), partial implant retaining surgery, arthroplasty resection without reimplantation, arthrodesis, and amputation (88). Table 4 summarizes treatment modalities.

Single- and two-stage revision surgeries are the primary approaches for treating PJI. In a single-stage revision, the infected prosthesis is replaced during the same surgery, resulting in shorter hospitalization and recovery times. Two-stage revision for complex, resistant, or culture-negative infections involves implant removal, an antibiotic therapy phase, and a secondary surgery to implant a new prosthesis.

Goud et al. (89) performed a meta-analysis and reported reinfection rates of 5.7% for THA and 12.7% for TKA in patients who had single-stage revision compared to 8.4% for THA and 16.2% for TKA in patients who had two-stage revision. Although the pooled sample size strengthens the analysis, heterogeneity among included studies and the predominance of retrospective data may limit the findings. Similarly, Kunutsor et al. (90) conducted a meta-analysis of knee arthroplasty, showing reinfection rates of 7.6% for single-stage and 8.8% for two-stage procedures, supporting therapeutic equivalence but limited by the absence of randomized trials and non-standardized inclusion criteria.

Liechti et al. (91) reported a 5-year infection-free survival rate of 74% and a reinfection rate of 25% following single-stage hip revision in a small retrospective study. Although the study demonstrates the feasibility of the approach, the limited cohort and the absence of a comparator arm restrict its generalizability. Lange et al. (92) retrospectively analyzed a national registry of chronic hip PJIs and reported an 82% 5-year infection-free survival and a 14.6% reinfection rate following two-stage revision. The findings highlight the favorable outcomes of two-stage revision; however, direct comparison may be limited due to differences in study design and cohorts.

Recently, Xie et al. (93) conducted a meta-analysis and found lower reinfection rates following single-stage revision compared to two-stage (15.3 vs 20.1%), with no difference in reoperation rates. The study’s methodology was robust but limited by reliance on retrospective evidence and inconsistent definitions of infection eradication. Matar et al. (94) retrospectively analyzed a large single-center cohort and reported lower failure rates in single-stage revision compared to two-stage (6.1 vs 12%) and comparable 10-year survivorship (72 vs 70.5%). Single-stage revision offers comparable or slightly better outcomes; however, it is essential to know that two-stage revisions are associated with more complex cases.

Overall, available evidence across meta-analyses and cohort studies demonstrates variation in reinfection and survivorship outcomes between single- and two-stage revision strategies for both THA and TKA. However, the predominance of retrospective designs, heterogeneity in surgical protocols, and lack of randomized-controlled trials limit the certainty of these findings. Further prospective research is required to refine patient selection criteria and establish standardized management algorithms for single- and two-stage revisions.

Although single- and two-stage revisions remain the standard for S. aureus PJI, recent evidence supports a more selective role for DAIR. Fontalis et al. (95) conducted a comprehensive review and reported DAIR success rates ranging from 33 to 100%, with outcomes strongly dependent on timing, organism virulence, and surgical quality. Success approached 80–90% when performed within 4 weeks of primary arthroplasty or within 7 days of symptom onset with modular component exchange. This review analyzed a wide range of studies across multiple institutions and infection types, emphasizing practical surgical factors, but may be limited by its qualitative synthesis and heterogeneity of included studies. Löwik et al. (96) retrospectively analyzed a multicenter cohort of 769 early PJIs and found no significant decline in DAIR efficacy up to 12 weeks post-arthroplasty. Approximately 60% of patients remained infection-free even when DAIR was performed beyond 6 weeks, provided it followed rapid intervention after symptom onset. This challenges IDSA guideline recommendations restricting DAIR to the first month post-surgery (24). While the study’s large, multinational sample enhances external validity, its retrospective design and variation in antimicrobial protocols introduce potential bias. Collectively, these findings suggest that DAIR may play a more significant role in PJI management than the current literature suggests. Nonetheless, both studies highlight the need for standardized surgical criteria and prospective validation.

Antibiotic-loaded cement/calcium sulphate beads effectively deliver high doses of antibiotics directly to infection sites (97). These biodegradable beads eliminate the need for removal after treatment. The selected antimicrobial should be effective against the isolated microorganism and retain its properties post-polymerization (98). The release process is biphasic: an initial peak within the first hours to days post-surgery, followed by a slow, constant release over weeks, months, or years (99). Antibiotic elution characteristics depend on polymer, room temperature, and pH.

The optimal duration of antibiotic therapy for PJI remains a topic of debate. Treatment length is often guided by surgical approach, pathogen, and patient factors. Current guidelines recommend 4–6 weeks of pathogen-directed therapy following single- or two-stage exchange and 3–6 months for DAIR (24). However, these recommendations rely mainly on expert consensus, with limited high-quality comparative evidence.

Yen et al. (100) conducted a meta-analysis comparing 6- and 12-week regimens and found no significant difference in treatment failure, suggesting shorter courses may suffice when debridement is adequate and infection control is achieved. Similarly, Olearo et al. (101) performed a large meta-analysis, including multiple surgical strategies, and observed no difference in failure rates between short (4–12 weeks) and long (≥12 weeks) courses. Both analyses were limited by heterogeneity and the use of retrospective data. In contrast, Bernard et al. (102) conducted a randomized-controlled trial, demonstrating higher treatment failure rates with 6 versus 12 weeks (18.1 vs 9.4%), particularly in DAIR and complex infections, which provides stronger evidence for prolonged courses in high-risk contexts; however, this may be limited by its methodology. Regarding the therapeutic route, Lima et al. (103) showed that oral therapy is non-inferior to intravenous therapy, supporting early transition to reduce hospitalization without compromising efficacy.

Collectively, antibiotic duration should be individualized. Shorter regimens appear appropriate following successful single- or two-stage exchange, while extended, often rifampin-based therapy remains advisable for DAIR or staphylococcal infections. The overall evidence quality remains moderate, constrained by heterogeneity and limited randomized data.

Current treatment options for PJIs involve aggressive procedures with high comorbidity, which are unsuitable for high-risk cases. The high prevalence of antibiotic resistance raises concerns and limits available antibiotic options (62). Developing new, less invasive treatment alternatives is essential to avoid aggressive and prolonged revision surgeries and improve the effectiveness of current strategies.

DAIR outcomes for MRSA PJIs are poor, making two-stage revision surgery with extended antibiotic treatment the most effective approach (24, 104). Intravenous vancomycin remains first-line, administered at 15–20 mg/kg every 8–12 h (following a 20 mg/kg loading dose) to achieve an AUC/MIC of 400–600, optimizing bactericidal activity while minimizing nephrotoxicity. Evidence from a prospective cohort study demonstrated that continuous high-dose vancomycin infusion achieved a 68% cure rate with mild, reversible nephrotoxicity while maintaining therapeutic AUC/MIC exposure (105). MRSA PJI may be further complicated by intermediate vancomycin resistance (VISA), resulting from cell wall thickening and decreased drug binding (106). Daptomycin (6–10 mg/kg IV daily) serves as an alternative for patients who are intolerant of or unresponsive to vancomycin (24). Antimicrobial therapy is typically continued for 6 weeks, followed by an oral rifampin-based combination regimen for 3 months for THA or 6 months for TKA when the prosthesis is retained or reimplanted (24, 104). Rifampin should be initiated a few days after starting antimicrobial therapy and debridement, as an adjunctive agent due to its biofilm activity, and administered at 300–450 mg twice daily in combination with an anti-staphylococcal agent to prevent resistance (24, 104). If the prosthesis is removed and not replaced, rifampin is unnecessary. Despite the widespread use of rifampin in S. aureus PJIs, evidence shows only a modest improvement in treatment success (10%) (107). However, there are currently no randomized-controlled trials evaluating optimal antimicrobial regimens for MRSA PJIs. If rifampin resistance exists, pathogen-specific intravenous therapy for 4–6 weeks is recommended (24).

Management can be further complicated for patients who are not candidates for surgery due to failed revisions, severe comorbidities, poor soft tissue condition, or high operative risk. Long-term oral suppressive antibiotic therapy (SAT) is the optimal non-surgical approach for treating PJI, with the goal of suppressing infection rather than eradicating it (24, 108). In a multicenter cohort study, SAT achieved a 50% infection control rate at 5 years (109), while another study demonstrated a 40% failure rate at 2 years (110). Predictors of failure were higher baseline C-reactive protein (CRP), older age, and S. aureus infection. Patients unable to undergo surgery should receive early pathogen-directed therapy based on susceptibility and patient-specific factors, including 4–6 weeks of intravenous, followed by an indefinite oral antibiotic regimen (24). As non-surgical management of PJI focuses on symptom management rather than curative treatment, a palliative approach may be warranted. This should include a multidisciplinary approach centered around the patient, with adequate pain control, physical therapy, and psychological support to improve the quality of life (111). There is a considerable research gap in this area; future studies should aim to elucidate best practices for life-long antibiotic suppression therapy and innovative non-surgical options.

Copper may be an intriguing novel option for treating PJIs, having been historically used as an antimicrobial until the introduction of antibiotics (112). Due to its well-known bacterial toxicity (Fig. 2), it is now being reconsidered for its therapeutic potential.

Mechanisms of copper toxicity. Copper violates the bacterial wall. The reducing environment of the cytoplasm reduces copper (Cu2+ to Cu+), which can participate in Fenton type reactions, produce highly reactive hydroxyl radicals, and affect lipids, proteins and DNA. Cu+ can also lead to thiol depletion in the GSH pool, proteins and free amino acids. Under anaerobic conditions, glutathione–copper complexes (GS–Cu–SG) can act as copper-donors for metalloenzymes. The most common mechanism of copper toxicity is the displacement of iron from iron–sulfur cluster proteins.

Copper is the third most abundant essential transition metal in humans and is necessary for enzymes involved in redox reactions (113). As a redox-active metal, copper is an ideal cofactor for enzymes in photosynthesis, respiration, detoxification, and iron metabolism (114). However, excess copper can be harmful, producing toxic reactive oxygen species (115). Consequently, copper transport and cellular homeostasis are strictly regulated. Over 90% of blood copper is bound to ceruloplasmin, transporting it to tissues, while less than 5% is protein-free. Intracellular copper is usually bound to chaperones, as free copper can be toxic (116). Measuring unbound copper in circulation could help detect toxic overload. The normal range for total blood copper is 85–180 μg/dL, with serum-free copper at 0–10 μg/dL. Exploring copper’s role in SF antimicrobial properties could lead to innovative treatments for infections.

Increased systemic and local copper availability leverages copper’s toxic properties to combat microbes (117) during infections. Free copper is toxic to most bacteria, which employ strategies to prevent intracellular/cytoplasmic copper accumulation. Most bacterial cuproproteins are located in the cytoplasmic membrane or periplasmic space, strictly regulating copper for physiologic processes, with studies showing less than 104 free copper atoms per bacterial cell (118, 119).

Bacteria counter copper toxicity by effluxing copper into the periplasmic space or extracellular compartment, copper sequestration within cytoplasm or periplasm by copper-binding proteins, and Cu(I) oxidation to less toxic Cu(II) ion (120, 121). Various copper export proteins have been identified in bacteria, with nearly all microorganisms possessing at least one copper-exporting P1B-type ATPase to prevent cytoplasmic copper accumulation (122, 123). Examples include CopA of E. coli, CopA1 and CopA2 of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, CopA and GolT of Salmonella typhimurium, and CopZ of Enterococcus hirae. CopA is activated under high extracellular copper concentrations (124, 125, 126, 127). Solioz et al. (122) identified the presence of P1B-type ATPases in gram-positive bacteria, such as S. aureus, which export Cu(I) out of the cytoplasm across the cell membrane.

Innovative strategies are essential to combat pathogenic microorganisms due to increasing antibiotic resistance. The use of copper surfaces in hygiene-sensitive areas has been developed (128). Recent studies have highlighted the potential of copper as a component of implant coatings to reduce bacterial colonization and biofilm formation in prosthetic joints. Copper-coated implants have shown promising results in preclinical models. Cardoso et al. identified copper as a favorable implant coating due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficacy and low cytotoxicity (129). Furthermore, Ricciardi et al. demonstrated that the sustained release of copper ions may contribute to continuous antimicrobial activity without compromising biocompatibility, showing its potential role in long-term infection prevention (130). These coatings can potentially be integrated during implant manufacturing or applied perioperatively as an infection control strategy (131). While promising, the clinical translation of copper-coated implants remains limited due to the lack of standardized production protocols, poor reproducibility, and an incomplete understanding of long-term pharmacokinetics and toxicity (132). Excess copper accumulation can cause oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and organ toxicity, thus having a potentially narrow therapeutic window (132). The current in vitro and small-animal models do not adequately simulate human physiological responses, highlighting the need for future robust clinical studies to optimize the clinical application of copper, evaluate its long-term efficacy, and determine its cost-effectiveness in preventing and managing PJIs.

Bacteriophages are viruses that selectively infect and lyse bacteria, and their specificity and ability to penetrate biofilms make them a promising tool in adjunctive management of PJIs. However, their effectiveness may be limited by the need for pathogen-specific matching and patient considerations. Multiple studies have shown potential for phage therapy in PJI treatment. Morris et al. reported that a phage cocktail, combined with vancomycin, significantly reduced the bacterial load and inflammation in S. aureus-induced PJI models (133). Schoeffel et al. described a case, where intraoperative and IV phage therapy were administered and successfully treated MRSA-PJI (134). Similarly, Cano et al. demonstrated a successful resolution of a persistent Klebsiella pneumonia PJI using IV phage therapy combined with minocycline (135). Despite encouraging findings, most evidence stems from case reports, and clinical translation barriers remain. There is a limited host range, variable lytic activity, and the potential for bacterial or immune-mediated resistance (136). In addition, phage pharmacokinetics and bacterial receptor variability are not well characterized, limiting reproducibility and clinical standardization. Thus, robust prospective clinic trials are needed to establish optimal delivery strategies, standardized treatment protocols, and assess long-term efficacy and safety.

Recent research has begun to explore the role of gut microbiota in the development of PJIs, giving rise to the concept of ‘gut-immune-joint-axis.’ This hypothesis posits that gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability may facilitate the translocation of bacteria or their metabolic byproducts into systemic circulation, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of PJIs (137). Chisari et al. demonstrate preliminary evidence supporting this association (138, 139). These findings link gut barrier dysfunction and microbial shifts to increased infection risk following TJA. Modulating the gut microbiota through probiotics, prebiotics, or other microbiota-targeted therapies presents a promising yet underexplored avenue for the prevention and management of PJI. However, current research remains limited; larger, mechanistic, and longitudinal studies are warranted to understand better the causal relationships and therapeutic potential of this gut–joint connection.

This narrative review has several limitations that should be considered. The conducted literature search was performed in a non-systematic approach, without definite inclusion and exclusion criteria. Thus, there may be selection bias regarding the included studies. Without a structured methodology, these specific findings may be challenging to reproduce. Further, the search may not have captured all the available evidence on S. aureus PJI in THA and TKA.

TJAs are highly successful, yet PJIs, especially caused by S. aureus, continue to pose significant challenges that demand comprehensive and evolving treatment strategies. Diagnosing and treating PJIs requires a thorough approach. Multiple classifications have been developed, and new findings and evidence continue to improve outcomes. Two-stage revision surgery remains the gold standard of infection management for TKA and THA. High-quality, multicenter studies are necessary to address unresolved issues, such as culture-negative and MRSA PJI. As TJAs increase globally, healthcare systems should prepare for a rise in PJIs.

Despite substantial advances in understanding S. aureus PJI, critical knowledge gaps remain. Future studies should elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing biofilm formation, quorum-sensing regulation, and resistance phenotypes to identify precise therapeutic targets. Standardized diagnostic protocols with enhanced sensitivity, such as optimized sonication and molecular assays, require validation, along with reliable biomarkers for early detection. Prospective research is needed to define optimal surgical and antimicrobial strategies through randomized trials comparing single- versus two-stage revisions and establishing evidence-based antibiotic duration and combinations. Copper-coated implants and bacteriophage therapy demonstrate promising antimicrobial potential but require rigorous translational and clinical evaluation. For patients unsuitable for revision surgery, further investigation into long-term SAT is warranted to optimize regimens and outcomes. These priorities emphasize the need for mechanistic, standardized, and translational research to improve the prevention and management of S. aureus PJI.
