BACKGROUND: Reports of primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS) 
are mainly restricted to clinically suspected cases, but biopsy-proven ones are 
rare. Here, we present results from a large multicentre cohort of patients with 
biopsy-proven PACNS (BP-PACNS). In particular, we provide insights into 
characteristics and treatment responses of PACNS subtypes.
METHODS: BP-PACNS patients treated between 1999 and 2021 were analysed. The 
outcome was assessed by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Between-group 
comparisons were performed by Kruskal-Wallis, χ2 or Fisher's exact tests.
RESULTS: In total, 57 patients were analysed (52% male). Of these, n=37 (65%) 
had a lymphocytic (L-PACNS), n=9 (16%) an amyloid-beta-related angiitis (ABRA), 
n=8 (14%) a granulomatous (G-PACNS) and n=3 (5%) a necrotising (N-PACNS) PACNS 
subtype. At the time of diagnosis, age differed significantly between groups 
(median age (years) L-PACNS 47, ABRA 64.5, G-PACNS 37, N-PACNS 65; p=0.008). The 
clinical course was mostly monophasic in L-PACNS and ABRA (65% and 75%, 
respectively), while relapsing-remitting in G-PACNS (63%). Median mRS at last 
follow up was 2 (IQR 1.25-4) in the study group. Worst outcome (median mRS 4) 
and highest mortality (25%) were seen in G-PACNS. Good induction treatment 
response was achieved in 77% of all BP-PACNS patients but was low in those with 
G-PACNS (29%).
CONCLUSIONS: In this large, multicentre series of BP-PACNS patients, G-PACNS had 
a worse functional outcome, a predominant relapsing-remitting disease and a 
poorer response to the induction therapy. An optimal first-line treatment 
regimen for G-PACNS patients should be further examined and established in 
larger studies to improve the outcome of G-PACNS patients.

Reports of primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS) are mainly restricted to clinically suspected cases. Thus, we provide data on biopsy-proven patients and insights into characteristics and treatment responses of PACNS subtypes.

Granulomatous PACNS (G-PACNS) patients do not respond well to the standard induction therapy. An optimal first-line treatment regimen for G-PACNS patients should be further examined and established in larger studies to improve the outcome of G-PACNS patients.

This study adds value to the research field since it focuses on an unmet clinical need to categorise PACNS patients to likely outcome and expected treatment response. Thus, this study helps to establish subtype-specific treatment options in terms of personalised medicine.

Primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS) is a rare inflammatory condition affecting the medium and small vessels of the CNS. The detailed pathogenic mechanisms mediating PACNS have still not been clarified. The clinical features are broad and range from headache, encephalopathy, cognitive impairment, seizures to focal neurological deficits.1 The diagnostic workup often encounters challenges since diagnostic procedures yield only non-specific results. To date, brain biopsy is the only eligible technique to confirm the diagnosis. However, even biopsies often provide false-negative results, since brain vessels in PACNS are affected segmentally, explaining the low sensitivity of this procedure.2–4 However, disease severity necessitates a high diagnostic accuracy for safely and rapidly instituting the required immunosuppressive treatment to improve the prognosis of each patient.

PACNS is either diagnosed by histological evidence, regarded as definite PACNS, or by angiogram in which characteristic changes of vasculitis can be found, categorised as suspected PACNS.5 6 Given that histological evidence is extremely difficult to establish, most reports of PACNS rely on highly suspected cases, which always bear the risk of misdiagnosis. In fact, reports on biopsy-proven cases are rare.

To date, the two largest study cohorts of PACNS were described by Salvarani et al
7 and Nehme et al.8 They reported 37% (71 of 191 patients) and 31% (61 of 200 patients) proven PACNS cases within their study populations, respectively.

So far, four different histopathological patterns are known: the lymphocytic pattern (L-PACNS), the amyloid-beta related angiitis (ABRA), the granulomatous (G-PACNS) and necrotising (N-PACNS) pattern. However, there is limited information on differences among patients with distinct histological patterns in terms of clinical symptoms, imaging and cerebral spinal fluid findings (CSF) findings as well as treatment response. Thus, to gain more insights into this devastating disease through confirmed PACNS patients, in particular into the subtypes, is of utmost importance. Furthermore, it is an unmet clinical need to categorise PACNS patients to likely outcome and expected treatment response. The ultimate goal is to establish subtype-specific treatment options in terms of personalised medicine.

Thus, we present results from a large multicentre cohort of patients with biopsy-proven PACNS (BP-PACNS), aiming to provide more information on the characteristics of the various PACNS subtypes. Moreover, we identify potential differences in treatment response according to the histological pattern of PACNS. Thus, our analysis helps to consider and develop new treatment approaches.

BP-PACNS patients treated in three different university hospitals (University Medical Center Hamburg Eppendorf, Charite Berlin and University Hospital Bonn, Germany) between 1999 and 2021 were analysed.

The biopsy findings were analysed by the neuropathologists of each participating clinic at their own discretion.

The following parameters were collected: time of symptom onset, age, gender, clinical symptoms, results of CSF, MRI, angiography and biopsy analysis, number of relapses and the different immunosuppressive treatments applied to the patients. A good treatment response was defined as a condition when a de-escalation of immunosuppressants was feasible in terms of lacking further relapses (new or progression of clinical symptoms and MRI findings) and achievement of a clinical stabilisation. A treatment non-responder was defined as a patient who died or treatment must have been adjusted due to recurrent flares. The functional outcome was assessed by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). The mRS was determined at each time of presentation until last follow-up.

Patients with highly suspected but not confirmed PACNS, patients with systemic vasculitis and secondary CNS affection or patients with non-inflammatory vasculopathies were excluded from this analysis.

Standard descriptive statistics are reported as numbers and percentages for categorical variables. For continuous variables, the median and IQR are provided. Between-group comparisons were performed by Kruskal-Wallis, χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Multivariable linear mixed model analysis was applied, using the SPSS procedure GENLINMIXED, to evaluate the influence of different independent variables on the mRS, after first disease exacerbation. Random intercepts were fitted to individual patients. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (V.28.0; IBM).

In total, n=57 patients were analysed. The patients were either treated at University Medical Center Hamburg Eppendorf (n=36, 63%), at Charité Berlin (n=14, 25%) or at University Hospital Bonn (n=7, 12%). Median follow-up was 41.5 months (IQR 7.5–73.5; n=56). One patient was lost to follow-up.

In brain biopsy, a lymphocytic pattern (L-PACNS) was seen in 65% (n=37) of all patients, 16% (n=9) had an ABRA, 14% (n=8) showed a G-PACNS and 5% (n=3) a N-PACNS pattern (figure 1). The detailed histopathological findings are summarised in online supplemental table e1.

Clinical symptoms showed no differences among the patients (n=56). In particular, headache (p=0.124), seizures (p=0.455), focal neurological deficits (p=0.900), encephalopathy (p=0.609) and cognitive impairment (p=0.433) did not differ between the subgroups. In the entire study population, focal neurological deficit was the most common symptom (77%), followed by cognitive impairment (63%), headache (59%), seizures (50%) and encephalopathy (45%).

CSF analyses showed pathological results in the majority of the cases, in particular elevated protein levels (69%, n=37/53) rather than pleocytosis (53%, n=28/53). Oligoclonal bands were found in twelve of 53 patients (23%), whereas in seven (21%) of L-PACNS patients, in three (38%) G-PACNS patients, two of the three N-PACNS patients and in none of the ABRA patients.

Inflammatory CSF (pleocytosis and/or increased protein level) results were observed in 79% (37/47) of the entire study group at time of symptom onset, in 84% (41/49) at time of diagnosis and 87% (46/53) during disease course. In 13% of the patients, the CSF result remained normal during the disease course.

Immunological parameters were negative in the majority of the patients (41/52, 79%). In particular, antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies and antibodies indicating a systemic vasculitis were assessed as normal in these patients. The ANA levels were mildly and non-specifically elevated in 21% of the patients (11/52).

Three-quarters (75%) of all patients showed contrast-enhanced lesions on brain MRI (42/56). White matter hyperintensities were seen in 41% (23/56), cerebral infarcts were present in 29% (16/56) and intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) occurred in 17% of all patients (10/56).

In about half of the L-PACNS and G-PACNS patients, T2-hyperintensities were seen, but in none of the ABRA patients. An ICH was detected in 50% of the ABRA (4/8) patients, but only rarely in other BP-PACNS subtypes. None of the patients had an entirely normal brain MRI.

MR angiography (MRA) could be evaluated in 46 of 57 patients (81%) and showed pathologies in 33% (15/46). In particular, MRA showed vessel irregularities in 39% (11/28) of the L-PACNS patients, in 25% (2/8) of the G-PACNS patients, in 67% (2/3) of N-PACNS cases, but in none of the ABRA patients.

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed in 17 cases of the entire study population (30%), of whom 82% (14/17) had abnormal findings. In 8 of 15 patients with pathological MRA results, DSA also revealed vessel irregularities (53%). In one patient, MRA was considered normal, but DSA revealed pathological findings. Five patients received a DSA without prior MRA.

Based on MRA findings, 26% (15/57) of the patients had an involvement of the medium-sized vessels of the brain. Based on DSA results, in 14% (8/57) of the entire study population, medium brain vessels were affected.

Vessel wall imaging (VWI) was performed in 39% (22/57) of the entire study population. In 82% (18/22) of these cases, VWI did not demonstrate any vessel wall enhancement.

In total, 94% (51/54) of the patients received steroid pulse therapy (online supplemental table e2). One patient with L-PACNS and two patients with ABRA did not receive any steroid pulse therapy. None of the patients received a steroid tapering regimen after a steroid pulse therapy.

A first-line induction treatment with cyclophosphamide was administered in 79% of the patients (41/52) in addition to steroids. Another four patients received rituximab (4/52; 8%). In four patients other first-line treatment approaches were applied after steroid pulse therapy (8%). One patient received a steroid pulse alone (2%). The two ABRA patients without steroids did not receive any immunosuppressive therapy.

Azathioprine (35%), methotrexate (31%) and mycophenolate mofetil (23%) were applied as maintenance therapy in most of the cases. Four patients received tocilizumab (8%) and three patients infliximab (6%) during the disease course.

In particular, in the G-PACNS subgroup, 5/8 patients were treated with cyclophosphamide, while two patients received rituximab and one patient mycophenolate-mofetil as the first-line therapy.

In 53 of 57 patients (93%), information on the disease course was available. The majority of the patients (49/53, 92%) was diagnosed at the time of disease onset. Four patients (4/52, 8%) experienced relapses before diagnosis was made.

Relapses occurred rarely in the entire study group (30%; 16/53). In particular, the majority of the L-PACNS (65%; 22/34) and ABRA (75%; 6/8) patients had a monophasic course, respectively, whereas in G-PACNS a relapsing-remitting disease type was prevalent (63%; 5/8). The latter patients had a median of n=2 (IQR 1–3) relapses, while L-PACNS (n=34, IQR 1–2) and ABRA patients (IQR 1–3.5) had n=1 clinical exacerbation, respectively.

The L-PACNS patients were followed up for a median of 41 months (IQR 9–72.5), G-PACNS patients for 28 months (IQR 3.25–125.25) and ABRA patients for 31.5 months (IQR 3.25–125.25). The three N-PACNS patients had a follow-up time of 25, 67 and 125 months, respectively. The follow-up periods showed no significant difference between the groups (p=0.784).

Treatment response to the induction therapy could be evaluated in 75% (43/57) of the study group. Good induction treatment response (steroids and a concomitant cyclophosphamide therapy in the majority of the cases) was achieved in 77% (33/43). Most of the L-PACNS (23/28; 82%) and ABRA patients (4/5; 80%) responded well.

In n=7 G-PACNS patients treatment response could be evaluated. Compared with the other subgroups, a good treatment response in G-PACNS subjects was numerically lowest (29%, 2/7) (figure 3). Of the five patients treated with cyclophosphamide, three showed a poor response. For one patient, the treatment effect could not be assessed, while the other responded well. One patient treated with rituximab and the individual who received mycophenolate mofetil were non-responders, while another patient treated with rituximab showed a good treatment response.

In this multicentre study, we collected comprehensive data of PACNS patients with histological evidence. We provide insights into the characteristics of different PACNS subtypes through comparative analyses. In particular, our results confer detailed information on clinical symptoms, laboratory and imaging findings, follow-up assessments and treatment responses according to the histological PACNS subtype.

A noteworthy finding of our study was the observation that G-PACNS patients had a worse functional outcome (assessed by mRS), a prevalent relapsing-remitting disease, the highest mortality rate and a poorer response to the induction therapy compared to the other PACNS subtypes. Based on our results, we propose that an optimal first-line treatment strategy, in particular for G-PACNS patients, should be discussed and further examined in larger studies to improve outcomes in this specific PACNS subgroup.

In our study cohort, L-PACNS was the most common prevalent histological pattern. Lu et al also collected data from BP-PACNS cases, but pooled analysis from previous case reports.9 They reported similar pattern distributions, while the two largest cohorts of PACNS (the French and the American cohort) found G-PACNS more frequent7 8 (41% and 30%, respectively) than in our study population (14%). However, the French study observed an association of ABRA with granulomatous vasculitis in most of the cases and, in consequence, included ABRA patients within the G-PACNS subgroup.8 Similar findings were described in the American cohort.7 Thus, a higher proportion of G-PACNS patients in these cohorts can be explained.

Several studies discussed whether ABRA should be classified as a distinct subgroup of PACNS instead of being a subtype of cerebral amyloid angiopathy-related inflammation.8 10–12 It has to be emphasised that prior reports confirmed that vessel wall disruption occurs in ABRA patients while being not present in inflammatory cerebral amyloid angiopathy.13 Therefore, we are still convinced to consider ABRA as a distinct subset of PACNS. Lu et al also reported on ABRA as a subtype of PACNS.9


The median age of our patients (47.5 years) was consistent with previous reports.8 9 Age differed significantly between the groups. The G-PACNS patients in the French Cohort were older (mean 50 years) than in ours (median 37 years; mean 36.5 years). Again, this may be due to the fact that they have included ABRA patients in that subgroup and, of note, ABRA patients are known to be older,9 as we also showed in our population (median 64.5 years).

In our study cohort, clinical symptoms did not differ between PACNS subtypes. Previous reports indicated that biopsy-proven subjects have fewer focal neurological deficits compared with PACNS patients diagnosed by angiography who, in fact, have medium vessel involvement.14 In contrast, most of our patients showed focal neurological deficits, similar to the findings by Lu et al.9 Of note, 26% of our study population had an affection of medium brain vessels on MRA, which may partially account for this finding.

We found positive CSF results in the majority of our patients (87%). Interestingly, positive CSF findings increased during the disease course. This result suggests that follow-up CSF analysis is reasonable, if initial findings are inconclusive. Similarly, Salvarani et al observed a comparably high rate of inflammatory CSF results in the proven PACNS subgroup.7


Nonetheless, in 13% of our patients no pathological CSF result was found during the entire disease course. However, a normal CSF finding does not rule out the diagnosis.

MRI findings in our population were consistent with previous reports. While lesion enhancement often occurred, cerebral infarcts were observed less frequently.7 8 Interestingly, two prior studies found vessel stenosis in about one-third of the histologically proven study cohort.9 14 Our analysis confirmed this finding, together indicating that involvement of medium brain vessels occurs in only a small proportion of BP-PACNS cases. Consistent with these results, VWI revealed no contrast enhancement in most of our cases.

Treatment recommendations currently originate from case reports of PACNS and studies on systemic vasculitis, owing to the lack of randomised controlled trials for this rare disease. To date, the common treatment of PACNS involves steroids and cyclophosphamide (or rituximab) as induction therapy, followed by a maintenance therapy, that is, azathioprine, methotrexate or mycophenolat-mofetil, irrespective of the PACNS subtype.14 This treatment strategy yielded the best outcome in a previous study15 and was also applied to the majority of our study subjects, resulting in a favourable overall outcome (mRS 2). Of note, maintenance therapy is not commonly applied.7 However, there is growing evidence that maintenance therapy leads to less disability and death in PACNS.7 15


In general, treatment durations in our study cohort were sufficiently long to assess the effect. Only isolated, shorter treatment durations occurred for individual reasons. Thus, we strongly believe that we were able to evaluate the disease course and treatment effects of the patients adequately and reliably.

Salvarani et al report histologically proven PACNS patients having a more benign course than angiogram-positive.7 However, they found a worse outcome in patients with ABRA compared with patients without Aß-deposition, a finding we could not confirm. Instead, we found ABRA associated with a more favourable outcome assessed on the mRS. Differences in sample size (n=24 vs n=8) between both patient groups may account for variations in patient outcomes. However, since ABRA patients are usually older and have accumulating risk factors, it would be necessary to have all subgroup details before drawing any conclusions.

However, we were able to confirm at least part of their results, with L-PACNS patients having a lower disability and mortality than G-PACNS patients (mortality 0 and 34%, respectively). Notably, the mortality rate in our G-PACNS subgroup was almost equally frequent (25%). We observed an overall mortality of 13% (7/53), consistent with other studies reporting a mortality of 8%–10.5%.9 15 Together, BP-PACNS subjects appear to have an overall low mortality rate.

However, G-PACNS patients had the highest mortality (25%) compared with the other PACNS subgroups. In addition, they showed the worst outcome (median mRS 4), a higher flare frequency (63%) and the poorest treatment response (29%). Interestingly, G-PACNS patients received different induction therapies, highlighting the uncertainty of the appropriate treatment regimen in these patients. Of note, cyclophosphamide was administered to the majority of G-PACNS cases. The worse outcome necessitates the discussion whether an induction therapy with steroids and cyclophosphamide is suitable and beneficial for G-PACNS patients instead of another treatment regimen. For the other PACNS subtypes, this treatment strategy appears to be highly reasonable, but probably not for G-PACNS. Confirming these findings, Salvarani et al demonstrated granulomatous vasculitis patients having the lowest response rate to an induction therapy with prednisolone alone or prednisolone and cyclophosphamide compared with the other PACNS patterns.7


Interestingly, in systemic granulomatous disease, that is, sarcoidosis in particular with CNS involvement, various treatment options were analysed and found to be potentially beneficial.16 It has to be noted that, in particular, TNFalpha-inhibitors (eg, infliximab) appear to play a pivotal role in reducing clinical deterioration in patients suffering from sarcoidosis.17 However, whether TNFalpha-inhibitors are also beneficial as an optimal first-line treatment strategy in G-PACNS patients still remains unknown.

Another striking finding of our analysis was that headache is associated with a more favourable outcome. Suffering from a headache is a condition with a high psychological strain which is known as an early indicator for disease activity. Thus, it is often followed by an early initiation of immunosuppressants which improves prognosis. Of note, this association was also found by two different studies,18 19 thus, a finding by chance appears to be unlikely. Moreover, our study yielded that a higher age was associated with a worse outcome. This is not surprising since age is a well-known predictor of worse outcome in many clinical conditions such as stroke or stroke thrombectomy.20 In contrast, gender association with outcome is still a matter of debate. While Schuster et al found male sex as a predictor of relapses in PACNS,21 other studies on vasculitis, that is, giant cell arteritis, observed female gender to be associated with more relapses.22 23 In fact, in our adjusted analysis, female gender was associated with a better outcome.

Our study has some limitations that have to be acknowledged. Inherent to all retrospective studies, missing or incomplete data can negatively influence the results. Moreover, the interpretation of our data is limited owing to the low sample size in each of the subgroups. Considering we only included three patients with N-PACNS, we are not able to draw any conclusions particularly from this specific subgroup.

One strength of our data was the multicentre recruitment of a large number of BP-PACNS patients, although being a rare disease and considering the challenge to prove the diagnosis. The long-term follow-up information of these patients is appreciable. It has to be acknowledged that we can definitely rule out having included patients suffering from diseases which bear the risk of resembling PACNS. This enhances the value of our data collection and its analysis significantly compared with other reports.

We propose that patients with G-PACNS must be evaluated differentially in terms of treatment strategies since they appear not to benefit from the standard induction treatments compared to the other subtypes. Our results of G-PACNS patients must be verified, and other treatment agents for this specific subgroup must be evaluated and established in further larger studies.
