# Allele-specific RNAi therapy corrects an extracellular matrix defect in Schuurs-Hoeijmakers syndrome.
**Authors:** Mekzine L, Pinzón N, Mamchaoui K, Kondili M, Cadot B, Bitoun M, Trochet D
**Journal:** Am J Hum Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ajhg.2025.07.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2025.07.010)

## Content

Allele-specific silencing by RNA interference offers a promising therapeutic approach for dominant inherited diseases through specific silencing of the mRNA produced by the mutated allele. Here, we report the development of such a strategy for Schuurs-Hoeijmakers syndrome (SHMS), a rare neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability, abnormal craniofacial features, and congenital malformations without available treatment. Most cases of SHMS are caused by a recurrent de novo heterozygous missense mutation (c.607C>T [p.Arg203Trp]) in the PACS1 gene, which encodes PACS1 (phosphofurin acid cluster sorting 1), a multifunctional sorting protein. Through an in vitro screening of fibroblasts derived from affected individuals, we identified several small interfering RNA (siRNA) sequences that specifically silence the PACS1 transcript harboring the recurrent PACS1 mutation while sparing the wild-type mRNA. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis of SHMS fibroblasts revealed alterations in extracellular matrix organization in mutant cells, including elevated COL8A1 expression and extracellular deposition. Treatment with the best selected allele-specific siRNA corrected the COL8A1 dysregulation. Altogether, this study provides the proof of concept of allele-specific RNA interference therapeutic for SHMS in cells derived from affected individuals and highlights a pathophysiological mechanism involving extracellular matrix dysfunction in this disorder. These results strengthen the allele-specific silencing approach as a robust, safe, and efficient therapy for dominant inherited diseases.

Allele-specific RNA interference silences the mutant PACS1 transcript causing Schuurs-Hoeijmakers syndrome, a rare neurodevelopmental disorder. RNA sequencing of cells from affected individuals revealed extracellular matrix defects corrected by siRNA treatment, providing a promising therapeutic approach for this dominant inherited disease with no current treatment.

Schuurs-Hoeijmakers syndrome (SHMS [MIM: 615009]) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability, abnormal craniofacial features, and congenital malformations. Affected individuals classically present facial dysmorphia associated with mild to moderate delayed speech and delayed psychomotor development. SHMS individuals can also suffer from seizures, autism, hypotonia, feeding difficulties, and other congenital anomalies affecting the brain, heart, and urogenital tract. This rare autosomal-dominant disease is due to dominant mutations in the PACS1 gene (MIM: 607492).1,2,3,4 Strikingly, almost all individuals affected by SHMS share the same missense de novo PACS1 variant (c.607C>T [p.Arg203Trp]) resulting in an arginine-to-tryptophan substitution at position 203.5,6,7

PACS1 gene encodes the phosphofurin acid cluster sorting 1 (PACS1) protein, a multifunctional cytosolic sorting protein involved in the targeting of proteins to their specific intracellular compartments.8,9 To date, more than 100 putative PACS1 client proteins have been identified.10 PACS1 is also a nuclear protein involved in chromatin stability, DNA repair,11,12 and nucleocytoplasmic RNA trafficking.13 PACS1 is composed of four major domains: an N-terminal atrophin-related region, a furin-binding region (FBR) that binds motifs on cargo, a middle region with auto-regulatory function and containing a nuclear localization sequence (NLS), and a large C-terminal region. The FBR is responsible for the binding to client proteins through the recognition of phosphorylated acidic cluster motifs, allowing transfer to their final destination.8,9 The recurrent p.Arg203Trp PACS1 alteration is located in the FBR and may affect the binding to protein cargo, but the pathophysiological mechanisms are still not fully understood. At the molecular level, the mutated PACS1 impacts the deacetylation of microtubules and Golgi integrity through HDAC6 interaction14 and increases the stability of the PACS1 protein and its propensity to form aggregates.4 At the functional level, PACS1 mutant alters cranial-neural-crest cell migration in zebrafish4 and induces a deregulated expression of genes involved in synaptic function and electrophysiological defects in cortical neuron organoids derived from affected individuals.15 These reported impacts associated with the upregulated PACS1 expression during brain development16 could underlie the neurodevelopmental phenotype.

The heterozygous PACS1 p.Arg203Trp alteration probably results in a gain-of-function or a dominant-negative effect,4,14 and absence of haploinsufficiency is confirmed in Pacs1−/− mice, where total absence of Pacs1 leads to a mild lymphocyte deficiency and no neurological phenotype,10 and in human, where a loss of function due to PACS1 homozygous deletion has been associated with a mild neurodevelopmental disorder without SHMS symptoms and incomplete penetrance.17 In this context, the specific suppression of the mutant allele is a therapeutic approach of choice for the PACS1 syndrome without available treatment. Allele-specific RNA interference (AS-RNAi) is a powerful tool used to silence the mutated allele of genes causing dominant inherited diseases while sparing the normal allele, thus preserving 50% of normal protein.18 Therapeutic benefit has been demonstrated in cells from affected individuals19,20,21,22,23 and animal models,24,25,26,27,28 and this strategy has been instigated in clinical trials.29,30 We report here the development of an AS-RNAi approach against the c.607C>T (p.Arg203Trp) PACS1 mutation causing SHMS. We show that AS-RNAi is able to restore a defect of the extracellular matrix (ECM) highlighted by a transcriptomic analysis performed in fibroblasts from affected individuals.

Healthy controls and PACS1 fibroblast cell lines derived from affected individuals were obtained from the Coriell Institute (https://www.coriell.org/; GM IDs GM27650, GM27159, GM27651, and GM27160) and immortalized by transduction of the catalytic subunit of human telomerase (hTERT) via the MyoLine platform for the immortalization of human cells (Center of Research in Myology, Paris, France). Informed consent for research studies was obtained according to institutional guidelines and in accordance with European recommendations and French legislation (French Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research and Innovation approval no. IE-2022-1687). Detailed information on the cell line is reported in Table 1. Cell lines were cultured at 37°C (5% CO2) in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Life Technologies, France) containing 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) supplemented with penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 μg/mL). Fibroblasts from affected individuals and control fibroblasts were immortalized using a lentiviral vector containing the sequence encoding hTERT as previously described.31 For the collagen synthesis study, 25,000 cells were seeded in 24-well plates (μ-Plate 24 Well ibiTreat; Ibidi, CliniSciences, France) in DMEM/10% FCS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. After 24 h, medium was changed with new DMEM medium containing ascorbic acid (0.125 mM) (Sigma-Aldrich, France) to activate collagen synthesis, and this medium was renewed every 48–72 h. Cell viability was assessed using the trypan blue exclusion method. An equal volume of cell suspension and 0.4% trypan blue solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was mixed, and live/dead cell counts were performed using the TC20 Automated Cell Counter (Bio-Rad, USA). For proliferation assays, cell lines were seeded at 65,000 cells/well in 12-well plates in growth medium. For transfection, cells at 70%–90% confluency were transfected with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) using RNAimax transfection reagent (Life Technologies, France) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNAs were isolated from cells using NucleoSpin RNA (Macherey-Nagel, France) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNAs (500 ng) were submitted to reverse transcription using the Superscript III reverse transcriptase kit (Life Technologies, France) using oligo-dT primers in a final volume of 20 μL. Reverse transcription was performed at 50°C for 50 min, and a final step of 85°C for 5 min was added. The PACS1 expression was quantified by semi-quantitative RT-PCR relative to the HPRT housekeeping gene expression. RT product (1 μL) was submitted to PCR performed at 96°C for 3 min followed by 30 cycles including denaturation at 96°C for 25 s, annealing at 58°C for 25 s, and polymerization at 72°C for 40 s, with a final step at 72°C for 5 min. The number of 30 PCR cycles has been selected to have amplification in the exponential range for PACS1 and HPRT. To quantify allele specificity of the assessed siRNA, an assay was developed using the Hpy188I restriction enzyme allowing discrimination between wild-type (WT) and mutant alleles after digestion of the RT-PCR products (Figure S1A). PCR was designed to amplify regions of the PACS1 transcript encompassing the mutation. RT product (1 μL) was submitted to PCR performed at 96°C for 3 min followed by 40 cycles including denaturation at 96°C for 25 s, annealing at 58°C for 25 s, and polymerization at 72°C for 40 s, with a final step at 72°C for 5 min. The number of cycles has been selected to be at the end of the exponential phase of amplification. Ten microliters of the 20 μL of PCR products was digested overnight at 37°C using 7 units of Hpy188I (New England Biolabs, France). Image acquisition of the PCR products after agarose gel electrophoresis was performed using a Geni2 gel imaging system (Ozyme, France), and the associated signal was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH; http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij). Allele-specific or total PACS1 and COL8A1 mRNA quantification was performed by real-time PCR using a Thermo ABI QuantStudio 6 Pro Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, France) and 1× SYBR Green (Roche, Switzerland) kit using 0.25 μM forward and reverse primers and 1 μL of cDNA. PCR conditions were a 5-min, 95°C pre-incubation step followed by 50 cycles with a 95°C denaturation for 10 s, 60°C annealing for 20 s, and 72°C extension for 10 s. Relative expression was calculated as mean values of 2−ΔΔCt normalized using the HPRT housekeeping gene. All the PCR primers used in this study were from Eurogentec (Belgium), and sequences are available on request. DNA sequencing was performed on 20 ng DNA/100 bp with 5 pmol of primer (Eurofins, France).

Samples were then sonicated two times for 10 s at 30% of maximum power of VCX 130 Vibra-cell ultrasonic processors (Sonics, USA). After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 4°C, 20 min), protein concentration in the supernatant was determined with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific Pierce, France). Samples were then processed under vacuum in the Bio-Dot SF apparatus (Bio-Rad, France). Membranes were stained with ATX Ponceau S red solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for total protein quantification. After washing in PBS, cells were permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature and blocked in 0.1% PBS-Triton X-100, 5% BSA and 5% donkey serum for 30 min. After PBS washes, cells were incubated with the secondary antibodies goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:600, Life Technologies, France), goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 568 (1:800, Life Technologies, France), or goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 568 (1:800, Life Technologies, France) for 60 min at room temperature. The slides were mounted with Vectashield mounting medium with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Vector Laboratories, Peterborough, UK). Images were acquired using an Axio Observer Apotome.2 microscope (Zeiss, Germany) using a 20× or 40× Plan NeoFluar Zeiss objective. The Golgi surface was automatically measured on tiled images by combining QuPath32 to define the cell area based on PACS1 staining and a macro developed in Fiji33 using Otsu thresholding to detect Golgi staining.33

Total RNAs (n = 3 per condition) were extracted on a silica column (NucleoSpin RNA Plus kit, 740984, Macherey-Nagel, France) and treated by DNase (Turbo DNA-free kit, AM1907, Invitrogen, France). DNA-free total RNA quality was checked using the TapeStation 2200 system (Agilent), ensuring RNA integrity number values greater than 9.2 and mutant-specific PACS1 silencing was validated by RT-PCR before RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), which was performed at the Genotyping/Sequencing Platform (iGenSeq ICM, Paris, France). Libraries were generated using Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep (ref. 20040534), where poly(A)+ RNA were isolated by poly(A) selection and the libraries were sequenced in the Illumina’s Nextseq 2000 sequencing system with at least 2 × 30 million reads 100-bp paired-end each per sample. Analysis of the RNA-seq data was performed at the Data Analysis Core facility (ICM, Paris, France). The quality of raw data was evaluated with FastQC, and alignment of the reads to the hg38 reference genome was validated for the 36 samples. Downstream analyses were conducted using the Quby analysis software to identify deregulated genes. The DESeq2 method with a p value of <0.05 and a counts per million reads mapped (CPM) threshold >1 in at least two samples was used to compare gene expression between two conditions with a threshold set at log2 fold change (log2FC) ±1 for all comparisons except for comparisons between mutant cells treated with scramble or si10-Mut PACS1 siRNA, where the FC level was lowered to log2FC ±0.5. The lists of deregulated genes obtained from the different comparisons were cross-referenced using an online Venn diagram tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). Over-represented biological pathways in the identified genes were analyzed using the Enrichr platform (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/).

Cells were harvested by trypsinization, and 20,000 cells were seeded in 24-well plates (three technical replicates for each condition). After 1-h incubation at 37°C in DMEM-10% FCS supplemented with penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 μg/mL), the non-attached cells were removed by washing with PBS, and attached cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Glass coverslips were mounted on slides with Vectashield medium (Vector Laboratories) containing DAPI to stain nuclei. Images were acquired using an Axio Observer Apotome.2 microscope (Zeiss, Germany) using a 2.5× Plan NeoFluar Zeiss objective, and the number of attached cells per μm2 of coverslip were counted in a defined region of interest using the ImageJ software find maxima process. Three independent experiments were carried out for each condition.

In silico analyses were conducted to assess the specificity and potential off-target effects of the si10T sequence. Nucleotide basic local alignment search tool (BLASTn) searches were performed on the NCBI website (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) against the Homo sapiens GRCh38.p14 genome and transcript database, using the “somewhat similar sequences” setting. To evaluate potential microRNA-like effects, the seed sequences from both the sense and antisense strands of si10T were compared against mature microRNA sequences from miRBase release 22.1 (https://www.mirbase.org), including both high-confidence entries (miRBase “high confidence” annotation) and the complete set of annotated mature microRNAs, using a custom Bash script. Searches were performed across all species as well as one restricted to Homo sapiens (hsa-miRs). Graphics and statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism software versions 6 or 9 (GraphPad Software, LaJolla, CA, USA). Values were expressed as means ± SEM. The number of samples (n) represents the number of independent biological replicates as indicated in the figure legends. We used non-parametric statistical tests to analyze our data when the normality could not be assumed (Shapiro-Wilk test) or tested (n too small). In this case, statistical comparisons between groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test for the siRNA screening or a Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s or uncorrected Dunn’ test when significant.

A screening for allele-specific siRNAs capable of silencing the mutant c.607C>T (p.Arg203Trp) PACS1 allele without affecting the WT allele was performed in immortalized fibroblasts from affected individuals. We developed an RT-PCR assay allowing the discrimination of WT and mutant mRNAs using the disappearance of the recognition site for the Hpy188I restriction enzyme in the mutated sequence (Figure S1A) and evaluated silencing efficacy and allele specificity for 12 of the 19 possible 19-nt-long siRNAs according to the position of their mismatch with the WT sequence (called si6 to si17, Figure S1B). These siRNAs were transfected at 30 nM for 2 days in cells from affected individuals before RNA extraction and RT-PCR analysis. A scramble siRNA was used as control. Eleven siRNAs (si6 to si12 and si14 to si17) significantly reduced the mutant/WT ratio to around 0.2 (corresponding to a proportion of 80% of WT and 20% of mutant PACS1 mRNA) compared to the scramble siRNA (Figure 1A). Quantification of each allele relative to HPRT mRNA confirmed a reduced expression of the mutant allele T and showed a concomitant increased expression of the WT allele C for all the tested siRNAs when compared to scramble siRNA (Figure S1C), suggestive of a genetic regulation promoting the expression of the untargeted allele. The total PACS1 mRNA, quantified by semi-quantitative RT-PCR, was excessively reduced relative to the expected 50% for si13 and si16, while it appeared near to the control value for si6, si7, si8, si9, si10, si11, si14, and si15 (Figure 1B). From these data, si8, si10, si11, si14, and si15, given the lower mutant/WT ratio without inappropriate reduction of the total PACS1 expression, were selected for further investigation. PACS1 protein expression was evaluated at low concentration by western blot, showing a decrease in protein level that did not exceed 50% (Figure S1D). Given that multiple bands were detected (around 130 kDa and a doublet between 70 and 100 kDa), the band corresponding to PACS1 was validated as the doublet using commercial validated pan-allelic PACS1 siRNA (not shown). To establish maintenance of the allele-specific silencing without cell toxicity at higher dosage, the properties of the best candidates (si8, si10, si11, si14, and si15) were evaluated at 100 nM. At this concentration, allele specificity against the mutant allele was maintained as demonstrated by the quantification of the T/C ratio (Figure 1C) and the quantification of each allele relative to HPRT expression (Figure S1E). The si8, si10, and si11 were identified as the best ones leading to lower mutant/WT ratio without inappropriate reduction of the total mRNA expression (Figure 1D). At this higher concentration, the compensatory mechanism increasing the expression of the WT allele was maintained (Figure S1E). We confirmed this molecular result for si8, si10, and si11 by qPCR, which showed a significant decrease in total mRNA level only for si8 (Figure S2A). At 100 nM, a decrease in PACS1 protein was observed for the five siRNAs and reached 50% for si8 (Figure 1E). A second RT-qPCR assay using primers designed for specific amplification of either WT or mutant mRNA (Figure S2B) confirmed the decrease of the mutant allele with the three best siRNAs and supported the possible increase in the WT allele for si11 and to a lesser extent for si10 (Figures S2C and S2D). Altogether, several screened siRNAs reached the criteria to be considered as allele-specific siRNAs (i.e., si8; si9, si10, si11, and si15), with si8 and si10 being the most effective at downregulating the mutant transcript while preserving WT expression. We selected si10 for further experiments, and sequencing of the PACS1 RT-PCR amplicon confirmed a drastic decrease in the mutant mRNA in heterozygous fibroblasts treated with the si10 siRNA at 30 nM (Figure S2E). Importantly, no signs of cytotoxicity were observed following transfection with 100 nM si10-RNA. Two days post transfection, cells showed normal morphology with no evidence of cell death (Figures S3A and S3B). Proliferation kinetics monitored by live-cell imaging revealed no difference compared to non-transfected controls, indicating that cell growth was not affected by the transfection (Figure S3C). To assess off-target effects, we performed a BLAST search of both si10 sense and antisense strands against the Homo sapiens GRCh38.p14 reference genome. No perfect matches were identified in any other human genes or pseudogenes, with the best partial match corresponding to the WT PACS1 allele, confirming high specificity for the intended target (Figure S4). Additionally, the si10 seed regions of both strands did not match any known human or conserved microRNA seed sequences listed in the miRBase v.22.1 database (considering both high-confidence microRNAs and the full set of reported entries), suggesting a low risk of off-target gene silencing via microRNA-like mechanisms. To evaluate the potential benefits of the allele-specific silencing, we analyzed the spatial distribution of the Golgi apparatus and acetylation of α-tubulin, both previously reported as defective in PACS1 mutant cells.14 Using giantin staining, no notable morphological difference was observed in the Golgi apparatus between mutant and WT fibroblasts (Figure 2A). Image analysis revealed a significant decrease in absolute Golgi size in affected individual 1 (Mut1) when compared to her mother (WT1) but not reproducible in the second affected individual-mother pair (Mut2 vs. WT2). When normalized to cell surface, the Golgi apparatus surface was significantly reduced in the two individual-mother pairs (WT1 vs. Mut1 and WT2 vs. Mut2) but with a high heterogeneity within each cell population (Figure 2B). Western blot analysis of total protein extracts showed comparable levels of acetylated α-tubulin (Ac-α-tubulin) between mutant and WT cells (Figure 2C). These findings suggest that neither of these biological parameters is suitable as a functional readout for assessing efficacy of the therapeutic strategy.

To identify potential functional readouts and assess the overall effect of the siRNA treatment on gene expression in PACS1 mutated fibroblasts, we performed RNA-seq for transcriptome analysis. Fibroblasts from two daughters affected by PACS1 syndrome and their unaffected mothers were analyzed under basal conditions and after 2 days of treatment with the selected si10-Mut PACS1 siRNA or scramble siRNAs (30 nM). The efficiency and allele specificity of the si10-Mut PACS1 treatment were validated by RT-PCR digestion, as described above, prior to performing the RNA-seq on biological triplicates per group. Data quality control indicated a read count of >55 million for all the samples except one (WT-Sc1-1, which had 27 million reads, Figure S5A). The GC content and insert size distributions were as expected. The alignment to the human hg38 reference genome showed a mapping rate for each sample greater than 99%, with homogeneous distribution per chromosome across samples and >95% of coding sequences (exons + UTR) per sample and no 3′ bias. Principal-component analysis (Figure S5B) revealed clustering of replicates within their respective groups, except for three outliers (WT1-Sc1-1, Mut1-si10-1, and Mut2-NT-2), which were excluded from further analyses. Next, we validated the efficacy and allele specificity of the silencing of the mutated PACS1 mRNA by the AS-siRNA. PACS1 CPM showed a ∼26% decrease of PACS1 CPM in mutants treated with si10 compared to scramble siRNA (MUT-siSc), whereas no reduction was observed in WT fibroblasts treated with si10 (Figure 3A). A slight decrease in total PACS1 CPM was also noted in mutant compared to WT cells. In addition, reads that corresponded to R203W PACS1 (T) or WT PACS1 (C) were counted using the Integrative Genomics Viewer, showing a 90% decreased of the R203W reads in mutant cells transfected with the si10 compared to siSc, while the number of WT PACS1 reads was similar in both conditions (Figure 3B). Finally, we validated that the treatment has no effect on PACS2 expression, the PACS1 paralog (Figure S5C). Together, these results demonstrate that the RNA-seq data meet the criteria for reliable differential expression analysis and confirm the allele specificity and efficacy of the si10 AS-siRNA.

Deregulated pathways in PACS1 mutated fibroblasts were first investigated by analyzing differential gene expression between mutant and WT cells under basal conditions using the DESeq2 method. The threshold was set at log2FC ±1 with a p value of <0.05. With these criteria, a total of 711 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in PACS1 mutant cells compared to healthy control cells, 375 of which were downregulated and 336 upregulated (Figure 3C and Table S1). To explore the biological relevance of these changes, we performed canonical pathway analysis using Enrichr on these 711 DEGs, focusing on Gene Ontology (GO) categories such as biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions, and Reactome gene sets (Figure 3D). The most significant enriched pathway was related to ECM organization, particularly involving collagen content. More broadly, enriched pathways were associated with biological processes involved in cell-extracellular environment interactions (e.g., matrix organization, cell migration, and ECM-receptor interactions) and cell proliferation. Notably, most of these pathways were upregulated except for cell migration, which was downregulated (Figures 3D and S6). Additionally, a few molecular pathways were significantly over-represented among the DEGs, including growth factor and receptor-ligand activity (both upregulated) and voltage-gated channel activity (downregulated). Other significantly downregulated pathways included those related to bile acids and bile salt synthesis (Figures 3D and S6). Given the involvement of pathways related to ECM organization and cell-environment interactions, we analyzed the adhesion capacity of these cells (Figure 3E). By quantifying the number of adherent cells 1 h after seeding on glass coverslips, we observed a 40%–50% reduction in cell density in mutant fibroblasts compared to controls (Figure 3F), indicating an impairment in their adhesion capacity.

The next step was to identify the genes responding to the 48-h treatment with si10-Mut PACS1. First, using the same parameters as for the basal comparison (i.e., log2FC ±1, p < 0.05), we identified 813 DEGs between mutant and WT cells transfected with the scramble siRNA. Subsequently, we used a Venn diagram tool to identify common genes between these 813 DEGs and the 711 DEGs found in basal conditions. 324 genes were specifically found as deregulated under basal conditions, and 426 were specifically deregulated after transfection. However, this analysis led to a list of 387 genes that are differentially expressed between mutant and WT cells with or without transfection (Figure 4A and Table S2). Pathways related to collagen and ECM organization remained over-represented among deregulated pathways, as attested by Enrichr Reactome analysis of these 387 DEGs (Figure 4B). Thereafter, si10-Mut PACS1-responding genes were identified through analysis of DEGs in mutant cells treated with si10-Mut PACS1 vs. scramble siRNA with an FC level lowered to log2FC ±0.5 to include only partially affected genes due to the short 48-h treatment. This analysis identified 1,848 DEGs in the si10-Mut PACS1-treated mutant cells compared to scramble-treated mutant cells. The Venn diagram tool was used to identify overlapping genes between these 1,848 si10-responding genes and the 387 common deregulated genes in mutant cells in basal and transfected conditions (Figure 4C). Among the 66 overlapping genes, 44 were improved by the treatment (Figure 4D and Table S3). Several of these 44 genes still belong to pathways relative to ECM organization (Figure 4E). Overall, transcriptomic analysis performed in fibroblasts derived from affected individuals highlighted a potential defect in the ECM, which may be partially rescued by a 48-h treatment with AS-siRNA at low concentration.

Given the over-representation of ECM-related pathways in our transcriptomic analyses, we focused on COL8A1 gene (MIM: 120251) encoding one of the two chains of type VIII collagen. RNA-seq data showed that COL8A1 exhibited an approximately 2.5-fold increase in expression in mutant cells, which was conserved after transfection with scramble siRNA (Figure 5A). COL8A1 mRNA overexpression in PACS1 mutant cells was confirmed by qPCR on a second set of mRNAs (Figure 5B). At protein level, no significant intracellular COL8A1 change was detected in mutant cells by western blot (Figure 5C) or by immunofluorescence on permeabilized cells (Figure S7A), both performed 2 days after seeding. We therefore assessed extracellular COL8A1 content on mutant and WT fibroblasts cultured for 8 and 12 days in the presence of ascorbic acid to enhance collagen synthesis. Under these conditions, extracellular COL8A1 mesh was detectable only in mutant cells (Figures 5D and S7B), and the signal intensity (gray value) measured over the width of the images was higher in mutants than in WT (Figure 5E). In addition, we examined type 1 collagen (COL1), the principal structural component of the fibroblast-derived ECM, to assess potential alterations in ECM organization in mutant cells. The COL1staining pattern appeared qualitatively different in mutant compared to WT cells (Figure S8A), while no quantitative difference was noticed, in agreement with the RNA-seq data (Figure S8B), and the fibronectin staining was unchanged (Figure S9). Secreted COL8A1 was also monitored by slot blot in cell-culture medium showing an increase of COL8A1 in medium from mutant compared to WT cells (Figure 5F) after 2 and 8 days of culture. Altogether, these results established a defect of COL8A1 that may be used as functional readout.

The si10-Mut PACS1 siRNA restored the COL8A1 mRNA content in the mutant cells to close to the WT values (Figure 5A). To continue further on the benefit of allele-specific silencing on COL8A1 defects, we performed COL8A1 immunofluorescence on cells cultured for 9 days in the presence of ascorbic acid and transiently transfected it three times with scramble or si10 siRNAs at 30 nM. Culture medium was also collected at several time points to assess secreted COL8A1 by slot blot (Figure 6A). Similar to non-transfected conditions, an abnormal extracellular COLA8A1 mesh was detected in mutant cells transfected with scramble siRNA but not in WT after 9 days of culture (Figure 6B). The COL8A1 extracellular mesh became undetectable in mutant cells transfected with si10-Mut PACS1 siRNA. Accordingly, the gray-value signal measured across images was higher in mutant cells transfected with scramble siRNA compared to WT cells treated with scramble siRNA and mutant cells treated with si10-Mut PACS1 siRNA (Figure 6C). COL8A1 slot blot was performed on cell-culture medium 2, 5, 7, and 9 days after cell seeding (Figures 6D and S10). In medium from mutant cells, a 3- to 4-fold increase in COL8A1 level compared with medium from WT cells was detected at days 2 and 5, reaching 5- to 7-fold at days 7 and 9. No effect of the si10-Mut PACS1 siRNA on COL8A1 level was detected at day 2, while a decrease close to WT COL8A1 level was observed in medium from treated mutant cells after day 5. A decrease in secreted COL8A1 was also observed in WT cell medium after si10 treatment at days 5 and 7 but not at days 2 and 9 (Figures 6D and S10). Altogether, these results confirm the abnormal increase in extracellular COL8A1 deposition in PACS1 mutant fibroblasts compared to WT cells and demonstrate that PACS1 allele-specific siRNA is capable of normalizing this defect.

SHMS associates a neurodevelopmental disorder leading to various neurological disabilities with systemic features affecting the eyes, digestive tract, heart, and/or skeletal muscles. More than 98% of affected individuals harbor the same mutation in the PACS1 gene, which appears as the priority target in a therapeutic perspective. The current study represents the first steps in the development of a therapy directed against this target by RNA interference combining both the efficacy and specificity required to silence the PACS1-mutated mRNA. In addition, we highlight a potential pathomechanism of SHMS centered on the ECM.

Several PACS1 allele-specific siRNAs identified in this study meet the key criteria of good allele-specific siRNAs by achieving around 50% reduction of expression of target mRNA and protein resulting from silencing of the mutant allele without affecting the WT. Among them, the si10T siRNA induced approximately 87% knockdown of the mutant PACS1 transcript while preserving WT expression, suggesting promising therapeutic potential. In addition, si10-RNA was well tolerated at high concentration, with no signs of cytotoxicity, and in silico analyses do not reveal potential off-targets or overlap with known microRNA seed sequences, supporting a favorable safety profile. Interestingly, an increase in WT transcript levels is observed sometimes after allele-specific siRNA treatment. Although inconsistently reported, similar effects have been described in other contexts23 and may reflect regulatory phenomena such as transcriptional feedback or transcriptional adaptation triggered by mutant mRNA degradation.34 Further studies will also be required to determine the level of mutant allele knockdown in vivo and to define the therapeutic threshold necessary for clinical benefit in PACS1 syndrome. Notably, partial reduction of mutant transcripts has been sufficient in other dominant genetic disorders. For example, a 30% decrease of mutant Myh6 mRNA prevented cardiomyopathy in a knockin mouse model,24 and we previously showed that around 40% reduction of mutant Dnm2 transcripts fully rescued the muscle phenotype in a mouse model of dominant centronuclear myopathy.25

A crucial issue in the therapeutic development of SHMS treatment is the functional evaluation of the developed solutions based on well-characterized readout. This requires in-depth knowledge of PACS1 functions that could be altered by mutations. Since PACS1’s identification in 1998 and its involvement in furin localization at the trans-Golgi network,9 PACS1 functions have been progressively enlarged by its involvement in addressing several other proteins to their specific subcellular compartments,35,36,37,38 the regulation of the mitochondrial pathway of apoptosis,39 amyloid precursor peptide formation,40 genome integrity,12 hormone secretion,41 and RNA processing.13 Through its binding with viral proteins, PACS1 is also involved in viral particle assembly, trafficking, or associated pathogenesis.42,43,44,45 In addition, expression of the c.607C>T (p.Arg203Trp) PACS1 variant affects neural crest cell migration in zebrafish4 and synaptic density or neuronal transmission in induced pluripotent stem cell-derived mature neurons from affected individuals.15 The multi-functional dimension of PACS1 suggests complex pathophysiological mechanisms of SHMS and a non-trivial identification of functional readout to evaluate therapy efficacy. Among the already reported pathomechanisms, we started by assessing the relevance of using the defects in the Golgi apparatus structure and tubulin acetylation identified in affected individual-derived fibroblasts14 as functional readout. Nevertheless, in our hands these two parameters appeared as not altered or only slightly modified and associated with an insufficient repeatability for it to be used as a robust readout to assess our therapeutic approach. The reasons for this inability to reproduce the two defects are unknown, and we had to adapt our strategy to identify a more robust readout for therapeutic evaluation.

With this objective, we performed RNA-seq of fibroblasts derived from affected individuals to acquire an overview of deregulated pathways and gene expression. This approach pointed toward a defect of the ECM especially affecting the collagen content. Among the ECM deregulated genes, we have chosen to focus on the functional evaluation of the allele-specific siRNA on COL8A1, showing a deregulated expression in two mutant cell lines in the transcriptomic analysis, which was confirmed by RT-PCR on a second set of mRNAs and validated at the protein level through increased extracellular content established by slot blot and immunostaining. This increase in COL8A1 in the ECM of affected individuals’ cells may logically follow the upregulation of COL8A1 gene expression, but we cannot exclude a per se increased COL8A1 secretion due to the PACS1 mutation relative to the role of PACS1 in the secretory pathway. Future studies will be required to clarify whether several mechanisms are indeed involved in this increase in COL8A1 deposition in the ECM, but our study shows that the silencing of the mutated PACS1 restores its expression and secreted amount, achieving proof of concept of the allele-specific silencing therapy at the molecular and functional level in SHMS cells derived from affected individuals. Of note, maternal fibroblasts were selected as controls to minimize genetic and sex-related variability, but they were not age matched. Some age-related transcriptomic differences have been previously reported in the fibroblast, such as in the ECM pathway.46,47 However, in our study the controls are younger than those used in aging studies, and we did not detect typical age-associated signatures such as reduced COL1 expression or increased MMP1 levels. Finally, no difference in COL8A1 expression was observed between the two maternal controls aged 39 and 55 years. These might be due to the immortalization process or the intermediate age range of the controls, but taken together these findings suggest that the transcriptional differences observed in fibroblasts from affected individuals are more likely attributable to the PACS1 mutation. The COL8A1 gene encodes one of the two chains of the non-fibrillar and network-forming type VIII collagen. Among its various cellular origins, that of endothelial cells was particularly well described, and type VIII collagen deregulation has been already involved in human pathophysiology, especially in conditions associated with vascular remodeling and angiogenesis, such as fibrosis and cancers.48,49 As the type VIII collagen shows a widespread tissue distribution including heart, kidney, eyes, and muscle,50 all affected in SHMS, it is therefore tempting to speculate that a collagen VIII defect may be one of the bases of the multisystemic symptomatology of SHMS. In this context, a parallel can be made between the importance of collagen VIII in the eye50,51,52 and the ocular manifestations reported in SHMS-affected individuals.5,7,53,54,55 The abnormal distribution of type VIII collagen in brain tumors56 may also suggest an involvement in central nervous system disabilities.

Beyond COL8A1, the current RNA-seq analysis pointed more broadly toward a defect of the biological processes involved in ECM organization and ECM-cell interactions in fibroblasts from SHMS-affected individuals. Therefore, this study provides evidence of an ECM defect in the multisystemic SHMS with clinical features including central nervous system disabilities and also, in a non-exhaustive manner, global developmental delay, musculoskeletal and gastrointestinal issues, and cardiac, ocular, renal, genitourinary, and immunologic abnormalities.5,7,55 Relative to the dual role of ECM in tissue structure and cell signaling, ECM defects may be involved in one or more features of the SHMS clinical spectrum, but we cannot exclude the potential involvement of the other deregulated pathways identified here. For example, the downregulated pathway of bile salt synthesis may be involved in the gastrointestinal symptoms. Additional studies will be required to better characterize involvement of the ECM or other pathways identified in our study in animal models or in vitro systems based on cells derived from affected individuals to better our knowledge of the disease and identification of symptomatic therapeutic targets.

Until now, management of individuals affected by SHMS is currently focused on treating symptoms of the disease,57 but therapeutic strategies have been initiated by targeting PACS1 expression or addressing specific pathomechanisms. These approaches include PACS1 down-expression using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) to block mRNA translation to protein but, to date, developed ASOs have been inefficient in specifically targeting the mutated PACS1.58 Nevertheless, ASO-mediated pan-allelic Pacs1 knockdown restores neuronal structure and function in a Pacs1 mouse model and in neural progenitor cells derived from affected individuals.14 Based on the binding of the mutated PACS1 to HDAC6, which potentiates HDAC6 activity and then modifies tubulin acetylation, ASO-mediated HDAC6 knockdown or HDAC6 inhibitors have been also used with similar benefits on neuronal structure and function.14 Here, we enlarge the therapeutic possibilities by achieving proof of concept of allele-specific silencing mediated by RNA interference. AS-RNAi, already instigated in clinical trials,29,30 is a strategy of choice for SHMS by combining three advantages compared to the previously developed strategies: (1) the specificity of RNAi enabling discrimination between two sequences differing by only one nucleotide; (2) the efficiency of RNAi allowing a high level of silencing; and (3) safety by preventing total knockdown of the targeted gene, as AS-RNAi protects PACS1 expression and function via the untargeted WT allele. SHMS also fulfills the condition for the development of AS-RNAi, which is the absence of haploinsufficiency, since a negative dominant effect or a gain of function are privileged as a consequence of the c.607C>T PACS1 mutation.4,14 We can therefore hypothesize that our allele-specific siRNA, applicable to almost all affected individuals sharing the same mutation, will be able to abolish the deleterious effects of mutated PACS1 and thus stop the progression of the disease or even reverse particular phenotypes. Importantly, several PACS1-dependent processes are clearly impacted by excessive PACS1 knockdown. Indeed, even if knockout mice do not develop a significant phenotype,10
PACS1 depletion impacts the secretory pathway,9,59 endosomes to Golgi retrograde trafficking,60 sorting of lysosomal enzymes,38 mitochondrial-directed apoptosis,39 cell-cycle progression,12 genomic stability,12 and calcium handling.10,41 These diverse impacts of PACS1 deficiency strongly reinforce the safety advantage of an allele-specific silencing sparing the WT allele of the ubiquitously expressed PACS1, an advantage also confirmed by the absence of a significant phenotype associated with a homozygous loss-of-function multi-exon heterozygous mutation identified in four members of the same family without SHMS-related symptoms and behaviors.17

In conclusion, our study provides evidences that PACS1-dependent mechanisms are involved in the regulation of the ECM component, offering new insights into PACS1 function and contributing to a better understanding of the pathomechanisms underlying SHMS. In addition, by developing an allele-specific silencing strategy that is able to rescue a phenotype in cells derived from affected individuals, we achieved one important step of preclinical development for future therapy to fight the PACS1 syndrome. The nature of the identified therapeutic molecule is compatible with packaging in viral vectors or lipid nanoparticles to provide widespread tissue expression of the siRNA required in a multisystemic syndrome. Our study paves the way toward future clinical trials and therapy for affected individuals.
