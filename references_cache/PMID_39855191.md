# Independent genetic strategies define the scope and limits of CDKL5 deficiency disorder reversal.
**Authors:** Song X, Xia Z, Martinez D, Xu B, Spritzer Z, Zhang Y, Nugent E, Ho Y, Terzic B, Zhou Z
**Journal:** Cell Rep Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xcrm.2024.101926](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2024.101926)

## Content

Cyclin-dependent kinase-like 5 (CDKL5) deficiency disorder (CDD) is a neurodevelopmental syndrome caused by mutations in the X-linked CDKL5 gene. The early onset of CDD suggests that CDKL5 is essential during development, but post-developmental re-expression rescues multiple CDD-related phenotypes in hemizygous male mice. Since most patients are heterozygous females, studies in clinically relevant female models are essential. Here, we systematically compare phenotype reversal across age and sex using two independent mouse models of CDD. We find that early re-activation of endogenous Cdkl5 in heterozygous females reverses most phenotypes, except working memory. Later re-expression improves several traits but has limited effects on cognitive function. Seizure prevention is more effective with early intervention in heterozygous females but becomes limited after seizure onset. These findings demonstrate the robust potential of CDKL5 re-expression to reverse CDD-related phenotypes in both sexes while underscoring the critical impact of age and disease stage in designing clinical trials.

Cyclin-dependent kinase-like 5 (CDKL5) deficiency disorder (CDD) is a developmental encephalopathy caused by mutations in the X-linked gene encoding CDKL5,1,2,3,4 a serine-threonine kinase highly expressed in neurons, especially the forebrain.5,6,7 Early-onset, refractory seizures are a prominent feature of CDD, making it one of the most common genetic epilepsies in children.8 Other commonly observed features include neurodevelopmental delays, hypotonia, intellectual disability, and autism.9,10 The disorder is predominant in females with heterozygous mutations in CDKL5.11,12

Mouse models of CDD have corroborated the genetic causality. The first CDD mouse model13 carries a Cdkl5 exon 6 deletion that mimics a clinically observed splice-site mutation14 and recapitulates key phenotypes of CDD, including learning and memory impairments as well as motor and social deficits.13 Additional genetic models present with similar behaviors, illustrating the reproducibility of disease-associated phenotypes in rodents.15,16,17,18 Spontaneous epileptic EEG discharges were observed in knockout neonatal pups19 but disappeared in adults.13,15,16,17,18 Upon aging, female mice with heterozygous loss of CDKL5, but not their hemizygous male and homozygous female knockout counterparts, exhibit epileptic seizures.20,21

Given that CDKL5 is a protein kinase known to regulate signal transduction pathways,13,22,23,24,25,26 multiple therapeutic attempts to target protein replacement or CDKL5-dependent signaling pathways have been conducted, demonstrating encouraging phenotypic amelioration in CDD mouse models.17,27,28,29,30,31,32,33 The identification of deficits in neural circuit communication via auditory- or visual-evoked event-related potentials, pupillometry or measurement of arousal, has also informed potential biomarkers to facilitate preclinical studies.13,34,35,36,37 Notably, recent advances in genetic medicine, such as gene replacement and gene correction, hold great potential for treating monogenic disorders such as CDD.38,39,40 However, the early onset of CDD raises questions about the potential of disease reversibility or curability. As a proof of concept, we re-expressed endogenous Cdkl5 using a genetic strategy from 6 weeks of age in hemizygous male mice carrying a floxed transcriptional stop cassette that prevents Cdkl5 expression until stop cassette removal by Cre-mediated recombination (named as Cdkl5STOP or STOP). We found that multiple anxiety-related, locomotor, social, and learning and memory deficits were ameliorated,36 suggesting CDD is likely treatable with a broader therapeutic time window than previously anticipated.

However, most CDD patients are females with heterozygous mutations in CDKL5.9,10 The extent of and the time window for effective phenotypic reversal across both sexes remain unknown. Thus, we carried out parallel phenotypic assessment of both hemizygous male and heterozygous female Cdkl5STOP mice at two distinct ages. To assess robustness and reproducibility of the reversal, we also generated a distinct mouse model, Cdkl5FLEX (FLEX), in which Cdkl5 exon 4 is inverted and flanked by two different pairs of loxP sites, rendering exon 4 amenable to Cre-mediated correction. We found that numerous CDD-related phenotypes, including epileptic spasms, are reproducibly mitigated in Cdkl5STOP and Cdkl5FLEX heterozygous female mice upon Cdkl5 re-expression from 6 weeks of age. In contrast, restoring Cdkl5 expression in late adulthood differentially diminishes the extent of rescue across distinct behavioral domains; notably, context- and cue-dependent learning and memory are not improved with late Cdkl5 re-expression. The working memory deficits present in both CDD models are not reversible regardless of the age of rescue or sex. These findings demonstrate robustness and reproducibility in reversing specific CDD-related phenotypes across two genetic models, particularly at younger ages, and establish the existence of a limited time window to improve learning and memory deficits in mouse models of CDD.

To determine the extent to which CDD-related deficits are reversible in heterozygous STOP female mice, a model that better represents the majority of CDD patients, we crossed females heterozygous for Cdkl5STOP with mice carrying UBC-CreER,41 which ubiquitously expresses a cytoplasm-sequestered Cre that will translocate into the nucleus upon binding tamoxifen.42 Following the same tamoxifen delivery protocol,36 we administered tamoxifen to mice of the following genotypes: Cdkl5+/+ without UBC-CreER (“WT”), Cdkl5+/+ with UBC-CreER (a Cre-only control named as “uCreER”), Cdkl5STOP/+ without uCreER (“STOP”), and Cdkl5STOP/+ with uCreER (rescue; “Res”). Given our previous observation that the inducible UBC-CreER can result in recombination in a small proportion of cells without tamoxifen,36 we included an additional control, Cdkl5STOP/+ with uCreER, treated with vehicle (corn oil) instead of tamoxifen (referred to as “Double”) (Figure 1A). Thus, Res and Double mice were compared side by side for every endpoint to specifically assess the extent of phenotype reversal that is the result of tamoxifen-mediated re-expression of CDKL5. We began tamoxifen/corn oil administration at six weeks, an early “post-developmental” age analogous to adolescence.43 We then subjected all treatment groups to the same behavioral assessment routine, beginning with the handling-sensitive elevated zero maze (EZM), followed by open field test (OFT), Y-maze assay, social choice test, rotarod test, olfaction assessment, scoring of hindlimb clasping, and finally fear conditioning test. Upon completion of behavioral assessment, all mice were collected for post hoc genotype verification and measurement of CDKL5 protein expression across different brain regions. CDKL5 protein levels were reduced to ∼50% in heterozygous STOP females, as expected, comparted to WT in cortical tissues. The presence of uCreER did not impact CDKL5 expression. Notably, in the Res group receiving tamoxifen, a nearly WT-level of CDKL5 protein was detected, demonstrating effective re-activation of endogenous Cdkl5 expression. In contrast, Double mice receiving corn oil had a small, but statistically insignificant, increase in CDKL5 protein expression compared to STOP animals, indicating a baseline level of recombination in the absence of tamoxifen in this genotype as previously reported36 (Figures 1C and 1D). Importantly, a reported kinase substrate of CDKL5, microtubule end-binding protein 2 (EB2),23 shows significantly restored phosphorylation at residue Ser222 in Res mice that is comparable to WT and uCreER, in contrast to the reduced phosphorylation levels in STOP and Double animals, further supporting re-expression of functional CDKL5 in this strategy (Figures 1C and 1E). Similar restoration of CDKL5 expression was also observed in other brain regions, including the hippocampus, striatum, cerebellum, and brain stem (Figure S1), though endogenous CDKL5 expression is much lower in the cerebellum and brain stem, consistent with earlier observations.13

Given that behavioral disturbances are commonly observed in CDD patients9,10 and mice lacking CDKL5 show reproducible anxiety-related phenotypes,13,16,36 we first subjected the different experimental groups of mice to EZM assay. Because administering anxiolytic treatment to mice leads to increased time spent in the open arm of EZM, an increase or decrease in the proportion of time spent in the open arm is considered to correlate with reduced or elevated anxiety-related behaviors, respectively.44 We therefore plotted the percentage of time each mouse spent in the open arm, compared to the closed arm, to normalize for different distances traveled by the test mice (Figures S2A and S2B). We found that heterozygous STOP females spent significantly more time in the open arm, compared to WT and uCreER, suggesting a decrease in anxiety-related behaviors. In contrast, the percentage of time is decreased to WT levels in Res mice, and this decrease is also statistically significant when compared to Double mice (Figure S2A). These results demonstrate the amelioration of anxiety-related phenotypes upon Cdkl5 re-expression in heterozygous STOP females.

In OFT, we found that heterozygous STOP females moved significantly more over the course of an hour than WT and uCreER, consistent with locomotor hyperactivity reported in CDD mouse models.13,36,45,46,47 In contrast, the locomotor activity of Res mice was reduced to control levels, demonstrating rescue of this hyperactivity phenotype (Figures 1F and S2C). Significant hyperactivity was also observed in STOP females during the EZM assay but rescued to WT and uCreER levels in Res mice (Figure S2B). Double mice displayed a similar level of locomotor activity as Res mice in both the OFT (Figures 1F and S2C, right panels) and EZM tests (Figure S2B, right panel), suggesting that the recombination mediated by baseline UBC-CreER activity in the absence of tamoxifen could be sufficient to mitigate the hyperactivity phenotype in Double mice.

Most CDD patients show notable autistic features with impaired social interaction,9,10 we next examined social behaviors using the three-chamber social approach test.13,47 We found that all experimental groups spent more time in the social chamber than the nonsocial chamber, regardless of Cdkl5 expression status (Figure S2D). Heterozygous STOP females spent significantly less time sniffing the social chamber and directly interacting with a novel, social stimulus mouse, compared to WT and uCreER. In Res mice, the amount of time spent sniffing the social chamber and directly interacting with a novel mouse increased to control levels, suggesting a reversal of social deficits upon restoration of Cdkl5 expression. Double mice spent significantly less time sniffing the social chamber and directly interacting with the stimulus mouse than Res mice, further supporting that the rescue of sociability in Res mice is due to tamoxifen-mediated re-expression of Cdkl5 (Figures 1G and 1H). Given that social interaction is dependent on olfactory function, we also subjected the same cohort of mice to an olfaction assay. While all experimental mice spent more time sniffing the social scent, consistent with their ability to distinguish social scents from nonsocial almond or water scents, we found that STOP and Double mice spent significantly less time sniffing a social scent compared to WT and uCreER, indicating an impaired response to social stimuli. This impairment was rescued in Res mice (Figure 1I). Therefore, both the social choice and olfaction assays demonstrate reversible social behaviors in heterozygous STOP females, consistent with previous finding in hemizygous males.36

Motor coordination and learning impairments, as well as hindlimb clasping, have previously been observed in CDD mice.13,16,36,45,46,47 We found that heterozygous STOP females recapitulated this phenotype on the rotarod test, which was evidenced by the decreased latency to fall from an accelerating, rotating rod compared to WT and uCreER. In contrast, Res mice show a similar latency to fall as WT and uCreER as well as a longer latency to fall relative to Double mice, suggesting the reversal of motor coordination and motor learning impairments (Figure 1J). Heterozygous STOP females also had significantly higher hindlimb clasping scores than WT and uCreER. Res mice showed a comparable degree of hindlimb clasping to WT and uCreER, and a significantly lower degree of hindlimb clasping relative to Double mice, indicating that the hindlimb clasping phenotype was rescued (Figure 1K). These data suggest that post-developmental restoration of Cdkl5 effectively ameliorates motor deficits in heterozygous STOP females.

Learning and memory deficits are clinically relevant phenotypes that are reproducibly observed across multiple CDD mouse models.13,16,36,45,46,47,48,49 We evaluated contextual- and cue-dependent learning and memory through a fear conditioning assay. All experimental groups showed an increase in percent time freezing after electric shock-mediated fear conditioning, indicating proper sensation of the electric shock, though both STOP and Double mice exhibited relatively blunted response likely related to their hyperactivity in the testing chamber (Figure S2E). Heterozygous STOP females exhibited significant impairments for both context- and cue-dependent memory recall as demonstrated by decreased percent time freezing relative to WT and uCreER. Res mice showed a significantly higher percent time freezing than STOP mice (Figures 1L and 1M). However, Double mice showed a significantly lower percent time freezing during the context- but not cue-dependent fear conditioning assays (Figure 1M, right panel). The lack of significant difference in Res and Double mice during cue-dependent fear conditioning is possibly the result of leaky UBC-CreER activity, suggesting that cue-dependent memory is likely sensitive to the small number of cells expressing CDKL5, similar to the hyperactivity phenotype (Figure 1F).

We previously found that working memory, as assessed by the Y-maze assay, did not show any statistically significant reversal in hemizygous STOP male mice upon rescue of CDKL5 expression from six weeks of age.36 Conditional ablation of Cdkl5 from six weeks of age did not impact working memory either,36 in contrast to working memory deficits observed in multiple CDD models.16,33,47,50 Similarly, heterozygous STOP female mice displayed a lower percentage of spontaneous alterations during the Y-maze assay than WT and uCreER, indicating an impairment in working memory. In contrast, there was no significant difference between STOP and Res mice, nor between Res and Double mice, suggesting that working memory deficits are not reversible in heterozygous STOP females despite the restoration of Cdkl5 expression (Figure S2F), consistent with previous findings in hemizygous STOP males,36 highlighting a potential limitation in reversing CDD-related phenotypes even at an adolescent age.

Given the common reproducibility issues associated with rodent behavioral phenotyping,51 we sought to independently replicate the rescue study using a different genetic model to specifically assess robustness and reproducibility of phenotypic reversal. Therefore, we repeated the aforementioned experiments using a newly generated Cdkl5FLEX mouse model in combination with CAGG-CreER.52 This line has ubiquitous and constitutive expression of CreER without any reported leaky Cre activity in the absence of tamoxifen. Thus, we administered tamoxifen from six weeks of age to mice carrying Cdkl5+/+ without CAGG-CreER (“WT”), Cdkl5+/+ with CAGG-CreER (named as “cCreER”), Cdkl5FLEX/+ without cCreER (“FLEX”), or Cdkl5FLEX/+ with cCreER (“Res”). Corn oil was similarly administered to Cdkl5FLEX/+ with cCreER (Double) as an additional control. We found that the FLEX strategy reduced CDKL5 protein expression in heterozygous FLEX females to ∼50% of WT, as expected, and that Double mice treated with corn oil did not show detectable increase in CDKL5 expression over FLEX, indicating the absence of leakage expression without tamoxifen. In contrast, CDKL5 protein was restored to nearly WT levels upon tamoxifen administration in Res mice (Figures 2C and 2D). The functional re-activation of CDKL5 is further supported by rescued phosphorylation levels of EB2 in Res mice versus heterozygous FLEX females (Figures 2C and 2E). These data support the validity of applying this independent genetic approach to interrogate phenotype rescue.

On the EZM assay, we found that heterozygous FLEX females spent significantly more time in the open arm compared with WT and cCreER, suggesting that heterozygous FLEX females recapitulate the reduced anxiety-related behavior observed in hemizygous male and heterozygous female STOP mice as well as other CDD mouse models.13,16,36 Notably, the Res mice behaved similarly to WT and cCreER controls and spent significantly less time in the open arm than FLEX mice. Res mice also spent significantly less time in the open arm than Double controls, further supporting that tamoxifen-mediated re-expression of endogenous Cdkl5 underlies the rescue of anxiety-related behaviors (Figures S3A and S3B).

During the OFT, we observed that heterozygous FLEX females spent significantly more time moving than WT and cCreER, suggesting that heterozygous FLEX female mice are hyperactive like other CDD mouse models. Res mice spent significantly less time moving during the OFT than FLEX mice, indicating rescued locomotor activity. Res mice also moved significantly less than the Double controls. These results highlight the reproducibility of reversing hyperactivity phenotype upon post-developmental re-expression of CDKL5 (Figures 2F and S3C). This is in contrast to the lack of locomotion difference between Res and Double mice of the STOP line carrying UBC-CreER (Figure 1F), further indicating that hyperactivity is likely sensitive to the number of cells expressing CDKL5.

Heterozygous FLEX female mice also demonstrated social interaction deficits comparable to STOP mice. During the 3-chambered social choice test, FLEX mice spent significantly less time sniffing the social chamber and directly interacting with a novel stimulus mouse than WT and cCreER, consistent with impaired sociability shown in previous studies.13,16,36,45 Res mice spent significantly more time sniffing the social chamber and interacting with the novel mouse than FLEX and Double mice (Figures 2G, 2H, and S3D). During the olfaction test, FLEX and Double mice spent less time sniffing a social scent compared to WT and cCreER, suggesting an impaired response to social stimuli, but this impairment was rescued in Res mice (Figure 2I). Together, these data suggest that social interaction deficits are reproducibly reversed following post-developmental restoration of CDKL5 expression in CDD heterozygous female models.

Subjecting the five genotypes of mice to rotarod test, we found that heterozygous FLEX females showed motor learning and coordination deficits similar to heterozygous STOP females, with FLEX mice showing decreased latency to fall relative to WT and cCreER. Res mice performed similarly to WT and cCreER and had a longer latency to fall compared to FLEX and Double, suggesting that motor learning and coordination deficits are effectively rescued (Figure 2J). Subjecting the same mice to the hindlimb clasping assay, we observed that FLEX mice exhibit higher scores than WT and cCreER, which is consistent with impairment of motor control. This impairment was notably rescued in Res mice, which showed lower scores than FLEX and Double controls (Figure 2K). Together, the rotarod and hindlimb clasping tests demonstrate that motor learning and control deficits can be reproducibly reversed upon re-expression of CDKL5 from six weeks of age.

We next assessed deficits in contextual- and cue-dependent learning and memory with the same fear conditioning strategy. Heterozygous FLEX female mice spent significantly less time freezing relative to WT and cCreER during both contextual- and cue-dependent fear conditioning test, consistent with impaired contextual- and cue-dependent learning and memory in CDD mouse models.13,16,36,45,46,47,48,49 In contrast, Res mice spent more time freezing than FLEX and Double mice, reaching similar levels to WT and cCreER (Figures 2L, 2M, and S3E). These results highlight reproducible rescue of learning and memory in CDD mice, at least at this age of restoring CDKL5 expression. Notably, Res females also showed more freezing time compared to the Double (Figure 2M), in contrast to the lack of difference between Res and Double mice in the STOP line coupled with UBC-CreER (Figure 1M). This finding supports the robust reversal of cue-dependent learning and memory but also indicates that this phenotype is likely sensitive to the number of cells expressing CDKL5.

During the Y-Maze assay, heterozygous FLEX females displayed a lower percentage of spontaneous alterations than WT and cCreER, suggesting impaired working memory. Notably, Res mice performed comparably to FLEX and Double mice, indicating a failure to rescue this impairment in working memory (Figure S3F). The inability to rescue working memory deficits is consistent with previous findings in hemizygous STOP males36 and heterozygous STOP females (Figure S2F). These results highlight that not all behavioral phenotypes are reversible upon post-developmental restoration of Cdkl5 expression.

Upon re-expression of endogenous Cdkl5 from six weeks of age, robust reversal of multiple CDD-related deficits, except working memory, is observed in two models carrying different reversible alleles of Cdkl5 and different CreER drivers. However, six weeks in mice represent a young age, while many CDD patients live beyond adolescence into adulthood.9,10 The extent to which an optimal time window exists for CDD reversal is unknown. To determine how robust and reproducible the reversal of CDD-related deficits is when accounting for temporal factors and accumulated deficits that may influence the efficacy of restoring endogenous Cdkl5 expression, we performed rescue experiments in both Cdkl5STOP and Cdkl5FLEX hemizygous male models by crossing them with UBC-CreER (Figure 3A) and CAGG-CreER (Figure 4A) mice, respectively, but administering tamoxifen at six months of age, a well-accepted adult age in mice. We divided each line into the same experimental groups as described earlier for behavior testing: WT (Cdkl5+/y), uCreER (Cdkl5+/y; UBC-CreER/+) or cCreER (CAGG-CreER/+), STOP (Cdkl5STOP/y) or FLEX (Cdkl5FLEX/y), and Res (Cdkl5STOP/y; UBC-CreER/+ or Cdkl5FLEX/y; CAGG-CreER/+). Again, corn oil was administered in place of tamoxifen to age-matched Double controls (Cdkl5STOP/y; UBC-CreER/+ or Cdkl5FLEX/y; CAGG-CreER/+) to specifically address the effects of tamoxifen-induced re-expression for phenotypic reversal from six months. We found that endogenous CDKL5 expression was completely abolished in both Cdkl5STOP and Cdkl5FLEX male mice in examined cortical tissues, further confirming the validity of these two genetic models. The presence of UBC-CreER and CAGG-CreER did not alter endogenous Cdkl5 expression in the CreER groups of either line. In Double mice carrying both the STOP and UBC-CreER with corn oil administration, a similar degree of leaky CDKL5 expression was detected as the previous study,36 but no detectable leakage was detected in Double mice carrying the FLEX allele and CAGG-CreER (Figures 3C, 3D, 4C, and 4D). Upon tamoxifen administration from six months of age, restoration of endogenous Cdkl5 expression to WT levels is observed in Res mice of both lines in comparison to WT and CreER (Figures 3C, 3D, 4C, and 4D). Similar absence of CDKL5 protein expression in the FLEX mice, but restored to nearly WT levels in Res mice, was observed in other brain regions, such as the hippocampus, striatum, cerebellum and brain stem (Figures S3G–S3N). Furthermore, significant elevation of EB2 phosphorylation was achieved in the Res mice using both genetic strategies, in contrast to hemizygous STOP, FLEX, and double males, indicating the re-expression of functional CDKL5 in both genetic strategies (Figures 3C, 3E, 4C, and 4E).

We subjected the aged male mice to the same paradigm of behavioral tests as described earlier and found that all aged, male mice, regardless of genotype or treatment, showed reduced locomotion speed on the EZM test. Those mice moved around locally but rarely crossed the open and closed arms on the EZM test during the 5-min test. The reduced tendency to actively move around the maze, likely related to the age of those mice, confounds the interpretation of EZM results in this study (Figures S4A, S4B, S5A, and S5B). Thus, the extent to which anxiety-related behaviors would be reversed upon re-activation of Cdkl5 expression from six months of age remains undetermined.

The reduced locomotion speed was mirrored during the OFT. Despite this, both Cdkl5STOP (Figures 3F and S4C) and Cdkl5FLEX (Figures 4F and S5C) hemizygous males traveled a greater total distance than the WT and CreER from their respective cohorts, indicating the persistence of hyperactivity as the result of losing CDKL5. Res mice from both lines traveled significantly less distance than the corresponding STOP or FLEX and Double mice (Figures 3F, S4C, 4F, and S5C). These results demonstrate a robust reversal of hyperactivity in aged adult males similar to that observed upon early restoration of CDKL5 expression36 (Figures 1 and 2).

When we evaluated the reversibility of social interaction deficits using the same three-chambered social approach assay, we found that both Cdkl5STOP (Figures 3G, 3H, and S4D) and Cdkl5FLEX (Figures 4G, 4H, and S5D) aged males spent significantly less time sniffing the social chamber and the novel stimulus mouse than WT and CreER, consistent with impaired sociability. In contrast, Res mice from both lines spent significantly more time sniffing the social chamber and novel mouse than the corresponding STOP or FLEX and Double (Figures 3G, 3G, 4G, and 4H), though the Res mice from Cdkl5STOP line did spend less time directly interacting with the novel social stimulus than WT (Figure 3H). All mice from the Cdkl5STOP (Figure 3I) and Cdkl5FLEX (Figure 4I) lines spent less time sniffing nonsocial scents compared to a social scent, indicating the ability to discriminate social cues, though STOP and FLEX mice showed reduced time sniffing social scents, consistent with impaired sociability. This impairment is notably reversed in Res mice from both lines upon comparison to Double mice (Figures 3I and 4I). Overall, these results suggest that social interaction deficits remain robustly reversible in aged males, though the effectiveness of reversal is slightly attenuated.

Reversal of motor coordination and motor learning deficits on the rotarod test was notably attenuated in late-reversal (six months) Res mice compared to early reversal (six weeks). There was a trend visible in the data for both Cdkl5STOP (Figure 3J) and Cdkl5FLEX (Figure 4J) lines, suggesting that Res mice performed somewhere in between WT and STOP or FLEX mice during the rotarod assay, though increased latency to fall of Res relative to STOP mice did not reach statistical significance in most trials. The increased latency to fall of Res mice relative to FLEX mice did reach statistical significance in several trials; however, it was a noticeably smaller effect than the one observed in younger Res mice (Figures 3J and 4J). A similar pattern is visible when comparing Res with Double mice from the Cdkl5STOP line, which show almost no statistically significant differences (Figure 3J), and comparing Res with Double mice from the Cdkl5FLEX line, which show a few more significantly different trials (Figure 4J). In contrast, the hindlimb clasping phenotype was significantly ameliorated in both Cdkl5STOP (Figure 3K) and Cdkl5FLEX (Figure 4K) lines, with Res mice displaying lower scores than STOP, FLEX, or Double mice. Notably, Res mice from both lines also had significantly higher scores than WT and CreER, suggesting that the amelioration of motor learning and motor control deficits is relatively limited, and show reduced potential of full reversal upon restoration of Cdkl5 expression from six months compared to six weeks of age.

The reversibility of learning and memory deficits was markedly diminished in aged, adult hemizygous male STOP and FLEX mice in both genetic reversal strategies. From the fear conditioning test, we found that STOP (Figures 3L, 3M, and S4E) and FLEX (Figures 4L, 4M, and S5E) hemizygous aged males spent significantly less time freezing in both contextual- and cue-dependent fear conditioning tests compared to WT and CreER littermates, recapitulating the impairments in learning and memory observed in younger mice.31 Unlike the rescue studies in younger males and females, however, neither contextual- nor cue-dependent learning and memory deficits were reversed in aged hemizygous male animals of the Cdkl5STOP (Figures 3L and 3M) or Cdkl5FLEX (Figures 4L and 4M) lines. This is illustrated by the absence of a statistically significant difference between Res mice and their STOP, FLEX, and Double littermates and suggests a significant limitation in reverse learning and memory deficits when re-expressing CDKL5 at a later age of six months, at least in hemizygous males.

CDD is characterized by infantile-onset, refractory seizures.9,10 Germline loss of Cdkl5 does not result in behavioral seizures in adult mice13,15,16,17,18 except epileptic electroencephalograms (EEGs) and spasms in neonatal pups19 and altered seizure susceptibility in adults.15,21,28,31,47 However, female mice with heterozygous loss of CDKL5 develop myoclonic and tonic-clonic seizures upon aging.20,21 The seizures had a median onset of 28 weeks of age and tended to increase in frequency and severity with age. This age-dependent manifestation of spontaneous seizures excludes behavioral tests to be conducted in older heterozygous females, as seizure occurrence confounds behavioral data interpretation. The seizure phenotype, on the other hand, provides us an opportunity to measure the reversibility of a CDD-relevant epileptic phenotype, despite that those age-dependent seizures in mice are different from the clinical presentation of CDD.

Both heterozygous STOP and FLEX females develop spontaneous behavioral seizures with aging, similar to heterozygous knockout and R59X females upon weekly observations. The behavioral seizure presentation in FLEX females was further documented by EEG recordings with electrographic signatures of epileptic spasms shown in the representative traces, compared to WT (Figures 5A–5D). We first monitored behavioral seizures on a weekly basis as we did before,21 but this approach is limited by the nature of random observations and seizures unlikely to occur constantly throughout the day. To quantitatively evaluate changes in the epileptic phenotype in a continuous and quantitative manner, we conducted video/EEG recording in awake, freely moving animals for 72 h when they reach ∼10 months of age.

Quantification of epileptic spasm rate for the early and late rescue cohorts showed that epileptic spasms were essentially absent in WT mice but observed in FLEX heterozygous females at a frequency similar to that reported previously.20,21 Res mice also displayed spasms, but a significantly smaller number of spasms per day were documented in the early rescue group, compared to FLEX females (Figure 5E). In contrast, the statistically significant decrease in the number of spasms was not present in the late rescue condition (Figure 5F). This suggests that epileptic spasms are largely preventable upon early post-developmental restoration of CDKL5 expression and that the efficacy of the prevention or reversal decreases significantly upon re-expressing CDKL5 at a later age of six months. Notably, the number of observed spasms varies largely among individual female mice, consistent with our previous observations.20,21 This is likely related to the random X chromosome inactivation in heterozygous females, leading to mosaic expression of CDKL5 at the cellular levels.

Next, we measured the frequency content of the baseline EEG using fast Fourier transform analysis. Changes in spectral power within a given frequency range are thought to represent altered neural network functioning and potential non-invasive, objective biomarkers for disease progression.53,54,55 We found that FLEX heterozygous female mice showed a statistically significant decrease in relative power in the delta frequency (0.1–4.0 Hz) in both the hippocampus and cortex and across both rescue cohorts (Figures 5G–5J). Notably, upon rescuing CDKL5 expression from six weeks, but not six months of age, a statistically significant elevation of the power in delta frequency band is achieved in both the hippocampus and cortex (Figures 5G–5J). The decrease in the delta frequency appears to be associated with increased fast activity in the alpha (8–13 Hz) and gamma (25–50 Hz) bands in both the brain areas, though a statistically significant rescuing effect at either frequency band is not detected between the Res and FLEX mice (Figures 5G–5J). Together, these data demonstrate that abnormalities in neural network communication are largely mitigated upon early post-developmental restoration of CDKL5, but the effectiveness of amelioration decreases with age in heterozygous FLEX females, which is consistent with limited behavioral phenotype reversibility in aged hemizygous male models (Figures 3 and 4).

CDD is characterized as a neurodevelopmental disorder with early onset of epileptic seizures, intellectual disability, impaired social functioning, and deficits in motor control.9,10 The developmental nature of the disorder has raised questions about disease curability and treatment potential.56 Notably, a recent study found that multiple CDD-related phenotypes are reversible in hemizygous male mice when endogenous Cdkl5 expression is restored from six weeks of age.36 While the reversal feasibility in male mice at an early post-developmental stage is encouraging, several imperative questions remain: (1) the reversibility in heterozygous female models, given that the majority of CDD patients are heterozygous for CDKL5 mutations; (2) the reversal potential at an older age as many CDD patients have grown beyond adolescence; and (3) the robustness and reproducibility of the reported reversal phenotypes as independent validation has yet to be conducted. In this study, we systematically addressed these questions using two different genetically modified mouse models.

We found that numerous CDD-related behavioral deficits are reversible in heterozygous females upon post-developmental restoration of CDKL5 using the same genetic strategy as the prior study in males.36 Importantly, these findings are replicated using two independent genetic alleles, Cdkl5STOP and Cdkl5FLEX, in combination with two different Cre drivers, UBC-CreER and CAGG-CreER, respectively. We have also observed a reduction in the frequency of epileptic spasms and normalization of EEG power spectra following early post-developmental rescue of CDKL5 expression, highlighting the potential to ameliorate CDD deficits within the early post-developmental time window.

One unexpected finding from our previous reversal study is the “leaky” Cre activity even without tamoxifen.36 We specifically included corn oil treatment control (Double) in this study but found that Double mice carrying Cdkl5STOP/+;UBC-CreER performed similarly to Res mice in OFT (Figure 1F) and cue-dependent fear conditioning test (Figure 1M). Thus, the additional cells expressing CDKL5 due to leaky UBC-CreER, on top of the ∼50% of cells expressing WT Cdkl5 in heterozygous females, appear to be sufficient to mitigate hyperactivity and cue-memory deficits observed in STOP females. Alternatively, locomotor activity and cue memory may be more amenable to rescue in general. This highlights a practical concern in future studies seeking to evaluate the effectiveness of novel therapies because different behavioral assays can be more or less sensitive to a given treatment. Therefore, a diverse and complementary panel of tests assessing different behavioral domains is recommended when deciding on the outcome measures for preclinical and clinical trials.

While most of the CDD-related deficits measured in this study are reversible, working memory is not. This is consistent with previous findings,36 implying that CDKL5 does play an indispensable role during brain development, at least prior to 6 weeks of age in mice. From the clinical perspective, this raises the possibility that certain CDD-related deficits in patients could require supplemental treatments in addition to genetic medicine approaches for expressing functional CDKL5. Indeed, a recent study demonstrated that stimulating mGluR5 activity using specific positive-allosteric-modulators led to significant improvement in synaptic and behavioral phenotypes, particularly working memory, in CDD mice.33 Thus, a combinational approach coupling pharmacological treatment with genetic medicine may have much improved therapeutic efficacy.

Upon comparing CDD-related phenotype reversal at different ages, we find that the ability of CDKL5 restoration to reverse CDD-related deficits decreases with age. Several phenotypes, such as impairments in motor learning and context- and cue-dependent learning and memory, cannot be effectively ameliorated after aging. Late post-developmental rescue of Cdkl5 was unable to mitigate the epileptic phenotype in heterozygous FLEX females either. The diminishing efficacy of phenotypic reversal upon aging, the different behavioral domains amenable to reversal, and the requirement of CDKL5 throughout adulthood together highlight that the age of patients to be recruited, the type of outcome measures, and the duration of the trial are all critical matters in CDD clinical trial designs.

Of note, the age-dependent onset of behavioral seizures in heterozygous STOP and FLEX female models is a confounding factor that precludes the assessment of behavioral phenotype reversal in females at an older age. Given that heterozygous females naturally express WT Cdkl5 in ∼50% of the cells, it remains possible that phenotypic reversal in aged heterozygous females would be more effective than their male counterparts.57 The natural expression of WT Cdkl5 in ∼50% of the cells also presents a challenge to treating CDD using gene therapy-related approaches, as WT-expressing cells will have high levels of CDKL5 expression upon gene delivery than its physiological levels. The extent to which overexpression of CDKL5 in WT cells may elicit detrimental effect in vivo remains unknown.

In summary, here we report that several CDD-related phenotypes, including hyperactivity, hindlimb clasping, and reduced sociability, are reproducibly reversed in two genetically modified mouse models across age and sex. Motor coordination also shows improvement, but significant limitations to ameliorating context- and cue-dependent learning and memory deficits are observed in late-reversal male mice. Impairments in working memory are not mitigated regardless of age and sex. Age-dependent seizure occurrence in females appears to be preventable upon early re-expression of CDKL5 but becomes limited if expressed after seizure onset. Thus, robust phenotypic improvement is achievable across multiple CDD-related functional domains, but notable limitations concerning cognitive function must be recognized. These findings outline the scope and limits of reversing CDD and point to age and sex as crucial factors to consider when designing CDD clinical trials.

This study employs genetic strategies to re-activate endogenous Cdkl5 expression in mice at its physiological levels. This genetic approach has no clinical feasibility. While the observed behavioral benefit is robust and reproducible, particularly upon reversing Cdkl5 expression at a young age, neuroanatomical correlates are not examined in this study and findings are restricted in mouse models. Furthermore, how CDKL5 dysfunction impairs working memory during development and the apparent discrepancy between human CDD and rodent models in seizure manifestation remain unaddressed.

The Cdkl5STOP mouse model was generated as previously described,36 and both hemizygous male and heterozygous female carriers were generated by crossing Cdkl5STOP heterozygous females to WT males obtained from the Jackson laboratory (C57BL/6J, stock no. 000664) to maintain the STOP colony, or UBC-CreER (stock no. 007001) or CAGG-CreER (stock no. 004682) obtained from the Jackson Laboratory to produce experimental cohorts. All lines were maintained on the C57BL/6J background.

The Cdkl5FLEX line was generated using CRSIPR mediated genome editing. Two gRNAs, (TGCAACTACAGCATTAGCTCTGG) and (TTTCACAGTAGGTTACCCAGGGG), targeted against the up-stream and down-stream region of Cdkl5 exon 4, were selected after screening 22 candidates for targeting efficacy and specificity. The template for homology-directed DNA repair was constituted by an inverted exon 4 flanked by two different pairs of lox sites (loxP and lox511) in opposite orientation, and inclusion of 880 bp of DNA as the 5′ homology arm and 1800 bp of DNA as the 3′ homology arm. Purified and linearized template DNA was co-injected with gRNA and Cas9 mRNA into a C57BL/6 background single-cell zygote to enable homology-directed repair, followed by implantation. To screen for positively targeted pups, PCR was performed using primers specific to the 5′ and 3′ integration sites on either side of the gRNA sequence. Southern blot was then used to verify the correct targeting in the F1 generation, after which an FLEX colony was established on the C57BL/6J genetic background.

Experimental cohorts of mice of all genotypes were weaned around 3–4 weeks of age with littermates being housed together. Mice were genotyped using a PCR-based strategy (Transnetyx Inc.).

Tamoxifen was prepared and administered as described previously.36 To make fresh stocks, tamoxifen was dissolved in corn oil at a concentration of 20 mg/mL by vortexing and slowly heating the solution at 40°C for 1 h. The stocks of tamoxifen were wrapped in aluminum foil to protect them from light and then stored at 4°C for a maximum of 1 week. Animal feeding needles (Roboz Surgical Store, 20 gauge, 1.5 inch length, curved; FN-7910) were used for oral gavage delivery of 0.3 mg/kg of tamoxifen once a week for a total of 3 doses and then 2 subsequent deliveries every other day after the third dose (total of 5 doses). See schematics in Figures 1A, 2A, 3A, and 4A for details.

All behavioral studies were conducted while blinded to genotype and similar to behavioral assessment described in previous studies.13,17,36,50,58,59,60 Mice were habituated to the testing room for at least 1 h before testing, excluding the context- and cue-dependent fear conditioning assays. Testing was always performed at the same time of day. The order of the tests was the same for each animal as previously described, beginning with the elevated zero maze, then open field test, Y-maze, 3-chambered social choice assay, accelerating rotarod assay, olfaction assay, hindlimb clasping scoring, and finally context- and cue-dependent fear conditioning. Behavioral data was analyzed by researchers blinded to genotype. Based on the phenotype differences expressed between WT and Cdkl5 hemizygous knockout males, as well as WT and Cdkl5 heterozygous knockout females, a power calculation was conducted to determine the number of mice required in each genotype comparison to detect statistical significance, leading to the examined cohort size of a minimum of 10 or 20 for hemizygous males or heterozygous females, respectively.

The elevated zeromaze (San Diego Instruments; California, USA) consists of a circular-shaped platform elevated 3 feet above the floor. Two opposite quadrants of the maze are enclosed (wall height, 12 inches), whereas the other two are open (wall height, 0.5 inches). Mice were placed in one of the closed quadrants and their movement traced over the course of 5 min. Analysis, including the quantification of percent of time spent in open arms and the number of entries, was performed manually using a stopwatch. An entry was defined as a transition from a closed to open arm, or vice versa, that involves all four paws. Total distance traveled over the entire assay was measured by a ceiling-mounted camera coupled to a video-tracking software (SmartScan 3.0), allowing for real-time analysis of all movements.

Locomotor activity was measured via an open-field test where mice were individually placed into, and allowed to explore, a 15″ x 15″ arena for a total of 30 min. A ceiling-mounted camera allowed for a video-tracking software (SmartScan 3.0) to real-time analyze the total distance traveled as well as the percent time spent in the center of the arena (defined as the central 25% of the total area).

Spontaneous alternation behavior was measured on a Y-maze apparatus (San Diego Instruments; California, USA), composed of three arms (Arm A: 8 in. × 5 in. x 3in.; Arms B and C: 6 in. × 5 in. x 3in.). For testing, the mouse was placed in Arm C, facing the center, and allowed to freely explore the maze for 5 min. A spontaneous alternation was defined an entry into the arm less recently explored. Percent spontaneous alternation was calculated as the number of spontaneous alternations over the total number of entries. For example, the sequence C,B,A,B,C,B,A,C (starting in arm C) resulted in a percent spontaneous alternation of 4/6 = 67%.

The social choice test was carried out in a three-chambered apparatus that consisted of a center chamber and two end chambers. Before the start of the test and in a counter-balanced sequence, one end chamber was designated the social chamber, into which a stimulus mouse would be introduced, and the other end chamber was designed the nonsocial chamber. Two identical, clear Plexiglas cylinders with multiple holes to allow for air exchange were placed in each end chamber. In the habituation phase of the test (Phase I), the test mouse was placed in the center chamber and allowed to explore all three chambers for 10 min. During this acclimation period, baseline measurements of how much time the mouse spent in each of the three chambers and the distance traveled by the test mouse were collected. In the social choice phase of the test (Phase II), an age-matched stimulus mouse (adult, gonadectomized A/J mice) was placed in the cylinder in the social chamber while a novel object was simultaneously placed into the other cylinder in the nonsocial chamber. During the subsequent 10 min social choice period, chamber times and numbers of transitions among chambers were again recorded as well as the percent time spent sniffing the social cylinder. In the direct social interaction test, the cylinders were removed simultaneously following the social choice test, and the amount of time test and stimulus mice spent in direct contact (sniffing, allogrooming) was measured for a total of 5 min. If fighting persisted for more than several seconds, the mice were removed from the apparatus and excluded from the study.

Mice were placed on an accelerating rotarod apparatus (Harvard Apparatus) for 16 trials (four trials a day for four consecutive days) with at least 15 min of rest between the trials. Each trial lasted for a maximum of 5 min, during which the rod accelerated linearly from 4 to 40 rpm. The amount of time for each mouse to fall from the rod was recorded for each trial.

For the training day, mice were placed in individual chambers (Med Associates) for 2 min followed by a loud tone (85 dB, 2 kHz) lasting 30 s that coterminated with a 2-s, 1.25-mA foot shock. Mice were left undisturbed for an additional 30 s in the chamber and then immediately placed back into their home cage. Freezing behavior, defined as no movement except for respiration, was determined before and after the tone-shock pairings and scored by FreezeScan NI version 2.00. To test for context-dependent learning, mice were placed back into the same testing boxes 24 h later for a total of 5 min without any tone or shock, and again measured the total time spent freezing. 4 h later, the same cohorts of mice were tested for cue-dependent fear memory by placing them into a novel chamber consisting of altered flooring, wall-panel inserts, and vanilla scent. After 2 min in the chamber, the cue tone (85 dB, 2 kHz) was played for a total of 3 min, and the total time spent freezing during the presentation of this cue tone was recorded.

Mice were tested for whether they could detect and differentiate odors in a habituation-dishabituation protocol modified from a previous study. Mice were presented with cotton-tipped wooden applicators dipped in either water, vanilla, or swiped across the bottom of an unfamiliar social cage. Each stimulus was presented for 2 min with a 1-min inter-trial interval. Time spent sniffing was defined as when the animal was oriented with its nose 2 cm or closer toward the cotton tip.

Mice were suspended by the base of their tail at least 6 inches above a flat surface for up to 1 min. If the hindlimbs were consistently splayed outward, away from the abdomen, the mouse was assigned a score of 0. If one hindlimb was retracted toward the abdomen for more than 50% of the time, the score was 1. If both hindlimbs were partially retracted for greater than 50% of the time, the score was 2. Finally, if both hindlimbs were entirely retracted and touching the abdomen for more than 50% of the time suspended, the mouse received a score of 3.

Mice were stereotaxically implanted with an electrode assembly under continuous isoflurane anesthesia similar to what was described previously.21 The electrode assembly consisted of 6 leads: 2 surface electrodes attached to miniature skull screws placed over the left and right frontal cortices (from bregma: A/P + 1.0 mm, M/L ± 1.5 mm); 2 depth electrodes in left and right hippocampus (A/P - 2.2 mm, M/L ± 2.0 mm, D/V - 1.3 mm); and a reference and a ground electrode also attached to miniature skull screws directly behind Lambda on either side of midline (from bregma: A/P - 5.2 mm, M/L ± 1.5 mm). Silver wires (0.13 mm diameter) were attached to each electrode and connected to an 8-pin head mount (Pinnacle Technology Inc., Lawrence, KS). The entire assembly was secured on the skull with dental cement (Ortho-Jet, Lang Dental, Wheeling, IL). Mice were given at least 72 h post-surgery to recover prior to being placed in a Plexiglas recording cage (Pinnacle). EEG waveform was amplified by a preamplifier with a gain of 10 μV (Pinnacle) at the head of the animal before being passed through a low-torque commutator to the Sirenia Acquisition system (Pinnacle) for final-stage amplification and filtering. EEG was sampled at 2000 Hz in a 12-h light/dark cycle with food and water provided ad libitum.

Following EEG implantation, video-EEG recording was performed on freely moving mice as described previously.21 Each mouse was recorded for at least 3 days. Video was watched by a reviewer familiar with the appearance of the seizures. EEG traces were processed with the application of a 1 Hz high-pass filter, 500 Hz low-pass filter, and 60 Hz notch filter and visualized within Serenia Basic (Pinnacle). All analysis was completed in MATLAB with software adapted from a recent study.53 Steps of analysis included the following.(1)Detection of poor-quality recording channels. Each recording channel for each recording period was analyzed using an automated artifact detector. Detection of a bad or noisy recording channel was completed by calculating the average of the root mean squared amplitude and skew of the voltage for each second of the recording. Channels were excluded from further analysis if they had a root mean squared amplitude less than 30 μV or more than 200 μV or a skew greater than 0.4.(2)Spike detection and quantification. Spikes were detected using an algorithm designed to screen for voltage deflections that were greater than 5 standard deviations above the mean recorded signal, with a maximum allotted half-width of 5–200 ms. Spikes were eliminated if they occurred within 10 s of a deflection found to be an artifact (i.e., a deflection with a half-width outside the 5- to 200-ms range). The first and last 5 min of each recording were removed automatically from spike calculations due to high probability of artifacts and noise.(3)Power spectra analysis. In order to analyze mutation-induced differences in background EEG frequency composition, recordings were divided into 5-s epochs for analysis. Epochs were collected during immobility of the animal and those containing large amplitude artifacts, likely because of movement (defined as a peak Z score of the root mean squared voltage amplitude greater than 3), were excluded from frequency analysis. Fast Fourier transform analysis was then completed on artifact-free epochs. Quantification of the power in each of the major EEG frequency bands (delta 0.1–4.0 Hz, theta 4–8 Hz, alpha 8–13 Hz, beta 13–25 Hz, and gamma 25–50 Hz) was completed. Power in each band was normalized to total power. A weighted average for the power in each band across all epochs was obtained.

Brains were removed and tissues from different brain regions, such as the cortex, hippocampus, striatum, cerebellum and brain stem, were dissected and later homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer: 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 25 mM Tris (pH7.4) with protease inhibitors (cOmplete EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Tablets). To extract protein, homogenized lysates were incubated on ice for 15 min and then centrifuged at 21,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant fraction was removed and sonicated using a Biorupter (Diagenode) for 3 alternating sessions at a maximum frequency for 15 s, each followed by a 60-s cooldown period. The lysate was then centrifuged at 21,000 g for 15 min at 4°C and the top aqueous layer carefully removed and collected as the total protein fraction.

Protein concentration was measured using a Bradford assay and Western blots were conducted as previously described.36 Briefly, protein lysates were prepared for gel electrophoresis by adding 4X LDS Sample Buffer to a final concentration of 1X and β-mercaptoethanol to a final concentration of 5%. Samples were heat-denatured at 75°C for 20 min, and 7.5μg or 25 μg of protein was loaded into each well of a 4–12% Bis-Tris gradient gel. Protein gels were run for 2 h at 125V at room temperature on a XCell SureLock mini-cell electrophoresis box using a PowerPac HC High-Current Power Supply, then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (0.45μm pore-size) at 27V for 1h and 10 min at room temperature. The resulting membrane was blocked with a 1:1 solution of Odyssey blocking buffer and 1X PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-CDKL5 (diluted 1:1000), mouse anti-β-Actin (diluted 1:10,000), rat anti-EB2[K52] (diluted 1:10,000), and rabbit anti-phospho-EB2[S222] (diluted 1:1000). Secondary antibodies (LI-COR) used were goat anti-rabbit IgG IRDye800CW, rabbit anti-mouse IgG IRDye680LT, and goat anti-rat IRDye680LT, all incubated for 50 min at room temperature at dilutions of 1:10,000. Standard protocols were used for the Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR) for protein visualization and quantification.

All graphs are plotted using Prism (GraphPad). Bolded center lines reflect the mean, and all error bars indicate s.e.m. For boxplots, the limits indicate the minimum and maximum with boxplot center line indicating the median. In all figures, p-values between 0.05 and 0.1 are shown explicitly, ∗ is used to denote all 0.01 < p < 0.05, ∗∗ for 0.001 < p < 0.01, ∗∗∗ for 0.0001 < p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ for p < 0.0001. Specific statistical methods are included in each figure legend except the one for Western blot comparison was analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons correction.
