# Magnetic resonance imaging features and clinical course of malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumors: single institution experience over two decades.
**Authors:** Kwok H, Hameed M, Hwang S
**Journal:** Skeletal Radiol (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00256-022-04171-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00256-022-04171-w)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To evaluate MR features and clinical course of malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumor (MMNST), previously known as melanotic schwannoma and considered indolent and rarely metastasizing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS: This IRB-approved retrospective study searched 31 patients (20 male: 11 female, mean age 48; range 15-76) with histologically confirmed MMNST in a single tertiary cancer center over 22 years. Pre-treatment MR was available in 12 patients and evaluated by two radiologists in consensus regarding lesion location, size, morphology, signal characteristics, contrast enhancement, local invasion, and presence of classic signs of peripheral nerve sheath tumors. Clinical outcomes, including local recurrence, metastasis, and survival, were examined in 12 patients for whom follow-up was available.

RESULTS: The spine was the most frequent site (13/31) among all identified cases. In 12 cases with MR, lesions were well-circumscribed in 11/12 cases, with a mean size of 4.5 cm (2.3-13.0 cm). Ten of 12 cases showed T1 hyperintensity. In 5/9 spinal MRI, tumor involved multiple levels. All lesions showed contrast enhancement, and local bone invasion in > 50%. A dumb-bell shape was common to all spinal lesions. Classical signs of nerve sheath tumors were uncommon. Among 12 patients with a mean follow-up of 4.8 years (range 1.3-10.2 years), six were disease-free, while two had recurrence or metastases, and four had died of metastases.

CONCLUSION: MMNST usually presents as a T1 hyperintense enhancing dumb-bell shaped mass in the spine. Multi-level involvement and bone invasion are common. MMNST is clinically aggressive with high rates of metastases and death.

Malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumor is composed of melanin-producing Schwann cells. It is a rare entity accounting for less than 1% of all nerve sheath tumors [ 1 ]. Previously considered indolent and rarely metastasizing [ 2 ], it is now established that MMNST behaves aggressively with recurrence rate and metastatic risk of 26–44% [ 3– 5 ]. In recognition of this biologic behavior, it has recently been renamed malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumor from its previous name melanotic schwannoma in the latest update of the World Health Organization classification [ 6 ].

Imaging plays an important role in the management of MMNST. First, the clinical presentation of MMNST is non-specific and may include pain, sensory abnormalities or mass effect depending on the site affected [ 1 ]. Imaging is often the mode by which it is first detected and characterized and by which the possible diagnosis of MMNST is first suggested. Second, imaging offers prognostic information from initial staging to post-treatment follow up. The latter is relevant because histopathological features of MMNST are not predictive of outcome, and long-term follow up is required to determine the metastatic potential of a lesion [ 4 ]. Thus, it is important for radiologists to be familiar with the imaging features of MMNST. Yet, in the current literature, there is scant information beyond case reports or small case series due to its rarity and its previously under-recognized malignant potential.

In this study, we aim to review the MR imaging features and clinical course in patients with MMNST over the last two decades at our tertiary cancer center and correlate our findings with those in the current literature on MMNST.

This retrospective study was compliant with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and had been approved by our Institutional Review Board, which waived the requirement for written informed consent. We searched our institutional database for pathology reports between January 2000 and January 2022 where diagnosis of MMNST was histologically confirmed. For each identified case, we obtained information pertaining to patient demographics, tumor location, treatment and clinical outcomes including local recurrence, metastasis and death, as well as Carney’s complex, a genetic condition often found in patients with MMNST from electronic records. We also searched our Picture Archiving and Communication System for pre-treatment imaging studies at the initial diagnosis and evaluated the imaging features where MR had been performed.

Two readers reviewed MR studies in consensus: a radiologist with 17 year-experience in musculoskeletal oncologic imaging (SH) and a radiologist 7-year post-residency training (HK). The primary tumors were assessed for morphology including lesion size, shape and margins. The lesions were evaluated for their local extent including involvement of adjacent neurovascular and osseous structures. When bone involvement was present, this was categorized as either without or with medullary space invasion on MR, with assessment supplemented by companion CT if available.

The MR signal intensities of the lesions on T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w) and post gadolinium T1w sequences were compared with those of skeletal muscle. For each lesion, the proportion of T1 and T2 hyperintense components was estimated as none, less than one-third, between one-third and two-thirds or more than two-thirds of the entire lesion. Lesions were also graded as either predominantly isointense, predominantly hyperintense, or predominantly hypointense to muscle. In equivocal cases, we measured signal intensities of the dominant part of the lesions and adjacent muscles using region of interest technique. Lesions were considered hyperintense or hypointense to muscle if their signal intensities differ from those of muscles by more than 20%. The degree of enhancement was assessed on subtraction imaging whenever possible. Based on lesion MR signal characteristics, we assessed the lesions for their compositions, such as the presence of fat, cystic component, hemorrhage, or melanin. We also searched specifically for “classical signs” of nerve sheath tumors, including split fat sign (fine rind of fat around the lesion), fascicular sign (small ring-like structures on T2w imaging), and target sign (T2 hyperintense rim around the central area) [ 7 ].

Between January 2000 and January 2022, the search for diagnosis of MMNST in the pathology database yielded 31 unique patients. The mean age at diagnosis was 48 years (range 15–76 years). Twenty patients were male, and 11 were female (1.8:1 male-female ratio). The spine was the most common location with 13 cases (42%), in the lumbar or sacral segments (n=7), followed by thoracic (n=4) and cervical segments (n=2). Non-spinal sites included skull base, head or neck region (n=4), paraspinal space (n=2), retroperitoneum (n=3), viscera (n=5), and skin, subcutaneous tissues and muscles of the torso and extremities (n=4). Grossly, the tumors often appeared as circumscribed or partially encapsulated masses with heavy pigmentation due to their melanin content (Fig. 1). Psammoma bodies, the presence of which has been linked to the inherited multi-neoplastic syndrome of Carney complex [ 1 ], were described in 5 cases. However, the diagnosis of the Carney complex was uncertain in the remaining patients due to a lack of documented clinical or pathologic findings.

Pre-treatment MR studies were available in 12 of the 31 cases, 9 spinal and 3 non-spinal lesions, and pre-treatment MRI was not available in 19 cases because they were clinical or pathologic consultation cases for second opinions. The lesions in the pre-treatment MRI range in size from 2.3 cm to 13 cm, with a mean size of 4.5 cm. The imaging findings are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Regarding spinal lesions, one was in the cervical spine, 2 were in the thoracic spine, and 6 were in the lumbar or lumbosacral spine. Four lesions (44.4%) were confined to a single vertebral level, whereas 2 lesions (22.2%) involved 2 levels and 3 lesions (33.3%) involved 3 levels. The mean size of the spinal lesions was 4.9 cm (range: 2.3 cm to 13 cm). They were predominantly circumscribed (8 out of 9) and all were intradural extramedullary masses with a “dumb-bell” configuration characterized by an intraspinal and an extraspinal component connected by a narrower “waist” traversing a neural foramen (Fig. 2). Involvement of nerve roots, and adjacent bones were present in all cases. In 4 lesions (44.4%), there was bone remodeling and or erosion at the neural foramina without medullary invasion, whereas medullary invasion was observed in 5 cases (55.6%) (Fig. 3). With respect to the non-spinal lesions, two lesions were in the extremities and 1 was in the paraspinal space, all appeared as circumscribed lobulated masses. One of these demonstrated bone invasion and another had local neurovascular encasement.

On T1w imaging, 10 (83%) lesions were hyperintense to muscle, with T1 hyperintense elements making up more than two-thirds of the lesions in 7 cases and between one-third and two-thirds of the lesions in 3 cases (Fig. 2). Two (17%) lesions were isointense to muscle without significant T1 hyperintense components. On T2w imaging with or without fat-suppression, 10 (83%) lesions were predominantly hyperintense to muscle, with T2 hyperintense elements making up more than two-thirds of the lesions in 9 cases and between one-third to two-thirds of the lesion in 1 case (Fig. 2). Two lesions were predominantly hypointense on T2 with no or little (less than one-third) T2 hyperintense elements. Of the 10 cases where gadolinium contrast had been administered, all showed enhancement, with 9 (90%) lesions showing enhancement in more than two-thirds of the tumors. The degree of enhancement however was difficult to gauge in many instances due to lesion intrinsic T1 hyperintensity and lack of subtraction imaging.

The cystic change was present in 3 (25%) lesions, and no fat signal was identified in any lesion. As mentioned, T1 hyperintensity was present in most (83%) of the lesions, but we could not reliably differentiate whether this was due to hemorrhage, melanin or both. Calcification was poorly evaluated on MR, but in 4 cases where companion CT was also available, no calcification was identified in the lesions.

Fascicular sign, which describes the presence of multiple ring-like T2 hypointense structures presumably representing longitudinal nerve bundles, was present in only 2 (17%) lesions (Fig. 4). Instead, we observed more confluent bands of T2 hypointensity within half the tumors in this series (Fig. 2), a feature not previously described with nerve sheath tumors. In some instances, we observed collagenous bands adjacent to tumor cells with melanin on histology, potentially accounting for this appearance (Fig 2). However, we were unable to confirm this by correlating imaging and histology side-by-side due to the retrospective nature of our study. Split fat sign, another classic sign of peripheral nerve sheath tumor, was present in only one lesion. The target sign was not identified in any of the lesions.

Treatment and follow up data were available in the medical records for 12 of the 31 patients, 7 of whom had pre-treatment MRI and 5 did not. The remaining 19 cases did not have follow-up data because they were presented to our institution for a second opinion and continued their care elsewhere. All 12 patients had a primary surgical resection, with a mean follow-up duration of 4.8 years (range: 1.3–10.2 years). Eight patients were alive at the time of this study: six were disease free, while 1 had local recurrence, and 1 had lung metastases. Four patients (33%) had died of metastases. Eight patients (67%) received radiotherapy or proton therapy, 5 of whom also received chemotherapy or immunotherapy.

Our study has reaffirmed the rarity of MMNST, with only 31 pathologically confirmed cases identified in our oncologic institution over more than two decades. Amongst the 12 for whom follow-up data were available, half of the patients developed local recurrence or metastases, and a third had deceased after a mean follow-up period of 4.8 years. This observed aggressive behavior was in line with the literature, which reported a local recurrence rate and metastatic risk of 26–44% and a 5-year disease-free-survival rate of 53% [ 3– 5 ]. This malignant potential of MMNST is now recognized and has prompted its recent re-classification as a malignant entity in the latest World Health Organization classification [ 6 ].

In our cohort, 13 of 31 cases (42%) with the diagnosis of MMNST in pathology database were of spinal location. Amongst 12 cases with pre-treatment MRI, a spinal location was in 9/12 (75%) with the lumbosacral spine being most frequent (67%). Like other spinal nerve sheath tumors, they were predominantly well circumscribed intradural extramedullary masses with a dumb-bell shape. However, unlike non-melanotic spinal schwannoma (Fig. 5), they were hyperintense on T1, and over half manifested more aggressive features such as bone destruction and multi-level involvement consistent with their malignant biology. Locally aggressive features were similarly observed in a few of the non-spinal MMNST.

Intrinsic T1 hyperintensity was a distinguishing feature of many MMNSTs in this series, presumably due to their melanin content. In general, fat and hemorrhage are the other common causes of T1 hyperintensity in soft tissue tumors. Therefore, when faced with a T1 hyperintense soft tissue tumor, the initial differential diagnosis should include fat-containing, hemorrhagic and melanotic entities. While the detection of fat seldom poses a challenge to radiologists, in our experience differentiating between hemorrhage and melanin can be difficult. This is because they both can be T1 hyperintense, T2 hypointense, and T2* hypointense, drawing parallels from studies of melanoma brain metastases [ 8 , 9 ]. While T2* intensity may become a discriminator as it is affected less by melanin than by hemorrhage [ 9 ], T2* was unavailable for our cases and is not routinely included in our imaging protocol. In its absence, we could consider that a diffuse distribution of T1 hyperintense and enhancing components, as observed in our series, would intuitively favor melanin over hemorrhage. In the specific scenario of a spinal mass, a dumb-bell shape in conjunction with diffuse T1 hyperintensity should raise suspicion for an MMNST because this combination of features is not typical for the other T1 hyperintense entities. For instance, while both myxopapillary ependymoma (Fig. 6) and spinal paraganglioma (Fig. 7) may have T1 hyperintense elements from hemorrhage, they can be distinguished from MMNST by the more focal appearance of their T1 hyperintensity and their lack of dumb-bell morphology. These entities also include other classical features such as conus location for myxopapillary ependymoma and flow voids in paraganglioma.

Other MR features of our imaging cohort included T2 hyperintensity and enhancement, but these were non-specific and added little diagnostic value. Classic signs of nerve sheath tumors, such as a fascicular sign, split fat sign and target sign, were infrequently encountered and seldom contributed to diagnosis. On the other hand, half of the lesions in this cohort contained T2 hypointense bands, a previously unreported feature of peripheral nerve sheath tumors, and resembled those of the collagenous components in desmoid tumors on imaging. On histology, there were bands of collagen lying adjacent to malignant cells with melanin pigment, potentially accounting for the low signal bands on MRI. Unfortunately, due to the study’s retrospective nature, we did not have all the histology material to correlate these findings. Further evaluation of this feature and its diagnostic utility may be warranted in future studies.

Due to the rarity of MMNST, much of the published literature is in the form of case reports and small case series. Recently, Soyland et al. [ 10 ] summarized 45 such case reports and series between 1979 and 2020, including 79 instances of spinal MMNST. These patients have a mean age of 44, a similar male predominance, and a comparable metastatic rate of 33% as our cohort. As in our study, most cases are of intradural, extramedullary spinal lesions, with dumbbell morphology and T1 hyperintensity also cited by the authors as common radiologic features. In Cheng et al. [ 11 ], the authors reported 1 case and summarized 9 prior cases of intradural intramedullary MMNST, commonly oval-shaped with T1 hyperintensity. There are other sporadic case reports of MMNST arising from a diverse range of locations, including trigeminal nerve [ 12 , 13 ], maxilla [ 14 ], neck [ 15 , 16 ], heart [ 17 ], bronchus [ 18 ], vagina [ 19 ] and within bones [ 20 ]. T1 hyperintensity is one of the most common MR features in these instances when imaging findings have been reported.

Aside from its diagnostic utility, imaging has additional roles in managing MMNST. Because histological features of MMNST are not predictive of outcome [ 4 ], long-term follow-up, which often incorporates imaging, is required given the uncertain metastatic potential. In addition, imaging is used to exclude possible concomitant neoplasms as part of the Carney complex, which is associated with MMNST.

There were several limitations to our study. First, the small number in this cohort meant that it was difficult to draw any statistically significant conclusions. On the other hand, our study confirms the rarity of MMNST as we had only 31 cases for a period of over two decades in a tertiary cancer center. Second, treatment and follow up data were not available for over half of the cases as they represented patients who presented for a second opinion only. Not having clinical follow-up in the 19 second opinion consultation cases might have influenced the results for our study. Despite this, the demographics and clinical outcomes of the remainder cohort were in line with the published literature. Third, MR techniques and protocols were variable amongst studies performed at different outside institutions prior to referral to our institution. In addition, MR technology in hardware such as MR coils and software such as imaging reconstruction with artificial intelligence has also evolved, thus influencing MRI image quality. Fourth, readers of different experiences may yield different interpretations, but inter-reader variability is intrinsic to all studies whether prospective or retrospective. Despite the small size of the study cohort, our result demonstrates distinct MRI features of melanin-containing tumors and aggressive clinical behavior of the MMNST.

In conclusion, MMNST is a rare malignant tumor with a propensity for local recurrence and metastases. On MR, the tumor presents as a well-circumscribed mass with distinctively T1 hyperintensity, which may help differentiate it from other tumors. In the spine, which is the most location of the tumor, MMNST presents as a dumb-bell shaped mass, often involving multiple levels and causing bone destruction.
