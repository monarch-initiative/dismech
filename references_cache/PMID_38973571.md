Cogan’s syndrome (CS) is a rare multisystem autoimmune disease classically defined as a nonsyphilitic interstitial keratitis and progressive audiovestibular symptoms1. Later, patients may present with other inflammatory eye diseases with progressive audiovestibular symptoms, which is defined as atypical CS2. The etiology and pathogenesis of CS remain largely unknown. Here, we report a female case of atypical CS with temporal bone histopathology findings.

A 67-year-old woman was evaluated for bilateral progressive hearing loss and fever. She had a history of bilateral chronic otitis media and mixed hearing loss (Fig. 1A), hepatitis C and renal tuberculosis. Physical exam and laboratory testing showed no signs of active infection in either the ear or in a major organ. Therefore, the patient was further evaluated for fever of unknown origin (FUO). Blood tests showed increased white blood cell (WBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), and perinuclear anti-neutrophile cytoplasmic antibodies (MPO-ANCA) levels (Fig. S1), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 101 mm, and rheumatoid factors, antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-SSA, SSB antibodies were all negative. Electrocardiogram, coronary angiography, physical examination and fundoscopic tests were within the normal range. The patient was treated with 50 mg of prednisolone which normalized her body temperature within a few days, followed by gradual decreases of the WBC number and MPO-ANCA level (Fig. S1). The prednisolone treatment also improved her hearing loss bilaterally (Fig. 1B). She was discharged 30 mg/day of prednisolone which was gradually tapered, and a low dose of prednisolone treatment was maintained for about one year until MPO-ANCA was undetectable. At 11 months after the discontinuation of prednisolone therapy, the patient experienced persistent headaches for a month followed by relapse of bilateral hearing loss (Fig. 1C), dizziness, bilateral conjunctival hyperemia, and mild fever. MPO-ANCA level was increased to 56 EU/ml (Fig. S1). The patient was readmitted to the hospital for evaluation. Ophthalmological examination revealed bilateral corneal marginal ulcers and episcleritis, which were treated with topical application of fluoroethylene and Cefmenoxime hydrochloride. A Schirmer test was normal bilaterally, and Rose Bengal and Fluorescein stain tests were negative. WBC, CRP, and MPO-ANCA increased (Fig. S1), ESR was 103 mm, while ANCA, anti-dsDNA antibody, Anti-SSA, SSB, antibodies, and C1q were negative. Because the patient presented audiovestibular and ocular symptoms without any evidence of other autoimmune inflammatory diseases, the case was diagnosed as atypical CS. Vestibular function testing and speech discrimination tests were planned, however, a few days after the 2nd admission, the patient had sudden severe back pain and died due to cardiac arrest.

At autopsy, there was no vasculitis in major vascular system. Severe myocarditis was observed and considered as the cause of sudden cardiac arrest. Temporal bones were collected as followed. The scalp was incised circumferentially about 5–6 cm above the external auditory canal and the brain was harvested for autopsy with other organs. Three incisions at middle cranial fossa, posterior cranial fossa, and mid-sagittal line of dorsum sella were placed and temporal bones were removed from skull base. The temporal bones were immediately submerged in 10% formalin. Postmortem time until fixation was approximately 5 hours. After fixation, the temporal bones were decalcified with 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), dehydrated with ethanol and embedded in celloidin. Serial horizontal sectioning was performed with a thickness of 20 μm and every 10th slide was selected and stained with H-E staining. The slides at modiolus level were chosen bilaterally for higher magnifications of imaging.

The temporal bone sections of our case were coupled with age-and-gender matched normal control section in Figure 2. Compared to the control, Reissner’s membrane extended into the scala vestibuli in all turns of the left inner ear (Fig. 2A left) and in the apical and basal turns of the right inner ear (Fig. 2A right). The control section and bilateral sections of our case showed degeneration of spiral ganglion cells in basal turns. These changes may be age-related histological abnormalities as reported3 (Fig.2A and B).

Bilateral apical and basal turns displayed flattened sensory epithelial cells and vacuolization in organ of Corti (OC) (Fig. 3A, C, D, F), although bilateral OC in middle turns had relatively normal morphology (Fig. 3B, E). Detailed histological observations in various regions of inner ear are summarized in table 1 and the corresponding magnified images are shown in supplemental figure 2.

CS is a rare chronic inflammatory disease with ocular and audiovestibular symptoms. Systemic symptoms were displayed in 30–50% of the patients. Although a previous report indicated a relationship between increased MPO-ANCA level and systemic symptoms in CS4, MPO-ANCA is not frequently used for assessment of CS patients. In this case, audiovestibular and systemic symptoms were significantly associated with MPO-ANCA level, suggesting that MPO-ANCA could be an important biomarker to monitor disease progression in some CS cases.

A few studies reported histological findings of temporal bones in the CS patients5,6. The findings in the previous two case reports and our case are summarized in supplemental table 1. The durations of illness in the other two cases were 31 years6 and 57 years5. Both patients showed fluctuating profound hearing loss and vertigo attacks when they were younger, but less symptomatic later in life. In contrast, the temporal bone specimens from our patient were collected during the active phase of fluctuating hearing loss and vestibular symptoms. The structures of bilateral middle turns of cochlea, saccules were relatively preserved compared to previous two cases. Because we were able to analyze temporal bones at two years of disease duration, our histological findings may reflect transitional changes caused by pathogenesis of CS.

It has been recognized that immune complexes and inflammations cause depositions of pigments in inner ears of patients with granulomatosis with polyangiitis7. Noteworthy, Cogan peptide and antibody against the peptide were found in some CS patients8. We observed brown pigment in the vascular-rich areas, such as the lateral wall, spiral ganglion cells, and modiolus areas as well as histological abnormalities during the acute period of CS, which may be evidence of inflammation. Inflammation disturbance in circulation and absorption of endolymph may cause endolymphatic hydrops and these histological abnormalities, that could subsequently trigger audiovestibular symptoms in CS.

We report a case of atypical CS with histological assessment of her temporal bone during the acute disease period. Our findings revealed endolymphatic hydrops and degenerative changes of various parts of the inner ear. These observations suggest that inflammation is a possible important contributor during an acute event in CS. Additionally, MPO-ANCA may be important biomarker in some CS cases.
