# Liver-directed gene therapy results in amelioration of progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis type 2 in mice.
**Authors:** Molina A, Trigueros-Motos L, Molina M, Martínez-García J, Palomo L, Pérez G, Odriozola L, Gázquez C, Tamarit B, Van Faassen M, Verkade HJ, Bouquet C, Combal JP, González-Aseguinolaza G, Smerdou C
**Journal:** Hepatol Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1097/HC9.0000000000001027](https://doi.org/10.1097/HC9.0000000000001027)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis type 2 (PFIC2) is a rare disease affecting the ABCB11 gene, encoding the bile salt export pump (BSEP). BSEP dysfunction impairs bile acid (BA) secretion, causing hepatic damage and leading to pruritus, cholestasis, hepatomegaly, and often fibrosis and end-stage hepatic disease. Treatments include ileal BA transporter inhibitors, surgical bile diversion, or ultimately, liver transplantation. Our aim was to develop a PFIC2 gene therapy approach based on the restoration of BSEP hepatocyte expression.

METHODS AND RESULTS: We designed several expression cassettes containing the human ABCB11 gene downstream of either a liver-specific constitutive promoter or a BA-inducible promoter. After in vitro screening, the AAV vectors with the best expression cassette for each promoter were tested in a PFIC2 mouse model. The AAV vector containing the constitutive promoter, named VTX-802, showed higher BSEP expression, resulting in better restoration of BA secretion 3 and 7 weeks post-treatment. We then performed a dose-range-finding study of VTX-802 in 5-week-old female PFIC2 mice in which the therapeutic efficacy was monitored until 5 months of age. Treated mice showed a sustained dose-dependent improvement in serum transaminase levels. These mice also exhibited significant, but partial, correction of hepatomegaly, and increased BA levels in bile and small intestine, indicating partial restoration of normal BA secretion.

CONCLUSIONS: VTX-802 restores hepatic BSEP expression and partially corrects disease phenotype in PFIC2 mice. To our knowledge, VTX-802 is the first gene therapy approach that could potentially benefit PFIC2 patients.

Progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC) comprises a group of genetically inherited diseases resulting from mutations affecting hepatocyte proteins involved in bile production and regulation.12 In particular, progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis type 2 (PFIC2) is associated with mutations in the ABCB11 gene, which expresses bile salt export pump (BSEP), a transmembrane protein of 160 kDa present in the canalicular membrane of hepatocytes.34 Bile acids (BAs) constitute one of the main components of bile, together with cholesterol and phosphatidylcholine. BAs are synthesized in hepatocytes and transported to the bile by BSEP, accumulating in the gallbladder and being secreted to the intestine to facilitate the digestion and absorption of fats. Then, ~95% of BAs are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum via the ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT), returning to the liver through the enterohepatic circulation.5 BAs also play a role as signaling molecules that activate specific nuclear receptors, like farnesoid X-activated receptor (FXR), and G protein-coupled bile acid receptors, like Takeda G Protein-Coupled Receptor 5 (TGR5), and mediate the bidirectional crosstalk along the gut–liver axis.67


PFIC2 is a rare genetic disease with an incidence of 1:100,000 cases in the general population.8 Mutations affecting the ABCB11 gene can have different outcomes depending on how the functionality of the BSEP transporter is affected.9 For example, some mutations lead to truncated forms of the protein, resulting in a complete loss of function,10 while others might only reduce its activity.11 BSEP loss-of-function mutations cause BA accumulation in the liver, leading to hepatocyte death, BA spillover in blood, severe pruritus, portal hypertension, hepatomegaly, liver failure, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) at an early age, and ultimately death in untreated patients.12 Symptoms of PFIC2 appear very soon after birth. Pharmacological treatments, such as ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), and surgical procedures, such as biliary diversion, have been the standard of care for many years to prevent liver damage, although they are not effective in many cases.13 Rifampicin has also been helpful to control pruritus, but without effect on disease progression. Nowadays, the use of IBAT inhibitors (IBATi), such as odevixibat (Bylvay) and maralixibat (Livmarli), which have been recently approved by the FDA and EMA,13 is the treatment of choice. These drugs prevent the reabsorption of BA in the intestine, lowering their blood levels and reducing pruritus.14 However, only approximately one-third of BSEP patients have mutations that respond to IBAT inhibition; therefore, for most patients, a liver transplant remains the only curative option.

In recent years, there have been huge advances in the development of gene therapy strategies for the treatment of rare genetic diseases, leading to the approval of more than 10 different therapies by the FDA and the EMA.1516 Many of these therapies are based on in vivo gene supplementation using adeno-associated virus (AAV) recombinant vectors, which mediate long-term expression in non-dividing cells.17 Although no gene therapy has been approved for PFIC diseases yet,18 we and others have shown that an AAV expressing multidrug resistant 3 (MDR3) protein can successfully prevent and reverse PFIC3 symptoms in a clinically relevant mouse model.192021


Based on these premises and given that no curative treatment, except liver transplant, is available for PFIC2 patients, in the present study we have designed an AAV vector for liver-specific human BSEP expression and evaluated its therapeutic efficacy in a Abcb11

−/−
 mouse model that recapitulates, especially in females, some human PFIC2 features, such as impaired BA secretion to the gallbladder and chronic liver damage leading to hepatomegaly.22 This AAV therapy was able to mediate sustained and functional BSEP expression in hepatic biliary canaliculi, significantly restoring BA secretion to bile and small intestine, which resulted in a decrease of serum transaminases and hepatomegaly.


Synthetic wildtype (WT) and codon-optimized sequences (co-hBSEP) of human ABCB11 cDNA coding for BSEP (NCBI Reference Sequence for WT: NP_003733.2) were synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China) and subcloned into a plasmid containing AAV2 inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) (pAAV) downstream of the human α-1-antitrypsin (A1AT) transcriptional regulatory sequence,23 following a strategy previously described by us.19 The production yield of the plasmid containing the WT sequence was consistently low, with frequent recombination events. Moreover, integration of the ABCB11 cDNA WT sequence into the pAAV was unsuccessful due to its instability (data not shown). These limitations were overcome by utilizing the co-hBSEP sequence (AAV-A1AT-co-hBSEP) (Figure 1A).

The co-hBSEP sequence was also cloned under the control of an optimized bile acid-inducible promoter based on the mouse BSEP promoter (imPr).24 For this, the A1AT promoter in AAV-A1AT-co-hBSEPco was replaced by the imPr sequence generated by GenScript (AAV-imPr-co-hBSEP) (Figure 1A). The imPr promoter contains an inverted repeat element (IR-1) that serves as a binding site for FXR, a nuclear receptor activated by BA.25 A small intron derived from minute virus of mice (MVM)2627 was introduced downstream of the promoter and upstream of the transgene sequence in both AAV-co-hBSEP constructs, generating AAV-A1AT-MVM-co-hBSEP and AAV-imPr-MVM-co-hBSEP plasmids, respectively (Figure 1A). Mice were socially housed in solid-bottom rodent caging with bedding material and environmental enrichment items, maintained in a 12-hour light and dark cycle, and water and food were provided ad libitum. The studies were approved by the ethical committee for animal testing of the University of Navarra (Number 081/17) and were performed according to current ethical guidelines for animal experimentation.

Animals were weaned, genotyped, and identified at 3 weeks of age, and treated at 5 weeks of age. Mice were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation and intravenously (i.v.) injected at the retro-orbital sinus with 10 µL/body weight (BW) of either physiological serum 0.9% (B. Braun Medical, Melsungen, Germany) (saline) or the different AAV vectors in phosphate-buffered saline solution with Pluronic F-68. BW and blood samples of the animals were periodically collected. The general condition of the animals was observed twice weekly, and any abnormality (such as wounds, coat ruffling, lethargy, etc.) was recorded. Before necropsy, mice were fasted overnight and then sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Livers and small intestines were dissected and weighed. BWs were recorded on the day before sacrifice, before the animals were fasted, and these values were utilized to calculate the liver weight/BW ratio.

Before blood collection, mice were fasted overnight. Blood was obtained from the retro-orbital plexus under isoflurane anesthesia, and samples were allowed to clot and centrifuged at 2000g for 15 minutes at room temperature to obtain serum. Alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and bile acids (BAs) were quantified using a HITACHI C311 analyzer (Chiyoda, Japan).

At the time of sacrifice, the gallbladder was excised from the liver, and bile was released by puncturing it inside a 1.5 mL tube with a needle. One microliter of bile was diluted to 1:200 in saline solution for BA quantification with a HITACHI C311 analyzer. Frozen small intestine was mechanically pulverized with a pestle and a mortar filled with liquid nitrogen. Thirty milligrams of small intestine powder were diluted in 500 µL sterile water, centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 minutes, and 100 µL of supernatant was used for quantification with the HITACHI C311 analyzer.

Bile acids in gallbladder bile were analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography in combination with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) essentially as previously described,30 the only difference being that an ACQUITY UPLC (Waters, Milford, MA) in combination with an XEVO TQ-s micro mass spectrometer (Waters) was used. Bile was analyzed at a dilution of 1:250 and 1:2000.

Three liver lobes were fixed in formalin (3.7%–4.0% w/v buffered to pH 7 and stabilized with methanol) for 48 hours. Then, liver lobes were water-washed to remove traces of the fixative, trimmed and transferred to 70% ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned in 3-μm sections. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Sirius Red (SR) staining were performed to evaluate histological variations and fibrosis, respectively. BSEP expression was detected by immunohistochemistry (IHC) using a rabbit α-BSEP primary antibody (LS-Bio, catalog number LS-C661867, dilution 1:2000; Seattle, WA) and anti-Rabbit EnVision+ System- HRP (Dako, catalog number K400311-2; Glostrup, Denmark). The percentage of tissue area positive for BSEP and SR staining was quantified using FIJI plugins developed by the Imaging Core Facility (CIMA, Universidad de Navarra) and the Aragón Health Research Institute (IIS Aragón), respectively. These plugins contain image-processing operations, like color segmentation, filtering, and particle counting, which facilitate tissue segmentation from background and allow determination of the ratio of positive area versus total tissue area.

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad software (Boston, MA). Outliers were identified and removed using the ROUT (robust regression and outlier removal) method with a Q value of 1%. The normality of the dataset was determined, analysis of variance performed, and the appropriate ANOVA method selected, or t test when indicated. Values are represented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The following values are considered significant: *p<0.05; **p<0.01; ***p<0.001; ****p<0.0001.

We first generated AAV plasmid constructs that contained a human codon-optimized BSEP sequence (co-hBSEP) downstream of either the liver-specific α-1-antitrypsin (A1AT) constitutive promoter,23 or a BA-inducible promoter (imPr),24 as described in the Methods section (Figure 1A). To further enhance expression, a small intron derived from minute virus of mice (MVM)26 was introduced upstream of the transgene sequence (Figure 1A). These plasmids were transfected into the human hepatic cell line Huh-7. For activation of the BA-inducible promoter, cells were co-transfected with an FXRα2-expressing plasmid, due to the low expression of this transcription factor in Huh-7 cells, and treated with chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), as previously described.24 Across all constructs, the inclusion of the MVM intron enhanced BSEP mRNA expression, although it only reached statistical significance in combination with the A1AT promoter (Figure 1B). Under basal conditions, the A1AT promoter exhibited slightly higher transcriptional activity compared with the imPr promoter. However, following BA induction, BSEP mRNA levels driven by the imPr promoter were markedly elevated, exceeding those achieved with the constitutive A1AT promoter. In these studies, BSEP protein expression was also confirmed by immunofluorescence (Figure 1C).

Based on the in vitro experiments, the A1AT-MVM-co-hBSEP and imPr-MVM-co-hBSEP expression cassettes, including the MVM intron, were selected for in vivo evaluation in the Abcb11

−/−
 mouse model.22 AAV8 viral particles containing these constructs were i.v. administered to Abcb11

−/−
 (KO) female mice at a dose of 6×1013 vector genomes (VGs)/kg. Considering the early onset of the disease in PFIC2 patients, juvenile (5-week-old) Abcb11

−/−
 mice were used. To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the vectors, mice were sacrificed 3 weeks after treatment, and BA secretion into the gallbladder and small intestine, as well as hepatomegaly, were assessed. As shown in Figure 2A, at this dose, both vectors increased BA concentration in bile from the gallbladder compared to untreated KO animals, with the AAV-A1AT-co-hBSEP vector showing slightly greater efficacy. However, at this time point, no significant differences were observed in intestinal BA levels and hepatomegaly between mice treated with both vectors, which were similar to those of non-treated mice (Supplemental Figure S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450).

Next, BSEP expression in the liver was analyzed by IHC. In AAV-treated KO animals, BSEP expression was detected at the bile canaliculi as observed in WT mice, while untreated KO mice lacked detectable expression (Figure 2B). Notably, this expression was stronger and covered a significantly larger area (>20-fold increase) in the AAV-A1AT-co-hBSEP group compared with the AAV-imPr-co-hBSEP group (Figure 2C). Moreover, the ratio of BSEP mRNA/vector genome copies in the liver was ~2-fold higher in the AAV-A1AT-co-hBSEP-treated group compared with AAV-imPr-co-hBSEP-treated mice (Figure 2D). A second in vivo experiment was conducted under the same conditions, but with mice sacrificed 7 weeks post-injection. The results were consistent with those of the first experiment, again demonstrating a slight therapeutic advantage of the AAV-A1AT-co-hBSEP vector over AAV-imPr-co-hBSEP (Supplemental Figure S2, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450).

Based on these data, the AAV-A1AT-co-hBSEP vector carrying the liver-specific constitutive promoter, named hereafter VTX-802, was selected as the most promising therapeutic candidate.

VTX-802 was i.v. administered to 5-week-old KO female mice at 3 different doses: 6×1013, 1.2×1014, and 1.8×1014 VG/kg. Serum samples were collected at 15 weeks post-treatment, and levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and BA were measured. In untreated KO mice, ALP, AST, and ALT levels were significantly higher compared with WT animals (Figure 3). Interestingly, a dose-dependent reduction of serum biomarkers was observed in KO mice treated with VTX-802. This resulted in a significant decrease of ALP and AST with the intermediate and high doses, as well as of ALT with the highest dose, in comparison with non-treated KO mice. In addition, at the highest dose, ALT and AST levels were comparable to those of WT mice (Figures 3B, C). No significant differences were observed in serum BA between WT and KO mice or between VTX-802-treated and untreated KO mice (Figure 3D).

Animals were sacrificed at 20 weeks of age (corresponding to 15 weeks post-treatment), and BA concentrations in bile obtained from the gallbladder and small intestine were analyzed. Furthermore, BA composition was determined in gallbladder bile obtained from KO mice receiving the highest dose of VTX-802 and was compared with that in WT and untreated KO mice. A dose-dependent increase in BA levels was observed in both the gallbladder and small intestine upon VTX-802 treatment, reaching more than 55% of normal levels at the highest dose (Figures 4A, B). Interestingly, BA composition analysis showed that, in terms of absolute concentration and percentage of total BA, VTX-802 treatment normalized taurocholic acid (TCA) secretion, which was markedly impaired in KO mice (Figures 4C, D). Cholic acid (CA) and other CA conjugates that are significantly reduced in KO mice showed an increase after VTX-802 treatment, but did not reach statistical significance compared with untreated KO mice (Supplemental Figure S3, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450). Interestingly, we also found that the concentrations of T-β-MCA (tauro-β-muricholic acid) and T-ω-MCA (tauro-ω-muricholic acid), which are primarily found in mice, were slightly higher in KO mice compared with WT mice, with no significant changes observed after VTX-802 treatment (Figure 4C). However, the percentage of T-β-MCA and T-ω-MCA, which was significantly higher in KO mice than in WT mice, was significantly reduced after treatment with VTX-802 in KO mice due to the recovery of TCA secretion (Figure 4D). Our data are in agreement with the presence of an alternative transporter that binds these BAs in mice with a C57BL/6 genetic background. In addition, treated animals showed a decrease in liver BA with the intermediate and high vector doses (Figure 4E) and a dose-dependent reduction in hepatomegaly (Figure 4F), while there were no significant differences in body weight among the groups (Supplemental Figure S4A, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450).

Analysis of vector genome and transgene mRNA copies demonstrated a clear dose–response and a sustained expression of the therapeutic transgene up to 15 weeks post-treatment for all tested doses (Supplemental Figure S4B, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450, and Figure 4G). Regarding BSEP protein expression, the liver area expressing BSEP in bile canaliculi showed a slight dose–response correlation, although no significant differences were observed between the different doses (Figure 4H and Supplemental Figure S5, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450). Liver fibrosis was assessed by Sirius Red staining, revealing mild and heterogeneous fibrosis in untreated KO mice. No major differences in overall fibrosis levels were observed following VTX-802 treatment, regardless of the dose, although a trend to reduced fibrosis was observed in some treated groups (Supplemental Figures S6A, 6B, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450). However, interestingly, liver regions with BSEP expression in treated animals were largely devoid of collagen fibers, which are key fibrosis components, whereas fibrosis was evident in areas lacking BSEP expression (Supplemental Figure S7, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450). In addition, a mild inflammatory infiltrate was observed in these BSEP-negative fibrotic areas (Supplemental Figure S7, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450).

PFIC2 is a rare and extremely severe inherited autosomal recessive monogenetic liver disease, due to BSEP deficiency, with a clear unmet clinical need, since the only available curative treatment is liver transplantation, a procedure that carries many associated burdens. An alternative therapeutic option for PFIC2 patients could be to restore BSEP expression in hepatocytes using an AAV-mediated gene supplementation strategy, as the one tested in the present study. PFIC2 is a good candidate to benefit from gene delivery to hepatocytes since BSEP expression is restricted to the liver, with no extrahepatic symptoms, and AAV vectors have demonstrated high efficiency in liver transduction.17 To select the best therapeutic candidate, several AAV plasmids were designed comprising a codon-optimized version of the ABCB11 cDNA sequence encoding the BSEP protein under the control of either a liver constitutive promoter (A1AT)23 or an inducible promoter (imPr) derived from murine BSEP promoter,24 and tested in vitro in human liver cells. The imPr promoter, which can be physiologically regulated by BA, was previously developed and tested in our laboratory, showing a good BA-dependent inducibility both in vitro and in vivo when used to drive the expression of a reporter gene.24 The addition of the MVM small intron before the ABCB11 cDNA sequence,26 was also tested and resulted in a slightly increased expression of ABCB11 mRNA in vitro for both promoters, leading to selection of the cassettes including the MVM intron. Interestingly, AAV plasmids having the imPr promoter showed significantly higher BSEP expression in transfected hepatic cells upon addition of a BA (CDCA) compared with the plasmids having the constitutive promoter.

For the in vivo selection of the therapeutic candidate, a model of PFIC2 based on Abcb11
−/− (KO) mice on a C57BL/6 genetic background was used.22 This mouse model reproduces many, though not all, of PFIC2 features described in patients, such as a drastic reduction of BA in bile and intestine, and liver damage, fibrosis, and hepatomegaly.31 However, some parameters, such as serum BA, have shown a very high variability in this model, and for that reason, they are not good therapeutic indicators in mice.22 In fact, we did not observe differences in serum BA between KO and WT mice (Figure 3D). This represents a limitation of this mouse model, since high serum BA levels are a clinically significant biomarker for PFIC2 patients. A possibility to increase serum BA could be to provide mice with a BA-enriched diet, as it was described for KO mice with a FVB/NJ background, which do not develop cholestasis with a normal diet.29 Furthermore, C57BL/6 KO mice show strong gender differences, with a clearer disease onset in females,3132 which led us to test BSEP-expressing AAV vectors only in KO female animals. Despite the limitations of this model, there are currently no better alternatives.29


The evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy of the 2 selected candidates, AAV-A1AT-co-hBSEP and AAV-imPr-co-hBSEP vectors, including the MVM intron, in juvenile KO female mice showed that the higher expression of the vector with the A1AT promoter in vivo was translated into a better restoration of BA secretion to bile and small intestine, both 3 and 7 weeks after treatment. The AAV-A1AT-co-hBSEP vector also showed higher expression of the transgene at the mRNA and protein levels in comparison to AAV-imPr-co-hBSEP. The reasons for the poorer in vivo performance of the imPr promoter compared with the constitutive one are not clear, but they may be related to differences in the type and/or amount of BA present in vivo in comparison to the in vitro assay, or to differences in FXR expression. These in vivo studies allowed us to select the AAV vector expressing co-hBSEP under the liver-specific constitutive A1AT promoter and containing the small MVM intron, named VTX-802, as the optimal candidate to be tested in a dose-range-finding study. Administration of VTX-802 was well tolerated even at the highest dose of 1.8×1014 VG/kg with no impact on the general health or well-being of the animals, since no alterations in serum biomarkers, body weight, or histological changes indicative of liver damage were observed. A good expression level of BSEP was measured 15 weeks post-treatment for all tested doses, a sign of a sustained and persistent expression of the therapeutic transgene. As expected, AAV genome and ABCB11 mRNA copy numbers increased with the vector dose, reaching the highest level at 1.8×1014 VG/kg. When BSEP expression was analyzed by IHC, a clear staining of the liver biliary canaliculi was observed in all vector-treated mice, indicating that this transporter protein was correctly localized and seemed to slightly increase with the dose. The lack of significant differences between doses could be due to the semiquantitative nature of IHC analysis, which is based on determining the percentage of the area covered by BSEP-positive canaliculi. It is also hypothesized that by increasing the dose, the amount of vector genomes and ABCB11 mRNA per cell increases, but not the number of transduced cells, resulting in small changes in the area positive for BSEP. Interestingly, BSEP expression was more pronounced around blood vessels, in contrast to WT mice, in which BSEP is expressed homogenously throughout the liver (Supplemental Figure S5, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450). This is probably due to better AAV8-transduction of hepatocytes that are closer to central veins, as previously described in mice.33


In agreement with a higher BSEP expression in KO mice treated with the 2 higher doses of VTX-802 (1.2×1014 and 1.8×1014 VG/kg), a more pronounced improvement in PFIC2 biomarkers was observed in these groups in comparison to saline-treated mice. Indeed, the high dose resulted in levels of transaminases that were close to WT levels 15 weeks after treatment. In accordance with serum biomarkers, a dose-dependent increase in BA secretion to the bile and small intestine was observed. Interestingly, at the 2 higher doses, BA levels were >50% of WT levels in both organs, also reaching 36% in bile with the lowest dose. This result is key, as it indicates that BSEP functionality was maintained up to 4 months post-treatment in mice treated with all tested doses. More importantly, the analysis of BA composition showed that VTX-802–mediated BSEP expression restores TCA excretion into gallbladder bile, which is severely impaired in KO mice, reaching levels close to those of WT mice (>70% of WT levels). TCA secretion in VTX-802–treated mice resulted in a reduction in the proportion of T-β-MCA and T-ω-MCA, which are the predominant bile acids in KO mice, since they are secreted into bile in a BSEP-independent manner. Another indicator of the efficacy of the vector was the partial reversion of hepatomegaly, which was similar and more pronounced with the intermediate and higher vector doses. The lower effect of our therapy on hepatomegaly, compared with biliary BA restoration, could be due to the fact that, as previously explained, AAV8-transduction is not homogeneously distributed (Supplemental Figure S5, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450); in particular, the periportal regions, in which hepatocytes are mainly involved in bile salt uptake from enterohepatic circulation and their secretion to bile, are not corrected and therefore most likely unable to secrete BSEP-dependent BAs. Furthermore, by 4 weeks of age, female KO mice already display a significantly higher liver-to-body weight ratio compared with WT mice (WT =4.9 ± 0.6%; KO =8.9 ± 2.1%; n =5–7).

Finally, the lack of significant improvement in liver fibrosis in treated mice could be explained by the fact that the degree of fibrosis in this mouse model was very mild, highly variable, and regional (Supplemental Figures S6A, B, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450), in agreement with some previous studies.34 A correlation between the accumulation of collagen deposits, higher levels of inflammatory infiltrates, and lower expression of BSEP was found in specific regions of the liver (Supplemental Figure S7, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C450). Although this phenomenon could be due to a lower AAV transduction in regions in which fibrosis is present, as described in previous studies,3435 it could also be hypothesized that the fibrosis could appear due to loss-of-BSEP expression in some liver areas. A possible way to overcome the limitation of regionally different BSEP expression could be the use of AAV vectors with serotypes able to provide a more homogeneous liver expression. For example, recent studies have shown that a heparan sulfate proteoglycan-detargeted AAV with LK03 serotype was able to reach all zones of liver lobules in mice.36 Furthermore, this serotype showed high transduction in rhesus macaques, being higher in hepatocytes around the portal triad, where bile ducts are present. This transduction profile could improve the therapeutic effect of the vector in PFIC2 patients.37


In summary, VTX-802 was able to improve serum biomarkers and significantly correct hepatomegaly and BA content in the bile and small intestine even at 4 months after treatment. These findings suggest that VTX-802 may represent a therapeutic option for the treatment of PFIC2 patients.

Remarkably, in PFIC2 patients with an overt disease, there is nearly no BA excretion in bile. Indeed, it is estimated that having a residual BSEP function that allows the excretion of minimal amounts of BA is sufficient to respond to IBATi-mediated BA diversion to the stool. In our study, we showed that VTX-802 was able to increase the BA excretion in the gallbladder to more than 55% of WT animals, which could help correct BSEP deficiency in clinical studies. Furthermore, since the current treatment of PFIC2 with IBATi is only efficient in patients with non-truncating BSEP mutations that result in some residual BSEP function, but not in those with truncating or other mutations leading to a complete lack of BSEP function, a combination with gene therapy could improve IBATi treatment efficacy and reach a broader population. This type of combination could also allow for reducing the vector therapeutic dose, increasing the safety of the treatment.

Given that PFIC2 is a pediatric disease that would require treatment at a very early age, it is likely that patients will need to receive additional doses of AAV vectors expressing BSEP later in life due to vector genomes being lost upon hepatocyte division. Although AAV re-administration presents important hurdles due to the induction of vector-neutralizing antibodies, there are several clinical options that could facilitate this procedure. Among them are the use of different AAV serotypes for subsequent injections, immune modulators to control immune responses to AAV, and IgG-cleaving endopeptidases.38 Remarkably, if the expression of BSEP is restored at high levels, as in this study, reduction of efficacy associated with liver growth may still allow for the retention of sufficient BSEP function to correct the disease throughout childhood and into adulthood. Finally, since the development of HCC has been associated with intrahepatic retention of hydrophobic BA, restoration of their secretion to bile by a gene therapy approach could also potentially lower the risk for oncogenic events.39


Restoring long-term hepatic BSEP expression and physiological bile secretion by means of a gene therapy approach will contribute to developing more efficient therapeutic options for these pediatric and adolescent patients still facing significant unmet medical needs.
