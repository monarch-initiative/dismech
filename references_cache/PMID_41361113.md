Fluoride plays a dual role in dental health-preventing caries at optimal levels 
but causing fluorosis when excessive. While most animal studies focus on young 
mice, age-related susceptibility to fluoride remains poorly understood. This 
study presents the first comprehensive analysis of developmental stage-dependent 
differences in fluoride toxicity, focusing on enamel formation and systemic 
fluoride clearance. Male C57BL/6J mice-adolescent (5-9 weeks) and mature 
(16-20 weeks)-were exposed to fluoride in drinking water (0, 50, 100, or 
125 ppm) for 6 weeks. Adolescent mice developed pronounced dental fluorosis, 
characterized by chalky white incisors, elevated Quantitative Light-induced 
Fluorescence (QLF) values, reduced enamel microhardness, and lower enamel 
mineral density (EMD). Histological analysis revealed disrupted ameloblast 
morphology, reduced KLK4 expression, and aprismatic enamel, with more severe 
effects in adolescents. In contrast, mature mice exhibited minimal changes in 
QLF, enamel hardness, and EMD. Systemic fluoride analysis showed significantly 
lower serum and urinary fluoride levels in adolescent mice compared to mature 
mice, indicating reduced excretion and increased tissue accumulation. These 
findings demonstrate that younger mice are more vulnerable to fluoride-induced 
enamel defects due to lower clearance than mature mice. This study provides 
critical evidence of age-related differences in fluoride toxicity, revealing 
heightened vulnerability during developmental stages. Our findings have 
significant public health implications, supporting the need for age-specific 
fluoride exposure guidelines to balance caries prevention and developmental 
fluoride toxicity.

Fluoride is effective in reducing dental caries, and an optimal level of fluoride (0.7 ppm) in drinking water has been identified as the most practical and cost-effective public health strategy for preventing dental caries (CDC 2024). Whereas, excessive fluoride ingestion (over 1.5 ppm fluoride in drinking water) for an extended period can cause dental and skeletal fluorosis (WHO 2017, 2019). Thus, fluoride has a narrow efficacy window, as approximately 2.1 times the recommended amount can cause fluorosis. Fluoride present in groundwater is highly noticeable in Asia and Africa (Podgorski and Berg 2022) (varying from 20 to 79.2 ppm), which has generated health issues. In areas rich in fluoride-containing minerals, the natural concentration of fluoride in drinking water is significantly higher than the safe/optimal levels, with well water containing up to about 10 ppm, or in some areas even higher concentrations (WHO 2017). A recent study on the global prediction model of fluoride in groundwater (with fluoride levels exceeding 1.5 ppm) estimated the human population at risk of health issues caused by excessive fluoride (Podgorski and Berg 2022). Approximately 180 million people worldwide are potentially negatively affected by fluoride, most of whom reside in Asia and Africa (Podgorski and Berg 2022). In addition to drinking water, there are several sources of fluoride (Cantoral et al. 2019), including food (e.g., seafood; 1.9 ppm), drinks (e.g., tea; 0.5–6 ppm (Regelson et al. 2021)), and dentifrices (e.g., toothpaste; 1000–1500 ppm).

Ingestion of excessive fluoride increases the risk of dental fluorosis during enamel formation at around the age of 8 years or younger (Health and Human Services Federal Panel on Community Water 2015). Dental fluorosis results from excessive fluoride intake during the critical periods of enamel formation (DenBesten and Thariani 1992), which occur prior to tooth eruption. In permanent teeth, enamel development takes place during early childhood (Nanci 2023). Therefore, fluoride exposure during this time can affect teeth that will not erupt until several years later, leading to visible signs of fluorosis once those teeth emerge. Clinically, dental fluorosis presents as hypomineralized enamel, characterized by white opacities, reduced mineral content, and increased protein accumulation, and a softer, more brittle enamel structure (Houari et al. 2023). About 99 percent of the body’s fluoride is found in mineralized tissues, including teeth and bones (Institute of Medicine Standing Committee on the Scientific Evaluation of Dietary Reference 1997). Children and infants take up higher proportions of absorbed fluoride (about 80–90% of ingested fluoride is retained in the body), compared to adults (about 50–60%) (Institute of Medicine Standing Committee on the Scientific Evaluation of Dietary Reference 1997). However, the age-related susceptibility to fluoride remains unclear.

This study aims to investigate the differences in fluoride’s effects on enamel formation between adolescent and mature mice, using our established mouse models of dental fluorosis. Many animal studies on fluoride and fluorosis utilize relatively younger mice, 3 to 9 weeks of age (Okamoto et al. 2025; Puranik et al. 2015; Suzuki et al. 2017, 2014a, b), with some mice 3 to 4 weeks of age at the time of weaning (Carvalho et al. 2009; Everett et al. 2002; Kobayashi et al. 2014; Lu et al. 2019; Lyaruu et al. 2014; Vieira et al. 2005a, 2005b) or very early fluoride exposures during fetal development (Hosokawa et al. 2025). However, reports on fluoride effects on older mice are limited, and little is known about it. The aim of this study is to reveal the difference in the fluoride effect on enamel formation between adolescent (under development) and mature mice (fully developed) using our established mouse dental fluorosis models (Suzuki et al. 2017, 2014a, 2014b). Regarding mouse age and developmental stages, the definition varies in mouse strains and studies (Brust et al. 2015; Fox 2007; JacksonLaboratory 2023; Mytidou et al. 2021; Radulescu et al. 2021; Yuan et al. 2011). In this study, we determined the adolescent and mature stages based on the Jackson Laboratory’s life history stages in C57BL/6J mice (Fox 2007; JacksonLaboratory 2023). According to a recent report in C57BL/6J mice (Mytidou et al. 2021), the period from 4 to 9 weeks after birth corresponds to adolescence, during which mice are physically, behaviorally, and sexually developing. By 9–10 weeks of age, mice have typically reached adulthood, characterized by behavioral maturity and a fully developed body. We therefore designated 5- to 9-week-old mice as adolescent and 16- to 20-week-old mice as mature in the present study.

Rodent incisors are widely used to study the enamel development process, known as amelogenesis (Suzuki et al. 2015, 2017; Suzuki and Bartlett 2014). Unlike most other mammalian teeth, rodent incisors grow continuously throughout their lifespan (Klevezal and Shchepotkin 2012; Ness 1964; Wise et al. 2002). This allows for the evaluation of the entire stages of amelogenesis. Amelogenesis occurs in several stages, including the secretory stage and the maturation stage (Hu et al. 2007). In the secretory stage, ameloblasts secrete matrix metalloproteinase 20 (MMP20) and cleave the most abundant enamel matrix proteins as the mineralized ribbons grow in width and thickness to form enamel (Bartlett 2013; Bartlett and Smith 2013; Okamoto et al. 2025; Simmer et al. 2012). In the maturation stage, ameloblasts secrete kallikrein-related peptidase 4 (KLK4) to degrade enamel matrix proteins and promote mass mineral deposition as the enamel hardens into its final form. We previously demonstrated that fluoride causes cell stress, including oxidative stress (Suzuki et al. 2015) and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress (Kubota et al. 2005), epigenetic modifications leading to gene expression changes (Yamashita et al. 2024), and the initiation of apoptosis in ameloblasts (Deng et al. 2020, 2019; Fujiwara et al. 2021; Suzuki et al. 2018). Fluoride decreases KLK4 transcription during the maturation stage via transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling in rodent models (Suzuki et al. 2014a). Due to KLK4 reduction by fluoride, enamel matrix proteins remain to suppress enamel crystal formation, resulting in aprismatic hypomineralized enamel (Den Besten 1999; DenBesten et al. 2011; Li et al. 2022).

Fluoride susceptibility differs between rodents and humans. In the laboratory setting, rodents often require fluoride concentrations exceeding 100 ppm to exhibit various toxicity phenotypes. Although the threshold varies by mouse strain, previous studies have demonstrated that concentrations above 50 ppm fluoride in drinking water for 6 weeks of treatment can cause dental fluorosis in mouse models (Carvalho et al. 2009; Everett et al. 2002; Kobayashi et al. 2014; Okamoto et al. 2025; Puranik et al. 2015; Suzuki et al. 2017, 2014a, 2014b; Vieira et al. 2005a, 2005b). The 6-week fluoride treatment duration is selected based on the complete turnover time of the mouse incisor, which occurs over approximately 35–45 days (Gonzalez Lopez et al. 2023; Zegarelli 1944). In humans, clinical dental fluorosis is typically associated with a fluoride concentration over 1.5 ppm in drinking water (WHO 2017, 2019). In contrast, murine models require significantly higher fluoride exposure, typically above 50 ppm (Everett et al. 2002), which is approximately 33.3 times greater than the 1.5 ppm threshold in humans (WHO 2019), to produce clinical features of dental fluorosis that are comparable to those seen in humans. This suggests that rodents are less susceptible to fluoride, possibly due to differences in the metabolism between mice and humans. Consequently, higher concentrations are necessary to elicit human health symptoms in mouse models. Based on previous studies, we evaluated higher doses of fluoride (50, 100, and 125 ppm) as excessive fluoride, which can induce dental fluorosis in mice.

This is the first study to comprehensively characterize age-related differential fluoride effects on amelogenesis, including tooth phenotypes, microhardness, enamel microstructures (enamel mineral density and enamel prism), ameloblast morphology, and fluoride clearance levels between adolescent and mature mice in dental fluorosis mouse models.

Sodium fluoride (NaF) Cat. S299-100 was obtained from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA).

Systemic administration of fluoride to the mice was performed following a previously established protocol (Suzuki et al. 2017). C57BL/6J mice (Strain #000664) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA) and bred in-house. To ensure consistency with our previous fluoride studies, we selected C57BL/6J mice, a widely used and genetically well-characterized strain that supports reproducibility and relevance in biomedical research. To reduce variability potentially introduced by hormonal factors, only male mice were evaluated in this study. The ages for adolescent and mature mice were determined based on the Jackson Laboratory’s life history stages in C57BL/6J mice and previous studies (Fox 2007; JacksonLaboratory 2023; Mytidou et al. 2021). 5- to 9-week-old male mice were designated as the adolescent group, and 16- to 20-week-old male mice were assigned as the mature group. Each adolescent and mature group was randomly divided into four sub-groups (4–6 mice per sub-group) and provided drinking water containing either 0, 50, 100, or 125 ppm fluoride as NaF for 6 weeks as previously reported (Okamoto et al. 2025; Suzuki et al. 2015). Throughout the experiment, the animals were fed a fluoride-free chow (F1515, rodent standard diet, AIN-76A) from Bio-Sev (Frenchtown, New Jersey, USA), beginning one week prior to fluoride water treatment until the end of the fluoride treatment period. During the fluoride treatment period, the body weight of each mouse was recorded once a week. No significant differences in body weight change among sub-groups of the same age group were detected (Supplementary Fig. S1). After 6 weeks of systemic fluoride treatment, the animals were euthanized. Mandibular and maxillary incisors were extracted for quantitative light-induced fluorescence (QLF), Vickers microhardness, Micro-Computed Tomography (micro-CT), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM–EDX) and histological analysis. Serum and urine were extracted for measurement of fluoride concentration. All procedures described in this protocol have been performed in accordance with guidelines and regulations for the use of vertebrate animals approved by the Institutional Animal Care Use Committee (IACUC) at Augusta University (where the corresponding author carried out experiments until January 2023. Registration number: 2019-0989) and at Nova Southeastern University (Registration number: 2023.02.MSuz1) which is accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC). A certificate of approval is available upon request.

After euthanasia, photographs of the maxillary and mandibular incisors were taken using a Nikon digital camera D7500 with AF-S DX Micro MIKKOR 85 mm f/3.5G ED VR lens (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Representative images from three mice per group are shown.

QLF has previously been used to assess the severity of fluorosis in mice (Carvalho et al. 2009; Everett et al. 2002; Okamoto et al. 2025; Sharma et al. 2011; Suzuki et al. 2017, 2014b; Vieira et al. 2005a; Yan et al. 2011). The mandibular incisors were dissected in pairs and subjected to QLF using a Nikon epifluorescence micro-camera (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a Chroma Gold 11006v2 set cube (exciter D360/40x, dichroic 400DCLP, and emitter E515LPv2). Fluorescence images of the teeth were converted to 8-bit grayscale values 0 (black) to 255 (white), and their intensities were analyzed using the ImageJ software (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). Average 8-bit grayscale intensities were taken from 10 sites on the enamel surfaces from both incisors and used as the overall value for each animal.

Resin-embedded and well-polished specimens were prepared as previously described (Okamoto et al. 2024). A mirror-finish surface specimen of the mandibular incisor was measured using a microhardness tester (ALPHA-MHT-1000Z) from PACE Technologies (Tucson, Arizona, USA). Indentation testing was performed with a load of 25 g for 10 s using a Vickers tip. The tip region of the enamel layers served as the evaluation site for microhardness testing. There was a total of twelve indentations in each incisor, and the average value of twelve indentations was calculated as enamel microhardness (HV) of each mouse.

Micro-CT images of mandibular incisors were acquired using a Skyscan 1176 micro-CT scanner (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) at a resolution of 18 μm. Scans were performed at 50 kV and 500 μA, Al 0.5 mm Filter, 270-ms integration time, Camera binning 2 × 2, Camera pixel size 11.95 µm, total degrees of rotation 360 degrees, Rotation step 0.5 degrees, Frame averaging OFF. After scanning, images were reconstructed using NRecon software (Bruker). CTAn (Bruker) was used for evaluating enamel mineral density during enamel formation. The reference line between the first and second molars was defined as the approximate transition point from the secretory stage to the mature stage. Furthermore, the enamel regions selected for measurement were divided into the following three areas; (1) Eruption area: Incisors that have erupted and are not surrounded by the alveolar bone. (2) Maturation area: Incisors completely covered by the alveolar bone, extending from the proximal root of the first molar in the mesial direction. (3) Secretory area: Incisors located between the mesial and distal roots of the second molar, surrounded by the alveolar bone. Each region of the maturation and secretory stages of mandibular incisor enamel was defined based on a previous report (Jalali et al. 2015; Suzuki and Bartlett 2019). Enamel mineral density (EMD) was measured in adolescent mice and mature mice in the same regions. Pseudo-colors were rendered onto the 3-D structures using CTvox 3.3.0 (Bruker) to maximize the visualization of the teeth and the enamel.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis of the enamel in mice were performed according to a previously established protocol (Okamoto et al. 2025). Maxillary incisors were embedded in epoxy resin (Epofix cold setting embedding resin) from Electron Microscopy Sciences (Hatfield, PA, USA). Resin-embedded samples were evaluated sagittally, and the specimen surfaces were polished using diamond abrasive sheets (30–0.3 μm grain size) from Maruto Instruments (Tokyo, Japan). The sagittally exposed and polished surfaces were ultrasonically cleaned to remove debris, dried for over 24 h, and then subjected to elemental mapping using SEM–EDX (Xplore 30 EDS), with Aztec Live platform/software (Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK) to determine the constituent elements of the maxillary incisor and their distribution. The analyzed elements included C, O, F, Na, Mg, P, Ca, Fe, and Sr. The region of interest was analyzed by mapping the enamel in the tip aspect. The specimens were subsequently decalcified using 40% phosphoric acid for 40 s. After decalcification, they were thoroughly rinsed under running water, cleaned using an ultrasonic device, and then dried. The dried specimens were gold-coated, and SEM images were obtained using a Quanta 200 SEM (FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA) as secondary electron images.

Mouse incisors were extracted after fluoride treatment for 6 weeks and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, demineralized with 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 3 weeks, and embedded in paraffin. Five μm sagittal sections of the maxillary incisor were made for hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining or immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis after deparaffinization. H&E staining was performed with Harris hematoxylin and Eosin-Y Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) after deparaffinization. For IHC, the sections were deparaffinized, hydrated, and heated overnight in 0.01 M citrate buffer at 60 °C to unmask antigenic sites. Sections were incubated with primary antibody: rabbit anti-KLK4 antibody (1:500) (Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA) or rabbit mAb IgG XP® Isotype negative control (DA1E) (1:200) (Cell signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) followed by incubation with a peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody, Vectastain® Elite® ABC-HRP Kit (PK-6101, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA), and ImmPACT® VIP substrate kit, HRP (SK-4605, Vector Laboratories). Sections were counterstained with methyl green for 4 min at 60 °C. Sections were analyzed using light microscopy (Revolve, ECHO, San Diego, CA, USA). Sections from at least three mice per group were assessed. Histological sections were independently assessed by two investigators who were blinded to the treatment groups, using a semi-quantitative scoring approach. To evaluate pathological changes in ameloblasts, each slide was examined for the presence of abnormal structures, defined as either disruptions in the ameloblast layer or the formation of ectopic cyst-like structures. Mice were classified as positive if at least one abnormal structure was identified, and negative if none were detected. The number of positive and negative cases was recorded for each experimental group. Representative images are shown in the figure (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 6S).

After six weeks of fluoride treatment, urine and serum samples were collected and stored at − 20 °C. Samples were thawed immediately prior to preparation for fluoride measurement.

Urine Samples: 20–50 µL of urine were first diluted 1:1 with ultrapure water, followed by a second 1:1 dilution with total ionic strength adjustment buffer (TISAB). This step adjusted the pH to 5–6, maintained constant ionic strength (Whitford 2017b) and ensured a minimum final volume of 75 µL required for fluoride analysis. The diluted samples were pipetted between the bottom of a Petri dish and the sensing surface of a double-channel fluoride ion-selective electrode (Orion 9609BNWP, Thermo Fisher Scientific) connected to an ion concentration meter (Orion Dual Star pH/ISE Meter, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Serum Samples: Diffusion samples were prepared as previously described (WHitford 2017a). Briefly, 75 µL of serum was added to 3 mL of ultrapure water in the bottom of a 60 × 15 mm Petri dish. A 2 mm hole was made in the lid, and the inner rim was coated with petrolatum. Fifty µL of fluoride-trapping solution (0.05 N NaOH) was dispensed in three drops onto the inside surface of the lid. The lid was sealed to the dish, and a hexamethyldisiloxane (HMDS)-saturated 3 N sulfuric acid solution was injected through the hole. The hole was immediately sealed with petrolatum. HMDS-facilitated diffusion (Taves 1968; Whitford 1994, 1996) was carried out for 4–5 h at room temperature on a variable rocker. After diffusion, the lid was removed, flipped upside down, and 20 µL of 0.2 N acetic acid was added to the NaOH trap to adjust the pH to 5–6. The solution was aspirated with a pipette and adjusted to 75 µL with ultrapure water inside the pipette tip. The 75 µL sample was then carefully pipetted between the bottom of a Petri dish and the sensing surface of a double-channel fluoride ion-selective electrode (Orion 9609BNWP, Thermo Fisher Scientific) connected to an ion concentration meter (Orion Dual Star pH/ISE Meter, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Calibration and Detection Limits: Fluoride calibration standards were prepared and treated identically to urine and serum samples. An eight-point calibration (0.049–200 ppm) for urine, and a five-point calibration (0.06–1.27 ppm) for serum was performed before the recording of each sample batch. Fluoride concentrations below 0.02 ppm were recorded as below the limit of detection (LOD).

Serum was collected after 6 weeks of fluoride treatment. Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) activity was measured using the Alkaline Phosphatase Assay kit (Colorimetric) (Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, serum was reacted with the ALP reaction solution for 60 min at room temperature under protection from light. The absorbance was then measured at 405 nm with a BioTek Synergy LX Multimode Reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Fluoride dose effects within each age group were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. To assess the differential effects of fluoride dose and age group (adolescent vs. mature), a two-way ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post hoc analysis. Student’s t-test was used for direct comparisons between two groups where appropriate. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

The graphical abstract was created using BioRender (https://BioRender.com) (Toronto, Canada).

Fluoride significantly induced dental fluorosis in the maxillary and mandibular incisors, manifesting as a change in tooth color to a chalky white opaque and white spot phenotype, in adolescent groups in a dose-dependent manner, in contrast to mature groups. Figure 1 shows three representative maxillary and mandibular incisors from each group in adolescent (Fig. 1A) and mature mice (Fig. 1B). In the control group (0 ppm fluoride), the enamel of incisors was yellow in both adolescent and mature groups. In adolescent groups (Fig. 1A), fluoride treatment groups (50 ppm, 100 ppm, and 125 ppm) exhibited a change in tooth color to a chalky white opacity, and white spots (arrowhead) in the maxillary and mandibular incisors. This fluoride-mediated tooth color change and white spots were less observed in mature groups (Fig. 1B), compared to adolescent groups.

Figure 2D shows significant main effects of fluoride dose (p < 0.01) and age (p < 0.05), indicating that both fluoride concentration and age significantly influence fluorosis severity, as measured by QLF. These findings suggest that systemic fluoride exposure results in more severe dental fluorosis in adolescent mice than in mature mice.

Previously, we reported that fluorosis teeth exhibit softer enamel with lower mineral and higher protein content (Suzuki et al. 2014a), decreasing enamel microhardness in mouse incisors (Okamoto et al. 2025; Suzuki et al. 2017). The Vickers microhardness was evaluated as a mechanical property of dental enamel (Okamoto et al. 2024). Figure 3 shows the Vickers microhardness of maxillary incisors in adolescent groups (Fig. 3A) and in mature groups (Fig. 3B). Fluoride induced softer enamel, significantly decreasing enamel microhardness (HV) (160.9 ± 91.2 HV at 50 ppm; p < 0.01, 57.9 ± 29.8 HV at 100 ppm; p < 0.01, and 87.5 ± 31.2 HV at 125 ppm; p < 0.01) compared to the control (274.8 ± 11.9 HV) in adolescent mice (Fig. 3A). Whereas, in mature mice, no significant differences in enamel microhardness were observed at any fluoride dose (Fig. 3B). Figure 3C, D present the results of a two-way ANOVA followed by post hoc analysis. In Fig. 3C, enamel microhardness following fluoride treatment (across all doses) was significantly higher in mature mice compared to adolescent mice, indicating that adolescent enamel is more susceptible to fluoride-induced reductions in hardness. Figure 3D shows significant main effects of fluoride dose (p < 0.01) and age (p < 0.01), as well as a significant interaction between fluoride dose and age group (p < 0.01). These results suggest that the fluoride dose–response relationship differs between the two age groups. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that fluoride’s impact on enamel is both dose-dependent and age-dependent—a pattern consistently observed throughout this study and supported by subsequent results.

Hydroxyapatite crystals of mammalian dental enamel are highly organized rod/interrod (prismatic) structures (Goldberg et al. 2014). This regular organization in enamel prisms is disrupted in dental fluorosis enamel (Min et al. 2018). Figure 5 shows representative SEM images of enamel prismatic structures in maxillary incisors. Control groups showed tightly packed bundles of crystallites that form rods (r) and interrods (ir) structures in adolescent (Fig. 5A/B) and mature mice (Fig. 5I/J). Meanwhile, the enamel prism structure was less affected by fluoride at 50 and 100 ppm (Fig. 5K–N) with remaining rods and interrods in mature groups compared to adolescent groups.

Figure 6 shows the ameloblast morphology in the secretory stage (SEC) and the maturation stage (MAT) in maxillary incisors of adolescent and mature mice treated with or without fluoride. The pathological sub-ameloblastic cyst-like formations associated with fluoride exposure suggest damage to ameloblasts, likely resulting from their local detachment or loss. This disruption contributes to severe hypomineralization of the enamel.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is highly expressed in the cells of mineralized tissues and plays a critical role in the formation of hard tissue, including bone metabolism and tooth formation. ALP levels and activity during the developmental phase of childhood and adolescence are significantly higher than in adults (Otero et al. 2011). Supplementary figure S7 shows serum ALP activity in adolescent and mature mice. On day 0 (onset of treatment), ALP activity was significantly higher in adolescent mice (0.097 ± 0.01 µmol/min/ml) compared to mature mice (0.03 ± 0.01 µmol/min/ml) (p < 0.01) (Supplemental Fig. S7A), indicating that adolescent mice are in a developmental phase characterized by active bone and tooth formation. Fluoride significantly increased ALP activities in serum in both adolescent and mature mice compared to controls (0 ppm) on day 42 (p < 0.01) (Supplemental Fig. S7B). Fluoride-mediated ALP activity levels showed a higher trend in adolescent mice (0.23 ± 0.08 µmol/min/ml) compared to mature mice (0.16 ± 0.05 µmol/min/ml) on day 42 (endpoint of treatment).

Infants and young children who are still developing have a greater susceptibility to environmental pollutants, including heavy metals and organic compounds compared to adults (MJ et al. 2012). Previous studies have reported that the fluoride absorption ratio in mineralized tissues is higher in children or younger animals than in adults or older animals (de Carvalho et al. 2008; Institute of Medicine Standing Committee on the Scientific Evaluation of Dietary Reference 1997; Whitford 1999; Whitford and Pashley 1983). However, the age-related susceptibility to fluoride remains unclear. Many animal studies on fluoride and fluorosis utilize relatively younger mice, which are under development (Carvalho et al. 2009; Everett et al. 2002; Hosokawa et al. 2025; Kobayashi et al. 2014; Lu et al. 2019; Lyaruu et al. 2014; Okamoto et al. 2025; Puranik et al. 2015; Suzuki et al. 2017, 2014a, b; Vieira et al. 2005a, b). Meanwhile, reports evaluating the effects of fluoride on older mice (fully matured mice) are limited.

To explore how biological responses to fluoride exposure vary with age, we included both adolescent and mature mice in our study design. While dental fluorosis in humans typically occurs during childhood, the continuously growing incisors of mice offer a unique model for evaluating fluoride effects across different life stages. Importantly, fluoride affects not only enamel formation but also dentinogenesis, reparative dentin processes (Okamoto et al. 2025), and bone formation (Kong et al. 2024), all of which are more active in younger individuals. These biological processes may contribute to the heightened sensitivity to fluoride observed in adolescent mice. These age-dependent differences suggest that younger mice may be more susceptible to fluoride-induced changes, providing valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying fluoride toxicity and its broader implications beyond enamel alone. This study is the first to report detailed age-related differences in the impact of fluoride on tooth development between adolescent and mature mice using mouse dental fluorosis models. We demonstrated that age-related differences in fluoride regulatory mechanisms, specifically in accumulation and excretion, contribute to the varying susceptibility to fluoride toxicity between adolescent and mature mice. These differences were characterized by (1) Variations in fluoride clearance from urine and serum, (2) Differences in the fluoride accumulation ratio in mineralized tissues, including teeth and bones, (3) Divergent levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity.

The age range of adolescent mice and mature mice varies depending on mouse strains and studies (Brust et al. 2015; Fox 2007; JacksonLaboratory 2023; Mytidou et al. 2021; Radulescu et al. 2021; Yuan et al. 2011). In this study, according to the Jackson Laboratory’s life history stages in C57BL/6 J mice (Fox 2007; JacksonLaboratory 2023) 5- to 9-week-old mice were designated as the adolescent group, and 16- to 20-week-old mice were assigned as the mature group. Adolescent mice are physically, behaviorally, and sexually developing, which is approximately equivalent to 12–17 years old in humans (Rose and Howlett 2024). Mature mice are fully transitioned to adulthood, characterized by behavioral maturity and a fully developed body, which is approximately equivalent to 20–30 years old in humans (Fox 2007; JacksonLaboratory 2023; Mytidou et al. 2021). In this study, we focused on the correlation of physiological stages (underdevelopment or fully developed) between mice and humans rather than the correspondence to human ages.

Our study demonstrates that fluoride has severe adverse effects on enamel formation in adolescent mice, including the induction of dental fluorosis, reduced microhardness and enamel mineral density (EMD), suppression of KLK4, and the formation of aprismatic enamel. A two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post hoc analysis revealed significant main effects of both fluoride dose and age, indicating that fluoride’s impact on enamel is influenced by both factors. This dose- and age-dependent pattern was consistently observed across the dataset. Notably, a significant interaction between fluoride dose and age group was found for microhardness, enamel mineral density (EMD), and fluoride concentrations (p < 0.01), suggesting that the fluoride dose–response relationship differs between the two age groups. As shown in Table 1 and supplementary Figure S3, the relative EMD percentage compared to 0 ppm is significantly decreased more in the maturation stage (MAT) compared to the secretory stage (SEC) in adolescent mice (Supplementary Figure S3A), suggesting that the maturation stage is more susceptible to fluoride toxicity. Meanwhile, these stage differences were not significant in mature mice (Supplementary Figure S3B). This suggests that fluoride has a diminished effect on maturation stages in adult mice, contributing to the lower susceptibility observed in adult mice.

On day 0 (onset of treatment) the enamel of adolescent mice was immature, with significantly lower EMD compared to that of mature mice in the maturation stage and Eruption areas (Fig. 8). During the experimental period, EMD significantly increased in adolescent mice from day 0 to 42 (end point), reaching levels compared to those of mature mice by day 42 (Fig. 8B). This suggests that adolescent mice were still developing during fluoride exposure. Moreover, studies examining the daily rate of eruption in incisors in rodents showed that the eruption rate was greatest in younger animals and slowed down with age (Harari et al. 2005; Klevezal and Shchepotkin 2012). The greater incisor eruption rate may contribute to the higher fluoride incorporation in teeth in adolescent mice. Previous studies indicated that younger animals exhibit lower fluoride excretion by the kidneys, with up to 80% being retained due to increased uptake by developing teeth and bones (Ekstrand et al. 1994a, 1994b; Whitford 1999). Our results are consistent with those of previous studies. Fluoride levels in urine and serum were significantly lower in adolescent mice than in mature mice (Fig. 7). Elemental line analysis revealed detectable accumulation levels of fluoride in enamel exclusively in adolescent mice that were treated with 125 ppm fluoride, compared to mature mice (Supplemental Fig. S4B). These results suggest that lower fluoride excretion in urine and greater fluoride accumulation in mineralized tissues during development can lead to a higher degree of fluoride overexposure in adolescent mice, resulting in more severe disturbances in enamel formation in adolescent mice compared to mature mice.

Several molecular mechanisms have been implicated in fluoride toxicity, including the induction of cellular stress—such as oxidative (Suzuki et al. 2015; Suzuki and Bartlett 2014) and endoplasmic reticulum stress (Kubota et al. 2005)—epigenetic modifications that alter gene expression (Deng et al. 2020; Yamashita et al. 2024), and the activation of apoptosis (Fujiwara et al. 2021; Suzuki et al. 2018). However, most of these findings are based on studies conducted in younger animals, and it remains unclear how these mechanisms may differ between adolescent and mature individuals. In this study, we found that serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity is significantly higher in adolescent mice (Supplementary Fig. S7). Previous research has shown that fluoride-induced elevation of ALP activity can stimulate osteoblast function and promote bone formation in both human and animal models (Miao et al. 2005; Perera et al. 2018; Song et al. 2011; Yan et al. 2007). While the specific role of serum ALP activation in the pathogenesis of dental fluorosis remains to be clarified, it may contribute to tooth malformation. The underlying molecular mechanisms, however, are not yet fully understood. In future studies, we aim to investigate age-related differences in fluoride-induced toxicological mechanisms, including cellular stress responses, epigenetic regulation, apoptosis, and the functional significance of ALP activity in dental abnormalities.

While further elucidation is required, beyond dental and skeletal fluorosis, other potential adverse health effects associated with excessive fluoride consumption have been reported. Recent epidemiological and laboratory (animal and tissue culture) studies suggest that overexposure to systemic fluoride is associated with immunological, reproductive, and neurodevelopmental abnormalities in children or younger animals (Guth et al. 2021; Hosokawa et al. 2025; Kampouri et al. 2023; Malin et al. 2024; Taylor et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024). Although our study focused on the effects of excessive fluoride on enamel development, our results provide crucial evidence for understanding the heightened sensitivity of children to systemic fluoride exposure compared to adults. Our animal study results suggest that children of actively growing developmental ages may be at higher risk of overexposure to fluoride.

To date, there are many sources of fluoride for daily consumption (Cantoral et al. 2019). In the United States, the prevalence of dental fluorosis among adolescents aged 16–17 years increased from 29.7% in 2001–2002 to 61.3% in 2011–2012 (Wiener et al. 2018). To effectively prevent dental caries while minimizing the risk of dental fluorosis, the appropriate amount of fluoride should be tailored to an individual’s needs (NIH). For example, the recommended amount of fluoride-containing toothpaste differs between children and adults (American Dental Association Council on Scientific 2014). Topical fluoride use through toothpaste, mouthwash, and professional treatment might be preferable to systemic consumption. Topical fluoride application enables adjustments in fluoride amounts based on age, lifestyle, and caries risk factors, thereby reducing the likelihood of dental fluorosis resulting from excessive systemic fluoride ingestion in developing individuals.  

Our findings demonstrate that adolescent mice are significantly more susceptible to fluoride toxicity, exhibiting greater systemic fluoride accumulation and reduced excretion compared to mature mice. This heightened vulnerability during active growth stages underscores the developmental risks associated with excessive fluoride exposure. The study provides essential evidence to inform oral health strategies that balance caries prevention with the risk of dental fluorosis, emphasizing the need for age-specific fluoride exposure guidelines.
