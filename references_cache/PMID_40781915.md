# Prenatal Characterization of Houge-Janssens Syndrome Type 2: A Case Report and Systematic Review of Fetal Phenotypes Associated With PPP2R1A Mutations.

BACKGROUND: Houge-Janssens syndrome type 2 (HJS2, OMIM 616362) is a rare 
neurodevelopmental disorder caused by pathogenic variants in PPP2R1A, typically 
characterized postnatally by hypotonia, developmental delay, intellectual 
disability, and distinctive craniofacial features.
METHODS: We describe a 28-year-old pregnant woman referred for increased nuchal 
translucency (4.4 mm) and high risk on first trimester screening. Noninvasive 
prenatal testing showed no common aneuploidies. At 23 weeks of gestation, fetal 
ultrasound revealed ventriculomegaly and suspected partial agenesis of the 
corpus callosum. Genetic testing included karyotyping, chromosomal microarray 
analysis (CMA), and trio-based whole exome sequencing (WES).
RESULTS: Karyotype and CMA were normal. WES identified a de novo heterozygous 
missense variant in PPP2R1A, NM_014225.6: c.548G>A (p.R183Q), classified as 
pathogenic. Following genetic counseling, the couple elected to terminate the 
pregnancy. Integrating our findings with 12 previously reported prenatal cases, 
we conducted a systematic review of fetal phenotypes associated with PPP2R1A 
variants. The most common features were ventriculomegaly (92%), agenesis or 
dysgenesis of the corpus callosum (50%), and congenital heart defects (42%).
CONCLUSION: We present the most comprehensive synthesis to date of prenatal 
phenotypes associated with PPP2R1A-related neurodevelopmental disorders. These 
findings provide crucial insights into the prenatal spectrum of HJS2 and 
highlight key sonographic indicators to support early diagnosis and genetic 
counseling.

Houge–Janssens syndrome type 2 (HJS2, OMIM 616362) is a rare autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by global developmental delay, hypotonia, varying degrees of intellectual disability, speech delay, facial dysmorphism, and, in some cases, epilepsy. It was first described by Houge et al. (2015). This syndrome is caused by pathogenic variants in PPP2R1A, a gene located on chromosome 19q13.41, encoding the alpha isoform of the scaffolding subunit A (Aα) of the protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) holoenzyme. PP2A is a major serine/threonine phosphatase involved in essential cellular processes, including growth, differentiation, and apoptosis, and is recognized for its tumor suppressor functions. Recent reviews have emphasized the functional diversity of PP2A holoenzymes—especially the PP2A‐B56 family—and their critical tumor suppressor roles, often counteracted by endogenous inhibitors such as CIP2A or SET in oncogenesis (Johnson et al. 2024; Pavic et al. 2023; Peris et al. 2023).

Somatic PPP2R1A mutations have been widely reported in multiple malignancies, especially gynecologic cancers such as endometrial and ovarian tumors (Calin et al. 2000; Dupont et al. 2010; McConechy et al. 2011; Nagendra et al. 2012; Ross et al. 2022; Shih Ie et al. 2011; Stružinská et al. 2023; Yoshida et al. 2022). These mutations impair PP2A phosphatase activity through dominant‐negative effects, thereby promoting tumorigenesis (Haesen et al. 2016). Structural analyses have shown that variants within A subunit HEAT repeats disrupt B subunit interactions and holoenzyme stability (Padi et al. 2025; Pavic et al. 2023). While the oncogenic roles of PP2A mutations have been well documented, the connection between germline mutations in PPP2R1A and neurodevelopmental phenotypes such as HJS2 has only recently been recognized. The link between PPP2R1A and neurodevelopmental disorders was first identified in 2015, when de novo missense mutations in the gene were reported in individuals with intellectual disability (Fitzgerald et al. 2015). Houge et al. subsequently demonstrated that missense mutations in PPP2R1A disrupt binding to specific PP2A regulatory subunits, thereby impairing substrate‐specific dephosphorylation and implicating PP2A dysfunction in congenital brain malformations (Houge et al. 2015).

To date, approximately 58 cases of PPP2R1A‐related neurodevelopmental disorders have been described, though prenatal phenotypes have been reported in only 12 cases (Baker et al. 2022; Fitzgerald et al. 2015; Gabriel et al. 2022; Hamdan et al. 2017; Houge et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2023; Lei et al. 2023; Lenaerts et al. 2021; Melas et al. 2021; Qian et al. 2023; Ruxmohan et al. 2021; Wallace et al. 2019; Xing et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2020). Here, we describe a prenatal case involving increased nuchal translucency (NT) and a de novo PPP2R1A pathogenic variant identified by whole exome sequencing (WES), and we summarize the fetal clinical features based on existing literature to better delineate the prenatal phenotype of HJS2.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the participant for genetic testing and publication of anonymized findings. Ethics committee approval was not required for this case report under institutional policy.

A 28‐year‐old gravida 1, para 0 woman (last menstrual period: May 30, 2023) presented with no history of first trimester infection, bleeding, or teratogen exposure. First trimester ultrasound at 12 weeks and 3 days of gestation revealed increased nuchal translucency (NT) measuring 4.4 mm with a crown‐rump length (CRL) of 49 mm (Figure 1A). Follow‐up ultrasound at 13 weeks and 4 days showed NT of 3.0 mm and CRL of 63 mm. First trimester combined screening revealed a high risk for trisomy 21 (risk: 1/22; PAPP‐A MoM: 0.18), while noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) reported low risk.

At 22 weeks and 3 days, detailed fetal ultrasonography showed bilateral ventriculomegaly (15 mm left, 20 mm right), “dangling choroid plexus” sign, upward displacement of the cerebellar vermis, indistinct cavum septum pellucidum, partial visualization of the corpus callosum, a Blake's pouch cyst communicating with the fourth ventricle, and suspected holoprosencephaly (Figure 1B‐C). The conus medullaris terminated between L2 and L3. Cardiac structure and function were unremarkable. Estimated fetal age based on femur and biparietal measurements was consistent with 21 weeks. The placenta was grade 0. Amniocentesis was performed at 23 weeks of gestation for genetic evaluation.

G‐banded karyotype analysis was performed on cultured amniotic fluid cells and parental peripheral blood lymphocytes. Cultures were prepared following standard protocols, including hypotonic treatment, fixation, Giemsa staining, and metaphase chromosome analysis at a resolution of 400–550 bands.

Genomic DNA was extracted from 10 mL of uncultured amniotic fluid using the Qiagen genomic DNA isolation kit. DNA was hybridized to the CytoScan 750 K array (Affymetrix, USA), which contains ~200,000 SNP probes and ~550,000 CNV probes. A total of 250 ng DNA was used for labeling and hybridization per manufacturer protocol. Scanning was performed using the Affymetrix 7G scanner, and data were analyzed with Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS) software.

Genomic DNA was extracted from fetal amniotic fluid and parental peripheral blood using a whole blood DNA extraction kit. Exonic regions were enriched using the KAPA HyperExome V2 probes and sequenced on the MGISEQ‐2000 platform (150 bp paired‐end reads). Variant annotation and interpretation were performed according to ACMG guidelines. Candidate pathogenic variants were validated by Sanger sequencing in the trio.

The amino acid sequence of PPP2R1A was retrieved from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/). The 3D protein structure was predicted using AlphaFold 3, selecting the highest confidence model (Model 0). PyMOL software was used to visualize structural changes and hydrogen bond alterations resulting from the variant compared to the wild‐type protein.

Cytogenetic analysis of cultured amniocytes revealed a normal fetal karyotype (46,XN). Chromosomal microarray analysis (SNP array) detected no pathogenic copy number variations. Trio‐based WES identified a heterozygous de novo missense variant in NM_014225.6 (PPP2R1A):c.548G>A (p.R183Q), which was absent in both parental genomes (Figure 2). The variant was validated by Sanger sequencing. This alteration is not present in the gnomAD database and has been previously reported as a de novo variant in four independent cases and once as a likely de novo case, all associated with neurodevelopmental phenotypes, albeit with variable specificity (Baker et al. 2022; Gabriel et al. 2022; Wallace et al. 2019; Xing et al. 2022).

(B) Sanger sequencing confirmed the de novo status of the variant in the trio. (C) Distribution of reported PPP2R1A variants with prenatal phenotypes.

The PPP2R1A gene is highly constrained for missense variation (Z = 4.46, gnomAD v2), indicating intolerance to such changes. According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines, the c.548G>A (p.R183Q) variant meets criteria for pathogenic classification (PM2‐P, PS2‐VS, PS4‐M, and PP2). The pathogenicity was actually proven in the past by several biochemical and functional assays: For this particular variant, Haesen et al. 2016 indeed reported severely affected formation of specific PP2A holoenzymes, decreased B56delta‐associated PP2A phosphatase activity in vitro, and a strong effect on cancer cell–specific growth and on oncogenic signaling. So there is no doubt at all that this is a nonfunctional, likely dominant‐negative variant. PPP2R1A is associated with HJS2 (OMIM 616362), which is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. Following genetic counseling, the couple elected to terminate the pregnancy; fetal autopsy was declined.

Structural modeling of the PPP2R1A protein using AlphaFold revealed that residue Arg183, located within the HEAT5 repeat of the A subunit, forms intramolecular hydrogen bonds with SER219, PRO179, GLN217, and SER187, and an intermolecular hydrogen bond with GLU214 on the B subunit, specifically the serine/threonine protein phosphatase 2A 56 kDa regulatory subunit gamma isoform (PPP2R5C), in the wild‐type conformation. After the R183Q substitution, the hydrogen bond with GLN217 was lost, and no hydrogen bonding was observed between GLN183 and GLU214 on PPP2R5C, suggesting disruption of A–B subunit interaction, potentially impairing PP2A complex assembly and function.

In 2020, our team reported a novel de novo PPP2R1A variant (NM_014225.5, c.656C>T, p.S219L) in a 9‐month‐old male with normal prenatal development. At 6 months of age, the infant presented with focal seizures, characterized by ocular fixation and clonic limb movements. During hospitalization, recurrent seizures were recorded. At 9 months, the child could sit with support and follow simple instructions. Brain MRI showed parenchymal volume loss, widened sulci and fissures, ventriculomegaly, and partial agenesis of the corpus callosum. Auditory brainstem evoked potentials (ABR) indicated both peripheral and central pathway damage. Video EEG revealed focal epilepsy with high‐amplitude delta waves spreading from the frontal lobes (Zhang et al. 2020).

Here, we report a fetal case carrying a de novo heterozygous PPP2R1A variant (c.548G>A, p.R183Q), first identified at 13 weeks gestation by increased nuchal translucency. At 14 weeks, first trimester screening indicated high risk for trisomy 21 (1/22), with decreased PAPP‐A. By 23 weeks, prenatal ultrasound revealed multiple structural abnormalities, including ventriculomegaly, suspected hydrocephalus, and partial agenesis of the corpus callosum. These findings underscore the utility of prenatal genetic testing in expanding the phenotypic spectrum of rare neurodevelopmental syndromes and supporting informed reproductive decision‐making.

Of the 58 known cases of PPP2R1A‐related neurodevelopmental disorders, only 12 have detailed prenatal phenotypic descriptions. Our review of these cases revealed that the most common prenatal feature was ventriculomegaly (92%, 11/12), followed by corpus callosum agenesis or dysgenesis (50%, 6/12), and congenital cardiac anomalies (42%, 5/12). Other prenatal signs included increased NT, microcephaly, macrocephaly, and subarachnoid effusions. These findings suggest that when such structural anomalies are identified prenatally, WES should be considered as a first‐tier test. WES enables comprehensive molecular diagnosis of monogenic causes underlying fetal anomalies and informs both prognosis and recurrence risk counseling.

All 12 reported prenatal cases carried PPP2R1A missense variants, indicating a likely dominant‐negative pathogenic mechanism (Table 1). The p.R183Q variant has now been identified in six fetuses, five of whom had ventriculomegaly and/or congenital heart defects, suggesting its pathogenic relevance in early neurodevelopment. The PPP2R1A protein comprises 15 tandem HEAT (Huntingtin, EF3, A subunit of PP2A, TOR1) repeats, with HEAT repeats 1–8 mediating interaction with regulatory B subunits (Houge et al. 2015). Pathogenic PPP2R1A variants cluster in HEAT repeats 4–7, which variably impair binding to B subunits depending on the mutation and its interacting partner.

Notably, mutations such as P179R, R183W (both in HEAT5), and W257C (in HEAT7) significantly disrupt the binding of PPP2R1A to the WAVE regulatory complex, essential for cytoskeletal dynamics and cell migration (Taylor et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2023). Among the 13 prenatally identified cases, 11 had pathogenic variants in HEAT5 (Figure 3, Table 1), consistent with a dominant‐negative mechanism that underlies disrupted PP2A function and neurodevelopmental abnormalities (Houge et al. 2015; Lenaerts et al. 2021).

Structural impact of the PPP2R1A p.R183Q variant on subunit interactions. (A) Wild‐type Arg183 forms hydrogen bonds with SER219, PRO179, GLN217, SER187 (A subunit), and GLU214 (B subunit). (B) Mutant Gln183 retains bonds with SER219, SER187, ALA186, but loses interaction with GLN217 and no longer forms a bond with GLU214 on the B subunit.

In prenatal diagnostics, PPP2R1A variants are predominantly de novo and often lack highly specific ultrasound features. Given the time‐sensitive and accuracy‐driven nature of prenatal genetic counseling, trio‐based WES is increasingly favored. It does not require preselection of candidate genes and allows detection of a broad range of neurodevelopmental disorder‐associated mutations. The present case contributes novel mutational evidence for PPP2R1A‐related HJS2, expanding the prenatal phenotype and offering molecular insight for future diagnosis and clinical management.
