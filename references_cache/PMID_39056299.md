# Do Palatoplasty Procedures Resolve Hypernasality as Effectively as Pharyngoplasty Procedures in Patients with 22q11.2 Deletion Syndrome?
**Authors:** Williams JL, Perry JL, Singh DJ, Sitzman TJ
**Journal:** Cleft Palate Craniofac J (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1177/10556656241266365](https://doi.org/10.1177/10556656241266365)

## Content

ObjectiveCompare the effectiveness of palatoplasty and pharyngoplasty procedures at resolving hypernasality in patients with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (22q).DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingMetropolitan children's hospital.PatientsFourteen patients with 22q presenting for management of velopharyngeal insufficiency.InterventionsPalatoplasty or pharyngoplasty procedure.Main Outcome MeasureResolution of hypernasality 12 months postoperatively.ResultsBoth procedure groups had a mean preoperative velopharyngeal gap of 6.2 mm during phonation. No patient who underwent palatoplasty achieved resolution of hypernasality; 1/7 patients had worse hypernasality, 4/7 had no change, and 2/7 had improved hypernasality. In contrast, hypernasality was resolved in 6/7 patients in the pharyngoplasty group, which was significantly (P = .03) higher than the palatoplasty group.ConclusionsIn patients with 22q, palatoplasty procedures may be less effective than pharyngoplasty procedures at resolving hypernasality. This may be due to underlying anatomic or physiologic differences, such as increased pharyngeal depth and hypodynamic muscles.

22q11.2 deletion (22q) is a multisystemic syndrome–the most common microdeletion syndrome in humans–caused by a deletion of genetic material that is frequently characterized by congenital heart defects, immunodeficiency, and palatal abnormalities.1–3 Additionally, most people (95%) with 22q present with a speech-language disorder.4 Many children with 22q may not begin using first words until after age 2 and their verbal output may be limited until 3 to 5 years of age.5 Resonance and speech sound disorders may also present and include hypernasality and compensatory misarticulation errors that require speech therapy.6,7 Compensatory errors are generally articulated as posterior-based productions and in the absence of velopharyngeal movement.

Velopharyngeal inadequacy (VPI) is the failure of the velopharyngeal mechanism to separate the oral and nasal cavities when articulating oral speech sounds and manifest as hypernasality (resonance disorder)8 and possible speech sound disorders (compensatory errors). Approximately 16% of people with 22q have a submucous cleft palate, 11% have an overt cleft palate, and 5% have a bifid uvula.9 While not all people with 22q have a palatal abnormality, up to 80% of people with 22q present with VPI. People with 22q can have multiple anatomic/physiologic differences of the velopharynx that contribute to VPI. These differences may include palatal clefting, increased pharyngeal depth due to velopharyngeal disproportion, decreased velar thickness, thin levator veli palatini muscles, discontinuity of the levator veli palatini muscles, decreased velar length, and pharyngeal hypotonia.10–15 To improve resonance, the anatomical difference must be addressed surgically. The optimal surgical procedure for addressing VPI in people with 22q remains controversial.

Surgeons typically treat VPI with one of two procedures: pharyngoplasty or palatoplasty.16 Pharyngoplasty procedures–pharyngeal flap and sphincter pharyngoplasty–are reported to resolve hypernasality in most patients17; however, there is a risk of obstructive sleep apnea.18–20 This risk is particularly concerning for people with 22q, as they have a higher prevalence at baseline of obstructive sleep apnea compared to the general population secondary to decreased upper airway tone and presence of congenital cardiac malformations.21–23 Palatoplasty procedures– Furlow Double-Opposing Z-Plasty, Buccal Myomucosal Flaps, and straight-line intravelar veloplasty (IVVP) repairs–directly address anatomical insufficiencies by lengthening the palate and retro-positioning the levator veli palatine muscle (LVP).24–27 Some authors have reported improved resonance using a combination procedure of a Furlow and sphincter pharyngoplasty.28,29 Palatoplasty procedures are associated with a lower risk of obstructive sleep apnea,30 making them an appealing option to manage VPI in patients with 22q.

A systematic review of speech outcomes in patients with 22q found that palatoplasty resolved hypernasality in only 11–18% of patients.31 However, new research has emerged that reports palatoplasty resolve hypernasality in up to 75% of patients with 22q.32,33 These recent findings suggest that palatoplasty might be preferred to manage VPI in patients with 22q.

Based on prior reported speech outcome findings, we transitioned from pharyngoplasty to palatoplasty to treat VPI in patients with 22q. We hypothesized that palatoplasty would be as effective as pharyngoplasty at resolving hypernasality. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the speech outcomes for patients with 22q who underwent a palatoplasty or pharyngoplasty to treat VPI at our medical facility.

A single center retrospective cohort study was performed to compare the effectiveness of palatoplasty and pharyngoplasty surgeries at resolving hypernasality in patients with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome. All speech recordings and MRI examinations were completed as standard clinical care. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained prior to the onset of this study.

Patients meeting all the following criteria were included: (1) diagnosis of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome; (2) communicated verbally using at least 3-word utterances; (3) presence of hypernasality on preoperative speech evaluation; (4) MRI completed between July 2018 and June 2021; (5) underwent palatoplasty or pharyngoplasty to treat hypernasality. Patients were excluded if (1) they had a pharyngeal flap in place at the time of preoperative evaluation and/or (2) profound sensor-ineural hearing loss. All patients reported their primary language as English. All speech evaluations were completed in English.

All patients were seen through VPI clinic for evaluation of resonance and speech. During the study period, 25 patients with 22q underwent a preoperative speech evaluation with an associated MRI. Eight patients did not undergo surgery and 3 patients had a pharyngeal flap in place at the time of MRI, excluding them from this study. The remaining 14 patients were included in this study. Seven patients underwent a palatoplasty: 6 underwent a Furlow Double-Opposing Z-Plasty and 1 underwent a straight-line mucosal incision with IVVP. Seven patients underwent a pharyngoplasty: 4 underwent a pharyngeal flap and 3 underwent a sphincter pharyngoplasty.

The diagnosis of 22q was confirmed through chart review of genetic testing which could have included: whole genome chromosomal microarray, whole genome oligonucleotide array, single nucleotide polymorphism microarray, chromosomal microarray analysis, and fluorescence in situ hybridization.

Patients were seen by a cleft team speech-language pathologist (SLP) pre- and postoperatively for a clinical visit during which they completed a speech evaluation. Patients’ guardians provided consent for clinical treatment which included obtaining audio recordings. The perceptual speech evaluation included an audio-recorded speech sample with 2 to 3 min of elicited conversation, counting, and a sentence or phrase repetition task using the American English Sentence Sample or American English Phrase Sample as a part of the Cleft Audit Protocol for Speech-Augmented-Americleft Modification (CAPS-A-AM).34 Recordings were collected in private clinic rooms using a laptop with a plug and play Logitech for Creators Blue Snowball Microphone placed on a table within 12 inches of the patient.

Nasometry was completed during the clinical speech evaluation using a Nasometer 6450 model II (Kay Pentax, USA). The Nasometer provides a percentage from 0 to 100 called nasalance which is the quotient of nasal sound pressure level to nasal + oral sound pressure level. Nasalance is related to the perception of resonance in speech. The five picture-cued subtests from the MacKay-Kummer Simplified Nasometric Assessment Procedures Test-Revised were administered including: bilabial plosives, lingual-alveolar plosives, velar plosives, sibilant fricatives, and nasals.35

All postoperative speech evaluations for this study were obtained at least 5 months after surgery but no more than 14 months after surgery. Patients were recalled at 6- and 12-months post-operation for assessment. Some patients were lost to follow-up after their 6-month postoperative assessment. For some of the patients in the palatoplasty group, the family elected to pursue additional surgical management prior to the 12-month postoperative speech evaluation. If a patient received multiple evaluations during this time frame, the final speech evaluation was used for analysis.

All recordings were made during a clinical evaluation at least 6 months prior to being re-rated.36 The play order of the samples was randomized using the randomize tool in Microsoft Excel.37 All recorded speech samples were rated by a single SLP with more than 9 years of experience assessing VPI. The rater was blinded to participants’ medical and surgical histories, including whether the sample was obtained pre- or postoperatively. A subset of samples (36%) was re-rated to calculate intra-rater reliability. The CAPS-A-AM rating scale was used to rate the severity of speech parameters related to VPI, including hypernasality and audible nasal emission.34 Hypernasality was rated on a 5-point ordinal scale: none, minimal, mild, moderate, severe. Audible nasal emission was rated on a 3-point ordinal scale: absent, occasionally present, frequently present. The presence of compensatory articulation errors was identified from the patient’s speech evaluation in the medical record and were classified as present or absent.

Patients completed a whole head MRI on a 3T Phillips Scanner using a fully awake, non-sedated, non-contrast protocol. The imaging protocol included a high-resolution T2-weighted turbo-spin-echo 3D sequence obtained at rest, followed by sagittal and oblique coronal T2 images obtained during sustained /i/ (“eee”) phonation. An SLP with over 15 years of experience in MRI evaluations of velopharyngeal anatomy performed the MRI measurements. The SLP was not involved in the direct care of any of the patients. Intra-rater reliability was calculated for measurement of continuous variables. The rater re-rated all samples to calculate intra-rater reliability. Continuous variables measured in millimeters included38: (1) velar length—distance from the posterior nasal spine to the tip of the uvula; (2) velar thickness; (3) effective velar length—linear distance from the posterior border of the hard palate to the point of levator muscle insertion into the velum; (4) pharyngeal depth—posterior nasal spine to the posterior pharyngeal wall; (5) velar gap during phonation. Ratio measures included: velopharyngeal ratio and effective velopharyngeal ratio. Categorical measures included levator veli palatini continuity and closure pattern. Supplemental Figure 1 outlines anatomical landmarks and how measurements were taken.

Patients were treated by one of three surgeons at the hospital. All three surgeons are certified by the American Board of Plastic Surgery and have surgically managed VPI for over 10 years. During the first half of the study period surgeons performed sphincter pharyngoplasty for patients with coronal closure patterns and pharyngeal flap for patients with sagittal closure pattern, circular closure pattern, or minimal to no palate movement. During the second half of the study period, surgeons performed palatoplasty for all patients. Each study period was relatively equal in length of time.

The data were summarized using frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and using mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. Hypernasality resolution was coded as a binary variable. Consistent with prior hypernasality outcomes research, a postoperative rating of none or minimal was defined as resolved.39 A postoperative rating of mild, moderate, or severe was defined as not resolved. Group comparisons were conducted via Fisher’s exact or Chi-squared test for categorical and Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare pre- and postoperative changes on MRI for patients who underwent a palatoplasty operation. The significance level was set at 0.05. Intra-rater reliability for speech ratings was calculated using a linear weighted Fleiss’ Kappa. Intra-rater reliability for continuous measurements on MRI were calculated using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software package STATA 17.40

The mean age at the time of preoperative evaluation was 11.39 (range 4.6 to 21.4) years for patients who underwent a palatoplasty and 10.75 (range 3.6 to 17.3) years for patients who underwent a pharyngoplasty. There was no statistically significant difference between groups for age (P = .95), gender, race, or ethnicity. Detailed patient demographics by procedure type is reported in Table 1.

Intra-rater reliability for hypernasality was k = 0.65 (SE 0.06) indicating substantial agreement. Intra-rater reliability for audible nasal emission was k = 0.51 (SE 0.10) indicating moderate agreement. Percent agreement for ratings was 91% for hypernasality and 80% for audible nasal emission. Intra-rater reliability for MRI measurements was ICC = 0.99.

Of the 7 patients who underwent a palatoplasty, 5 patients completed both a pre- and postoperative MRI as standard clinical care due to concern for persistent hypernasality and for further treatment planning. The pre- and postoperative MRI scans were used to assess anatomical changes following palatoplasty.

On postoperative MRI, there were favorable anatomic changes despite the lack of improvement in hypernasality. Velar length increased by a mean of 5.2 mm (SD = 2.0). Effective velar length was increased by a mean of 5.4 mm (SD = 4.0), indicating successful retro-positioning of levator veli palatini. Velar thickness increased by a mean of 1.4 mm (SD = 1.4). Even though velar length increased, velar thickness increased and the levator veli palatini muscles were more in anatomical position, the mean gap size during phonation only decreased by a mean of 2.3 mm (SD = 4.3). This resulted in a residual postoperative mean gap size of 4.4 mm during phonation, which has been shown to be related to the presence of hypernasality.41

In the present study, pharyngoplasty was more effective than palatoplasty at resolving hypernasality in patients with 22q. Both groups of patients had a preoperative mean velopharyngeal gap of 6.2 mm during phonation and there was no significant difference between groups for demographic variables or anatomical measures of the velopharynx. In the pharyngoplasty group, all patients had improved resonance compared to only 29% of patients in the palatoplasty group. One patient (14%) in the palatoplasty group had a blinded rating of normal hypernasality both pre- and postoperatively. No other patients in the palatoplasty group had hypernasality resolution. In contrast, 86% of patients in the pharyngoplasty group had resolution of hypernasality.

A systematic review of resonance outcomes in patients with 22q found palatoplasty resolved hypernasality in 11–18% of patients.31 Our findings were consistent with the systematic review, as only 14% of patients in the palatoplasty group had a normal postoperative hypernasality rating; however, it should be noted the patient was rated on blinded review as having minimal hypernasality both pre- and postoperatively. Most patients (57%) in the palatoplasty group had no change in their hypernasality rating postoperatively and in one case hypernasality was judge more severe. The preoperative gap size on MRI ranged from 1.2 mm to 11.5 mm in the palatoplasty group. Prior research found a correlation between gap size measured during sustained phonation on MRI and perceptual ratings of hypernasality.41 The lack of improved hypernasality postoperatively in this group resulted in several families wanting to pursue additional surgical management. Subsequently these patients underwent a postoperative MRI for further treatment planning.

On postoperative MRI following palatoplasty, velar length increased by a mean of 5.2 mm, the levator veli palatini was more correctly positioned and velar thickness increased. Our postoperative MRI findings showed that palatoplasty lengthened the palate in patients with 22q and anatomical changes were maintained one year postoperatively. However, despite achieving more favorable anatomy, a mean velopharyngeal gap of 4.4 mm remained during phonation.40–43 While velar length was increased, the lack of decrease in gap size during phonation suggests that other underlying anatomical differences—such as increased pharyngeal depth and hypodynamic levator veli palatini muscles—may make palatoplasty less effective in this population. Further research is needed to identify what anatomical markers on MRI would predict hypernasality resolution using palatoplasty in patients with 22q. These findings also highlight the need for future research to investigate the possibility of a neurological etiology of VPI in patients with 22q in addition to the established anatomical etiology.44

The impetus in the present investigation for using a palatoplasty procedure was undertaken following two recent studies reporting high rates of hypernasality resolution following palatoplasty in patients with 22q.32,33 The present study employed additional experimental control measures to address methodological concerns in prior studies.45 First, the two surgical groups were nearly identical, with no statistically significant differences between groups for preoperative anatomical measures of the velopharynx. Second, speech outcomes were evaluated in a consistent fashion: the same speech sample was collected pre- and postoperatively for all patients; the outcome for all patients was assessed in the same postoperative interval, and degree of hypernasality was blindly rated. Our findings contradict those reported by Brooker et al. who reported an aggregate of 351 patients; however only 37 patients were identified as presenting with 22q.32 In addition, Bezuhly et al. reported their experience treating 78 patients with submucous cleft palate, including 23 patients with 22q.33 They performed Furlow Double-Opposing Z-Plasty on the 9 patients with 22q who had a closure rating of 0.7 or greater, with all other patients (n = 14) receiving a superiorly based pharyngeal flap. The median time to normal resonance for patients with 22q who underwent a Furlow Double-Opposing Z-Plasty was 150 weeks, approximately 3 years after surgery.

The extended time to normal resonance reported by Bezuhly et al. raises a subtle but important aspect that should be addressed with patients prior to surgery – how long are they willing to wait to achieve normal resonance? This issue was also discussed in a research study by Mehendale et al. The researchers evaluated results of palatoplasty for treating submucous cleft palate in patients with 22q.43 They reported 6 of 25 patients (24%) receiving palatoplasty achieved hypernasality resolution, but this was measured at a mean follow-up of 3.6 years (range 0.7 to 12.7 years). In that series 11 of the 25 patients went on to receive Hynes pharyngoplasty, with a mean time between the two operations of 1.8 years (range 0.6 to 5.9 years). The extended time to achieve normal resonance with this stepwise approach to VPI management further brings into focus the need to discuss patient’s expectations for time to achieve normal speech when considering palatoplasty for management of VPI, particularly for this clinical population.

Patients with 22q that have persistent hypernasality after palatoplasty often have a pharyngoplasty. D’Antonio et al. reported all four patients with 22q in their study who underwent a Furlow Z-Plasty required additional surgery.46 Ghanem et al., in a 10-year follow-up study, found that 59% of patients with 22q who received anatomical muscle dissection for VPI treatment needed further surgery.47 These findings underscore the importance of informing patients and their families about the possible need for additional surgery following palatoplasty. Additionally, the role of speech therapy must be discussed if there are compensatory articulation errors present as a part of the education provided to families.

In summary, given that palatoplasty has been shown to be less effective for treating VPI in this population and many patients with 22q eventually require pharyngoplasty, surgeons and medical teams may consider recommending pharyngoplasty as the primary procedure to treat VPI in patients with 22q.

These data must be interpreted in the context of the study design. The data were collected at a single institution and the sample size was small. Replication in a larger, more diverse cohort–ideally with randomization of patients to reduce potential for bias–needs to be conducted. The present study did not compare obstructive sleep apnea incidence between groups, since not all patients underwent pre- and postoperative polysomnography; consequently, results are confined to comparing speech outcomes of the procedures. Additionally, future research should assess if there are differences in quality-of-life measures between the two procedure types on caregiver and/or patient expectations.

In this study, pharyngoplasty was more effective than palatoplasty in resolving hypernasality in patients with 22q. Patients who underwent palatoplasty achieved an increase in velar length, but this did not lead to resolution of hypernasality, suggesting that underlying anatomical differences such as increased pharyngeal depth and hypodynamic muscles may make palatoplasty less effective at resolving hypernasality in patients with 22q.
