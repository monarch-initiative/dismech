# IKAROS protein stability is regulated by its early N-terminal region and C-terminal dimerization domain.
**Authors:** Klangkalya N, Esteve-Sole A, Gil Silva AA, Stoddard JL, Niemela JE, Prader S, Dueckers G, Igel L, Niehues T, Stewart-Bates BC, Mousallem T, Fleisher TA, Rosenzweig SD, Kuehn HS
**Journal:** Clin Immunol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.clim.2025.110469](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clim.2025.110469)

## Content

IKAROS, encoded by IKZF1, is a six zinc-finger (ZF) transcription factor integral to lymphocyte development and function. IKZF1 mutations affecting DNA-binding (ZF1-4) and dimerization (ZF5-6) have been extensively reported and result in human disease. Herein, we investigated IKZF1 mutations affecting protein stability. We identified ten individuals in three families carrying IKZF1 mutations mapping either to the pre-ZF1 area (D22N), or the dimerization domain (M494Vfs*86, Y503*) presenting with infections, immune dysregulation and/or lymphoproliferation with incomplete clinical penetrance. IKAROS expression was reduced in all mutation-carrier evaluated. Protein stability was decreased for D22N, V52L (another pre-ZF1 variant reported in COSMIC), Y503* and Del1-116, a laboratory-designed mutant encompassing the pre-ZF1 area. Mutants Y503* and Del1-116 also exhibited other impaired functions. IKAROS N-terminal pre-ZF1 area, encompassing a previously uncharacterized protein stability-associated region (PSAR), is crucial for IKAROS stability. Variants in the IKAROS PSAR leading to decreased protein stability and IKAROS haploinsufficiency seem sufficient to result in immune defects and IKAROS-associated diseases.

IKAROS, a zinc finger (ZF) transcription factor encoded by the IKZF1 gene, plays a pivotal role in shaping the development and function of the hematological and immune systems. Its influence spans the entire lifecycle of immune cells in both human and mice, from the earliest stages of blood cell formation in the fetal liver and adult bone marrow through the maturation of B and T lymphocytes.[1–3] IKAROS’ functionality is determined by its structural components, primarily the N-terminal DNA-binding domain (ZF1-ZF4) and the C-terminal dimerization domain (ZF5-ZF6).[4] The first three ZFs enable sequence-specific DNA binding and targeting to pericentromeric heterochromatin (PC-HC), while the fourth enhances binding specificity.[5, 6] IKAROS demonstrates a strong affinity for particular DNA motifs, such as GGGAA, which is essential for its transcriptional regulation.[7] The C-terminal ZFs mediate homo- and heterodimerization between IKAROS isoforms, and with other family members like HELIOS, AIOLOS, EOS, and PEGASUS.[8–10] Mutations affecting these crucial domains can drastically impair IKAROS functions, leading to different forms of IKAROS-associated diseases.

IKZF1 mutations are associated with a spectrum of primary immunodeficiencies (PIDs)/inborn errors of immunity (IEI) and hematological disorders causing increased susceptibility to infections, immune dysregulation, and malignancy.[11] These mutations fall into four categories: haploinsufficiency, dimerization defective, dominant negative, and gain-of-function. Each mechanism produces distinct immunological and clinical phenotypes, from mild antibody deficiencies to severe, combined immunodeficiencies.[11–16] These wide range of IKAROS-associated diseases highlights the protein’s crucial role in maintaining a functional and healthy immune system.

Protein stability is a critical factor in the function and activity of IKAROS. The C-terminal dimerization domain is known to contribute to its structural integrity, and mutations in these area can result in protein misfolding, increased degradation, and impaired cellular functions.[13] Furthermore, protein stability is a process tightly regulated by post-translational modifications (PTMs) and protein interactions, and disruptions of this balance have been associated with a variety of diseases, including inflammatory, cancer, and metabolic conditions.[17]

Recently, a study on AIOLOS, another member of the IKAROS family of transcription factors, demonstrated that its pre-ZF1 N-terminal region plays a crucial role in maintaining protein stability and function.[18] This led us to investigate whether a similar region in IKAROS might also influence stability.

In this work, we present clinical and in-vitro evidence that patients carrying deleterious variants affecting IKAROS N-terminal region upstream of ZF1, herein designated as IKAROS protein stability-associated region (PSAR), as well as in the dimerization domain, show reduced IKAROS protein stability and are associated with human disease.

Informed consent was obtained from all research subjects or their guardians involved in this study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki under institutional review board-approved protocols at National Institutes of Health/National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases protocol.

PBMCs from patients and all available relatives were immunophenotyped, and genomic DNA was isolated from these cells for whole-exome sequencing, with prioritized IKZF1 variants confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Plasmids were engineered to express various IKZF1 variants, including patient-associated mutations or targeted deleted regions. Studies were performed on patient or transfected cells, including analyses of IKAROS protein expression, co-immunoprecipitations (IP), immunofluorescence, electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), and protein stability by cycloheximide chase, as previously described.[13] Further details can be found in the Supplementary Materials section.

The index patient (A.II.1) is a 5-year-old male who developed benign recurrent cervical lymphadenopathy, typically worsening during infections. Two lymph node biopsies were performed, both revealing primarily reactive lymphocytic infiltrates with no evidence of malignancy. Lymphocyte phenotyping revealed reduced CD8+ T cells, CD19+ B cells, and switched memory B cells. Immunoglobulin levels were normal, but vaccine protective titers were not assessed because the patient has not been vaccinated.

The index and his father (A.I.1) carry an IKZF1:c.64G>A (p.D22N) heterozygous variant. Both the patient’s father and mother are healthy. Research genetic material was available from A.I.1, A.II.1 (index), and his mother; cells were available from A.I.1.

B.I.1, the index patient, is a 51-year-old male, who was diagnosed with anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-negative vasculitis at the age 37. He experienced chronic, recurrent pulmonary symptoms, which were investigated through a pulmonary CT scan, bronchoscopy, and lung biopsy. The findings were consistent with bronchiolitis obliterans and chronic interstitial lymphocytic pneumonia, both considered to be due to inflammatory conditions associated with vasculitis. The patient also presented other symptoms of immune dysregulation, as chronic aphthosis. No recurrent, severe or opportunistic infections were reported. B-cell lymphopenia, low serum IgG and IgA levels and impaired response to Pneumovax (23-valent polysaccharide pneumococcal vaccine) were detected. Over the course of his disease, he received several immunomodulatory drugs, including prednisolone, cyclosporine, tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, and azathioprine, along with subcutaneous immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgGRT).

The index and his two children carry an IKZF1 c.1480_1481delAT; (p.M494Vfs*86) heterozygous variant. His 18-year-old son (B.II.1) is clinically asymptomatic, while his daughter (B.II.2) has been diagnosed with ANCA-negative vasculitis as her father. She has been treated with dapsone and subcutaneous IgGRT. Both siblings exhibit B-cell lymphopenia, borderline low IgG levels, and poor specific antibody responses to protein or polysaccharide vaccine antigens. The index patient’s father was reported to have IgA and IgM deficiency; however, no related symptoms were noted. He passed away from alcohol-induced liver cirrhosis as described in a previous report.[19] Research genetic material was available from B.I.1 (index), B.II.1 and B.II.2; cells were available from B.II.1 and B.II.2.

The index patient (C.II.1) is a 21-year-old woman who had recurrent ear infections as an infant, and at age 16 years was diagnosis with a high-risk B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Her leukemia treatment was followed by multiple complications. The patient suffered from acute vascular necrosis of the knees due to corticosteroid treatment, and an episode of intrathecal methotrexate toxicity. She also developed recurrent cutaneous manifestations, ranging from toe cellulitis to scattered lesions requiring admission and intravenous antibiotics treatment, and a severe case of necrotizing fasciitis of the left breast requiring emergent mastectomy. Diagnostic findings varied; some yielding positive microbial cultures (e.g., Group A Streptococci in necrotizing fasciitis, Escherichia coli in a left hip infection), others aseptic. Histopathological findings were not always conclusive either. Her most recent bone marrow biopsy showed no residual disease. Immunological profiling demonstrated normal total T cells with an inverted CD4/CD8 ratio, decreased switched memory B cells and impaired response to polysaccharide antigens. The index, her mother and her three siblings carry an IKAROS c.1509C>G; (p.Y503*) heterozygous variant. The patient’s mother (C.I.1) has a history of breast cancer, currently in remission. One of her sisters (C.II.2) was diagnosed with specific antibody deficiency in childhood and treated with IgRT, her other sister (C.II.4) and brother (C.II.3) remain asymptomatic. Research genetic material was available from the whole family; cells were only available from C.I.1 and C.II.1 (index).

Due to limited PBMCs sample availability for families A and B, we used T-cell blasts or EBV-transformed B cells to evaluate IKAROS expression.

IKAROS protein expression was assessed in PBMCs or EBV-transformed B cells derived from patients and healthy controls (HCs) using immunoblotting (Figure 1B). The cells from patients carrying IKZF1 variants D22N, M494fs and Y503* IKAROS showed a marked decreased in IKAROS expression, with a mean expression level of 29, 51 and 44% of that in HCs, respectively. The mutant proteins from the M494fs and Y503* variants were not detected. Reduced IKAROS protein expression was confirmed by flow cytometry in the EBV-transformed B cells from the D22N (A.I.1) and M494Vfs*86 (B.II.2), consistent with the immunoblotting results (Supplemental Figure 1A). We further investigated IKAROS expression in B- or T-cells (Supplemental Figure 1B). In B cells from patients with the Y503* variant, flow cytometry revealed reduced IKAROS protein levels as indicated by mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). As cells from the M494Vfs*86 had to undergo four days transcontinental shipping for research evaluation, T-cell blasts were generated before testing. Flow cytometric analysis of these cells also demonstrated reduced IKAROS protein levels. cDNA sequencing of total PBMCs from the patients showed that the mutant transcript allele was present at comparable levels to the WT allele, ruling out mRNA decay causing the reduced IKAROS protein expression (Figure 1C). Further quantitative PCR from EBV-B cell lines (A.I.1 and B.II.2) showed comparable IKZF1 levels between HCs and the patients (Supplemental Figure 1C). These findings suggest that the decreased protein levels are likely due to protein instability rather than a transcriptional defect.

To assess IKAROS protein stability, HEK293T-transfected cells expressing WT or mutant IKAROS were treated with cycloheximide, a protein synthesis inhibitor. For the cycloheximide chase assay, an 18 hours-incubation time was selected as an intermediate midpoint after evaluating time points between 4–24 hours. Due to the intrinsic genetic complexity of mutant IKAROS c.1480_1481delAT leading to a M494V missense change and a frameshift resulting in 86 missense amino acids before finding a neo stop codon, only the D22N and Y503* mutants were generated and tested. Both D22N and Y503* showed decreased protein stability after cycloheximide treatment, with protein levels reduced by 44% for both mutants, compared to a 27% reduction in WT IKAROS (Figure 2). These results strongly suggest that the decreased protein expression detected in the patients is likely dependent on reduced IKAROS protein stability.

While mutation D22N maps to the pre-ZF1 area, a region of unrecognized biologic function, Y503* directly impacts ZF6, crucial for homo- and heterodimerization for IKAROS and its family members. When homodimerization was investigated, mutant protein D22N dimerized with WT IKAROS at levels comparable to the WT-WT interaction. In contrast, mutant Y503* was unable to fully bind with WT IKAROS, although a residual dimerization band was detected (Figure 3A). Since IKAROS also forms heterodimers with other IKAROS family members, we also performed co-immunoprecipitation assays between mutant Y503* and WT HELIOS, AIOLOS, and EOS. As observed with the impaired homodimerization, mutant Y503* was unable to fully heterodimerize with WT HELIOS, AIOLOS and EOS. Of note, Y503* also retained some residual heterodimerization capacity likely through a preserved ZF5 but abnormal ZF6 function, where mutation Y503* sits (ZF6: amino acids 490–514). These results demonstrate that Y503* leads to an abnormal homo- and heterodimerization capacity not only impacting on IKAROS functions but also on the functions of the other IKAROS family members with which it interacts.

To further assess the impact of these mutations, WT or mutant IKAROS proteins were expressed in NIH3T3 cells to evaluate pericentric heterochromatin (PC-HC) localization. IKAROS D22N displayed a punctate staining pattern similar to that of WT IKAROS. Conversely, the mutant Y503* exhibited a diffuse staining pattern throughout the nucleus, with no PC-HC localization, displaying a loss of IKAROS-specific pericentromeric targeting capability. In co-transfection experiments, cells expressing both WT and Y503*, the Y503* mutant maintained its diffuse staining pattern but not interfering with the PC-HC localization of the WT IKAROS, ruling out a dominant negative effect (Figure 3B).

To evaluate the ability of IKAROS mutants to bind DNA, EMSA were performed (Figure 3C). These experiments showed that the D22N mutant retained the ability to bind DNA target sequence comparable to the WT protein. In contrast, the Y503* mutant exhibited decreased DNA binding, suggesting that impaired IKAROS dimerization likely leads to a loss of this functional activity.

IKAROS is known to undergo PTMs, one of which is SUMOylation. SUMOylation influences numerous IKAROS functions, as nuclear transport, transcription regulation and protein stability.[13, 20] Thus, we investigated SUMOylation on the D22N and the Y503* IKAROS mutants. Our findings revealed similar to WT SUMOylation for D22N, while a reduced and abnormal SUMO1 interaction pattern for Y503* (Figure 3D). These findings demonstrate that the disruption of the dimerization domain interferes with normal IKAROS SUMOylation, a process related to protein stability.

To further characterize the IKAROS N-terminal pre-ZF1 region, we generated eight single amino acid missense expression vectors recapitulating variants mapping to this region that have been previously associated with hematologic diseases and cancers in Cosmic: i.e., P18T, E29D, M31V, P34S, S41L, V52L, R69H and D81N.[21] While none of these variants affected DNA binding by EMSA, V52L like D22N was associated with a significantly decreased protein stability without affecting PC-HC localization either (Figure 4A–D). These data show that at least two missense variants in the pre-ZF1 region, spanning from amino acids 1–116, affect protein stability without impacting on DNA binding or PC-HC localization. Hereafter, we designated this area as IKAROS protein stability-associated region (PSAR).

To assess the functional impact of IKAROS PSAR as a whole, we engineered a plasmid devoid of IKAROS N-terminal 116 amino acids (Del 1–116) but retaining the full DNA binding (ZF1–4) and dimerization (ZF5–6) domains. Protein expression analysis confirmed both the WT and Del 1–116 constructs were efficiently translated, with the deletion mutant showing the expected lower molecular weight. Similar to D22N and V52L missense variants, the Del 1–116 mutant showed a significantly lower stability/increased degradation when compared to the WT, with a 67% mutant protein reduction versus a 37% reduction in WT protein in the cycloheximide chase assay (Figure 5A). Of note, while the D22N and V52L mutant proteins only showed a defective protein stability, the Del 1–116 mutant presented impaired PC-HC localization, as well as different DNA binding and SUMOylation patterns when compared to the WT control. (Figure 5B, C and D). These defects may likely reflect the impact of a major structural disturbance resulting from the experimental removal of > 20% of IKAROS WT sequence (i.e., deletion of 116-out-of-519 IKAROS WT amino acids). Aside from the abovementioned limitations, these data altogether highlight the role of IKAROS pre-ZF1 region, encompassing amino acids 1–116, in IKAROS protein stability (Figure 4A).

Recently, we demonstrated the critical functional role of the N-terminal region in AIOLOS, another member of the IKAROS family of transcription factors.[18] In that study, we found that AIOLOS’ N-terminal region plays a major role in protein stability and function. In HEK293T transfection experiments, we found that the deletion of its N-terminal region led to reduced protein expression. These findings underscore that AIOLOS N-terminal region is essential for maintaining its integrity and stability. Since IKAROS and AIOLOS are structurally and functionally related, insights from AIOLOS helped us to further understand IKAROS. Mutations in IKZF1 are important contributors to PIDs. Previous studies have been mainly focused on defects within or directly affecting the ZF domains critical for DNA-binding and dimerization, and resulting in diverse disease phenotypes.[3, 11, 12] Patients with IKZF1 haploinsufficiency typically exhibit common variable immunodeficiency (CVID)-like symptoms, including low B-cell counts, low serum immunoglobulin levels, increase susceptibility to bacterial infections, autoimmune disorders, and a higher risk of malignancies. Mutations disrupting dimerization usually result in hematological disorders, immune dysregulation, and hypogammaglobulinemia, while dominant negative mutations usually cause more severe and opportunistic infections and increased risk of lymphoid and myeloid malignancies. In contrast, gain-of-function mutations mostly result in autoimmunity, allergies, and plasma cell proliferation.16

In this work, through a systematic functional analysis, we demonstrate that IKZF1 mutations affecting IKAROS stability are associated with human disease with incomplete clinical penetrance, similarly as described in patients carrying IKZF1 haploinsufficiency, dimerization or gain-of-function mutations.11 As previously reported, IKZF1 deleterious variants impacting the dimerization domain are associated with defective homo- and heterodimerization, as well as abnormal DNA binding, PC-HC localization, SUMOylation, and reduced protein stability.[13] Herein, we report that IKZF1 missense mutations mapping to IKAROS pre-ZF1 region, an area of previously unknown biologic activity that we designated as a PSAR, also affecting protein stability but with no impact on DNA binding, PC-HC localization or SUMOylation, and still associating human disease.

In particular, we show that the patient carrying the IKAROS D22N variant experiences haploinsufficiency, likely due to the absence of mutant protein expression and overall reduced WT IKAROS protein levels, while not affecting other major functions on the transcription factor. This IKAROS reduction likely alters B-cell differentiation and function, similar to findings in other haploinsufficiency patients, as well as the murine models, where decreased IKZF1 expression lowers the threshold for B-cell receptor activation and impairs germinal center formation.[3] In line with these observations, the patient exhibited reduced total B cell numbers and switched memory B cells. The studies on IKAROS V52L, another human mutation mapping to the PSAR, that is not reported in gnomAD but is associated with hematologic diseases in COSMIC, further supports the relevance of the PSAR and this mechanism of disease.

The IKAROS M494Vfs*86 mutation has been previously reported in patients with PIDs, although its impact on IKAROS expression in primary cells has not been explored.[19] Herein, we assessed IKAROS expression in PBMCs, T-cell blasts, and EBV-transformed B cells, observing markedly reduced IKAROS protein levels. Since the M494Vfs*86 mutation maps to the ZF6 domain, which is involved in dimerization, it is likely that this variant impairs dimerization. Previous studies have shown that mutations affecting dimerization reduce protein stability, which leads to diminished functional activity of IKAROS.[13] While the mutation complexity prevented us to exactly recapitulate its biologic behavior, it is reasonable to speculate that the decrease in WT IKAROS protein levels and the absence of mutant protein in our study are likely due to the instability of the mutant protein, similar to other dimerization-disrupting mutations known to reduce protein stability.

Our findings on IKAROS Y503* demonstrate that this variant disrupts several key mechanisms essential for its function and stability. First, the Y503* mutation impairs IKAROS’ ability to bind DNA properly and localize to PC-HC, as shown by immunofluorescence staining (Figure 3), weakening its regulatory role in gene expression. Additionally, EMSA results indicate that Y503* disrupts oligomerization, a process crucial for stable DNA binding and complex formation, while it also exhibits only very limited homodimerization with WT IKAROS, likely due to alterations in the ZF6 domain, which is essential for this function.[13] The defect in homodimerization limits functional interactions, aligning with previous findings that deletion of the ZF5–6 domain or the presence of dimerization-defective mutants disrupts SUMOylation.[13] The inability to form functional oligomers or homodimers may expose mutant IKAROS to rapid degradation pathways, such as ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, leading to a shortened half-life and overall decrease in accumulation. Finally, we observed disrupted SUMOylation in the Y503*, which may further contribute to protein instability by limiting protective post-translational modifications. Recent studies have shown that SUMOylation regulates IKAROS’s transcriptional activity, highlighting the impact of this defect on its regulatory function.[20] Together, these combined defects in DNA binding, oligomerization, homodimerization, SUMOylating and stability, all contribute to the IKAROS Y503* variant’s mechanism of disease.

In summary, this study identifies mutations that affect IKAROS protein stability, including mutations disturbing dimerization and a newly identified PSAR, resulting in altered immune functions and human disease. Importantly, our study also expands the current understanding of IKAROS biology and protein regulation by establishing that mutations in two distinct regions – the PSAR and the dimerization domain – can lead to protein instability and by themselves result in IKAROS haploinsufficiency and IKAROS-associated diseases.
