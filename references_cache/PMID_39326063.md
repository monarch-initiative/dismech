# DNAJB1-PRKACA Fusion Drives Fibrolamellar Liver Cancer through Impaired SIK Signaling and CRTC2/p300-Mediated Transcriptional Reprogramming.
**Authors:** Gritti I, Wan J, Weeresekara V, Vaz JM, Tarantino G, Bryde TH, Vijay V, Kammula AV, Kattel P, Zhu S, Vu P, Chan M, Wu MJ, Gordan JD, Patra KC, Silveira VS, Manguso RT, Wein MN, Ott CJ, Qi J, Liu D, Sakamoto K, Gujral TS, Bardeesy N
**Journal:** Cancer Discov (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1158/2159-8290.CD-24-0634](https://doi.org/10.1158/2159-8290.CD-24-0634)

## Content

This work combines functional studies in model systems and examination of human tumor specimens to define a central oncogenic pathway driven by DNAJB1-PRKACA fusions in FLC. DNAJB1-PRKACA-mediated inactivation of the SIK stimulates CRTC2-p300-mediated transcription to drive tumor growth. The findings illuminate pathogenic mechanisms and inform therapeutic development.

Fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC) is a distinct and rare type of liver malignancy that primarily affects younger individuals (1, 2). Although its reported age-adjusted incidence rate is low—0.02 per 100,000—recent epidemiologic data suggest that it is underreported due to frequent misdiagnosis as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC; ref. 3). Patients typically lack cirrhosis or other risk factors and present with a large tumor burden and locally invasive or metastatic disease (4). There are no standard treatments for advanced FLC, and clinical trials with targeted, conventional, and immune therapies have shown limited benefit. Outcomes are poor for advanced disease, with a 5-year overall survival of less than 10% (2).

The genomic profile of FLC is remarkably homogeneous, with virtually 100% of patients harboring a fusion of exon 1 of the DNAJB1 heat shock protein and exons 2 to 10 of PRKACA, a protein kinase A (PKA) catalytic subunit, usually as the only genomic alteration (4, 5). This fusion has also been identified in rare oncocytic papillary neoplasms of the pancreas and bile duct (6, 7) but otherwise has not been reported as a recurrent change in any other tumor type. PKA is normally stimulated by elevations in cyclic AMP levels due to upstream activation of the G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR)–adenylyl cyclase pathway (8, 9). DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion (PKAfus) results in constitutive PKA kinase activity and downstream changes in MAPK and aurora kinase A signaling (10–12). Expression of PKAfus promotes tumorigenesis in the mouse liver and alters the cell differentiation program of human hepatocyte organoids (13, 14). Moreover, genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of PKAfus limits the growth of FLC models (15, 16). However, the broad and essential roles of PKA in normal physiology present a barrier to the clinical use of PKA inhibitors (15, 17). Thus, addressing the specific oncogenic functions of PKAfus and defining associated targetable vulnerabilities remain fundamental questions in the biology of FLC and related tumors and are needed to guide therapeutic development. Here, we sought to define the core program by which PKAfus drives tumorigenesis.

The oncogenic functions of PKAfus have been elusive. The highly restricted spectrum of tumor types harboring this alteration suggests that the fusion protein may drive neoplastic growth via a unique pathway, potentially involving tissue-specific or developmental context-specific processes. To determine whether PKAfus might have a broader role in inducing cellular transformation, we engineered NIH3T3 immortalized mouse embryo fibroblasts to express this oncogene using a doxycycline (DOX)-inducible system (NIH3T3PKA-fus cells). NIH3T3PKA-fus cells exhibited activation of PKA as determined by a phospho-specific antibody to VASP-(S329), an established PKA substrate (Fig. 1A). Injection of NIH3T3PKA-fus cells into NSG mice provided with DOX-supplemented drinking water resulted in rapid tumor growth by 6 to 8 weeks (5/5 mice), whereas no significant tumors were formed by NIH3T3PKA-fus cells without DOX supplementation or by control cells transduced with the corresponding empty vector (NIH3T3EV cells; Fig. 1B). Upon DOX withdrawal, NIH3T3PKA-fus tumors showed loss of PKA activity, associated with prominent growth inhibition and reduction in Ki67-stained proliferating cells (Fig. 1C–E). These findings in fibroblasts suggest that PKAfus functions through a general, rather than highly context-specific, oncogenic pathway to initiate and maintain tumor growth.

To explore PKAfus signaling in FLC, we first developed primary cultures from two patient-derived xenografts (PDX), FLX1 and FLC4 (“Methods”). FLX1 cells remained stable during in vitro culture, with a consistent proliferation rate and sustained expression of PKAfus, making this model amenable to in-depth mechanistic studies. Cells isolated from the FLC4 PDX gradually lost PKAfus expression in vitro; thus, work with this model was confined to short-term cultures (<4 passages). We evaluated PKA signaling in FLX1 cells and fresh FLC4 cultures and in a series of 12 other liver cancer cell lines comprising intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) and HCC models. Western blot analysis showed that FLX1 and FLC4 expressed PKAfus and had robust baseline PKA activity compared with each of the ICC and HCC cell lines, as determined by phospho-antibodies to VASP and the PKA substrate motif [Fig. 1F (top three rows)]. Thus, FLC cells are sharply distinguished from other types of liver cancer cells by their constitutive PKA activity.

Prompted by the NIH3T3 cell transformation data, we examined whether the program downstream of PKAfus converges on established oncogenic pathways. In this regard, we have reported that activating mutations in GNAS (encoding the G Protein α-stimulatory subunit) drive pancreatic tumorigenesis via a program involving PKA activation and resulting direct phosphorylation of the salt-inducible kinases (SIK1-3; in SIK2, residues S343, S358, T484, and S587; ref. 18), which leads to sequestration of SIKs from their substrates (19, 20). It is not clear whether PKAfus has distinct targets compared with wild-type PKA. Notably, we found that FLX1 and FLC4 had high levels of phospho-SIK2-(S343) compared with the other 12 liver cancer cell lines analyzed [Fig. 1F (fourth row); antibody validation is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A]. Importantly, phosphorylation of the established SIK substrate, CRTC2, was low in these models and robust in the other liver cancer cell lines [Fig. 1F; ratio of slower (phosphorylated) and faster (nonphosphorylated) migrating forms], consistent with reduced SIK activity in FLX1 and FLC4. Similar results were seen in the setting of ectopic PKAfus expression, with NIH3T3PKA-fus cells showing prominent elevation in phospho-SIK2-(S343) compared with NIH3T3EV control, indicating that PKAfus directly phosphorylates and inactivates SIK2 (Fig. 1G). Overall, the data suggest that selective inactivation of the SIK tumor suppressors may be a key hallmark of FLC (represented schematically in Fig. 1H).

Next, we directly examined the impact of PKAfus kinase activity on the signaling circuitry and growth of our FLC models using the novel, selective, small molecule PKA inhibitor (PKAi), BLU0588 (15). Notably, FLX1 cells were highly sensitive to BLU0588, showing a significant reduction in viability at low drug concentrations (33–500 nmol/L), whereas the series of ICC and HCC cell lines were insensitive to up to at least 1,000 nmol/L BLU0588 (Fig. 2A), consistent with the lack of PKA activity in the latter models (Figs. 1F and 2B). Treatment of FLX1 cells with 100 nmol/L BLU0588 abolished PKA activity, as reflected by loss of phospho-VASP-(S239) and phospho-SIK2-(S343) (Fig. 2B). Moreover, phosphorylation of CRTC2 was prominently increased, consistent with the reactivation of SIKs [Fig. 2C (first two lanes)]. Because SIKs have functional redundancy (19, 20), small molecule kinase inhibitors that inactivate SIK1-3 (SIKi) provide valuable tools to study the biological roles of this family. We found that induction of phospho-CRTC2 was suppressed by cotreatment with the pan-SIK inhibitor, HG-9-91-01 (21, 22), confirming that SIK reactivation mediated this phosphorylation change [Fig. 2C (compare second and fourth lanes)].

Because constitutive PKA signaling blocks SIK cellular function, we next examined the role of SIK reactivation in the therapeutic response to PKAi treatment. Notably, growth inhibition by PKAi was nearly completely overcome by either HG-9-91-01 or by a second, structurally distinct SIK inhibitor, SK-124 (Fig. 2D; western blot analysis shown in Supplementary Fig. S2A; ref. 23). We conducted complementary studies using DOX-inducible expression of a dominant-negative mutant of the PKA regulatory subunit (PRKAR1AG325D; refs. 24, 25). As with PKAi treatment, PRKAR1AG325D blocked PKA activity and strongly suppressed downstream SIK signaling and growth of FLX1 cells, both of which were overcome by HG-9-91-01 (Supplementary Fig. S2B and S2C). Furthermore, knockdown of PKAfus using pooled siRNAs targeting DNAJB1 and PRKACA gave similar results (Supplementary Fig. S2D and S2E). FLC4 cells also exhibited responsiveness to PKAi that was overridden by either HG-9-91-01 or SK-124, although the proliferative changes were more modest in this slow-growing model (Supplementary Fig. S2F and S2G). Finally, the growth of NIH3T3PKA-fus cells was reduced by BLU0588 treatment and rescued by HG-9-91-01 or SK-124, whereas control NIH3T3EV cells were not affected by PKA inhibition (Supplementary Fig. S2H and S2I). These data suggest that SIKs are central oncogenic targets of PKAfus.

Next, to explore the contribution of these pathways to FLC tumor growth in vivo, we generated FLC xenografts by subcutaneous injection of FLX1 cells into NSG mice. Mice were treated with BLU0588 once tumors reached a starting volume of 500 mm3. PKA inhibition resulted in pronounced tumor shrinkage (Fig. 2E and F). Remarkably, coadministration of YKL-05-099, a derivative of HG-9-91-01 with improved pharmacokinetic properties suitable for in vivo studies (26), completely blocked the antitumor effect of BLU0588 [Fig. 2E and F (red line)]. Immunostaining demonstrated that BLU0588 treatment greatly attenuated tumor cell proliferation (Ki-67 staining), which was likewise rescued by YKL-05-099 (Fig. 2G and H). Signaling analysis confirmed that BLU0588 inactivated PKA signaling, as reflected by reductions in phospho-VASP-(S239) and phospho-SIK2-(S343), whereas CRTC2 phosphorylation was increased, consistent with SIK reactivation (Fig. 2I). Accordingly, YKL-05-099 cotreatment suppressed CRTC2 phosphorylation (Fig. 2I). These biological and signaling effects were fully recapitulated upon genetic inactivation of PKA in xenografts generated from the FLX1-PRKAR1AG325D model. Acute induction of this dominant-negative mutant (by provision of DOX) in fully formed tumors caused prominent tumor regression and increased phosphorylation of SIK targets [CRTC2-(S171) and HDAC7-(S155)], which were rescued by YKL-05-099 (Supplementary Fig. S2J–S2M). We extended these studies to the FLC4 PDX model (i.e., the passaged primary tumor without in vitro culture). BLU0588 and YKL-05-099 induced similar effects in FLC4 PDXs as those observed in FLX1 xenografts (Fig. 2J–N). Thus, the data demonstrate that the PKAfus-SIK axis is a central pathway supporting FLC tumor growth in vivo, establishing SIKs as critical tumor suppressors inactivated by PKAfus.

Transcriptional regulators are key, physiologically relevant substrates of SIKs (20). We hypothesized that a major aspect of SIK-mediated tumor suppression, overridden by PKAfus, also involves transcriptional control. Consequently, we examined the global mRNA expression profile of FLX1 cells treated with vehicle, PKAi (100 nmol/L BLU0588), SIKi (250 nmol/L HG-9-91-01), or the combination. This allowed us to assess the transcriptional program controlled by PKAfus and specifically evaluate how the inactivation of SIK contributes to this program. Compared with vehicle, PKAi treatment resulted in differential expression of 794 genes (394 repressed, 400 induced), SIKi, 40 genes (28 repressed, 12 induced), and the combination, 412 genes (278 repressed, 134 induced). Principal component analysis showed that PKA inhibition accounted for 87% of the variance in the dataset (captured in PC1). Remarkably, this shift in PC1 was reduced by >50% when cells were additionally treated with SIKi (Fig. 3A). Thus, PKAi has widespread effects on gene expression in FLX1 cells, whereas SIKi treatment has little effect at baseline, consistent with the lack of baseline SIK activity due to phosphorylation by PKAfus. By contrast, SIKi prominently suppresses transcriptional changes resulting from PKAi treatment, consistent with a critical role of SIK reactivation in the PKAi response.

To further evaluate the relationship between SIK activity and transcriptional changes driven by PKAfus, we compared the differentially expressed genes in the PKAi versus vehicle and PKAi versus combination PKAi + SIKi conditions (thus identifying the genes and pathways that are altered by PKAi in a SIK-dependent manner). Notably, there was a strong overlap in the set of repressed genes (i.e., Up in DMSO vs. PKAi and Up in Combo vs. PKAi; Fig. 3B and C). We refer to this intersection as the SIK-suppressed Targets of PKA (SsTOP) gene set (Supplementary Table S1). Query of MSigDB Hallmark gene expression signatures using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed strongly decreased expression of cell cycle genes in each comparison (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Importantly, however, the prominent overlap in gene expression changes remained after removing cell cycle-associated genes (i.e., E2F targets; see “Methods”), indicating that the transcriptional effects were not secondary to the proliferative state [Supplementary Fig. S3B and S3C; Supplementary Table S2 shows the SsTOP (-E2F) gene set]. There was less overlap in the set of genes upregulated by PKAi (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Fig. S3D), although GSEA revealed common enrichment of interferon-related pathways (Supplementary Fig. S3A). These data demonstrate that PKAfus controls gene expression primarily through inactivating SIKs.

The preferred SIK substrates harbor the basophilic sequence (R/KXXS/TXP), which corresponds to the 14-3-3 binding motif when phosphorylated. These substrates include the CREB-regulated transcription coactivators (CRTC1, 2, and 3) and type IIA HDACs [HDAC4, 5, 7; Fig. 1H (above)]. SIK-mediated phosphorylation of these substrates leads to their cytoplasmic retention (20). In the normal liver, CRTCs, in conjunction with the CREB1 transcription factor (TF), couple glucagon signaling to the control of metabolic gene expression. This process is triggered by glucagon receptor-mediated activation of PKA and subsequent inhibition of SIK (21). The LKB1/STK11 tumor suppressor opposes this pathway by stimulating SIKs through phosphorylation of a conserved site in their activation loops (T175 in SIK2). Importantly, in STK11 mutant lung cancer cells, CRTC2 is unphosphorylated/nuclear and serves as a key driver of CREB-mediated transcription and tumorigenic growth (27, 28).

We used the CollecTRI TF–target interactions collection (29) to analyze gene regulation downstream of PKAfus-SIK signaling in FLX1 cells (“Methods”). Notably, the paralogs ATF1 and CREB1 were among the TFs in which inferred activity was most downregulated by PKAi (vehicle vs. PKAi) and rescued by SIKi (PKAi + SIKi vs. PKAi), ranking first and fourth in the latter comparison (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Table S3A and S3B). Based on these observations and our finding that CRTC2 is less phosphorylated in cells expressing PKAfus (Figs. 1F, G and 2C), we explored the role of CRTC2/CREB downstream of PKAfus. GSEA revealed that PKAi treatment caused a broad decrease in expression of a CRTC2/CREB gene target signature (Supplementary Table S3C; refs. 28, 30, 31), which was significantly rescued by combined SIKi treatment [Fig. 3E; enrichment for all four conditions based on single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) is shown in Fig. 3F]. These data indicate that CRTC2 and CREB1/ATF1 are activated and mediate transcriptional reprogramming downstream of PKAfus-SIK signaling.

We examined the SsTOP and CRTC2/CREB signatures in another PDX-derived FLC model (LI5132) for which data for PKAi-induced gene expression changes are available (BLU0588 treatment; ref. 15). Both gene signatures were strongly suppressed by PKAi treatment in this model (i.e., Up in DMSO; Fig. 3G). We next evaluated the concordance between PKAi-downregulated genes in the LI5132 and FLX1 models. Downregulated genes in LI5132 were also depleted in FLX1 by PKAi treatment when compared with both DMSO and PKAi + SIKi combination treatment (Fig. 3H). These findings reinforce the conclusion that SIK inactivation is a major mechanism by which PKAfus positively regulates transcription and suggest that the SsTOP and CRTC2/CREB gene signatures are conserved read-outs of oncogenic PKAfus signaling.

qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that PKAi downregulated the expression of CRTC2 targets in both FLX1 and FLC4 cells and that this downregulation was rescued by SIKi (Fig. 3I). Moreover, siRNA-mediated knockdown of CRTC2 suppressed this gene signature, demonstrating that CRTC2 indeed controls these targets in FLX1 cells (Fig. 3J). Similarly, ectopic expression of PKAfus induced CRTC2 targets in NIH3T3 cells (Supplementary Fig. S3E), an effect suppressed by PKAi and rescued by SIKi cotreatment (Supplementary Fig. S3F). Finally, these genes were downregulated by CRISPR-mediated KO of Crtc2 in NIH3T3PKA-fus cells (Supplementary Fig. S3G).

Next, we explored these transcriptional changes in vivo using our FLC xenograft and PDX models. Importantly, RT-qPCR established that PKAi and SIKi treatment regulated the set of CRTC2 targets in FLX1 and FLC4 tumors (Fig. 3K and L), in line with in vitro observations. These results highlight the pronounced stimulation of CRTC2 by PKAfus and point to this transcriptional coactivator as a potential downstream mediator of tumor cell growth.

We extended these studies by examining the functions of CRTC2-interacting TFs downstream of PKAfus-SIK. First, we tested the impact of the CREB/ATF family inhibitor (CREBi), 666-15, which blocks the binding of the phosphorylated kinase-inducible domain region of CREB1/ATF1 to the p300 histone acetyltransferase, a key partner in CREB-mediated transcription (32, 33). Its activity can be monitored by gauging the phosphorylation of CREB-S133 (or of the analogous conserved site, ATF1-S63); 666-15 has a reported cellular half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for CREB of 81 nmol/L (32). Treatment of FLX1 cells and NIH3T3PKA-fus cells with 250 nmol/L 666-15 reduced phospho-CREB-(S133) by ∼80% (Supplementary Fig. S4A and S4B) and attenuated CRTC2 signature target gene expression to a degree comparable with that observed with 250 nmol/L PKAi treatment (Fig. 3M; Supplementary Fig. S4C). Importantly, combined treatment with PKAi and CREBi did not cause further reductions compared with single-agent treatments, indicating the specificity of these effects.

CREB1 and ATF1 are part of a larger group of >20 basic-region leucine zipper (bZIP) domain-containing TFs that bind DNA sequences similar to the cyclic AMP response element (CRE) motif recognized by CREB1 and ATF1 (34). Of these, ATF4, ATF6, and the CREB3 family have been reported to physically interact with CRTCs (35). These factors are localized to the ER under basal conditions but are activated by inducers such as ER stress, which triggers their proteolytic cleavage and subsequent translocation to the nucleus. We tested whether these factors contribute to the SsTOP signature by treating cells with the selective and potent inhibitor of ATF6, Ceapin-A7 (36), or with the global inhibitor of serine proteases, AEBSF (37, 38). Both Ceapin-A7 (6 μmol/L) and AEBSF (300 μmol/L) blocked the induction of ER stress response genes (HSPA5, HERPUD1, and ERO1B) resulting from thapsigargin treatment (100 nmol/L), demonstrating effective target inhibition by these compounds [Supplementary Fig. S4D and S4E (left graphs)]. By contrast, Ceapin-A7 and AEBSF had only limited effects on the expression of curated CRTC2 targets and of other highly regulated genes from the SsTOP signature, except for the suppression of FOS by Ceapin-A7 [Supplementary Fig. S4D and S4E (right graphs)]. Similar results were observed in NIH3T3PKA-fus cells (Supplementary Fig. S5A and S5B). These data support CREB1/ATF1 as the primary TFs downstream of PKAfus-SIK signaling.

Consistent with its predicted oncogenic role, knockdown of CRTC2 suppressed the proliferation of FLX1 while not affecting a series of control HCC and ICC cell lines (Fig. 4A; western blot for CRTC2 is shown below the graph). We further tested the specificity of CRTC2 in supporting the growth of PKAfus-transformed cells by generating CRISPR-mediated knockouts of Crtc2 in NIH3T3PKA-fus cells, NIH3T3EV cells, and NIH3T3 cells transformed with a different fusion oncogene, FGFR2-PHGDH (39). Indeed, Crtc2 ablation selectively impeded the growth of the NIH3T3PKA-fus model compared with control NIH3T3EV and NIH3T3FGFR2-fus cells (Fig. 4B; CRTC2 target genes are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3G and S5C).

We also found that the CREBi, 666-15, strongly suppressed the growth of FLX1 cells at concentrations as low as 100 nmol/L, whereas other liver cancer cell lines HCC and ICC (N = 6 total) were largely insensitive to at least 250 nmol/L 666-15 (Fig. 4C). The extent of growth inhibition with 666-15 was comparable with that of achieved with PKAi treatment at relevant therapeutic concentrations. Moreover, growth suppression resulting from combined PKAi and 666-15 treatment only moderately exceeded that of single-agent treatments, supporting the specificity of the effects. NIH3T3PKA-fus showed relative response to treatment with CREBi, PKAi, or the combination (Supplementary Fig. S5D); furthermore, NIH3T3PKA-fus cells were more sensitive to CREBi treatment compared with NIH3T3EV control cells (Supplementary Fig. S5E). By contrast, the ATF6α inhibitor, Ceapin-A7 (0.1–10 μmol/L), had no significant effect on the proliferation of FLX1 or NIH3T3PKA-fus cells (Supplementary Fig. S5F). We conclude that both the hallmark CRTC2 targets controlled by the PKAfus-SIK axis and the associated proliferative program are dependent on CREB1 and/or ATF1.

Finally, we tested the requirement for CRTC2 in PKAfus-driven tumorigenesis in vivo. We found that Crtc2 inactivation strongly impaired tumor formation by NIH3T3PKA-fus cells while not restricting the tumorigenicity of NIH3T3FGFR2-fus cells (Fig. 4D and E). Thus, CRTC2/CREB activity is selectively essential downstream of the PKAfus oncogene.

Next, we investigated these transcriptional circuits in primary patient samples. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction (UMAP) of RNA-seq data from liver tumor specimens from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) study demonstrated that tumors with DNAJB1-PRKACA fusions (FLCs or FLC-like HCCs; see “Methods”) projected as a cluster distinct from HCCs and ICCs (which lacked fusions; Supplementary Fig. S6A). The CRTC2/CREB signature was notably enriched in FLC/FLC-like tumors compared with HCCs and ICCs, based on single-sample GSEA (Fig. 5A). We also analyzed two additional sets of samples consisting of FLCs and paired normal liver from published studies (MSK/Cornell samples; refs. 40, 41) and a new series from Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center (FHCC; Supplementary Fig. S6B and S6C). The data showed a highly significant increase in CRTC2 targets in FLCs compared with normal liver in both data sets (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Fig. S6D). Thus, activation of the CRTC2/CREB program is a defining feature of FLC.

The intersection of the broader set of genes suppressed by PKAi and rescued by SIK inhibition in FLX1 cells [SsTOP genes; Fig. 3C (above)] may provide a more comprehensive signature representing the oncogenic function of PKAfus in FLC. ssGSEA demonstrated that SsTOP genes, both without and with the removal of E2F targets (SsTOP-E2F), were prominently enriched in FLC/FLC-like tumors relative to HCC and ICC, and normal liver (Fig. 5C and D; Supplementary Fig. S6E–S6H), consistent with a major role for PKAfus-mediated suppression of SIK in shaping the FLC transcriptome in vivo.

We also examined the relationship between the PKAfus-SIK program and a series of transcriptional pathways previously reported as distinguishing features of FLC tumors, namely genes associated with FLC superenhancers (40), specific neuroendocrine genes (42), and amino acid/urea cycle metabolism genes (Supplementary Table S4; refs. 43, 44). ssGSEA confirmed that these pathways were enriched in our set of FLCs relative to other liver cancers and to the normal liver (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. S6I and S6J), as did an analysis of individual genes (Supplementary Table S5). Remarkably, examination of RNA-seq data from FLX1 cells (from Fig. 3A–C) revealed that each pathway was suppressed by PKAi treatment and rescued by SIKi cotreatment as demonstrated by ssGSEA (Fig. 5F and G; Supplementary Table S6) and heat map analysis (Fig. 5H). Finally, a set of the topmost upregulated genes in FLC samples versus normal liver (Supplementary Table S4; ref. 41) displayed a comparable strong regulatory pattern by PKAi and SIKi in FLX1 cells (Fig. 5I; Supplementary Table S6). These findings directly link PKAfus-SIK signaling to the distinct molecular hallmarks of FLC. Collectively, the data strongly support PKAfus-mediated SIK inactivation as the central axis reprogramming transcription and driving tumorigenesis in FLC.

The CRTC2-CREB transcriptional complex also contains the p300 acetyltransferase (45). Small molecule inhibitors of p300 and its paralog CBP (that block acetyltransferase activity or binding to acetylated histones) are under clinical development (46, 47), suggesting a possible strategy to target CRTC2 dependency in PKAfus-driven tumors. P300 maintains gene expression programs by lysine acetylation of histones, including lysine 18 and 27 on histone 3 (H3K18 and H3K27). Additionally, p300 has nonhistone substrates such as CRTC2, in which acetylation of lysine 628 increases its stability and/or transcriptional activity (48, 49). Furthermore, p300 is a SIK2 substrate (Ser-89), which may suppress p300 acetyltransferase activity (50). Unexpectedly, we found that NIH3T3PKA-fus cells had a marked increase in Ep300 protein levels compared with NIH3T3EV cells, whereas p300 (EP300) mRNA expression levels were unchanged (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. S7A). Similarly, PKAi treatment of FLX1 cells caused a pronounced reduction in p300 protein expression, without affecting mRNA levels, whereas SIK inhibition prominently rescued p300 protein expression (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Fig. S7B).

We conducted immunofluorescence staining to corroborate and extend these findings. The data showed that p300 was primarily nuclear under each of the various conditions (Fig. 6C and D). Additionally, p300 protein was upregulated in both NIH3T3PKA-fus cells and NIH3T3EV cells treated with SIKi compared with NIH3T3EV cells at baseline (Fig. 6C). In FLX1 cells, immunostaining confirmed that p300 protein expression was suppressed by PKAi and restored by SIKi cotreatment (Fig. 6D). We established the specificity of the immunofluorescence signaling by treating the different cell lines with the p300/CBP degrader (PROTAC), dCBP-1 (51), which resulted in the expected loss of staining (Supplementary Fig. S7C). Finally, we explored the basis for the changes in p300 levels by examining the impact of PKAfus on p300 protein turnover using cycloheximide chase experiments. We found that PKAfus expression significantly increased p300 half-life (∼2–3.86 hours; Supplementary Fig. S7D), indicating that the regulation of p300 expression by the PKAfus-SIK pathway involves controls of protein stability.

Strikingly, changes in p300 expression were associated with alterations in global levels of H3K27 acetylation, a marker of activated gene enhancers (52). In NIH3T3 cells, PKAfus expression or SIKi treatment caused a sharp increase in H3K27 acetylation levels (Fig. 6E). Similarly, PKA inhibition starkly suppressed H3K27 acetylation in FLX1 cells, an effect that was significantly rescued by SIK inhibition (Fig. 6F). As a control for the staining, cells were treated with the p300/CBP acetyltransferase inhibitor, A485 (46), which abrogated the H3K27 acetylation signal (Supplementary Fig. S7E). Importantly, the effect of the PKAfus-SIK pathway on this mark was specific, as PKAfus activation/inhibition and SIKi treatment had no effect on histone H3K9 acetylation, a chromatin modification that is also associated with transcriptional activation but is not catalyzed by p300/CBP (Supplementary Fig. S7F and S7G). Notably, consistent with the positive interplay between CRTC2 and p300, treatment with A485 shut down the CRTC gene signature in both NIH3T3PKA-fus and FLX1 cells (Fig. 6G). These data reveal novel functions of the PKAfus-SIK axis in controlling p300 protein levels and global H3K27 acetylation. Moreover, they highlight the cooperative role of p300/CBP in promoting CRTC2-mediated transcriptional activity in PKAfus-transformed cells.

Next, we tested the contribution of aberrant p300/CBP acetyltransferase activity in supporting the growth of our PKAfus-driven models. We found that FLX1 and FLC4 cells were sensitive to A485, with a prominent reduction in growth at 100 to 500 nmol/L of the drug, whereas control ICC and HCC cell lines were resistant at up to 1,000 nmol/L (Fig. 6H). FLX1 cells were also selectively sensitive to p300/CBP degradation with low concentrations of dCBP-1 (Supplementary Fig. S8A; ref. 51). To distinguish the roles of p300 and CBP, we utilized JQAD1, a specific degrader of p300 that spares CBP (53). Indeed, JQAD1 blocked the growth of FLX1 cells, whereas HCC and ICC cell lines were insensitive (Supplementary Fig. S8B). Western blots confirmed the activity of dCBP-1 against both p300 and CBP and demonstrated the specificity of JQAD1 for p300 (Supplementary Fig. S8C). NIH3T3PKA-fus cells also exhibited increased sensitivity to A485, dCBP-1, and JQAD1 compared with control NIH3T3EV (Supplementary Fig. S8D–S8G). We extended these results by genetic knockdown, with siRNA targeting EP300 causing marked decreases in the proliferation of FLX1 compared with control liver cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S8H; knockdown efficiency is shown in Supplementary Fig. S8I and S8J). Similarly, NIH3T3PKA-FUS cells were sensitive to Ep300 knockdown by siRNA treatment, whereas the viability of NIH3T3EV cells was not affected (Supplementary Fig. S8K and S8L). Thus, PKAfus aberrantly activates p300, leading to a selective vulnerability to p300 inhibition.

Finally, we explored the efficacy of A485 against PKAfus-driven tumors in vivo. Treatment of FLX1 xenografts and FLC4 PDX tumors (starting volume ∼400 and ∼200 mm3, respectively) with A485 led to prominent tumor regression associated with strong shutdown in cell proliferation (Fig. 6I–N). Furthermore, A485 markedly attenuated the growth of NIH3T3PKA-fus tumors (Supplementary Fig. S8M–S8O). Together, these data indicate that overactivation of the p300 acetyltransferase is required for the oncogenic program driven by PKAfus.

Cancers with fusion oncogenes often exhibit a more stable genome with fewer genomic alterations than other cancers and display marked dependence on the fusion oncogene for their malignant growth (54). Thus, understanding the mechanisms of tumorigenesis driven by these pivotal alterations has particular significance. Here, we discovered that PKAfus kinase activity promotes FLC cell line and xenograft growth by impairing SIK activity and that aberrant induction of CRTC2 and p300 acetyltransferase are critical downstream components of this oncogenic program. Moreover, we demonstrated that the SIK-CRTC2-controlled transcriptional signatures are prominently activated tumor specimens of patients with FLC. Collectively, these studies define a core signaling axis by which PKAfus reprograms gene expression and incites cell transformation.

Our work demonstrates that PKAfus causes pronounced oncogene addiction in FLC models, extending prior conclusions (15, 16) and providing new mechanistic insight. Importantly, whereas PKAfus has many substrates (12), our studies demonstrate the overarching role of phosphorylation and inactivation of the SIK isoforms for PKAfus-driven growth. Concurrent SIK inhibition was sufficient to override the antitumor response to PKA inhibition in FLC models in vitro and in vivo. SIK inhibition also largely restored the expression of genes downregulated by PKA inhibition. The SIK-suppressed Target of PKA (SsTOP) gene signature was strongly enriched in tumor samples of patients with FLC compared with normal liver and other types of liver cancer. Thus, SIK inhibition is crucial for PKAfus-driven transcriptional reprogramming and tumorigenic growth of FLC models and seems to be a defining feature of primary FLC tumors. Accordingly, it will be of interest to examine the role of SIKs in other notable functions of PKAfus, such as translational initiation (10), control of liver differentiation (14), and additional features of FLC such as metabolic derangements and immunosuppression (43, 44).

These findings also have further implications relating to the genomics of the disease. The DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion event is accompanied by the deletion of dozens of intervening genes in which loss could contribute to malignant phenotypes. Moreover, the chimeric protein has potential neomorphic, noncatalytic functions conferred by the DNAJB1 moiety (11). The strong suppression of tumor growth by both PKAi treatment and genetic inactivation of PKAfus—and the rescue in both contexts by SIK inhibition—reinforce the primacy of PKAfus kinase activity toward SIKs as a pathologic mechanism. Thus, the deletion of the intervening genes and the noncatalytic functions of the chimeric protein may not be critical for oncogenesis, although we cannot dismiss the possibility that roles for these alterations were not effectively assayed in our in vitro and in vivo systems.

SIKs have functional redundancy in several biological contexts, including suppression of lung cancers driven by mutant KRAS (27, 55). In FLC tumors and FLX1 cells, all SIKs are robustly expressed, with SIK1 showing the highest levels, consistent with its transcriptional induction by CRTCs as a negative feedback mechanism (56). SIK1 undergoes nuclear/cytoplasmic shuttling, with PKA-mediated phosphorylation promoting cytoplasmic retention. SIK2 and SIK3 are primarily cytoplasmic, and phosphorylation by PKA seems to restrict SIK2/3 kinase activity by increasing the binding of 14-3-3 proteins (20). Accordingly, it will be important to resolve the relative roles of the specific SIK1-3 paralogs in PKAfus-mediated tumor growth and gene regulation and to gauge their trafficking in response to PKAfus activity. In addition, PKAfus shows less restricted compartmentalization and promiscuous kinase activity compared with wild-type PKA (12). Thus, the phosphorylation of substrates other than SIKs by PKAfus likely contributes to multiple facets of FLC biology.

We define CRTC2 and p300 as essential downstream effectors of PKAfus. CRTC2 was preferentially dephosphorylated in FLC cells compared with other liver cancer cell lines, due to their low SIK activity, and was selectively required for the growth of FLC and NIH3T3PKA-fus cells.

Moreover, our in vitro studies indicate that the classic PKA-activated CREB1/ATF1 family TFs are critical for this program. In normal physiology, CRTC2 has evolutionarily conserved functions regulating genes that orchestrate adaptive changes in metabolism, cell stress response, and inflammation. Elucidating the key transcriptional targets of CRTC2 and validating the role of CREB1/ATF1 as partners of CRTC2 in vivo will be key next steps in further elucidating the PKAfus-driven oncogenic program. The strong regulation of urea cycle/amino acid metabolism genes by the PKAfus-SIK axis and enrichment of the genes in FLC tumors suggest that the pathway may be particularly important in promoting metabolic alterations to fuel malignant growth.

In addition to its partnership with sequence-specific DNA-binding TFs, CRTC2 has important cooperative interactions with p300 (35, 45). CRTC2 recruitment of p300 increases histone acetylation at enhancer elements to activate gene expression. Second, CRTC2 is stabilized/activated by p300-mediated acetylation of Lys-629. Moreover, SIKs have been implicated in the negative regulation of p300 through phosphorylation of the Ser-89, which has been reported to impair binding to CRTC2 and other transcriptional regulators. Unexpectedly, we found that PKAfus-mediated SIK suppression led to marked elevations in p300 protein levels—associated with a lengthened half-life—and increased global H3K27 acetylation, suggesting that p300 may have particular prominence in this setting. Accordingly, p300 inhibitor treatment demonstrated efficacy in FLC models and NIH3T3PKA-fus cells, associated with the downregulation of CRTC2 signature genes. These findings provide a preclinical rationale for the exploration of p300 as a target in FLC, alongside other recently reported candidate therapeutic approaches (57, 58). Drug development against p300/CBP is presently a very active area, with agents with multiple different modes of action being pursued (HAT inhibitors, bromodomain inhibitors, and degraders). Additional study of p300 biology in FLC and testing these distinct types of agents across multiple models of the disease will be important to guide the clinical translation of such strategies. Our work also suggests the potential value of developing treatments targeting other components downstream of PKAfus, including CREB and CRTC2 inhibitors, and possibly SIK activators.

Notably, we show that signaling by this rare fusion oncogene converges on a pathway controlled by disparate genomic changes that are common in distinct tumor types, with SIK inactivation linking the function of PKAfus to both STK11 tumor suppressor loss and gain-of-function mutation of GNAS, which are particularly prevalent in lung cancer and pancreatic tumors, respectively (18, 27, 55, 59). This functional intersection of major genomic drivers of diverse tumor types suggests that further exploration of their biological/pathophysiological relatedness may define a fundamental new disease state.

A limitation of our study is the paucity of available models of FLC. It will be important to test the findings in additional patient-derived cell lines and xenografts as these become available. It will also be important to evaluate the relationship between PKAfus-SIK signaling and the other prominent alterations identified in FLC cells, such as effects on mTOR, HER2, and aurora kinase (2, 10, 57, 58), and to carefully compare the potential of targeting each therapeutically.

Six- to ten-week-old NSG mice (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ, Strain #00557, The Jackson Laboratory) of both sexes were used in the studies below. Mouse weights ranged from ∼16 to 28 g. All in vivo studies involved subcutaneous implant of human FLC cells (FLX1 xenograft model), human FLC tumor fragments (FLC4 PDX model), or murine NIH3T3 cell derivatives into NSG mice, treatment by oral gavage or intraperitoneal injection, and serial monitoring by digital calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using volume = (length × width2)/2, with measurement using electronic calipers. Studies were not performed in a blinded manner. No power calculations were performed in advance. Mice were monitored daily by veterinary services. Treatment was discontinued and dietary supplements were provided if mice showed signs of poor body condition, such as hunched posture, lethargy, 15% weight loss, or reduced grooming. Tumor size guidelines were strictly adhered to (>2 cm in greatest diameter). Carbon dioxide was used for euthanasia based on institutional guidelines and ethical considerations for research with laboratory animals.

For FLX1 and FLC4 xenograft studies, 1 × 106 cells were injected into each mouse. Mice were randomized into treatment groups once tumors reached ∼400 mm3 (FLX1) or 200 mm3 (FLC4). For PKAi and SIKi studies, tumor-bearing mice were treated with vehicle, BLU0588 (45 mg/kg), YKL-05-099 (20 mg/kg), or both BLU0588 and YKL-05-099 daily for 5 days (100-μL oral gavage for BLU0588 and 100-μL intraperitoneal injection for YKL-05-099). BLU0588 (obtained from Blueprint Medicines) was dissolved in 5% DMSO and 20% Solutol, using 0.5% methylcellulose as the solvent. YKL-05-099 was dissolved in 5% DMSO and 90% corn oil. A485 (company) was dissolved in a solution of 10% DMSO and 90% corn oil. SIKi and Combo groups were pretreated for 2 days with YKL-05-099. For p300 inhibitor studies, tumor-bearing mice were treated with vehicle or A485 (100 mg/kg) twice daily via intraperitoneal injection. For the induction of PRKAR1AG325Din vivo, FLX1 PRKAR1AG325D cells were injected subcutaneously into NOD SCID mice (1 × 106 cells each). Mice were randomized into treatment groups once tumors reached ∼200 mm3 and then treated with vehicle, YKL-05-099 (20 mg/kg), or DOX-supplemented water daily for 5 days. NIH3T3EV cells and NIH3T3PKA-fus (1 × 106 cells), treated or not with DOX, were injected in NOD SCID mice, with or without DOX-supplemented drinking water. After 7 weeks, tumor-bearing mice from the NIH3T3PKA-fus “DOX on” group were maintained on DOX or switched to off DOX conditions for 6 days. Tumor samples were collected 4 hours after the last treatment and collected for analysis (snap frozen in liquid nitrogen) and histology processing.

IHC was performed by iHisto Inc. (iHisto.io). Samples were processed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Paraffin sections were then deparaffinized and hydrated using the following steps: 15 minutes in xylene twice; 5, 5, and 5 minutes in 100%, 100%, and 75% ethanol, respectively; and 5 minutes in PBS at room temperature repeated three times. Antigen retrieval was achieved by boiling the sections in 10-mmol/L sodium citrate for 10 minutes in a microwave oven and 5 minutes of cooling at room temperature. Sections were washed with PBS three times and treated with 3% H2O2 for 15 minutes and 5% BSA for 20 minutes. Primary antibody Ki67 (ab15580) was used for the staining. Five to ten fields per independent tumor from each treatment condition were taken using Olympus dp74 microscope and used for quantification with QuPath 0.5.1.

RNA was extracted from cultured cells under different treatment conditions using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN) and FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, RC112) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Snap-frozen tumor tissues were homogenized using the Kimble Kontes Pellet Pestle (DWK Life Science 7495400000). Total RNA was quantified and assessed for quality using a NanodropTM spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher), and cDNA was synthesized using the LunaScript RT SuperMix Kit or HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper; Vazyme, R312). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction real-time qRT-PCR was carried out with diluted cDNA, appropriate primers, iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix, or Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q712) using the Bio-Rad CFX384 Optical Reaction Module. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−DDCt method. Heatmaps were used to represent fold change (FC) in qPCR or RNA-seq data. Briefly, Log2(FC) values were standardized across all samples to generate a z-score for expression. Red represents positive, and blue represents negative z scores for each gene, as indicated. RNA sequencing was performed and analyzed at Novogene using a standing workflow with sample quality control (assessed through Library QC) and polyA enrichment. Subsequently, 150 bp paired-end sequencing was performed (Illumina PE150).

Three sources of patient genomic data were used. Transcriptome data for HCC (N = 360 samples), cholangiocarcinoma (CCA, N = 36 samples), and FLC or FLC-like HCC (N = 6 samples) from TCGA database were accessed through the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (http://cbioportal.org). The samples classified as FLC/FLC-like HCC all harbored the DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion and were from patients aged 17 to 34 at diagnosis. For normal liver-FLC comparisons, FASTQ files were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, RRID:SCR_005012) database with the accessions, GSE181922 (45) and GSE63018 (MSKCC-Cornell series; ref. 41). In addition, a new dataset with normal liver and FLC (FHCC series) is available with the accession, GSE250059. The raw counts for FLC samples were derived from reads aligned to the human reference genome (hg38, GENCODE v40) using the STAR aligner v2.7.7a (RRID:SCR_004463; ref. 61). After alignment, the resultant files were indexed using SAMtools v1.19.2 (RRID:SCR_002105; ref. 62) and gene expression levels were quantified using featureCounts (RRID:SCR_012919; ref. 63). Normalization of all raw counts was done using log-transformed TPM. The inclusion criteria for the FLC group were the presence of a DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion. For the other liver tumors, the TCGA designation of HCC or CCA served as the inclusion criteria. One tumor reported in TCGA data had a DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion from a 67-year-old patient and had a divergent gene expression from the other FLCs and was excluded from the analysis. No other samples were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2. Dimensionality reduction was conducted with UMAP using the umap package. Single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) was performed with “ssGSEA2.0” (64) using gene expression data normalized using TPM. For the enrichment statistic, area under the enrichment score curve was used. All ssGSEA scores were then z-score normalized within their respective datasets. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis for FLX1 samples involved comparing expression profiles between DMSO and PKAi groups, as well as between combination treatment (Combo) and PKAi-treated groups. DEG analysis was implemented using the edgeR package (RRID:SCR_012802; ref. 65), with genes filtered to include only protein coding and lncRNA genes, and a minimum expression of two counts per million per group (CPM). Thresholds included an FC of 1.75 and a FDR of 0.05, generating distinct upregulated and downregulated signatures for PKAi-vehicle and PKAi-Combo comparisons. We used the GSEA software (66) for enrichment analysis. Principal component analysis was done using DESeq2. Statistical significance among groups was tested using two-sample t tests for FLX1 comparisons and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for the rest. The transcriptional signatures were curated from the literature. CRTC2 target genes were from studies of genetic inactivation of CRTC2 and/or chromatin immunoprecipitation in LKB1/STK11 mutant lung cancer cells and in mouse liver with or without CRTC2 loss (Supplementary Table S3C; refs. 28, 30, 31). To account for potential secondary effects of cell cycle differences on transcriptional signatures, genes within the E2F targets gene set from the MSigDB Human Hallmark database (Supplementary Table S7) were removed from the dataset in specific analyses (SsTOP-E2F signature). The established FLC gene signatures (Supplementary Table S4) included genes associated with superenhancers in FLC (40), neuroendocrine genes expressed in FLC (42), and a set of the most upregulated genes in FLC versus normal liver (Sorensen gene set; ref. 41). The amino acid/urea cycle gene signatures are from (43, 44) and curation from the KEGG (RRID:SCR_012773) urea cycle gene set (Supplementary Table S4; GLUD2 and ASL were not upregulated in the set of FLCs studied here and, therefore, were not included in the heat map in Fig. 5H.

Prediction of TF activity was performed as follows: Bulk RNA-seq data were normalized as log-transformed counts per million (lcpm), experimental design metadata, and DEGs were obtained via the limma package in R, focusing on protein-coding genes. Log-fold change (logFC) and associated t values were extracted from the DEGs for subsequent analysis. We inferred TF activities using the decoupleR package, which integrates prior knowledge of TF–target interactions. For this, we utilized the CollecTRI network, a curated collection of TF–target relationships encompassing 43,178 interactions from 12 different sources (29). The network was retrieved from OmniPath and provided a robust framework for identifying perturbed TFs. The univariate linear model (ULM) method was employed for TF activity inference. For each sample in the dataset, ULM fits a linear model predicting gene expression based on the TF’s interaction weights. The t value of the slope from this model was used as the TF activity score, in which positive scores indicate activation and negative scores indicate inactivation. Data were filtered using cut-off values for TF activity (ULM score >2; P < 0.05) and TF expression (cpm > 1 in at least one sample). The top differentially regulated TFs that met these criteria were identified and visualized using ggplot2, focusing on the top 10 for each comparison. Supplementary Tables S3A and S3B show the TFs with downregulated activity upon PKAi treatment compared with vehicle and compared with combined PKAi/SIKi treatment (Combo), respectively, with the data representing the differential TF activity, baseline TF activity (in the vehicle or Combo condition), and baseline TF expression (lcpm values).

Quantification and statistical analysis were performed with GraphPad Prism software. One-way ANOVA was used to determine significant differences between three or more populations. Two-way ANOVA was used to compare groups on two different categorical variables. *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001; ns, P > 0.05. Statistics were performed and graphs were generated in GraphPad Prism v10.2.2.
