BACKGROUND: For infants with biliary atresia, the only treatment that can 
establish bile flow and delay need for liver transplant is the Kasai 
portoenterostomy (KP). Unfortunately, the KP has variable success. In this 
study, we hypothesized that intravenous N-acetylcysteine (IV NAC) treatment 
following KP would improve bile flow.
METHODS: This was a phase 2 study following the two-stage "minimax" trial 
design. Participants received IV NAC (150 mg/kg/day) for 7 days after KP, and 
the primary endpoint was achieving total serum bile acids (TSBA) ≤10 μmol/L 
within 24 weeks of KP. Secondary endpoints were clinical markers and the 
occurrence of sentinel events.
RESULTS: There were 12 participants in stage 1 who received treatment, with none 
achieving TSBAs ≤10 μmol/L within 24 weeks of KP. As a result, no participants 
were enrolled in stage 2. There were 32 adverse events in 11 participants, 
including 5 serious adverse events which were considered part of the 
participants' natural clinical course and not directly attributable to NAC 
treatment. Analyses of secondary outcomes demonstrated no difference in clinical 
markers or occurrence of sentinel events between study participants and matched 
historical controls.
CONCLUSIONS: This study demonstrates how the two-stage "minimax" trial design 
can be used to efficiently evaluate potential therapies for BA. Although the 
primary endpoint was not met, NAC therapy was generally well-tolerated. NAC 
therapy may prove efficacious in future trials with (i) a less stringent primary 
endpoint and/or (ii) a longer course of treatment (NCT03499249).

For infants with biliary atresia (BA), the Kasai portoenterostomy (KP) is the only treatment with the potential to delay need for liver transplant.12 The KP involves removing obstructed bile ducts and creating a liver-intestine anastomosis for bile flow.23 Unfortunately, the success of the KP is variable, and, to date, trials with adjunctive medical therapies such as corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin have not demonstrated clinical benefit.45 The lack of post-KP medical therapies explains in part why BA is the most common indication for pediatric liver transplant, accounting for 60% of liver transplants in infants <1 year of age and 30% of all liver transplants in children.67


In this study, we hypothesized that intravenous N-acetylcysteine (IV NAC) could improve bile flow after KP. NAC provides the amino acid cysteine, which drives production of the anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory molecule glutathione. Glutathione, in turn, is concentrated in bile and thought to be a choleretic agent.89 In preclinical rodent models of BA, NAC treatment was associated with reduced liver injury and improved survival.10 To test whether treatment could improve outcomes in infants, we performed a phase 2 study of IV NAC for 7 days post-KP using the two-stage “minimax” trial design, with an endpoint of total serum bile acids (TSBA) ≤10 µmol/L within 24 weeks of KP.

This was an investigator-initiated, non-randomized, phase 2 study approved by the institutional review board at Baylor College of Medicine and the Food and Drug Administration (NCT03499249, IND#135796; full protocol in Supplemental Digital Content Appendix, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C17), which followed a trial design reported previously.11 All participants’ parent(s) provided written informed consent. Assessments and testing were performed in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul at Texas Children’s Hospital, and a data safety monitoring board comprised of investigators not associated with the study site oversaw the study. The study was funded by support from Baylor College of Medicine and private philanthropy, and medication was supplied by Cumberland Pharmaceuticals, Inc.

Eligible participants were diagnosed with BA and received a KP before 90 days of life at Texas Children’s Hospital. The BA diagnosis was established by intraoperative cholangiogram and later confirmed by histological assessment of biliary remnants. Exclusion criteria included evidence of decompensated liver disease (INR >1.3 despite parental vitamin K administration) or other severe concurrent illnesses. Enrollment began in May 2018 and ended in December 2020, with follow-up completed in December 2022.

For secondary analyses, data from study participants were compared to data from historical controls. Historical controls were diagnosed with BA and treated with the KP at Texas Children’s Hospital in the 7 years preceding the study (2011–2017). Historical controls were chosen to match study participants in a 2:1 ratio, based on age at which the KP was performed (<30 d of life, between 31 and 60 d of life, or between 61 and 90 d of life).

IV NAC 150 mg/kg/day was administered continuously for 7 days, starting within the first 24 hours following KP. Participants also received standard therapies (ursodeoxycholic acid, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, fat-soluble vitamin supplementation, and high-MCT containing infant formula when able to tolerate oral nutrition) but no experimental therapies such as corticosteroids.4 This dose was previously shown to be safe in children with non-acetaminophen induced liver failure.12 All participants received the same dose for the same duration, without any dose reductions or extensions.

The primary endpoint was achieving good bile drainage, as defined by TSBA levels ≤10 μmol/L with native liver within the first 24 weeks following KP. TSBAs were measured with the enzymatic method through commercial clinical laboratories. Occurrences of adverse events were collected during treatment and for 21 days afterward.

For secondary endpoints, anthropomorphic measurements and laboratory values collected as part of standard-of-care were recorded at the following times: pre-KP (baseline values); 3 and 7 days post-KP; 2, 4, 8, 12, 18, and 24 weeks post-KP; and at 12, 18, and 24 months of life. Determination of splenomegaly was based on ultrasound findings, while thrombocytopenia was defined as platelets <150,000/mm3. Ascites was recorded when diuretics were needed for fluid accumulation. Portal hypertension was defined as having at least one of the following: varices documented by endoscopy, ascites, or a combination of splenomegaly and thrombocytopenia.

The sample size was calculated using the two-stage “minimax” design, based on estimates from unpublished historical data and past reports of ~5% of infants achieving TSBA levels ≤10 μmol/L within 6 months of KP.1314151617 The null hypothesis was that treatment would have no effect, that is, only 5% of participants treated with IV NAC would achieve TSBAs ≤10 μmol/L. The alternative hypothesis was that 25% of participants treated with IV NAC would achieve TSBAs ≤10 μmol/L. To test the null hypothesis, the two-stage “minimax” design required that 12 participants receive treatment initially (stage 1; Figure 1A). If ≥1 participant in stage 1 achieved TSBAs ≤10 μmol/L, another 4 participants would receive treatment in stage 2. The null hypothesis would be rejected if ≥3 of 16 participants achieved TSBAs ≤10 μmol/L within 24 weeks post-KP, with a type 1 error rate of 5% and power of 80%.

Secondary endpoints were compared between study participants and matched historical controls. Demographic information was compared using chi-squared and the Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Survival with native liver and other time-to-event outcomes were summarized using Kaplan–Meier methods, and differences in survival curves between groups were calculated with the log-rank test. Analyses were performed using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp LLC).

There were 13 eligible participants between May 2018 and December 2020, all of whom enrolled in the study. They were 38.5% female (n=5), 53.9% Hispanic (n=7), and 15.4% gestational age <37 weeks (n=2; Table 1). Their average age at KP was 36 days of life (Supplemental Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C18). Of the 13 enrolled participants, 12 received the complete 7-day course of IV NAC (Figure 1B). None achieved TSBAs ≤10 μmol/L within 24 weeks of KP in stage 1, and, as a result, no further patients were enrolled in stage 2.

In this study, we hypothesized that NAC treatment, through glutathione production, would improve outcomes in BA. We reasoned that glutathione production could benefit patients with BA in 2 ways. First, glutathione is thought to drive bile flow, as suggested by high glutathione concentrations in bile from both human and experimental animals.89 Glutathione-induced bile flow could help maintain an open conduit between the liver and the intestine created with the KP. Second, glutathione may be protective, as evidenced by improved liver injury with NAC treatment in different preclinical models of BA.101819 Glutathione moves from the bile into cells via a process mediated by GGT, an enzyme expressed on the surface of cholangiocytes.202122 Glutathione inside the cell could reduce the oxidative and inflammatory damage assumed to occur in BA.

To test our hypothesis, TSBAs were used as a marker of bile flow. In contrast, previous biliary atresia studies have used serum bilirubin as a marker of bile flow and normalization of levels as a trial endpoint.45 However, normalized serum bilirubin levels have limitations as a trial endpoint, because they do not always associate with better long-term outcomes. For example, one study reported that many infants who achieved normal serum bilirubin levels after KP still developed splenomegaly (71%), thrombocytopenia (45%), ascites (18%), and need for liver transplant (18%) by 2 years of life.3 Another study reported that 50% of infants who achieved normal serum bilirubin levels after KP still needed a transplant at some point in childhood.23


In this phase 2 trial, we were not able to detect a positive effect with IV NAC that would justify a larger phase 3 trial. Treatment with IV NAC for 7 days was not associated with a reduction of TSBAs ≤10 µmol/L within 24 weeks of KP (primary outcome). In accord with previous reports, treatment with NAC was safe.24252627 Most observed adverse events were expected with the natural course of BA, and the one participant with tachycardia had symptoms prior to IV NAC initiation. In addition, when compared to historical controls, treatment with IV NAC was not associated with significantly better or worse laboratory values or occurrence of sentinel events (secondary outcomes).

A limitation of this study is the stringent TSBA cutoff of ≤10 µmol/L for the primary outcome. This cutoff was initially chosen based on unpublished historical data, a previous study comparing TSBA and vitamin levels after KP, and normal TSBA ranges for healthy children and adults.14151617 However, subsequent studies after trial initiation have shown that TSBAs vary widely in BA, even when infants achieve good bile flow. For example, in infants achieving normalized bilirubin levels and presumably good bile flow after KP, TSBAs still ranged from 2 to 322 µmol/L (median 70 µmol/L) 24 weeks after KP.28 TSBAs ≤10 µmol/L were only seen in 2.2% (n=3/137) of infants, and a higher cutoff of ≤40 µmol/L was associated with better outcomes including transplant-free survival. Hence, the ≤10 µmol/L cutoff used in this trial may have been too stringent to detect some potential benefits of IV NAC.

A second limitation is the short duration of the trial, which may have missed a benefit if the treatment period was longer. We chose 7 days of IV NAC treatment to ensure therapy was delivered immediately after KP, thereby maximizing the chance to potentially promote bile flow and liver recovery. In addition, 7 days of IV NAC overlapped with the routine post-KP hospitalization period and did not require additional inpatient hospitalization solely for IV NAC. However, the 7-day IV NAC course finished before any participant achieved signs of good bile flow such as bilirubin normalization. Future possible avenues include extending NAC treatment with different oral formulations, which to date have not yet been performed because of NAC’s strong sulfur taste.

A third limitation is the use of historical controls for the secondary outcomes analyses. The study participants and historical controls were treated similarly at the same institution; however, the historical controls had laboratory values drawn on an inconsistent schedule which made laboratory comparisons less robust. For example, only 7 of 24 historical controls had 3 days or 7 days post-KP laboratory values. At 3 days post-KP, study participants trended toward lower AST and ALT levels relative to baseline compared to historical controls, perhaps indicating less liver injury with NAC. At 3 days post-KP, study participants also trended toward higher GGT levels relative to baseline compared to historical controls, raising the possibility that NAC-induced glutathione may increase expression of its associated enzyme GGT.22 In contrast, at 7 days post-KP, study participants had higher conjugated bilirubin (Bc) levels relative to baseline compared to historical controls. Additional studies with larger groups of historical controls would be needed to determine the clinical significance of any of these changes.

An important strength of this study is that it demonstrates how BA studies can adopt the two-stage “minimax” design to determine whether a larger phase 3 trial is warranted. To our knowledge, this is the first BA therapeutic trial using this design; however, oncology studies frequently use the two-stage “minimax” design because of its many advantages.13 First, the trial design identifies robust rather than incremental responses, which are needed in diseases with high morbidity or mortality. Second, the trial design employs a small number of patients, in part by relying on historical controls to set the null hypothesis. Third, the trial design has early stopping rules, to avoid spending extra time and resources investigating therapies unlikely to be effective. These trials can be easily designed, using tables found in the Simon original reference or computations from online “minimax” calculators (for example, https://biostatistics.mdanderson.org/shinyapps/Simon2S/).

BA may be an ideal disease for future trials with the two-stage “minimax” design. BA is in need of robust therapies, because no therapies since the development of the KP in the 1950s have been proven to reduce BA’s large liver transplant burden.2 Ideal BA trials are also small initially, because BA is relatively rare with a birth prevalence of 1:10,000–18,000.1 Designs involving fewer patients allow preliminary testing at single sites, before larger testing through multisite trials. Finally, BA trials can benefit from testing in stages with early stopping rules. This allows investigators to evaluate different therapies efficiently, until a promising therapy is identified for more definitive phase 3 trials.

In conclusion, 7 days of IV NAC was not associated with reducing TSBAs to ≤10 µmol/L within 24 weeks post-KP. The results are limited by the stringent primary outcome, short duration of therapy, and use of historical controls for secondary outcomes analyses. Future trials using this trial’s two-stage “minimax” design may be useful in testing oral formulations of NAC or other therapies with a promising rationale for improving outcomes in BA.
