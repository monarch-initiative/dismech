# PEHO syndrome caused by compound heterozygote variants in ZNHIT3 gene.
**Authors:** Õunap K, Muru K, Õiglane-Shlik E, Ilves P, Pajusalu S, Kuus I, Wojcik MH, Reimand T
**Journal:** Eur J Med Genet (2020)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ejmg.2019.04.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejmg.2019.04.017)

## Content

PEHO syndrome is characterized by Progressive Encephalopathy with Edema, Hypsarrhythmia, and Optic atrophy, which was first described in Finnish patients. A homozygous missense substitution p.Ser31Leu in ZNHIT3 was recently identified as the primary cause of PEHO syndrome in Finland. Variants in ZNHIT3 have not been identified in patients with PEHO or PEHO-like syndrome in other populations. It has therefore been suggested that PEHO syndrome caused by ZNHIT3 variants does not occur outside of the Finnish population. We describe the first patient outside Finland who carries compound heterozygous variants in ZNHIT3 gene causing PEHO syndrome. Trio genome sequencing was carried out and the identified variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The patient filled all diagnostic clinical criteria of PEHO syndrome. We identified biallelic missense variants in ZNHIT3 gene: the c.92C > T p.(Ser31Leu) variant (NM_004773.3), which is described previously as causing PEHO syndrome and the second novel variant c.41G > T p.(Cys14Phe). There are only eight heterozygous carriers of c.41G > T variant in the gnomAD database and it is predicted damaging by multiple in silico algorithms. The ZNHIT3-associated PEHO syndrome exists outside of the Finnish population.

The PEHO syndrome (MIM 260565)-Progressive Encephalopathy with Edema, Hypsarrhythmia, and Optic atrophy, was first described by Salonen et al in 14 Finnish patients (Salonen et al., 1991). The diagnostic criteria include early onset seizures, infantile spasms with hypsarrhythmia, early arrest of mental development, profound hypotonia with no head support or ability to sit, and absent visual fixation from the first months of life (Somer 1993). Additional features include facial edema, microcephaly developing by 12 months of age, facial dysmorphism and optic atrophy. Brain MRI and CT show early progressive brain atrophy primarily involving the cerebellum and brainstem (Somer et al., 1993). Patients without optic nerve atrophy and brain imaging abnormalities, but fulfilling other PEHO criteria, are often described as a PEHO-like syndrome (Anttonen and Lehesjoki 2017). PEHO syndrome is most common in the Finnish population with an estimated incidence of 1:74,000 (Somer 1993). By combining homozygosity mapping in Finnish families with Sanger sequencing of positional candidate genes and with exome sequencing (ES) a homozygous missense substitution c.92C>T (p.Ser31Leu) in ZNHIT3 was identified as the primary cause of PEHO syndrome (Anttonen et al., 2017). We describe the first patient outside of Finland who carries compound heterozygous variants in ZNHIT3 gene causing PEHO syndrome; one is the recurrent Finnish variant, but the second is a novel variant.

The proband was born at term by Caesarean section due to breech position with birth weight 3190g (−0.5 SD), length 50 cm (0 SD), OFC 34.5 cm (−0.5 SD) and Apgar scores of 7 and 8. She is the only child in this family; family history is unremarkable.

At the age of one month, she was hospitalised due to muscular hypotonia and lethargy. Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome was diagnosed on electrocardiography. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are shown and described in Fig. 1A, C.

At the age of 6 months, her length was 67 cm (−1 SD), weight 7590g (−1 SD), OFC 41 cm (−1.5 SD). Facial phenotype is shown in Fig. 2A. Extremely severe muscular hypotonia, peculiar skin folds and profound developmental delay was noted. She had absent visual fixation, abnormal rotatory eye movements, and no head control. She had peculiar large nipples, but they were not inverted.

At the age of 10m generalized seizures started, first described as myoclonic, but later as infantile spasms. EEG showed hypsarrhythmia (multifocal burst suppressions from both hemispheres) and antiepileptic treatment was administered. At the age of 1 year, she was hospitalized due to status epilepticus, which complicated by aspiration pneumonia, sepsis and a urinary tract infection. On clinical evaluation, microcephaly (OFC was 41 cm (−3.5 SD)) was once again noted and low weight 8.5 kg (−2.5 SD) was noted for the first time. In addition, she had severe central hypotonia with distal spasticity, exaggerated deep tendon reflexes, clonus, a positive Babinski sign, and feeding difficulties (requiring tube feeding). Brain MRI results are given in Fig. 1D and computed tomography (CT) scan results in Fig. 1B. At 17m of age, an ophthalmologist clarified that the child is blind: there was no pupillary reaction to light, absent eye fixation, chaotic movement of eye bulbus, pale and small disks, and temporal disk pallor (Fig. 2B,F).

At the age of 3.5 years she has progressive microcephaly (OFC 46.5 cm (−2.5SD)), weight was 15 kg (−0.5SD). She had no eye contact (Fig. 2C), tapering fingers (Fig. 2E), severe muscular hypotonia, dystonias, myoclonus, exaggerated deep tendon reflexes, and a positive Babinski sign and clonus bilaterally. EEG showed pathological slow background activity with multifocal and bilateral interictal epileptic activity. There were ictal episodes in sleep (myoclonus and two spasms). Brain MRI results are given in Fig. 1E.

At the age of 8.5 years, her height is 126 cm (−1 SD), weight 24.8 kg (−0.5 SD), OFC 47 cm (−4 SD). She has decerebrate posture with contractures, brisk tendon reflexes with clonuses, and scoliosis. Her face is edematous with an opened mouth appearance (Fig. 2D). She demonstrates the first signs of early puberty. She is fed by gastrostomy tube. The most recent EEG demonstrated extremely low background activity with multifocal bilateral interictal epileptic discharges and ictal tonic spasms on awakening. Brain MRI results are shown in Fig. 1F–G. Compared to the images at the age of 3y 6m there was minimal interval change.

She was tested for intrauterine infections. A metabolic workup, chromosomal microarray analysis, methylation test for Prader-Willi/Angelman syndrome, COL4A1 and JAM3 gene sequencing, muscle biopsy and an interferon level measurement in the serum and cerebrospinal fluid were performed with normal results.

Trio ES was initially performed in the clinical diagnostic setting at Tartu University Hospital (Estonia) to search for genetic variants in disease-associated genes. The exome was enriched using SureSelect Human All Exon V5 kit (Agilent), and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq platform.

Trio genome sequencing (GS) and data processing were performed by Genomics Platform at the Broad Institute of Harvard and MIT (Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared from 500 ng to 1.5 ug of genomic DNA using a PCR-Free protocol. These libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten with 150 bp paired-end reads and a target mean coverage of >30×. Genome sequencing data was processed through a pipeline based on Picard, using base quality score recalibration and local realignment at known indels. We used the BWA-MEM aligner for mapping reads to the human genome build 37 (hg19). Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (indels) were jointly called across all samples using Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) HaplotypeCaller package version 3.4. Default filters were applied to SNP and indel calls using the GATK Variant Quality Score Recalibration (VQSR) approach. Lastly, the variants were annotated using Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) (McLaren et al., 2016). Written informed consent was obtained from the family for the routine clinical study and later for research.

Trio ES of the proband and parents did not identify clearly pathogenic variants in known genes causing Mendelian disorders. Analysis was performed one year before ZNHIT3 was identified as a cause of PEHO syndrome.

On a trio-based GS analysis, variant filtration for de novo or recessive variants with allele frequencies below 1% in ExAC and gnomAD databases (Lek et al., 2016) revealed two high quality heterozygous missense variants in ZNHIT3. The c.92C>T p.(Ser31Leu) variant (NM_004773.3) was inherited from the mother and is described previously as causing PEHO syndrome in the homozygous state (Anttonen et al., 2017). The second variant c.41G>T p.(Cys14Phe) has not been reported as disease causing and was inherited from the father. There are only eight heterozygous carriers of the c.41G>T variant in gnomAD database and multiple in silico algorithms indicate a damaging effect (CADD 29, REVEL 0.9, PolyPhen2 0.999). This missense variant affects a highly conserved residue (conserved across species from Drosophila to human) in the HIT-type zinc finger (zf-HIT) domain. Both variants were confirmed on Sanger sequencing in the index patient and her parents (see Supplementary figure for the chromatograms of for both variants in this trio).

Reported variants have been submitted to the Global Variome shared Leiden Open Variation Database (patient ID: 00229643).

We describe 8.5 years old female who fills all of the diagnostic clinical criteria of PEHO syndrome (Somer 1993) and carry compound heterozygosity for the “Finnish variant” and the very rare new variant, which affects a highly conserved residue in the zf-HIT domain.

After the initial description in 1991 (Salonen et al., 1991), the underlying etiology of PEHO syndrome remained unknown for many years. In 2017 Anttonen et al showed that loss-of-function of ZNHIT3, caused by homozygosity for the p.Ser31Leu variant, caused autosomal recessive PEHO syndrome in 26 Finnish PEHO patients (Anttonen et al., 2017). All of these patients fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for PEHO syndrome, except for infantile spasms (89%). Functional studies demonstrated that both knockdown and genome editing of znhit3 in zebrafish embryos recapitulate the patients’ cerebellar defects, microcephaly and edema. Moreover, knockdown of Znhit3 in cultured mouse granule neurons and ex vivo cerebellar slices indicate that ZNHIT3 is indispensable for granule neuron survival and migration (Anttonen et al., 2017).

During last few years, five other genes (KIF1A, CCDC88A, GNAO1, CDKL5 and PRUNE1) has been described to cause PEHO or PEHO-like syndrome (Abdulkareem et al., 2019; Gawlinski et al., 2016; Langlois et al., 2016; Nahorski et al., 2016; Zollo et al., 2017). De novo disease-causing variants in KIF1A were first described as causing PEHO syndrome, but with large phenotypic variability even in patients with the same variants (Langlois et al., 2016). Soon after that, Nahorski et al described a consanguineous family of British origin with three children born with features of PEHO syndrome and a homozygous frameshift mutation in CCDC88A gene (Nahorski et al., 2016). Quite recently, another consanguineous family with a novel homozygous nonsense mutation of the CCDC88A gene causing PEHO-like syndrome was published (Abdulkareem et al., 2019). It has also recently been hypothesized that PEHO and PEHO-like syndrome may represent a severe end of the spectrum of the early-onset encephalopathies (Gawlinski et al., 2016). Among two patients with a prior diagnosis of PEHO syndrome and two patients with PEHO-like features, a disease-causing de novo variant in known genes (GNAO1+HESX1 and CDKL5) was associated with neurodevelopmental disorders in two of four individuals. After the identification of ZNHIT3 homozygous p.Ser31Leu variant in Finnish PEHO patients (Anttonen et al., 2017), an international group analysed a cohort of well-defined non-Finnish PEHO syndrome (n=9) and PEHO-like syndrome (n=15) cases from variable ancestries and failed to identify additional mutations in ZNHIT3 (Salpietro et al., 2017). The same group recently characterized a new autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental and degenerative disorder caused by biallelic mutations in PRUNE1, with some of the affected individuals showing PEHO or PEHO-like features (Zollo et al., 2017). It was therefore concluded that autosomal recessive PEHO syndrome due to ZNHIT3 variants is likely to be exceptionally rare outside of Finland, especially in association with the isolated founder p.Ser31Leu mutation. However, based on the available clinical data, these patients do not explicitly fulfill the diagnostic criteria of PEHO syndrome and thus are PEHO-like (Anttonen and Lehesjoki 2017).

Chitre et al recently evaluated 19 children from 11 families; seven had PEHO and 12 had PEHO-like syndrome and a pathogenic genetic variant was identified in 15 of them (PCLO, SCN2A, PLAA, UBA5, CASK, CCDC88 and SCN1A gene (Chitre et al., 2018). Based on their results, the authors concluded that PEHO and PEHO-like syndrome are clinically and genetically diverse entities and represent the endpoint of many severe epileptic disorders.

It has been suggested that PEHO is no longer an appropriate clinical label outside of the Finnish population (Chitre et al., 2018). However, our described case argues against this point. Although Estonians and Finns are from the same Finno-Ugric language group, they are genetically rather different (Nelis et al., 2009). In our patient, the maternal p.Ser31Leu variant is classified as the Finnish PEHO allele, but as much as the mother knows of her family pedigree, she has no Finnish roots. The paternal p.Cys14Phe has not described before.

We describe the first case of the ZNHIT3-associated PEHO syndrome outside Finland. At the same time, we agree with the recent published data regarding the clinical and genetic heterogeneity of ‘PEHO spectrum disorders’ associated with variants in different progressive encephalopathy genes and the need for a comprehensive diagnostic work-up. We suggest that all the known genes causing PEHO and PEHO-like severe early-onset encephalopathies should be included in early-onset epileptic encephalopathy panels. In case of a severely ill child, the performing trio ES or GS is the most effective way for specifying the etiology.
