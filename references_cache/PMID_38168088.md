The NFIX gene encodes a DNA-binding protein belonging to the nuclear factor one 
(NFI) family of transcription factors. Pathogenic variants of NFIX are 
associated with two autosomal dominant Mendelian disorders, Malan syndrome (MIM 
614753) and Marshall-Smith syndrome (MIM 602535), which are clinically distinct 
due to different disease-causing mechanisms. NFIX variants associated with Malan 
syndrome are missense variants mostly located in exon 2 encoding the N-terminal 
DNA binding and dimerization domain or are protein-truncating variants that 
trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) resulting in NFIX haploinsufficiency. 
NFIX variants associated with Marshall-Smith syndrome are protein-truncating and 
are clustered between exons 6 and 10, including a recurrent Alu-mediated 
deletion of exons 6 and 7, which can escape NMD. The more severe phenotype of 
Marshall-Smith syndrome is likely due to a dominant-negative effect of these 
protein-truncating variants that escape NMD. Here, we report a child with 
clinical features of Malan syndrome who has a de novo NFIX intragenic 
duplication. Using genome sequencing, exon-level microarray analysis, and RNA 
sequencing, we show that this duplication encompasses exons 6 and 7 and leads to 
NFIX haploinsufficiency. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of 
Malan Syndrome caused by an intragenic NFIX duplication.

Malan syndrome (previously called as “Sotos syndrome 2”) is a rare disorder clinically characterized by postnatal overgrowth, macrocephaly, distinctive facial features, skeletal anomalies, intellectual disability, and behavior problems [Gurrieri and others 2015; Macchiaiolo and others 2022; Malan and others 2010; Priolo and others 2018]. Malan syndrome is caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the NFIX gene, including loss-of-function missense variants, protein-truncating variants that trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), and deletions of the entire NFIX gene. The underlying disease-causing mechanism is haploinsufficiency of NFIX.

A different set of pathogenic variants in the NFIX gene cause Marshall-Smith syndrome, a disorder clinically characterized by markedly accelerated skeletal maturation at birth, respiratory difficulties, failure to thrive, and characteristic facial traits [Aggarwal and others 2017; Marshall and others 1971; Shaw and others 2010]. Marshall-Smith syndrome is associated with heterozygous protein-truncating variants clustered between exons 6 and 10 of the NFIX gene. In contrast with Malan syndrome, previous studies have shown that protein-truncating variants associated with Marshall-Smith syndrome can escape NMD and confer a possible dominant-negative effect, which may contribute to the increased phenotypic severity of Marshall-Smith syndrome as compared to Malan syndrome. Of note, a recurrent Alu-mediated deletion of exons 6 and 7 account for about one quarter of patients with Marshall-Smith syndrome [Schanze and others 2014].

Here, we report on a novel molecular mechanism in Malan syndrome - an intragenic NFIX duplication occurred in the Alu-mediated recurrent copy number variation (CNV) hotspot - in a patient referred to the Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN). We identified this mechanism through reanalysis of a previously non-diagnostic genome sequencing, supplemented by exon-level XON array, and RNA sequencing. Combined with the clinical evaluation, these findings helped provide a definitive diagnosis of Malan syndrome in this undiagnosed individual.

A 5-year-old boy was referred to the University of Utah Intermountain West clinical site of the Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN). This patient was delivered at 39 weeks of gestation to a G5P4 33-year-old mother with gestational diabetes by induced vaginal delivery because of low amniotic fluid. His birth weight was 3224 g, length was 50.8 cm, and head circumference (HC) was 35.6 cm (all appropriate for gestational age). He was noted to have hypotonia at a few months of age, global developmental delays at 4 months of age: he was not reaching, vocalizing, or rolling. He began sitting at 10 months, started walking around age 3, and spoke his first word at about the same time. At 29 months of age, his development was equivalent to that of a 10-month-old child. At the age of 6 years, he has an individualized education plan in school. The Vineland developmental scale found a composite score of 62 (mean 100, standard deviation (SD) of 15), consistent with moderate developmental delay.

He was noted to have macrocephaly at 15 months of age (HC 50.6 cm, +2.6 SD) that became more pronounced at age 2 (HC 52.8 cm, +3 SD) and has persisted with a HC of 55 cm at 5.5 years of age. His height was 90 cm at age 2 years (75th percentile) and 124 cm at 6 years (84th percentile). His weight was 12.2 kg (32nd percentile) at age 2 years and 18.5 kg at 6 years (20th percentile).

He has spells that are possibly consistent with seizures and has been treated with an antiepileptic drug. An electroencephalogram showed nonspecific diffuse background slowing. A brain MRI at age 5 showed megalencephaly, dysgyria with oversulcation, cerebellar overgrowth resulting in mild Chiari I malformation. There were nonspecific scattered small T2 hyperintense foci in the cerebral white matter.

Medically, he had recurrent otitis as an infant, and ear tubes were placed at 3 years of age. Additional medical findings included gastroesophageal reflux, drooling, amblyopia, joint laxity, soft skin, constipation, and urinary tract infections. His facial traits included bilateral epicanthal folds, downslanting palpebral fissures, prominent philtrum, broad mouth, broad fingers with wrinkled skin on palms, and scarce hair (Figure 1).

He had prior extensive non-diagnostic genetic evaluation including chromosomal microarray analysis, Fragile X testing, Sotos syndrome testing, Autism/intellectual disability NGS panel, trio exome sequencing, and trio genome sequencing.

Written informed consents were obtained from the patient’s family for publication of clinical information and photos.

Trio-GS was initially performed at Illumina Clinical Services Laboratory in 2018. We requested the raw FASTQ files and performed the reanalysis using the Emedgene software (Illumina Inc.) for both sequence and structural variants. Structural variants were also called and analyzed using in-house developed software RUFUS (https://github.com/jandrewrfarrell/RUFUS) and Smoove (https://github.com/brentp/smoove)[Layer and others 2014].

DNA from skin fibroblasts of this patient was assessed by the CytoScanTM XON array (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacture’s protocol. The data was analyzed for copy number change in NFIX gene using the Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS) software (v4.2.0.80).

As part of UDN workflow, RNA-seq was performed at Baylor Genetics as previously described [Murdock and others 2021]. Analysis of fibroblast RNA-seq data was performed as previously described [Lewis and others 2020]. Briefly, count matrices were generated using Genomic Features and Genomic Alignments [Lawrence and others 2013]. DESeq2 was then used to perform differential expression analyses [Love and others 2014]. Fibroblasts from seven other UDN patients were used as controls and P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Heatmaps were made using the Pheatmap R package and volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 R package.

In the reanalysis of the raw GS data, we identified a de novo intragenic microduplication in the NFIX gene (5.9 kb, GRCh37 genomic coordinate chr19:13185721–13191640, Figure 2a). Using exon-level CytoScan Xon array, we confirmed that this duplication encompasses NFIX exons 6 and 7 (Figure 2b). The two breakpoints located in introns 5 and 7 overlap with two AluY repeats with high sequence identity. Further analysis of GS reads revealed that the duplicated segment was in tandem and in direct orientation relative to the original locus. This configuration is expected to result in frameshift and a premature termination codon which may trigger NMD or produce a truncated protein product (p.Gly368AlafsTer51). Fibroblasts derived RNA-seq showed approximately 50% of NFIX transcript level compared to controls and did not detect the mutant transcript (Figure 3), suggesting that this duplication resulted in NFIX haploinsufficiency, likely due to NMD.

Clinical reassessment was also consistent with a diagnosis of Malan syndrome.

Pathogenic variants of NFIX are associated with Malan syndrome and Marshall-Smith syndrome [Malan and others 2010; Martinez and others 2015; Mulder and others 2020; Zenker and others 2019]. These two allelic disorders are distinct in their clinical presentations and are attributed to different disease-causing mechanisms (Figure 4). In this study, we identified an intragenic duplication of NFIX in a patient with Malan syndrome and showed that this duplication results in haploinsufficiency of NFIX. This case report presents a novel molecular mechanism associated with Malan Syndrome.

It is important to note that this intragenic duplication of NFIX is different from 19p13 microduplications encompassing the entire NFIX and some other genes [Trimouille and others 2018]. Patients with 19p13 microduplications exhibit a reversed phenotype of Malan syndrome, with short stature, and small head circumference in addition to intellectual disability. This reversal in phenotype may be attributed to the opposite effect of triplosensitivity rather than haploinsufficiency of NFIX.

This duplication, which encompasses exons 6 and 7 of NFIX, is flanked by two AluY repeats, and therefore it is expected to be a recurrent event arising from Alu-Alu recombination. Of note, the reciprocal Alu-mediated deletion of exons 6 and 7 has been reported in patients with Marshall-Smith syndrome. In addition, our data indicates that this recurrent copy number variation (CNV) hotspot in NFIX is very likely to be missed by routine genomic microarray or NGS testing due to its small size. When Malan or Marshall-Smith syndrome is clinically suspected but remains molecularly undiagnosed after extensive genetic evaluation, the 5.9 kb recurrent CNV region encompassing NFIX exons 6 and 7 needs to be scrutinized using an appropriate assay.

Previous studies have shown that a significant proportion of UDN cases could be attributed to copy number variant that had been missed on prior testing, and some cases have been solved by using GS and RNA-seq [Cope and others 2020; Murdock and others 2022]. This study adds to the growing body of evidence supporting the diagnostic utility of combined GS and RNA-seq for rare Mendelian diseases, as well as the value of GS reanalysis.
