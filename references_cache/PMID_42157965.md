# Gene therapy rescues cone function in opn1mw (-/-) /opn1sw (-/-) /Nrl (-/-) mice, an all-cone model of blue cone monochromacy.
**Authors:** Cahill ME, Chmelik K, Ashcraft ME, Brothers BA, Guan T, Nguyen J, Puja A, Eminhizer M, Shaw LM, Barbera RJ, Sechrest ER, Du J, Deng WT
**Journal:** Mol Ther Adv (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.omta.2025.201657](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.omta.2025.201657)

## Content

Blue cone monochromacy (BCM) is a congenital vision disorder caused by the loss of L- and M-cone function, leading to severe loss of visual acuity and color perception deficits. To test gene therapy for BCM in a model that mimics the densely packed foveal cones of human retinas, we generated a triple knockout (TKO) mouse model (Opn1mw -/- /Opn1sw -/- /Nrl -/-) with an all-cone retina lacking cone opsins. These mice exhibit abolished cone function, disrupted phototransduction, and altered metabolite profiles resulting from loss of opsin. We then administered AAV-mediated human L-opsin gene supplementation therapy at 1 and 5 months of age to assess cone treatability before and after cone degeneration occurs. Treatment restored cone function, replenished essential cone phototransduction proteins, and partially regenerated some cone outer segment membrane structure. Rescue effects persisted for at least 5 months post-treatment in both younger and older treated groups. These findings demonstrate that opsin-deficient, densely packed cones remain viable targets for gene therapy and support the clinical potential of this approach in BCM patients.

Cone photoreceptors in the human retina are crucial for color perception and high-acuity vision. The retina contains approximately 6 million cone photoreceptors, categorized into three distinct types. Short-wavelength-sensitive (S-) cones, which detect blue light, make up about 3%–5% of all cones and are predominantly found in the peripheral retina among the rod photoreceptors. Long-wavelength-sensitive (L-) and medium-wavelength-sensitive (M-) cones, responsible for detecting red and green light, respectively, account for the remaining 95% of the cone population. The exceptional visual acuity provided by cone photoreceptors is due to the dense concentration of L- and M-cones within the macula, particularly in the fovea, where they are most abundant.1,2,3

Blue cone monochromacy (BCM) is an X-linked congenital vision disorder caused by the loss of function in both L- and M-cone photoreceptors. Patients with BCM experience severe impairments in visual acuity and color discrimination, along with photophobia, myopia, and pendular nystagmus.4,5 The condition is linked to mutations in the OPN1LW and OPN1MW gene cluster, which encodes L- and M-opsins. L- and M-opsins are visual pigments that absorb light to initiate the phototransduction pathway. They also serve as key structural components of the cone outer segments (COSs), which contain numerous stacked discs housing all phototransduction proteins. In BCM patients, the COSs of L- and M-cones are significantly shortened due to the loss of these opsins. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging of BCM patients reveals thinning of the foveal outer nuclear layer (ONL) at an early age, indicating cone degeneration. However, the cone inner segments in the remaining cones persist, making them a promising target for gene therapy.6,7,8

One of the most common causes of BCM is a deletion mutation in the upstream locus control region, which eliminates the expression of both L- and M-opsins.9,10,11,12,13,14,15 We previously demonstrated that gene replacement therapy can restore cone function, promote regeneration of COS, and replenish essential phototransduction proteins in Opn1mw−/−/Opn1sw−/− (DKO) mice, a model for BCM with deletion mutations.16 However, mouse retinas are rod dominant and only 3% of photoreceptor cells are cones. Additionally, these cones are dispersed across the entire retina, unlike human L- and M-cones, which are densely packed within the fovea. Therefore, a critical question remains: can densely packed, opsin-deficient foveal cones still be rescued?

To better mimic BCM fovea cones and to test gene therapy feasibility in such conditions, we crossed DKO mice with Nrl−/− mice to generate an all-cone retina model. Nrl−/− mice lack the neural retina leucine zipper (Nrl) transcription factor required for the differentiation of rod photoreceptors. Consequently, these mice do not develop rods but instead form an all-cone retina composed of M- and S-cones.17,18 The Nrl−/− retina exhibits two key features that mimic the human macula: (1) cones are expressed densely and adjacent to one another without interpolation of rods and (2) COS interact directly with the retinal pigment epithelium (except those inside rosettes) instead of being obscured by the taller rods. Nrl−/− mice have been widely used as a genetic background to examine mutations or alleles associated with cone dysfunction, and they have been proven valuable in understanding the mechanisms of cone degeneration in various forms of achromatopsia and Leber congenital amaurosis.19,20,21,22,23,24

We demonstrated that all-cone retinas of Opn1mw−/−/Opn1sw−/−/Nrl−/− triple knockout (TKO) mice exhibit abolished cone visual responses, a significant reduction in essential cone phototransduction proteins, and altered metabolites associated with cone metabolism. Thus, the retinas of these mice not only structurally mimic the fovea of human BCM but also display similar retinal functional and morphological abnormalities, phenocopying BCM with deletion mutations. As a result, TKO mice serve as a more accurate and valuable model for testing cone gene therapy for BCM. We then performed adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene therapy and demonstrated restored cone function and replenished essential COS proteins. Our results advance the feasibility of gene therapy for BCM by showing that densely packed cones lacking opsins can still be rescued.

It has been shown that Nrl−/− mice undergo retinal degeneration during the first 4 months of age. This degeneration slows but persists up to 7 months, eventually stabilizing around 10 months of age. The disorganized retina of Nrl−/− mice is characterized by rosettes, or ring-shaped deformities, in the ONL composed of cones. These rosettes are more abundant in young mice and gradually become less prominent with age.25 Studies have also shown that retinal degeneration in Nrl−/− mice is accompanied by microglial activation and migration from the inner retina to the outer retina to remove degenerating cells, a phenomenon that begins at 3 weeks and peaks at 2 months of age.25

To investigate the impact of cone opsin loss on metabolism, we performed targeted metabolomics using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) on retinas from TKO and Nrl−/− mice at P30. Multivariate analysis revealed a clear separation in the retinal metabolome between TKO and Nrl−/− mice, demonstrating that TKO significantly impacts retinal metabolome (Figure 2A). Among the 115 metabolites detected, 13 were significantly altered in TKO retinas compared to Nrl−/− mice, with 9 increased and 4 decreased (Figure 2B). Strikingly, the 9 increased metabolites in TKO retinas were all acylcarnitines, including short- (Iso C4:0, C4:0), medium- (C6:0), and long-chain (C14:0, C18:0, C18:1) species (Figures 2B and 2C). These acylcarnitines often reflect the changes of corresponding acyl-CoA pools, which are crucial for fatty acid and energy metabolism. Among the four reduced metabolites, the amino acids proline and methionine, and the cyclic nucleotides cGMP and cAMP, were decreased approximately 2-fold in TKO mice (Figure 2D). These metabolites play critical roles in phototransduction and cell signaling, as well as in protein synthesis, post-translational modifications, and autophagic signaling. We performed gene therapy in 1-month-old TKO eyes using AAV8-Y733F-mediated delivery of human L-opsin cDNA driven by the cone-specific promoter PR2.1 via subretinal injection. Since cone opsin is the most abundant outer segment protein, we chose the AAV8-Y733F capsid, which has been shown to confer higher transduction in photoreceptors than AAV5 and AAV8.26 We demonstrated that gene replacement therapy successfully rescued cone function at 1 month post-injection (Figures 3A and 3B), with the rescue persisting for at least an additional 5 months (Figures 3C and 3D).

At 1 month post-injection (1M+1M), the averaged photopic b-wave amplitude in treated TKO mice was 244.8 ± 20.5 μV (average ±SEM, n = 6), significantly higher than contralateral uninjected eyes, which have no measurable b-wave amplitudes (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001), and approximately 61% of age-matched Nrl−/− controls (401.2 ± 16.3 μV, average ± SEM, n = 6, p < 0.0001) at 120 cd∗s/m2. Interestingly, by 5 months post-injection (1M+5M), the ERG b-wave amplitudes of treated eyes were statistically comparable to those of age-matched Nrl−/− mice. At 120 cd∗s/m2, the b-wave amplitude was 159.7 ± 19.8 μV for treated TKO and 183.8 ± 10.8 μV for Nrl−/− (average ±SEM, n = 6, p = 0.0988). These results suggest that although treated TKO eyes experience some functional decline over time, the loss was considerably smaller than the natural age-related decline observed in the Nrl−/− controls.

In untreated eyes, we observed residual membrane structures immediately apical to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE); however, they lacked disc organization that is characteristic of COS (Figure 4). Western blot analysis revealed robust AAV-mediated L-opsin expression in 1M+5M treated TKO retinas, while no expression was detected in untreated eyes (Figure 5D). The L-opsin levels in treated TKO eyes were drastically higher than endogenous M-opsin levels in Nrl−/− retinas. This difference arises because Nrl−/− retinas express much higher S-opsin than M-opsin.17 Previous studies showed that the PR2.1 promoter drives transgene expression in mouse S-cones.28,29 Therefore, the higher L-opsin levels in treated eyes likely result from AAV-mediated L-opsin transducing both M- and S-cones in the TKO retinas.

Collectively, these data show that gene therapy rescued cone function, regenerated COS structure, and replenished COS-specific proteins in the cone-dominant BCM mouse model when TKO mice were treated at 1 month of age. This rescue effect persisted for at least 5 months post-treatment.

Nrl−/− mice experience significant retinal degeneration in the first 4 months of age with minor degeneration continuing until around 7 months. We showed that TKO retinas also experience degeneration during the first 5 months of age (Figure 1B). To test whether gene therapy is still effective after cones have degenerated, we treated TKO mice at 5 months of age using the same AAV vector and followed up at 1 and 5 months post-injection. We showed that therapy was still effective when treatment was administered at 5 months and lasted for at least another 5 months after injection.

At 1 month post-injection (5M+1M), the average photopic b-wave amplitude at 120 cd∗s/m2 was 100.9 ± 18.8 μV, approximately 80.0% of that in age-matched Nrl−/− controls (126.0 ± 39.4 μV, average ±SEM, n = 6/each group, p = 0.1355) (Figures 6A and 6B). By 5 months post-injection (5M+5M), treated TKO mice had b-wave amplitudes of 76.6 ± 4.95 μV, approximately 80.3% of that in age-matched Nrl−/− controls (95.4 ± 11.3 μV, n = 6/each group), significantly higher than contralateral untreated controls (p < 0.0030) (Figures 6C and 6D). Western blot analysis in 5M+5M treated TKO retinas probing for L/M-opsin revealed abundant AAV-mediated L-opsin expression, while untreated eyes lacked M-opsin (Figure 7B). Again, the higher L-opsin levels in treated eyes compared to Nrl−/− controls can be attributed to the PR2.1 promoter indiscriminately driving L-opsin expression in S-cones.

In this study, we demonstrated that the all-cone, opsin-deficient retinas of TKO mice closely resemble the foveal cones of BCM patients with deletion mutations. TKO retinas have abolished cone function, significantly reduced essential COS phototransduction proteins, and altered metabolites associated with cone metabolism and cell signaling. We demonstrated that AAV-mediated gene replacement therapy restored cone function, elevated the expression levels of essential COS proteins, and partially regenerated the COS structure. These findings advance BCM gene therapy research by demonstrating that densely packed, opsin-deficient cones can still be rescued.

Although cones in TKO mice degenerate, cone loss is less severe than in Nrl−/− retinas between 1 and 5 months of age. Additionally, as early as P30, TKO retinas have significantly fewer rosettes than Nrl−/− retinas, likely due to the absence of COS in these mice. A lack of rosettes has also been observed in other retinal disease models on the Nrl−/− background.30,31,32 In all these cases, COS formation was affected.

Cone-rich retinas have been shown to contain significantly higher levels of long-chain and very long-chain fatty acid.33 The elevated acylcarnitine levels in TKO retinas may reflect OS membrane degradation due to cone degeneration, or impaired OS elongation resulting from opsin deficiency. Acylcarnitines are also vital for fatty acid oxidation, facilitating fatty acid transport into mitochondria. The accumulation of multiple acylcarnitine species suggests impaired mitochondrial metabolism in TKO cones. Methionine is a major methyl donor for DNA, protein, and phospholipid methylation, which are crucial for cone development and survival.34,35 Previous studies have shown that DNA methylation is increased in degenerating photoreceptors.36 Hypermethylation of metabolic genes, e.g., ELOVL2, may further compromise fatty acid elongation and photoreceptor outer segment synthesis in TKO retinas.37 The cyclic nucleotides cAMP and cGMP were substantially reduced in TKO retinas. As key secondary messengers, cGMP and cAMP regulate phototransduction, lipid metabolism, and glycolysis.38,39,40 Their reduction is consistent with the loss of opsin and decreased phototransduction proteins in BCM patients.41

Proline is also reduced in TKO mice retinas. Proline is an essential component for collagen synthesis, mitochondrial metabolism, and redox balance and also mediates metabolic communication between the RPE and retina.42,43 Reduced proline levels may disrupt this inter-tissue communication and increase retinal vulnerability to oxidative stress. This is supported by studies showing that proline supplementation enhances resistance to oxidative damage and improves visual function.44

TKO mice showed robust ERG rescue when treatment was administered at 1 month of age. Although there is some function decline over time, treatment significantly halted the degeneration process. In contrast, Nrl−/− retinas experienced significant age-related degeneration during the same period. By 5 months of age, degeneration significantly slowed in both TKO and Nrl−/− mice. Treatment administered at this age showed some decline over time, but with a similar degeneration rate seen in the Nrl−/− controls. These results suggest that gene therapy slowed the natural degeneration that is associated with the Nrl−/− background when treatment was applied at an early age.

Previously, we showed that in DKO mice, a rod-dominant retina mouse model for BCM with deletion mutations, treatment before 3 months of age resulted in robust rescue. However, rescue efficacy was significantly decreased when DKO mice were treated at 5 months of age. Since the retinal structures and degenerative statuses of the DKO versus TKO mouse strains are different, comparing their optimal ages for rescue may not be meaningful. However, it would be interesting to know whether densely packed opsin-deficient cones in TKO mice survive better than DKO cones, which are distributed across the entire retina among the rods. TKO cones are in direct contact with the retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cell layer, which may provide better survival of cones.

In summary, our study demonstrates that AAV-mediated gene therapy effectively restores cone function and essential cone phototransduction proteins in a cone-dominant mouse model of BCM. TKO mice recapitulate key features of human BCM, providing a relevant platform to assess therapeutic potential. These findings support the feasibility of gene replacement strategies for BCM affecting central vision.

Methods are previously described.49 In brief, retinas were collected immediately from euthanized mice and sonicated in protein extraction buffer (0.23 M sucrose, 5 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1x protease inhibitor cocktail [MilliporeSigma]). 100 μg protein of each sample was run in a 10% Mini-PROTEAN TGX gel (BioRad), and an Immun-Blot Low Fluorescence PVDF membrane (BioRad) was used to rapidly transfer the blot. The primary antibodies TUBA4A and OPN1LW/MW were used to detect loading control protein and endogenous/transgenic OPN1MW protein levels, respectively.

Methods for the collection, fixation, sagittal cross sectioning, and immunohistochemistry staining of mouse retinas are previously described.49 In brief, mouse eyes were collected, and a 20G needle was used to perforate the cornea. Eyes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 1x PBS before and after the cornea was removed and then washed and incubated in 20% sucrose in 1x PBS overnight. Eyes were then incubated in 10% sucrose and 50% O.C.T. compound in 1x PBS before being flash frozen in cryomolds with 100% O.C.T. compound. These samples were sectioned and placed onto Superfrost plus microscope slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before proceeding with immunohistochemistry staining.

Methods for the collection, fixation, and preparation of retina samples for TEM are previously described.50 In brief, eyes were collected and the corneas punctured using a 20G needle before fixation in 2% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 100 mM cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4). The cornea and lens were removed before incubating in fixative for 2 days at room temperature. Tissue was stained with 2% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer and 1% uranyl acetate before dehydration and then was embedded in Poly/Bed 812 resin (Polysciences Inc.). Once cured, tissue sections were mounted onto grids and stained with 3% Reynold’s lead citrate before imaging with the JEOL JEM-1010 transmission electron microscope.
