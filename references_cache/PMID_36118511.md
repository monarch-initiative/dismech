# Early-Onset Neurodevelopmental Movement Disorder Secondary to Novel Mutation in KCNN2.
**Authors:** Fearon C, Grippe TC, Chen R, Lang AE
**Journal:** Mov Disord Clin Pract (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mdc3.13535](https://doi.org/10.1002/mdc3.13535)

## Content

The number of genes associated with early‐onset movement disorders has expanded significantly in recent years.
1
,
2
 Increasingly, these disorders are discovered via next‐generation sequencing (NGS) methods.
2
 Given the widespread use of NGS across many clinical and non‐clinical settings, the discovery of incidental genetic findings is not uncommon.
3
 Furthermore, while the unbiased and comprehensive genetic evaluation achieved by NGS leads to a significantly increased diagnostic yield for neurological disorders, such methods frequently identify previously unobserved variants for which clinical significance is unclear.
4
,
5
 Since it is likely that such variants in genes associated with movement disorders will be increasingly detected by clinicians and researchers from other specialties, the significance of such findings may need to be determined by a movement disorders specialist. We present such an example, in which a novel mutation in the small conductance calcium‐activated potassium channel 2 gene (KCNN2), recently described as a cause of early‐onset dominant neurodevelopmental movement disorders,
6
,
7
 was discovered in a research NGS study for congenital heart disease.

A 19 year‐old right‐handed man with a background of complex cyanotic congenital heart disease, anxiety and migraine was referred with a complex hyperkinetic movement disorder. He had delayed motor milestones (first efforts to walk at 18 months but difficulty with balance until 4 years) and delayed speech (at age 4, he could only be understood by his mother). At the age of 4, tremor of his hands was first noticed while attempting art in Kindergarten. He had difficulty writing and typing during schooling and, in addition, he experienced jerks of his upper limbs causing him to accidentally throw objects across the classroom. He had difficulty learning to ride a bicycle due to poor balance. His mother noticed in photos that his head would be turned to one side. He completed mainstream schooling with special needs assistance. The clinical picture was non‐progressive and there was no relevant family history.

Physical examination (Video 1)  revealed a right torticollis with left laterocollis. He had slow rapid alternating movements of his tongue. There were flurries of upper limb tremulousness at rest and with posture and action. In addition, there were superimposed myoclonic jerks of upper limbs, lower limbs and trunk. Rapid alternating movements were slow without fatigue or decrement, but persistent mirror movements of right upper and lower limb were seen. His tone was normal. Reflexes were brisk with downgoing plantars. There was mild unsteadiness on performing tandem gait.

The patient's examination video demonstrates a right torticollis with left laterocollis. He has slow rapidly alternating movements of his tongue and truncal jerks while performing this task. There are flurries of upper limb tremulousness at rest and with posture and action and superimposed myoclonic jerks of upper limbs, lower limbs and trunk. There is no dysmetria. Rapidly alternating movements are slow without decrement but with persistent mirror movements of right upper and lower limb. Reflexes are brisk and there is mild but definite midline ataxia. Jerks become more prominent when pouring between two cups and a combination of tremor and jerks are seen when attempting Archimedes spirals.

Neurophysiological assessment revealed an intermittent postural tremor with mean frequency of 7 Hz affecting upper and lower limbs, paraspinal and abdominal muscles, more prominent during movement. In addition, myoclonus was seen in the trunk, upper limbs and lower limbs, spontaneously and in response to touch or loud noise. The EMG burst durations varied from 50 to 300 ms (Fig. 1). Jerk‐locked EEG–EMG evaluation showed no short‐latency EEG spike preceding EMG discharges, there were no abnormal responses to median nerve stimulation, and median nerve somatosensory evoked potentials showed normal amplitudes.

There were rhythmic, synchronous EMG bursts with mean frequency of 7 Hz involving the wrist flexor and extensor muscles (B) spontaneous myoclonus recorded from the left side while standing. The EMG bursts have variable durations. The durations of three EMG bursts are marked.

Whole genome sequencing as part of a research study in congenital heart disease detected a de novo heterozygous mutation of KCNN2 (c.1709C>T:p.Ser570Phe). This variant has not previously been reported.

KCNN2 is a member of a family of genes which encode small conductance calcium‐activated potassium channels (SK). KCNN2 is strongly expressed in the human cerebellum, caudate nucleus and hippocampus and its functions include regulation of neuronal membrane excitability and modulation of spike‐firing frequency in dendritic spines.
8
,
9
 Although KCNN2 mutations have only recently been described as a cause of early‐onset dominant neurodevelopmental movement disorders in humans, spontaneous mutations in rodents have previously been proposed as preclinical models for Parkinson's disease and essential tremor. A mouse model (with deletion of the 5′ region of Kcnn2) demonstrates early‐onset rapid tremor and locomotor disturbance, while a rat model (with a missense substitution in Kcnn2) exhibits rapid tremor with superimposed myoclonic jerks).
10
,
11

To date, there have been only two previous reports of the movement disorders seen in patients with KCNN2 mutations. The first described a pedigree of five affected individuals with “tremulous” myoclonus‐dystonia.
6
 The proband demonstrated cervical dystonia with 5 to 6‐Hz tremor of her upper limbs and superimposed jerks of >100 ms duration, similar to our patient's presentation. A second series described a spectrum of neurodevelopmental movement disorders in 10 individuals with KCNN2 mutations and demonstrated that several KCNN2 variants led to loss‐of‐function of SK channels using the patch‐clamp technique.
7
 Intellectual disability occurred in all patients but ranged from mild to severe. Developmental delay occurred in 90% of individuals, and movement disorders in 60% (tremor, ataxia, myoclonus, dystonia, chorea, tics). Autistic spectrum disorders were seen in 60% of patients with comorbid anxiety in 30%. Other features reported included seizures, neuropathy, microcephaly and nystagmus. Importantly, half of the patients who had imaging in this series demonstrated diffuse periventricular hyperintensities on MRI. Due to a permanent pacemaker in situ, MRI brain could not be performed in our patient.

Our patient, with a novel mutation in KCNN2, adds to the small number of patients with neurodevelopmental movement disorders associated with this gene. The electrophysiological findings of ~7 Hz tremor, EMG discharges of variable durations from 50 to 300 ms resemble that of myoclonus dystonia.
12
 These features, together with absence of short latency discharge preceding myoclonus and normal evoked potentials, favor a subcortical origin of the myoclonus. To date, there appears to be a heterogeneous phenotype with respect to degree of intellectual disability and the movement disorders present. The full spectrum of KCNN2‐associated disease and its true prevalence as well as the possibility of genotype–phenotype correlations remain to be elucidated.

Importantly, the variant in our patient was detected as an incidental variant of uncertain significance in a research study for an entirely unrelated phenotype (congenital cardiac disease). However, the clinical findings are strikingly similar to those which have been described in the small number of reported patients with KCNN2 mutations to date. Given how rapidly new genetic neurological diseases are being discovered via NGS methods, patients with such incidental findings from studies in clinical and research settings may need to be evaluated by relevant neurological specialists (including movement disorders specialists) in order to determine their significance. It is also clear that a similar reciprocal approach will be required for incidental non‐neurological variants detected in NGS obtained by movement disorders neurologists. Such an interdisciplinary care approach will require significant planning and resources in order to be successfully achieved.
