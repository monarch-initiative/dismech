# Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia Due to Compound Heterozygous Variants in CFAP221 with Obstructive Azoospermia: Young's Syndrome May Be a Phenotype of Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia.
**Authors:** Ito M, Morimoto K, Ohashi M, Wakabayashi K, Miyabayashi A, Yamada H, Hijikata M, Keicho N
**Journal:** Intern Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.2169/internalmedicine.3978-24](https://doi.org/10.2169/internalmedicine.3978-24)

## Content

We report the case of a 42-year-old man with bronchiectasis who had a history of infertility treatment for obstructive azoospermia. Young's syndrome was suspected based on the triad of obstructive azoospermia, sinusitis, and bronchiectasis. He had normal electron microscopy findings, normal nasal nitric oxide levels (116 nL/min), and no situs inversus. However, we found compound heterozygous variants considered pathogenic in CFAP221. This led to a diagnosis of primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD). Distinguishing PCD from Young's syndrome in patients with the triad of obstructive azoospermia, sinusitis, and bronchiectasis is challenging. Young's syndrome may be a phenotype of PCD.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a genetically heterogeneous disorder characterized by impaired function of motile cilia in various organs. The global prevalence of PCD is estimated to be 1 in 7,500 individuals (1). Most PCD cases are inherited in an autosomal recessive or an X-linked manner. To date, over 50 causative genes of PCD have been identified (2).

PCD is associated with rhinosinusitis, otitis media, bronchiectasis, situs inversus and infertility. Infertility affects 75% of male and 61% of female patients with PCD, depending on ultrastructural and gene defects (3). Male infertility due to PCD is believed to be caused by impaired sperm motility. However, male infertility can also occur in PCD patients with abnormalities in dynein axonemal heavy chain 5 (DNAH5), which is not a component of sperm flagella. A study in mice with DNAH5 deficiency showed immotile cilia in the efferent ducts. In addition, human individuals with DNAH5 variants exhibit oligozoospermia and normal sperm motility (4). Therefore, recent suggestions indicate that the impairment of efferent duct ciliary motility is also a factor in male infertility (5).

The triad of obstructive azoospermia, sinusitis, and bronchiectasis is characteristic of Young's syndrome, but can be observed in patients with cystic fibrosis (CF) and PCD. Therefore, distinguishing between these diseases is crucial. Young's syndrome is believed to be linked to childhood mercury exposure, although this remains a topic of debate (6). In this report, we discuss the similarities between PCD and Young's syndrome and explore their potential as a phenotype of heterogeneous PCD.

We report a case of PCD caused by compound heterozygous variants in the cilia- and flagella-associated protein 221 gene (CFAP221) with obstructive azoospermia. To our knowledge, there has only been one previous report of three siblings with classical PCD symptoms caused by CFAP221 variants worldwide, and this is the first case report of an adult patient with PCD carrying pathogenic CFAP221 variants.

A 42-year-old man was referred to Fukujuji Hospital, Japan Anti-Tuberculosis Association for bronchiectasis treatment. He presented with cough and sputum that had persisted since 37 years of age and had a history of lower airway symptom exacerbations. He was an 11 pack-year smoker, with a history of surgery for a ventricular septal defect at 4 years of age and had developed pneumonia and otitis media during childhood. There was no situs inversus.

Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed mild cylindrical bronchiectasis in the middle lobe and partial consolidation without bronchiectasis in the left lower lobe (Fig. 1). The mean modified Reiff score was 1. Sinus CT showed fluid retention in the maxillary and ethmoidal sinuses (Fig. 2). There were no abnormalities in the sphenoid or frontal sinuses and no signs of hypoplasia or aplasia. Haemophilus influenzae was detected using a sputum culture test.

At 29 years of age, he underwent an infertility examination and treatment by a specialized urologist. His semen lacked sperm, but his testes were well-developed, and his sperm tracts were normally palpated. Hormonal levels of luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, prolactin, and total testosterone were all within the normal ranges. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with obstructive azoospermia. His karyotype was normal (46XY) with no Y chromosome microdeletions detected. Following testicular sperm extraction surgery, a large number of sperm with slight movement were retrieved. He later successfully fathered a baby through intracytoplasmic sperm injection using his wife's egg.

We suspected Young's syndrome because of the presence of obstructive azoospermia, sinusitis, and bronchiectasis. To confirm the diagnosis, we conducted nasal nitric oxide (nNO) measurements, electron microscopy (EM) of the ciliary ultrastructure, and a genetic analysis. The nNO level was 116 nL/min and the EM showed a normal ciliary ultrastructure. Sanger sequencing of the PCR products revealed compound heterozygous variants of CFAP221 (Fig. 3). Identification of a small deletion in exon 15 of NM_001271049.2 on one chromosome (chr2:119,625,637-119,625,640), within the region affected by a larger deletion on the other chromosome (chr2:119,624,023-119,642,209, spanning exons 15-21), allowed for the determination of a compound heterozygote status without the need for a genotypic analysis of the parents because the same region on paternal and maternal chromosomes was involved. Consequently, the patient was diagnosed as having PCD. While the sweat test for the diagnosis of CF was unavailable at our facility, enriched RNA-seq with exome oligos (Illumina) and variant calling using the STAR aligner in accordance with the GATK best practice pipeline did not reveal any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the CF Transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene.

Although his bronchiectasis was mild because he had a cough with sputum and a history of bronchiectasis exacerbations, we introduced the airway clearance technique once or twice daily.

This report presents a rare case of PCD caused by compound heterozygous variants of CFAP221, with obstructive azoospermia. Bustamante-Marin et al. reported French-Canadian siblings with PCD caused by CFAP221 variants in 2020 (7). Currently, there are no other documented cases of PCD attributed to CFAP221 variants.

PCD is characterized by impaired function of motile cilia. Motile cilia have a 9 + 2 structure with 9 pairs of outer microtubules surrounding a central pair apparatus (CPA) (8). CFAP221, also known as primary ciliary dyskinesia protein 1 (PCDP1), is associated with CPA (9). The reduced expression of PCDP1 complex members in Chlamydomonas reinhardtii leads to failure of the C1d central pair projection assembly. This leads to a reduced beat frequency and altered waveforms (10). A mouse model with CFAP221 variants displayed a decreased ciliary beat frequency in respiratory epithelial cilia and infertility caused by a lack of mature flagella (11).

In a previous report on three siblings with CFAP221 variants, the EM findings were normal. While there was no significant difference in ciliary beat frequency, high-resolution videos of ciliated cells showed an aberrant circular pattern of ciliary waveforms (7). In the present case, the EM findings were also normal; however, the beat frequency and beat pattern could not be assessed.

In the present case, the nNO level was 116 nL/min, which was higher than the cutoff value for the diagnosis of PCD (77 nL/min). The three previously reported cases also exhibited normal nNO levels (122 nL/min, 171 nL/min, and 189 nL/min) (7). It has been noted that PCD cases due to some genetic mutations show nNO levels higher than 77 nL/min, especially in patients with a normal ultrastructure (12,13). Raidt et al. suggested using a higher cutoff value of nNO 107.8 nL/min, for PCD patients with normal ultrastructure (13). However, all previous cases of PCD with CFAP221 variants, including the present case, showed nNO levels higher than 107.8 nL/min. The ideal cutoff value for nNO levels in PCD patients with CFAP221 variants should be reconsidered.

Young's syndrome is suspected when a triad of obstructive azoospermia, sinusitis, and bronchiectasis is present. In 1970, David Young reported lung involvement in 54% of patients with obstructive azoospermia (14). In 1978, cases with the triad of obstructive azoospermia, sinusitis, and bronchiectasis or bronchitis were defined as having Young's syndrome (15). Since the number of cases of Young's syndrome declined after 1955, one hypothesis suggests that mercury exposure may be a cause of Young's syndrome. In a prospective study conducted between 1975 and 1993 on 146 subfertile men with epididymal obstruction, 82% were diagnosed with Young's syndrome and mercury exposure was identified in 11 cases (16). However, it is uncertain whether Young's syndrome still exists as an independent clinical entity today (6).

The history of PCD dates back to 1904, when cases of situs inversus and bronchiectasis were reported (17). In the 1970s, the discovery of a link between abnormalities in sperm flagella and motile cilia led to the recognition of the immotile-cilia syndrome, later termed PCD. It presents with male infertility, bronchiectasis, sinusitis, and situs inversus (18,19). The first genetic variant of PCD was discovered in 1999 (20). The period of increased awareness and research on PCD coincided with a decline in the number of cases of Young's syndrome.

In patients with Young's syndrome, the epididymal head appears enlarged with plenty of sperm, and the vasa deferentia is patent (21,22). Therefore, obstructive azoospermia in Young's syndrome is believed to result from epididymal obstruction; however, the exact cause remains unclear. PCD caused by DNAH5 variants also presents with dilation of the epididymal head and oligozoospermia, which is similar to Young's syndrome (4). In humans, coiled efferent ducts occupy a major portion of the epididymal head (23). Motile cilia in mice have been shown to create turbulence and prevent sperm agglutination in the efferent duct (24). Impaired ciliary motility of the efferent duct may explain the obstructive azoospermia observed in patients with Young's syndrome. Several case reports have described PCD along with obstructive azoospermia (25-29). In our case, normal ciliary ultrastructure and nNO levels were observed with no situs inversus. Without advancements in genetic testing, our case may not have been identified as PCD, suggesting that PCD caused by CFAP221 variants could have been misdiagnosed as Young's syndrome (Table).

CF is associated with obstructive azoospermia, including congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens, congenital unilateral absence of the vas deferens, or epididymal obstruction. Friedman et al. reported that the incidence of CFTR mutations in patients with Young's syndrome (1 of 26 cases) was not significantly different from the expected carrier frequency (30). In another report, no CFTR mutations were found in patients with Young's syndrome (31). These results indicate that the pathogenesis of Young's syndrome differs from that of CF. However, several cases of Young's syndrome with CFTR gene mutations have been reported (32,33). Therefore, the differentiation of CF is important for an accurate diagnosis in patients with obstructive azoospermia.

One limitation of the present case was that the function and movement of motile cilia could not be assessed using high-speed video microscopy (HSVM). HSVM is complex and requires suitable living cell conditions, standardized criteria, and air-liquid interface culture (8). Only a few facilities in Japan are able to perform HSVM. Patients with PCD caused by CFAP221 variants typically show normal EM findings, normal nNO levels, and no situs inversus. Therefore, genetic testing is crucial for the diagnosis of PCD caused by CFAP221 variants. In addition, in the present case, we were unable to examine the epididymal head or evaluate the ciliary motility of the efferent duct. Further investigation is required to elucidate the mechanisms of obstructive azoospermia in PCD caused by CFAP221.

We present a case of PCD caused by compound heterozygous variants of CFAP221, leading to obstructive azoospermia. The bronchiectasis observed in the present case was mild. Further research is required to determine the impact of CFAP221 variants on airway lesions. PCD and obstructive azoospermia can present together, and Young's syndrome may be a phenotype of PCD. Distinguishing PCD is vital in bronchiectasis patients with obstructive azoospermia. Further research, in collaboration with a specialized urologist, is necessary to explore the causes and pathogenesis of Young's syndrome and whether Young's syndrome still exists today.
