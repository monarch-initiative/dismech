Zeta-chain associated protein kinase 70 kDa (ZAP-70) deficiency is one of the rare immunodeficiency disorders due to autosomal recessive homozygous or compound heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in the ZAP-70GENE. In the literature, patients with ZAP-70 deficiency have been reported with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations including recurrent respiratory infections (81.8%), cutaneous involvement (57.9%), lymphoproliferation (32.4%), autoimmunity (19.4%), enteropathy (18.4%) and increased risk of malignancies (8.1%). The most common immunological phenotype in those patients was low CD8+ T cell counts (97.9%) and normal non-functioning CD4+ T cell. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation was applied as a curative treatment for this disorder.

Keywords:genetics, haematology (incl blood transfusion), immunology

T cells provide protection against pathogens and tumours. T-cell responses are initiated by receptors that are responsible for identification of major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-bound antigenic peptide fragments (pMHC) derived from tumours and pathogens. The pMHC is recognised by T-cell antigen receptor (TCR) α and β chains, derived from rearranging gene segments. Transduction of a successful recognition event occurs across the plasma membrane and intracellular signalling circuitry via the TCR-associated invariant CD3 and ζ chains. Zeta-chain associated protein kinase 70 kDa (ZAP-70) was first identified as a 70 kDa tyrosine phosphoprotein that rapidly associated with the TCR ζ chain in response to TCR stimulation.1 2

ZAP-70 is a member of the spleen tyrosine kinase family. The human ZAP-70GENEis located on chromosome position 2q11.2. It contains 2 non-coding and 12 coding exon that encode a 619-amino acid protein ZAP-70. Structurally, ZAP-70 is composed of two SH2 domains and a carboxyterminal kinase domain that are separated by interdomain A and B.2

ZAP-70 has an essential role in TCR signalling and T-cell differentiation and function. Activated ZAP-70 regulates motility, adhesion and cytokine expression of specific lymphocytes. Any mutation in ZAP-70 leads to unstable protein that cannot perform its normal function. It is demonstrated that ZAP-70 deficiency causes a combined immunodeficiency (CID) with a selective absence of CD8+ T cells, with a normal or elevated level of circulating non-functioning CD4+ T cells and normal immunoglobulin levels. Thus, deficiency of ZAP-70, like some other CIDs has variable clinical presentations. However, the lack of functional T cells in patients with ZAP-70 deficiency make them more susceptible to bacterial, viral and fungal infections.3 4

A male toddler presented with acute haemolytic anaemia, reticulocytosis, positive direct Coombs test and difficulty to get a matched blood for transfusion. He was diagnosed with autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA) and treated with pulse steroids in the form of methyl prednisolone (30 mg/kg/day) for 3 days, followed by a gradual withdrawal. Nevertheless, the haemolytic attack recurred three times during steroid withdrawal. Due to the relapse of haemolysis, mycophenolate (MMF) 600 mg/m2two times per day was started with partial response.

Three months later, the patient presented with severe non-haemolytic normocytic/normochromic anaemia with reticulocytopenia, absolute count was 0.05 and chest infection that necessitated hospital admission. Bone marrow aspiration (BMA) revealed normal cellularity, depressed erythropoiesis with intracellular inclusion bodies and the myeloid-to-erythroid (M/E) ratio was 20. Therefore, acquired pure red cell aplasia was suspected and virology studies revealed a positive parvovirus PCR. Over 3 weeks of hospital admission, he was on antibiotics, frequent blood transfusion and continued on MMF and steroid withdrawal but no specific treatment for parvovirus, as intravenous immunoglobulin, was given.

Two weeks later, he was readmitted to the hospital with a picture of peripheral blood pancytopenia with hepatosplenomegaly associated with persistent high-grade fever and cough. Another BMA revealed normocellular bone marrow with cellularity of 60% erythroid and megakaryocytic hyperplasia and the M/E ratio was 0.5. Thereafter, another virology studies showed Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) IgM positive, which was not confirmed by EBV PCR. His EBV IgM turned to be negative after a month of supportive care with gradual recovery of myeloid and megakaryocytic suppression. Since then, the patient suffered from frequent infections such as upper respiratory tract and gastroenteritis with variable severity that were treated at home or in hospital. The frequency of blood transfusion started to decrease after 4 months of the parvovirus infection. Seven months after the first attack of AIHA, his anaemia is controlled on MMF 600 mg/m2two times per day and the steroid therapy was stopped 2 months ago. On examination, he had normal growth, his percentiles were on the 95th centile. Other examination was normal apart from the mild splenomegaly that was 2 cm below the costal margin with a rounded border, a smooth surface and a preserved notch.

Family history showed a positive consanguineous marriage, this child is the second living child out of four siblings. He has two sisters who died with the same clinical presentation, but they were not diagnosed. One died at age of 2 months and the second died at age of 1.5 years. Both siblings died due to severe anaemia and uncontrolled sepsis.

The whole exome sequencing (WES) was done, which revealed a homozygous deletion mutation at chromosome g.98341645 referenced as (NC_000002.11) at c.493 nucleotide referenced as (NM_001079.4) with protein deletion histidine to threonine in amino acid 165 referenced as (NP_001070.2) (His165ThrfsTer4) at termination code of exon 4 in the frameshift at tyrosine kinase domain. This mutation was evaluated as pathogenic, according to the American College of Medical Genetics criteria for the classification of pathogenic variants with autosomal recessive mode of inheritance. Once diagnosed, chemoprophylaxis was started to prevent the recurrent infections. The patient and his healthy living sibling were scheduled for HLA matching as a preparation for allogenic bone marrow transplantation (BMT).

This is a case of familial and early onset AIHA with frequent infections. The haemolytic attacks were poorly controlled with frequent relapses in immunological tests showed normal CD4 level but low CD8 level and other immunological tests were normal. One of the most important differential diagnoses was immunodeficiency like CVID or SCID.

The patient started to show gradual improvement with keeping the haemoglobin level >8 g/dL. The patient is stable and does not need blood transfusion for 2 months. There is a complete recovery of the platelets count and the white blood cell count. There is no new attack of autoimmune haemolysis and the infection rate was controlled by chemoprophylaxis.

ZAP-70 deficiency is a rare CID, which was previously reported in only 50 cases included in 33 case reports. It is due to autosomal recessive homozygous or compound heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in the ZAP-70GENE, which is important in T cell maturation.4

In this case, the mutation was a homozygous frameshift deletion variant of theZAP-70 GENEdetected in exon 4. In the reported cases, there were three types of described mutations inZAP-70 GENEmutation. The most common mutation is the complete loss-of-function mutation targeting kinase domain causing the most severe aggressive presentation. The second mutation is the hypomorphic mutations which have been described in five patients who were presented with less severe symptoms compared with patients with complete loss. The third mutation is described as compound heterozygous, disrupting the self-autoinhibition of the ZAP-70 protein increasing TCR signalling activating autoreactive B cells mediating autoimmune disorders. These different types of ZAP-70GENEmutations may lead to variability in the phenotypic spectrum.5 6

Our case presented at the age of 3 years and his two sisters died by severe anaemia and sepsis at age of 2 months and 1.5 years, respectively. There is a variable age of presentation within the same family. Most cases in the previous reports were presented by age of 1 year. One report described a child aged 2 months presented with an extensive rash at age of 3 days associated with chronic diarrhoea with homozygous missense mutation in the ZAP-70GENE(c.37G>A).5

The main presentation of this case was refractory AIHA and a delayed presentation of recurrent infections. Similarly, most of the previous reports described different presentations such as autoimmune disorders in the first year of life (eg, recurrent eczema, autoimmune cytopenia) and severe infections (eg, recurrent respiratory infections, otitis media). Uncontrollable bullous pemphigoid, colitis, nephrotic syndrome and acquired haemophilia due to a factor VIII autoantibody have also been reported.6

Patients with ZAP-70 mutations are more prone to opportunistic infections either bacterial or fungal, althoughPneumocystis jiroveciiand cytomegalovirus pneumonitis are less common. In our case, infection with parvovirus led to acquired pure red cell aplasia. There are no previous reports documenting the parvovirus infection in these cases. EBV infection was associated with peripheral pancytopenia, organomegaly and lymphadenopathy. Of interest is a previously reported ZAP-70 deficiency in a baby aged 21 months with compound heterozygous ZAP-70GENEmutation c.703-1G>A and c.1674G>A (p. Met558Ile) with immune dysregulatory response to presumed EBV infection. He developed lymphoproliferation and lymphoma. This aggressive response to EBV infection might be explained by the impaired T-cell response that allows the EBV-infected cells to become proliferating blasts, which can result in lymphoproliferative disease or lymphoma.7 8

Our case had positive family history with similar presentation in two sisters who died very early in life. Similarly, a Turkish family of a consanguineous parents had two siblings who died with severe sepsis, the second one was diagnosed with ZAP-70 deficiency with a novel homozygous missense mutation atNM_001079.3; c.C1747>T, located in exon 12 of the gene. The mutation led to an arginine to cysteine substitution at amino acid 514 (R514C), located in the kinase domain of ZAP-70. Genetic counselling and pregestational diagnosis of this familial disease is of utmost importance to save other siblings of the affected families.9

The immunological profile detected low CD8 cells but normal CD4 count with normal immunoglobulins levels (table 1). Many previous reports documented a low CD8 level with normal immunoglobulin level and a low or normal CD4 level which was non-functioning. This highlights the importance of ZAP-70GENEin the T cell signalling.6 9

In our case, the ZAP-70 protein expression was not assessed. In previously reported cases, the ZAP-70 protein expression in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was assessed using flow cytometry which was either reduced or absent.5

The AIHA of our case was controlled by MMF for 5 months and was not responsive to steroids. However, it is still a short follow-up period, and more time is needed to monitor the overall response to therapy. Previously published reports confirmed that BMT might give those children another chance of life with total reconstitution of their immune system.4 8We recommend WES analysis for the diagnosis of cases presented early in life with refractory AIHA.

Zeta-chain associated protein kinase 70 kDa deficiency is a rare mutation with limited described cases in the literature.

A high index of suspicion is needed in any case presenting with early onset autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA), recurrent infections and in cases with a similar condition in the family.

Whole exome sequencing is highly recommended for the diagnosis of cases presented with early in life, refractory AIHA.
