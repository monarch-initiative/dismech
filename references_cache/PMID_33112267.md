# Recombinant human parathyroid hormone (1-84) is effective in CASR-associated hypoparathyroidism.
**Authors:** Hawkes CP, Shulman DI, Levine MA
**Journal:** Eur J Endocrinol (2020)
**DOI:** [10.1530/EJE-20-0710](https://doi.org/10.1530/EJE-20-0710)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Gain-of-function mutations in the CASR gene cause Autosomal Dominant Hypocalcemia Type 1 (ADH1), the most common genetic cause of isolated hypoparathyroidism. Subjects have increased calcium sensitivity in the renal tubule, leading to increased urinary calcium excretion, nephrocalcinosis and nephrolithiasis when compared with other causes of hypoparathyroidism. The traditional approach to treatment includes activated vitamin D but this further increases urinary calcium excretion.

METHODS: In this case series, we describe the use of recombinant human parathyroid hormone (rhPTH)1-84 to treat subjects with ADH1, with improved control of serum and urinary calcium levels.

RESULTS: We describe two children and one adult with ADH1 due to heterozygous CASR mutations who were treated with rhPTH(1-84). Case 1 was a 9.4-year-old female whose 24-h urinary calcium decreased from 7.5 to 3.9 mg/kg at 1 year. Calcitriol and calcium supplementation were discontinued after titration of rhPTH(1-84). Case 2 was a 9.5-year-old male whose 24-h urinary calcium decreased from 11.7 to 1.7 mg/kg at 1 year, and calcitriol was also discontinued. Case 3 was a 24-year-old female whose treatment was switched from multi-dose teriparatide to daily rhPTH(1-84). All three subjects achieved or maintained target serum levels of calcium and normal or improved urinary calcium levels with daily rhPTH(1-84) monotherapy.

CONCLUSIONS: We have described three subjects with ADH1 who were treated effectively with rhPTH(1-84). In all cases, hypercalciuria improved by comparison to treatment with conventional therapy consisting of calcium supplementation and calcitriol.

Hypoparathyroidism is an uncommon metabolic disorder that is characterized by hypocalcemia and hyperphosphatemia due to inadequate supply or effectiveness of circulating parathyroid hormone (PTH). It may occur either as an isolated condition or as a component of a more complex developmental, metabolic, or endocrine syndrome. Molecular genetic studies indicate that isolated hypoparathyroidism is caused by mutations in a variety of genes, including genetic defects that impair synthesis (i.e., PTH gene defects1, 2 or secretion (i.e., CASR gene defects34 and GNA115–7) of PTH as well as defects that impair the embryological development of the parathyroid glands (GCM2 gene defects8–10). Heterozygous mutations in the CASR gene located on chromosome 3q13.3-q21 that lead to a gain-of-function of the calcium sensing receptor (CaSR) are the most common genetic cause of isolated hypoparathyroidism11–13, and result in a disorder that is also termed autosomal dominant hypocalcemia type 1(ADH1; OMIM 601198)14. In addition, gain-of-function CASR mutations can cause type 5 Bartter syndrome15. The CaSR is a class C G protein-coupled receptor that is expressed abundantly in the parathyroid gland and, to a lesser extent, along the length of the kidney tubule, where it plays a central role in calcium homeostasis by sensing the concentration of extracellular calcium ([Ca2+]o), thereby regulating secretion of PTH from the parathyroid glands and reabsorption of calcium in the kidney.

Gain-of-function mutations of the CASR gene increase the sensitivity of the receptor to [Ca2+]o, which reduces PTH secretion from parathyroid cells despite low serum calcium levels16, 17. Moreover, the presence of CaSRs with increased sensitivity to calcium in the renal tubule also impairs reabsorption of calcium4, and accounts for renal excretion of calcium that is greater in subjects with ADH1 than in subjects with other forms of forms of hypoparathyroidism in which renal excretion of calcium is increased due to PTH deficiency alone18. As a result, nephrocalcinosis and nephrolithiasis are more common in subjects with ADH1 than other forms of hypoparathyroidism19. The combination of both loss of PTH and gain of CaSR activity provides a dual mechanism for hypercalciuria in ADH1, which has raised concerns that urinary calcium excretion would not be as effectively reduced by PTH replacement therapy in this population as in subjects with other forms of hypoparathyroidism. Consequently, subjects with CASR mutations were excluded from clinical trials of recombinant human PTH(1–84) (rhPTH(1–84)), which have demonstrated long-term efficacy and safety in adult subjects with other forms of hypoparathyroidism20. Based on the absence of proven efficacy for rhPTH(1–84) in subjects with ADH1, treatment guidelines for this condition continue to recommend conventional therapy consisting of calcitriol plus calcium supplementation21, 22.

On the other hand, previous reports have suggested that teriparatide, a biologically active peptide that consists of the first 34 amino acids of human PTH, could reduce urinary excretion of calcium in at least some subjects with ADH1 when administered subcutaneously via multiple daily injections23–29 or by subcutaneous pulsatile infusion via an insulin pump30. Here we describe the use of rhPTH(1–84) to treat subjects with ADH1 and demonstrate that once-daily injection can normalize serum levels of calcium while significantly reducing urinary calcium excretion.

We performed a retrospective chart review of three subjects with genetically-confirmed ADH1 who were treated with rhPTH(1–84). This study was performed in accordance with the policies and procedures of the institutional review board of the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia and was exempt from review as a limited case series. The potential pathogenicity of all genetic variants that we identified were interpreted and classified using criteria recommended by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics31.

This female subject was born via spontaneous preterm vaginal delivery at 35 weeks gestation following a pregnancy complicated by maternal Crohn disease. Her birthweight was 2.65 kg. From birth she was noted to have mild hypomagnesemia, elevated serum phosphorous levels, and low serum calcium and PTH concentrations. She was initially managed with Similac PM 60/40 plus supplemental calcium carbonate (400 mg elemental calcium daily) and calcitriol (0.15 mcg daily). On this regimen, her serum calcium remained between 8 and 10.6 mg/L (reference range 8.9 – 10.4 mg/dL) with serum phosphorous levels of 7.2 to 8.5 mg/dL (reference range 3 – 6 mg/dL) and magnesium levels of approximately 2.1 mg/dL (reference range 1.5 – 2.5 mg/dL). Her renal ultrasound at two months of age demonstrated nephrocalcinosis.

Case 1 was subsequently diagnosed with ADH1 due to a de novo missense variant in exon 7 of CASR (c.2495 T>C; p.F832S) that had been previously reported32 and demonstrated to behave as pathogenic gain-of-function variant. All first degree relatives were tested and negative for this mutation. From age 1 year to age 9.5 years, she was treated with various doses of calcitriol and calcium with poor gastrointestinal tolerance of all calcium salts. Despite attempts to maintain serum calcium levels that were between 8 and 8.5 mg/dL, the subject demonstrated persistent hypercalciuria (urinary calcium:creatinine ratio ranged from 1900 to 4400 mg/g), with insignificant improvement from use of hydrochlorothiazide. She also experienced frequent episodes of tetany, manifesting as an inability to walk.

At the age of 9.5 years, she began treatment with subcutaneous rhPTH(1–84) at an initial dose of 50 mcg daily, with subsequent titration such that over time her dose varied at times between 50 and 75 mcg once per day and discontinuation of oral calcium carbonate and calcitriol (0.5 mcg daily). Her appetite improved and weight increased from the 10th percentile to the 15th percentile. On rhPTH(1–84) monotherapy the subject was able to maintain serum calcium and phosphorus levels within target range and her hypercalciuria resolved (Table 1). Subsequent renal ultrasounds showed stable bilateral medullary nephrocalcinosis without improvement.

Case 2 was a full term male who was delivered by caesarian section following a pregnancy complicated by maternal gestational diabetes. He had a birthweight of 3.8 kg. He was noted to be “jittery” at one week of age, and biochemical studies showed him to have a low serum calcium level (5 mg/dL; ref 8.9 – 10.4 mg/dL), elevated serum phosphorous level (11.1 mg/dL, ref 4 – 8 mg/dL), and nearly undetectable concentration of intact PTH (3 pg/mL; ref 12 – 65 pg/mL). His magnesium level was 1.4 mg/dL (ref 1.7 – 2.3 mg/dL), 25-hydroxyvitamin D was 16 ng/mL (ref 30 – 100 ng/mL) and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D was 110 pg/ml (ref 31 – 87 pg/mL). He was subsequently treated with calcium (2100 mg elemental calcium daily) and calcitriol (0.3 mcg daily), as well as Similac PM 60/40 formula prior to transition to a normal diet.

Throughout childhood his serum calcium levels had been difficult to control and frequent medication dose adjustments were required. He frequently had tetany, manifesting first as tightness in his legs and feet followed by difficulty opening his hands. He presented to the emergency department up to three times each year with symptomatic hypocalcemia. Despite taking potassium citrate and chlorothiazide or hydrochlorothiazide, he continued to have marked hypercalciuria, and he developed multiple, recurrent renal stones. He underwent lithotripsy at the age of 2 years, and subsequent renal ultrasounds regularly demonstrated new renal stones. He was found to have to a novel pathogenic missense variant (c.2459 G>A; p.S820N) in exon 7 of the CASR gene6 that was considered disease-causing by Mutation Taster33, and which accounted not only for his ADH1 but also type 5 Bartter syndrome with persistent hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and polyuria. All first degree relatives were tested and negative for this mutation.

At age 9 years and 7 months he began treatment with once-daily subcutaneous rhPTH(1–84), initially with 25 mcg daily and subsequently titrated upwards to 50 mcg daily. On this regimen, his serum and urinary calcium concentrations improved, and he could be weaned from calcitriol and calcium (Table 1). His follow-up renal ultrasound examinations showed stable medullary nephrocalcinosis and stable bilateral renal stones without improvement.

Case 3 presented at 10 months of age with hypocalcemia and a seizure, and hypoparathyroidism was diagnosed. At diagnosis, she was also noted to have nephrocalcinosis. She was treated with calcitriol and calcium supplementation, and calcium-sparing diuretics were later added due to ongoing hypocalcemia and hypercalciuria. A de novo, previously reported pathogenic missense variant (c.379 G>A; p.E127K) that was shown to behave as a gain-of-function variant19 in exon 3 of the CASR was identified and she was diagnosed with ADH1. At the age of 12 years, she began treatment with twice-daily subcutaneous teriparatide (Forteo®, Lilly, PTH(1–34)) with magnesium and cholecalciferol supplementation, and had symptomatic improvement with normalization of her urinary calcium:creatinine ratio. She had a trial of continuous teriparatide infusion via an insulin pump but had recurrent infusion site failures, and on at least one occasion this led to hypocalcemic tetany that was so severe that she required hospitalization. She resumed therapy with subcutaneous teriparatide twice daily until her insurance company refused to cover the expense. Her only hospitalization was related to pump failure but she described symptoms of low (tingling) or high (“heavy, nauseated”) calcium. She self treated symptoms acutely with extra oral calcium and confirmed on lab testing. When she felt “high”, she would stop calcium. Calcium levels were measured as often as twice a week.

At the age of 23 years Case 3 was begun on rhPTH(1–84) at a dose of 25 mcg twice daily. Her dose was titrated to 50 mcg twice daily, to 75 mcg once daily, and eventually to 50 mcg twice daily 4 days per week and 50 mcg once daily 3 days per week. She also takes cholecalciferol 1000 u daily and magnesium supplementation. Urinary calcium/creatinine ratio has remained in the normal range and 24-hour urinary calcium excretion has remained below 250 mg. Renal ultrasound examinations have demonstrated persistent medullary nephrocalcinosis. Her serum creatinine is mildly elevated but her estimated GFR remains in the normal range. She is currently attending school and working full time.

Chronic manifestations of hypoparathyroidism include hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, abnormal skeletal remodeling, neurocognitive complaints, and reduced quality of life. While conventional treatment with calcium plus an activated form of vitamin D may correct hypocalcemia and to some extent hyperphosphatemia, it does not adequately address these other features, and can lead to (or aggravate) hypercalciuria, nephrolithiasis and nephrocalcinosis. Although hormone replacement therapy with rhPTH(1–84) has been demonstrated to be an effective adjunct to conventional therapy in subjects with hypoparathyroidism20, 34, 35, the clinical trials have excluded subjects with ADH1 due to CASR mutations. Here we report that treatment with rhPTH(1–84) can improve serum and urinary calcium concentrations, and reduce the need for calcium and calcitriol treatment, in subjects with ADH1. In the two youngest subjects, Cases 1 and 2, urinary calcium excretion normalized with this treatment while serum calcium levels were increased to levels that avoided frequent episodes of tetany. Case 3 showed continued normalization of urinary calcium on rhPTH(1–84) that had been previously achieved while using teriparatide.

The increasing use of molecular analysis to characterize subjects with isolated hypoparathyroidism has revealed that nearly 50% of subjects have CASR gene mutations that are predicted to lead to a gain-of-function19 with an estimated prevalence of ADH1 in the general population of one in 70,00036. The conventional treatment of subjects with hypoparathyroidism consists of calcium and an active form of vitamin D (e.g. calcitriol), which cannot completely correct the adverse consequences of PTH deficiency, most notably the abnormally low rate of skeletal remodeling37. In addition, renal complications of hypoparathyroidism, including hypercalciuria, nephrocalcinosis, and nephrolithiasis, may be due to treatment with calcium and active vitamin D rather than the disease itself. Although teriparatide and rhPTH (1–84) are significantly more expensive than conventional treatment, these agents may provide cost savings through reduced emergency department visits, fewer renal stones, and preservation of normal renal function.

While teriparatide29, 30, 38–40 and rhPTH(1–84)20 have been demonstrated to be efficacious in children and adults with various forms of hypoparathyroidism, concerns based on the opposing actions of PTH and CaSR-signaling in the renal tubule (that PTH therapy might not be effective in subjects with activating mutations of CASR) has moderated enthusiasm for use of these agents in these subjects. For example, in the proximal tubule, type 6 adenylyl cyclase (ADCY6) modulates acute PTH-stimulated cAMP formation and urinary Pi excretion, whereas CaSR antagonizing this PTH effect through downregulation of the Gs–ADCY6–cAMP pathway. A similar mechanism likely occurs in the thick ascending limb (TAL), where CaSR signaling counteracts PTH-induced calcium reabsorption41. Finally, activation of CaSR signaling in the intestine decreases expression of TRPV6, which leads to inhibition of 1,25(OH)2D3-induced calcium transport42, 43. Nevertheless, various case reports23–27 and at least one long-term study28 have documented beneficial effects of teriparatide on serum levels of calcium and phosphorus and reduced levels of urinary calcium when administered to subjects with ADH1 by multiple daily injections or by pump infusion30. Our retrospective analyses demonstrate that PTH replacement therapy with rhPTH(1–84) is also able to reduce urinary calcium excretion while normalizing serum levels of calcium in subjects with ADH1 but has the advantage over teriparatide of requiring only a single daily injection in most subjects. The effectiveness of fewer daily injections with rhPTH(1–84) has been attributed to likely differences in the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamics profiles of teriparatide and rhPTH(1–84), which account for the significantly greater duration of effect of rhPTH(1–84) compared to teriparatide44, 45. Although not proven in this case series, the reduction in urinary calcium achieved by treatment with rhPTH(1–84) is very likely to reduce the risk development or progression of nephrocalcinosis and renal stones in these subjects as the development of this complication is associated with hypercalciuria and higher doses of calcitriol19, 22.

This case series provides a physiological insight into regulation of urinary calcium excretion in ADH1. Given that inactivating mutations of the CASR gene result in a primary increase in the fractional clearance of calcium in the kidney, it has been generally assumed that these renal effects predominated over PTH deficiency as a basis for hypercalciuria in subjects with ADH1, especially with treatments that raised the glomerular filtered load of calcium pari pasu with increasing serum calcium levels. However, in these subjects, we have shown that rhPTH(1–84) treatment can significantly decrease renal calcium clearance and overcome the renal effect of this mutation on urinary calcium loss. Thus, low PTH concentrations may be a more important determinant of hypercalciuria in this condition than assumed, and PTH replacement therapy can normalize urinary calcium excretion despite the PTH-independent calciuric effects of the activating CASR mutation. This becomes critically important in ADH1, where chronic management with conventional therapy can lead to marked increases in urinary calcium excretion and thereby further increase the known risks of renal stones and renal insufficiency46.

In addition to the clinical efficacy of rhPTH(1–84) in raising calcium levels and reducing urinary calcium excretion in this condition20, there may be skeletal effects of this approach to consider. In subjects with hypoparathyroidism, abnormal skeletal microarchitecture47 and trabecular structure48 have been reported although cancellous bone volume48, and bone mineral density49 may be increased. These structural changes occur as a result of reduced bone remodeling in hypoparathyroidism and, although not proven, may confer increased fracture risk50. rhPTH(1–84) treatment can normalize markers of bone metabolism20 and improve some of these bone density changes within two years51. PTH therapy has conflicting effects on bone in different part of the skeleton, with effects that are principally anabolic on cancellous bone and catabolic on cortical bone20, 52. Although most studies have been reassuring, a report of a single subject with ADH1 who was treated continuously with teriparatide from the age of 6 years to the age of 20 years described skull thickening with diffuse sclerotic and lytic lesions, increased trabecular bone on bone biopsy, and decreased radial bone density25. Moreover, preclinical studies of PTH and PTH analogs given in high doses to rats for long periods of time had demonstrated increased incidences of osteosarcoma53, 54, which led to recommendation against use of these agents in pediatric individuals who have open epiphyses and in adults who have an increased risk of osteosarcoma. Despite appropriate concerns about the effects of chronic PTH treatment on bone structure and remodeling rates37, the emerging consensus is that bone dynamics and quality are improved34, 55, 56 and that theoretical concerns regarding development of osteosarcoma have been overstated45, 57. We do note, however, that availability of rhPTH(1–84) has been suspended in the United States due to a potential issue related to rubber particulates originating from the rubber septum of the Natpara® (Takeda Pharmaceutical Company Limited) cartridge58.

In conclusion, we have described three cases of ADH1 treated effectively with rhPTH(1–84). In all three cases, hypercalciuria improved in comparison to treatment with conventional therapy consisting of calcium supplementation and calcitriol treatment. Although PTH replacement therapy may offer subjects with ADH1 clinical advantages over conventional therapy, these subjects may obtain even greater benefits from the use of calcilytic drugs that are currently under development59, either as monotherapy or with PTH. These compounds have the potential to reverse the deleterious effects of excessive CaSR signaling in the parathyroid gland, thereby restoring calcium-regulated PTH secretion, and in the kidney, thereby normalizing renal loss of calcium, potassium and magnesium60, 61. Moreover, calcilytic therapy may also improve bone strength in subjects with ADH160, as activated CaSR signaling has direct adverse effects on the skeleton62. The development of new and more effective therapies for subjects with ADH1 will encourage even more widespread genetic testing for this condition, and enable even greater precision therapy.
