# Coats-like vasculopathy in patients with an inherited retinal disease: a case series and literature review.
**Authors:** Mavris N, Vofo BN, Khateb S, Levy J
**Journal:** Eye (Lond) (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41433-025-03778-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41433-025-03778-2)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To examine clinical characteristics, genetic associations, and visual outcomes of Coats-like vasculopathy (CLV) in patients with inherited retinal disease (IRD).

METHODS: A literature review of studies published through December 30, 2023, and a cohort analysis of cases from Hadassah Medical Center were conducted. Data from 47 studies (163 patients, 277 eyes) and 10 institutional cases (17 eyes) were analysed using descriptive statistics.

RESULTS: Two novel CLV-associated genes, LRP5 and KIZ, were identified in our cohort. Literature findings showed that 69.9% of cases had bilateral asymmetric CLV, with 38.7% of patients being legally blind at their final assessment. The mean interval between IRD onset and CLV diagnosis was 10.38 ± 10.23 years. While baseline best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) showed no significant difference between unilateral CLV-affected vs. non-CLV-affected eyes (51.19 vs. 72 ETDRS letters, respectively; p = 0.051), BCVA was significantly different at CLV onset (29.19 vs. 69.12 ETDRS letters, respectively; p < 0.001) and at the final visit (19.93 vs. 63.55 ETDRS letters, respectively; p < 0.001). Visual outcomes were similar across treatment modalities (laser, cryotherapy ± laser).

CONCLUSIONS: CLV in IRD patients demonstrates clinical and genetic heterogeneity, with significant visual impairment regardless of treatment. The discovery of LRP5 and KIZ expands the genetic landscape of CLV. The profound and progressive vision loss in CLV-affected eyes underscores the need for early detection and tailored management strategies.

Subject terms: Retinal diseases, Medical research

Coats’ disease is a rare, idiopathic retinal disorder predominantly affecting young males [1]. Coats’ disease is characterized by unilateral retinal telangiectasia and exudative retinopathy, leading to intraretinal and subretinal exudates. The disease typically presents in the first decade of life, often with symptoms that include vision loss and/or strabismus [2]. Histopathologically, Coats’ disease is defined by abnormal retinal blood vessels with leakage of serum and lipids. Although the pathogenesis is not fully understood, it is believed to involve a breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier, with subsequent vascular leakage [3].

Coats-like exudative vasculopathy (CLV) has emerged recently as a noteworthy clinical entity in the field of ophthalmology, characterized by retinal telangiectasia and exudation, similar to the classic features associated with Coats’ disease. However, one aspect that distinguishes CLV from Coats’ disease is its association with inherited retinal diseases (IRDs), a group of disorders marked by genetic mutations that cause retinal degeneration and vision loss [4]. CLV was first reported in 1956 in a patient with retinitis pigmentosa (RP) [5]; later, the term “Coats-like RP” was coined to describe this condition as a distinct disease related to RP [6]. Since then, multiple cases of CLV presenting alongside IRD have been described [4, 7].

The co-occurrence of CLV with IRDs raises critical questions regarding the nature of their relationship. For example, is CLV a coincidental finding, or is it a consequence of the underlying IRD? Do any genetic pathways contribute to its development? Finally, how does CLV contribute to long-term visual outcomes in these patients?

Concerning CLV, our understanding of the pathogenesis, progression, and outcome is limited. Publications regarding CLV in the context of IRD indicate that CLV is rare and can present with diverse clinical manifestations, ranging from peripheral retinal telangiectasia to extensive exudative retinal detachment [4, 8]. While some studies identified CLV as a significant complication in patients with an IRD [7], the relationship between specific genetic mutations and the variability in visual outcome remains poorly understood. Advances in next-generation sequencing have increased our ability to detect genetic variations in genes such as CNGB1 (encoding cyclic nucleotide-gated channel subunit beta 1), thus increasing the list of mutations associated with CLV [4, 8]. Moreover, recent findings suggest that genetic variations in CRB1, which encodes Crumbs homolog 1, significantly contribute to the risk of developing CLV [4].

Here, we systematically investigated and synthesized data regarding the clinical characteristics, genetic associations, and visual prognosis of CLV in the context of IRD. Our results shed new light on the age of onset, patterns of disease progression, and treatment efficacy, thereby addressing critical gaps in our understanding of CLV in patients with IRD and guiding future research and clinical practice.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed clinical data for 10 patients treated at the Hadassah Medical Center, Jerusalem, Israel, and data obtained from a literature search. We aimed to compare clinical characteristics, genetic associations, and visual outcomes of CLV between these two cohorts. The literature review specifically addressed the following outcomes:

Comparison of best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) between the CLV-affected eye vs. the non-CLV-affected eye in unilateral cases;

Clinical anatomical factors that affect visual outcome, including exudative retinal detachment (ERD), exudate, telangiectasia, macular exudation, retinal hemorrhage, vitreous hemorrhage, neovascularization, and vasoproliferative tumor; and

Visual outcome in the eye treated exclusively with laser monotherapy vs. eye treated with cryotherapy, either alone or combined with laser therapy (cryotherapy ± laser).

The Ethics Review Committee at Hadassah Medical Center approved all procedures and methods for data collection. Our patient selection process included patients who presented with signs of CLV and IRD. A CLV fundus was defined by the presence of aneurysmal dilation, vascular telangiectasia, and intraretinal or subretinal lipid exudation on examination. A diagnosis of IRD was based on a comprehensive clinical assessment that included a clinical examination, medical history, ocular history, family history, multimodal imaging, and genetic and/or electroretinography testing, where available.

A review of the electronic healthcare database at Hadassah Medical Center was conducted to extract data regarding the relevant patients, including the electronic medical records, imaging results, and genetic testing results. The pathogenicity of previously unidentified variants was assessed based on the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics [9].

Ultra-widefield pseudocolour, autofluorescence (green), and fluorescein angiogram images were captured using an Optos imaging system (Optos PLC, Dunfermline, UK). In addition, macular optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans were obtained using a Spectralis imaging device (Heidelberg Engineering, Inc., Heidelberg, Germany).

CLV treatment modalities included laser photocoagulation, cryotherapy, anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents, intravitreal/systemic steroids, pars plana vitrectomy, and brachytherapy. Laser photocoagulation, the initial treatment of choice, was primarily employed to manage telangiectatic vessels, a common early manifestation in these patients [10, 11]. The decision to implement combination therapies was guided by the complexity and severity of the clinical presentation. For cases refractory to laser treatment, intravitreal anti-VEGF agents were administered as an adjunct therapy. In severe cases with complications such as ERD, combination therapies were utilized, involving cryotherapy, systemic/intravitreal steroids, and, in some cases, pars plana vitrectomy [12–15]. In highly treatment-resistant cases that have not responded to the previous treatments, plaque brachytherapy was specifically applied to manage vasoproliferative tumors [16].

A comprehensive literature review was performed to identify reported cases with signs of CLV and IRD. The PubMed and EMBASE databases were searched for articles published through December 30, 2023, using the following search terms: “coats like”, “coats-like”, “coats type”, and “coats-type”. The search strings were tailored to each database’s specific format to maximize retrieval of relevant publications.

Two independent reviewers (authors NM and BV) screened each record and manually entered the relevant data into an Excel spreadsheet using a standardized template during the study selection and data collection process. The initial screen was based on the publications’ titles and abstracts, followed by a detailed full-text review to determine each study’s eligibility. We included retrospective case series and case reports that provided sufficient diagnostic data regarding CLV in patients with IRD. We excluded publications that were not written in English, lacked specific data regarding CLV, where an expert opinion/picture/perspective, involved eyes with unknown laterality, or focused on conditions other than IRD. Any discrepancies that arose at any time during the review process were resolved by discussion or—if necessary—input from a third reviewer (author JL).

For our review, we systematically collected the publication’s first author’s name, the publication year, and key patient outcomes, including clinical features, visual acuity (converted to LogMAR values), and treatment for CLV in patients with IRD. This included various measurements, time points, and analyses. In addition, we collected data regarding the participants’ demographics, genetic profiles, and IRD type. In cases of missing and/or unclear information, we made no informed assumptions.

Using published conventions [17, 18], we assigned LogMAR values of 1.98, 2.28, 2.7, and 3 in cases in which the patient could count fingers, detect hand motion, perceive light, or not perceive light, respectively.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), and differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. For assessing visual outcome, we aggregated the data obtained for both eyes (OD and OS) across groups, using the mean difference to evaluate the change in BCVA. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous outcomes were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, or the Mann-Whitney U test, where appropriate. The prevalence of categorical parameters was analyzed using logistic regression to assess the association with binary outcomes, and the Chi-square test was used to determine independence between categorical variables.

We found a significant direct correlation between BCVA and the presence of ERD (Spearman rho: 0.339, p < 0.0001). Conversely, no other clinical signs—including exudate, telangiectasia, macular exudation, retinal hemorrhage, vitreous hemorrhage, neovascularization, or vasoproliferative tumor—were significantly correlated with changes in visual acuity.

We found no statistically significant difference in age at the last visit between the patients with ERD (35.89 ± 18.64 years) and the patients without ERD (40.12 ± 25.49 years; p = 0.435). Notably, however, the patients with ERD had significantly worse final BCVA (1.99 ± 2.54 LogMAR) compared to the patients without ERD (1.31 ± 1.4 LogMAR; p < 0.001).

Additionally, the mean BCVA at the final visit, in eyes that were followed-up by observation only (i.e., were not treated), was 1.70 ± 1.09 LogMAR (n = 63) and did not reach statistical significance compared to the laser monotherapy group (p = 0.32) or the group that received cryotherapy ± laser therapy (p = 0.26). To note, a larger percentage of eyes in the cryotherapy ± laser group had presented with ERD (22/31 eyes, 71.0%) compared to the eyes in the laser monotherapy group (9/30, 30.0%; p = 0.003). In contrast, the observation group had ERD in 31/61 eyes (51.0%), showing no significant difference when compared with the cryotherapy ± laser group (p = 0.104) or the laser monotherapy group (p = 0.098).

This study presents a highly comprehensive analysis and a detailed comparison of CLV in patients with IRD, examining both our Hadassah cohort and the existing literature. It confirms CLV as a heterogeneous condition influenced by different gene mutations that manifest in various clinical presentations. Our analysis highlights the characteristics shared within our cohort, emphasizing their similarities with those documented in the literature. This review underscores these aspects, reinforcing the variability and complexity of CLV.

The mean age at CLV diagnosis in our cohort, predominantly in the third decade, aligns with the literature review, though it contrasts with previous reports suggesting a fourth-decade diagnosis [4, 6]. Notably, we found a relatively long mean interval between the onset of IRD symptoms and the diagnosis of CLV, both in our cohort (16.2 years) and in the literature review (11.31 years). This interval underscores the presence of a critical observation period during which the diagnosis of CLV may be delayed.

Our study’s unique contribution lies in the identification of two novel mutations in the LRP5 and KIZ genes, previously associated with other conditions [53, 54], that are associated with CLV. The patient with the mutation in LRP5 had a clinical picture of RP and presented with signs of CLV at 48 years of age. Mutations in the LRP5 gene were previously reported to cause familial exudative vitreoretinopathy (FEVR) [55]. Moreover, disease entities such as RP can be associated with retinal telangiectasia, a sign of CLV disease that clinically resembles FEVR; thus, although a variant in LRP5 may explain the CLV signs, it is less likely to explain the RP. Therefore, it is reasonable to conclude that CLV must be a complication of RP. While mutations in the LRP5 appear quite prevalent with IRDs [56], mutations in the KIZ are uncommon [57]. These rare KIZ mutations provide an important insight into the genetic complexities of CLV, highlighting the importance of comprehensive genetic screening in IRD cases. This could reveal novel genetic pathways or mechanisms contributing to the disease’s complexity and variability in clinical presentations. By identifying these two genes as playing a role in CLV, we expand the already broad spectrum of genes associated with CLV and suggest that CLV may arise due to IRD-related complications.

Importantly, our results challenge the conventional understanding that genetic consistency is prevalent among CLV patients [7]. Eleven patients in the literature review and one in our cohort had negative genetic testing results. This variation highlights the potential role of environmental and/or additional genetic factors in the development of CLV. In addition, both our cohort and the literature review show significant discrepancies in the familial occurrence of IRD versus CLV, supporting the notion that CLV is predominantly a secondary manifestation rather than a primary genetic condition.

On the other hand, the occurrence of vertical transmission within a family [27] and the presence of CLV in a set of identical twins [21] provides compelling evidence of a genetic component. Therefore, our results support the notion of a dual-factor hypothesis, in which both a genetic predisposition and an environmental trigger are needed for the exudative phenotype to manifest [7]. Nevertheless, these findings underscore the need for further research regarding the genetic and secondary factors that contribute to the development of CLV in patients with an IRD.

The etiology of the vascular changes remains only partially known. Pruett previously suggested that toxic byproducts resulting from the degradation of photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium may cause a vasodilatory response [6, 23], while Khan et al. proposed that inherent microvascular leakage in RP may lead to ERD through retinal hypoxia [6, 23]. This process is further exacerbated by the vasculopathy’s inferotemporal location and fluid accumulation due to gravity, thereby contributing to the often inferior position of the RD. Given these insights, we propose that the term “vasculopathy” more accurately captures the underlying vascular dysfunction and its consequences than “vitreoretinopathy”, which implies broader retinal and vitreal involvement. Thus, we advocate for the use of “vasculopathy” to describe these specific pathological changes.

Bilateral asymmetric CLV was present in most patients, both in our cohort and the literature review (70% and 69.9%, respectively). This finding underscores the heterogeneous presentation of CLV and suggests the need to conduct a thorough bilateral assessment even if symptoms appear unilateral. Moreover, our finding that telangiectasia was the most prevalent sign of CLV, coupled with predominant inferior/temporal periphery involvement, indicates their diagnostic significance [6, 23]. ERD has been hypothesized to adversely affect visual outcome in patients with CLV [4, 7, 8], and our results from literature review conclusively support this hypothesis by showing a significant correlation between the presence of ERD and higher LogMAR scores, validating ERD as a prognostic factor for poor outcome.

In our cohort, CME was observed in ~50% of the eyes affected by CLV, indicating a higher incidence than the results in the literature review ( ~ 17%). This discrepancy could suggest variability in the manifestation of IRD-related symptoms or differences in the patient populations studied. While the literature suggests that CME is not a direct result of CLV but rather a manifestation of underlying IRD [58, 59], our findings highlight the need for further investigation into the relationship between CME and CLV.

CLV has been reported to cause progressive visual deterioration. For example, Varela et al. noted differences in BCVA at baseline between patients with CLV and patients without CLV [4]; however, it is unclear whether the loss of visual acuity was due solely to CLV or was also affected by the IRD. Our literature review study addressed this question by specifically examining unilateral CLV, thereby distinguishing between the effects of CLV and IRD concerning vision. Interestingly, we found that CLV-affected eyes had significant visual deterioration, whereas non-CLV-affected eyes had relatively stable visual acuity. This supports the aggressive nature of CLV, highlighted by nearly 40% of patients in our literature review becoming legally blind by their final visit, a rate higher than previously reported [4].

Furthermore, initial differences in BCVA were observed between the Hadassah and literature cohorts (0.58 ± 0.38 LogMAR vs. 0.92 ± 0.89 LogMAR, respectively; p < 0.001). However, these differences had diminished by the final visit, with both cohorts exhibiting similar BCVA values (1.75 ± 0.97 LogMAR for Hadassah vs. 1.69 ± 1.06 LogMAR for literature; p = 1.0). This convergence suggests that, despite early variances, the long-term outcomes of CLV tend to be uniformly severe across different patient groups.

Building on these observations, our study captures the differential impact of various treatment modalities on disease progression. Specifically, our literature review found that eyes treated with cryotherapy +/- laser treatment had a notably higher prevalence of ERD. Thus, ERD may serve as a marker for severe disease, helping drive the decision regarding whether to adopt more intensive treatments. Additionally, although visual outcomes did not significantly differ between patients managed observationally and those undergoing either laser monotherapy or cryotherapy ± laser therapy, the high prevalence of ERD in the latter group highlights the complexity of managing CLV. These observations imply that while severe clinical presentations may warrant aggressive treatments, they do not necessarily correlate with better visual outcomes, emphasizing the need for treatment strategies tailored to each patient’s presentations and the severity of the disease.

A major strength of this study is its relatively large scale. As the largest of its kind, this study included 173 cases of CLV associated with IRDs, providing a unique depth of data that strengthened our findings’ reliability and applicability. Another strength lies in our comparative analysis of unilateral cases, which allowed us to isolate the effects of CLV in affected eyes, thereby providing a clearer view of its specific effects on visual outcomes. Furthermore, while our departmental cohort is smaller, it complements the broader literature review by providing detailed, case-specific insights that enrich the findings. Finally, our detailed clinical characterization of factors that affect visual outcome—notably, the effect of exudative retinal detachment on visual deterioration—provides valuable insights for diagnosing and managing CLV. Despite these strengths, however, our study has several limitations that warrant discussion. First, the retrospective nature of case reviews and variability in clinical documentation may have introduced slight bias in the data interpretation. Furthermore, the relatively small number of cases and the patients’ diverse responses to various treatment modalities may limit our ability to generalize our results across broader patient populations.

In conclusion, this study elucidates the complex interplay between genetic mutations and CLV in patients with IRDs and quantifies its impact on visual outcomes. The comprehensive analysis, comparing our Hadassah cohort with the existing literature, confirms the heterogeneous nature of CLV, which is influenced by various genetic mutations and manifests in diverse clinical presentations. We identified novel genetic mutations, particularly in the LRP5 and KIZ genes, that provide deeper insights into their etiological complexity. These genetic discoveries underscore the variability of CLV presentation and its potential as a secondary complication in patients with existing IRDs. Our detailed analysis of unilateral cases has demonstrated that the presence of CLV significantly affects visual prognosis, confirming the association between ERD and worsened visual outcomes. Lastly, our results indicate that the choice of treatment, whether laser monotherapy or combined with cryotherapy, does not significantly influence final visual outcomes.

CLV is a rare but severe complication of inherited retinal diseases (IRDs), marked by retinal telangiectasia, exudates, and extensive exudative retinal detachment. CLV typically manifests in the third decade of life and affects approximately 0.5% of individuals with an IRD. Despite its severe effects on visual outcome, many cases have been identified without a genetic cause, highlighting the need for further genetic studies.

This study confirms that CLV contributes to visual deterioration independent from the underlying IRD by demonstrating a significant decline in visual acuity in the affected eye but not in the unaffected eye in unilateral cases. We also identified two novel genetic markers (LRP5 and KIZ) associated with CLV in patients with an IRD. In addition, we quantified the impact of exudative retinal detachment (ERD) on visual outcome, indicating that the presence of ERD likely serves as a major factor in determining visual prognosis.
