# Prospective Validation of the Movement Disorder Society Prodromal Multiple System Atrophy Criteria in Pure Autonomic Failure.
**Authors:** Millar Vernetti P, Palma JA, Biaggioni I, Shibao CA, Freeman R, Gibbons C, Singer W, Coon EA, Miglis MG, Krismer F, Fanciulli A, Goldstein DS, Betensky RA, Kaufmann H
**Journal:** Mov Disord (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mds.70417](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.70417)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The Movement Disorder Society (MDS) research criteria for possible prodromal multiple system atrophy (PP-MSA) have not been prospectively validated.

OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the PP-MSA criteria in a longitudinal cohort of patients with pure autonomic failure (PAF) and to assess whether additional clinical features improve their predictive value.

METHODS: Seventy-six patients with PAF enrolled across eight centers in the Natural History Study of the Synucleinopathies were followed for ≥6 years or until phenoconversion. Participants were classified according to MDS PP-MSA criteria and followed for development of MSA, Parkinson's disease, or dementia with Lewy bodies. Diagnostic performance was assessed longitudinally. Analyses were repeated using a stricter olfactory threshold (University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test [UPSIT]≤28) as an exclusion criterion.

RESULTS: Thirty-eight participants met PP-MSA criteria, of whom 12 phenoconverted to MSA within 6 years. Sensitivity was 100% at year 1 and 92% at year 6, whereas specificity ranged from 56% to 67%. Positive predictive value (PPV) ranged from 21% to 34%. Applying a stricter olfactory threshold improved specificity (90%-97%) and PPV (53%-83%), with a modest reduction in sensitivity (to 83%). Participants who phenoconverted to MSA were younger, had more severe urinary dysfunction, and greater orthostatic heart rate responses.

CONCLUSIONS: The PP-MSA criteria demonstrate high sensitivity but limited specificity in patients with PAF. A stricter olfactory threshold improves diagnostic performance and may enhance cohort enrichment for clinical trials. © 2026 International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society. Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare, rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by autonomic failure, parkinsonism, and cerebellar ataxia.1 Historically, the diagnosis of MSA required overt motor symptoms.2 However, longitudinal studies indicate that MSA, like other synucleinopathies, is preceded by a prodromal phase marked by autonomic dysfunction, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD), and subtle motor changes.3–9 Identifying individuals during this prodromal stage offers an opportunity for early diagnosis, improved counseling, and clinical trial enrollment.

The Movement Disorder Society (MDS) recently proposed research diagnostic criteria for possible prodromal MSA (PP-MSA)10 to identify individuals at an early stage of the disease. These criteria combine non-motor features such as neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (nOH), urogenital dysfunction, or RBD, with subtle motor signs while excluding features suggestive of alternative synucleinopathies, including olfactory loss,11 which is common in prodromal Parkinson’s disease (PD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB).12

The diagnostic performance of the MDS PP-MSA criteria has not been prospectively evaluated. In particular, their ability to distinguish individuals who will develop MSA from those who will phenoconvert to PD, DLB, or remain stable is uncertain.

Patients with pure autonomic failure (PAF) provide an opportunity to address this question. PAF is a synucleinopathy characterized by nOH, which may precede the development of MSA, PD, and DLB.7–9,13,14

In this study, we prospectively evaluated the performance of the MDS PP-MSA criteria in a multicenter cohort of patients with PAF and assessed whether additional clinical features improve the identification of patients who will develop MSA.

We analyzed data from the Natural History Study of the Synucleinopathies (NHSS; ClinicalTrials.gov
NCT01799915), a multicenter, prospective, observational study funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The methodology of the NHSS has been previously described.8.13

Participants were required to have a diagnosis of PAF based on established criteria.8.13 All participants underwent comprehensive annual evaluations, including standardized autonomic testing (heart rate variability to deep-paced breathing at 6 breaths/minute, blood pressure and heart rate response to the Valsalva maneuver, and passive head-up tilt at 60° for 10 minutes), detailed neurological examinations by experienced autonomic and movement disorders specialists, and olfactory testing using the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) (Sensonics International, Haddon Heights, NJ, USA) and recording the total score, as well as the classification of olfactory function following normative data provided by the manufacturer. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was used to screen for cognitive impairment. MoCA scores of 18–25 were considered in the mild cognitive impairment (MCI) severity range, in line with published cut-off values.15 Comprehensive neuropsychological testing was not performed; therefore, the MCI designation reflects a screening categorization rather than a formal diagnosis. Probable RBD was assessed using the validated RBD-single question16 and, where feasible, confirmed with polysomnography. Post-void residual (PVR) urine volume was measured using a non-invasive, portable, ultrasound-based, urinary bladder scanner (BladderScan Prime Plus, Verathon, Bothell, WA, USA), immediately after voiding. Subtle motor signs included mild bradykinesia, slight rigidity, reduced arm swing, or impaired tandem gait, mild gait instability, dysarthria, or oculomotor features judged by a movement disorders-trained clinician as subtle, not satisfying diagnostic criteria for PD or cerebellar ataxia, rather than a fixed Unified Multiple System Atrophy Rating Scale (UMSARS-II) threshold, consistent with the MDS PP-MSA criteria.10

Participants with PAF were assessed at each visit and classified as having PP-MSA when they met MDS research criteria.10 These criteria require the presence of subtle motor signs insufficient to meet diagnostic criteria for PD, DLB, or cerebellar ataxia.

Anosmia was used as an exclusion criterion and defined as UPSIT≤18.17 Investigators assigning PP-MSA status were blinded to subsequent phenoconversion.

For participants classified as PP-MSA, baseline was defined as the timepoint at which subtle motor signs were first detected, either at enrollment or during follow-up. For participants classified as not-PP-MSA, baseline was the time of enrollment.

We use the term phenoconversion to describe the transition from a mainly autonomic phenotype to an overt movement disorder. Participants were longitudinally followed to determine phenoconversion to clinically probable or established MSA, PD, or DLB based on established diagnostic criteria.10.18.19 A diagnosis of definite MSA required neuropathological confirmation when available.

In patients with PAF, phenoconversion to MSA typically occurs within 5 years of the onset of symptomatic nOH.7.8.13.14 Phenoconversion beyond this interval is uncommon. Therefore, only participants with a minimum follow-up of 6 years or documented phenoconversion within this period were included in the analysis. Results should be interpreted within this timeframe.

Given prior evidence that a more stringent olfactory threshold improves prediction of phenoconversion to MSA,8 we repeated analyses using UPSIT≤28 to define abnormal olfaction (ie, hyposmia), rather than UPSIT≤18, conventionally used for identifying anosmia.

Continuous variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, χ2 test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Diagnostic performance of the PP-MSA criteria was evaluated annually following baseline by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), diagnostic accuracy, positive likelihood ratio (LR+), and negative likelihood ratio (LR−).

Definitions were as follows: (1) true-positives: participants who met PP-MSA criteria and subsequently developed MSA; (2) false-positives: participants who met PP-MSA criteria but did not develop MSA, including those who phenoconverted to PD or DLB; (3) true-negatives: participants who did not meet PP-MSA criteria and did not develop MSA; and (4) false-negatives: participants who did not meet PP-MSA criteria but subsequently developed MSA.

Sensitivity was defined as the proportion of participants who met PP-MSA criteria among those who developed MSA. Specificity was defined as the proportion of participants who did not meet PP-MSA criteria among those who did not phenoconvert to MSA. PPV was defined as the proportion of participants who developed MSA among those meeting PP-MSA criteria, and NPV as the proportion who did not develop MSA among those not meeting the criteria. Diagnostic accuracy was calculated as the proportion of participants who were correctly categorized (true-positives and true-negatives). Likelihood ratios (LR+ and LR−) quantified the change in probability of developing MSA associated with meeting or not meeting PP-MSA criteria. Because our cohort also included participants from one of the datasets used for the development of the MDS PP-MSA criteria,13 we also compared the sensitivity analysis of this subset (development cohort) with that of the new participants who had not been included in the development cohort (ie, validation cohort), and with the full cohort (Table S1).

In addition, we explored the effect of previously identified predictors of phenoconversion to MSA8.13 not currently considered in the PP-MSA criteria (demographic characteristics, cognition, dream-enactment behavior, blood pressure, heart rate, and catecholamines during tilt-table test, and scales assessing neurological and autonomic symptoms [UMSARS, Composite Autonomic Symptom Score-31, COMPASS-31], as well as PVR urinary volumes in a small set of participants), using univariate logistic and multinomial regression due to the small sample size.

Between September 2011 and May 2023, 208 patients with PAF were enrolled in the NHSS, of whom 176 had olfactory testing. The full cohort included in the analysis consisted of 76 patients with PAF at baseline with at least 6 years of follow-up, or who phenoconverted within 6 years (Table S1 and Fig. S1). Of these, 38 met the MDS PP-MSA criteria, including absence of anosmia (defined as UPSIT≤18), whereas 38 were categorized as non-PP-MSA because they either did not develop subtle motor signs during follow-up or had anosmia. RBD was confirmed with polysomnography in 6 participants: 3 who met PP-MSA criteria (2 developed DLB and 1 did not phenoconvert), and 3 who were categorized as non-PP-MSA (2 participants developed PD and DLB, and 1 did not phenoconvert). Baseline characteristics of participants classified as PP-MSA are presented in Table 1.

During follow-up, 12 participants phenoconverted to MSA (4 cerebellar type and 8 parkinsonian type), including 6 (50%) with clinically probable MSA, 4 (33%) with clinically established MSA, and 2 (17%) with neuropathologically established MSA. Twenty-one participants phenoconverted to DLB, including 14 (67%) probable and 7 (33%) possible DLB. Twenty-three participants phenoconverted to PD, 20 (87%) clinically probable, and 3 (13%) with clinically established PD.

The full longitudinal breakdown of patient classifications and diagnostic performance metrics is detailed in Table 2. Among the 38 participants categorized as PP-MSA at baseline, 8 (21%) developed MSA by year 1, 11 (32%) by year 3, and 11 by year 6 (32%), thus correctly capturing all MSA conversions within the first 5 years of follow-up. One additional participant developed anosmia during follow-up and therefore no longer met criteria for PP-MSA before subsequently converting to clinically established MSA and later confirmed having neuropathologically established MSA (Table 2). Other than this participant, no others classified as not-PP-MSA phenoconverted to MSA during the study follow-up.

Accordingly, the PP-MSA criteria had perfect sensitivity (100%) and NPV (100%) during the early years of follow-up, declining slightly to 92% sensitivity by year 6 (Table 2).

However, many participants classified as PP-MSA did not subsequently develop MSA. Among the 38 PP-MSA participants, 30 had not developed MSA by year 1, including 4 who phenoconverted to PD/DLB and 26 who remained classified as PP-MSA. By year 3, 11 had phenoconverted to PD/DLB, and 11 remained PP-MSA, while 5 developed anosmia and no longer met PP-MSA criteria. By year 6, 13 participants had phenoconverted to PD/DLB, and 8 remained PP-MSA, with one additional participant developing anosmia.

As a result, the PPV of the PP-MSA criteria increased modestly over time, from 21% at year 1 to 34% at year 6, whereas specificity remained low (56% at year 1, 67% at year 6), yielding a diagnostic accuracy of 71%. The LR+ ranged from 2.27 to 2.79 during follow-up.

When a stricter olfactory threshold (UPSIT≤28) was applied as an exclusion criterion, only 16 participants met PP-MSA criteria at baseline (Table 2). Two participants with UPSIT scores≤28 subsequently developed MSA, one at year 3 and another at year 6, reducing sensitivity from 100% at year 1 to 83% at year 6.

However, this threshold excluded all participants who phenoconverted to PD/DLB (18 at year 1, 33 by year 3, and 43 by year 6) and most participants who did not phenoconvert (19 of 21). Consequently, specificity improved substantially (90% at year 1, 94% at year 3, and 97% at year 6). PPV also increased (53%, 71%, and 83% at years 1, 3, and 6, respectively), while NPV remained high (100% at year 1, 98% at year 3, and 97% at year 6), yielding a diagnostic accuracy of 91% at year 1 and 95% at year 6. Similarly, the LR+ increased markedly (9.71 at year 1, 14.77 at year 3, and 26.67 at year 6) while the LR− remained low (0 at year 1, 0.1 at year 3, and 0.17 at year 6).

Tables 2 and 3 summarize the diagnostic performance of the PP-MSA criteria using anosmia (UPSIT≤18) and hyposmia (UPSIT≤28) as exclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

These findings support the prognostic value of the PP-MSA criteria and indicate that a stricter olfactory threshold substantially improves specificity with only a modest reduction in sensitivity.

Participants who phenoconverted to MSA were significantly younger at baseline (median 62 years, IQR 50–68) than those who phenoconverted to PD/DLB or did not phenoconvert (72 years, IQR 69–78; P = 0.01). Similarly, the onset of autonomic failure occurred earlier in MSA converters (57 years, IQR 49–66) compared with PD/DLB converters and non-phenoconverters (66 years, IQR 62–74; P = 0.01). The onset of subtle motor signs was also earlier in MSA (62 years, IQR 50–68) than in the other groups (72 years, IQR 69–78; P = 0.01).

At study entry, the duration of autonomic failure was shorter in MSA converters (3 years, IQR 1–4) compared with PD/DLB converters and non-converters (6 years, IQR 4–9; P = 0.05). The time from onset of autonomic failure to phenoconversion or last follow-up was significantly shorter in MSA converters (4 years, IQR 3–6) than in non-converters (12 years, IQR 8–16; P = 0.015), but not significantly different from PD/DLB converters (6 years, IQR 4–11; P = 0.056).

Consistent with these findings, younger age at study entry (odds ratio [OR] 1.33, 95% confidence interval [95% CI] 1.08–1.65; P = 0.01), younger age at onset of autonomic failure (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.03–1.28; P = 0.01), and younger age at onset of subtle motor signs (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.08–1.65) were associated with higher odds of developing MSA within 6 years of follow-up. A shorter duration of autonomic failure at study entry also increased the odds of MSA (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.00–1.79; P = 0.05) (Table 4). Additional comparisons are summarized in Table 4, and comparisons between MSA converters, PD/DLB converters, and those who remained as PAF are presented in Table S2.

Even after excluding participants with anosmia (UPSIT≤18), olfactory function differed markedly between groups. Participants who phenoconverted to MSA had significantly higher baseline UPSIT scores (median 34, IQR 31–36) than those who phenoconverted to PD/DLB or did not phenoconvert (23, IQR 21–28; P < 0.001). Normosmia was more common among MSA converters (50% vs 12%, P = 0.016), whereas severe hyposmia was less frequent than in PD/DLB converters and non-converters (17% vs 57%, P = 0.027).

Consistent with these observations, higher UPSIT scores were associated with increased odds of developing MSA (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.08–1.49; P < 0.001). Normosmia markedly increased the likelihood of MSA (OR 7.68, 95% CI 1.47–40; P = 0.016), whereas severe hyposmia strongly decreased the probability of MSA (OR 0.15, 95% CI 0.03–0.81; P = 0.027).

All participants presented with neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (nOH). Supine and upright blood pressure values during tilt-table testing were similar between participants who developed MSA and those who phenoconverted to PD/DLB or remained stable.

However, heart rate responses during orthostatic stress differed between groups. Participants who developed MSA showed a higher heart rate during tilt testing both at 3 min (86 bpm, IQR 75–102 vs. 75 bpm, IQR 71–84; P = 0.03) and at the time of lowest blood pressure (86 bpm, IQR 75–93 vs. 76 bpm, IQR 67–85; P = 0.04). A higher proportion of MSA converters had a heart rate exceeding 85 bpm at the nadir of blood pressure during tilt (55% vs. 15%; P = 0.03).

Because the drop in blood pressure during tilt was similar between groups, the ΔHR/ΔSBP ratio was significantly higher in participants who developed MSA (0.34, IQR 0.14–0.88) compared with PD/DLB converters and non-converters (0.15, IQR 0.07–0.31; P = 0.03).

In logistic regression analyses, higher heart rate during tilt (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.01–1.17; P = 0.03), higher heart rate at the time of lowest blood pressure (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.15; P = 0.04), heart rate > 85 bpm during tilt (OR 6.8, 95% CI 1.23–37.5; P = 0.04), and a higher ΔHR/ΔSBP ratio (OR 11.68, 95% CI 1.24–109; P = 0.03) were associated with increased odds of developing MSA (Table 4). Supine hypertension, symptomatic orthostatic hypotension, and plasma norepinephrine levels (supine and during passive head-up tilt) were not significantly different between groups.

Urinary dysfunction was more pronounced in participants who later developed MSA. Scores for UMSARS-I item 10 (urinary symptoms) were significantly higher in MSA converters (P = 0.01). Clinically significant urinary symptoms requiring treatment were present in 58% of MSA converters compared with 20% of other participants (P = 0.025), and intermittent catheterization was more frequently required (36% vs. 5%; P = 0.043).

Among participants with available measurements (7 participants in the PAF/PD/DLB group, and 5 in the MSA group), post-void residual (PVR) urine volume was markedly higher in MSA converters (275 mL, IQR 200–326) compared with PD/DLB converters and non-converters (18 mL, IQR 0–43; P = 0.047). A greater proportion of MSA converters had PVR>100 mL (80% vs. 14%; P = 0.041).

In regression analyses, greater urinary symptom severity (OR 3.0, 95% CI 1.27–7.06; P = 0.01), urinary symptoms requiring treatment (OR 5.6, 95% CI 1.24–25.3; P = 0.025), and higher PVR (OR 1.22 per 10 mL increase, 95% CI 1.01–1.36; P = 0.047) were associated with increased odds of developing MSA (Table 4).

This study validates the MDS research criteria for PP-MSA in a prospective cohort of patients with PAF. The criteria demonstrated high sensitivity for identifying individuals who subsequently developed MSA, but limited specificity, reflecting inclusion of patients who phenoconverted to PD or DLB or did not phenoconvert.

Applying a stricter olfactory threshold substantially improved specificity and PPV, with only a modest reduction in sensitivity (Fig. 1). These findings indicate that olfactory function is an important modifier of diagnostic performance within the PP-MSA framework. Of note, one participant who developed anosmia during follow-up and therefore no longer met PP-MSA criteria subsequently phenoconverted to MSA, that ultimately was neuropathologically confirmed. Anosmia is extremely rare in MSA but does not categorically exclude the diagnosis and may rarely emerge in late prodromal or early symptomatic disease. A similar patient with anosmia and neuropathologically established MSA who had cytoplasmic glial inclusions in the olfactory bulb20 has also been reported. Applying the stricter UPSIT≤28 threshold would have missed these cases, contributing to the modest drop in sensitivity (92% to 83%) and underscoring that no single criterion is fully discriminating.

Patients who phenoconverted to MSA differed clinically from those who did not. They were younger, had more severe urinary dysfunction, and exhibited greater orthostatic heart rate responses resulting in a higher ΔHR/ΔSBP ratio (Fig. 2). These features may assist in further risk stratification within PP-MSA, although they are not currently included in the criteria.

These findings have implications for clinical trial design. While the PP-MSA criteria provide a sensitive approach to identifying at-risk individuals, their specificity may be insufficient for efficient cohort selection. Applying a stricter olfactory threshold and incorporating additional clinical features may improve cohort enrichment.

A potential concern is that 36 of 76 participants overlapped with prior natural history analyses that informed the conceptual framework of the MDS PP-MSA criteria. However, this does not represent circular validation. Nearly half of the cohort (40 participants) was independently recruited, and diagnostic performance was unchanged when analyses were restricted to this subset, with no differences in sensitivity or specificity (Table S1). Performance was identical in the independent subset, arguing strongly against circularity. In addition, classification as PP-MSA was performed blinded to phenoconversion outcomes.

This study has several limitations. The number of MSA converters was modest, but the prospective design, standardized assessments, and consistency with prior studies support the robustness of the findings. Diagnoses were primarily clinical; however, two cases had neuropathological confirmation, providing gold standard validation. The cohort was restricted to patients presenting with nOH, and findings may not generalize to other prodromal trajectories such as isolated RBD or urogenital presentations. In our study, only six cases had RBD diagnosed with polysomnography, and probable RBD was based on the RBD single-question screen. PVR urine volume was available in only 32% of PP-MSA participants, limiting the precision of effect estimates (reflected in the wide confidence intervals), and urodynamic studies were available in only three participants who phenoconverted to MSA with elevated PVR, confirming neurogenic bladder voiding dysfunction. However, given that the participants who phenoconverted to MSA were younger and had a lower proportion of males, it is more likely that lower urinary tract symptoms and increased PVR volumes were related to neurogenic bladder voiding dysfunction rather than bladder outlet obstruction. This pattern is consistent with prior work showing that severe urinary retention discriminates MSA from PD21. In addition, biomarkers were not evaluated. Emerging evidence suggests that immunohistochemical detection of cutaneous alpha-synuclein, cerebrospinal fluid α-synuclein seeding assays, and neurofilament light chain can improve prediction and diagnostic differentiation between MSA and Lewy body diseases (PD and DLB).22–26 Future incorporation of these biomarkers into a two-step enrichment strategy, including clinical screening followed by biomarker confirmation, may further enhance early detection and trial design.

In conclusion, the MDS PP-MSA provides a sensitive method for identifying individuals at risk of MSA in the setting of PAF. Their specificity can be improved by applying a stricter olfactory threshold and other clinical features, which may enhance their utility for early diagnosis and clinical trial enrichment.
