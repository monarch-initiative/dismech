# Propafenone Overdose Presenting With Brugada Phenocopy, QRS Widening, and Ventricular Fibrillation.
**Authors:** Xiong Q, Xie Y
**Journal:** JACC Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaccas.2026.107048](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaccas.2026.107048)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Brugada phenocopy (BrP) denotes reversible Brugada-type electrocardiogram changes from transient triggers, requiring management distinct from congenital Brugada syndrome.

CASE SUMMARY: A 19-year-old man collapsed at home, regained consciousness in transit, and soon after emergency department arrival had recurrent syncope with ventricular fibrillation, which was defibrillated (200 J). The electrocardiogram showed PR interval approximately 200 milliseconds, QRS interval approximately 160 milliseconds, QTc interval approximately 489 milliseconds, lateral J-waves, and coved ST-segment elevation in V1-V2. A review of his phone confirmed propafenone ingestion (approximately 2.5 g over 3 days). Intravenous sodium bicarbonate led to rapid QRS interval narrowing and clinical stabilization.

DISCUSSION: The triad of diffuse conduction delay, V1-V2 coved morphology, and J-waves in acidemia with dose-verified exposure supports drug-induced BrP. Early bicarbonate reverses sodium-channel blockade; normalization and normal cardiac magnetic resonance argue against a fixed substrate, so implantable cardioverter-defibrillator is unnecessary.

TAKE-HOME MESSAGES: BrP resolves after correcting reversible triggers. In sodium-channel blocker toxicity with QRS interval widening, early sodium bicarbonate is first-line therapy.

A previously healthy 19-year-old man had sudden syncope at home accompanied by generalized tonic movements after 3 days of taking pills purchased online together with beer. He gradually regained consciousness during ambulance transport but reported chest tightness and palpitations. On emergency department arrival, his SpO2 was 92% and blood pressure was 70/30 mm Hg. Minutes later, he developed recurrent loss of consciousness, with the monitor revealing ventricular fibrillation. A single 200-J unsynchronized shock restored sinus rhythm. The patient had no known structural heart disease and had mild depressive symptoms with social withdrawal. There was no family history of sudden death or syncope, and the parents had normal ECG results.

The differential diagnosis includes acute coronary syndrome, fulminant myocarditis, propafenone or other sodium-channel blocker toxicity, “disulfiram-like” reaction, primary Brugada syndrome (BrS), electrolyte derangement, and seizure with postictal ECG changes. Negative biomarkers/echocardiogram/computed tomography and the ECG pattern (global conduction delay with J-waves and coved V1–V2) prioritized drug-induced sodium-channel blockade.

After return of spontaneous circulation, the 12-lead ECG showed sinus rhythm with PR prolongation (approximately 200 milliseconds), marked QRS interval widening (approximately 160 milliseconds), prolonged QTc interval (approximately 489 milliseconds), and a rightward QRS axis. There were prominent J-waves in the lateral precordial leads and coved-type ST-segment elevation in V1–V2, consistent with a Brugada phenocopy (BrP) pattern in the context of global conduction slowing. Arterial blood gas demonstrated metabolic acidosis with pH 7.20, HCO3– 14.9 mmol/L, partial arterial pressure of carbon dioxide 38 mm Hg, partial arterial pressure of oxygen 123 mm Hg, and lactate 10 mmol/L. Serial high-sensitivity troponin and creatine kinase-myocardial band remained negative; serum electrolytes, D-dimer, and coagulation studies were within reference ranges. Chest computed tomography and transthoracic echocardiography were unremarkable. On hospital day 2, a review of the patient's phone confirmed ingestion of propafenone 50-mg tablets × 50 over 3 days (total approximately 2.5 g, approximately 33 mg/kg) taken with beer. A predischarge cardiac magnetic resonance showed normal biventricular size and function with no myocardial edema on T2/T2-mapping and no late gadolinium enhancement, arguing against myocarditis or scar.

Advanced airway and mechanical ventilation were initiated; norepinephrine supported blood pressure. Given the ECG/arterial blood gas profile, intravenous (IV) sodium bicarbonate was administered in boluses, followed by infusion to maintain pH 7.50–7.55 with careful monitoring of serum sodium and potassium. QRS duration narrowed progressively (Figure 1C) and hemodynamics improved. No Ia/Ic agents or amiodarone were given. Lipid emulsion and extracorporeal support were reserved for potential deterioration and were not required. The patient was extubated on day 2.

The patient was discharged on day 7 without neurologic deficit. Recovery-phase ECGs are shown in Figure 2, highlighting near normalization of QRS interval and resolution of Brugada-like changes before discharge and at early follow-up. At 2-week follow-up, Holter showed sinus arrhythmia with minor ST-T changes and no malignant arrhythmias (Supplemental Appendix). He received education to avoid Ia/Ic antiarrhythmics, tricyclic antidepressants, cocaine, and large doses of local anesthetics, and to seek care for fever-related ECG changes. Psychological support and medication-safety counseling were provided. BrP describes transient Brugada-type ECG patterns provoked by identifiable, reversible conditions in patients without congenital BrS.1 Typical precipitants include myocardial ischemia, metabolic derangements (eg, hyperkalemia and acidosis), mechanical compression, pulmonary disease, and drug-induced sodium-channel blockade; resolution of the trigger normalizes the ECG and portends a benign substrate compared with BrS. In contrast, BrS is a primary channelopathy with a fixed predisposition to ventricular arrhythmias, diagnosed by a spontaneous or provoked type 1 Brugada pattern in V1–V2 (preferably recorded in high intercostal spaces) plus clinical features.2 Our patient's marked global conduction delay (PR/QRS/QTc prolongation) with coved ST-segment elevation in V1–V2 and lateral J-waves, together with a clear toxic exposure and rapid normalization after targeted therapy, fulfills modern descriptions of BrP rather than BrS. Mislabeling BrP as BrS can lead to unnecessary restrictions and interventions; careful documentation of reversibility and follow-up ECGs after washout are therefore essential.

Because the Brugada ECG pattern is intrinsically dynamic, follow-up recordings are crucial to distinguish a transient phenocopy from concealed BrS. Experimental and mapping studies have shown that the epicardial right ventricular outflow tract and anterior right ventricular base, which lie beneath the right precordial leads, display a higher density of the transient outward potassium current than the left ventricle and the endocardium. This regional transient outward potassium current gradient accentuates repolarization abnormalities in BrS and explains why the diagnostic coved ST-segment elevation is most prominent in leads V1–V3. By moving V1 and V2 1 or 2 intercostal spaces higher, the electrodes are brought closer to the right ventricular outflow tract epicardium, which increases the likelihood of unmasking a type 1 Brugada pattern in patients with a high index of suspicion.3 For this reason, at near 1-year follow-up, we obtained both a standard 12-lead ECG and high right-precordial tracings (V1′–V2′ one intercostal space higher and V1″–V2″ two intercostal spaces higher, with V3 kept in the standard position), all of which showed no type 1, type 2, or type 3 Brugada morphology. These findings, together with the normal cardiac magnetic resonance and the absence of a family history of sudden death, strongly support a purely drug-induced BrP rather than latent BrS. In patients with persistent or equivocal Brugada-type ST-segment elevation after correction of potential triggers, further evaluation with ajmaline challenge and/or genetic testing for Brugada-associated variants may be warranted in accordance with current guideline recommendations.

Mechanistically, propafenone (class Ic) produces potent, use-dependent fast Na+-channel blockade, slowing phase 0 upstroke and widening the QRS interval; acidemia increases drug ionization and channel binding, amplifying conduction delay and facilitating malignant ventricular arrhythmias.4 This explains the patient's biphasic presentation (prehospital syncope, emergency department recurrence into ventricular fibrillation) on a background of metabolic acidosis and large cumulative dose. The triad of 1) Brugada-like ST-segment elevation in V1–V2, 2) conspicuous J-/Osborn waves, and 3) diffuse conduction slowing strongly favors toxic Na+-channel blockade over primary BrS.

Sodium bicarbonate is first-line therapy for toxin-induced sodium-channel blockade: the sodium load counters channel blockade, whereas alkalinization reduces drug affinity to the channel and narrows QRS interval. Evidence, most robust for tricyclic antidepressant poisoning, supports bicarbonate as the foundational antidote;5 multiple propafenone-overdose reports describe rapid ECG reversal and hemodynamic stabilization with bicarbonate, mirroring our case.6,7 Notably, sodium bicarbonate is mechanism specific—it is less effective when QRS interval widening reflects non-Na+ mechanisms (eg, bupropion gap-junction inhibition), underscoring the value of ECG pattern recognition and toxicologic context.8

For rescue therapy when bicarbonate and standard measures fail, IV lipid emulsion has been used in lipophilic cardiotoxins (including isolated propafenone cases), but high-quality evidence is limited; consensus statements suggest selective, case-by-case use rather than routine administration.9

In reversible BrP without arrhythmic substrate after correction (normal imaging and biomarkers; ECG normalization on follow-up), implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation is generally unwarranted; guideline-directed implantable cardioverter-defibrillator therapy applies to BrS with prior cardiac arrest or sustained ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation, or to selected high-risk phenotypes.10 Patient counseling should emphasize strict avoidance of Na+-blocking antiarrhythmics and vigilance during febrile illnesses, which can unmask Brugada patterns even in otherwise healthy individuals.2

Propafenone overdose can mimic BrS and precipitate ventricular fibrillation. Recognizing the QRS-widening Brugada-like pattern with acidosis, confirming the absence of structural disease by cardiac magnetic resonance, and initiating early IV sodium bicarbonate allow rapid electrical and clinical recovery, often obviating advanced therapies.
