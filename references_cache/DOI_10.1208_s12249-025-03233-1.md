# The Road to Precision Nanomedicine: An Insight on Drug Repurposing and Advances in Nanoformulations for Treatment of Cancer
**Authors:** Yasmina Elmahboub, Rofida Albash, Sadek Ahmed, Salwa Salah
**Journal:** AAPS PharmSciTech (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1208/s12249-025-03233-1](https://doi.org/10.1208/s12249-025-03233-1)

## Content

Abstract
Cancer remains one of the most significant global health challenges, with its burden continuing to rise. The limitations of conventional anticancer therapies caused by the lack of tissue selectivity, demands urgent development of safer and more selective therapies to target tumors. Identifying the fundamental cancer hallmarks provided a comprehensive understanding of cancer biology for effective tumor targeting, encompassing tumor-promoting inflammation, metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, genomic instability, phenotypic plasticity, epigenetic reprogramming, and polymorphic microbiomes. Moreover, drug repurposing is a cost-effective and time-saving method for cancer therapy that accelerates the drug discovery process by reusing drugs for new indications. Current research is focusing on combining drug repurposing with nanocarriers that enhance tumor targeting, reduce the side effects, and improve the bioavailability of the drug in a single nanoformulation. This article analyzes various types of nanoparticles encapsulating different classes of drugs, such as phenelzine, fexofenadine, telmisartan, losartan, metformin, canagliflozin, atorvastatin, and fenbendazole, highlighting their anticancer effects and the influence of nanocarriers on the drug’s therapeutic effect. Results revealed that drug-encapsulated nanoparticles enhanced antitumor effects compared to the free drug solutions. This is attributed to the synergism from the nanocarrier’s functionalization, sustained drug release, and improved cellular uptake within tumors that leads to targeting multiple cancer hallmarks. Additionally, this review highlights the present challenges in the clinical translation of nanoformulation and demonstrates how artificial intelligence may facilitate drug screening and identification, therapeutic optimization, and large-scale manufacture. Finally, using these technologies in combination with drug repurposing presents a promising direction for advancing cancer treatment.

Introduction
Cancer is a leading cause of significant societal, economic, 
and public health challenges in the twenty-first century, with 
a high rate of mortality reported globally. In accordance with 
the American Cancer Society, around 20 million new cases 
of cancer were diagnosed in 2022, and 9.7 million cancer-
related deaths were reported worldwide. These statistics 
reveal that about one in five individuals may develop cancer 
during their Lifetime. Currently, it is predicted that by 2050, 
the number of new cancer cases will increase to over 35 mil-
lion patients [1]. Meanwhile, novel cancer drug development 
has become extremely expensive and time-consuming. Also, 
it is estimated that releasing a new anticancer drug to the 
market could cost approximately USD 2–3 billion [2 ]. In 
response to these challenges, drug repurposing has emerged 
as a promising strategy that involves using existing drugs 
for new indications. This approach offers a cost-effective 
and time-efficient alternative for accelerating cancer drug 
development [2].
Beyond its advantages, the success of cancer drug 
repurposing depends on a thorough understanding of can-
cer biology, particularly the molecular pathways and cel-
lular mechanisms driving tumor progression, to achieve 
effective tumor targeting. Additionally, many repurposed

Pathogenesis of Cancer
Cancer is defined as a complex multifactorial disease involv-
ing the transformation of normal cells to malignant neo-
plasms [4 ]. The causes of Such transformation have been 
studied for decades. In the year 2000, the researchers Hana-
han and Weinberg conducted a groundbreaking study that 
shaped the future of cancer research. Their work outlined the 
main hallmarks of cancer, and the key biological capabili-
ties acquired during the transformation of cancer cells from 
normal cells to malignant tumors. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the 
hallmarks of cancer include resistance to growth-inhibitory 
signals, sustaining growth signals, avoidance of apoptosis, 
uncontrolled cell division and induction of angiogenesis, as 
well as activating tissue invasion and metastasis [5].
These properties allow the cell to Survive, proliferate 
and spread in the body in an uncontrolled manner. Such 
hallmarks provided the foundation for understanding can-
cer development; however, in 2011 Hanahan and Weinberg 
published an updated version of the study that introduced 
new insights into cancer development. This updated frame-
work identified additional characteristics and emerging 
hallmarks that may contribute to the classical hallmarks of 
cancer, including (Fig. 1):
Tumor-Promoting Inflammation
Hanahan et al. [6 ] focused on the capability of inflam-
matory cells to release reactive oxygen species (ROS) that 
cause mutations in cancer cells, potentiating their progres-
sion in the body. A recent study confirmed that inflammation 
can promote cellular transformation, promotion, survival, 
proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis. These 
effects are attributed to chronic tissue injury, injury-induced 
cell proliferation, and tissue repair. Also, inflammation 
potentiates tumor growth and progression through the 
release of cytokines and chemokines, as well as inducing 
epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which further 
promotes cancer progression [4 , 6]. Furthermore, there is 
a strong correlation between inflammatory mediators and 
the immune response to tumors. Both tumor cells and host 
cells produce cytokines that influence immune activity. For 
instance, tumor-derived cytokines such as Fas ligand, vas -
cular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and transforming 
growth factor-beta (TGF-β) can actively suppress the body’s 
antitumor immune response; thereby, tumors evade immune 
surveillance [4].
Reprogramming Energy Metabolism
Cancer cells rely on glycolysis to produce energy rather 
than mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, which 
acquires low adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. 
Although glycolysis generates less ATP per glucose mol-
ecule, cancer cells compensate for this reduced efficiency 
by upregulating glucose transporters (GLUT1), leading to 
increased glucose uptake into the cytoplasm. Additionally, 
glycolytic metabolism has been linked to the activation of 
certain oncogenes and the mutant tumor suppressor gene 
TP53, that promote cell proliferation and inhibit apopto-
sis. Moreover, hypoxic conditions within tumors further 
enhance glycolysis, by which the hypoxia response system 
activates the glucose transporters and glycolytic enzymes, 
ultimately increasing glucose utilization. These genetic 
changes upregulate glycolytic enzymes and glucose trans-
porters, promoting glycolytic fueling. This shift provides 
cancer cells with a metabolic advantage, supplying the nec-
essary energy and building blocks for rapid cell growth and 
division [6].
Evading Immune Destruction
The immune system plays a vital role in detecting and 
eliminating cancer cells. However, tumors can evade

immune surveillance through multiple mechanisms. One key 
factor is that cancer cells undergo genetic alterations, lead-
ing to the production of tumor-specific antigens. These anti-
gens allow the immune system to distinguish malignant cells 
from normal ones [6 ]. In normal physiological conditions, 
the immune system is activated in response to the antigen in 
the following steps: Firstly, T cell receptors recognize spe-
cific antigens presented on the surface of antigen-presenting 
cells (APCs) by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) 
molecules. This detection initiates the first signal for T cell 
activation [7]. Secondly, the co-stimulatory receptor (CD28) 
on the T cell binds to its ligands on the APC, providing 
a second signal that is necessary for full T cell activation. 
Once activated, T cells can effectively eliminate cancer cells. 
However, to prevent excessive immune activation and poten-
tial tissue damage, T cells downregulate their activity. Inhib-
itory receptors Such as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated 
antigen 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed cell death protein 1 
(PD-1) are responsible for downregulating T cell activation. 
Consequently, PD-1 binds to its ligands (PD-L1 and PD-L2), 
blocking the first signal (antigen recognition) and inhibiting 
T cell activation. While CTLA-4 binds to CD80 and CD86, 
which are the ligands that bind to CD28, with higher affinity 
than CD28, blocking the second signal (co-stimulation) and 
preventing T cell activation [ 7]. The balance between co-
stimulatory signals and inhibitory mechanisms is known as 
immune checkpoints. Under normal conditions, these check-
points are essential for maintaining self-tolerance, prevent-
ing autoimmunity, and avoiding excessive tissue damage in 
response to infections [8].
In the context of cancer, the PD-1 pathway can be 
exploited by tumor cells to evade immune detection. Many 
tumors express PD-L1 on their surface, which can bind to 
PD-1 on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. This interaction 
inhibits T cell activity, allowing tumor cells to proliferate. 
Additionally, regulatory T cells (Treg cells), a key media-
tor of immune suppression, also express PD-1. Since many 
tumors are highly infiltrated with Tregs, further suppression 
of immune response occurs, leading to enhanced tumor pro-
liferation while shielding tumors from immune attacks [8 ].
Genome Instability and Mutation
Tumors undergo multiple mutations over time, allowing 
them to grow uncontrollably. Normally, cells have repair 
mechanisms to fix DNA damage in response to mutations. 
Among the important genes involved is TP53, which acts 
as a guardian of the genome, where it monitors DNA dam-
age and triggers cell cycle arrest or apoptosis in response to 
genetic damage.
On the contrary, cancer cells frequently exhibit defects 
in the gene TP53, which results in an elevated mutation rate 
where these mutations disrupt the surveillance mechanisms 
responsible for maintaining genomic integrity, thereby pro-
moting uncontrolled growth of cancer cells [6 ].
While the previous hallmarks provide a valuable frame-
work to understand the biology of cancer, they do not involve 
the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms that ena-
ble tumors to acquire such malignant traits and are insuf -
ficient to completely address the complex nature of cancer

Unlocking Phenotypic Plasticity
The ability of cells to alter their identity and function 
(phenotype) into another cell type is referred to as cellu-
lar plasticity. In normal physiology, this can be a beneficial 
adaptation. For instance, cellular plasticity may occur in 
response to inflammation or injury. This process is known 
as transdifferentiation. Such a mechanism serves as a protec-
tive process against chronic damage by replacing damaged 
tissues or forming barriers to withstand pathogenic condi-
tions. However, dysregulated or excessive cellular plastic-
ity, particularly when occurring across multiple tissues, may 
increase susceptibility to cancer progression [10]. A recent 
study confirmed that plasticity is fundamental for cancer 
initiation and progression, allowing cancer cells to adapt 
and evade therapy, leading to resistance of cells to different 
treatment strategies [9, 11].
Nonmutational Epigenetic Reprogramming
As mentioned earlier, genetic mutations are a main cause 
of cancer development; however, alterations in genetic 
expression without modifying DNA sequence can also 
serve as a significant cause of cancer progression. This 
phenomenon is called epigenetic changes [9 ]. One of the 
causes of epigenetic modifications is the abnormal physical 
characteristics of the TME, which will be explained later 
in the article. A key example of TME-driven epigenetic 
regulation is the reversible activation of invasive behavior 
in cancer cells at the margins of solid tumors. This process 
is mediated by EMT, a developmental regulatory mechanism 
that promotes cell plasticity and metastatic potential with 
enhanced migration, survival, and drug resistance capabili-
ties by driving epigenetic modifications [9].
Polymorphic Microbiomes
It’s well-known that the human body hosts diverse micro-
biomes that engage in complex interactions with their host. 
Notably, interindividual variability in these microbiomes 
can profoundly influence cancer development, progression, 
and therapeutic response [9 ]. A recent study reported that 
distinctive microbiomes in individual patients can promote 
tumorigenesis through physical adhesion or release of 
metabolites and toxins, which results in genetic instability, 
epigenetic alterations, and the activation of various signaling 
pathways. Additionally, this process can disrupt the immune 
system and trigger inflammation. The crosslink between the 
microbiome and the host's cells, including tumor cells and 
immune cells, is complex and could result in initiating alter-
ations at genetic, epigenetic, transcriptional, and metabolic 
levels, which requires further study [12].
Understanding TME
The TME is a complex ecosystem surrounding the tumor 
that is responsible for cancer metabolism and progression. 
This environment is characterized by acidic nature and 
hypoxic conditions, along with the production of endog-
enous hydrogen peroxide  (H2O2) and changes in the expres-
sion of the extracellular matrix proteins. It’s worth men-
tioning that the acidic pH derived from anaerobic glycolytic 
excretion of protons  (H+) and lactate by membrane proteins 
is a crucial factor for cell invasion, as it promotes expression 
of angiogenic factors, including VEGF A and interleukins 
(IL). Moreover, as tumors grow, cells in the center become 
hypoxic due to their increased distance from blood vessels. 
To overcome this limitation and ensure continued growth, 
tumor cells increase the production of angiogenic factors 
like VEGF, which stimulate the formation of new blood 
vessels. However, the neovascularization results in blood 
vessels with abnormal structures, as the endothelial cells of 
tumor-associated vessels have multiple gaps that contribute 
to vascular leakiness. Additionally, ROS as  H2O2, plays a 
crucial role in various physiological processes at low con -
centrations. However, elevated levels of  H2O2 lead to oxida-
tive distress, which eventually causes cellular damage and 
dysfunction. Interestingly, cancer cells exploit ROS produc-
tion to their advantage, where cells utilize ROS to modulate 
signaling pathways, increase proliferation, adapt to hypoxia, 
and develop chemotherapeutic resistance [7].
Together, these hallmarks allow cancer cells to develop 
and bypass normal growth controls, leading to uncontrolled 
cell division and tumor progression. As illustrated, the devel-
opment these hallmarks involves a complex mechanism that 
is regulated by multiple signaling pathways. Interestingly, 
germline mutations are a minor cause of all cancers, since 
the majority of tumors develop as a result of somatic muta-
tions and environmental factors. The environmental causes 
of cancer are usually associated with inflammation. Other 
factors such as chronic infections, smoking, inhaled pol -
lutants, and obesity, also contribute to cancer development 
[4]. Hence, profound understanding of cancer pathogenesis 
enables the development of effective treatment strategies by 
targeting these underlying mechanisms. Currently, the stand-
ard anticancer treatments rely on a combination of surgery, 
radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, where chemotherapy and 
radiotherapy primarily exert their effects by inducing DNA 
damage to cancer cells. However, these treatments also tar-
get healthy tissues, leading to DNA injury in the surrounding 
normal cells, resulting in systemic toxicities.

Other common side effects include the potential for can-
cer recurrence and the development of drug-resistant cancer 
cells, along with complications that are treatment-related 
secondary malignancies and organ-specific damage, such as 
nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, 
and ototoxicity [13, 14].
Besides the debilitating side effects of conventional 
therapies, multidrug resistance (MDR) of tumors accounts 
for nearly 90% of mortality in cancer patients treated with 
traditional chemotherapies or novel targeted drugs. MDR 
arises through multiple molecular mechanisms, including 
increased drug efflux, enhanced metabolism of drugs, and 
elevated DNA repair capacity, along with genetic mutations 
and epigenetic modifications. Collectively, these responses 
reduce the therapeutic effectiveness and substantially com-
plicate cancer treatment [15].
The predominant mechanism underlying MDR in can-
cer involves upregulation of 4 ATP-binding cassette (ABC) 
transporter proteins, such as p-glycoprotein (MDR1), 
multidrug resistance-related protein 1 (MRP1), multidrug 
resistance-related protein 2 (MRP2), and the breast cancer 
resistance protein (ABCG2). These efflux pumps actively 
expel diverse chemotherapeutic agents from tumors [16]. 
Additionally, upregulation of antiapoptotic proteins, Such as 
B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) and activation of pro-survival 
signaling pathways are also responsible for the development 
of MDR [17].
Consequently, current research studies are directed toward 
identifying optimal therapeutic agents that demonstrate both 
minimal adverse effects and maximal anticancer efficacy. 
Drug repurposing has emerged as an innovative strategy in 
this scope, offering a promising approach for anticancer drug 
discovery.
Drug Repurposing
Discovering and developing a new drug from initial research 
to regulatory approval is a tedious, expensive, and time-con-
suming journey. On average, this process could take 13 years 
with a cost that could reach about USD 2–3 billion to bring 
a new drug to the market. Therefore, the drug repurposing 
approach is a time-saving process in which drugs approved 
by regulatory agencies such as the Food and Drug Admin-
istration (FDA) are redeveloped and reused for new indi-
cations [2 ]. With MDR severely compromising treatment 
efficacy across cancer types, pharmaceutical companies are 
increasingly adopting drug repurposing strategies to identify 
novel anticancer agents capable of bypassing the mecha -
nisms of drug resistance. For instance, verapamil has been 
shown to act as a p-glycoprotein inhibitor that efficiently 
reverses drug resistance in ovarian cancer [17]. Similarly, 
cyclosporine A, a potent p-glycoprotein inhibitor, can effec-
tively overcome paclitaxel resistance in cancer cells with 
overexpression of p-glycoprotein [18]. Over the past decade, 
hundreds of drugs from various pharmacological classes 
have been explored for their potential anticancer properties. 
While some of these drugs have advanced into clinical tri -
als, others remain under investigation in preclinical studies.

These successful examples demonstrates the signifi-
cance of drug repurposing in modern drug development. 
Therefore, understanding the core principles that guide 
drug repurposing is essential. Typically, drug repurposing 
generally relies on two core principles. Either a drug effec-
tive against one disease may acquire therapeutic potential 
for other conditions due to common pathophysiological 
mechanisms between diseases, or a drug may interact with 
multiple targets or pathways through their off-target effects 
and multi-pathway modulation. As a result, identifying 
novel drug indications can emerge through either hypoth-
esis-driven or serendipitous discovery [83]. Among these, 
hypothesis-driven approaches have been widely investigated 
and employed as a promising repurposing method. These 
approaches involves experimental and computational strate-
gies, as illustrated in Fig. 2, which summarizes both strate-
gies along with the techniques used to achieve successful 
drug repurposing outcomes.
Starting with experimental strategies, where techniques 
such as binding assays and phenotypic screening are com-
monly used. Firstly, binding assays examine the interaction 
between ligands and target proteins by measuring changes 
in protein thermal stability or solubility upon ligand bind-
ing. Techniques such as cellular thermal shift assay, ther -
mal proteome profiling, and proteome integral solubility 
alteration (PISA) are widely used to identify protein targets 
for tested compounds [84, 85]. A prime example of this 
method is the use of the antiparasitic drug pyrimethamine 
for cancer therapy. Using the PISA technique, it was evident 
that pyrimethamine’s MoA is attributed to the inhibition of 
Human dihydrofolate reductase that eventually blocks the 
signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) 
pathway, which promotes tumorigenesis [85]. These results 
were revealed by the stabilization of the human dihydro-
folate reductase enzyme to heat denaturation, proving that 
this enzyme is the direct molecular target of the drug [85]. 
Pyrimethamine also significantly reduced ATP levels in 
cancer cells. Notably, the discovered anticancer mechanism 
of pyrimethamine primarily targets the cancer hallmarks of 
metabolic reprogramming and sustained growth signals [85].
On the other hand, phenotypic screening identifies com-
pounds by testing large compound libraries against bio-
logical systems, such as cell lines or animal models, where 
compound identification is based on the induction of pheno-
typic alterations in the cells. When the active compounds are 
identified, further studies are conducted to determine their

relevance to the disease state and to detect their underly -
ing MoA [84]. For example, a recent study performed high-
throughput phenotypic screening of 1,280 FDA-approved 
drugs against human colorectal cancer model. The results 
revealed that statins, particularly pitavastatin, exhibited 
the most potent anticancer effect. In order to validate these 
results, in vitro cytotoxicity assay was conducted which 
revealed that treatment with pitavastatin induced pro-inflam-
matory gene expression within the tumor, promoting antitu-
mor immunity [86].
By combining phenotypic screening and binding assay, 
researchers can identify potential drug candidates for repur-
posing, thereby accelerating and improving the overall drug 
discovery process [87].
Currently, conventional drug repurposing research pri-
marily aims to identify the MoA similarities and drug effect; 
discover new indications of drugs by screening the current 
pharmacopeia against novel biological targets; analyze com-
mon drug properties, including molecular structure and 
adverse effects; or elucidate the relationship between drugs 
and diseases [83]. In contrast, many repurposing techniques 
lack disease specificity, as they depend on drug mechanism 
and pathway data obtained from various biological frame-
works, where information may not always be available for 
relevant drugs [88]. This methodological variability often 
leads to inconsistencies in the reported findings across dif-
ferent studies.
To address these limitations, computational repurposing 
strategies are rapidly emerging due to the recent expansion 
of publicly available large-scale biomedical, biological, and 
electronic health-related data, combined with the enhanced 
high-performance computing capabilities [83].

article implemented a ML model using 2854 compounds

using a methodical approach. The initial phase involved data 
preparation and collection, including the retrieval, organiza-

and testing of ML models.

 IC50 values [96]. This curation was essential, as establishing

the ChemSAR standardization tool is employed for optimi-

disconnecting metals, application of normalization rules, 
reionization of acids, and recalculating stereochemistry of 
the compounds [96]. Consequently, chemical structures are

tors. These descriptors, as PaDEL descriptor, encode a com-
pound's physicochemical properties (structural, topological, 
geometrical, and electrochemical) to make chemical struc-
tures quantifiable for computational analysis and predictive 
modeling [97].

predictive variables and excluding irrelevant ones. This 
process decreases overfitting and enhances computational 
efficiency by conserving predictability time [97]. Among the 
feature selection methods that the authors implemented was 
recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) 
owing to its high precision. RFECV identifies the most sig-
nificant and relevant features based on the level of correla-
tion calculated between the variables and the target [96].

to ML model development. In this framework, predictive ML 
models are trained on curated datasets of active compounds 
and their properties, enabling the prediction of activity for

(LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM),

Nanotechnology in Cancer
Since cancer is a complex multifactorial condition with 
genetic, molecular, and cellular origins, it necessitates 
innovative diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for effec-
tive treatment. Nanotechnology-based medicine emerges 
as a promising solution along with the evolution of drug 
repurposing, offering novel drug delivery systems, multi-
functional diagnostic tools, and therapeutic agents to combat 
cancer more effectively. Nanomedicine employs nanotech-
nology to prevent and treat diseases using nanoscale mate-
rials, such as biocompatible nanoparticles (NPs) for dif-
ferent applications, such as drug delivery and diagnostics 
[101]. NPs acquire a size range between 1 and 1000 nm, 
which provides a unique physicochemical property owing 
to their small size and large surface area.
In recent years, biocompatible NPs have been imple-
mented in cancer treatment research to mitigate the con-
straints of conventional therapies, including low drug solu-
bility, poor bioavailability, limited tumor localization, and 
lack of tissue specificity. These NPs offer multiple advan-
tages for anticancer drug delivery, including rapid therapeu-
tic onset while sustaining drug release, prolonging tumor 
retention time, and enhancing stability in biological fluids. 
Furthermore, NPs facilitate targeted cellular delivery and 
help alleviate drug resistance [3, 102]. Studies revealed that 
some drug delivery systems as organic-based NPs, have 
the potential to overcome p-glycoprotein-mediated efflux 
through their capability to bypass and inhibit p-glycoprotein 
[103].
Building on these advances and the growing emphasis 
on personalized medicine, research is currently focused on 
engineering diverse types of NPs to tailor treatments to meet 
individual patient needs. Notably, the following types of 
NPs currently show the greatest clinical potential for cancer 
therapy:
1. Organic NPs
Organic NPs, classified into lipid-based and polymeric 
nanomaterials, are among the most widely utilized nanocar-
riers for cancer treatment. Lipid-based NPs include a range 
of various structures such as liposomes, niosomes, nano-
structured lipid carriers, solid lipid NPs, and lipid nanoemul-
sions. In contrast, polymeric NPs are either formulated using 
of synthetic biodegradable polymers such as polylactide-co-
glycolide (PLGA), polylactic acid (PLA), polycaprolactone 
(PCL), and polyethylene glycol (PEG), or natural proteins 
like gelatin and albumin [104].
Notably, several lipid and polymer-based NPs received 
regulatory approvals and currently used in treating differ -
ent types of cancer. For instance, lipid NPs such as Doxil® 
(PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin) and DaunoXome® 
(non-PEGylated liposomal daunorubicin), in which both are 
used for treating AIDS-related Kaposi's sarcoma. Myocet® 
(non-PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin) is another exam -
ple, approved in Europe and Canada is used for metastatic 
breast cancer treatment [102]. Regarding polymeric NPs, 
Abraxane, an albumin encapsulating paclitaxel NPs, is cur-
rently approved for the treatment of metastatic breast cancer 
[44]. These successful examples highlight the significance 
of nanocarriers in cancer therapy by providing improved 
therapeutic efficacy with minimal side effects. Building upon 
these clinically approved nanomedicines, recent research 
has investigated the encapsulation of repurposed pharma -
ceuticals into NPs, emphasizing their potential to improve 
therapeutic outcomes in a safe and selective manner. For 
instance, loading phenelzine into liposomes reduced its sys-
temic side effects due to limited off-target distribution of the 
drug, while simultaneously enhancing tumor permeability 
and providing a targeted drug delivery [100]. Additionally, 
fexofenadine encapsulated in albumin NPs improved drug 
delivery through the enhanced permeability and retention 
(EPR) effect, owing to its small size and prolonged residence 
time within the tumors, which is attributed to the controlled 
drug release, thereby enhancing its anticancer potential [44]. 
Moreover, encapsulating albendazole in zein NPs showed 
greater solubility and bioavailability, which improved the 
drug’s therapeutic efficacy [105]. The enhanced solubil-
ity profile of albendazole is attributed to the hydrophobic 
regions of zein, which facilitate the encapsulation of lipo -
philic drugs. Similarly, it has been reported that zein NPs 
overcome MDR in cancer cells via modulating efflux pump 
activity, which in turn augmented the overall anticancer 
effect of drug-loaded zein NPs [47].
These results demonstrate how the physicochemical char-
acteristics of nanocarriers, along with the synergistic effects 
of integrated polymers and enhanced drug targeting, can 
significantly improve the therapeutic efficacy of repurposed 
drugs. The discussion section provides additional detailed 
examples of these applications.
2. Inorganic NPs
The current trend in nanomedicine development focuses 
on integrating therapeutic, diagnostic, and targeting capabili-
ties into a single nanoplatform [106]. These ‘all-in-one’ nan-
oplatforms can combine molecular imaging contrast agents 
with therapeutic agents, enabling simultaneous diagnosis 
and treatment. This concept has given the rise to nanothera-
nostics, where a single hybrid nanostructure is capable of 
performing diagnostic and therapeutic functions, including

237 Page 12 of 32
combinations such as chemo-photothermal, chemo-photo-
dynamic, and photothermal-photodynamic therapies [107].
Among various nanomaterials, inorganic NPs show great 
promise as an anticancer agent, particularly in nanothera-
nostics, due to their unique features such as precise target-
ing, thermostability, unique optical properties, ultra-small 
size, large surface area, controllable physical shape, and 
magnetic-responsive properties. Inorganic NPs are primar -
ily composed of metals, metal oxides, carbon-based mate-
rials, and mesoporous silica. Common examples of inor -
ganic NPs include gold, silver, iron oxide, and zinc oxide 
NPs, as well as quantum and carbon dots [106, 108]. The 
morphology of NPs critically influences these properties, 
as inorganic NPs exist in various shapes, including porous 
structures and nanorods, unlike organic NPs that are usually 
spherical [108]. Moreover, due to their potential toxicity, 
inorganic NPs are often coated with biocompatible polymers 
to improve their safety profile [108]. Notably, a wide range 
of inorganic NPs has been explored for their nanotheranos-
tic potential in cancer treatment. Among them, superpara -
magnetic iron oxide NPs (SPIONs) have gained significant 
interest for their magnetic hyperthermia effect, where local-
ized heat is generated when exposed to an oscillating mag-
netic field. Since tumors acquire low tolerance to moderate 
temperature elevations (42–49 °C), this localized heating 
can effectively induce cancer cell death [109]. Integrating 
this technology with the concept of drug repurposing could 
provide a promising approach to improve cancer therapy 
using a single nanoformulation, as illustrated by ferumoxy-
tol. Ferumoxytol is an FDA-approved ultra-small SPOIN 
with an iron oxide core coated with dextran. Ferumoxytol is 
originally used to treat iron deficiency anemia in the United 
States; however, recent studies revealed that it exhibits anti-
leukemic effects both in vitro and in vivo, primarily through 
ferroptosis. Specifically, ferumoxytol exerts its anti-leuke-
mic effects by targeting cancer cells that express low levels 
of ferroportin, leading to increased intracellular iron accu-
mulation and subsequent cell death. This cytotoxic effect 
is attributed to the composition of the NPs, which triggers 
oxidative stress through the excessive ROS production due 
to the presence of iron species, which results in cell death 
[110–112]. The MoA of the drug effectively targets the can-
cer hallmark, avoidance of apoptosis.
Although clinical trials for leukemia treatment have 
not yet been conducted, ferumoxytol's application extends 
beyond its anticancer properties. Notably, recent studies 
have investigated the diagnostic potential of ferumoxytol, 
with ongoing clinical studies assessing its usage as a mag-
netic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agent to evaluate 
the early tumor response in glioblastoma patients who are 
undergoing radiotherapy and temozolomide treatment [113]. 
Ferumoxytol represents a successful example of a nanothera-
nostic formulation in drug repurposing, demonstrating both 
therapeutic efficacy and diagnostic capability within a single 
nanocarrier.
Overall, various types of NPs have been studied over the 
years to find an optimal nanosystem that combines good 
physicochemical features, therapeutic efficacy, and safety 
for cancer treatment. Building on this foundation, the com-
bination of drug-loaded nanocarriers and drug repurpos-
ing techniques, powered by a thorough comprehension of 
cancer biology and its distinguishing characteristics, offers 
a promising strategy that could address the therapeutic chal-
lenges in oncology. Herein, this review highlights the cur -
rent advancements in cancer therapy, including the appli-
cation of nanotechnology and drug repurposing to target 
specific cancer pathways for effective and precise tumor 
elimination. With a critical point of view, different types of 
NPs are evaluated by comparing findings from various stud-
ies, focusing on their anticancer properties, targeting mech-
anisms, and physicochemical characteristics. Ultimately, the 
goal of this narrative review is to highlight promising drug-
loaded NPs and potential therapeutic strategies that may 
improve cancer treatment outcomes by critically analyzing 
current literature.
Methods
This narrative review is conducted in accordance with Pre-
ferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-
Analyses (PRISMA) framework guidelines for analyzing the 
selected articles. Firstly, the search methodology was based 
on searching electronic databases such as PubMed, Science-
Direct, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Google Scholar.
The search included publications from 2010 to 2025, 
using the following keywords: ‘drug repurposing’, ‘nano-

‘antidepressant as anticancer’, ‘antiparasitic as anticancer’,

were downloaded. Any duplicated articles were removed 
using Mendeley Reference Manager software. Furthermore, 
abstracts and conclusions were screened to select the articles 
that aligned with the purpose of the review.
The inclusion criteria involved the following:
• Most recent research articles between the years 2018 to 
2025.
• Articles published in the scope of health sciences and 
pharmaceutical innovation emphasizing the treatment of 
cancer using drug repurposing approach.
• Drugs discussed should be incorporated in or used with 
a nanocarrier.

Discussion
During the last decade, different classes of small drug mol-
ecules have been explored for potential anticancer effects. 
Among them are antidepressants, antihistamines, antihyper-
tensives, antidiabetics, antihyperlipidemics, and antipara -
sitics. Several drugs belonging to these classes have been 
reported to acquire antitumor effects against several cell 
lines. This section demonstrates case studies of different 
drugs, such as Phenelzine, Fexofenadine, Losartan, Tel-
misartan, Metformin, Canagliflozin, Atorvastatin, and Fen-
bendazole, highlighting the recent insights on experimental 
drug repurposing approach. Each case is analyzed to explore 
the impact of encapsulating these drugs into different types 
of NPs, emphasizing how nanoformulation enhances their 
therapeutic potential for cancer treatment [ 44, 47, 59, 99, 
100, 114–116].
Repurposing of Antidepressants
Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) such as phenelzine 
are responsible for blocking monoamine oxidase-A (MAO-
A) enzyme that increases serotonin levels in the brain for 
the treatment of depression and neurological diseases like 
Parkinson’s disease. Recently, researchers reported that 
serotonin can modulate immune cell function by activating 
serotonin receptors on T cells, thereby enhancing its anti-
tumor effect [ 100]. Notably, MAOIs are capable of reduc-
ing tumor-associated macrophages (TAM), which promote 
tumorigenesis and metastasis, as well as the secretion of 
anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10. This environ -
ment enhances the development of Treg cells, which inhib-
its excessive immune response [117]. Additionally, immune 
checkpoint proteins as PD-L1 are overexpressed on TAM, 
resulting in the suppression of cytotoxic T lymphocyte activ-
ity, thereby promoting resistance to immunotherapy [100, 
118]. Thus, by depolarizing TAMs (modifying the differ -
entiation of macrophage subtypes), MAOIs act as potential 
TME-modulating agents, creating a more favorable environ-
ment for antitumor immune responses, including immune 
checkpoint blockade (ICB). While phenelzine emerges as a 
promising immunotherapeutic agent, its systemic adminis-
tration at therapeutic doses can induce aggressive behavior, 
presenting a significant clinical challenge [100].
Hence, to mitigate the neuronal adverse effects of phen-
elzine, improve drug efficacy, and achieve targeted delivery 
to cancer cells, a recent study suggested the encapsulation of 
phenelzine within crosslinked multilamellar liposomal vesi-
cles (cMLVs-PZ) on a mouse melanoma model (B16-OVA) 
(Table  IV). This multi-layered crosslinking architecture 
provides a substantial potential as drug carriers due to the 
improved serum stability, efficient cellular drug uptake, and 
ultimately targeting the delivery of phenelzine to malignant 
cells [100 ]. This study primarily focuses on investigating 
these aspects of the NPs along with evaluating the develop-
ment of aggression in the mouse model through a series of 
characterization tests as follows:
Preparation and Characterization of the Proposed 
NPs
Initially, the authors fabricated cMLVs-PZ NPs using thin 
film hydration method followed by PEGylation of the vesi-
cle. The resultant NPs acquired a size of 220 nm, which 
facilitates NPs transportation to tumor cells via the EPR 
effect. Also, a high entrapment efficiency (EE%) was 
observed, reaching 85%, suggesting a sufficient amount of 
the drug encapsulated to be delivered to the target site [100].
The Safety and Efficacy of the Prepared NPs
To evaluate the therapeutic activity of cMLVs-PZ NPs, the 
authors examined the reduction in tumor size in the B16-
OVA model following treatment with the proposed NPs 
compared to the free drug solution. While the administration 
of the free drug resulted in a reduction in tumor size, treat-
ment with cMLVs-PZ NPs produced a significantly greater 
decrease in the size of the tumor, demonstrating an enhanced 
antitumor effect of cMLVs-PZ NPs. This finding is attrib-
uted to the targeted delivery of phenelzine to TME and the 
stimulation of immune cells within the tumor [100]. Besides 
the potent anticancer effects of cMLVs-PZ NPs, the safety 
profile of the drug is a crucial aspect in drug delivery. Thus, 
the authors investigated the neurological impact of cMLVs-
PZ NPs by measuring phenelzine’s level in the mouse brain. 
The results showed that the treatment with cMLVs-PZ NPs 
resulted in a minimal amount of the drug reaching the brain, 
compared to the free drug, where a higher amount of the 
drug was found in the mouse brain. These findings sup-
port the hypothesis that cMLVs-PZ NPs limited phenelzine 
delivery to the brain while preferentially delivering it to 
the tumor. This targeted delivery can be attributed to the

it has been reported that FEXO enhances T cell antitumor 
efficacy via decreasing the number of immunosuppres-

promotes the expression of cytokines as IL-10 and TGF-β,

cancer therapy as it co-delivers PD-L1 aptamer with FEXO 
in a single nanocarrier, such as albumin NPs (PDL1-FEXO 
NP). This nanosystem targets different pathways for cancer 
therapy while taking the benefits of loading the drug into 
albumin NPs, such as ultra-small size, excellent biocompat-

effect that further promote the anticancer effect of the drug 
[44]. To confirm such properties, the authors subjected the

Repurposing of Antihypertensives
Different classes of antihypertensive drugs have been 
explored for their potential antitumor effect. Among them 
are angiotensin II (ANG II) receptor antagonists as telmisar-
tan and losartan [99]. To begin with, the renin–angioten-
sin–aldosterone system (RAAS) is a complex hormonal 
system that plays a crucial role in maintaining hormone 
release, cardiovascular homeostasis, and cellular prolifera-
tion. Clearly, ANG II is the key component of the RAAS, 
produced by the action of angiotensin-converting enzyme 
(ACE). It exerts its effects primarily through binding to the 
angiotensin type 1  (AT1) receptor, in which its stimulation 
leads to vasoconstriction, harmful tissue remodeling, and 
induction of oxidative stress. Recent evidence shows that 
tumor cells in various tissues express ANG II receptors as 
lung, thyroid, breast, stomach, colorectal, and prostate can-
cer. It’s worth noting that ANG II has been recently found to 
act as a growth factor and enhances angiogenesis by activat-
ing VEGF expression through stimulation of  AT1, promoting 
cancer cell proliferation. Therefore, blocking ANG II using 
 AT1 receptor antagonists (ARBs) could serve as a potential 
antitumor therapeutic strategy [119].
Besides the aforementioned anticancer pathway of ANG 
II blockers, another approach has been examined to target 
cancer cells by activating the proliferator-activated receptor 
gamma (PPARγ), a nuclear receptor and transcription fac-
tor that regulates gene expression [99]. Normally, PPARγ 
plays a key role in several physiological and pathological 
processes, including lipid and glucose metabolism, inflam-
mation, and cellular differentiation. Also, PPARγ is involved 
in modulating inflammatory and immune responses [120].
In the context of cancer, PPARγ activation has been 
shown to directly interfere with critical signaling pathways 
or to enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to radiotherapy, 
thereby inhibiting tumor growth. Additionally, evidence 
indicates that the activation of PPARγ results in tumor sup-
pression by upregulating anti-inflammatory mediators and 
inducing apoptosis. Thus, PPARγ modulators have been 
used in combination with other drugs to produce synergis-
tic therapeutic effects. However, the function of PPARγ in 
cancer is multifaceted, as its activity may, in certain circum-
stances, facilitate tumorigenesis, depending upon the cancer 
type and microenvironment [120]. Thus, PPAR-γ activators 
as telmisartan have been explored for their potential anti-
tumor action. In 2014, a study proved that telmisartan was 
responsible for the upregulation of PPARγ expression in 
lung cancer cell line, leading to receptor activation, which 
in turn inhibited cell growth. The cytotoxic and antiprolif-
erative properties of telmisartan were reduced when PPARγ 
activity was blocked using a PPARγ antagonist, which con-
firmed that telmisartan’s MoA in the lung cancer cell line is 
mediated through PPARγ activation [121]. Recently, several 
studies examined the impact of loading telmisartan in NPs 
on its anticancer effect.

to exert its action [99].
Moreover, a drug release profile was performed to assess 
the quantity of the drug released from the nanoformulation 
at the target site. The test was conducted using physiological 
pH (pH 7.4) and acidic pH (pH 5.5 to simulate TME). It was

82% of the drug released over 50 h. However, over the same 
period, only 25% of the drug was released at pH 7.4, showing 
the high stability of the proposed NPs at physiological pH. 
Such difference in the amount of drug released is attributed 
to the swelling of chitosan in acidic pH and the protonation 
of amino groups. This is also confirmed using Fourier Trans-
form Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), which revealed that at 
acidic pH, hydrolysis of the amide bond between chitosan’s 
amine group and the carboxylic group of the drug facilitates 
its release in the tumor. On the other hand, the amide bond 
remained intact at pH 7.4, confirming its stability in the blood 
circulation and very low drug release. Last but not least, mag-
netization analysis confirmed that the magnetic NPs retained 
their magnetic properties after chitosan coating, suggesting 
their suitability for targeted drug delivery applications [99].

To determine the efficacy of TEL-MNP-CS on prostate can-
cer cells, a cytotoxic assay was conducted. In comparison to 
the free drug solution, it was shown that TEL-MNP-CS had 
the highest cytotoxicity compared to the free drug, indicat-
ing targeted drug delivery with the aid of pH-responsive 
magnetic NPs. The high cytotoxic effect observed is attrib-
uted to the prolonged drug release in the tumor’s acidic envi-
ronment [99].
Ultimately, this investigation demonstrates the successful 
repurposing of telmisartan using an experimental approach. 
Also, the study suggests that the anticancer MoA of TEL-
MNP-CS may be attributed to PPARγ activation which may 
result in targeting cancer hallmarks such as tumor-promoting 
inflammation and avoidance of apoptosis. Employing pH-
responsive magnetic NPs-based was significant, as it offers 
major benefits to enhance telmisartan’s delivery to tumors 
through its targeted and controlled drug release in the acidic 
environment of the tumors. This targeted drug release and 
high cytotoxicity is attributed to the chitosan coating and the 
magnetic properties of the NPs. Nonetheless, a significant 
gap in this research lies in the absence of in vivo  studies, 
which are vital for monitoring the drug's behavior within the 
biological milieu. Furthermore, while the study suggests that 
the anticancer MoA of TEL-MNP-CS may be attributed to 
PPARγ, direct experimental evidence confirming the PPARγ 
stimulation within the cellular context is necessary, as it was 
not clearly illustrated by the authors.
Losartan Combined Mesoporous Platinum NPs
Continuing with experimental of repurposing antihyperten-

acquire antiproliferative effects through immunomodula-

Hashemzehi et al. [32] confirmed losartan’s ability to hin-

an increase in the number of CRC cells arrested in the G1

on the inflammatory response, specifically by increasing the 
production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines

article addresses a potent cancer treatment strategy using 
3 different aspects. Firstly, using losartan to decrease the 
tumor’s extracellular matrix, which is abundant in fibroblasts 
and collagen that act as a barrier for drug transportation to 
solid tumors as Neuro-2a. This is attributed to losartan’s 
ability to modulate both collagen and fibroblasts, thereby

subsequent cell cycle disruption [47].
Overall, Elmahboub et al.’s [47] work demonstrated the

studies are crucial to pave the way for the potential clinical 
translation of metformin-zein NPs as a novel cancer therapy.

Repurposing of Antihyperlipidimics
Statins are used primarily to treat hypercholesterolemia; 
however, they have been widely reported to acquire 
significant anticancer effects. One of the recently pub-
lished articles addresses the repurposing of atorvastatin 
to treat breast cancer. Originally, atorvastatin exerts its 
action by suppressing hydroxymethylglutaryl-coenzyme 
A (HMG-CoA) reductase, which inhibits the production 
of the precursors of cholesterol synthesis, mevalonate. 
Regarding cancer, atorvastatin inhibits cellular prolifera-
tion and induces apoptosis via inhibiting the production 
of mevalonate, which inhibits tumor growth, as well as 
reducing PD-L1 expression within tumor extracellular 
vesicles. This leads to an enhanced antitumor immune 
response, characterized by increased activity of  CD8+T 
cells,  CD4+T helper cells and decreased activity of Treg 
cells [59].
Lipophilic statins in particular possess a great feature, 
imparted by their high hydrophobicity, that enables them to 
easily penetrate cancer cell membrane. This feature has been 
hypothetically linked to the high cytotoxicity of lipophilic 
statins as atorvastatin against tumors. Based on this con-
cept, the authors encapsulated atorvastatin in solid lipid NPs 
conjugated with lactoferrin (LF-ATR-SLN NPs) as an anti-
cancer targeting moiety (Table IV). Conjugating lactoferrin 
enhances the NPs’ internalization within the tumor, since it 
interacts with low-density lipoprotein receptor-related pro-
teins and negatively charged glycosaminoglycans, facilitat-
ing the uptake by tumor cells and drug delivery. Besides, 
lactoferrin exhibits a potent antitumor effect through its 
immunomodulating properties, as it was evident that it 
suppresses Tregs cells and TAM, while simultaneously 
boosting cytotoxic T cells, helper T cells, and natural killer 
cells, strengthening the immune response against the tumor. 
Furthermore, lactoferrin can alter the microbiome within 
the TME, hindering tumor growth, invasion, and metasta-
sis [59]. Thus, such a combination in one nanoformulation 
proposes a strong treatment approach, in which the authors 
deeply investigated as shown below.
Fabrication of LF‑ATR‑SNL NPs
Firstly, solid lipid NPs were fabricated using modified hot 
high shear homogenization technique in which atorvastatin 
was added to the lipid mixture. Following the formation of 
the loaded NPs, lactoferrin is then mixed with the result-
ant solution to form LF-ATR-SLN NPs. Consequently, in 
vitro characterization was performed, and results revealed 
a smooth surface of NPs with no aggregation. Regarding 
the drug’s encapsulation, no crystalline structures have been 
observed, suggesting successful encapsulation of the drug in 
the molecular state. The EE% also confirmed a high amount 
of drug encapsulation, reaching 94%. This high value is 
Suggested to be attributed to the large Lipid content in the 
NPs, which improved drug loading. Furthermore, PS and 
ZP acquired values of 168 nm and − 7.05 mV, respectively. 
PS values are small enough to allow efficient penetration of 
tumor cells, while the ZP indicates stable dispersion of the 
particles in the system.
In vitro release profile was conducted to ensure Success-
ful drug release at the target site in a Sufficient amount. It 
was found that LF-ATR-SLN NPs released the drug in a 
biphasic fashion, with an initial rapid release in the first 5 h, 
while in 24 h the drug was released in a more Sustained 
pattern, reaching 79%. The percentage of drug release in 
24 h have been compared between the decorated NPs and 
undecorated ones, in which LF-ATR-SLN NPs showed to 
release a lower amount of drug, which might be attributed 
to the shielding of the surface NPs by lactoferrin, slightly 
hindering drug release [59].

been confirmed by cytotoxicity assay, which showed that 
LF-ATR-SLN NPs produced the most pronounced tumor 
growth inhibition of the breast cancer cell line among all 
other formulations (undecorated NPs and free drug). This 
high cytotoxic effect is attributed to the targeting ligand 
present on the NP’s surface that promoted its cellular inter-
nalization via ligand-receptor interaction. To further verify 
this, the authors used confocal laser scanning microscopy 
(CLSM) to visualize the cellular internalization of LF-ATR-
SLN NPs. Results revealed that lactoferrin-decorated NPs 
exhibited the most intense fluorescence signals in compari-
son to the undecorated NPs and the free dye. This indicated 
that the targeting moiety indeed increased the cellular inter-
nalization of the NPs [59].

and inhibition of mevalonate production. Drug encapsulation 
in solid lipid NPs decorated with lactoferrin demonstrated a 
further increase in the drug’s therapeutic efficacy, as revealed 
in the pronounced reduction in tumor volume and growth

lactoferrin itself acquires a potent anticancer effect through its

 CD8+ T cells,  CD4+ T cells, and PD-L1 expression. Further-

media that simulates the TME is crucial to fully comprehend 
the release pattern of the drug within the tumor.
Repurposing of Antiparasitics
Among the most commonly used antiparasitics are alben-
dazole, mebendazole, and fenbendazole. Their MoA relies 
on disrupting microtubule function in cytoplasm in both 
parasites and mammalian cells. They achieve this by inhib-
iting the polymerization of β-tubulin, a key component of 
microtubules. This disruption subsequently interferes with 
glucose uptake and transport within the cell, leading to gly-
cogen depletion, impeding cell mitosis [123]. The antitumor 
effects of these drugs is exerted through disrupting glucose 
metabolism, leading to cell death. Recently, albendazole and 
mebendazole have been shown to inhibit tumor growth in in 
vitro studies and animal models. This has led to their repur-
posing as potential anticancer agents [124].
Several studies have been published demonstrating the 
antiproliferative activity of albendazole-loaded NPs. For 
instance, albumin NPs were developed to enhance the solu-
bility and systemic anticancer effects of albendazole against 
ovarian cancer cells. These drug-loaded NPs demonstrated 
significantly higher cytotoxicity in ovarian cancer cells com-
pared to normal cells. Similarly, another study showed that 
encapsulating albendazole in mesoporous silica NPs dra-
matically induced high cytotoxicity against hepatocellular 
carcinoma and cervical cancer [105, 125]. The entrapment 
of the drug in nanosystems has been shown to significantly 
improve the solubility profile of albendazole, which in turn 
enhances its bioavailability. A recently published study also 
employed a similar strategy, in which the authors utilized 
zein as a cargo material for albendazole on a CRC cell line, 
where the NPs exhibited a strong antitumor effect [105]. 
Similarly, mebendazole has also been loaded into differ -
ent types of NPs; among them are the folic acid decorated-
chitosan NPs compressed into a biocompatible implant for 
treatment of triple-negative breast cancer. Folic acid deco-
ration is used to target breast cancer cells due to the over -
expression of folate receptors on tumors. Such an approach 
showed a pronounced tumor growth inhibition and metas-
tasis compared to unloaded NPs and drug solution. Impor -
tantly, the proposed NPs alleviated the hepatotoxic effects of 
mebendazole that have been observed upon oral administra-
tion, while maintaining a potent therapeutic activity in vivo  
[126]. This confirms the enhanced anticancer effects of the 
implant system while reducing the side effects of the drug.

Current Challenges and Future Perspective
Despite the major advantages proposed by drug repurpos-
ing, obstacles still persist. One key limitation is that certain 
repurposing techniques may lack disease specificity due to 
limited or irrelevant mechanistic and pathway data obtained 
[88]. Moreover, the difficulty in identifying drug-target inter-
action due to limited availability of high-resolution drug-
target structural data is a challenge for drug repurposing 
techniques, such as molecular docking. Another significant 
challenge is the variation in experimental conditions across 
studies, which can lead to biased or non-reproducible data, 
especially when dealing with gene expression signature data 
[83].
In addition, issues regarding selecting the optimum dos-
ing require extensive investigations, as the dosage used for 
the original indication may differ from that required for the 
new therapeutic use. Therefore, establishing a dose–response 
relationship investigations are required before proceeding to 
clinical trials [130].
In parallel with the challenges associated with drug repur-
posing, the successful application of nanocarrier systems 
also faces significant issues, particularly regarding their 
safety, stability, and scalability, all of which are critical for 
successful clinical translation. It has been reported that long-
term exposure to NPs has been associated with oxidative 
stress, inflammatory response, and cytotoxicity to normal 
cells [131]. The key factors contributing to NP’s toxicity 
include the type of NPs, surface charge, PS, and morphol-
ogy. For instance, organic NPs exhibit excellent biodegra-
dability and limited toxicity compared to inorganic NPs, 
specifically, metal-based NPs, which, upon their prolonged 
exposure, could induce genotoxicity and inflammation [132]. 
In addition, positively charged NPs demonstrate an enhanced 
cellular uptake due to the strong electrostatic interactions 
with the cell membrane. However, cationic liposomes, for 
instance, may cause cytotoxicity through organ accumula-
tion and generation of ROS [133]. Also, positively charged 
NPs can also interact with the DNA inside the cell, resulting 
in DNA damage [134]. Moreover, maintaining optimal PS 
is vital, as large particles could trigger an immune response, 
while small NP size (below 100 nm) has a higher surface 
area that can interact with cellular components and accu-
mulate in non-target tissues and organs, such as the liver 
or lungs, eventually causing organ toxicity [131, 135]. The 
morphology of the NPs also plays a significant role in their 
toxicity, as different shapes, particularly non-spherical 
forms, can exhibit altered clearance rates and tissue pen-
etration, resulting in accumulation in various organs and an 
increased risk of chronic toxicity [135]. Thus, maintaining 
optimum physicochemical properties of the NPs could pre-
vent long-term toxicity.
Beyond the safety and toxicity concerns, another major 
challenge lies in scaling up laboratory production of nano-
carriers for industrial manufacturing. Given that nanoformu-
lations are delicate and require highly controlled process-
ing, can pose a significant barrier for clinical translation and 
market approval. For example, laboratory-scale production

methods of NPs, such as thin film hydration is challenging 
to be employed due to inefficient hydration of large lipid 
films. In contrast, methods such as ethanol injection and 
homogenization techniques can be scaled up; however, care-
ful optimization of the process is critical to prevent overheat-
ing, foaming, and degradation, as slight alterations in mix-
ing time, temperature, or composition can cause significant 
batch-to-batch variability in the NP’s physical properties and 
therapeutic efficacy [136]. Currently, microfluidic technol-
ogy offers a more reliable and continuous solution to tradi-
tional production methods, owing to the precise control over 
process parameters [137]. Moreover, batch reproducibility 
is an essential aspect of industrial production, and since 
NP’s production involves strict processing, manufacturing 
a single large batch to supply the market over an extended 
period may improve batch reproducibility but requires pre-
cise production. Also, statistical process control, in which 
data from each batch is analyzed to identify trends, enables 
early detection of deviations and allows for timely correc-
tions to maintain consistency [136].
Furthermore, maintaining the stability of nanocarriers is 
a major hurdle, as formulations such as liposomes are sus-
ceptible to physical and chemical instability, including drug 
leakage, PS enlargement, and lipid oxidation, which limits 
their shelf life [138]. Thus, stabilizers are often added to 
nanoformulations to prolong their shelf life. Additionally, 
freeze-drying with cryoprotectants is sometimes employed 
to improve the formulations’ stability [136, 138]. In addi-
tion, these sensitive NPs will also require temperature con-
trol throughout the supply chain, as nanoformulations must 
be stored under refrigeration (2–8 °C) to maintain stability 
over their shelf life [136].
In addition to the challenges related to NPs’ production, 
ensuring the sterility of nanoformulations presents another 
crucial step in the production process. For example, filtra-
tion of highly concentrated or large NPs could cause filters to 
clog; hence, NPs are often produced under aseptic conditions 
to avoid the need for terminal sterile filtration [136]. Moreover, 
monitoring solvent residues and endotoxin levels is essential 
for the industry to adhere to Good Manufacturing Practice. 
Consequently, this complex, multistep manufacture increases 
the production costs compared to conventional drug manufac-
ture, placing an additional financial burden on pharmaceuti-
cal companies. Furthermore, these production challenges are 
combined with strict regulatory requirements. For example, 
the FDA requires extensive and thorough characterization of 
NPs, including, PS, release kinetics, drug loading, stability, 
and batch-to batch reproducibility, before granting the nano-
formulation’s approval [136]. Nevertheless, this approach 
may fail to account for the pharmacokinetic and pharmaco-
dynamic data of the nanomedicine, which can result in incon-
sistent evaluation of safety and efficacy [135]. In contrast, the 
European Medicines Agency focuses on the safety and quality 
of nanomedicine, including ensuring the safety of any novel 
excipient used along with product quality validation [136].
In order to improve the overall production process and 
safety of NPs and facilitate regulatory approval, artificial 
intelligence (AI) and ML are now being investigated in 
advanced stages of nanomedicine development, includ-
ing NPs manufacturing, process optimization, and toxicity 
prediction. Currently, AI and ML models can be employed 
to identify the correlation between the characteristics of 
nanomaterials and their toxicity [139]. Regarding industrial 
manufacture, using AI models in NPs production could opti-
mize process parameters in terms of NPs’ shape, PS, release 
profiles, and drug loading. Also, the integration of robotics 
to automate NPs manufacture, through precise control of 
chemical conditions, temperature, pressure, and reaction 
time, has become crucial. This automation improves preci-
sion and efficiency by reducing the need for manual inter -
vention [140]. In addition, using specific algorithms, these 
technologies are capable of identifying optimal synthesis 
pathways, predicting the properties of the NPs produced, 
and adjusting parameters as necessary [140]. This empha-
sizes the importance of integrating AI into NPs’ production 
in order to achieve cost-effective and sustainable industrial 
manufacturing.
Finally, AI integration in drug development and produc-
tion represents a transformative strategy in oncology, where 
computational insights directly drive the design of precision 
nanomedicines, paving the way for smart nanotherapeutics 
manufacturing. However, AI and ML models exhibit some 
drawbacks that could limit their use. For instance, data het-
erogeneity, quality issues, limited data sharing by pharma-
ceutical companies, concerns over data privacy and patient 
information protection, as well as issues related to model 
interpretability and cybersecurity in AI-based systems.
To address these barriers, extensive research is necessary 
to train ML models, develop algorithms that produce more 
precise and reliable predictions, and enhance data protection 
and model security [139, 141]. Overcoming these obstacles 
will bridge the computational approach with nanomedicine 
innovations in several aspects of NPs production [139, 141]. 
To sum up, the integration of ML and AI across the early 
and advanced phases of drug repurposing, from initial drug 
screening and mechanism of action prediction to NPs design, 
large-scale manufacturing, and regulatory approval, repre-
sents a transformative and ambitious pipeline for accelerat-
ing drug development and clinical translation.
Conclusion
An emerging cancer treatment approach such as drug 
repurposing is revolutionizing cancer therapy by using 
FDA-approved drugs such as phenelzine, fexofenadine,

telmisartan, losartan, metformin, canagliflozin, atorvasta-
tin, and fenbendazole, as antitumor agents loaded in tailored 
nanocarriers. Such an approach synergistically reduces sys-
temic toxicity and enhances therapeutic efficacy, as shown in 
the phenelzine-loaded crosslinked multilamellar liposomes 
study, in which drug encapsulation eliminated its neurologi-
cal side effects.
Moreover, ligand-targeted NPs demonstrate exceptional 
precision in drug delivery, which leads to potent antican-
cer effects. For example, lactoferrin-conjugated solid lipid 
NPs loaded with atorvastatin provide a tumor-specific deliv-
ery through ligand-receptor interactions, which results in 
enhanced cellular uptake and improved cytotoxicity against 
breast cancer cells. Also, a significant inhibition of tumor 
growth was found upon decorating canagliflozin liposomes 
and fexofenadine-loaded albumin NPs with PD-L1-targeting 
peptide. Similarly, in prostate cancer models, fenbendazole-
loaded MCM-48 NPs functionalized with β-lactoglobulin 
not only attained 1.8-fold greater cytotoxicity but also sig -
nificantly increased ROS production, overcoming MDR in 
tumors. In addition, concurrently targeting multiple cancer 
hallmarks is a significant aspect of drug repurposing and 
nanoformulations. As revealed in the metformin-encap-
sulated zein NPs, which demonstrated the modulation of 
metabolic pathways through AMPK activation while simul-
taneously reducing inflammatory markers. Similarly, the pH-
sensitive PD-L1-targeted canagliflozin liposomes inhibited 
PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK/ERK signaling, degraded 
PD-L1, and promoted the radiosensitization of tumors via 
the downregulation of DNA damage repair-related proteins. 
Further innovations include telmisartan-loaded Chitosan-
coated magnetic NPs, which target the acidic TME for selec-
tive drug release in which 82% of telmisartan was released 
in acidic pH, versus 25% drug release at physiological pH, 
and the combination of losartan with mesoporous platinum 
NPs, which significantly improves PTT along with TME 
modulation to achieve 80% tumor suppression.
In conclusion, combining repurposed drugs with bio -
degradable nanocarriers demonstrate the synergistic effect 
of such a treatment strategy in terms of potentiation of 
anticancer effects, selective tumor destruction via targeting 
specific cancer hallmarks, such as metabolic reprogram-
ming, and improved immune response. Such outstanding 
benefits exerted by a single nanoformulation with the high 
flexibility to tailor NPs according to drug’s properties, 
treatment strategy, and patient needs highlight their high 
potential for clinical translation. Despite the aforemen-
tioned advantages of the repurposed nanosystems, some 
limitations still exist. This review focused on experimental 
drug repurposing using nanocarrier-based systems; there-
fore, further research should be conducted to include other 
repurposing strategies and how these approaches could 
accelerate drug discovery while optimizing therapeutic 
efficacy. Besides preclinical investigations, industrial pro-
duction is also critical and requires comprehensive evalua-
tion, due to the large-scale production challenges, such as 
the complexity of NPs design, batch-to-batch variability, 
and limited reproducibility. To overcome these challenges, 
the possibility of implementing ML and AI in industrial 
production to control the production process and predict 
and minimize errors, as well as the long-term safety of 
repurposed nanomedicines, requires extensive investiga-
tions. Ultimately, overcoming these challenges through 
multidisciplinary studies will be critical to advancing 
repurposed nanomedicines from the research phase toward 
successful clinical application.
