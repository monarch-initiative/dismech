BACKGROUND: There are limited data available on incidence and survival of 
patients with choroid plexus tumors (CPT). This study provides the most current 
epidemiological analysis of choroid plexus tumors from 2004 to 2017 in the 
United States.
METHODS: Data on 2013 patients with CPT were acquired from the Central Brain 
Tumor Registry of the United States in collaboration with the Centers for 
Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National Cancer Institute, from 
2004 to 2017. CPT cases were classified by the following pathological subtypes: 
choroid plexus papilloma (CPP), atypical choroid plexus papilloma (aCPP), and 
choroid plexus carcinoma (CPC). Frequencies and age-adjusted incidence rates 
(AAIR) per 100 000 and rate ratios per 100 000 (IRR) were reported for age, sex, 
race, and ethnicity for each pathological subtype with 95% confidence intervals 
(95% CI). Using CDC's National Program of Cancer Registries survival database, 
survival curves and hazard ratios (HRs) evaluated overall survival from 2001 to 
2016.
RESULTS: CPP had the highest overall incidence (AAIR: 0.034, 95% CI: 
0.033-0.036), followed by CPC (AAIR: 0.008, 95% CI: 0.008-0.009) and aCPP (AAIR: 
0.005, 95% CI: 0.005-0.006). Incidence was highest among children less than one 
year old among all subtypes (CPP AAIR: 0.278; aCPP AAIR: 0.140; CPC AAIR: 
0.195), reducing as patients aged. Overall survival was worse among patients 
with CPC, being five times more likely to die compared to patients with CPP (HR: 
5.23, 95% CI: 4.05-7.54, P < .001).
CONCLUSIONS: This analysis is the most current and comprehensive study in the US 
on the incidence and survival for CPT. Population based statistics provide 
critical information in understanding disease characteristics, which impact 
patient care and prognosis.

CPT cases were classified by the following pathological subtypes: choroid plexus papilloma (CPP), atypical choroid plexus papilloma (aCPP), and choroid plexus carcinoma (CPC). Frequencies and age-adjusted incidence rates (AAIR) per 100 000 and rate ratios per 100 000 (IRR) were reported for age, sex, race, and ethnicity for each pathological subtype with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Using CDC’s National Program of Cancer Registries survival database, survival curves and hazard ratios (HRs) evaluated overall survival from 2001 to 2016.

Incidence was highest among children less than one year old among all subtypes (CPP AAIR: 0.278; aCPP AAIR: 0.140; CPC AAIR: 0.195), reducing as patients aged. Overall survival was worse among patients with CPC, being five times more likely to die compared to patients with CPP (HR: 5.23, 95% CI: 4.05–7.54,P< .001).

Keywords:brain tumors, choroid plexus, epidemiology, incidence, survival

The choroid plexus is a secretory neuroepithelial tissue present in the ventricles of the brain that produces cerebrospinal fluid and is essential for proper development of the nervous system.1Choroid plexus tumors (CPTs) usually arise within the ventricles, although rarely they occur away from the ventricles, presumably from ectopic choroid tissue. CPT are rare, with an age-adjusted incidence rate of 0.05 per 100 000 from 2013 to 2017 in the United States (US).2CPTs consist of three pathological subtypes including choroid plexus papilloma (CPP, WHO grade 1), atypical choroid plexus papilloma (aCPP, WHO grade 2), which are classified as low grade CPT, and choroid plexus carcinomas (CPC, WHO grade 3), which are malignant.3Ten to twenty percent of brain tumors diagnosed in children occur from birth to one year of age.4CPTs account for one to four percent of all pediatric brain tumors and primarily affect children less than two years of age.5Because these tumors are highly vascular, there is an increased risk of serious blood loss during surgery which complicates surgical resection and limits the ability to completely resect these tumors.6Low grade CPT can be treated successfully with gross total resection.7Radiation and chemotherapy may be used to treat recurrent CPT or CPC despite resection status.8aCPP has a higher tendency to recur than grade 1 CPP, and there is debate about optimal or necessary adjuvant treatment after surgery.9Methylation can be used in some cases alongside histologic grade and p53 status in predicting how aggressive or likely to recur CPT might be.10,11P53 status in the tumor is prognostic, and CPTs that are p53 wild type can often be cured without radiation.12However, due to the rarity of CPT and lack of prospective studies, there is limited data on optimal adjuvant treatment after surgery.13,14

To our knowledge, there has not been a recent, large-scale epidemiological study evaluating incidence and survival of CPT in both children and adults in the US. Using data from 2004 to 2017 provided by the Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States (CBTRUS), this study provides a contemporary evaluation of CPT incidence and overall survival for CPT pathological subtypes by demographic and clinical characteristics. Additionally, this report includes the largest number of adult CPT cases in the current literature and bridges the age gap in reported statistics for CPT. The analysis presented here provides comprehensive population-based information that is critical for understanding disease characteristics which may impact patient care and prognosis.

This study was conducted as an exempt protocol approved by the Duke University Institutional Review Board.

Incidence data were obtained from CBTRUS in collaboration with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC) National Program of Cancer Registries (NPCR) and the National Cancer Institute’s (NCI) Survival, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program.15CBTRUS data provide the largest aggregate population-based data focused exclusively on primary brain and other CNS tumors, which covers ~100% of the US population. First-sequence, pathologically confirmed cases of CPT diagnosed between 2004 and 2017 were identified using theInternational Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition(ICD-O-3) histology and morphology code 9390/0 (CPP), 9390/1 (aCPP), and 9390/3 (CPC).16Additional survival analyses were performed using the CDC’s NPCR survival database, which consists of 45 US registries.17This dataset was limited to cases diagnosed from 2004 to 2016. The NPCR survival database is a subset of CBTRUS, which includes state registries covered by NPCR.

SEER*Stat (version 8.3.9) was utilized to generate frequencies, age-adjusted incidence rates (AAIR), and incidence rate ratios (IRR).18All incidence rates are presented per 100 000 population and are age-adjusted to the 2000 US standard population.19Frequencies were determined for age at diagnosis, sex, race, ethnicity, surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy and stratified by pathological subtype. Chi-square tests were performed to assess differences in proportions. Due to insufficient sample size, patients that were American Indian/Alaska Native and Asian or Pacific Islander were categorized together as “Other”. Patients of unknown or unspecified race were excluded from race-specific analyses. Surgery subgroups were defined by SEER site specific surgery codes for brain and CNS: Biopsy only (00), subtotal resection (20, 21, 40), and gross total resection (30, 55). Patients with surgery code “00” are classified as “biopsy only”, as only patients with pathological confirmation are included in this analysis. AAIRs and IRRs per 100 000 persons were generated for age at diagnosis, sex, race, ethnicity, and with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Rates were suppressed when counts were fewer than 16 within a cell, but are included in totals, except when data are suppressed from only one cell to prevent identification of the number in the suppressed cell.

Survival analyses were performed to assess differences in overall survival among pathological subtypes using R Software (version 4.1.0). Univariate Kaplan Meier analysis was performed to assess differences in survival by pathological type. Log rank tests were performed to evaluate statistical differences in survival curves. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was performed for pathological subtype and adjusted for age at diagnosis, sex, race and ethnicity, and surgery. Additionally, multivariable models stratified by CPP and CPC were evaluated by the same demographic and clinical factors. Model estimates for aCPP were not presented due to the low number of events. Hazard ratios (HR) and associated 95% CI were reported. Due to lack of availability of these data fields in the survival database, radiotherapy and chemotherapy information were not included as covariates in the model. Similarly, genetic information including p53 status in tumor and patients or molecular telomerase alterations were not available. The Cox proportional hazard assumptions were tested and were not found in violation.

All tables and figures were generated using R Software (version 4.1.0).Pvalues under .05 were considered statistically significant.

From 2004 to 2017, there were 2013 cases of CPT identified, with the majority consisting of CPP (1675; 72%) followed by CPC (356; 17%), and aCPP (233; 11%). Age distribution varied significantly among pathological subtypes, with significantly less cases among children younger than one-year-old at diagnosis with CPP (155 cases; 11%) compared to aCPP (78 cases; 35%) and CPC (109 cases; 32%,P< .001). Sex and race distribution did not vary among subtypes. Surgical extent of resection did not vary among subtypes, with the majority of all CPT patients receiving a gross total resection. The proportion of aCPP patients who did not receive surgery cannot be reported due to low sample size. A significantly larger percentage of CPC patients received radiotherapy (19%) compared to CPP (2%) and aCPP (7.5%). Among CPC patients, 61% received chemotherapy (Table 1).

Overall, from 2004 to 2017, CPTs had an AAIR of 0.048 per 100 000 (95% CI: 0.046–0.050). Among pathological subtypes, CPPs had highest overall incidence (AAIR: 0.034, 95% CI: 0.033–0.036), followed by CPCs (AAIR: 0.008, 95% CI: 0.008–0.009), and aCPPs (AAIR: 0.005, 95% CI: 0.005–0.006) (Figure 1A). CPCs occurred 76% less than CPPs (IRR: 0.24, 95% CI: 0.22–0.28,P< .001) and aCPP occurred 85% less than CPPs (IRR: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.13–0.18,P< .001) (Figure 1B). Incidence was highest among children less than one year old among all subtypes (CPP AAIR: 0. 278, 95% CI: 0. 236–0. 325; aCPP AAIR: 0.140, 95% CI: 0.111–0.174; CPC, AAIR 0.195, 95% CI: 0.160–0.236), and declined significantly as patients aged (Figure 2,Suppl Table 1). Patients who were Black had significantly lower incidence when compared to patients who were White among CPPs (IRR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.43–0.63,P< .001), aCPPs (IRR: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.22–0.67,P< .001), and CPCs (IRR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.49–0.98,P= .038), Similarly, patients who were “Other” race had significantly lower incidence when compared to patients who were White among CPPs (IRR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.31–0.57,P< .001). There were 1763 patients assessed in the NPCR survival data set from 2004 to 2016. Patients with CPC had a notably lower survival compared to the other pathological subtypes (log rankP< .001) (Figure 4A). Median survival could not be calculated as over 50% of the patient population survived through the study period for all subtypes. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards modeling showed that patients with CPC were over five times more likely to die at any given point in time compared to patients with CPP (HR: 5.53, 95% CI: 4.05–7.54,P< .001). Overall survival also significantly differed between patients with CPP and aCPP (HR: 1.73, 95% CI: 1.08–2.80,P= .024) (Figure 4B,Suppl Table 2). Among patients with CPP, those who were Black were 2.3 times more likely to die at any given point in time compared to patients who were White (HR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.26–4.21,P= .007). CPP patients who achieved gross total resection had improved survival compared to those who received biopsy only and had a 69% reduction in risk of death at any point in time (HR: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.16–0.6,P< .001). In a similar trend, CPC cases who had received gross total resection had improved survival compared to patients who received biopsy only, though this was not significant (HR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.26–1.16,P= .118). To our knowledge, the work presented is the most current and comprehensive analysis of CPT incidence and survival by pathological subtype in the US. This study covers approximately 100% of the US population, providing nationally representative statistics for CPT. Previous studies were less comprehensive, focused on patient populations from a single institution or registry, or confined to a single CPT histology only.6,7

It is well-established that the incidence of CPTs is highest among children, particularly infants.3,6,14This study reinforces these findings, showing that the highest incidence occurs among children under one year of age year across all pathological subtypes, with decreasing incidence with increasing age. However, unlike this study, due to the nature of CPT predominance in children, adult cases are often excluded in studies focusing on these tumors. While rare, adult cases of CPT do occur, particularly among patients with CPP, who experienced an AAIR of 0.03 per 100 000 from 2004 to 2017 for cases those older than 40 years of age.

In general, patients who are White have a higher incidence of primary brain tumors when compared to other racial groups.20The results of this study align with previous findings, as incidence among patients who were White was highest among all three pathological subtypes. Survival was impacted by race as well, with CPP patients who were Black and CPC patients who were designated as “Other” having worse overall survival compared with patients who were White for their respective pathological subtypes. This reinforces previous studies, which found an overall survival advantage among patients who were White with CPP.4This study also identified ethnicity incidence differences, with patients who were Hispanic having lower incidence compared to non-Hispanics.

Overall, survival outcomes for CPT tumors resulted in greater than 50% of cases for all three pathological subtypes surviving through the study period. Patients with CPC had the poorest survival outcomes and were over 5-times more likely to die at any given point in time compared to patients with CPP. Survival among patients with CPT was impacted by surgical resection. A prior, single-institutional case series determined that gross total resection resulted in improved survival outcomes for low grade CPT patients.7This current study supports this finding; CPP patients who received a gross total resection had improved survival outcomes compared to patients who received no surgical intervention when adjusted for age, sex, race, ethnicity, and surgery. Patients were treated with radiation significantly more often in CPC cases (21%) than CPP (<2%) and aCPP (8%) cases. Future studies are needed to fully evaluate the impact of radiation treatment on CPT survival, which must also take into account p53 status, as p53 mutated tumors have a very poor outcome, even with radiation and intensive chemotherapy.12

There are limitations to this study. As with all cancer registry studies in the US, there is currently no mechanism for central pathology review of cases within the registry system, and pathology code assignment at case registration is based on pathology information contained in the patient’s medical record. No data are provided through current registries on the frequency of the association of CPTs, especially CPC, with the inheritable Li-Fraumeni cancer predisposition Syndrome (LFS), which is due to germline mutations in the P53 tumor suppressor gene and loss of p53 heterozygosity in the tumor. Some 50–60% of children with CPC under two years of age have underlying LFS. LFS patients with p53 mutant tumors have a very poor prognosis even with aggressive surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy.12,21Assessing germline p53 status, particularly in CPC patients, has implications for the patient and family members, as LFS patients are at risk for several other tumors including adrenocortical carcinoma, malignant glioma, medulloblastoma, and bone and soft tissue sarcomas, in the setting of LFS.14Furthermore, the survival outcomes for children with CPC who are P53 wild type are far superior to those with P53 mutation, for whom outcome is very poor.21Within the incidence data, there is no distinction between no radiation therapy and unknown radiation status, and these levels are categorized together for this analysis. This prevents the reporting of the true number of cases that or did not receive radiotherapy. With cancer registry data, surgical information is can be impacted by the type of reporting source. It is presumed that a number of cases reported as “no surgery” received surgery at different location, such as another state, and was not yet reported, or consolidated into a single record, by the reporting central cancer registry as each patient would have had received surgery at some point in their diagnosis and treatment. Due to insufficient case counts, chemotherapy information could not be reported. Additionally, treatment information was limited in our survival data, and survival characteristics could not be assessed for radiotherapy or chemotherapy.

This study is the largest and most current population study on CPT in the US and shows that these tumors occur primarily in patients who are White and in those younger than one year of age. Gross total resection was shown to improve overall survival in CPP cases. Population-based statistics provide critical information to healthcare providers, clinicians, and researchers on disease characteristics and burden, and contribute to improvements in patient care and prognosis.
