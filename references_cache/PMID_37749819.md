BACKGROUND: Thymic carcinomas (TCs) and thymic neuroendocrine neoplasms (TNENs) 
are two aggressive subtypes of thymic malignancy. Traditional therapy for 
advanced TCs and TNENs has limited outcome. New genomic profiling of TCs and 
TNENs might provide insights that contribute to the development of new treatment 
approaches.
METHODS: We used gene panel sequencing technologies to investigate the genetic 
aberrations of 32 TC patients and 15 TNEN patients who underwent surgery at 
Shanghai Chest Hospital between 2015 and 2017. Patient samples were sequenced 
using a 324-gene platform with licensed technologies. In this study, we focused 
on clinically relevant genomic alterations (CRGAs), which are previously proven 
to be pathogenic alterations, to identify the pathology-specific mutational 
patterns, prognostic signatures of TCs and TNENs.
RESULTS: The mutational profiles between TCs and TNENs were diverse. The genetic 
alterations that ranked highest in TCs were in CDKN2A, TP53, ASXL1, CDKN2B, 
PIK3C2G, PTCH1, and ROS1 , while those in TNENs were in MEN1, MLL2, APC, RB1 , 
and TSC2 . Prognostic analysis showed that mutations of ROS1, CDKN2A, CDKN2B, 
BRAF, and BAP1 were significantly associated with worse outcomes in TC patients, 
and that mutation of ERBB2 indicated shortened disease-free survival (DFS) and 
overall survival (OS) in TNEN patients. Further investigation found that the 
prognosis-related genes were focused on signal pathways of cell cycle control, 
chromatin remodeling/DNA methylation, phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3K)/protein 
kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), and receptor tyrosine 
kinase (RTK)/RAS/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling.
CONCLUSION: We profiled the mutational features of 47 Chinese patients with 
thymic malignancy of diverse pathologic phenotypes to uncover the integrated 
genomic landscape of these rare tumors, and identified the pathology-specific 
mutational patterns, prognostic signatures, and potential therapeutic targets 
for TCs and TNENs.

Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) are a type of primary tumors in the mediastinum and include thymomas, thymic carcinomas (TCs), and thymic neuroendocrine neoplasms (TNENs).[1] These tumors are rarly compared with other cancers, with an incidence rate of 0.13/100,000 person-years.[2] In adults, TETs account for 25–30% of mediastinal tumors, of which 75–85% are thymomas, 14–22% TCs, and less than 5% are TNENs.[3–5] In the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons (ESTS) thymic database, a total of 1122 patients were registered between 2007 and 2017; among them, 28% of surgical cases had TC and 6.6% had TNEN.[6] In Korea, between 1999 and 2017, among 5812 patients diagnosed with TETs, the proportions of patients with thymoma, TC, and TNEN were 58.9%, 38.1%, and 3.0%, respectively.[7] Due to the rarity of TETs, there is insufficient clinical and genetic understanding of these diseases; therefore, more clinical and genetic insights are needed to guide treatment.

Surgery is the main treatment strategy for early-stage TC and TNEN.[8] The completeness of surgical resection influences recurrence rate and prognosis. Other risk factors associated with recurrence include specific histology, clinical stage, and molecular signature.[9] TCs at advanced stages exhibit aggressive phenotypes, and the 5-year survival is only 36%.[10–12] Compared with TCs, TNENs are rare and comprise only 0.4% of all neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs).[13–16] TNENs are thought to originate from thymic neuroendocrine cells, which are a minor cell population scattered in the normal human thymus, and exhibit even greater aggressiveness than TCs.[14,17–19] TNENs include four histological subtypes, namely typical carcinoid, atypical carcinoid (AC), large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), and small cell carcinoma (SmCC). The 5-year overall survival rates of AC vary in published series, from 20% to 80%, and the prognosis of LCNEC and SmCC is very poor.[15,16,18,20–23] Studies found increasing numbers of chromosomal gains and losses in TNENs from TC to AC to LCNEC and SmCC that reflect the increasing clinical aggressiveness of these tumors.[20]

With the development of targeted therapies and immunotherapy, the treatment of other cancers has been improved, while the management of TCs and TNENs still mainly relies on traditional treatments, such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy. A multimodal approach incorporating surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy is needed for advanced or recurrent TCs and TNENs. The development of novel targeted therapies for TCs and TNENs has been hindered due to the lack of knowledge regarding genetic mechanisms. Immunohistochemical analyses revealed the overexpression of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), KIT proto-oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase (KIT), insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor (IGF-1R), and neurotrophic receptors in thymus epithelial tumors.[24] Identifying molecular drivers and potential therapeutic targets has profound implications for patient management. However, genomic alterations, especially those that are subtype-specific or potential drug targets, remain unknown. The rapid development of next-generation sequencing technology has improved understanding of the mutational profile of TCs and TNENs.[25] However, due to the rarity of TCs and TNENs, previous studies have included relatively limited numbers of patients. This study investigated the mutational landscape of TCs and TNENs. We performed comprehensive genomic profiling for 47 Chinese patients to elucidate potentially clinically relevant genomic alterations.

This study was approved by the Shanghai Chest Hospital Institutional Review Board Committee (No. KS 1970). All participants signed an informed consent form prior to being included in the study. A total of 50 patients were recruited at Shanghai Chest Hospital. Disease staging was assessed according to the Masaoka-Koga system,[26] and the histological typing of the tumors was classified according to the 2015 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors.[27] The histological subtype of each sample was evaluated by two pathologists using hematoxylin and eosin (HE)-stained sections following the 2015 WHO criteria. In cases of disagreement or uncertainty, a third pathologist was consulted, and a consensus was reached through discussion. The main inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) undergone radical surgery; (2) sufficient tissue samples for genetic test; (3) treatment naïve (without pre-radio or chemotherapy); and (4) complete postoperative follow-up data. Patients' clinical characteristics and medical history were collected. Three cases were excluded due to DNA degradation and thus 47 patients were included in the final analysis. Among them, 32 cases were TCs and 15 were TNENs.

Briefly, DNA was extracted from routine formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) surgical specimens and was tested by HANGZHOU DIAN BIOTECHNOLOGY Co., LTD. (Hangzhou, China) using a 324-gene platform FoundationOne CDx (F1CDx) (https://www.foundationmedicine.com/genomic-testing/foundation-one-cdx). All testing was performed in a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified, College of American Pathologists (CAP)-accredited laboratory (HANGZHOU DIAN BIOTECHNOLOGY Co., LTD). F1CDx is a qualitative gene panel sequencing-based in vitro diagnostic test that uses targeted high throughput hybridization-based capture technology to detect substitutions, insertion and deletion alterations (indels), and copy number alterations (CNAs) in 324 genes and selected gene rearrangements, as well as genomic signatures including microsatellite instability (MSI) and tumor mutational burden (TMB) using DNA isolated from FFPE tumor tissue specimens. TMB was assessed as described by Chalmers et al.[28] TMB ≥10 mutations/Mb (a threshold provided by FDA in solid tumor) [29] was recognized as hypermutation (TMB-H). Five microsatellite (MS) detection sites (BAT25, BAT26, D5S346, D2S123, and D17S250) recommended by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) were used for assessing the characters of microsatellite. The instability of one site is called low-degree microsatellite instability (MSI-L), the instability of two or more sites is called MSI-H, and the stability of all five sites is microsatellite stability (MSS). MSI is clinically important as it is associated with an increased risk for cancers, but MSI-high (MSI-H) colorectal carcinoma (CRC) and endometrial carcinoma have a better prognosis than those with MSI-stable (MSS) tumors.

The Genomic Data Commons (GDC) TCGA thymoma dataset was downloaded from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena Browser (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). TC samples were selected by filtering the primary diagnosis field using "Thymic carcinoma, (not otherwise specified) NOS." We identified 11 TET samples in the TCGA dataset. Somatic mutations were downloaded from the MuTect2 variant aggregation and masking file (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/?dataset=TCGA-THYM.mutect2_snv.tsv&host=https%3A%2F%2Fgdc.xenahubs.net&removeHub=https%3A%2F%2Fxena.treehouse.gi.ucsc.edu%3A443), and copy number variations were obtained from GISTIC focal copy number estimates (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/?dataset=TCGA-THYM.gistic.tsv&host=https%3A%2F%2Fgdc.xenahubs.net&removeHub=https%3A%2F%2Fxena.treehouse.gi.ucsc.edu%3A443). Genes were then filtered to overlap with the set covered by the F1CDx panel.

Patients were devided into two groups based on their pathologic profiles, and patients with TCs were classified as Group 1 and those with TNENs were classified as Group 2. All genes harboring somatic mutations were mapped onto MSigDB hallmark gene sets [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B775] and 10 canonical pathways [Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B776], namely cell cycle, Hippo, Myc, Notch, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT), receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)–RAS, transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signaling, p53, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways based on previous definition.[30,31] MSigDB hallmark gene sets are coherently expressed signatures derived by aggregating many MSigDB gene sets to represent well-defined biological states or processes. Mutations of genes were reflected onto the pathway level, and recurrent mutated pathways were subsequently depicted using OncoPrints.

Recurrent mutated genes were defined as genes identified in at least two samples in either Group 1 or Group 2. Kaplan–Meier plot was applied to evaluate the prognostic value of each candidate gene using log-rank test provided by the survival and survminer package in R (version 4.0.3, https://www.r-project.org/). The recurrent mutated genes were also examined in a multivariate Cox regression model adjusting for age, smoking history, tumor stage, and postoperative radio-/chemotherapy.

Data are presented as median (Q1, Q3) or n (%) as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.3 and corresponding plots were generated by ggplot2 (version 3.4.1, https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/ggplot2/index.html). The P-value cutoff for statistical significance was set as 0.05.

A total of 32 TC patients and 15 TNEN patients from Shanghai Chest Hospital were included in this study. The clinicopathological characteristics and patient details are shown in Table 1. The main pathological subtype of the TC group was thymic squamous carcinoma (TSCC) (81.3%, 26/32), and 6 patients in TC group also exhibited neuroendocrine components. Of the 15 TNEN patients, 12 cases were AC, 1 case was typical carcinoid, and 2 cases were LCNEC. After a median follow-up period of 41.5 months, there were 22 recurrences and 13 deaths.

The TMB of most patients was low. Only four (8.5%, 4/47) patients had hypermutation, including three (9.4%, 3/32) TC patients and one (6.7%, 1/15) TNEN patient. The TMB values of two groups were not significantly different. Only one (2.1%) patient exhibiting high TMB was MSI-H (21.43 mutations/Mb) [Figure 1E]. The patient was male and a former smoker, and was diagnosed with TC. No significant difference in OS [Figure 1F] and DFS [Figure 1G] was observed between TMB-H high patients and others.

Identifying repeatedly mutated genes is key to discovering important proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. We identified significantly mutated genes and pathways that appeared in at least two cases in Group 1 (more than 5% patients) or in Group 2 (more than 10% patients).

We confirmed many differences in the mutational profiles between the two groups. In TC patients, CDKN2A alteration (31.3%, 10/32) was one of the most frequently observed genetic events, followed by TP53 (28.1%, 9/32), ASXL1 (21.9%, 7/32), and CDKN2B (18.8%, 6/32) [Figure 2A]. In TNEN patients, mutations in MEN1 (46.7%, 7/15) and MLL2 (33.3%, 5/15) were the most frequently observed events, followed by APC (20.0%, 3/15), RB1 (20.0%, 3/15), and TSC2 (20.0%, 3/15) [Figure 2A].

In TCGA samples, the loss of CDKN2A (40%), CDKN2B (40%), and MTAP (40%) were frequently observed, followed by BRCA2 (20%), CCND2 (20%), and CD274 (20%) alterations [Figure 2B]. Although the landscape of our samples was quite different from that of TCGA, the most frequent gene mutations were similar between the two cohorts [Figure 2].

All mutated genes were mapped to the MsigDB Hallmark gene sets and the 10 canonical pathways to depict altered pathways in TCs and TNENs. p53 pathway and E2F target pathways were the top altered pathways in both groups among Hallmark gene sets [Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B777]. In TC patients, the top altered pathways were E2F targets, p53 pathway, Wnt/beta-catenin signaling, and G2M checkpoint [Figure 3A]. Immune-related pathways, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) signaling, nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), interleukin 2 (IL2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (Stat5) signaling, allograft rejection, and inflammatory response, were also frequently altered. The altered pathways in TNEN patients included p53 pathway, myogenesis, mitotic spindle, and PI3K/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling [Figure 3B].

We examined the 10 canonical pathways (RTK–RAS, Nrf2, TGFβ, PI3K, p53, cell cycle, Myc, Hippo, Wnt, and Notch) and the relationship between these pathways and the prognosis-related gene alternations.

Of the 10 canonical pathways, RTK–RAS signaling pathways harbored most mutated genes in TC patients, followed by Notch, cell cycle, PI3K, and TP53 [Figure 4A]. The genes related to prognosis were focused on RTK–RAS, PI3K, and cell cycle pathways. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that mutations in ROS1 (OS: P = 0.006; DFS: P = 0.033) and BRAF (OS: P = 0.027; DFS: P = 0.003) of the RTK–RAS pathway and CDKN2A (OS: P = 0.015; DFS: P <0.001) and CDKN2B (OS: P = 0.006; DFS: P <0.001) of the cell cycle pathway were correlated with worse outcomes for both OS and DFS. TSC2 (P <0.001) of the PI3K pathway was associated with shorter DFS [Figure 4A]. After adjusting for the influencing factors, including age, smoking history, tumor stage, and postoperative radio-/chemotherapy, ROS1 mutation was shown as an independent predictor of worse outcomes for both OS (P = 0.029, hazard ratio [HR] = 11.31, 95% CI 1.28–99.80) and DFS (P = 0.023, HR = 6.26, 95% CI 1.29–30.47). Mutations in CDKN2A (P = 0.010, HR = 8.21, 95% CI 1.67–40.37) and CDKN2B (P = 0.028, HR = 9.38, 95% CI 1.28–68.65) also predicted a shorter DFS in TC patients. Thymic malignancies are rare. TCs are divided into several subtypes according to pathologic features, and the predominant histology subtype is squamous cell carcinoma.[32] Although integrative recognition of genetic alterations in TC is important, few studies have focused on the mutational profiles of these subtypes and investigated the oncogenic mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. In this study, we described the genetic features of 47 Chinese patients with thymic malignancy. Our results might contribute to new targeted drug development and a comprehensive understanding of rare tumors.

In our study, comprehensive genomic profiling of TCs revealed that loss of CDKN2A and CDKN2B and non-synonymous mutations of ASXL1 and TP53 were frequent genetic alterations. CDKN2A and CDKN2B genes are located on 9p21.3, a locus at which frequent homozygous and heterozygous deletions occur in many primary human tumors. These genes encode p16 and p15, respectively, which are cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors that target cyclin-dependent kinases 4 and 6 (CDK4/6), and loss of their expression may contribute to uncontrolled growth in human cancer.[33–35] Previous studies of thymic malignancies have identified loss of CDKN2A as the most frequent genetic event of TC,[36,37] which was consistent with our findings. In addition, the lack of p16 protein in TETs has been associated with poorer disease-related survival.[38] In our study, CDKN2A mutation also showed broad prognostic value. It was significantly correlated with shorter DFS for the entire cohort and both shorter DFS and OS in TC patients. TP53 mutations are among the most frequently detected alterations in human cancers and may serve as prognostic and predictive markers. These mutations have been previously reported as enriched in TETs and are associated with a poor prognosis.[25,39] However, in our study, TP53 gene mutation did not correlate with a worse outcome in TC or TNEN patients. ASXL1 mutations are frequently found in hematological tumors, and loss of ASXL1 promotes myeloid transformation.[40] A previous study of 78 TET patients in the US identified two patients (4%) with ASXL1 mutations.[41] We observed a much higher ASXL1 mutation frequency in TC patients (22%) than the previous study. This result indicates that the mutational profile of thymic malignancies had population-specific distributions.

In our investigation of the prognostic value of the mutational profiles, the results indicated that ROS1 and CDKN2B mutations predicted significantly worse outcomes for both OS and DFS in TC patients. ROS1 is a member of the tyrosine-kinase insulin-receptor family. The fusion of ROS1 gene in non-small cell lung cancer is an important oncogenic event and therapeutic target.[42–44] Drugs targeting ROS1, including ceritinib, crizotinib, and lorlatinib, are widely used in clinical practice. [45–47] Here we reported that single nucleotide variations/indels of ROS1 were closely associated with worse clinical outcomes of TCs in our Chinese patient cohort.

TNENs demonstrated a unique mutational profile. We identified enriched mutations in MEN1 and MLL2 in TNENs. MEN1 is a tumor suppressor gene involved in the activation of gene transcription as part of an mixed-lineage leukemia 1 (MLL1)/MLL2 (KMT2A/B)-containing protein complex that exhibits methyltransferase activity on lysine 4 of histone H3.[48,49] MEN1 abnormality is a key tumorigenesis event in hormone-producing tumors such as parathyroid, pancreatic endocrine, and anterior pituitary tumors.[49]
ERBB2 mutations are associated with both shorter OS and DFS in TNENs. In previous studies, overexpression of ERBB2 and activating ERBB2 mutations were identified as biomarkers for certain breast cancer subtypes and patients with those mutations showed sensitivity to tyrosine kinase inhibitors.[50] ERBB2 is also a target for cancer drugs.[51]

TMB and MSI are two biomarkers that could predict response to immunotherapy.[52] TMB is a predictive biomarker for the efficacy of immunotherapy and might be a valuable tool in selecting patients who will benefit from programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed cell death protein 1 ligand (PD-L1) therapy. However, TMB and MSI data for thymic malignancies were previously lacking. Herein, we found that thymic malignancy usually had low TMB and exhibited MSS.

In investigating the relationships between the mutated genes and altered pathways in the thymic malignancy patients, we found that in TCs, the top altered pathways were E2F targets, P53 pathway, Wnt/beta-catenin signaling, and G2M checkpoint. The E2F transcription factors are downstream effectors of the retinoblastoma protein (pRB) pathway and are required for the chronological regulation of numerous genes essential for DNA replication and cell cycle progression. p53 is a key regulator of cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and senescence in damaged cells. The Wnt/beta-catenin pathway regulates stem cell pluripotency and cell fate during development and is activated in cancers. The Wnt/beta-catenin pathway, which regulates stem cell pluripotency and cell fate decisions during development and is activated in cancer, was also highly altered. The highly altered pathways in TNEN patients included myogenesis, mitotic spindle, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway controls many physiological functions and cellular processes, including cell proliferation, growth, survival, motility, and metabolism.

This study had several limitations. First, the use of gene panel sequencing technology that included 324 genes and could calculate TMB and MSI might provide limited information compared with other next generation sequencing (NGS) approaches, such as whole-genome sequencing and whole-exome sequencing. However, we were able to use this technology to identify potential targets and predict responses to immunotherapy that might guide clinical treatment. Second, we found that several gene alternations were associated with reduced DFS and OS in this rare malignancy. However, these results require a large sample to verify, and further research is needed to reveal the underlying mechanism. Finally, although we identified several pathways that might be potential therapeutic targets, in vitro investigation and clinical trials are required for further validation.

In conclusion, we profiled the mutational profiles of 47 Chinese patients with thymic malignancy of diverse pathologic phenotypes, including TC and TNEN, to uncover the integrated genomic landscape of these rare tumors. Our results help illustrate the tumorigenesis and progression mechanisms of TCs and TNENs. Our research provides a relatively comprehensive portrait of the mutational profiles of Chinese patients and suggests potential novel biomarkers for prognosis and therapeutic targets.
