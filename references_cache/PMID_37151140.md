Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a common central nervous system (CNS) 
vasculopathy, which in some cases is associated with subacute encephalopathy, 
seizures, headaches, or strokes due to vascular inflammation directed against 
vascular amyloid accumulation. The pathological subtypes of inflammatory CAA 
include CAA-related inflammation (CAAri) with mostly perivascular lymphocytic 
infiltrates, or amyloid-beta (Aβ)-related angiitis (ABRA) with transmural 
granulomatous inflammation. CAAri and ABRA probably represent part of the 
spectrum of CNS vasculopathies, intermediate between CAA and primary CNS 
vasculitis, and they are closely related to Aβ-related imaging abnormalities and 
other manifestations of an inflammatory response directed against Aβ in the 
leptomeninges and cerebral parenchyma. As treatment strategies in Alzheimer's 
disease shift toward potentially effective antiamyloid immunotherapy, the 
incidence rate of inflammatory CAA (which is probably an underrecognized 
condition) is likely to increase. Its clinical features are varied and include 
subacute encephalopathy, behavioral symptoms, headaches, seizures, and focal 
neurological deficits, which necessitate a high degree of suspicion for this 
disorder that often responds to treatment. The recent definition of the typical 
clinical and radiological syndrome has increased its recognition and may 
eliminate the need for invasive histological sampling in at least some affected 
patients. Here we review the pathophysiology, clinical spectrum, and approach to 
diagnosis, and discuss illustrative cases that highlight the wide range of 
clinical presentations.

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a relatively common age-related disorder characterized by amyloid-beta (Aβ) deposition in the walls of the leptomeningeal and cortical arteries and arterioles.123 Between 10% and 40% of elderly patients manifest Aβ and its smaller peptides Aβ40 and Aβ42 in the tunica media and adventitia. Aβ was first identified in the brain in 1909, and this eosinophilic, insoluble, extracellular protein is also present in the senile plaques of Alzheimer’s disease (AD); indeed, pathological evidence of CAA is present in 80%–94% of brains with concomitant AD.1456789101112 Loss of vessel wall elasticity and increased fragility due to amyloid deposition leads to vessel rupture.113 Typically considered to produce cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) and larger lobar intracerebral hemorrhages (ICHs), the clinical spectrum of CAA has recently expanded to include nonhemorrhagic manifestations including transient focal neurological episodes, chronic cognitive decline and dementia, small cortical infarcts, cortical atrophy, and leukoencephalopathy.3141516171819 Imaging typically reveals lobar CMBs, cortical superficial siderosis or subarachnoid hemorrhage, and cortical atrophy,1415 and polarization microscopy examination of Congo-red-stained specimens demonstrates the diagnostic yellow-green birefringence of Aβ.113

While CAA is often a noninflammatory vasculopathy, some patients present with subacute encephalopathy, behavioral symptoms, headaches, seizures and/or focal deficits associated with asymmetric hyperintense signals on T2-weighted (T2W) or fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images with or without gadolinium enhancement.714202122232425262728 Distinct from the usual presentation of CAA, this rare clinical syndrome is correlated with pathological evidence of inflammation against vascular Aβ deposits in leptomeningeal and cortical vessels.141623242729 The nomenclature of this condition varies, with different terms used in attempts to distinguish it from both noninflammatory CAA and primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS). We used the term inflammatory CAA (iCAA) for this syndrome and followed the findings of Salvarani et al.8 in distinguishing two pathological subtypes: one with a perivascular nondestructive inflammatory infiltration (CAA-related inflammation [CAAri]) and one with a vasculitic transmural, often granulomatous, inflammatory infiltrate (Aβ-related angiitis [ABRA]).

iCAA may be thought of as an inflammatory disorder intermediate between noninflammatory CAA and PACNS, and its clinical features and prognosis are more strongly correlated with vascular inflammation than with Aβ deposition.8293031 Instead of lobar ICH (a common presentation of CAA), patients with iCAA may present subacute cognitive decline, focal neurological deficits, or seizures, which are associated with leptomeningeal involvement with or without white-matter infiltration on imaging.12329 Patients with iCAA tend to be younger than those with CAA and have a significantly higher incidence rate of the apolipoprotein E (ApoE) ε4/ε4 genotype, in contrast to the ApoE ε2 genotype that is associated with the risks of perivascular and spontaneous ICH.723323334 The central role of vascular inflammation allows the possibility of effective therapy, and immunomodulatory therapy has led to clinical and radiographical improvements in most patients.2329

Aβ deposition is closely related to age: a Japanese study of 400 autopsies found that the incidence rate of CAA was 4%–10% in those aged 50–59 years, markedly increasing to 42%–45.8% in those aged >90 years.35 As part of the widely recognized inflammatory spectrum of Aβ-driven pathologies of aging, iCAA mainly only presents in older people and at a later age than PACNS. The mean age at diagnosis is 67 years, which is younger than that of 76 years for noninflammatory CAA.372327313637 The youngest patient pathologically proven to have iCAA was 42 years old.31 While the incidence rate was calculated at 0.13/100,000 in a Japanese survey,38 iCAA is likely to become more common in the future due to the aging population.39 Vascular inflammation was present in 49% of a cohort of patients with CAA. Even allowing for the highly biased sample of that cohort, this suggests that iCAA is not uncommon.836 This condition often responds to treatment and has diverse clinical manifestations, with the wide spectrum of clinical and imaging features necessitating a high degree of suspicion from the clinician, especially since mild manifestations of iCAA may be missed in busy dementia and memory clinics if they are not thoroughly investigated.3640 A pathological examination via either brain biopsy or autopsy was previously considered essential for a diagnosis, but clinical and imaging findings are now thought to be reliable indicators of iCAA with good sensitivity and excellent specificity, enabling early and effective immunosuppressive treatment.1341 Moreover, the long-term outcomes remain unclear in sequential radiographical and clinical evaluations.42

Here we described three patients from our center each with distinct clinical manifestations and disease outcomes over time, and review the broad spectrum of clinical and imaging features associated with iCAA.

A 59-year-old male farmer with Graves’ disease and hypertension presented to the hospital in October 2012 with a 2-week history of lethargy, nausea, unsteadiness, and confusion. Papilledema, left visual neglect, and mild left hemiparesis were also noted at admission. Brain computed tomography revealed a hypodense, nonenhancing lesion in the right parietotemporal region with a mass effect and midline shift. This lesion was hyperintense on T2W imaging, and involved the subcortical and deep white matter with numerous CMBs noted in the same region on gradient echo (GRE) sequences without contrast enhancement (Fig. 1A). While pending a biopsy for possible glioma, dexamethasone was administered to the patient, which rapidly improved his focal symptoms and confusion. A biopsy specimen obtained from the right parietal region revealed an old hemorrhage with surrounding hemosiderin-laden macrophages and reactive astrocytosis. The biopsy was considered nondiagnostic, and the patient remained on a tapering dexamethasone dose with nearly complete improvement in his symptoms at a review 3 months later, with the exceptions of persistent left-foot numbness, visual perceptual problems, visual agnosia, and difficulties with stereopsis. Repeat magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed marked resolution of the right parietal T2W signal change and mass effect, with CMB persistence (Fig. 1B).

No further clinical change was noted until readmission to hospital in May 2020, at which time acute right hemiplegia and aphasia occurred due to a large left putaminal hemorrhage. Extensive changes were noted on susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), which were consistent with lobar and deep CMBs. White-matter signal intensities were only minimally increased compared with those in the postoperative scan (Fig. 1C). A review of the original biopsy was requested, which indicated marked hyalinosis of small blood vessels and typical apple-green birefringence of Aβ on polarized-light microscopy with Congo red staining. No vascular inflammation was observed.

A 61-year-old male forklift driver with hypertension, dyslipidemia, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease presented to his doctor with a 2-month history of persistent holocranial headaches associated with nausea, subjective balance problems, low mood with suicidal ideation, visual hallucinations, and occasional blurred vision. His wife reported that he had become increasingly vague and forgetful with poor concentration, tangential conversation, bradyphrenia, poor sleep, and slowed cognitive processing. He scored 20 (out of 30) on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) with low scores in subtests of visuospatial and executive function, verbal fluency, and recall. No focal lateralizing deficits were noted. A contrast MRI study revealed subcortical T2W and FLAIR signal changes with numerous CMBs in the posterior right frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes (Fig. 2A). Workups for systemic autoimmune disease and vasculitis, autoimmune encephalitis, and neuroinfection were negative. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed elevated protein at 6.19 g/L (reference range 0.15–0.40 g/L) with 61×106/L white blood cells (86% lymphocytes, 6% monocytes, and 8% polymorphs) with negative flow cytometry and oligoclonal bands. Digital-subtraction angiography revealed no evidence of vasculitis, and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron-emission tomography (PET) was negative for systemic malignancy and limbic encephalitis. Empirical therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone and subsequent oral prednisolone induced an improvement in the CSF analysis to 22×106/L white blood cells (all lymphocytes) and a protein level of 0.53 g/L. A biopsy specimen obtained from the right parietal lesion revealed perivascular lymphocytic infiltration with pale amorphous eosinophilic material deposition in the vessel walls. This biopsy specimen was Congo-red-positive and presented apple-green birefringence under polarized light, which was consistent with amyloidosis. Focal vessels within the cortex and meninges presented with a ‘double barrel’ contour. Extravasation of red blood cells into the parenchyma, reactive astrocytosis, and infiltration of perivascular and transmural histiocytes along with occasional multinucleated giant cells in meningeal vessels were noted. These features were consistent with iCAA, favoring ABRA.

He had been admitted to a peripheral hospital 6 years previously with a few-week history of headache with vomiting, photophobia, and visual hallucinations. The CSF opening pressure (>34 cm CSF) and protein (1.37 g/L) were elevated, with 6×106/L polymorphs and 18 lymphocytes. Multiplex viral PCR was negative, and MRI was not performed. The episode was presumptively treated for viral meningitis using acyclovir and steroids and the patient was discharged with full recovery.

Following histological confirmation of iCAA, the patient again received high-dose oral prednisolone and mycophenolate at 500 mg BD. His MoCA score improved to 28 after 6 weeks. Prednisolone was slowly tapered after 4 months, but interval MRI performed 7 months after the biopsy revealed increased SWI blooming in the parietal lobes with increased subcortical FLAIR hyperintensities, and acute cortical infarction in the paramedian left frontal lobe (Fig. 2B). While the patient initially declined cyclophosphamide therapy, instead preferring repeat intravenous methylprednisolone and an increase in the mycophenolate dose to 1 g BD, he did agree to commence it after a further ischemic stroke occurred 3 months later (Fig. 2C). He remained stable on close clinical and imaging follow-ups 6 months later.

An 80-year-old male with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease presented to the hospital with focal-onset seizures that involved the left arm with secondary generalization, with a 3-week history of headache and unsteadiness while walking. He had elevated blood pressure, dysarthria, persistent left hemiparesis, left facial weakness, and left visual neglect. Imaging revealed bilateral subcortical and periventricular white-matter microangiopathic changes with cerebral atrophy. An asymmetric area of white matter with a T2W signal hyperintensity was observed in the subcortical right parietal region extending to the lateral ventricle, which had not been observed in MRI performed 8 months previously. Multiple superficial and deep CMBs were noted on SWI in both studies, including in the right parietal region (Fig. 3).

Despite optimal blood pressure control and the use of antiseizure medication, he continued to experience left focal motor seizures and became progressively drowsier over the following 2 weeks. The course was complicated by bouts of aspiration pneumonia. Electroencephalography (EEG) revealed a right temporoparietal focus of epileptiform activity with periodic lateralized epileptiform discharges (PLEDs, Fig. 4). He remained drowsy after the seizures ceased, with a right-gaze preference, left visual and sensory neglects, and left hemiplegia. An extensive evaluation for infective or inflammatory disease was unremarkable apart from mildly elevated protein in the CSF. Given that the clinical and imaging findings were consistent with possible iCAA and after excluding ongoing infection, intravenous methylprednisolone was administered at 1 g daily for 5 days followed by oral prednisolone. Rapid improvements in alertness and left-sided weakness were observed over the following week, and rehabilitation was initiated. He unfortunately developed a urinary tract infection of Pseudomonas species that led to urosepsis, subsequent bouts of aspiration pneumonia and recurrent delirium, and steroids were rapidly tapered. Worsening lung function with atelectasis induced respiratory failure, and he died 3 months after the initial presentation to the hospital. An autopsy was declined.

All patients or their next of kin provided informed consent for publication prior to inclusion in this report. Ethics committee approval was not deemed necessary for this retrospective case series.

A pathological examination via biopsy or autopsy was previously considered the gold standard for diagnosing iCAA.14 Aβ deposition in cortical or leptomeningeal vessels leads to both vascular damage and an inflammatory response that often colocalizes to areas with Aβ deposition, suggesting that the amyloid itself is proinflammatory.3742 Multiple CMBs and small nonhemorrhagic infarctions in the cerebral cortex with rarefaction of the underlying white matter are evident in brain parenchyma examinations.23 Increased permeability of the blood–brain barrier due to the inflammatory response leads to vasogenic edema and immune effector cell infiltration, which are detected as hyperintense signal changes and leptomeningeal contrast enhancement on T2W MRI.142 SWI– and to some extent GRE imaging–is useful in demonstrating CMBs as hypointense spots or blooming. A microscopy examination demonstrates accumulations of mononuclear and multinucleated white blood cells–often with immunohistochemistry of monocyte/microglial and lymphocyte lineage– and reactive astrocytes around amyloid-laden vessels in the cerebral cortex and leptomeninges.43444546 Apple-green birefringence under polarized-light microscopy after Congo red staining confirms the presence of Aβ around all inflammation sites, which is sometimes internalized by macrophages.142347

Vascular dysfunction is a common consequence of this inflammatory response.23 The typically patchy involvement of the leptomeningeal and cortical arteries produces fibrous thickening, infarcts of varying sizes and ages, and ischemic myelinopathy.4849 Large multinucleated foreign-body giant cells may be found lying both internal and external to Aβ deposits. The inflammatory response may be nondestructive and restricted to lymphocytic perivascular cuffing (CAAri) or may also involve the vessel wall with transmural or intramural, and often granulomatous, inflammatory infiltrate (ABRA).138142023242729303137394249505152 However, no definitive distinguishing clinical, radiological, or prognostic features have yet been identified, and it is yet to be determined whether ABRA and CAAri are separate disorders or merely different aspects of a single disease process.314202753 This uncertainty is compounded by the terms iCAA and CAAri being used interchangeably by different authors.

ABRA is an angiodestructive process associated with fibrinoid necrosis and granulomatous inflammation involving small cerebral arterioles that may be more closely related to PACNS than CAA without inflammatory infiltrates, and these subtypes may both represent points on a continuum between CAA and PACNS.814203039525354 Although ABRA resembles PACNS pathologically, imaging findings and the colocalization of Aβ deposits with areas of vasculitic involvement indicate its relationship with CAAri.144547 A review of patients pathologically diagnosed to have PACNS found that 26%–31% also had ABRA, suggesting that a significant proportion of pathological PACNS diagnoses are actually ABRA.831 The two subtypes of iCAA may coexist, with a possible progression from perivascular inflammation to partial and then transmural inflammation.55 ABRA may be more common than CAAri, as it presented nearly three times more frequently (36% vs. 13%) in a series of patients with CAA who underwent biopsy.8

While the exact nature of the relationship between amyloid deposition and inflammation has historically been debated, with theories revolving around increased vascular permeability secondary to inflammation that permits the influx of circulating amyloid precursor proteins, iCAA is currently generally considered to arise from an autoimmune response to vascular Aβ.38142154565758 This concept is supported by patients with iCAA possibly carrying antibodies against Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 in the blood and CSF. Intrathecal production of these antibodies was demonstrated, and antibody titers have been shown to decrease after steroid therapy.1159606162 Piazza et al.60 found that antibody titers tend to be correlated with clinical and imaging findings. These autoantibodies could potentially serve as biomarkers for iCAA in the future, which would discriminate this condition from noninflammatory CAA or from other neuroinflammatory disorders and even help in monitoring the treatment response, although further studies are needed to confirm this.71461 Antibody-mediated spread of the immune response to different brain regions is thought to induce the diffuse cerebral involvement typical of iCAA, in contrast to the regional, occipital dominance of CMBs in noninflammatory CAA.1439

We are at the cusp of a new era in the therapeutics for AD, with anti-Aβ therapy being poised to change AD management.26 The occurrence of imaging abnormalities following therapy with bapineuzumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody directed against Aβ (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities [ARIA]), supports the hypothesis that iCAA is a spontaneous counterpart of confluent vasogenic edema (ARIA-E), and sulcal effusions and microhemorrhages (ARIA-H) of immunotherapy-induced inflammation observed in 17% of patients treated using bapineuzumab.8145363646566 The anti-Aβ antibodies in late-phase clinical development (lecanemab, aducanumab, and gantenerumab) have all been associated with ARIA development.67 While ARIA is often asymptomatic with a normal CSF analysis, meningoencephalitis following immunization using Aβ presents similarly to iCAA with subacute cognitive decline, CSF inflammation, similar imaging findings, and an inflammatory cellular response around amyloid-laden blood vessels, especially in the leptomeninges.3686970 Meningoencephalitis with variable clinical features developed in 6% of patients who received immunization of Aβ1-42, which included rapid neurological deterioration with CSF pleocytosis and transient imaging findings that were strikingly similar to those of iCAA.71 Microglial and lymphocytic infiltration with multinucleated giant cells that cleared amyloid from plaques was observed neuropathologically.6971 An animal model of Aβ-induced giant-cell and macrophage inflammation with phagocytosis has also been used,72 and Aβ has been found to induce an inflammatory cascade in human vascular cells.73 ARIA, anti-Aβ immunotherapy-induced meningoencephalitis, CAAri, and ABRA may all represent varying degrees of an inflammatory response directed against Aβ in the leptomeninges and cerebral parenchyma.3

Up to 75% of patients with iCAA are homozygous for the ApoE ε4/ε4 genotype, which is associated with Aβ deposition and microglial and complement activation in addition to its better-known profile as a genetic risk factor for sporadic AD and noninflammatory CAA.142023246374757677 The ApoE ε4/ε4 genotype was carried by 71%–77% of patients with iCAA compared with only 4%–5.1% of those with noninflammatory CAA.2324 ARIA were more commonly observed in carriers of the ApoE ε4/ε4 genotype following bapineuzumab treatment.66 The ε4 genotype may promote amyloid deposition in addition to a proinflammatory effect.277879

Symptoms are often subacute at onset, but may have been present for years before a diagnosis in some cases.54808182 A systematic review published in 2014 found that two-thirds of the patients had symptoms for more than 1 month before presentation, and had a mean age of 66.9 years (range 42–89 years) at presentation.27 Among 113 patients, 36% had a previous diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment or AD before iCAA symptoms developed.36 Three-quarters of patients presented with acute or subacute encephalopathy with or without focal neurological signs (46%–58%) and headache (22%–41%), with seizures in one-third.81114232427293031363739548083 Constitutional symptoms are often absent, although patients may complain of dizziness or nausea.3 Immune-mediated vascular function changes are believed to be responsible for iCAA symptoms, and induce relatively rapid cognitive and/or functional decline over weeks to months, which are factors in the differential diagnosis for rapidly progressing dementia syndrome.238485 Symptoms have been loosely grouped into three categories (encephalopathy or coma, multifocal, and unifocal neurological deficits27), and they correspond to how inflammation is distributed, which is often subcortically in the cerebral hemispheres.3 Relevant focal deficits identified included visual symptoms (14%), aphasia (14%–26%), ataxia (7%), and hemiparesis (16%–20%).1122273747 The presentation of ICH is not typical, unlike in noninflammatory CAA.82339

Typical imaging findings in iCAA may be specific enough to obviate the need for a brain biopsy, and include patchy or confluent white-matter hyperintensities on T2W MRI and multiple lobar CMBs centered at the junction between gray and white matter or cortical superficial siderosis on GRE sequences or SWI. The colocalization of CMBs with white-matter signal changes or leptomeningeal enhancement, as seen in our patients, is highly suggestive of iCAA.129 White-matter signal changes correspond to cerebral vasogenic edema due to the diffuse lobar or multilobar inflammatory process, appear infiltrative and poorly marginated, and involve the subcortical U-fibers.18232429488286 T2W signal changes in the lobar region can be considered a marker of disease severity and help in monitoring the treatment response and disease relapse, often improving with corticosteroid treatment or even spontaneously.32486 Corticosubcortical CMBs indicate intravascular Aβ deposition in more than 90% of patients129378788 and nearly two-thirds of patients with iCAA have >10 of these CMBs, while superficial siderosis was noted in half.36 Unlike noninflammatory CAA, a regional occipital dominance pattern is not observed in iCAA, possibly due to the dissemination of the inflammatory response to multiple brain regions via the spread of pathogenic antibodies.114293977 SWI is considered to be more reliable and sensitive than GRE imaging for detecting microhemorrhages, the burden of which may be related to symptomatology.1148990 Neither routine MRI nor SWI can directly detect amyloid deposits.42 Unlike in noninflammatory CAA, 28%–63% of patients with iCAA demonstrated contrast enhancement,114213647 and compared to PACNS, leptomeningeal contrast involvement and ICH are observed more frequently in iCAA.829 The presence of leptomeningeal enhancement has been proposed for discriminating iCAA from noninflammatory CAA with a sensitivity of 70.4% and specificity of 92.6%.1429 Contrast enhancement is twice as common in ABRA as in CAAri, probably due to the angiodestructive nature of the pathological process in ABRA.1420 No angiographic evidence of vasculitis has been found.2937

Stroke-like symptoms present in up to 46% of patients with iCAA.23 While these may be misdiagnosed as transient ischemic events or capsular warning syndrome,91 imaging evidence of true cerebral infarcts with diffusion restriction is often absent.23 Infarction is observed more often in ABRA than in CAAri, but is generally less common than in PACNS. iCAA differs from PACNS in that its patients are often older and more often present with cognitive symptoms, hallucinations, and seizures, while hemiparesis and visual symptoms resulting from cerebral infarction may be less common at the diagnosis.830 Among 48 patients with iCAA in a recent case series,92 9 presented with stroke-like symptoms and 7 of 32 had imaging evidence of punctate acute or subacute infarcts, all smaller than 10 mm in the largest in-plane dimension. Patient 2 in our study suffered recurrent cerebral infarctions despite corticosteroid treatment, which was similar to previous findings of multiple cerebral infarctions in patients with ABRA.8

Seizures in iCAA have not been well characterized but are supposedly generalized.234760 ABRA was found to present with generalized seizures in 8 of 34 patients, and was often associated with encephalopathy.3093 Given the frequent occurrence of encephalopathy, nonconvulsive seizures may be more common than often thought.37 Patient 3 in our study presented with encephalopathy and recurrent focal motor seizures with evidence on EEG of PLEDs that responded to steroid therapy. This was remarkably similar to the patient with iCAA reported by Watanabe et al.,28 who was shown to have nonconvulsive status epilepticus with PLEDs on EEG, which responded to corticosteroids. Unlike our patient, the MRI of that patient demonstrated disseminated white-matter signal changes, while CMBs were not localized to the areas of the white-matter signal changes in EEG and MRI. Prominent involvement of the cortex along with subcortical white matter through the inflammatory process was thought to provide the structural substrate for epileptogenesis.

Patient 1 presented with a steroid-responsive cerebral-lesion-like mass with imaging resolution after a course of oral steroids and Aβ deposition demonstrated at biopsy. The large and confluent areas of T2W signal changes can occasionally mimic a tumor.37 A mass lesion (namely amyloidoma) is seen in about 15% of patients with iCAA, and biopsy is often required to differentiate the inflammatory process from a tumor.81121298194 A mass effect is prominent, but there is typically no leptomeningeal enhancement.21 As in other types of iCAA, the colocalization of CMBs and white-matter signal changes contribute to making the correct diagnosis, which must be considered to avoid unnecessary surgical procedures.112124 Routine inclusion of SWI or GRE sequences in the diagnostic workup of brain masses would be helpful, especially in elderly patients presenting with subacute cognitive decline without parenchymal contrast enhancement. However, diagnosing pseudotumoral iCAA is challenging and biopsy is often needed.21

CSF protein is elevated in 80% of patients with iCAA, around half of whom present lymphocytic pleocytosis, as would be expected from an inflammatory process.7814232731373980 Oligoclonal bands are often absent in the CSF. The presence of anti-Aβ antibodies in the CSF serves as a biomarker to distinguish iCAA and ARIA from multiple sclerosis and other inflammatory conditions, which could obviate the need for brain biopsy and may be used to monitor the treatment effect.859606182

The realization that a reliable clinicoradiological diagnosis of iCAA can be made prior to pathological confirmation has contributed to increasing the recognition of the condition and renders brain biopsy unnecessary in a significant proportion of cases.132437 White-matter vasogenic edema with evidence of hemosiderin deposition can reliably indicate iCAA in the appropriate clinical context of acute or subacute brain dysfunction symptoms.2429374142 The diagnostic criteria initially proposed by Chung et al.37 in 2011 suggested that probable iCAA be considered in patients older than 40 years with supportive clinical and imaging features, with definite iCAA being reserved for cases with pathological confirmation. The updated 2016 criteria in Table 1 of Auriel et al.41 that detail white-matter lesion patterns and evidence of hemorrhage on MRI have been widely applied, and have 82% sensitivity and 97% specificity for a probable iCAA diagnosis, but only 68% specificity for possible iCAA. An excellent response to immunosuppressive treatment (typically high-dose corticosteroids) supports an iCAA diagnosis.23243741 Brain biopsy may be considered if patients fail to respond to 3 weeks of corticosteroid therapy after a probable iCAA diagnosis or if prolonged or potent immunosuppression is being considered.3143741 A biopsy specimen should be obtained from an area with radiological abnormality to improve the diagnostic yield, preferably from the cortex or leptomeninges.14296195 Evidence of amyloid-associated perivascular, transmural, or intramural vascular inflammation in the brain and leptomeninges enables a definite iCAA diagnosis.3742 However, early steroid treatment may obscure evidence of an inflammatory response in subsequent biopsy specimens, which may have happened to our patient 1.96 Moreover, a negative biopsy does not exclude an iCAA diagnosis due to the segmental distribution of pathological changes.

Urgent treatment directed at suppressing the inflammatory response should be considered in patients with probable iCAA to minimize therapeutic delays, even in the absence of pathological confirmation.2937 The ideal treatment regimen is not well defined: Castro Caldas et al.27 found no differences in outcome between patients treated using corticosteroids alone and those who also received cytotoxic agents. High-dose corticosteroids are most often employed (typically intravenous methylprednisolone at 1 g/day for 3–5 days followed by a prolonged tapering dose of oral steroids), but multiple immunomodulatory agents have been used, including cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, mycophenolate, and intravenous immunoglobulin.3714244756 As in our patient 2, treatment with cyclophosphamide or other immunosuppressants may be required more often in ABRA than in CAAri.320 As exemplified by our patient 1, long-term blood pressure control is important in preventing ICH due to noninflammatory CAA.53

iCAA is most commonly a monophasic illness. Relapses were observed in only 2 of 60 patients in 1 case series,82 but have been observed in as many as 25% of patients in other studies.244780 These occurred at 2–7 years after the initial presentation with white-matter vasogenic edema and/or infarcts colocalizing with CMBs in locations distinct from those associated with the initial presentation, as seen in our patient 2, and were often correlated with corticosteroid tapering or immunosuppression cessation.2224293747538286

The hemorrhagic manifestations typical of CAA may precede or follow iCAA development.29 Serial pathological evaluations (an initial biopsy at the time of iCAA diagnosis and an autopsy study 3 years later) have demonstrated severe CAA with perivascular inflammatory changes at presentation, with subsequent resolution of most of the inflammation but with the CAA persisting,23 which was similar to the temporal evolution of symptoms in our patient 1, who initially presented with iCAA, and then with ICH and imaging features of noninflammatory CAA 8 years later. Another case report53 described progressive neuropsychiatric decline over 5 years after an initial presentation with iCAA, with increased occurrence of cerebral microhemorrhages on MRI and finally lobar ICH, which were consistent with the typical clinical features of noninflammatory CAA. Overall, 13 of 113 patients with iCAA subsequently developed ICH, 8 of which occurred within the first 3 months. ICH incidence was not related to the number of microhemorrhages.36

Imaging evidence of inflammation may precede the development of symptoms consistent with iCAA. Tominaga et al.42 demonstrated an area of abnormal T2W signals without microhemorrhage on MRI that preceded symptom onset by 10 years. The white-matter lesion was subsequently enlarged when the CMBs developed later. The authors hypothesized that low-level inflammation, representing an immune reaction to Aβ deposition rather than to CMBs, may persist for many years before becoming overtly symptomatic. Lasocki and Kee97 similarly described CAAri initially presenting as asymptomatic brain lesions detected in routine imaging for melanoma surveillance in a patient treated using the immune checkpoint inhibitor nivolumab, who subsequently progressed to an acute confusional state despite cessation of the offending medication. The temporal evolution prior to symptom development indicated that inflammation related to amyloid deposition may be an important component of iCAA pathogenesis. Aβ-sensitive imaging with amyloid-binding PET ligands such as Pittsburgh compound B may therefore be considered in the evaluation of atypical subcortical white-matter lesions, even in the absence of clinical symptoms, although these ligands cannot be used to discriminate vascular from parenchymal Aβ deposits.4279 CSF anti-Aβ antibody testing may in the future play a role in noninvasive iCAA diagnoses.60

Prompt empirical treatment using high-dose corticosteroids with or without additional immunosuppressive therapy can improve clinical and radiological outcomes in patients with iCAA, even when initiated without pathological confirmation.142992 Clinical improvement may precede the associated imaging findings and is evident within a few weeks.7 Treatment is often prolonged but is effective: 55% of patients were asymptomatic or left with mild disability at 24 months after diagnosis in a previous study, although the mortality rate was not negligible.39 A systematic review found that 58% of patients improved with immunosuppressive treatment, whereas 28% declined further.27 Delayed treatment, a lack of CSF pleocytosis or leptomeningeal enhancement, and cognitive dysfunction were identified as negative prognostic factors.40425298 There were no significant differences between the prognoses of CAAri and ABRA.27

iCAA is probably an underdiagnosed condition that represents part of the spectrum of amyloid-related neurological disorders on a continuum between PACNS and CAA, and is closely related to ARIA following anti-Aβ antibody therapy. It can present with marked variations in clinical symptoms attributable to an inflammatory response to Aβ deposition in the brain. While generally acute or subacute in its time course, iCAA may be radiologically evident even years before symptom onset and can follow a relapsing course. The recent definition of the typical clinical and radiological syndromes has increased the recognition of CAA, which may eliminate the need for invasive histological sampling in at least some patients. Corticosteroids, and additional immunosuppressive therapy in some patients, improve clinical and radiological outcomes in iCAA.
