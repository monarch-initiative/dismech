# High-fidelity PAMless base editing of hematopoietic stem cells to treat chronic granulomatous disease.
**Authors:** Bzhilyanskaya V, Ma L, Liu S, Fox LR, Whittaker MN, Meis RJ, Choi U, Lawson A, Ma M, Theobald N, Burkett S, Sweeney CL, Lazzarotto CR, Tsai SQ, Lack JB, Wu X, Dahl GA, Malech HL, Kleinstiver BP, De Ravin SS
**Journal:** Sci Transl Med (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1126/scitranslmed.adj6779](https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.adj6779)

## Content

X-linked chronic granulomatous disease (X-CGD) is an inborn error of immunity (IEI) resulting from genetic mutations in the cytochrome b-245 beta chain (CYBB) gene. The applicability of base editors (BEs) to correct mutations that cause X-CGD is constrained by the requirement of Cas enzymes to recognize specific protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs). Our recently engineered PAMless Cas enzyme, SpRY, can overcome the PAM limitation. However, the efficiency, specificity, and applicability of SpRY-based BEs to correct mutations in human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) have not been thoroughly examined. Here, we demonstrated that the adenine BE ABE8e-SpRY can access a range of target sites in HSPCs to correct mutations causative of X-CGD. For the prototypical X-CGD mutation CYBB c.676C>T, ABE8e-SpRY achieved up to 70% correction, reaching efficiencies greater than three-and-one-half times higher than previous CRISPR nuclease and donor template approaches. We profiled potential off-target DNA edits, transcriptome-wide RNA edits, and chromosomal perturbations in base-edited HSPCs, which together revealed minimal off-target or bystander edits. Edited alleles persisted after transplantation of the base-edited HSPCs into immunodeficient mice. Together, these investigational new drug-enabling studies demonstrated efficient and precise correction of an X-CGD mutation with PAMless BEs, supporting a first-in-human clinical trial (NCT06325709) and providing a potential blueprint for treatment of other IEI mutations.

Ex vivo hematopoietic stem and progenitor stem cell (HSPC) gene therapy using self-inactivating lentiviral vectors (LVs) have provided clinical benefit for treatment of multiple inborn errors of immunity (IEI), including Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD), X-linked severe combined immunodeficiency (X-SCID), ADA-deficient SCID, and Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome ( 1– 4 ). However, potential vector insertional oncogenesis remains a concern as observed in cerebral adrenoleukodystrophy ( 5 ), or aberrant fusion transcripts such as high mobility group AT-hook 2 (HMGA2)+ clonal expansion in X-SCID ( 6 ) and β-thalassemia ( 7 ). LV-mediated constitutive gene over-expression and the lack of physiological regulation are also unacceptable for many IEIs due to mutations in tightly-regulated genes, such as CD40L-deficient hyper-IgM syndrome ( 8 , 9 ). Thus, there is a major unmet need for more efficient and safer gene therapies for IEIs. Recent progress in the development of genome editing technologies may help solve these key challenges. Next-generation CRISPR-Cas-based genome editing approaches permit user-specifiable genetic alterations to alter cellular function efficiently in HSPCs and engineered T cells ( 10– 13 ). By enabling the correction of the underlying genetic mutations, rather than overexpressing a corrective transgene, genome editing approaches could offer an improved safety profile with broad applicability for many IEIs.

CGD is an inherited disorder that is typically caused by a defective phagocyte nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase complex, resulting in reduced bactericidal reactive oxidative species for host defense. Patients with CGD experience recurrent invasive infections, hyperinflammation and inflammatory bowel disease ( 14– 16 ), with increased morbidity and early mortality from infection rates of 0.3/year, despite antimicrobial prophylaxis ( 15 ). Ex vivo gene therapy for X-linked CGD (X-CGD) to restore canonical gp91phox protein expression using lentiviral vector-transduced autologous HSPCs has treated 19 patients to date, three of whom have lost their grafts for reasons as yet unclear ( 2 ). Peripheral blood granulocytes from treated patients express gp91phox at about 1/3 of normal donor amounts due to a relatively weak chimeric promoter ( 2 , 17 ). Permanent semi-random vector insertion, the use of weak exogenous promoter to reduce risks of transactivating nearby oncogenes, and the lack of physiological regulation are all issues with this previous approach that provide rationale to explore other approaches including targeted genome editing.

We recently demonstrated that genome editing could be applied to restore endogenous gp91phox expression at physiological amounts ( 17 ). To correct the CYBB c.676C>T mutation on the X chromosome, we utilized a CRISPR-Cas9 nuclease to create a targeted DNA double-strand break (DSB) with subsequent repair via homology-directed repair (HDR) using a short 100-nt DNA donor. However, relatively inefficient HDR and adverse effects of Cas9 induced DSBs have been reported, including activation of the DNA damage response, unwanted insertion or deletion mutations at the on-target site, large chromosomal deletions, translocations, chromothripsis, and others ( 18– 24 ). Next-generation genome editing technologies including base editors (BEs) overcome some of these concerns by correcting genetic mutations without intentionally introducing DSBs ( 25 )( 26– 28 ). BEs are comprised of a Cas9 nickase fused to a DNA deaminase domain, which initiates the installation of C-to-T or A-to-G genome edits via cytidine base editors (CBE) or adenine base editors (ABE) respectively). A guide RNA (gRNA) is expressed in trans to direct the BE to a target site that is flanked by a protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM). The efficiency, specificity, and product purity of BEs have been engineered and improved ( 29 , 30 ), supporting their use in preclinical studies. However, the deaminase domain of BEs can typically only act within a short ‘edit window’ of the target site, restricting edit efficiency and flexibility ( 29 , 30 ). The edit window is typically nucleotides 4–9 of the spacer region of the target ( 29 ), which means that base edits are possible at a fixed distance from PAM of the target site. Thus, PAM availability is one of the major determinants of which regions of the genome are accessible for base editing.

The conventional PAM for Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9) is NGG, where ‘N’ is any nucleotide ( 31– 33 ). For base editing approaches to correct point mutations, there is often not an NGG PAM available in the correct position of the target site to appropriately place the edit window of the BE over the edit of interest. Thus, engineered SpCas9 ‘PAM variant’ enzymes or alternate Cas9 orthologs are required to enable more precise positioning of the deaminase domain. We recently engineered an SpCas9 enzyme named SpRY to have a broadened and highly permissive PAM, primarily editing sites with NRN PAMs but also tolerating NYN PAMs (where R is A or G; Y is C or T) ( 34 ). Experiments using SpRY nuclease revealed efficient editing in cell lines ( 34 ), plants ( 35– 37 ), zebrafish ( 38 ), and C. elegans ( 37 ). Furthermore, SpRY was successfully adapted as both a CBE and ABE, and recently was combined with a higher activity ABE8e adenine deaminase domain ( 39 ) to enable more efficient A-to-G edits ( 35 , 38 ).

Here, we optimized and applied ABE8e-SpRY and ABE8e-WT for ABE-mediated correction of two CYBB mutations for potential treatment of X-CGD. We achieved high on-target editing efficiencies that resulted in precise mutation correction in HSPCs, leading to phenotypic restoration in ex vivo edited primary cells. We also assessed and applied high-fidelity SpRY enzymes ( 40 , 41 ) to improve the safety profile by reducing off-target and bystander edits observed with conventional SpRY. Using X-CGD as an example, we showed that the flexible ABE8e-SpRY base editor is a promising approach for ex vivo precision treatment of IEIs that are caused by single nucleotide mutations.

To explore BE-mediated correction of IEI-causative mutations, we first generated a cell-based model using CBEs to install a common c.676C>T mutation within CYBB exon 7 that is known to cause X-CGD (Fig. 1, A to C). We utilized HEK 293T cells due to ease of transfection to simplify the evaluation of various BE constructs and gRNAs, leading to the creation of clonal cells bearing the specific CYBB c.676C>T mutation (Fig. 1C and fig. S1, A to E). Next, to evaluate ABE-mediated corrective approaches (Fig. 1D), we designed a series of gRNAs that tiled the ABE edit window across the target CYBB c.676C>T mutation (denoted by target nucleotide positions of A3 through A9 numbered from the PAM proximal end of the individual spacers for each gRNA; Fig. 1E). None of these target sites encoded an NGG PAM (Fig. 1E), precluding the use of conventional wild-type (WT) SpCas9-based ABEs. Therefore, we utilized ABE8e-SpRY that should in principle recognize each PAM encoded by the various target sites. Transfections were performed in a heterozygous CYBB c.676C>T cell line using plasmids encoding ABE8e-SpRY and each gRNA (fig. S2), which led to correction of the c.676C>T mutation for each gRNA (Fig. 1F). With certain gRNAs, we observed low unwanted ‘bystander’ editing of nearby adenosine or cytosine bases (Fig. 1, G and H, fig. S3A), similar to what has been previously described for ABEs when the C of a TC motif falls in positions 5 or 6 of an ABE target site ( 42 ).

A potential caveat of engineered SpCas9 enzymes with relaxed PAM tolerances is the enhanced likelihood of genome-wide off-target edits (due to an expanded genomic search space potentially leading to additional off-target site encounters) ( 36 , 43 ). To minimize potential off-target edits, we combined SpRY with previously described high-fidelity mutations in SpCas9 that reduce off-target editing at mismatched sites ( 40 , 41 ). When testing ABE8e-SpRY-HF1 and ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi constructs, with most gRNAs we observed similar on-target editing compared to ABE8e-SpRY (Fig. 1I). We also observed that both SpRY-HF1 and SpRY-HiFi constructs led to somewhat reduced bystander editing (fig. S3B). Because gRNA A7 harbors an NGT PAM, we also explored the use of other previously described engineered SpCas9 PAM variant enzymes that might have similar or improved genome-wide specificities relative to SpRY. We observed that SpG ( 34 ) and SpCas9-NRTH ( 44 ) offered similar or slightly higher on-target editing compared to SpRY, though also led to slightly increased bystander editing of nearby adenines (fig. S4), presumably due to their increased on-target efficiencies.

To conserve limited numbers of patient CD34+ HSPCs for pre-clinical validation studies, we optimized base editing in HSPCs from healthy human donors (HD) via a surrogate editing approach using a different gRNA. We envisioned evaluating editing in HD HSPCs using ABE8e-SpRY with the surrogate gRNA targeting an adenine located in an intronic splice acceptor site in CYBB intron 6 (named i6–2), located 3 nucleotides upstream of the CYBB exon 7 c.676C>T mutation (Fig. 2A). This surrogate target site permitted the use of the same base editor (ABE8e-SpRY), and targeting the splice site provided a functional readout via loss of gp91phox expression in base edited cells. This workflow should permit the evaluation of key parameters of HSPC culture and electroporation using HD HSPCs, where the optimized methods could later be translated into patient-derived cells.

Peripheral blood mobilized CD34+ HSPCs were electroporated with mRNA encoding ABE8e-SpRY and a synthetic i6(–2) gRNA, and we assessed A-to-G editing at CYBB intron 6 (–2). Cells were further cultured for 2 days following EP prior to harvest for analysis. We evaluated various HSPC culture and stimulation conditions prior to EP. Conventionally, CRISPR-Cas9 nucleases are delivered to HSPCs after 2 days of ex vivo culture with cytokines (including stem cell factor (SCF), thrombopoietin (TPO), Feline McDonough Sarcoma (fms)-related tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt3L) to induce cell proliferation for optimal delivery of genome editing reagents and cell-cycle dependent HDR-conversion to desired genetic sequence( 45– 47 ) (Fig. 2B). Because BEs do not rely on cell cycling ( 48 ), we wondered whether ex vivo cell culture duration could be shortened to preserve cell stemness and engraftment capability. However, the efficiency of base editing as determined by next-generation sequencing (NGS) was superior after 2 days ex vivo culture compared to a single-day culture (p = 0.0303) (Fig. 2C), suggesting the potential importance of genomic accessibility for efficient base editing. Auxiliary reagents such as UM171 that promote HSPC expansion ( 49 ) or the cytoprotectant glycerol, previously shown to be beneficial for CRISPR-Cas9 nuclease-mediated editing of HSPCs ( 11 ), did not substantially alter base editing efficiency in HSPCs (Fig. 2D).

Successful base editing of the surrogate CYBB i6 (–2) site at the splice acceptor preceding exon 7 can result in the disruption of CYBB expression of gp91phox protein (Fig. 2A). Because the CYBB gene is primarily expressed in mature phagocytes, CD34+ HSPCs from a male healthy donor were myeloid-differentiated ex vivo before evaluating gp91phox expression by flow cytometry. The loss of gp91phox expression in base-edited samples indicated successful editing and disruption of the splice acceptor (Fig. 2E). Because the editing process appeared to be well tolerated, we evaluated the impact of a repeat EP of ABE8e-SpRY mRNA with gRNA i6(–2) two days after the first EP. The cells underwent further ex vivo culture for two days after the 2nd EP before harvest for cryopreservation and analysis. HSPCs base-edited twice showed a greater loss of gp91phox expression (67%) compared to single EP (42%) myeloid-differentiated cells (Fig. 2E).

To determine if base-edited HSPCs engrafted and persisted long term, ABE8e-SpRY and gRNA i6(–2) treated HSPCs (BE-HSPCs) (n=3 HDs) were transplanted into immunodeficient NSG adult (6–8-week-old) mice (1.8 to 2.3 ×106 cells/animal, n=15 mice (1xEP), n=8 mice (2xEP), n=4 mice (HD)). Evaluation of recipient mouse peripheral blood for gp91phox+ myeloid cells revealed stable knock-down in gp91phox expression following transplant (Fig. 2F). Human CD45+ engraftment rates in spleen and bone marrow showed no difference between 2xEP (20.8%, n=8) or 1xEP (24.7%, n=15) (Fig. 2G). Myeloid-differentiated bone marrow cells revealed a slightly greater loss of gp91phox expression in CD15+ myeloid cells after 2x EP (with 67.6% gp91phox+) than 1x EP (42.2% gp91phox+) relative to 95.1% gp91phox+ in naïve HD cell, approximately a 60% knock-down in gp91phox expression (Fig. 2E). NADPH oxidase release of reactive oxidative species (ROS) upon stimulation reduces dihydrorhodamine to fluorescent rhodamine detectable by flow cytometric assay (DHR), which revealed similar knock-down in function in 1x- and 2x-EP HSPCs (Fig. 2H). Engrafted HSPCs differentiated into multi-lineage immune cells in normal distribution compared to HD HSPCs in bone marrow (Fig. 2I) and spleen (Fig. 2J).

After establishing our workflow in HD HSPCs, we then evaluated base editing in X-CGD patient-derived CD34+ HSPCs using ABE8e-SpRY mRNA and synthetic gRNAs A4-A7 targeting CYBB c.676C>T (Fig. 1E). Assessment of on-target editing after 1x or 2x EP revealed a range of efficiencies with gRNAs A4 (mean: 42.3%) and A5 (mean: 55.9%) achieving the highest A-to-G conversion (Fig. 3A), consistent with the results from the HEK 293T experiments (Fig. 1F). A previous study showed that two rounds of base-editing in human HSPCs yielded improved editing efficiency, albeit at the cost of initial reduction of cell viability ( 11 ). Confirmation of editing via NGS revealed a significant increase in editing efficiency with the 2x EP from 42.3% to 52.5% (Mann-Whitney test, p=0.0047) with c.676C>T gRNA A4, and a nonsignificant increase from 55.9% to 69.2% (Mann-Whitney test, p=0.1143) with c.676C>T gRNA A5 (Fig. 3A). In previous CRISPR nuclease experiments, chemical modification of DNA donor molecules or synthetic gRNAs improved editing efficiencies by reducing the rate of degradation of nucleic acid components ( 17 , 50 ). Given our all-RNA approach, we added RNase inhibitor to our electroporation mixture to dampen RNA degradation by contaminating RNases, which did not substantially improve editing efficiencies (Fig. 3B).

A driving motivation for targeted gene correction is the restoration of physiological expression of the target gene, compared to gene therapies that result in non-natural and suboptimal expression of transgenes via exogenous promoters and regulatory elements ( 2 ). Our assessment of myeloid-differentiated CD34+ HSPCs revealed expression of gp91phox protein at similar mean fluorescent intensities as healthy donors, suggesting a similar amount of protein expression on a per cell basis as HD cells, consistent with restoration of physiological expression (Fig. 3C). Correction of gp91phox expression also restored NADPH oxidase complex function for ROS production detected by a DHR flow cytometry assay (Fig. 3D). We observed that myeloid-differentiated HSPCs treated with ABE8e-SpRY mRNA and the c.676C>T A5 gRNA were 51.7% (n=13) gp91phox+ after 1x EP in vitro and 59.0% (n=5) gp91phox+ after 2x EP in vitro (Fig 3E).

To explore the versatility of our ABE8e-based editing approach, we next extended our approach to target a second CYBB mutation: c.1075 G>A in an X-CGD patient who does not have a matched donor for allogeneic stem cell transplant. For this mutation, there is an array of target sites bearing various PAMs (Fig. 4A). Although three of the target sites harbor conventional NGG PAMs that place the target adenine within the canonical ABE8e edit window, we also explored the use of PAM variant enzymes with other gRNAs, due to the presence of nearby adenines where bystander editing may create variants of uncertain functional consequences. In experiments using CYBB c.1075G>A patient-derived HSPCs electroporated with ABE8e-SpG with c.1075G>A gRNA A5, ABE8e-WT with gRNAs A6-A8, or ABE8e-SpRY with gRNA A9, we observed comparably high on-target editing ranging from about 80 to greater than 95% (Fig. 4B). With ABE8e-SpRY-HF1 and c.1075G>A gRNA A5, we observed lower editing (about 48%; Fig. 4B). We demonstrated that gp91phox expression in BE-treated CYBB c.1075G>A HSPCs (98.4%) was similar to healthy donors (94.2%) (Fig. 4c), and over 65% DHR+ (85% of normal HD) cells consistent with restored NADPH oxidase function (Fig. 4C). The high levels of gp91phox+ in myeloid differentiated cells increased with myeloid differentiation (d19 compared with d33) (Fig. 4D). This efficient base editing was associated with few bystander edits resulting in a non-synonymous ATC>GTC change resulting in I357V, and a non-synonymous GAC>GGC causing in D360G (Fig. 4E). Using cell surface markers (CD34, CD38, CD90 and CD133), we compared relative frequencies of CD34 subsets following 1x- or 2x-EP and noted a decreased trend in the CD34+38− subset but maintenance of the most primitive CD34+CD38−CD90+CD133+ subset proportions despite two-time EP in CYBBc.1075 mutation patient HSPCs (Fig. 4F). A similar experiment using HD cells electroporated with mRNA encoding ABE8e-SpRY and gRNA i6(–2) showed similar maintenance of CD34 subsets after 1x (green) or 2x (blue) electroporation (Fig. 4G).

Overall, the more efficient correction of CYBB c.1075G>A with ABE8e-WT and gRNA A8 (mean, 84.4%; n=7) compared to c.676C>T correction when using ABE8e-SpRY and gRNA A4 (mean, 49.5%; Mann-Whitney test, P = 0.0006; n=19) or gRNA A5 (mean, 51.7%; Mann-Whitney test, P = 0.0020; n=10) highlights the variability of editing between targets (possibly due to the greater binding affinity of wild-type SpCas9 ABEs compared to SpRY, gRNA folding, or other target-specific features), although both editing approaches yielded sufficiently high editing for potential clinical translatability (Fig. 4H) ( 36 , 51 , 52 ).

Quiescent primitive HSPCs account for long term engraftment following transplant and the ability to edit true HSPCs remains an important objective to ensure persistence of genetic correction. To compare the ability to edit more quiescent CD34 subsets, we treated healthy donor HSPCs with ABE8e-SpRY mRNA and the surrogate CYBB i6 (–2) gRNA, then sorted cells into CD34+ subpopulations based on cell surface markers CD34, CD38 ( 53 ), CD45RA ( 54 ), CD90( 55 ) and CD133. including into common myeloid progenitors (CMPs; CD34+, CD38+, CD45−), multi-potent progenitors (MPPs; CD34+, CD38−CD45RA−, CD90−), multi-lymphoid progenitors (MLPs; CD34+, CD38−, CD45RA+), and HSCs (CD34+, CD38−, CD45RA−, CD90+, CD133+) ( 46 ). Analysis of editing via NGS revealed similar editing efficiencies via the surrogate i6 (–2) gRNA across all sorted CD34+ subpopulations, including the most primitive CD34+CD38−CD45RA−CD90+CD133+ HSC subset after a single EP that was preserved, if not improved, following the 2x EP protocol (Fig. 4G). This suggested that the editing process did not substantially alter the distribution of CD34+ subsets that are often associated with cell stemness and ability to engraft.

To evaluate engraftment potential and long-term persistence of gene correction, human patient-derived CD34+ HSPCs bearing the CYBB c.676C>T mutation base edited with ABE8e-SpRY mRNA and gRNA A5 were transplanted into immunodeficient NSG mice that were analyzed by peripheral blood draws about every 4 weeks until final harvest at euthanasia at 20–23 weeks (Fig. 5A). Serial analysis of recipient mouse peripheral blood by flow cytometry for gp91phox+ cells revealed stable engraftment (about 10%) after an initial decline with about 60% of engrafted cells demonstrating restored gp91phox expression in mice transplanted with BE-treated patient HSPCs (Fig. 5, B to D). We observed much higher phenotypic correction in human CD45+ cells from mice transplanted with BE-treated cells compared with our previously reported Cas9 nuclease-based method using a single stranded DNA donor for HDR-mediated correction (Fig. 5D) ( 11 ). Peripheral blood NADPH oxidase activity at 20–23 weeks in recipient mice showed comparable DHR+ cell percentages from either 1x or 2x EP conditions that reached similar NADPH oxidase function to HD control cells (about 52% and about 46% for 1x and 2x EP, respectively, compared to about 60% for cells from mice transplanted with HD cells) (Fig. 5E).

At euthanasia (20–23 weeks), similar engraftment of HSPCs were observed in 1x EP cells treated with ABE8e-SpRY and CYBB c.676C>T gRNA A5 compared to naïve patient HSPCs (Fig. 5F), suggesting that electroporation or base editing did not interfere with engraftment in bone marrow (mean of 27% (1x EP, n=14mice) versus 37% (naive, n=7mice), n=2 experiments). Higher engraftment was observed with doubly electroporated HSPCs (about 61%, Fig. 5F).

Fifty-five percent of myeloid-differentiated bone marrow (BM) cells of mice transplanted with HSPCs treated with ABE8e-SpRY and CYBB c.676C>T gRNA A5 were positive for gp91phox expression and DHR (Fig. 5G). Of note, cells from mice treated with 1x and 2x EP HSPCs similarly expressed gp91phox, but the percentage of DHR+ cells trended higher from mice transplanted with 2x EP HSPCs (Fig. 5G). Edited HSPCs retained the ability to develop into immune cell lineages in peripheral blood (Fig. 5H) and bone marrow (Fig. 5I)

Similar phenotypic outcomes were observed for CYBB c.1075G>A patient HSPCs edited with ABE8e-WT mRNA and gRNA A8, except with higher mean correction at 54% (1xEP), 62% (2xEP) gp91phox compared to HD (84%); Fig. 5J). Mice transplanted with CYBB c.1075G>A base-edited HSPCs displayed restored gp91phox expression at 62% (1xEP), 39% (2xEP) compared to 89% (HD) and corresponding 17.7% (1xEP), 4.9% (2xEP) and 36% (HD) DHR+ in myeloid-differentiated bone marrow cells (Fig. 5K).

We investigated potential consequences of base editing HSPCs, including unintended edits at off-target sites located throughout the genome, unwanted edits of nearby DNA bases within the target site (bystander edits), and potential cellular toxicities or genomic instability caused by the base editor or electroporation protocol. To reduce the likelihood of unwanted genome-wide edits, we tested the compatibility of ABE8e-SpRY-HF1 and ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi with gRNA A5 in CYBB c.676C>T HSPCs. Unlike in HEK 293T cells where we observed comparable performance between the SpRY and SpRY high-fidelity variants via plasmid-based transfection (Fig. 1G), when electroporating a typical dose of ABE mRNA into HSPCs, we observed that the high-fidelity variants resulted in somewhat reduced on-target correction (Fig. 6A). When electroporating a 2x dose of ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi mRNA, we then observed higher on-target correction more comparable to ABE8e-SpRY editing efficiency (Fig. 6B). The higher dose of ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi mRNA (400 ug/mL EP) retained undetectable bystander editing (Fig. 6B). These results suggested that the high fidelity Cas9 enzymes were compatible with ABE8e-SpRY when delivered via mRNA into primary human HSPCs, though their use may necessitate an increased dose of mRNA to achieve comparable editing to ABE8e-SpRY. Of note, mutation repair remained stable in spleen and bone marrow following transplantion into mice (Fig. 6C). In experiments in CYBB c.1075G>A HSPCs, the use of a high fidelity ABE8e-SpCas9-HF1 mRNA along with the c.1075 gRNA A8 led to comparable on-target correction compared with ABE8e-WT at an equivalent mRNA dose (Fig. 6D).

To nominate potential genome-wide off-target sites, we performed the CHANGE-seq assay that captures gRNA/Cas9 nuclease-dependent DNA DSBs from a library of fragmented genomic DNA molecules ( 56 ). We used genomic DNA from patient-derived CYBB c.676C>T cells to ensure the detection of the on-target site as a positive control. (Fig. 7A). Across replicate experiments using genomic DNA from two separate donors, we identified 32 potential off-target candidates with consistent off-target nomination between both genomic DNA samples (specificity ratio=0.75; Fig. 7A and Table S1). CHANGE-seq experiments using the same patient genomic DNA but with the high fidelity variant SpRY-HF1 nuclease identified generally fewer off-targets and substantially reduced unwanted cleavage of a prevalent off-target site with ABE8e SpRY on chromosome 4 (Fig. 7A and fig.S5, A and B). Most of the CHANGE-seq-nominated sites were located in intergenic regions or introns and therefore less likely affect protein expression or function (Table S1). To determine if the nominated sites exhibited bona fide off-target editing in HSPCs, we performed targeted multiplex amplicon sequencing of the on-target site and the top 50 CHANGE-seq nominated off-target sites using genomic DNA from CYBB c.676C>T HSPCs treated with ABE8e-SpRY or ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi mRNA and gRNA A5 (Fig. 7B, fig. S6, Table S2). With ABE8e-SpRY, we observed detectable off-target editing at about 21 of the 50 off-target sites (Fig. 7B). Utilizing the higher fidelity enzyme variant ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi substantially reduced detection of off-target editing to 6 remaining off-target sites (Fig. 7B and fig. S6). Four of the off-target sites were located in intergenic regions, and the other 2 were located in introns of the LAMB1 gene and a putative long noncoding RNA (~250 bp and ~2kb from exons, respectively; Table S2).

Next, we performed whole exome sequencing (WES) to survey all protein-coding sequences in the human genome ( 57 ). The ability to achieve very high sequencing coverage via WES (500–1,000x at~$1,000) is superior compared to whole genome sequencing (30x at ~$10,000) because the exome comprises only about 1% of the genome ( 58 ). WES was performed on genomic DNA from HSPCs from two patients carrying CYBB c.676C>T and treated with ABE8e-SpRY and gRNA A5, alongside their respective naïve untreated controls (fig. S7A). The WES analysis achieved about 500x coverage for each of two different library preparation protocols that together provided a total of about 1,000x combined coverage for each treated sample, enabling detection of consistent off-target editing observed at efficiencies below 1% (at least 3 supporting reads in 500x mean coverage).

To analyze the WES data, we first applied a pipeline designed specifically for low frequency variant calling (Agilent SureCall). Using human genome reference (hg38), about 60,000 variant calls were made in all samples, including treated and untreated samples (fig. S7B). Most of the variant calls were germline variants Because they were present in the patient untreated naïve samples. After filtering out the germline variants from the treated sample, tens of thousands of variants remained in the treated sample (fig. S7B), as previously reported ( 59 ). However, over 80% of these private variants are known single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). These are likely germline variants that failed to be filtered out and not true BE-derived off-targets.

Although sensitive detection of potential off-target editing is critical for safety, false-positive variant calls can equally be detrimental. Single-sample variant calling fails to jointly account for variant quality and evidence in the control and edited samples, whereas germline style calling (GATK Best Practices) makes allele frequency assumptions that off-target variants will often violate. To more effectively balance sensitivity and precision, we used the genome-seek workflow (https://github.com/OpenOmics/genome-seek) to perform variant calling jointly in pairs of control and edited samples. This pipeline removed most false positive mutation calls and confirmed only one mutation at chr12:57727421 in the ArfGAP With GTPase Domain, Ankyrin Repeat And PH Domain 2 (AGAP2) gene that was absent in naïve but present in all 4 treated libraries [both CS (Covaris shearing) and EF (enzyme fragmentation)]. The frequency of this variant, however, was low (3/751, 12/760, 3/722, and 2/755) in all treated samples. This may represent a bona fide BE-induced variant, or sequencing error/noise due to lower coverage (100–200x) in the naïve samples.

Our WES analysis detected almost no off-target edits and confirmed efficient on-target conversion of the mutant T allele to the WT C allele in treated samples (>80% variant allele frequency, VAF). The WES data also identified a bystander edit 2 bp upstream of the target base as shown by NGS of the target site (Fig. 7C). The bystander edit affected the intronic splice acceptor site in CYBB intron 6 (named i6–2) that was targeted in our HD model.

To evaluate potential off-target RNA edits that have been reported previously ( 60 ), a transcriptome analysis by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of CGD BE-HSPCs from 2 donors were compared with naïve unmanipulated HSPC samples. This transcriptome analysis did not identify any off-target edits at sites remote from the intended on-target site. At the target CYBB transcript, although most of the exon-exon junctions were normal, there was a low frequency of abnormal junctions of nearby exons (Ex5-Ex6, Ex7 to Ex8) to create Ex5 to Ex8 junctions. This exon-skipping resulted from a bystander edit on the splice site expected to abrogate gp91phox expression in those cells (~1%), which would effectively reverse the phenotypic impact of correction in ~1% of edited cells (Fig. 7D). RNA-seq of the two CGD BE-HSPCs and naïve CGD HSPCs found no evidence of off-target RNA A-to-G edits compared to the naïve sample. The A-to-G variants that were observed were increased in chromosome 6 that corresponds to polymorphisms in the HLA, region detected in both the BE and naïve samples.

To detect if BE treatment caused large structural alterations, we performed conventional karyotyping, which did not detect any large chromosomal rearrangements in HSPCs treated with ABE8e-SpRY and the CYBB c.676C>T A5 gRNA (Fig. 8A). We then performed optical genome mapping (OGM) to detect genomic alterations using labeled long DNA molecules (>300 kb) to provide a high-resolution genome-wide analysis of large structural variants (SVs). We established the sensitivity of the OGM assay at below 5% VAF at 1.5 terrabase (tb) coverage and 1% at 5 tb by spiking in genomic DNA from the well-characterized Kasumi-1 acute myeloblastic leukemia cell line, which contains an 8;21 chromosome translocation. Most of the SVs observed in the treated sample (Fig. 8B) were filtered out after comparison with the naïve sample, leaving one SV. Upon manual examination, this single SV was also found in the naïve sample, indicating that no SVs unique to the base-edited sample were identified within the detection limit of OGM (about 1%) (Fig. 8C). We also performed a single cell electrophoresis comet assay that detects DNA damage such as a loss of DNA supercoiling, which causes formation of a ‘comet tail’ during electrophoresis. Other factors such as cell membrane status may also affect comet formation. At 2 days after EP, CD34+ HSPCs treated with positive control CRISPR-Cas9 nuclease and gRNA ( 17 ), or puromycin, a known cytotoxic agent, resulted in abundant comets (Fig. 8D). Comets were also observed in CYBB c.676C>T HSPCs treated with ABE8e-SpRY and the A5 gRNA, although to a lesser extent than nuclease-treated cells, and the abundance of comets decreased rapidly by day 3 and resolved by day 5 (Fig. 8E). In contrast, Cas9 nuclease-treated cells continued to display damage, except when treated with hu-genetic suppressor element (GSE)-56 ( 46 ) ( 61 ), and nearly all puromycin-treated cells exhibited comets (Fig. 8E). The transient detection of comets in ABE-treated HSPCs may be in response to the nickase activity of the base editors or endonucleolytic repair following deamination.

Here we developed efficient and precise base editing approaches to correct two CYBB mutations in patient-derived HSPCs. The use of ABEs can many of the challenges associated with conventional lentiviral gene therapy or when using CRISPR-Cas nucleases with HDR donor templates. ABE-mediated editing results in improved cell viability, engraftment, and persistence of edited cells. The PAMless base editor SpRY permits broader access to target sites encoding alternate PAMs, whereas high-fidelity mutations can improve safety by minimizing unintended off-target edits. Transient delivery of fidelity-enhanced ABE-SpRY mRNA and gRNA via ex vivo electroporation into patient HSPCs resulted in durable on-target editing with minimal unwanted cis bystander or genome-wide off-target edits. The efficiency and safety of the approach presented here, combined with the flexibility of high fidelity SpRY-based ABEs, could support the extensibility of this blueprint for the development of genetic treatments for other IEI-causative single nucleotide mutations. Based on this work, we have initiated an open label, phase I/II clinical trial (NCT06325709).

BEs offer a potentially less genotoxic approach to install single nucleotide changes without intentionally generating DSBs or the requirement for an exogenous donor template. Many initial challenges of BEs have been overcome through iterative engineering ( 29 , 30 ), including designs to improve on-target base editing efficiency( 30 , 39 , 62 ) to minimize Cas-dependent and -independent off-target DNA and RNA editing, to reduce bystander editing, and to expand the applicability of BEs to a broader range of genomic sites ( 60 , 63 ) ( 34 , 64 , 65 ). Our data demonstrated the efficacy and precision of base editing in HSPCs and suggest that this approach offers advantages compared to LV-transduced cells( 66 ), including the avoidance of the risks of LV integration near oncogenes.

Delivery of ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi via mRNA into HSPCs ex vivo largely mitigated most of the off-target editing events observed with ABE8e-SpRY. Deep WES or targeted sequencing of CHANGE-seq-nominated sites revealed no evidence of off-target editing in coding regions of the genome and only lower efficiency off-target editing at a small number of off-target sites with ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi. Although the ABE8e-SpRY-HF1 construct had slightly attenuated on-target activity compared to ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi, it is possible that it could have further minimized off-target editing. WES reliably detected both the on-target edit and a bystander edit 2 base pairs upstream of the CYBB c.676C>T target/mutation. A caveat to interpreting this data is that the limited sampling of cells in a bulk, highly polyclonal population (detection sensitivity of 0.4) may not be adequate to detect random, cell-specific mutations. Previous attempts to address this limitation have performed WES using large numbers of single cell clonal colonies ( 67 ) or in clonally shrunken populations ( 68 ). Tens of thousands of mutation variants were reported following base editing; however, because similar numbers of substitutions were observed The WES analysis here demonstrated multiple potential sources for false positive variant calls, including germline mutations that failed to be filtered out, which can be addressed using a more stringent bioinformatics pipeline. Although clonally shrunken populations of cells from limited colonies offer greater sensitivity to detect variants, such analyses remain susceptible to overcalling from failure to filter out germline mutations.

The transcriptome analysis detected no off-targets other than an edit to a splice site in CYBB intron 6 that resulted in exon-skipping and aberrant exon 5-exon 8 junction at a low (~1%) efficiency. The absence of detectable transcriptome OTs in this study contrasts with previous results ( 60 ), likely attributable to transient mRNA delivery compared to plasmid delivery followed by sorting for highly transfected cells with robust BE expression.

As further studies demonstrate the safety and efficacy of BE-mediated correction of mutations, wider accessibility of these gene editing therapies is dependent an evolving regulatory framework to ease financial and time costs associate with each bespoke therapy. We hope that once a technology is proven safe and efficacious for correcting a small number of mutations for a disease, the same general approach should be extensible to other mutations but with less regulatory scrutiny outside of the gRNA- or enzyme-specific safety profiling. Once our experimental approach was established to validate the efficiency and safety of BE-mediated correction of CYBB c.676C>T, our optimization of a second approach to correct CYBB c.1075G>A proceeded more rapidly. Towards that goal, our experimental blueprint of critical preclinical processes for establishing functional gene correction in HSPCs should be translatable to other mutations, with the hope of simplifying subsequent therapeutic edits for X-CGD and other hematopoietic diseases.

There are some limitations of our study. Although we observed few off-target edits, the continued development of methods to carefully monitor potential adverse events following infusion of BE-treated HSPCs will be necessary to ensure the safety of this new class of gene editing therapies (for example, how to measure biological consequences of off-target edits). Future studies will be required to determine the functional and immune consequences of correcting a nonsense mutation that results in expression of a full-length protein not previously surveilled by the immune system. Furthermore, it is possible that an inflamed bone marrow milieu in CGD patients may impair engraftment of edited cells, but extensive studies and intervention following the loss of lentivirally-transduced cells in the gene therapy trial for X-CGD did not support this possibility( 2 ). Close monitoring of treated participants will improve our understanding of marrow versus immune environment for engraftment of neo-antigen expressing cells following genetic therapies. Lastly, beyond the use of BEs for genome editing, other more nascent technologies capable of precise mutation correction may be useful for treating IEI-causative mutations including. prime editors( 69 , 70 ), DNA polymerase editors( 71 ), click editors ( 72 ), etc. BEs are currently more efficacious than these other approaches but may carry risks of genotoxic consequences resulting from overexpression of deaminase domains.

In summary, we demonstrated highly efficient and specific base-editing to correct two distinct X-CGD mutations. Our data highlight the versatility of fidelity-enhanced SpRY base editors, enabling previously inaccessible edits and avoidance of bystander edits in patient-derived HSPCs. For diseases where biomarkers or molecular assays for protein function exist, such as most IEIs, the degree to which bystander edits confound or diminish the intended correction can be readily assessed. The combined advantages of base editing over previous nuclease- or lentiviral-based methods may lead to a new regulatory framework to streamline the optimization of genetic therapies using base-edited HSPCs to treat rare and common IEIs.

We hypothesized that the adenine base editor (ABE) ABE8e-SpRY-ABE8e could more efficiently correct mutations that cause X-linked chronic granulomatous disease (X-CGD) than previous CRISPR-Cas9 nuclease and donor template approaches. Our objective was to investigate the efficiency, safety, and applicability of SpRY-BEs to correct two A>G mutations that cause X-CGD for eventual translation to the clinical setting. Blood samples were obtained with ethical approval (NIAID protocols 05-I-0213 and 94-I-0073), and informed consent from all patients was received in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All samples had at least three biological replicates unless stated otherwise due to limited patient material. The novelty and investigational nature of this research did not allow predefined power calculations for sample sizes used. Due to the highly specific nature of the experiments, the operator conducting these experiments was not blinded to the samples’ conditions and samples were not randomized.

Editing efficiency in bulk transfected cells was assessed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) essentially as previously detailed ( 34 ) using PCR round 1 and round 2 primers (Table S3). Next-generation sequencing was performed to a depth of ~1,000+ reads per sample and base editing outcomes were analyzed using CRISPResso2 ( 73 ) in single amplicon mode with the following commands: --guide_seq [SPACER] -w 20 --cleavage_offset −10 --base_editor_output --conversion_nuc_from A --conversion_nuc_to G.

To correct the CYBB c.676C>T mutation in our heterozygous HEK 293T cell line, cells were seeded and transfected, genomic DNA extracted, and genomic loci amplified and sequenced as described above. Transfections contained 70 ng of ABE expression plasmid (Table S4) and 30 ng of a gRNA expression plasmid (Table S3). On-target editing efficiency from experiments using the heterozygous CYBB c676C>T cell line was analyzed using CRISPResso2 as described above and calculated at the heterozygous c.676 base as: (G% in treated cells – G% in unedited cells) / (100% – G% in unedited cells); and otherwise calculated as % edited base for all other positions in the spacer. The A-to-G and C-to-T/G/A base editing efficiencies at adenines and cytosines in positions 1 through 12 of the target site spacer (counting from the PAM distal end of the spacer) were determined and graphed using GraphPad Prism (v. 10.2.3).

Human CD34+ HSPCs were collected from healthy male donors or male patients with X-CGD after written informed consent (NIAID protocols 05-I-0213 and 94-I-0073).

Cryopreserved CD34+ HSPCs were thawed and cultured in StemSpan II supplemented with SCF, TPO and FLT3L (100ng/mL) and UM171 (35nmol/mL) for two days before base editing. Base editor mRNA (~400 μg/mL; CELLSCRIPT™) and gRNA (~200 μg/mL; Synthego) were delivered by electroporation (Program HPSC#3, MaxCyte Biosystems ATx instrument) to ~50–200×106 cells/mL. Base editor mRNAs were in vitro transcribed from plasmid templates and incorporated a 100% substitution of the UTP content with YTP (pseudoUTP) (CELLSCRIPT™). IVT mRNAs were post-transcriptionally capped to ≥95% and poly(A) tailed to >200-A’s and subsequently purified for removal of double-stranded RNA content. HSPCs were cultured for an additional two days before harvest, or in some samples, a second electroporation with base editing reagents for a 2x-BE. For optimization studies, RNase inhibitor was added to EP mix at concentrations as indicated, and at 1.6×103U/ml for all other studies. Glycerol was used at 2% final concentration.

Genomic DNA was extracted by DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (QIAGEN) from HSPCs at 2–5 days post-EP for analysis of editing efficiency by high throughput sequencing (Illumina). Identification of candidate off-targets sites was performed in vitro on male X-CGD CD34+ cells using CHANGE-seq for large-scale genome-wide profiling of off-targets ( 56 ). Targeted deep sequencing was performed using primers described in Table S3.

Myeloid differentiation of CD34+ HSPCs by culture in IMDM + 20% FSC for 10 −14 days before evaluation for gp91phox expression and NADPH oxidase function that are physiologically expressed only in mature phagocytes as previously described ( 74 , 75 ). Expression of yH2AX (pS139) was performed using the Apoptosis, DNA Damage and Cell Proliferation Kit (BD Biosciences) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

CD34+ HSPCs (CGD or HD naïve, base-edited) were transplanted into six- to eight-week-old female or male immunodeficient NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ mice (The Jackson Laboratory, JAX5557) (NIAID Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under animal use protocol LCIM-1E). Ideally, 1–2 million cells per animal were transplanted with busulfan conditioning 20mg/kg 24 hours prior to transplant, and 3 to 5 mice per group. Analysis was performed at 8-, 12- and 18–26-weeks after transplant on peripheral blood and bone marrow (18–26 weeks). Complete blood counts were monitored following transplant, and animal welfare formally documented to assess tolerability of transplants with BE-HSPCs. Experimenters were not blinded to the sample conditions injected into mice, and mice were randomized to treatment groups.

For off-target validation studies, CYBB c.676C>T CGD patient HSPCs were edited via electroporation (as described above) using ABE8e-SpRY or ABE8e-SpRY-HiFi mRNA and gRNA A5 or remained unedited (naïve). gDNA was extracted (DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit; Qiagen). The on-target site and top 50 CHANGE-seq nominated off-target sites (Tables S1 and S2) were analyzed via rhAmpSeq according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Integrated DNA Technologies), prior to sequencing on an Illumina sequencing platform. CRISPResso2 was used to quantify base editing efficiencies. Using the amplicon primer coordinates from the rhAmpSeq pool, the amplicon sequences for each of the on- and off-target sites were extracted using the UCSC genome browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/). For each site, the chromosome position of the amplicon was inputted as a custom track and the amplicon sequence was obtained using the table browser function, selecting the sequence annotation output format with hg38 assembly as the chosen dataset. These amplicon sequences were used for analysis of the rhAmpSeq data from a 300c NextSeq sequencing run, as input into CRISPRessoPooled (v. 2.0.30) to calculate the efficiency of base editing using the following commands: --min_reads_to_use_region 100 -w 20 --cleavage_offset −10 --base_editor_output --conversion_nuc_from A --conversion_nuc_to G. For both on- and off-target sites, the quantification window nucleotide percentage table outputs were merged across treated and untreated control samples to streamline analysis. The A-to-G and C-to-T/G/A base editing efficiencies at adenines and cytosines in positions 1 through 12 of the target site spacer (counting from the PAM distal end of the spacer) were determined and graphed using GraphPad Prism (v. 10.2.3).
