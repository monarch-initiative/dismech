# Novel Variants in VARS2 Demonstrate the Phenotypic Variability of a Rare Mitochondriopathy That Responds to Valine Supplementation.
**Authors:** Marquez J, Viviano S, Rahman F, Strohbehn SD, Allworth A, Perez N, University of Washington Center for Rare Disease Research, Undiagnosed Diseases Network, Saneto RP, Anna S 1st, Penón Portmann M, Blue EE, Glass IA, Deniz E, Shelkowitz E
**Journal:** J Inherit Metab Dis (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jimd.70053](https://doi.org/10.1002/jimd.70053)

## Content

Mitochondriopathies are a diverse group of disorders caused by disruption of typical mitochondrial function. Heterogenous in nature, many of these disorders arise due to variants in genes encoding key mitochondrial proteins involved in transcription and translation of mitochondrial machinery. One such gene, VARS2, encodes a mitochondrial aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase that catalyzes the attachment of valine to its cognate tRNA molecule. Bi-allelic variants in VARS2 have been linked to several forms of mitochondrial encephalopathies or cardiomyoencephalopathies. While associated clinical phenotypes vary, they can include developmental delays, axial hypotonia, limb spasticity, and epilepsy. Here, we describe three additional clinical cases of VARS2-related mitochondriopathy with sequencing-confirmed variants in VARS2 that illustrate the phenotypic variability of this disorder. These include three novel variants: Lys225Glu, Cys281Tyr, and Leu732Cysfs*29. We further assess the pathogenicity and severity of the effects of the variants underlying these cases in a Xenopus model of disease through assaying both cardiac function and brain size. In addition, we use this model of VARS2 loss of function to assess the therapeutic potential of previously proposed amino acid supplementation. Through this approach, we demonstrate that the beneficial effects of valine supplementation in VARS2 mitochondriopathy may be dependent on residual enzyme activity.

Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (ARS) are a family of enzymes that play a pivotal role in protein synthesis. They catalyze the esterification reaction that links a transfer RNA (tRNA) with its cognate amino acid, matching the anticodon triplet of the tRNA ( Chaliotis et al. 2017 ; Rajendran et al. 2018 ; Gomez and Ibba 2020 ). This process, known as “charging” the tRNA, is essential for efficient and accurate protein synthesis. The human genome contains 37 genes that encode unique ARS proteins ( Chaliotis et al. 2017 ; Rajendran et al. 2018 ). These can be further divided between ARS proteins functioning in nuclear encoded gene translation and those functioning in mitochondrial encoded gene translation. Mitochondrial aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase proteins (mt-ARS) are a group of nuclear-encoded enzymes that facilitate conjugation of each of the 20 amino acids to its cognate tRNA molecule within the mitochondrion ( Szymanski et al. 2001 ; Brindefalk et al. 2007 ). Diseases related to mt-ARS deficiencies are associated with syndromes that predominantly affect the central nervous system and can produce highly characteristic MRI patterns, such as in DARS2, EARS2, and AARS2 leukodystrophies ( Steenweg et al. 2012 ; Pauly et al. 2021 ; Stellingwerff et al. 2021 ; Roux et al. 2021 ). Despite carrying out a function that is common to all known cells, recessive variants in the genes encoding these enzymes can cause a diverse range of either tissue specific or multi-system disorders ( Fine et al. 2019 ; Turvey et al. 2022 ).

Combined oxidative phosphorylation deficiency 20 (OMIM # 612802) is caused by biallelic variants in the VARS2 gene. This gene encodes the mt-ARS protein that catalyzes the attachment of valine to its cognate tRNA molecule upon translation of mitochondrial genes ( Nagase et al. 2001 ; Bonnefond et al. 2005 ; Meiklejohn et al. 2013 ; Garin et al. 2020 ). Bi-allelic variants in VARS2 lead to mt-ARS dysfunction and cause multisystemic disease. Recent case reports and case series have improved our understanding of the clinical presentation of this disorder (Taylor et al. 2014; Baertling et al. 2017 ; Alsemari et al. 2017 ; Bruni et al. 2018 ; Ma et al. 2018 ; Uittenbogaard et al. 2019; Begliuomini et al. 2019 ; Chin et al. 2019 ; Friedman et al. 2019; Kušíková et al. 2021 ; Constante et al. 2024; Strader et al. 2024). These have been described overarchingly as encephalopathies or cardiomyoencephalopathies. Resultant pathologies often also include progressive developmental delay, axial hypotonia, limb spasticity, and intractable epilepsy. Interestingly, a manifestation of VARS2 related mitochondriopathy that appears to occur more commonly than in other ARS deficiencies is pulmonary hypertension ( Itoh et al. 2019 ; Fine et al. 2019 ; Pauly et al. 2021 ; Turvey et al. 2022 ; Kalotay et al. 2023 ).

While clinical data on the phenotypic manifestations of VARS2 mitochondriopathy are burgeoning, our mechanistic understanding of this disorder is still in its nascency. Vertebrate models of this disease have provided validation of the pathogenicity of VARS2 loss-of-function in the multisystemic phenotype observed in patients. Knockdown of vars2 in embryonic Danio rerio results in a heart failure phenotype despite typical specification of the cardiac ventricle and atrium based on gene expression ( Kayvanpour et al. 2022 ). Cell culture models have in turn revealed that absence of VARS2 leads to upregulation of the integrated stress response ( Kayvanpour et al. 2022 ).

In our efforts to exemplify the varied phenotypic presentation of VARS2 mitochondriopathy, we describe 4 individuals from 3 unrelated families with 4 novel variants in VARS2 that contribute to bi-allelic VARS2 dysfunction. There is limited though compelling evidence in other ARS deficiencies that cognate amino acid supplementation may modify disease severity ( Kok et al. 2021 ; Oswald et al. 2023 ). We hypothesized that a vertebrate model of disease would provide a platform to better assess the benefits of such an approach. In our novel vars2 loss of function Xenopus model, we observed a response to valine supplementation for amelioration of cardiomyopathy and survival in a variant dependent manner.

Genomic DNA for probands underwent either clinical exome (I-III-4 and III-III-3) or genome (II-IV-6) sequencing and copy number variant calling. Sequencing reads were aligned to reference sequences based on NCBI RefSeq transcripts and the human genome GRCh37/hg19 (Families I and III) or GRCh38/hg38 (Family II). The identified variants in VARS2 were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. For consistency, throughout this manuscript we describe variants in VARS2 based in relation to NM_020442.6.

Clinical information and imaging were obtained from review of medical charts with permission provided by individuals or legal guardians following informed consent. Included photographs were provided following explicit permission provided by an individual’s legal guardians following informed consent.

Xenopus tropicalis were raised, housed, and cared for in-house. We induced ovulation and collected embryos by in vitro fertilization as previously described ( del Viso and Khokha 2012 ). Embryos were raised in 1/9 x modified Ringer’s solution (MR) (0.1 M NaCl, 1.8 mM KCl, 2.0 mM CaCl2, 1.0 mM MgCl2, and 5.0 mM HEPES-NaOH) þ gentamicin. Staging of Xenopus tadpoles was performed as previously described ( Kakebeen and Wills 2019 ; Zahn et al. 2022 ).

We injected 1-cell Xenopus embryos with 800 pg sgRNA along with 1.6 ng Cas9 to target vars2 based on reference sequence XM_004917033.4 designed to generate F0 knockout embryos (CRISPR1: negative strand target sequence 5′-GGTGTCCACATTGTCCCGTAGGG-3′ in exon 2, CRISPR2: positive strand target sequence 5’-AGGACCAATTCACCAGACGCAGG-3’ in exon 9). Guide RNA was designed based on sequence information accessed through Xenbase ( Fisher et al. 2023 ) and CRISPRScan ( Moreno-Mateos et al. 2015 ). For rescue experiments vars2 mRNA was generated from XM_004917033.4 in a pCSDest backbone. mRNAs were generated using the SP6 mMessage machine kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Variants were generated using the Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (New England BioLabs) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

We amplified the sequence adjacent to the CRISPR cut site using a polymerase chain reaction with either Phusion HighFidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB) or Platinum SuperFi II DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher). Resultant products were sequenced and analyzed via Synthego browser-based platform (ice.synthego.com).

Stage 45 Xenopus embryos were immobilized and imaged using either a Thorlabs Ganymede 900 nm spectral domain–OCT system or a Thorlabs Telesto 1325 nm spectral domain–OCT system. The cardiac 2D cross-sectional images were obtained as a continuous scan. The maximum and minimum distance between opposing ventricular walls was identified and measured using Thorlabs software as previously described ( Deniz et al. 2017 ; Marquez et al. 2025 ). The values presented for fractional shortening are the average of 3 cardiac contraction cycles.

Stage 45 embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, 0.1% Tween-20, in phosphate buffered saline 4 hours at room temperature and then washed in 0.1% Tween-20, in phosphate buffered saline. For brain imaging, animals were stained based as previously described with a β-Tubulin primary antibody (DSHB, clone E7, 1:100) and a fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody (Life Technologies) ( Naert et al. 2021 ). Tadpole brains were imaged on a Zeiss AxioZoom stereomicroscope with a Zeiss AxioCam. Brain size was measured as a two-dimensional area in FIJI ( Schindelin et al. 2012 ).

Valine, glycine, or phenylalanine was diluted to 100 μM, 250 μM, or 500 μM in 1/9 x modified ringer media with gentamicin prior to incubation of embryos in this medium following fertilization and injection. Fresh medium of the same supplemented concentration was added daily.

Power calculations were conducted to determine sample sizes for evaluating large effect sizes of vars2 depletion and rescue of this phenotype compared to vars2 depletion. Statistical analysis and tests carried out were determined prior to experimentation. The statistical significance of fractional shortening and brain area were tested using Mann-Whitney U tests (p-values less than 0.05 were deemed significant). ANOVA with post-hoc tukey tests were used to compare control to all other groups in rescue and supplementation experiments.

Individual I-III-4 (Figure 1) came to medical attention prenatally. Prenatal echocardiography demonstrated fetal bradycardia and systolic heart failure. He was delivered via Cesarean section at 35 weeks and 2 days completed gestation. He was small for gestational age (6.6 percentile, Z-score: −1.5). His head circumference was 32 centimeters at last evaluation on day of life 1 (Z-score: −0.1339 adjusted for gestational age). He required immediate intubation for respiratory failure following delivery. He had persistent lactic acidosis (range: 9.1->20.0 mmol/L on day of life 1 and day of life 2 respectively with a reference range of 0.5–2.2 mmol/L) and clinical as well as electroencephalographic signs of encephalopathy. Cranial ultrasound demonstrated a small caliber left ventricle (which can constitute a normal variant of morphology) and left periventricular echogenicity (Figure 1). An echocardiogram demonstrated severe pulmonary hypertension and biventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. A chromosomal SNP-microarray was non-diagnostic although it demonstrated widespread loss of heterozygosity (LOH) of approximately 66 mega-bases across the autosomes. Clinical trio exome sequencing was pursued based on a suspicion of an underlying inborn error of metabolism. This identified a homozygous likely pathogenic variant in VARS2 (NM_020442.6; c.1168G>A; p.Ala390Thr) in a region of LOH (Table 1). No other variants of likely or uncertain significance were reported. Electron transport chain (ETC) testing was performed on skeletal muscle biopsy tissue and demonstrated decreased complex I and complex IV levels. He died from complications of severe pulmonary hypertension at 5 days of age.

Individual II-IV-6 (Figure 1) was born at 39 weeks completed gestation to parents who are first-cousins. Her head circumference was 51.7 centimeters at last evaluation at age 7 (Z-score: +0.24). She required a 3-month neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) stay due to respiratory distress that required intubation following delivery. She was found to have lactic acidosis (range: 0.71–7.34 mmol/L on day of life 7 and day of life 1 respectively with a reference range of 0.7–2.1 mmol/L) and required extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for right ventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (interventricular septal thickness Z-score: +9.5 diastole, +7.8 systole; left ventricular mass index Z-score: +1.3) and pulmonary hypertension observed on echocardiogram. At 3 months, repeat echocardiography demonstrated a shift to asymmetric left ventricular hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (interventricular septal thickness Z-score: +13.4 diastole, +11.8 systole; left ventricular mass index Z-score: +3.6). A chromosomal SNP-microarray was non-diagnostic although it demonstrated widespread LOH of approximately 147 mega-bases across the autosomes. Clinical trio exome sequencing was pursued based on a suspicion of an underlying inborn error of metabolism. This identified a homozygous variant of uncertain significance (VUS) in VARS2 (NM_020442.6; c.842G>A; p.Cys281Tyr) (Table 1). ETC testing of cultured fibroblasts demonstrated no abnormalities. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain at 3 years of age demonstrated bilateral periventricular white matter hyperintensities and mineralization of the globi pallidi bilaterally. She required gastrostomy for nutritional support following their NICU stay until 5 years of age. She developed generalized tonic-clonic largely nocturnal seizures at 5 years of age, that were responsive to levetiracetam. She has severe proximal appendicular hypotonia and speech delay, articulating approximately 20 words at 7 years of age. Family II also has a history of having a previous child (II-IV-5) who died from an unknown cause at 1 month of age that also had lactic acidosis and cardiomyopathy. Based on this history, it is now considered likely that this child also inherited these bi-allelic VARS2 variants, although testing was not completed for this individual.

Individual III-III-3 (Figure 1) was born at 36 weeks and 4 days completed gestation and had an uncomplicated newborn period. His head circumference was 47.6 centimeters at last evaluation at age 3 (Z-score: −1.45). At two months of age, he presented in respiratory distress with hypoxia at which point echocardiography demonstrated right ventricular dysfunction with mild wall hypertrophy with flattening of the intraventricular septum. During a subsequent hospitalization for diminished oral intake, he was found to have transient lactic acidosis (7.2 mmol/L at age with a reference range of 0.5–2.2 mmol/L). He required gastrostomy for nutritional support. Following an early period of mild motor delays, which were attributed to prolonged hospitalizations, his development has, thus far at 4 years of age, been largely typical for age. Chromosomal SNP-microarray and fragile X testing were non-diagnostic. Clinical trio exome sequencing was pursued to determine an underlying cause of his early developmental delays and cardiomyopathy. This identified a heterozygous VUS (NM_020442.6; c.673A>G; p.Lys225Glu) in VARS2 and a heterozygous likely pathogenic variant in VARS2 (NM_020442.6; c.2193del; p.Leu732Cysfs*29) in trans (Table 1). No other variants of likely or uncertain significance were reported. ETC testing from cultured fibroblasts demonstrated decreased complex IV levels. A brain MRI at 7 months of age demonstrated subtle mild anterior bilateral periventricular white matter hyperintensities and a borderline thin corpus callosum.

Of the structural anomalies noted in this case series, cardiac and intracranial abnormalities are among the most prominent features. Both cardiomyopathy and variable degrees of cerebral atrophy have previously been reported for individuals with bi-allelic VARS2 loss of function variants ( Diodato et al. 2014 ; Baertling et al. 2017 ; Alsemari et al. 2017 ; Bruni et al. 2018 ; Begliuomini et al. 2019 ; Chin et al. 2019 ). We sought to test whether these features were evident in Xenopus via vars2 depletion. Xenopus hearts have a partially septated atrium and ventricular trabeculations that allow for modeling of human heart development with greater structural fidelity compared to other aquatic models that lack atrial division. Furthermore, Xenopus provides a much higher throughput and less costly system than mammalian models.

We first tested whether we could achieve CRISPR-based editing of vars2 using two distinct guide RNAs (Figure 2A). In pooled embryos we detected edits in vars2 through PCR based inference of CRISPR edits (ICE) analysis ( Conant et al. 2022 ). This serves as a measure of the proportion of reads that have undergone editing and the types of edits achieved. The first guide RNA yielded editing that predominantly caused frameshift mutations in the second exon and resulted in nonsense mediated decay of vars2 transcripts (CRISPR1) (Figures 2B, S1A, and S1B). The second guide RNA yielded editing that predominantly caused an in-frame mutation in the ninth exon and resulted in an atypical Vars2 amino acid sequence upon translation of the resulting vars2 transcripts (CRISPR2) (Figures 2B, S1C, and S1D)..

We proceeded to assay whether this editing caused cardiac dysfunction and brain differences that emulated the phenotypes seen in individuals with bi-allelic VARS2 variants. By using optical coherence tomography (OCT), we were able to assess cardiac function in both control and crispant embryos. Through this approach, we detected a decreased cardiac shortening fraction in crispants generated with both guide RNAs compared to controls (Figure 2C, Video 1). Cardiac cycle rate in turn did not appear to be significantly affected in these in crispants compared to controls (Figure S2). We then assayed cross-sectional brain size using β-Tubulin immunofluorescence. This allowed us to observe a deficit in size of the developing brain in vars2 crispants (Figure 2D). Additional phenotypes observed in the crispant tadpoles included edema (Figure S3) that is often seen in tadpoles with cardiac dysfunction ( Kaltenbrun et al. 2011 ) and a split tail phenotype that was observed only in animals subject to CRISPR1 treatment (Figure S3 and Figure S4). A split tail phenotype has previously been described for tadpoles with dysfunctional FGF signaling mediated caudal patterning ( Amaya et al. 1991 ; Ataliotis et al. 1995 ).

We initially attempted to rescue the resultant phenotypes by introducing mRNA encoding VARS2, yet no appreciable improvement in cardiac function or craniofacial development was observed. This prompted our analysis of differences between VARS2 in humans and Vars2 in other vertebrates. Human VARS2 includes an N-terminal transit peptide sequence that is absent from this protein in Xenopus and other aquatic vertebrates and appears specific to mammalian organisms (Figure 3A). We hypothesize that transport of this protein is likely carried out in a distinct manner in mammals that is not well modeled in Xenopus. As such, we evaluated rescue with mRNA representative of individual patient variants based on the Xenopus vars2 coding DNA sequence. We determined that this approach may best model patient variants given the highly conserved amino acid regions where these variants are found (Figure 3B). Simultaneously, this facilitated rescue without the complicating factor of the distinct transit peptide sequence found in the corresponding VARS2 protein.

We sought to investigate the impact of identified variants on VARS2 function in Xenopus to better understand the observed phenotypic variability (Table 1 and Figure 1). Since all individuals harboring these variants had cardiac manifestations, we focused on cardiac function when evaluating the variants. We expressed mRNA representing the identified patient missense variants in crispant embryos generated with CRISPR1 at the one cell stage. Wildtype vars2 mRNA ameliorated the cardiac deficits demonstrating both the utility of our rescue assay as well as the specificity of our CRISPR knockout (Figure 4).

Introduction of mRNA representing the identified VARS2 variants (p.Lys225Glu, p.Cys281Tyr, and p.Ala390Thr) improved cardiac function to varying degrees (Figure 4). While introduction of vars2 mRNA simulating p.Lys225Glu and to a lesser extent p.Cys281Tyr seemed to improve cardiac function, this was not observed for the vars2 mRNA simulating the p.Ala390Thr variant (Figure 4A). Rescue of brain size with the same dose of vars2 wild-type mRNA displayed similar trend (Figure 4B).

We compared these apparent degrees of severity with a variety of predictions generated by in silico tools ( Ng and Henikoff 2003 ; Adzhubei et al. 2010 ; Ioannidis et al. 2016 ; Wu et al. 2021 ; Cheng et al. 2023 ; Schubach et al. 2024 ). These were broadly conflicting in their predictions of the effect of the variants we describe in this study (Table S1). Yet we did note that both PolyPhen-2 and VARITY scores generally correlated with the relative degree of variant dysfunction in our model.

Previous work in cell culture models of ARS deficiencies have demonstrated benefits of cognate amino acid supplementation ( Kok et al. 2021 ; Oswald et al. 2023 ). While clinical implementation of this approach is thus far limited, it represents a promising avenue for treating ARS deficiency ( Kok et al. 2021 ; Oswald et al. 2023 ). We hoped to gain further insight into this approach by evaluating it in a vertebrate model for VARS2 related mitochondriopathy.

To further test the hypothesis that valine supplementation can modify the phenotypic severity of VARS2 related disease, we introduced valine into the culture media used for viviculture of Xenopus embryos (Figure S3). While valine supplementation could serve as a test of the effects of cognate amino acid supplementation, glycine was chosen to serve as a comparator based on its similarities as a branched chain amino acid. We first tested a range of concentrations of valine and glycine (100 μM, 250 μM, or 500 μM) added to the media of control Xenopus embryos. We observed no increased embryonic death or clear adverse phenotypic consequences at these concentrations of glycine or valine (Figures 5A and 5C). We next assayed the severity of cardiac dysfunction in vars2 crispant embryos exposed to supplemental valine. Intriguingly, the response in the degree of cardiomyopathy as well as overall survival appeared to be dependent on the guide RNA used to achieve vars2 editing. Animals that underwent CRISPR editing that predominantly led to frame shift editing displayed no statistically significant change in degree of cardiac dysfunction or survival when supplemented with valine at any of the tested concentrations, while the highest dose of glycine supplementation appeared to have a negative impact on overall survival (Figures 5B and 5D). Surprisingly, animals that underwent CRISPR editing that predominantly led to in-frame editing displayed improved cardiac function when supplemented with either glycine or valine at all tested concentrations (Figure 5C). Yet, statistically significantly improved survival was only achieved with the highest tested dose of supplemented valine, while the highest dose of glycine seemed to have a negative impact on survival (Figure 5F). While both supplementation with glycine or valine improved cardiac function, this is not a non-specific effect that can be mediated by any supplemented amino-acid, as supplementation of phenylalanine did not achieve this effect (Figure S5).

The increasing number of reported cases of ARS related mitochondriopathy have begun to demonstrate common overlapping phenotypes as well as a spectrum of manifestations for individual enzymes of the ARS family. Biallelic pathogenic variants in VARS2 seem to have a propensity for causing hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with accompanying pulmonary hypertension, hypotonia, developmental delays, and epilepsy ( Baertling et al. 2017 ; Alsemari et al. 2017 ; Bruni et al. 2018 ; Ma et al. 2018 ; Begliuomini et al. 2019 ; Chin et al. 2019 ; Kušíková et al. 2021a). Interestingly, among these reports the most common variant described, (c.1010C>T; p.Thr337Ile), does not appear to lead to cardiomyopathy in the homozygous state though can do so in trans with another pathogenic variant in VARS2 (Kušíková et al. 2021b). This supports a specific genotype-phenotype correlation in the cardiac manifestations of this mitochondriopathy.

The 4 individuals from 3 unrelated families described here further illustrate the varying severity of VARS2 related mitochondriopathy. They varied in both degree of cardiomyopathy and neurodevelopmental differences. While individual I-III-4 died early in life from complications related tocardiomyopathy, individual II-IV-6 has had relatively stable cardiomyopathy despite its manifestation in the neonatal period, and individual III-III-3 had identification of a cardiomyopathy only after an illness at 2 months of age without preceding signs of cardiac dysfunction.

There are no clear correlations of disease severity based on the location of VARS2 variants within the structure of VARS2. Nearly all variants that have been identified for VARS2 and putatively contribute to disease are found within or near the tRNA synthetase enzymatic region of VARS2 (Figure 1D). Yet there does not seem to be any “hotspots” of disease-causing variants within this region. Only the variant we identified at Lysine 225 affects a previously reported site of protein modification. This site was identified as a site of ubiquitylation as part of a high throughput study ( Udeshi et al. 2013 ). As we learn more about different variants in VARS2 this site may prove important as ubiquitylation can lead to not only degradation through the canonical proteosomal pathway, but also to activition, localization, and interactions of modified proteins. Among the in silico prediction tools we evaluated for concordance with variant severity in our model, only PolyPhen-2 and VARITY correlated with our functional assay. As these tools weigh physicochemical properties between reference and variant encoded amino acids more highly than other tools tested, we hypothesize that these factors may be important for future efforts to predict theseverity of VARS2 variants, though this merits further study as additional instances of VARS2 related mitochondriopathy are reported.

In our cohort of patients with VARS2 variants, it appears that disease severity may well be derived from different degrees of protein disruption. The described individual harboring homozygous VARS2 p.Ala390Thr variants had the most severe disease course and in our functional assay was the least able to improve cardiac function. This is suggestive of a particularly important reliance on alanine or a particularly disruptive presence of threonine in this position for the tRNA synthetase function of VARS2. In contrast, the p.Lys225Glu, and even the p.Cys281Tyr variants demonstrate some degree of rescue in our Xenopus assay, suggesting that these may be less damaging, hypomorphic alleles. Indeed, the p.Lys225Glu variant was identified in individual III-3 who harbored a potentially more deleterious frameshift variant (p.Leu732Cysfs). Even so, he has thus far displayed typical development. All other reported cases of VARS2 mitochondriopathy have had developmental impacts save for one individual reported to have no atypical developmental or neurological findings, though he died at 47 days of age due to heart failure (Kušíková et al. 2021b). We hypothesize that this variant may only contribute to disease when present in trans with a more severe loss of function allele.

We were struck by previous reports of individuals with ARS deficiencies benefiting from supplementation with the cognate amino acid ( Kok et al. 2021 ; Oswald et al. 2023 ). In our estimation, it is not mechanistically intuitive that such supplementation could overcome deficits in aminoacylation. Yet the potential benefits with minimal known risks of amino acid supplementation are certainly appealing as a method of managing ARS deficiencies. We sought to test this in a vertebrate model that could both complement previous cell culture studies by observing the multi-organ impacts of this supplementation approach, as well as provide greater statistical power to capture the benefits of supplementation when compared to patient based observational studies.

Through these efforts we were able to observe the benefits of valine supplementation in vars2 deficient embryos only in a subset of cases. The cardiac phenotype improved upon supplementation with valine in animals with in-frame editing of vars2 but not in those having had frame shift editing of vars2 that would lead to nonsense mediated decay and complete loss of vars2 (Figure 5). Importantly these benefits were evident at doses of supplemental amino acids relevant for human physiology ( Wurtman et al. 1968 ; Armstrong and Stave 1973 ). While values may vary by reference laboratory and age, plasma concentration of valine is typically within the range of 100–350 μmol/L while that glycine is typically within the range of 120–500 μmol/L. While we expected possible benefits observed in our model to be dose dependent, we observed improved cardiac function to be achieved in animals with in-frame editing of vars2 with all tested concentrations of valine. This leads us to believe that even a low dose of valine supplementation may be effective in improving cardiac function.

We were surprised to observe cardiac phenotypic improvement in animals with in-frame editing of vars2 when supplementing with glycine (Figure 5). We initially intended for supplementation with glycine to serve as a treatment comparison to valine given its similar properties as a relatively hydrophobic amino acid. The revelation that its positive impact on cardiac function also appears to be limited to animals with in-frame editing of vars2 leads us to posit that the benefits observed due to glycine supplementation may also be a result of an indirect increase in valine availability. Indeed, there is some evidence to suggest that serum concentrations of valine are increased in response to glycine increases in a manner that can stimulate protein synthesis ( Wang et al. 2014 ; Wu et al. 2020 ). Alternatively, based on evidence that the integrated stress response (ISR) is activated in the setting of amino acid deprivation ( Kayvanpour et al. 2022 ), it’s possible that supplementation with glycine may improve cardiomyopathy and overall survival through downregulation of the ISR. Glycine supplementation has also been demonstrated to inhibit oxidative stress in a subset of tissues ( Wang et al. 2014 ). Similarly, glycine supplementation seems to reverse the amino acid concentration changes observed in the setting of inflammation ( Wu et al. 2020 ). This suggests that glycine may exert an independent anabolic effect that could reduce the activity of the ISR. Yet the negative impacts of high dose glycine on tadpole survival may be due to glycine accumulation. Such an accumulation has been shown to impair energy metabolism and redox homeostasis through decreased activities of respiratory chain complexes leading to bioenergetic dysfunction ( Moura et al. 2014 , 2016 ).

While our study focused on modeling cardiac function due to the morbidity and mortality of cardiac dysfunction in the individuals reported. Yet, the combination of a lack of dose dependent effect of valine or glycine supplementation on cardiac function improvement along with the observation that only the highest dose of valine resulted in an improved survival suggests that cardiac function is not the only factor impacting survival. Indeed, it seems likely that important benefits of valine supplementation not captured by our assays extend beyond the heart and combine to improve survival.

Our findings further support the utility of early cognate amino acid supplementation in ARS deficiencies. Such an approach likely relies on retained partial function of ARS proteins. This is in agreement with a previously proposed mechanism common to recessive cytosolic ARS deficiencies where diminished aminoacylation fails to meet translational demand, but can be overcome through introduction of additional amounts of the cognate amino acid ( Fuchs et al. 2019 ). This is also similar to what has been proposed in mechanistic investigations into why some individuals with mitochondrial ARS deficiencies such as HARS2 deficiency would benefit from such supplementation ( Qiu et al. 2023 ). In the absence of a currently recognized noncanonical function for VARS2, we speculate that supplementation of valine in a state of decreased VARS2 function (e.g. in the setting of missense or other small in frame deletions) may have a meaningful effect on the kinetics of valine incorporation into polypeptide chains as they are synthesized. Thus, the question of which individuals with VARS2 related mitochondriopathy might most benefit from valine supplementation requires ongoing efforts to achieve a functional understanding of variant effects on VARS2.

The novel variants and varying disease severity of VARS2 related mitochondriopathy we describe expands the spectrum of known disease manifestations. As others have described, disease severity is highly variable, and we describe severe, intermediate and mild cases. We are hopeful for ongoing delineation of genotype-phenotype correlations, though these are far from evident based on variant frequency, or the protein region seemingly affected. Yet, the varying severity of disease due to differing VARS2 variants are supported by our functional assay of these in a vertebrate model. Our observation that valine supplementation improves phenotypic severity in a vertebrate model in a variant dependent manner suggests that empiric amino acid supplementation is of potential benefit to individuals with VARS2 related mitochondriopathy via dietary modification.
