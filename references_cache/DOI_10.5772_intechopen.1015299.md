# How and When to Treat Visually-Evoked Seizures
**Authors:** Dorothee Kasteleijn-Nolst Trenite, Boulenouar Mesraoua
**Journal:** Seizure and Epilepsy - Current and Emerging Treatments [Working Title] (2026)
**DOI:** [10.5772/intechopen.1015299](https://doi.org/10.5772/intechopen.1015299)

## Content

Sunlight flickering through trees, or on water and snow, are well-known triggers in so-called photosensitive epilepsy patients. However, the amount and impact of visual stimuli have expanded greatly: our modern world is full of artificially created strong visual stimuli, such as colorful flashing programs displayed on large TV/computer screens and striped patterns. These stimuli can easily evoke epileptic seizures, besides causing general discomfort and headaches. Adolescents and females are most susceptible. It is important that, besides physicians, the public, technical light producers, architects, and video game content designers learn to recognize these stimuli and adapt them appropriately. Depending on the type of epilepsy, visual sensitivity can occur in up to 40% of patients, but this is often not diagnosed or treated. Prevention, treatment options, and practical information to monitor medication effects and avoid unnecessary seizure relapses are discussed.

IntroductionVisually 
evoked seizures are any type of epileptic seizures that are provoked by 
either natural stimuli, such as flickering sunlight through trees, on water, or on snow, 
or by artificial stimuli. The artificial stimuli are very prominent in our current 
environment: flickering fluorescent and LED lamps, white and colored flashing 
lights, life in discos, festivals, theaters, or those transmitted through TV and other 
screens [ 1 ]. Even red flashing toys have been shown to be capable of evoking seizures 
in susceptible children [ 2 ]. Striped patterns, such as those in clothes and architecture, 
can also provoke seizures, but in combination with colors and lights, as seen in 
cartoons and video games, they form the most provocative stimulus.
A diagnosis is based on a detailed clinical history of situations in which visual 
stimuli might have played a role, as confirmed by epileptiform EEG discharges 
during intermittent photic stimulation (IPS) or stimulation with other visual 
stimuli [ 3 , 4 ]. Photic stimulation can produce several types of EEG responses 
that are physiologically normal, such as photic following at flash rate and harmonics 
(so-called driving response) and orbitofrontal photomyoclonus (muscle artifacts). 
Epileptiform IPS-evoked responses are focal or generalized stimulus-independent 1                                                                                                                                        


responses 
and are referred to as photoparoxysmal response (PPR). Clinical 
expressions of the PPR are subtle, such as (eye-lid) myoclonia, absences, and 
short-lasting (focal) seizures; especially when stimulation is prolonged, generalized 
tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS) can be evoked. Seizures evoked by visual stimuli in 
daily life resemble the clinical signs and symptoms observed during the PPR [ 5 ].
Most prevalence studies in epilepsy patients, therefore, have been conducted 
using the IPS-EEG, showing that the PPR is found at all ages, in 0.5–40% depending 
on the type of epilepsy and ethnic background, especially around puberty and in 
females [ 6 ]. Photosensitivity can be detected in young children with Dravet 
syndrome (DS) and adolescent JME patients, both at 40%, while up to 6.5% of 
patients aged 50 or older [ 7 ]. In the latter group and Dravet children, the female 
preponderance is no longer significant, though.In 
this article we will focus on different scenarios on how and when to treat children 
and adults, who have exclusively or predominantly seizures and complaints related 
to strong visual stimuli.2.
Preventive measures2.1
Nonpharmacological preventive measures
As is logical, avoidance of exposure to flashing lights, such as changing lifestyles 
(e.g., no longer visiting discotheques and festivals), and simple measures to limit 
the intensity of incoming light (wearing dark spectacles and lowering the brightness 
of computer and TV screens) are effective [ 8 ]. Blue-colored glasses can be very 
helpful as well, blocking out the very provocative red lights, but not every 
photosensitive patient benefits from them; individual differences do exist, and 
testing the effect of blue glasses with IPS is advised [ 9 , 10 ].
If flashing lights and patterns with a strong contrast suddenly and unexpectedly 
appear, avoidance by looking away is not always possible; therefore, it is best to 
cover one eye. This has the same effect as wearing dark glasses [ 8 ].
New technological systems are under development to warn patients and/or 
change flash frequencies and colors to prevent the occurrence of seizures. This is 
especially relevant for video games and cartoons.
2.2When nonpharmacological preventive measures might be insufficient
Many studies from different countries and patient categories have shown that 
children and adolescents between 8 and 30 years of age are the most vulnerable to 
having flickering-light-evoked seizures [ 5 ]. The likelihood of evoking a PPR is 
flash frequency-dependent and varies per individual (age, medication, and 
genetic background): female adolescents are usually the most sensitive, with PPR 
evoked by frequencies even between 2 and 60 Hz (see Figure 1 ). Being sensitive 
to a whole range of frequencies means that the risk of visually induced seizures in 
daily life is much greater than being sensitive to only 23 Hz, for example [ 11 ]. 
Examples include malfunctioning fluorescent lights, flickering LED lamps, and 
fireworks.

Pharmacological treatment3.1
How to start treatment
Most patients with a clear history of seizures evoked by visual stimuli in daily life 
will show epileptiform EEG discharges during IPS – so-called photoparoxysmal EEG 
responses or PPRs – if the entire stimulation procedure is conducted according to the 
consensus guidelines [ 3 ]. The choice of photic stimulator is important – ensuring 
high enough brightness and testing of all eye conditions [ 3 , 13 ]. Epileptiform EEG 
discharges can be evoked in photosensitive patients at any time and repeatedly when 
awake, without evoking GTCS; it can therefore be considered a surrogate marker for 
epilepsy.
Before starting treatment, a baseline measure of the sensitivity range needs to be 
determined, even when the patient is already on ASM or other medication ( Figure 2a ). 
This could have mitigated the sensitivity, but a baseline measure before starting 
(additional) treatment is paramount. In general, PPR threshold measurements can Seizure

3.2How to evaluate treatment
Photosensitivity in epilepsy patients thus offers standardized repeated IPS 
assessments to evaluate age effects (maximum expression during puberty), the 
effect of dark and colored glasses, and medication (acute and in steady state). It can 
be monitored on an individual level by determining the photosensitivity ranges in 
combination with blood level measurements before and after drug treatment: one 
combines pharmacodynamic measures like the photosensitivity ranges with 
pharmacokinetic ones. Diminishment of range equals an antiepileptic protective 
effect (see above). It is not necessary to have complete abolishment of 
photosensitivity to establish a clinically important effect; sensitivity to, for example, 
23–25 Hz translates into very few daily provocations compared to a sensitivity range 
of 2 to 50 Hz ( Figure 2b ).
AED dosages can also be titrated using photosensitivity ranges. Especially in 
children, they can be adequately treated with a relatively low dosage of an 
antiepileptic drug, and searching for the lowest effective dose is thus possible. 
During adolescence, the prescribed dosage usually needs to be raised, partly because 
of an increase in body weight and partly due to an increase in photosensitivity itself. 
Sex differences can also be detected/monitored, as shown during the evaluation of 
intra-individual ranges in female and male patients titrated on valproate (VPA) 
medication [ 19 ]: female patients did not respond as well as males, and no explanation 
other than clear differences in pharmacodynamic effect could be given. It is not yet 
clear if this is the same for other ASMs as well.

3.3How to stop treatment
Therapy should be maintained at least until after puberty, although sensitivity 
can continue in a limited number of cases until the fifth decade of life [ 5 ]. IPS in the 
laboratory can help predict and prevent relapse after partial and total withdrawal of 
the drug.
VPA and LEV have been shown in various studies to completely suppress both 
spontaneous epileptiform discharges as well as PPRs. At some point, withdrawal of 
ASM must be discussed in seizure-free patients. Best practice is to first lower the 
prescribed dose to 50% of the total dose, repeat IPS, and, if there is still no IPS 
sensitivity, ASM can be safely reduced further. If sensitivity reappears, the 
medication should be reinstated. When ASM has been further reduced and stopped, 
a repeat IPS will indicate whether the patient will remain seizure-free or not. Early 
withdrawal of VPA carries a high risk of seizure recurrence, as has been documented, Seizure

3.4Choice of ASM
3.4.1 In general
From the start of the development of ASM, it was noticed that compounds like 
phenobarbital, benzodiazepines, and phenytoin were effective in photosensitive 
epilepsy patients as well as in epilepsy patients in general [ 22 ]. However, the 
development of important ASMs such as VPA and LEV started with studies in 
photosensitive epilepsy patients and was tested in acute single doses and in steady- 
state using the EEG biomarker PPR [ 23 , 24 ]. This has led to the belief that only these 
drugs are beneficial in photosensitive patients and exclusively in idiopathic 
generalized epilepsies. Subsequent trials and real-life experiences showed the

on the many new ASMs, which have fewer or different side-effects than the older 
ones [ 32 ].
3.4.2 Specific syndromes and epilepsies, highly connected to visual sensitivity
3.4.2.1 Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy
About 40% of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) patients are photosensitive, and 
85% of JME patients can be treated successfully when compliance is considered [ 33 ]. 
The frequency of relapses in JME is, however, the highest among all epilepsies, so 
most patients are forced to receive ASMs for life [ 34 ]. Photosensitivity might play 
a role in this, but no studies are known.
The most common use of ASM in 305 people with JME was evaluated in 2019, 
with VPA and carbamazepine most often prescribed as first-order ASM, whereas 
lamotrigine was most commonly started as the second trial, LEV as the third trial, 
and topiramate as the fourth trial.
A meta-analysis of risk factors related to drug resistance in JME found 
especially psychiatric factors, having a combination of different seizure types, and 
focal EEG abnormalities; a connection with photosensitivity was not shown. The 
ASMs used were VPA, LEV, LTG, topiramate, zonisamide, clobazam, or 
clonazepam [ 34 ].
3.4.2.2 Childhood absence epilepsy and juvenile absence epilepsy
About 20% of childhood absence epilepsy (CAE) and juvenile absence epilepsy 
(JAE) patients are photosensitive [ 35 ].
Responses to initial ASMs were 94% and 77% in the CAE and JAE groups in 
a prospective study of 62 patients [ 36 ]. They found generalized spike-and-wave 
discharges during IPS in 6% of CAE patients and 17% of JAE patients. The 
photoparoxysmal discharges were observed in 43% of JAE patients who did not 
respond to the first ASM and in only 9% of patients who responded to the first ASM; 
however, this difference was not statistically significant [ 36 ]. Another study did 
find, among all factors predicting failure to ASMs, photoparoxysmal EEG responses 
in children with CAE [ 37 ].Seizure

3.4.2.3
Jeavons syndrome
Jeavons syndrome (JS) is also known as epilepsy with EEM and 
photosensitivity; eyelid fluttering occurs mostly in relation to (sun)light. Two 
types of epileptiform discharges can follow eye closure/eye fluttering, and this 
is typical for a specific patient: 1. High-voltage irregular spikes or 2. Spike-and- 
wave discharges [ 38 ]. This difference in response is the same when discharges are 
evoked by IPS. If type 2 is evoked, an absence (with or without EEM) can be seen, 
which is of (much) shorter duration than a classical absence. Many EEM patients 
will have a clinical history of autoinduction of epileptiform discharges by slow 
eye closures; they are among those with the greatest sensitivity to light at eye 
closure [ 11 ].
It is not common practice to consider autoinduction unless the child uses 
handwaving, is in a trance, and shows compulsive behavior [ 39 ]; often, a diagnosis of 
tics is given [ 40 ].
Although there are other reflex mechanisms, like touch or pattern viewing, to 
evoke epileptiform discharges, eye closure is the easiest (“sunflower” children 
normally change from handwaving to eye closure induction as they get older). 
Patching one eye (diminishment of retinal light influx by half) is effective in 
reducing self-induced seizures [ 41 ].
When photosensitivity is diminished by age or the use of appropriate doses of 
ASM, EEM can continue into the forties or fifties and is seen especially when 
autoinduction is involved; a self-sustaining pathophysiologic mechanism must be in 
place by then.
In clinical practice, the distinction between EEM with self-induced seizures or 
sunflower syndrome with handwaving in front of the eyes is not very clear. It 
appears that, in all, treatment is very difficult – 80% of patients have drug-resistant 
epilepsy [ 39 , 42 ]. This search for appropriate treatment is demonstrated in a recent 
case report of a 13-year-old boy: only after adding acetazolamide did the boy become 
seizure-free [ 43 ].
Surprisingly, fenfluramine has been found to very successfully suppress not only 
photosensitivity but especially the therapy-resistant form of self-induction using 
sunlight and/or striped patterns [ 44 ]. It is important to take into account that 
noncompliance in adult JS patients is common [ 45 ].
3.4.2.4 DS (severe myoclonic epilepsy of infancy)
About 40% of DS patients are photosensitive and/or pattern-sensitive, and 
this is often already noticeable in the first year of life, before the seizures 
become more frequent and difficult to treat [ 46 ]. Like in other developmental 
epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs), HV and IPS are often skipped during EEG 
recordings.
Self-induction is relatively frequent, and fenfluramine has shown efficacy in DS 
patients as well [ 47 ]. In the past years, many new ASMs have been registered for the 
indication of DS. VPA is the drug of choice as first-line treatment in DS; second-line 
treatment should be a combination of VPA, stiripentol (STP), and clobazam, with 
cannabidiol (CBD) and fenfluramine (FFA) as alternative second-line drugs. The 
ketogenic diet is effective in some patients [ 48 ]. New compounds like lemizole, How

Idiopathic photosensitive occipital lobe epilepsy
As the name already suggests, all patients are photosensitive [ 49 ].
Idiopathic photosensitive occipital lobe epilepsy (IPOE) is an idiopathic 
localization-related epilepsy characterized by occipital photic-induced seizures with 
typical visual symptoms and a good prognosis, although, in exceptional cases, it can 
develop into the progressive Lafora disease.
It is often mistaken at the beginning of the disease for a migraine with aura.
3.4.2.6 Progressive myoclonic epilepsies
Up to 100% of patients with progressive myoclonic epilepsies (PME) show strong 
reactions to IPS; the course of the disease determines the likelihood of an (ab)normal 
PPR at some point in time. Unverricht–Lundborg Disease (ULD) starts off as 
a photosensitive JME, and treatment is likewise effective for a few years. Neuronal 
ceroid lipofuscinoses show high-voltage visual evoked responses, and LaFora disease 
exhibits focal PPRs over the occipital area before becoming generalized. At the end of 
the disease period, strong action-induced myoclonic jerks or seizures are very 
debilitating and difficult to treat [ 50 ].
The treatment overall does not differ from that commonly used in other 
photosensitive epilepsies and is mainly based on valproate treatment, while other 
drugs (LEV, zonisamide, topiramate, phenobarbital, etc.) are usually added, mainly 
to control other types of reflex myoclonus [ 50 , 51 ].4.
Concluding RemarksPhotosensitivity 
(sensitivity to light and patterns) in epilepsy manifests as 
epileptic seizures of varying severity (ranging from eyelid flutter to GTCS and, 
rarely, status epilepticus). Flickering sunlight, colored lights, computer screens, 
striped patterns, video games, etc., can repeatedly provoke seizures and are, as such, 
noted by caretakers and patients themselves. Individual differences exist in the type 
of evoked seizure (myoclonus or absence), degree of sensitivity, and the 
effectiveness of preventive measures and ASM.
This genetically determined sensitivity can be diagnosed and quantified 
repeatedly and with great care using intermittent photic and pattern stimulation at 
different flash frequencies, as well as the respective size and contrast of striped 
patterns. The evoked epileptiform EEG responses are called PPR and can be 
accompanied by signs and symptoms that are recognizable by the patient during the 
EEG and connected to daily life situations. PPRs can be used to monitor the efficacy Seizure 
and Epilepsy – Current and Emerging Treatments10


of 
ASM and help prevent the recurrence of seizures after the withdrawal of 
medication.
Many new ASMs and genetic modification therapies are on the market or in 
development; it is time to use and evaluate compounds other than VPA and LEV in 
visually sensitive patients; the type of epilepsy and syndrome should play an 
important role.Author
