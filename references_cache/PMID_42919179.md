# Variants and genotype-phenotype correlation of CRYBB2 gene in eight Chinese families with congenital cataract and other congenital ocular anomalies.
**Authors:** Zhang H, Tian F, Chang P, Wang D, Zhao Y, Li S, Zhang R, Zhao YE
**Journal:** BMC Ophthalmol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12886-026-05161-5](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12886-026-05161-5)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: The aim of this study is to explore the clinical phenotypes of congenital cataract caused by CRYBB2 variants and the surgical prognosis, so as to establish the association between the CRYBB2 gene and the genetic phenotypes of congenital cataract, and provide a reference for genetic counseling and treatment prognosis.

METHODS: A total of 120 families with congenital cataract were included in this study. Whole exome sequencing and Sanger sequencing were carried out to detect and confirm genetic variants. Bioinformatics analysis was employed to evaluate the occurrence frequency, conservation, pathogenic potential, and hydrophobicity of the mutated amino acids. Families with CRYBB2 gene variations were screened out for further analysis. Afterward, bioinformatic analysis through several computational predictive programs was performed to assess impacts of variations on protein structure and function.

RESULTS: Eight pathogenic or likely pathogenic CRYBB2 variants were identified in eight unrelated families, including three novel variants (c.355G > C:p.G119R, c.462C > A:p.Y154*, c.84_89del: p.Q28_G29del) and five reported variants (c.355G > A:p.G119R, c.436G > A:p.V146M, c.463 C > T:p.Q155*, c.481G > A:p.G161R, c.563G > C:p.R188P). The associated cataract phenotypes included total cataract, cerulean cataract, and perinuclear cataract. Among the study cohort, two patients presented with both microphthalmia and microcornea, while one patient exhibited microphthalmia accompanied by posterior capsule coloboma (PCD).

CONCLUSIONS: This study expands the mutational spectrum of CRYBB2 and highlights its association not only with congenital cataract but also with ocular developmental abnormalities such as microphthalmia and microcornea. These findings improve understanding of CRYBB2-related phenotypic diversity and provide valuable information for molecular diagnosis and genetic counseling.

Congenital cataract, the leading causes of visual impairment and blindness in children, remains a significant health-care burden in children worldwide. Its prevalence ranges from 12 to 136 per 100,000 births [1]. Approximately 50% of congenital cataracts are linked to genetic factors, with autosomal dominant inheritance being the most prevalent [2, 3]. To date, variants in more than 300 genes have been associated with congenital cataracts according to Cat-Map website (http://cat-map.wustl.edu/) [4]. Of these, approximately 50% are associated with crystallins, around 25% with connexins (gap junction proteins), while the remaining variants are distributed among other genes [5].

Congenital cataract is characterized by impaired and aberrant expression of crystallin, leading to the aggregation of lens proteins that obstructs light transmission through the lens. Crystallin proteins, which constitute approximately 90% of soluble lens proteins in humans, can be categorized into two categories based on their characteristics: α-crystallins and β/γ-crystallins [6]. The solubility and stability of these proteins are essential for preserving the optical transparency and high refractive index of the lens throughout the life span [5]. The βB2-crystallin is one of the most abundant proteins in water soluble lens extracts, which is why it has long been referred to as the “basic principle” crystalline [7].

In this study, we report eight congenital cataract patients with CRYBB2 variants predicted to be pathogenic or likely pathogenic, and seek to elucidate the mechanisms by which these variants induce alterations in lens proteins. We also report three cases of congenital cataract combined with microphthalmia and/or microcornea caused by CRYBB2 gene variants. This finding offers novel insight into the intrinsic connection between lens development and other ocular structures, while also emphasizing the complexity of early developmental cataractogenesis and crystallin biology.

These findings extend the mutational spectrum of crystallin genes in the Chinese congenital cataract population and provide clues for exploring the genetic mechanisms underlying congenital cataract.

A total of 120 families with congenital cataracts with or without other congenital ocular anomalies including microphthalmia, microcornea and PCD were recruited from the Eye Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Hangzhou, China) between 2023 and 2024. Among these cases, pathogenic variants in eight families were identified in CRYBB2; four demonstrated familial inheritance patterns (Fig. 1), while the others were classified as sporadic. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eye Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

All patients and available family members underwent a comprehensive ophthalmologic clinical examination by expert ophthalmologists, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), slit-lamp examination, anterior segment photography, IOL Master 500/700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany), and fundus examinations with dilated pupils. The following parameters were recorded: BCVA, axial length, corneal diameter, corneal curvature, lens thickness, cataract phenotype, and the presence of other congenital ocular anomalies. Intraoperative observations were further performed to assess the location of lens opacity and the presence of pre-existing ocular abnormalities.

Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients and available family members. Genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial DNA extraction kit (Simgen, Hangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

DNA samples from affected individuals were subjected to whole-exome sequencing (WES). WES was performed using IDT’s xGen Exome Research Panel V1.0 (Integrated DNA Technologies, San Diego, USA) and the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) for sequencing.

Variant pathogenicity interpretation followed the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the recommendations of the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (SVI) Expert Group. Rare variants were filtered based on allele frequencies in public population databases; variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 5% in the 1000 Genomes Project and gnomAD were excluded. In addition, a more stringent threshold (MAF < 0.0001 in gnomAD v4) was applied for rare variant prioritization.

Genomic annotation was performed based on the Human Genome reference GRCh37 (hg19), RefSeq, dbSNP, 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3, and gnomAD v4. Interpretation and disease reference databases included DGV, DECIPHER, OMIM, UCSC, ClinVar, HGMD, and PubMed, with database versions specified where applicable.

Potentially pathogenic variants identified from WES data were selected for further validation. The corresponding exon sequences were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. PCR primers were designed according to candidate variant sites, and amplification of target gene regions was performed using genomic DNA from family members as templates. To validate candidate variants, PCR products were subjected to Sanger sequencing. Sequencing results were analyzed using Chromas 2 software to assess whether variants co-segregated with the disease phenotype within available family members.

Amino acid conservation analysis was performed using the VarSite database. Three-dimensional structures of wild-type and mutant proteins were predicted using Phyre2 and visualized with PyMOL software (Version 1.5). Amino acid hydrophobicity profiles were analyzed using the ProtScale tool integrated with the UniProt database. The effects of amino acid substitutions on protein stability were predicted using MAESTROweb.

Among the eight unrelated families, four (F1, F3, F5, and F8) followed an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, whereas the remaining families were sporadic cases. Preoperative cataract phenotypes were fully documented in five patients (F1, F3, F4, F5, and F6) who underwent surgery at our institution. Notably, F2, F7, and F8 were excluded from subtype analysis due to incomplete historical surgical records. Extralenticular anomalies included microphthalmia with posterior capsular defects (F1) and microphthalmia-microcornea complexes (F3 and F7).

Whole-exome sequencing and subsequent variant analysis identified eight CRYBB2 variants in eight unrelated congenital cataract families (Table 1). All identified variants were heterozygous. The familial cases showed an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. Among these variants, three were novel variants, including c.355G > C:p.G119R in exon 5, c.462 C > A:p.Y154* in exon 6, and c.84_89del: p.Q28_G29del. The remaining five variants were previously reported variants, including c.355G > A:p.G119R, c.436G > A:p.V146M, c.463 C > T:p.Q155*, c.481G > A:p.G161R, and c.563G > C:p.R188P. All variants were absent from the gnomAD v4 database and were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines(Table 1). Segregation analysis was performed in available family members, and co-segregation with the disease phenotype was observed in familial cases (Fig. 1B, F, H, K, N, Q, T, V). Multiple sequence alignment analysis showed that all eight variants were highly conserved across different species, as visualized by WebLogo 3 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Family 1 and family 2 harbored different CRYBB2 variants (c.355G > A:p.G119R and c.355G > C:p.G119R) affected the same amino acid residue, resulting in the identical amino acid substitution at position 119 of βB2-crystallin. Interestingly, although these two variants resulted in the same amino acid substitution (G119R), their associated clinical manifestations appeared to differ. The proband of family 1 presented with severe microphthalmia characterized by markedly reduced axial lengths (15.82 mm in the right eye and 16.37 mm in the left eye) and total cataract (Fig. 1C). In contrast, the proband of family 2 had normal ocular size and development. The detailed cataract morphology in family 2 could not be evaluated because lens photographs were unavailable.

In family 3, variant analysis identified a c.436G > A transversion in exon 5 of CRYBB2, resulting in the substitution of valine at position 146 by methionine (V146M). In family 7, a c.563G > C transversion in exon 6 of CRYBB2 was identified, leading to the replacement of arginine at position 188 by proline (R188P). Both variants were predicted to affect conserved residues of βB2-crystallin. Notably, the probands in both families exhibited microphthalmia and microcornea, suggesting an association between these CRYBB2 variants and abnormal ocular development.The structural characteristics and predicted effects of these variants are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1(C, D,G, H).

Two adjacent nonsense variants, c.462 C > A:p.Y154* and c.463 C > T:p.Q155*, were identified in families 4 and 5, respectively. Both variants resulted in premature termination of βB2-crystallin. Clinically, the probands of both families presented with cerulean cataract without ocular developmental abnormalities. Both probands had normal axial lengths and corneal diameters. Notably, the lens opacity patterns were highly similar between the two families, characterized by blue dot-like lens opacities.(Table 2; Fig. 1L, O).

In family 6, a novel c.481G > A transition in exon 6 of CRYBB2 was identified, resulting in the amino acid substitution p.G161R. This variant affected a highly conserved residue within βB2-crystallin. The proband exhibited perinuclear cataract (Fig. 1R), while ocular development was normal, with no evidence of microphthalmia or microcornea.

In family 8, a deletion variant was identified. Variant analysis revealed a loss of bases between positions 84 and 89 in exon 6 of CRYBB2 that resulted in the deletion of glutamine at position 28 and glycine at position 29. (c.84_89del: p.Q28_G29del). The proband presented with congenital cataract without ocular developmental abnormalities. The predicted structural and functional effects of this variant are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1(I, J).

The βB2-crystallin is one of the most abundant proteins in water soluble lens extracts, which is why it has long been referred to as the “basic principle” crystalline [7]. βB2 is also the least altered protein throughout the aging process [8]. It stands out as the most thermally stable and soluble among all β-crystallins, remaining soluble throughout aging, and plays a crucial role in preserving the solubility of hetero-oligomers during their isolation [9, 10]. Therefore, Therefore, investigating CRYBB2 variants is essential for further understanding the genetic basis and pathogenic mechanisms underlying congenital cataract.

In vertebrate lenses, β-crystallins exist as homo- and hetero-oligomers, which are essential for maintaining lens transparency and optical properties. βB2-crystallin, encoded by CRYBB2, consists of two homologous domains connected by a linker peptide. Each domain contains two Greek key motifs composed of antiparallel β-sheets, resulting in a total of 16 β-strands within the protein. These β-strands play an important role in mediating intermolecular interactions between β-crystallins and maintaining the stability of crystallin complexes [7].

The p.Y154* and p.Q155* variants identified in this study are located at the beginning of the fourth Greek key motif. These truncating variants are predicted to cause loss of the C-terminal region, including structural elements involved in intermolecular interactions, which may impair crystallin complex formation. Previous studies have suggested that these variants may result in the loss of three β-strands involved in domain interactions, thereby reducing protein–protein interactions and affecting lens transparency [11]. Disruption of β-strand organization caused by genetic variants or post-translational modifications may compromise the formation and stability of crystallin complexes, ultimately contributing to cataract development.

Furthermore, any disruption of the β-strands by either post-translational modification or mutation will disturb the complex formation that is critical for lens transparency. It has been reported that, based on X-ray structures of 29 proteins, Val acts as a strong β-former, while Pro functions as a strong β-breaker [7]. This may be able to explain why variants in family 3 (p.V146M) and family 7 (p.R188P) cause lens opacities.

Previous studies have suggested that CRYBB2 variants are strongly associated with cerulean cataract [12]. This association appears to be relatively specific, as cerulean cataract is rarely observed in patients carrying variants in other cataract-associated genes. Among the reported CRYBB2 variants, c.463 C > T:p.Q155* is one of the most frequently identified variants associated with cerulean cataract. Consistent with previous reports, the proband carrying this variant in our cohort exhibited a typical cerulean cataract phenotype, further supporting the association between p.Q155 and this specific cataract morphology. he c.463 C > T:p.Q155* variant introduces a premature termination codon and results in the loss of 51 amino acids from the C-terminal region of βB2-crystallin. Interestingly, the novel c.462 C > A:p.Y154* variant identified in our study affects the adjacent residue and produces a similar truncating effect, with loss of the C-terminal region. The proband carrying the p.Y154* variant also presented with cerulean cataract, suggesting that disruption of the C-terminal region of βB2-crystallin may contribute to the development of this specific cataract phenotype. However, further functional studies are required to validate this hypothesis.

Cerulean cataract was first described by Vogt and consists of predominantly peripheral punctate, cerulean and dust-like opacifications organized in concentric layers [13]. The opacities can be found in any part of the lens and mainly concentrated in the cortex. Affected individuals are usually asymptomatic during early childhood, and visual acuity is often well preserved. Therefore, cataract extraction is rarely required before adulthood. In the literature, 18 reports have described the c.463 C > T:p.Y155* variant, among which 15 reported clinical phenotypes. Ten of these cases were classified as cerulean cataract, accounting for approximately 67% of reported cases (Table 3). These findings further suggest a potential genotype–phenotype correlation between C-terminal truncating variants of βB2-crystallin and the cerulean cataract phenotype.

Although minor variations in lens opacity patterns may exist, the cataract phenotypes observed in these families were highly similar. The common characteristics included relatively mild and slowly progressive lens opacities, typically presenting as punctate or pulverulent opacities, with limited impact on visual development during childhood. Consequently, affected individuals often seek medical attention at an older age when visual impairment becomes apparent. Consistent with previous reports, the two patients with cerulean cataract in our cohort were diagnosed at relatively advanced ages (14 and 6 years, respectively) and achieved favorable postoperative visual outcomes.

In contrast, the probands in Family 1 and 3 both suffered from total cataracts with particular dense opacity in the central zone. In addition, they also have a common feature, that is, the thickness of the lens is less than 2 mm, obviously thinner than normal thickness [14, 15]. Previous studies indicate that the leakage and absorption of lens substances resulting from capsule defects are responsible for lens thinning [15]. During surgery, it can be found that the proband in Family 3 has irregular lines on the anterior capsule, indicating the existence of preexisting anterior capsule defect (Fig. 1I). The proband in Family 1 has opacity and fibrosis on the posterior capsule (Fig. 1D), indicating that this is a pathological posterior capsule. Although no obvious defects can be seen, we can speculate that the patient may have had a small defect before, which led to cortical leakage and absorption, causing the lens to become thinner. At the same time, small defect will lead to the activation of the repair system, resulting in fibrosis and plaque of the posterior capsule and dense plaque of the posterior cortex of the lens. Approximately 20% of posterior polar cataracts are associated with a congenital defect in the posterior capsule [16]. It is possible that preexisting capsule defects promote reparative and organizational processes, which in turn accelerate the formation of capsule opacity and lens opacity in patients, ultimately leading to the rapid progression of cataracts. Therefore, the two patients developed total cataracts at a very young age (two months and five months respectively). Currently, the main diagnostic criterion of the preexisting posterior capsule before surgery is a thin lens [15]. The findings of this study once again prove the accuracy of this diagnostic criterion.

Congenital cataract may occur alongside other ocular anomalies. Many patients with congenital cataracts also have microcornea, defined by horizontal corneal diameter (HCD) <10 mm at birth in an eye of normal axial length (AXL), and microphthalmia, defined as a small underdeveloped eye, with an axial length of more than two standard deviations below the age adjusted mean. In our cohort, several patients carrying CRYBB2 variants exhibited microphthalmia and/or microcornea, suggesting that the phenotypic spectrum associated with CRYBB2 variants may extend beyond isolated lens opacity formation.

Notably, ocular developmental abnormalities were observed in patients carrying CRYBB2 variants in our cohort. Three of eight patients (37.5%) exhibited microcornea and/or microphthalmia, suggesting that the phenotypic spectrum of CRYBB2-related cataracts may extend beyond isolated lens opacity to include abnormalities of ocular development. To further investigate the genetic background of congenital cataract associated with ocular developmental abnormalities, we analyzed a cohort of 120 patients with congenital cataract. Among them, 20 patients with microcornea and/or microphthalmia carried pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants involving a diverse range of genes. These genes included crystallin genes (CRYAA, CRYBB1, CRYBB2, CRYGC, and CRYGD), gap junction genes (GJA3 and GJA8), ocular developmental regulators (PAX6, PITX3, and MAF), and syndromic genes associated with multisystem disorders, including NHS, OCRL, BCOR, and EPHA2. These findings highlight the genetic heterogeneity underlying congenital cataract accompanied by ocular developmental abnormalities and suggest that CRYBB2 may represent one of the contributors involved in this broader developmental phenotype.

In conclusion, this study investigated eight unrelated families with congenital cataracts, including patients with or without additional ocular developmental abnormalities. A total of eight pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in CRYBB2 were identified, including three novel variants, thereby expanding the mutational spectrum of CRYBB2-associated cataracts. In addition to various cataract phenotypes, including total cataract, cerulean cataract, and perinuclear cataract, several patients carrying CRYBB2 variants exhibited ocular developmental abnormalities, such as microcornea and microphthalmia. These findings suggest that the phenotypic spectrum associated with CRYBB2 variants may extend beyond lens opacity formation and provide new insights into the potential role of CRYBB2 in ocular development. Our study expands the genetic spectrum of congenital cataracts and associated ocular developmental abnormalities in the Chinese population and provides valuable information for molecular diagnosis and genetic counseling in the era of precision medicine.
