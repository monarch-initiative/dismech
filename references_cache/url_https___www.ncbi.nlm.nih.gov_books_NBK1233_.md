['h2'],smoothScroll: false" itemprop="text"><div id="melas.Summary" itemprop="description"><h2 id="_melas_Summary_">Summary</h2><div><h4 class="inline">Clinical characteristics.</h4><p>MELAS (<i>m</i>itochondrial <i>e</i>ncephalomyopathy, <i>l</i>actic <i>a</i>cidosis, and <i>s</i>troke-like episodes) is a multisystem disorder with protean manifestations. The vast majority of affected individuals develop signs and symptoms of MELAS between ages two and 40 years. Common clinical manifestations include stroke-like episodes, encephalopathy with seizures and/or dementia, muscle weakness and exercise intolerance, normal early psychomotor development, recurrent headaches, recurrent vomiting, hearing impairment, peripheral neuropathy, learning disability, and short stature. During the stroke-like episodes neuroimaging shows increased T<sub>2</sub>-weighted signal areas that do not correspond to the classic vascular distribution (hence the term "stroke-like"). Lactic acidemia is very common and muscle biopsies typically show ragged red fibers.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Diagnosis/testing.</h4><p>The diagnosis of MELAS is based on meeting clinical diagnostic criteria and identifying a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in one of the genes associated with MELAS. The m.3243A&#x0003e;G pathogenic variant in the mitochondrial <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> <i>MT-TL1</i> is present in approximately 80% of individuals with MELAS. Pathogenic variants in <i>MT-TL1</i> or other mtDNA genes, particularly <i>MT-ND5</i>, can also cause this disorder.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Management.</h4><p><i>Treatment of manifestations:</i> Treatment for MELAS is generally supportive. During the acute stroke-like episode, a bolus of intravenous arginine (500 mg/kg for children or 10 g/m<sup>2</sup> body surface area for adults) within three hours of symptom onset is recommended followed by the administration of a similar dosage of intravenous arginine as a continuous infusion over 24 hours for the next three to five days. Coenzyme Q<sub>10</sub>, L-carnitine, and creatine have been beneficial in some individuals. Sensorineural hearing loss has been treated with cochlear implantation; seizures respond to traditional anticonvulsant therapy (although valproic acid should be avoided). Ptosis, cardiomyopathy, cardiac conduction defects, nephropathy, and migraine headache are treated in the standard manner. Diabetes mellitus is managed by dietary modification, oral hypoglycemic agents, or insulin therapy. Exercise intolerance and weakness may respond to aerobic exercise.</p><p><i>Prevention of primary manifestations</i>: Once an individual with MELAS has the first stroke-like episode, arginine should be administered prophylactically to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke-like episodes. A daily dose of 150 to 300 mg/kg/day oral arginine in three divided doses is recommended.</p><p><i>Prevention of secondary complications</i>: Because febrile illnesses may trigger acute exacerbations, individuals with MELAS should receive standard childhood vaccinations, flu vaccine, and pneumococcal vaccine.</p><p><i>Surveillance:</i> Affected individuals and their at-risk relatives should be followed at regular intervals to monitor progression and the appearance of new symptoms. Annual ophthalmologic, audiology, and cardiologic (electrocardiogram and echocardiogram) evaluations are recommended. Annual urinalysis and fasting blood glucose level are also recommended.</p><p><i>Agents/circumstances to avoid:</i> Mitochondrial toxins, including aminoglycoside antibiotics, linezolid, cigarettes, and alcohol; valproic acid for seizure treatment; metformin because of its propensity to cause lactic acidosis; dichloroacetate (DCA) because of increased risk for peripheral neuropathy.</p><p><i>Pregnancy management:</i> Affected or at-risk pregnant women should be monitored for diabetes mellitus and respiratory insufficiency, which may require therapeutic interventions</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Genetic counseling.</h4><p>MELAS is caused by pathogenic variants in mtDNA and is transmitted by maternal inheritance. The father of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> is not at risk of having the mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>. The mother of a proband usually has the mtDNA pathogenic variant and may or may not have symptoms. A man with a mtDNA pathogenic variant cannot transmit the variant to any of his offspring. A woman with a mtDNA pathogenic variant (whether symptomatic or asymptomatic) transmits the variant to all of her offspring. Prenatal testing and <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> for MELAS is possible if a mtDNA pathogenic variant has been detected in the mother. However, because the mutational load in embryonic and fetal tissues sampled (i.e., amniocytes and chorionic villi) may not correspond to that of all fetal tissues, and because the mutational load in tissues sampled prenatally may shift in utero or after birth as a result of random mitotic segregation, prediction of the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> from prenatal studies cannot be made with certainty.</p></div></div><div id="melas.Diagnosis"><h2 id="_melas_Diagnosis_">Diagnosis</h2><p>Clinical diagnostic criteria for MELAS (<i>m</i>itochondrial <i>e</i>ncephalomyopathy, <i>l</i>actic <i>a</i>cidosis, and <i>s</i>troke-like episodes) have been published [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.hirano.1992.125">Hirano et al 1992</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.yatsuga.2012.619">Yatsuga et al 2012</a>] (see <a href="#melas.Establishing_the_Diagnosis">Establishing the Diagnosis</a>).</p><div id="melas.Suggestive_Findings"><h3>Suggestive Findings</h3><p>MELAS (<i>m</i>itochondrial <i>e</i>ncephalomyopathy, <i>l</i>actic <i>a</i>cidosis, and <i>s</i>troke-like episodes) <b>should be suspected</b> in individuals with the following features.</p><div id="melas.Clinical_Features"><h4>Clinical Features</h4><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Stroke-like episodes before the age of 40 years</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Acquired encephalopathy with seizures and/or dementia</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Recurrent headaches</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Muscle weakness and exercise intolerance</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Cortical vision loss</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Hemiparesis</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Recurrent vomiting</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Short stature</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Hearing impairment</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Normal early psychomotor development</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Peripheral neuropathy</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Learning disability</div></li></ul></div><div id="melas.Brain_Imaging"><h4>Brain Imaging</h4><p>
<b>Brain MRI</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>During the stroke-like episodes, the affected areas:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Have increased T<sub>2</sub> signal;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Do not correspond to the classic vascular distribution (hence the term "stroke-like");</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Are asymmetric;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Typically involve predominantly the posterior cerebrum (temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes);</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Can be restricted to cortical areas or involve subcortical white matter [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.hirano.1992.125">Hirano et al1992</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.sproule.2008.133">Sproule &#x00026; Kaufmann 2008</a>].</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Slow spreading of the stroke-like lesions occurs in the weeks following the first symptoms, typically documented by T<sub>2</sub>-weighted MRI [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.iizuka.2003.1238">Iizuka et al 2003</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Diffusion-weighted MRI shows increased apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) in the stroke-like lesions of MELAS, in contrast to the decreased ADC seen in ischemic strokes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.kolb.2003.11">Kolb et al 2003</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>MR angiography is usually normal and MR spectroscopy shows decreased N-acetylaspartate signals and accumulation of lactate [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.sproule.2008.133">Sproule &#x00026; Kaufmann 2008</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b>Head CT.</b> Basal ganglia calcifications are occasionally seen.</p></div><div id="melas.Electromyography_and_Nerve_Conduct"><h4>Electromyography and Nerve Conduction Studies</h4><p>Findings are consistent with a myopathic process, but neuropathy may coexist. Neuropathy can be axonal or mixed axonal and demyelinating [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.k_rpp_.2003.216">K&#x000e4;rpp&#x000e4; et al 2003</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.kaufmann.2006b.746">Kaufmann et al 2006b</a>].</p></div><div id="melas.Suggestive_Laboratory_Findings"><h4>Suggestive Laboratory Findings</h4><p><b>Lactic acidosis both in blood and CSF.</b> Lactic acidemia is very common. CSF lactate is also elevated in most affected individuals.</p><p>Lactic acidemia is not specific for MELAS syndrome as it can occur in other mitochondrial diseases, metabolic diseases, and systemic illness. Other situations (unrelated to the diagnosis of MELAS) in which lactate can be elevated are acute neurologic events such as seizure or stroke. On the other hand, lactate level can be normal in a minority of individuals with MELAS syndrome [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.hirano.1994.4">Hirano &#x00026; Pavlakis 1994</a>].</p><p><b>Elevated CSF protein</b> rarely surpasses 100 mg/dL.</p><p>
<b>Muscle biopsy</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">Ragged red fibers (RRFs) with the modified Gomori trichrome stain, which represent mitochondrial proliferation below the plasma membrane of the muscular fibers causing the contour of the muscle fiber to become irregular. These proliferated mitochondria also stain strongly with the succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) stain giving the appearance of ragged blue fibers.</div><div class="half_rhythm">Although RRFs are present in many other mitochondrial diseases e.g., MERRF (myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibers), most of the RRFs in MELAS stain positively with the cytochrome <i>c</i> oxidase (COX) histochemical stain, unlike other mitochondrial diseases where RRFs do not react with COX.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">An overabundance of mitochondria in smooth muscle and endothelial cells of intramuscular blood vessels, best revealed with the SDH stain ("strongly succinate dehydrogenase-reactive blood vessels," or SSVs)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">Respiratory chain studies on muscle tissue: typically multiple partial defects, especially involving complex I and/or complex IV. However, biochemical results can also be normal.</div></li></ul><p>Note: Muscle biopsy is not required to make this diagnosis; <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> is frequently used in lieu of muscle biopsy to <a href="#melas.Establishing_the_Diagnosis">establish the diagnosis</a>.</p></div></div><div id="melas.Establishing_the_Diagnosis"><h3>Establishing the Diagnosis</h3><p>Two sets of clinical diagnostic criteria for MELAS (<i>m</i>itochondrial <i>e</i>ncephalomyopathy, <i>l</i>actic <i>a</i>cidosis, and <i>s</i>troke-like episodes) have been published:</p><dl class="temp-labeled-list"><dt>I.</dt><dd><p class="no_top_margin">A clinical diagnosis of MELAS can be made if the following three criteria are met [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.hirano.1992.125">Hirano et al 1992</a>]:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Stroke-like episodes before age 40 years</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Encephalopathy characterized by seizures and/or dementia</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Mitochondrial myopathy is evident by the presence of lactic acidosis and/or ragged-red fibers (RRFs) on muscle biopsy.</div></li></ul><p><b>AND</b> at least two of the following criteria are present:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Normal early psychomotor development</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Recurrent headaches</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Recurrent vomiting episodes</div></li></ul></dd><dt>II.</dt><dd><p class="no_top_margin">A clinical diagnosis of MELAS can also be made in an individual with at least two category A <b>AND</b> two category B criteria [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.yatsuga.2012.619">Yatsuga et al 2012</a>]:</p><p>
<b>Category A criteria</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Headaches with vomiting</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Seizures</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Hemiplegia</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Cortical blindness</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Acute focal lesions on neuroimaging (See Suggestive Findings, <a href="#melas.Brain_Imaging">Brain Imaging</a>.)</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Category B criteria</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>High plasma or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) lactate</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Mitochondrial abnormalities on muscle biopsy (See Suggestive Findings, <a href="#melas.Suggestive_Laboratory_Findings">Suggestive Laboratory Findings</a>.)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>A MELAS-related <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> (See <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.genetic_causes_of_melas/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTgeneticcausesofmelas">Table 1</a>.)</div></li></ul></dd></dl><p>The diagnosis of MELAS <b>is established</b> in a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> who meets the clinical diagnostic criteria discussed above and who has a pathogenic (or <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a>) variant in one of the genes listed in <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.genetic_causes_of_melas/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTgeneticcausesofmelas">Table 1</a> identified by <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a>.</p><p>Note: (1) Per American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics&#x000a0;/ Association for Molecular Pathology variant interpretation guidelines, the terms "<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>" and "<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a> variant" are synonymous in a clinical setting, meaning that both are considered diagnostic and can be used for clinical decision making [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.richards.2015.405">Richards et al 2015</a>]. Reference to "pathogenic variants" in this <i>GeneReview</i> is understood to include likely pathogenic variants. (2) Identification of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> variant of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/uncertain-significance/">uncertain significance</a> in one of the genes listed in <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.genetic_causes_of_melas/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTgeneticcausesofmelas">Table 1</a> does not establish or rule out the diagnosis. (3) Pathogenic variants can usually be detected in mtDNA from leukocytes in individuals with typical MELAS; however, the occurrence of "<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heteroplasmy/">heteroplasmy</a>" in disorders of mtDNA can result in varying tissue distribution of mutated mtDNA. Hence, the pathogenic variant may be undetectable in mtDNA from leukocytes and may be detected only in other tissues, such as buccal mucosa, cultured skin fibroblasts, hair follicles, urinary sediment, or (most reliably) skeletal muscle.</p><p>Molecular genetic testing approaches can include a combination of <b><a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a>-targeted testing</b> (single-gene testing, concurrent or serial single-gene testing, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multigene-panel/">multigene panel</a>) and <b>comprehensive</b>
<b><a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing</b> (<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exome-sequencing/">exome sequencing</a>, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exome-array/">exome array</a>, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genome-sequencing/">genome sequencing</a>) depending on the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>.</p><p>Gene-targeted testing requires that the clinician determine which <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a>(s) are likely involved, whereas <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing does not. Individuals with the distinctive findings described in <a href="#melas.Suggestive_Findings">Suggestive Findings</a> are likely to be diagnosed using gene-targeted testing (see <a href="#melas.Option_1">Option 1</a>), whereas those with a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> indistinguishable from many other inherited disorders with seizures and weakness are more likely to be diagnosed using genomic testing (see <a href="#melas.Option_2">Option 2</a>).</p><div id="melas.Option_1"><h4>Option 1</h4><p>When the phenotypic and laboratory findings suggest the diagnosis of MELAS, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> approaches can include <b>serial single-<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> testing</b> or use of a <b><a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multigene-panel/">multigene panel</a></b>.</p><p><b>Serial single-<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> testing</b> can be considered if (1) mutation of a particular gene accounts for a large proportion of the condition <b>or</b> (2) clinical findings, laboratory findings, ancestry, or other factors indicate that mutation of a particular gene is most likely.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Typically, blood leukocyte DNA is initially tested for the <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.pathogenic_variants_in_mitochond/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTpathogenicvariantsinmitochond">m.3243A&#x0003e;G</a> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in <i>MT-TL1</i>, which is present in approximately 80% of individuals with typical clinical findings.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If this is normal, targeted testing for the pathogenic variants <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.pathogenic_variants_in_mitochond/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTpathogenicvariantsinmitochond">m.3271T&#x0003e;C</a> and <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.pathogenic_variants_in_mitochond/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTpathogenicvariantsinmitochond">m.3252A&#x0003e;G</a> in <i>MT-TL1</i> and <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.pathogenic_variants_in_mitochond/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTpathogenicvariantsinmitochond">m.13513G&#x0003e;A</a> in <i>MT-ND5</i> is considered next.</div></li></ul><p><b>A <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/multigene-panel/">multigene panel</a></b> that includes <i>MT-TL1, MT-ND5</i>, and other mtDNA genes of interest (see <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.genetic_causes_of_melas/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTgeneticcausesofmelas">Table 1</a> and <a href="#melas.Differential_Diagnosis">Differential Diagnosis</a>) may also be considered. Note: (1) The genes included and the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sensitivity/">sensitivity</a> of multigene panels vary by laboratory and are likely to change over time. (2) Some multigene panels may include genes not associated with the condition discussed in this <i>GeneReview</i>; thus, clinicians need to determine which multigene panel is most likely to identify the genetic cause of the condition while limiting identification of pathogenic variants and variants of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/uncertain-significance/">uncertain significance</a> in genes that do not explain the underlying <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>. (3) In some laboratories, panel options may include a custom laboratory-designed panel and/or custom phenotype-focused <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exome/">exome</a> analysis that includes genes specified by the clinician. (4) Methods used in a panel may include <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sequence-analysis/">sequence analysis</a>, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/deletion-duplication-analysis/">deletion/duplication analysis</a>, and/or other non-sequencing-based tests.</p><p><b>Entire mitochondrial <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genome-sequencing/">genome sequencing</a></b> that includes <i>MT-TL1, MT-ND5</i>, and other mtDNA genes of interest (see <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.genetic_causes_of_melas/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTgeneticcausesofmelas">Table 1</a> and <a href="#melas.Differential_Diagnosis">Differential Diagnosis</a>) is most likely to identify the genetic cause of the condition while limiting identification of pathogenic variants and variants of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/uncertain-significance/">uncertain significance</a> in genes that do not explain the underlying <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>.</p><p>For an introduction to multigene panels click <a href="/books/n/gene/app5/#app5.Multigene_Panels">here</a>. More detailed information for clinicians ordering genetic tests can be found <a href="/books/n/gene/app5/#app5.Multigene_Panels_FAQs">here</a>.</p></div><div id="melas.Option_2"><h4>Option 2</h4><p>When the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> is indistinguishable from many other inherited disorders characterized by seizures and weakness, <b>comprehensive</b>
<b><a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing</b> (which does not require the clinician to determine which <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a>[s] are likely involved) is the best option. <b>Exome sequencing</b> is most commonly used; <b><a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genome-sequencing/">genome sequencing</a></b> is also possible. Many laboratories require that the clinician specify if the mitochondrial genome should be included as part of the comprehensive genomic testing.</p><p>For an introduction to comprehensive <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing click <a href="/books/n/gene/app5/#app5.Comprehensive_Genomic_Testing">here</a>. <b>Neurologic manifestations</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Stroke-like episodes</b> present clinically with partially reversible aphasia, cortical vision loss, motor weakness, headaches, altered mental status, and seizures with the eventual progressive accumulation of neurologic deficits.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Dementia</b> affects intelligence, language, perception, attention, and memory function.</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Both the underlying neurologic dysfunction and the accumulating cortical injuries due to stroke-like episodes contribute to dementia.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Executive function deficits have been observed despite the relative sparing of the frontal lobe on neuroimaging, indicating an additional diffuse neurodegenerative process in addition to the damage caused by the stroke-like episodes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.sproule.2008.133">Sproule &#x00026; Kaufmann 2008</a>].</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>
<b>Epilepsy</b>
</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Focal and primary generalized seizures can occur.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Primary generalized seizures in MELAS can occur in the context of normal neuroimaging or be accompanied by neuroimaging abnormalities including stroke-like episodes, white matter lesions, cortical atrophy, and corpus callosum agenesis or hypogenesis (see Suggestive Findings, <a href="#melas.Brain_Imaging">Brain Imaging</a>).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Seizures can occur in MELAS as a manifestation of a stroke-like episode or independently, and may even induce a stroke-like episode [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.finsterer.2015.1553">Finsterer &#x00026; Zarrouk-Mahjoub 2015</a>].</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Migrainous headaches</b> in the form of recurrent attacks of severe pulsatile headaches with frequent vomiting are typical in individuals with MELAS and can precipitate stroke-like episodes. These headache episodes are often more severe during the stroke-like episodes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.ohno.1997.781">Ohno et al 1997</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Hearing impairment</b> due to sensorineural hearing loss is usually mild, insidiously progressive, and often an early clinical manifestation [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.sproule.2008.133">Sproule &#x00026; Kaufmann 2008</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Peripheral neuropathy</b> is usually a chronic and progressive, sensorimotor, and distal polyneuropathy. Nerve conduction studies typically show an axonal or mixed axonal and demyelinating neuropathy [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.kaufmann.2006b.746">Kaufmann et al 2006b</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Early psychomotor development</b> is usually normal, although developmental delay can occasionally occur.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Psychiatric illnesses</b> including depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety, psychosis, and personality changes can occur in MELAS [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.anglin.2012.506">Anglin et al 2012</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b>Myopathy</b> presents clinically as muscle weakness and exercise intolerance.</p><p>
<b>Cardiac manifestations</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Both dilated and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy have been observed, however, the more typical is a non-obstructive concentric hypertrophy [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.sproule.2008.133">Sproule &#x00026; Kaufmann 2008</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Cardiac conduction abnormalities including Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome has been reported occasionally [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.sproule.2007.1625">Sproule et al 2007</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b>Gastrointestinal manifestations.</b> Recurrent or cyclic vomiting is common. Other manifestations include diarrhea, constipation, gastric dysmotility, intestinal pseudo-obstruction, recurrent pancreatitis, and failure to thrive [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.fujii.2004.1126">Fujii et al 2004</a>].</p><p>
<b>Endocrine manifestations</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Diabetes</b> mellitus occurs occasionally, with an average age of onset of 38 years. Diabetes can be type 1 or type 2. Individuals with type 2 diabetes can initially be treated with diet or sulfonylurea, although significant insulinopenia can develop and affected individuals may require insulin therapy (see <a href="#melas.Management">Management</a>) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.maassen.2004.s103">Maassen et al 2004</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Short stature.</b> Individuals with MELAS syndrome are typically shorter than their unaffected family members. Growth hormone deficiency has occasionally been reported [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.yorifuji.1996.621">Yorifuji et al 1996</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Hypothyroidism, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and hypoparathyroidism</b> are infrequent manifestations [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.elhattab.2015.4">El-Hattab et al 2015</a>].</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Other manifestations</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Renal manifestations</b> that may include Fanconi proximal tubulopathy, proteinuria, and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.hotta.2001.1236">Hotta et al 2001</a>]</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Pulmonary hypertension</b> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.sproule.2008.497">Sproule et al 2008</a>]</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Dermatologic manifestations</b> that may include vitiligo, diffuse erythema with reticular pigmentation, and hypertrichosis [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.kubota.1999.469">Kubota et al 1999</a>]</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Chronic anemia</b> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.finsterer.2011.100">Finsterer 2011</a>]</div></li></ul><p><b>Natural history and life expectancy.</b> The disease progresses over years with episodic deterioration related to stroke-like events. The course varies from individual to individual.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>In a cohort of 33 adults with the pathogenic m.3243A&#x0003e;G variant in <i>MT-TL1</i> who were followed for three years, deterioration of sensorineural function, cardiac left-ventricular hypertrophy, EEG abnormalities, and overall severity were observed [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.majamaavoltti.2006.1470">Majamaa-Voltti et al 2006</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>In a natural history study of 31 individuals with MELAS and 54 symptomatic and asymptomatic obligate <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a> relatives over a follow-up period of up to 10.6 years, neurologic examination, neuropsychological testing, and daily living scores significantly declined in all affected individuals with MELAS, whereas no significant deterioration occurred in carrier relatives.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The death rate was more than 17-fold higher in fully symptomatic individuals compared to <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a> relatives. The average observed age at death in the affected MELAS group was 34.5&#x000b1;19 years (range 10.2-81.8 years). Of the deaths, 22% occurred in those younger than 18 years.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The estimated overall median survival time based on fully symptomatic individuals was 16.9 years from onset of focal neurologic disease [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.kaufmann.2011.1965">Kaufmann et al 2011</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>A Japanese prospective cohort study of 96 individuals with MELAS confirmed a rapidly progressive course within a five-year interval, with 20.8% of affected individuals dying within a median time of 7.3 years from diagnosis [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.yatsuga.2012.619">Yatsuga et al 2012</a>].</div></li></ul></div><div id="melas.Causes_of_Phenotypic_Variability"><h3>Causes of Phenotypic Variability</h3><p>For all mtDNA pathogenic variants, clinical expression depends on three factors:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Heteroplasmy.</b> The presence of a mixture of mutated and normal mtDNA</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Tissue distribution</b> of mutated mtDNA</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Threshold effect.</b> The vulnerability of each tissue to impaired oxidative metabolism</div></li></ul><p>While the tissue vulnerability threshold probably does not vary substantially among individuals, mutational load and tissue distribution do vary and may account for the clinical diversity seen in individuals with MELAS. Correlations between the frequency of the more common clinical features and the level of mutated mtDNA in muscle, but not in leukocytes, have been observed [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.chinnery.1997.1713">Chinnery et al 1997</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.jeppesen.2006.1701">Jeppesen et al 2006</a>]. As-yet-undefined nuclear DNA factors may also modify the phenotypic expression of mtDNA pathogenic variants [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.moraes.1993.43">Moraes et al 1993</a>].</p><p>The m.3243A&#x0003e;G <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, the most frequent variant associated with MELAS, is associated with diverse clinical manifestations (i.e., progressive external ophthalmoplegia, diabetes mellitus, cardiomyopathy, deafness) that collectively constitute a wide spectrum ranging from MELAS at the severe end to asymptomatic <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a> status. More severe phenotypes may be the result of a higher abundance of the pathogenic variant in affected organs [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.elhattab.2015.4">El-Hattab et al 2015</a>].</p></div><div id="melas.GenotypePhenotype_Correlations"><h3>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</h3><p>No clear <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genotype-phenotype-correlations/">genotype-phenotype correlations</a> have been identified (see <a href="#melas.Causes_of_Phenotypic_Variability">Causes of Phenotypic Variability</a>).</p></div><div id="melas.Penetrance"><h3>Penetrance</h3><p>In mtDNA-related disorders, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> typically depends on mutational load and tissue distribution, which show random variation within families (see <a href="#melas.Causes_of_Phenotypic_Variability">Causes of Phenotypic Variability</a>).</p></div><div id="melas.Nomenclature"><h3>Nomenclature</h3><p>Typically designated by the acronym MELAS, this disorder may also be referred to as mitochondrial myopathy, encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes.</p></div><div id="melas.Prevalence"><h3>Prevalence</h3><p>The prevalence of MELAS has been estimated to be 0.2:100,000 in Japan [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.yatsuga.2012.619">Yatsuga et al 2012</a>]. Valproic acid should be avoided in the treatment of seizures [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.lin.2007.105">Lin &#x00026; Thajeb 2007</a>].</p><p>Metformin should also be avoided because of its propensity to cause lactic acidosis [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.sproule.2008.133">Sproule &#x00026; Kaufmann 2008</a>].</p><p>Dichloroacetate, which reduces lactate by activating the pyruvate dehydrogenase enzyme, should be avoided in MELAS syndrome. A study evaluating the effect of dichloroacetate in individuals with MELAS syndrome was terminated because of onset or worsening of peripheral neuropathy, indicating that dichloroacetate can be associated with peripheral nerve toxicity [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.kaufmann.2006a.324">Kaufmann et al 2006a</a>].</p></div><div id="melas.Evaluation_of_Relatives_at_Risk"><h3>Evaluation of Relatives at Risk</h3><p>Molecular genetic testing of at-risk maternal relatives may reveal individuals who have high mutational loads and are thus at risk of developing symptoms. No proven disease-modifying intervention exists at present. However, asymptomatic individuals can undergo regular surveillance for early detection of complications.</p><p>See <a href="#melas.Related_Genetic_Counseling_Issues">Genetic Counseling</a> for issues related to testing of at-risk relatives for <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> purposes.</p></div><div id="melas.Pregnancy_Management"><h3>Pregnancy Management</h3><p>Infertility may preclude pregnancy in some affected individuals. Women with MELAS should receive <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> prior to pregnancy. During pregnancy, affected or at-risk women should be monitored for the development of diabetes mellitus and respiratory insufficiency, which may require therapeutic interventions [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.d_azlobato.2005.654">D&#x000ed;az-Lobato et al 2005</a>].</p></div><div id="melas.Therapies_Under_Investigation"><h3>Therapies Under Investigation</h3><p>The transfer of nuclear DNA from fertilized oocytes or zygotes containing a mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> to an enucleated recipient cell could theoretically prevent transmission of mtDNA diseases; proof of this concept has been demonstrated in pronuclear transfers from abnormally fertilized zygotes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.craven.2010.82">Craven et al 2010</a>].</p><p>Search <a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">ClinicalTrials.gov</a> in the US and <a href="https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/ctr-search/search" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">EU Clinical Trials Register</a> in Europe for access to information on clinical studies for a wide range of diseases and conditions.</p></div></div><div id="melas.Genetic_Counseling"><h2 id="_melas_Genetic_Counseling_">Genetic Counseling</h2><p>
<i>Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with
information on the nature, mode(s) of inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them
make informed medical and personal decisions. The following section deals with genetic
risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic
status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or
ethical issues that may arise or to substitute for consultation with a genetics
professional</i>. &#x02014;ED.</p><div id="melas.Mode_of_Inheritance"><h3>Mode of Inheritance</h3><p>MELAS is caused by pathogenic variants in mtDNA and is transmitted by maternal inheritance.</p></div><div id="melas.Risk_to_Family_Members"><h3>Risk to Family Members</h3><p>
<b>Parents of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The father of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> does not have the mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The mother of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> usually has the mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> and may or may not have symptoms. In some mothers, the pathogenic variant may be undetectable in mtDNA from leukocytes and may be detected in other tissues, such as buccal mucosa, cultured skin fibroblasts, hair follicles, urinary sediment, or (most reliably) skeletal muscle.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Alternatively, the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> may have a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> somatic mitochondrial <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Sibs of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The risk to the sibs depends on the genetic status of the mother.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the mother has the mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, all the sibs of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> will inherit the mtDNA pathogenic variant and may or may not have symptoms. Women with higher levels of mutated mtDNA in their blood may have a greater likelihood of having affected offspring [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.chinnery.1998.1889">Chinnery et al 1998</a>].</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Offspring of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>All offspring of females with a mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> will inherit the variant.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Offspring of males with a mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> are not at risk of inheriting the variant.</div></li></ul><p><b>Other family members.</b> The risk to other family members depends on the genetic status of the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>'s mother: if she has a mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, her sibs and mother are also at risk.</p></div><div id="melas.Related_Genetic_Counseling_Issues"><h3>Related Genetic Counseling Issues</h3><p><b>Interpretation of test results of asymptomatic at-risk family members</b> is extremely difficult. Prediction of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> based on test results is not possible.</p><p>
<b>Family planning</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The optimal time for determination of genetic risk and discussion of the availability of prenatal/<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> is before pregnancy.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is appropriate to offer <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> (including discussion of potential risks to offspring and reproductive options) to young adults who are affected or at risk.</div></li></ul></div><div id="melas.Prenatal_Testing_and_Preimplantati"><h3>Prenatal Testing and Preimplantation Genetic Testing</h3><p>Once the specific mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in the mother has been identified, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-testing/">prenatal testing</a> for a pregnancy at increased risk and <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> (PGT) for MELAS are possible. However, prenatal testing for mtDNA pathogenic variants causing MELAS is of uncertain utility.</p><p>Changes in mutational load during pregnancy were evaluated in a small study of nine pregnancies in five women from families with the <a href="/books/NBK1233/table/melas.T.pathogenic_variants_in_mitochond/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobmelasTpathogenicvariantsinmitochond">m.3243A&#x0003e;G</a> mtDNA <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>. Mutational loads in chorionic villi (which were analyzed once) and in amniocytes (analyzed once or twice during pregnancy) were found to be stable [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.bouchet.2006.788">Bouchet et al 2006</a>]. Eleven pregnancies with fetal mutation levels 35% or lower (assessed with <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-testing/">prenatal testing</a> or PGT) resulted in healthy children who have been followed for one month to five years; one pregnancy, with 63% mutation level in the fetus, was terminated [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.monnot.2011.116">Monnot et al 2011</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.treff.2012.1236">Treff et al 2012</a>].</p><p>Interpretation of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-testing/">prenatal testing</a> results is complex for the following reasons:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The mutational load in the mother's tissues and in fetal tissues sampled (i.e., amniocytes and chorionic villi) may not correspond to that of other fetal tissues.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The mutational load in tissues sampled prenatally may shift in utero or after birth as a result of random mitotic segregation.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Prediction of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>, age of onset, severity, or rate of progression is not possible.</div></li></ul></div></div><div id="melas.Resources"><h2 id="_melas_Resources_">Resources</h2><p>

The MELAS-associated variants typically result in impaired mitochondrial translation leading to decreased mitochondrial protein synthesis, which affects the electron transport chain (ETC) complex subunits. Decreased synthesis of ETC complexes results in impaired mitochondrial energy production. The inability of dysfunctional mitochondria to generate sufficient energy to meet the energy needs of various organs results in the multiorgan dysfunction observed in MELAS syndrome. Energy deficiency can also stimulate mitochondrial proliferation. Angiopathy due to mitochondrial proliferation in smooth muscle and endothelial cells of small blood vessels leads to impaired blood perfusion in microvasculature, contributing to the complications observed in MELAS &#x02013; particularly stroke-like episodes. In addition, growing evidence suggests that NO deficiency occurs in MELAS and can contribute significantly to its complications [<a class="bk_pop" href="#melas.REF.elhattab.2015.4">El-Hattab et al 2015</a>].</p><p><b>Genes.</b> See <b>Normal <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene-product/">gene product</a></b> and <a href="/books/NBK1233/#melas.molgen.TB">Table B</a>.
