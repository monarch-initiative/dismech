Limited targeted agents are approved for pediatric sarcomas. Tyrosine kinase (TK) inhibitors (TKi) have shown clinical efficacy in some, but not all, young patients with sarcoma. A major obstacle preventing further advances and clinical implementation is the lack of predictive response biomarkers to guide TK-targeted treatments. TK-activating fusions or mutations are rare in these patients. RNA overexpression of TKs is a frequent feature. The unresolved question is when upregulated TK expression is associated with kinase activation and signaling dependence. We explored the TK molecular landscape of 107 patients with sarcoma from the ZERO Childhood Cancer Precision Medicine Program (ZERO) using whole-genome and -transcriptome sequencing. Phosphoproteomic analyses of tyrosine phosphorylation (pY) and functionalin vitroandin vivoassays were performed in cell lines and patient-derived xenografts (PDX). Our analysis shows that although novel genomic driver lesions are rare, when present they are therapeutically actionable as exemplified by a novelLSM1–FGFR1fusion identified in a patient with osteosarcoma. We further show that in certain contexts, TK RNA expression can indicate TK pathway activity and predict TKi sensitivity. We highlight the utility of FGFR inhibitors inPAX3–FOXO1fusion–positive rhabdomyosarcomas (FP-RMS) characterized by highFGFR4andFGF8RNA expression levels and FGFR4 activation (FGFR4_pY). We demonstrate marked tumor growth inhibition in all FP-RMS PDXs treated with single-agent FGF401 (FGFR4-specific inhibitor) and single-agent lenvatinib (multikinase FGFR inhibitor) and report a clinical response to lenvatinib in a patient with relapsed metastatic FP-RMS. Altogether, we identified new patients with sarcoma who may benefit from FGFR inhibitors, most notably FP-RMS via FGFR4/FGF8 coexpression.

Sarcomas are a heterogeneous group of rare, malignant connective tissue tumors disproportionally affecting the young (1). Standard-of-care treatment has hardly changed in 3 decades, consisting of a “one-size-fits-all” approach combining surgery, radiotherapy, and (poly)chemotherapy (1–4). Survival is limited despite aggressive therapy, particularly in the advanced setting (5%–20%), and side effects of treatment can be severe (1–4). For the vast majority of pediatric sarcomas, no targeted agents are currently approved (1), even as more sarcoma genomes are sequenced. This highlights a significant unmet need for novel and more specific treatments and a rationale to look beyond DNA-based molecular profiles.

One attractive class of therapeutic targets are tyrosine kinases (TK), which are expressed either on the cell membrane [receptor tyrosine kinases (RTK)] or intracellularly. Aberrant RTK or TK signaling can drive cell autonomous growth signals and repress apoptosis in pediatric and adolescent and young adult (AYA) sarcomas (5–11). Despite preclinical evidence supporting the utility of certain TK inhibitors (TKi) for pediatric and AYA sarcoma treatment, clinical trials, without biomarker inclusion criteria, have not demonstrated efficacy for most patients (5). The value of molecular biomarkers in general is emphasized by precision medicine programs, in which 44% to 86% of patients with cancer receiving a targeted therapy showed sustained responses (5,12,13). Although “bona fide” activating genomic TK events such as mutations or fusions are rare in pediatric sarcomas, outlier TK gene expression is much more common and is increasingly recognized as potentially targetable (12,14). For example, three pediatric patients with sarcoma receiving a targeted treatment based on RNA expression levels had sustained responses of up to 50 weeks (12). Furthermore, the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) inhibitor futibatinib (FGFR1-4i) showed efficacy in patients withFGFR-amplified cancers, even in the absence of structural variants (SV) or activating mutations (15). This suggests that many more pediatric and AYA patients with sarcoma might benefit from a TKi based on gene expression levels. The unresolved question is how to determine when TK overexpression truly indicates TK activation and could hence represent a valid therapeutic target.

Our objectives were to investigate the molecular landscape of TKs in pediatric and AYA patients with sarcoma from the ZERO Childhood Cancer Precision Medicine Program (ZERO) to identify novel cases which might benefit from TKi therapy (12,13). Utilizing whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and whole-transcriptome sequencing [RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)] data, together with phosphoproteomics and functionalin vitroandin vivoassays in sarcoma cell lines and unique patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models we show that (i) although novel TK-activating genomic lesions are rare, they are present and therapeutically actionable, and (ii) in certain molecular contexts, TK RNA overexpression can represent a legitimate therapeutic target. We highlight how outlier RNA expression levels of bothFGFR4andFGF8are associated with FGFR4 activation and predict sensitivity to FGFR inhibitors (FGFRi), notably inPAX3–FOXO1fusion–positive rhabdomyosarcomas (FP-RMS).

The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received institutional board approval as follows: the pilot study (TARGET) was approved by the Sydney Children’s Hospitals Network Human Research Ethics Committee (LNR/14/SCH/497) and open from June 2015 to October 2017. The PRISM clinical trial (NCT03336931), conducted as part of ZERO, was approved by the Hunter New England Human Research Ethics Committee of the Hunter New England Local Health District (reference no. 17/02/015/4.06) and the New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee (reference no. HREC/17/HNE/29). Parents/legal guardian for participants <18 years or participants >18 years of age provided informed consent for study inclusion (12). Written informed consent was obtained for each participant prior to inclusion in the study. Patients aged 21 years or younger with suspected or confirmed diagnosis of a very rare or high-risk malignancy (at diagnosis, relapse, or refractory disease), defined as expected probability of survival of less than 30%, could be consented and were eligible for registration on the study. Registration of patients older than 21 years was possible with approval from the study chair when the patient was diagnosed with suspected or confirmed high-risk pediatric-type cancers. Patients with a sarcoma/other soft-tissue malignancy diagnosis were included in this article. Reportable profiles of a subset of these patients were published by Wong and colleagues (12) and Lau and colleagues (16).

WGS was conducted at the Kinghorn Centre for Clinical Genomics, Garvan Institute of Medical Research (Australia), using the Illumina HiSeq X 10 platform with a paired-end read length of 150 bases. Methods have been previously detailed, including further downstream processing pipelines for Strelka somatic single-nucleotide variants (SNV) and short indel benchmarking, noncoding promoter variation, and variant filtration (12).

Whole-transcriptome RNA-seq was conducted at the Murdoch Children’s Research Institute (Australia) and performed with the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Preparation Kit as previously detailed (12). Raw gene counts, transcripts per kilobase million (TPM), fragments per kilobase million, and isoform expression values were calculated using RSEM (v1.2.31), and fusion detection was called using Arriba (v1.1.0). RNA outlier expression analysis was conducted in R, and we note that the reporting of RNA expression has been subject to change over time, as thez-score is calculated in comparison with the ZERO cohort at the time of reporting (17). All RNA-seq expression values are represented as TPM, unless stated otherwise (e.g.,z-score).

In the ZERO program, variants are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ or ‘pathogenic’ in accordance with published guidelines (18). Outlier gene expression levels are defined asz-score >2 or < −2 as standalone changes or >1.5 and <−1.5 if supported by expression differences in related genes/pathways. “Benign,” “likely benign,” or “unknown significance” were not reportable (12).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA; CAMERA, calculates group differences) and single-sample GSEA (calculates enrichment score for individual samples) analyses were performed on the full transcriptomics dataset, using standard parameters (reactome.org). For GSEA, we compared FP-RMS versus other sarcomas.

RNA expression data on patients with sarcoma was extracted from publicly available datasets that included transcriptomics data of patients or PDXs with FP-RMS and fusion-negative RMS (FN-RMS), including the Pediatric Preclinical Testing Consortium [cBioPortal;n = 54sarcoma (6 FP-RMS and 5 FN-RMS)] and OpenPedCan [v15; PedcBioPortal;n = 583sarcoma (8 FP-RMS and 7 FN-RMS); ref.19]. Similar data were extracted for sarcoma cell lines from DepMap [n = 137sarcoma (11 FP-RMS and 13 FN-RMS;DepMap.org); ref.20].

For the generation of the LSM1–FGFR1 construct, patient RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #18080051) under standard conditions. LSM1–FGFR1 full-length cDNA was PCR amplified from patient cDNA using AmpliTaq Gold DNA Polymerase with Buffer II and MgCl2(Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #N8080241) using the following primers: LSM1 FL forward: 5′-ATG​AAC​TAT​ATG​CCT​GGC and FGFR1 FL reverse: 5′ ACAGGGACGGACAGGTGG according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Full-length LSM1–FGFR1 cDNA was then subcloned into the MSCV-IRES-GFP retroviral vector using standard procedures—restriction digest, ligation of the digested product into MSCV-IRES-GFP using T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs, cat. #M0202L), and transformation of NEB Stable cells (New England Biolabs, cat. #C3040H). Retroviral transfection and transduction of target cell lines with respective plasmids, and empty vector controls, was performed as previously described (21). Flow cytometry analysis of transduced cell populations was used to confirm >90% GFP-positive cells, and cells were sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS; UNSW Flow Cytometry Facility, UNSW Sydney) before proceeding to functional assays.

Guide RNAs (gRNA) targeting human SPRY2 were designed using the CHOPCHOP web-based tool for CRISPR/Cas9-guide design (22). The top two ranked guides (SPRY2 sgRNA#1 and SPRY2 sgRNA#2) were selected and cloned into FgH1tUTG. Rh30 cells were transduced with FUCas9Cherry and FgH1tUTG containing SPRY2 sgRNA#1, SPRY2 sgRNA#2, or no sgRNA (control) lentiviruses, as previously described. A double-positive population of >90% was confirmed by flow cytometry analysis of GFP and mCherry expression in transduced cells. Guide expression was induced by the addition of 1μg/mL doxycycline (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #D9891-1G) for 72 hours. Following guide induction, double-stranded breaks have been induced, and doxycycline is washed out of the media. Bulk CRISPR-edited populations were analyzed by Western blot to assess guide efficiency at least 4 days after doxycycline induction. For SPRY2, sgRNA#1 was determined to be the most efficient guide at inducing knockout (KO) in bulk cell populations, and these cell populations were subjected to single cell sorting (UNSW Flow Cytometry Facility) to generate single-cell clonal populations. CRISPR-edited single-cell SPRY2 KO clone #7 was subjected to functional analysis.

Protein extraction was performed by the addition of RIPA lysis buffer containing cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, cat. #11697498001) and PhosSTOP (Roche, cat. #4906845001) phosphatase inhibitor to cell pellets followed by disruption by vortexing. PDX tumor lysates were generated by homogenization of a snap-frozen tumor piece in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitor cocktail and PhosTOPtop. Protein quantification was performed by Pierce BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #23225). Membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies diluted in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #A7906) or 5% skim milk in Tris base sodium chloride (Merck, cat. #T6664) with 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #11332465001; TBST) and incubated overnight at 4°C: anti–p-FGFR1 antibody (milk, Abcam, cat. #Ab173305) at 1:1,000, anti-FGFR1 antibody (milk, Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #9740) at 1:5,000, anti–p-AKT antibody (BSA, Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #4060) at 1:500, anti-AKT antibody (milk, Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #4691) at 1:500, anti–p-ERK1/2 antibody (BSA, Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #9106S) at 1:500, anti-ERK1/2 antibody (BSA, Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #4695) at 1:500, anti-FGF8 (milk, Abcam, cat. #81384) at 1:250, anti-FGFR4 (BSA, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat. #136988) at 1:100, or anti-actin antibody (milk, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #A2228) at 1:1,000. Horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary donkey antirabbit (GE HealthCare, cat. #GEHENA934) or sheep antimouse (GE HealthCare, cat. #NA931) were diluted 1:1,000 in 1% BSA or 5% milk in TBST and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. Signals were visualized and quantified on the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Touch System (Bio-Rad Laboratories) using Image Lab software v6.1 (Bio-Rad Laboratories; ref.23).

Human Phospho-Receptor Tyrosine Kinase Array Kit (R&D Systems, cat. #ARY001B) and Proteome Profiler Human Phospho-Kinase Array Kit (R&D Systems, cat. #ARY003C) were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Signals were visualized on the ChemiDoc Touch System and quantified using Image Lab software v6.1 (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Samples were lysed in 4% sodium deoxycholate at 95°C for 5 minutes. Following sonication, protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #23225). Protein (1.5 mg) was used as starting material. For reduction and alkylation, 10 mmol/L Tris (2-carboxy-ethyl)-phosphine-HCl (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #77720) and 40 mmol/L 2-chloroacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #C0267; pH 7–8) was added to the lysates and boiled at 95°C for 5 minutes. Lysates were then digested with LysC (Wako, cat. #129–02541) and trypsin (Promega, cat. #XV528X) at an enzyme-to-substrate ratio of 1:100 and incubated overnight at 37°C with shaking at 1,500 rpm. The rest of the protein was used for phospho-tyrosine enrichment by affinity purification. Protein digest (1 mg) was acidified to 1% trifluoroacetyl (TFA), and the resulting cleared supernatant was desalted using Sep-Pak tC18 column (Waters, cat. #WAT036815). The peptides were eluted with 6 mL 0.1% TFA/40% acetonitrile (ACN) and dried overnight using a lyophilizer and reconstituted in 1.8 mL IAP wash buffer [1% n-octyl-b-D-glucopyranoside (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #494459), 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #75259), and 150 mmol/L NaCl, pH 7.4 (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #S9888)]. Fifty micrograms each of P-Tyr-1000 (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #8954), P-Tyr-100 (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #9411), and P-Tyr-20 (BD Biosciences, cat. #610000) antibodies were coupled to 60 μL of protein G sepharose beads slurry (Zymed, cat. #10–1,241)] and incubated overnight with peptide samples at 4°C with gentle shaking. The conjugated antibody beads were washed 3 times with IAP buffer and further washed 3 times with water before phosphopeptide elution with 100 μL of 0.15% TFA. Samples were then desalted on a C18 column and evaporated to dryness in a vacuum concentrator (CentriVap Labconco).

Samples were analyzed on an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive HF Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples were injected onto a 100 μm, 2-cm nanoviper PepMap 100 trap column, eluted, and separation performed on a RSLC nano column 75 μm × 50 cm and a PepMap 100 C18 analytic column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #164942). Peptides were eluted using an LC gradient of 2.5% to 42.5% ACN/0.1% formic acid (FA) at 250 nL/minutes over 150 minutes. The mass spectrometer was operated in the data-dependent acquisition mode to automatically switch between full MS scans and subsequent MS-MS acquisitions. Survey full scan MS spectra (m/z 300–1,750) were acquired in the Orbitrap with 70,000 resolution (at m/z 200) after accumulation of ions to a 1 × 106target value with a maximum injection time of 30 ms. Dynamic exclusion was set to 30 seconds. Up to 10 most intense charged ions (z ≥ +2) were sequentially isolated and fragmented in the collision cell by higher-energy collisional dissociation with a fixed injection time of 120 ms, 17,500 resolution and automatic gain control target of 1 × 105. The raw MS files were processed using MaxQuant software (v6.12.0) with the following parameters: FDR <0.01, precursor mass tolerance set to 20 ppm, fragment mass tolerance set to 0.5 Da, minimum peptide length of six amino acids, enzyme specificity set to trypsin/P & LysC, human UniProt database (v2020), and a maximum number of missed cleavages of 2. Fixed modifications were limited to carbamidomethyl (C), and variable modifications were set to oxidation (M), acetyl (protein N-term), and Phospho (STY). The “match between runs” option in MaxQuant was selected using the default parameters (24).

Both raw and log2-transformed phosphocounts were used to initially visualize and examine phosphorylation levels. We next subjected our imputed phospho-tyrosine (pY) dataset to two independent assays that each unbiasedly predict kinase activation patterns based on substrate specificity. The first one, PhosMap (25), estimates kinase activity for individual samples based on the assumption that kinase activation is reflected by the phosphorylation state of its substrates; the mean value method was used to quantify kinase activity from phosphopeptide abundances. The second one, the Kinase Library (26), identifies hyperactive or suppressed kinases between groups (FP-RMS versus other sarcomas) by comparing up- and downregulated phosphorylation sites against known kinase motifs. Phosphorylation sites were scored and ranked against characterized kinases to determine likely kinase–substrate pairs, and motif enrichment was assessed by comparing the frequency of predicted kinases among regulated versus unchanged sites. The one-sided Fisher exact test was used to identify kinases significantly associated with increased or decreased phosphorylation in FP-RMS versus other sarcomas.

For growth inhibition assays, cells were seeded into 96-well plates in a final volume of 100 µL (sarcoma cell lines) or 200 µL (Ba/F3 cells) of normal culture medium. Rh3 cells were seeded at 8  ×  103cells/well, Rh5, Rh36, Rh41, M25FV24C cells at 6  ×  103cells/well, RD and EW8 cells at 4  ×   103cells/well, and Rh30, ES8 and MG63 cells at 3  ×   103cells/well. Ba/F3 cells were seeded at 1  ×   105cells/mL or 3  ×   105cells/mL in 96-well round-bottom plates. Serial dilutions of inhibitors (Selleck Chemicals) erdafitinib (cat. #S8401), lenvatinib (cat. #S5240), FGF401 (roblitinib; cat. #S8548; refs.27,28), AZD4547 (cat. #S2801), regorafenib (cat. #S1178), imatinib (cat. #S2475), ponatinib (cat. #S1490), or 10 µmol/L thonzonium bromide (100% cell death control, Focus Bioscience, cat. #HY-B1246) were added to growth media in the presence or absence of doxycycline (1 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #D9891) or FGF-8a (10 ng/mL, PeproTech cat. #100-25A) and FGF-8b (10 ng/mL, PeproTech cat. #100-25) and incubated under normal culture conditions of 5% CO2for 48 or 96  hours prior to assessment by resazurin assay. Responses were analyzed by four-parameter logistic model by non-linear regression and area under the curve (AUC) in GraphPad Prism 9.2.0, with statistical analysis by one-way ANOVA with the Tukey multiple-comparisons test (GraphPad Software; ref.23). To investigate whether drug sensitivities observed in short-term viability assays could be clinically meaningful, we calculated the maximum single dose (Cmax) and steady state (Css) concentrations achievable for a patient for each drug.

GFP-positive Ba/F3 cells expressing MSCV,LSM1–FGFR1, orFGFR1OP2-FGFR1(OP2-FGFR1) were subjected to an interleukin 3 (IL3) withdrawal assay as previously described (21).

Cells were harvested following 8-hour (cell cycle assay) or 96-hour (apoptosis assay) treatment with serial dilutions of inhibitors (Selleck Chemicals) erdafitinib (cat. #S8401), lenvatinib (cat. #S5240), or FGF401 (cat. #S8548). To evaluate cellular apoptosis, samples were stained with Annexin V and 7-AAD solutions (29). To investigate cell-cycle effects, treated cells were fixed and stained with propidium iodide (29).

Cells were seeded on 6-well tissue culture plates. After 48 hours, serial dilutions of erdafitinib, lenvatinib, or FGF401 were added to growth media. Cells were incubated for 2 weeks (treatments replenished at days 4, 8, and 11) prior to the addition of 0.5% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #C0775) in 50% methanol for 10 minutes. Crystal violet was removed by repeated rinses with water, and plates were dried overnight before imaging on the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Touch System (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

One of the most notable RNA features was the overexpression ofFGFRs across sarcoma subtypes (Fig. 2A), prompting further analysis of FGF ligands, FGF binding proteins, and other closely related FGF-pathway mediators (Fig. 2B–D; Supplementary Table S4).FGFR1overexpression was observed across multiple subtypes, including selected cases of FN-RMS, osteosarcoma, MPNST, and GIST. For the first time, we observed individual instances of outlierFGFR1expression in a patient with FMX (zcc434) andEWSR1–PATZ1round cell sarcoma (zcc366), among the highest levels detected in our cohort (Fig. 2C). TheEWSR1–PATZ1tumor also had the highest levels ofFGFR3andFGFRL1(Fig. 2C). In addition to the known association with the FP-RMS subtype (Supplementary Fig. S4C), increasedFGFR4expression was also observed in one patient with DSRCT and one with FN-RMS(Fig. 2C).

OvertFGF8expression was observed exclusively in patients with FP-RMS (Fig. 2BandD; Supplementary Fig. S4D). FGF8 is an FGFR4 ligand (31), and there was a significant positive correlation betweenFGFR4andFGF8RNA expression, primarily driven by patients with FP-RMS (Fig. 2E). Although FGF8 and FGFR4 have been reported in FP-RMS and potential dependencies onFGF8andFGFR4genes in RMS noted (Fig. 2F), a link between coexpression of those genes and FGFRi had not been thoroughly explored in FP-RMS (32–34). Gene expression was not correlated with CNVs for eitherFGFR4orFGF8(Supplementary Fig. S4E and S4F). None of the patients with FP-RMS with outlierFGFR4expression harbored an activatingFGFR4mutation.SPRY2, a negative regulator of the FGFR pathway, was highly expressed in two patients with FN-RMS samples and to a lesser extent also in one patient with infantile fibrosarcoma and one with HGU (Fig. 2B). None of the patients with FP-RMS in our cohort exhibited elevatedSPRY2levels. GSEA analysis on the full RNA expression dataset independently identified three FGFR4-related pathways to be significantly enriched in FP-RMS versus all other sarcomas (Supplementary Fig. S4G and S4H). To corroborate our findings, we interrogated two larger, publicly available transcriptomics datasets with up to 583 patients with sarcoma. This confirmed the FGFR4/FGF8 coexpression signature to be a recurrent molecular feature across FP-RMS (Fig. 2G). We also examined FGFR4 protein expression with IHC across a TMA of 47 patients with RMS (9 FP-RMS, 2PAX7-FOXO1-RMS, and 36 eRMS), supplemented with larger tissue sections of 14 FP-RMS, 2 FN-RMS, 2 osteosarcoma, and 1 HGU PDX models. FGFR4 staining was present and diffuse (homogeneous) in 100% of the samples of patients with FP-RMS andPAX7–FOXO1RMS and PDX models, diffuse or patchy (heterogeneous) in 78% of FN-RMS, and negative in 22% of FN-RMS and 100% of osteosarcoma and HGU samples. The homogeneous expression of FGFR4 in FP-RMS further supported a rationale to focus on FGFR4 as a potential drug target in this subtype (Fig. 2H–L; Supplementary Fig. S5A).

Western blot analysis of three patients with FP-RMS with snap-frozen PDX material also confirmed the presence of FGFR4 and FGF8 proteins (Supplementary Fig. S5B).

Although wild-type (WT)FGFR4overexpression has been reported in FP-RMS before, its role in the absence of an activating mutation has been highly controversial. Despite reportedin vitroactivity of some FGFR-targeted kinase inhibitors in FP-RMS (35,36),in vivotargeting of WTFGFR4with such inhibitors (e.g., ponatinib) yielded disappointing results, leading to the consensus that targeting WTFGFR4with TKIs in FP-RMS is futile (37). Accordingly, there has been a reticence to move FGFR4-targeting kinase inhibitors toward clinical applications for FP-RMS. As FGF ligand expression has been shown to contribute to FGFR pathway activation in several cancer types (38), we hypothesized that concurrentFGF8andFGFR4overexpression in FP-RMS might drive FGFR4 activation and predict FGFRi sensitivity.

We tested these hypotheses in a panel of sarcoma cell lines, including FP- and FN-RMS, which demonstrated similarFGFRpathway RNA expression profiles to patient samples (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S6A, Supplementary Table S5), including highFGFR4in all four FP-RMS cell lines and one FN-RMS cell line and highFGF8expression exclusive to the four FP-RMS cell lines. SimilarFGFR4andFGF8expression patterns were observed across the 137 sarcoma cell lines, including 11 FP-RMS and 13 FN-RMS, in DepMap, underlining the representability of our panel (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Rh3, Rh5, and Rh41 FP-RMS cells most closely resemble molecular profiles of patients with FP-RMS patient withFGFR4+/FGF8+RNA coexpression and lowSPRY2levels. The positive correlation betweenFGFR4andFGF8RNA expression detected in patient samples was also observed in FP-RMS sarcoma cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S6C). pY mass spectrometry analysis (MS-pY) showed FGFR4 tyrosine phosphorylation (FGFR4_pY) in all four FP-RMS cell lines and in FN-RMS Rh36 cells (Fig. 3B; ref.10). There was a positive correlation between FGFR4_pY and bothFGFR4andFGF8RNA expression in sarcoma cell lines (Fig. 3CandD). To explore this relationship in more realistic patient models, we used sarcoma PDX models generated within the ZERO program that have been screened inin vivodrug efficacy testing to support clinical decision-making (39). Fourteen sarcoma ZERO PDX models with matched RNA-seq data, including six models of patients with FP-RMS, underwent MS-pY profiling. We observed pronounced FGFR4 activation in all tested FP-RMS PDX models (Fig. 3E) at the commonly phosphorylated residues associated with activation (pY642/643) and a positive correlation of FGFR4_pY withFGFR4(Fig. 3F) andFGF8(Fig. 3G) RNA expression. PDX models of Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma, MRT, and HGU examined concurrently had low or no detectable FGFR4_pY (Fig. 3E), consistent with lowFGFR4andFGF8RNA expression (Fig. 2B). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of kinase activities predicted from phosphoproteomic data further illustrated that FP-RMS clustered largely together, characterized by a pFGFR4 signature as virtually the only overactivated TK (Fig. 3H). A subsequent independent characterization of substrate specificity of all TKs further confirmed significant activation of FGFR4 in FP-RMS versus other sarcomas (Fig. 3I; ref.26). Together, these data indicate not only that bothFGFR4andFGF8are associated with FGFR4 activation but also suggest FGFR4 to be the single most important activated TK in FP-RMS.

We included four FP-RMS cell lines (Rh3, Rh5, Rh41, and Rh30) with theFGFR4+/FGF8+ RNA coexpression signature, five sarcoma lines lacking this signature (two FN-RMS, two Ewing sarcoma, and one osteosarcoma; negative controls), and Ba/F3 cells engineered to express theFGFR1OP2-FGFR1(OP2-FGFR1) fusion oncogene (positive control; ref.40). Microscopic evaluation further suggested that our colorimetric viability readout may have underestimated the observed drug effects (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. S8–S11). For example, microscopic examination of Rh41 FP-RMS cells treated with 1 and 10 µmol/L lenvatinib indicated that many cells were dead or dying (Fig. 4C). We also performed long-term colony assays on three FP-RMS lines of varying susceptibility to lenvatinib, erdafitinib, and FGF401. These data confirmed that the FP-RMS Rh5 and Rh41 cells were sensitive to FGFRi and also showed that FGFRi effects in FP-RMS are better captured in long-term versus short-term viability assays (Fig. 4DandE). This was most apparent with the FGFR4-specific inhibitor FGF401 (Fig. 4F), which markedly reduced colony formation even at 0.01 µmol/L in both Rh5 and Rh41, whereas Rh30 cells, which express high levels of the negative pathway regulator SPRY2, were significantly more resistant (P< 0.001). SPRY2 KO in Rh30 resulted in a trend toward increased FGFRi sensitivity with cell-cycle analysis (Supplementary Fig. S12A and S12B). Exogeneous FGF8 addition resulted in a significant decrease in colony-forming capacity following erdafitinib treatment, and a similar trend was observed with FGF401 or lenvatinib (Supplementary Fig. S12C and S12D). This suggests that although SPRY2 (which is only occasionally overexpressed in FP-RMS) may be partly involved in mediating FGFRi resistance, FGF8 (which is a more common overexpression feature in FP-RMS) seems to be the more important mediator in regulating FP-RMS cell progression and FGFR4i sensitivity.

To explore whether lenvatinib, erdafitinib, and FGF401 were inducing apoptotic cell death in FP-RMS cell lines, we performed a flow cytometry–based Annexin V/7-AAD analysis (Fig. 4G). This showed increased Annexin V expression in FGFRi-treated cells compared with untreated controls, indicating apoptotic cell death. To assess whether this was preceded by cell-cycle arrest, we measured the proportion of cells in G1-, S- and G2–M-phase upon FGFRi exposure. These flow cytometry data show that all three tested agents induced the G1cell-cycle arrest (P< 0.0001,Fig. 4H) prior to apoptosis (Fig. 4G). Using antibody arrays, we further showed on-target effects of each FGFRi by reducing FGFR4_pY (Fig. 4I). Downstream AKT1/2/3 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation was affected to varying degrees by the different drugs. Overall, the most pronounced decreases in FGFR4_pY and downstream mediators with all drugs were observed in Rh5, the best responding cell line (Fig. 4J).

We observed a sustained inhibition of tumor growth in the zcc81 FP-RMS PDX model, in which none of the mice reached event (Fig. 5GandK). In the predicted non-responding zcc275 model, all mice progressed while on lenvatinib treatment (Fig. 5HandL). We further noted tumor volume reductions of up to 44% as best response at any moment in time in individual mice bearing FP-RMS models treated with lenvatinib (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Histologic examination of FP-RMS zcc27 and zcc31 PDX models revealed that vehicle control and FGF401- and lenvatinib-treated tumors from both models looked morphologically similar (Fig. 5MandN). There were no apparent differences in the number of apoptotic cells, and only minimal necrosis (∼5%) was identified in lenvatinib-treated tumors from both models. The Ki-67 proliferative index on the other hand was reduced in FGFR-treated PDXs, most notably in FGF401-treated tumors (<5% Ki-67–positive cells in zcc27; 10%–20% in zcc31) compared with lenvatinib (40% Ki-67–positive cells in zcc27; 20% in zcc31) and vehicle (40% both models). These findings are in keeping with a cytostatic rather than a cytotoxic drug effect.

As lenvatinib is combined with etoposide and ifosfamide in patients with osteosarcoma, we tested the potential of this combination in FP-RMS (41,42).In vitro, lenvatinib combined with etoposide, and the active metabolite of ifosfamide (palifosfamide) resulted in additive antitumor effects, including more cell death in Rh5 FP-RMS cells treated with the combination compared with the highest tested concentrations of each drug alone (Supplementary Fig. S14). We also tested this combination in sarcoma PDX models (Supplementary Fig. S13). Although the HGU zcc275 PDX model did not respond to clinically relevant doses of etoposide and ifosfamide, the FP-RMS PDX models were unexpectedly highly sensitive to this same chemotherapy regimen, precluding the assessment of the effects of adding lenvatinib to this regimenin vivo. Although complete tumor regressions were observed, the potential of this combination in FP-RMS remains to be established.

Based on our preclinical work, FGFRi are now prospectively suggested as an option (“recommended”) to high-risk patients with FP-RMS in ZERO who failed standard of care, based on theFGFR4/FGF8coexpression signature.

The potential for TKi to impact on patients with FP-RMS is illustrated in the following patient response in the ZERO cohort. A 15-year-old male, diagnosed with stage 4 group 4 FP-RMS and bony metastases (zcc153) was enrolled on ZERO and subsequently presented at MTB. ThePAX3–FOXO1gene fusion was confirmed. Other reportable genomic findings included highCDK4andCCND3expression. Further analysis revealed this patients’ gene expression profile to be consistent with our proposed signature of sensitivity, with elevated RNA expression of bothFGFR4(520 TPM) andFGF8(50 TPM) and activation of FGFR4 by MS-pY analysis in this patients’ PDX model (Fig. 3E).

The patient progressed despite multiagent chemotherapy and radiotherapy to the primary and metastatic masses. Surveillance magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated multifocal perianal and retroperitoneal relapse of FP-RMS (Fig. 6A). He was treated on a study of lenvatinib for patients with relapsed or refractory solid malignancies and had a partial response on MRI to four cycles of lenvatinib (Fig. 6B). After a 4-week pause of lenvatinib for whole-brain radiotherapy due to symptoms and imaging findings consistent with a central nervous system leptomeningeal relapse, lenvatinib was recommenced. Follow-up MRI imaging demonstrated continued stability or improvement in the abdominal disease (Fig. 6C), although ultimately disease progression occurred at all sites. Although we cannot exclude that lenvatinib acted, perhaps in part, through one of its non-FGFR4 targets, this patients’ transcriptomic (Fig. 2AandB; Supplementary Fig. S4G and S4H; Supplementary Table S6) and phosphoproteomic (Fig. 3E,H, andI) profile is, similar to other patients with FP-RMS, heavily indicative of FGFR4 activation and signaling dependence. There was no evidence toward activation of any other lenvatinib target (VEGFR/PDGFR/KIT), and these profiles are virtually identical to our other FP-RMS models that did respond to both lenvatinib and the FGFR4-selective inhibitor FGF401in vivo(Fig. 5).

In this study, we integrated data from a comprehensive, multiplatform precision medicine program to paint a complete and unbiased picture of actionable TK aberrations in one of the largest and most diverse sarcoma cohorts of high-risk pediatric and AYA patients with sarcoma investigated to date. Our approach coupled whole-genomic and -transcriptomic TK features in clinical sarcoma samples to phosphoproteomics and used functionalin vitroandin vivoassays in sarcoma cell lines and unique PDX models to validate newly identified therapeutically actionable aberrations in the FGFR pathway. The key finding is that FGFRi may be beneficial to a subset of patients with sarcoma who would currently not have such drugs included in their clinical treatment plans. This includes patients with novel but rare activating fusion oncogenes (such asLSM1–FGFR1) but perhaps more importantly patients in whom the patterns of receptor and ligand expression may be associated with activated signaling.

Our most significant finding is the potential utility of FGFR4-directed kinase inhibitors in FP-RMS, characterized by theFGFR4+/FGF8+coexpression signature. ActivatingFGFR4mutations are rare in RMS (∼7.5–10%; ref.43), butFGFR4+/FGF8+overexpression and associated FGFR4_pY activation signatures we report are recurrently observed across FP-RMS. This signature renders FP-RMS particularly vulnerable to FGFR inhibition in multiplein vitroandin vivomodels. The fact that we observed significantin vivotumor growth inhibition with relatively small group sizes underlines the effect size of our FGFRi. The apparent inhibitory effects observed with the clinically applicable multikinase inhibitor lenvatinib in ourin vivoPDX models, including tumor volume reductions of up to 44%, provides important preclinical data on its potential clinical utility. The clinical response, including partial tumor regression for several months while maintaining a good quality of life, to single-agent lenvatinib in a patient with relapsed FP-RMS harboring our molecular signature provides compelling anecdotal evidence supporting the potential impact these drugs may have. Our findings are especially relevant to advanced sarcomas, which are notoriously difficult-to-treat tumors with a dismal prognosis. All patients and patient-related PDX models included in this study were deemed high-risk, meaning their chances of survival were <30%. Pazopanib is the only approved targeted agent for metastatic soft-tissue sarcoma in adults (excluding GIST adipocytic sarcoma), and its approval was based on a significantly improved progression-free survival of 3 months compared with placebo, without prolonging overall survival (44). Although clinical trials and more research on FGFR-based combinations in FP-RMS are required, our data offer an exciting proposition for a tumor which, when metastatic, has a miserable prognosis.

The oncogenic role of elevated TK expression in the absence of a genomic aberration is controversial, and RNA overexpression of WTFGFR4is no exception. Several previous studies investigated the role and targetability of FGFR4 in RMS, predominantly in the context of activating mutations. In murine models, there is conflicting data whether WTFGFR4overexpression is sufficient to transform primary mouse myoblasts (45,46).FGFR4knockdown further enhanced FP-RMS Rh4 growth in one study (45) but killed FP-RMS Rh28 and Rh30 in another (36). The latter also demonstrated that treatment of FP-RMS cells withPD173074(FGFR1/VEGFR2 inhibitor with additional FGFR4 inhibitory effects) reduced Rh28 cell growthin vitroand induced apoptosis in Rh28 and Rh30 cells.In vivo,PD173074treatment induced Rh28 tumor regression,albeit at toxic levels.The authors acknowledge that nonspecific toxicity may have influenced study outcomes and concluded that pharmacologic blockade of FGFR4 will require alternative approaches. Ponatinib at 30 mg/kg daily further lacked efficacy in RMS772 xenografts transduced with WTFGFR4(37), and more recently the FGFR1-4 inhibitor futibatinib lackedin vitroandin vivoefficacy in FP-RMS cell models, though this included only two FP-RMS cells (RH4 and SCMC) that did not exhibit high FGFR4 activation (47). Although the presence of FGF8 was not investigated, DepMap data indicate that FGFR4+/FGF8+ coexpression was similar to our models. Assuming this expression was retained in the former study, this suggests that the lack of response may be drug-specific, a finding consistent with ourin vivoerdafitinib data, which is also an FGFR1-4 inhibitor.

The contradictions in the literature surrounding widespread applicability of FGFR kinase inhibitors in FP-RMS have resulted in a reticence to move forward into clinical applications. This is reflected within ZERO; prior to this study, highFGFR4expression was not considered targetable with TKis in patients with FP-RMS and thus did not lead to clinical recommendation of FGFRi. AlthoughFGFR4can function as an oncogene, the important question is whether targetingFGFR4overexpression is a therapeutically valid approach. Our data argue that in some molecular contexts, WTFGFR4is a legitimate kinase drug target, in which we highlight its coexpression withFGF8. This may be relevant for other FGFR4+/FGF8+ cancers too, e.g., Wilms tumors (OpenPedCan), thereby extending our potential impact. It is important to note that this functional information is particularly relevant for predicting efficacy to FGFR kinase inhibitors. For FGFR4-directed therapies using other mechanisms to elicit antitumor effects, e.g., evoking tumor cell death via immune cells such as FGFR4-chimeric antigen receptor T cell therapy, or via FGFR4 antibodies conjugated to cytotoxic moieties such as radionuclides/(cyto)toxins, the mere presence of FGFR4 on tumor cells may suffice to result in therapeutic benefits (48,49).

The activation and actionability of WTFGFR4was recently also demonstrated in breast cancer models. FGFR4 protein overexpression and phosphorylation in a luminal B breast cancer PDX model conferred FGFR4 inhibitor sensitivity. TheFGFR4gene was not amplified, suggesting a transcriptional or posttranscriptional mechanism of activation much like our findings (24). In murine breast cancer models, a neutralizing anti-FGF8 antibody further showed potent effectsin vitroandin vivo.This application has not been investigated in RMS (50).

An interesting question is whetherFGF8is the only ligand indicating FGFR4 activation in FP-RMS. FGF19 is a well-known ligand of FGFR4, and its expression together with WTFGFR4and coregulatorKLBwas identified as a marker of FGF401-sensitive hepatocellular carcinoma (27,28,51), leading to the first-in-human phase I/II study of FGF401 (27,28,52). In our study, we found no evidence ofFGF19orKLBupregulation in FP-RMS. There is evidence to indicate that FGF8 expression is induced by the canonical driver fusion of FP-RMS,PAX3–FOXO1(32). Notably, bothFGF8andFGFR4are dependency genes in FP-RMS (34). We now comprehensively describe FGFR kinase inhibitor sensitivity, including pronounced single-agentin vivoefficacy, in FP-RMS that has not been previously established but is consistent with the hypothesis that FGF8/FGFR4 signaling is a critical oncogenic signaling pathway in FP-RMS.

In addition to the FGFR pathway, our comprehensive approach also captured outlier RNA expression of other TKs and associated mediators in sarcoma. An important therapeutic question that flows from our work is which of these or other TK RNA overexpression signatures in sarcoma are also actionable targets? These will not be recognized by genomic aberrations, but instead by functional preclinical studies, (phospho)proteomics, and drug screens.

In conclusion, our studies provide a compelling rationale to implement screening of the complete genomic and transcriptomic landscape of TKs to identify unrecognized drivers and expression signatures that can be therapeutically targeted in pediatric and AYA sarcomas. Multiple unbiased assays independently identified FGFR4 as the single most activated TK in FP-RMS, and all our tested FP-RMS models responded to the FGFR4-specific inhibitor FGF401 and the multikinase FGFR inhibitor lenvatinibin vivo. As a direct result of our work, the FGFRi lenvatinib, with or without chemotherapy, is now prospectively suggested as an option for high-risk patients with FP-RMS in ZERO who exhausted other treatments. This has already resulted in clinical benefit for a patient with FP-RMS who progressed through multiagent chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Although future work addressing FGFR resistance mechanisms in more detail is warranted, including the examination of compensatory signaling pathways, we believe FGFRi represent a promising therapeutic avenue for patients with FP-RMS, deserving further clinical and combination therapy-directed investigations.
