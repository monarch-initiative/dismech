# Allogeneic haematopoietic cell transplantation in advanced systemic mastocytosis in the new era: A CIBMTR study.
**Authors:** Ustun C, Zhang MJ, Peterson A, Baek A, Agha M, Alkhateeb H, Chhabra S, Coltoff A, de Lima M, Gandhi A, Ho V, Kassim A, Lin A, Gowda L, Borthakur G, DeAngelo DJ, McGuirk J, Mensah F, Nadiminti KV, Nishihori T, Pantin J, Trunk A, Uberti J, Marcucci G, Gotlib J, Akin C, Hamadani M, Pullarkat V, Valent P, Grunwald M, Juckett M, Oran B, Saber W, Burns LJ
**Journal:** Br J Haematol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/bjh.70154](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjh.70154)

## Content

We investigated the outcomes of patients with advanced systemic mastocytosis (AdvSM) undergoing allogeneic haematopoietic cell therapy (alloHCT) in the US. Twenty-seven adult (median age, 58 years) patients with AdvSM received alloHCT from 2014 to 2021. Most patients were white (93%), male (63%) and had systemic mastocytosis (SM) with associated haematological neoplasm (SM-AHN, 70%), received peripheral blood grafts (89%) and non-myeloablative regimens. KIT mutation was detected in 14 of the reported 16 patients (87.5%). Post-transplantation cyclophosphamide (PTCy) was used in approximately 50% of the cohort. Approximately 1 year after alloHCT, median bone marrow mast cell burden decreased from 15% pre-alloHCT to 1.5% (range 0-8) at 1 year post-alloHCT. The median serum tryptase decreased from 55 ng/mL (range 3-400 ng/mL) pre-alloHCT to 18 ng/mL (range 0-481 ng/mL) at 1 year post-alloHCT. At 1 year, progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were 59% (95% confidence interval [CI], 41%-77%) and 74% (95% CI, 56%-89%) respectively. In the subset of patients with SM-AHN, 1-year PFS and OS were 74% (52%-91%) and 79% (95% CI, 58%-94%) respectively. The cumulative incidence of relapse/progression for the entire population of patients with SM (n = 27) was 4% (95% CI, 0-14) at 100 days and 15% (95% CI, 4-31) at 1 year. For the subset of patients with AHN, the cumulative incidence was 11% (95% CI, 1-28) at 100 days and 21% (95% CI, 6-42) at 1 year. Relapse/progression was the cause of death in six of 12 patients (5 died of AHN and 1 died of SM progression). Eighteen and nine patients received a KIT inhibitor before alloHCT and after alloHCT respectively. In addition, six patients received a KIT inhibitor for the treatment of relapse/progression of SM after alloHCT. Although it is difficult to delineate the exact effect of alloHCT and KIT inhibitors because of the size of the study in this era, the use of alloHCT and KIT inhibitors pre- and post- alloHCT is increasing. In the near future, larger studies are required to provide further insights into this subject.

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is a rare heterogenous hematologic malignancy of mast cells (MCs), with most patients (approximately 90%) having an indolent course (non-advanced SM). 1 In a study, the survival of these patients with indolent forms comparable with a age-matched healthy individuals. 2 Its diagnosis, although well described, may be delayed or missed because of rarity of the disease. 3 , 4 The survival of the remaining patients is shortened by SM due to organ dysfunction and/or associated hematologic neoplasm (AHN). 2 , 5 SM with unfavorable prognosis is categorized as advanced SM (AdvSM) 6 and comprises aggressive SM (ASM), mast cell leukemia (MCL), and SM-AHN. In patients with SM-AHN, almost all hematologic malignancies are myeloid; chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN), and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) are the most common hematologic malignancies. The KIT D816V mutation is the driver of genetic abnormality of neoplastic MCs in most patients with SM. Of note, the KIT D816V mutation can also be detected in non-mast cell lineages (e.g., neutrophils, eosinophils, monocytes) 7 , especically advanced forms (SM-AHN), and indicate poor prognosis 8 . Organ dysfunction due to neoplastic MCs is an important characteristic of AdvSM. 9 These abnormalities are called “a C finding” as the “C” indicates the need of cytoreduction. 3 Liver fibrosis, even without abnormal liver functions tests, has been observed in patients with AdvSM. 10 , 11

The prognosis of patients with AdvSM patients has been improving with the therapeutic use of novel KIT inhibitors (e.g., midostaurin, avapritinib). 12 , 13 Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (alloHCT) has been used in this rare disease, 14– 18 Even some guidelines are now available on alloHCT; 19 , 20 the definitive indications, efficacy, and complications of alloHCT for AdvSM remain to be clarified. 14 , 15 A thorough understanding of the role for transplantation is particularly important in the recent era when KIT inhibitors for treating SM have been approved. Avapritinib, in a retrospective analysis, improved overall survival (OS) in patients with AdvSM compared with the best available treatment. 21 Moreover, there have been remarkable changes in the clinical practice of alloHCT that may improve clinical outcomes. Post-transplantation cyclophosphamide (PTCy) has successfully decreased graft-versus-host disease (GVHD); 22 , 23 however, it seems to be associated with more infections. 24 Although as a non-comparative CIBMTR study, this study also cannot give efficacy data, we still investigated the outcomes of alloHCT in patients with AdvSM in this new era given a paucity of outcome data.

Adults (age ≥ 18 years) with SM who received alloHCT in the United States (US) during 2014–2021 were identified in the CIBMTR (Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research) Registry. CIBMTR is a research collaboration between the Medical College of Wisconsin and NMDP. Almost 100% of patients who receive an allogeneic HCT in the US have their data reported to CIBMTR. Given the rarity of SM, supplemental data were collected from participating centers for this study. All patients included in this study gave written consent to participate in the CIBMTR Research Database and have their data included in observational research. This study was approved by the NMDP institutional review board.

Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as death or disease relapse/progression; patients who were alive and in remission were censored at the date of last contact. Overall survival (OS) was defined as death from any cause; surviving patients were censored at date of last contact. Non-relapse mortality (NRM) was defined as death without relapse/progression of SM or AHN; patients who were alive and did not experience relapse/progression were censored at the date of last contact. The competing risk was disease relapse/progression. Neutrophil recovery was defined as initial absolute neutrophil count >500/mm3 for 3 consecutive laboratory values. In the absence of neutrophil recovery, patients were censored at the date when the patient was last known to be alive. The competing risk was death without neutrophil recovery. Acute GVHD and chronic GVHD (limited and extensive) were defined by the consensus criteria. 25 , 26 Patients who did not have GVHD were censored at the date of subsequent transplant or date of the last contact, whichever occurred earlier, if applicable. Death and relapse without GVHD were competing risks. Causes of death were the primary causes reported by centers. Relapse/progression was defined as relapse from complete remission or progression of disease as determined by molecular, flow cytometry, cytogenetic/fluorescent in situ hybridization, radiographic or hematological/clinical findings

Characteristics of patients, disease, transplant, post-transplant factors, and primary causes of death were described. The median and range were determined for continuous variables and percent of the total for categorical variables. Median follow-up was determined using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. PFS and OS were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method. Survival probabilities were calculated from the date of alloHCT to the date of event occurred or the date of censoring for those who remained event-free. Neutrophil recovery, acute GVHD and chronic GVHD were calculated using cumulative incidence estimates with competing risks. Relapse/progression was calculated by using the cumulative incidence estimate.

A total of 27 patients from 19 centers met the selection criteria and were included in this study. Five centers, representing a total of 6 patients, declined participation due to the study’s need for supplemental data submission. Three, 5, and 19 patients had MCL, ASM, and SM-AHN, respectively. The median follow-up time was 36 months. The characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. The median age was 58 years (range 32–72 years), and 17 patients were male (63%). The majority were White (25 patients, 93%), while 1 patient was Black or African American (3.5%) and another patient was multiracial (3.5%). The median time from diagnosis to alloHCT was 237 days (89–4417 days). The hematopoietic cell transplantation comorbidity index (HCT-CI) ranged from 0 to ≥ 5, with most patients having an HCT-CI of more than 3 (41% with HCT-CI of 3–4; 22% HCT-CI of ≥ 5). KPS was <90% in 44% of patients (n=12). The years of transplant ranged from 2014 to 2021; more patients underwent alloHCT in recent years (i.e., 52% of the cohort received alloHCT between 2020 and 2021). KIT mutation was detected in 14 of reported 16 patients (87.5%).

Most patients received a peripheral blood stem cell graft (n=24, 89%). Related (48%; 26% human leukocyte antigen (HLA) identical sibling and 22% other related donors) and unrelated donors (48%) were equally common. Two-thirds of patients (n=18, 67%) received a nonmyeloablative/reduced-intensity conditioning (NMA/RIC) regimen. Total body irradiation (TBI) was used in 26% of alloHCT (n=7). The most common GVHD prophylaxis regimens included post-transplantation cyclophosphamide (PTCy, 41%; combined with standard mycophenolate and tacrolimus in 11 patients) or tacrolimus (44%; combined with methotrexate in 14 patients). In vitro T cell depletion (e.g., antithymocyte globulin or alemtuzumab) was rarely used (3 patients, 11%).

A combination of KIT inhibitors and chemotherapy was the most commonly used treatment for SM before alloHCT (44%) (Table 2). Eighteen patients received a KIT inhibitor before alloHCT.

The median MC percentage in the bone marrow biopsy decreased from 15% pre-alloHCT (median 29 days, range 15–93 days prior to alloHCT) to 8% (0–60%) at first bone marrow (BM) biopsy (median 41 days, range 28–125 days) after alloHCT. After approximately 1 year after alloHCT (median 363 days, range 279–570 days), the median MC burden was down to 1.5% (range 0–8%). MC values for both timepoints were available in 13 patients (Figure 1A). The median serum tryptase decreased from 55 ng/mL (range 3–400 ng/mL prior to alloHCT) pre-alloHCT (median 30 days, range 34–127 days) to 40.3 ng/mL (range 2.9–458 ng/mL) at the first BM evaluation (median 46 days, range 6–378 days). Approximately 1 year after alloHCT (median 360 days, range 278–602 days), the median serum tryptase was down to 18 ng/mL (range 0–481 ng/mL). Tryptase values for both time points were available in 13 patients (Figure 1B).

The cumulative incidence of relapse/progression for the entire population of patients with SM (n=27) was 4% (95% CI, 0–14) at 100 days and 15% (95% CI, 4–31) at 1 year. Regarding subtypes of SM, 3 of 5 patients with ASM, 1 of 3 patients with MCL, and 3 of SM-AHN experienced relapse/progression. For the subset of patients with AHN, the cumulative incidence of relapse/progression was 11% (95% CI, 1–28) at 100 days and 21% (95% CI, 6–42) at 1 year. Regarding types of AHN, 3 of 6 patients with AML and 6 of 13 patients with other myeloid malignancies experienced relapse/progression.

Nine patients received a KIT inhibitor (33.3%) after alloHCT. Eight of these 9 patients had had a KIT inhibitor before alloHCT. Among these 9 patients, 4 received for treatment of SM progression, 3 received for maintenance, and 2 received for maintenance and treatment of progression. Of these 9 patients, 6 were survived while 3 died (mortality occurred 1 year after alloHCT in all 3 patients). None of the patients receiving a KIT inhibitor as maintenance post-alloHCT, although small number (n=4), died.

A total of 7 patients experienced relapsed/progression of SM; as mentioned above, 6 of these received a KIT inhibitor for treatment [alone in 3 (1 avapritinib, 1 midostaurin, 1 midostaurin and avapritinib) and 3 as in combinations of KIT inhibitors and chemotherapy (1 midostaurin/cladribine, 1 midostaurin/azacitidine, and 1 with several combinations)].

All patients had neutrophil engraftment by day 30. Grade I-IV acute GVHD was 19% (95% CI, 6–35%) at 100 days; grade III acute GVHD was observed in 1 patient while no patients had grade IV GVHD. Chronic GVHD at 1 year were and 44% (95% CI, 26–64%), respectively.

At 1 year, PFS and OS were 59% (95% CI, 41–77%) and 74% (95% CI, 56–89%) for the entire patient population, respectively (Figure 2A and 2B). At 1 year, PFS and OS for the subset of patients with SM-AHN were 74% (95% CI, 52–91%) and 79% (95% CI, 58–94%), respectively (Figure 3A and 3B). For the entire population, at 2 years PFS was 47% (95% CI, 29–66%) and OS was 69% (51–85%); however, the number of patients at risk at these time points was so small (<15) that the estimates are unstable.

A total of 12 patients died, half of patients (n = 6) died from recurrence or persistence of the primary disease. Of these 6 patients, 5 died of AHN progression, and 1 died of SM progression. NRM at day 100 and 1-year was 15% (95% CI, 4–31%) and 26% (95% CI, 11–44%), respectively. NRM for the subset of patients with SM-AHN at day 100 and 1-year was the same, 5% (95% CI, 0–20%).

Half (n = 6) of the patients who died did so from other causes, with bacterial infection, fungal infection, acute respiratory distress syndrome, acute GVHD, chronic GVHD, and VOD each being the primary cause of death in one patient.

In this study, we have demonstrated that alloHCT is an effective strategy for patients with high-risk AdvSM. Two other retrospective, multicenter studies with a larger number of patients have evaluated alloHCT in SM. 14 , 15 One study reported the outcomes of 71 patients who received alloHCT in Germany during 1999–2021. 15 The other study reported 57 patients who received alloHCT globally (mainly the US and Europe) during 1990–2013. 14 There are similarities among these three studies; the vast majority of patients receiving alloHCT had SM-AHN subtype (66% in the global, 85% in the German, and 70% in this study) (Table 3). Most patients were White males. Almost all patients received peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (with a minority receiving bone marrow or umbilical cord blood transplantations). Almost half of the reported patients had a Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) of >90%.

Some differences, likely due to the era in which alloHCT was performed, were observed among the three studies. Although in each study most of the patients received an HLA full-matched graft, the percentage was lower in the most recent German study (70%) 15 and in our study (70%) compared to the previous, global study (95%). 14 This is due to more haploidentical transplantations (11% in the German study and 22% in the present study). Median age, perhaps expectedly given that it included early years of alloHCT, was younger in the global study (46 years) 14 compared with the German study (59 years) 15 and this study (58 years).

This study also differed significantly from the other studies on GVHD prophylaxis regimens and the intensity of conditioning regimens. PTCy, which was only used in the present study, accounted for almost half of the patients’ for GVHD prophylaxis. Most patients received RIC/NMA in this study, compared with the German study (38%) and the global study (37%). Accordingly, the frequency of TBI use at myeloablative conditioning (MAC) doses was much lower (7%) in this study than in the German (15%) and the global study (28%).

The 1-year OS for all patients in this study was 74%, while 62% was reported in both the German and the global studies. Significant decreases (> 50%) in serum tryptase and bone marrow MCs were observed in most patients in each of the three studies. However, progression in SM also occurred: PFS at 1 year was ranged between 52% and 59% in these three studies. In the 2 larger studies, PFS and OS were durable at 3 years, 51% and 57%, respectively, in the global study, 39% and 50% in the German study.

Acute GVHD seems to be lower in this study compared with the other studies (grade II to IV in 46% of the German study patients and 40% in the global study). This might be explained by the increased use of PTCy, which decreases GVHD. 27 The chronic GVHD rate is similar (44%) to the prior studies (42% in the global study and 31% in the German study).

NRM at one year was 23% in the German study, 20% in the global study, and 26% in the present analysis.

The present study uses descriptive statistics due to the limited number of patients. However, in other studies the subtype of SM, conditioning intensity, karyotype, and AHN disease response status at time of HCT, and use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) post-HCT have been reported to impact outcomes. In the global study, patients with SM-AHN had superior survival compared those with no AHN (in particular, those with MCL). 14 In the German the study, the number of patients without AHN was very limited, which makes it challenging to draw a conclusion. However, MCL-AHN was found to be associated with poor prognosis in that study. 15 MAC improved survival in the global study 14 but not in the German study. 15 Complex karyotype and no-response in AHN before alloHCT were poor prognostic factors in the German study. 15 The German group noted that the absence of Pre-HCT TKI use was associated with poor PFS (HR 2.8 [95% CI 1.3–5.9], P = 0.007). 15

Prior studies 14 , 15 did not find a significant impact of TBI use, donor age, donor type, graft source, or HLA-matching status on outcomes. However, these results should be cautiously examined given the patient numbers are small.

Although all three studies have heterogeneous populations and limited numbers of patients, we can still detect some consistent observations. There was no increase in unusual complications (e.g., graft failure, GVHD, VOD of liver). AlloHCT can provide durable PFS in approximately half of patients with SM. Patients with SM-AHN benefit the most, whereas in patients with MCL, the outcomes are not optimal. Recent changes in alloHCT practice seem to result in superior outcomes—as observed in other diseases—for example, decreased acute GVHD with the use of PTCy. AlloHCT is now available to more patients with lower KPS and no HLA-identical donor; our study suggests a lack of compromised transplant outcomes in these patients. Whether alloHCT should be performed in patients with very good responses to TKIs that can improve OS 21 remains unknown. TKIs have been used before and after alloHCT. 18 , 28 This may have some positive consequences; however, there are not yet enough patients with long-term follow-up to clearly evaluate the effects of TKIs on outcomes. It is also noticeable that alloHCT is regaining more attention in the treatment of AdvSM likely due to the availability of these novel agents. 28 Currently, more patients can be eligible for alloHCT due to better organ functions with better disease responses to treatment.

A major limitation of this study is the small number of patients. However, since almost all patients undergoing an alloHCT in the US are required to be reported to CIBMTR, this data likely reflects the impact of alloHCT in patients with advSM in the USA. Another limitation was the short follow up after alloHCT. Because of the nature of the study (retrospective, registry study), some important data on SM are not available in all patients, including KIT mutations. KIT mutation is not only important for diagnosis but perphaps has some prognostic implications in particular in patients with MCL- the nonmutated state was a poor prognostic factor for alloHCT in the German Study. 15 The kinetic of SM response after alloHCT poses a significant challenge for retrospective studies in assessing response. MCs might be more resistant to conventional chemotherapy regimens than AHN. In a very interesting study, MCs can survive AML-induction type treatment and thus account for positive mesurable residual disease testing in the absence of AML cells. 29 The persistence of MC after conditioning regimen in early phase of alloHCT is not uncommon. 30 Whether these patients with persistent MCs will progress to a full-blown disease or improve with graft-versus-mast cell effect 31 is unknown.

Data have been maturing in patients with AdvSM receiving avapritinib. In a recent report of 107 patients (20% ASM, 66% SM-AHN, and 14% MCL), 83 were evaluated for efficacy. 32 The median ORR was 73%, including CR + CRh of 29%. Responses were observed similarly across the subtypes. Progression was observed in 14% of patients, with the majority of these cases occurring in the AHN component. The median treatment duration was 33.2 months, but neither the median OS nor the median PFS was reached. When compared with best available treatment, avapritinib lead to significantly better survival (adjusted hazard ratio (HR) (95% confidence interval (CI)): 0.48 (0.29, 0.79); p = 0.004). 21

Considering the safety and efficacy (deeper and durable responses) of new KIT inhibitors in most of advSM, but still observing progression in SM-AHN (especially progression can occur in an unpredictable nature), 33 alloHCT should be considered in patients with high-risk SM-AHN who respond to first line therapy. SM-AHN is a higly heterogeneous disease that can encompass numerous combinations: SM can be indolent (e.g., ISM) or aggressive (e.g., ASM) or in leukemic form (i.e.,MCL) as we all as AHN can vary (from CMML to MDS to AML). Obviously, this heterogeneity makes transplantation decisions and response evaluations particularly challenging. 33 The therapeutic benefit of alloHCT is less clear in patients with ASM and MCL. In this rare disease, an international, prospective study of all patients with AdvSM is needed. This study should focus on standardizing data collection, therapies based on the patient’s treatment journey, disease testing, and reporting of results, and should include extended follow-up.
