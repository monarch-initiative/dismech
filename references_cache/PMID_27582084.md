# KCNA4 deficiency leads to a syndrome of abnormal striatum, congenital cataract and intellectual disability.
**Authors:** Kaya N, Alsagob M, D'Adamo MC, Al-Bakheet A, Hasan S, Muccioli M, Almutairi FB, Almass R, Aldosary M, Monies D, Mustafa OM, Alyounes B, Kenana R, Al-Zahrani J, Naim E, Binhumaid FS, Qari A, Almutairi F, Meyer B, Plageman TF, Pessia M, Colak D, Al-Owain M
**Journal:** J Med Genet (2016)
**DOI:** [10.1136/jmedgenet-2015-103637](https://doi.org/10.1136/jmedgenet-2015-103637)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Voltage-gated potassium channels are highly diverse proteins representing the most complex class of voltage-gated ion channels from structural and functional perspectives. Deficiency of these channels usually results in various human disorders.

OBJECTIVES: To describe a novel autosomal recessive syndrome associated with KCNA4 deficiency leading to congenital cataract, abnormal striatum, intellectual disability and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder.

METHODS: We used SNP arrays, linkage analyses, autozygosity mapping, whole-exome sequencing, RT-PCR and two-electrode voltage-clamp recording.

RESULTS: We identified a missense variant (p.Arg89Gln) in KCNA4 in four patients from a consanguineous family manifesting a novel syndrome of congenital cataract, abnormal striatum, intellectual disability and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. The variant was fully segregated with the disease and absent in 747 ethnically matched exomes. Xenopus oocytes were injected with human Kv1.4 wild-type mRNA, R89Q and WT/R89Q channels. The wild type had mean current amplitude that was significantly greater than those recorded from the cells expressing the same amount of mutant mRNA. Co-expression of the wild type and mutant mRNAs resulted in mean current amplitude that was significantly different from that of the wild type. RT-PCR indicated that KCNA4 is present in mouse brain, lens and retina. KCNA4 interacts with several molecules including synaptotagmin I, DLG1 and DLG2. The channel co-localises with cholinergic amacrine and rod bipolar cells in rats and is widely distributed in the central nervous system. Based on previous studies, the channel is highly expressed in outer retina, rod inner segments, hippocampus and concentrated in axonal membranes.

CONCLUSION: KCNA4 (Kv1.4) is implicated in a novel syndrome characterised by striatal thinning, congenital cataract and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. Our study highlights potassium channels' role in ocular and neuronal genetics.

Voltage-gated potassium channels (Kv) are highly diverse membrane-bound proteins among the complex classes of ion channels from structural and functional perspectives.1 These tetrameric channels are structurally organised from mostly identical subunits containing voltage sensors and six transmembrane regions and are arranged around an ion conducting pore.2 The involvement of potassium channels in cell-excitability modulation through voltage-dependent calibration of potassium ion membrane permeability affords them pivotal role in a variety of functions—from neuronal excitability and neurotransmitter release to cardiac and smooth muscle contraction, insulin secretion and other epithelial cell functions.3

KCNA4 encodes one of the Kv members known as the Shaker-type K+ channel ‘Kv1.4’. The physiological and molecular biology of this class of channels have been well documented.1 Kv1.4 is believed to contribute to the fast repolarising phase of action potentials and has been found in striatal neurons. The channel also has high level of expressions in the rat hippocampus, the cerebral cortex, the dorsal cochlear nuclei and substantia nigra and has higher expression in globus pallidus and cerebral peduncle in comparison with the caudate–putamen and the subthalamic nucleus.4
5 KCNA4 is known to be highly expressed in developing rat outer retina during P8, whereas it is found in rod inner segments co-localising with SAP97 during adulthood.6

Here, we studied four patients (from a consanguineous Saudi family) whose consent was obtained according to Institutional Review Board-approved protocols (IRB-AP) (KFSHRC-RAC #2120022) (figure 1A). The healthy parents are first-degree cousins. The phenotype—inherited in an autosomal recessive manner—was characterised by congenital cataract, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), cognitive impairment, microcephaly, growth retardation and dystonia;7 however, it has not been not linked to a gene to date.

The four siblings (three female and one male), born to healthy parents and five other healthy siblings, exhibited nearly identical clinical phenotypes (table 1). Mild delay in gross motor skills in early childhood, growth retardation and microcephaly were noted in all patients. Bilateral cataracts were present. Moreover, the eldest sibling developed secondary glaucoma and corneal opacity in the left eye. Formal neuropsychiatric assessment confirmed moderate ADHD and marked linguistic delay and dysarthria, though the youngest exhibited severe ADHD but milder linguistic disability. Neurological examination revealed brisk deep tendon reflexes (with positive Babinski response), cogwheel rigidity, dystonic posturing and spastic gate. Cognitive function was impaired in all four siblings (range and mean IQ were 77–89 and 83, respectively).

The patients had unremarkable complete blood counts; renal, liver and thyroid function tests; urinalysis, urine reducing substances, mucopolysaccharide screen results, plasma immunoglobulin concentrations, serum lactate levels, acylcarnitine profile, urine organic acid analysis, very-long-chain fatty acid analysis, plasma amino acids analysis, serum biotinidase assay and galactose-1- phosphate uridyltranferase assay results. Chromosomal analysis and array CGH was normal. MtDNA sequencing was negative. DMPK gene analysis showed 17 CTG repeats in both alleles of the gene in the eldest sibling. An unusual pattern was noted in the brains of all the four patients on MRI, which included bilateral thinning of the lentiform nucleus ( predominantly involving the putamina) and swelling of the caudate heads.

Epstein-Barr virus-transformed lymphoblast cell lines (LCLs) were prepared according to standard protocols from the patients. Subsequently, nucleic acids were extracted from blood and LCLs using commercially available kits, PureGene DNA Purification Kit (Gentra Systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) and a Paxgene Blood RNA kit (Qiagen, Valencia, California, USA), respectively. DNA samples underwent a genome-wide SNP scan using Axiom 2.0 assay (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, California, USA). Autozygome analysis was conducted by AutoSNPa (Carr et al, 2006) using the software’s default settings. Pedigree check and linkage analysis (parametric multi-point analysis with a recessive model of inheritance) were done using PedCheck8 and GeneHunter9 using Easy Linkage software.10 The linkage analysis was done assuming full penetrance and allele frequency of 0.0001 in a population. The analysis revealed an interval of genome-wide scan-indicated logarithm of the odds score of 4.0896 (figure 1B). The left (start) and right (end) limiting SNPs in the linkage interval are rs11602300 and rs7118193, respectively. The autozygome analysis revealed four small ROH blocks within the larger linkage interval (figure 1C). The genes in the first block were completely sequenced and negative for any homozygous mutation. The second block expanded on chr11: 29.1–31 Mbp and contained 12 genes including KCNA4. The third and fourth blocks comprised 43 and 32 genes, respectively. While pursuing the positional cloning on the linkage interval, a DNA sample from individual II-4 was sent out for exome sequencing for diagnostic testing (HiSeq Platform from Illumina). The sample was also sequenced at our institution using Ion-torrent platform (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA). Exomic sequences were captured using the SureSelect kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, California, USA). All genetic coordinates for linkage and autozygosity analyses as well as sequence reads for exome sequencing described in this study were according to Genome build 2009 (GRch37/hg19).

Initially, variants were filtered according to the homozygosity status. Then, the ones within the autozygome were considered, following that rare variants <0.0001 in publicly available databases (Exact, 10 000 genome) were selected. Then, rare variants within the in-house Saudi controls were removed, thereby shortlisting the KCNA4 variants (NM_002233; c.G266A; p.Arg89Gln) as the final target (figure 1D). The variant was found twice in ExAC database in a heterozygous state. Using Sanger sequencing, we confirmed the homozygous status of the variant in the patients and the carrier state of the parents (figure 1A, E; see online supplementary figure S1). Moreover, a large cohort (n=428) was negative for the variant. For possible mutation(s) outside the autozygome region, 266 genes related to cataract have been screened for possible homozygous and compound heterozygous mutations. Out of these, 20 rare variants in 14 genes were selected for Sanger sequencing in the family. None of the variants were fully segregated in the family (see online supplementary table S1A–D).

Based on Ensembl curation, KCNA4 genomic sequence extends on chr11 (~30 010–30 017 kb) (figure 2A). In human, the gene has single open reading frame and generates two transcripts, but only one of which is translated into the protein (figure 2A). The channel has various domains such as low complexity segments (LCSs), superfamily domains and transmembrane helices and the variant, p.Arg89Gln, resides on the second LCS domain (figure 2A). To see the expression of the major human transcript, we performed RT-PCR on human tissue panel using KCNA4-specific primers, which revealed high expression of mRNA in the brain but low-to-moderate expression in the testis, lung, kidney, colon and heart (figure 2B).

In order to see eye-related expression of KCNA4 mRNA in the mouse, we dissected brain, lens and retinal tissues from adult female mice aged 3–4 months, (n=2) and extracted RNA using Trizol/chloroform (Ambion/Life Technologies). Then the cDNA was generated using Verso cDNA kit (catalogue AB-1453/a, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and was used for PCR (94°C, 30 s; 72°C, 30 s; 60°C, 30 s) ×35 cycles (DreamTaq, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Analysis of KCNA4 transcript using RT-PCR indicated presence of the mRNA in the tested tissues (figure 2C).

To understand the functional consequences of the variant, p.Arg89Gln was introduced into human Kv1.4 cDNA using the QuikChange Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Stratagene) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The nucleotide sequences of the mutant channel were determined by automated sequencing. The channel’s subunit was subcloned into the Xenopus oocyte expression vector pBF2 which provides 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions from the Xenopus β-globin gene flanking a polylinker-containing multiple restriction sites. Capped mRNAs were synthesised in vitro using the SP6 mMESSAGE mMACHINE kit (Ambion). Oligonucleotides were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Italy).

Xenopus laevis care and handling were in accordance with the standards of institutional guidelines and in compliance with both national and international laws and policies. Frogs underwent no more than two surgeries, separated by at least 3 weeks. Frogs were anaesthetised with an aerated solution of 3-aminobenzoic acid ethyl ester. Stage V–VI oocytes were isolated and stored at 16°C in fresh ND96 medium (mM):NaCl96, KCl 2, MgCl2 1, CaCl2 1.8, HEPES 5, gentamicin 50 μg/mL. Wild type (WT) or mutant Kv1.4 mRNAs (50 nL) were injected into the oocytes (Nanoject, Drummond, Broomall, Pennsylvania, USA) at the concentration of 10 ng/mL. The reagents were supplied by SIGMA (Italy).

Electrophysiological recordings and data analysis were performed as previously described.11–15 Briefly, two-electrode voltage-clamp recordings were performed from Xenopus oocytes at ~22°C, 2 days after mRNA injection. A GeneClamp 500 amplifier (Axon Instruments) interfaced to a PC computer with an ITC-16 interface (Instrutech, New York, USA) was used. Microelectrodes were filled with 3 M KCl and had resistances of 0.1–0.5 MΩ. The recording solution contained (mM): NaCl 96, KCl 2, MgCl2 1, CaCl2 1.8, HEPES 5, pH=7.4. Currents were evoked by voltage commands from a holding potential of −80 mV. The recordings were filtered at 2 kHz and acquired at 5 kHz with Pulse software (HEKA elektronik GmbH, Germany). Data analysis was performed via: IGOR (Wavemetrics), PulseFit (HEKA elektronik GmbH, Germany) and KaleidaGraph (Synergy Software, USA). Leak and capacitive currents were subtracted using a P/4 protocol. To determine the statistical significance unpaired Student’s t-test was used and p values <0.05 were considered significant.

Figure 3A, B shows representative whole-cell currents recorded at +60 mV from oocytes expressing the human Kv1.4 WT, R89Q and WT/R89Q channels. All currents displayed rapid inactivation that is characteristic of the Kv1.4 channel.14
15 The bar graph in figure 3B shows that cells injected with WT cRNA had a mean current amplitude (17±0.7 μA, n=8) that was significantly greater than those recorded from cells expressing the same amount of R89Q cRNA (14±0.9 μA, n=8, p<0.05). Co-expression of WT with R89Q resulted in a mean current amplitude (14±0.9 μA, n=8, p<0.05) that was also significantly different from WT. Thus, the mutation R89Q results in a loss of function in the Kv1.4 channel, leading to the observed decrease in the whole-cell current.

In an attempt to identify the underlying genetic underpinning of a previously described novel syndrome,7 exome sequencing was performed on the DNA of the index patient in the family. Given the comprehensive and proven-to-be-effective filtering of the sequencing data,16
17 the family segregation and the screening of a large ethnically matched control cohort, KCNA4 variant was deemed to be responsible for the disease seen in our patients. Although the homozygous variant was not seen in our in-house 747 exome data as well as publicly available datasets such as Exome Variant Server and 1000 Genome Project in a homozygous state, we took an extra step to screen more control cohort that also reconfirmed our findings. Hence, we concluded that the c.G266A is the most plausible candidate variant for the disease. Furthermore, the electrophysiological assessment of Kv1.4 Arg89Gln channels reveals a reduction in the average whole-cell current amplitude (compared to the control WT current), which indicates that this variant results in a loss-of-function defect.

Among the A-type channels (Kv1.4, Kv3.3, Kv3.4, Kv4.1, Kv4.2 and Kv4.3) only Kv3.3, Kv4.2 and Kv4.3 have been linked to hereditary disorders.18
19 It is noteworthy that these disorders are mostly seen in an autosomal dominant fashion.20–25 One of the first observations pertaining to our study is that the novel disorder observed in our patients is in recessive mode. Though rarely encountered, autosomal recessive mode has been detected among other Kv channels and related molecules such as deficiencies found in the products of KCNV2, KCNE1, KCNJ10, KCNQ1, CNTN2, CNTNAP2 and HCN4 genes.26–38 Hence, homoallelic KCNA4 variant can be a requirement for the channel’s dysfunction. One of the reasons may be related to the fact that the variant is not residing on any of the following critical Pfam (a database of collection of common protein domains and families) domains, tandem inactivation domain (PF07941; position 1–77 amino acids), tetramerisation-type BTB domain (PF02214; position 178–266 amino acids), ion transport domain (PF00520; position 373–559 amino acids) and two-pore ion selectivity domain (PF07885; position 486–560 amino acids). Rather it lies almost in the beginning of the second LCS, which may have a secondary functional role. It has been shown that such segmental sequences occur in high abundance and unusual size and concur with palindromic sequences in proteins.39–41 The functional role of these segments has not been well understood; it has been speculated that using their ability to expand quickly in time, such abundance may potentiate into increased protein sequence space hence evolving into novel protein functions.39
40 Moreover, a possible role in evolutionary role has also been proposed.41 Considering the main functional domains of the protein, on the other hand, such as Pfam tandem inactivation domain, LCS will have lesser functional properties; hence, dysfunction of two alleles would be required for the gene’s deficiency. Based on this hypothesis it is highly likely that a single allele deficiency of KCNA4 can be observed in human if a mutation appears within the boundaries of such critical domains. This view is compatible with the previous findings on potassium channels in which mutations in critical domains frequently lead to deficiencies in dominant state20
42–45 as well as in other genetic deficiencies.17
46–49

The Kv1.4 belongs to A-type current subclass of potassium channels and produces fast repolarising action potentials. The channel has diverse functional actions such as neuronal excitability, neurotransmitter release and muscle contraction. It has six membrane-spanning domains with a shaker-type repeat in the fourth segment. KCNA4 was initially isolated and cloned from human ventricular libraries.50 The channel is widely distributed in the central nervous system and is concentrated in the axonal membranes in close proximity to the terminals.51 It is highly expressed in the eye as well as cerebral cortex, hippocampus, posterolateral and posteromedial ventral thalamic nuclei and substantia nigra.5
6
51–53 The gene seems to be developmentally regulated in mice and rat and becomes apparent as early as P3 in the rat’s retina and p10 in the mouse hippocampus.6
52 Hence based on these studies and functional properties in the eye and nervous system as well as tissue/organ-specific distribution of the protein and the mRNA (see online supplementary table S2), it is highly conceivable that the deficiency of the protein should cause abnormalities in related organs such as the brain and eye. As previously shown, our patients have abnormalities in the basal ganglia and the lens.7 The striatal thinning observed in our patients may be a result of early neuronal death, as previous investigation done on cultured striatal neurons from transgenic mice that lack Kv1.4 and Kv4.2 showed higher vulnerability to ischaemic insults.54 However, the channel’s connection with the development of bilateral cataract remains unclear. Although our RT-PCR experiments clearly shows that KCNA4 is expressed in the lens, it is not clear whether this expression is solely coming from lens fibre cells, epithelial cells or both. Interestingly, it has been suggested that, because lens epithelial cells are in electrical and metabolic syncytium, not all individual cells express potassium channels nor do they need to.55

Since, lens lacks direct blood supply; basic homeostasis needs of cells are met through a microcirculatory system that depends on transmembrane transport of solutes driven by fluid flow.56 Such activity is created by differences in the electromotive potential between inner fibre cells and anterior surface cells. Recent experiments performed on whole lenses as well as fibre cells indicate that the epithelial cells and newly differentiating fibre cells are likely to employ Na/K pumps and K+ channels together to create the negative electromotive potential necessary to maintain this internal circulation.57 We speculate the disturbance of this microcirculation as a potential mechanism. However, further investigations ought to be performed before Kv1.4 would be implicated in such a process.

In contrast to presynaptic co-localisation of Kv1.4 on glutamatergic axons and the terminals in the rat hippocampus and cortex, the channel post-synaptically co-localises with glutamate receptors mainly in dendrites of OFF-bipolar cells that innervates cone receptor terminals in the outer plexiform layer of the gold-fish retina.58–61 However, complete mechanism of excitatory response of Kv1.4 in human has not been well understood. Based on this we cannot speculate on whether the variant may have a role on the channel’s inhibitory or excitatory function. One explanation may be put forward in the light of a study that investigated interaction properties between NH2–termini of Kv1.4 and Kv1.5 channels versus α-actinin-2 using yeast two-hybrid analysis and in vitro binding assays.62 These experiments revealed that α-actinin-2 interacts with the N-termini of both channels. Intriguingly, the deletion analysis based on in vitro binding assays were particularly delineating and indicated that the binding region of the actinin lies within the NH2− termini amino acids that include the variant (c.G266A; p.Arg89Gln) site whereas the channel’s interaction sites lie within α-actinin-2 internal spectrin repeats.62 Based on this, it is highly likely that interaction between the actinin and Kv1.4 is compromised, leading to decreased efficiency of the channel’s activity. It is equally interesting to note that increased level of Kv1.4 (50% upregulation) was detected in hippocampus of A β-treated rats, (Alzheimer’s disease animal model). Hence it was speculated that the upregulation of Kv1.4 in the brain may have a critical role in the cognitive impairment related to the disease.63 Collectively our results implicate the involvement of KCNA4 deficiency in this novel disorder of ocular abnormality, intellectual disability and abnormal striatum.
