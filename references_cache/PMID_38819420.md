# Intellectual Developmental Disorder with Speech Delay and Axonal Peripheral Neuropathy (IDDSAPN): A Mixed Phenotype.
**Authors:** Panda PK, Mandal S, Gupta D, Elwadhi A, Sharawat IK
**Journal:** Ann Indian Acad Neurol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.4103/aian.aian_1017_23](https://doi.org/10.4103/aian.aian_1017_23)

## Content

Dear Editor,

In recent times, several neurodevelopmental, neuromuscular, and neurodegenerative disorders have been reported, which present with a mixed phenotype suggesting both upper and lower motor neuron involvement and even associated epileptic encephalopathy. A number of diseases with intellectual disability (ID) have been associated with peripheral neuropathy and vice versa.[12] Andermann and Arts syndromes are a few other examples of such diseases with coexisting ID and neuromuscular weakness.[2] Apart from areflexia, hypotonia amyotrophy, severe progressive weakness, loss of sensation in limbs, and tremors, children with Andermann syndrome may also have ID, seizures, contractures, scoliosis, and psychiatric symptoms. Similarly, patients with Arts syndrome have sensorineural hearing loss, ID, muscular hypotonia, muscle weakness, visual impairment, ataxia, and immunologic problems and is not only limited to ID and neuromuscular weakness.[3] Intellectual developmental disorder with speech delay and axonal peripheral neuropathy (IDDSAPN) and infantile-onset multisystem neurologic, endocrine, and pancreatic disease are also two such extremely rare, autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental disorders with ID and peripheral neuropathy.

An 11-month-old female infant, firstborn to a third-degree consanguineous couple, had no antenatal or perinatal adverse events and a birth weight of 2.9 kg. She presented with floppiness and delay in the attainment of motor milestones noted from early infancy. The baby attained head control at 6 months and rolled over at 8 months and at the time of presentation, she was able to transiently bear her weight with the help of her upper limbs in sitting position. She had unilateral palmar grasp, but had not attained immature or mature pincer grasp. In contrast to this, the language and social milestones were nearly preserved, and she was able to utter bi-syllables. Recently, the parents also felt her babbling sounded more like a conversation or meaningful word as if she was trying to talk to them. She had no history of seizures, involuntary movements, or any other neurologic complaints. On physical examination, her weight, length, and head circumference for age were between -1 and -2 standard deviation. She had both axial and appendicular hypotonia, hyporeflexia for bilateral knee jerks, and distal areflexia in bilateral lower limbs. There was no facial dysmorphism or neurocutaneous marker and tongue fasciculation. Her motor and mental development quotient (DQ) according to Developmental Assessment Scale for Indian Infants was 54 and 82, with the overall DQ being 68. We initially suspected neuromuscular diseases like spinal muscular atrophy and congenital muscular dystrophy. However, serum creatinine phosphokinase was normal and the SMN gene deletion analysis/SMN gene sequencing was normal. Nerve conduction study showed bilateral lower limb-predominant motor–sensory axonal polyneuropathy, involving the lower limbs more predominantly than the upper limbs. Amplitude of compound motor action potential was reduced in bilateral median, ulnar, tibial, and peroneal nerves, with preserved distal latency and conduction velocity. Sensory nerve action potentials could be recorded normally in bilateral medial and ulnar nerves, but was absent in bilateral sural nerves.

But as the workup for spinal muscular atrophy and congenital muscular dystrophy was negative and there was evidence of axonal polyneuropathy and partially preserved antigravity movements, we suspected mixed phenotype-like disorders with peripheral neuropathy and coexisting developmental delay (e.g., IDDSAPN, TECPR2-related hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy with ID, and MCM3AP-related peripheral neuropathy, autosomal recessive, with or without impaired intellectual development). Hence, we performed screening for neurometabolic diseases and also magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and spine, suspecting a mixed phenotype. We also decided to rule out reversible causes like hypothyroidism and vitamin B12 deficiency, which is quite prevalent in our region, although the clinical phenotype did not exactly match with these disorders. MRI of the brain and spinal cord, thyroid function test, and serum vitamin B12 level were within normal limits. Workup for inborn error of metabolism was negative.

Since the diagnosis remained inconclusive after all these tests, we finally went ahead with whole exome sequencing. Whole exome sequencing detected a novel pathogenic, homozygous loss-of-function 3ʹ splice site variation in intron 5 of the nuclear export mediator factor (NEMF) gene (c. 507-2A > T)(3ʹ Splice site) that affects the invariant AG acceptor splice site of exon 5. Both parents were found to be carriers for the above variant. The child was initiated on physiotherapy and occupational therapy, and parents were counseled about the disease and its subsequent recurrence in future pregnancies. We also screened for associated abnormalities of IDDSAPN in this case, like kyphosis and scoliosis, but X-rays of the spine were normal.

IDDSAPN is an autosomal recessive neurogenetic disorder characterized by global developmental delay, axonal neuropathy, motor impairment, and severe speech delay noticed from infancy or early childhood. Affected patients often learn to walk independently between 3 and 4 years of age, but might have an ataxic or unsteady gait; also, dysarthria is found in a proportion of cases. Most patients demonstrate progressive distal muscle weakness, lower limb atrophy, foot drop, hyporeflexia, and deformities of the foot or hand, secondary to peripheral neuropathy. However, some affected individuals have only mildly impaired intellectual development and may also have autistic features or attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder.[4]

Preaxial polydactyly, kyphosis, scoliosis, thumb abduction anomalies, valgus knee position, squint, impaired smooth pursuit, and saccades were also present in a few cases. In one kindred, neuroimaging showed cerebral or cerebellar atrophy. In one kindred, the neuromuscular weakness was progressive causing loss of ambulation, making the patients bedridden or wheelchair bound, whereas in another kindred, the affected siblings showed episodic deterioration after stress or injury.[4]

Including the current reported case, a total of 26 cases of IDDSAPN from 14 kindreds have been reported to date in three different reports.[456] Their clinical characteristics have been described in Table 1. Most patients had biallelic frameshift or nonsense loss-of-function mutation, while some patients had missense or even splice site mutations like our case. One patient manifested a milder phenotype with a heterozygous missense variant with only mild ID and late-onset peripheral neuropathy, probably due to dominant negative impacts from variants in the NEMF gene. Future research is likely to identify potential modifier genes and whether this disease follows pseudo-dominant inheritance.[45]

The NEMF gene mapped to chromosome 14q22 encodes a 362-amino acid protein that contains a nuclear targeting signal and acts as a “rescue factor” to resolve stalled ribosomes that occur during translation. NEMF is part of the ribosome-associated quality control (Rqc) complex.[5]

Rqc2, the homolog of NEMF protein in yeast, recognizes these aberrant stalled ribosomes. During protein translation, ribosomes can stall for many reasons like absence of termination leading to translation into the mRNA poly (A) tail (as in nonstop mRNA) or the deficiency of charged tRNAs.[7] Ribosome stalling sequesters ribosomal subunits from the translation-competent pool are sequestered by this ribosomal stalling and, in turn, may also produce incomplete (aberrant) nascent polypeptide chains which are potentially toxic.[7] While these nascent polypeptide chains are linked to the tRNA, they obstruct the exit tunnel in the 60s ribosome. NEMF or its homolog Rqc2 in yeast recognizes this nascent polypeptide chain–tRNA/60S aberrant structure and facilitates its ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation by recruitment of the E3 ligase LTN1.[4] In case ubiquitylation fails, Rqc2 has an additional way of clearing these 60S-anchored nascent chains by catalyzing the C-terminal elongation with untemplated alanine and threonine tails (CAT tails). This modification subsequently acts as a direct proteolysis signal in eukaryotes by exposing the lysine residues previously buried in the ribosomal exit tunnel for ubiquitylation. Expression of NEMF gene is relatively different between excitatory and inhibitory neurons of the cerebral cortex, with enriched expression in excitatory neurons and weaker expression in the other.[5] Furthermore, in developing excitatory neurons, its expression is particularly strong, suggesting its important role in early neurodevelopment.

To conclude, this case expands the genotypic spectrum of IDDSAPN, a rare neuromuscular disorder with developmental delay/ID.
