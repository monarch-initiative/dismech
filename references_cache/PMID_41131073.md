connective tissue. The PAX3/PAX7-FOXO1 fusion-positive alveolar subtype has poor 
clinical outcomes, with frequent recurrence, metastasis, and low survival. 
Current therapies may be limited by the tumor microenvironment containing 
cancer, immune, and fibroblast cells. In cancer, cells that transform into 
cancer-associated fibroblasts support growth and other malignant properties of 
the tumor. To study the role of cancer-associated fibroblasts, we isolated cells 
from the lung metastases of a patient with rhabdomyosarcoma that are positive 
for fibroblast biomarkers. Compared to normal lung fibroblasts, these 
cancer-associated fibroblasts secreted distinct factors that specifically 
supported the growth and migration of fusion-positive rhabdomyosarcoma cells. 
The cancer-associated fibroblasts also promoted expression of immune checkpoints 
and conferred resistance to cyclophosphamide, a chemotherapy commonly used to 
treat this disease. We further characterized the secretory phenotype of 
cytokines and growth factors produced by these cancer-associated fibroblasts and 
targeted CXCR4 expression inhibition, which induced cytotoxicity at increased 
sensitivity. This study establishes a model of cancer-associated fibroblasts 
from metastatic fusion-positive rhabdomyosarcoma. Altogether, our results 
describe tumor-promoting mechanisms of growth, migration, and treatment 
resistance supported by the tumor microenvironment, and offer a novel 
therapeutic strategy for the treatment of rhabdomyosarcoma.

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is the most common pediatric soft tissue sarcoma 1. Among various subtypes, 
alveolar RMS is generally more aggressive, with characteristic chromosomal translocations t(2;13) or t(1;13) 
resulting in a fusion gene between transcription factors, paired box proteins (PAX3 or PAX7) to forkhead in 
rhabdomyosarcoma (FKHR; also known as FOXO1)2,3. Fusion-positive RMS (FP-RMS) differs from embryonal 
and other less common types of RMS in incidence by age, location of tumor, and prognosis. Although it affects 
all ages equally, FP-RMS constitutes a larger portion of RMS in older children and adults and primarily occurs 
in the torso, arms, and legs.
Based on the stage and group, high-risk FP-RMS has a poor prognosis, with a 5-year survival of ~ 25% 4. 
Despite current treatment options which include surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy, many patients 
experience refractory and/or relapsed disease. Hence, innovative therapeutic approaches are urgently needed for 
high-risk patients. However, therapies may be hindered by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment 
(TME) in sarcomas 5. Although RMS and cells in its TME express immune checkpoints such as programmed 
death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), checkpoint inhibition with monoclonal antibody therapy has not yet proven effective in 
RMS6. Altogether, these highlight the need to better understand mechanisms of therapy resistance and immune 
evasion in RMS.
The TME is highly dynamic and heterogenous, containing various immune cells and fibroblasts, among other 
components7. Tumor-infiltrating immune cells including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory 
T-cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and neutrophils (TANs) may promote tumor progression

through anti-inflammatory signaling or inhibition of antigen presentation by dendritic cells (DCs), cytotoxic 
activity of T-cells, and functions of other effector cells. There is also significant evidence that tumor immunity is 
extensively regulated by fibroblasts, the predominant cell in connective tissue with a critical role in remodeling 
the extracellular matrix (ECM). Quiescent fibroblasts are activated by transforming growth factor-beta (TGFβ) 
or other stimuli, resulting in characteristic expression of α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA), morphological 
changes, and secretion of factors involved in the wound healing response, inflammation, and tissue fibrosis 8,9. 
Within the TME, precursor cells may be transformed into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) that support 
tumors with an enhanced secretory phenotype driving proliferation, metastasis, resistance to therapy, and other 
aggressive characteristics, such as tumor vascularization through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-
mediated proangiogenic signaling. Although previous studies have found minimal recruitment of CAFs in RMS, 
the role of fibroblasts in tumorigenesis, metastasis, and progression of the disease have not been extensively 
characterized10.
To study interactions between the TME and RMS, we established a unique model derived from the tumor 
of a patient with PAX3-FOXO1 FP-RMS. Using this model, we demonstrated that CAFs enhance the growth, 
migration, and drug resistance of FP-RMS cells, properties that contribute to aggressive disease. Lastly, by 
analysis of the secretory phenotype in these CAFs, we identified CXCR4 inhibition as a novel therapeutic 
strategy targeting the TME of FP-RMS.
Results
CAF cells exhibit elongated spindle morphology and mesenchymal biomarkers
To study the role of CAFs in the TME of RMS, we isolated a fibroblast-like cell from tumor metastases in the lung 
of a patient with PAX3-FOXO1 FP-RMS. These cells exhibited an elongated, spindle-shaped morphology (Fig. 
1A). Notably, the fibroblasts were negative for the tumor antigens, PAX3-FOXO1 fusion and N-myc expression 
(Fig. 1B)2,11. Instead, the cells were positive for mesenchymal biomarkers, including PDGFR-β and α-actin, 
that are expressed in normal fibroblasts and CAFs12. Compared to WI-38 fibroblasts derived from normal lung 
tissue13, the elevated level of these markers in fibroblasts isolated from FP-RMS lung metastases suggests an 
activated state consistent with CAFs.
CAF-conditioned media enhances FP-RMS cell proliferation and migration
Because CAFs have been shown to regulate cancer cell growth, we initially tested the effect of CAF cells on 
RMS proliferation. To do so, RMS cells were cultured in conditioned media prepared from CAF cells, which 
increased the metabolic activity of FP-RMS Rh41 cells derived from lung metastases 2,14–16 in a concentration-
dependent manner up to 35% (Fig. 2A). Similarly, the activity of metastatic FP-RMS Rh30 cells derived from 
bone marrow was also increased up to 26% by CAF-conditioned media. However, the growth of muscle-derived 
fusion-negative RD cells 17 was not affected. To confirm that CAFs promote FP-RMS cell proliferation, Rh41 
cells cultured in CAF-conditioned media were compared to conditioned media from WI-38 fibroblasts derived 
from lung tissue as a control13. Compared to normal fibroblasts, CAF-conditioned media significantly increased 
proliferation of Rh41 cells by 44% on day 8 (Fig. 2B). Next, we investigated the effect of CAF cells on RMS 
motility and migration by the scratch wound healing assay18. CAF-conditioned media significantly increased the 
migration of FP-RMS Rh41 and Rh30 cells by 54% and 29%, respectively (Fig. 3). In congruence with metabolic 
assays, the migration of RD cells was not affected.

CAFs confer immune evasion and resistance to chemotherapy in FP-RMS cells
In addition to cancer cell growth, CAFs are also known to play a role in immune evasion and therapeutic 
resistance19. The expression of an immune checkpoint protein, PD-L1, was significantly increased in RMS cells 
cultured in CAF-conditioned media (Fig. 4). Compared to control, CAF-conditioned media increased the level 
of PD-L1 by greater than 2-fold in Rh41 cells. PD-L1 was also increased by 81% and 41% in Rh30 and RD cells, 
respectively. To determine whether CAFs may confer resistance to therapy in RMS, we treated Rh41 cells with 
vincristine, actinomycin D, and cyclophosphamide, drugs that are commonly used in the treatment of RMS 20. 
Compared to control cells in WI-38-conditioned media, CAF-conditioned media significantly decreased the 
sensitivity of Rh41 cells to cyclophosphamide, with a ~ 7-fold increase in the IC 50 value (Fig. 5). However, 
susceptibility to vincristine and actinomycin D did not change.

growth factors (Fig.  6A and Supplementary Fig. S1). CAF cells secrete numerous proteins, including the most

Discussion
CAFs are a major component of the TME with various tumor-promoting functions that have been demonstrated 
in different cancer models 9. RMS cells are known to express FGF and TGF-β 29,30, which are required for the 
transformation of precursor cells into CAFs31,32. In this study, we established a model from the lung metastases 
of PAX3-FOXO1 FP-RMS with cells exhibiting fibroblast morphology and mesenchymal biomarkers such as 
PDGFR-β and α-smooth muscle actin that are expressed in CAFs12. As expected, these CAF cells lacked markers 
of the tumor, particularly the oncogenic fusion. Using these cells as a model, we studied the role of CAFs in the 
dynamics of RMS.
Compared to conditioned media from normal lung fibroblasts, CAF-conditioned media enhanced cell 
proliferation and migration of FP-RMS cells, indicating that CAF cells secrete key growth factors and cytokines 
contributing to various hallmarks of cancer33,34. PDGF in CAF-conditioned media may drive proliferation, due 
to highly active PDGFR-mediated signaling in FP-RMS35. Our analysis also revealed several chemotactic factors 
in CAF-conditioned media, including MCP-1 and LIF involved in the directional migration of osteosarcoma 
and RMS cells, respectively 21,36. Furthermore, MCP-1 specifically promotes cell migration through the MAPK 
pathway21 and metastasis of breast cancer and osteosarcoma to the lung37,38.
Interestingly, the proliferation and migration of fusion-negative cells was unaffected, suggesting specificity 
for growth and invasion of FP-RMS in our model. This apparent specificity could be provided by the interaction 
between tumor cells and fibroblasts in the metastatic niche of the lung or bone marrow, frequent sites of metastasis 
for FP-RMS39. Accordingly, the effect from lung CAFs was more pronounced in metastatic FP-RMS cells derived 
from the lung. In addition to factors promoting proliferation and migration, the metastatic potential of FP-RMS 
cells may be further supported by VEGF-mediated angiogenesis, as this signaling pathway has been implicated 
in FP-RMS40,41.
CAFs also contribute to an immunosuppressive TME through mechanisms involving PD-L1, an important 
immune checkpoint protein that is regulated by IL-6 through activation of STAT3 signaling22,23. In mice, CAF-
derived ENA-78 (also known as CXCL5), which was increased in CAFs compared to normal murine fibroblasts, 
promoted expression of PD-L1 by tumor cells 42. In lung cancer, ENA-78 helps tumor cells avoid immune 
surveillance through upregulation of PD-L1 and attraction of neutrophils 43. Similarly, CAFs may support PD-

Lastly, we explored targeting CAFs in the TME. Like these CAF cells isolated from FP-RMS, the SDF-1/
CXCR4 axis is upregulated in breast and lung carcinoma CAFs 52,53, highlighting a potential candidate for 
therapy. Consistent with increased SDF-1-mediated signaling, CAFs were more sensitive to CXCR4 inhibition 
than normal lung fibroblasts. Further, CXCR4 is also expressed in FP-RMS tumor cells54,55 and high expression 
is associated with poor outcomes in RMS 56, raising the possibility of CXCR4-targeted therapy with activity 
directly against the tumor and its TME.
Previous studies have reported limited involvement of CAFs in sarcomas 10. Tarnowski et al. found that 
macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) secreted from RMS cells promoted adhesion and vascularization 
while reducing recruitment of CAFs and metastatic potential57. Consistent with CAF-mediated pro-tumorigenic 
signaling, downregulation of MIF conversely increased recruitment of fibroblasts and orthotopic tumor growth. 
Later, D’ Agostino et al. further corroborated little recruitment of CAFs into subcutaneous RMS tumors 58. 
Minimal interaction was also noted in vitro between RMS cells and normal fibroblasts, similar to the effects of 
conditioned media from normal fibroblasts in our model. These observations may also reflect context-specific 
differences in primary RMS compared to metastatic disease.
In summary, we generated a model of CAFs in the TME of PAX3-FOXO1 FP-RMS. Our study describes 
mechanisms of growth and survival supported by the TME and identifies CXCR4 as a target to inhibit these 
tumor-promoting functions. Future studies should aim to develop therapeutic strategies targeting the TME in 
combination with other standard and emerging therapies for the treatment of RMS.

Methods
Patient cells
Approval for experiments involving human tissue samples was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Board 
of Alberta (Ethics ID: HREBA.CC-16-0287_REN8). The study was conducted in accordance with all applicable 
guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The tumor specimen from 
the lung metastases of a patient with PAX3-FOXO1 FP-RMS was collected and incubated in tumor digestion 
medium at 37 °C for 1 h on the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, Auburn, CA, USA). Single cell 
suspensions were prepared by passing the sample through a strainer and washing in phosphate-buffered saline 
(PBS). Single cell cloning was performed by limiting dilution to isolate CAF cells.
Cell culture
RD and WI-38 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, V A, USA). 
Rh30 and Rh41 cells were obtained from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ; 
Braunschweig, Germany). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum 
(FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator.
Conditioned media
Early passage (3–6 multiple population doublings) CAF and WI-38 cells were seeded at 10 6 cells per ml of 
complete media in 6-well plates. After 72 h, the cell culture media was centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min to remove 
contaminating cells and the supernatant was passed through 0.45 μm syringe filters. Finally, the conditioned 
media was frozen in aliquots at −20 °C and freshly thawed right before use.
Immunoblotting
Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing 0.1% protease inhibitors and protein concentration was quantified 
using the DC Protein Assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Proteins in 
Laemmli sample buffer were then resolved by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) in 
4–15% acrylamide, followed by transfer to nitrocellulose membranes using the Trans-Blot Turbo transfer system 
(Bio-Rad). Prior to antibody hybridization, the membranes were cut based on estimated molecular weights and 
blocked in 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (TSB-T) 
at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies 
diluted with 2% BSA in TBS-T against the following: α-Actin (1:1000; sc-32251, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 
Dallas, TX, USA), β-Actin (1:1000; sc-47778), CXCR4 (1:1000; sc-53534), FOXO1 (1:1000; sc-374427), N-Myc 
(1:1000; sc-142), PDGFR-β (1:1000; sc-374573), PD-L1 (1:1000; #13684, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, 
MA, USA), phosphorylated (threonine 202/tyrosine 204; 1:1000; #9101) and total (1:1000; #9102) MAPK, and 
phosphorylated (tyrosine 705; 1:1000; #9131) and total (1:1000; #12640) STAT3. After, the membranes were 
washed with TBS-T and incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit or -mouse secondary antibody (1:10,000; 
#7074 and #7076, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) for 1  h at room temperature. Finally, the 
membranes were developed with chemiluminescent substrate on a ChemiDoc imaging system, followed by 
analysis to estimate the molecular mass of proteins in kilodaltons (kDa) and crop for clarity and conciseness 
using Image Lab 6.1 software (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).
Cell proliferation assay
To assay cell proliferation based on metabolism, cells were seeded in triplicate at 10 4 cells per well in 96-
well plates and cultured with complete RPMI control or 5–10% (v/v) CAF-conditioned media in 200 µl final 
volume. After 96 h, metabolic activity was determined using alamarBlue® (alamar blue) reagent (Thermo Fisher 
Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, alamar blue reagent was added 
to 10% volume of media in each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1–4 h, followed by fluorescence measurements 
(excitation/emission: 560/590 nm). To assay proliferation based on cell number, cells were seeded at 2.5 × 104 
cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured with 10% (v/v) conditioned media from WI-38 or CAF cells. Cell 
numbers were determined every 2 days with a hemacytometer cell counting chamber.
Cell migration assay
Cells were seeded at 10 6 cells per well in 6-well plates. After reaching full confluency, a scratch void of cells 
was scraped using a 200 µl pipette tip, debris was removed by washing with PBS, and fresh media was added 
containing 10% (v/v) conditioned media from WI-38 or CAF cells. Cell migration was monitored by phase-
contrast live imaging over 12 h using an IX71 inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and measuring the 
difference in wound closure area at the 0- and 12-h time points using ImageJ2 software (National Institutes of 
Health, Bethesda, MD, USA)59,60.
Cytotoxicity assay
Cells were seeded in triplicate at 10 4 cells per well in in 96-well plates with 10% (v/v) WI-38- or CAF-
conditioned media and treated with 1 pM–1 µM vincristine (VCR; S1241, Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, 
USA), 1 pM–1 µM actinomycin D (ACD; S8964), 1 nM–1 mM cyclophosphamide (CTX; E4608), 0.25–16 µM 
NM922 (NovoMedix, San Diego, CA, USA) or respective volume of dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) vehicle control 
in 200  µl of media. After treatment for 96  h, cells were washed with PBS and cell viability was determined 
using alamar blue reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as described above. Half-maximal 
inhibitory concentrations (IC50) were determined by non-linear regression using Prism 10 software (GraphPad 
Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Cytokine profiling
WI-38- and CAF-conditioned media was submitted to Eve Technologies (Calgary, AB, Canada) for analysis 
to simultaneously detect the levels of analytes using the Human Cytokine/Chemokine 96-Plex Discovery 
Assay Array (HD96). Each sample was run undiluted in triplicate and protein concentrations were quantified 
according to a standard curve.
Statistical analysis
Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three replicates ( n = 3). The student’s t-test or 
analysis of variance (ANOV A) followed by post hoc test was performed for experiments involving two groups 
or more than two groups, respectively, at a significance of p < 0.05. Analysis was conducted using Prism 10 
(GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).
