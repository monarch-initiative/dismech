# Functional antibody responses and protection against symptomatic dengue.
**Authors:** Chua CLL, Vo HTM, Victorio CBL, Upasani V, Yap BJM, Teo A
**Journal:** Commun Med (Lond) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s43856-026-01922-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43856-026-01922-x)

## Content

Exposure to dengue virus (DENV) triggers the development of anti-viral antibodies that may contribute to either protection or disease progression. Antibodies exist in different isotypes and subtypes, with diverse specificities and functions. IgG antibodies are produced during primary and secondary DENV infections, where they play a role central role in mediating protective immunity, and may also contribute to antibody-dependent enhancement in a secondary heterotypic infection. Although IgG antibodies are widely recognised as key mediators of protective immune responses during DENV infection, antibody specificities, types and functions that reliably correlate with protection remain unclear. A better understanding of anti-DENV antibodies and their roles in protection against symptomatic dengue is essential especially in guiding the design of dengue vaccines. In this review, antibody functions and effector mechanisms in DENV infection are discussed, with a focus on naturally acquired immunity and neutralising antibody responses following vaccination. In addition, current knowledge gaps in antibody functions during DENV infection will be highlighted.

Dengue is a mosquito-borne viral disease caused by four closely related but antigenically distinct dengue virus (DENV) serotypes. Approximately 100-400 million infections occur each year, with recent estimates indicating that the disease is responsible for 32.5 age-adjusted disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) per 100,000 people globally1. Dengue prevalence is expected to increase with warmer temperatures and urbanisation, and unprecedented outbreaks have been observed in the Americas and Asia2,3. Highlighting the continuing global health burden and the importance of effective prevention strategies such as administration of vaccines.

Dengue vaccine design is guided by our understanding of antibody responses to natural DENV infection. During secondary DENV infection, antibody responses may be biased towards the previously encountered serotype, a phenomenon known as original antigenic sin or immune imprinting. This is an important consideration for dengue vaccination, where balanced antibody responses against all four DENV serotypes are desired. Ideally, a dengue vaccine should elicit broad antibody responses that target all four serotypes and potent antibodies that bind epitopes capable of neutralising the virus. Importantly, the vaccine should avoid eliciting antibodies that contribute to antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), a process in which anti-DENV IgG facilitates viral entry into Fcγ receptor (FcγR)-expressing cells, leading to enhanced infection4. In dengue, neutralising antibody titres are commonly quantified using plaque reduction neutralisation tests (PRNT) and remain the primary immunological measure for assessing vaccine-induced antibody responses. However, neutralising antibody titres measured by PRNT do not fully predict whether a person is protected from infection or disease, as individuals with high neutralising antibody titres against mature dengue virions may still experience symptomatic disease5.

Besides neutralisation, anti-DENV antibodies can mediate a range of antibody-mediated effector functions including antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) and antibody-dependent complement deposition (ADCD), Fig. 16–8. However, there remains limited understanding of how these antibody functions contribute to protective immunity and whether they can serve as reliable correlates of protection against dengue. In this article, we first review the antigenic targets of DENV-specific antibodies. We then examine the functional properties of anti-DENV antibodies, emphasising their roles in mediating protective immunity and contributing to disease pathogenesis, followed by a discussion of neutralising antibody responses induced by current dengue vaccines. Finally, we highlight key knowledge gaps in antibody-mediated immunity in dengue and discuss future directions to address these gaps. DENV is a spherical-shaped enveloped virion consisting of a single-stranded positive-sense RNA genome, approximately 11 kb in size, Fig. 2. The four DENV serotypes share approximately 65-70% amino acid sequence identity, contributing to cross-reactive antibody responses that may mediate protective neutralisation or, under certain conditions, ADE. Genetic variation within individual DENV serotypes can also affect antibody responses, with antibodies elicited by natural infection or vaccination showing different levels of neutralisation against different DENV2 genotypes9. The virion is composed of three structural proteins: the nucleocapsid (C) protein, which encases the viral genome; the pre-membrane (prM) protein, which surrounds the nucleocapsid; and the envelope (E) protein, which is arranged in icosahedral symmetry. Immature virions display a prominent surface topology comprising 60 trimeric spikes, each formed by three prM–E heterodimers, with the furin cleavage site on prM exposed for cleavage during viral maturation10. The cleavage of the prM protein to M protein by host furin enzymes drives the formation of mature, infectious virions, whereas E protein plays a crucial role in viral attachment, fusion and entry into the host cell. Structurally, E protein comprises a globular head anchored to the viral membrane through two transmembrane domains, and a stem region that connects the head to the anchor. The globular head consists of three domains: envelope domain I (EDI), envelope domain II (EDII) and envelope domain III (EDIII), connected by flexible hinges11. Intriguingly, mature dengue virions are not rigid particles but continuously undergo dynamic conformational fluctuations, a phenomenon known as “viral breathing”. These fluctuations can transiently expose otherwise cryptic epitopes, including the fusion loop epitope, which is largely buried at the E protein dimer interface in mature virions12. In addition to the structural proteins, the DENV genome encodes seven non-structural (NS) proteins (NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B and NS5) that contribute to viral replication and assembly. Although absent in mature virions, NS proteins play important roles in immune evasion by interfering with antiviral responses. For example, NS1 facilitates the evasion of complement response, while NS2 and NS4 have been implicated as antagonists of type-1 interferon signalling13. Primary exposure to DENV leads to the development of a repertoire of anti-DENV antibodies (IgA, IgG and IgM) that may reduce the risk of severe complications in subsequent infections (Box 1). Some of these antibodies, specifically IgG, display neutralising function. High titres of neutralising antibodies confer protection against symptomatic dengue and reduced viremia in subsequent infections15–17. Neutralising antibodies bind to specific epitopes on virions, effectively blocking viral binding and fusion to host cells, thereby preventing internalisation. In dengue, antibodies with neutralising function primarily target DENV E protein, particularly the lateral ridge of EDIII, which is responsible for host receptor binding. Structural studies using murine monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) such as 9D12, 1A1D-2, and 3H5 have shown that these antibodies neutralise DENV by binding to epitopes located on EDIII, thereby inhibiting viral attachment and entry into host cells18,19. Similarly, other engineered and human mAbs such as Ab513, 5J7 and 2D22 also bind to EDIII and block viral attachment to host cells. Among these, human mAb 5J7 is unique in its ability to dislodge virions that are already attached to host cells and block their subsequent fusion with host membranes20–22.

The protective effects of anti-EDIII antibodies have been further demonstrated in vivo. For example, passive transfer of the monoclonal antibody Ab513 into DENV-infected mice significantly reduced viraemia and alleviated disease manifestations, including thrombocytopenia and vascular leakage20. Similarly, immunisation with EDIII domain peptides in mice and non-human primates induced highly neutralising IgG responses that conferred protection against viraemia following DENV challenge23,24. More recently, bivalent vaccine formulations targeting both the EDIII domain and the NS1 protein have been shown to elicit strong antigen-specific IgG responses with potent neutralising activity and complement-dependent cytotoxicity against DENV-infected cells25,26. Interestingly, passive transfer of immune sera from vaccinated mice conferred protection against lethal DENV challenge in dengue-naïve mice25. Together, these studies demonstrate that EDIII-focused antibody responses, particularly when complemented by NS1-directed immunity, can mediate robust in vivo protection by simultaneously limiting viral replication and disease pathology.

Although anti-EDIII antibodies can neutralise DENV, they are predominantly serotype-specific and generally present at low titres in dengue patients; during secondary DENV infection, these anti-EDIII antibodies can be cross-reactive and less effective across serotypes27,28. Importantly, in natural infection, anti-EDIII antibodies are not the primary neutralising antibodies responsible for protection, given that DENV immune sera retained neutralising activity even after the depletion of anti-EDIII antibodies29,30. This finding suggests the viral envelope may contain other major neutralising targets, including quaternary epitopes, which form only when envelope proteins assemble on the intact virus surface, and conformation-dependent epitopes, which are exposed only when the virus rearranges its structure during different stages of its life cycle28.

There are five main antibody isotypes – IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE and IgD, each distinguished by unique heavy chain domains that define their structure and unique functions. In addition, antibodies can be further classified into different subclasses. For example, IgG, which is the most abundant isotype in the circulation, consists of four subclasses: IgG1, IgG2, IgG3 and IgG4. Each antibody has two functional domains, the antigen-binding fragment (Fab) and the crystallisable fragment (Fc). The Fab domain recognises antigenic targets on pathogens and mediates neutralisation, while the Fc domain interacts with FcR-expressing cells to regulate effector functions including ADCP, ADCC, and ADCD.

FcRs are cell surface receptors expressed on host immune cells including neutrophils, NK cells, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells and mast cells. There are several classes of FcRs, and they are identified based on the type of antibody that they interact with. For example, those that recognise IgG are known as FcγR, whereas those that recognise IgA are known as FcαR.

Previous studies have shown that dengue patients with more than two exposures can develop potent and broadly neutralising antibodies targeting the E-dimer epitope (EDE), a quaternary epitope spanning all three E protein domains (EDI, EDII and EDIII) across adjacent E protein monomers31,32. Unlike anti-EDIII antibodies, anti-EDE antibodies can neutralise all four DENV serotypes as they target highly conserved regions of the E protein that are only accessible on mature DENV virions. The EDE is highly conserved because it lies at a critical interface needed for prM binding and proper virion maturation, which explains its conservation across all DENV serotypes33. Additionally, the presence of these antibodies correlated with reduced risk of symptomatic dengue31,32. In summary, the EDE represents a promising antigenic target for the development of dengue vaccines.

Symptomatic dengue has been observed even in individuals with high levels of neutralising antibodies15,34. This indicates that neutralising antibody titre alone is insufficient to fully explain protection, and that additional qualitative features of antibody responses may influence disease outcome. While non-neutralising antibodies are commonly linked to ADE and a higher risk of severe dengue in subsequent heterotopic infections, repeated DENV exposure can drive affinity maturation and increase antibody avidity, reflecting the overall binding strength of antibodies to multivalent antigens35. Reports from Sri Lanka and Nicaragua paediatric cohorts showed that individuals with primary or secondary exposure exhibited comparable neutralising antibody titres; however, repeated DENV exposures enhance the breadth of neutralising antibodies against heterotopic serotypes for up to 12 months15,34. Consistent with this, analysis of samples from the Nicaragua cohort demonstrated that increased antibody avidity correlated with protection against symptomatic dengue in secondary dengue36. Notably, not all DENV infections lead to increased antibody avidity. Individuals who experienced more severe disease following secondary DENV-2 infection showed a greater decline in avidity over time, which may reflect impaired affinity maturation and germinal centre responses during severe dengue37.

While neutralisation is a key antibody function, antibodies targeting the prM and E proteins that lack neutralising function may contribute to protection through Fc-dependent effector functions. One of these effector functions is ADCC, where IgG antibodies bind viral antigens on DENV-infected cells and trigger killing of infected cells by natural killer cells through engagement of FcγRIIIa (CD16a). To date, direct evidence for ADCC in dengue comes primarily from in vitro experimental studies and an early paediatric study conducted in Thailand. This prospective study reported that high levels of preexisting anti-DENV IgG1 were associated with ADCC, which correlated with lower plasma viraemia during secondary DENV infections38. In addition, experimental studies revealed that FcγRIIIa expression levels correlated with ADCC activity, which enhanced interferon-γ production that abolished ADE39,40.

ADCP involves the opsonisation of virions or infected cells with antibodies, usually IgG, leading to their uptake and clearance by Fcγ receptor-expressing phagocytes such as monocytes. Studies have reported that antibodies that promote ADCP can target both structural and non-structural proteins of DENV. Monoclonal antibodies targeting prM and envelope epitopes promoted ADCP of DENV-infected cells by peripheral blood mononuclear cells41. Importantly, DENV genomic material did not accumulate within monocytes, suggesting that phagocytic uptake did not result in productive infection. Antibodies targeting NS1 were also shown to promote ADCP42. Using recombinant monoclonal IgG and IgA antibodies against NS1, the authors demonstrated that these antibodies can mediate ADCP through engagement of FcγRI (CD64) and FcαRI (CD89). This suggests that ADCP in vivo depends not only on the presence of opsonising antibodies but also on the availability of phagocytes expressing the relevant Fc receptors. In addition, studies have shown that acute dengue is associated with shifts in circulating monocyte subsets, including changes in the relative proportions of classical, intermediate and non-classical monocytes43. As these subsets differ in their Fc receptor expression and phagocytic capacity, such changes in proportions may influence the efficiency of ADCP during infection.

Beyond ADCC and ADCP, antibodies can also engage the classical complement cascade. By binding to DENV antigens, these antibodies facilitate the deposition of complement components (C1q) on virions or infected cells, leading to virolysis or complement-mediated lysis of infected cells. The TAK-003 vaccine was reported to induce complement-fixing antibodies, primarily IgG1, against all four DENV serotypes, highlighting vaccine-induced ADCD mechanism44. In the Nicaragua paediatrics study, patient plasma was shown to mediate complement-dependent lysis of DENV virions and infected cells in vitro6. In the same study, higher ADCD activities against virions and infected cells were observed in children with inapparent infection compared with symptomatic infection, suggesting a protective role in the ADCD pathway. More recent work leveraging the same cohort has extended these findings to cross-reactive immunity. Plasma antibodies capable of mediating complement-dependent virolysis of Zika virus virions strongly correlated with protection from severe dengue45. This finding identifies cross-reactive complement-mediated virolysis as a functional correlate of protection, highlighting a mechanism by which prior flavivirus exposure may confer protection against DENV infection.

Although research has primarily focused on IgG-mediated responses, DENV infection can trigger IgA responses, including IgA plasmablast activation and antibody secretion46. DENV-specific IgA can display neutralising function and block IgG-mediated ADE47. Additionally, IgA1 mAb directed against EDIII promoted phagocytosis of DENV-infected cells by monocytes41. In human macrophage cultures, IgA-DENV complexes did not induce ADE or enhance inflammation, which otherwise may predispose to severe dengue48,49. It has been suggested that during acute infection, monocytes exhibit decreased FcαR expression and increased FcγR expression; the reduction in FcαR expression may further limit the potential for IgA-mediated ADE48. While IgA appears to have a protective role, a recent study also unravelled a potential pathogenic role of IgA in dengue. It was demonstrated that IgA antibodies, specifically anti-NS1 IgA induced after primary Zika virus infection, are associated with increased risk of severe DENV-2 infection50. These IgA antibodies activate neutrophils via Fcα receptors, driving NETosis and degranulation, which precede vascular leakage51.

ADE promotes increased viral replication and inflammation, both of which are associated with increased disease severity in clinical and animal models of dengue52–54 (Box 1). Although ADE is not the sole mechanism underlying severe dengue, as severe disease can also occur during primary infection, it remains an important contributor to disease pathogenesis, particularly during secondary infection55,56. It was previously reported that neutralising antibodies may also enhance DENV infection when present at sub-neutralising concentrations57. In human studies, low levels of neutralising antibodies due to waning immunity or cross-reactive antibodies with limited neutralisation capacity have been associated with an increased risk of severe dengue58,59. An earlier Vietnamese study also showed that maternal-derived neutralising antibodies, which are present at low titres ( < 1:20), were also associated with severe dengue in infants with primary DENV infections60.

Apart from antibody concentration, epitope specificity is also an important determinant of ADE. Although prM and E are highly immunogenic, natural infection preferentially elicits antibodies to immunodominant but poorly protective epitopes, such as prM and the E-protein fusion loop, which bind virus efficiently yet fail to block entry or fusion, resulting in weak neutralisation and a propensity for ADE. Non-neutralising IgG antibodies targeting prM and the fusion loop within EDII can restore infectivity of otherwise non-infectious immature virions, thereby facilitating ADE61. These antibodies bind viral particles to form immune complexes that are subsequently internalised by Fcγ receptor–bearing immune cells, leading to productive viral replication. Consistent with this mechanism, experimental studies have demonstrated that non-neutralising monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies targeting prM and E proteins promote ADE and exacerbate disease severity in mouse models59,62.

Interestingly, the glycosylation profile of antibodies is also implicated in ADE and disease severity. Afucosylated IgG1s, which lack a fucose molecule, recognise the E protein, and their levels correlate with disease severity during secondary DENV infection63,64. Additionally, the glycosylation status of anti-DENV IgG, regardless of vaccination status, has been attributed to contributing to high rates of symptomatic dengue65. These IgG variants exhibit increased affinity for FcγRIIIa (CD16a), and the afucosylated IgG-DENV complexes can promote viral replication in monocytes and splenic macrophages, implicating their role in ADE54,66. Notably, targeted disruption of afucosylated IgG–DENV immune complexes by nanobodies abolished ADE, highlighting the importance of Fc–Fcγ receptor interactions in this process67. While enhanced FcγRIIIa binding by afucosylated IgG may also promote NK cell-mediated ADCC, the contribution of this pathway to protection during DENV infection remains unclear4. It is also unknown whether the opposing effects of afucosylated antibodies are mediated by distinct antibody populations or depend on factors such as antibody neutralising capacity and the FcγRIIIa-expressing cell populations engaged. Supporting a detrimental role, a recent study of DENV-seropositive Dengvaxia recipients found that elevated anti-DENV IgG1 afucosylation before and after vaccination was associated with increased susceptibility to subsequent DENV infection. Moreover, non-neutralising, highly afucosylated post-vaccination IgG enhanced DENV replication in vitro and in vivo through an FcγRIIIa-dependent mechanism65.

NS1 protein is highly conserved across all DENV serotypes. It is expressed on the surface of DENV-infected cells and is secreted during the early stages of infection, serving as an important diagnostic biomarker for point-of-care testing during the acute phase of dengue. Beyond its role as a biomarker, high levels of circulating NS1 have also been linked to severe dengue, as the protein can directly interact with endothelial and immune cells, triggering inflammation and compromising vascular integrity49.

Antibody responses directed against NS1 have been increasingly linked to dengue pathogenesis via molecular mimicry. Anti-NS1 antibodies derived from both clinical samples and animal models were shown to cross-react with host plasminogen protein and enhance its activation; these antibodies were more frequently observed in adults with severe dengue68–70. Enhanced plasminogen activation may increase fibrinolysis, which has been reported alongside thrombocytopenia during acute dengue71. Furthermore, anti-NS1 antibodies isolated from DENV-infected adults have been shown to bind endothelial cells and platelets in vitro, inducing endothelial damage and antibody-mediated platelet phagocytosis72,73.

However, the contribution of these proposed mechanisms to dengue pathogenesis in humans remains unclear. In a Thai paediatric cohort, circulating NS1 and complement activation were associated with disease severity, while experimental studies showed that anti-NS1 antibodies enhanced complement activation, which may contribute to vascular leakage74. At the population level, the clinical associations of anti-NS1 antibodies appear context-dependent: anti-NS1 antibody levels were associated with an increased risk of dengue haemorrhagic fever during secondary infection in a Sri Lankan cohort75, whereas no such association with symptomatic dengue was observed in a paediatric cohort in the Philippines5. More recently, a Thai paediatric cohort showed that anti-NS1 antibody isotypes and IgG subclasses were differentially associated with disease severity, with higher anti-NS1 IgG1, IgG4 and IgA levels observed in patients with dengue haemorrhagic fever, whereas anti-NS1 IgM levels were higher in patients with dengue fever76. It remains unclear whether these discrepant findings reflect underlying biological differences, such as age and prior immune history, or methodological factors, including differences in antibody detection platforms, with one study using ELISA and the other employing a microsphere-based assay.

In contrast to these pathogenic associations, clinical studies also suggest that anti-NS1 antibodies may have beneficial functions. Recent studies in Thai, Vietnamese and Colombian patients during the acute phase of secondary dengue revealed that high levels of anti-NS1 antibodies were inversely correlated with NS1 protein levels, suggesting a potential role for these antibodies in NS1 clearance76–78. In addition, in vitro studies showed that NS1 proteins expressed on infected cells or coupled onto microspheres are recognised by anti-NS1 antibodies that promote ADCP, ADCC and ADCD, which may protect against NS1-associated pathology8,42,48,79. However, disease outcome appears to be influenced by the specific NS1 epitopes targeted by antibodies, as distinct NS1 epitope recognition patterns have been reported in patients with and without severe dengue75,80. Certain protective anti-NS1 antibodies can simultaneously engage multiple functional regions of NS1, thereby blocking key steps in NS1-mediated endothelial dysfunction80. This suggests that anti-NS1 antibody responses are functionally heterogeneous, with epitope specificity likely shaping whether these antibodies mediate protection or contribute to pathology.

While neutralising antibodies remain the standard correlate of protective immunity, their association with protection has shown inconsistency across different studies. One reason for this discrepancy may be attributed to differences in assay formats. The plaque reduction neutralisation test (PRNT) is a cell-based assay and is currently considered the gold standard for quantifying antibody neutralising activities in flaviviruses. However, PRNT assays are laborious and have low throughput, making them time-consuming and resource-intensive to perform. In addition, results may display high variability across laboratories mainly due to inconsistent protocols and materials5. The number of viral passages can also significantly impact PRNT outcomes by introducing genetic and phenotypic changes that alter viral population heterogeneity and epitope presentation, potentially affecting neutralisation titres81. An alternative assay known as the focus reduction neutralisation test (FRNT) can be performed in a 96-well format, hence offers higher throughput compared to PRNT. However, the assay is limited by factors such as the need for immunostaining and microscopy-based analysis, which can introduce variability and increase labour intensity. Both PRNT and FRNT require the use of live DENV. However, the maturation state of the viruses can be influenced by culture conditions, including temperature, culture duration and the cell line used for virus propagation, such as mammalian Vero or mosquito-derived C6/36 cells. These factors can alter the ratio of immature to mature virions and consequently affect the display of epitopes. These conformational transitions significantly modulate epitope accessibility and their recognition by antibodies, thereby influencing antibody binding efficacy and neutralisation potential81. Furthermore, DENV is commonly propagated in mammalian cell lines, such as epithelial or fibroblast cells, which do not represent its primary target cells in vivo, such as monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells; hence, the titres may not fully reflect antibody activity under physiologically relevant conditions5,81.

There are several approaches that may reduce variability and improve reliability in neutralisation assays. One method is to map functional epitopes that are directly involved in virus neutralisation and express them as recombinant antigens to enable high-throughput screening of neutralising antibody responses for profiling and vaccine development80,82. In addition, functional assays should be standardised using well-characterised laboratory strains and validated against clinical DENV isolates to ensure both reproducibility and physiological relevance. Standardisation efforts should consider key biological variables including the viral maturation state, which can influence epitope presentation and antibody accessibility5,15. In recent years, novel high-throughput neutralising assays such as microneutralisation assays or flow cytometry-based assays have been introduced. These may improve scalability but require rigorous validation against established assays83,84. Besides neutralising antibodies, there is evidence suggesting that FcR-mediated immune functions can also serve as correlates of immunity, highlighting the need to develop assays that can precisely characterise protective immune responses in dengue. For instance, neutrophils, NK cells, CD4 and CD8 T cells play crucial roles in DENV infection, underscoring the importance of using robust whole-blood assays to comprehensively assess cellular immune functions85. In vitro functional assays measuring NK cell activation markers, including CD107a expression and IFN-γ production, are commonly used to assess ADCC, while flow cytometry–based phagocytosis assays are applied to quantify ADCP. Complement-engaging antibody functions in dengue can additionally be evaluated using ADCD assays, which quantify Fc-mediated complement deposition, typically C3, following antibody binding to DENV antigens. This involves using fluorescence-based techniques such as flow cytometry or bead-based multiplex platforms. Furthermore, NETosis assays, including measurement of myeloperoxidase–DNA complexes and immunofluorescence-based visualisation of neutrophil extracellular traps, can be employed to assess Fc-mediated neutrophil activation induced by antibodies. These functional immune assays can also help characterise the antibody subclasses involved in protection and assess the longevity of their responses through longitudinal sampling. However, many of these remain in their infancy, with limited cross-study validation and unclear protective thresholds. Moving forward, validation in longitudinal cohort and vaccine studies will be important to determine whether these functional readouts reliably reflect protective immunity in dengue.

Current dengue vaccine development efforts have explored multiple platforms including live-attenuated and chimeric viruses, inactivated viruses, recombinant proteins and mRNA technology. However, the three currently licensed or approved dengue vaccines, including CYT-TDV (Dengvaxia), TAK-003 (Qdenga) and Butantan-DV vaccines, are all live-attenuated tetravalent vaccines. While they are designed to induce immune responses against all four DENV serotypes, variations in their underlying genetic structural compositions result in distinct immunogenic profiles. CYT-TDV is a chimeric vaccine engineered with an attenuated yellow fever 17D virus backbone, substituting its outer structure with prM and E genes from the four DENV serotypes. However, CYD-TDV lacks DENV-derived NS proteins and therefore does not induce the same breadth of DENV NS-specific T-cell responses as natural DENV infection, nor does it induce humoral responses against DENV NS1. In contrast, the DENV structural components of the vaccine induce neutralising antibodies against all four DENV serotypes, although the specificity of these responses varies according to serotype and baseline immune status. In dengue-naïve vaccinees, CYD-TDV induced predominantly type-specific neutralising antibodies against DENV-4 and cross-reactive neutralising antibodies against DENV-1, DENV-2 and DENV-386. In previously exposed individuals, vaccination predominantly boosted cross-reactive neutralising responses. Importantly, CYD-TDV use in dengue-naïve individuals has been associated with an increased risk of severe dengue following subsequent natural infection. This increased risk was not observed in individuals with prior DENV exposures. The exact mechanisms underlying this differential risk remain unclear, although it has been proposed that in seronegative vaccine recipients, subsequent natural DENV infection may immunologically resemble a secondary infection, in which pre-existing antibodies could contribute to ADE86.

TAK-003 is a tetravalent vaccine comprising an attenuated DENV-2 strain and three chimeric vaccine viruses containing the prM and E genes of DENV-1, DENV-3 and DENV-4 on the attenuated DENV-2 backbone. This vaccine elicits both type-specific and cross-reactive neutralising antibodies, with the highest titres against DENV-287,88, as well as induces DENV-specific T-cell responses that persist for up to three years89. TAK-003 also induces DENV-2 NS1-specific IgG antibodies that cross-react with NS1 from the other DENV serotypes and inhibit NS1-induced endothelial hyperpermeability in vitro90. In addition, this vaccine promotes affinity maturation of DENV-specific antibodies against all four serotypes, with high-avidity antibodies persisting for up to one year after vaccination91. Vaccinees also developed other antibodies including IgA, IgM, IgG1 and IgG3, with IgG1 responses strongly correlating with both neutralising antibody titres and complement-fixing antibody responses44.

Butantan-DV (Butantan Dengue Vaccine) comprises attenuated DENV-1, DENV-3 and DENV-4 vaccine viruses and a chimeric DENV-2 component containing the prM and E genes of DENV-2 on an attenuated DENV-4 backbone. Phase 3 trial using this vaccine indicates high vaccine efficacy across age groups (2 to 59 years of age)92. It was recently reported that a single dose of Butantan-DV induces neutralising antibodies against multiple DENV serotypes, with most vaccinees developing trivalent or tetravalent responses93. However, non-neutralising effector functions for Butantan-DV have not been extensively reported, limiting insight into the mechanisms underlying protection.

Despite these advances, challenges remain in the development of dengue vaccines. Dengvaxia has been discontinued because of declining global demand, while the rollout of Butantan-DV in Brazil was recently suspended as a precautionary measure pending further safety investigations94. In addition, vaccine efficacy varies across DENV serotypes and functional antibody responses remain incompletely evaluated. Further studies are therefore needed to define the immune mechanisms associated with protection and to support the development of vaccines that provide durable and balanced protection against all four DENV serotypes, regardless of prior dengue exposure.

Most studies of dengue vaccines have focused on neutralising antibody responses, while non-neutralising antibody functions remain less well characterised. Given the association between afucosylated IgG and dengue severity, it remains unclear whether dengue vaccines induce afucosylated IgG and whether these antibodies enhance FcγRIIIa-mediated effector functions without exacerbating immunopathology. The durability of FcR-mediated antibody responses following vaccination remains less well characterised than neutralising antibody responses, particularly across different dengue vaccines and Fc-mediated functions. Longitudinal studies that assess Fc-mediated functions and Fc glycosylation alongside neutralising antibody responses may help define the contribution of these responses to vaccine-induced protection.

Several aspects of Fc-mediated antibody responses following DENV infection remain unclear. Serotype-specific neutralising antibodies typically emerge following primary infection; however, it remains unclear whether antibodies that mediate FcR-dependent functions show similar serotype specificity or exhibit cross-reactivity across DENV serotypes. Repeated DENV exposure can promote affinity maturation and the development of antibodies with increased neutralising potential, but whether repeated exposure similarly enhances the maturation and potency of FcR-mediated antibody functions is unknown. Longitudinal studies comparing primary and subsequent DENV infections, together with assessment of Fc-mediated functions across DENV serotypes, may help determine how antibody specificity and function change with repeated exposure.

The contribution of different FcR-expressing effector cell populations to antibody-mediated responses also remains poorly understood. For example, monocytes can be classified into classical, intermediate and non-classical subpopulations, and changes in these populations during DENV infection have been reported. However, it remains unclear whether individual monocyte subpopulations contribute differently to antibody-mediated protection and disease pathogenesis. Further studies examining FcR-mediated antibody functions in classical, intermediate and non-classical monocyte subpopulations may help clarify their respective roles in protection and disease pathogenesis.

The long-term changes in FcR-mediated antibody responses following DENV infection also remain incompletely understood. Waning neutralising antibody titres have been associated with an increased risk of severe dengue during secondary infection, but whether declining FcR-engaging antibodies continue to confer protection or contribute to disease pathogenesis is not known.

Some human DENV-neutralising monoclonal antibodies have been shown to exhibit transmission-blocking activity by reducing DENV infection of Aedes aegypti mosquitoes94,95. However, whether antibody responses following natural infection or vaccination influence human-to-mosquito transmission remains unclear. Mosquito membrane-feeding assays using blood from naturally infected or vaccinated individuals may help determine whether these antibody responses influence DENV acquisition and transmission by mosquitoes.

Antibodies directed against DENV proteins have been implicated in both protection and pathology, but the antibody characteristics that determine these different outcomes remain incompletely understood. Although several E protein quaternary and NS1 epitopes targeted by protective antibodies have been identified, their conservation and contribution to protective antibody responses across the four DENV serotypes remain incompletely characterised. The relative contributions of antibody specificity, neutralisation and Fc-mediated functions to protection or disease pathogenesis also remain unclear. Further studies combining epitope mapping combined with neutralisation and Fc-functional assays may help identify antibody specificities associated with protection. Linking these antibody characteristics with clinical outcomes in longitudinal studies may further help distinguish protective from potentially pathogenic antibody responses.

DENV exposure results in the development of a diverse repertoire of antibodies, some of which confer protective immunity, while others may contribute to disease pathogenesis. Collaborative research efforts have greatly advanced our understanding of antibody responses and target epitopes in dengue, paving the way for the development of dengue vaccines. Nevertheless, concerns about ADE remain, particularly in dengue-naïve individuals, as vaccination in the absence of prior infection may mimic a primary exposure and increase the risk of severe disease upon natural infection due to suboptimal antibody responses. Although neutralising antibodies are commonly studied as correlates of protection, antibodies capable of neutralising DENV may promote ADE at sub-neutralising concentrations. Other antibody effector functions have also been identified as potential contributors to protective immunity, but the factors determining whether antibody responses are protective or pathogenic remain unclear. As dengue remains a growing global health threat, deeper insights into antibody functions and their specific targets—whether protective or pathogenic—will be critical for guiding the development of safer and more effective next-generation vaccines. To achieve this, several key knowledge gaps must be addressed to better define antibody immunity in dengue.
