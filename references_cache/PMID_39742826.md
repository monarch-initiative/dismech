Free sialic acid storage disorder (FSASD) is a rare autosomal recessive 
lysosomal storage disease caused by pathogenic SLC17A5 variants with variable 
disease severity. We performed a multidisciplinary evaluation of an adolescent 
female with suspected lysosomal storage disease and conducted comprehensive 
studies to uncover the molecular etiology. The proband exhibited intellectual 
disability, a storage disease gestalt, and mildly elevated urine free sialic 
acid levels. Skin electron micrographs showed prominent cytoplasmic vacuolation. 
Clinical exome and genome sequencing identified a maternally-inherited SLC17A5 
variant: c.533delC;p.Thr178Asnfs*34. RNASeq of proband skin fibroblasts revealed 
exon 3 skipping, which was not detected in RNA from proband blood or parental 
fibroblasts. Targeted deep sequencing of proband fibroblast DNA revealed a 
184 bp deletion in ∼15 % of reads, encompassing the 3' end of exon 3. Illumina 
Complete Long Read sequencing confirmed the deletion was in the 
paternally-inherited allele and found in a mosaic state in proband fibroblasts 
and muscle but not in blood or buccal cells. Functional studies, including 
SLC17A5 knockout cells and transient transfections of mutated SLC17A5 
demonstrated pathogenicity of the identified variants. We report an adolescent 
female with atypical FSASD with tissue-specific mosaicism for an intragenic 
deletion in SLC17A5, explaining the atypical clinical course, mild biochemical 
abnormalities, and long diagnostic process.

Biallelic pathogenic variants in SLC17A5, the gene encoding sialin, cause an ultra-rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disease known as free sialic acid storage disorder (FSASD)1. Sialic acid (N-acetylneuraminic acid) is an essential component of the ubiquitously distributed glycan-linked oligosaccharides that play a crucial role in a variety of biological processes2. Therefore, it is not unexpected that the FSASD phenotype is characterized by multisystemic involvement. There is a phenotypic spectrum of clinical presentation and severity, ranging from mild, slowly progressive disease to severe FSASD. The slowly progressive form, commonly referred to as Salla disease, presents with mild developmental delay, epilepsy, and hypotonia, while severe FSASD is associated with severe developmental delay, hepatosplenomegaly, dysmorphic features, poor weight gain, progressive neurodegenerative course, and early death or fetal demise due to severe hydrops3-5.

The SLC17A5 gene is located on chromosome 6q13 and contains 11 coding exons that encode a 495 amino acid lysosomal transmembrane transport protein known as sialin. Sialin is a 12-transmembrane domain lysosomal protein that exports, through a proton-coupled process, sialic acid and other acidic sugars from lysosomes to the cytosol6. Alterations in sialin function caused by SLC17A5 pathogenic variants lead to intra-lysosomal accumulation of free sialic acid and its subsequent urine excretion where levels can be 10 to 100 times the normal range7,8. The spectrum of phenotypes and severity exhibited by individuals with FSASD correlates with their genotypes and free sialic acid levels9. Homozygosity for the p.Arg39Cys missense variant causes Salla disease4,9, the most attenuated phenotype, whereas severe loss-of-function and other more damaging variants cause a severe FSASD course. Intermediate phenotypes are found, for example, in individuals with compound heterozygous genotypes in which one of the pathogenic variants is p.Arg39Cys and the other is a more damaging variant9.

Despite the introduction of cutting-edge molecular techniques that have revolutionized the field of medical genetics including exome sequencing (ES) and genome sequencing (GS)10, many patients with suspected Mendelian disorders do not receive a molecular diagnosis even after extensive molecular testing11. Emerging technologies such as RNAseq and long read DNA sequencing are promising and may contribute to solving some of these undiagnosed disorders12,13.

Here, we present the long diagnostic journey of an adolescent female who had been suspected of having a lysosomal storage disease since early infancy but had long escaped molecular confirmation. The underlying molecular etiology was eventually identified as a novel, tissue-specific, mosaic copy number variant after performing comprehensive molecular and functional studies through the Undiagnosed Disease Network (UDN).

The proband’s initial evaluation and diagnostic workup were carried out in a clinical setting. Due to the lack of definitive diagnosis, the proband was enrolled in the Undiagnosed Diseases Network (UDN) through the Washington University Clinical Site using the general UDN protocol entitled “Clinical and Genetic Evaluation of Individuals With Undiagnosed Disorders Through the Undiagnosed Diseases Network,” (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT02450851). The parents provided informed consent for research participation in the UDN. This study was approved by the Washington University School of Medicine Human Research Protection Office.

Trio clinical exome sequencing (ES) was performed from whole blood derived DNA at GeneDx (Gaithersburg, MD), using the Agilent SureSelect XT2 All Exon V4 kit, and an Illumina HiSeq 2000 sequencer with 100 base pairs (bp) paired-end reads. 98.8% of the target region was covered at 10x, with a mean depth of 264x. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome build GRCh37 (hg19), and variants were analyzed with XomeAnalyzer software.

Trio clinical GS was performed from whole blood derived DNA at Illumina (San Diego, CA), using sequencing-by-synthesis (SBS) chemistry and paired-end reads. The genome was sequenced to an average of ≥ 30x coverage, and over 99% of the genome was covered at 10x coverage. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome build GRCh38 (hg38), and variants were analyzed with Illumina’s in-house software.

Sialic acid assay in urine samples was performed using High Pressure Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) with flourecnce detection (Greenwood Biochemical Laboratory, Greenwood, SC) (Supplementary Material).

Research reanalysis was performed on the raw exome and genome data obtained from GeneDx and Illumina, respectively. We followed Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK)14 best practices for alignment (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org) of the ES and GS data to the human reference genome build GRCh38. We used GATK to jointly call single nucleotide (SNV) and indel variants and annotated them with ANNOVAR.15 We used a multi-caller then merge approach to call structural variants (SVs), using two read depth and two split read callers (Manta,16 Smoove,17 CNVnator18 and CNVkit19). SURVIVOR20 was used to merge the calls into a single file, and AnnotSV21 was used to annotate SVs. We prioritized SVs identified by multiple callers and/or that impact coding or splice regions. We called known Short Tandem Repeats (STRs) using Expansion Hunter.22

RNAseq was performed using proband total RNA extracted from skin fibroblasts using RNeasy extraction kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). Total RNA integrity was determined using Agilent 4200 Tapestation. Library preparation was performed with 1 μg of total RNA, and ribosomal RNA was removed with KAPA RiboErase kits (Roche, Indianapolis, IN). mRNA was then fragmented, and cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript III (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and random hexamers and sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq-6000 using 150 bp paired end reads, targeting 60M total reads. RNAseq data were analyzed using HISAT223 for alignment to GRCh38 and StringTie24 for gene expression quantification. We used DROP25 to detect aberrant expression, aberrant splicing, and monoallelic expression of heterozygous genomic variants. We manually validated all calls in Integrated Genomics Viewer26 (IGV, https://software.broadinstitute.org/software/igv/).

RNA was extracted from fibroblasts from the proband and her parents and from proband blood using PaxGene RNA tubes and RNeasy extraction kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). The extracted RNA was used to synthesize cDNA with SuperScript III (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). To confirm SLC17A5 RNA splicing, we designed PCR primers (Supplementary Material) that would specifically amplify cDNA product from exons 2 through 7 of the SLC17A5 transcript (NM_012434.5). We gel purified PCR bands and performed Sanger sequencing of the individual PCR products.

Genomic DNA from proband fibroblasts was extracted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) and used to construct libraries utilizing the KAPA Hyper Library Kit (Roche, Indianapolis, IN) on the SciClone NGS instrument (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA) targeting 350 bp inserts. One μg of amplified library was added into an overnight hybridization with a xGen Custom Hyb Panel (n=84 probes, Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA) targeting approximately 6 kb of SLC17A5, from exon 2 to exon 4. The captured product was sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform, yielding approximately 68Gb of sequence data.

Genomic DNA from proband fibroblasts, muscle, buccal cells, and blood was extracted (DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD)), and 50 ng input DNA was used to create Illumina Complete Long Read (ICLR) sequencing libraries according to manufacturer protocols. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument using S4 flow cells. Data were aligned to GRCh38 using the Illumina DRAGEN ICLR WGS pipeline and were manually reviewed with IGV.27

To study the effects of exon 3 skipping, we generated wild-type SLC17A5-FLAG (isoform 1 of SLC17A5, protein length 495 amino acids, predicted molecular weight 54.6 kDa) and exon 3-skipped SLC17A5del-HA constructs. HEK-293T cells were transiently transfected with these plasmids. Before harvesting, cells were rinsed in cold PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 1% NP40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA) with protease (genDEPOT, Baker, TX) and phosphatase (genDEPOT, Baker, TX) inhibitors. Cells were left in lysis buffer on a nutator for 30 minutes at 4°C. After lysis, the solution was centrifuged at 15,700g for 20 minutes, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were measured with the bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Pierce Biotechnolog, Rockford, IL), using bovine serum albumin as standard. Lysates were separated on a sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to polyvinyl difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Blots were incubated in blocking buffer (5% dried skimmed milk in Tris-buffered saline, pH 7.4, and 0.2% Tween-20, TBST) followed by overnight incubation with the following primary antibodies: FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO; Cat# F1804, 1:2000), HA (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA; Cat# 3724S; 1:1000), Ubiquitin (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA; Cat# 3933S; 1:1000), GAPDH (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL; Cat# 60004-1-Ig; 1:20000). The following day, membranes were washed 3 times for 10 minutes each in TBST before incubation for 1 hour with secondary horseradish peroxidase (HRP) antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. Detection was carried out with ECL Western blotting detection reagent (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL). Images were detected with ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

For cellular phenotyping studies, A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells were edited and provided by the Genome Engineering and Stem Cell Center (GESC @ McDonnell Genome Institute) of Washington University. A synthetic gRNA with the sequence “GGGATGCAGAAACTCAAGGA” which falls near the third exon to generate a frameshifted indel to knock-out (KO) the SLC17A5 gene was used. Multiple clones were selected, and two (termed 1E4 and 1E6) demonstrated successful editing (SLC17A5 KO). These clones were grown under standard tissue culture conditions, plated in 96-well plates, and stained with Hoechst, Lysotracker, and Cell Mask (for plasma membrane). The cells were imaged with a Molecular Devices InCell 6500 HS confocal microscope in widefield mode with a 20x ULWD objective. Molecular Devices InCarta segmented the cells and extracted the features. We then ran a shallow artificial neural network using TensorFlow with these feature-sets to produce the phenotypic scores listed. Plots were made with Tibco Spotfire.

The proband is now a 16-year-old adolescent female who originally presented at age 16 months for evaluation of developmental delay and dysmorphic features (Fig. 1A-I). At the age of 16 month, she was noted to have coarse facial features, broad nasal bridge, short anteverted nose, and periorbital edema, but no organomegaly found. These findings were consistent with a lysosomal storage disease gestalt, which prompted urine glycosaminoglycans, urine oligosaccharides, and serum lysosomal enzymes; chromosomal microarray analysis; and a skeletal survey, all of which were unremarkable except for mild elevation in oligosaccharides.The proband was reevaluated at age 27 months and was progressing in her development. She was assessed by orthopedics for bilateral tight heel cords, which were managed with serial castings. By age 3 years, she continued exhibiting similar facial features (Fig 1.A), required three sets of tympanostomy tubes for recurrent otitis media, and adenoidectomy and tonsillectomy for obstructive sleep apnea. She had very brittle teeth with significant decay and chipping since late infancy (Fig. 1E). In addition, she exhibited mild thoracolumbar scoliosis and gingival hypertrophy (Fig. 1A)). Her physical exam at age 3 years was remarkable for a diastolic murmur, for which she was evaluated by cardiology. Her echocardiography showed a thickened and deformed aortic valve causing moderate aortic valve insufficiency and mild aortic valve stenosis with moderately dilated left ventricle. She was placed on furosemide and enalapril. Her repeat skeletal survey at age 38 months showed oarshaped ribs (Fig. 2A). She underwent bilateral tendo-Achilles lengthening when she was age 3.5 years and continued using ankle foot orthotics. She later started exhibiting movement restrictions in large joints, including knees and elbows, which further exacerbated her walking difficulties. Since age 5 years, she started exhibiting progressive interphalangeal joint stiffness associated with difficulties in her fine motor skills.

From the cardiac standpoint, she was stable on enalapril but developed dilation of the ascending aorta at age 5 years and her echocardiography at age 11 years demonstrated a dysplastic mitral valve with thickened leaflets with mild to moderate mitral regurgitation. In addition, she was noted to have hypertension at age 12 years which is well controlled with lisinopril.

The proband began wearing an orthotic brace for progressive scoliosis at age 6 years (Fig. 2B). She was also noted to have periarticular subcutaneous nodules surrounding her knees and elbows (Fig. 1F); three of these were excised and the pathology showed granuloma annulare. Because of her progressive scoliosis with conservative treatment (Fig. 2C), she underwent a surgical repair (spinal fusion) at age 9 years.

She exhibited recurrent otitis media and required 6 sets of myringotomy tubes between 6 months and 8 years of age. Multiple hearing studies demonstrated mild mixed conductive and sensorineural hearing loss.

In addition to the previously seen rib abnormalities, repeat skeletal survey at age 13.5 years demonstrated progressive and diffuse thickening of the calvaria (Fig. 2D). In addition, despite using orthotic splints, her finger contractures continued to worsen (Fig. 1I). At age 13 years, she underwent left-sided congenital pes cavus reconstructive surgery. She had atypical precocious puberty, having her menarche at age 8 years, thelarche occurred around 11 years of age, and adrenarche shortly thereafter. She was placed on an oral contraceptive pill when she was 11 years old for irregular menses. Her hormonal evaluation and a brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at age 14 years were unremarkable and showed no corpus callosum findings, cerebellar abnormalities, or white matter changes.

She displayed neurodevelopmental delay, including walking at age 18-20 months. Her fine motor skills were initially on target, but later difficulties were observed because of interphalangeal joint restriction and stiffness. She was 1-2 years behind in her speech milestones since early childhood and had mild-moderate intellectual disability. She has attended special classes since early childhood and has an individualized education program in school. Her reading and math skills at age 15 years were at the second grade level. She is currently receiving life skills at school, depends on her family for most daily life activities, struggles with attention and focus. She is also seen as being very social. Her parents are of Northern European ancestry; both are healthy. The proband has two older healthy siblings. The family history is non-contributory.

On physical exam at age 15 years, her weight was at 76 %ile (Z= 0.70), height at <1st percentile (Z= −2.33), body mass index (BMI) at 94th percentile (Z= 1.58), and occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) at 78th percentile (Z=0.76). She had coarse facial features (Fig. 1D), periorbital edema without corneal clouding, thick and flaring ala nasi, depressed nasal bridge, thick tongue (Fig. 1G), high-arched palate, and gingival hypertrophy (Fig. 1H). Her ophthalmological examination was significant for faint corneal whorling with reticulate pattern, very tortuous blood vessels, thin macula, and cupped optic nerves. Her teeth were discolored and chipped, and she had dental braces. Her cardiac exam was significant for 2-3/6 systolic murmur on the upper precordium. There was no organomegaly. She had residual scoliosis and left-sided pes cavus deformity (both repaired previously). In addition, interphalangeal joint contractures in both hands (claw hands) were observed.

Because of the possible diagnosis of a lysosomal storage disease, the proband underwent a skin biopsy at age 3.5 years. Light microscopy of toluidine blue stained sections of skin showed finely vacuolated mononuclear cells in the dermis consistent with vacuolated histiocytes/macrophages, or, less likely, fibroblasts. Electron micrographs (Fig. 3A, B) showed prominent cytoplasmic vacuolation in diverse cell types, including endothelial cells, sweat gland epithelium, pericytes, smooth muscle, Schwann cells, and mononuclear cells consistent with histiocytes/macrophages. Some of the vacuoles were radiolucent. Other vacuoles contained light flocculent material and dark granular material. In one vacuole, there was a structure suggestive of a dense ring; however, definite dense rings and laminations were not seen.

At age 14 years, the proband underwent a muscle biopsy, which revealed multiple round intracytoplasmic vacuoles (Fig. 3C-H) that were mostly empty and focally contained some loose membranous, granular, or fibrillary structures (Fig. 3D). These vacuoles were predominantly seen within the histiocytes/macrophages (Fig. 3C) and, to a slightly lesser degree, endothelial cells (Fig 3E). Rarely, scattered individual vacuoles or aggregates involved myofibers (Fig. 3F). Focally, there was slight increase in the numbers of mitochondria (Fig. 3G) which also included the presence of some enlarged forms, albeit without any apparent abnormalities in their internal architecture. Abnormal aggregates of mitochondria in muscle fibers were frequently accompanied by lipid droplets (Fig. 3G). In addition, focal presence of lipopigment (Fig. 3H) and scattered intracytoplasmic glycogen deposition were identified.

The mean levels of 3 urine measurements from the proband of total sialic acid was 1304±346 nmole/mg creatinine (normal:165-690) and free sialic acid was 1211±308 nmole/mg creatinine (normal: 123-508). The first sialic acid assay in urine was performed after receiving the clinical ES results that showed a meternally inherited pathogenic variant in SLC17A5. The levels of total and free sialic acid from the proband’s mother were normal.

Chromosomal Microarray Analysis (CMA; version 7.2 Baylor) was non-diagnostic. Repeat CMA using Affymetrix Whole Genome-Human SNP Array 6.0 showed a 9.5 Mb region of homozygosity on chromosome 8p (chr8:29,292,720-38,781,040; hg19), which was of unclear significance. Trio clinical ES (GeneDx, 2013) and ES reanalysis (2016) revealed a maternally-inherited single nucleotide deletion pathogenic variant designated as NM_012434.5: c.533delC in exon 4 of SLC17A5 (Supplemnatry Material). This variant has been previously reported in association with FSASD1,28-30 and leads to a frameshift (p.Thr178Asnfs*34). A second variant in SLC17A5 was not identified. Targeted full SLC17A5 gene sequencing and deletion/duplication studies were non-diagnostic. GS and targeted deep sequencing of SLC17A5 and other candidate genes did not identify additional variants in SLC17A5 or other alternative molecular diagnoses. All of these sequencing studies were performed on DNA derived from proband peripheral whole blood.

Due to a lack of molecular explanation for the proband’s phenotype, she was enrolled into the UDN when she was age 13 years. As part of her diagnostic evaluation through the UDN, a skin biopsy was performed. RNAseq was performed on RNA obtained from proband fibroblasts and identified skipping of exon 3 of SLC17A5 in ~50% of the reads (Fig. 4A, B). RNAseq data also revealed the absence of the maternally-inherited frameshift variant, indicating that this allele undergoes nonsense mediated decay. Both wild-type splicing and exon 3 skipping were identified from the paternally-inherited allele, suggesting mosaicism or leaky splicing. PCR and Sanger sequencing of cDNA derived from fibroblasts from the proband and her parents as well as RNA from proband whole blood confirmed exon 3 skipping in the proband’s fibroblasts, but exon skipping was not observed in RNA from fibroblasts from either parent or from proband blood, demonstrating tissue mosaicism (Fig. 4C-E). These studies also confirmed nonsense mediated decay of the transcript from the allele containing the frameshift variant in the maternal RNA sample.

Targeted capture and deep massively parallel sequencing (mean depth of 300,000X) of an approximately 6 kb region of SLC17A5 (from 5’ of exon 2 through 3’ of exon 4) in DNA from the proband fibroblasts revealed a 184 bp deletion in ~15% of reads, encompassing the 3’ end of exon 3, including the donor splice site (Fig. 4F). The coordinates of the deletion are: Chr6:73,641,566-73,641,749 (hg38). This variant is classified as likely pathogenic based on the ACMG criteria and has been submitted to ClinVar (Accession#: RCV003992111.1) (Supplementary Material). We did not identify this 184 bp deletion upon re-review of the ES and GS data from blood derived proband DNA. To confirm these findings, we designed primers to amplify a 686 bp fragment encompassing the mosaic deletion and performed PCR on DNA derived from both proband’s blood and fibroblast. We identified the wild-type 686 bp product in DNA derived from proband blood but detected both wild-type and a smaller product in DNA from proband fibroblasts (Fig. 4G). Gel extraction and Sanger sequencing of individual bands confirmed that the smaller DNA product harbored the 184 bp deletion and confirmed the breakpoints that had been identified in the deep sequencing data.

To both further investigate tissue mosaicism of the 184 bp deletion and to confirm that this mosaic intragenic deletion was present in the paternally-inherited allele, we performed Illumina Complete Long Read (ICLR) sequencing on DNA derived from proband fibroblasts, whole blood, buccal cells, and muscle biopsy tissue. While the DNA obtained from both buccal cells and muscle tissue was extremely limited, the 50ng input requirement for ICLR permitted these studies. We obtained data with an average N50 of 6905 bp and average phase block N50 of 209,818 bp, confirming that the mosaic deletion arose in the paternally-inherited allele and the frameshift variant was present in the maternally-inherited allele. We did not identify the 184 bp deletion in DNA samples from proband blood or buccal cells, but we identified the 184 bp deletion in 25% of paternally-inherited alleles from proband fibroblasts and in 42% of paternally-inherited alleles from proband muscle tissue (Fig. 4H).

To study the effects of SLC17A5 exon 3 skipping on sialin protein, we transiently transfected and expressed the wild-type and SLC17A5 exon 3-skipped variant constructs in HEK293T cells. Due to the lack of a reliable human sialin antibody, anti-FLAG and anti-HA antibodies were used, resulting in a high molecular weight smear observed for both wild-type SLC17A5 and SLC17A5 exon 3-skipped constructs under typical Western blot conditions (Suppl. Fig.1A-lanes 2 and 3). We also observed multiple smaller molecular weight bands which could represent cleaved products, as it is common to see a high molecular weight smear with transmembrane proteins.31 Because heat-related protein aggregation can cause loss of resolution in Western blotting, we repeated the experiment at room temperature and found discrete bands at expected sizes for each protein (Suppl. Fig.1B - lanes 2 and 3). Co-expression of the wild-type and SLC17A5 exon 3-skipped constructs decreased the expression of both proteins (these changes were seen in lower molecular weight bands as well as in the smear, Suppl. Fig.1A & B, lane 4). Co-expression of wild-type sialin with an unrelated protein (RBX1 with the same tag as the SLC17A5 exon 3-skipped construct) did not change the pattern of wildtype sialin protein (Suppl. Fig.1A & B, lane 5).

To determine whether the reduction in wild-type SLC17A5 protein levels observed upon co-expression with the exon 3-skipped construct was due to proteasomal degradation, we treated cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 (Suppl. Fig.2). Proteosome inhibition did not affect protein levels of the wild-type protein and did not restore levels of wild-type sialin when co-expressed with the exon 3-skipped (SLC17A5del) construct. Taken together, these results indicate that sialin is an aggregate-prone protein and suggest that exon 3-skipped sialin has a dominant negative effect.

To understand the cellular impact of SLC17A5 knockout (KO), we generated two cell lines from human A549 cells. A parental line was used as wild-type (WT), and two independent KOs (clones 1E4 and 1E6) were generated to ensure that the observations were not due to a clonal anomaly. After imaging and deep cellular profiling of the A549 cells, we noticed that the distribution of lysosomal and membrane features in the KO cells were different from the WT (Fig. 5A, B). We developed a multi-parametric ‘phenotypic score’ to measure the differences between the WT and the KO cells, which included morphology and density of the plasma membrane and lysosomes. The list of feature sets, the cell data form Figure 5, and the code that was used to generate the phenotypic score are provided as Supplementary Excel sheet. Both clones had nearly a 3-fold difference in phenotypic score as compared to the WT, with 1E4 being slightly stronger than 1E6 (Fig. 5C). Although lysosomes drove most of the differences between the WT and KO cells, it was clear that the 1E4 clone had an even stronger lysosomal phenotype from the WT, but the two KO clones were comparable when including cellular features that measured membrane and lipid localization in the cell, indicating a more complex and nuanced cellular phenotype induced by knocking out SLC17A5 (Fig. 5D). Overall, in the A549 cell line, SLC17A5 expression impacted the cellular morphology, specifically the lysosome and membrane lipid localizations. These cells can be used in future studies to develop treatments and assay for their ability to ‘rescue’ the cellular phenotype in high-throughput ways.

The clinical course and phenotypic features of the proband, including her lysosomal storage disease gestalt, elevated levels of total and free sialic acid in her urine, and the presence of intracytoplasmic inclusions in fibroblasts and muscle, all pointed to FSASD. At one time, the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis of FSASD was the demonstration of elevated free sialic acid in the lysosomes but not in the cytoplasm of cultured fibroblasts; however, currently, molecular studies are employed instead4,5. Nevertheless, conventional molecular techniques, including ES and GS, failed to identify the “missing” second SLC17A5 variant in the proband. Furthermore, the relatively mild biochemical abnormalities and broad differential diagnosis added to the clinical complexity and diagnostic delay. Only after utilization of more comprehensive molecular studies, including deep sequencing, long read DNA sequencing, and RNASeq, was the molecular diagnosis established, ending a more than 15-year diagnostic odyssey. Her presentation is reminiscent of a previously reported individual with mild biochemical and clinical phenotype who was found to have an insertion in intron 9 of SLC17A5 caused by a long-interspersed element-1 retrotransposon, which was only detected after applying sophisticated molecular technologies.32

Long read sequencing is emerging as a diagnostic tool for individuals with a high index of susception for a Mendelian disorder. The different techniques used today enable high quality sequencing of long DNA segments, mapping of repetitive regions of the genome, phasing of the variants, and detection of methylation status of CpG islands.12,13,33,34 The specific approach utilized here, Illumina Complete Long Reads, provided definitive haplotype phasing of the mosaic variant despite the limited amount of available DNA.

The two pathogenic SLC17A5 variants identified in the proband are damaging and expected to cause a severe FSASD presentation. The maternally-inherited variant results in loss of function through nonsense-mediated decay. This variant is relatively common in the nonFinnish population (93/1,178,886 alleles in gnomAD; last accessed 11/22/2024 ) and also reported in at least 4 FSASD patients in the literature1,28-30. Our protein expression data suggest that the intragenic deletion in the paternally-inherited allele results in a dominant negative effect with possible accumulation of sialin aggregates. However, the cellular significance of this effect is unclear since the second allele in the proband does not produce a protein product. The relatively mild to moderate clinical course in the proband along with mild elevation of her urinary sialic acid could be explained by significant residual sialin function in various tissues due to mosaicism of the variant in the paternally-inherited allele in some tissues as demonstrated in proband fibroblast and muscle cells.

The pathophysiology of FSASD is not well understood, and the role of intra-lysosomal free sialic acid accumulation in disease pathology is unclear. Although the design of this study is not focused on this topic, our data suggest that the pathophysiology of FSASD is more complex than merely aberrant storage of free sialic acid in the lysosome. The muscle pathology in the proband showed mitochondrial proliferation and elongation accompanied by accumulation of lipid droplets, all of which indicate some mitochondrial dysfunction. Similarly impaired lysosomal-mitochondrial interactomes are being increasingly reported in other lysosomal storage disorders and neurodegenerative conditions.35,36 In addition, our in vitro data using a SLC17A5 KO human cell line (A549) showed that lysosomal and membrane distribution, morphology and density of the plasma membrane were different than the wild-type, indicating a more complex cellular phenotype associated with SLC17A5 deficiency. This is not surprising since sialylation is a very important post-translational modification of membrane glycolipids and glycoproteins, including brain gangliosides, and altered sialylation has been shown to affect myelination.37

The clinical presentation of the proband provides evidence that tissue-specific mosaicism can explain atypical or milder presentations of metabolic diseases. The clinical heterogeneity seen in inborn errors of metabolism and primary mitochondrial disorders can be partially explained by mosaicism (for nuclear genes) and heteroplasmy (for mitochondrial genes).38,39 It is possible that tissue-specific mosaicism can explain a portion of molecularly undiagnosed disorders.

Approximately 250 individuals with FSASD have been reported (https://www.orpha.net/). The Finnish founder missense variant p.Arg39Cys in SLC17A5 is present in either homozygous or heterozygous state in the majority of these individuals. The the estimated worlwide prevalence as reported in Orphanet is 1 per 1,000,000 (https://www.orpha.net/) and therefore the predicted number of affected individuals globally is ~8,000. This predicted prevalence is far higher than the number of individuals with FSASD who have been reported in the medical literature, and this discrepancy may be explained by multiple factors including embryonic lethality, shortened lifespan because of disease complications, milder presentations that escape detection, and asymptomatic presentations. Furthermore, nonspecific early symptoms of FSASD that overlap with many other neurodegenerative disorders, lack of a standard clinical test for urine free sialic acid measurement, and unfamiliarity of providers with FSASD can also be additional factors for undiagnosed FSASD cases. It is also likely that other individuals, similar to our proband, remain undiagnosed because of atypical molecular etiologies, which require complex molecular methodologies to solve.

One of our study’s limitations is that we did not measure sialic acid levels in the proband’s fibroblasts or other cell lines. Furthermore, the lack of reliable human specific sialin antibodies hindered our ability to perform confirmatory experiments on proband’s fibroblasts. Lastly, the data obtained from SLC17A5 KO human A549 cells have also not been verified on the proband’s fibroblasts.

In conclusion, this unique FSASD presentation of tissue-specific mosaicism of an intragenic deletion in SLC17A5 illustrates the complexity of establishing a molecular diagnosis that can arise with atypical molecular mechanisms. Our work also emphasizes the importance of pursuing cutting-edge molecular technologies when there is a high index of suspicion based on a specific phenotype but an incomplete molecular diagnosis.
