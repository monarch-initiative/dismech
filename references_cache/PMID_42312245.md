# Expanding the Spectrum of BCAP31-Associated Diseases: Early-Onset Parkinson Disease.
**Authors:** Ishiguro M, Funayama M, Park DH, Hatano T, Nakazato T, Luo Y, Osawa M, Li Y, Yoshino H, Hattori N, Kanai K
**Journal:** Neurol Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1212/NXG.0000000000200404](https://doi.org/10.1212/NXG.0000000000200404)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Hemizygous variants in B-cell receptor-associated protein 31 (BCAP31) can cause deafness, dystonia, and cerebral hypomyelination syndrome, which typically presents in infancy.

METHODS: We performed whole-exome sequencing in a family with 2 affected individuals who exhibited congenital hearing loss and adult-onset motor symptoms, including Parkinson disease (PD) and cerebellar ataxia. To assess the frequency of BCAP31 variants, we analyzed 234 Japanese individuals with either recessively inherited familial PD or early-onset PD using Sanger sequencing.

RESULTS: Whole-exome sequencing identified a novel BCAP31 variant (c.302G>A; p.Arg101Lys) shared by both individuals. The proband developed parkinsonism in early adulthood; responded well to levodopa; and later exhibited wearing-off phenomena, dyskinesias, and cerebellar signs. Sanger sequencing of a Japanese population with PD identified 3 additional BCAP31 variants; however, all 3 variants were predicted to be benign and showed no enrichment compared with public databases.

DISCUSSION: This report links a BCAP31 variant to adult-onset PD. Our findings suggest that BCAP31 should be considered in male patients with early-onset PD and atypical features such as congenital deafness and cerebellar involvement.

Pathogenic variants in BCAP31, which encodes B-cell receptor–associated protein 31, cause deafness, dystonia, and cerebral hypomyelination (DDCH) syndrome (MIM #300475). This gene was first identified in 11 male patients from 3 separate families with X-linked intellectual disability.1 Clinically, patients with DDCH syndrome present with severe motor and intellectual disabilities, congenital deafness, dystonia, white matter hypomyelination, and cerebral atrophy. Furthermore, the onset of motor symptoms occurs at birth or in infancy. Although one family has been reported as developing motor symptoms in adulthood,2 to date, most patients with BCAP31 variants have died before the age of 3 years and have presented with severe intellectual disability and developmental delays in addition to the 3 major symptoms (deafness, dystonia, and cerebral hypomyelination).3-8
BCAP31 is located on chromosome Xq28, between the ATP-binding cassette subfamily D member 1 (ABCD1) gene and the solute carrier family 6 member 8 (SLC6A8) gene. Similar phenotypes have been observed in cases of microdeletions and partial deletions involving these flanking genes.9-11

In this study, we identified 2 patients from a single family carrying a novel BCAP31 variant, both of whom presented with congenital deafness and movement disorders.

For whole-exome sequencing (WES), genomic DNA was collected from 6 members of the original family (Figure 1). In addition, a validation cohort of 234 male patients with familial and/or early-onset PD underwent BCAP31 sequencing. All participants in the validation cohort were selected based on the following criteria: (1) a mode of inheritance consistent with recessive PD, or sporadic early-onset PD (onset before 50 years of age); (2) male sex; (3) the absence of pathogenic variants in any of the following PD-related and dementia-related genes: SNCA, PARK2, UCHL1, PINK1, PARK7, LRRK2, ATP13A2, GIGYF2, HTRA2, PLA2G6, FBXO7, VPS35, EIF4G1, DNAJC6, SYNJ1, DNAJC13, CHCHD2, GCH1, NR4A2, VPS13C, RAB7L1, BST1, C19orf12, RAB39B, MAPT, PSEN1, GRN, APP, and APOE. The demographics of the validation cohort are presented in Table 2. In our genome bank, no families had undergone pedigree analyses across multiple generations similar to those of the proband family, in which an X-linked mode of inheritance was definitively established. We, therefore, screened autosomal recessive families as candidates because X-linked inheritance can sometimes be hidden.

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Library preparation for WES was conducted using the Twist Exome 2.0 Kit (Twist Bioscience, San Francisco, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed using 150-bp paired-end reads on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), generating more than 6 GB of data and approximately 40 million reads per sample. We obtained an average coverage of above 70×.

Raw sequencing reads (FASTQ files) were trimmed using Trimmomatic12 (version 0.36) and aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA, version 0.7.17-r1188).13 Variant calling was conducted using the Genomic Analysis Toolkit (GATK, version 4.1.9.0)14 following the GATK Best Practices Workflow for Germline Short Variant Discovery (SNPs and indels). The resulting variants were annotated using ANNOVAR.15 Filtering criteria were as follows: (1) single-nucleotide variants or indels located on the X chromosome; (2) variants located within exons or splice sites; (3) allele frequency <0.001 in the general population according to gnomAD v3.1.2; (4) exclusion of synonymous substitutions; and (5) variants that were hemizygous in the proband and the affected uncle, and heterozygous in the mother. Average coverage was calculated using the “samtools depth” program. The WES target bed file was obtained from the Twist Bioscience website.16

BCAP31 gene analysis by Sanger sequencing was performed on 6 members of the original family and 234 individuals in the validation cohort. Exons 2–8, which are the coding exons of BCAP31 (accession number: NM_001139457.2), were amplified using PCR with exon-specific primers. The sequences of both the PCR and sequencing primers are listed in the Supplemental eTable. Primers were designed using the Primer3 program. BCAP31 has pseudogenes (BCAP31P1 and BCAP31P2) on chromosome 16 with 95.5% homology. The primers were, therefore, carefully designed to target sequences unique to BCAP31, thereby avoiding amplification of the pseudogene. The PCR products were purified using the ExoSAP-IT enzymatic cleanup protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). DNA sequencing was performed using a commercial Sanger sequencing service (Azenta, Tokyo, Japan). The DNA sequence of each participant was aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using the BLAT17 program to detect variants. The pathogenicity of the detected variants was assessed using the ClinVar database. Minor allele frequencies in the Japanese general population were obtained from 60KJPN, a jMorp database provided by ToMMo.18

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Juntendo University, Tokyo, Japan (approval number: M08-0477-M11). All participants provided written informed consent to participate in the genetic research described herein.

Anonymized data not published within this article will be made available by request from any qualified investigator.

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on 6 family members (Figure 1). The proband (patient IV-1) presented with deafness, parkinsonism, and cerebellar ataxia. The proband's maternal uncle (patient III-3) also exhibited deafness and cerebellar ataxia. The remaining 4 family members—III-1, III-2, III-4, and IV-2—were unaffected.

The average coverage of the 6 participants who underwent WES was 78×. Using WES data, an analysis of the hemizygous variants shared by the 2 patients but not by the proband's younger brother (IV-2) identified 2 variants: PAS domain containing repressor 1 (PASD1) (accession number: NM_173493.3) c.866G>A: p.Gly289Asp (rs199829404) and BCAP31 c.302G>A: p.Arg101Lys. Because the PASD1 variant (c.866G>A: p.Gly289Asp, rs199829404) was predicted to be “benign,” with a Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) score of 0.001, we concluded that the disease-causing variant in this family was BCAP31 c.302G>A: p. Arg101Lys (Table 1). This variant was predicted to be highly pathogenic, with a CADD score of 26.9. Furthermore, it was determined to be a novel variant and was not registered in any public variant databases. The proband's mother (III-2, unaffected) was heterozygous for this variant.

To assess the frequency of this and other rare BCAP31 variants among patients with recessive inherited familial PD and early-onset PD, we examined the BCAP31 sequence in a validation cohort (Table 2). The c.302G>A variant identified in the original family was not detected in the cohort. However, 3 other BCAP31 variants were identified among the 234 individuals. All 3 variants were predicted to be benign, and their allele frequencies did not differ significantly between the general population and our cohort (Table 3).

Patient IV-1 was a 48-year-old man diagnosed with PD. His gestation and birth were uneventful. He had congenital deafness from birth, but according to his mother, he exhibited no motor delays or intellectual disability. He worked as both a computer technician and a dental technician until the age of 27 years.

Aged 28 years, he developed tremor in his right hand as well as gait disturbance. At 32 years, he began taking levodopa, to which he responded well; however, he experienced marked wearing-off phenomena. Dopamine agonists and selegiline were prescribed but were discontinued because of the occurrence of hallucinations. He subsequently developed cerebellar symptoms, including nystagmus and saccadic eye movement.

Despite worsening motor symptoms, he was unable to tolerate increased doses of levodopa medication because of increased agitation. By age 40 years, he had progressed to Hoehn and Yahr stage V during “off” periods and required institutional care. A gastric fistula was placed because he was unable to take medication or consume food and drink during off periods. During “on” periods, he experienced severe dyskinesia and impulse control disorders, including hypersexuality. In a levodopa challenge test, his score in Part III of the Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale improved from 88 to 44 points, representing a 50% improvement. At the age of 42 years, he underwent deep brain stimulation surgery because of the progressive worsening of wearing-off phenomena. Considering the risk of exacerbating his dyskinesia and psychiatric complications, the electrodes were implanted into the globus pallidus internus. After the procedure, both off time and dyskinesia showed temporary improvements. However, a gradual recurrence of wearing-off symptoms was subsequently observed. Although his cognition was preserved, the patient exhibited progressive psychiatric symptoms, including hypersexuality, visual hallucinations, and agitation, which limited the titration of anti-parkinsonian therapy. By the age of 48 years, his functional status had markedly deteriorated, with activities of daily living consistent with Hoehn and Yahr stage IV during on periods and stage V during off periods. To ease motor complications and psychosis, he was prescribed levodopa (total daily dose: 400 mg, administered in 6 divided doses). Additional medications included trazodone (50 mg), paroxetine (10 mg), quetiapine (75 mg), and tiapride (25 mg).

Brain MRI revealed no white matter hyperintensities and only mild cerebral atrophy. N-isopropyl-p-123I-iodoamphetamine SPECT (123I-IMP-SPECT) showed reduced uptake in the left frontal lobe, occipital lobe, and cerebellum (Figure 2A). Moreover, dopamine transporter SPECT demonstrated a reduction of dopamine transporter uptake (Figure 2B), and 123I-metaiodobenzylguanidine (123I-MIBG) myocardial scintigraphy revealed a preserved heart-to-mediastinum ratio (Figure 2C). The proband's mother (III-2) was a carrier of the gene but remained asymptomatic.

The proband's maternal uncle, Patient III-3, also had congenital deafness from birth. He exhibited neither motor delays nor intellectual disability during early development. In his 40s, he developed cerebellar ataxia, raising the suspicion of spinocerebellar degeneration. He was institutionalized in his 50s. At the age of 59 years, he was able to stand and walk with assistance, presenting a wide-based gait. In addition, he was capable of crawling on all fours without assistance. Neurologic examinations revealed gaze-evoked nystagmus and slow saccadic eye movements. He demonstrated increased muscle tone in the wrists and mild bradykinesia but did not exhibit definitive features of parkinsonism.

In this study, we identified a novel hemizygous variant in BCAP31 (c.302G>A; p.Arg101Lys) in 2 male patients from the same family. Both individuals presented with congenital hearing loss, and one developed early-onset PD in his late 20s, which was later accompanied by cerebellar symptoms. To our knowledge, adult-onset PD has not previously been described in association with BCAP31 variants. This suggests a broader phenotypic spectrum than that previously recognized for BCAP31-related disorders.

BCAP31 has been previously associated with juvenile-onset myotonia, severe intellectual disability, and DDCH syndrome, which is a fatal neurodevelopmental disorder of infancy. By contrast, our patients exhibited no early intellectual disability or delayed motor development, and one maintained professional employment until his late 20s. This relatively mild phenotype and the presence of adult-onset motor symptoms suggest that the p.Arg101Lys variant may represent a hypomorphic allele with partial loss of function, rather than being a complete loss-of-function variant (as is typically seen in DDCH syndrome).

Notably, cerebellar ataxia and hypoperfusion were detected using 123I-IMP-SPECT in this case, whereas cardiac sympathetic innervation was preserved on 123I-MIBG myocardial scintigraphy. These findings are atypical in sporadic PD and further support the possibility that BCAP31 dysfunction contributes to a unique neurodegenerative disorder involving both nigrostriatal and cerebellar pathways. Considering the role of BCAP31 in endoplasmic reticulum protein transport and the regulation of apoptosis,19 the identified variant may cause selective neuronal vulnerability, particularly within midbrain and cerebellar circuits.

The absence of the p.Arg101Lys variant in a validation cohort of 234 male patients with early-onset or familial PD, as well as in a large public genome database, suggests that BCAP31-related parkinsonism is extremely rare. Although 3 other BCAP31 variants were identified in our cohort, all 3 were predicted to be benign, and their frequencies in the cohort did not significantly differ from those in the general population. Nevertheless, this study provides evidence that pathogenic BCAP31 variants may contribute to the development of early-onset PD.

Our findings have several clinical implications. First, BCAP31 should be considered a candidate gene in male patients with early-onset PD accompanied by congenital deafness. Second, our case indicates that X-linked genes that are traditionally associated with severe childhood-onset disease may also present as milder, adult-onset phenotypes. Third, given the X-linked inheritance pattern, genetic testing and counseling are essential for female carriers even in the absence of clinical symptoms.

There are several limitations to this study. No functional assays were performed to validate the pathogenicity of the identified variant. Furthermore, although the variant was absent from public databases and the validation cohort, the possibility of other genetic or environmental modifiers cannot be excluded. In addition, this study did not examine autosomal genes. Further research using functional models and patient-derived cells is necessary to clarify the precise mechanisms by which the BCAP31 variant contributes to neurodegeneration.

In conclusion, this report expands the clinical spectrum of BCAP31-related disorders by identifying a novel variant associated with early-onset parkinsonism and cerebellar dysfunction in adulthood. These findings contrast with previously reported cases of DDCH syndrome in that our patients exhibited relatively mild, adult-onset symptoms. Our results suggest that BCAP31 variants may underlie atypical forms of PD and highlight the importance of considering X-linked genes in the genetic evaluation of early-onset parkinsonism, especially when patients exhibit nonmotor symptoms such as hearing impairment and cerebellar involvement.
