# A biallelically active embryonic enhancer dictates GNAS imprinting through allele-specific conformations.
**Authors:** Iwasaki Y, Reyes M, Jüppner H, Bastepe M
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-025-56608-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-56608-0)

## Content

Genomic imprinting controls parental allele-specific gene expression via epigenetic mechanisms. Abnormal imprinting at the GNAS gene causes multiple phenotypes, including pseudohypoparathyroidism type-1B (PHP1B), a disorder of multihormone resistance. Microdeletions affecting the neighboring STX16 gene ablate an imprinting control region (STX16-ICR) of GNAS and lead to PHP1B upon maternal but not paternal inheritance. Mechanisms behind this imprinted inheritance mode remain unknown. Here, we show that the STX16-ICR forms different chromatin conformations with each GNAS parental allele and enhances two GNAS promoters in human embryonic stem cells. When these cells differentiate toward proximal renal tubule cells, STX16-ICR loses its effect, accompanied by a transition to a somatic cell-specific GNAS imprinting status. The activity of STX16-ICR depends on an OCT4 motif, whose disruption impacts transcript levels differentially on each allele. Therefore, a biallelically active embryonic enhancer dictates GNAS imprinting via different chromatin conformations, underlying the allele-specific pathogenicity of STX16-ICR microdeletions.

Genomic imprinting is an epigenetic phenomenon in which parental alleles show differential gene expression patterns. Imprinted loci are involved in pivotal biological processes, including development, growth, and hormone actions, and aberrant genomic imprinting is found in various diseases1. However, the regulation of allele-specific gene expression is poorly defined, thus restricting the understanding of the disorders caused by perturbed imprinting.

GNAS is a complex imprinted locus encoding the stimulatory G protein α-subunit (Gsα), an essential signal transducer for numerous G protein-coupled receptors. Several additional transcripts are expressed monoallelically from GNAS using alternative first exons located within differentially methylated regions (DMRs). Derived from the sense strand are NESP55, XLαs, and A/B transcripts, and derived from the antisense strand is the GNAS-AS1 transcript (Fig. 1A). These GNAS products play key physiological roles as noncoding or coding RNAs2,3. The NESP55 transcript is necessary for establishing maternal GNAS methylation imprints, including the A/B DMR. On the paternal allele, the absence of A/B methylation and/or A/B expression leads to silencing of Gsα expression in a limited number of differentiated tissues through an unknown mechanism, and thus, suppression of A/B transcription by NESP55-dependent methylation on the maternal allele is pivotal for maintaining normal Gsα expression level in these tissues. The XLαs transcript encodes a unique signaling protein that is partially identical to Gsα and required for intrauterine and early postnatal growth4–10. A representative disease caused by aberrant GNAS imprinting is pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1B (PHP1B), a disorder of end-organ resistance to hormones that signal via Gsα, including, most prominently, the parathyroid hormone3,14,15. Patients with PHP1B display altered methylation along the maternal GNAS allele, with all patients having a loss of methylation at the A/B DMR3. Methylation at the maternal GNAS DMRs, including A/B, is introduced during oogenesis in mice16 and likely in humans17. Although methylation at most imprinted loci is protected from genome-wide demethylation after fertilization, the A/B DMR undergoes a second wave of demethylation and remethylation in an early postzygotic period4,18,19. Using human embryonic stem cell (hESC) models of PHP1B, we have recently identified a small region in the STX16 locus, ~170 kb centromeric of GNAS, that is pivotal for maternal A/B methylation by enhancing NESP55 transcription in this postzygotic period4. Microdeletions affecting this region, referred to as the GNAS imprinting control region at STX16 (STX16-ICR)4, disrupt GNAS imprinting and are the most frequent cause of autosomal dominant PHP1B (AD-PHP1B)3,15,20–25 (Fig. 1A). Importantly, STX16-ICR microdeletions are pathogenic only when maternally inherited3,15,20, suggesting that the STX16-ICR operates only on the maternal allele. However, this region lacks differential methylation20, and STX16 mRNA is biallelically expressed, at least in some differentiated cells21 and hESCs4. Therefore, the mechanism governing the allele-specific pathogenicity of STX16-ICR microdeletions remains to be elucidated.

Here, using hESC models of PHP1B, we show that the regulatory role of STX16-ICR on GNAS is not confined to the maternal allele. In addition to regulating the maternal expression of NESP55, STX16-ICR dictates the biallelic expression of XLαs. Allele-specific chromatin conformations involving STX16-ICR and GNAS underlie differential transcriptional regulation between parental alleles. We further provide evidence that STX16-ICR loses its regulatory role during differentiation, allowing a transition of GNAS imprinting to a somatic cell-specific status. These findings reveal the broad regulatory role of STX16-ICR in GNAS imprinting and provide insights into the allele-specific effect of STX16 deletions in PHP1B pathogenesis.

In contrast to the exclusively maternal NESP55 transcription4,6,16, we recently noticed that XLαs, whose unique exon is ~12 kb telomeric of the NESP55 exon (Fig. 1A), is biallelically expressed despite maternal methylation of most surrounding DMRs in hESCs4. Also, deleting STX16-ICR on either parental allele caused diminished XLαs mRNA levels in these cells4. Therefore, we hypothesized that STX16-ICR may biallelically regulate different GNAS transcripts. To test this hypothesis, we employed hESCs (HUES62 cells), which showed the expected methylation pattern and normal copy number of GNAS (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Using these cells, we performed chromatin conformation capture (3C)-qPCR and 3C-PCR, focusing on the interaction between STX16-ICR and several different regions inside and outside the GNAS locus in WT hESCs. The results showed that STX16-ICR interacted with both NESP55 and XLαs DMRs in hESCs, and the interaction frequency was highest at the NESP55 DMR, and the XLαs DMR showed a lower level interaction (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). STX16-ICR did not show detectable interaction with the restriction site closest to the A/B DMR or the Gsα promoter (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig.2A, E)4.

To identify the allele on which these chromatin interactions occur, we performed 3C-qPCR using hESCs with maternal or paternal STX16-ICR deletion (Δmat and Δpat STX16-ICR, respectively)4. hESCs with Δmat STX16-ICR, but not those with Δpat STX16-ICR, showed almost no interaction between the STX16-ICR and the NESP55 DMR (Fig. 1D). By contrast, both Δmat and Δpat STX16-ICR hESCs showed similar levels of interaction between STX16-ICR and the XLαs DMR (Fig. 1E). To further confirm the interacting allele, we amplified 3C samples in WT hESCs such that each PCR product contains an informative heterozygous SNP and sequenced the product. The amplicon detecting the interaction between STX16-ICR and the NESP55 DMR with primers #1 and #2, which contains a heterozygous SNP (rs3787497; Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. 2B–F), carried only the maternal allele of this SNP (Fig. 1G). By contrast, two independent 3C-PCR amplicons detecting the interaction between STX16-ICR and the XLαs DMR showed both alleles of heterozygous SNPs, rs8125112 and rs117651194, using primers #1–#3 and #1–#4, respectively (Fig. 1F, H, and I and Supplementary Fig. 2C, D, G, and H). Thus, the STX16-ICR interacts with the NESP55 region only on the maternal allele, whereas it interacts with the XLαs region biallelically, suggesting that the role of STX16-ICR in regulating GNAS is not confined to a single allele.

To test whether the enhancer action of STX16-ICR occurs biallelically in hESCs, we examined an active enhancer mark, H3K27ac, in this region. ENCODE chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-seq data showed the presence of this mark in hESCs (Fig. 2A). To clarify the allelic distribution of this mark, we performed CUT&RUN qPCR using Δmat and Δpat STX16-ICR hESCs. Next, we focused on an active promoter mark, H3K4me3, which was shown to be present in the NESP55 and XLαs regions in hESCs by ENCODE ChIP-seq data (Fig. 2C). In hESCs with maternal deletion of the NESP55 region (Δmat NESP55), but not paternal deletion (Δpat NESP55), the CUT&RUN qPCR detected a significantly reduced H3K4me3 signal compared to that in WT hESCs (Fig. 2D). This result indicated that H3K4me3 is primarily present on the maternal allele, consistent with maternal monoallelic NESP55 transcription. Notably, in Δmat STX16-ICR, but not in Δpat STX16-ICR, hESCs, the H3K4me3 mark was attenuated in the NESP55 region (Fig. 2D), supporting the conclusion that maternal STX16-ICR enhances the NESP55 promoter activity in cis, likely reflecting maternal allele-specific interaction with the enhancer at STX16-ICR (Fig. 2D). According to ENCODE ChIP-Seq data, the NESP55 region also had a weak peak for the repressive histone mark, H3K27me3, in hESCs (Fig. 2C), and CUT&RUN qPCR analysis of Δmat and Δpat NESP55 hESCs revealed that this mark was detectable primarily on the maternal allele (Supplementary Fig. 3)26. At the putative XLαs promoter region, the H3K4me3 signal was comparable between Δmat and Δpat STX16-ICR hESCs, consistent with the biallelic interaction of this region with STX16-ICR (Fig. 2E). These results collectively supported our model that biallelically active enhancer at STX16-ICR interacts maternally with the NESP55 DMR and biallelically with XLαs regions.

Imprinted, maternal NESP55 transcription is pivotal in GNAS imprinting, and human and mouse studies suggested that the genomic region comprising the NESP55 promoter and first exon gains methylation in an early postzygotic period only on the paternal allele6,17. We, therefore, hypothesized that CpG DNA methylation compromises the interaction between the paternal NESP55 region and STX16-ICR, thereby limiting the transcription of NESP55 to a single allele. Supporting this idea, the NESP55 promoter in hESCs also harbored a ChIP-seq signal peak for CTCF (Fig. 2C), which may contribute to chromatin loop formation but cannot bind to DNA containing CpG methylation27. CUT&RUN qPCR showed that CTCF recruitment to the NESP55 promoter was significantly diminished in Δmat, but not Δpat NESP55 hESCs, indicating that CTCF is recruited primarily to the maternal NESP55 promoter (Fig. 3A). To further test whether CpG methylation determines imprinted NESP55 expression, we treated hESCs with an inhibitor of DNMT1 (DNMT1i), the maintenance DNA methyltransferase. Bisulfite next-generation sequencing of the NESP55 promoter region surrounding the CTCF binding motif revealed that DNMT1i treatment led to significant hypomethylation at multiple CpGs (Fig. 3B). This treatment also changed the interaction between STX16-ICR and the NESP55 region from maternally monoallelic (see Fig. 1D, G) to biallelic (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, NESP55 imprinting was relaxed, i.e., the NESP55 expression, which was exclusively maternal in vehicle-treated hESCs (Fig. 3D), became biallelic and was significantly elevated in DNMT1i-treated hESCs (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. 4A). GNAS-AS1, a normally paternal transcript, was also elevated by about 2-fold in DNMT1i-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 4B), presumably reflecting the predicted biallelic expression of this transcript, although a lack of informative polymorphisms in this region prevented us from demonstrating it. Thus, at least on the paternal allele, both NESP55 and GNAS-AS1 are expressed, suggesting that the artificial hypomethylation did not recapitulate the physiological status. Nonetheless, these results collectively support our hypothesis that paternal CpG methylation prevents the interaction between the paternal NESP55 region and STX16-ICR, thereby ensuring exclusively maternal NESP55 transcription.

In contrast to maternally monoallelic interaction with NESP55, STX16-ICR showed biallelic interaction with XLαs. Since the active enhancer mark, H3K27ac, was present on both parental alleles of STX16-ICR, we reasoned that this region also operates as an enhancer for XLαs transcription. We thus focused on the putative XLαs promoter region, which showed, based on publicly available ChIP-seq data from hESCs, an enrichment of the active promoter mark, histone H3K4me3 (Fig. 2C), and was immediately upstream of a CAGE-seq peak indicating the transcriptional start site (Fig. 4A). While the putative XLαs promoter region alone showed weak activity in luciferase reporter assays, the presence of STX16-ICR magnified its activity in hESCs (Fig. 4B). Within the cloned XLαs promoter region was AS2, a diagnostically important region to categorize PHP1B patients28,29. AS2 shows a low methylation level in hESCs (~3.6%)28, in contrast to most DMRs surrounding the XLαs exon (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Since the function of AS2 remained unclear, we generated truncated XLαs promoter constructs, revealing that AS2 is the core XLαs promoter responsive to the enhancer at STX16-ICR (Fig. 4B). We recently demonstrated that the pluripotency factor OCT4 is required for NESP55 transcription and recruited to a consensus motif within STX16-ICR in hESCs4. Ablating this OCT4 motif in our luciferase reporter construct almost abolished the effect of STX16-ICR on XLαs promoter activity (Fig. 4C), indicating that the regulation of XLαs by STX16-ICR also depends on the OCT4 motif.

Based on these findings, we next hypothesized that STX16-ICR functions as an enhancer that is biallelically active for XLαs transcription and specific for embryonic cells. To address this hypothesis, we amplified the XLαs transcript using a forward primer specific for the XLαs exon and a reverse primer in exon 13 in WT, Δmat, and Δpat STX16-ICR hESCs (Fig. 5A) and verified transcript identity by Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 5A). We identified the allelic origin of the XLα transcript by sequencing a heterozygous SNP (rs7121) in exon 5 (Fig. 5A)4. In WT hESCs, both XLαs alleles were expressed (Fig. 5B). In Δpat STX16-ICR hESCs, XLαs expression was exclusively maternal, and in Δmat STX16-ICR hESCs, it was exclusively paternal (Fig. 5C, D). Next, we tested whether the regulatory role of STX16-ICR on NESP55 and XLαs transcripts is lost upon differentiation of hESCs. Since XLαs is paternally expressed in multiple somatic cells2,8, we hypothesized that XLαs expression becomes monoallelic and independent of STX16-ICR during differentiation. We thus differentiated WT and STX16-ICR-deleted hESCs towards proximal renal tubule (PRT) cells, which not only express XLαs30 but also play a causal role in the most significant hormone resistance in PHP1B (PTH resistance). We used a previously reported differentiation protocol (Fig. 5E)31 and confirmed, in differentiated PRT-like cells, the loss of OCT4 gene expression and the emergence of PTH receptor expression. The latter was determined by mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C) and the binding of fluorescently labeled PTH (Fig. 5F, G). When WT hESCs were differentiated toward PRT-like cells, NESP55 expression level became nearly undetectable, suggesting that the enhancer in STX16-ICR is not operative in differentiated cells (Fig. 5H). While we could readily measure the XLαs expression levels in WT PRT-like cells, similar to in WT hESCs (Fig. 5H), its allelic origin became exclusively paternal (Fig. 5I). PRT-like cells derived from Δpat STX16-ICR hESCs also showed exclusively paternal XLαs expression (Fig. 5J), contrary to that observed in undifferentiated Δpat STX16-ICR hESCs (see Fig. 5C). Δmat STX16-ICR PRT-like cells showed paternal XLαs expression (Fig. 5K); this allelic origin was the same as that observed before differentiation (see Fig. 5D). These results suggested that due to the lack of influence of STX16-ICR upon differentiation, XLαs showed paternal expression by default regardless of which parental copy of STX16-ICR was deleted.

To confirm that the enhancer at STX16-ICR is inactive in PRT-like cells, we analyzed the H3K27ac mark in STX16-ICR by CUT&RUN qPCR, which showed that this active enhancer mark is no longer present in PRT-like cells (Fig. 5L). Accordingly, 3C-qPCR showed that STX16-ICR did not interact with either the NESP55 or XLαs regions in PRT-like cells (Fig. 5M, N). These results suggested that STX16-ICR governs NESP55 and XLαs expression specifically in the early embryo and that loss of its regulatory role during differentiation is associated with the transition of GNAS imprinting into a distinct status, i.e., loss of NESP55 expression and silencing of maternal XLαs transcription.

In PRT-like cells, the NESP55 region did not show the active promoter histone mark, H3K4me3, while it did show the repressive mark, H3K27me3 (Fig. 5O, P), consistent with transcriptional silencing. In contrast, CTCF bound to the NESP55 promoter in PRT-like cells at a similar level to undifferentiated hESCs (Fig. 5Q), indicating that the silencing of NESP55 expression in PRT-like cells is not due to the absence of CTCF recruitment. Similarly, CTCF binding to the XLαs promoter in hESCs, the AS2 region, was comparable between PRT-like cells and hESCs, although we could not determine the allelic distribution of CTCF in this region (Supplementary Fig. 5D). The AS2 region still showed H3K4me3 upon differentiation to PRT-like cells (Fig. 5R), consistent with active XLαs transcription. The AS2 methylation level in PRT-like cells, measured by MSRE-qPCR, was 20.5%, which was higher than that in hESCs yet still lower than the level of methylation in the surrounding regions (~50%), including AS1 and XLαs DMRs (Supplementary Fig. 5E, F). Thus, it was unlikely that AS2 alone is the primary determinant of the exclusively paternal XLαs expression.

Finally, we tried to confirm the embryonic stage-specific enhancer function of STX16-ICR by showing the pivotal role of the OCT4 motif in the regulation of GNAS transcripts. To identify the allelic nature of OCT4 recruitment to STX16-ICR in hESCs, we sequenced the OCT4 CUT&RUN amplicons from this region containing a heterozygous SNP (rs2296524) adjacent to the OCT4 motif (Fig. 6A), which showed biallelic amplification (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, both Δmat and Δpat STX16-ICR hESCs showed similar levels of OCT4 recruitment, indicating that OCT4 was recruited to STX16-ICR on both parental alleles (Fig. 6C). Next, we used CRISPR/Cas9 and generated hESC single-cell clones with small deletions (7-22 nucleotides) overlapping the OCT4 motif on the maternal allele (ΔOCT4 +/Mat−), the paternal allele (ΔOCT4 Pat−/+), or both alleles (ΔOCT4 −/−) (Fig. 6D, Supplementary Fig. 6A–E, and Supplementary Table. 1). Clones with maternally or biallelically, but not those with paternally, disrupted OCT4 motif showed severely blunted NESP55 transcript levels (Fig. 6E), consistent with NESP55 expression being exclusively maternal. By contrast, XLαs expression was significantly, but incompletely, attenuated in both maternally and paternally disrupted clones, and it was dramatically reduced in biallelically disrupted clones (Fig. 6F). The current study showed that STX16-ICR operates as a biallelically active, embryonic stage-specific enhancer of GNAS. Furthermore, we provided evidence that differential chromatin conformations underlie the allele-specific regulation of GNAS transcripts by STX16-ICR (Fig. 7A). Whereas STX16-ICR enhances NESP55 transcription on the maternal allele, it activates XLαs transcription biallelically. NESP55 transcription is necessary for methylation at maternal GNAS DMRs, especially A/B4, and maternal A/B methylation is essential for allowing maternally monoallelic Gsα expression in specific endocrine or hormone-responsive tissues32. Accordingly, the importance of STX16-ICR for tissue-specific Gsα imprinting is restricted to the maternal allele. Therefore, rather than monoallelic enhancer activity, the allele-specific chromatin interactions between STX16-ICR and GNAS DMRs explain why only maternal STX16-ICR microdeletions cause A/B loss of methylation and consequently PHP1B.

DNA methylation at its target is likely crucial for the allele-specific action of STX16-ICR. The NESP55 DMR remains unmethylated until fertilization and gains methylation postzygotically on the paternal allele in humans and mice6,17,33–35. We showed in the current study that this paternal methylation at the NESP55 region is the primary determinant of maternally monoallelic interaction with STX16-ICR and maternally monoallelic NESP55 transcription. By contrast, biallelic XLαs transcription in hESCs was driven by a core promoter, AS2, that is almost devoid of methylation28. A methylated promoter is generally associated with heterochromatin36, which likely prevents interaction with enhancers. Therefore, it is plausible that STX16-ICR can enhance the maternal NESP55 promoter and both maternal and paternal XLαs promoters due to a paucity of methylation at these sites but cannot exert its action on the methylated paternal NESP55 promoter. In hESCs, the NESP55 is more strongly expressed compared to XLαs; this difference likely reflects the higher level of interaction with STX16-ICR, as shown in the 3C assay.

Enhancer-mediated regulation of allele-specific transcription is also observed in other imprinted loci1,37,38, suggesting a shared regulatory mechanism of genomic imprinting. However, enhancer activity on each parental allele is inconsistent among these loci. A biallelically active enhancer regulating monoallelic transcription through differential chromatin conformations has been documented in the H19-IGF2 locus37,39 and other imprinted loci40,41. The allele-specific binding of CTCF contributes to chromatin looping and serves as an allele-specific insulator at those imprinted loci39–42. Since the allele-specific interaction between STX16-ICR and GNAS resembles the patterns observed in these imprinted loci, CTCF at the maternal NESP55 promoter might insulate the enhancer effect of STX16-ICR. However, arguing against this assumption, the STX16-ICR biallelically controls the expression of XLαs, which is located distal to NESP55. Another possibility is that CTCF in the proximal NESP55 promoter contributes to the enhancer-dependent activation of NESP55 transcription, as described previously for other loci43. While there is no clear CTCF ChIP-seq signal inside STX16-ICR, the interaction with GNAS may entail CTCF recruitment to nearby regions, given that CTCF multimerization typically contributes to chromatin loop formation44. Contrary to GNAS and other imprinted loci, imprinted transcripts from DLK1-DIO3 are regulated by a mechanism involving allele-specific enhancer activity38. Thus, locus-specific mechanisms likely underlie enhancer-mediated imprinting regulation.

The current study also revealed a differentiation-induced transition of imprinted gene expression from the GNAS locus, and the loss of STX16-ICR enhancer activity seems critical for this transition. In hESCs, NESP55 expression is exclusively maternal, whereas XLαs is expressed biallelically (Fig. 7A). Both transcripts are controlled by STX16-ICR. Upon differentiation, while NESP55 expression is nearly abolished, XLαs expression becomes exclusively paternal and independent of STX16-ICR (Fig. 7B). This imprinting status of GNAS in differentiated cells is important for phenotypes resulting from GNAS aberrations. Particularly, XLαs loss is associated with perinatal growth retardation and feeding difficulties after birth in humans and mice, and these phenotypes are only observed when the paternal XLαs copy is absent7,8,45,46, consistent with the paternal-only expression of XLαs in differentiated cells.

Two complementary mechanisms may explain the establishment of this differentiated cell-specific, paternally monoallelic XLαs expression. First, the biallelically active enhancer at STX16-ICR in hESCs becomes inactive upon differentiation; thus, XLαs expression is no longer dictated by this enhancer on either allele. Second, a somatic cell-specific cis-regulatory element(s) likely drives paternally monoallelic XLαs expression. Since the AS2 region shows far less than 50% methylation in PRT-like cells, the AS2 methylation level alone cannot explain exclusively paternal XLαs expression. Rather, another region(s) that is ~50% methylated, conceivably within AS1 or XLαs DMRs, is likely involved in the differentiated cell-specific, imprinted XLαs transcription.

The current study has some limitations. First, some differences in the GNAS methylation status have been reported among different hESC/hiPS cell lines19,47,48. Although the hESC line we used showed a normal differential methylation pattern at most GNAS DMRs, the imprinting status of GNAS-derived transcripts might be different in other cell lines, particularly in some hiPSCs with abnormal GNAS methylation patterns. Second, we could not specifically determine the putative cis-regulatory element that dictates monoallelic XLαs expression in differentiated cells. We also cannot exclude the possibility that AS2 still controls XLαs expression in some somatic cells because the maternal XLαs allele is transcribed in some bone marrow stromal cells49. Further studies are required to characterize the somatic cell-specific regulatory mechanism of XLαs. Lastly, enhancers may regulate more than one locus, as shown in other loci50. Since we did not investigate the interaction of STX16-ICR and gene loci other than GNAS, we cannot exclude the possibility that this enhancer also controls the transcription of additional genes.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that allele-specific chromatin conformations involving an early embryonic enhancer at STX16 regulate imprinted transcription from GNAS, shedding light on the pathogenic mechanism of diseases caused by genetic and epigenetic defects of GNAS. More broadly, our findings provide important insights into the regulation of allele-specific gene expression at imprinted loci.

The 3C assay was performed using a previously reported protocol with moodifications4,55. Four to five million hESCs were crosslinked in 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, and crosslinking was quenched in 125 mM glycine. Cells were lysed in the buffer (10 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl, 0.2% NP-40, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail) for 15 min on ice. After homogenization, nuclear pellets were collected by centrifugation at 2500 g for 5 min, and pellets were washed with 1× rCutSmart buffer (New England Biolabs). Chromatins were denatured at 65 °C for 10 min in the presence of 0.1% SDS, and after adding Triton-X at 1% final concentration, chromatins were digested with 1000 U of HindIII-HF (New England Biolabs) at 37 °C overnight. After heat inactivation of HindIII, the proximity ligation of digested chromatins was performed using 15-fold diluted chromatins and 5000 U/ml of T4 ligase (New England Biolabs) at 16 °C for 3 h in 1× T4 ligase buffer, 1% Triton-X, 0.1 mg/ml BSA, and 1 mM ATP. Chromatin was reverse-crosslinked by proteinase K at 65 °C overnight, and DNA was purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN).

Two BAC clones containing STX16-ICR and GNAS (RP11-1059P14 and RP11-984I1, respectively) were mixed in equimolar amounts, and PCR amplification efficiency of each primer set was calculated based on the Ct values obtained from serially diluted control samples. The relative crosslinking frequency at each amplicon was calculated based on the Ct values of the 3C samples. For 3C samples in which amplification was undetectable, we used 45 (maximum PCR cycle number) as a Ct value.
