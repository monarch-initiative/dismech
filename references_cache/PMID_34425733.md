# A rare canonical splice-site variant in VPS13B causes attenuated Cohen syndrome.
**Authors:** Daich Varela M, Motta FL, Webster AR, Arno G
**Journal:** Ophthalmic Genet (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1080/13816810.2021.1970194](https://doi.org/10.1080/13816810.2021.1970194)

## Content

BACKGROUND: To describe a patient with a history of obesity, retinal dystrophy, type II diabetes, and mild cognitive impairment; found to harbour biallelic splice-site variants in VPS13B.

MATERIALS & METHODS: A complete ophthalmic evaluation was performed at Moorfields Eye Hospital (London, United Kingdom), consisting of measurement of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), slit lamp and dilated fundus evaluation, colour, autofluorescence and near-infrared retinal imaging, spectral domain-optical coherence tomography, and electroretinogram (ERG). Whole-genome sequencing was performed as part of the UK's 100,000 Genomes Project.

RESULTS: A 26-year-old Pakistani man with normal appearance, stature, and head size presented with decreased BCVA and severely constricted visual fields to our Ophthalmic Genetics clinic. He had a history of obesity, type II diabetes, and mild cognitive impairment. His evaluation showed retina-wide, severe photoreceptor dysfunction in both eyes, with undetectable scotopic and photopic ERG waveforms. Genomic analysis identified a homozygous rare splice donor variant in the VPS13B gene (c.5024+2T>C) that was demonstrated to lead to skipping of the in-frame exon 31 (p.Gln1607_Ser1675delinsHis).

CONCLUSIONS: Exon 31 skipping in VPS13B may lead to a hypomorphic change, with partial gene function and an incomplete, mild Cohen syndrome-like phenotype.

Introduction 42 
Cohen syndrome (CS, MIM #216550) was first described in 1973 by M. Michael Cohen 43 
Jr. and colleagues in United States of America. 1 This multisystemic, autosomal 44 
recessive syndrome is characterized  by a cheerful disposition, slender limbs, 45 
leukopenia, recognisable craniofacial features, growth and developmental 46 
abnormalities, persistent hypotonia, enlarged corpus callosum, high myopia, and 47 
retinal dystrophy.2 However, broad clinical heterogeneity has been reported.  48 
At least 200 cases of CS have been reported to date, affecting populations worldwide 49 
such as Amish, European, Brazilian, Japanese, and Finnish.3–6 CS is overrepresented 50 
among the latter, with around 17% of all cases diagnosed in Finland. 3,7 Through 51 
linkage and haplotype analysis of affected Finnish individuals, the causative gene  of 52 
CS, VPS13B (MIM #607817; also called COH1) was discovered 30 years after its 53 
original description.8–10 The longest transcript of VPS13B (NM_017890) consists of 62 54 
exons and spans a genomic region of around 864 kb. 9 It encodes a 4022 amino acid 55 
residue Golgi apparatus transmembrane protein , which harbors two regions 56 
homologous to yeast vacuolar protein sorting -associated protein 13  (VPS13).11 It is 57 
widely expressed  and it has been associated with glycosylation and intracellular 58 
trafficking of proteins.12,13 It has been reported that impaired VPS13 function causes 59 
decreased neuritogenesis.11 60 
 61 
Over 230 pathogenic variants have already been described in VPS13B (Human Gene 62 
Mutation Database -HGMD- Professional 2020.4 - accessed on 20.02.2021), all of 63 
which are associated with variants of CS. Founder mutations have been identified in 64 
Amish ( c.8459T>C, c.9258_9259insT), Irish travelers ( c.4471G>T) and 65 
Greeks/Mediterranean (c.11564delA), besides the Finnish (c.3348_3349delCT).14  66 
Here, we present findings of an individual who was referred to our Ophthalmic 67 
Genetics clinic at Moorfields Eye Hospital for ev aluation due to retinal dystrophy and 68 
was found to have homozygous splice-site variants in VPS13B.  69

Clinical Report 91 
The proband was the third child of consanguineous parents (first cousins) of Pakistani 92 
descent. He was born at term after an uneventful pregnancy, with no malformations 93 
noted at birth and normal newborn hearing screening. At around age 4, his parents  94 
noticed he was tripping and having difficulties navigating in dim environments . An 95 
optometry evaluation revealed constricted visual fields  and further ocular exams led 96 
to the diagnosis of retinal dystrophy soon after.  97 
 98 
Growing up, mild cognitive impairment caused him to attend a special needs school, 99 
where he completed primary and high school education. He started struggling w ith 100 
obesity since late childhood  and had a gynecomastia surgery at age 21 . He was 101 
diagnosed with type II diabetes at age 22, which was managed with diet and 102 

 5 
metformin. There was no family history of eye disease and at the time of his evaluation, 103 
he was married and had an unaffected 4-year-old daughter. 104 
 105 
He first came to Moorfields Eye Hospital as a 26-year-old man with normal 106 
appearance, stature and head size  (Fig 1 A & B ). He was already registered sight 107 
impaired and reported decreasing central vision since teenage years. His BCVA was 108 
20/2000 (logMAR 1.8)  in the right eye (OD) and 20/80 (logMAR 0.6)  in the left eye  109 
(OS). His refractive error was o f mild myopia (spherical equivalent: -0.50 diopters), 110 
equal on both eyes (OU) . Confrontational visual field testing demonstrated 5-10 111 
degrees’ central fields, symmetric OU. His anterior segment exam  was positive for 112 
cortical blue do t lens opacities, visually non -significant. His p osterior segment 113 
assessment showed pale optic nerve heads, severe vessel attenuation and peripheral 114 
pigmentary changes  360 degrees OU  (Fig. 2A). Autofluorescence revealed foveal 115 
hypoautofluorescence on both eyes, being the right eye more severely affected ( Fig. 116 
2B). Macular OCT showed profound loss of the outer layers on the right eye and a 117 
bull’s eye pa ttern on the left eye, with decreased overall macular thickness  on both 118 
eyes(Fig. 2C). Electrophysiology testing was consistent with a generalized loss of rod 119 
and cone function. This was assessed by undetectable pattern, scotopic and photopic 120 
ERG OU. A full blood count showed normal platelet, red and white cell count.  121 
 122 
Over an 8-year follow up, his BCVA gradually decreased to hand movements OD and 123 
20/125 (logMAR 0.8) OS. His field of view constricted to below 5 degrees and he got 124 
registered severely sight impaired. He also developed posterior subcapsular cataracts 125 
OU and had phacoemulsification surgery OS, with posterior chamber intraocular lens 126 
implant. Given the extent of the retinal dystrophy OD, a lensectomy was not advis ed. 127 
Fundoscopy showed progressive and extensive retinal dystrophy affecting the majority 128 
of the fundus OU. No signs of diabetic retinopathy or macular edema were noticed at 129 
any point. 130

Discussion 147 
Given the clinical heterogeneity of CS and the vast pleiotropy of VPS13B, a delayed 148 
diagnosis commonly occurs . It has been postulated that CS may be a frequently 149 
underdiagnosed condition, mostly among individuals with unexplained developmental 150 
delay or intellectual disability.18,19  151 
Several attempts have been made towards delineating diagnostic criteria for CS. The 152 
Finnish group that first discovered VPS13B proposed eight major characteristics : 153 
developmental delay, microcephaly, typical facial gestalt, truncal obesity with slender 154 
extremities, overly sociable behavior, joint hypermobility, high myopia and/or retinal 155 
dystrophy, and neutropenia . They postulated that patients with six or more of these 156 
features should be categorized as true CS , while those with five or fewer could have 157 
“Cohen-like syndrome”. 20 Horn et al. proposed short stature  and hypotonia as two 158 
other major criteria.21 El Chehadeh et al. analyzed a cohort of 14 genetically confirmed 159 
CS patients and concluded that the features that should prompt VPS13B screening 160 
were chorioretinal dystrophy and neutropenia.22 Rodrigues et al . suggested thinking 161 
of CS in infants with microcephaly , early-onset hypotonia, neutropenia, a nd global 162 
developmental delay.23 Hennies et al. considered that the hallmarks of the condition 163 
were the typical facial gestalt, myopia, and  developmental delay. 6 Chandler et al. 164 
proposed learning difficulties, retinal dystrophy and neutropenia as strong clinical 165 
indicators for establishing a diagnosis.24 A consensus is yet to be built. 166 
 167 

 7 
Our patient presented with retinal dystrophy, obesity, type II diabetes and mild 168 
cognitive impairment. Th ese would only correspond to three features of CS (retinal 169 
dystrophy, obesity  and developmental delay) and classify him as a Cohen -like 170 
syndrome patient. Other d ifferential diagnoses  were indeed  considered for him: 171 
Bardet-Biedl syndrome (the absence of postaxial polydactyly and renal abnormalities 172 
was not typical), 25 Diabetes And Deafness, Maternally Inherited (MIDD; it does not 173 
present with cognitive impairment and the retinal phenotype corresponds to a pattern 174 
macular dystrophy , not a widespread photoreceptor d ysfunction as seen in this 175 
patient)26 and Prader -Willi syndrome (retinal dystrophy  is not a part of this 176 
syndrome).27  177 
Facial features of CS (thick hair and eyebrows, low hairline, high -arched and wave -178 
shaped eyelids, long and thick eyelashes, prominent nasal root, high and narrow 179 
palate, smooth or short philtrum, and prominent upper central incisors) have been 180 
reported absent in the past, and can vary with age and across ethnicities.3,5,28 Type II 181 
diabetes has been associated with CS, however it is still not considered a diagnostic 182 
criterion.29  183 
Some of the ophthalmic features reported in CS are progressive, high myopia (over 7 184 
diopters by the second decade of life), astigmatism, and chorioretinal dystrophy. The 185 
latter has been characterized as a cone–rod dystrophy (with a bull’s eye maculopathy 186 
pattern) that appears du ring the first decade of life and evolves to a generalized 187 
pigmentary retinopathy with the triad of vessel narrowing, bone spicules and pale optic 188 
discs by early adulthood. Children may complain of reduced acuity, night blindness 189 
and constricted field. 30 Macular edema and retinoschisis have also been described, 190 
as well as early cataracts.31–33  191 
Copy number variants (CNV) and particularly intragenic deletions have been reported 192 
as an important cause of CS. 34,35 We found that the rare homozygous splice -site 193 
variant in VPS13B led to exon 31 skipping.  This variant transcript is not likely to 194 
undergo nonsense mediated decay since there is not a reading frame shift and 195 
therefore no resultant premature termination codon. Thus, it is likely to lead to a mature 196 
protein lacking the 68 residues encoded by exon  31 (deletion of 69 residues and 197 
insertion of a histidine). We hypothe size that the mutant protein may retain some 198 
biological function because the functional domains of VPS13B are not lost due to 199 

 8 
skipping of exon 31 and the patient’s extra-ocular phenotype is mild compared to the 200 
biallelic loss of function (LOF) disease seen in typical CS patients. Thus, the protein 201 
may retain enough function to mitigate the impact on extra -ocular tissues. However, 202 
the true functional effect on the protein is yet to be eluc idated and the patient 203 
presented with a severe ophthalmic phenotype, therefore it is alternatively possible 204 
that a distinct role of VPS13B exists in the retina or that the skipped exon encodes a 205 
domain essential and specific to retinal function. 206 
Most of the patients with CS carry variants that result in premature termination.36 The 207 
pathogenicity of missense changes and exon skipping is yet to be clarified. The latter 208 
mechanism has been reported in VPS13B, associated with different phenotypes. The 209 
variant c.2934+1_2934+2delGT led to skipping of exon 20 (out of frame) and, in trans 210 
with an intragenic deletion , represents a biallelic LOF genotype causing a complete 211 
CS phenotype.36 The splice-site mutation c.6940+1G>T generat ed exon 38 skipping 212 
(out of frame) and (in compound heterozygosity with a frameshift deletion) was seen 213 
in a Chinese patient with developmental delay, obesity, high myopia and dysmorphic 214 
facial features.12 Interestingly, skipping of the in frame exon 57 in trans with a second 215 
splicing mutation  (c.5983+2dupT, shown to reduce transcript level) has been 216 
associated with a mild form of CS , showing only neutropenia and retinopathy. 37 217 
Gueneau et al. related the incomplete phenotype of this patient with a possible residual 218 
effect of VPS13B protein. Moreover, skipping of exons 8 to 15 (out of frame), 32 and 219 
33 (out of frame) resulted in a mild phenotype with intellectual disability and hypotonia 220 
in two young Japanese siblings.38  221 
In conclusion, we report an individual of Pakistani origin, homozygous for a rare splice-222 
site mutation in VPS13B that leads to in frame skipping of  exon 31. He presented with 223 
features from CS spectrum  (retinal dystrophy, developmental delay  and obesity), no 224 
other plausible variants to explain his phenotype were found in a survey of the virtual 225 
gene panel, and mild forms of CS have been reported in individuals with residual levels 226 
of VPS13B. Therefore, we propose VPS13B as the causative gene of his phenotype, 227 
possibly through a hypomorphic mechanism , and report an additional case in which 228 
exon skipping in VPS13B can lead to an attenuated syndrome. This case adds to the 229 
understanding of this complex gene and the delineation of  genotype-phenotype 230 

 9 
correlations. We suggest considering the possibility of biallelic non-LOF variants in 231 
VPS13B in patients whose disease partially fulfils CS phenotype.  232
