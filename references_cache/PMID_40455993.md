# A mouse model of Jansen's metaphyseal chondrodysplasia for investigating disease mechanisms and candidate therapeutics.
**Authors:** Höppner J, Firat D, Parvez-Khan M, Reyes M, Hanna P, Yadav PS, Dean T, Ramos-Torres KM, Brugarolas P, Collins MT, Wein MN, Liu S, Gellman SH, Schipani E, Kronenberg HM, Gardella TJ, Jüppner H
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.2500176122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2500176122)

## Content

Jansen's metaphyseal chondrodysplasia (JMC) is a rare disorder caused by activating mutations in the parathyroid hormone (PTH)/PTH-related peptide (PTHrP) receptor (PTH1R). Patients exhibit short stature, dysmorphic bones, and severe growth plate abnormalities, as well as hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, hypophosphatemia, and reduced plasma PTH levels. Humanized PTH1R (hPTH1R) mice expressing the H223R-hPTH1R JMC mutation die early without breeding. We therefore generated and characterized a stable mouse line expressing the T410R-hPTH1R allele, which confers a milder disease phenotype in patients. Mutant mice show near-normal longevity and reproductive capacity yet exhibit a profound skeletal phenotype characteristic of the disease. The long bones of T410R mice are markedly misshapen and have expanded metaphyses with disarrayed chondrocyte zones in growth plates and reduced primary spongiosa. PET/CT scanning revealed diminished uptake of [18F]-sodium fluoride in the growth plate area, consistent with reduced mineralization and vascularization. Genetic ablation of Hdac4 rescued the growth plate abnormalities in T410R mice, thereby establishing the PTH1R-Gαs-cAMP-PKA-SIK3-HDAC4/5 pathway as the main mediator of growth plate abnormalities in JMC. Serum calcium was elevated and endogenous PTH was suppressed in T410R mice, and both parameters could be normalized by acute injection of an optimized PTH inverse agonist peptide. The T410R mouse thus represents a stable animal model of JMC that recapitulates the abnormalities in skeletal development and mineral ion homeostasis which characterize this disease. The mice should help efforts to further define the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying the JMC phenotype and to develop a potential mode of therapy.

Skeletal development and longitudinal bone growth are highly coordinated processes that involve precisely timed cellular events occurring in primordial bone formation centers, such as the growth plates of long bones (1). The proliferation and maturation of chondrocytes in growth plates are regulated by a number of locally produced morphogenetic factors, including parathyroid hormone-related peptide (PTHrP) (2–4). PTHrP is produced by chondrocytes in the subarticular region of the growth plate and is thought to act in a concentration-dependent manner on more deeply embedded chondrocytes to promote their proliferation and to delay their maturation into hypertrophic cells, thereby preventing premature growth plate closure and ensuring proper bone elongation (1, 2, 5).

PTHrP exerts its effects via the parathyroid hormone (PTH)/PTHrP receptor (PTH1R), a class B G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) (6, 7). This receptor is abundantly expressed in prehypertrophic growth plate chondrocytes. PTH1R activation promotes chondrocyte proliferation and delays hypertrophic differentiation, in part by regulating transcription factors, such as HDAC4. In addition, PTH1R activation suppresses the expression of Indian hedgehog (Ihh), thereby delaying growth plate mineralization (8–10). As the growth plates expand and the distance between the source of PTHrP and chondrocytes expressing the PTH1R increases, these cells cease to proliferate, undergo hypertrophy and ultimately apoptosis, or become osteoblasts and thereby contribute to the formation of primary spongiosa and nascent bone tissue. Proper regulation of this process of chondrocyte differentiation is thus a key requirement for normal longitudinal bone growth.

In addition to the developing skeleton, the PTH1R is also highly expressed in cells of mature bone as well as kidney. Here, it transmits stimuli provided by the receptor’s second canonical ligand, PTH, to maintain mineral ion homeostasis. In distal renal tubule cells, PTH1R signaling promotes calcium reabsorption while in proximal renal tubules, it promotes phosphate excretion as well as the conversion of 25(OH) vitamin D into 1,25(OH)2‚ vitamin D, which, in turn, promotes intestinal calcium absorption (11, 12). In bone, PTH1R signaling activates osteoblastic cells and indirectly enhances formation and activity of osteoclastic cells to thereby promote bone formation and bone resorption, respectively. The PTH1R thus mediates the actions of two endogenous ligands, PTH and PTHrP, which both bind to the same receptor and activate Gαs / cAMP signaling yet play distinct biological roles (4).

Mutations in the PTH1R cause several human disorders affecting skeletal biology and mineral ion homeostasis. Inactivating PTH1R mutations cause primary failure of tooth eruption (PFE) (OMIM# 125350), if present on one allele, or Blomstrand lethal chondrodysplasia (BLC) (OMIM# 215045), if present on both alleles. Heterozygous activating PTH1R mutations cause Jansen’s metaphyseal chondrodysplasia (JMC) (OMIM# 156400), a rare autosomal dominant disorder found in about 20 patients worldwide and most notably characterized by impaired long bone development (13–16). In some cases, JMC is further associated with scoliosis, cranial bone abnormalities that can cause nerve entrapment and damage, and severe hypercalcemia and hypophosphatemia, which lead to osteomalacia as well as nephrocalcinosis and possibly chronic kidney disease (17–19). Currently, there is no effective treatment for JMC.

Five different PTH1R mutations have been identified as a cause of JMC, each of which affects one of three amino acid residues, His223, Thr410, or Ile458, that are all located at the cytoplasmic base of a transmembrane helix (H) in the receptor, namely H2, H6, or H7, respectively (14). In cell-based assays, these JMC mutations result in constitutive, agonist-independent cAMP signaling (15). In normal growth plate chondrocytes, the PTH1R acts through the cAMP-PKA-SIK3-HDAC4/5 pathway to promote cell proliferation and control differentiation (3, 20–23). Excessive signaling through this pathway is therefore predicted to result in the bone abnormalities seen in JMC patients; however, only few biopsies of JMC growth plates have been reported and hence histological features of the disease are still poorly defined, as are the underlying cellular abnormalities (24).

As a means to investigate the underlying mechanisms, we recently generated humanized PTH1R mice with the H223R mutation, the most frequent cause of JMC (17). Early mortality of these H223R mice and failure to breed, however, permitted only limited analyses of this prototype JMC model (25). Transgenic mice expressing the H223R-PTH1R in specific bone cell types are also available (26, 27), but they do not express the mutant allele in growth plate chondrocytes or kidneys. Consequently, these mice do not enable investigations into the effects of the mutant PTH1R in other key tissues and hence are of limited use in determining how a pharmacologic agent might work systemically when tested as a candidate therapeutic. Lack of a viable and accurate JMC mouse model has thus impeded investigation into the cellular and molecular mechanisms that drive the skeletal and renal defects that occur in patients and how they might be treated pharmacologically.

In humans, the T410R-PTH1R mutation is associated with a milder JMC phenotype, as compared to the H223R mutation, which is reflected by a lower level of constitutive cAMP signaling in transfected cells (15, 17). These observations prompted us to investigate whether introducing the T410R mutation into our humanized PTH1R (hPTH1R) mice (28) could lead to a stable JMC model mouse line. As reported herein, we find that T410R-hPTH1R mice grow and breed normally and display phenotypic changes that are fully consistent with the human disease (17). The bones of these T410R mice thus exhibit profoundly expanded growth plates with enlarged zones of proliferative chondrocytes and reduced areas of hypertrophic chondrocytes and primary spongiosa. Serum calcium levels are elevated, while serum phosphate and PTH levels are decreased in the T410R mice. We also find that the growth plate abnormalities in T410R mice can be corrected by genetic Hdac4 ablation, a finding consistent with the notion that overactivity of the cAMP-PKA-SIK3-HDAC4/5 pathway underlies the skeletal phenotype in JMC. We further show that a single injection of an optimized PTH inverse agonist (PTH-IA) peptide (27, 29–32) reduces serum calcium and increases serum PTH levels in T410R mice. The T410R mice thus provide a robust platform for studying JMC pathogenesis and testing candidate agents as potential therapies.

To develop a mouse model of JMC (25–27), we altered two nucleotides in our humanized mice expressing the hPTH1R. By improved-Genome editing via Oviductal Nucleic Acids Delivery (i-GONAD) the ACG triplet encoding threonine 410 (T410) was replaced with AGA encoding arginine (R410) (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) (33, 34), which furthermore generates the sequence TCTAGA that is recognized by the endonuclease XbaI. Guide RNA, repair template, and recombinant Cas9 were injected into zygotes carried by wild-type (wt) CD1 female mice that had been mated approximately 16 h earlier with homozygous hPTH1R males (28), as described previously (25). PCR amplification and sequencing of genomic DNA from F0 pups using primers specific for the human PTH1R allele confirmed the expected T410R mutation in 1 of 9 and 1 of 4 male pups, respectively, from two independent i-GONAD procedures. One pup, however, died for unknown reasons. The remaining male pup was subsequently mated with WT hPTH1R females (28). Compared to WT-hPTH1R littermates (hereafter, WT mice), heterozygous T410R-hPTH1R offspring (hereafter, T410R mice) showed near-normal weight gain and comparable sizes, and no differences in life expectancy were observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). T410R mice, however, developed skeletal deformities, including kinking of the spine and an abnormal gait pattern, which became visible after about 6 wk of life and worsened progressively with age. T410R males and females were fertile and produced litters comparable in size to those of WT hPTH1R females, with the mutant allele transmitted at the expected Mendelian frequency. This allowed establishment of a stable breeding line.

Over the first two months of life, T410R mice developed pronounced distension of the physis, particularly in the distal femur and proximal tibia, as observed via µCT imaging at postnatal days P12, P37, and P65 (Fig. 1B). Histological analysis and RNA in situ hybridization of 3-d-old mice (P3) revealed significantly expanded zones of Col2a1-positive chondrocytes in T410R animals compared to WT littermates, which is consistent with increased chondrocyte proliferation. In addition, Ihh expression was altered and did not form the well-defined layer of prehypertrophic cells encountered in WT growth plates (Fig. 2).

In older T410R mice (P65), the growth plates were even further expanded and consisted predominantly of proliferative chondrocytes, as defined by the abundance of Col2a1-expressing cells. Col10a1-positive hypertrophic chondrocytes were found only in a few isolated regions near the bone collar perichondrium in bones of T410R mice, whereas they formed a defined narrow zone across the growth plate in bones of WT mice (Fig. 2). Overall, these findings revealed a disrupted growth plate architecture in T410R mice, which pointed to a defect in the processes that regulate the maturation of chondrocytes from the proliferative phase into the hypertrophic phase during postnatal life. The expanded proliferative chondrocyte zone, the reduction of Ihh expression, and the progressive growth plate expansion align with a mechanism involving excessive PTH1R signaling and are consistent with our prior findings in growth plates of H223R-hPTH1R mice (25).

In addition to the characteristic skeletal and growth plate abnormalities, JMC patients typically exhibit blood biochemical marker profiles that are similar to those seen in patients with primary hyperparathyroidism, yet without elevated blood PTH levels. Consistent with these findings in the human disease, T410R mice displayed elevated total serum calcium levels compared to WT mice, which were accompanied by significantly decreased serum phosphate levels and suppressed endogenous PTH(1-84) levels (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Serum markers of bone formation (P1NP) or bone resorption (CTX-1) were not different for T410R and WT mice.

We next assessed bone metabolism in vivo utilizing [18F]-sodium fluoride (NaF)-positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT). This imaging modality can be used for diagnosing disorders of mineralization and ossification as well as for monitoring treatment and disease progression (35–40) as the [18F]-NaF tracer binds to newly formed hydroxyapatite, hence specifically reflecting osteoblastic activity (41–43). As shown in Fig. 4, within minutes after tracer i.p. injection, 4-wk-old WT animals showed strong [18F]-NaF uptake in the immediate region of the growth plates, as particularly evident in the proximal tibiae. This result is consistent with specific uptake in the primary spongiosa, which is an area of active bone formation. Little or no uptake was detected in areas of either cortical or medullary bone, consistent with a relatively low rate of matrix deposition in these regions. In T410R littermates, [18F]-NaF uptake was barely detectable in growth plates of proximal tibiae, and only a weak signal was observed in the periphery of the growth plates of these mice. These findings are in line with a reduced rate of bone mineralization in T410R mice due to impaired maturation of chondrocytes from the proliferative to the hypertrophic state and the subsequent formation of primary spongiosa. Consistent with impaired growth plate chondrocyte hypertrophy and hence presumably reduced secretion of proangiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (44), immunohistochemistry (IHC) for Endomucin, an endothelial cell marker, revealed reduced vascularization adjacent to the growth plate in T410R mice as compared to their WT littermates (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Overall, these findings reflect the importance of chondrocytes undergoing hypertrophy for allowing proper vascular invasion as well as formation of primary spongiosa and support the notion that osteoblasts can derive from chondrocytes (45–48).

Constitutive cAMP generation by the PTH1R JMC mutants, as observed in vitro, is hypothesized to drive the skeletal phenotype observed in patients. However, direct evidence linking excess signaling downstream of the mutant PTH1R to the JMC phenotype has not been established. In the normal growth plate, PTH1R activation stimulates the Gαs/PKA signaling pathway, which results in phosphorylation and subsequent inactivation of salt-inducible kinase 3 (SIK3), leading in turn to reduced phosphorylation of SIK3 substrates, most notably histone deacetylase 4 (HDAC4). As a result of dephosphorylation, HDAC4 translocates to the nucleus where it suppresses the transcription of Mef2 and Runx2, two critical regulators of hypertrophic chondrocyte differentiation (3, 20, 22, 23, 49). To investigate whether growth plate changes in T410R-hPTH1R mice are caused by excessive activation of the PKA–SIK3–HDAC4 pathway, we crossed T410R mice with mice harboring an Hdac4 null allele (exon 6 deletion) (50).

As shown in Fig. 5, Hdac4 ablation partially restored the growth plate phenotype in 3-d-old T410R mice, and this effect was gene-dose dependent. Thus, heterozygous Hdac4 deletion partially reduced the size of the growth plates in T410R mice, while biallelic Hdac4 deletion restored the growth plate to its normal architecture. Interestingly, in the absence of HDAC4 (Hdac4−/−) the growth plates of T410R mice appeared more expanded than those of WT-PTH1R littermate control mice. Although HDAC4 is the primary downstream target of PTH1R signaling, HDAC5 can partially compensate for the loss of HDAC4 (3). Enhanced HDAC5 activation by constitutive T410R-hPTH1R signaling could therefore explain the phenotypic differences observed between WT and T410R mice when each carries the homozygous Hdac4 deletion.

Previously, we demonstrated that a backbone-modified PTHrP(7–36)-based inverse agonist (IA) peptide, incorporating both α and β amino acids (α/β-PTH-IA, also referred to as peptide 3; SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), exhibited efficacy in young (~P3−P22) transgenic mice expressing the H223R-PTH1R in osteoblastic cells under the control of the collagen type 1a1 promoter (C1HR mice) (31). This α/β-PTH-IA thus significantly reduced the accumulation of trabecular bone mass in C1HR mice after three weeks of once-daily injection, while a previously evaluated all-α amino acid PTH-IA peptide yielded similar effects on bone in C1HR mice (P7−P24) only with twice-daily administration (27). A limitation of the transgenic C1HR mice, however, is that they express the H223R-PTH1R mutant almost exclusively in bone-forming osteoblasts and therefore do not recapitulate the full spectrum of skeletal and renal abnormalities seen in JMC patients, including hypercalcemia, hypophosphatemia, and suppressed PTH levels. We therefore assessed whether humanized T410R mice would enable the detection of changes in these disease-related parameters of mineral ion homeostasis upon injection with the α/β-PTH-IA.

When tested in vitro, the modified PTH-IA significantly reduced basal as well as PTH(1-34)-stimulated cAMP formation in HEK293 cells expressing the WT or the T410R-PTH1R mutant. The potency of the α/β-PTH-IA as an inverse agonist and as an antagonist was about ninefold greater on the T4101R-PTH1R (and the H223R-PTH1R) mutant than on the WT-PTH1R (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B–F). Comparable shifts in binding affinity had been previously observed when JMC mutants and the WT-PTH1R were evaluated in radioligand competition assays with PTH-IA peptides containing all α-amino acids (32). These findings could potentially be due to lower surface expression levels of the mutant PTH1Rs and/or alterations in receptor conformation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F).

The α/β-PTH-IA peptide was then tested in T410R mice on day 55 of life (baseline). At this age, T410R mice exhibited significantly elevated ionized calcium (iCa2+) levels compared to WT mice (Fig. 6A). A single subcutaneous (s.c.) injection of the α/β-PTH-IA (500 nmol/kg) caused significant reductions in blood ionized Ca2+ levels, as compared to the levels at baseline, in both WT and T410R mice at 4 h postinjection, but the magnitude of the decrease was more pronounced in T410R mice. Recovery to baseline iCa2+ levels occurred faster in WT mice (at 6 h postinjection) than in T410R mice (at 8 h postinjection) (Fig. 6B).

We further examined serum PTH levels at 5 h after injection. Consistent with previous findings, baseline serum PTH levels were suppressed in T410R mice compared to WT mice that were both treated with vehicle. Injection of the α/β-PTH-IA significantly increased serum PTH levels in both WT and T410R mice (Fig. 6C).

In this study, we generated and characterized a JMC model mouse that has normal long-term survival and can breed, thus allowing for the establishment of a stable JMC knock-in mouse line. The overall phenotype of T410R mice faithfully recapitulates key disease hallmarks of JMC, including misshapen bones and high-normal blood calcium levels with low PTH. This is in contrast to previously reported mice that recapitulate only limited aspects of JMC, either because of tissue-specific expression of the H223R mutant (27, 31, 51, 52) or because humanized H223R-hPTH1R mice had a reduced lifespan and were unable to breed (25). T410R mice will now enable longer-term studies of the cellular and molecular mechanisms, thus allowing a comprehensive assessment of the disease pathologies that define JMC. Importantly, the robust phenotype of T410R mice, involving both bone and mineral ion abnormalities, provides a useful setting to evaluate candidate therapeutics for efficacy in vivo.

The T410R mice exhibited progressive skeletal abnormalities that affected particularly the growth plates. These include a profound expansion of the zone of Col2-positive proliferative chondrocytes, a substantial reduction in number and an abnormal spatial distribution of Col10-positive hypertrophic chondrocytes, as well as a profoundly diminished area of primary spongiosa and associated mineralized tissue. These findings align with a model by which elevated constitutive cAMP signaling by the mutant PTH1R impairs the transition of chondrocytes from the proliferative phase to the hypertrophic state, which ultimately leads to noticeable reductions in bone mineralization and distortions in bone morphology. We showed that Hdac4 ablation rescues the abnormal growth plate phenotype in T410R mice in a gene-dose-dependent manner, thus establishing the cAMP-PKA-SIK3-HDAC4/5 pathway as a central driver of the skeletal pathogenesis of JMC. These results also suggest that HDAC4 or 5 may be a potential therapeutic target for JMC, although the broad expression of these two proteins brings concern of off-target effects. Nevertheless, class IIa HDAC inhibitors have been developed (53, 54) and could prove helpful, for example, in further assessing the underlying disease mechanisms in the current T410R mice.

Beyond skeletal abnormalities, T410R mice exhibit biochemical features consistent with elevated PTH1R signaling in both bone and kidney, including hypercalcemia, hypophosphatemia, and suppressed endogenous PTH levels, thus closely mimicking key features of the JMC phenotype. Importantly, we demonstrated that a single injection of a PTH inverse agonist (α/β-PTH-IA, also referred to as peptide 3) (31) reduced blood calcium levels while increasing blood PTH levels in T410R mice. These findings highlight the potential of the T410R model for efficiently evaluating candidate therapies for JMC in vivo. In contrast to previous studies with a PTH-IA comprising only α-amino acids which required twice-daily injections for three weeks, Peptide 3 in C1HR mice demonstrated similar partial correction of bone mass parameters, with only once-daily injections (27, 31). The T410R mice, which expresses the mutant receptor in physiologically relevant tissues (including growth plates, bone, and kidney), thus enable screening of candidate PTH-IA analogs for efficacy in vivo using an acute, single-injection protocol.

We used heterozygous T410R-hPTH1R mice in our injection experiments, so it is possible that a component of the reductions in blood ionized Ca2+ that we observed following PTH-IA injection involved inhibitory actions at the WT-hPTH1R allele. While we cannot exclude this possibility, it seems unlikely given that the endogenous PTH levels were significantly suppressed in the T410R mice and thus signaling activity of the WT-hPTH1R is expected to be very low (32). In addition, our in vitro data indicate that inverse agonist potency is higher on the JMC receptors than on the WT-PTH1R. Reductions in ionized Ca2+ levels obtained in the T410R mice after a single PTH-IA injection lasted for 6 to 8 h, after which levels returned to the mildly elevated baseline state. Normalization of blood Ca2+ levels throughout the day is an important therapeutic goal for JMC patients. Improving the potency and bioavailability of inverse agonists could help advance the goal of achieving sustained calcium normalization when treating JMC patients. Correcting the bone and growth plate abnormalities also stands as an important goal of JMC therapy, which the T410R mice should also help addressing, although not pursued in the current study.

Taken together, our findings represent a proof-of-concept step in the overall effort to derive a therapy for JMC and reinforce the feasibility of using an inverse agonist to suppress in vivo the constitutive signaling of a disease-causing mutant PTH1R and to advance our understanding of the underlying disease mechanisms. Our findings in the T410R mice indicate that it is possible to develop therapeutics targeting a constitutively active PTH1R.

Vega et al. previously reported that deletion of Exon 6 of the HDAC allele resulted in a loss-of-function of the gene (50). Therefore, we utilized i-GONAD as described earlier. Hdac4 ablation was achieved by two guide RNAs targeted against genomic DNA within the gene, upstream and downstream of exon 6 (Hdac4 guide 1: 5’-tttcagcaacagtcaccatatcataag-3’; Hdac4 guide 2: 5’ tttatgttgctcttagagtcgtttcat-3’). PCR amplification and subsequent Sanger sequencing (foreward primer: 5’-ttgctgatgcctagtggaagg-3’; reverse primer: 5’-tggaggggttctgttgtccta-3’) from F0 i-GONAD offspring pups yielded three different deletion sizes (1589 bp, 1910 bp, and 2128 bp), all deleting the entire exon 6 of the Hdac4 gene. Mice with the 2128 bp deletion were selected for further mating. Consistent with the report by Vega et al. mice homozygous for the deletion of exon 6 died postnatally within the 5 d of life.
