In this study, we discuss a female patient referred to cardiology with left ventricular hypertrophy at mid-ventricular segments resulting in a mid-cavitary obstruction and a left ventricular apical aneurysm. The patient had normal epicardial coronary arteries, but presented with recurrent cerebrovascular events. The patient had a positive family history for sudden cardiac death. Cardiac MRI detected positive features of left ventricular mid-cavity obstruction, left ventricular apical aneurysm and delayed gadolinium enhancement, with Holter monitoring assessment displaying segments of non-sustained ventricular tachycardia. Genetic analysis detected an myosin light chain 3 (MYL3) gene mutation. The patient will be referred to receive an implantable cardioverter defibrillator.

TheMYL3gene mutation is a rare variant in patients with familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. To our knowledge, the presence of a left ventricular apical aneurysm has not been previously reported in literature concerning theMYL3gene mutation. The presence of this abnormality further increases the risk of sudden cardiac death.

Keywords:cardiovascular medicine, arrhythmias, genetics, genetic screening / counselling

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is one of the most common inherited cardiomyopathies with an estimated prevalence of 1:500.1The disease is characterised by an unexplained left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy accompanied by a non-dilated left ventricle in the initial stages. HCM is a heterogeneous autosomal dominant disorder with variable penetrance of expression.2Although the pronounced genetic heterogeneity of the condition has complicated efforts to understand its genetic aetiology, ongoing molecular genetic studies have led to the detection of established mutations in the causal genes. The two genes in which most mutations have been described are the β-myosin heavy chain (MYH7) gene and the myosin binding protein C (MYBPC3) gene. WhileMYH7andMYBPC3exhibit the greatest prevalence among the causal genes, mutations in the α-tropomyosin (TPM1), cardiac α-actin (ACTC1) and myosin light chain 3 (MYL3) genes have also been associated with HCM. TheMYL3gene encodes the myosin essential light chain (ELC) of the sarcomere,3whereby the ELC has two isoforms expressed in fast skeletal muscle: a short isoform and a long isoform. Cardiac musculature has been shown to only exhibit the long isoform, and studies have postulated the role of ELC as a modulator of crossbridge kinetics and optimum force production.

A female patient with a background of HCM with associated mid-cavitary obstruction and LV apical aneurysm has been under cardiology follow-up for a few years. Her coronary angiogram had revealed normal epicardial coronary arteries. The patient has a positive family history for occurrence of sudden cardiac death (SCD), with her deceased son having expressed similar phenotypic features.

The patient has experienced multiple cerebrovascular accidents (CVAs). The CVAs are characterised by the onset of a transient ischaemic attack with associated retinal artery occlusion. This is followed by a right-focused hemiparesis in another tertiary centre, with an infarct in the left gangliocapsular region determined to be the underlying cause. Long-term anticoagulation was initiated 2 years later when the patient was admitted to a specialist stroke unit for a left corona radiata infarct. The pathophysiology of the multiple CVAs were deemed likely to be of cardioembolic origin. Concurrent extra-axial haemorrhage extension into the posterior fossa, potentially secondary to long-term anticoagulation, was also observed.

A couple of years later, she was admitted with arrhythmic disturbances consistent with atrial flutter and atrial fibrillation. The atrial fibrillation proved to be refractory to pharmacological and direct current cardioversion.

In the most recent admission, the patient presented with complaints of increasing orthopnoea and associated pedal oedema. Serum brain natriuretic peptide was recorded at 4394.00 pg/mL, and repeat transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed an ejection fraction (EF) of 50%, mild LV systolic dysfunction, mid-cavity gradient of 36 mm Hg with no evidence of LV outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) or systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve. She was diagnosed with New York Heart Association class III congestive cardiac failure and treated with guideline-directed therapy for heart failure.

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples using QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and subsequently quantified with Qubit fluorometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). As per preparation, 200 ng of Qubit quantified DNA was fragmented into 180–220 bp inserts. The corresponding fragments were end-repaired, adenylated and ligated with indexed adapters. In order to generate the gDNA library, the adapter-ligated fragments were amplified with adapter-specific primers along with size selection and purification. The acquired gDNA library was hybridised to the xGen Exome Research Panel v1.0 (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, Iowa, USA). The libraries were then captured using Streptavidin M270 beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and washed to clear unbound DNA molecules. The libraries then underwent amplification and purification, with the final preparation analysed for fragment size distribution with the use of Tape Station (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). The libraries were quantified with Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq 4000 (illumina, San Diego, California, USA) in order to produce 100 bp paired-end sequences. Burrows-Wheeler Aligner-MEM was used to align the preprocessed reads to the reference human genome (hg19) that was procured from the University of California Santa Cruz database. The Sentieon version of Genome Analysis Toolkit was applied for read processing, and variant calling steps after which the aligned reads were sorted and duplicates were removed. Base quality score recalibration was conducted with known variants derived from the Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database (v149) while germline variant calling was implemented with the Haplotyper command in Sentieon. Identified variants were annotated using the Variation and Mutation Annotation Toolkit pipeline with the Variant Effect Predictor program and Ensembl database. The pipeline process included the annotation of variants into recognised variants available in public databases (The Exome Aggregation Consortium, 1000G, the Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database, the Genome Aggregation Database, Exome Variant Server, 1000 Japanese Genome and MedGenome-Germline Variant Database). The coverage of the cardiomyopathy panel genes is available within the supplementary material (online supplemental appendix 1). Furthermore, pathogenicity classes were determined using pathogenictity prediction programs (Polymorphism Phenotyping, Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant, Likelihood Ratio Test, MutationTaster).4

TTE identified biatrial dilation, LV hypertrophy predominantly in the mid-ventricular segments, mid-cavitary rest gradient of 33 mm Hg, mild-to-moderate LV systolic dysfunction, grade II diastolic dysfunction and no evidence of LVOTO or SAM of the mitral valve at rest. Subsequent cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging modality assessment detected an EF of 49% with positive morphologies of mid-cavitary HCM, obliterated in systole with flow acceleration in the LV apical aneurysm and diffuse delayed gadolinium enhancement (figures 1 and 2) (videos 1 and 2). Further, it is pertinent to note that the patient never exhibited symptoms of syncope or presyncope in her clinical presentation narrative. Holter analysis revealed sinus rhythm with runs of non-sustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT).

DNA analysis using targeted gene capture revealed a heterozygous missense variation in exon 4 of theMYL3gene characterised by an amino acid substitution of glutamic acid being replaced with lysine at codon 152 (c.454G>A variant). Segregation analysis showed the observed variant in the patient’s surviving son, who did not express the associated phenotype.

Thus, consideration of all these features led to the primary differential diagnosis of HCM withMYL3gene mutation.

Risk stratification for an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) was conducted due to the presence of an LV apical aneurysm, NSVT, LVSD, late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on CMR and SCD in first-degree relatives. Accordingly, a decision was made for recommendation of an automatic ICD for the purpose of primary prevention of SCD.

Familial HCM associated with theMYL3gene mutation is known to be a rare variant in comparison with mutations affiliated with theMYH7andMYBPC3genes (table 1). A study undertaken by Kaskiet alreported that mutations inMYL3, TNNT2, ACTCandTNNI3each accounted for less than 5% of the cases involving HCM expressed in preadolescent children.5In the Kaski study, one patient that was diagnosed with HCM tested positive for theMYL3variant. Unfortunately, no further details regarding the specific morphology of the LV hypertrophy were provided.

TheMYL3variant has not been referenced in the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project, which constitutes approximately 6500 individuals of European and African American heritage.6The variant has also not been detected in the ExAC Aggregation Consortium (ExAC) database, 1000 Genomes database and gnomAD database. The low prevalence of theMYL3variant indicates that this is not a common benign variant.

TheMYL3variant is characterised by a non-conservative amino acid substitution and, therefore, likely to affect properties such as the polarity, size and charge of the secondary protein structure. Further, amino acid substitution occurs at the calcium binding EF-hand domain which is generally conserved across species. In silico mutational analysis using prediction software, like SIFT, Polyphen-2, MutationTaster2 and LRT, illustrates a damaging impact that will adversely affect protein function.

Poetteret alnoted an infrequent phenotypic subtype characterised by significant hypertrophy expressed by the cardiac papillary muscles in conjunction with adjacent ventricular involvement, resulting in a mid-cavity obstruction.7Olsonet alreported a similar phenotype expressed by multiple family members exhibiting theMYL3Glu143Lys variant. Structural assessments of one particular individual highlighted mid-cavitary LV hypertrophy with mild dynamic obstruction noted on systole along with further features denoting a restrictive physiology. A particular point of interest was the dynamic progression in peak intracavitary LV gradient from 16 to 41 mm Hg across a span of 2 years. This reported feature demonstrates the potential for dynamic progression of the condition and may well indicate consideration of serial morphological measurements of the structures. As per TTE and CMR modalities, the proband expressed structural abnormalities consistent with a mid-cavity obstructive phenotype and an LV apical aneurysm. Serial TTE assessments across a 3-year follow-up displayed no further advancements in the intracavitary gradient and an absence in LVOTO. These features demonstrate a similarity in the expressed phenotypes of the referred cases mentioned above. All three of the phenotypes exhibited mid-cavitary obstruction in conjunction with LV hypertrophy with no evidence of LVOTO. However, significant differences were also noted with the two other cases referred from literature not presenting any evidence of an LV apical aneurysm.

Maronet aldescribes LV apical aneurysms as an under-recognised subset of the HCM spectrum with a reported 2% prevalence in the patient group reviewed. Obstructive atherosclerotic coronary artery disease and left anterior descending coronary artery stenosis were excluded as a cause of LV aneurysm formation. Genetic analysis of the set revealed no positive mutations in theMYL3gene. The development of LV apical aneurysm may predispose patients to complications involving ventricular tachyarrhythmias, thromboembolisations and ventricular rupture in the context of immature aneurysms.

The presence of an LV apical aneurysm in the presence of anMYL3mutation has not been previously reported in available literature. This is significant given the clinical context of this patient in an SCD perspective along with their history of repeated CVAs.

Segregation analysis was conducted on family members with the asymptomatic younger son of the proband testing positive for a heterozygous expression of the mutation, and the asymptomatic brother of the proband testing negative. Given the pathogenic nature of this variant, there is a possibility that the asymptomatic son who is a heterozygous carrier of the variant may develop phenotypic features at a later stage of his life. Accordingly, serial follow-ups will be conducted to detect early expression of the physical manifestations of the variant.

In view of the multitude of high-risk features—including the presence of SCD in first degree relatives, NSVT, significant LGE and LVSD—an LV apical aneurysm will be an important variable for consideration in long-term management of HCM. The presence of these high-risk factors for SCD is an indication for primary prevention of SCD via implantation of an ICD.

TheMYL3variant is a rare variant reported in literature with previously reported phenotypic expressions of mid-cavity obstruction in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

TheMYL3gene mutation may predispose individuals to a left ventricular apical aneurysm in addition to previously reported phenotypic features of left ventricular hypertrophy with associated mid-cavitary obstruction, further amplifying the risk of sudden cardiac death.

It is essential to conduct a comprehensive genotype and phenotype assessment in the presence of risk factors contributing to adverse complications, including heart failure and sudden cardiac death.

Cascade screening is imperative due to the potential presence of risks factors leading to sudden cardiac death.
