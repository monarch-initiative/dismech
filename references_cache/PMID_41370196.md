# Clinical characteristics, management, and hematopoietic cell transplantation of patients with TLR8 gain-of-function.
**Authors:** Arnold DE, Kaviany S, Aluri J, Calvo KR, Mehta SS, Tobin JM, Schuettpelz LG, Saucier N, Schmitz EG, Murguía-Favela L, Wright NAM, De Ravin SS, Rao VK, Holland SM, Bleesing JJ, Loughran TP Jr, Feith DJ, Powell J, Ratan A, Warren JT, Pai SY, Bednarski JJ, Connelly JA, Cooper MA
**Journal:** Blood Adv (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1182/bloodadvances.2025016338](https://doi.org/10.1182/bloodadvances.2025016338)

## Content

Toll-like receptor 8 (TLR8) gain-of-function (GOF) somatic variants were recently identified as causing severe neutropenia, lymphoproliferation, and immune dysregulation. We report the expanded clinical and laboratory phenotype and management of 10 patients, including the original cohort of 6 male patients. We identify the first female patient with TLR8 GOF who presented during infancy with pure red cell aplasia due to a germ line TLR8 variant, and 2 new disease-causing somatic variants in male patients. Eight patients had somatic mosaicism with peripheral blood variant allele fractions of 7% to 26%, and age of disease onset of 9 months to 28 years. All patients had neutropenia, most with severe neutropenia refractory to medical therapy. Anemia and thrombocytopenia were common. Bone marrow characteristically demonstrated severe myeloid hypoplasia and activated T-cell infiltrates and/or aggregates. An increased number of large granular lymphocytes (LGLs) was identified in 5 patients. Seven patients underwent allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT). High rates of post-HCT cytopenia of unclear etiology and graft-versus-host disease were observed. Five patients are surviving at 1 to 3 years after HCT with full donor myeloid and T-cell chimerism, and resolution of disease phenotype. The 2 patients who presented during childhood and did not undergo HCT ultimately died from disease. In conclusion, TLR8 GOF is an X-linked dominant disorder that should be considered in male and female patients with cytopenia, particularly severe neutropenia, lymphoproliferation with immune dysregulation, increased LGLs, and new to this cohort, red cell aplasia. Disease is refractory to medical management, and curative, allogeneic HCT should be considered early after diagnosis. This trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as #NCT04339777.

Toll-like receptor 8 (TLR8) is an endosomal pattern recognition receptor primarily expressed by myeloid cells that recognizes microbial single-stranded RNA degradation products, with activation ultimately resulting in the production of NF-κB–dependent cytokines and proinflammatory signature.1,2 Novel missense variants in the TLR8 gene, located on the short arm of chromosome X (Xp22.2), were recently identified in 6 unrelated male patients with severe neutropenia, lymphoproliferation, and immune dysregulation, known as INFLTR8 (inflammation, neutropenia, bone marrow failure, and lymphoproliferation caused by TLR8).3 Of note, 5 of the 6 patients had genetic mosaicism with whole blood variant allele fractions (VAFs) <30%, and variants were detected in multiple tissues, including skin fibroblasts. All TLR8 variants led to a gain-of-function (GOF) of the encoded protein, with patient-derived cells exhibiting hypersensitivity to ligand stimulation, resulting in increased NF-κB activation and cytokine production. The neutropenia was refractory to granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) and corticosteroids, and 3 patients underwent allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplant (HCT). However, details of transplant characteristics and outcomes were lacking, providing little guidance on how best to proceed with these patients.

We provide here an extensive description of the clinical and laboratory phenotype in 10 patients with TLR8 GOF variants, including the previously reported 6 patients. The first female patient with a germ line TLR8 pathogenic variant is reported, as well as a patient with somatic mosaicism who presented during adulthood. Seven of the patients underwent allogeneic HCT. Finally, we investigated several potential TLR inhibitors on patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) cells differentiated to macrophages to identify a potential adjunctive therapy to allogeneic HCT.

Ten patients with genetic variants identified in the TLR8 gene resulting in GOF were included. The 6 previously reported patients correspond to patients 1 to 6 here (Table 1).3 Written informed consent was obtained for all participants at their home institutions, and these studies were approved by the institutional review boards. Patient 8 underwent HCT on a clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04339777). Deidentified clinical and laboratory data were collected from participating centers. Severe neutropenia was defined as absolute neutrophil count (ANC) <500 cells per μL. Mild, moderate, and severe anemia were defined as hemoglobin >10 mg/dL to lower limit of normal, 8 to 10 mg/dL, and <8 mg/dL, respectively. Mild, moderate, and severe thrombocytopenia were defined as platelet counts 100 × 109/L to 150 × 109/L, 50 × 109/L to 100 × 109/L, and <50 × 109/L, respectively. Splenomegaly was classified as mild if noted on imaging or just palpable on examination, moderate if extending to the umbilicus, and severe if extending past the umbilicus. HCT-comorbidity index and Immune Deficiency and Dysregulation Activity (IDDA2.1) scores were calculated per published guidelines.4,5 Neutrophil recovery after HCT was defined as the first of 3 consecutive days with a peripheral blood ANC ≥500 cells per μL, and platelet recovery was defined as the first day with a platelet count ≥20 × 109/L without transfusion support for 7 consecutive days. Primary graft failure was defined as lack of neutrophil recovery or neutrophil recovery with donor chimerism <5% by day +42. Secondary graft failure was defined as loss of donor cells with detection of <5% donor chimerism in whole blood after initial neutrophil recovery. New variants identified in patients 7 and 8 (p.P432Q and p.P432R) or reported in a family with anemia (p.V434L)8 were tested using HEK-Blue Null1 NF-κB reporter cells (InvivoGen) lacking TLR8 expression as previously described.3 Briefly, reporter cells (Lipofectamine; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) were transiently transfected with 500 ng (or indicated concentration) of wild-type (WT) or variant TLR8 plasmid, and 48 hours after transfection, cells were stimulated with TL8-506 (InvivoGen) for 18 hours. NF-κB transcriptional activity was measured by quantifying secreted alkaline phosphatase by QUANTI-BLUE assay (InvivoGen). Fold change in secreted alkaline phosphatase activity was calculated by normalizing data to untransfected wells without addition of TL8-506. Statistical analysis was performed by 1-way analysis of variance with P < .05 considered as significant. The frequencies of the germ line and some somatic TLR8 variants were verified by droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) as previously described.3

The STEMdiff Hematopoietic Kit (05310, Stemcell Technologies, Inc) was used to differentiate patient-derived iPSCs expressing either WT or p.P432L TLR8 GOF into CD34+ hematopoietic progenitor cells. Macrophages were differentiated from patient iPSC-derived CD34s using the Myeloid Expansion Supplement II (Stemcell Technologies).3

Patient iPSC-derived macrophages with WT TLR8 or p.P432L variant were plated at a density of 50 000 cells in a 96-well plate with or without previous incubation with 5μM CU-CPT9a (InvivoGen), 50μM chloroquine (CHQ; InvivoGen), or 25 μg/mL CPG-52364 (BOC Sciences) for 30 minutes, followed by stimulation with TL8-506 (1 μg/mL) for 18 hours. Secreted cytokines in the supernatant were measured using a bead array kit for interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alfa (AimPlex kit, Mayflower Bioscience). Statistical analysis was performed by analysis of variance with P < .05 considered as significant.

Disease manifestations included cytopenia, infection, oral ulcers, and/or inflammatory disease (Table 1; Figure 1D). All patients had neutropenia; 9 patients had severe neutropenia, 3 with a reported ANC of 0 cell per μL. Patient 10 had an ANC nadir of 780 cells per μL before HCT. All 10 patients had mild to moderate thrombocytopenia; 5 patients had mild to moderate anemia; and 3 patients had severe anemia, including patient 10, who presented at age 2 weeks with pure red cell aplasia. Most (n = 7) patients had a history of oral ulcers, and all patients had a history of bacterial and/or fungal infection, which were often severe. Pneumonia was particularly common (n = 6), including cases of Nocardia and Klebsiella pneumonia (1 patient each), as well as Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in a patient who received alemtuzumab. Four patients experienced episodes of sepsis, including Clostridium septicum septicemia in 2 patients, and typhlitis was reported in 3 patients. Patient 7 notably had typhlitis due to C. septicum, Candida lusitaniae, and mucormycetes requiring multiple surgical interventions, including resection of the ascending and transverse colon, as well as myositis and fasciitis of the leg and perineum. In addition, all patients had evidence of lymphoproliferation with lymphadenopathy and/or splenomegaly. Six patients had hypogammaglobulinemia requiring immunoglobulin replacement. Other disease manifestations included systemic juvenile idiopathic vs psoriatic arthritis, recurrent inflammatory colitis, and inflammatory skin boils (1 patient each).

The immune phenotype was variable, but common findings included an inverse CD4:CD8 T-cell ratio with skewing to the memory phenotype, increased terminal effector memory T cells, and increased double-negative T cells (T-cell receptor αβ+ and/or T-cell receptor γδ+ T cells).3 Hypogammaglobulinemia with decreased naïve and transitional B cells and low to absent class-switched memory B cells was also common. Increased proportion of CD4+ T helper 1 cells and increased expression of programmed cell death protein-1 on CD4+ and CD8+ memory T cells were observed in some patients (supplemental Figure 1). Proinflammatory serum cytokines, including IL-18, interferon gamma (IFN-γ) or CXCL9, IL-12 and soluble interleukin 2 receptor α were commonly, but not universally, elevated. Of note, patient 10 did not have the typical immune phenotype (supplemental Figure 2). Patient 1 had positive anti-La and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies. The remaining 8 patients (patients 2-9) for whom testing was performed had negative autoantibody testing.

Bone marrow biopsies ranged from hypocellular to hypercellular with varying degrees of productive erythropoiesis and megakaryopoiesis (Table 1). Most patients had marked myeloid hypoplasia to agranulocytosis, and the presence of predominantly CD8+ T-cell lymphoid infiltrates or aggregates. There was no evidence of dysplasia on any of the bone marrow biopsies. CD8+ T cells typically showed an activated phenotype, and several (n = 5) patients had CD8+/CD57+ large granular lymphocytes (LGLs). Patient 8’s bone marrow biopsy slides and flow cytometry are presented in Figure 2A-F and supplemental Figure 2, respectively, as a demonstrative example. T-cell receptor repertoire was oligoclonal in 2 patients and polyclonal in 6 patients for whom testing was performed. Two patients received treatment for LGL leukemia (1 with oligoclonal LGLs and 1 with polyclonal LGLs) before discovery of TLR8 GOF. Conversely, patient 10 had bone marrow findings that demonstrated a myeloid predominance with almost no red cell precursors and a marked increase in infiltrating T cells forming aggregates (supplemental Figure 3). All patients with severe neutropenia (patients 1-9) ultimately had an absent or poor response to G-CSF and steroids. Other therapies that were trialed in >1 patient included sirolimus (n = 4), mycophenolate mofetil (n = 2), and JAK inhibitors (n = 2), all without response. One patient received alemtuzumab for suspected evolving T-cell LGL leukemia, and 1 patient received azathioprine, methotrexate, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab therapy for 3 to 4 months with no improvement in neutrophil count. Hydroxychloroquine, which is thought to inhibit endosomal TLR signaling,10 was also trialed in 1 patient, again without effect. The patient (patient 5) with recurrent inflammatory colitis also received multiple immunosuppressive medications for treatment of gastrointestinal disease without improvement in neutrophil count.

Seven patients underwent allogeneic HCT. Of the 3 patients who did not undergo HCT (patients 1, 6, and 9), only 1 was alive at last contact. Patient 1 had a history of diaphragmatic paralysis during a septic event, and developed pulmonary fibrosis in the setting of chronic ventilation; he died aged 19 years from end-stage lung disease and complications of bone marrow failure. Patient 6 died aged 8 years from sepsis. Patient 9 was the only one to present during adulthood, and was lost to follow-up aged 42 years.

Transplant data were available for 6 of 7 patients who underwent allogeneic HCT. Details of the transplant procedures are presented in Table 2, and measures of burden of disease at baseline before HCT are presented in supplemental Table 2. Age at HCT ranged from 11 months to 20 years. Patient 7 was critically ill at the onset of conditioning with complicated polymicrobial typhlitis. The remaining patients had stable disease at the time of transplant, including bacterial pneumonia on antibiotic therapy in patient 8. One patient had colitis, and was on sirolimus and ruxolitinib immunosuppressive therapy. Baseline splenomegaly ranged from mild to severe (mild = 1, moderate = 4, severe = 1). The median HCT-comorbidity index score was 4.5 (range, 0-7), and the median IDDA2.1 score was 23.25 (range, 17.70-86.25). Patient 7 and patient 8 had LGLs present in the bone marrow at 41% and 21%, respectively, of lymphocytes in the bone marrow aspirate before HCT. No patient received immune suppression to specifically decrease hyperinflammation in preparation for HCT. Donor and stem cell sources were varied. Two patients received busulfan or treosulfan and fludarabine-based conditioning; 2 patients received thiotepa, fludarabine, low-dose cyclophosphamide and total body irradiation (TBI) 200 cGy; and 1 patient received medium-dose busulfan, fludarabine, and TBI 200 cGy. Transplant outcomes are listed in Table 2 and supplemental Table 2. All surviving patients experienced primary engraftment, with neutrophil and platelet recovery at 10 to 23 days and 26 to 42 days, respectively. The patient (patient 2) who received busulfan and fludarabine conditioning developed severe veno-occlusive disease (VOD) complicated by renal failure after HCT, and had secondary graft failure. He received a stem cell boost on day +21 without effect, and subsequently underwent a second HCT with the same donor, and fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and TBI conditioning with successful engraftment. However, he died ∼2.5 months after the second transplant from multisystem organ failure. Patient 7, who was critically ill with polymicrobial typhlitis, successfully engrafted with 100% donor myeloid and T-cell chimerism on day +13, but died on day +15 from ongoing preexisting infection and hyperinflammatory response treated with steroids, IV immunoglobulin and plasmapheresis.

Patient 5 developed lower gastrointestinal aGVHD (grade unknown, but grade 2 at minimum), and patient 8 developed steroid-refractory grade 2 skin aGVHD that was successfully treated with ruxolitinib. Patient 2 developed grade 3 aGVHD following their second HCT. Patient 3 developed severe lung cGVHD currently stable on low-dose prednisone, cyclosporine, belumosudil, and extracorporeal photophoresis. Historical treatments also included high-dose corticosteroids, ruxolitinib, rituximab, and axatilimab. The patient is awaiting cadaveric lung transplant.

Three patients developed pancytopenia and/or thrombocytopenia of unclear etiology after HCT. Patient 3 developed pancytopenia at ∼day 100 that resolved with weaning of immune suppression, and subsequently developed mild thrombocytopenia that improved with rituximab. Patient 8 developed platelet-refractory thrombocytopenia 3 weeks after HCT, which resolved with high-dose IV immunoglobulin and a short course of corticosteroids. Of note, thrombocytopenia occurred in the setting of weight gain and an increase in bilirubin level, concerning for evolving VOD. Criteria for VOD were never met, but the etiology of thrombocytopenia is unclear in this case. Patient 10 developed pancytopenia at day 60. They received multiple immunosuppressive agents, including steroids, and pancytopenia ultimately resolved with ruxolitinib and abatacept. There were no clear infectious triggers for cytopenia in any of the patients, and donor myeloid and T-cell chimerism was 100% during episodes of cytopenia. None of the patients had bone marrow biopsies after HCT.

Patient 3 has had poor immune reconstitution after HCT in the setting of cGVHD. The other 3 surviving patients had reasonable peripheral blood lymphocyte counts at 1 year after HCT (supplemental Table 3). Patient 10 remained on immunoglobulin replacement after rituximab for autoimmune cytopenia.

Ultimately, 4 patients (patients 3, 5, 8, and 10) are alive at 1 to 3 years after HCT. All patients have had complete resolution of disease manifestations, including normalization of neutrophil count, with 100% donor whole blood, myeloid, and T-cell chimerism at last follow-up. Organomegaly and preexisting infections and inflammatory disease resolved, and IDDA2.1 score at last follow-up was decreased compared with baseline in all patients (supplemental Table 2).

The patient (patient 4) for whom detailed transplant data were not available received a haploidentical transplant from their mother with nonmyeloablative conditioning and posttransplant cyclophosphamide for GVHD prophylaxis. He received multiple donor lymphocyte infusions for declining donor chimerism after HCT, but was alive and well with full donor chimerism at the last follow-up 3 years after HCT.

Given the lack of efficacious medical therapy for patients with INFLTR8, we investigated the effects of different TLR inhibitors on TLR8-GOF activity in patient iPSC-derived macrophages with WT/WT and WT/p.P432L (the most common pathogenic variant) TLR8. CU-CPT9a is a selective inhibitor of TLR8 signaling that binds to TLR8 in its inactive conformation; CHQ is a lysosomotropic agent that increases the pH of the endosome and prevents TLR8 activation;10,11 and CPG-52364 is a small molecular inhibitor of TLR7/8 previously investigated as a potential treatment for systemic lupus erythematosus.12,13 Cells were pretreated with the different inhibitors before stimulation with high-dose TLR8 ligand (TL8-506; Figure 2G-I), and secreted IL-6 was measured after 18 hours. The results demonstrate that CU-CPT9a inhibited WT TLR8 activity, but cells with the p.P432L variant were resistant to CU-CPT9a treatment (Figure 2G). CHQ and CPG-52364 both inhibited IL-6 production by macrophages expressing WT and GOF TLR8 (Figure 2H-I). Similar results were obtained measuring tumor necrosis factor levels (data not shown).

We previously described the GOF effects of mosaic and germ line TLR8 variants in a cohort of 6 patients. We identified 3 additional patients with mosaicism who harbored the TLR8 variant at the same amino acid position, p.P432. Two of these patients had a different amino acid change (p.P432R, p.P432Q), and the third patient harbored the previously reported variant p.P432L. Functional testing validated GOF of the TLR8 protein, suggesting that the position p.P432 in TLR8 is a hot spot for somatic variation, particularly during embryogenesis, and has significant impact on TLR8 function. We also identified the first female patient who harbored a p.P432L germ line variant, and who presented during infancy with pure red cell aplasia, highlighting that TLR8 GOF is an X-linked dominant disorder. Interestingly, a clinical assay for X-chromosome inactivation demonstrated no skewing of her peripheral blood cells (56:44 ratio by promoter methylation analysis), suggesting that the presence of the TLR8 variant was not detrimental to cell survival. In addition, TLR8 does not appear to undergo X-inactivation in immune cells,14 and we anticipate that female patients with germ line or somatic TLR8 GOF variants will manifest disease.

Severe neutropenia with myeloid hypoplasia and lymphoid infiltrates and/or aggregates on bone marrow biopsy remains the most unifying clinical feature in these patients. Lymphoproliferation with immune dysregulation, including an inverted CD4:CD8 T-cell ratio, expansion of activated T cells with skewing to the memory phenotype, and abnormal B-cell maturation with low/absent switched memory B cells were also common. T cells in the bone marrow typically demonstrate an activated phenotype, and many patients had CD8+/CD57+ LGLs present, which may be an additional clinical phenotype to consider for TLR8 sequencing, especially in pediatric or young adult patients. We also previously demonstrated elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, the B-cell survival factor B-cell activating factor, and soluble Fas ligand.3 Interestingly, although TLR8 signaling typically upregulates type 1 IFNs, patients do not have a type 1 IFN signature.3 The nature of T- and B-cell dysregulation is unclear, but is expected to be cell-extrinsic given that T and B cells do not express TLR8. It is important to note that the female patient (patient 10) had a myeloid predominance with marked erythroid hypoplasia on bone marrow biopsy, and lacked the inverted CD4:CD8 ratio and skewing to the memory phenotype commonly seen. It is possible that her phenotype may have evolved to myeloid hypoplasia over time, as occurred in patient 4. Nevertheless, the absence of characteristic findings on bone marrow biopsy and immune phenotyping does not preclude a diagnosis of TLR8 GOF.

The etiology of the severe neutropenia remains incompletely elucidated, but is likely cell-extrinsic given the significant burden of disease in patients with mosaicism in a small fraction of cells, and may be related to the abnormal activated T-cell population. As for the neutropenia seen with LGL leukemia, the hypotheses for neutropenia include high soluble Fas ligand triggering apoptosis of neutrophil and/or myeloid progenitors and direct destruction by infiltrating T cells.15

The etiology of pure red cell aplasia in patients 4 and 10 is also unknown. However, a recent study that crossed transgenic mice expressing human TLR8 with lupus mice demonstrated that expression of human TLR8 in lupus mice induces fatal anemia via myeloid cell dysregulation,16 indicating a potential mechanism in our patients. Liang et al also recently reported a family with inherited nonhemolytic anemia and a germ line TLR8 GOF variant.8 TLR8 is expressed in the erythroid lineage, including erythroid progenitors, and they hypothesized that red cell-intrinsic signaling through TLR8 was responsible for the phenotype. Interestingly, the family had milder disease not consistent with INFLTR8. We tested the TLR8 variant reported (p.V434L), and although very close to the most common amino acid altered in INFLTR8 (p.P432), its activity is significantly lower than seen with the p.P432L INFLTR8 disease-causing variant (supplemental Figure 6). Increased TLR8 function was also recently associated with systemic lupus erythematosus, due to GOF variants in UNC93B1 leading to altered and increased TLR7 and TLR8 function.17,18 Thus, there is likely a spectrum of disease associated with human TLR8 function, and we hypothesize that INFLTR8 represents the severe end of this spectrum.

Regardless of the mechanism, neutropenia was ultimately refractory to medical management in all patients, including growth factors, steroids, and multiple immunomodulatory medications, and patients suffered from severe, life-threatening infections causing significant morbidity and mortality. As such, early allogeneic HCT should be strongly considered in pediatric and adolescent patients with TLR8 GOF variants. Five of seven patients who underwent allogeneic HCT are alive at 1 to 3 years after HCT, with full myeloid and T-cell donor chimerism and resolution of disease phenotype, including normal peripheral blood cell counts. The degree of donor chimerism necessary for disease correction is unknown, but we suspect 100% donor myeloid chimerism may be necessary given the dominant nature of the small number of clones with somatic variants.

Notably, the patient (patient 2) who received high-dose busulfan developed severe VOD and multisystem organ failure after HCT followed by secondary graft failure. They ultimately died following the second HCT. Patient 7 received treosulfan-based conditioning, and died from a severe hyperinflammatory response early after HCT in the setting of uncontrolled infection. Significant toxicity was also observed in the patients who received less myeloablative conditioning, including grade 2 to 4 aGVHD or severe cGVHD in 3 of 4 patients for whom detailed information was available. It is possible that epithelial cells or tissue-resident macrophages with TLR8 GOF, which may be ineffectively cleared with chemotherapy conditioning,19 are primed for an excessive inflammatory response in the setting of tissue injury. Although this hypothesis is unproven, it may be prudent to avoid high-dose busulfan for patients with TLR8 GOF due to concerns for increased risk of chemotherapy-related toxicity. Inclusion of immunosuppressive agents targeting macrophages in GVHD prophylaxis may also be considered in the future. In addition, the implication of continued TLR8 GOF expression in tissues after HCT and risk of late effects is unclear.

Three patients developed cytopenia after HCT that responded to high-dose IV immunoglobulin, steroids, and/or other immunosuppressive agents, suggesting an autoimmune etiology. All patients had 100% donor myeloid chimerism in the peripheral blood. None of the patients had bone marrow biopsies, so it is unclear if a TLR8 GOF remnant in the bone marrow early after HCT could have contributed to cytopenia. Furthermore, none of the patients had testing for TLR8 variants after HCT. Ultimately, the reasons for post-HCT cytopenia and any connection to underlying TLR8 GOF are unclear, but it will be interesting to investigate further as more patients receive transplants.

Most patients with somatic mosaicism had variant TLR8 identified in skin fibroblasts, suggesting a mutational event during embryonic development, but with onset of disease ranging from 9 months to 15 years. The trigger(s) for disease manifestation is unclear, but may be environmental or even an endogenous ligand. One patient who presented during adulthood aged 28 years with somatic mosaicism with a VAF of 15% was alive without allogeneic HCT at last contact aged 42 years. Nonhematopoietic cell lines such as skin fibroblasts were never tested for the patient, so it is unclear if they harbored the mutation in utero or acquired the variant later in life, potentially resulting in milder disease. Whether such patients require definitive therapy is unknown, but it will be important to fully characterize newly diagnosed patients via genetic testing of multiple cell lines moving forward. It should be noted, however, that patient 5 had somatic mosaicism with a VAF of 17% in his blood, not detected in his skin fibroblasts, but still had severe disease necessitating allogeneic HCT. As such, the absence of the TLR8 GOF variant in nonhematopoietic cells does not necessarily equate to milder disease.

It would be desirable to identify TLR inhibitors that effectively reduce inflammation and may serve as adjuncts to HCT. We tested 3 potential inhibitors, and identified CHQ, a relatively nonspecific inhibitor through endosomal pH alteration, and CPG-52364, a small molecular inhibitor of TLR7/8, as effective in vitro. Interestingly, CU-CPT9a, a selective inhibitor of TLR8 signaling that binds to TLR8 in its inactive conformation and prevents downstream signaling,20 had good activity with WT but failed to inhibit TLR8 GOF activity. As CU-CPT9a inhibits TLR8 signaling by directly binding to the receptor, we hypothesize that TLR8 GOF might alter the binding affinity of the TLR8 receptor or predispose it to an active conformation, which is not favorable for CU-CPT9a binding, thereby reducing the effectiveness of the inhibitor. Future work to test these and other TLR8 inhibitors will be important to identify therapies that may better bridge patients to HCT.

In conclusion, TLR8 GOF and the INFLTR8 phenotype is an X-linked dominant disease that should be considered in both male and female patients with severe and/or refractory neutropenia, pure red cell aplasia, evidence of lymphoproliferation with immune dysregulation, and myeloid hypoplasia with activated T-cell infiltrates or aggregates and/or CD8+/CD57+ LGLs on bone marrow biopsy. A high index of suspicion is necessary, as somatic mutations may be missed on standard exome and genome analysis, especially in female patients where VAFs may be lower due to the presence of 2 X chromosomes. Alternative methodologies to identify variants, such as targeted deep sequencing, may be required. Most disease manifestations, including severe neutropenia, are refractory to currently available medical therapies, including G-CSF, steroids, and immunomodulatory medications. Allogeneic HCT is well tolerated and may be curative with full donor myeloid and T-cell chimerism and resolution of disease phenotype. High rates of post-HCT cytopenia of unclear etiology and GVHD were observed, which we hypothesize may be due to residual cells with TLR8 GOF; however, the etiology of these complications remains to be elucidated. For now, it may be prudent to avoid traditional myeloablative conditioning due to concerns for increased risk of chemotherapy-related toxicity.
