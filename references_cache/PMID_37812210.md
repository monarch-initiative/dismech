# The safety and efficacy of decortication for stage III drug-resistant tuberculous empyema.
**Authors:** Yao L, Wang B, Chen X, Liu Q, Sheng J, Liu X, Dai X, Jiang Y
**Journal:** Interdiscip Cardiovasc Thorac Surg (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1093/icvts/ivad166](https://doi.org/10.1093/icvts/ivad166)

## Content

OBJECTIVES: The goal of this study was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of decortication for stage III drug-resistant tuberculous empyema (TE).

METHODS: We analysed all patients with stage III TE who underwent decortication between March 2015 and October 2019 at Wuhan Pulmonary Hospital. The patients were divided into 2 groups according to drug-susceptibility testing of bronchoscopy lavage fluid, pleural effusion and tissue specimens, including a drug-resistant group and a drug-sensitive group. We collected and compared the preoperative, perioperative and postoperative data from the 2 groups to evaluate the safety and efficacy of decortication for stage III drug-resistant TE.

RESULTS: In total, 135 cases met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled, including 30 cases in the drug-resistant group and 105 cases in the drug-sensitive group. No deaths were recorded for the entire study population. Compared to the drug-sensitive group, the drug-resistant group had longer operation times (259.8 ± 78.4 min vs 187.2 ± 56.0 min, P = 0.00), a larger volume of intraoperative blood loss [300 (200,400) ml vs 200 (130, 300) ml, P = 0.00] and a higher intraoperative transfusion rate (5/30, 16.7% vs 4/105, 3.8%, P = 0.04). The rate of complications was significantly higher in the drug-resistant group (23; 76.7%) than in the drug-sensitive group (53; 50.5%) (P = 0.01). Recurrence was not reported in any of the patients. Twenty-three (76.7%) patients in the drug-resistant group and 90 (85.7%) patients in the drug-sensitive group recovered to an "excellent" level, and 3 cases in each group recovered to a "poor" level; there was no significant difference between the 2 groups in surgical effects (P = 0.21).

CONCLUSIONS: Decortication is a safe, effective and feasible option for patients with stage III drug-resistant TE, although the operation is difficult and risky.

Drug-resistant tuberculosis (TB) has always represented a problem for TB prevention and control and is defined by higher morbidity and mortality, sequelae, higher costs and greater complexity. The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies drug-resistant TB into 5 categories: (i) isoniazid-resistant TB; (ii) rifampicin-resistant (RR) TB; (iii) multidrug-resistant TB (TB resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin), (iv) pre-extensively drug-resistant TB, which is multidrug-resistant TB with resistance to a fluoroquinolone and finally (v) extensively drug-resistant TB, which is TB resistant to rifampicin, plus any fluoroquinolone, plus at least 1 further priority A drug (bedaquiline or linezolid). The Global Tuberculosis Report 2022, released by the WHO, reported that there were 10.6 million new patients with TB worldwide in 2021, including 450,000 MDR/RR-TB patients. Overall, MDR/RR-TB accounts for 3.6% of all new TB patients and 18% of retreated TB patients [ 1 ]. Tuberculous empyema (TE) is a common extrapulmonary form of TB for which drug-resistance is becoming increasingly common [ 2 ]. Tuberculous empyema develops through several stages of progression: stage I is an exudative phase characterized by a clear, thin sterile pleural effusion; stage II represents a fibrinopurulent phase, in which the fluid becomes thick, infected and purulent; and stage III is an organizing or consolidation phase, which involves the formation of granulation tissue and lung encasement [ 3 ]. The most clinically pertinent feature of stage III TE is the fibrous pleural rind, which covers the visceral pleura and the parietal pleura [ 4 , 5 ]. Chronic fibrous pleural rind can compress the lungs and prevent full lung expansion [ 3 ]. In addition, this condition can result in deformation of the thorax and in scoliosis. Simple closed thoracic drainage is usually ineffective, and it is difficult for anti-tuberculous drugs to penetrate the thickened fibrous pleural rind, thus resulting in poor treatment efficacy [ 6 ]. Therefore, to treat stage III TE effectively, it is necessary to combine surgical treatment with anti-tuberculosis therapy (ATT) [ 7 ].

Stage III TE requires decortication to allow the lung to re-expand, reduce the degree of thorax deformation and create conditions for subsequent ATT to finally achieve a cure. Drug-resistant TB has a longer ATT cycle and a poorer effect than drug-sensitive TB [ 8 ]. However, whether drug-resistant TE can influence the safety and efficacy of an operation has yet to be reported. At present, there is no comparative study relating to the treatment of stage III drug-resistant TE and stage III drug-sensitive TE with decortication. To resolve this uncertainty, more evidence on surgical outcomes is urgently needed. This study retrospectively and comparatively analysed clinical data relating to the use of decortication for drug-resistant and drug-sensitive stage III TE in our department. Our goal was to evaluate its safety and efficacy and to provide guidance for the optimal surgical treatment of stage III drug-resistant TE in areas with a high TB burden.

This retrospective study was approved by the ethics committee of Wuhan Pulmonary Hospital (Approval No 2021–24), which waived the need to obtain informed patient consent.

This study involved patients with stage III TE who underwent surgical treatment in the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Wuhan Pulmonary Hospital in China from March 2015 to October 2019. The inclusion criteria were as follows: stage III TE was diagnosed prior to surgery, and the patient underwent a thoracotomy in our department; the results of the aetiological and pathological examinations of the pleural effusion or tissue samples were consistent with TE and ATT for more than 2 months prior to the operation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: the patient was unable to complete ATT after surgery; redo operation; age < 18 years; and patient had an incomplete clinical follow-up.

Before and during the operation, samples of sputum, bronchoscopy lavage fluid, pleural effusion or tissues were acquired from the patients and subjected to drug sensitivity tests, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis culture, the Gene Xpert MTB/RIF assay, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction and the Genotype MTBDR plus (Hain) test, to determine the diagnosis and drug resistance. According to the results of drug sensitivity testing, the patients enrolled were divided into 2 groups: a drug-resistant group and a drug-sensitive group. We obtained and recorded the following information for each patient: general information and a range of clinical features including body mass index, haemoglobin, serum albumin and lung function prior to surgery, imaging, microbiological analyses, pleural tissue histopathology, surgical records and immediate postoperative complications.

The surgical indications included a trapped lung caused by a thickened pleural cortex and empyema with progression or recurrence following effective ATT [ 9 ]. All procedures were performed with the patient under general anaesthesia with double lumen intubation and the patient in the lateral position. Depending on the extent of the abscess, a posterior lateral incision was created between the sixth or seventh intercostal space into the thoracic cavity. If the intercostal space was narrow, the rib segment in the incision was removed, and the thoracic cavity was entered through the rib bed. The operation then proceeded with removal of part of the parietal fibrous pleural rind below the incision to expose the empyema cavity. After entering the cavity, the contents were suctioned, and a specimen was collected for microbiological testing. Then, blunt separation was carried out to remove thoroughly and carefully the visceral, parietal and diaphragmatic fibrous pleural rind. For patients with a concomitant pulmonary parenchymal lesion, pulmonary wedge resection or lobectomy was performed, depending on the situation. Finally, adhesions between the lung and the anterior and posterior mediastinum were separated to free the lung as much as possible. Next, the chest cavity was washed repeatedly with normal saline, and strict haemostasis was obtained. Two chest drains were placed in the anterior and posterior thoracic cavity.

All patients received ATT under the guidance of TB doctors according to the drug sensitivity test results following surgery. The drug-resistant group received an ATT regimen according to WHO guidelines, and the drug-sensitive group received a standard ATT regimen (isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, pyrazinamide). If there was no air leak and chest tube drainage was <100 ml/day, the chest tubes were removed. Follow-up was performed in the thoracic surgery and TB outpatient clinics on a monthly basis thereafter to monitor the therapeutic effects and side effects of the drug regimen. In the first, second and third months after the operation, chest X-rays or chest computed tomography (CT) scans were reviewed to evaluate lung re-expansion. If the lung re-expansion was good, then chest CTs were reviewed once every 3 to 6 months. Before withdrawing the drug, CTs were reviewed to determine whether there was a recurrence of empyema. We evaluated the surgical effect from 3 aspects: the degree of postoperative lung re-expansion and whether there was thickened parietal pleural rind or cavity (Table 1). All patients were followed up until 1 year after the withdrawal of ATT.

Statistical analyses and data management were performed using SPSS 19.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). For continuous variable data, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to analyse whether the data conformed to a normal distribution, and if so, the mean ± standard deviation was used. If the data did not conform to a normal distribution, then we used medians with interquartile ranges. The independent-sample t-test was used to compare continuous variables if normally distributed while the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical information was expressed using numbers and percentages (n, %). The χ2 test or the Fisher exact test was used to compare categorical variables between groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

One hundred and fifty-two cases with a complete set of clinical data were identified; of these, 7 cases were excluded because they were < 18 years old; 6 cases were lost to follow-up; and 4 cases involved a redo surgery. Finally, a total of 135 cases were enrolled. All patients had a negative sputum smear and sputum culture preoperatively. Forty-five patients were diagnosed preoperatively from the aetiology of the bronchoscopy lavage fluid or the pleural effusion; 77 patients were diagnosed from the aetiology of surgical specimens obtained postoperatively; and 13 patients with negative aetiology were confirmed through a pathological analysis. According to drug-sensitivity test results, the patients were divided into 2 groups, including 30 cases in the drug-resistant group [MDR/RR-TB TE (n = 15), isoniazid-mono-resistance TE (n = 11), pre-extensively drug-resistant TE n = 4)] and 105 cases in the drug-sensitive group (Fig. 1).

No deaths were reported in the study population. General information related to the patients enrolled is shown in Table 2. Most of the patients were men [88.9% (120/135)] and of a young age [28 years (23, 47) years]. Smokers and alcohol drinkers accounted for 35.6% and 17.0% of the study population, respectively. The most common presentation was chest distress, followed by cough, dyspnoea on exertion, chest pain and fever. The duration of clinical symptoms ranged from 2 months to 3 years with 62 patients (45.9%) being symptomatic for more than 6 months. The disease involved the right side in 79 patients (58.5%), the left side in 54 patients (40.0%) and was bilateral in 2 patients (1.5%). Complications with diabetes were rare (6; 4.4%). The majority of patients (121; 89.6%) were registered as new patients, and more than half (72; 53.3%) had pulmonary TB. The mean duration of preoperative ATT was 4 (3, 6) months. The median body mass index and haemoglobin and serum albumin levels before surgery were 20.8 kg/m2 (range 14.9–27.4 kg/m2), 131.9 g/l (range 91.0–168.0 g/l) and 39.5 g/l (range 30.4–49.1 g/l), respectively. Lung function before surgery was decreased in 2 groups. Analysis showed no significant differences in the general characteristics of the patients when compared between the 2 groups (P > 0.05).

Perioperative and postoperative details are described in Table 3. Analysis showed that the mean operation time in the drug-resistant group was 259.8 ± 78.4 min, significantly longer than that in the drug-sensitive group (187.2 ± 56.0 min; P = 0.00). The mean volume of intraoperative blood loss in the drug-resistant group was 300 (200, 400) ml; this was significantly greater than that in the drug-sensitive group [200 (130, 300) ml; P = 0.00]. Intraoperative transfusions were required in 16.7% (5/30) of patients in the drug-resistant group; this proportion was significantly greater than that in the drug-sensitive group (P = 0.04). There was no significant difference between the 2 groups in terms of additional pneumonectomy, postoperative chest tube drainage time, complete lung expansion rate and complete lung expansion time (P > 0.05). Seventy-six (56.3%) patients experienced immediate postoperative complications. The most common postoperative complication (70; 51.9%) was prolonged air leak (PAL), followed by delayed bleeding at the surgical site (3; 2.2%), wound infection (2; 1.5%) and chylothorax (1; 0.7%). Complications were significantly more frequent in the drug-resistant group (23; 76.7%) compared to the drug-sensitive group (53; 50.5%) (P = 0.01). Recurrence was not reported in any of the patients. Twenty-three (76.7%) patients in the drug-resistant group and 90 (85.7%) patients in the drug-sensitive group recovered to an “excellent” level, and 3 cases in each group recovered to a “poor” level; there was no significant difference between the 2 groups in surgical effects (P = 0.21). Stage III TE usually requires surgical treatment. The goals of surgical interventions include controlling the source of infection through complete fibrous pleural rind removal and lung re-expansion [ 4 ]. Decortication is the standard operation for stage III TE [ 4 , 10 ]. Many factors affect the risk of treatment and the efficacy of decortication, including the timing of the operation, the empyema cavity range and the thickness of the fibrous pleural rind [ 4 , 7 , 11 ]. The basis for curing TE, a chronic infectious disease, is effective ATT; it is also the key to determining the risk and efficacy of decortication [ 7 ]. Drug-resistant TE has a long ATT cycle and poor effect. There are many uncertainties related to the role of decortication in drug-resistant TE. Therefore, we conducted this study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of decortication for stage III drug-resistant TE.

Our results indicate that the operating time, volume of intraoperative blood loss and intraoperative transfusion rate in the drug-resistant group were greater than those in the drug-sensitive group, thus suggesting that the operations were more difficult and the bleeding risk was higher in stage III drug-resistant TE. There are several reasons for these effects. First, for TE caused by the progression of tuberculous pleurisy, the sensitivity of pathogenic detection of pleural effusion is low [ 12 ]. A preoperative diagnosis is often based on the clinical manifestations of the patients and the personal experience of the physicians. Less than one-third of the drug-resistant cases of TE in this study were diagnosed preoperatively. It is difficult to effectively control the chest infection caused by drug-resistant TB using a standard drug-sensitive regimen for diagnostic ATT before the operation. Second, the course of ATT for drug-resistant TE is long, and the effect is poor. Even if the standard ATT regimen is used for treatment, the symptoms and imaging features suggest improvement in the short term, but the control of infection is still worse than that of drug-sensitive patients. When a TB infection is not controlled, the thoracic cavity becomes an acute/subacute inflammatory environment; the boundary between the fibrous pleural rind and the pleura is unclear; and there are extensive nutrient vessels. It is difficult to remove the fibrous pleural rind by blunt separation, and the bleeding from the peeling wound is serious, resulting in a difficult operation and a large volume of intraoperative blood loss [ 13 ]. Therefore, for patients with poor ATT efficacy, it is suggested that a chest puncture or a pleural biopsy should be performed before the operation to determine the diagnosis and whether the patient has drug-resistant TB. For patients with drug-resistant TE, or those with drug-resistant TB confirmed in other parts of the body outside of the thorax before surgery, the period of ATT should be appropriately extended before the operation; furthermore, the operation should be performed after the infection is controlled and is stable.

The surgical treatment of TB, especially in patients with drug-resistant tuberculosis, is associated with a high risk of surgical complications [ 14 , 15 ]. The existing literature reports that the incidence of surgical complications for multidrug-resistant pulmonary TB can be as high as 11.6%–29.0% [ 16 , 17 ]. The incidence of complications during surgery for chronic TE is 28.9%–85.3% [ 4 , 18 , 19 ]. The common complications include PAL, wound infection and thoracic haemorrhage. In this study, the incidence of complications in the drug-resistant group was 76.7%, which was higher than that in the drug-sensitive group (50.5%). The surgical complications included PAL, thoracic haemorrhage, wound infection and chylothorax; of these, the incidence of PAL was the highest. Several causative factors underlie these complications. First, poor control of infection in the thoracic cavity and an unclear boundary between the fibrous pleural rind and the visceral pleura result in serious lung injury during surgery. In addition, early postoperative pleural re-adhesion can limit lung re-expansion, and the residual thoracic cavity can lead to PAL [ 20 ]. Second, severe thoracic inflammation and extensive blood loss from wounds in the pleura and lungs after decortication can result in a massive postoperative haemorrhage. This diffuse oozing of blood is different from the bleeding caused by the common intercostal artery and bronchial artery injury after thoracic surgery. A second thoracotomy might not be able to stop the bleeding in a timely and accurate manner. In our study, 3 patients with postoperative thoracic haemorrhage were treated with blood transfusions and fluid replacement only to maintain sufficient blood volume. After the formation of blood clots in the thoracic cavity, the bleeding stopped. In the later stages, the period of time during which drainage tubes were placed in the thoracic cavity could be prolonged, and blood clots were dissolved and discharged through the drainage tube; this situation can lead to a successful cure. We considered that the following criteria were indications for conservative treatment: The patient's vital signs were stable; the haemoglobin level in the drainage fluid of the chest tube was significantly lower than that of the peripheral blood; and there was a gradual decrease in the hourly drainage volume of the chest tube. Third, in the drug-resistant group, 2 patients with wound infection were identified on the 41st and 65th days after surgery; a subcutaneous abscess and sinus formation at the scar of the surgical incision were found to be connected with the thoracic cavity. Furthermore, we noted encapsulated fluid and fibrous pleural rind formation in the thoracic cavity. After debridement, a dressing change and drainage of the infected wound, decortication and local thoracoplasty were performed in months 9 and 12 after the operation, which resulted in the patient being cured. It is possible that the postoperative thoracic TB infection was not effectively controlled and subsequently penetrated into the subcutaneous layer through the incision. It is also evident that the unstable control of infection is also the main factor responsible for the postoperative complications associated with drug-resistant TE.

For those diagnosed with drug-resistant TB, standardized ATT should be adopted as soon as possible after surgery. The effect of ATT is the main factor that determines the difficulty and risk of decortication and is also the fundamental reason for the difference between the drug-resistance group and the drug-sensitive group in this study. Due to differences in time to diagnosis, drug-resistance type, treatment course and the patient's tolerance to anti-tuberculosis drugs, the treatment effects of drug-resistant TE can differ significantly. Furthermore, the time required to gain control over the infection can also differ widely. It is more difficult to judge the optimum timing of surgery in drug-resistant patients. Our team found that patients with stage III presenting with low-density lines around the thickened fibrous pleural rind on preoperative CT imaging may be good candidates for open decortication [ 21 ]. In this study, we found that surgery for stage III drug-resistant TE is difficult and risky and that the surgical effects on postoperative compared to preoperative chest CT scans do not differ significantly from those of the drug-sensitive group, thus indicating that the prognosis of patients with stage III drug-resistant TE does not differ significantly from that of patients with stage III drug-sensitive TE after surgery and standardized ATT.

This study has some limitations that need to be considered. First, it was a single-centre retrospective study with a small sample size. Thus, our findings may be affected by selection bias, technical conditions and other factors. Second, due to the limited sample size, a subgroup analysis of different drug resistance types in the drug resistance group was not carried out; furthermore, there may have been differences in treatment difficulty and the effects of different drug resistance types in TE. Therefore, more focused prospective studies, with larger sample sizes, are needed to verify the results of this study.

In conclusion, decortication is safe, effective and feasible for patients with stage III drug-resistant TE, although the operation is difficult and risky. However, the premise of a successful operation is to determine the diagnosis and drug-resistance type as soon as possible and control the infection with effective ATT.
