# Lithium effects in the frontolimbic circuitry: a systematic review of neuroimaging findings in bipolar disorder.
**Authors:** Boere E, van der Wee NJA, de Leeuw M
**Journal:** Transl Psychiatry (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41398-026-03868-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-026-03868-z)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Lithium is a cornerstone treatment for bipolar disorder, but the biological mechanisms underlying its therapeutic effects remain incompletely defined. We adopt a network-level, cross-modal perspective focused on frontolimbic circuitry and explicitly examine links between neuroimaging findings and clinical lithium response.

METHODS: Following PRISMA guidelines, we searched PubMed and Embase to September 3, 2025. Studies of humans were eligible when they compared lithium-treated bipolar subjects with non-lithium bipolar subjects, other treatment, healthy controls, or within-subject baselines. Risk of bias was assessed with RoB 2, ROBINS-I, NOS/NOS-CS, and BrainNet Europe criteria, as appropriate.

RESULTS: We included 115 studies (randomized, n = 6; non-randomized, n = 25; observational cohort, n = 2; cross-sectional, n = 78; post-mortem, n = 4). Across modalities, lithium treatment was most consistently associated with larger volumes in the hippocampus, amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and prefrontal cortex (PFC), and with higher white-matter integrity within frontolimbic circuitry. Spectroscopy and functional findings were more heterogeneous in cross-sectional designs. Only a minority of studies directly related imaging to clinical response, converging on increased amygdala-PFC/ACC connectivity, hippocampal integrity, and hippocampal, amygdalar, PFC, and ACC volumes. Overall risk of bias was substantial, yielding low-to-moderate certainty of evidence.

DISCUSSION: Findings support the hypothesis that lithium's therapeutic effects may be mediated by modification of the frontolimbic circuitry, while underscoring the need for preregistration, rigorous confounding control, standardized imaging pipelines, and longer follow up to capture duration-dependent effects. Ultra-high field lithium MR imaging and imaging transcriptomics may enable the development of connectomic biomarkers predictive of clinical response.

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a psychiatric disorder marked by recurring manic and depressive episodes. Key symptoms include elevated mood in mania and decreased mood in depression. With a global age-standardized prevalence rate of 454.6 per 100,000 people and an increasing number of years lived with disability, currently exceeding 8 million worldwide, BD represents a high burden of disease [1]. BD has consistently been linked to impairments in occupational, interpersonal, and financial functioning, even after episodes are stabilized [2]. Beyond the manic and depressive episodes, BD is also associated with several neuropsychological abnormalities that are independent of mood states. These include dysregulation of executive functions [3, 4], verbal learning and memory [5, 6], and impairments in emotion regulation and reward processing [7–11]. However, the etiology and pathophysiology of BD remain incompletely understood [12].

An increasing number of findings from neuroimaging studies suggest that an aberrant frontolimbic circuitry underlies the clinical manifestations of BD. The frontolimbic circuitry refers to a specific brain circuitry that has consistently been linked with emotional and cognitive processes, such as executive attention and memory functions, experiencing emotions, and the regulation of affect [13]. Whereas many previous neuroimaging studies of BD typically focused on specific brain areas, a frontolimbic network-based approach aligns with a recent paradigm shift in neuroscientific research, moving toward a connectomic paradigm that focuses on structural and functional connectivity [14].

So far, the findings of neuroimaging studies in BD have implicated the involvement of the frontolimbic circuitry, encompassing the hippocampus, amygdala, and thalamus, along with anterior cingulate cortex, ventrolateral and ventromedial prefrontal cortices, and orbitofrontal cortex [15–17] (Fig. 1). Lithium, regarded as a cornerstone of pharmacological BD treatment [18–21], may exert its therapeutic stabilizing effects through modification of the frontolimbic circuitry. Several neuroimaging studies support this hypothesis.

First, an exploratory analysis of pooled structural MRI data from the largest neuroimaging study of BD to date, conducted by the Enhancing Neuro Imaging Genetics through Meta-analysis Bipolar Disorder Working Group (ENIGMA-BD), identified smaller hippocampal and thalamic volumes, along with larger lateral ventricles, in BD subjects not treated with lithium compared with healthy controls (HC). In contrast, hippocampal volumes in lithium-treated subjects were comparable to those of HC, and thalamic volumes were larger compared to those not treated with lithium [22, 23].

Second, a mega- and meta-analysis of diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies involving 3033 subjects by the ENIGMA Consortium found higher widespread frontolimbic connectivity in lithium-treated BD subjects compared to those not treated with lithium [24]. In another large-scale meta-analysis of 57 voxel-based DTI studies, which included 4631 individuals with BD, lithium treatment was associated with higher connectivity in the corpus callosum, bilateral anterior thalamic radiation, right corticospinal tract, and left superior longitudinal fasciculus, compared to BD subjects not treated with lithium [25].

Third, a comprehensive review of 11 fMRI studies, six of which were longitudinal, confirmed aberrant brain connectivity and activation in specific brain regions in BD compared to HC. Additionally, lithium treatment correlated with a reduction of these aberrations [26].

Fourth, a meta-regression analysis of voxel-based morphometry (VBM) studies found that lithium treatment was associated with larger anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) volumes in BD subjects compared to those not treated with lithium [27].

Fifth, a systematic review of proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) studies reported lower levels of the neuronal marker N-acetyl aspartate (NAA) in the hippocampus and frontal structures in BD subjects compared to HC. In contrast, higher NAA levels were found in lithium-treated BD subjects compared to those without lithium, suggesting greater neuronal viability in the lithium-treated group [28].

These findings align with preclinical studies suggesting that lithium targets frontolimbic regions. For instance, a rodent study showed increased total brain and cortical gray matter volumes after four weeks of lithium administration [29]. In two other rodent studies, lithium administration normalized mania-like behavior induced by genetic modifications in frontolimbic structures [30, 31]. Furthermore, chronic lithium treatment in manic rats was associated with the upregulation of genes in the amygdala and frontal cortex, while downregulating genes in the hippocampus: the opposite pattern was observed in depressed rats [32].

Taken together, findings from structural MRI, DTI, and fMRI studies – and, to a lesser extent, from VBM and 1H-MRS studies - indicate BD-related aberrations compared with HC, most notably smaller volumes and lower connectivity in frontolimbic brain structures. At the same time, growing evidence suggests that lithium may attenuate these aberrations. However, previous reviews have primarily focused on individual brain regions or imaging modalities, often without embedding these results within a network-based framework or considering their translational implications. The present review seeks to address this gap by systematically synthesizing evidence across structural, functional, and molecular imaging studies, with particular emphasis on the frontolimbic network as a unifying circuit implicated in mood regulation and lithium’s therapeutic effects. Importantly, we assess both the methodological rigor of the primary studies and the extent to which neuroimaging findings correlate with clinical lithium treatment response, providing a translational perspective that has been largely missing from previous reviews. In doing so, we aim to move beyond a descriptive overview toward a critical synthesis that integrates convergent evidence, highlights methodological limitations, and outlines future directions for developing neuroimaging-based markers of lithium response.

Following PRISMA guidelines [33] and in consultation with a medical information specialist (librarian), we conducted a systematic search in PubMed using the following terms: (“Bipolar Disorder”[Mesh] OR “bipolar affective disorder”[tiab] OR mania[tiab]) AND (“Limbic System”[Mesh] OR “Frontal Lobe”[Mesh] OR Limbic[tiab] OR Frontolimbic[tiab] OR Hippocampus[Mesh] OR Hippocampus[tiab] OR Amygdala[Mesh] OR Amygdala[tiab] OR Thalamus[Mesh] OR Thalamus[tiab] OR Thalamic[tiab] OR “Prefrontal Cortex”[Majr] OR “Prefrontal Cortex”[tiab] OR PFC[tiab] OR “Gyrus Cinguli”[Majr] OR cingulate[tiab] OR ACC[tiab]) AND Lithium[Mesh].

An additional search was conducted in Embase with the following terms: (“Bipolar Disorder”/ OR “bipolar affective disorder”.ti,ab. OR mania.ti,ab.) AND (“Limbic System”/ OR “Frontal Lobe”/ OR limbic.ti,ab. OR frontolimbic.ti,ab.) AND “Lithium”/.

Search results were restricted to human research and English-language publications.

Database searches were last updated on September 3, 2025.

The reference lists of the included studies, as well as those from previous review papers on neuroimaging in BD, were screened for additional articles.

Results were screened for relevance and eligibility by two independent reviewers. Full texts were reviewed to confirm eligibility. Studies were included if they compared BD subjects receiving lithium treatment with BD subjects receiving other treatment, BD subjects without treatment, HC or if BD subjects on lithium treatment served as their own controls. Studies involving BD subjects that reported subgroup analyses related to lithium treatment were also included.

Data extraction was independently conducted by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. The following data were extracted.

Participants: Number of lithium-treated BD subjects, mean age (with standard deviation (SD) or range), mood state at the time of scan acquisition, and mean episode severity.

Study characteristics: Design and acquisition method.

Intervention: Lithium treatment.

Comparison: Presence/absence of a control group (non-lithium BD, HC).

Outcomes: Identified frontolimbic structures and whether clinical response to lithium was reported.

If age, mood state, and severity scales for BD subjects on lithium were not reported in the primary paper (e.g., in subgroup analyses), data from BD subjects regardless of lithium were used. If this was still not specified, group scores from the subgroup with the highest number of lithium-treated BD subjects were used. If other data were not provided in the primary study, this was marked in the results (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, all studies were reviewed for commercial funding.

Risk of bias was assessed for each study using one of the following tools, as appropriate:Cochrane Risk of Bias tool version 2 (RoB 2) for randomized trials [34];The Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) for non-randomized trials [35];The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational cohort studies [36];The adapted NOS-CS scale for cross-sectional observational studies [37]; orBrainNet Europe criteria for post-mortem brain studies [38].

Most structural MRI studies, including three within-subject designs and a mega-analysis [22, 39–63] reported larger hippocampal volumes in lithium-treated BD patients compared to non-lithium BD or similar volumes relative to HC. One study found a decrease in left hippocampal volumes in non-responders, compared to baseline [58], while two region-of-interest (ROI)-based MRI studies reported negative findings [64, 65].

DTI-studies, including one within-subject study [66–69], linked lithium with higher integrity of the hippocampal cingulum, and the ENIGMA-BD study found greater integrity in the fornix [24].

Spectroscopy results were mixed: one study reported similar hippocampal NAA levels in lithium-treated BD subjects compared to HC [70], while another found increased NAA over time correlated with antidepressant efficacy [71]. Both observed elevated glutamate when serum lithium exceeded 0.5 mmol/L [70, 71].

Molecular imaging showed increased hippocampal serotonin 5-HT1A receptor binding [72] and lithium accumulation on ultra-high field (7 Tesla; 7 T) lithium MRS (Li-MRS) [73]. Another 7 T MRI study found focal atrophy in lithium non-responders compared to responders [74].

Structural MRI consistently reported larger amygdalar volumes in lithium-treated BD [44–46, 51, 52, 55, 57, 58, 75–77], though three ROI-based studies found no effect [64, 65, 78].

Functional studies indicated normalization of aberrant amygdala connectivity. Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) showed increased amygdala-ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) connectivity correlating with clinical improvement [79]. Another rs-fMRI study reported increased baseline hypoconnectivity in depressed subjects and reduced baseline hyperconnectivity in manic subjects within the amygdala-centered network, with both changes correlating with clinical improvement [80]. In task-based fMRI, baseline amygdala-ventrolateral PFC (vlPFC) hyperconnectivity in manic subjects decreased after six weeks of lithium [81].

Two reports from a pseudorandomized trial [82, 83] suggested rapid suppression of amygdala overactivation, although the study was underpowered to detect arm-level differences. Another study found that longer lithium exposure was associated with greater amygdala-parahippocampal connectivity, possibly mediated by NK (CD56 + ) cells [84].

Structural connectivity analyses further indicated normalized diffusivity in the amygdala-subgenual tracts [85] and marked fractional anisotropy (FA) increases in the stria terminalis [69].

Molecular evidence included increased 5-HT1A receptor binding [72], and postmortem analysis showed higher glial density in lithium-exposed BD brains [86]. By contrast, another histological study reported reduced neuronal counts without lithium correlation [87].

Most MRI studies, including one within-subject design [22, 39, 40, 44, 55, 88, 89], found larger thalamic volumes in lithium-treated BD. DTI analyses indicated higher FA and axial diffusivity [24, 66, 67]. A postmortem study found increased oligodendrocyte numbers and density with lithium, despite unchanged volumes [90].

Several structural MRI studies reported larger ACC volumes [44, 46, 88, 91–94], though one study found reductions compared to baseline in non-responders [58].

DTI suggested higher integrity [67, 85].

A longitudinal cognitive task-based fMRI study reported increased hypoconnectivity between the ACC, vlPFC and left insula, along with reduced hyperconnectivity in several other regions after lithium treatment [81]. Emotional task-based studies found greater activation in the right dorsal ACC and bilateral lingual gyrus in lithium-treated BD subjects compared with both HC and non-lithium BD [11], and greater cingulate activity in lithium responders relative to non-responders [95]. Another study observed changes in amygdala-ACC connectivity among remitted patients; however, as it included two treatment arms (one with lithium), it was underpowered to detect between-arm differences [82].

Spectroscopy findings were mixed: some reported higher NAA, a marker of cell integrity [96, 97], while others found no effect [98–100]. Myo-inositol, a marker for glia cells, was variably increased [96, 101], decreased [97, 102, 103], or unchanged [104]. Choline, a marker for membrane cell turnover, was reduced in one study [103], unchanged in another [104].

Glutamate-GABA ratios were normalized to HC levels with lithium [105], though other glutamatergic studies found no association [98, 106, 107].

Structural MRI and advanced modeling studies reported larger frontal and prefrontal volumes and greater gray matter density in lithium-treated patients, resembling HC [40, 44, 58, 88, 94, 108–110]. DTI suggested normalization of diffusivity abnormalities [111].

Functional imaging showed enhanced amygdala-PFC connectivity linked to clinical improvement [79] and reductions in hyperconnectivity [81]. Lithium responders exhibited greater frontal activation in emotional tasks compared with lithium non-responders [95]. A task-based study found lithium reduced dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC) activation during reward-related decision-making, although this was not associated with symptomatic change [112].

Two between-subjects 1H-MRS studies reported higher NAA levels with lithium treatment [113, 114], while another associated lithium response with elevated choline in the dlPFC [115]. A within-subject study reported decreased myo-inositol, although this did not remain significant after correction [116].

Positron emission tomography (PET) findings indicated increased dlPFC blood flow [117], and postmortem analyses showed upregulated expression of neuroprotective insulin-like growth factor binding proteins [118]. By contrast, two structural MRI studies found no volume differences in the subgenual or orbitofrontal cortex [119, 120]; three additional MRS studies found no associations with myo-inositol or NAA [99, 121, 122], and another within-subject study showed a decrease of glutamate only when lithium was combined with chronotherapy, but not with lithium alone [123].

Lithium effects were also reported globally or across networks [24, 69, 94, 124–128], [24, 69, 84, 129, 130], including the internal capsule [24, 66, 69, 131, 132], insula [46, 67, 81, 88, 95], basal ganglia [39, 40, 44, 64, 73, 95, 133–137], corpus callosum [24, 66, 69, 111, 138, 139], nucleus accumbens [39, 140, 141], and occipital cortex [47, 66, 67, 81, 98, 142]. Other studies reported negative findings [143–150].

Risk of bias across studies varied by design (Supplementary Tables S2-S6). Among six randomized trials appraised with RoB 2, two were at high risk, driven primarily by informative missingness due to relapse-related drop-out. The remaining four had some concerns; recurring issues included single-blind designs with arm-aware clinical management, post-stabilization randomization and flexible dosing, whereas outcome measurement was generally protected by blinded assessors.

Across 25 non-randomized intervention studies rated with ROBINS-I, all were at overall serious risk of bias due to confounding by indication, baseline/prognostic imbalances, concomitant medications, or regression to the mean in before–after contrasts. In addition, seven studies were at serious risk on the missing-data domain because of substantial and/or differential attrition, sparse reporting of reasons, and no prespecified methods to address informative dropout.

For cross-sectional studies appraised with the adapted NOS-CS (n = 78), total scores typically ranged 6–9 (most scoring 7–8). Most papers did not report an a priori sample-size/power calculation, recruitment/response rates, or characteristics of non-responders, commonly costing two of the five available points in the Selection domain. Comparability was often limited - e.g., incomplete adjustment for clinical, demographic, or technical covariates – so one of the two points was frequently deducted in that domain. Two additional observational studies scored 8.5/9 and 6/9 on the original NOS. For post-mortem studies (BrainNet Europe criteria; n = 4), overall quality was moderate to moderate–high.

Of 115 studies, 9 (8%) reported commercial support to various degrees, 99 (86%) reported non-commercial funding only, and the remaining 7 (6%) did not report their funding status (Supplementary Table S7).

Distinct from prior reviews, our Discussion opens from a network-level, cross-modal perspective: we integrate structural, functional, and molecular evidence within a unified frontolimbic framework and relate these circuit patterns to clinical lithium response, while appraising study quality (Table 1). Across modalities, lithium treatment in BD was most consistently associated with larger volumes in the hippocampus, amygdala, ACC, and prefrontal regions and with higher white matter integrity within frontolimbic pathways, with several within-subject studies showing shifts toward healthy control-like profiles. By contrast, spectroscopy findings were more heterogeneous in cross-sectional designs. Importantly, only a minority of studies explicitly linking imaging to clinical response show convergence on increased amygdala-PFC/ACC connectivity [79–81], hippocampal integrity [68], and hippocampal, amygdalar, PFC, and ACC volumes [56, 58, 108], alongside selected neurometabolite changes in the hippocampus and ACC [71, 101, 102], underscoring translational relevance and motivating targeted, less biased designs.

Across study designs, risk of bias was considerable, limiting the overall certainty of evidence. The small body of randomized studies was further constrained by relapse-related attrition and incomplete blinding; non-randomized intervention studies were consistently threatened by confounding and missing data; cross-sectional studies often lacked pre-specified power calculations and adequate covariate adjustment; and the overall quality of post-mortem work was moderate to moderate-high.

Several common issues could inflate apparent lithium ‘normalization’: a) preferential selection of clinically stable and adherent patients into lithium groups; b) differential attrition of relapsers, effectively removing non-responders from analysis; c) flexible dosing and post-stabilization randomization; and d) before-after contrasts vulnerable to regression to the mean. Conversely, confounding by indication could attenuate observed effects if more severe or treatment-resistant patients are more likely to receive lithium or polypharmacy. In addition, analytic flexibility within imaging pipelines – particularly unreported processing choices - may further bias results in either direction.

Taken together, this risk-of-bias profile suggests that evidence from non-randomized studies offers, at best, low-to-moderate certainty, while findings gain greater credibility when replicated in within-subject or randomized designs.

Where feasible, future syntheses should examine whether associations persist after restricting analyses to lower-risk studies or after adjusting for key factors such as age, mood state at the time of scan acquisition, illness duration, and concomitant medications.

The suggested normalization of frontolimbic structure and function with lithium is of particular interest given that BD has been described as a potentially neuroprogressive disorder [151], characterized by decreased gray matter volumes and increased ventricular sizes [152]. The results of this review suggest that lithium treatment may be associated with the restoration of frontolimbic volume, function, and integrity, potentially counteracting neuroprogression in BD.

Lithium’s apparent normalizing effects on frontolimbic circuitry appear distinct from those of other commonly prescribed agents in BD. For instance, treatment with anticonvulsants has been associated with lower cortical and hippocampal volumes, as well as widespread reductions in integrity, as measured with DTI, compared with BD subjects not receiving anticonvulsants [22, 67, 129]. In BD subjects treated with antipsychotics, lower cortical volumes and reduced widespread integrity have also been reported compared with those not on antipsychotic treatment [24, 129]. Similarly, treatment with antidepressants in BD subjects has been linked to lower intra-axonal signal fraction and a higher mean diffusivity [67], although this correlation was not found in a larger DTI study [24].

The search also identified additional regions of interest, including the basal ganglia, which are involved in regulating emotions, behavior, and movement [153]; the corpus callosum, a major interhemispheric hub facilitating affective, language, and sensorimotor functions [154]; the nucleus accumbens, whose shell is considered part of the limbic network and plays a key role in the reward system [155]; and the occipital cortex, whose involvement has been linked to emotional learning in conjunction with the amygdala [156].

These findings should be interpreted in light of the methodological considerations detailed in the Limitations section.

Although a direct comparison between BD subtypes I and II in an ENIGMA-BD study (N = 1710) found similar hippocampal and amygdalar volumes in the primary analysis (not restricted to lithium users), the same study also reported smaller volumes in BD subtype I and similar volumes in BD subtype II when compared with HC [22]. A similar pattern was observed in another ENIGMA-BD study focusing on hippocampal subfields [50]. These findings may suggest subtype-specific spatial patterns [157]. Notably, a recent study using transcriptome-wide association analyses and Mendelian randomization distinguished BD I and II genomically [158], and another recent study reported subtype-dependent differences in genetic architecture [159]. These observations imply subtle frontolimbic differences between BD I and II that are independent of lithium treatment. Most included studies did not differentiate by subtype, which could have contributed to type II errors.

Aging has been associated with decreased brain volumes [160] and reduced brain integrity as measured by DTI [161]. In addition, BD has been linked to accelerated brain aging compared to HC, with suggestive evidence that lithium may attenuate this acceleration [162]. Several included studies reported that higher age correlated with lower subcortical volumes [22, 51, 135] or lower white matter integrity [69]. However, most primary studies did not include age as a covariate in analyses despite reporting mean age.

A large meta-analysis of fMRI studies identified different activation patterns depending on mood state during emotion, reward, and working-memory tasks [15]. Additionally, a meta-analysis of resting-state functional connectivity in BD found decreased connectivity within the limbic network in acute episodes but not in the euthymic state [163]. Three included studies correlated findings with mood state during scan acquisition [80, 120, 139], whereas many other studies did not report or stratify by mood state, which may have influenced results.

Several long-term studies correlated neuroimaging findings with lithium duration. For instance, a within-subject study reported an increase in hippocampal volume between baseline and the first follow up at 23 months, with a smaller increase between first and second follow-ups at 48 months [61]. In a between-subjects study with three conditions (no lithium, lithium treatment duration shorter than 24 months, and longer than 24 months), hippocampal volumes after 24 months of lithium resembled HC, whereas shorter duration resembled non-lithium BD [59]. Amygdalar volumes were larger in both lithium treatment groups than in non-lithium BD [57]. Regression analysis in 34 BD subjects (median duration 12 months; range 2-115) showed a significant effect of duration on left amygdala volume and a trend-level effect on the right [52]. Another study found that duration (mean 55 ± 49 months) correlated with gray matter volumes only in the carriers of the rs334558*G genotype [94]. Two DTI studies correlated duration (means 166 ± 149 and 69 ± 72 months) with higher whole-brain mean FA and with higher FA in multiple white matter tracts [84, 125].

Given that most within-subject studies in this review lasted 4-8 weeks, lithium effects in those studies may have been underestimated [40, 58, 68, 71, 79–83, 101, 108, 126, 136, 146].

Less commonly discussed potential confounders include gender [22, 77, 148] and illness duration [53, 54, 129], although other studies reported no association with illness duration [22, 52, 113].

To minimize risk of bias, future work should prioritize preregistered protocols; concealed allocation and double-blind procedures where randomization is possible; standardized imaging pipelines with publicly available analytic code (e.g., the ENIGMA initiative); prospective strategies for handling missing data; and careful control for indication, mood state at time of scan acquisition, illness duration, and concomitant medications. Longer follow-up is also warranted, as several associations appear to strengthen with treatment duration, whereas brief 4-8 week studies may underestimate effects.

This review has several limitations, which can be grouped into three levels: study-level, field-level, and review-level.

Risk of bias was a major limitation across designs. Most included studies were cross-sectional, restricting causal inference. Sample sizes were frequently small, and clinical heterogeneity (e.g., age, illness duration, concomitant medications) was common. Few studies stratified findings by BD subtype, mood state at the time of imaging, or lithium duration. Methodological variability across modalities, such as field strengths and analytic pipelines, further complicated comparisons.

The translational link to clinical outcomes remains underpowered and potentially biased, as only a minority of studies examined clinical associations. Multiple studies focused on the hippocampus and amygdala as an a priori region of interest, which may have introduced regional bias.

Our synthesis was limited to English-language publications, which may have introduced language bias. Despite adherence to PRISMA guidelines, publication bias cannot be excluded. Substantial heterogeneity and risk-of-bias profiles prevented formal meta-analysis and limit certainty of conclusions. Lastly, this review was initiated prior to the widespread adoption of open-science practices and was therefore not preregistered in a database.

Recent developments in imaging transcriptomics offer opportunities to clarify mechanisms underlying clinical lithium response [164]. Detailed brain atlases enable identification of genes with spatial expression patterns that correlate with neuroimaging phenotypes; for example, disease risk-gene expression profiles of BD and three other psychiatric conditions have been compared with atlas-based VBM and resting-state fMRI connectomics, followed by gene-set enrichment to assess disease specificity [165].

To advance these developments, the field needs a robust connectomic phenotype associated with lithium response in BD. Newly introduced ultra-high field lithium MRI at 7 T potentially enables more detailed in vivo mapping of brain lithium concentrations and regional distribution patterns, thereby overcoming sensitivity and spatial-resolution constraints of lithium imaging at 3 T [73, 166]. Prior lithium imaging work suggests that brain and serum lithium concentrations correlate primarily in responders [167, 168], and preclinical data indicate that aberrant blood-brain barrier transport may underly between-subject differences in brain lithium concentrations [169]. Linking 7T-derived brain lithium distributions with clinical outcomes could help define a neuroimaging phenotype for lithium response in BD [170].

Identifying genes with spatial expression profiles that correlate with this phenotype may further elucidate the neurobiology of lithium response. Large-scale resources (e.g., BRAIN Initiative Cell Census Network) can support exploration of underlying genetic and epigenetic mechanisms [171]. Candidate genes can be informed by recent machine learning-enhanced studies of lithium response [172–175].

This systematic review examines neuroimaging findings related to lithium treatment in bipolar disorder, highlighting its potential to normalize frontolimbic circuitry, though with low-to-moderate certainty given study quality. The strongest evidence for lithium ‘normalization’ comes from cross-sectional designs (Table 1), which are more vulnerable to design-related bias, whereas randomized and within-subject designs, though fewer, provided more credible but less extensive support. Correlations between neuroimaging findings and clinical response remain limited. Future research should adopt greater methodological rigor, incorporate longer follow-up to capture duration-dependent effects, and leverage advanced techniques such as ultra-high field lithium MRI and imaging transcriptomics to identify connectomic phenotypes linked to clinical outcomes.
