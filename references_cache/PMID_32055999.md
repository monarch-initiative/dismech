# The monogenic basis of human tuberculosis.
**Authors:** Boisson-Dupuis S
**Journal:** Hum Genet (2020)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00439-020-02126-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00439-020-02126-6)

## Content

The pathogenesis of tuberculosis (TB) remains poorly understood, as no more than 5-10% of individuals infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis go on developing clinical disease. The contribution of human genetics to TB pathogenesis has been amply documented by means of classic genetics since the turn of the twentieth century. Over the last 20 years, following-up on the study of Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD), monogenic disorders have been found to underlie TB in some patients. Rare inborn errors of immunity, such as autosomal recessive, complete IL-12Rβ1 and TYK2 deficiencies, impairing the IL-12- and IL-23-dependent induction of IFN-γ, were initially identified in a few patients. More recently, homozygosity for a common variant of TYK2 (P1104A) that selectively disrupts cellular responses to IL-23 was found in two cohorts of TB patients. It shows high penetrance in areas endemic for TB and appears to be responsible for about 1% of TB cases in populations of European descent. Both rare and common genetic etiologies of TB affect IFN-γ immunity, providing a rationale for novel preventive and therapeutic approaches for TB control, including the use of recombinant IFN-γ.

Tuberculosis (TB) is an airborne infectious disease typically caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis). It is one of the oldest human diseases, having been found on Egyptian mummies dating back to 3,500 years BC. It has been given different names at different times and in different civilizations (e.g. phthisis from Greek, consumption from Latin, yaksma from Sanskrit in India, and chaky oncay in the Incan language)(Zink et al. 2001). TB is still endemic in many countries and, in 2017, 10 million new individuals developed the disease and 1.3 million people died from it (WHO 2018), making TB the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent (Furin et al. 2019). Historically, TB is also the greatest killer of Europeans, causing about 1 billion deaths in the last 2,000 years. The lack of an effective vaccine, the advent of multidrug-resistant TB, and the HIV epidemic have created an urgent need for new preventive and therapeutic strategies (Furin et al. 2019; WHO 2018). Improvements in our understanding of TB pathogenesis should help us to achieve this medical goal (Abel et al. 2018). Such studies are also of biological interest, given the long-standing relationship between humans and M. tuberculosis (Gagneux 2018). A quarter of the world’s population is estimated to be infected with M. tuberculosis (Houben and Dodd 2016), although this proportion could be over-evaluated (Behr et al. 2018, 2019). However, only a minority of infected individuals (no more than 5% to 10%) develops clinical disease (Ernst 2012; Flynn and Chan 2001; O’Garra et al. 2013). The development of TB reflects an impairment of host resistance to M. tuberculosis, as suggested by the much greater risk in patients with certain acquired immunodeficiencies, such as HIV infection and anti-TNF treatment (Kwan and Ernst 2011; Minozzi et al. 2016). However, the pathogenesis of TB in individuals with no overt immunodeficiency remains elusive. Evidence obtained since 1910, on the basis of classic genetics studies, such as human epidemiological surveys and familial aggregation studies (Abel et al. 2014; Abel et al. 2018; Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2015; Casanova and Abel 2002; Comstock 1978; Kallmann and Reisner 1943; Stead 1992) has revealed that TB has a strong genetic basis in humans. Experimental studies in various animal models have also provided compelling evidence in support of this conclusion (Fortin et al. 2007; Lurie 1941). However, classic association studies focusing on common variants of many candidate genes have not yielded consistent or reproducible results (Abel et al. 2014; Abel et al. 2018). One of the most convincing results obtained to date was perhaps that for NRAMP1 polymorphisms, which were found to have a heterogeneous effect across populations, epidemiological settings, ages and clinical phenotypes (Bellamy et al. 1998; Greenwood et al. 2000; Malik et al. 2005). Likewise, a positional cloning approach showed variants of the TOX gene to have a role restricted to early-onset pulmonary TB (Baghdadi et al. 2006; Grant et al. 2013). Genome-wide association studies also met with little success, identifying only a few variants with modest effects, and failing to replicate associations previously found in candidate gene studies (Abel et al. 2018).

Patient-based studies performed in parallel to these population-based studies made greater progress. A few rare primary immunodeficiencies (PIDs; i.e. monogenic inborn errors of immunity) (Bousfiha et al. 2018), such as chronic granulomatous disease, were found to confer a predisposition to TB (Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2015; Conti et al. 2016; Deerojanawong et al. 1997; Erdos et al. 2011; Frix and Bronson 1986; Galkina et al. 2007; Lee et al. 2016; Resnick et al. 2002; Spinner et al. 2014; Verma et al. 2008). Patients with these PIDs are typically prone to multiple infectious diseases. By contrast, MSMD is characterized by selective susceptibility to weakly virulent mycobacteria (BCG vaccines and environmental mycobacteria (EM)) (Bustamante et al. 2014; Rosain et al. 2018; Rosenzweig and Holland 2011). This condition is reviewed by Bustamante J, in this issue. The genetic study of MSMD paved the way for the demonstration that TB can also be genetic. Briefly, two kinds of presentations have been defined: (i) isolated MSMD, in which patients are susceptible only to mycobacterial disease, and (ii) syndromic MSMD, in which patients suffer from mycobacterial disease in the context of one or a few other clinical phenotypes (infectious or otherwise). Mutations of 15 genes (IFNGR1, IFNGR2, NEMO, IL12B, IL12RB1, IL12RB2, IL23R, CYBB, SPPL2A, STAT1, IRF8, ISG15, TYK2, RORC, JAK1) (Beaussant-Cohen et al. 2019; Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2018; Kong et al. 2018; Martinez-Barricarte et al. 2018) have been identified, defining 30 genetic etiologies according to the mode of inheritance, the presence or absence of mutant protein production and the precise biochemical abnormality. These findings highlight the tremendous genetic heterogeneity of MSMD, contrasting with its striking physiological homogeneity. Indeed, all genetic etiologies of MSMD impair IFN-γ-mediated immunity, by decreasing or abolishing IFN-γ production or the response to this cytokine (Dupuis et al. 2000). These elegant studies unequivocally revealed the crucial and non-redundant role of IFN-γ as an antimycobacterial molecule, confirming that it acts much more as a macrophage activating factor than as an antiviral IFN (Nathan et al. 1983). All MSMD patients harbor very rare (< 0.1%), or private variants (absent from the general population), with the exception of TYK2 P1104A (see below). This is consistent with the estimated prevalence of MSMD of about 1/50,000 births. Interestingly, incomplete clinical penetrance for MSMD (individuals with the morbid genotype but without the clinical phenotype) has been observed for most defects (Bustamante et al. 2014; de Beaucoudrey et al. 2010; Fieschi et al. 2003; Kreins et al. 2015). The only known genetic etiologies of MSMD displaying complete penetrance for which several families have been identified are AR complete IFN-γR1 and IFN-γR2 deficiencies (Jouanguy et al. 1996; Jouanguy et al. 1997a; Jouanguy et al. 2000; Martinez-Barricarte et al. 2014; Newport et al. 1996). Defects associated with a residual production of, or response to IFN-γ display incomplete penetrance. Strikingly, the levels of residual IFN-γ are inversely correlated with clinical penetrance, suggesting that human IFN-γ immunity is a quantitative trait determining the outcome of mycobacterial invasion (Dupuis et al. 2000).

The study of MSMD paved the way for the demonstration that TB could be genetic. The identification of siblings of MSMD probands carrying the same genetic defect as the index case, but suffering from TB, with or without the clinical phenotype of MSMD provided the necessary proof-of-principle (Aksu et al. 2001; Allende et al. 2001; Bustamante et al. 2011; Chapgier et al. 2006; de Beaucoudrey et al. 2010; Dorman et al. 2004; Edeer Karaca et al. 2012; Filipe-Santos et al. 2006; Jouanguy et al. 1997b; Prando et al. 2013; Sasaki 2002; Sologuren et al. 2011). Additional proof-of-concept was provided when several patients with TB but no familial history of clinical MSMD were found to carry causal mutations in MSMD genes, mainly in the most common etiology of MSMD, IL-12Rβ1 deficiency (Altare et al. 2001; Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2015; Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2011; Caragol et al. 2003; Özbek et al. 2005; Tabarsi et al. 2011). Indeed, two of 50 (4%) children with severe TB were found to display complete IL-12Rβ1 deficiency (Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2011). The patients carried homozygous very rare (R173W) or private (K305X) variants, abolishing IL-12Rβ1 expression at the cell surface and cellular responses to both IL-12 and IL-23, thereby decreasing the production of IFN-γ by lymphocytes (Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2011; de Beaucoudrey et al. 2010). Private and very rare homozygous mutations causing complete TYK2 deficiency were also recently discovered in two patients with isolated TB, and in patients with syndromic MSMD (Kilic et al. 2012; Kreins et al. 2015; Minegishi et al. 2006). TYK2 is a Janus kinase involved in four cytokine signaling pathways (those mediated by IL-12, IL-23, IFN-α and IL-10). The consequences of complete TYK2 deficiency are different according to the affected pathway: (i) Impaired responses to IL-12 and IL-23 (as in patients with IL-12Rβ1 deficiency) result in mycobacterial susceptibility (MSMD and TB) (Fieschi et al. 2003; Kreins et al. 2015), (ii) impaired responses to IFN-α lead to viral infections although less severe than those in patients with IFNAR1 or IFNAR2 deficiencies, probably due to residual responses to IFNs (Duncan et al. 2015; Hernandez et al. 2019; Hoyos-Bachiloglu et al. 2017), and (iii) impaired responses to IL-10 are clinically silent, probably due to residual responses to this cytokine (whereas patients with complete IL-10, IL-10RA and IL-10RB deficiencies suffer from early-onset colitis (Engelhardt and Grimbacher 2014; Engelhardt et al. 2013; Glocker et al. 2011; Mao et al. 2012)). Interestingly, only half the patients with complete TYK2 deficiency have suffered from BCG disease despite all being vaccinated with BCG, attesting to the incomplete penetrance of this defect for MSMD. Finally, two complete TYK2 deficient patients presented with TB as their sole clinical phenotype in two patients. These rare genetic etiologies of common infectious diseases provided proof-of-principle that TB can be monogenic, but do not account for TB as a global public health problem.

Following on from the study of complete TYK2 deficiency, the role of a common TYK2 missense variant (P1104A) was recently investigated. A strong enrichment in individuals homozygous for TYK2 P1104A was found in a cohort of TB patients originating from countries outside Europe in which TB is currently endemic (Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2018). The odds ratio (OR) of developing TB for homozygotes relative to ancestry-adjusted controls was 89.3 (95% CI, 14.7–1,725). Homozygous carriers also displayed a predisposition to MSMD, albeit to a lesser extent, with an OR of 23.53 (95% CI, 2.9–483) (Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2018). Penetrance for TB was estimated to be high (at least 50%) in areas of endemic disease, whereas that for MSMD is much lower, at no more than 0.5%, consistent with M. tuberculosis being much more virulent than BCG and EM. The minor allele frequency (MAF) of P1104A is 4.2% in the European population (from gnomAD), giving a frequency of homozygous individuals of about 1/600 in Europe. The MAF of P1104A is about 1–2% in other parts of the world, except in East Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, where it is much rarer, if not entirely absent. Based on ancient DNA data, the frequency of P1104A was found to have decreased in Europe over the last 4,000 years, accounting for its presence in modern-day Europeans and consistent with its purging by endemic TB. No disease has killed more Europeans than TB over the last 1,000 years. In cells, the TYK2 P1104A protein is produced in normal amounts and has normal scaffolding properties. However, its catalytic activity is impaired, and it cannot phosphorylate itself or any other JAK or STAT substrate. Nevertheless, it can still be phosphorylated by other JAKs. It therefore functions as a substrate, but not as an enzyme, which is specifically detrimental for the IL-23 pathway. Cells from patients homozygous for P1104A display impaired responses to IL-23, as severe as that in patients with complete TYK2 deficiency, whereas the other three cytokine pathways are unaffected (Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2018). Interestingly, both IL-12 and IL-23 independently stimulate the production of IFN-γ by various cell types (Martinez-Barricarte et al. 2018). Both IL-12Rβ2 (affecting only IL-12 response) and IL-23R (affecting only IL-23 responses) deficiencies are genetic etiologies of MSMD with a particularly low penetrance, relative to that of IL-12Rβ1 deficiency (affecting both IL-12 and IL-23 responses), which has a penetrance of about 50% (Fieschi et al. 2003; de Beaucoudrey et al. 2010; Martinez-Barricarte et al. 2018). Likewise, TYK2 P1104A homozygosity that specifically impairs IL-23-induced IFN-γ-mediated immunity results in susceptibility to MSMD with a low penetrance similar to that of IL23R and in susceptibility to TB with a high penetrance due to the higher virulence of M. tuberculosis.

The role of P1104A homozygosity in TB was confirmed in a large cohort of European ancestry (Kerner et al. 2019). Data from the United Kingdom (UK) Biobank cohort revealed that homozygosity for P1104A TYK2 was more frequent in TB patients than in TB-free individuals, with an OR of 5 (1.96–10.31). However, this OR was probably underestimated, as most of the controls in this study were not exposed to or infected with M. tuberculosis, as they were born between 1939 and 1970 and lived in the UK, a country in which TB endemicity declined rapidly over this period. Homozygosity for the TYK2 P1104A variant may underlie a sizable proportion of TB cases, perhaps accounting for ~1% of TB cases in Europeans and ~0.33% of cases in most other regions of the world (i.e., outside Europe, sub-Saharan Africa, and Eastern Asia). Homozygosity for P1104A is the first common monogenic cause of TB to have been identified. This disorder, which is present in about 1/600 individuals of European descent, is as common as hemochromatosis and is, therefore, the most common autosomal recessive disorder in this population. It is also interesting to consider its life-time impact, with an estimated relative risk (RR) between 8 and 16 (Kerner et al. 2019). A parallel can be made with the contribution of BRCA1 to breast cancer. The heterozygous BRCA1 genotype is found in about 1/1,000 women, whose RR for breast cancer is about 15, and this genotype accounts for about 1% of cases (Armstrong et al. 2019; Turnbull et al. 2018). It is estimated that TB may have killed up to 1 billion people in Europe over the last 2,000 years (Kerner et al. 2019), suggesting that about 10 million people may have died due to TYK2 P1104A homozygosity. Future studies based on ancient DNA should attempt to track the frequency trajectories of the P1104A allele over time and space, in different regions of the world, following up on our initial observations for a small population of ancient Europeans (Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2018). Interestingly, homozygosity for TYK2 P1104A was also found to have a strong protective effect (ORs from 0.1 to 0.3) against various inflammatory or autoimmune disorders (Dendrou et al. 2016; Diogo et al. 2015; International Genetics of Ankylosing Spondylitis Consortium et al. 2013; International Multiple Sclerosis Genetics Consortium et al. 2013; Jostins et al. 2012; Tsoi et al. 2012), providing another example of an allele with opposite effects in infectious diseases and auto-inflammatory/auto-immune conditions (Barreiro and Quintana-Murci 2010; Fodil et al. 2016; Langlais et al. 2017). TYK2 inhibitors are currently being developed to treat some of these conditions. It will therefore be important to assess the risk of TB before and during such treatment, as is currently the case for anti-TNF immunotherapy (Gadina et al. 2018; Minozzi et al. 2016; Papp et al. 2018).

Exciting and encouraging progress has been made toward understanding genetic predisposition to TB over the last two decades. Two types of monogenic predisposition were discovered: very rare or private mono- or biallelic mutations in a few patients (MSMD-causing genotypes, found in no more than 1/50,000 individuals), and homozygosity for a common variant in many more patients (a genotype found in 1/600 Europeans). These predispositions to weakly virulent (MSMD) and more virulent (TB) mycobacteria are allelic, and due to inborn errors of IFN-γ immunity. These findings add weight to the notion that IFN-γ-mediated immunity is a quantitative trait determining the outcome of mycobacterial infections (MSMD with high penetrance in severe defects and low penetrance in partial defects, whereas TB shows high penetrance for both severe and partial defects consistent with the low and high virulence of these two types of mycobacteria, respectively) (Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2015; Boisson-Dupuis et al. 2018; Bustamante et al. 2014; Dupuis et al. 2000; Martinez-Barricarte et al. 2018). Understanding genetic predisposition to TB is of clinical importance for the prevention and the treatment of the disease. It should make it easier to target individuals at high risk of TB when defining optimal cohorts for trials of candidate TB vaccines. It could also pave the way for the genetic counseling of families. Moreover, injections of recombinant IFN-γ may be beneficial in TB patients with impaired IFN-γ production (e.g. homozygotes for TYK2 P1104A), as it is in MSMD patients with IL-12Rβ1 deficiency (Alangari et al. 2011; Holland 2001; Holland et al. 1994) or other patients with unexplained deficiency (Coulter et al. 2007; Seneviratne et al. 2007). Many questions remain unanswered. What proportions of TB cases are due to monogenic predisposition caused by rare variants? By common variants? Are all monogenic forms of TB related to IFN-γ? What are the determinants of incomplete penetrance? Are there digenic or oligogenic forms of TB? What is the proportion of genetic cases at large? The next two decades will see us strive to define the human genetic architecture of TB.
