BACKGROUND: In alpha-gal syndrome (AGS), IgE antibodies form against the glycan
galactose-α-1,3-galactose (alpha-gal) in mammal products rather than food
proteins. Alpha-gal glycolipids and glycoproteins activate human basophils
sensitized with AGS plasma in an IgE-dependent fashion. However, it is unknown
whether alpha-gal specific IgE (sIgE), independent of other blood proteins, is
sufficient for mediating basophil and mast cell activation.
OBJECTIVE: We sought to determine whether alpha-gal antigens could activate
passively sensitized rat basophilic leukemia (RBL) SX-38 cells, which express
human IgE receptors and are commonly used to model allergen/IgE-mediated mast
cell activation in food protein allergy.
METHODS: Using the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats
technology, we created a novel, alpha-gal-deficient RBL cell line, alpha-gal
knockout RBL SX-38, passively sensitizing cells with sera from AGS donors or
with novel alpha-gal sIgE clones, and then stimulated with alpha-gal
glycoproteins. To assess effector cell activation, we measured cell surface
expression of activation marker CD63 by flow cytometry and mediator release
through β-hexosaminidase release assays.
RESULTS: After alpha-gal antigen stimulation, the percentage of CD63+ alpha-gal
knockout RBL SX-38 cells sensitized with AGS sera increased 3-fold compared with
cells sensitized with control serum. Select human AGS IgE clones facilitated
alpha-gal antigen-dependent and antigen-independent CD63 upregulation. Cells
sensitized with pooled AGS sera released β-hexosaminidase in an
alpha-gal-independent fashion. We saw no β-hexosaminidase release above
background in cells sensitized with alpha-gal sIgE clones.
CONCLUSIONS: Certain alpha-gal-specific human IgE clones may partially activate
allergic effector cells independent of antigen, potentially lowering thresholds
for subsequent alpha-gal-induced or antigen-independent allergic effector cell
degranulation. This may affect duration and severity of allergic symptoms in
patients with AGS.

Select alpha-gal-specific human IgE clones may partially activate allergic effector cells independent of antigen, potentially lowering thresholds for subsequent alpha-gal-induced or antigen-independent allergic effector cell degranulation.

Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) is a leading driver of anaphylaxis in adults(1). In AGS, an allergy to galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose (alpha-gal) found in mammalian (“red”) meat, immunoglobulin (Ig)E antibodies form against a glycan (alpha-gal) rather than a food protein (1). Globally, the development of alpha-gal-specific IgE (alpha-gal sIgE) has been linked to bites from thirteen different tick species, includingIxodes ricinusin Europe (2) and the ectoparasiteAmblyomma americanum(also known as lone star ticks) in the US (2–4).

We have shown that alpha-gal glycolipids and glycoproteins activate human basophils sensitizedin vitrowith plasma from AGS patients in a similar timeframe as food protein allergen and in an IgE-dependent fashion (5). Using an indirect basophil activation test (BAT), in which healthy donor basophils were stripped of native IgE and passively sensitized with plasma from volunteers with AGS, we showed that in an IgE-dependent fashion, alpha-gal-glycosylated compounds increased the percentage of human basophils expressing CD63 (5), a tetraspanin protein enriched in late endosomes, lysosomes, and lysosomal related organelles (LROs), including secretory granules in mast cells and basophils (6,7). Alpha-gal glycolipids and glycoproteins could also directly induce CD63 upregulation in basophils from a European cohort of participants with AGS and history of bites fromIxodes ricinus(8,9). Patient symptomatology and confirmatory skin prick and intradermal testing using alpha-gal-containing antigens implicate mast cell activation and degranulation in AGS (10), but there are no published studies demonstrating alpha-gal-induced activation of mast cellsin vitro.

We previously observed that alpha-gal sIgE concentrations in human AGS sera used to passively sensitize basophils in indirect BAT did not correlate with frequency of activated basophils, reflecting the clinical observation that serum concentrations of alpha-gal sIgE do not consistently correlate with the severity of allergic reactions in AGS (5). Whether the quality of the alpha-gal sIgE clones, rather than sIgE quantity, determines the nature and magnitude of allergic effector cell activation is unknown. Additionally, whether alpha-gal sIgE antibodies alone, independent of human blood components, are sufficient for mediating basophil and mast cell activation is not known.

The rat basophil leukemia cell line RBL SX-38 expressing the human IgE receptor α, β, and γ chains (11), is morphologically distinct from basophils and mast cells. However, it shares phenotypic and functional characteristics with both allergic effector cell types. Since RBL SX-38 cells, like mast cells, express c-kit and use the same cellular machinery as mast cells to facilitate secretory granule transit (12), they are frequently used to model the mast cell-driven allergic effector phase in IgE-allergen interactions, including food protein allergy (11). RBL SX-38-based allergic effector cell activation testing may also increase the convenience of these types of assays while reducing confounders or constraints. BAT testing, for example can be confounded by non-releaser primary human basophils that do not upregulate CD63 despite allergen stimulation in up to 25% of individuals (13,14). While antigen-induced degranulation of human basophils can also be assessed through beta-hexosaminidase or histamine release (13), these tests are rarely performed since basophils must be used in BAT assays within 4 to 24 hours after collection, constraining the timing and decreasing convenience of the assay (15). By contrast, antigen-induced mediator releasein vitrofrom RBL SX-38 cells, including beta-hexosaminidase, is frequently measured(11,12,16,17).

We have previously shown that RBL SX-38 cells can be sensitized with novel human monoclonal IgE antibodies to the food-protein allergen, peanut. Peanut-sensitized RBL SX-38 cells degranulated, releasing beta-hexosaminidase after stimulation with crude peanut extract, providing anin vitromodel of the allergic effector phase in peanut allergy (16). In this study, we used CRISPR to create a novel, alpha-gal-deficient RBL cell line, AGKO RBL SX-38. Our goal was to model the AGS effector phase using a cell culture system widely used to study food-protein-induced allergic effector cell activation without the complication of endogenous, rat-derived alpha-gal antigen in the model effector cells. We further investigated the functionality of novel alpha-gal-specific human IgE monoclonal antibodies (18). We evaluated the ability of alpha-gal sIgE clones to activate allergic effector cells, independent of human sera, testing whether IgE quality and clonality determine the nature of allergic effector cell activation in AGS.

Rat basophilic leukemic (RBL) SX-38 cells transfected with human FcεRI receptors (α, β and γ chains) were provided by Dr. Michelle Hernandez (UNC-Chapel Hill), with permission for use from Dr. Jean-Pierre Kinet (Beth Israel Deaconess Hospital). RBL SX-38 cells were maintained in MEM (Gibco), supplemented with 10% FBS (Corning), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 1.2 mg/mL G418 (Geneticin, Corning), at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To achieve higher FcεRI expression, the concentration of G418 was increased from 1.2 mg/mL to 2.4 mg/mL during culture. FBS and G418 were removed at 24h and 48h, respectively, prior to functional assays.

Laboratory of Allergic Diseases 2 (LAD2) cells (gifted by Arnold Kirshenbaum (NIH/NIAID) via Soman Abraham (Duke)) were thawed at room temperature and transferred to a flask with culture medium (StemPro-34, StemPro-34 nutrient supplement (GIBCO), 2 mM L-Glutamine (GIBCO), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (GIBCO)) and 200 ng/mL stem cell factor (SCF, GIBCO) at 37°C, 5% CO2. Cells were subcultured weekly using the culture medium with 100 ng/mL SCF and maintained at 37°C, 5% CO2. Cells were grown for 3 to 4 weeks before experiments.

Our strategy to knock out theGgta1gene involved targeting the coding region of rat terminal exon 6 with two guide RNAs (gRNAs) designed to delete 527 bp of the coding sequence (Fig 1). Cas9 gRNAs targeting the 5’ and 3’ regions of exon 6 were designed with Benchling software and cloned into a T7 promoter vector after activity testing and selection. RBL SX-38 cells were grown to confluence in a T75 flask, and 2×106cells were nucleofected with a CRISPR reagent mix comprised of 10 μM Cas9 protein and 30 μM of each gRNA using a nucleofection kit (V4XC-2032, SF cell line 4D-Nucleofector kit, Lonza) and the Lonza RBL-2H3 cell protocol. Nucleofected cells were plated in a T25 flask containing 5 mL of RBL SX-38 media (MEM with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 1.2 mg/mL geneticin). Cells were cultured until 80% confluence and then prepared for sorting using a FACS Aria II (Becton Dickinson) to dispense single cells into a 96-well plate. Next, genomic DNA was prepared from the expanded clones and screened using PCR to confirm the presence of the deletion allele and the absence of the wild-type allele (data not shown). Six clones (A3, A7, B7, 1F10, 1F12, 1G4) were identified. Human FcεRI expression and human IgE binding was similar between A3 and the parent RBL SX-38 cell line (seeFigure 1D), so A3 AGKO RBL SX-38 cell line was used for subsequent experiments.

To assess the presence of α-gal-glycoproteins in parent RBL SX-38 and AGKO RBL SX-38 clones and LAD2 cells, we prepared cell lysates using lysing buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, cat# 9803S) and fresh protease inhibitor and incubated the mixture on ice for 30 minutes. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 145,000 g for 15 minutes, and the protein concentration was quantified by BCA protein assay (Thermo Scientific, cat#23225). 5 μg of cell lysate was mixed with 4X SDS loading buffer and boiled at 100°C for 10 minutes, before being fractionated on 4% to 20% SDS-PAGE gels (Invitrogen, 150V for 90 minutes). After transfer to a nitrocellulose membrane, 5% BSA (diluted with TBST: 10mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20) was used to block non-specific binding and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. Membranes were incubated with an anti-α-gal (anti-galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose-beta-1,4-N-acetylglucosamine) monoclonal antibody (M86; Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA, 1:100) at 4°C overnight, followed by a goat anti-mouse IgM HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000, incubated at room temperature for 1 hour).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) experiments were conducted at 20 °C on a Wyatt DynaPro PlateReader-II. Samples of human monoclonal anti-α-Gal-IgE antibody clones 10H8, 1D13, 16D9, 7G6, 5E15, and peanut-specific human IgE clones 13D9 and 16A8 were prepared at a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL and run in a 384-well microplate with clear, flat-bottom square wells. 10 acquisition scans were performed on each sample, each with a 5-second acquisition time. Auto-attenuation was enabled for all acquisitions.

For binding analyses of human anti-α-gal-IgE to different glycoproteins, a 96-well plate was coated with 20 μg/mL of beef thyroglobulin (BTG, Sigma, cat#T100-500MG), α-gal-human serum albumin (α-gal-HSA, Dextra, NGP3203), and cetuximab (Eli Lilly) or peanut extract and incubated overnight at 4°C. To block nonspecific binding, the plate was blocked with 2% DPBS-BSA (Merck, cat#126575-100MG) for 2 hours at room temperature. Human monoclonal anti-α-Gal-IgE antibody clones 10H8, 1D13, 16D9, 7G6, AGS sera, healthy control sera, and peanut-specific human IgE clone 16A8(16,19,20) were added and plates incubated at room temperature for another 2 hours. HRP-conjugated anti-human IgE Fc antibody (Southern Biotech, cat#9160-05, diluted in 1% DPBS-BSA to 1:8000) was added and the 96-well plates developed using 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), with optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm.

On Day 1, A3 AGKO RBL SX-38 cells (2×104per well) were seeded in MEM medium with 10% FBS (without G418) and incubated overnight at 37°C in 96-well plates. On Day 2, cell culture supernatant was removed, and cells were sensitized with MEM medium (without FBS) containing human monoclonal anti-α-Gal-IgE antibodies 10H8, 16D9, 1D13, 7G6; peanut-specific human monoclonal IgE antibodies 2C9, 16A8, or 38B7 (antibody concentration ranged from 500ng/ml – 1000 ng/ml); AGS sera (diluted 1:5), and normal control sera (1:5) overnight at 37°C. On Day 3, the 96-well plates were washed twice with HBSS buffer (Gibco) containing 20mM HEPES to remove excess IgE antibodies. The cells were then stimulated with buffer (HBSS and 20 mM HEPES), BTG (10, 50 and 100 μg/mL), α-Gal-HSA (10, 50 and 100 μg/mL), and cetuximab (10, 50 and 100 μg/mL) or peanut extract (1 or 5 μg/mL) for 45 minutes at 37°C without CO2. In some experiments, contaminating LPS was removed from the BTG preparation using a Detoxi-Gel endotoxin removal column (Pierce). The cells were treated with 1% Triton X-100 in DPBS buffer to evaluate maximum β-hexosaminidase release. The 96-well plates were centrifuged at 4°C for 10 minutes to stop AGKO RBL SX-38 cell activation. To measure β-hexosaminidase release, 50 μL of supernatant was transferred into a new 96-well plate and mixed well with 50 μL of p-nitrophenyl-N-acetyl-β-d-glucosaminide (Sigma, cat#N9376, 2mM in 0.1 M sodium citrate, pH 4.5). The mixture was incubated for 1 hour at 37°C without CO2. The reaction was stopped by adding 100 μL of 0.4 M glycine (pH 10.8), and the OD values were read at 405 nm.

To assess the ability of monoclonal α-Gal-IgE antibodies to bind to parent RBL SX-38 and AGKO RBL SX-38 cells, RBL cells (1×105per well) were seeded in MEM medium (without FBS and G418) in 24-well plates and treated overnight with MEM medium (without FBS) containing human monoclonal anti-α-Gal-IgE antibody 16D9. The next day, cells were harvested using cell scrapers and transferred into 5 mL round bottom polystyrene FACS tubes. After washing the cells with FACS buffer (DPBS + 2% FBS + 2mM EDTA), a mixture of Zombie-red (1:1000, Biolegend, cat#423109), BV421-anti-human FcεRI (1:300, Biolegend, clone#AER-37), and AF647-anti-human IgE (1:200, Biolegend, clone#MHE-18) antibodies was added and cells incubated on ice for 30 minutes.

To analyze CD63 expression, AGKO RBL SX-38 cells were seeded in 24-well plates as described above, sensitized overnight with human sera (diluted 1:5) or with human monoclonal IgE antibodies in MEM medium (without FBS), and on day 3, washed to remove unbound IgE. Cells were then stimulated with BTG (50μg/mL), α-Gal-HSA (50 μg/mL), and cetuximab (50 μg/mL) or peanut extract for 45 minutes at 37°C without CO2. After alpha-gal antigen or peanut antigen stimulation, the cells were harvested using cell scrapers, washed twice in 5 mL round bottom polystyrene FACS tubes, and incubated with a mixture of Zombie-red (1:1000, Biolegend, cat#423109), BV421-anti-human FcεRI (1:300, Biolegend, clone#AER-37), and FITC-anti-rat CD63 (5 μL/tube, Miltenyi Biotec, clone#REA444) for 30 minutes on ice. FACS analysis was performed on an LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences), and data analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8).

Statistical significance was determined using GraphPad Prism (Version 10). One-way ANOVA followed by multiple comparisons tests was used to compare three or more independent groups. Two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test were used for comparisons of outcomes affected by two independent factors. APvalue less than 0.05 was considered significant. Outlier samples were identified using the robust regression and outlier removal (ROUT) with a false discovery rate (Q value) of 5%.

Since RBL SX-38 cells contain alpha-gal compounds, we used CRISPR technology to edit the ratGgta1gene, creating an RBL SX-38 cell line devoid of functional galactosyltransferase and unable to synthesize alpha-gal (“alpha-gal knockout” or AGKO RBL SX-38,Figure 1A). We confirmed that alpha-gal was not detected on glycoproteins derived from any of six AGKO RBL SX-38 clones (Figure 1B) using mouse anti-alpha-gal IgM clone M86 (21) and western blot. We saw no gross differences in cell morphology among AGKO RBL SX-38 clones and the parent RBL SX-38 cells (Figure 1C). Using flow cytometry, we identified two AGKO RBL SX-38 clones, A3 and 1G4, with similar expression of the human IgE receptor, FcεRI (hFcεRI) to parent RBL SX-38 cells (Figure 1D). In addition, we used novel, alpha-gal-specific human IgE monoclonal antibody (mAb) 16D9 (18) to sensitize the AGKO RBL SX-38 cell clones and the parent RBL SX-38 cells and saw comparable IgE binding amongst clones A3, 1G4 and the parent RBL SX-38 cells (Figure 1E). We used the A3 AGKO RBL SX-38 clone for subsequent investigations.

CD63 (LAMP3) upregulation on basophils and mast cells is frequently used as a readout for allergic effector cell activation, including in AGS (5,8,22). To determine whether alpha-gal compounds could activate AGKO RBL SX-38 cells sensitized with alpha-gal sIgE antibodies, we passively sensitized A3 cells overnight with healthy control serum (Table 1, sample C6) or pooled sera from 17 volunteers with physician-diagnosed AGS (seeTable 1, AGS Sera Pool A and diagramed inFigure 2A). The total IgE concentration of the pooled sera was 400 kU/L; alpha-gal sIgE concentration was 4 kU/L. Sensitized cells were stimulated with buffer alone or with alpha-gal glycoproteins: beef thyroglobulin (BTG), alpha-gal conjugated to human serum albumin (alpha-gal-HSA), and the cancer chemotherapeutic monoclonal antibody cetuximab (23,24). Following stimulation with alpha-gal, the frequency of CD63+AGKO RBL SX-38 A3 cells sensitized with AGS sera Pool A increased more than 3-fold compared to cells sensitized with control serum, as detected by flow cytometry (Figure 2B,C). When A3 cells were sensitized with individual AGS sera (Table 1, samples B1-B6), the mean percentage of activated CD63+A3 cells doubled compared to cells sensitized with individual control sera (Table 1, samples C1-C6 andFigure 2D), even after stimulation with buffer alone. Stimulating A3 cells sensitized with three of the six individual AGS sera (samples B3, B5, and B6) with alpha-gal glycoproteins, did not induce a sizeable increase in percentage of CD63+A3 cells above what was already seen with sensitization alone (SeeFigure E1in theOnline Repository). In A3 cells sensitized with the remaining sera (samples B1, B2, B4) alpha-gal glycoprotein stimulation induced modest alpha-gal-specific increases in percentages of CD63+A3 cells compared to buffer stimulation (Figure E1,Online Repository). When samples identified as outliers using the robust regression and outlier removal (ROUT) with a false discovery rate (Q value) of 5%, were excluded (i.e., AGS serum sample B3 and control sample C3), stimulating AGKO RBL SX-38 A3 cells sensitized with individual AGS sera with alpha-gal glycoproteins increased the mean percentage of CD63+cells by 1.3-fold compared to buffer stimulation alone (Figure 2D).

Thus, we also determined the relative mass abundance of sIgE mAb aggregates with hydrodynamic sizes greater than 20 nm to assess whether human alpha-gal sIgE mAbs that induced antigen-independent CD63 expression preparations contained antibody aggregates 20 nm or larger. Both alpha-gal sIgE clones 10H8 and 16D9 preparations had the highest percentage of particles 100 nm or greater (Figure 4B). For Alpha-gal sIgE mAbs 1D13 and 7G6, the largest relative mass abundance of particles had peak hydrodynamic sizes between 1 and 10 nm (Figure 4B). Although 10H8 and 16D9 both had high frequencies of particles 100 nm or greater, only 16D9 induced alpha-gal-independent upregulation of CD63 on A3 cells (Figure 3B). Conversely, alpha-gal sIgE clones 1D13 and 7G6 with peak hydrodynamic sizes of 10 nm or less could also induce CD63 expression on A3 cells independent of alpha-gal, though to lesser levels than 16D9 (Figure 3B). We hypothesized that the increased percentages of activated CD63+RBL SX-38 cells following sensitization with alpha-gal sIgE mAbs or human AGS sera—without additional cognate antigen stimulation—was influenced by the breadth of antigen recognition by the alpha-gal sIgE mAbs. To test this, we used enzyme linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) to characterize the alpha-gal glycoprotein binding profiles of the IgE antibodies. We used different alpha-gal glycoproteins (BTG, cetuximab, alpha-gal HSA) and peanut as capture antigens for the ELISA. 16D9, 7G6, 1D13, and 10H8 showed distinct alpha-gal glycoprotein binding patterns (Figure 4C). All alpha-gal IgE clones tested (16D9, 7G6, 10H8, 1D13) and pooled AGS sera (Sera Pool A) bound cetuximab and BTG (Figure 4C). However, 1D13 and 10H8 did not bind the synthetic glycoprotein alpha-gal-HSA, in contrast to 16D9, 7G6, and pooled AGS sera (Figure 4C). Thus, 16D9 and 7G6, like pooled AGS sera, demonstrated the greatest breadth of alpha-gal antigen binding. Sensitization with human AGS sera alone or with these clones alone, could drive alpha-gal antigen-independent and alpha-gal antigen-dependent increases in CD63+AGKO RBL SX-38 A3 cells (Figures 2and3). By contrast, 1D13 and 10H8, the clones with the narrowest glycan-binding breadth (Figure 4C), showed significantly less (1D13) or no (10H8) antigen-independent upregulation of CD63 compared to 16D9 and 7G6 (Figure 3CandF). A3 cells sensitized with 1D13 also showed the greatest alpha-gal antigen-specific increase in percentages of CD63+activated cells after alpha-gal glycoprotein stimulation (Figure 3F). Non-AGS control sera and the peanut sIgE mAb 16A8 did not bind to any of the alpha-gal glycoproteins. Peanut sIgE mAb 16A8 did bind to peanut (Figure 4C). Taken together, these data suggested that the breadth of antigen recognition by each alpha-gal sIgE clone was associated with the ability of the alpha-gal sIgE clone to enhance the frequency of CD63+activated A3 cells with sensitization alone.

A3 cells sensitized with human peanut sIgE mAbs 2C9 and 16A8 or 38B7 alone did release significantly higher levels of β-hexosaminidase when stimulated with peanut extract, compared to cells sensitized with peanut sIgE mAbs and stimulated with buffer (Figure 6E), as previously described (16,19,20), and compared to cells sensitized with alpha-gal sIgE mAbs (Figure E2CandD,Online Repository). We created a novel, alpha-gal-deficient RBL cell line, AGKO RBL SX-38. These cells, like parent RBL SX-38 cells, can be sensitized with human sera or human monoclonal IgE antibodies. Additionally, activation and mediator release can be assessed by measuring CD63 expression through flow cytometry and β-hexosaminidase release through colorimetric assays. Using this system, we showed that sensitizing alpha-gal-deficient RBL SX-38 cells with AGS sera upregulated CD63 expression and induced degranulation independent of alpha-gal antigen to a greater extent than sensitizing with control sera. We also observed alpha-gal antigen-specific activation and degranulation of RBL SX-38 cells sensitized with individual or pooled sera from participants with AGS. The alpha-gal-induced activation and degranulation varied across individual samples and between the two AGS sera pools tested. In addition, the highest alpha-gal sIgE concentrations or alpha-gal sIgE : total IgE ratios did not always correspond to the greatest levels of allergic effector cell activation and mediator release (Figures E1andE3), similar to our previous observations with respect to alpha-gal-mediated activation of human basophils (5). Our findings suggest that alpha-gal sIgE quality and clonality, rather than quantity, may determine the nature of allergic effector cell activation in AGS.

To begin to address this, we used the alpha-gal-deficient RBL SX-38 cells to evaluate the function of novel alpha-gal-specific human IgE monoclonal antibodies produced by B cell hybridomas generated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of alpha-gal allergic individuals (18). We showed functional diversity in human alpha-gal sIgE clones. AGS sera and alpha-gal sIgE clones 16D9 and 7G6 promoted alpha-gal-induced and antigen-independent activation of AGKO RBL SX-38 cells, reflected by increased CD63 surface expression (Figures 2and3). Sensitization with monoclonal alpha-gal sIgE antibody 1D13 primarily promoted alpha-gal antigen-dependent CD63 upregulation. Antigen-independent CD63 expression induced by sensitization alone with 1D13 was much less than that triggered by sensitization with 16D9 and 7G6 (Figure 3). In contrast to alpha-gal sIgE clones 16D9, 7G6, and 1D13, 10H8 did not facilitate alpha-gal antigen-dependent or independent increases in CD63 (Figure 3).

Pre-formed, alpha-gal-specific human IgE aggregates were unlikely to be driving antigen-independent, IgE-mediated CD63 upregulation. We found comparable percentages of aggregates in preparations of clones 10H8 and 16D9, while clones 7G6 and 1D13 had no aggregates(Figure 4AandB). Yet 16D9, 7G6, and to a lesser extent 1D13, but not 10H8, induced CD63 upregulation in an antigen-independent fashion (Figure 3). This suggested that antigen-independent CD63 upregulation did not depend on antibody aggregates.

Pooled AGS sera and all alpha-gal sIgE clones tested bound to cetuximab and BTG (Figure 4C). However, 1D13 and 10H8 did not bind the synthetic glycoprotein alpha-gal-HSA, in contrast to 16D9, 7G6, and pooled alpha-gal sera (Figure 4C). The differences in whether and how alpha-gal sIgE clones activated AGKO RBL SX-38 cells may reflect this differential glycan-binding breadth. Notably, the alpha-gal sIgE mAbs 16D9 and 7G6, with the ability to induce alpha-gal antigen-independent CD63 expression on par with AGS sera (Figure 2and3), had alpha-glycoprotein binding patterns similar to human AGS sera. By contrast, clones 1D13 and 10H8 had narrower alpha-gal glycoprotein binding breadth (Figure 4C) which corresponded with little or no alpha-gal antigen-independent CD63 upregulation. Given their wider binding breadth, alpha-gal sIgE clones 16D9 and 7G6 may allow endogenous, RBL-derived, non-alpha-gal glycans to be bound, resulting in background activation of sensitized RBL cells. Addition of exogenous alpha-gal enhances activation. By contrast, sensitization alone with 1D13 and 10H8, clones with the narrowest glycan-binding breadth, induced little or no activation of AGKO RBL cells. Rather, 1D13 activated AGKO RBL SX-38 cells primarily in an alpha-gal-dependent fashion while 10H8 did not, even after stimulation with alpha-gal compounds. Taken together, these data suggest that differences in alpha-gal epitope specificity, binding affinity, or charge and conformation of Fab hypervariable regions of each IgE clone may influence capacity to aggregate IgE-FcεRI complexes, as proposed for cytokinergic IgE that induce mast cell cytokine production independent of antigen stimulation (27).

Sensitization with human AGS sera induced β-hexosaminidase release even without alpha-gal-antigen stimulation. Alpha-gal-specific β-hexosaminidase release was not observed in this system except at beef thyroglobulin or cetuximab concentrations of 50–100 μg/ml (Figure 5). This finding is consistent with previously published data suggesting that robust alpha-gal-induced allergic effector cell activationin vitrofrequently requires alpha-gal-glycoprotein concentrations 10 to 100-fold higher than the concentrations needed for food-protein-induced allergic effector cell activation (8,16,18). Notably, in this system, sensitizing with alpha-gal sIgE clones, independent from AGS sera, did not induce β-hexosaminidase release above background regardless of whether RBL SX-38 cells were subsequently stimulated with alpha-gal (Figure 6).

Taken together, our observations suggest that for some alpha-gal sIgE clones attached to the IgE receptor (e.g. 1D13), alpha-gal-antigen stimulation is required to induce optimal signal transduction upstream of the migration of CD63+lysosomal organelles to the cell surface. However, for other alpha-gal sIgE clones (e.g. 16D9, 7G6), binding to FcεRI may position CD63-containing granules at the cell surface, irrespective of stimulation with alpha-gal antigens. In either case, additional components in AGS sera are needed to promote RBL degranulation with mediator release, represented in this system as β-hexosaminidase release.

Previous studies using neutralizing antibodies against CD63 in RBL cell lines have shown that even though CD63 upregulation promotes robust degranulation and mediator release, functional CD63 is not required for mediator release. β-hexosaminidase release induced by PMA / ionomycin stimulation that bypasses IgE/IgE receptor signaling did not require functional CD63 (28). β-hexosaminidase release mediated by antigen binding to IgE/IgE receptor complexes on RBL cell surfaces also occurred in the absence of functional CD63 but was significantly blunted compared to β-hexosaminidase release in the presence of functional CD63 (28). Passive cutaneous anaphylaxis induced by cutaneous injection of dinitrophenol (DNP)-HSA into rats sensitized with DNP sIgE occurred in rats treated with anti-CD63, albeit reduced in magnitude by 50% (29). These studies suggest that mediator release by allergic effector cells can be divorced from CD63 expression and function in these rodent model systems, although the magnitude of mediator release is diminished.

We speculate that in our AGKO RBL SX-38 system, the discrepancy between CD63 and β-hexosaminidase release with both peanut and alpha-gal could be due to the nature of the food-antigen/food-specific IgE interaction. Future studies are required to determine whether high affinity interactions between peanut and peanut-specific IgE in this system activate signal transduction pathways that facilitate secretory granule and mediator release independent of, or at least, less dependent on CD63 translocation from lysosomes/endosomes to the cell membrane. By contrast, lower affinity interactions between alpha-gal and alpha-gal sIgE bound to IgE receptors on the RBL cell surface may promote initial membrane mixing between secretory granules and the cell surface membranes with translocation of CD63 to the cell surface and docking of secretory granules below the cell surface, but not complete fusion with the cell membrane. This phenomenon has been described previously in RBL cells as an explanation for why allergic and inflammatory mediators are not always completely released from RBL cells after antigen stimulation (30). We speculate that additional factors in human sera may stabilize and enhance interactions between alpha-gal sIgE and IgE receptors, increasing recruitment of proximal signal transduction molecules like LYN and SYK, needed for subsequent mobilization of the machinery for secretory granule docking, fusion, and full degranulation. This may happen independently of alpha-gal antigen with some sera samples, while the addition of alpha-gal antigen helps bolster the AGS-serum driven degranulation response with other serum samples.

Stabilizing components in the sera from alpha-gal allergic volunteers may include alpha-gal-specific non-IgE antibodies (IgG or IgM) that either enhance alpha-gal sIgE mAb Fc-binding to hFcεRI or Fab-binding to alpha-gal-compounds, as has been described for birch pollen allergen Bet v1 (31). Non-IgE antibodies may also enhance aggregation of hFcεRI-alpha-gal sIgE mAb complexes more efficiently than alpha-gal sIgE mAb alone, through super-crosslinking, also previously described for Bet v1-induced human mast cell degranulation (32). IgG or IgM-mediated enhancements to IgE-allergen binding and/or IgE-hFcεRI receptor aggregation may explain why AGS sera, but not individual alpha-gal sIgE mAbs, or pairs or trios of alpha-gal sIgE mAbs, facilitate β-hexosaminidase release from alpha-gal-sensitized allergic effector cells. Cytokines or other pro-inflammatory factors induced by tick bite in AGS patient sera, may also promote AGKO RBL SX-38 degranulation.

We cannot rule out that rat-derived AGKO RBL cells may possess other glycans recognized by polyclonal IgE in human sera from AGS participants facilitating sufficient hFcεRI crosslinking, independent of alpha-gal antigen, to drive β-hexosaminidase release. Indeed, the ELISA assays performed with different alpha-gal sIgE clones and pooled AGS sera, reinforce that even the different alpha-gal sIgE clones demonstrated variability in their alpha-gal-glycoprotein binding breadth. The breadth of glycoprotein binding exhibited by alpha-gal-specific and non-alpha-gal-specific IgE in human sera may allow for non-alpha-gal glycans on the AGKO RBL cells and, presumably, in consumed mammalian products to activate alpha-gal sensitized allergic effector cells.

Detailed molecular and structural characterization of the alpha-gal sIgE clones are needed to identify molecular determinants of alpha-gal sIgE-mediated anaphylaxis. These include IgE antibody affinity, specific glycan epitopes bound, the affinity of sIgE clones for alpha-gal glycans and positioning of the glycans in the binding pockets of these IgE clones, similar to those published for peanut-specific IgE clones (19,20). In addition, differences in glycosylation of the IgE antibodies themselves may affect IgE binding to mast cells and basophils and signaling pathways activated downstream of IgE/IgE receptor interactions (33). This could also impact the ability of select alpha-gal sIgE antibodies to drive CD63 expression, but not degranulation, in the RBL SX-38 system in both an antigen-specific and an antigen-independent fashion not seen with the peanut-specific monoclonal antibodies.

Characterizing the functional capacity of different alpha-gal sIgE clones will provide helpful information for future diagnostic tests where the presence or predominance of a particular alpha-gal sIgE antibody clone could be used as a diagnostic predictor of the severity of AGS allergic reactions. Additionally, our finding that certain IgE clones appear to cause allergic effector cell activation in both alpha-gal antigen-dependent and -independent ways, could have prognostic value. The presence of specific alpha-gal sIgE clones may serve as a biomarker or predictor of patients who transition from alpha-gal antigen-driven mast cell activation to alpha-gal allergen-independent activation.

Similar to what has been published by our group and others (5,8,9), we demonstrate that alpha-gal antigens can induce effector cell activation within the same time frame as protein allergen inin vitroorex vivostudies. This continues to support the idea that the delay in absorption, processing and trafficking of alpha-gal antigen to the allergic effector cell likely contributes to the delay in symptom onset in AGS.

While RBL cells are widely used to model allergic effector cells, including mast cells (12), they do not fully represent primary mast cells, which are typically suspended in culture rather than adherent, and heterogeneous in morphology and size (12,34). Future studies using human mast cell lines or primary human mast cells and alpha-gal sIgE clones or AGS sera are needed to assess how alpha-gal sIgE can activate mast cells in an alpha-gal antigen-independent fashion and implications for disease duration and severity in patients with AGS. Understanding the functional capacity of diverse alpha-gal sIgE clones may have diagnostic and prognostic value in the future for managing patients with AGS.
