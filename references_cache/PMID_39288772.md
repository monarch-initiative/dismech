# Genetic profiles and clinical features in subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphomas.
**Authors:** Okamura Y, Makishima K, Suehara Y, Suma S, Abe Y, Matsuoka R, Sakamoto T, Hattori K, Yokoyama Y, Kato T, Nanmoku T, Iwasaki T, Nishiyama K, Kato K, Takeuchi Y, Makishima H, Nakamura N, Chiba S, Sakata-Yanagimoto M
**Journal:** Cancer Sci (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cas.16345](https://doi.org/10.1111/cas.16345)

## Content

Subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma (SPTCL) is a rare peripheral T-cell lymphoma characterized by cutaneous lesions and immunologic manifestations. The five-year survival rate of SPTCL has been reported to be over 80%, indicating a favorable prognosis. Recent studies have uncovered recurrent germline variants in HAVCR2, encoding an immunomodulator. In this study, we integrated whole-exome sequencing data from 60 samples collected from 36 SPTCL patients, encompassing six patients of our cohort and 30 patients of publicly available data. We identified 138 somatic mutations in skin tumors of 24 patients and HAVCR2 germline mutations in 23 of 29 patients. HAVCR2 p.Tyr82Cys mutations were identified in four of six Japanese patients. During the clinical courses of four patients, cyclophosphamide, hydroxydaunomycin, vincristine, and prednisone were administered to all patients, but it resulted in incomplete responses in all four patients. However, disease conditions of all patients remained stable with additional treatment, including autologous peripheral blood stem cell transplantation. Over a 7.5-year median follow-up, one patient developed autoimmune-related diseases, while one developed other hematological malignancy, resulting in death. To our knowledge, this is the first report of recurrent HAVCR2 germline mutations in Japanese patients, suggesting the necessity for long-term follow-up.

Subcutaneous panniculitis‐like T‐cell lymphoma (SPTCL) is a rare subtype of peripheral T‐cell lymphoma (PTCL), accounting for 2% of all cutaneous lymphomas in Japan.

 Moreover, it generally demonstrates a favorable prognosis with a 5‐year overall survival rate of 80%–85.7%.

Recently, recurrent germline variants of HAVCR2 encoding T‐cell immunoglobulin and mucin‐domain containing‐3 protein were reported in patients with SPTCL.
8
 Several hotspot sites (including p.Tyr82Cys and p.Ile97Met in HAVCR2 mutations) are likely dependent on ethnicity. p.Tyr82Cys mutations were identified in Asian patients (10/13 patients),
9
 whereas p.Ile97Met mutations were identified in Caucasians (3/12 patients),
8
 North Africans (1/7 patients), and Sub‐Saharan Africans (1/4 patients).
10
 Furthermore, somatic mutations are often observed in genes associated with epigenetic pathways or the PI3K pathway in SPTCL tumors.
4
, 
9
, 
11
 These genomic abnormalities observed in patients with SPTCL are consistent with their clinical features and are likely owing to excessive activation of the immune system.

In this study, we examined the genetic characteristics and long‐term outcomes of Japanese patients with SPTCL. We integrated the whole‐exome sequencing (WES) data of 60 samples collected from 36 SPTCL patients in our cohort and public data and compared the genetic features of Japanese patients with SPTCL with those of other ethnic groups. Additionally, we examined the clinical courses of four Japanese patients with SPTCL in our cohort, highlighting the significance of long‐term follow‐up and the need to pay attention to the occurrence of complications.

Six patients with SPTCL diagnosed by hematopathologists reviewing biopsy samples of subcutaneous nodules between July 2000 and July 2019 were enrolled in this retrospective study (Table 1 and Table S1). Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) samples of skin tumor samples were collected from four patients. Bone marrow (BM)‐ or peripheral blood (PB)‐derived DNA was available for six patients. Clinical information is available for four patients (Table 2). The data availability of each patient is summarized in Table S2. This study was approved by the review board of the University of Tsukuba Hospital and conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Genomic DNA was extracted from the FFPE samples using a Generead DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen). WES libraries were constructed using Human All Exon v7 SureSelect XT kits (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The captured exome libraries were multiplexed and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X platform (Illumina). WES data for skin tumors and PB or BM from 30 patients with SPTCL (skin tumors, 27; PB or BM, 23) were obtained from the European Genome Phenome Archive (EGA) (EGAS00001002765
12
 and EGAS00001003282
13
) and integrated into the data. We used PB or BM samples as germline controls for somatic mutation analysis (Table S1). Mapping and mutation calling were performed using the Genomon2 pipeline.
14
 We applied the criteria of candidate somatic mutations: (i) Fisher's exact p‐value ≤0.06; (ii) EB‐call p‐value ≤0.05; (iii) ≥4 variant reads in tumor samples; (iv) a variant allele frequency (VAF) in tumor samples ≥0.05; (v) a VAF in matched normal samples <0.10; and (vi) not scored by genomicSuperDups. These candidate mutations were further filtered by removing known variants listed in the 1000 Genomes Project (October 2014 release), dbSNP 138, Exome Sequencing Project (ESP) 5400, and the Human Genome Variation Database (April 2016 release). To further assess the accumulation of germline variants in SPTCL, we applied the following criteria: (i) exon splicing; (ii) ≥4 variant reads in samples; (iii) VAF ≥0.5; (iv) not scored by genomicSuperDups; (v) <0.7% in the 1000 Genomes Project (October 2014 release). We performed Fisher's exact test with Bonferroni correction and compared the results with data from gnomAD v2.1.1.
15
 Following the extraction of candidate somatic and germline mutations, mapping and sequencing errors were excluded by visual inspection using Integrative Genomics Viewer 2.6.3.
16



We integrated the WES data of 60 samples (tumors, 31; PB or BM, 29) collected from 36 patients, including six patients with SPTCL in our cohort, and 30 patients whose data were publicly available. Six patients (SP2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and UPN 10) were Japanese (Table S1). The coverage of WES in our cohort ranged from ×65 to ×249, with a median of ×133 and a mean of ×137, while those of the total ranged from ×23 to ×249, with a median of ×142 and a mean of ×143. We initially analyzed the WES data of 24 tumor‐normal paired samples to elucidate the somatic mutation profile of SPTCL. We identified 138 somatic mutations (range, 0–36; mean, 5.8; median, 1.5 per sample): 108 missense mutations, three nonsense mutations, nine non‐frameshift deletions, and 18 frameshift mutations (Table S3). The VAFs of these somatic mutations ranged from 0.05 to 0.46, with a median of 0.10 and a mean of 0.13. Recurrent mutations were observed in TET2 in two patients. Genes exhibiting mutations in SPTCL tumors were enriched for those related to “regulation of small GTPase mediated signal transduction” or “RHO GTPase cycle” according to Gene Ontology analysis using Metascape (https://metascape.org) (Figure S1). Mutations in genes related to epigenetics (SAP130 and TET2) were observed as previously reported.

 Tumors from four Japanese patients harbored 12 somatic mutations (range, 0–11; mean, 3.0; median, 0.5 per sample). None of the somatic mutations were detectable in two out of four samples from Japanese patients, and four out of 20 samples were detected in non‐Japanese patients.

Next, we analyzed germline mutations using WES data from PB or BM samples of 29 patients with SPTCL. Recurrent HAVCR2 mutations were observed in 23 of the 29 patients (79%) (Table S4). Twenty‐two patients harbored p.Tyr82Cys mutations (20 homozygous, one heterozygous, and one p.Tyr82Cys and p.Thr101Ile compound heterozygous mutation). One patient harbored heterozygous p.Ile97Met mutations. Germline p.Tyr82Cys mutations were identified in four (SP3, 4, 5, and UPN10) of six Japanese patients (67%): three homozygous and one heterozygous. No other HAVCR2 mutations (including p.Ile97Met) were observed in Japanese patients (Figure 1 and Table S4).

Subsequently, we evaluated the clinical characteristics of four Japanese patients (SP2, 3, 4, and 5) to elucidate their clinical features. The baseline patient characteristics are discussed in Table 1. The median age was 51 years (range, 36–65 years), and two patients were female. Before the diagnosis of SPTCL, two patients (SP4 and 5) with HAVCR2 mutations had a 5‐year and 4‐year history of panniculitis, respectively. Patient SP4 manifested with cytophagic histiocytic panniculitis, whereas patient SP5 presented with lupus erythematosus profundus. Both patients were treated with a combination of prednisolone (PSL) and cyclosporine A (CyA). Following diagnosis, two patients (SP2 and 3) were initially administered PSL before chemotherapy, leading to progressive disease or a stable condition (Figure 3). All patients underwent first‐line chemotherapy consisting of cyclophosphamide, hydroxydaunomycin, vincristine, and PSL (CHOP) at an average interval of 7.3 weeks (range, 0–19; median, 5.1) from diagnosis. This therapeutic regimen resulted in all four patients in refractory/relapse regardless of HAVCR2 mutation status.

Three patients (SP3, 4, and 5) who did not achieve CR after CHOP received etoposide, carboplatin, methylprednisolone, and cytarabine (modified ESHAP, mESHAP), resulting in two CR and one PR. These three patients received autologous peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (auto‐PBSCT). Two of the three patients (SP3 and 4) remained in CR after auto‐PBSCT (Figure 3). During the follow‐up period after chemotherapy, patient SP2 relapsed at a different skin location from the initial diagnosis 2 months after CHOP and underwent skin excision with no recurrence. Patient SP5 relapsed 2 months after auto‐PBSCT, although the disease remained stable with continuous CyA. However, Epstein–Barr virus‐positive diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (EBV+ DLBCL) developed 86 months after auto‐PBSCT, and patient SP5 died 94 months after the diagnosis of SPTCL. Additionally, patient SP4 developed Graves' disease 40 months after the diagnosis of SPTCL (Figure 3).

All four patients survived more than 5 years (Table 2).

Although there have been several reports of SPTCL, information on the regional genetic features and detailed prognosis of patients with SPTCL is limited. To our knowledge, this is the first study examining germline HAVCR2 mutations in a cohort of Japanese individuals. In this study, 67% of Japanese patients with SPTCL harbored the germline p.Tyr82Cys mutation in HAVCR2. According to previous reports, hotspot sites of germline HAVCR2 mutations show regional variation, and p.Tyr82Cys mutations were identified in Asians.
4
, 
9
 p.Tyr82Cys mutations accumulated in Japanese patients with SPTCL, which is consistent with findings observed in other Asian populations. Despite difficulties in diagnosing SPTCL owing to its rarity, the genetic characteristics of Japanese patients with SPTCL would be useful as a reasonable approach for precise diagnosis.

In SPTCL tumors, the number of somatic cell mutations is remarkably low compared with that in other T‐cell malignancies, suggesting that germline mutations in HAVCR2 (encoding a crucial immune molecule) may serve as a key factor in the pathogenesis of SPTCL. Meanwhile, for one patient without HAVCR2 mutation in our cohort, we could not identify other candidate genes associated with SPTCL. Histopathologically, this patient showed less significant rimming, probably indicating a lower tumor cell having somatic mutations content in skin samples compared with other patients. It might contribute to the difficulty in identifying somatic mutations in this patient.

The 5‐year survival rate in our cohort was consistent with exceptionally favorable outcomes in previous reports. However, an inferior prognosis was observed in a patient who developed a hematological malignancy stemming from immunodeficiency‐related auto‐PBSCT and immunosuppressive therapy. Notably one patient developed autoimmune diseases, consistent with the notion that the underlying nature of SPTCL is that of excessive immune activation, despite its classification as a tumor.

This study demonstrated resistance to CHOP in patients with SPTCL and underscored the necessity for additional therapeutic approaches (including auto‐PBSCT) despite the 80% response rate of initial treatment including CHOP or immunosuppressive therapy.
2
 Conservative immunosuppressive therapies are proposed as treatment options for SPTCL.
20
 In our cohort, PSL showed limited efficacy against SPTCL (SP3), although a combination regimen of PSL and CyA or CyA alone was effective in managing non‐neoplastic panniculitis in patients with HAVCR2 mutations or SPTCL at relapse after auto‐PBSCT (SP4 and 5). These results suggest that CHOP is probably inadequate for Japanese patients with SPTCL and alternative regimens should be investigated.

In conclusion, the p.Tyr82Cys mutation was identified in 67% of the Japanese patients with SPTCL. The emergence of autoimmune diseases or other hematopoietic tumors is possible even in the remission phase of SPTCL, and these conditions may occasionally affect prognosis. Our findings suggest the need for long‐term follow‐ups to manage these complications.
