# A Synonymous DLG4 Variant (c.771G>A) Causes Exon 9 Skipping via Paternal Germline Mosaicism in DLG4-Related Synaptopathy.
**Authors:** Chen J, Zhao Q, Zhang X, Wu N, Xi X, Zeng Y, Wu S, Yuan K, Liu Z, Wu X, Zeng S
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.70246](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.70246)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The synonymous DLG4 variant is annotated with conflicting pathogenicity interpretations, and its molecular mechanism remains uncharacterized. Paternal germline mosaicism has been inferred but never molecularly confirmed in DLG4-related synaptopathy.

OBJECTIVE: To functionally validate the pathogenicity of the synonymous DLG4 variant NM_001365.4:c.771G>A (p.Ala257=), and elucidate its inheritance mechanism in a proband with neurodevelopmental delay.

METHODS: We performed trio-whole-exome sequencing in a proband with neurodevelopmental delay. The pathogenicity of the identified variant was assessed using bioinformatic predictors and validated through Sanger sequencing, RNA sequencing, and RT-PCR. The inheritance pattern was investigated via high-depth amplicon sequencing of paternal sperm DNA.

RESULTS: The synonymous DLG4 c.771G>A variant (absent in gnomAD) induced complete exon 9 skipping, resulting in a frameshift and premature termination codon. The variant was present at 3.83% variant allele frequency in paternal sperm, establishing paternal germline mosaicism as the origin.

CONCLUSION: Our study resolves conflicting interpretations of the DLG4 c.771G>A variant by demonstrating its pathogenic splice-disrupting effect. To our knowledge, this provides the first molecular confirmation of paternal germline mosaicism in DLG4-related synaptopathy. We note that maternal germline mosaicism was not assessed. These findings underscore the dual necessity of functional analysis for synonymous variants and sensitive mosaicism detection for accurate genetic counseling.

Intellectual disability (ID) and global developmental delay (GDD) are common neurodevelopmental presentations with significant genetic heterogeneity (Vissers et al. 2016). DLG4‐related synaptopathy, caused by heterozygous loss‐of‐function variants in the postsynaptic density gene DLG4, exemplifies this genetic burden and is characterized by variable cognitive and motor impairment (Rodríguez‐Palmero et al. 2021). However, two mechanistic aspects of this disorder remain incompletely characterized.

First, synonymous variants, frequently presumed neutral, can disrupt exonic splicing regulatory elements and produce loss‐of‐function transcripts, yet their pathogenic contribution to DLG4 disorders remains largely unexplored. Transcriptome sequencing has emerged as a powerful diagnostic tool for validating splice‐altering variants in Mendelian disease (Cummings et al. 2017), but its application to DLG4 synonymous variants has been limited. Second, while germline mosaicism has been invoked to explain recurrence of apparently de novo dominant disorders (Breuss et al. 2021; Candace et al. 2018), it has never been molecularly confirmed in DLG4‐related synaptopathy, precluding accurate recurrence risk quantification (Lecoquierre et al. 2025).

Here, we address both gaps by functionally characterizing the synonymous DLG4 variant c.771G>A (p.Ala257=) and quantifying its transmission through paternal germline mosaicism. This integrated approach resolves conflicting ClinVar interpretations for this variant and establishes a measurable basis for genetic counseling in families with sporadic DLG4 disorders.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University (no. LLSL‐2025‐076). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents for the genetic analyses, as well as for the publication of clinical data and the use of accompanying figures. The procedures were performed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Trio‐based whole‐exome sequencing was performed on the proband and both parents. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 51104). Exome capture was performed using the GenCap capture kit (MyGenostics Inc., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the DNA Sample Prep Reagent Set (MyGenostics, Beijing, China). Paired‐end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on the DNBSEQ T7 platform (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China). The mean sequencing depth exceeded 200× for all three trio members, with > 95% of target bases covered at ≥ 20×.

Raw sequencing data were saved in FASTQ format. Adapter sequences, primers, low‐quality bases, poly‐A tails, and other unwanted sequences were trimmed using Cutadapt to generate high‐quality clean data. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (UCSC hg19) using the BWA algorithm implemented in Sentieon software. Duplicate reads were removed, base qualities were recalibrated, and single‐nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions were called using the Sentieon driver module, generating VCF files. Variant annotation was performed using ANNOVAR against multiple public databases, including dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD), OMIM, ESP6500, ExAC, and Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD). Functional effects of variants were predicted using SIFT, PolyPhen‐2, MutationTaster, and GERP++. Copy number variations were analyzed using CNVkit.

Variant pathogenicity was classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG/AMP) guidelines (Richards et al. 2015). Candidate pathogenic variants were identified through a three‐step filtering strategy: (i) variants with fewer than five reads supporting the alternative allele and an allele frequency below 25% were excluded; (ii) variants with an allele frequency greater than 5% in general population databases, including the 1000 Genomes Project (1000G), ESP6500, and gnomAD, were removed; (iii) synonymous variants that were not documented in the HGMD and ClinVar and predicted to have no impact on splicing were also filtered out. Remaining variants were subsequently regarded as candidate variants for further comprehensive analysis.

Peripheral blood samples were collected from the younger sister of the proband, and genomic DNA was extracted using the same protocol as described above. Sanger sequencing was performed to validate the candidate variant. Sperm samples were obtained from the father, and DNA extraction was conducted following the identical method. Targeted single‐site high‐depth sequencing was further applied for the validation of the candidate variant.

The DLG4 c.771G>A (NM_001365.4) variant was identified in the proband at a sequencing depth of 98, with 40 alternate reads and a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 0.41.

RNA sequencing was performed using the RNA‐SeqCare transcriptome service (commercially provided by Aegicare Technology Co. Ltd., Shenzhen, China) to assess global gene expression and alternative splicing (AS) events. Total RNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples (minimum volume: 2.5 mL; collected in PAXgene tubes), and quantified using spectrophotometry (A260/280 = 1.8–2.0; minimum concentration: 30 ng/μL). RNA integrity was verified by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN ≥ 8.0). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (cDNA fragmentation: 150–200 bp), involving poly‐A selection of mRNA, fragmentation, double‐stranded cDNA synthesis, and adapter ligation. Dual‐indexed adapters (NEBNext Multiplex Oligos) were ligated. Paired‐end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq (> 50 million reads per sample; average depth: 40× coverage). Raw reads underwent quality control using FastQC (v0.11.9) to assess quality and Trimmomatic (v0.30; LEADING:20, TRAILING:20) for adapter trimming and quality filtering. High‐quality clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (GRCh37) using HISAT2 (v2.0.5; MAPQ ≥ 20) (Kim et al. 2019). AS events were identified and quantified using rMATS (v3.2.5; significant events defined by |ΔPSI| > 10%, FDR < 0.05 [Benjamini–Hochberg], junction counts ≥ 20), which quantifies exon inclusion levels by calculating Percent Spliced In (PSI) values. Gene expression levels were estimated using featureCounts (v1.5.0‐p3) with uniquely mapped reads (> 85%) normalized to FPKM. Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 (v1.16.1), considering adjusted p < 0.05 as significant. Sample processing was randomized across batches.

To validate the aberrant splicing of DLG4 identified by RNA sequencing, RT‐PCR combined with Sanger sequencing was performed. Total RNA was extracted from the proband's peripheral blood using the PAXgene Blood RNA Kit (QIAGEN), according to the manufacturer's protocol (Takara, Japan). The 20 μL reverse transcription reaction contained 500 ng total RNA, oligo(dT) primers, and random hexamers. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1 (DLG4‐QPCR‐F/R). PCR was carried out in a 25 μL reaction volume containing 0.4 μM each primer, with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min; 35 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. A healthy control sample was amplified in parallel. The RT‐PCR products were resolved on a 2% agarose gel (120 V, 35 min) and visualized alongside a 100 bp DNA ladder. Distinct bands were excised and purified using a commercial gel extraction kit, then sequenced bidirectionally on an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer. Sequencing chromatograms were analyzed using Chromas software (Technelysium, Australia) to verify the exact nature of the splicing events.

In silico splicing predictions were performed following the recommendations of the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Splicing Subgroup (Walker et al. 2023). The effect of the DLG4 c.771G>A variant on splice‐site usage was assessed using SpliceAI (Jaganathan et al. 2019) and Pangolin (Zeng and Li 2022). A SpliceAI Δ score ≥ 0.2 or a Pangolin Δ score ≥ 0.2 was considered indicative of a deleterious effect on splicing. Concordant predictions from both algorithms were evaluated as supporting evidence (PP3) under the ACMG/AMP guidelines (Richards et al. 2015).

The proband, a young male child, presented with GDD, marked speech impairment, and an unsteady gait. Formal standardized developmental assessment scores were unavailable, as the proband was initially evaluated at a local institution and was referred without comprehensive neuropsychological testing. However, detailed clinical evaluation by a pediatric neurologist documented significant delays across gross motor, fine motor, language, and social‐adaptive domains relative to age‐matched norms. Routine clinical investigations, including metabolic screening and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), revealed no significant abnormalities. Trio‐based whole‐exome sequencing identified a heterozygous synonymous variant in the DLG4 gene (c.771G>A, p.Ala257=) located in exon 9 (NM_001365.4) in the proband (Figure 1A). The variant's annotation as Pathogenic or Likely Pathogenic in ClinVar (Landrum et al. 2025) (PS4_Supporting), combined with its absence from population cohorts (gnomAD, v2.1.1) (PM2_Supporting) (Karczewski et al. 2020), provided supporting evidence for pathogenicity under ACMG/AMP guidelines (Richards et al. 2015). Nonetheless, given that its functional impact required experimental validation, further investigation was warranted. Subsequent transcriptomic analysis confirmed aberrant splicing (Figure 1B,C), supporting a loss‐of‐function (LOF) mechanism consistent with PSD‐95 haploinsufficiency.

Sanger sequencing confirmed the apparent de novo origin of the variant, as it was absent in the peripheral blood‐derived DNA of the proband's parents and sister (Figure 2A–C). Given its clinical relevance, we performed high‐depth sequencing on the father's sperm DNA, which revealed low‐level mosaicism at a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 3.83% (Figure 3), thereby confirming paternal germline mosaicism.

DLG4‐related disorder is established to result from haploinsufficiency (Moutton et al. 2018; Rodríguez‐Palmero et al. 2021), a mechanism supported by the gene's high intolerance to LoF variation in gnomAD (pLI = 1.00) (Karczewski et al. 2020). Our RT‐PCR assay provided direct experimental evidence of exon 9 skipping caused by the variant. Because the length of exon 9 is not a multiple of three, this event is predicted to cause a frameshift and a premature termination codon (PTC), leading to LoF (Jaffrey and Wilkinson 2018). Following the ClinGen Splicing Subgroup guidance, we applied PVS1 (Very Strong evidence for pathogenicity due to LoF) based on this functional splicing evidence (Abou Tayoun et al. 2018; Walker et al. 2023). Together with PM2_Supporting, these criteria are sufficient to support a Pathogenic classification. Although the variant was absent in parental blood, paternal germline mosaicism precludes the application of PS2, as this criterion requires the variant to be truly absent in both parents (Richards et al. 2015; Karczewski et al. 2020). This finding nonetheless provides crucial information for accurate recurrence risk counseling. The previously cited computational evidence (PP3) was superseded by the functional data and therefore not included in the final classification.

During a subsequent pregnancy, prenatal diagnosis via amniocentesis identified the same DLG4 variant in the fetus (Figure 2D). Following comprehensive genetic counseling, the pregnancy was terminated due to the predicted severe neurodevelopmental phenotype associated with pathogenic DLG4 variants. This decision was based solely on the confirmed prenatal diagnosis of the pathogenic variant and the associated high risk of severe neurodevelopmental impairment. No non‐genetic factors, including fetal structural anomalies, maternal health conditions, or socioeconomic considerations, influenced this reproductive decision.

To our knowledge, this study presents a confirmed case of paternal germline mosaicism involving a pathogenic DLG4 splice‐site variant (c.771G>A) in a Chinese individual, and provides the first molecularly validated evidence worldwide of germline mosaicism‐associated DLG4‐related neurodevelopmental disorder (Rodríguez‐Palmero et al. 2021; Sheets et al. 2018). When situated within the broader DLG4 variant landscape, our case highlights a distinct mutational mechanism, with approximately 77 pathogenic or likely pathogenic DLG4 variants have been reported to date, the overwhelming majority comprising nonsense (n = 23) and frameshift (n = 20) mutations that result in haploinsufficiency, the established disease mechanism for this gene (Levy et al. 2024; Haseena et al. 2024; Rodríguez‐Palmero et al. 2021; Tokunaga et al. 2024; Brea‐Fernández et al. 2022; Benedetta et al. 2024) (Table 3). These canonical LOF variants predominantly cluster within the critical PDZ and SH3 domains, which are essential for PSD‐95's scaffolding interactions with NMDA receptors and synaptic organization (Haseena et al. 2024). By contrast, the c.771G>A variant we identified is located in exon 9, a region not previously associated with pathogenic mutations, and represents a rare class of synonymous splice‐disrupting variants that are functionally equivalent to canonical truncating mutations yet evade standard bioinformatic filtering (Rodríguez‐Palmero et al. 2021). Although this variant is synonymous at the amino acid level (p.Ala257=), our functional analysis demonstrates that it disrupts the canonical splice donor site. Consequently, exon 9 skipping shifts the reading frame, as its length is not a multiple of three. This frameshift introduces a PTC that undergoes nonsense‐mediated decay (NMD) (Haseena et al. 2024), ultimately resulting in a LoF effect, consistent with the gene's established haploinsufficiency mechanism (Jaffrey and Wilkinson 2018; Moutton et al. 2018). Our findings underscore that synonymous variants at splice sites require stringent functional assessment, as they can profoundly impact RNA processing and protein function (Cummings et al. 2017; Haseena et al. 2024).

Comparative phenotypic analysis further illuminates the spectrum of DLG4‐related disorders. The proband's presentation, characterized by GDD, marked speech impairment, and ataxia, and notably lacking epilepsy or intellectual regression, aligns with the core neurodevelopmental phenotype yet exhibits notable distinctions from previously reported cases. For instance, several recent cohorts have documented epilepsy and intellectual regression as prominent features (Levy et al. 2024; Tokunaga et al. 2024; Benedetta et al. 2024), whereas our patient presented without these comorbidities. This phenotypic variability, occurring even among functionally convergent LoF mechanisms, underscores the substantial clinical heterogeneity of DLG4‐related synaptopathy and suggests that factors beyond haploinsufficiency, such as residual protein function from alternative transcripts, cryptic splice‐site usage, or genetic modifiers, may modulate phenotypic expressivity (Karabatic et al. 2025; Vaz‐Drago et al. 2017; Levy et al. 2022). Our case therefore contributes not only a novel mutational entry to the DLG4 atlas but also an important data point for refining genotype–phenotype correlations.

Beyond DLG4 specifically, our findings contribute to the growing recognition of parental germline mosaicism as a quantifiable and clinically actionable mechanism in neurodevelopmental disorders (Bai et al. 2022). Germline mosaicism has long been invoked to explain the recurrence of ostensibly de novo dominant conditions, including epileptic encephalopathies (Candace et al. 2018; Møller et al. 2019), autism spectrum disorders (Sherman et al. 2021), and ID syndromes (Breuss et al. 2021; Campbell et al. 2014; Rahbari et al. 2016). Recent advances in deep sequencing have transformed this phenomenon from a theoretical risk into a measurable entity, enabling direct quantification in parental gametes and refined recurrence risk counseling (Breuss et al. 2020; Lecoquierre et al. 2024; Bernkopf et al. 2023). However, among the expanding list of neurodevelopmental disorder genes, DLG4 has remained notably absent from this molecular evidence base; previous inferences of mosaicism relied solely on sibling recurrence patterns without direct gamete analysis (Rodríguez‐Palmero et al. 2021). By quantifying paternal mosaicism at 3.83% VAF through sperm DNA sequencing, our study bridges this gap and situates DLG4 within the same clinical framework now applied to other high‐risk neurodevelopmental genes. This broader contextualization reinforces that germline mosaicism is not an exceptional explanation for sporadic cases but a concrete, non‐negligible etiological factor that must be proactively investigated across neurodevelopmental disorders to enable precise genetic counseling and informed reproductive decision‐making (Bernkopf et al. 2023; Breuss et al. 2021).

This study delineates a novel pathogenic mechanism underlying DLG4‐related neurodevelopmental disorder, demonstrating its inheritance via paternal germline mosaicism of a synonymous splice‐site variant (c.771G>A). We provide functional evidence that this variant disrupts mRNA splicing, leading to exon 9 skipping and consequent loss of PSD‐95 function, a finding that resolves prior ambiguity in variant interpretation and affirms its pathogenicity. Crucially, the identification and quantification of mosaicism in paternal sperm provide a molecularly confirmed recurrence risk assessment for a DLG4‐related disorder. These results underscore the necessity of incorporating RNA‐level assays into the diagnostic workflow for synonymous and splice‐region variants, and they highlight the critical role of germline tissue analysis in families with apparently sporadic cases. Our findings affirm that germline mosaicism is a measurable risk factor warranting proactive investigation to enable precise genetic counseling and informed reproductive decision‐making.
