# The multitalented TIP60 chromatin remodeling complex: wearing many hats in epigenetic regulation, cell division and diseases.
**Authors:** Santopietro MV, Ferreri D, Prozzillo Y, Dimitri P, Messina G
**Journal:** Epigenetics Chromatin (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13072-025-00603-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13072-025-00603-8)

## Content

The TIP60 complex is an evolutionarily conserved, multifunctional chromatin remodeling complex involved in critical cellular processes, including DNA repair, transcription regulation, and cell cycle control. Although its molecular organization and functions have been extensively studied, a comparative synthesis of its context-specific roles across evolutionarily distant species and pathological conditions is important to fully grasp its biological and clinical significance. In this review, we provide an integrative overview of the TIP60 complex, emphasizing its composition and conserved functions in Homo sapiens and Drosophila melanogaster, with comparative insights from plant systems. We explore how TIP60 complex dysregulation contributes to the molecular pathology of cancer and neurodevelopmental disorders, highlighting recent mechanistic insights. We also examine the emerging interplay between TIP60 complex subunits and long non-coding RNAs, which are increasingly recognized as pivotal regulators of genome accessibility and transcriptional programs. Finally, in this intriguing scenario, we highlight the non-canonical functions of the TIP60 complex in mitosis and cytokinesis, underscoring its moonlighting roles in maintaining genomic and cellular integrity, beyond its established contribution to epigenetic regulation. By connecting these diverse aspects, our review aims to provide an integrated perspective on the TIP60 complex and its expanding functional landscape in health and disease.

ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes are multiprotein cellular machines classified into four distinct families (SWI/SNF, ISWI, NURD/Mi-2/CHD, and INO80) and typically consist of evolutionarily conserved subunits, one of which carries an ATPase domain [1]. These complexes harness the energy derived from ATP hydrolysis to modify chromatin architecture by sliding or displacing nucleosomes, as well as replacing canonical histones with histone variants [1]. Such alterations disrupt histone–DNA interactions, increasing the accessibility of nucleosomal DNA to specific proteins or enzymes required for initiating critical cellular and biological processes, such as DNA transcription, replication, and repair. Thus, the activity of chromatin remodeling complexes is essential for regulating epigenetic gene expression, maintaining genome stability, and enabling cells to respond dynamically to environmental and developmental cues.

Interestingly, some chromatin remodeling complexes exhibit flexible subunit composition, which may vary depending on cellular and developmental contexts. For example, in mammals, the BAF, PBAF, GBAF, esBAF, npBAF, and nBAF subcomplexes are characterized by combinatorial assembly during development. Some subunits are shared across multiple complexes, while others are complex-specific, resulting in subcomplexes that are unique to particular tissues [2, 3].

Mutations in genes encoding subunits of ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes, as well as other epigenetic regulators such as histone deacetylases and methyltransferases, can lead to chromatin dysfunction. Consequently, a wide range of defects may arise, contributing to human developmental diseases, chromosomal instability and cancer [2, 4, 5].

Beyond epigenetic regulation, chromatin remodeling complexes have been shown to play moonlighting functions in various cellular processes. For example, during cell cycle progression, subunits of the human TIP60 and SRCAP complexes relocate to the cell division apparatus, where they perform essential roles in ensuring proper execution of mitosis and cytokinesis [6], further emphasizing their crucial involvement in preventing genomic instability.

The evolutionarily conserved human TIP60 complex (also known as TIP60/p400, TIP60/EP400, or NuA4/TIP60), hereafter referred to as hTIP60-C, is a member of the INO80 family. It represents a fusion of the S. cerevisiae SWR1 and NuA4 complexes which mediate the incorporation of the variant histone H2A.Z (Htz1) into chromatin and the acetylation of histones H4, H2A and H2A.Z, respectively [1, 7–11]. With an approximate molecular mass of 1.8 MDa, hTIP60-C comprises at least 20 subunits and performs both chromatin-remodeling and histone-modifying activities. Specifically, hTIP60-C promotes the exchange of canonical H2A with the variant H2A.Z and catalyzes the acetylation of histones H4, H2A, and H2A.Z. The two activities are carried out by the EP400 ATPase and the TIP60 (KAT5) acetyltransferase, respectively.

Ultrastructural studies on the functional architecture of hTIP60-C have highlighted that it is organized into distinct functional modules, including the histone acetyltransferase (HAT), the actin-related protein (ARP), and the TRRAP modules [9–11]. This modular organization enables the integration of histone acetylation with chromatin remodeling. Moreover, EP400 consistently emerges as a central structural scaffold that connects and coordinates these modules, while TRRAP plays a crucial role in recruiting the TIP60 complex to specific genomic loci. Through these coordinated activities, the TIP60-C in humans and mice regulates transcription, facilitates DNA repair, and plays a crucial role in maintaining stem cell pluripotency and guiding lineage specification [1, 12–16]. Most notably, cancer-related mutations in EP400 were mapped on the high-resolution structure of the hTIP60-C [10]. A group of mutations are predominantly clustered in the EP400 ATPase domain and may affect the hTIP60-C function by impairing the ATPase activity, while other mutations occur on the interaction surface of EP400 with other hTIP60-C subunits. In general, most of these mutations are predicted to destabilize hTIP60-C assembly.

The Drosophila melanogaster TIP60-C (dTIP60-C), also known as DOM/TIP60, was originally characterized from S2 cells [17] and found to be structurally homologous to its human counterpart. The authors also provided evidence showing that the dTIP60-C combines both histone acetylation and exchange of canonical H2A with the unique Drosophila H2A variant (H2A.v). The exchange reaction is catalyzed by the Domino ATPase (DOM) and enhanced by dTIP60-mediated acetylation of nucleosomal phospho-H2Av. Recent findings suggest that, in Drosophila melanogaster cell lines, ATPase and acetyltransferase activities are not carried out by a single complex but rather by two distinct complexes, called DOM-A.C and DOM-B.C, each defined by the presence of the DOM-A or DOM-B isoform, respectively, and characterized by unique functions and subunit compositions [18, 19]. The DOM-A.C is functionally equivalent to the S. cerevisiae NuA4 complex [7], catalyzing the acetylation of H4K12, while the DOM-B.C is responsible for the incorporation of H2A.V into chromatin, similar to the SWR1 complex [8]. However, it remains unclear whether these two distinct complexes are also assembled in vivo and across different Drosophila cell types. As previously mentioned, in mammals the composition of chromatin remodeling complexes can vary between cell types [3]. For example, the transition from neural stem/progenitor cells to post-mitotic neurons involves the replacement of BAF45a and BAF53a with BAF45b and BAF53b subunits in the npBAF complex [20]. For simplicity and to avoid confusion, we will continue to refer to the complex in D. melanogaster as dTIP60-C. As in humans, the dTIP60-C performs essential functions including chromatin regulation, DNA repair, and ensuring the proper development and differentiation of both somatic and germline cells [18, 21–24]. Moreover, the epigenetic functions of dTIP60 HAT contribute to the regulation of learning, memory, and circadian processes [25–28].

Genome-wide expression analysis following dTip60 HAT depletion in cell cultures revealed the up- or down-regulation of approximately a thousand genes, indicating that the complex is involved in both gene activation and repression [29]. Among the downregulated genes, there is a strong enrichment for those with chromatin-related functions. Thus, the chromatin remodeling activity of the dTIP60-C complex may contribute to epigenetic silencing processes, particularly in the establishment and maintenance of heterochromatin organization. This conclusion is supported by findings showing that mutations in genes encoding various dTIP60-C subunits act as dominant suppressors of position effect variegation (PEV), thereby reactivating gene expression [23, 30–35]. In this context, it is tempting to speculate that dTIP60-C might also influence the regulation of hundreds of protein-coding genes and other sequences embedded within constitutive heterochromatin [36].

The SWR1, NuA4, INO80 complexes have been also identified in plants and play roles similar to those of their counterparts in humans and animals [37–41]. For instance, the A. thaliana SWR1 complex regulates gene transcription by enabling H2A.Z deposition. Interestingly, the OsINO80 complex in rice facilitates H3K27me3 establishment, promotes H3K9me2 deposition, and maintains transposable element silencing. While many subunits of the A. thaliana complexes share evolutionary conservation with their human counterparts, some are plant-specific, as observed in the SWR1 complex. As expected, mutations or depletion of subunit-coding genes result in chromatin alterations and aberrant gene expression, potentially affecting flowering time, stress responses, and other developmental processes [37, 38, 40, 41].

A functional interplay between TIP60 HAT and Aurora B, the kinase subunit of the chromosomal passenger complex [46], has been identified as important in coordinating acetylation and phosphorylation events during mitosis, thereby preventing genomic instability. At the outer kinetochore, TIP60 HAT interacts with phosphorylated Aurora B, and directly acetylates it at lysine 215 [47]. This specific modification is crucial for the proper activity of Aurora B, as it ensures correct kinetochore-microtubule attachment, spindle checkpoint activation, and the correction of erroneous attachments, ultimately preventing chromosomal instability [46–51]. Conversely, when TIP60 HAT-mediated acetylation of Aurora B is downregulated, the kinase activity of Aurora B is no longer properly regulated, leading to its hyperactivity and subsequent hyperphosphorylation of various substrates. This results in aberrant chromosome segregation and aneuploidy. In agreement, overexpression of Aurora B, observed in several human cancers, leads to hyperphosphorylation of substrates, disrupting mitosis and enhancing genomic instability, a key driver of tumorigenesis.

Intriguingly, Bose et al. [52] reported that TIP60 HAT-mediated acetylation of Aurora B at lysines 85 and 87 negatively regulates the kinase. Loss of TIP60 HAT activity removes this inhibitory control, resulting in increased Aurora B kinase activity. In contrast, according to Mo et al. [47] reduced TIP60 HAT levels affect Aurora B acetylation at lysine 215, eliminating a positive regulatory modification required for proper Aurora B function during cell division. Despite the apparent discrepancies, both studies converge in showing that TIP60 HAT downregulation leads to Aurora B hyperactivation, albeit through distinct mechanisms. Taken together, these findings suggest a dual role for TIP60 in the regulation of Aurora B activity, which appears to depend, at least in part, on the specific acetylation sites involved.

Recent studies have shown that during early mitosis TIP60 HAT also acetylates Bub1, a core component of the spindle assembly checkpoint [53], at lysines 424 and 431 on kinetochores [54]. These acetylation events enhance Bub1 kinase activity, enabling it to phosphorylate the centromeric histone H2A at threonine 120, which in turn recruits Aurora B and Shugoshin 1 to ensure faithful chromosome segregation. Thus, TIP60 HAT may promote Aurora B activation through both direct acetylation at the outer kinetochore [47] and indirect modulation via Bub1 acetylation [54]. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and may function in parallel to ensure proper mitotic progression.

TIP60 HAT also cooperates with key tumor suppressors to preserve genomic integrity. For instance, TIP60 HAT-dependent acetylation of p53 at lysine 120 promotes apoptosis following DNA damage, representing a dual mechanism of carcinogenesis suppression [55]. Furthermore, TIP60 HAT is required for ATM activation in response to DNA damage, a crucial step in the DNA damage response (DDR) that triggers cell-cycle arrest and connects chromatin remodeling to genome surveillance mechanisms [56, 57].

The relevance of hTIP60-C extends beyond cancer, as recent studies have also highlighted its involvement in neurodevelopmental diseases. Dominant missense hTIP60 HAT variants in humans lead to a deficiency in histone acetylation and transcriptional dysregulation of multiple genes, resulting in a neurodevelopmental syndrome characterized by sleep disturbances, cerebellar atrophy, and facial dysmorphisms [58]. In addition, whole-exome sequencing in humans has identified allelic variants of the EP400 gene associated with epilepsy and other neurodevelopmental conditions. Consistent phenotypes have also been observed in zebrafish and Drosophila models [59].

Interestingly, the lack or dysfunction of TIP60-HAT in mouse also leads to a variety of developmental defects, highlighting its evolutionary conserved functions [60–64]. In particular, the impact of TIP60 HAT loss was studied in a neuronal stem/progenitor cell (NSC)-specific knockout mouse model. It was found that TIP60 HAT-deficient embryonic brains resulted in microcephaly, with reduced cell proliferation, neural differentiation defects, and impaired neuronal migration [13, 65]. Moreover, homozygous neural crest-specific deletion of TIP60 HAT or EP400 in mice induces facial malformations and orofacial clefting, supporting the essential roles of both remodelers in facial development and palatogenesis [66]. In line with these findings, a variety of craniofacial abnormalities were observed following depletion of the CFDP1 gene, the ortholog of the SWC5 subunit of the S. cerevisiae SWR1 complex [67]. Intriguingly, RNAi-mediated knockdown of CFDP1 in human cells results in chromosome condensation defects [68], reminiscent of those observed in cells lacking MCPH1, a gene whose mutations cause autosomal recessive primary microcephaly [69].

Importantly, human genes implicated in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease have Drosophila orthologs whose alternative splicing is regulated by TIP60 HAT, suggesting a conserved role for this protein in RNA processing relevant to neurodegenerative disorders [70]. Similarly, MRG15, another subunit of the TIP60 complex, has RNA-binding activity and is essential for pre-mRNA splicing during spermatogenesis, as demonstrated in conditional MRG15 knockout mice [71].

Targeting the subunits of the hTIP60-C has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of cancer and other diseases [72–76]. The compounds developed for this purpose fall into three main categories: bisubstrate inhibitors, synthetic compounds, and natural products. Representative examples include TH1834, NU9056, and MG149, which have shown potential in modulating TIP60 HAT activity and downstream effects.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides that usually do not encode for proteins but play essential roles in epigenetic processes [77]. An increasing number of lncRNAs have emerged as crucial players in chromatin regulation and gene expression by recruiting chromatin remodeling complexes and modulating their activity [78–80].

LncRNAs can also recruit a variety of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to form lncRNA–protein complexes that act in a wide range of biological processes [81]. Among reported interactions between lncRNAs and remodeling complexes, well known examples include lncRNAs interacting with Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), which facilitate its binding and spreading across targeted chromatin [82–88]. Other lncRNAs can influence the activity of chromatin remodelers, either inhibiting or promoting their ATPase functions, which are essential for proper complex assembly and function [89, 90].

Diverse lncRNAs are known to interact with subunits of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex. For example, lncRNA Mhrt binds Smarca4 (Brg1), preventing over-expression myosin heavy chain 7, thereby mediating cardioprotection [91]. The SWI/SNF complex subunits interact with NEAT1 lncRNA to contribute to the formation of nuclear paraspeckles, acting as key architectural components [92], but they can also be implicated in carcinogenesis. The LncTCF7 directly binds SWI/SNF complex subunits, and its overexpression promotes tumorigenic activity in liver cancer stem cells [93]. The SWINGN lncRNA interacts with SMARCB1, a core subunit of the SWI/SNF complex, and modulates the activation of the GAS6, as well as a network of pro-oncogenic genes [94].

As previously mentioned, dTIP60-C subunits possess RNA-binding activity associated with splicing functions [70, 71]. In addition, several hTIP60-C subunits have also been shown to interact with lncRNAs, not only in normal cellular processes but also in carcinogenesis, where such interactions may play critical regulatory roles. For instance, the lncAKHE interacts with GAS41 (also known as YEATS domain-containing protein 4) to enhance NOTCH2 signaling activation, and its overexpression promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression [95]. LINC00839 promotes colorectal cancer progression by serving as a scaffold for the recruitment of RUVBL1 (also called Pontin) to the hTIP60-C [96]. This, in turn, increases the activity of the complex, resulting in histone acetylation at the NRF1 gene promoter and consequent enhancement of its expression. Two lncRNAs, CASC7 and RNCR3 (LINC00599) act as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), regulating the function of hTIP60-C subunits, BRD8, in prostate cancer and ING3 in colorectal cancer, respectively [97, 98].

Finally, the roX1 and roX2 lncRNAs contribute to the recruitment of MOF, a dTIP60-closely related acetyltransferase of the MSL complex which is involved in histone H4 acetylation at lysine 16 [99–101], thus ensuring proper chromatin accessibility on the male X chromosome in D. melanogaster [102]. This finding suggests a conserved role of lncRNA-mediated regulation of chromatin remodelers in dosage compensation across species.

As previously mentioned, besides its functions in chromatin regulation, the TIP60 HAT plays a crucial role at kinetochores in regulating the proper kinase activity of Aurora B and Bub1 [54–57] and interacts with tumor suppressors, such as p53 and ATM, in maintaining genomic integrity.

Moreover, the TIP60 HAT and other subunits of the human complex have been proposed as functional components of the cell division apparatus, playing direct roles in cell cycle control [6, 103–110], a phenomenon which has been largely overlooked. Specifically, during cell-cycle progression, subunits of the hTIP60-C, many of which are shared with the paralogous SRCAP complex, can change their subcellular localization (Figs. 1 and 2), relocating to the centrosome, spindle, and midbody (MB). Among these subunits, BAF53a and TIP60 HAT interact with each other during telophase, as well as with α-tubulin, Aurora B, CIT-K, and other MB-associated cytokinesis regulators, with the MB recruitment of these regulators depending on BAF53a and TIP60 HAT [107]. Importantly, the interaction with tubulins and microtubules appears to be a recurrent “signature” of other chromatin remodelers carrying mitotic functions [103, 104, 106–108]. Taken together, these findings indicate that, during cell-cycle progression, hTIP60-C subunits act as functional components of the centrosome, spindle, and MB, thereby ensuring proper execution of cell division. Consistently, dysregulation of hTIP60-C subunits have been implicated in cancer development (Table 1). Interestingly, the functional relocation of chromatin remodelers to cell division apparatus has also been described for the dTIP60 complex subunits [6, 107], highlighting the evolutionary conservation of the moonlighting roles of chromatin remodelers, which contribute to cell cycle control, beyond their established functions in epigenetic regulation.

Moreover, as previously discussed, alterations in hTIP60-C impact neurodevelopment. Notably, autosomal recessive primary microcephaly (MCPH) has been linked to mutations in genes encoding components of the mitotic machinery, with MCPH gene products localizing to the centrosomes during interphase or to the spindle poles during mitosis [111, 112]. In light of these findings, it is plausible that dysfunctions in hTIP60-C subunits, and the resulting defective cell division, may contribute to the onset of a variety of neurodevelopmental disorders.

The TIP60-C plays a pivotal role in the epigenetic regulation of gene expression in eukaryotes, with evolutionary conservation observed even in plants, underscoring its fundamental functions across distantly related species. Dysregulation of hTIP60-C and its subunits is frequently linked to cancer and developmental diseases, highlighting the complex as a promising therapeutic target.

Research on the interactions between lncRNAs and chromatin remodelers may uncover new challenges and directions in cell biology. Investigating how lncRNAs interact with TIP60-C subunits, modulate its activity, and potentially contribute to the structural organization of the mitotic apparatus [6] could provide valuable insights into epigenetic regulation and the control of cell division during development and differentiation.

Beyond its role in chromatin regulation, the non-canonical functions of TIP60-C in the organization and function of the centrosome, spindle, and midbody represent an overlooked area in cell biology. Further studies in this field could provide valuable insights into the link between defective cell division and human diseases, reinforcing the critical role of TIP60-C in maintaining genomic and cellular stability. The integration of advanced techniques such as CRISPR-based gene editing and high-resolution live-cell imaging [113] will be crucial for gaining a deeper understanding of the cellular roles of the "multitalented" TIP60-C, potentially opening new therapeutic avenues for cancer and developmental diseases.
