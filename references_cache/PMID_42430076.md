Keratoconus is the most common corneal ectatic disorder worldwide. Typically 
manifesting in adolescence, it imposes a substantial burden on quality of life 
and, if untreated, may progress to advanced stages requiring corneal 
transplantation. Riboflavin/UV-A corneal cross-linking (CXL) has become the 
cornerstone therapy for stabilizing corneal biomechanics and halting disease 
progression. CXL acts through UV-A photo-activated riboflavin-driven 
photochemistry that induces covalent cross-links within stromal proteins. 
Extensive theoretical, experimental, and clinical evidence has clarified its 
mechanism of action, demonstrating ultrastructural stromal reorganization, 
resulting in biomechanical stiffening and a sustained long-term effect. Over 
time, riboflavin ophthalmic formulations for CXL, delivery strategies, and 
treatment protocols have progressed and expanded beyond the epithelium-off 
approach to include safer and comparably effective transepithelial techniques. 
In addition, novel chemical cross-linkers are being investigated as 
non-light-based alternatives for corneal stabilization. Beyond corneal ectatic 
disorders, CXL is increasingly being explored for additional clinical 
indications, including infectious keratitis, bullous keratopathy, and 
inflammatory dry eye. This systematic review describes the most recent advances 
in understanding the mechanism of action of CXL and associated ultrastructural 
changes and synthesizes current evidence on the evolution of treatment protocols 
and their expanding clinical indications. By critically appraising available 
data, we aim to guide clinical decision-making and identify priorities toward 
safer, more effective, and individualized CXL therapies.

Key Summary Points 
Corneal cross-linking (CXL) has evolved from 
the original Dresden protocol into a broad 
therapeutic platform for keratoconus and 
other corneal disorders through advances in 
riboflavin delivery, UV-A photoactivation, 
and personalized treatment strategies.
Emerging transepithelial approaches have 
improved the safety profile of CXL while 
progressively narrowing the efficacy gap with 
conventional epithelium-off protocols.
Theranostic-guided corneal UV therapy ena-
bles real-time monitoring of stromal ribofla-
vin concentration and photoactivation, sup-
porting personalized and precise treatment 
strategies and enabling accurate prediction of 
the CXL outcome.
Emerging CXL strategies are exploring 
spatially controlled riboflavin delivery and 
adaptive UV-A-mediated photoactivation 
of riboflavin to enable patient-specific bio-
mechanical corneal remodeling based on 
individual clinical and tomographic charac-
teristics.
CXL is performed in indications beyond 
corneal ectasia, including as an adjunctive 
treatment for infectious keratitis and in 
selected cases of bullous keratopathy and dry 
eye syndrome.
INTRODUCTION
Keratoconus is a progressive, usually bilateral, 
chronic disease of the cornea characterized by 
stromal thinning, biochemical and biomechanical 
tissue weakening that leads to corneal bulging 
and visual impairment. The etiopathogenesis of 
keratoconus is multifactorial, including genetic 
and environmental risk factors. A familial history is 
present in approximately 18% of cases, as reported 
in various epidemiological studies, underscoring 
the importance of hereditary factors in the 
disease’s development [21, 112, 126]. In addition 
to its genetic heterogeneity, several environmental 
factors, notably dysregulated immunity responses 
associated with allergy and the habit of eye 
rubbing, play a significant role in triggering or 
accelerating disease progression [31, 113, 162]. 
Keratoconus often has an insidious clinical onset in 
adolescence and manifests with progressive visual 
decline caused by irregular astigmatism, myopia, 
high-order aberrations (HOAs), and in advanced 
cases, corneal scarring. Due to its onset at a young 
age, the disease can have a profound negative 
impact on patients’ quality of life [60, 85].
Treatment of keratoconus begins with glasses 
or contact lenses to improve visual acuity. While 
contact lenses can often provide improved visual 
performance, up to 40% of patients may not 
wear them due to poor tolerance, ocular surface 
discomfort, difficulty with fitting and handling, 
or advancing corneal irregularity. Moreover, 
contact lenses do not affect the natural course 
of the disease or halt its progression, which 
remains closely associated with worsening 
visual function over time [12]. Para-surgical 
intervention, such as corneal cross-linking 
with riboflavin and ultraviolet-A (UV-A) light 
(CXL), aims to stabilize the corneal structure 
and biomechanics, thereby slowing or arresting 
disease progression. In more advanced cases, 
corneal transplantation, either deep anterior 
lamellar keratoplasty (DALK) or, less frequently, 
penetrating keratoplasty (PK), may be required 
to replace severely damaged corneal tissue and 
restore vision. However, corneal transplantation 
remains associated with significant long-
term risks and graft failure, reinforcing the 
importance of early disease stabilization. 
Notably, the risk for re-grafting in keratoconus 
is higher than 30% over a long-term period 
(≥ 10 years), which makes corneal graft not a 
definite solution for the disease since the risk 
of graft rejection persists throughout life [50, 
206]. For these reasons, an early, safe, and 
effective intervention, such as CXL, represents 
a fundamental strategy for reducing disease 
burden. By slowing ectatic progression, CXL 
may reduce both visual disability and healthcare 
burden [30, 57].

The CXL procedure was originally designed 
for the treatment of corneal ectatic disorders, 
such as keratoconus and post-laser refractive 
surgery ectasia. More recently, it has shown 
promise in additional indications, including 
the treatment of bullous keratopathy and the 
decontamination of corneal tissue in cases 
of infectious keratitis caused by pathogenic 
microorganisms [194]. New indications of 
crosslinking are emerging in the field of 
inflammatory dry eye management, due to its 
capacity in reducing metalloproteinases activity 
and ocular surface inflammation [129].
Since its introduction in 2003 in Europe, 
and its later adoption in the United States in 
2016, CXL has become a widely studied and 
accepted treatment. To date, over 3000 scientific 
publications have documented its experimental 
and clinical outcomes. The original Dresden 
protocol involves epithelial removal (epi-off), 
followed by 30 min of 20% dextran-enriched 
0.1% riboflavin and UV-A irradiation at 3 mW/
cm2 for 30 min (total dose 5.4 J/cm 2). Despite 
its efficacy, epi-off CXL carries inherent risks. 
The most frequent adverse events are mild and 
transient, particularly corneal haze and corneal 
edema, which generally resolve completely 
within 1 year. Severe adverse events leading to 
permanent sequelae are uncommon, with a 
cumulative incidence ≤ 1.5%, including infectious 
keratitis (0.6%) and corneal scarring (0.5%) [174]. 
Furthermore, the conventional Dresden protocol 
requires a minimum stromal thickness of 400 µm 
after de-epithelialization to minimize the risk 
of endothelial damage, a concern that may be 
exacerbated by dextran-induced intraoperative 
corneal thinning and inadequate riboflavin 
saturation [54]. To overcome these limitations, 
transepithelial (epi-on) protocols have been 
developed to preserve the epithelium, reduce 
complications, and expand eligibility to thinner 
corneas, although their clinical efficacy has only 
recently improved. Advances in epi-on CXL have 
addressed earlier limitations through improved 
riboflavin formulations, optimized delivery 
methods, and refined UV irradiation, enabling 
treatments increasingly tailored to the individual 
cornea [24, 36, 156]. Beyond UV-A light-induced 
cross-linking, emerging chemical cross-linkers 
offer a promising avenue for corneal stabilization, 
potentially providing non-light-based strategies to 
strengthen the cornea and complement existing 
CXL therapies.
In this systematic review, we aim to provide a 
comprehensive overview of the evolving role of 
CXL in the treatment of keratoconus and corneal 
ectatic disorders, as well as in other emerging 
indications such as bullous keratopathy, 
infectious keratitis, and inflammatory dry 
eye. We update current knowledge on the 
mechanism of action of CXL and associated 
ultrastructural changes and summarize evidence 
from experimental and clinical studies on 
treatment protocols. By critically evaluating the 
available evidence, this review aims to inform 
clinical decision-making and identify future 
directions for more personalized treatment 
strategies of pathological corneal conditions.
BASIC SCIENCE OF CORNEAL 
CROSS‑LINKING
Photochemistry of CXL
Riboflavin is a molecule classified as one of the 
B vitamins  (B2). It is a heterocyclic compound 
consisting of a flavin molecule to which a chain 
formed by ribitol is attached [89]. It is a unique 
photochemical agent because it can act as a pho-
tosensitizing agent and the light-excited molecule 
itself can also act as an electron donor [76]. Ribofla-
vin absorbs maximally in the blue (λmax = 445 nm) 
and in the near ultraviolet (λmax = 373 nm); it also 
has two absorbance peaks in UV-C at about 260- 
and 225-nm [115, 182]. When excited by near 
ultraviolet or by blue light, riboflavin exhibits a 
strong green fluorescence; the UV-induced fluores-
cence emission has a maximum at 534 nm (range, 
480–600 nm) [79]. Upon UV-A irradiation, ribofla-
vin in its excited triplet state can trigger two com-
plementary photochemical pathways, type I and 
type II mechanisms, and can photosensitize the 
oxidization of a great variety of substrates, such as 
amino acids (Fig. 1) [32, 115, 140, 159].
In the type I mechanism, triplet riboflavin 
abstracts an electron or hydrogen atom from 
the substrate, forming riboflavin radicals and 
substrate radicals. These reactive intermediates,

particularly involving amino acids such as 
lysine, hydroxylysine, arginine, cysteine and 
tyrosine, can recombine directly or react with 
oxidized groups to form new covalent bonds 
or undergo further reactions. A key outcome is 
the formation of Schiff bases between lysine or 
hydroxylysine residues, which contributes to 
the generation of cross-linking bonds among 
collagen fibrils and proteoglycan core proteins. 
This radical-mediated process is favored when 
riboflavin concentration is high, can proceed 
under hypoxic conditions and is considered the 
main pathway responsible for the biomechanical 
stiffening effect of CXL [107, 110, 171]. 
Oxygen may, however, contribute to riboflavin 
regeneration by oxidizing both riboflavin 
radicals and reduced riboflavin, thereby slowing 
the decrease in riboflavin concentration. This 
allows more riboflavin molecules to be excited to 
high-energy states, promoting the crosslinking 
reaction [185].
In the type II mechanism, the excited triplet 
riboflavin transfers energy to molecular oxygen, 
generating singlet oxygen ( 1O2) as the main 
reactive oxygen species (ROS), and to a lesser 
extent superoxide anion  (O2–) [84, 173]. Singlet 
oxygen is highly electrophilic and reacts with 
susceptible amino acid side chains, including 
methionine, cysteine, and aromatic amino 
acids, in particular tyrosine. These oxidative 
modifications promote the formation of 
covalent cross-links, such as dityrosine bonds 
and methionine sulfoxide bridges. Because 
oxygen availability can influence this pathway 
by affecting the steady-state concentration 
of tyrosyl radicals and their dimerization 
efficiency, its contribution is greater where 
oxygen tension is relatively high [84, 172, 185]. 
However, oxygen has poor solubility in the 
aqueous stromal environment, and under the 
highly photo-oxidative conditions generated 
by UV-A light exposure in the presence 
of riboflavin, it is rapidly and irreversibly 
consumed. Consequently, the pathway’s activity 
is transient, producing an immediate, localized 
depletion zone that restricts both the spatial 
extent and the uniformity of oxygen-mediated 
cross-linking to the most anterior stromal layers.

oxygen concentration and using pulsed UV 
irradiation have been explored to modulate 
intrastromal oxygen availability, although

Overall, the relative contribution of type I and 
type II mechanisms to the formation of corneal 
cross-linking bonds depends on riboflavin 
concentration, UV-A irradiance and effective 
riboflavin photoactivation, oxygen availability, and 
the abundance of oxidizable substrates, particularly 
lysine, hydroxylysine, methionine, and tyrosine. 
Type II can contribute at the beginning of UV 
irradiation and in the most anterior stroma, while 
type I is more relevant as oxygen is consumed 
and in anterior as well as deeper stromal regions. 
Enhancing riboflavin penetration may therefore 
strengthen type I-mediated cross-linking, overall 
improving the homogeneity of biomechanical 
stiffening across the corneal thickness. It is now 
well established that in the presence of an adequate 
stromal concentration of riboflavin (≥ 0.012%), the 
type I direct photochemical pathway plays a central 
role in generating additional cross-linking bonds 
among stromal proteins [110]. Experimental studies 
on human donor eyes have shown that corneal 
riboflavin concentration decreases exponentially 
during UV-A irradiation at both 3 mW/cm2 and 
10 mW/cm2 for total 5.4 J/cm2 energy dose [118]. 
This behavior is consistent with the knowledge 
that the UV-A light-mediated photo-degradation of 
riboflavin in the stroma is spatially homogeneous 
and temporally monotonic decreasing [114, 
118, 184]. Since the intrastromal riboflavin 
concentration is relatively low (≤  0.04%), the 
overall photochemical process can be considered 
pseudo-first order under anaerobic conditions 
[114, 118]; consequently, an exponential kinetic 
consumption of riboflavin is expected. This implies 
that, in an ambient environment (i.e., 21% partial 
pressure of oxygen), the role of type I mechanism 
is predominant in driving the formation of new 
chemical bonds between stromal proteins during 
CXL; in addition, the concentration of riboflavin 
directly influences the efficiency of oxygen 
utilization [84, 118, 185]. However, there remains a 
lack of studies systematically assessing the effects of 
oxygen supplementation in relation to quantified 
corneal riboflavin concentrations. Further studies 
are needed to elucidate the effect of high-irradiance 
and/or high-energy dose UV-A protocols on the 
kinetics of CXL and the relative contributions of 
type I and type II pathways on clinical outcomes.
Ultra‑Structural and Biomechanical Changes 
Induced by CXL
Keratoconic corneas themselves represent a chal-
lenge. The biomechanical, structural, and mor-
phological properties of the keratoconic cornea 
vary widely between patients, and even across 
regions of the same cornea. Beyond inter- and 
intra-corneal variability, the photochemistry of 
CXL is strongly influenced by the penetration 
of riboflavin, the absorption of UV-A light radia-
tion, and local oxygen availability, all of which 
ultimately determine the depth and magnitude 
of stromal changes.
Experimental studies have demonstrated that 
CXL significantly enhances the biomechanical 
strength of the corneal tissue by increasing its 
rigidity, inducing ultrastructural changes such 
as increased interfibrillar spacing and greater 
collagen fiber diameter [15]. Translating these 
findings into clinical practice, the improved 
mechanical resistance enables the cornea to bet-
ter withstand normal physiological stress, such 
as intraocular pressure, thereby stabilizing the 
progression of keratoconus and other corneal 
ectatic disorders.
On the ultra-structural level, one of the 
earliest and most consistent findings after CXL 
is keratocyte apoptosis within the anterior 
stroma [142, 168]; this process occurs almost 
immediately following treatment. Repopulation 
by activated keratocytes begins at deeper stromal 
layers after one to three months and is associated 
with increased stromal reflectivity and transient 
corneal haze. Most corneas show substantial 
recovery by six to twelve months after CXL. Sub-
basal nerve fibers are also affected, with early 
degeneration followed by slow regeneration 
over months to years, explaining the transient 
reduction in corneal sensitivity and changes 
in nerve tortuosity observed with confocal 
microscopy that may persist long term.
Collagen fibril architecture undergoes 
measurable remodeling after CXL. Studies using 
sophisticated imaging techniques (electron 

 Ophthalmol Ther
microscopy, two-photon microscopy, X-ray 
scattering) have documented nearly constant 
riboflavin diffusion between 100 µm and 250 µm 
in epi-on delivery protocols and to 300 µm 
in epi-off delivery protocols before declining 
toward the endothelium. Following UV-A 
irradiation, these studies also reported modest 
increases in fibril diameter, reduced interfibrillar 
spacing, and enhanced packing regularity within 
the treated stroma [3, 20, 72]. Anterior collagen 
lamellae demonstrate compaction and improved 
interlamellar cohesion, changes that are believed 
to reduce the tendency for lamellar slippage 
under stress. Biochemically, there is evidence 
for new covalent bonds among collagen fibrils 
resembling those formed by natural enzymatic 
processes, such as lysyl oxidase activity [141]. 
These changes improve biomechanical tissue 
strength and increase resistance to enzymatic 
digestion, overall conferring greater structural 
integrity [183]. Proteoglycans also participate in 
the remodeling process; CXL has been shown 
to upregulate small leucine-rich proteoglycans, 
such as lumican, decorin, and keratocan, while 
downregulating matrix metalloproteinases, 
which further stabilizes the extracellular matrix 
and contributes to improved fibril spacing and 
transparency [208]. Based on these insights, 
in 2015 the authors proposed revising the 
terminology of the procedure, recommending 
that the term “collagen” be omitted from “corneal 
collagen cross-linking” in order to more accurately 
reflect the procedure’s broader impact on the 
corneal proteins and extracellular matrix [115, 
116].
The mentioned ultra-structural modifica-
tions translate into measurable biomechanical 
improvements. Ex vivo experiments using sev-
eral testing methodologies (atomic force micros-
copy, strip extensiometry, inflation testing, 
dynamic tonometry, Brillouin microscopy, opti-
cal coherence elastography) demonstrated a sig-
nificant increase in tissue elasticity and decrease 
in hysteresis after CXL, with effects concen-
trated in the anterior 200 μm of the stroma [15, 
96]. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) has been 
pivotal in quantifying nano-scale biomechani-
cal effects of CXL in human corneas, determin-
ing the supra-molecular changes in collagen 
fibril/bundle mechanics and the surrounding 
proteoglycan matrix [38, 101, 102]. Localized 
stiffening of the anterior stroma has also been 
confirmed by ex vivo depth-resolved Brillouin 
microscopy and by in vivo optical coherence 
elastography imaging, with both techniques 
independently validating this finding [51, 170]. 
Several testing techniques further confirmed 
increases in tangent, tensile and shear moduli, 
highlighting the anisotropic and depth-depend-
ent nature of CXL-induced stiffening [15, 69, 
93].
Taken together, ultrastructural and biome-
chanical studies have shown that CXL induces 
measurable, depth-dependent increases in stro-
mal stiffness, with the greatest effect localized to 
the anterior 200 μm [205]. These changes arise 
from a cascade of molecular and supramolecu-
lar modifications, including changes in collagen 
fibrils’ interactions, proteoglycan stabilization, 
and reduced tissue viscoelasticity, which are 
directly linked to the spatial dynamics of ribo-
flavin distribution and UV-A light absorption 
[79, 114, 118, 171, 185]. Nonetheless, the influ-
ence of heterogeneity in baseline ultrastructure, 
biomechanics, and treatment response remains 
incompletely understood. The traditional 
empirical ‘one-protocol-fits-all’ CXL treatment 
approach cannot adequately account for vari-
ability in corneal architecture and biomechan-
ics, nor does it consider riboflavin stromal dis-
tribution and UV-A-mediated photoactivation, 
thereby contributing to the unpredictability of 
treatment outcomes and reinforcing the need for 
tailored strategies to maximize the therapeutic 
benefit of CXL for each patient.

Eligibility Criteria for CXL
Current recommendations for eligibility to 
perform CXL prioritize treatment of progressive 
keratoconus. Nevertheless, definition of disease 
progression remains a subject of ongoing 
debate [59]. The most widely accepted approach 
involves documenting progression through 
multiple single indicators assessed over at least 

Ophthalmol Ther 
two consecutive visits within the past 12 months 
or a longer observation period.
The  Kmax index remains the most endorsed 
single metric for detecting clinically meaning-
ful steepening of the cornea and is therefore 
retained as the primary endpoint for defining 
progression, supported by additional confirma-
tory structural and functional parameters to 
ensure diagnostic robustness. Currently widely 
accepted eligibility criteria are summarized in 
Table 1. Candidates must have evidence of mani-
fest keratoconus (i.e.,  Kmax ≥ 47 D) and sufficient 
corneal thickness, which is typically required to 
be ≥ 400 µm at the thinnest point, although the 
most recent 0.22% riboflavin formulations are 
approved for use in thinner corneas, down to 
370 µm [117].
Several authors have emphasized the need to 
improve the prediction of disease progression 
and optimize the timing of CXL treatment by 
incorporating multiple tomographic biomarkers 
and novel analytical approaches based on zonal 
indices of anterior curvature and corneal thick-
ness [82]. Recent studies have shown that zonal 
average analysis of corneal tomography data sur-
rounding the point of maximum curvature is 
more sensitive and accurate than single-point 
indices for detecting progressive disease over 
long-term observation [82, 123]. Likewise, the 
ABCD grading system, which evaluates a 3-mm 
optical zone centered on the thinnest corneal 
point, has been shown to be more sensitive to 
identify disease progression than  Kmax alone 
[196].
There is broad expert consensus that 
CXL should not be restricted by patient age 
in keratoconic eyes showing evidence of 
progression, although most patients are treated 
between 12 and 40 years [59]. Given the higher 
likelihood of rapid progression in younger 
individuals, experts recommend performing 
CXL even at first diagnosis in adolescents with 
high-risk factors, such as a positive family 
history, atopy, allergy, chronic conjunctivitis, 
blepharitis, or associated skin diseases. Disease 
progression within 1 year has been reported in 
up to 88% of pediatric patients, and keratoconus 
remains one of the most frequent indications 
for corneal transplantation in childhood, 
accounting for approximately 15–20% of all 
corneal transplants performed in individuals 
under 18 years of age [27, 95].

The Original Dresden Protocol
Riboflavin/UV-A corneal cross-linking accord -
ing to the original Dresden protocol represents 
the historical cornerstone of keratoconus man-
agement. The procedure was first introduced 
by Wollensak, Spoerl, and Seiler in 2003 at the 
University of Dresden, marking the first clinical 
translation of photochemical tissue stabilization 
into ophthalmology. The original technique 
involves the removal of the central 9 mm of cor-
neal epithelium (“epithelium-off” or “epi-off”) 
followed by application of a 0.1% riboflavin 
solution in 20% dextran T-500 onto the stroma 
for 30 min. Subsequently, the cornea is irradi-
ated with UV-A light (370 ± 5 nm wavelength) at 
an irradiance of 3 mW/cm2 for 30 min, deliver-
ing a total energy dose of 5.4 J/cm2 [203].
The clinical efficacy of the original Dresden  
protocol has been established across numer-
ous randomized controlled trials and large case 
series. In progressive keratoconus, stabilization 
or improvement in maximum keratometry 
 (Kmax) is observed in a median of 70% of eyes at 
1 year and longer period [73, 156, 202]. Numer-
ous long-term studies demonstrate sustained 
disease control, minimal endothelial cell loss, 
and low rates of retreatment up to 15 years after 
treatment [147, 156, 174, 175]. Failure rates, 
defined as an increase in  Kmax ≥ 1.0 D or contin-
ued visual deterioration, ranged from 14 to 33%, 
depending on disease severity, corneal thickness, 
and preoperative  Kmax [49, 97, 156].
The most frequent complications included 
delayed epithelial healing (~ 5%), sterile corneal 
infiltrates (~ 2%), infectious keratitis (~ 1%), 
transient corneal haze (≥ 30%), and corneal 
scarring (~ 1%) [27, 37, 97, 174]. Rare but severe 
events, such as corneal melt and endothelial 
decompensation have been described mostly in 
females with thin corneas or following infection, 
or abuse of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory 
drug (NSAID) eye drops or improper riboflavin 
application and/or UV irradiation [97, 174, 188].
Despite its proven efficacy and status as 
the benchmark for corneal cross-linking, 
the standard Dresden protocol has several 
limitations. These include the long duration of 
the procedure and the requirement for epithelial 
removal, which can cause postoperative pain, 
increase the risk of infection, lead to corneal 
scarring, and delay visual recovery [97 ]. In 
addition, dextran-induced corneal dehydration 
during riboflavin soaking may result in 
significant stromal thinning, increasing the 
risk of endothelial damage if corneal thickness 
falls below 400 µm [204]. While the Dresden 
protocol’s long-term safety and efficacy are 
well established, these concerns have prompted 
multiple protocol modifications aimed at 
reducing treatment time, improving patient 
comfort, and extending eligibility to thinner 
corneas. Such advancements have led to 
accelerated, transepithelial, and personalized 
approaches, which are described in the following 
sections.
“Accelerated” CXL Protocols
To reduce the treatment time of the stand-
ard Dresden protocol , accelerated CXL (A-CXL) 
protocols were developed based on the Bun-
sen–Roscoe law of reciprocity, which assumes 
that equivalent total fluence yields comparable 
biological effects when irradiance and exposure 
time are inversely adjusted. Common regimens 
include 9 mW/cm2 for 10 min, 18 mW/cm 2 for 
5 min, and 30 mW/cm 2 for 3 min (5.4 J/cm 2 
total dose).
However, experimental and clinical data 
indicate that reciprocity is only partially 
valid, as oxygen availability and riboflavin 
photochemistry significantly influence efficacy. 
The law of reciprocity between irradiance and 
exposure time applies only within a limited 
range, as shown by Lombardo et al. [118] using 
theranostic UV device. These findings explain 
why the 9 mW/cm2 for 10 min regimen is now 
regarded as the optimal accelerated protocol, 
providing an effective balance between 
treatment efficiency and safety [70, 130, 179, 
192].
Higher irradiances (≥ 18  mW/cm 2) have 
been associated with reduced biomechanical 
stiffening and shallower stromal demarcation 
lines, and irradiances ≥ 45 mW/cm 2 have been

reported not to further increase corneal stiffness 
[65, 98, 201].
Despite these limitations, A-CXL is widely 
adopted due to shorter procedure time, 
improved comfort, and generally comparable 
efficacy and safety to the standard protocol. 
Meta-analyses show that 9 mW/cm2 for 10 min 
achieves outcomes similar to the standard Dres-
den protocol CXL, while higher irradiances yield 
more variable results. The 9- or 10- mW/cm 2 
protocols are therefore considered the optimal 
balance between efficiency and predictability 
[65, 70, 98, 118, 128, 130, 134, 177, 179, 192].
Thin corneas require individualized protocols 
to avoid excessive photochemical effects and 
endothelial risk. Pachymetry-guided approaches, 
such as the M nomogram, adjust irradiance and 
exposure time while maintaining a fixed flu-
ence of 5.4 J/cm2 (Table 2) [134]. These strategies 
improve control of cross-linking depth and have 
shown favorable long-term outcomes in kerato-
conus and post-refractive ectasia [134]
Selective Epithelial Removal and Excimer 
Laser‑Assisted Techniques in Corneal 
Cross‑Linking
Several CXL approaches have incorporated 
selective epithelial removal strategies to balance 
treatment efficacy with faster visual recovery 
and reduced postoperative discomfort. Different 
patterns of partial de-epithelialization have been 
explored, including vertical stripe debridement 
techniques designed to facilitate riboflavin pen-
etration through exposed stromal areas while 
preserving epithelial bridges that may promote 
faster epithelial healing. In a matched compara-
tive study, Hashemi et al. [67] reported that 
both total and partial epithelial removal were 
effective in stabilizing keratoconus progression, 
with mean  Kmax changes of 0.4 D and 0.0 D, 
respectively. Partial debridement was associated 
with slightly greater improvement in corrected 
distance visual acuity and better preservation of 
central corneal thickness. Similarly, Galvis et al. 
[55] reported long-term ectasia stabilization over 
a 5-year follow-up using a partial de-epitheliali-
zation approach.
Other geometry-based approaches have included 
preservation of a central epithelial island to protect 
the visual axis and improve postoperative recovery. 
This strategy has been associated with reduced 
postoperative discomfort, lower rate of corneal 
haze, and faster visual rehabilitation. In a recent 
comparative clinical trial, Khalil et al. [83] reported 
effective stabilization of paracentral progressive 
keratoconus with significantly lower postoperative 
pain and faster visual recovery compared with 
conventional complete epithelial removal.

assisted epithelial removal has further refined 
debridement strategies through transepithelial 
phototherapeutic keratectomy (t-PTK). Unlike 
conventional mechanical debridement, t-PTK 
enables highly uniform epithelial removal at

Transepithelial CXL Protocols
Transepithelial (“epithelium-on” or “epi-on”) 
CXL was developed as a less invasive alterna-
tive to the epi-off procedure, with the aim of 
improving patient comfort, accelerating visual 
recovery, and reducing the risk of complications 
associated with epithelial removal.
The original transepithelial CXL (2010) used 
15% dextran-enriched 0.1% riboflavin applied to 
intact epithelium but showed poor stromal pen-
etration due to the epithelial barrier and hyper-
tonic dextran-based formulation. Stabilization 
rates were low (~ 10%), highlighting the need for 
improved formulations and delivery strategies 
[23, 29, 176, 181]. The intact epithelium acts as a 
substantial barrier to dextran-enriched riboflavin 
formulation, resulting in very low or virtually 
no stromal concentration of the photosensitizer.
Over the past two decades, substantial 
efforts have focused on optimizing riboflavin 
formulations to enhance corneal penetration 
through the intact epithelium and to 
achieve reproducible and effective outcomes 
with transepithelial CXL techniques. New 
formulations (dextran-free, hypotonic, higher 
riboflavin concentration, and permeation 
enhancers such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic 
acid (EDTA), trometamol (Tris), sodium chloride 
(NaCl), benzalkonium chloride (BAK), sodium 
iodide (NaI), and vitamin E TPGS) improved 
epithelial penetration, with hypotonicity 
and increased riboflavin concentration being 
key determinants, as hypotonicity creates an 
osmotic gradient that facilitates the penetration 
of riboflavin through epithelial tight junctions 
[24, 25, 71, 79, 90, 117, 157]. Viscoelastic-
free, high-concentration formulations (0.22% 
riboflavin) achieved the best stromal uptake, 
explaining variability in clinical outcomes 
[71, 117]. These differences underscored the 
critical importance of carefully considering the 
composition and physicochemical properties 
of riboflavin formulations among different 
products, which may partly explain the high 
variability in clinical outcomes.
To further enhance riboflavin penetration 
through the epithelium, corneal iontophoresis-
assisted delivery has been developed [46, 61]. 
This method applies a mild electrical current 
to drive riboflavin into the stroma, achieving 
higher intrastromal concentrations than 
manual passive diffusion while maintaining 
epithelial integrity [ 8, 119 ]. The ionized 
substances are driven into the tissue primarily 
by two mechanisms, such as electromigration 
(i.e., the motion of molecules based on flow 
of current) and electro-osmosis (i.e., the 
motion of the solvent). Experimental studies 
have demonstrated that although the stromal 
concentration of riboflavin after corneal 
iontophoresis was lower than conventional 
epi-off delivery, the stiffening effect on the 
anterior cornea (after 10 mW/cm 2 for 9 min 
UV-A irradiation) was almost comparable to 
that of conventional epi-off CXL [124]. In 
addition, clinical studies have assessed the 
long-term safety and efficacy of this approach 
in the treatment of progressive keratoconus 
with transepithelial CXL approach [16, 22, 
119, 120, 135, 163, 197]. Long-term studies, 
including the RCT NCT02117999 have shown 
considerable variability, with  Kmax flattening 
ranging from – 0.3 D to – 1.1 D and progression 
rates exceeding 25%, particularly in younger 
patients who exhibit higher failure rates [22, 36, 
119, 120, 163].
Intraoperative oxygen supplementation has also 
been proposed to improve clinical outcomes in 
transepithelial CXL approaches [159]. Laboratory 
studies demonstrated that delivering oxygen 
directly to the corneal surface during irradiation 
increases the availability of reactive oxygen 
species (ROS) and enhances the biomechanical 

Ophthalmol Ther 
stiffening effect of CXL [75]. Clinical translation 
of these findings has confirmed that oxygen 
supplementation can improve outcomes, especially 
in accelerated and transepithelial protocols 
[132]. Mazzotta et al. [132] reported significant 
 Kmax flattening when oxygen was continuously 
delivered during pulsed UV-A exposure in a high-
irradiance transepithelial protocol (30 mW/cm2 
power density for total 7.2 J/cm 2 energy dose). 
These results suggested that maintaining adequate 
oxygen concentration at the corneal surface could 
restore the efficiency of the type II photochemical 
pathway and enhance the transepithelial cross-
linking effect, particularly in conditions where 
oxygen diffusion is limited, such as during 
high-irradiance UV-A exposure. In this view, 
recent experimental studies and mathematical 
models have shown that oxygen may contribute 
to riboflavin regeneration by oxidizing both 
riboflavin radicals and reduced riboflavin, 
thereby sustaining a type I photopolymerization 
mechanism and prolonging the availability of 
reactive species during UV-A irradiation [185]. 
Taken together, these findings support the concept 
that controlled oxygen availability may have 
implications for optimizing treatment protocols. 
However, further evidence is needed, and real-time 
monitoring of riboflavin during CXL (as enabled 
by emerging theranostic technology) may help 
clarify the relative contributions of type I and type 
II mechanisms to corneal cross-link formation, 
particularly when intraoperative oxygen 
supplementation is used to improve the efficacy 
and reproducibility of epi-on CXL protocols.
Pulsed UV-A irradiation, typically using a 1-s 
on/1-s off pattern, has been shown in a few clinical 
studies to produce deeper stromal demarcation 
lines compared with continuous exposure at 
the same total fluence [18, 131, 143, 154]. The 
rationale for high-fluence and pulsed irradiation 
protocols derives from the dynamic interplay 
between UV-A exposure, oxygen consumption, 
and replenishment. Continuous high-intensity 
irradiation rapidly depletes stromal oxygen, 
limiting ROS-mediated cross-linking photo-
chemistry; in contrast, introducing intermittent 
“off” phases between light pulses may allow partial 
oxygen re-diffusion into the stroma, sustaining the 
type II photo-polymerization pathway [84, 185, 
201]. Authors demonstrated that pulsed irradiation 
at either 10 mW/cm 2 or 30 mW/cm 2 for total 
5.4 J/cm2 energy dose achieved a demarcation line 
depth of 200–250 µm, significantly deeper than 
that observed with continuous light exposure 
(150–180  µm). However, under laboratory 
conditions, pulsed UV-A irradiation alone did 
not consistently enhance corneal biomechanical 
strength compared with continuous exposure [94].
Two identical phase 3 RCTs of epi-on CXL 
have been recently completed in the U.S with 
FDA approval. Both the NCT03442751 and 
NCT05759559 trials enrolled 201 subjects with 
progressive keratoconus, aged 12–55 years old 
(n = 280 eyes), using a 2:1 allocation to CXL 
versus sham treatment. The primary endpoint 
was the change in  Kmax at 12 months, reporting 
an average flattening of 0.4  D in the treatment 
group compared with an average steepening of 
0.7 D in controls. Severe adverse events occurred 
in 0.5% of treated eyes, and overall safety was 
generally acceptable. While peer-reviewed 
publications are not yet available, the study 
results are publicly reported on clinicaltrials.
gov. Two more phase 3 RCTs of epi-on CXL 
are ongoing in the U.S; the APRICITY trials 
(NCT06100939 and NCT06100952) are two 
identical multicenter, double-masked, sham-
controlled studies in patients aged 8–45 years 
old. Up to 800 patients have been enrolled 
across 26 clinics, randomized 1:1 to active 
treatment or sham, with bilateral treatments 
permitted. Primary endpoint focuses on 
12-month visual acuity. Both trials are ongoing, 
with no published results at the time of review.
As described in Sect.  "Theranostic-guided 
corneal UV therapy", emerging iontophore-
sis-assisted approaches integrated with thera-
nostic technology enable spatially controlled 
delivery of riboflavin into the stroma, with the 
potential to improve treatment customization 
based on corneal geometry and biomechanical 
characteristics.
Comparative Clinical Outcomes Epi‑On vs. 
Epi‑Off CXL Protocols
Epi-on CXL is safer but historically less effective, 
with higher progression rates (up to 75%) in 
early studies [4, 176, 181, 209]. However, modern 

 Ophthalmol Ther
optimized protocols (corneal iontophoresis, 
enhanced formulations, oxygen, pulsed UV) 
are reducing this gap [17, 39, 81, 105, 119, 120, 
145, 146, 198, 200]. The meta-analysis by D’Oria 
et al. [40] pooled data from 17 comparative 
studies, demonstrating that when optimized 
formulations and enhanced delivery methods 
are used, the rate of keratoconus progression 
does not differ significantly between epi-on 
and epi-off CXL protocols. Visual, topographic, 
pachymetric, and endothelial parameters were 
comparable at 12- and 24 months, while the 
incidence of postoperative corneal haze was 
significantly lower in epi-on CXL procedures.
Taken together, the current body of evidence 
supports that optimized transepithelial CXL 
protocols, particularly those incorporating 0.2% 
riboflavin formulation, iontophoresis delivery, 
oxygen supplementation, and pulsed high-flu-
ence UV-A irradiation, can achieve comparable 
disease stabilization to epi-off CXL protocols 
in adult patients with progressive keratoconus. 
However, younger patients (< 18 years) or those 
with advanced disease stage may still benefit 
from the epi-off CXL approach, which provides 
the highest and most reproducible stiffening 
effect, including long-term stabilization of  Kmax, 
until further evidence demonstrates the equiva-
lence of emerging epi-on and data-driven per-
sonalized approaches (Table 3).
Overall, treatment choice should be individu-
alized based on age, disease severity, and corneal 
thickness, as technological advances progres-
sively narrow the clinical efficacy gap between 
epi-on and epi-off CXL.
Corneal Cross‑Linking in Pediatrics
Keratoconus in the pediatric population 
poses a unique therapeutic challenge due 
to its rapid progression and higher risk of 
vision loss compared to adults. Numerous 
clinical studies have confirmed the safety 
and efficacy of CXL in pediatric patients, 
consistently demonstrating its ability to halt 
disease progression with a low incidence of 
adverse events, comparable to those observed 
in adult cohorts. Standard CXL proved to be a 
safe treatment for progressive KC in pediatric 
patients with an anterior curvature progression 
rate of up to 8% at ≥ 10 years of follow-up [195]. 
Accelerated CXL was also an effective and safe 
long-term strategy to prevent progression 
of KC in pediatric patients, with a 10-year 
follow-up re-progression rate of 9.14% [11]. A 
24% regression rate could be contemplated in 
the pediatric patients who were aged 15 years 
and younger at the time of inclusion in the 
treatment protocol [133].
Collectively, these findings provided robust 
support for the clinical efficacy and safety of 
CXL in children (< 12 years old) and adolescents 
(< 18 years old), with treatment performed at a 
median age of 15 years [27, 45, 95, 133, 150, 151, 
167]. Across meta-analysis studies and several long-
term series, the mean change in  Kmax was reported 
as − 0.8 ± 0.4 D, which is almost equivalent to 
adult data outcome, while endothelial cell density 
(ECD) did not significantly change (− 39 ± 51 cells/
mm2) over a mean follow-up of two years, and 
corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) showed 
to stabilize, with a mean change of − 0.04 ± 0.05 
LogMAR in the same period. These results were 
notably in contrast to untreated controls, where 
 Kmax and visual acuity worsened in the same 
period. Specifically, Padmanabhan et al. [151] 
reported outcomes in 336 patients followed for 
up to seven years after epi-off CXL, with a mean 
 Kmax decrease of − 1.20 ± 3.55 D and stable CDVA. 
Only 24% experienced progression, compared with 
an estimated 88% progression risk in untreated 
pediatric patients [27]. Or et al. [150] corroborated 
these findings in a 5-year retrospective analysis of 
88 eyes, showing disease stabilization in treated 
eyes compared to continued progression in 
untreated fellow eyes.
Meta-analyses have also clarified protocol-
specific outcomes [2 , 7, 78, 106, 169]. Several 
authors showed that standard (3 mW/cm 2) 
and accelerated (10 mW/cm 2) epithelium-off 
CXL protocols were equally effective up to four 
years postoperatively, confirming both as viable 
therapeutic options [27, 78, 106, 150, 169]. The 
authors further demonstrated the feasibility of 
accelerated CXL in children, highlighting its 
shorter treatment time and comparable efficacy, 
with stable uncorrected distance visual acuity 
(UDVA) and CDVA maintained for up to 5 years.

Safety of Corneal Cross‑Linking
Literature data shows that in most cases CXL 
treatment-related adverse events are mild and 
transient, with corneal haze  being the most 
common. Severe complications following CXL 
are rare [37, 97, 174, 188]. It should be noted 
that complications were not consistently or 
systematically reported across clinical studies; 
therefore, the true incidence of adverse events 
may be higher than reported. Below is a 
summary by type and incidence (%) of adverse 
events after CXL:
• Severe adverse events (cumulative inci-
dence: ≤ 1%).
These include bacterial keratitis, herpetic 
keratitis, corneal melting, corneal scar, 
phototoxic endotheliitis, corneal burn, which 
may result in severe vision loss.
• Moderate adverse events (cumulative inci-
dence: ≤1%).
These include persistent corneal haze, uveitis, 
recurrent or persistent epithelial defects, sterile 
corneal infiltrates, transient epithelial erosions, 
corneal hydrops, and limbal neovascularization, 
and epithelial islands in LASIK flap (iatrogenic 
corneal ectasia).
• Mild adverse events: (cumulative incidence: 
28%).
These include transient eye pain, transient 
ocular pruritus, transient corneal haze, transient 
corneal edema, punctata keratopathy, transient 
visual clouding, dry eye sensation, conjunctival 
hyperemia. In more than 90% of cases, these 
conditions are resolved within 6 months after 
surgery. Notably, all adverse events with per-
manent sequelae were recorded only after the 
epi-off CXL protocols and were never reported 
after transepithelial CXL protocols. Epithelial 
removal is, therefore, the main risk factor for 
these adverse events in the corneal cross-linking 
procedure.
Severe adverse events in young patients 
were rare, with only a few reports of postop-
erative infections and corneal hydrops; moder-
ate adverse events were infrequent (≤ 3%) and 
mostly included sterile corneal infiltrates and 
delayed epithelial healing. Finally, persistent 
corneal haze has been reported among the most 
relevant long-term adverse events, although the 
reported incidence varies considerably across 
studies [78, 106, 174].
COMBINED CXL PROTOCOLS
Techniques like transepithelial photorefractive 
keratectomy and phototherapeutic keratectomy 
when combined with CXL, are grouped under 
the so-called “CXL plus” procedures. Their aim 
is to improve corneal regularity, visual acuity, 
and contact lens tolerance. Although corneal 
ablation in keratoconus was once viewed 
with caution due to biomechanical concerns, 
advances in imaging and customized ablation 
profiles have made it a viable option in carefully 
selected cases, enabling limited tissue removal 
while preserving stability.
Corneal cross-linking can also be combined 
with intracorneal ring segment implantation 
to both halt disease progression and improve 

Ophthalmol Ther 
corneal shape, particularly in more advanced 
stages [42].
Overall, these combined strategies reflect a 
shift toward tailored interventions that optimize 
both biomechanical stabilization and visual out-
comes in keratoconus management.
CXL Plus
The first report of combined CXL with sequen-
tial topography-guided PRK (photorefractive 
keratectomy) appeared in 2007 with the Athens 
protocol, later expanded by the Cretan protocol  
[86, 99]. Simultaneous approaches avoid ablat-
ing already cross-linked tissue, while sequential 
treatments (PRK performed 4–6 months after 
epi-off CXL) have also shown favorable visual 
and refractive outcomes [87, 100]. Overall, exci-
mer laser ablation after CXL is a safe and effec-
tive option to partially correct sphero-cylindrical 
error and reduce higher-order aberrations in 
mild-to-moderate keratoconus, with outcomes 
largely independent of age, gender, or timing.
The simultaneous method offers a distinct 
advantage by preventing interference with cross-
linked corneal tissue during laser ablation. How-
ever, retrospective case series examining corneal 
wavefront-guided trans-PRK performed 4- to 
6 months after epi-off CXL also demonstrated 
promising visual and refractive outcomes in 
these patients. This paves the way for therapies 
wherein patients who have undergone prior 
CXL could benefit from topography-guided 
or wavefront-guided excimer laser ablation to 
improve their visual acuity.
Across CXL Plus protocols, postoperative cor-
neal stability is generally comparable, though 
treatment parameters must be tailored to cor-
neal thickness and cone location [42]. Common 
eligibility criteria across the different protocols 
include patient- and tissue-related parameters, 
as follows:
• targeted keratoconus stage: stage II (range 
stages I–II) according to the Krumeich 
classification;
• average baseline age: 18.5 years old (range 
14–30 years);
• average baseline stromal thinnest point: 
435 μm (range 300–450 μm);
• average stromal ablation: 50 μm (range 
40–55 μm);
• average optical zone diameter 5.5–6.5 mm.
The use of mitomycin C following CXL in 
simultaneous topography-guided t-PTK or 
t-PRK has been associated with an increased 
risk of corneal haze and is therefore not 
recommended.
More recently, the Central Corneal Remode-
ling (CCR) protocol has introduced customized 
transepithelial ablation combined with accel-
erated CXL to achieve smoother, more regu-
lar corneal geometry through large, gradual 
transition zones guided by ray-tracing-based

CXL Combined with Intracorneal Ring 
Segments
Intracorneal ring segments (ICRS), introduced 
in the 1980s for myopia correction, act by flat-
tening the anterior cornea. Their use has since 
expanded to keratoconus, where they improve 
corneal symmetry, reduce myopic astigmatism, 
and may delay the need for corneal transplan-
tation in selected cases [153].
Candidates are typically adults with contact 
lens intolerance, CDVA between 0.4 and 0.8 
LogMAR, corneal thickness > 480 µm at the 
implantation site, alignment between refrac-
tive and keratometric axes, and no central scar-
ring [166]. Femtosecond laser-assisted implan-
tation has improved precision and safety 
compared with manual techniques; after cre-
ating an entry incision and stromal channels at 
70–80% of corneal thickness, the ring segments 
are carefully inserted into these tunnels using 
specialized forceps.
Despite their proven efficacy, synthetic ICRS 
procedures have been associated with severe 
complications, such as segment migration, 
stromal thinning leading to melt or necrosis, 
segment extrusion, corneal neovascularization, 
and infectious keratitis [34, 43, 44, 207]. In 
CXL combined with ICRS implantation, the 
reported rate of corneal perforation caused 
by the ICRS is 3.6%, while ICRS extrusion or 
explantation occurs in 4.6% of cases. To address 
these risks, corneal allogenic intrastromal 
ring segments (CAIRS) have emerged as a 
promising alternative [80]. Using donor 
corneal tissue shaped with a femtosecond 
laser, CAIRS offer improved biocompatibility 
and flexibility, with the potential for a better 
safety profile while maintaining comparable 
refractive outcomes. They can be implanted 
either simultaneously with or after cross-
linking. The surgical technique closely 
resembles that of ICRS implantation using a 
femtosecond laser; it involves the placement 
of customized, semicircular segments of 
human eye-bank donor corneal tissue into 
preformed intrastromal channels, with some 
recent modifications to facilitate implantation. 
As for ICRS, depending on the individual 
requirements of the patient, either single or 
double CAIRS segments can be employed 
[104]. However, unlike synthetic rings, CAIRS 
segments can be fashioned with a femtosecond 
laser to any desired arc length or positioned 
circumferentially within intrastromal channels. 
Overall, by integrating the morphological 
and biomechanical advantages of synthetic 
ICRS, CAIRS may provide a more favorable 
benefit–risk profile, reducing the incidence of 
adverse events while maintaining comparable 
refractive and corneal shape improvements 
[10, 104, 153, 190].
The crosslinking of CAIRS expands its poten-
tial indications by enhancing the mechanical 
rigidity of the segments, thus facilitating sur-
gical manipulation and implantation. Moreo-
ver, crosslinking promotes decellularization, 
minimizing rejection, corneal melt, counteract-
ing long-term volume loss and contributing to 
improved structural stability and preservation of 
the intended therapeutic effects over time [ 64, 
136]. Nevertheless, CAIRS implantation remains 
an evolving technique that requires long-term 
evaluation to assess tissue integration, the dura-
bility of the refractive outcomes, and the risk of 
late-onset complications [144].
In summary, both ICRS and CAIRS, par-
ticularly when combined with CXL, provide 
valuable options for visual rehabilitation in 
keratoconus, especially in patients with poor 
spectacle-corrected vision or contact lens intol-
erance, though careful selection and long-term 
evaluation remain essential [74, 80].
Two-Step Treatment with ICRS + CXL and PRK
A few studies have assessed a combined staged 
regimen of ICRS implantation followed by CXL 
and PRK [5 , 126]. This approach includes vari-
ations such as ICRS with either simultaneous 
or delayed CXL, followed by PRK (with concur-
rent CXL if not performed earlier) as a second-
stage procedure, typically scheduled at least six 
months after the first stage to allow refractive 
stability. The authors have reported improve -
ments in both UDVA and CDVA as well as in 
corneal topographic indices in patients with 
keratoconus. Regarding safety and adverse

events, the literature indicates that while this 
combined staged approach is generally safe, it 
is not without risks [5 , 126]. Potential compli-
cations include segment migration and extru-
sion, delayed epithelial healing, corneal haze, 
inadequate vision recovery, and progression of 
existing keratoconus. Therefore, careful patient 
selection and adherence to surgical protocols are 
essential to minimize these risks and optimize 
outcomes.
EMERGING INDICATIONS OF USE
CXL in Infectious Keratitis
Keratitis is an inflammation of the corneal tissue 
with multiple etiopathogenesis. The causes can 
be infectious, immune, toxic, or traumatic. Due 
to the increase of antibiotic resistance in the last 
decade, the medical community is looking for 
new methods of treatment of ophthalmic dis -
eases with infectious etiopathogenesis. The CXL 
procedure is among the treatment options that 
has proven to be safe and effective as an adju-
vant treatment for minimizing antibiotic use.
The photo-oxidative reaction induced by 
riboflavin and UV-A light exerts antimicrobial 
activity through ROS-mediated lipid peroxida -
tion of microbial cell membranes and enhances 
stromal resistance to enzymatic degradation, 
thereby helping to limit infectious progression 
[56, 91, 115].
Historically, the antimicrobial properties 
of the riboflavin/UV-A light photo-oxidative 
reaction were first demonstrated in laboratory 
settings as well as in veterinary clinical 
applications (VET-CXL) using high power 
density (30 mW/cm 2) after earlier attempts 
with conventional 3 mW/cm 2 irradiation 
proved ineffective [47, 128]. These findings 
paved the way for translation into human 
clinical practice [ 160, 187]. Nevertheless, 
evidence for CXL as an adjunct in treatment 
of bacterial keratitis remains limited, 
although several scattered open studies and 
a few RCTs have suggested that combining 
CXL with standard antibiotic therapy can 
accelerate epithelial healing and reduce the 
risk of corneal perforation [13, 35, 127]. In a 
randomized clinical trial (CLAIR), adjuvant 
CXL did not significantly improve 3-month 
visual outcomes but reduced culture positivity 
and complication rates, suggesting benefit in 
selected cases [155].
Fungal keratitis poses a greater challenge 
due to rising antifungal resistance; CXL has 
been explored as an adjunct in these cases; 
early observational and randomized studies 
show mixed results, with pilot studies report-
ing reduced ulcer size, shorter healing time, and 
decreased medication frequency when CXL is 
combined with antifungal therapy, although 
the procedure was more effective in superficial 
fungal keratitis rather than deep infections [193, 
199].
In the treatment of infectious keratitis, severe 
adverse events after CXL were reported in about 
2% of cases, including corneal melting (0.3%) 
and corneal perforation (1.6%). These events can 
often be natural consequences of the underly-
ing microbial infection and may therefore also 
reflect a lack of therapeutic efficacy rather than 
a direct complication of the treatment. Overall, 
clinical evidence for CXL in infectious keratitis 
is heterogeneous and generally low to moder-
ate quality; differences in study design, treat-
ment protocol, pathogen type, depth of corneal 
ulceration and outcome measures complicate 
study comparisons [26, 189]. Well-powered 
multicenter randomized trials are needed to 
define optimal CXL protocols for treating ker-
atitis and infectious corneal ulcers, including 
intraoperative measure of corneal riboflavin (a 
core element of theranostic-guided approaches) 
and subgroup analyses based on microbial eti-
ology, potentially identifying patients most 
likely to benefit and improving treatment 
personalization.
CXL is a promising adjunctive therapy for 
infectious keratitis, especially in bacterial and 
superficial fungal ulcers. High-irradiance proto-
cols have been confirmed to be more effective 
than low-intensity approaches, with potential to 
reduce healing time, minimize medication load, 
and prevent complications. Systematic reviews 
and meta-analyses have indicated that adjuvant 
CXL, when combined with standard antimicro-
bial therapy, may provide superior outcomes in 

 Ophthalmol Ther
certain cases, particularly in fungal keratitis, by 
promoting faster corneal healing [48].
CXL in Bullous Keratopathy
Bullous keratopathy (BK) is a corneal disease 
with primary (Fuchs’ endothelial dystrophy, 
FED) or secondary (iatrogenic) etiopathogen-
esis. It consists of a progressive imbibition of 
the stroma with progressive reduction of cor-
neal transparency and formation of epithelial 
bullae, causing light sensitivity, eye pain and 
vision loss. The therapy is medical in early dis-
ease stage through the application of hyper-
tonic and/or anti-inflammatory eye drops and 
surgical (DSAEK, DMEK) in the most advanced 
cases.
The application of CXL in treatment of BK 
has been investigated for more than ten years 
as adjunctive therapy aimed at alleviating 
symptoms and improving corneal integrity 
in eyes with advanced endothelial dysfunc-
tion. Histopathological analyses demonstrated 
that CXL can alter stromal swelling behavior 
by compacting the collagen matrix, reducing 
interfibrillar spacing and stromal water content 
and lowering stromal swelling pressure, which 
in turn improves epithelial adhesion and cor-
neal transparency [ 9, 19]. By stabilizing the 
anterior stroma and reducing its permeability 
to fluid, it was hypothesized that CXL could 
reduce corneal edema, alleviate pain associ-
ated with epithelial bullae, and possibly restore 
corneal optical quality. Overall, although the 
effect is often temporary, CXL may represent 
a palliative option in patients who are unsuit-
able for keratoplasty, with the advantage of a 
relatively favorable safety profile and absence 
of severe adverse events.
A few RCTs and retrospective studies assessed 
the efficacy of CXL in pseudophakic BK and 
corneal edema secondary to FED. Despite 
the small sample sizes in individual studies, 
the available reports consistently indicated 
that pain reduction represented the most 
reproducible outcome, with relief in some 
cases lasting for several months [28, 178]. 
Corneal thickness often showed a transient 
decrease after treatment, but this effect was not 
maintained over time, while central corneal 
clarity generally remained unchanged. Visual 
acuity improved in more than two-thirds of 
treated eyes; however, such improvement was 
not durable, even in cases where pachymetric 
stabilization and symptomatic relief were 
consistently observed. No severe or moderate 
adverse events were reported, and the most 
common mild adverse event was corneal 
erosion, with an incidence of about 3% [9 , 19, 
28, 178]. Systematic reviews have consolidated 
these results [180]. The authors concluded 
that CXL in BK may provide temporary 
symptomatic relief and transient pachymetric 
reduction, but the effect is not sustained and 
does not translate into clinically significant 
functional visual rehabilitation. Pain relief, 
when present, represents the main clinical 
benefit.
In summary, more than a decade of clinical 
work has clarified the potential role of CXL in 
the management of bullous keratopathy. While 
the initial rationale was biologically sound and 
early reports raised hopes for a non-surgical 
solution, controlled clinical trials and system-
atic reviews have shown that the benefits are 
transient, case-dependent, and primarily limited 
to symptomatic relief. Visual rehabilitation and 
restoration of corneal transparency remain out 
of reach in advanced endothelial disease, and 
endothelial corneal transplantation continues 
to be the only definitive treatment for these 
conditions.
Crosslinking in Dry‑Eye
CXL, traditionally regarded as a purely 
biomechanical intervention for halting ectatic 
progression in keratoconus, is increasingly 
emerging as a biologically active procedure 
capable of modulating ocular surface 
inflammation and tear film homeostasis. This 
concept is particularly relevant in patients with 
keratoconus and concomitant allergic ocular 
surface disorders and dry eye disease (DED). 
In these patients, ocular surface inflammation, 
eye rubbing, and tear film instability establish 
a self-perpetuating pathogenic loop that may 
contribute not only to symptom burden but 

Ophthalmol Ther 
also to ectatic progression [113]. Recent clinical 
evidence suggests that epi-off A-CXL may 
exert beneficial effects in this population of 
patients extending beyond stromal stiffening. 
In a recent study, 15 pediatric patients (30 eyes) 
with progressive keratoconus and concomitant 
allergic ocular surface disease and dry eye 
disease (DED), characterized by elevated tear 
matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) levels, were 
treated with epithelium-off A-CXL. At 1 year, in 
addition to disease stabilization, clinical efficacy 
was associated with a significant reduction in 
tear MMP-9 levels and qualitative resolution 
of inflammatory infiltrates on in vivo confocal 
microscopy [129]. These findings support the 
hypothesis that CXL may exert additional 
biological effects beyond biomechanical 
reinforcement. Reactive oxygen species 
generated during the procedure may modulate 
inflammatory signaling and epithelial-stromal 
remodeling processes, potentially interrupting 
the itch-rub-inflammation cycle frequently 
observed in allergic and pediatric phenotypes 
[113]. These preliminary observations support 
the concept that CXL may exert effects beyond 
corneal stiffening, potentially influencing ocular 
surface homeostasis. This raises the possibility 
of biomarker-guided applications and warrants 
further investigation of CXL-based strategies 
in selected inflammatory dry eye conditions 
beyond keratoconus [129, 139, 165].
EMERGING CORNEAL 
CROSS‑LINKING TECHNOLOGIES 
AND APPROACHES
In recent years, a few novel technologies have 
emerged to advance CXL beyond conventional 
riboflavin/UV-A light protocols. Among these, 
ruthenium-induced CXL and rose bengal-
mediated CXL using visible light have been 
tested in laboratory. The former approach 
used a water-soluble ruthenium photo-
initiator activated by blue light (430 nm) to 
achieve effective corneal stiffening [62]. In 
bovine corneas treated with this ruthenium-
based method, mechanical testing revealed 
a significant increase in corneal stiffness and 
no significant cellular damage or apoptosis in 
corneal epithelial or limbal stem cells. In the 
latter approach, rose bengal is illuminated by 
green light (522 nm) to stiffen the cornea or 
increase its resistance to enzymatic digestion. 
Preclinical studies in animal models (e.g., 
porcine, rabbits, lamb) have shown that the 
treatment induces a similar stromal effect 
compared to riboflavin/UV-A light CXL [14]. 
While promising, the cellular toxicity and 
potential for corneal pigmentation caused by 
rose bengal, despite being studied for many 
years, necessitate thorough evaluation and 
appropriate regulatory and conformity testing 
before clinical use in humans [186].
A more recent innovation is theranostic-
guided corneal UV therapy, which integrates 
real-time monitoring of riboflavin corneal dif-
fusion, and its UV-A light-mediated photo-acti-
vation, enabling patient-specific, adaptive treat-
ment and more reproducible clinical outcomes, 
independent of the treatment protocol chosen 
by surgeon [164]. This technology has been fully 
validated and CE-marked through preclinical 
and clinical studies, demonstrating its safety, 
efficacy, and reliability for routine clinical use.
Finally, IVMED-80 represents a pharmacologic 
cross-linking approach to treating keratoconus, 
distinct from riboflavin/UV-A corneal cross-
linking, as it is a copper-containing eye drop 
designed to activate lysyl oxidase and stimulate 
natural enzymatic cross-links within the corneal 
stroma [103].
While all these approaches illustrate the 
breadth of technological advancement in CXL, 
this review will focus exclusively on emerg-
ing technologies supported by clinical data. 
This ensures that the discussion is grounded in 
robust, evidence-based practice and that efficacy 
and safety have been evaluated through estab-
lished regulatory pathways before any emerging 
technology is considered for routine clinical use.
Theranostic‑Guided Corneal UV Therapy
Theranostics, an approach that integrates real-
time molecular imaging diagnostic feedback 
with targeted therapy, was introduced in the 
management of keratoconus to address current 

 Ophthalmol Ther
limitations of the procedure, particularly the 
variability in clinical efficacy and uncertainties 
among available protocols, which differ 
in riboflavin delivery methods and UV-A 
irradiance, as described in previous sections. 
By directly measuring the corneal biological 
response to riboflavin absorption and UV-A 
exposure, theranostic-guided corneal UV 
therapy enables treatment personalization, 
delivering precise riboflavin dosing and UV-A 
light-mediated photoactivation tailored to each 
cornea, independent of the surgeon-selected 
protocol [108–111, 117, 118, 121, 122, 164].
A series of preclinical studies has been pivotal 
in developing and validating theranostic 
technology for keratoconus treatment [108–110, 
117, 118, 122]. These studies quantified 
stromal riboflavin concentration during CXL 
and demonstrated that riboflavin availability 
and UV-A-mediated photoactivation strongly 
correlate with biomechanical stiffening, with 
high predictive accuracy (94%) [79, 108, 109, 
117, 184]. Real-time theranostic imaging 
biomarkers (riboflavin score and theranostic 
score) enabled reliable prediction of treatment 
efficacy, confirming both pre-irradiation corneal 
riboflavin concentration and its photoactivation 
as key determinants [104, 108, 109, 122]. 
Importantly, photochemical kinetics varied with 
stromal concentration and UV-A power, even at 
constant energy dose. The findings validated 
theranostics as a robust, protocol-independent 
tool for personalized CXL and highlighted 
significant variability in riboflavin penetration, 
with most epi-on formulations failing to reach 
effective stromal levels (i.e., riboflavin score was 
lower than efficacy threshold of 0.4), except for 
enhanced 0.22% riboflavin formulation [117].
The ARGO trial (NCT05457647) was a 
multicenter, randomized study validating 
theranostic-guided corneal cross-linking and the 
predictive value of riboflavin score and theranostic 
score for  Kmax flattening [164]. Fifty patients with 
progressive keratoconus underwent epi-off or 
epi-on CXL using a UV-A device with integrated 
theranostic software, without affecting procedural 
adherence. All eyes received 0.22% riboflavin 
followed by UV-A irradiation at 10 mW/cm2 for 
9 min (energy dose: 5.4 J/cm 2). The theranostic 
system showed high predictive performance 
(91% accuracy, 95% precision) for 1-year  Kmax 
outcomes. Clinically, mean  Kmax flattening was 
1.5 ± 1.6 D with significant visual improvements 
and no severe or moderate adverse events. 
Notably, true positives treated with epi-on CXL 
achieved – 1.3 ± 0.9 D  Kmax reduction, exceeding 
values typically reported in the literature and in 
recent FDA-approved epi-on CXL protocols (– 0.4 
D  Kmax flattening) [4, 22, 23, 29, 39, 40, 105, 163, 
164, 176, 181, 198, 200, 209]. In addition, true 
positives treated with epi-off CXL achieved a 
– 2.1 ± 2.0 D  Kmax reduction, outperforming the 
average outcomes reported for conventional CXL 
[73, 147, 174, 175, 202].
Recently, the integration of controlled corneal 
iontophoresis with a theranostic UV-A device has 
enabled spatially patterned riboflavin delivery, 
concentrating it in the weakest corneal regions 
to achieve targeted photo-polymerization. In a 
preclinical study [122], this platform delivered 
0.22% riboflavin through the intact epithelium, 
generating a reproducible central-to-peripheral 
gradient (~ 40% higher centrally) by modulating 
current amplitude (between 0.5 mA and 2 mA), 
application time (between 4 min and 10 min), 
and aperture design. Real-time theranostic moni-
toring confirmed controlled stromal distribution, 
while ex vivo corneal topography showed consist-
ent corneal flattening.
Preliminary clinical results at 6 months in 
patients with advanced progressive keratoco -
nus supported these findings, demonstrating 
significant improvements in visual acuity (– 0.4 
LogMAR in UDVA and – 0.3 LogMAR in CDVA, 
respectively), a hyperopic shift of + 1.2 D, and 
mean  Kmax flattening of 1.9 D (Fig. 2) [111].
Collectively, these preclinical and clinical 
investigations established theranostic-guided 
corneal UV therapy as a predictive, data-driven 
strategy that provides real-time intraoperative 
metrics, enabling a shift from fixed, “one-size-
fits-all” CXL to individualized keratoconus treat-
ment [121, 164]. The combination of spatially 
patterned riboflavin delivery through controlled 
corneal iontophoresis with theranostic-guided 
UV-A light irradiation highlight the potential for 
precision keratoconus treatment tailored to the 
individual corneal characteristics, while under-
scoring the need for long-term studies to confirm 
sustained efficacy.

Chemical Cross‑Linkers and Synergistic 
Enhancement
IVMED-80 is a topical copper-based eye drop 
developed to increase lysyl oxidase activity. 
Lysyl oxidase is a critical enzyme that regulates 
the extracellular matrix and facilitates the con-
version of lysine into reactive aldehydes [103]. 
These reactive aldehydes play a role in creat-
ing cross-links between stromal proteins, as 
described in Sect. "Photochemistry of CXL". A 
phase 1/2a randomized, double-masked, pla-
cebo-controlled trial (NCT05241145) assessed 
the safety and efficacy of IVMED-80 in 31 
patients over a 6-month period. The results 
indicated a statistically significant reduction in 
 Kmax by 1.8 D in the 16-week treatment group 
compared to placebo (p  = 0.019). No treatment-
related adverse events were reported. Following 
these results, IVMED-80 received Orphan Drug 
Designation from the FDA and the therapy is 
currently advancing to phase 3 clinical trials, 
although no clinical study has yet been initiated 
or registered at the time of review.
FUTURE DIRECTIONS
CXL is firmly established as the standard 
treatment for progressive keratoconus and 
iatrogenic ectasia, with substantial evidence 
supporting its long-term safety and efficacy. 
However, interpretation of comparative safety 
and efficacy outcomes across different CXL 
protocols remains challenging because of the 
considerable heterogeneity of the available 
literature. Variations in riboflavin formulations, 
delivery methods, UV-A irradiation parameters, 
oxygen supplementation strategies, follow-up 
duration, and definitions of disease progression 
or treatment success may significantly influence 
reported outcomes. Consequently, caution is 
warranted when comparing efficacy results 
across studies, and further standardization of 
study designs and outcome measures is needed 
to facilitate meaningful comparisons among 
treatment approaches.
Despite its clinical success, CXL has histori-
cally been performed using largely empirical 
and protocol-based approaches, with consid-
erable variability in clinical outcomes among 
patients and treatment protocols. Reported pro-
gression or retreatment rates still vary widely 
across studies, reflecting the complex interplay 
between riboflavin formulation, stromal pen-
etration, UV-A irradiance, oxygen availability, 
corneal thickness, disease stage, and intrinsic 
biological variability of the corneal extracel-
lular matrix. This heterogeneity highlights the 
need for more standardized, reproducible, and 
progressively individualized treatment strate -
gies. In this context, a structured, evidence-
based framework is required to support clinical 
decision-making. A decisional flow diagram is

At the same time, theranostic technologies

a new paradigm in CXL by enabling 
intraoperative assessment of riboflavin stromal

to improve treatment reproducibility across 
surgeons and clinical centers by supporting 
adaptive modulation of procedural parameters 
according to individual corneal characteristics.

profiling, and molecular indicators may 
progressively support biomarker-driven 
approaches capable of refining patient selection, 
predicting treatment response, and optimizing 
timing and treatment settings.

METHOD OF LITERATURE SEARCH
An extensive literature search was conducted 
to identify relevant studies addressing CXL for 
keratoconus and related ectatic disorders. The 
search covered the period from January 2003, 
corresponding to the introduction of the Dres-
den protocol, through November 2025.
The primary source was the PubMed/MED-
LINE database. Search terms included com -
binations of the following keywords: corneal 
cross-linking, corneal collagen cross-linking, 
riboflavin and UV light, CXL, Dresden pro-
tocol, accelerated CXL, transepithelial CXL, 
iontophoresis-assisted CXL, riboflavin, ultravi-
olet-A, keratoconus, corneal ectasia, keratitis, 
bullous keratopathy, oxygen supplementation, 
pulsed irradiation, biomechanical stiffening, 
intracorneal ring segments, corneal allogenic 
intrastromal ring segments, theranostics, 
chemical corneal cross-linking, PRK, and PTK. 
Boolean operators (“AND”, “OR”) were used 
to refine the results and include variations in 
terminology.
Articles were included if they provided an 
abstract and sufficient methodological detail to 
support critical appraisal. Priority was given to 
randomized clinical trials, prospective or com-
parative studies, systematic reviews, and meta-
analyses, however, the most recent clinical data 
on emerging approaches were also considered. 
Relevant experimental and translational stud-
ies were also reviewed to provide a mechanis-
tic understanding of the CXL mechanism of 
actions and ultrastructural effect.
Reference lists of the retrieved articles and 
key review papers were screened manually to 
identify additional pertinent publications. 
The selection process was independently 
cross-checked by multiple authors to ensure 
consistency and completeness.
