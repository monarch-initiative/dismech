# Effect of Domperidone Therapy on Gastroparesis Symptoms: Results of a Dynamic Cohort Study by NIDDK Gastroparesis Consortium.
**Authors:** Sarosiek I, Van Natta M, Parkman HP, Abell T, Koch KL, Kuo B, Shulman RJ, Farrugia G, Grover M, Hamilton FA, Pasricha PJ, Yates KP, Miriel L, Wilson L, Yamada G, Tonascia J, McCallum RW, National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) Gastroparesis Clinical Research Consortium (GpCRC)
**Journal:** Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.cgh.2021.05.063](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cgh.2021.05.063)

## Content

BACKGROUND & AIMS: The use of domperidone (DOM) for gastroparesis (GP) remains controversial and limited. We aimed to present outcomes of DOM therapy for treatment of patients participating in the multicenter National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases Gastroparesis Clinical Research Consortium (GpCRC) Registries (GpR).

METHODS: The GpCRC cohort consisted of patients with GP (75%) and with GP-like symptoms but with normal gastric emptying (25%). The DOM group initiated therapy during the 96 weeks of enrollment in GpR1 and GpR2. Patients who had previously taken or who were on DOM therapy at enrollment were excluded from this analysis. The control group did not use domperidone (non-DOM group) before or after enrollment. The following outcome measures were identified: change from baseline in Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index total score, with 3 subscales, plus Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease and Patient Assessment of Upper Gastrointestinal Disorders-Quality of Life scores.

RESULTS: Overall, of 748 patients, 181 (24%) were in the DOM group, whereas 567 were in the non-DOM group. Sixty-three percent of participants had idiopathic GP. At baseline, DOM patients compared with non-DOM patients were significantly younger, had lower body mass index, non-Hispanic ethnicity, a higher annual household income, lower narcotic utilization, lower supplemental and complimentary medication use, and were more likely to have delayed gastric emptying time, as well as worse nausea and fullness scores. Compared with non-DOM patients, DOM patients experienced moderate but significantly more improvement in GP outcome measures: Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index total score (P = .003), nausea (P = .003), and fullness subscales (P =.005), upper abdominal pain score (P = .04), Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease score (P = .05), and Patient Assessment of Upper Gastrointestinal Disorders-Quality of Life score (P = .05).

CONCLUSIONS: Utilizing the method of pragmatic modeling to evaluate long-term treatment of GP in a large GpCRC database, DOM treatment resulted in moderately but significantly improved GP. NOTE: This project was based on data generated by 2 GpCRC Registry studies recognized under the Clinicaltrial.gov numbers: NCT00398801 and NCT01696747 symptoms compared with a group receiving standard-of-care but not DOM.

Gastroparesis (GP) is a diagnosis based on the presence of chronic upper gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms attributed to gastric neuromuscular dysfunction in the absence of any alternative causes, with confirmation of delayed emptying of a solid test meal.1,2 After initial clinical evaluation and attention to comorbid conditions, therapies utilizing dietary modifications, glucose control, and pharmacologic agents are often initiated.3 However, having only one United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved prokinetic drug (metoclopramide) creates challenges for health care providers involved in the care of patients with GP.4

Domperidone (DOM), a dopamine receptor antagonist, has antiemetic properties by blocking D2 receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone, while also having prokinetic effects in the stomach and proximal duodenum by blocking D2 receptors in the enteric nervous system.5 This agent can be beneficial to patients with GP who are refractory to other available drugs, or who have side effects.6 DOM is recognized therapy for GP, used more commonly outside the United States, as DOM is available in the United States only through an FDA Investigational New Drug (IND) application and institutional review board approval.7–9

The Gastroparesis Clinical Research Consortium (GpCRC) supported by the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), involves a number of academic GI Motility Centers conducting diagnostic and therapeutic studies in gastroparetic patients. An observational registry of patients with symptoms of GP follows patients prospectively over time. As the majority of investigators were/are in possession of DOM INDs and use this medication for treatment of GP, this pragmatic study was performed to assess the clinical status of patients treated with DOM.10–13 Our investigation was aimed to focus on the demographics, clinical profile, and symptom status of patients with GP receiving DOM compared with patients with GP not on DOM but receiving other approved medical therapies in order to assess the role of DOM in treatment of gastroparetic symptoms.14

Our study was designed to test the following hypotheses: (1) improvement in Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI), defined as decrease in overall GCSI score from baseline to 96 weeks, will be greater in patients with DOM treatment than patients without DOM therapy; and (2) health outcomes will be better for patients with DOM treatment than patients without treatment with DOM.

This study was designed to investigate the patients who were enrolled in 2 long-term observational NIDDK/National Institutes of Health (NIH) GpCRC prospective studies. More specifically, Gastroparesis Registry 1 (GpR1) (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00398801) and Gastroparesis Registry 2 (GpR2) (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01696747) patients were enrolled with documented diabetic or idiopathic GP, as well as patients with typical GP symptoms but normal gastric emptying. In the GpR1 study, patients were recruited at 6 academic sites, and their follow-up visits were conducted every 16 weeks. In the GpR2 study, patients were enrolled at 9 centers and were followed with clinical visits every 24 weeks.

GpCRC GpR1 and GpR2 patients who were taking DOM were under the care of the Consortium Investigators who obtained FDA IND status, and were legally acknowledged for prescribing this drug for their qualified patients. FDA Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, Division of Gastrointestinal Products provided potential sources for obtaining DOM.

Decisions to start DOM in qualified patients were made after all criteria of the FDA protocol of recommendations were met, based on a clinical presentations, electrocardiogram (ECG) recordings, and laboratory results, which were obtained, analyzed, and interpreted.

Prospectively collected data included demographic profiles, medical/surgical histories, physical exams and blood tests. Also assessments of gastroparetic symptoms and psychological function measures, as well as most recent documentation of upper GI endoscopies and 4 hour nuclear medicine gastric emptying scintigraphic were obtained.

GpR1 and GpR2 patients who were not taking DOM at enrollment and who made at least 1 follow-up visit were eligible for this study. We used all visit records during the 96-week treating period.

To assess the treatment effect of DOM, we compared 2 groups: patients who initiated DOM during 96 weeks of follow-up, and those who never initiated therapy with DOM (hereafter, referred to as DOM = “domperidone-exposed” and non-DOM = “domperidone-unexposed”).

The unit of observation for treatment effects is the patient-visit. This analysis divides each patient’s follow-up time into intervals defined by their follow-up visit and whether or not the patient had initiated DOM by the end of the interval. Once a patient initiates DOM, the patient is considered on-treatment for all subsequent visits. Intervals in which the patient is on DOM are compared with intervals with no reported DOM use. The on-treatment effects are assessed in patient-visits after initiation of DOM through week 96. The off-treatment effects are estimated in patient-visits prior to initiation of DOM and all patient-visits in patients who never took DOM up to and including week 96. Therefore, 1 patient could conceivably contribute to both the off-treatment and the on-treatment effects.

In more detail, we compared the change from baseline in symptom scores between patients on versus not on DOM at the current visit averaged across 96 weeks of follow-up adjusting for previous off-treatment visit symptom scores. For patients who never took DOM, the previous off-treatment visit is defined as the visit preceding the current visit. For patients who initiate DOM during follow-up, the previous off-treatment visit is defined as the visit preceding the current visit up until the patient initiates DOM during the current interval. Once DOM is initiated, the previous off-treatment visit is established as the most recent visit without DOM.

The Patient Assessment of Gastrointestinal Disorders–Symptom Severity Index (PAGI-SYM) is a self-administered symptom severity assessment instrument, designed and validated to measure common GI symptoms in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) or dyspepsia/GP.15 The Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI) total score increases with symptom severity and ranges from 0 to 5. It is presented as an average of 3 subscales (nausea/vomiting, post-prandial fullness/early satiety, and bloating), and each subscale is calculated as an average of 2–4 items in a 6-point Likert scale.16

The disease-specific quality of life (QOL) instrument, Patient Assessment of Upper Gastrointestinal Disorders Quality of Life (PAGI-QOL) survey, which scores 30 factors from 0 (none of the time) to 5 (all of the time), was also used.17 Patients were asked how often in the past 2 weeks their GI problems have been affecting different aspects of their QOL and well-being. There are 5 domains: Daily Activities, Clothing, Diet and Food Habits, Relationship, and Psychological Well-Being and Distress. Overall PAGI-QOL scores were calculated by taking means of all subscores after reversing item scores; thus, a mean PAGI-QOL score of 0 represents poor QOL, whereas a score of 5 reflects the best life quality.

Outcomes of interest were identified as improvements in symptoms of GP measured by the change in the GCSI total score and nausea/vomiting, postprandial fullness/early satiety, and bloating subscales, as well as GERD scale and constipation score16 and QOL as assessed by PAGI-QOL total score.17 Three categorizations of the outcome measures were utilized from these instruments: (1) change in scores from baseline; (2) improvement from baseline by 1 point or more; and (3) any improvement from baseline.

Institutional Review Board approval was obtained at Clinical Centers and the Data Coordinating Center. Patients provided written GpR1 and GpR2 informed consents.

All authors had access to the study data and reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Characteristics at enrollment were compared between patients who initiated DOM during 96 weeks of follow-up versus those who never initiated DOM using the t test for unequal variance for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. The following outcomes at each patient-visit were assessed: GCSI total score, nausea subscale, fullness subscale, bloating subscale, GERD scale, constipation score, and PAGI-QOL total score. Treatment effects for DOM were also assessed using propensity score modeling.18,19 Propensity scores for each outcome were calculated separately using logistic regression, modeling DOM treatment on 1 time-dependent variable – the continuous version of the outcome of interest at the previous visit (for those taking DOM, the outcome was imputed to the value of the visit prior to taking DOM for all visits at or after taking DOM) – and the following 21 time-independent variables at enrollment: GpR1 versus GpR2 study, gender, age, race, ethnicity, income, body mass index, gastroparesis etiology, severity, duration of symptoms, 2-hour solid retention, 4-hour solid retention, delayed gastroparesis status, narcotic pain medications, gastric electrical stimulator, alternate therapies, PAGI-QOL total score, Beck Depression Inventory, State Anxiety score, and Trait Anxiety score. Propensity scores outside common support were excluded, with the remainder stratified into quintiles and used for covariate adjustment in a random effects linear regression model for change in outcome and random effects logistic regression model for both improvements of 1+ point and any improvement. Crude random effects models and covariate adjusted random effects models were also calculated to compare the effect of propensity score adjustment. P-values were 2-sided and not corrected for multiple comparisons. Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC; SAS software, version 9.3 of the SAS system for Windows; 2002–2010. StataCorp, 2011) and Stata 15.1 (Stata statistical software: release 12; StataCorp LP, College Station, TX).

There were 591 patients enrolled into GpR1, of whom 19 had missing historic records or scintigraphy data at baseline, 71 took DOM prior to baseline, and 58 did not have adequate follow-up data, resulting in 443 evaluable patients in GpR1 not receiving DOM. There were 506 patients enrolled into GpR2, of whom 29 were previously enrolled in GpR1, 35 had missing historic documentation or scintigraphy data at baseline, 59 took DOM prior to baseline, and 78 did not have any follow-up data, resulting in 305 evaluable patients for assessing DOM in GpR2. Combining the 2 registry studies, there are 748 evaluable patients of whom 181 (24%) initiated DOM, and 567 (76%) never were exposed to DOM, during the 96 weeks of follow-up (Figure 1).

The baseline demographics and clinical profiles of the 181 DOM and the 567 non-DOM patients are summarized in Table 1.

Idiopathic was the predominant etiology of GP in both groups: 69% in the group receiving DOM, and 61% of those not taking DOM. As compared with non-DOM patients, DOM patients were younger with a mean age of 41 versus 44 years of age (P =.02) and had a lower mean body mass index of 26.5 versus 28.3 kg/m2 (P =.003). The demographics were similar in both groups except that Hispanic ethnicity was significantly lower for DOM patients (4%) versus non-DOM patients (12%) (P =.002), and annual household income of ≥$50K was significantly higher in DOM patients, 60% versus 49% in non-DOM patients group (P =.01) (possibly related to a high monthly out of pocket cost for DOM). DOM patients also were taking less narcotic medications (P =.03) and alternative therapies (P =.02). Eighty-two percent of DOM patients had delayed gastric emptying time compared with 72% of non-DOM patients (P =.006).

PAGI-QOL questionnaires did not reveal any differences between 2 groups of analyzed patients at their baselines (Table 1).

DOM patients had higher nausea/vomiting and postprandial fullness/early satiety subscales at baseline, than non-DOM patients (P =.004 and P =.02, respectively). Overall, only 41 of 748 (5%) participants were utilizing a gastric electrical stimulation (GES) therapy; 6% of non-DOM patients had received a GES system to help control their GP symptoms, compared with 3% of DOM patients (P =.09).

Kaplan-Meier estimates of cumulative probability of initiation of DOM at 96 weeks were 39% (95% confidence interval [CI], 34%–45%) for GpR1 and 11% (95% CI, 7%–15%) for GpR2. Sixty-five percent (100 of 154) and 63% (17 of 27) of patients initiated DOM prior to their first follow-up visit in GpR1 and GpR2 studies, respectively (Figure 2). The median time on DOM following initiation was 32 weeks (95% CI, 25–35 weeks).

Both the unadjusted and propensity score-adjusted regression models are shown in Table 2. The number of patients and number of visits with complete data for each outcome ranged from 728 to 737 and 2359 to 2400, respectively. Results of the propensity-adjusted analysis show that as compared with non-DOM patients, DOM patients experienced moderate but significantly better GP symptom improvement in many outcomes; specifically, change in GCSI total score (delta, −0.19; 95% CI, −0.32 to −0.06; P =.003); any decrease (ie, improvement) in GCSI score (odds ratio [OR], 1.56; 95% CI, 1.05–2.33; P =.03); change in nausea subscale (delta, −0.25; 95% CI, −0.43 to −0.09; P =.003); any decrease in nausea subscale (OR, 1.67; 95% CI, 1.10–2.54; P =.02); change in fullness subscale (delta, −0.22; 95% CI, −0.37 to −0.06; P =.005); any decrease in fullness subscale (OR, 1.68; 95% CI, 1.09–2.60; P =.02); change in upper abdominal pain score (delta, −0.21; 95% CI, −0.41 to −0.01; P =.04); change in GERD scale (delta, −0.15; 95% CI, −0.29 to 0.00; P =.05); and change in PAGI-QOL total score (delta, 0.12; 95% CI, 0.00–0.24; P =.05).

There were no significant interactions of the DOM treatment effects by etiology subgroup for any of the outcomes.

A comparison of the treatment effect in patients taking DOM versus the overall treatment effect is shown in Supplementary Table 2. Although the 2 estimates of the treatment effect varied in many outcomes, the direction of the treatment effect was the same.

Owing to the the disproportionate number of patients initiating DOM in the 2 studies, a comparison of the treatment effect in patients enrolled in GpR1 versus GpR2 is now presented in Supplementary Table 3. There were no differences in the estimate of the treatment effect in any outcome except for the fullness subscale (treatment effect, −0.11 in GpR1 vs. −0.64 in GpR2; interaction P =.01).

Because many patients initiated DOM between baseline and the first follow-up visit, a comparison of the treatment effect using change from the first follow-up visit as the “new baseline” versus using change from the “original” baseline visit is presented in Supplementary Table 4. This analysis excludes patients who initiated DOM at the first follow-up visit. The size and direction of the treatment effect is similar between the 2 methods.

In general, the treatment effect from the propensity score-adjusted analyses were attenuated both in absolute value and significance as compared with the unadjusted model suggesting confounding by indication.

Covariate adjusted models were performed as a sensitivity analysis to propensity score adjusted models. Results were similar to the unadjusted and propensity score adjusted data (Supplementary Table 1).

GpCRC investigators were responsible for following the patients in their regular clinics, including checking potassium and magnesium levels and obtaining ECGs to assess QTc intervals. The registry did not collect the actual potassium, magnesium, and ECG data obtained on patients. Based on the design of our registry studies and limitations of our obtained data, we were not able to determine any significant, DOM-specific ECG changes related to QTc intervals. Additionally, GpCRC investigators were responsible for interpretation of serious adverse events and their clinical implications for patients followed in the registries. Based on accumulated serious adverse event reports with safety data, participants had not experienced significant cardiovascular or other possible DOM-related complications during follow-up.

Our goal of this study was to prospectively capture observational information by utilizing data from the GP registries obtained from the NIH Gastroparesis Consortium Centers in order to help assess the efficacy of DOM used for clinical care of patients with symptoms of GP. From this present study, we have learned that patients receiving DOM were more symptomatic at baseline than non-DOM group of patients, indicating they were clinically “worse.” Yet, statistically significant reductions in nausea, vomiting scores in particular, as well as fullness and satiety and abdominal pain, were achieved with DOM treatment. This analysis was conducted as a comprehensive attempt to control for all variables by implementation of a multivariable patient-mix adjustment model using propensity scores. Results show that DOM was moderately superior to the therapies using best medical judgement being provided by the specialized centers for GP.

The registry database for gastroparesis, established by the NIDDK/NIH GP Clinical Research Consortium, provides opportunities to report on therapies that are not frequently used in nonacademic medical centers and clinics, in order to be able to generate meaningful numbers of participating patients. Gastric Electrical Stimulation is an example where the NIH database provided a statistically valid means for assessing what clinical impact stimulation (Enterra Therapy) may have in severe GP.20 Similarly here, the efficacy of DOM was able to be comprehensively analyzed. We believe that our conclusions deliver a meaningful clinical contribution regarding the therapeutic efficacy of DOM, in managing patients with GP who are refractory to the therapies approved and available in the United States.

DOM has been under surveillance in the European Union after scattered reports of cardiac adverse events, thus limiting its indications for only the prevention of nausea and vomiting. The duration of treatment has been suggested to be limited to 7 days, with the maximum daily dose of 30 mg.21 On the other hand, the usual dosing recommended in Europe and elsewhere in the world is 10 mg up to 4 times a day (before meals and at bedtime) 40 mg/day maximum total dose. Side effects requiring discontinuation of treatment have been reported to occur in 12% with the majority of patients experiencing an improvement in symptoms of GP, particularly postprandial fullness, nausea, and vomiting.22 Similar results were also recorded after 30 mg/day of DOM for 6 weeks, when again early satiety, postprandial fullness, nausea, and overall symptom severity significantly improved from baseline to the final week of treatment.23,24

DOM is widely used around the world for the treatment of gastroparesis, but is not approved by the FDA in the United States; hence, it still remains under the investigational drug definition. Its official utilization is only possible based on a special program administered by the FDA, which recognizes DOM as an investigational drug, which can only be obtained via the FDA’s Expanded Access to IND Programs. In order to achieve such IND designation, the IND application must be submitted by a particular physician, and then the FDA may grant an authorization for the doctor to prescribe this medication.

DOM should be considered for patients with GP who have not responded to or had limited exposure to Reglan and other prokinetics because of their side effects, and who also met the FDA IND DOM-specific protocol requirements.

GpCRC investigators utilized comparable doses to those in the literature based on their patient responses, sometimes requiring adjustments as suggested by the IND protocol, and guided by lab results and clinical status of DOM-exposed patients with GP. Based on the site-specific records and a preference of the treating physician, the initial dose of DOM was 10 mg 3 or 4 times a day. If needed, the dose could be increased to 20 mg 3 or 4 times a day, with the maximal dose of 80 mg/day.25 Based on consensus of all DOM-prescribing investigators, the average dose of this agent was 40 mg/day. ECGs, although performed at certain intervals at each of the individual centers, as required by FDA protocol, were not captured in our registry database, so we do not have specific information on development of dysrhythmias or prolongation of the QTc interval. Such prolongation has been reported in literature up to 10% of DOM users, but it is frequently asymptomatic regarding clinically important cardiac symptoms. Although GpCRC investigators did not specifically capture this information, there was no “drop out” rate from this analyses, and no DOM-related “cardiac deaths” were documented at any of the NIDDK/NIH GpCRC centers.

We recognize several limitations in our investigation, which were not originally incorporated and addressed in the GpCRC GpR1 and GpR2 protocols. Because of the nature of such registry studies and the format of obtained observations, it was not feasible for this particular investigation to obtain more definitive details, such as the specific dosage range of DOM, which was utilized in those patients with GP. Although patients were followed with checking postassium, magnesium, and ECGs, this data was not incorporated into our registry data collection system. Also, additional, gastric emptying results were not available for analysis, and their values were not captured during their clinical research visits, as follow-up GES were not required based on GpR1 study design.

In conclusion, this study documents in a clinical practice scenario, that DOM treatment can result in significant improvement in symptoms of GP. In order to clarify safety questions, such as potential cardiovascular side effects, and establishing the most efficacious dose of DOM, a large prospective trial should be conducted in patients with GP. Future investigations should also incorporate patient’s genetic polymorphisms in order to help identify patients who could subjectively benefit from this class of drug, while not experiencing adverse effects.
