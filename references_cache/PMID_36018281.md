# INTRAPULMONARY TREATMENT WITH A NOVEL TLR4 AGONIST CONFERS PROTECTION AGAINST KLEBSIELLA PNEUMONIA.
**Authors:** Hernandez A, Zhou J, Bohannon JK, McBride MA, Gibson-Corley KN, Patil NK, Owen AM, Burelbach KR, Sherwood ER
**Journal:** Shock (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1097/SHK.0000000000001977](https://doi.org/10.1097/SHK.0000000000001977)

## Content

Objectives: Nosocomial pneumonia is a common complication in critically ill patients. The goal of this study was to examine the efficacy of the Toll-like receptor 4 agonist 3-deacyl phosphorylated hexacyl disaccharide (3D PHAD), in a clinically relevant murine model of pneumonia, and assess the cellular mechanisms that mediate the protective response. Design: Mice received intrapulmonary 3D PHAD (20 μg) or vehicle for 2 consecutive days before challenge with intrapulmonary Klebsiella pneumoniae (2.3 × 10 3 colony-forming units). Mice were followed for 14-day survival, pulmonary K. pneumoniae burden, lung leukocyte profile, leukocyte phagocytic capacity, and cytokine production. Pneumonia severity and leukocyte recruitment were further assessed by histological evaluation. Setting: Research laboratory. Subjects: Wild-type, male C57BL/6 J mice. Interventions: Intrapulmonary treatment with 20 μg 3D PHAD for 2 consecutive days. Measurements and main results: Intrapulmonary treatment with 3D PHAD decreased lung K. pneumoniae colony-forming units and pneumonia severity with an associated improvement in survival compared with mice treated with vehicle. The numbers of neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages in the lungs of 3D PHAD-treated mice were higher than those in vehicle-treated mice before infection but were not significantly different from vehicle-treated mice at 48 h after K. pneumoniae challenge. Lung innate leukocytes from 3D PHAD-treated mice had increased phagocytic capacity. Treatment with 3D PHAD alone increased cytokines in the lungs but decreased cytokines in plasma during K. pneumoniae pneumonia as compared with control. Conclusions: Intrapulmonary treatment with 3D PHAD augments innate immunity in the lung and facilitates resistance to K. pneumoniae pneumonia.

Hospital-acquired infections contribute significantly to increased morbidity and mortality in hospitalized patients and generate a significant economic burden on health care systems in the United States (1). Critically ill, surgical, and elderly patients are vulnerable to nosocomial pneumonia (1-3). Furthermore, an increase in antibiotic resistance among hospital-acquired pathogens is a rising global threat (4,5). The pharmaceutical industry has historically responded to antibiotic resistance by developing new antibiotics effective against emerging resistant strains. However, global market pressure has compromised new antibiotic development, leaving an important need for novel therapeutic approaches to manage infections (6). Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) agonist–induced trained immunity provides a means of meeting that need.

Trained immunity, also known as innate immune memory, is conferred by priming innate leukocytes with a pathogen-derived ligand and results in long-term, broad-spectrum resistance to infections via augmentation of leukocyte recruitment and antimicrobial capacities (7). Our laboratory has shown that the TLR4 agonist monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA) induces sustained resistance against infection with clinically relevant fungal and bacterial pathogens (8). In a separate study, we showed that the novel TLR4 agonist 3-deacyl phosphorylated hexaacyl disaccharide (3D PHAD) is equipotent with MPLA for inducing resistance to infection (9). However, the ability of TLR4 agonists to enhance innate immune responses within the lung has not been studied. Lung leukocytes are continuously exposed to environmental antigens, which may preclude trained immunity. Therefore, studies are needed to determine whether direct administration of immune training agents into the lung will induce trained immunity and augment resistance to pulmonary infections.

Numerous pathogens contribute to nosocomial pneumonia in critically ill patients. Infections with Klebsiella pneumoniae are of particular concern because of generation of biofilms and the emergence of antibiotic resistant strains (10-13). The purpose of the present study was to examine the ability of intrapulmonary 3D PHAD to enhance innate immune function in the lung and protect against K. pneumoniae pneumonia. We demonstrated that intrapulmonary instillation of 3D PHAD was well tolerated, enhanced innate immunity, and conferred resistance to K. pneumoniae pneumonia. To our knowledge, these are the first studies to show enhanced immune responses in the lung by intrapulmonary TLR4 agonist administration.

Fifteen- to 17-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were acquired from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and acclimated for 7 days before experiment. Studies were approved by Vanderbilt University Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and complied with the National Institutes of Health guidelines on the care and use of laboratory animals.

3-Deacyl phosphorylated hexaacyl disaccharide (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL) was reconstituted with 0.2% trimethylamine in endotoxin-free water at 1 mg/mL, followed by sonication for 60 min in a 40°C water bath. 3-Deacyl phosphorylated hexaacyl disaccharide was diluted with lactated Ringer’s solution to a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL (20 μg/100 μL) before intrapulmonary administration.

Klebsiella pneumoniae (American Type Culture and Collection, Manassas, VA; ATCC 43816) was grown overnight in 30 mL of tryptic soy broth at 37°C with rotation at 200 revolutions/min. The culture was interrupted at 22 h, centrifuged at 2,500g for 15 min at 4°C and washed twice with sterile 0.9% normal saline. Using a standardized absorbance curve developed using multiple dilutions of this specific batch of K. pneumoniae, we calculated the stock colony-forming unit (CFU) and suspended bacteria to a concentration of 2.3 × 103 CFU/100 μL for infection studies.

Mice were anesthetized using 4% isoflurane for 3 min and suspended by front incisors onto a treatment platform, tongue withdrawn with forceps, and the treatment solution or inoculum was deposited above the vocal cords (14). Aspiration of vehicle, study drug, or bacterial inoculum was confirmed by direct visualization. Intrapulmonary vehicle or 3D PHAD (20 μg) was administered at 48 and 24 h before infection. The 20-μg dose of 3D PHAD is based on our previous studies showing effective protection against systemic infections (9). The dose of 20 μg of 3D PHAD was selected, as this dose was noted to be highly efficacious without evidence of adverse effects when administered via i.p. and i.v. routes in our previously published studies (9). For noninfection experiments, mice were killed 24 h after the second dose of vehicle or 3D PHAD.

Mice received intrapulmonary 2.3 × 103 CFU of K. pneumoniae in 100 μL of 0.9% normal saline (14,15). The dose of 2.3 × 103 was selected based on our target to maintain a subacute pneumonia model with survival beyond 48 h to allow for assessment of the innate immune response to infection (16). One cohort of mice was followed for 14-day survival. A second cohort was killed at 48 h after infection to collect blood, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid, and lung tissue.

Blood was collected in heparinized tubes by carotid artery laceration under anesthesia and centrifuged at 2,119g for 10 min at 4°C for plasma collection. Following euthanasia, a sternotomy was performed to expose the lungs and trachea. A 20-gauge blunt needle was inserted into the trachea and injected with 2 mL of 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for collection of BAL. In experiments where lung tissue was harvested and processed, lungs were excised completely.

After counting cells (TC20 Automated Cell Counter; BioRad, Hercules, CA), BAL was centrifuged at 300g for 6 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected for cytokine analysis, and the cells were resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 1 × 107/mL for flow cytometry analysis.

Lung tissue was minced with scissors in 3 mL of digestion medium consisting of grade II DNase 1 (0.03 mg/mL and collagenase D [0.7 mg/mL]) in Hanks balanced salt solution and subsequently incubated at 37°C with agitation (200 revolutions/min) for 90 min. Digested lung tissue was aspirated through an 18-gauge blunt needle with a 10-mL syringe, thrice, and passed through a 70-μm strainer (17). Red blood cell lysis buffer (Red Blood Cell Lysing Buffer Hybri-Max; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was added for 3 min, and remaining cells were washed and suspended in PBS to a concentration of 1 × 107/mL.

Resuspended BAL and lung leukocytes were incubated with anti–mouse CD16/32 (eBioscience, San Diego, CA) at 1 μL/mL for 5 min to block nonspecific Fc receptor–mediated antibody binding. One million cells were transferred to polystyrene tubes with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies or isotype controls, incubated for 20 min at room temperature, washed with 2 mL of cold PBS, centrifuged at 300g for 6 min, and resuspended in 100 μL cold PBS. Cell samples were immediately analyzed using an Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA), and data analysis was performed with the Accuri C6 software (Franklin Lakes, NJ).

Antibodies used included antilymphocyte antigen 6 complex locus G6D-R-phycoerythrin (Ly6G-PE), antilymphocyte antigen 6 complex locus C1 (Ly6C)-peridinin chlorophyll protein-cyanine 5.5 (PerCP-Cy5.5), anti–EGF-like module-containing mucin-like, hormone receptor-like 1 (F4/80)–fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), CD11c-FITC, CD64a/b-PE, Siglec-F-PerCP-Cy 5.5, CD3-FITC, NK1.1-PE, CD4-FITC, CD8-allophycocyanin, and appropriate isotype controls (eBioscience, San Diego, CA). Conjugated antibody concentrations for PE, PerCP-Cy 5.5, and allophycocyanin were 0.2 and 0.5 mg/mL for FITC. Neutrophils were identified as F4/80−Ly6G+, monocyte-derived macrophages as F4/80+Ly6C−, monocytes as F4/80+Ly6C+, alveolar macrophages as CD64+CD11c+Siglec-F+, interstitial macrophages as CD64+CD11c+CD11b+, CD3 T cells as CD3+, CD8 T cells as CD8+, natural killer cells as CD3−NK1+, and CD4 T cells as CD4+ (17).

Bronchoalveolar lavage and plasma concentrations of chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL-1) (keratinocyte chemoattractant), CXCL-2 (macrophage inflammatory protein 2), IL-6, TNF-α, interferon γ (IFN-γ), and IL-1β were measured using a Bio-Plex mouse bead array according to the manufacturer’s directions (BioRad) and analyzed using a MagPix MultiPlex Reader (BioRad).

Mice were treated with intrapulmonary vehicle or 3D PHAD followed by BAL and lung tissue cell collection as described previously. Upon centrifugation, cells were resuspended to a concentration of 2 × 106 cells/mL followed by addition of 100 μg of pHrodo-tagged Escherichia coli bioparticles (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 40 min, and then flow cytometry antibodies were added as described previously followed by incubation for 20 min at room temperature for 60 min. Cell types and pHrodo mean fluorescence intensity were measured using flow cytometry.

Lungs were harvested and digested as described previously. Lung digest from multiple mice was pooled and aliquoted among culture plates (150 × 15-mm sterile non–tissue culture–treated bacteriological Petri dish; Falcon, Glendale, AZ) in 20 mL RPMI 1640 with glutamine and 25 mm HEPES supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution, and 10 ng/mL mouse recombinant macrophage colony-stimulating factor (full media). Upon red blood cell lysis, cells were cultured in full media for 1 h to allow resident macrophages to adhere to the culture plate, and then media with nonadherent cells was removed, and fresh warm media was added to the adherent macrophage culture and immediately treated with vehicle or 3D PHAD 1 μg/mL (18). After 24 h, the cells were washed with PBS, and media was replaced. After 72 h, cells were washed in PBS and detached using a 10-min incubation with Accutase (Invitrogen/ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA). Cells were replated in a tissue culture–treated 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in 100 μL full media for 24 h. Then, cells were washed five times using an adjusted media (RPMI 1640 with glutamine and 25 mm HEPES with 0.1% fetal bovine serum and macrophage colony-stimulating factor without 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution) (19). Klebsiella pneumoniae inoculum was prepared by diluting bacterial stock to 1 × 107, 1 × 106, and 1 × 105 CFU/100 μL adjusted media to yield a multiplicity of infection of 100, 10, and 1, respectively. Media was removed from wells of macrophages and replaced with 100 μL of K. pneumoniae. The plate was centrifuged at 515g at 4°C for 4 min and then incubated for 1 h at 37°C. Media with extracellular bacteria was removed. Cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 1 h in adjusted media with 300 μg/mL gentamicin to kill extracellular bacteria. Cells were washed in PBS and macrophages lysed with 0.02% Triton-X 100 in PBS. Lysate was serially diluted and plated for enumeration of surviving intracellular bacteria.

Harvested lungs were injected with 1 mL of 10% neutral-buffered formalin and allowed to fix by immersion into 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 h. Fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin and cut at 5-μm thickness before being stained with hematoxylin-eosin. Eosin-stained slides were reviewed by a board-certified veterinary pathologist who was blinded to treatment groups. Inflammation was subcategorized into bronchopneumonia, consolidation, alveolitis/pneumonitis, and perivascular inflammation. Five mice per group were analyzed histologically, and the entire slide section was evaluated.

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.3.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data from multiple group experiments were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post hoc multiple-comparisons test and Kruskal-Wallis test for comparison of the median. Survival data were analyzed using the log-rank test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Mice were treated with intrapulmonary vehicle or 3D PHAD before inoculation with K. pneumoniae. A cohort of mice was killed at 6 or 48 h after inoculation for the collection of BAL to measure bacterial burden, and a second cohort was followed for 14 days to assess survival (Fig. 1A). Mice treated with 3D PHAD demonstrated significantly lower bacterial burden at 6 and 48 h after K. pneumoniae challenge (Fig. 1B) and demonstrated significantly higher survival compared with vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 1C).

When compared with vehicle-treated mice, histopathology examination showed that 3D PHAD–treated mice exhibited mild to moderate neutrophilic alveolitis (Supplemental Fig. 1A, http://links.lww.com/SHK/B496). Vehicle-treated mice infected with K. pneumoniae exhibited overt bronchopneumonia and pulmonary consolidation, whereas mice treated with 3D PHAD showed mild to moderate neutrophilic alveolitis with absence of consolidation (Supplemental Fig. 1B, http://links.lww.com/SHK/B496).

Mice were treated with intrapulmonary vehicle or 3D PHAD before inoculation with K. pneumoniae. Mice were killed 24 h after the second dose of intrapulmonary vehicle or 3D PHAD (no infection) or at 6 or 48 h after K. pneumoniae challenge for the collection of BAL (Fig. 2A). The numbers of neutrophils, monocytes, and alveolar macrophages in BAL were measured using flow cytometry. Intrapulmonary 3D PHAD significantly increased total BAL neutrophils, monocytes, and alveolar macrophages (Fig. 2B). Following challenge with K. pneumoniae, 3D PHAD–treated mice had an increase in BAL neutrophils at 6 h and monocytes at 48 h as compared with vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 2B).

In a cohort of mice treated with vehicle or 3D PHAD alone, the lungs were excised following BAL and homogenized to measure tissue leukocytes (Fig. 3A). Lungs from 3D PHAD treated mice demonstrated an increase in neutrophils, monocytes, interstitial macrophages, and CD8+ T cells, but not NK or CD4+ T cells as compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 3B).

Mice were treated with intrapulmonary 3D PHAD or vehicle as described previously and killed 24 h after the second treatment. Leukocytes from BAL and lung homogenate were incubated with pHrodo-labeled E. coli bioparticles for 60 min to allow for phagocytosis and assessed by flow cytometry. Bronchoalveolar lavage neutrophils from mice treated with 3D PHAD showed a decrease in phagocytic capacity of pHrodo-labeled E. coli bioparticles (Fig. 4). No significant change in phagocytosis was observed among BAL monocytes or alveolar macrophages. However, neutrophils, monocytes, and interstitial macrophages in lung homogenate from 3D PHAD–treated mice demonstrated enhanced phagocytosis compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 4).

To assess in vitro K. pneumoniae phagocytosis, lung tissue resident macrophages were isolated and treated with 3D PHAD for 24 h, washed, and allowed to rest for 3 days followed by coincubation with K. pneumoniae for 2 h. Compared with vehicle-treated macrophages, 3D PHAD–treated macrophages demonstrated enhanced phagocytosis as demonstrated by increased numbers of intracellular bacteria at multiplicities of infection of 10 (10 bacterial/macrophage) and 100 (100 bacteria/macrophage) (Supplemental Fig. 2, http://links.lww.com/SHK/B497).

Mice were treated as described previously with intrapulmonary vehicle or 3D PHAD before inoculation with K. pneumoniae. Bronchoalveolar lavage or plasma was collected at 24 h after the second dose of 3D PHAD or at 48 h after inoculation with K. pneumoniae. Mice treated with 3D PHAD alone demonstrated a significant increase in BAL IL-1β, IL-6, IFN-γ, CXCL-1, TNF-α, and CXCL-2 (Fig. 5 and Fig. 6). However, in 3D PHAD–treated mice at 48 h after K. pneumoniae infection, higher IFN-γ and lower CXCL1 concentrations were noted in BAL (Fig. 5). 3-Deacyl phosphorylated hexaacyl disaccharide treatment alone resulted in an increase in plasma levels of IL-6 and IFN-γ (Fig. 6). However, 3D PHAD–treated mice demonstrated lower plasma concentrations of IL-6, IFN-γ, CXCL-1, TNF-α, and CXCL-2 at 48 h after intrapulmonary K. pneumoniae challenge (Fig. 6).

The present study demonstrates prophylactic treatment with 3D PHAD confers resistance to K. pneumoniae pneumonia, a common pathogen causing nosocomial pneumonia with a high frequency of antibiotic resistance (10,11,13,20). Protection is associated with augmented recruitment of innate leukocytes to the lung, improved phagocytosis, and concomitant improvement in bacterial clearance with decreased severity of bronchopneumonia and improved survival. Although 3D PHAD treatment alone increased lung cytokine production, the concentrations of cytokines in the lung were not different between control and 3D PHAD–treated mice after K. pneumoniae infection. In addition, plasma cytokines were significantly lower in 3D PHAD–treated mice with pneumonia compared with controls indicating decreased systemic inflammation.

Nosocomial pneumonia is a frequent infection in critically ill patients, is primarily associated with prolonged intubation, and leads to increased morbidity and mortality (21,22). The emergence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens has further complicated this problem by making treatment of hospital-acquired pneumonia more difficult (21). Prolonged intubation predisposes patients to ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) by introducing a foreign body into the airway and changing the respiratory tract microbiome via frequent suctioning, chronic aspiration, and antibiotic exposure (23). Evidence indicates that alterations in the respiratory microbiome facilitate pulmonary dysbiosis, altered immune function, and an increased burden of nosocomial pathogens (3,24). Novel treatments that have been proposed to protect intubated patients from VAP include probiotics to restore the respiratory tract microbiome and bacteriophage-based therapies (25,26). Clinical trials using probiotics have been inconclusive, with some trials showing a lack of efficacy (25). Bacteriophage-based therapies are in their infancy, thereby highlighting the need for discovering novel therapeutics to augment lung immune function and protect against pulmonary infections. Results of our study indicate that intrapulmonary treatment with 3D PHAD augments innate antimicrobial functions in the lung and decreases the severity of a clinically relevant model of pneumonia. In association with this augmented antimicrobial response, we do not know whether 3D PHAD treatment impacts the pulmonary microbiome, which is an area of research that will further inform the impact of 3D PHAD on the pulmonary system.

Trained immunity is a phenomenon where innate leukocytes exposed to pathogen-associated molecular patterns become primed to elicit a heightened, broad-spectrum response to subsequent infection (7,27-29). For example, treatment with the TLR4 ligand MPLA derived from gram-negative bacteria confers resistance against gram-negative (Pseudomonas aeruginosa), gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus), and fungal (Candida albicans) pathogens, as well as polymicrobial sepsis by cecal ligation and puncture (8,30-34). In our published studies using 3D PHAD or MPLA, we showed that the protective phenotype persists for at least 10 days after treatment (8,35). Little is known about trained immunity in the lung, especially after intrapulmonary instillation of immune training agents. The present study shows that intrapulmonary instillation of 3D PHAD facilitates recruitment of innate leukocytes into the lung interstitium and alveolar spaces and enhances their phagocytic capacity, resulting in augmented clearance of K. pneumoniae. The enhanced bacterial clearance is associated with decreased plasma cytokines. The latter observation indicates decreased systemic inflammation during pneumonia in 3D PHAD–treated mice. Intrapulmonary instillation of 3D PHAD induced low-level cytokine production in the lungs. However, we did not observe evidence of adverse effects associated with intrapulmonary administration of 3D PHAD in mice. Collectively, these observations suggest that induction of trained immunity in the lung could be beneficial in critically ill patients who are predisposed to VAP.

Our study has limitations. We used healthy mice, which may not be relevant to the critically ill patient population, which often has an immunosuppressed phenotype. Thus, it is possible that this model of infection may yield different results in immunosuppressed mice. Nevertheless, this study provides proof of concept that the lung immune system can be trained to facilitate resistance to pneumonia.

In conclusion, intrapulmonary administration of 3D PHAD facilitated recruitment of innate leukocytes into the airways and lung parenchyma, improved bacterial clearance, decreased bronchopneumonia, and increased 14-day survival during K. pneumoniae pneumonia. Tissue-resident lung macrophages from 3D PHAD–treated mice demonstrated enhanced phagocytosis. Thus, intrapulmonary 3D PHAD treatment induces enhanced antimicrobial immunity against pneumonia caused by K. pneumoniae, independent of antibiotic therapy. 3-Deacyl phosphorylated hexaacyl disaccharide has potential for development as an immunotherapeutic agent for protection against pneumonia and warrants further investigation.
