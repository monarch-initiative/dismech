# Iron homeostasis governs erythroid phenotype in polycythemia vera.
**Authors:** Bennett, Cavan, Jackson, Victoria E, Pettikiriarachchi, Anne, Hayman, Thomas, Schaeper, Ute, Moir-Meyer, Gemma, Fielding, Katherine, Ataide, Ricardo, Clucas, Danielle, Baldi, Andrew, Garnham, Alexandra L, Li-Wai-Suen, Connie SN, Loughran, Stephen J, Baxter, E Joanna, Green, Anthony R, Alexander, Warren S, Bahlo, Melanie, Burbury, Kate, Ng, Ashley P, Pasricha, Sant-Rayn
**Journal:** American Society of Hematology (2023)
**DOI:** [10.17863/cam.95022](https://doi.org/10.17863/cam.95022)

## Content

Polycythemia vera (PV) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm driven by activating mutations in JAK2 that result in unrestrained erythrocyte production, increasing patients' hematocrit and hemoglobin concentrations, placing them at risk of life-threatening thrombotic events. Our genome-wide association study of 440 PV cases and 403 351 controls using UK Biobank data showed that single nucleotide polymorphisms in HFE known to cause hemochromatosis are highly associated with PV diagnosis, linking iron regulation to PV. Analysis of the FinnGen dataset independently confirmed overrepresentation of homozygous HFE variants in patients with PV. HFE influences the expression of hepcidin, the master regulator of systemic iron homeostasis. Through genetic dissection of mouse models of PV, we show that the PV erythroid phenotype is directly linked to hepcidin expression: endogenous hepcidin upregulation alleviates erythroid disease whereas hepcidin ablation worsens it. Furthermore, we demonstrate that in PV, hepcidin is not regulated by expanded erythropoiesis but is likely governed by inflammatory cytokines signaling via GP130-coupled receptors. These findings have important implications for understanding the pathophysiology of PV and offer new therapeutic strategies for this disease.

Introduction 1 
Polycythemia Vera (PV) is a Philadelphia chromosome-negative myeloproliferative neoplasm 2 
(MPN) driven by activating mutations in JAK21-4 that cause  unrestrained erythrocyte 3 
production, increasing patients’ hematocrit and hemoglobin concentration. Over 95% of cases 4 
harbour the JAK2 V617F mutation, with the remainder usually exhibiting a mutation in JAK2 5 
exon 125,6. Complications of elevated hematocrit include venous and arterial thrombosis, and 6 
systemic symptoms including headache, visual disturbances , and pruritis7. Therapy typically 7 
includes regular venesection to maintain hematocrit below 45% 6,8. This phase of the disease 8 
may continue for years before some patients develop fibrotic or leukemic transformation.  9 
 10 
Iron availability for erythropoiesis ( and other tissues) is  governed by hepcidin , the master 11 
regulator of systemic iron homeostasis. Hepcidin  is produced by the liver and  occludes and 12 
internalizes the sole cellular iron exporter , ferroportin9,10, preventing recycled iron in 13 
macrophages and dietary iron absorbed in the intestine from reaching the plasma and hence the 14 
bone marrow 11. Elevated hepcidin thus reduces iron availability, while suppressed hepcidin 15 
enhances it 12. Systemic iron homeostasis is maintained via transcriptional regulation of 16 
hepcidin, whereby iron loading upregulates transcription via the canonical BMP -SMAD 17 
signaling pathway13. Hepcidin transcription is  suppressed by iron deficiency , partly via  18 
Matriptase-2 (encoded by TMPRSS6) mediated downregulation of Hemojuvelin, a coreceptor 19 
for BMP signaling. Increased erythropoiesis also suppresses hepcidin via the erythroid -20 
secreted hormone erythroferrone (ERFE)14, which acts to inhibit BMP signaling15. Hepcidin is 21 
also upregulated by inflammation16 (via IL6-driven JAK-STAT signaling17). 22 
 23 
Systemic iron metabolism and PV may be closely intertwined.18 Most PV patients present with 24 
iron deficiency at diagnosis .19 Overt iron deficiency may mask the elevated haemoglobin 25 

 
 6 
associated with PV.20 Venesection induces iron deficiency to limit further erythropoiesis.  1 
Recent reports have indicated that treatment of PV patients with hepcidin analogues 21 or 2 
pharmaceutical upregulation of hepcidin in pre -clinical models of PV 22,23 may ameliorate 3 
disease phenotype. Given emerging interest in the manipulation of iron homeostasis in PV, 4 
comprehensive characterization of hepcidin regulation and its role in PV disease is imperative.  5 
 6 
Here, we establish and validate an association between disordered systemic iron homeostasis 7 
and risk of PV diagnosis through unbiased genome-wide association analyses and evaluate the 8 
iron phenotype in a large cohort of PV patients. We then show that hepcidin levels are critical 9 
to governing the severity of the erythroid phenotype using preclinical models of Jak2-V617F-10 
driven PV. Further, we demonstrate that in PV, hepcidin is not downregulated by 11 
erythroferrone. We also demonstrate an inflammatory phenotype in PV that may influence 12 
hepcidin through GP130 -coupled receptors. These findings provide novel insights into 13 
understanding the pathophysiology of PV and have important implications for new therapeutic 14 
interventions.  15

Results 1 
Genome-wide association analysis links PV diagnosis with HFE variants 2 
We undertook a GWAS of 440 PV cases and 403,351 controls in the UK Biobank ( fig. 1A). 3 
We tested 9,191,064 variants genome -wide (fig. 1B). Three genetic loci had SNPs with 4 
genome-wide significant (p<5E-8) associations. Figure 1C details the top SNP at each loc us. 5 
The most significant association was with rs62541556 (p=8.85E-21), a germline SNP in JAK2 6 
that tags the 46/1 haplotype implicated in MPNs and is associated with increased susceptibility 7 
to V617F-mutant clonal hematopoiesis30,31. The other genome -wide significant associations 8 
were with rs79220007 in HFE (p=1.42E-14) and rs3836364 in FBLN2 (p=4.75E-8).  9 
 10 
Interestingly, the top SNP in HFE, rs79220007, is in very high linkage disequilibrium (r2=1.0) 11 
with rs1800562, a SNP known to cause C282Y, the most common mutation causing HFE-12 
related Hemochromatosis, an autosomal recessive disorder producing iron overload 32,33. 13 
Among PV cases, there was an excess of individuals with the AA h omozygous (C282Y) 14 
genotype, compared to what would be expected under Hardy -Weinberg equilibrium (2.7 15 
expected vs 35 observed). Given the over-representation of AA genotypes in PV cases, and the 16 
established role of homozygous C282Y variants in disease, we re-examined the HFE locus for 17 
associations with PV under a recessive model. The association with rs1800562 was more 18 
statistically significant under the recessive model than the additive model (p=2.63E -26 vs 19 
p=2.91E-14; Supplemental Table 2, fig. 1 D), with t he homozygous AA genotype associated 20 
with 12.58 times increased odds of PV, compared to G/G or G/A genotypes. Commensurately, 21 
there was more than ten -fold increase in the number of individuals with a PV diagnosis 22 
for C282Y (AA) homozygous individuals (N = 35, 1.35%, Supplemental Table 3), compared 23 
to those with GA or GG genotypes (N=69, 0.12%, and N=336, 0.1%, respectively). 24

first deleted hepcidin in PV mice using a previously published inducible hepcidin knockout 24 
(iHamp-KO) mouse model 28. Since mutant Jak2-V617F expression in hepatocytes was not a 25 

 
 14 
concern in mice unable to express hepcidin, we crossbred LSL-Jak2-V617F; CreERT2T/+ mice 1 
with iHamp -KO mice, creating mice with simultaneously inducible systemic mutant Jak2-2

Inflammatory cytokines may upregulate hepcidin in PV  18

analysis on liver s from BM transplanted PV and control animals  revealed 1466 (598 22 
downregulated; 868 up regulated) differentially expressed genes (DEGs; fig. 7A and 23 
Supplemental Table 6) in PV mice compared to controls. The transcriptional profile of livers 24 
from PV mice was different from controls (fig. 7B). Gene Ontology (GO) pathway analysis of 25

mice compared to control ( fig. 7C, top 3 bars ). Consistent with this analysis,  gene set 2 
enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the M SigDB hallmark gene sets revealed upregulation of 3 
the inflammatory response pathway in the liver of PV mice (fig. 7C, fourth bar).  4

We thus explored the hypothesis that soluble factors (including cytokines) may upregulate 6 
hepcidin in PV patients using a h uman in vitro hepatocyte model. Liver-derived HepG2 cells 7

(fig. 7C, fifth to seventh bars). To determine whether IL6 alone is responsible for hepcidin 14 
upregulation in PV, BM-transplanted mice were treated with anti -IL6 or control (ant i-IgG) 15

revealed that in addition to IL6, IL11, OSM, and LIF increase HAMP mRNA levels; whereas 9 
IL27, CT-1, and CNTF have no effect and CLCF1 decreases hepcidin expression (fig. 7J). The 10 
increase in hepcidin expression by IL11, OSM and LIF (as well as IL6) was confirmed in a 11 
second hepatocyte cell line, Huh7 (fig. 7K). We then determined whether inhibition of GP130 12 
could normalize hepcidin expression in HepG2 cells treated with PV plasma. HepG2 cells 13 
grown in media supplemented with PV plasma and anti-GP130 antibodies no longer exhibited 14

Discussion 1 
Here, we provide population genetic and experimental evidence that diagnosis and clinical 2 
features of PV are influenced by systemic iron  homeostasis. This work establishes a central 3 
role for iron homeostasis in influencing the erythroid phenotype in PV and provides a rationale 4 
for the use of therapies that modify iron metabolism to treat this disease.    5 
 6 
Our GWAS implicates the HFE locus as a key region associated with PV diagnosis, formally 7 
linking systemic iron regulation and PV. The top SNP in HFE associated with PV is in very 8 
high linkage disequilibrium with the pathogenic HFE C282Y variant (which causes 9 
hemochromatosis). Interestingly, this disease-causing SNP is not associated with diagnosis of 10 
other MPNs (Essential Thrombocythemia and Primary Myelofibrosis, Supplemental Table 7), 11 
indicating iron metabolism is linked to PV exclusively. Provision of iron to  PV may worsen 12 
hematocrit46. Conversely, as we show, iron deficiency is common in patients with PV 19, and 13 
may conceal diagnosis47.  Previous small studies have examined the prevalence and effects of 14 
HFE C282Y variants in PV and have not observed associations48,49. However, here, utilization 15 
of large datasets enabled an unbiased approach to discover and validate the role of HFE in PV 16 
diagnosis. Cases of PV were defined via the ICD system and subject to contemporaneous 17 
criteria at the time of disease diagnosis.  18 
 19 
HFE is an upstream regulator of hepcidin , perhaps via stabilisation of BMP-type I receptors 20 
(ALK3) to facilitate BMP signaling .50,51 C282Y variants prevent HFE from reaching  the cell 21 
surface, limiting its function, reducing hepcidin expression, and causing excess iron 22 
absorption37,52. HFE variants may thus enhance iron availability for erythropoiesis, increasing 23 
the likelihood of presentation and diagnosis of PV.  24 

 
 19 
Given the established role of HFE in hepcidin regulation, we reasoned the ultimate mechanism 1 
for the genetic association with disease phenotype was via changes in hepcidin, and hence we 2 
dissected the role of hepcidin in PV using pre-clinical models.  Genetic deletion of hepcidin in 3 
our PV model increased erythroid parameters, indicating that in PV, unrestricted erythroid iron 4 
access results in unfettered increased hematocrit and hemoglobin production. Conversely, 5 
physiologic upregulation of hepcidin expression through RNA interference of Tmprss6 6 
induced reductions in serum iron, depriving the BM of iron and causing reduced hematocrit  7 
and hemoglobin concentration. Hepcidin is thus critical in determining the erythroid phenotype 8 
in PV.  9 
 10 
The dependence of the PV erythroid phenotype on systemic  iron homeostasis provides the 11 
mechanistic rationale for the suite of emerging treatments that target iron metabolism for PV.   12 
The mainstay of current treatment for PV is venesection to decrease hematocrit below 45%, at 13 
which serious thrombotic events become less likely8,53. Venesection lowers the hematocrit by 14 
removing red cells and inducing systemic iron deficiency, 18 thus ameliorating further 15 
erythrocyte production53. However, venesection can cause unwanted adverse effects including 16 
vasovagal reactions due to fluid shifts 54, as well as symptoms such as chronic fatigue due to 17 
systemic iron deficiency 55, and incurs direct and indirect health care costs associated with 18 
patient visits. Some patients are unable to  tolerate venesection due to the severity of adverse 19 
events or do not achieve satisfactory hematocrit responses and hence require second-line non-20 
targeted agents. New therapeutic options for PV are thus needed. Withholding iron from the 21 
BM by inhibiting iron export to the plasma offers an exciting therapeutic opportunity to replace 22 
therapeutic venesection to treat PV. 56 We demonstrated that upregulation of endogenous 23 
hepcidin levels through liver-specific TMPRSS6 siRNA deprives the serum of iron and reduces 24 
hematocrit and hemoglobin concentration in our PV  model. A similar approach using anti -25 

 
 20 
sense oligonucleotide therapies has likewise recently shown promising preclinical results22. A 1 
Phase 2 clinical trial showed that a hepcidin mimetic can obviate  the need for venesection in 2 
previously venesection-dependent PV patients21. A clinical candidate of the TMPRSS6 siRNA 3 
used here (SLN124) is entering Phase 1 and 2 clinical trials  (NCT05499013) in PV patients. 4 
Our data indicate that liver iron stores were not altered by TMPRSS6 siRNA treatment, but 5 
splenic iron stores increased, consistent with this therapy redistributing iron stores rather than 6 
inducing systemic iron depletion, a feature that could protect pati ents from symptomatic iron 7 
deficiency. TMPRSS6 siRNA treatment is unlikely to result in vasovagal responses. Since 8 
hepcidin mimetics and TMPRSS6 siRNA therapies can be administered by subcutaneous 9 
injection, these therapies could potentially be self-administered, reducing healthcare costs.   10 
 11 
In our clinical study, we found that patients exhibit low iron stores with concordant reductions 12 
in hepcidin that are not likely influenced by small increase s in ERFE. Iron depletion and low 13 
iron stores have been observed in some previous studies 18,57 although others have reported 14 
hepcidin levels similar to healthy controls.58,59 Although we detected elevated ERFE in our PV 15 
model and PV patients, Erfe deletion did not modify hepcidin expression or alter disease 16 
phenotype. These findings may reflect the smaller degree of ERFE elevation in PV compared 17 
with diseases of ineffective erythropoiesis such as thalassemia 40.  In keeping with this, we 18 
found that hepcidin:ferritin ratios are not reduced in PV patients, unlike in thalassemia where 19 
ratios are lower.60 Elevations of ERFE in our PV model were comparable to elevations detected 20 
in experimental ‘low’ level ERFE-overexpressing mice, which do not alter hepcidin expression 21 
and produce only modest elevations in serum and liver iron61. 22 
 23 
Numerous inflammatory cytokines (including IL6) are elevated in PV patients, some of which 24 
may portend inferior prognosis 42,44,62. We found preliminary evidence to suggest IL-6 family 25 

 
 21 
cytokines (beyond IL-6 itself), which signal via GP130-coupled receptors63, may be implicated 1 
in hepcidin regulation  in PV. Consistent with our data, previous studies have indicated that 2 
anti-IL-6 treatment of PV mice does not alter hematologic phenotype. 64 Further 3 
characterization of the role of the se cytokine(s) in hepcidin regulation in PV will be an 4 
important continuation of this work. 5 
 6 
Characterization of the functional role of hepcidin and erythroferrone in PV was undertaken in 7 
a novel knock-in inducible Jak2-V617F transplant model, with induction of the phenotype by 8 
tamoxifen delayed to ensure recovery of the mouse from the toxicity of the transplant 9 
procedure, which could itself induce inflammation and perturb hepcidin. 65 The hematologic 10 
and splenic phenotype of this model , and the degree of upregulation of erythroferrone,  was 11 
consistent with human PV disease although less severe than previous knock-in models of Jak2-12 
V617F PV which have exhibited a stronger phenotype, with higher hemoglobin and hematocrit 13 
levels and greater degrees of splenomegaly; 22,39,66,67 these models may exhibit more marked 14 
elevations in erythroferrone, and deletion of erythroferrone in these models may have induced 15 
a change in hepcidin levels and changes in systemic iron physiology. Evaluation of the role of 16 
erythroferrone in alternative models of PV remains an ongoing research need.  17 
 18 
In summary, our findings implicate systemic iron regulation as a key determinant of the clinical 19 
severity of PV and lay the foundation for strategies that modify iron regulation as potential 20 
therapeutics for this disease.   21
