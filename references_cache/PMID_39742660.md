The cohesin complex is critical for genome organization and regulation, relying on specialized co-factors to mediate its diverse functional activities. Here, by analyzing patterns of similar gene requirements across cell lines, we identify PRR12 as a mediator of cohesin and genome integrity. We show that PRR12 interacts with NIPBL/MAU2 and the cohesin complex and that the loss of PRR12 results in reduced cohesin localization and a substantial increase in DNA double-strand breaks in mouse NIH-3T3 cells. Additionally, PRR12 co-localizes with NIPBL to sites of DNA damage in a NIPBL and cohesin-dependent manner. We find that the requirement for PRR12 differs across cell lines, with human HeLa cells exhibiting reduced sensitivity to PRR12 loss compared to mouse NIH-3T3 cells, indicating context-specific roles. Together, our work identifies PRR12 as a regulator of cohesin and provides insight into how genome integrity is maintained across diverse cellular contexts.

The precise organization and regulation of the genome is critical for cellular function. In human cells, over 6 feet of linear DNA must be compressed into a nucleus of only ~10 μm in diameter. Despite the need to organize and compact the genome to only a fraction of its length, the genome must remain dynamic and accessible to allow for key regulatory processes including gene expression, DNA repair, DNA replication, and cell division. The ring-like cohesin complex performs multiple essential functions that contribute to overall genome organization and function1–3. The cohesin complex is comprised of two structural maintenance of chromosome proteins (SMC1, SMC3) and a Kleisin subunit (Rad21)4–6. Additionally, a STAG protein (STAG1/2/3) interacts with the complex and is required for the association of cohesin with the DNA7–9. In mitosis, cohesin acts to ensure that sister chromatids remain physically connected until anaphase onset, a function known as sister chromatid cohesion10,11. Cohesin also contributes to diverse aspects of genome regulation throughout the cell cycle, including DNA organization, gene expression, DNA replication, and post-replicative DNA repair12–20. The diverse functional activities of the cohesin complex depend on its association with multiple co-factor proteins that regulate the complexes localization, stability, and function in a context-specific manner (Figure 1A)2,21,22. For example, the NIPBL/MAU2 complex acts to stabilize cohesin on DNA and promote DNA loop extrusion22–28. Additional regulators further contribute to cohesin establishment, maintenance, stability, and removal (Figure 1A)2,21,29.

The complete loss-of-function of the cohesin complex results in cellular and organismal lethality30. However, mutations in the genes that regulate cohesin can result in developmental and neurological disorders in human patients, diseases collectively known as cohesinopathies30–32. Cornelia de Lange syndrome (CdLS), the most well-known cohesinopathy, results from mutations in NIPBL and is characterized by severe cognitive delays, craniofacial and limb dysmorphism, cardiac defects, and growth delays30,31,33–36. Similarly, Roberts syndrome, which exhibits comparable developmental and cognitive delay phenotypes as CdLS, results from mutations in the cohesin acetyltransferase ESCO237,38. Despite the critical importance for cohesin regulatory factors for human development and health, loss of many of these proteins in standard human cell culture systems often results in only mild phenotypic consequences39. One explanation for these discrepancies may be the situation-specific roles that the cohesin complex plays in genome regulation that are not fully captured using standard cell lines. These challenges have posed significant limitations to our understanding of how cohesin is regulated and the precise mechanisms by which its co-factors function. Additionally, the limited number of co-factors identified to date cannot account for all of the known functions of cohesin, suggesting additional regulators remain to be discovered. Differences in cellular origin, gene expression programs, or a cell’s mutational spectrum can create varying requirements and sensitivities for proteins across distinct cell lines. By exploiting these differences, it is possible to identify specific situations in which cohesin co-factor proteins become essential for cellular viability, and utilize these situations to analyze their function.

Here, we exploited the differential gene requirements of cohesin co-factor proteins in combination with gene co-essentiality analysis to identify genes that function in cohesin regulation. By comparing differential genetic dependencies across diverse cell lines with established cohesin regulators, we identify the functionally-uncharacterized protein PRR12 as a regulator of cohesin and genome integrity. We find that PRR12 interacts with NIPBL/MAU2 and the cohesin complex, and that the loss of PRR12 results in reduced chromatin-bound cohesin and a substantial increase in DNA double-strand breaks. Importantly, we show that the requirement for PRR12 differs significantly across cell lines, with HeLa cells showing reduced sensitivity to PRR12 loss in comparison to NIH-3T3 cells, which display dramatic defects in genome integrity in the absence of PRR12. Together, these data highlight the power of utilizing a comparative genomics approach for the identification and investigation of cohesin regulatory mechanisms.

Recent large-scale analyses using systematic CRISPR/Cas9-based functional genetics profiling has analyzed gene requirements across more than 1000 different human cell lines39. In addition to revealing the functional requirements for a specific gene target in individual cell lines, this analysis reveals patterns of gene sensitivities, where the degree to which a gene is essential for proliferation or survival varies in a cell line-dependent manner. These patterns of genetic requirements can be used predict gene function, as genes that function in shared molecular pathways are predicated to display similar requirements40,41. Here, we exploited these co-essentiality predictions to identify previously uncharacterized genes that participate in core cellular processes. We focused our analysis on the cohesin pathway, which participates in genome organization, gene expression, cell division, and genomic integrity. Using CRISPR scores from Project Achilles (DepMap) we analyzed correlations in gene requirements across human cell lines39. In this manner, we used established cohesin factors as “baits” to identify previously unknown co-functional cohesin regulators. For example, NIPBL is an established cohesin regulator that participates in DNA loop extrusion and DNA entrapment24,26–28,42. Using NIPBL as a bait, we observed multiple established cohesin regulators as displaying strongly correlated gene requirements across cell lines including the NIPBL-binding partner, MAU2, which displays a correlation of 0.70 (Figures 1Aand1B). In addition to established players in cohesin biology, we identified the previously uncharacterized gene, PRR12, as the second strongest hit, with a correlation score of 0.44. In a reciprocal analysis, co-essentiality correlations for PRR12 revealed strong relationships with multiple known cohesin regulators (Figures 1Aand1C), providing support for a role for PRR12 in cohesin-related pathways.

PRR12 is a large 2036 residue protein that is comprised of proline and glycine-rich regions, as well as two predicted AT hook motifs and an annotated domain of unknown function (DUF) (Figure 1D). The primary amino acid sequence for PRR12 is highly conserved across vertebrate organisms, including 93.6% amino acid identity between the mouse and human PRR12 proteins. Although the cellular functions of PRR12 have not be analyzed previously, mutations in PRR12 lead to severe developmental and neurological disorders in humans43–46(Figure 1D). Notably, the resulting phenotypes observed in human patients with PRR12 mutations are similar to those reported when known cohesin regulators are perturbed, for example in Cornelia de Lange Syndrome which results from mutations in NIPBL31,43,46–48. Thus, based on co-varying functional requirements with established players in cohesin biology and these reported patient mutations, we chose to investigate the cellular function of PRR12.

To investigate the relationship between PRR12 and cohesin, we first sought to analyze the localization of PRR12 in cells. The nuclear-localized cohesin complex displays specific spatial and temporal dynamics that are critical to its function, with lower levels in G1 when cohesin is most dynamic, increased levels in S phase and G2 as cohesin becomes entrapped onto DNA to establish cohesive cohesin, and then reduced levels in mitosis due to the removal of the majority of cohesin through the WAPL-based prophase pathway49–53. Similar to the observed localization of cohesin54, ectopically-expressed GFP-PRR12 localized to the nucleus in interphase cells, was undetectable on DNA in mitosis, and rapidly re-localized to the nucleus as daughter cells enter G1 (Figure 2A).

To further assess the cell cycle dynamics of PRR12 localization, we utilized an antibody against PRR12 and a cell synchronization approach to quantify the nuclear localization of PRR12 in G1, S, and G2. Similar to cohesin, we found that the levels of nuclear-localized PRR12 changed across the cell cycle in Hela cells, with a basal level in G1 followed by a significant nuclear accumulation of PRR12 in S and G2 (Figures 2Band2C). Based on immunofluorescence with the anti-PRR12 antibody, we also observed a similar localization behavior and cell cycle dynamics in NIH-3T3 cells (Figures 2Dand2E).

The localization of PRR12 to chromatin is consistent with a role for PRR12 in genome function. However, as cohesin and PRR12 are both nuclear-localized in interphase similar to many DNA-associated proteins, it is challenging to detect precise co-localization. We therefore analyzed the localization of cohesin and PRR12 in late telophase/G1 cells, a timepoint during which both cohesin and PRR12 can be detected re-localizing to the DNA in discrete populations (Figure 2F). By analyzing the localization of GFP-PRR12 and the core cohesin components, SMC3 and STAG2, in late telophase/G1 NIH-3T3 cells, we observed close co-localization of all three proteins (Figure 2F).

Multiple populations of cohesin exist within the nucleus, with the presence of both chromatin-bound and soluble pools23,54,55. To assess the chromatin association of PRR12, we performed biochemical fractionation experiments in which the chromatin bound fraction was separated from soluble nuclear proteins42,56. Using this approach, we identified an enrichment of PRR12 (as detected using anti-PRR12 antibodies) in the chromatin fraction relative to whole cell extracts suggesting PRR12 is bound to the DNA or tightly associated with the nuclear matrix (Figure 2G). We identified multiple potential PRR12 isoforms using this approach, with bands for the predicted canonical form of PRR12 observed at approximately 211 kDa as well as ~175 kDa and ~166 kDa species (also see44,46), each of which were eliminated in PRR12 knockout cells (Figure 2G). An association of PRR12 with chromatin is consistent with the predicted AT hook domains in PRR12 (Figure 1D) as well as the close co-localization with the cohesin complex (Figure 2F). Finally, to determine if the localization of PRR12 is dependent on NIPBL or cohesin, we used a CRISPR/Cas9-based conditional gene knockout approach (Figure S1A). Knockout efficiencies were confirmed using TIDE analysis (Figure S1B). In the absence of NIPBL and SMC3, the chromatin association of PRR12 was reduced but not eliminated (Figure 2G). Together, this co-localization analysis is consistent with a close relationship between PRR12 and the cohesin complex.

The co-localization of PRR12 with cohesin suggests that these proteins may interact. To test this, we conducted affinity purifications coupled with mass spectrometry to identify the proteins that interact with PRR12 in cells. To this end, we isolated PRR12 via GFP immunoprecipitation from both Hela and NIH-3T3 cell lines stably expressing human GFP-PRR12 and compared these relative to control GFP immunoprecipitations performed in HeLa cells. In both HeLa and NIH-3T3 cells, GFP-PRR12 immunoprecipitated with all of the established subunits of the cohesin complex in addition to multiple cohesin-associated proteins, including NIPBL and MAU2 (Figure 3AandSupplementary Tables 1A–D). Notably, multiple proteins in the DNA damage response pathways were also identified in both samples. Consistent with this experiment, prior work also isolated PRR12 in large-scale cohesin immunoprecipitation experiments57.

To identify which domains of PRR12 are involved in the interaction with the cohesin complex, we conducted structural predictions using Alphafold58. The N-terminal 1564 residues of PRR12 are predicted to be largely unstructured, with the potential presence of polyproline helices due to the large number of proline residues. The C-terminus of PRR12 is predicted to have two highly-conserved, structured domains that we will refer to as binding domain 1 (residues 1564-1677) and binding domain 2 (residues 1840-2036) (Figures 3B,S2A,S2B, andS2C). Binding domain 1 is predicted to be composed of 8 contorted anti-parallel beta-sheets and two helices whereas binding domain 2 is predicted to contain a bundle of 7 helices flanked by 3 anti-parallel beta-sheets (Figure S2A). Given the strong co-essentiality of PRR12 with NIPBL and the identified interaction between NIPBL-PRR12 in our affinity purifications, we used Alphafold-Multimer to predict the structure of a potential PRR12-NIPBL complex (Figure 3C)59. We found that two highly conserved regions in PRR12, one in binding domain 1 (residues 1629-1637) and the other in binding domain 2 (residues 1840-1850 and 1873-1888), are predicted to be located at the interface of PRR12 and NIPBL (Figure 3C).

To test if the C-terminal region of PRR12 is sufficient for the direct interaction between PRR12 and NIPBL or the cohesin complex, we stably expressed a GFP-tagged construct of the C-terminus of human PRR12 in both HeLa and NIH-3T3 cells and performed immunoprecipitation experiments as described above. These immunoprecipitations revealed that C-terminal region of PRR12 (residues 1258-2036) was sufficient to mediate its associations with NIPBL, cohesin, and the other associated co-factors (Figure 3AandSupplementary Tables 1E–F), suggesting that this constitutes its core interacting domain. To quantify the specific changes in protein abundance in proteins associated with the C-terminus of human PRR12 compared to control GFP constructs, we performed quantitative Tandem Mass Tag (TMT)-based mass spectrometry (Figure 3DandSupplementary Table 2). Using this approach, we identified NIPBL and MAU2 as among most abundant interactors with the C-terminal domain of PRR12 (residues 1258-2036). Overall, these results demonstrate that PRR12 interacts with the cohesin complex in cells and suggests this interaction is facilitated by the NIPBL/MAU2 complex.

Analysis of varying gene requirements across different human cell lines indicated that PRR12 displays a similar pattern of dependencies as multiple cohesin regulatory proteins (Figure 1Band1C)39. In these analyses, the functional requirement or “essentiality” for a given gene is calculated based on the relative change in proliferation under competitive growth conditions. CRISPR gene scores less than −0.5 represent fitness-conferring genes in most cell lines, whereas scores less than −1 are consistent with significant lethality. We found that the requirement for PRR12 varied across human cell lines. However, in each case, the corresponding effect on cellular fitness following the loss of PRR12, as reflected by the CRISPR score, suggests that PRR12 is largely dispensable for viability in human cell lines (Figure 4A). This is in contrast to data from human patients, in which mutations within PRR12 results in significant developmental abnormalities (Figure 1D)43–46. These data highlight the importance of PRR12 for human health but indicate the function of the protein may only be required in specific cellular situations, for examples in specific cell types or developmental stages. We therefore sought to identify a cellular situation in which PRR12 was significantly required to better explore its function. In contrast to the tested human cell lines, large-scale CRISPR/Cas9-based functional screens in mouse cells indicated a sensitized requirement for PRR12 (Figure 4B)60. We observed a similar pattern of behavior for multiple cohesin co-factors, with more modest fitness requirements in human cells, but substantially increased requirements in mouse cells (Figures 4Aand4B).

To test these behaviors and the differing requirements for PRR12 and cohesin regulatory proteins between cell lines directly, we used a cellular competition assay to test the relative growth of control cells and knockout cells that are co-cultured for a direct comparison of their fitness behavior (Figure 4C). For this approach, we choose to analyze human HeLa cells and mouse NIH-3T3 cells. In HeLa cells, SMC3 knockout cells were rapidly lost from the population, with 63% of knockout cells lost from the population by day 4 of gene knockout and 99% of knockout cells lost by day 7 (Figure 4D). These data are consistent with the documented essential role for SMC3 in cellular viability. In contrast, PRR12 and MAU2 knockout cells displayed a more modest fitness defect. We observed a reduced fraction of PRR12 or MAU2 knockouts relative to controls, with 48% and 61% fewer cells, respectively, observed at the end of the 7-day time course. In contrast, using a similar cellular competition assay, we found that NIH-3T3 cells were highly sensitive to the loss of the cohesin subunit RAD21, NIBPL, and PRR12 with similar growth dynamics throughout the time course. By day 7, 79%, 74% and 73% of PRR12, NIPBL, and RAD21 knockout cells had been lost, respectively (Figure 4E).

The reduced sensitivity to PRR12 loss in human cell lines has made understanding the function of PRR12 challenging. Conversely, the strong requirement for PRR12 in mouse cells provides a situation under which PRR12 is required for cell growth and viability. As the PRR12 protein is highly conserved between mouse and humans (Figure 3B) and displays similar localization behavior and protein interactions between NIH-3T3 and HeLa cell lines (Figures 2and3), this provides a powerful strategy to assess PRR12 function and its potential role in cohesin biology. To define the functional contributions of PRR12 and provide insights into why this protein is differentially required across cellular contexts, we therefore choose to conduct comparative functional studies in both NIH-3T3 cells and HeLa cells.

Using our comparative gene knockout approach, we next sought to define the consequences of PRR12 loss and how this may differ across different cell lines. Analysis of PRR12 knockout NIH-3T3 cells revealed severe defects in DNA organization and genome integrity including the presence of micronuclei (32.5%) and bi-nucleated cells (9.5%) (Figures S3AandS3B). Consistent with the predicted co-dependency between PRR12 and cohesin co-factors, knockout of NIPBL (22.2% micronuclei, 5.8% binucleation) and RAD21 (31.3% micronuclei, 10.9% binucleation) also resulted in similar DNA morphology defects (Figures S3AandS3B). Knockout of PRR12 in HeLa cells also resulted in DNA abnormalities, but these phenotypes were significantly reduced in their penetrance and severity relative to PRR12 knockout NIH-3T3 cells (Figures S3AandS3C). Notably, knockout of MAU2 resulted in a similar percentage of micronuclei and binucleated cells as knockout of PRR12 in HeLa cells, whereas SMC3 knockouts resulted in a much more severe phenotype (Figures S3AandFigure S3C).

The loss of genome integrity is a core phenotype observed when cohesin is perturbed61. To determine if the loss of PRR12 impacts cohesin function, we analyzed cohesin localization using immunofluorescence. Using an antibody against SMC3, we found that the nuclear localization of cohesin was significantly decreased in PRR12 knockout NIH-3T3 cells (Figures 5Aand5B), indicating PRR12 is required for the nuclear localization of cohesin. A similar reduction in nuclear-localized SMC3 was observed in NIPBL, MAU2, and RAD21 knockout NIH-3T3 cells (Figures 5A,5B,S3D, andS3E). In contrast, PRR12 knockout in HeLa cells resulted in only a very modest decrease of nuclear localized SMC3 when compared to control knockout cells (Figures 5Cand5D). Importantly, the observed differences in cohesin localization in PRR12 knockout NIH-3T3 and HeLa cells is not due to CRISPR/CAS9 guide efficiency differences as similar results were detected across multiple guides targeting PRR12 in NIH-3T3 (Figures S3FandS3G) and HeLa (Figures S3HandS3I) cell lines. To determine if the loss of PRR12 affects the chromatin-bound population of cohesin, we next conducted chromatin fractionation experiments. In the absence of PRR12 in NIH-3T3 cells, the cohesin subunits SMC1a and STAG2 were modestly decreased in chromatin bound fractions (reduced to ~70% of control levels), suggesting PRR12 contributes to the chromatin localization of cohesin (Figure S4A). Consistent with prior work, we observed a modest decrease in chromatin-bound SMC1a in NIPBL knockout NIH-3T3 cells, with levels similar to those observed in our PRR12 knockout sample (Figure S4A)42,62. However, we note that the population-based nature of this assay and the fact that Cas9-based DNA damage will not create nonsense mutations in every cell, this may underrepresent the extent of this effect. Similar to our immunofluorescence assays, knockout of PRR12 in HeLa cells did not result in a measurable decrease in chromatin bound SMC1A or STAG2 (Figure S4B).

The cohesin complex performs multiple roles in regulating genome integrity throughout the cell cycle, including mediating the attachment of sister chromatids in mitosis63. To test whether loss of PRR12 is required for sister chromatid cohesion, we performed chromosome spreads. Despite a measurable decrease in chromatin-bound cohesin (Figures 5A,5B, andS4A) loss of PRR12 did not cause pronounced sister chromatid defects in NIH-3T3 (Figure S4C) or HeLa cells (Figure S4D). Importantly, these results are consistent with our work and published reports in which depletion of NIPBL/MAU2 results in a significant decrease in chromatin-bound cohesin (Figures 5Aand5B) but does not result in pronounced defects in sister chromatid cohesion62,6465.

Our co-dependency predictions and protein interaction experiments indicated that PRR12 interacts with the NIPBL/MAU2 complex (Figures 1Band1C)39,58,59. We therefore sought to test whether loss of PRR12 altered NIPBL localization. In our immunofluorescence-based assay, knockout of PRR12 in NIH-3T3 cells resulted in a modest decrease of NIPBL localization, suggesting NIPBL is sensitive to but not dependent on PRR12 for its localization (Figures 5Eand5F). Using our chromatin fractionation approach, the amount of chromatin-bound NIPBL and MAU2 did not display clear changes in PRR12 knockout NIH-3T3 cells (Figure S4E). However, due to the nature heterogenous nature of the DNA repair events generated using the inducible Cas9 knockouts, small changes may be difficult to detect in a pooled context. Similar to prior work, we find that NIPBL is destabilized in the absence of MAU2, indicating these proteins function together as a complex (Figures S3JandS3K)62,66,67. A similar destabilization was observed for MAU2 in the absence of NIPBL in NIH-3T3 cells (Figure S4E). Additionally, as previously reported33, we find that NIPBL is capable of localizing to DNA independently of the cohesin complex (Figures 5Eand5F). In contrast, in HeLa cells, loss of PRR12 resulted in a significant increase in nuclear-localized NIPBL (Figures 5Gand5H), indicating the relationship between these proteins may differ between cell lines. This increase was not observed for MAU2 using our chromatin fractionation approach (Figure S4F).

Our data indicates PRR12 contributes to the localization of cohesin (Figures 5A,5B, andS4A). To test whether this effect is specific to cohesin or could reflect a more global change in genome organization, we next sought to test the localization of other established genome organizers. We first quantified the localization of CTCF, a protein important for directing the localization of cohesin, but which does not rely on cohesin for its nuclear localization14,49,68. In contrast to the robust loss of cohesin in PRR12 knockout NIH-3T3 cells, CTCF levels were not significantly changed in PRR12 knockout cells compared to controls (Figures S4GandS4H). Similarly, we assessed the localization of SMC4, a component of the condensin complex that mediates DNA compaction, and observed only very modest differences between control, PRR12, and RAD21 knockout NIH-3T3 cells (Figure S4IandS4J). Together, these data implicate PRR12 as a regulator of cohesin.

Our co-essentiality predictions identified a strong relationship between the requirement for NIPBL/MAU2 and PRR12 suggesting these proteins may function in the same pathway (Figures 1Band1C). In support of a direct interaction between these proteins, our co-immunoprecipitations using GFP-PRR12 identified an interaction between the C-terminus of PRR12 with NIPBL/MAU2 (Figures 3Aand3D). To further test whether PRR12 functions together with NIPBL/MAU2, we sought to co-deplete PRR12 and NIPBL/MAU2 and assess whether this resulted in additive defects to cell viability or cohesin localization. In HeLa cells, simultaneous knockout of PRR12 and MAU2 resulted in a modest increase in cell death compared to knockout of either MAU2 or PRR12 alone (Figure S5A). A similar trend was observed in NIH-3T3 cells, where knockout of PRR12 and NIPBL resulted in a modest additive increase in cell death compared to single knockouts (Figure S5B). We next sought to examine cohesin localization in the absence of PRR12 and NIPBL/MAU2. For this analysis we choose to focus on HeLa cells, where knockout of either protein alone only results in modest cohesin defects, allowing for more sensitized detection of cohesin localization changes. Similar to previous results, knockout of PRR12 or MAU2 resulted in modest decreases in nuclear-localized SMC3 (Figures S5CandS5D). Importantly, simultaneous knockout of PRR12 and MAU2 did not result in an additive change to SMC3 levels (Figures S5CandS5D), suggesting these proteins function in the same pathway to regulate cohesin localization in cells.

Together, our data suggest that PRR12 may function together with NIPBL/MAU2 in regulating cohesin localization. The NIPBL/MAU2 complex promotes cohesin stability, in part, by counteracting WAPL, a cohesin destabilizer that acts to remove chromatin-bound cohesin51. In support of this function, prior work found that the co-depletion of WAPL and NIPBL restored the net levels of cohesin complexes on DNA66,69. To assess whether PRR12 also contributes to cohesin stability through a similar mechanism, we tested the consequences of targeting WAPL. In contrast to PRR12 knockout NIH-3T3 cells, in which nuclear localized SMC3 was significantly reduced, we found that simultaneous knockout of WAPL and PRR12 significantly increased the amount nuclear SMC3 to levels similar as control cells (Figures 5Iand5J). These data suggest PRR12 may function to help stabilize DNA-bound cohesin complexes, counteracting WAPL-mediated cohesin removal similar to NIPBL/MAU2. These data provide evidence that PRR12 likely contributes to regulating of the same cohesin pools as the NIPBL/MAU2 complex.

In our functional analysis, we found that PRR12 NIH-3T3 knockout cells appeared to have an increased nuclear size, consistent with altered genome behavior or a cell cycle arrest. To test cell cycle progression directly, we used FACS analysis of DNA content to define the proportion of cells in G0/G1, S phase, and G2/M. In contrast to control cells (G0/G1=43.5%, S =37.6%, G2/M =18.9%), we observed significantly more PRR12 knockout cells in G2/M (G0/G1 =48.4%, S=12.3%, G2/M=39.3%) with few S phase cells (Figure 6A). SMC3 knockout cells also exhibited an increase in the proportion of G2/M cells (G0/G1=65.7%, S=4.2%, G2/M=30.1%) compared to controls, whereas NIPBL knockout cells contained a higher proportion of cells in G1 phase (G0/G1=63.5%, S=21.8%, G2=14.7%). In contrast, we did not observe a cell cycle arrest in PRR12 knockout or MAU2 knockout HeLa cells (Figure S6A), consistent with the differences observed in competitive growth assays for these genes (Figures 4Dand4E). It is important to note that HeLa cells lack p53 function, which could account for the differences in cell cycle behaviors observed across the cell types when PRR12, NIPBL/MAU2, or cohesin are lost. However, p53 positive human cell lines in the DepMap database also do not display a strong requirement for PRR12 function39.

The accumulation of cells in G2 in the absence of PRR12 suggests these cells may undergo a G2 checkpoint-mediated cell cycle arrest, which acts to prevent cells from entering mitosis in the presence of DNA damage or incomplete DNA replication70–72. We therefore sought to test whether PRR12 knockout cells exhibit DNA damage that could trigger the G2 checkpoint. To this end, we quantified the presence of DNA damage response factors in PRR12 knockout cells. We found that PRR12 knockout NIH-3T3 cells displayed a significant increase in the number of cells containing foci for multiple DNA damage response proteins, including γH2AX, TP53BP1, and RAD51B, compared to control cells (Figures 6B,6C,6F,6G,S6B, andS6C). Knockout of either NIPBL or RAD21 in NIH-3T3 cells also resulted in an increase in the accumulation of γH2AX, TP53BP1, and RAD51B foci, although this effect was milder compared to PRR12 knockout cells (Figures 6B,6C,6F,6G,S6B, andS6C). Similarly, knockout of PRR12, MAU2, and SMC3 resulted in an accumulation of DNA damage response proteins in HeLa cells, but to a lesser extent overall to that observed in NIH-3T3 cells (Figures 6D,6E,6H, and6I). The presence of TP53BP1 and RAD51B foci are indicative of the presence of DNA double strand breaks in the absence of PRR12. Finally, to determine if the G2 checkpoint is activated in cells lacking PRR12, we analyzed phosphorylation of ATM at Ser1981 (pATM), which only becomes phosphorylated in response to DNA lesions73,74. In PRR12 knockout NIH-3T3 cells, we observed the presence of pATM foci, consistent with the observed cell cycle arrest (Figures S6DandS6E). Similar to previous results, simultaneous knockout of both PRR12 and MAU2 did not result in an additive change in DNA damage compared to levels observed in either single knockout (Figures S6FandS6G). Together, these data indicate that PRR12 is required for genomic integrity.

In the absence of PRR12, cells accumulate spontaneous DNA double-strand breaks, eventually resulting in a G2 checkpoint-mediated arrest (Figures 6AandS6D). Our mass spectrometry analysis revealed an interaction between PRR12 and multiple regulators of DNA damage across all tested samples (Figure 3A), suggesting that PRR12 may function in the repair of DNA lesions. To further test this hypothesis, we assessed the localization of PRR12 in the presence of DNA damage. In control cells, GFP-PRR12 localized diffusely throughout the nucleus. In contrast, treatment with the DNA topoisomerase inhibitor etoposide resulted in an accumulation of GFP-PRR12 at DNA lesions, co-localizing with γH2AX (Figures 7A,7Band7C). The cohesin complex is important for the regulation of DNA repair, specifically in the repair of double strand breaks by promoting homologous recombination17,19,75,76. Previous work identified NIPBL as critical for the regulation of cohesin-mediated DNA damage repair, and NIPBL accumulates at DNA damage sites77,78. Using an antibody specific to NIPBL, we found that NIPBL and GFP-PRR12 co-localized to sites of DNA damage marked by γH2AX, suggesting that PRR12 interacts with NIPBL at these sites (Figures 7A,7B, and7C).

We next sought to define if the localization of PRR12 at sites of DNA damage is dependent on NIPBL or cohesin. To this end, we assessed the localization of GFP-PRR12 in NIPBL or SMC3 knockout cells in the presence of DNA damage. In the absence of both NIPBL and SMC3, no accumulation of GFP-PRR12 was observed at γH2AX sites, indicating PRR12 requires both cohesin and NIPBL to accumulate at sites of DNA damage (Figures 7D,7E, and7F). Conversely, to determine if NIPBL requires PRR12 for its accumulation at DNA damage sites, we performed the reciprocal experiment, quantifying the accumulation of NIPBL at DNA lesions in the absence of PRR12. Importantly, in the absence of PRR12, NIPBL localization was significantly reduced but not lost from γH2AX sites. This suggests that PRR12 contributes to, but is not fully required, for the localization of NIPBL at sites of DNA damage (Figures 7G,7H, and7I). Together, these data indicate an essential role for PRR12 in maintaining genomic integrity and support a model in which PRR12 functions alongside NIPBL and cohesin in the repair of DNA lesions.

Here, we exploited the context-specific requirements inherent for cohesin functional diversity in combination with co-essentiality analysis to identify cohesin-regulatory proteins. Using this approach, we identify the previously uncharacterized gene PRR12 as a binding partner of NIPBL/MAU2 and a co-regulator of cohesin. In the absence of PRR12, cells display a significant reduction in nuclear-localized cohesin, a phenotype that can be rescued upon co-depletion of the cohesin destabilizer protein WAPL, suggesting PRR12 may contribute to the stability of cohesin on DNA. Furthermore, our affinity purifications revealed that PRR12 interacts with the cohesin complex via its C-terminal domain and we predict these interactions are mediated via a direct association with the NIPBL/MAU2 complex. In support of PRR12 functioning alongside the NIPBL/MAU2 complex, co-depletion of PRR12 and MAU2 did not result in additive defects to cohesin localization. However, it is important to note that, although similar phenotypes were observed in NIH-3T3 cells upon NIPBL or PRR12 knockout, the severity of these phenotypes differs in some situations. These data suggest that the function of PRR12 may not overlap entirely with NIPBL. Additionally, although PRR12 displays strong co-dependencies with the NIPBL/MAU2 complex across cell lines, the requirements for these proteins do differ in some situations. Together, these data, in combination with our affinity purification analysis, suggest that PRR12 together with NIPBL/MAU2 contribute to shared and distinct functions.

In addition to a reduction of chromatin-bound cohesin, we found that PRR12 knockout cells accumulate DNA damage. Cohesin function is critical to the repair of DNA lesions17,20,75,76, and the localization of the cohesin complex to sites of DNA damage occurs in a NIPBL/MAU2-dependent manner77,78. Our data support a model in which PRR12 may function together with NIPBL in mediating the response to DNA damage. In support of this model, we observed the co-localization of GFP-PRR12 with NIPBL at DNA lesions, and this localization is dependent on both NIPBL and cohesin. Additionally, our affinity purifications identified interactions between PRR12 and multiple DNA damage response proteins. PRR12 null cells have also been found to have an increased sensitivity to genotoxic agents in large-scale screens across multiple human cell lines79,80. Reciprocally, the increased expression of PRR12 in large-scale CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) screens conferred resistance to DNA damage-inducing agents in multiple human cell lines81,82. Alternatively, it is possible that the accumulation of spontaneous DNA breaks in the absence of PRR12 is a direct consequence of PRR12 loss. For example, PRR12 could contribute to DNA replication or function to relieve topological stress. Indeed, most spontaneous DNA double strand breaks occur during DNA replication83and our work identified modest interactions between PRR12 and the MCM2-7 replicative helicase complex (Supplementary Table 1A–F). Cohesin also plays a role in replication fork progression and recent work has found that the cohesin-associated protein STAG2 is particularly important for this role24,84–89. It is important to note that, similar to the dual role for cohesin in both DNA replication and the mediation of DNA damage, a role for PRR12 in both protecting against and response to DNA lesions either alongside cohesin or in addition to its cohesin associated roles does not need to be mutually exclusive.

Our work also demonstrates that the requirement for PRR12 differs across cell lines, with NIH-3T3 cells significantly sensitized to PRR12 loss in comparison to HeLa cells. Indeed, in most tested human cell lines, PRR12 is not considered essential for cell proliferation or survival based on large-scale CRISPR screens39, with only select human cell lines displaying a growth defect in the absence of PRR12. However, in multiple mouse cell lines, loss of PRR12 results in significant growth defects (Figures 4Band4E)60. Despite this significant difference in the requirement for PRR12, the protein sequence for PRR12 is highly conserved across species, with 93.6% identity between human and mouse cells. In addition, we observed similar localization dynamics and protein interactions for PRR12 in both human and mouse cell lines, suggesting the observed differential requirements do not reflect differences in PRR12 properties across species. Instead, these data most likely reflect differences in the underlying sensitivities of different cell lines to gene loss that may be influenced by the presence of functional redundancies, gene expression differences, altered sensitivities to stress, or overall differences in genome architecture.

Despite modest phenotypes in cultured human cell lines, mutations in PRR12 in human patients results in severe developmental and neurological impairments43–46. All of the documented variants are heterozygous mutations that occurred de novo. Predicted loss-of-function variants in PRR12 are extremely rare according to the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD(v3 and v2.1) suggesting that PRR12 is highly intolerant to haploinsufficiency46. Mutations in core cohesin regulators, including NIPBL, result in similar developmental and neurological defects, broadly defined as cohesinopathies. How disruption in NIPBL or PRR12 gene function manifest in disease pathologies remains poorly defined. The observed differences in gene requirements for PRR12 and known cohesin regulators in cell lines and human patients highlight the importance of studying the function of these genes in their native contexts. Together, our study highlights the power of identifying the cellular situations in which these genes are most required as a tool to reveal their function and to identify players in these pathways. Exploiting these differences will provide a means to define the complex mechanisms by which cohesin is regulated as well as providing critical insight into how disruption of these processes drives disease.

Although our current CRISPR/Cas9-based targeting approach provides a powerful strategy to perturb PRR12 and known cohesin components across multiple cell types, the polyclonal nature of this approach limits the ability to assess the temporal effects of PRR12 loss. Due to the essential role of cohesin, perturbation of the cohesin subunits and its regulators can be especially challenging. Future work utilizing a PRR12 degron or other targeted approaches will complement our findings to dissect the role of PRR12 in regulating on cohesin localization and the DNA damage response.

The proteins were allowed to cool to room temperature before the addition of 40mM iodoacetamide. The proteins were incubated with iodoacetamide for 30 minutes in the dark and then 2.5% v/v phosphoric acid was added. Next, 6 times the volume of S-trap binding buffer (90% MeOH, 100mM TEAB, pH 7.55) was added to the acidified proteins before the proteins were loaded onto a S-Trap microcolumns (ProtiFi). The column was washed 4 times with S-trap binding buffer and then 1 μg of trypsin diluted in 50mM TEAB pH 8.5 was added to the column and the proteins were digested on-column overnight at 37°C in a humidified incubator. The digested peptides were then eluted with three separate elutions: 50mM TEAB, 0.2% formic acid then 50% acetonitrile/0.2% formic acid. The elutions were pooled and quantified with a fluorometric peptide assay kit (Pierce) and then the eluted peptides were lyophilized overnight. Lyophilized peptides were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and then injected onto a Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid (Thermo Fischer Scientific) connected to a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system. Proteins were identified in Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Sequest HT. Percolator was used to validate Peptide-spectrum matches.

The tandem mass tag proteomic experiments were performed in HeLa cells that ectopically expressed GFP or a C-terminally GFP tagged PRR12 (1258-2036) construct. The immunoprecipitations were performed exactly as described above with one major difference. After lyophilizing the tryptic peptides, the samples were processed further for quantitative proteomics. First, 1μg of peptide from each sample were resuspended in 50 mM TEAB pH 8.5 and isoberic labeling was performed using the TMT10plex Isobaric Labeling Reagent Set (Thermo Fisher) at a 20:1 mass ratio of TMT label:peptide. This reaction was carried out for 1 hour at 25°C before it was quenched by the addition of hydroxylamine to a final concentration of 0.3%. The samples were pooled and lyophilized overnight. The Pierce High pH Reversed-Phase Peptide Fractionation Kit (Thermo Scientific) was used to fractionate the pooled peptides following the manufacturer’s protocol. The eight resulting fractions were lyophilized overnight. Each of the fractions were then resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and injected onto a onto a Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid (Thermo Fischer Scientific) connected to a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system. Proteins were identified in Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Sequest HT. Percolator was used to validate Peptide-spectrum matches. Peptide abundances were quantified by Reporter Ion Quantifier. To account for differences in immunoprecipitation efficiency across the samples the mean abundances were normalized to the mean GFP abundance.

Quantification of fluorescence intensity was conducted on unprocessed, maximally projected images using FIJI/image J and Cell Profiler. For image quantification, all images for comparison were acquired using the same microscope and acquisition settings. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (GraphPad Software). Details of statistical tests and sample sizes are provided in the figure legends.

The requirement for genes varies across cellular contexts. By exploiting these differences to define genes that show similar requirement patterns, Nguyen et al. identify a previously uncharacterized regulator of cohesin, PRR12, that functions together the NIPBL/MAU2 complex to promote genomic integrity.
