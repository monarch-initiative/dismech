BACKGROUND: Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) are rare neoplasms arising in the 
mediastinum, including thymic carcinomas and thymomas. Due to their rarity, 
little is known about the genomic profiles of TETs. Herein, we investigated the 
genomic characteristics of TETs evaluated in a large comprehensive genomic 
profiling database in a real-world setting.
METHODS: We included data from two different cohorts: Foundation Medicine Inc. 
(FMI) in the United States and the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced 
Therapeutics (C-CAT) in Japan. Samples profiled were examined for all classes of 
alterations in 253 genes targeted across all assays. Tumor mutational burden 
(TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) were also evaluated.
RESULTS: A total of 794 patients were collected in our study, including 722 
cases from FMI and 72 cases from C-CAT. In the FMI data, CDKN2A (39.9%), TP53 
(30.2%) and CDKN2B (24.6%) were frequently altered in thymic carcinoma, versus 
TP53 (7.8%), DNMT3A (6.8%), and CDKN2A (5.8%) in thymoma. TMB-high (≥10 
mutations/Mb) and MSI were present in 7.0% and 2.3% of thymic carcinomas, and 
1.6% and 0.3% of thymomas, respectively. Within C-CAT data, CDKN2A (38.5%), TP53 
(36.5%) and CDKN2B (30.8%) were also frequently altered in thymic carcinoma, 
while alterations of TSC1, SETD2 and LTK (20.0% each) were found in thymoma.
CONCLUSIONS: To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest cohort in which 
genomic alterations, TMB and MSI status of TETs were investigated. Potential 
targets for treatment previously unbeknownst in TETs are identified in this 
study, entailing newfound opportunities to advance therapeutic development.

Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs), including thymic carcinomas and thymomas, are rare neoplasms arising in the mediastinum.1 Complete surgical resection is standard treatment for resectable tumors and chemotherapy is indicated for patients with advanced or metastatic TETs.2 Cytotoxic therapy, such as platinum agents, anthracyclines and taxanes are active in TETs, but have not been assessed in randomized controlled trials.3, 4, 5 Molecular targeted drugs or immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as lenvatinib, sunitinib, everolimus, and pembrolizumab may be options of second-line treatment for thymic carcinoma.6, 7, 8, 9 However, second line and later activity of these drugs are limited with response rate of about 20%-30%, and high rates of toxicity. The rarity of the disease and lack of randomized controlled trials are challenges.

While previous reports have examined the genomic alterations of TETs to explore potential new therapeutic targets, limited sample numbers led to variability and disagreement among prior studies.10,11 In light of these challenges, it is imperative to investigate larger cohorts where we can more thoroughly comprehend the genomic landscape and achieve statistical power for therapeutic target discoveries. Moreover, there have been no reports investigating tumor mutational burden (TMB), particularly TMB-high status (TMB ≥ 10 mutations per megabase), or microsatellite instability (MSI) in a sizable TET cohort—both of which have been shown to have clear clinical relevance and are Food and Drug Administration approved biomarkers in all advanced or metastatic tumors.12, 13, 14 Herein, we investigated the genomic characteristics of thymic epithelial tumors evaluated in a large real-world database and a regional dataset.

We examined data derived from samples from a cohort of United States thymic epithelial tumors from the Foundation Medicine Inc. (FMI) research database and a Japanese cohort from the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics (C-CAT). C-CAT is engaged in a national project for collecting genomic analysis and clinical results from patients in Japan. Both cohorts consisted of samples submitted in the course of routine clinical care.

A hybrid capture-based next-generation sequencing assay was performed on patient samples in a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) certified, College of American Pathologists (CAP) accredited, New York State approved laboratory (FMI, Cambridge, MA, USA).15,16 For the FMI cohort, we assessed all classes of alterations in 253 genes targeted across all three assays (FoundationOne®CDx, FoundationOne®, and FoundationOne®Heme). Short variants, including base substitutions and short insertions or deletions (indels), copy number alterations (gene amplifications and homozygous deletions), as well as gene fusions and rearrangements were detected as described previously.15,16 Additional filtering was applied to exclude common germline variants based on presence in public databases (e.g. dbSNP, ExAC, 1000 genomes); of note, known pathogenic germline variants (e.g. in BRCA1, BRCA2) were considered as reportable. Only alterations predicted to be pathogenic based on several factors such as presence in COSMIC database, literature evidence and support, alterations resulting in truncations or deletions of known tumor suppressor genes or those resulting in amplification and activation of oncogenes, were processed for downstream analysis; variants of unknown significance were excluded.17 For the C-CAT cohort, we assessed all classes of alterations in 324 genes targeted with the FoundationOne®CDx assay. TMB was determined on 0.8-1.11 Mb of sequenced DNA.18 MSI was determined on 95-114 loci,19 and the calculation of MSI-high frequency excluded cases in which MSI could not be determined.

We also collected limited patient characteristics, including histology (thymic carcinoma/thymoma), age (≥75/<75 years), and sex (male/female). Genetic alterations, TMB, and MSI were stratified by histology, age, and sex.

Approval for the Foundation Medicine portion of this study, including a waiver of informed consent and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act waiver of authorization, was obtained from the Western Institutional Review Board (protocol #20152817). This study, including the C-CAT portion was also approved by the institutional review board of Juntendo University Hospital (Approval number E21-0098).

Prevalence was calculated relative to the total number of assessable samples. For FMI data, gene alterations with prevalence values >1% in the overall cohort (Figure 1) or in each individual histological cohort (Figure 2) were considered for downstream pairwise analysis. For each pairwise comparison, an odds ratio and P value were computed using Fisher’s Exact Test. P values were then adjusted to account for multiple hypothesis testing with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05. All analyses were performed in Python 3.7.6 while visualizations were produced in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data from a total of 794 patients were collected for our study, including 722 cases from FMI and 72 cases from C-CAT (Table 1). In the FMI data, 334 female patients (46.3%) and 388 male patients (53.7%) were included with a median age of 59 years (IQR, 47-68 years). Of 414 cases of thymic carcinoma, 164 patients (39.6%) were female and median age was 61 years (IQR, 52-69 years). Of 308 cases of thymoma, 170 patients (55.2%) were female and median age was 56 years (IQR, 44-65 years). In C-CAT data, in total, 35 female patients (48.6%) and 37 male patients (51.4%) were included with a median age of 56 years (IQR, 48.8-67 years). Of 52 cases of thymic carcinoma, 23 patients were female (44.2%) and median age was 58.5 years (IQR, 50.8-72 years). The genomic landscape, including the most frequently identified gene alterations and biomarkers (TMB, MSI), for patients with thymic carcinoma and thymoma are shown in Figure 1A, Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, Supplementary Figures S1 and S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2023.101627. The most frequently altered genes in thymic carcinoma were CDKN2A (39.9%), TP53 (30.2%), CDKN2B (24.6%), BAP1 (8.2%), TET2 (8.0%), KIT (8.0%), SETD2 (7.7%), NFKBIA (7.7%), ASXL1 (7.0%) and KMT2D (6.0%). TMB-high and MSI-high cases were 7.0% and 2.3％, respectively. In thymoma, the most frequently altered genes were TP53 (7.8%), DNMT3A (6.8%), CDKN2A (5.8%), CDKN2B (4.6%), HRAS, CHEK2 (3.6% each), TET2, KMT2A (3.3%), BCOR (2.9%), PIK3CA and MTOR (2.3% each). TMB-high and MSI-high cases were 1.6% and 0.3%, respectively. Patterns of gene alterations in major signaling pathways suggest their potential functional significance in thymoma and thymic carcinomas (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2023.101627).

Comparing gene prevalence between thymic carcinoma and thymomas revealed a significantly higher prevalence of alterations in CDKN2A, TP53, CDKN2B, BAP1, TET2, KIT, SETD2, NFKBIA, ASXL1, KMT2D, ARID1A, PBRM1, CREBBP, SPEN, KDM6A, and FBXW7 in thymic carcinoma relative to thymomas; only KMT2A was significantly more prevalent in thymomas (Figure 1B and C). The prevalence of TMB-high was significantly higher in thymic carcinoma relative to thymoma (7.0% versus 1.6%; P = 0.0024).

We examined whether genomic profiles differed based on clinical factors. Examining sex, we found similar frequencies of alterations between male and female patients, with no significant differences identified in thymic carcinomas or thymomas (Figure 2A and B). When dichotomizing based on age (<75 years versus ≥75 years), the prevalence of TMB-high and NFKBIA were dramatically elevated in older thymic carcinoma patients (TMB-high 5.2% age <75 years versus 21.3% age ≥75 years, P = 0.028; NFKBIA 6.0% age <75 years versus 21.3% age ≥75 years, P = 0.035) (Figure 2C). In thymoma, prevalence of HRAS and TET2 were significantly more prevalent in older patients (17.6% versus 1.8% and 14.7% versus 1.8%, respectively) (Figure 2D).

The top 10 most frequent somatic gene alterations in patients with thymic carcinoma and thymoma from C-CAT are shown in Figure 3. In thymic carcinoma, CDKN2A (n = 20, 38.5%), TP53 (n = 19, 36.5%), CDKN2B (n = 16, 30.8%), KMT2D, MTAP (n = 12, 23.1% each), NFKBIA (n = 11, 21.2%) were frequently altered. In thymoma, alterations of TSC1, SETD2, LTK (n = 4, 20.0% each) and CD22, KEL, ATM, KMT2A, NOTCH1, TP53 (n = 3, 15.0% each) were found. In patients with thymic carcinoma, a subtype of histology was registered in 26 cases and comprised 25 cases of squamous cell carcinoma and one case of basaloid carcinoma. In patients with squamous cell carcinomas, we analyzed the frequency of genetic alterations (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2023.101627) and found that CDKN2A (n = 13, 52.0%), TP53 (n = 9, 36.0%), CDKN2B (n = 8, 32.0%) were frequently altered. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the largest cohort of TET genomic profiles; thereby providing important updates of gene alteration frequencies identified in smaller prior studies. Also, our study compared gene alterations according to age and sex, and included the Japanese cohorts, which has not been reported in the prior studies.

Our study showed that genetic alterations in TETs are predominantly in tumor suppressor genes. The frequency of CDKN2A, TP53, and CDKN2B alterations was high, especially in thymic carcinoma. These alterations have been reported in multiple cancer types, and a few have been reported individually in TETs.20, 21, 22, 23, 24 Recently, Girard et al24 reported on comprehensive genomic profiling of TETs with 90 thymoma patients and 174 thymic carcinoma patients, and their results are consistent with the findings in our study.

CDKN2A and CDKN2B, encoding p16 and p15 respectively, inhibit cyclin dependent kinase and regulate cellular proliferation by preventing the normal cell cycle.25
CDKN2A also encodes p14ARF and activates TP53.25 Alterations in these genes may lead to an inappropriate progression through the cell cycle and uncontrolled tumor cell proliferation.25 In contrast to our study, a previous report which performed whole exome sequencing of 117 thymic epithelia tumors did not show alterations in CDKN2A.11 The deep targeted sequencing approach allowed us to identify CDKN2A alterations, the vast majority of which were homozygous deletions. Additionally, patients in our cohort are frequently late-stage and may have received prior treatment, which could further lead to some of the observed differences. Several inhibitors for these cyclin dependent kinases have been investigated recently, and a positive response was found in thymic carcinoma.26,27 These drugs may constitute a treatment option, and it might also be possible to select patients who would benefit from these treatments by using gene panel testing. TP53 is also a well-known tumor suppressor gene that has a wide range of functions including cell cycle arrest and DNA damage repair.28
WEE1 inhibitors, which impair adequate DNA repair and generate mitotic catastrophe, showed favorable efficacy in patients with p53 mutant ovarian cancer, although TP53 alteration may not be a perfectly predictive biomarker.29,30 These kinds of treatments can be expected in TETs in the future.

Other genetic alterations have been reported in other cancer types but are rarer in TETs. Some, such as ASXL1, SETD2, and KIT, were previously reported in TETs, while others, including RB1, CHEK2, and SPEN, are reported here for the first time, aided at least in part by the larger cohort size.21,31,32 In other cancer types, there have been remarkable advances in molecular targeted therapies, including inhibitors of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway (found around 10% in thymic carcinoma in our study) or the RAS/MAP kinase pathway (found around 5% in thymic carcinoma in our study), targeted therapy for alterations in DNA repair genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, PALB2 or CHEK2 like PARP inhibitors, and targeted therapy for alterations in epigenetic regulation such as ARID1A, SMARCB1 or SMARCA4 like EZH2 inhibitors.33, 34, 35, 36 Inhibitors of the FGF/FGFR pathway are also in development for neoplasms with FGF or FGFR gene alterations. These genetic alterations were identified in our study, and TETs, especially thymic carcinoma, may benefit from these agents.

In thymoma, on the other hand, no gene alteration was found with a frequency exceeding 10% in the larger FMI cohort. KMT2A was the only gene alteration that was more frequent in thymoma than in thymic carcinoma. KMT2A encodes the protein which binds DNA and methylates histone H3 at lysine-4.37 While KMT2A rearrangements have been reported as initiators of neoplasms, especially in acute myeloid and lymphoid leukemias, the precise mechanism remains unclear.37 Possible targeted therapies for KMT2A, such as DOT1L and menin inhibitors, are now under investigation in patients with leukemia.38 One study reported that KMT2A rearrangements were associated with thymoma, especially with aggressive histologic features (type B2 and/or B3 histology).39 Further studies are warranted to better evaluate KMT2A as a therapeutic target in thymoma. DNMT3A was the second most frequently identified gene alteration in thymoma, although not significantly more common compared to thymic carcinoma. DNA Methyltransferase 3 Alpha (DNMT3a) encoded by DNMT3A gene is DNA methyltransferase enzyme which is essential for the development of mammalian tissue.40,41 In a previous study, the downregulation of DNMT3a expression was found to be associated with thymoma with myasthenia gravis.42 Further studies with clinical information are also awaited. HRAS, which is one of the Ras family genes, is also promising therapeutic target. RAS protein maintains GTP- and GDP-bound states, which trigger various downstream effector molecules including RAF/MEK/ERK, PI3K/Akt/mTOR downstream cascades. Thus, these mutations lead to activation and can direct cells to grow and divide without control.43 A farnesyltransferase inhibitor, for instance, demonstrated encouraging efficacy in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma with HRAS mutations.44 Future exploration of the association between HRAS and thymoma is also desirable.

Our study also identified the prevalence of TMB-high and MSI-high in a large cohort. PD-L1 expression, TMB and MSI are potential predictors of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors.45,46 Previous studies have shown that PD-L1 expression can be elevated in some patients with TETs and several trials have investigated this therapeutic modality with some responses, but also high rates of autoimmune toxicity.8,47, 48, 49, 50, 51 In addition to this, our study identified that a certain number of patients showed TMB-high and MSI-high, especially in thymic carcinoma which may be an indicator of higher response to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. Of note, among thymic carcinoma patients, TMB-high tumors were found to be significantly more common in older patients (aged ≥75 years) compared to younger patients (aged <75 years). Therefore, this is worthy of further exploration as a potential biomarker for selection of immunotherapy in thymic carcinoma.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not analyze the association between genomic alterations and patients’ clinical characteristics such as staging, histological subtypes, or survival. In a prior study comprising a smaller cohort of tumors profiled at Foundation Medicine, 68% of the malignant thymomas were of the A/AB/B1/B2 type and 32% were B3.24 The subtypes of thymic carcinomas included thymic squamous cell carcinomas (∼40%), thymic undifferentiated carcinomas (∼31%) and thymic neuroendocrine carcinomas (∼17%), as well as rarer subtypes including thymic adenocarcinomas, basaloid carcinomas, thymic lymphoepithelioma-like carcinomas and thymic sarcomatoid carcinomas. Despite a larger cohort size in the present study, a similar breakdown of histological subtypes may be expected. Second, C-CAT data, especially in the thymoma cohort, had limited numbers, and further cases are needed to evaluate the frequency of genomic alterations in the Japanese cohort. Third, because of using a specific gene panel, we were limited in our ability to examine some genes not included in this gene panel, such as the thymic cancer specific oncogene, GTF2I.52 Fourth, the C-CAT cohort was limited in size and may show racial/regional differences in comparison to the overall FMI cohort. This may explain some of the differences in gene alteration prevalence between the two cohorts, especially in thymomas. In contrast, the strength of this study is that it had an overall relatively large sample size and used a uniform diagnostic method, which allowed us to show the frequency of genetic alterations in these rare tumors. Further, compared to prior studies, these data are derived from a real-world cohort which may be preferentially enriched for more advanced cases; thus, we believe that this data may provide a better representation for practical clinical applications.

In conclusion, these large analyses of genomic alterations and TMB/MSI status in TETs confirmed several known genomic alterations and the prevalence of TMB-high and MSI-high, and additionally identified several other genomic alterations that may lead to therapeutic development opportunities.
