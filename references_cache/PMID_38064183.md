# BIO101 stimulates myoblast differentiation and improves muscle function in adult and old mice.
**Authors:** Serova M, Didry-Barca B, Deloux R, Foucault AS, Veillet S, Lafont R, Dilda PJ, Latil M
**Journal:** J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jcsm.13326](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcsm.13326)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Muscle aging is associated with a consistent decrease in the ability of muscle tissue to regenerate following intrinsic muscle degradation, injury or overuse. Age-related imbalance of protein synthesis and degradation, mainly regulated by AKT/mTOR pathway, leads to progressive loss of muscle mass. Maintenance of anabolic and regenerative capacities of skeletal muscles may be regarded as a therapeutic option for sarcopenia and other muscle wasting diseases. Our previous studies have demonstrated that BIO101, a pharmaceutical grade 20-hydroxyecdysone, increases protein synthesis through the activation of MAS receptor involved in the protective arm of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. The purpose of the present study was to assess the anabolic and pro-differentiating properties of BIO101 on C2C12 muscle cells in vitro and to investigate its effects on adult and old mice models in vivo.

METHODS: The effects of BIO101 on C2C12 differentiation were assessed using myogenic transcription factors and protein expression of major kinases of AKT/mTOR pathway by Western blot. The in vivo effects of BIO101 have been investigated in BIO101 orally-treated (50 mg/kg/day) adult mice (3 months) for 28 days. To demonstrate potential beneficial effect of BIO101 treatment in a sarcopenic mouse model, we use orally treated 22-month-old C57Bl6/J mice, for 14 weeks with vehicle or BIO101. Mice body and muscle weight were recorded. Physical performances were assessed using running capacity and muscle contractility tests.

RESULTS: Anabolic properties of BIO101 were confirmed by the rapid activation of AKT/mTOR, leading to an increase of C2C12 myotubes diameters (+26%, P < 0.001). Pro-differentiating effects of BIO101 on C2C12 myoblasts were revealed by increased expression of muscle-specific differentiation transcription factors (MyoD, myogenin), resulting in increased fusion index and number of nuclei per myotube (+39% and +53%, respectively, at day 6). These effects of BIO101 were like those of angiotensin (1-7) and were abolished with the use of A779, a MAS receptor specific antagonist. Chronic BIO101 oral treatment induced AKT/mTOR activation and anabolic effects accompanied with improved physical performances in adult and old animals (maximal running distance and maximal running velocity).

CONCLUSIONS: Our data suggest beneficial anabolic and pro-differentiating effects of BIO101 rendering BIO101 a potent drug candidate for treating sarcopenia and possibly other muscle wasting disorders.

Skeletal muscles represent the most abundant tissue in the body that undergo gradual changes with aging.
1
 Sarcopenia is characterized by the loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength and physical performance that occurs with advancing age and may lead to frailty. Sarcopenia is prevalent in 6–19% of those ≥60 years of age in the general population and is recognized by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10‐MC).
2



During aging, skeletal muscles harbour not only structural changes but also modifications at cellular and subcellular levels. The role of protein turnover is recognized as a major determinant of muscle atrophy with impaired muscle protein synthesis and resistance to anabolic stimuli.
3
 The AKT‐mTOR‐p70S6K cascade is a critical pathway regulating protein synthesis, and AKT activation is adequate to increase muscle mass.
4
 AKT controls both protein synthesis by activating the kinase mTOR and protein degradation by repressing the transcription factor FoxO. Compared with young animals, the skeletal muscle of aged mice demonstrates significant deficits in phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR.

Along with the protein turnover, myogenic differentiation contributes to muscle quality and homeostasis. The ability to generate during post‐natal growth and to regenerate skeletal muscle in adults is mainly due to muscle stem cells called satellite cells. C2C12‐derived myoblasts is a well‐established model of muscle differentiation. C2C12 cells possess in vitro ability to proliferate, differentiate, and fuse into mature myotubes. This process is orchestrated by myogenic regulatory factors (Myf5, MyoD, myogenin, and MRF4).
7
 Myogenin induction marks the entry of myoblasts into the terminal differentiation program and subsequent fusion with adjacent differentiating myoblasts to form multinucleated myotubes, or with pre‐existing myofibers to enlarge them.

Myogenic differentiation is driven and regulated by multiple proteins and signalling pathways. AKT, mTOR, AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK), and FoxO3
8
, 
9
 notably coordinate the balance between muscle growth and atrophy. As an example, AKT directly increases MyoD transcriptional activity and subsequent muscle differentiation.
10
 In addition, sarcopenia has been shown to disturb the myogenic program, inducing a progressive reduction in the regenerative capacity of the skeletal myogenic stem cells, which are critical for sustaining repair in response to muscle damage or trauma.
11



Accumulating evidences suggest that the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS) is involved in maintaining skeletal muscle homeostasis, making it a potential therapeutic target for muscle wasting disorders.
12
 The activation of the classical RAAS arm (the ACE/AngII/AT1R axis) has been proven to participate in muscle pathogenesis to promote muscle wasting accompanied with pathological muscle remodelling.
13
 By contrast, the RAAS protective arm (ACE2/Ang‐(1‐7)/Mas1R) counteracts the deleterious effects of the classical arm. Indeed, in rodents, Ang‐(1‐7) alleviated muscle dysfunction in a number of pathological conditions, including Ang II‐induced skeletal muscle atrophy.
14
 In a sarcopenic mice model, the ability to repair skeletal muscle after hindlimb immobilization was restored upon Losartan treatment, an AT1 receptor blocker.
15
 On the other hand, the sustained activation of ACE2/Ang‐(1‐7)/Mas1R axis was shown to prevent muscle wasting. Ang‐(1‐7) treatment enhanced skeletal muscle motor function in in old ACE2KO and wild‐type mice.

Ecdysteroids are secondary metabolites found in several plants that serve as endocrine disrupters to protect them from phytophagous insects. Despite the chemical structure similar to mammal steroids, ecdysteroids do not display androgenic or estrogenic properties in mammals. Anabolic properties of ecdysteroids were widely described in animal models and notably associated with increased Ca2+ influx and stimulation of protein synthesis in muscle cells.
18
 The mechanism of action of ecdysteroids in mammals is still a matter of debate, and we recently proposed that 20‐hydroxyecdysone (20E) targets MAS receptor, a key member of protective arm of RAAS.
19



The aim of our study was to assess anabolic and pro‐differentiating effects of BIO101, a pharmaceutical preparation (purity ≥97%) of 20E from Cyanotis sp. on muscle cells using in vitro C2C12 cell line and to investigate its effects on adult and old mice models.

Except otherwise mentioned, all the reagents and chemicals were from Sigma. A779 was from Tocris. Ang‐(1‐7) peptide was custom‐prepared by the IBPS peptide synthesis platform (Sorbonne University). The pharmaceutical‐grade production of 20‐hydroxyecdysone (≥97%) was from Patheon (Regensburg, Germany). The resulting GMP drug substance was named BIO101. When indicated for in vivo experiments, BIO101 was complexified with (2‐hydroxypropyl)‐β‐cyclodextrin. Equimolar mix of BIO101 and cyclodextrin was solubilized in MeOH. The mixture was stirred, concentrated to dryness to obtain a white solid and then solubilized in H2O/ACN before lyophilization (48 h). The ratio cyclodextrin/BIO101 is determined by LCMS (here, 250.5 mg of BIO101 in 1 g of complex).

The C2C12 mouse myoblast cell line was from ATCC (CRL‐1772). Except otherwise mentioned, culture media, serum, antibiotics and supplements were from Life Technologies. All cultures contained 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin and are maintained under 5% CO2 at 37°C. For C2C12 proliferation, cells were maintained in DMEM/F‐12 (50/50) mix containing 2.5 g/L glucose supplemented with 15% FBS. To induce C2C12 differentiation, the medium was replaced by DMEM/F‐12 mix supplemented with 2% horse serum.

Cells were allowed to differentiate for various times in the presence or absence of various concentrations of BIO101 (see figure legends for details). After fixation and blocking (10% FBS, 0.1% Triton X100, 0.1% Tween 20), the cells were labelled with a mouse anti‐myosin heavy chain (MHC) (MF‐20; DSHB), a mouse anti‐myogenin (F5D; DSHB) or a mouse anti‐MyoD (5.8A; Thermo Fisher Scientific) primary antibody. A goat anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated or a CyTM3‐conjugated affiniPure donkey anti‐mouse IgG secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch) was used as secondary antibody (Life Technologies). All images were acquired with an epifluorescence microscope Leica DM6000B. Image analysis were performed using ImageJ software. The number of DAPI‐stained nuclei per myotube was counted, and the diameters of MHC‐positive myotubes were measured. The fusion index was calculated as the ratio of nuclei in fused MHC‐positive cells (≥2 nuclei per cells) to the total nuclei number.

C2C12 cells cultures under various conditions (see figure legends for details) were harvested directly in ice‐cold RIPA buffer (Life Technologies) containing protease (Sigma) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Life Technologies). For immunoblotting, cell lysates were electrophoresed on 5–15% gradient SDS‐polyacrylamide gels, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Bio‐Rad) and incubated with primary antibodies diluted at 1:1000 overnight at 4°C. Antibodies against phospho‐AKT (Ser473), phospho‐P70S6K (Thr389), phospho‐S6 (Ser235/236), phospho‐AMPKα (Thr172), phospho‐ACC (Ser79) and endogenous AKT, P70S6K, S6, AMPKα, ACC, GAPDH and vinculin were from Cell Signalling Technologies. Anti‐MHC and anti‐Myogenin were from Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank. MyoD monoclonal antibody (5.8A) was from Invitrogen. Secondary antibodies were HRP‐linked anti‐rabbit and anti‐mouse IgG (Cell Signalling Technologies). Membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies and the bands were revealed using Clarity™ Western ECL Substrate and XRS + ChemiDoc™ imager (Bio‐Rad).

All procedures were performed in accordance with the French national authority guidelines for the detention, use, and the ethical treatment of laboratory animals based on European Union directives 2010/63/EU and were approved by the institutional animal care and use Ethics Committee ‘N°005 Charles Darwin’ at the Sorbonne University (agreement number A75‐13‐15). All mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. All animals were housed at a 12 h day and night cycle with food and water ad libidum.

For in vivo multi‐dosing studies, BIO101 was employed at 50 mg/kg/day. Indeed, in previous studies, when mice were treated with either 5 or 50 mg/kg/day, we observed that only the higher dosage was able to significantly increase the protein content of the soleus and significantly decrease the myostatin gene expression of the same muscle (data not shown).

Young adult (3 months), middle‐aged adults (13 months) and old adults (22 months) C57Bl6/J female mice were used in two different experiment settings: 1/young adult mice were orally‐treated by daily gavage for 28 days with vehicle (water; n = 15) or BIO101 (n = 15) at a dose of 50 mg/kg/day; 2/old mice (22 months) were orally treated by daily gavage for 14 weeks with vehicle (cyclodextrin; n = 8) or BIO101 complex (n = 10) at a dose of 50 mg/kg/day. The control adult mice (12 months) received only the vehicle (n = 10). All animals of this experiment set were submitted to a high fat diet for the duration of the experiment (4442 kcal/kg; proteins 19.8%, lipids 23% and carbohydrates 39.5%) and body weight recordings were performed weekly. At the completion of the study, adult and old animals were 17‐ and 26‐months old, respectively.

Mice were submitted to running tolerance test on a horizontal motorized treadmill, and their maximal running speed (Vmax) and maximal running distance were recorded at the at the completion of the experiment. Treadmill speed was increased to 20 cm/s within 2 min. The speed was then increased by 5 cm/s every 5 min until mice became exhausted. Mice were removed from the treadmill when they received five electric shocks (0.4 mA) in <10 s.

Muscle contractility and weakness (specific maximal force) were evaluated by measuring the in situ tibialis anterior muscle (TA) contraction in response to nerve stimulation, as previously described.
20



Normal distribution of data was assessed using d'Agostino–Pearson test, or Shapiro–Wilk when n < 8. Statistical comparison between two groups was performed using a parametric Student's t‐test, or a non‐parametric Mann–Whitney test. If data were expressed as percentage of control, analysis were performed with a parametric one‐sample t‐test, or a non‐parametric one‐sample Wilcoxon test. For the comparison of more than two groups, statistical analysis was performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by a Fisher LSD post hoc test for multiple comparisons, or a Kruskal–Wallis test followed by a Dunn's test when the data were not normally distributed. For the analysis of MyoD and MyoG expression kinetics, two‐way ANOVA was performed followed by a Fisher LSD post hoc test for multiple comparisons. All results are displayed as mean ± SEM and considered significant at P‐value ≤0.05 (*), ≤0.01 (**), ≤0.001 (***). All statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.

BIO101 effects were investigated on 6‐days‐differentiated C2C12 murine myotubes. After 2 days of treatment with BIO101 (5 μM), a significant (+ 26%; P < 0.001) increase in myotube diameter was observed compared with control conditions (Figure 1A). These effects are consistent with an activation of the anabolic PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Indeed, by densitometric analysis of p‐AKT/AKT and p‐P70S6K/P70S6K western blots, we observed a rapid increase of AKT phosphorylation and its downstream target P70S6K in BIO101‐treated C2C12‐derived myotubes compared with untreated cells (Figure 1B). In addition, BIO101 treatment was associated with a rapid increase in the phosphorylation of AMPKα and acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC) (Figure 1C), suggesting effects on energy metabolism. AMPK is known to phosphorylate ACC and therefore inhibit its activity, leading to an inhibition of de novo fatty acid synthesis and a stimulation of fatty acid oxidation.

A potential impact of BIO101 on myoblast proliferation was investigated using BrdU assay (see Supporting Information). After 48 h of treatment with BIO101 at concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 50 μM, no significant pro‐proliferative activity of C2C12 myoblasts was detected (Figure S1).

Knowing that BIO101 was able to induce AKT phosphorylation during the first step of C2C12 differentiation, we investigated its ability to promote early differentiation on C2C12 myoblasts, by treating cells with increasing concentrations of BIO101 (from 0.1 to 5 μM) along the differentiation process, for 6 days. After 6 days of differentiation, MHC immunostaining demonstrated a significant increase (+45% with P < 0.001) in myotube diameter following 5 μM BIO101 treatment (Figure 3A, left panels) compared with untreated myotubes accompanied with a significant increase of two parameters reflecting the differentiation efficiency such as fusion index and number of nuclei per myotube (+39% and +53%, respectively, with P < 0.001; Figure 3A, right panels). Nuclei number distribution per myotube showed that BIO101 treatment significantly increased the proportion of myotubes containing a high number of nuclei (Figure 3B) with a reduced percentage of small myotubes containing two to four nuclei by 17% (from 52.5 ± 2.5% to 35.6 ± 2.8%, P < 0.001). The treatment also favoured myotubes containing more than 11 to 20 or more than 30 nuclei (+69% and +106%, respectively, compared with untreated control cells) (Figure 3B).

The pro‐differentiating potential of BIO101 was studied by evaluation of MyoD, myogenin and MHC protein expression, along with the differentiation process by using the most efficient BIO101 concentration on C2C12‐derived myotubes (5 μM). Increased level of MyoD protein was observed from the second day of differentiation in the presence of BIO101 as well as higher myogenin and MHC protein expression when compared with untreated cells (Figure 3C). MyoD and myogenin nuclear staining, almost undetectable at day 0, were detectable from day 1 and increased progressively over 6 days in both vehicle‐treated and BIO101 treated cells (Figure 3D,E, P < 0.05 and P < 0.001 for time effect, respectively). BIO101 treatment was responsible for an improved kinetics of differentiation as judged by the global increase of MyoD and myogenin‐positive cells over 6 days of differentiation (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01 for treatment effect, respectively) (Figure 3D,E, right panels).

We studied the in vivo effects of BIO101 (50 mg/kg/day) chronic oral administration for 28 days on skeletal muscle quality and function of 3‐month‐old young adult mice under normal diet conditions (see Supporting Information). Vehicle‐treated young adult mice had almost no increase of weight gain throughout the duration of the study (+0.09 ± 0.2 g) while BIO101‐treated animal show a moderate weight gain (+0.3 ± 0.2 g, P = ns) (Figure S2A) with a slight but significant absolute body weight increase at the end of treatment compared with vehicle‐treated mice (+4.3%, P < 0.01, data not shown) and a significant increase of the gastrocnemius and TA muscle mass of 4.9% (P < 0.05) and 7% (P < 0.01), respectively (Figure S2B), compared with vehicle‐treated mice. Young BIO101‐treated mice exhibited a trend toward increased maximal running distance compared with untreated mice (+19%, ns, Figure S2C). We next performed TA muscle in situ contractility tests in which we observed a significant increase (+9%, P < 0.05) of maximal absolute strength in BIO101‐treated adult mice (96.3 ± 2.6 g compared with 88.4 ± 2.2 g in untreated‐mice), without modification of maximal specific strength (Figure S2D). Except for an increase of half‐relaxation time, the contractile parameters remained essentially unchanged (data not shown).

Gastrocnemius muscles from BIO101‐treated mice displayed higher level of AKT phosphorylation with a slight increase in p‐S6/S6 level without reaching statistical significance (Figure S3A) and no modification of AMPK and ACC protein expression and phosphorylation (Figure S3B). Based on in vitro and in vivo results, we next investigated the effects of BIO101, in a mouse model of sarcopenia. Under high‐fat diet conditions, 22‐month‐old C57Bl6/J mice were orally treated for 14 weeks with either vehicle or 50 mg/kg/day of BIO101. Twelve‐month‐old (adult) C57Bl6/J mice were used as control and were orally treated for 14 weeks with vehicle. Vehicle‐treated old mice had a very moderate weight gain throughout the duration of the study (+2.24 ± 0.29 g) compared with BIO101‐treated old mice (+4.48 ± 0.68 g) (Figure 5A). Interestingly, we observed that BIO101‐treated mice had the same weight curve evolution as adult mice (+4.84 ± 0.29 g). This weight gain improvement in BIO101‐treated old mice was however not associated with an increase of the gastrocnemius (Figure 5B), TA and quadriceps (Figure S4A, Supporting Information) mass compared with vehicle‐treated mice. At the completion of treatment, in toto muscle functionality was assessed by recording mice maximal speed (Vmax) on treadmill. As expected, we observed a significant decrease of running maximal speed in vehicle‐treated old mice (0.44 ± 0.02 m/s) compared with adult mice (0.55 ± 0.02 m/s), as a consequence of aging (−20.4%, P < 0.001). BIO101 treatment of old animals increased the maximal running speed (0.515 ± 0.02 m/s) compared with vehicle‐treated (+17%, P < 0.05) and significantly compensated the loss of running velocity (Figure 5C). Since the administration of BIO101 improved mice performance, we evaluated in situ TA muscle properties (Figure S4, Supporting Information). As expected, absolute maximal strength as well as specific maximal strength are decreased in vehicle‐treated old mice group compared with vehicle‐treated adult mice (−15.3% and −12.1%, respectively). These losses of muscle strength tend to be compensated by BIO101 treatment (+14.8% of maximal specific strength vs vehicle‐treated old mice (Figure S4B). Twitch duration time, half‐relaxation time and fatigue resistance are not modified with BIO101 treatment in old mice (Figures S4C–E). Analysis of protein extracts from gastrocnemius muscles from vehicle and BIO101‐treated old mice revealed a significant increase in the phosphorylation of AKT (P < 0.001) accompanied with an increase in p‐P70S6K (Figure 5D). Consistent with our in vitro results on C2C12, BIO101 treatment of old mice resulted in significant increase of MyoD protein expression and AMPK pathway activation (P < 0.05; Figure 5E).

In the present study, we have shown that BIO101 treatment stimulates anabolism in C2C12 differentiated myotubes with a rapid increase in phosphorylation of AKT and P70S6K, a major downstream target of mTOR, associated with 20% larger myotube diameters than in control conditions (Figure 1). These observations are consistent with our previous study in which we showed a dose‐dependent increase of protein synthesis in BIO101‐treated C2C12 differentiated myotubes,
19
 and with Gorelick–Feldman and colleagues' work,
18
 demonstrating a potent activation of AKT after 2 h of treatment with increasing doses of 20E, followed by an increase in protein synthesis. Indeed, MAS signalling, known to be activated by BIO101 (20E)
19
 also includes downstream AKT/mTOR activation.
21
 The AKT/PKB kinase controls both protein synthesis by activating the mTOR kinase and reduces protein degradation by repressing the transcription factor FoxO.
9



AMPK emerges as a master regulator of mitochondrial homeostasis, coupling mitochondrial dynamics to mitophagy which is involved in removal of dysfunctional mitochondria to preserve skeletal muscle function. We showed that BIO101 treatment increases the AMPKα phosphorylation and of its downstream target ACC (Figure 1C), suggesting that the drug candidate could modulate transcriptional programs involved in energy metabolism regulation. AMPK's role as an energy sensor is particularly critical in tissues displaying highly changeable energy turnover such as skeletal muscle.
22
 BIO101 effects on AMPK phosphorylation status warrants further studies to understand if the drug has the potential to reduce markers of skeletal muscle fragility and enhance muscle fibre oxidative capacity by stimulating mitochondrial biogenesis.
23



We observed that AKT/mTOR and AMPK pathways' activation in response to BIO101 treatment occurs in C2C12 myoblasts when differentiation starts (Figure 2). This is consistent with previous studies showing (i) that AKT activation may positively regulate MyoD expression and therefore myogenic differentiation
10
 and (ii) that PI3K/AKT activation can increase both MyoD and myogenin transcription. Rapamycin, the specific mTOR inhibitor, exerts an inhibitory effect on C2C12 myogenesis, implying that a mTOR pathway may be required for multiple processes during muscle differentiation.
24
 In addition, AMPK regulates myogenin expression through phosphorylation of histone deacetylase 5 (HDAC5).
25
 Altogether, these data and our current study strongly suggest that BIO101stimulates C2C12 in vitro myogenesis via AKT/mTOR and AMPK activation. These effects are associated with the progressive increase in myotube diameters demonstrating the anabolic effect of the 20E (Figure 3). Previously, a pro‐differentiating activity coupled with myotube hypertrophy of 20E was described by Parr and colleagues
26
 qualifying 20E as phytoestrogen proposed to act through a (nuclear) oestrogen receptor (ERβ). The mechanism of action that we proposed, in which the MAS receptor being activated by 20E
19
 would cooperate with an internal membrane‐bound ERx, is consistent with these data.


In vitro anabolic effects on murine myotubes described in Figures 1A,B and 2A are consistent with benefits observed in adult mice chronically treated with BIO101. Indeed, the increase in body weight and the hypertrophy of gastrocnemius and TA muscles following BIO101 treatment (Figure S2A,B) were in accordance with our in vitro data. Anabolic in vivo effects of BIO101 were also consistent with increased p‐AKT and p‐S6, the mTOR downstream targets, in gastrocnemius muscle of BIO101‐treated old mice. In addition, this increase was found in adult and especially in old mice treated orally with 50 mg/kg/day BIO101, suggesting possible anabolic effects both on normal and sarcopenic muscles.
27



In our work, we have demonstrated for the first time the increase of AMPKα phosphorylation in response to 20E in C2C12 cells and in mice muscles in vivo. These observations are consistent with the work of Buniam et al,
28
 who observed a significant increase of p‐AKT, p‐FOXO1, p‐AMPKα, and FGF21 expression in the liver of 20E‐treated rats.

Sarcopenia is a process of muscle breakdown that occurs in older people/animals. In humans, sarcopenia has three main characteristics: loss of muscle mass, loss of muscle quality (strength, mobility = dynapenia) and a change in body composition in favour of fat mass linked to lipid infiltration of appendicular muscles, which is exacerbated in obese subjects (sarcopenic obesity).
29
 Our experimental model (Figures 5 and S4) like other ones using animals fed a high‐fat diet
30
 recapitulates the three main features of sarcopenia. Indeed, in old animals subjected to a high‐lipid diet, we observed that (i) the muscle mass (Figures 5B and S4A) and (ii) fatigue resistance (Figure S4E) were significantly lower than in adult animals. Furthermore, the maximal absolute strength (P0, Figure S4B) tends to be decreased in old vs adult mice. Twitch duration is significantly increased in old mice compared with adult ones (Figure S4C). Finally, (iii) the model used also demonstrates that the high fat diet has a detrimental effect on the body composition of animals aged 26 months. The percentage of fat mass and lean mass is respectively significantly increased and decreased when old animals are subjected to this diet (data not shown).

As previously demonstrated by others,
31
 we observed that, under high fat diet, old animals gained significantly less body weight than adult ones (Figure 5A). In this context, we showed that BIO101 compensated for a weaker ability to gain body weight as a consequence of aging.

On the contrary to what was observed in young adults (Figure S2B), BIO101 chronic oral treatment did not translate in old animal into a significant increase in gastrocnemius muscle mass (Figure 5B). Similar observations were made on quadriceps and TA (Figure S4A). Interestingly, chronic oral administration of BIO101 was responsible for a significant increase of in toto performance when compared with old untreated animals (Figure 5C). Most importantly, we demonstrated that BIO101 treatment of old animals can compensate for the significant loss of running velocity as a consequence of aging. The pharmacological properties of BIO101 on muscle strength and mobility, as detected in animal models of sarcopenia, are quite relevant with regard to the evolution of its definition by clinicians (European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People, EWGSOP2), with a clear focus on muscle strength and physical performance as key characteristics of sarcopenia and less importance given to the loss of muscle mass.
32



In the present study, we demonstrated that BIO101 pro‐differentiating effects require MAS receptor activation. Indeed, A779, a MAS receptor specific antagonist, abolished the positive BIO101 effects on myogenic differentiation by both BIO101 and MAS receptor's endogenous ligand Ang‐(1‐7) (Figure 4). Interestingly, it was shown that Ang‐(1‐7) reduces AngII‐mediated atrophic effects in skeletal muscles by restoring muscle strength and mass through a mechanism dependent on MAS receptor and AKT activation.
14
, 
33
 In addition, Ang‐(1‐7) prevented the decrease of MHC levels and myotubes diameter, and the increase of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production induced by TGF‐β1 in C2C12 cells and TA muscle of mice.
34
 In this last study, the use of A779 inhibited the anti‐atrophic effect of Ang‐(1‐7).

In the last years, the involvement of RAAS in skeletal muscle homeostasis and function has been increasingly recognized. Changes in the activity or responsiveness of the RAAS occur with aging. These changes predispose the elderly people to various pathological responses including fibrosis, inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, heart failure, cancer, diabetic injury or muscle disorders.
35
 In addition, it has been demonstrated that Ang II is a major player in the appearance of sarcopenia.
36



The prevalence of sarcopenia is estimated to range from 14% in persons aged between 65 and 70, to 53% in those above 80 years of age. Around the world, the number of people aged ≥60 years old was estimated to be 600 million in the year 2000, a number that is expected to rise to 1.2 billion by 2025 and 2 billion by 2050. Thus, there is an urgent medical need to treat sarcopenia and to reduce the risk of mobility disability in elderlies. In this context, the identification and development of drug candidates that activate MAS receptor and the protective RAAS axis is very promising. BIO101, a pharmaceutical grade formulation of 20E, is orally administrable, with a good safety profile
37
 and has completed a phase 2 clinical study, with promising results on 400 m walk test in patients with sarcopenia at risk of mobility disability.
38
 MAS receptor activators could constitute in the future a new class of drug for the treatment of sarcopenia and other muscle wasting diseases.

Data S1. Supplementary methods:

Proliferation assays. C2C12 cells were seeded at a density of 400 cells/well in 96‐well plates. Cells were allowed to adhere for 24 h at 37°C and were then treated with various concentrations of BIO101 (see figure legends for details) for 48 h. Effects of BIO101 on cell proliferation were determined using BrdU incorporation assay. BrdU reagent was added for the last 6 h to proliferating C2C12 cells and the incorporation was determined using colorimetric BrdU Cell Proliferation ELISA Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) following supplier's instructions.


In situ muscle contractile performance.

Mice were anaesthetised using pentobarbital (60 mg.kg−1 intraperitoneally). The knee and foot were fixed with pins and clamps, and the distal tendon of the muscle was attached with a non‐elastic thread to a lever arm of a servomotor system (305B, Dual‐Mode Lever, Aurora Scientific). The sciatic nerve was proximally crushed and distally stimulated by a bipolar silver electrode using supramaximal square‐wave pulses of 0.1 ms duration (10 V). Absolute maximal force was measured as the force that was generated during isometric contractions in response to electrical stimulation (frequency of 75–150 Hz, train of stimulation of 500 ms). Absolute maximal force was determined at L0 (length at which maximal tension was obtained during the tetanus). Absolute maximal force was normalized to the muscle weight as an estimation of specific maximal force. Fatigue resistance was expressed as the time needed for the P0 of the muscle to drop to half its initial value and was measured with one continuous contraction (50 Hz for 45 s).
