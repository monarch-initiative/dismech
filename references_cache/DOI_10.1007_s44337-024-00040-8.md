Abstract
ITP is a disease caused by the immune system attacking platelets, and treatment options have been one of the hot 
topics of research. In recent years, significant progress has been made in understanding the complex pathogenesis of 
ITP , and the disease is becoming clearer, facilitating the development of new therapeutic approaches that target these 
pathways. The main therapeutic options for ITP have shifted from the traditional glucocorticoids, immunoglobulins, 
Rituximab, and TPO-RAs to an intensive study of novel agents, including splenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors, neonatal Fc 
receptor inhibitors, neuraminidase inhibitors, plasma cell-targeted therapy, and classical Complement Pathway Inhibi-
tion. This review aims to summarize the pathogenesis of ITP and to review the available therapeutic options. We hope 
that this review will deepen clinicians’ understanding of advances in ITP pathophysiology and novel drugs to optimize 
the clinical management of ITP .
Keywords Immune thrombocytopenia · Pathogenesis · Treatment
1 Introduction
Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is a hematological disorder caused by immune system dysfunction, leading to low 
platelet levels and bleeding in the skin and mucous membranes [1 ]. Patients may present with asymptomatic, or mild 
skin and mucous membrane bleeding; some patients with severe bleeding will be life-threatening. According to the latest 
consensus of the International Task Force on Thrombocytopenia, the incidence of ITP in children is 1.9–6.4/100,000, and 
the incidence of ITP in adults is 3.3–3.9/100,000 [2 –4]. The pathogenesis of ITP is intricate, and recent years have seen 
substantial advancements in uncovering different facets of ITP pathogenesis, there are ongoing investigations into new 
therapies that target these pathways.
Glucocorticoids as first-line therapy, can achieve short-term efficacy in most patients, but many adverse effects 
limit the long-term use of glucocorticoids. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is commonly used to treat ITP with 
a marked tendency to bleed, but its efficacy is short-lived [5 ]. CD20 monoclonal antibodies and thrombopoietin 
receptor agonists (TPO-RAs) are second-line therapeutic agents for ITP with favorable early remission rates, but some 
patients show drug dependence or poor efficacy [6 , 7]. With the in-depth study of the pathophysiology of ITP , new

2  Pathogenesis
Initially, studies on the pathogenesis of ITP focused on humoral immunity, in which IgG-based platelet antibodies 
produced by B lymphocytes bind to platelets, leading to phagocytosis of platelets by the monocyte-macrophage 
system, resulting in increased platelet destruction and shortened lifespan, which is considered to be the most clas-
sical pathogenesis of ITP . However, as the understanding of the pathogenesis of ITP has gradually increased, it has 
been found that humoral immunity can no longer fully explain the pathogenesis of ITP . More and more studies have 
shown that not only humoral immunity abnormalities but also cellular immunity abnormalities exist in the patho -
genesis of ITP . In addition, with the rapid development in the field of pathophysiology, it is gradually revealed that 
apoptosis and epigenetics are also important parts of the pathogenesis of ITP .
2.1  Humoral immunity
More IgG-type autoantibodies are present in ITP patients, while IgM-type and IgA-type autoantibodies are less common 
[8, 9]. Among IgG-type autoantibodies, IgG1 and IgG3 have a higher affinity, and the binding sites are mainly glycoprotein 
IIb/IIIa and glycoprotein Ib/IX-V on the surface of platelets or megakaryocytes. After binding to specific glycoproteins

on the platelet membrane, autoantibodies are cleared by macrophages through Fcγ receptor-mediated phagocytosis 
as they pass through the spleen and liver, leading to increased platelet destruction, shortened lifespan, and reduced 
peripheral platelet counts [10]. In addition to this observation, recent reports of antibody-induced platelet desialylation 
and apoptosis suggest the pathophysiology of ITP . Peng J et al. [11] found a significantly higher proportion of ITP patients 
with anti-GPIb/IX antibodies, and the mechanism of platelet destruction in this group of patients may be different from 
that of anti-GPIIb/IIIa antibodies in a FcγR-dependent manner. Junne Li et al. [12] demonstrated in animal experiments 
that anti-GPIb/IX antibodies induced the platelet membrane glycoproteins to undergo desaccharification under the 
effect of sialidase, thus exposing N-acetylglucosamine, which is recognized by hepatocyte desialylated glycoprotein 
receptors after desaccharification. The deglycosylation of platelet membrane glycoproteins under the action of sialidase 
exposes N-acetylglucosamine, which undergoes desialylation after deglycosylation and is recognized by the hepatocyte 
desialylated glycoprotein receptor, which in turn is cleared by Kupffer’s cells and hepatic cells. Desialylation of platelets 
may be another pathogenetic mechanism of ITP .
In patients with ITP , autoantibodies against platelet cytoplasmic proteins were detected in addition to the classic 
autoantibodies against platelet membrane antigens. This suggests that antigen-presenting cells (APC) are involved in 
the degradation of platelet proteins and present the degradation products to T lymphocytes [13]. In addition, Cines, D.B 
et al. proposed other mechanisms for ITP antibody production, including antigenic cross-reactivity (mimicry), somatic 
mutations, and elimination of defects in autoreactive B-cell clones [14]. In most autoimmune diseases, B lymphocytes 
are considered to be the pathogenic cell population because of their ability to secrete autoantibodies, and a subpopula-
tion of B lymphocytes with a suppressive regulatory role is called regulatory B cells (Breg). Breg has a CD19 + CD24high-
CD38high immunophenotype, and this cell population is activated by CD40 stimulation through the STAT3 signaling 
pathway, which results in the release of IL-10, CD80, and CD86, and inhibits the induced differentiation of Th1 cells [15]. 
Meanwhile, the pathophysiology of ITP is largely dependent on the increased level of B-cell activating factor (BAFF) 
[16, 17], which is essential for the survival, maturation, and differentiation of B cells. BAFF promotes the survival, pro -
liferation, and differentiation of B lymphocytes by binding to three ligands, namely B-cell activating factor receptor, 
transmembrane activated calmodulin ligand, and B-cell maturation antigen, and the overexpression of BAFF can cause 
severe autoimmune diseases [18]. Excessive BAFF expression has been linked to serious autoimmune disorders [19]. 
Pertaining research has demonstrated that in individuals with active ITP , there exists a positive correlation between 
CD62P on platelets and BAFF levels [20]. Meanwhile, the serum level of BAFF is high in ITP patients, and glucocorticoids 
can effectively reduce BAFF [21].
Taken together, these studies suggest the presence of pathogenic B cells and abnormal levels of cytokines in ITP 
patients, leading to the production of platelet antibodies. These antibodies, which bind via platelets and megakaryocytes, 
trigger platelet destruction in the spleen and liver as well as defects in megakaryopoiesis.
2.2  Cellular immunity
2.2.1  Th1/Th2
The primary subset of CD4 + T cells are called helper cells (Th), and these cells can further divide into subpopulations with 
distinct roles, including Th1, Th2, Th17, and Th22. Th1 cells play a major role in cellular immunity and secrete cytokines 
like TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-2 that promote cell-mediated immune responses and boost the growth and activation of natural 
killer cells, macrophages, and cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Th2 cells primarily contribute to humoral immunity and gen-
erate cytokines that primarily promote B cell proliferation and differentiation as well as the generation of antibodies. 
These cytokines include IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, and IL-10 [22, 23]. There is an imbalance in the Th1/Th2 ratio in ITP patients, with 
a predominance of Th1 cells [24], and some studies have reported that the degree of elevated Th1/Th2 ratio is positively 
correlated with the severity of the disease, and negatively correlated with the platelet count in peripheral blood [25].
Meanwhile, T-bet is a major regulator of Th1 cell differentiation, increasing the production of IFN-γ and decreasing the 
production of Th2 cells and Th17 cells. GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA-3) is a major regulator of Th2 cells and promotes 
differentiation of naïve Th cells into Th2 cells. T-bet is present only in Th1 cells, while GATA-3 is expressed during Th2 cell 
production. Hammam AA et al. found that high expression of T-bet and low expression of GATA-3 in ITP patients indi -
cated the presence of Th1/Th2 polarization in ITP patients, which may be related to the regulation of T-bet and GATA-3 
[26]. Chemokines and their receptors are involved in cellular migration and proliferation, molecule adhesion, T cell dif-
ferentiation, and cytokine production. For example, CXCR3 is expressed uniquely on Th1 and CCR3 is expressed mainly 
on Th2 cells. Some studies have demonstrated abnormal expression of CCL5, CXCL11, CCL11, CCR5, CXCR3, CCR3, and

2.2.2  Th17/Treg
Th17 cells are also a subpopulation of CD4  + helper T cells and are considered pro-inflammatory T cells as they mainly 
produce pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-21, and IL-22. Among them, IL-17 is the characteristic cytokine 
of Th17 cells [29]. In patients with ITP , a study found a significant positive correlation between plasma IL-17 levels and 
Th17 cell counts and a negative correlation with platelet counts [30]. These findings suggest that IL-17 plays a key role 
in both the pathophysiology of ITP and in promoting the Th17 immune response.
Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are CD4 + T lymphocytes with unique immunosuppressive functions. CD4 + CD25 + Treg is 
the most dominant group in the Treg cell family and mainly produces the cytokines TGF-β and IL-10. TGF-β inhibits the 
synthesis and secretion of IL-6 through the STAT1 pathway, thereby inhibiting the differentiation of Th1 and Th17 cells, 
and thus suppressing immune platelet destruction [31]. At the same time, it has been found that the reduced level of Treg 
cells breaks the immune homeostasis of the body, the secretion of inhibitory cytokines is reduced, the immunosuppres-
sive capacity of the body is weakened, T cells are abnormally activated, and the inhibitory capacity of B lymphocytes to 
proliferate and produce antibodies is subsequently weakened, and antibody production is increased, which ultimately 
leads to the pathophysiology of ITP [32]. TNF-α is secreted by monocytes and macrophages as a pro-inflammatory factor 
that may disrupt Treg cell function. One study found [33] that ITP patients had higher concentrations of TNF-α in their 
serum, and blocking TNF-α in vitro induced stronger Treg expansion, suggesting that TNF-α may be responsible for the 
imbalance of Treg cells in ITP patients.
All this evidence has confirmed the objective presence of Th17/Treg imbalance in ITP patients, which is part of the 
pathophysiology of ITP and may be related to the abnormal expression of cytokines such as IL-17, TNF-α, TGF-β, and 
others.
2.2.3  Th22
Th22 cells are a newly discovered subpopulation of CD4 + T cells, which mainly produce cytokines such as IL-22, IL-13, 
and TNF-α, and do not secrete γ-interferon, with IL-22 being the most important characteristic factor. Th1, Th17, and 
Th22 cell expression levels were all markedly elevated in peripheral blood from ITP patients, and there was a positive 
correlation between Th22 cell expression and Th1 and Th17 cell expression [34, 35].
2.2.4  Th9
Th9 cells are a newly discovered subpopulation of Th cells that preferentially secrete the cytokines IL-9, TNF-a, TGF-β, and 
IL-4 to promote the differentiation, survival, and proliferation of Th9 cells [36]. The production of IL-9 is solely dependent 
on the expression of the transcription factor PU.1, which is why the transcription factor PU.1 is often referred to as the 
master regulator of Th9 cells [37]. It has been found that Th9 and IL-9 are significantly reduced in patients with active 
ITP compared to healthy subjects and that there is a positive correlation between Th9 and Th17 cells, and between IL-9 
and IL-17 levels.
2.2.5  Tfh
Follicular helper T cell(Tfh) cells are one of the unique subpopulations of CD4 + T cells, which play an important role in 
assisting the proliferation and differentiation process of B cells in the germinal centers, and are the main cells assisting 
the production of antibodies by B cells in the germinal centers [38]. Tfh cells have their unique chemokine receptors, 
i.e., high expression of CXCR5 and low expression of CCR7, and secrete different co-stimulatory molecules (CD40L) 
and inducible co-stimulatory factor (ICOS), as well as inhibiting PD-1 and producing large amounts of IL-21. The most 
significant cytokine for Tfh cells among them is IL-21 [39]. Compared to the normal group, ITP patients were able to 
secrete significantly more IL-21, and after receiving medication, the concentration of IL-21 decreased significantly. These

2.2.6  CD8 + T lymphocytes
CD8 + T lymphocytes mainly include CD8 + CD28 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), CD8 + CD28-suppressor T lymphocytes 
(Ts cell), and CD8 + Tregs. They can lead to ITP by mediating autologous platelet lysis, inhibiting megakaryocyte apoptosis, 
and inducing platelet deacetylation and platelet clearance in the livers of ITP patients [41]. CTLs mainly secrete cytotoxic 
proteins, such as perforin and granzyme B, which not only directly lyses platelets and induce platelet apoptosis, but also 
further reduce platelet production by increasing megakaryocyte apoptosis. Ts cells play an inhibitory role in the prolif-
eration of T cells, attenuating the cytotoxic function of T lymphocytes by upregulating leukocyte immunoglobulins like 
receptor subfamily B4 (LILRB4) and receptor B2 (LILRB2) expression to induce tolerogenic antigen-presenting cells and 
thereby inhibit immune activation, Ts cells can also reduce T-cell activation by producing suppressor factor IL-10 and 
cell contact. Li H et al. [42]. Found that the percentage of Ts cells was decreased in patients with active ITP compared 
to controls. Functional analysis showed that the immunosuppressive function of Ts cells was impaired in patients with 
ITP . Ma L et al. [43]. Found that CD8( +) T cells limited the severity of thrombocytopenia by establishing a mouse model, 
and thrombocytopenia was improved after infusion of CD8( +) T cells. In vitro co-culture studies showed that CD8( +) 
Tregs inhibited proliferation, platelet-associated immunoglobulin G production, CTL cytotoxicity, platelet apoptosis, and 
clearance of both CD4( +) T cells and CD19( +) T cells. In addition, they found increased production of anti-inflammatory 
interleukin-10 in co-culture studies and in vivo after steroid treatment. These data further elucidate the involvement of 
CD8 + T lymphocytes in ITP and suggest the therapeutic potential of CD8( +) T lymphocytes in immune thrombocytopenia.
All this evidence has confirmed that T-cell immunity is crucial for ITP . Indeed, the cellular mechanism behind the 
pathophysiology of ITP consists of aberrant T-cell subsets, such as lower Tregs and an imbalance of Th17, Th2, and Th1 
and cytotoxic CD8 + T cells. This may be related to abnormal cytokine secretion, abnormal expression of chemokines, 
and cytotoxic effects.
2.3  Megakaryocyte
Megakaryocytes are strongly affected in ITP , as evidenced by impaired platelet development and release, which may 
be related to TPO deficiency [44]. TPO is a megakaryocyte growth factor, and Grozovsky R et al. found that it can be 
produced by senescent desialylated platelets recognized by the liver as phagocytosed through the JAK/STAT3 signaling 
pathway [45]. This deficiency can be reversed with TPO-RA [46]. However, it has been shown that megakaryocytes are 
directly cleared by neutrophils and macrophages despite normal or mildly elevated plasma TPO levels [47]. Approximately 
two-thirds of ITP patients have plasma autoantibodies that significantly inhibit the maturation of MKs from TPO-treated 
CD34 + hematopoietic progenitor cells [48]. Different autoantibodies may have different affinities for MKs, thereby induc-
ing different morphological and functional changes in platelets and even apoptosis [49].
2.4  Apoptosis
Apoptosis is the most common form of programmed cell death (PCD) in physiology. Both megakaryocytes and platelets 
contain functional pathways for PCD, and genetic or induced (e.g., by autoantibodies) dysregulation of these PCD path-
ways may lead to thrombocytopenia or inefficient platelet production in ITP [51]. Houwerzijl EJ et al. [52]. Have identified 
signs of pathologically enhanced or abnormal cell death in platelets and megakaryocytes in patients with ITP . The intrinsic 
(mitochondrial) pathway of apoptosis is tightly regulated by members of the Bcl-2 family of proteins. Bak and Bax are key 
mediators in the apoptotic process [53], and the activities of Bak and Bax are also tightly controlled by members of the 
anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family.Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, and Mcl-1 are expressed in megakaryocytes [54]. Bcl-xL and Mcl-1 synergistically 
regulate megakaryocyte survival, and Bcl-xL inhibits the pro-apoptotic activity of Bak to maintain platelet survival [55, 
56]. Deng G et al. [57]. Examined the expression of apoptotic markers in the peripheral blood of patients with chronic ITP , 
and found a significantly higher level of Bak and Bax and a significantly lower level of Bcl-xL in platelets of these patients 
compared to healthy controls. These evidences suggest that abnormal apoptosis in megakaryocytes and platelets may 
contribute to the development of ITP .

2.5  DNA methylation
In recent years research on epigenetics in human diseases has been ongoing and several studies have revealed several 
major epigenetic mechanisms and their role in the etiology of ITP , including DNA methylation [22]. DNA methylation 
refers to the process of chemical modification of specific bases on a DNA sequence to acquire a methyl group through 
covalent bonding in the presence of DNA methylation transferases (DNMTs). This process can cause changes in chro -
matin structure, DNA conformation, and stability, thereby controlling gene expression [58]. Several studies on ITP have 
identified abnormal DNA methylation [59–62]. Compared with normal controls, the expression levels of NOTCH1 and its 
downstream Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-10, and GATA3) were significantly lower, and the expression levels of Th1 cytokines 
(IFN-γ, IL-12, and TNF-α) were significantly higher in the ITP group [59]. Tao J et al. [60]. Found that the expression of 
DNMT3A and DNMT3B mRNAs in ITP patients was significantly lower than in healthy controls, and the concentration of 
SAH, the substrate, and product of methylation reaction, was significantly higher than that of healthy controls. CD70, 
a member of the TNF family, was also detected as a methylation-sensitive immune gene, and overexpression of CD70, 
DNMTs, and MBD2 was detected in CD4 T cells of ITP patients with a lower methylation index of the CD70 promoter than 
that of healthy controls [61, 62]. This can lead to prolonged lifespan of T- and B-lymphocytes, increased IFN-γ secretion, 
decreased platelet counts, and ultimately accelerated ITP progression.
3  Therapeutic advances
3.1  Syk inhibitors
We mentioned earlier that phagocytosis mediated by autoantibody binding to Fcγ receptors is an important part of the 
pathogenesis of ITP . The splenic tyrosine kinase (Syk) pathway not only prevents platelet destruction by inhibiting FcγR 
signaling in macrophages but also reduces plasma cell formation and autoantibody production by inhibiting BCR sign-
aling [63]. Fostamatinib, an orally bioavailable small molecule SYK inhibitor, was approved by the FDA in April 2018 for 
the treatment of ITP that has been refractory to other drug therapy ITP that is ineffective [64]. According to two phase III 
clinical studies [65], FIT1(NCT02076399) and FIT2(NCT0207641)., overall responses occurred in 43% of patients on Fostam-
atinib vs. 14% on placebo, table responses occurred in 18% of patients on Fostamatinib vs. 2% on placebo. First, 100 mg 
twice daily was the recommended dose. After a month, if platelet counts did not rise to at least 50 ×  109/L, the dose was 
increased to 150 mg twice daily. Fostamatinib was well tolerated in patients with ITP and the most common adverse 
events included asthma, high blood pressure, and increased liver enzymes were the most frequent side effects [66].
Fostamatinib is a novel ITP therapeutic option targeting an important mechanism in the pathogenesis of ITP , which 
has achieved favorable efficacy and approved indications for ITP . Other SYK inhibitors being evaluated in ITP are HMPL-
523 (NCT03951623), and SKI-O-703 (NCT04056195). In addition, SYK inhibitors are being evaluated for other indications 
such as Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (NCT03742258), Systemic lupus erythematosus (NCT06238531), post-transplant 
immune-mediated hemocytopenia (NCT05509582), Chronic Active Antibody Mediated Rejection (NCT03991780).
3.2  BTK inhibitors
In the humoral immunity section, we briefly described the role of autoantibody production by B lymphocytes and BAFF 
overexpression in the pathogenesis of ITP . Similar to Syk, Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) is a key kinase in the BCR signaling 
cascade and BAFF-R signaling [67]. BTK inhibitors not only block platelet phagocytosis in the spleen and liver but also by 
inhibiting B-cell activation and reducing autoantibody production. Rilzabrutinib is an oral reversible covalent inhibitor 
of Bruton’s tyrosine kinase. In a phase 1–2 clinical trial, 24 out of 60 patients (40%) met the primary endpoint of platelet 
response [69]. 18 out of 45 patients (40%) who started Rilzabrutinib therapy at the highest dose (800 mg) achieved the 
primary endpoint of platelet response. The most common treatment-related adverse events of any grade were diarrhea 
(32%), nausea (30%), and fatigue (10%). Rilzabrutinib 400 mg BID is being evaluated in the ongoing LUNA 3 multicenter, 
double-blind, placebo-controlled phase III study [70].
Based on the important role of the pathogenic process intervened by BTK inhibitors, BTK inhibitors have shown 
very good advantages in the treatment of B-cell malignancies and some B-cell immune disorders, and the related 
drug candidates are also being used in the treatment of other autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis

3.3  FcRn inhibitors
As mentioned earlier, high levels of IgG-type autoantibodies are present in patients with ITP . Neonatal Fc receptor 
(FcRn) belongs to the MHC class I family of molecules, which recovers pathologic or normal IgG, prolongs the half-
life, and promotes the distribution of IgG throughout tissues [73]. At the same time, it leads to the production of 
anti-platelet antibodies through the presentation of IgG antigens to the immune cells and plays an immune response 
play a role [74]. Thus, blocking FcRn decreases pathologic IgG and decreases platelet clearance in ITP . Two drugs are 
already in clinical studies, Rozanolixizumab, and Efgartigimod. In a recent open-label phase 2 trial (NCT02718716) [75], 
66 adult ITP patients were treated across multiple centers. The results indicated that within the single subcutaneous 
injection group (15 mg/kg or 20 mg/kg), 66.7% and 54.5% of patients had platelet counts ≥ 50 ×  109/L, respectively, 
and 50% of patients had platelet counts ≥ 50 ×  109/L within the first week of infusion. Additionally, only 15 mild to 
moderate treatment-related adverse events were reported for Rozanolixizumab, indicating that it was well tolerated. 
In a phase II clinical trial in 2020 [76], 38 patients with ITP were enrolled and randomized 1:1:1 into 3 groups receiv -
ing placebo, or Efgartigimod at a dose of 5 mg/kg or 10 mg/kg. The Efgartigimod treatment group induced a rapid 
reduction in total IgG levels compared with the placebo group. Platelet counts were ≥ 50 ×  109/L at any given time 
in 7 patients (53.8%) in the Efgartigimod treatment group and 6 patients (50.0%) in the placebo group. 6 patients 
(46.2%) in the Efgartigimod 5 mg/kg group, 5 patients (38.5%) in the Efgartigimod 10 mg/kg group, and one patient 
(8.3%) in the placebo group had platelet counts ≥ 100 ×  109/L at any given time.
Rozanolixizumab (NCT04224688) and Efgartigimod (NCT04225156) are currently being evaluated in Phase 3 studies 
in adult study participants with ITP . In addition, safety and efficacy evaluations of Efgartigimod PH20 subcutaneous 
injection for the treatment of ITP (NCT04687072) are underway.
3.4  Neuraminidase inhibitors
Unlike classical Fc-FcγR-mediated phagocytosis, excessive desialylation of platelets may be another pathogenesis of 
ITP [12]. Oseltamivir phosphate, an antiviral neuraminidase inhibitor, induces glycoprotein desialylation, and it has 
been demonstrated that this drug increases platelet counts in patients with refractory ITP when treating influenza. 
Thus Oseltamivir may be the key to ITP treatment, which has prompted studies of Oseltamivir for ITP . Revilla N et al. 
[77] applied Oseltamivir (75 mg bid for at least 5 days) in combination with TTG in 10 patients with refractory ITP . 
Bid for at least 5 days) in combination with TPO-RAs, and 66.7% of patients showed sustained platelet response. The 
latest two phase I/II clinical trials are currently being conducted in China and Canada [78], led by Prof. Hou Ming’s 
team, in 96 patients over 18 years of age with primary ITP , randomly assigned to the dexamethasone combined with 
Oseltamivir group (dexamethasone 40 mg/d for 4d + Oseltamivir 75 mg bid for 10d) and the dexamethasone alone 
group (40 mg/d for 4d) and the results showed that the combination therapy group had a faster initial response 
rate (86%: 66%, p  < 0.05) and a longer sustained remission rate (53%:30%, p  < 0.05). Colunga-Pedraza PR et al. [79]. 
Included eligible ITP patients given Oseltamivir orally 75 mg twice daily for 5 days. All patients responded at some 
time point (ORR = 100%, 6 were PR and 1 reached CR). Six months after taking oseltamivir, three patients (42.9%) 
maintained their response and one patient reached [CR] (14.3%). They observed an initial increase in platelet count, 
but this response was not sustained. A pilot, randomized, double-blind study of oseltamivir versus placebo in the 
treatment of ITP (NCT03520049) is underway, but results have not been reported.
3.5  Plasma cell targeted therapy

B-cell therapy alone (e.g., Rituximab). These therapies affect only B-lymphocytes or short-acting plasma cells, but not

cell-targeted therapy. Li G et al. [80] found that in vitro Bortezomib induced apoptosis of LLPCs in a concentration-
dependent manner and significantly reduced the proteasome activity of LLPCs. Similarly, in a mouse model of ITP , 
bortezomib eliminated LLPCs and alleviated thrombocytopenia in the model mice. There are several case reports of suc-
cessful treatment of ITP [81, 82]. In addition, a single-center clinical trial(NCT03013114) of Bortezomib for the treatment 
of ITP with high antiplatelet antibody levels and a phase 2 study(NCT05599880) evaluating the efficacy and safety of 
Bortezomib in patients with relapsed/refractory ITP have not yet been completed and reported. Daratumumab, a drug 
represented by the CD38 monoclonal antibody, is another drug used to clear clonal plasma cells in the treatment of ITP . 
Crickx E et al. [83] reported that among five patients with refractory ITP using Daratumumab (off-label use), two patients 
induced a complete remission at week 4, and one patient induced a delayed response at week 12.Strü ßmann T et al. [84] 
reported recovery of platelet counts and sustained remission for 20 months in patients after 6 weeks of Daratumumab. 
It is being evaluated in clinical trials (NCT05562882; NCT04703621) for the treatment of ITP , and future studies on it. Its 
safety and efficacy in ITP is anticipated [72].
3.6  Complement inhibitors
Similarly, the pathophysiology of ITP does not ignore the complement pathway. Antibody binding to platelet glyco -
proteins initiates the complement pathway, which in turn causes complement-dependent cytotoxicity, which destroys 
platelets. Complement activation simultaneously encourages splenic macrophages to phagocytose platelets. Conse -
quently, blocking the classical complement pathway has emerged as a novel therapeutic approach for ITP [13]. Sutim-
limab opens the possibility that ITP inhibits the classical complement pathway. In a phase 1, open-label, single-arm, 
multicenter, 2-part study (NCT03275454) [85], mean platelet counts in 12 patients rose to 54 ×  109/L after the first dose 
of Sutimlimab and remained ≥ 50 ×  109/L throughout Part A. 5 of these patients (42%) achieved a durable platelet count 
response (follow-up ≥ 50 ×  109/L) and 4 patients achieved a complete response (≥ 100 ×  109/L). 7 of the 12 patients entered 
Part B after receiving a washout period. Patients in this portion who were re-treated with Sutimlimab experienced a 
rapid increase in mean platelet counts to the level of prior response to treatment, with mean platelet counts typically 
sustained above 50 ×  109/L and usually sustained above 100  ×  109/L. This study demonstrated that Sutimlimab rapidly 
and durably increased platelet counts in patients with chronic ITP . No other ongoing clinical studies of Sutimlimab in 
the treatment of ITP have been reported.
4  Conclusion
The recurrent, heterogeneous, and unstable nature of ITP has prompted the emergence of numerous new drugs. Based 
on accumulated novel mechanisms of action, clinical data, and drug safety, Sky, BTK, FcRn, complement inhibitors, and 
plasma cell-targeted therapies represent promising treatment modalities for patients with refractory ITP . These new 
therapies offer opportunities for patients with ITP who have not responded to first- or second-line treatments, and cli-
nicians should respond to them in the context of the patient’s prior treatment, extent of disease, financial situation, or 
willingness to participate in trials. However, due to the heterogeneity between patients, we are not sure which part of 
the patient’s immunity is abnormal. Therefore personalized treatment is not yet achievable, but we still hope to improve 
the remission rate of ITP with these new therapies. As monotherapy makes it difficult to maintain long-term remission 
in ITP , combination or sequential therapy may be a future focus, utilizing a combination of different agents to prolong 
remission and improve remission rates in refractory ITP .
There are some limitations to this paper. We have not provided a comprehensive discussion of the pathogenesis, 
diagnosis and treatment of ITP . Also, as a narrative review, we have not assessed the quality of the studies or literature 
mentioned.
