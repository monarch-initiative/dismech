ABSTRACT
Although the combined presence of ataxia and pyramidal features has a long differential, the
presence of a true spastic-ataxia as the predominant clinical syndrome has a rather limited differ-
ential diagnosis. Autosomal recessive ataxia of Charlevoix-Saguenay, late-onset Friedreich
ataxia, and hereditary spastic paraplegia type 7 are examples of genetic diseases with such a
prominent spastic-ataxic syndrome as the clinical hallmark. We review the various causes of
spastic-ataxic syndromes with a focus on the genetic disorders, and provide a clinical framework,
based on age at onset, mode of inheritance, and additional clinical features and neuroimaging
signs, that could serve the diagnostic workup.

The list of diseases that can lead to the combined presence of cerebellar ataxia and spasticity is
quite extensive and includes both acquired and genetic disorders. Regarding the latter, many
clinicians will struggle to decide whether the patient has a hereditary ataxia with spasticity or
hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) with cerebellar features. Both groups of disorders are genet-
ically and clinically heterogeneous, but additional symptoms and signs can point to a certain
diagnosis. Despite the presence of practical guidelines for the molecular diagnosis, many clini-
cians still end up requesting a multitude of investigations.
1
Here, we review the spastic-ataxias and propose an approach for diagnostic workup. Our
main message is that recognizing a spastic-ataxia as the core clinical phenotype actually leads to
a more restricted differential diagnosis, and subsequently to a more focused diagnostic workup.
We will discuss 1) the acquired spastic-ataxias, 2) the genetic spastic-ataxias, and 3) spastic
paraplegias with signs of ataxia and ataxias with some pyramidal features. From a population
perspective, genetic spastic-ataxias are rare. But seen from the perspective of a patient with a
spastic-ataxia, common genotypes as well as rare forms can be discerned, the latter typically
being represented by one or a few affected families worldwide. Considering such relative prev-
alences should be part of the diagnostic approach of these diseases.
ACQUIRED SPASTIC-ATAXIAS Before considering the possibility of a genetic cause and jump to molecular
tests, possible acquired causes of spastic-ataxia should be ruled out, especially because some are treatable (table 1). A
sporadic occurrence, a late onset of disease, and a rather (sub)acute onset with a rapidly progressive disease course
could be the clinical leads here, as well as additional neurologic and systemic features or imaging abnormalities. A
discussion of the individual causes as listed in table 1 falls outside the scope of this review.

Autosomal recessive ataxia of Charlevoix-Saguenay
(MIM #270550). ARSACS is a distinct form of early-
onset genetic spastic-ataxia, first described in the
Charlevoix-Saguenay region of Northeastern Que´bec in
Canada in 1978.
2 Since the discovery of theSACS gene
(encoding the protein sacsin) in 2000, a worldwide oc-
currence of ARSACS has been reported.
3 The classic
phenotype is a slowly progressive ataxia that starts in
early childhood (mostly before age 13), with severe
lower limb spasticity and sensorimotor axonal neuropa-
thy.
4 Brain MRI often shows vermian cerebellar atro-
phy as well as linear hypointensities in the pons and
middle cerebellar peduncles on T2 sequences.
5,6 Cervi-
cal spinal cord atrophy may be observed as well, but
does not seem a consistent feature.
6,7 Additional, but
variable features are retinopathy (increased demarcation
of the retinal nerve fibers embedding parts of the vessels
near the disc), dysphagia, urinary urgency, and subtle
dystonic features.
4 Mitral valve prolapse and mental im-
pairment have only occasionally been reported since the
original publication, and are perhaps unrelated to the
SACS mutation itself.
2,7
In patients with an early-onset (that is, below the
age of 25 years) cerebellar ataxia, in whom Friedreich
ataxia (FRDA) was excluded through mutation anal-
ysis, the frequency of SACS mutations has been re-
ported to be as high as 37%.
4 In a recent study from
Belgium, the mutation frequency in patients with the
typical ARSACS triad (cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal
tract signs, and peripheral neuropathy) was slightly
lower (28.6%), possibly due to broader inclusion cri-
teria used (e.g., no restrictions for age at onset).
7
Atypical phenotypes (e.g., presenting with an iso-
lated polyneuropathy or absence of polyneuropathy)
and late-onset forms have been identified in the Bel-
gian cohort.
7
Late-onset Friedreich ataxia (MIM #229300). FRDA
is the most common form of hereditary ataxia in Cauca-
sian populations, with a prevalence of 1/29,000 in the
Western world.
8 The classic phenotype of this recessive
disease consists of progressive gait and limb ataxia, dys-
arthria, loss of vibration sense, and ocular fixation insta-
bility with an onset before age 25. Associated features
are cardiomyopathy in the majority of patients, axonal
sensory neuropathy, and sometimes sensorineural deaf-
ness, optic atrophy, scoliosis, and diabetes. MRI may
demonstrate cervical cord atrophy, while cerebellar at-
rophy is often lacking. The most common disease-
causing molecular mechanism is a GAA trinucleotide
repeat expansion in intron 1 of the FXN gene that en-
codes frataxin.
Since the discovery of this gene, atypical and late-
onset phenotypes not fulfilling the Harding or Quebec
Cooperative Study criteria have been found in up to
25% of patients.
9 In late-onset Friedreich ataxia
(LOFA), defined as an onset after age 25 years and usu-
ally caused by smaller GAA repeat expansions, patients
more often have lower limb spasticity and retained or
even brisk reflexes.
10 There are some reports of a pre-
dominantly spastic paraparetic or tetraparetic pheno-
type in LOFA.
11–13 Spastic-ataxia can be the presenting
symptom in late-onset cases, soFXN mutation analysis
should be considered when the onset is in (late) adult-
hood.
14,15 Compared to the classic FRDA, patients with
LOFA seem to have a milder and more slowly evolving
disease with fewer skeletal abnormalities (scoliosis) and
sphincter disturbances, and less frequent cardiomyopa-
thy. Cerebellar atrophy, although rarely seen in classic
FRDA, was found in 5 out of 9 patients with the late-
onset variant.
10
Hereditary spastic paraplegia type 7 (MIM #607259).
Spastic paraplegia type 7 (SPG7), encoding para-
plegin, accounts for 5% to 12% of autosomal reces-
sive HSPs; its prevalence is estimated at 2– 6/
100,000.
16 In sporadic cases of HSP with adult onset,
mutations in SPG7 have been found in up to 7%. 17
SPG7 phenotypes span the gamut between a
rather pure spastic paraplegia (with or without im-
paired vibration sense and sphincter disturbances)
and a predominantly spastic-ataxic phenotype with
mild dysarthria, oculomotor signs including nystag-
mus, and cerebellar atrophy on brain MRI. Cerebel-
lar signs may even be or become more pronounced
than the spastic paraplegia. Other features such as
dysphagia, subtle cognitive impairment, pale optic
discs, ptosis, strabismus, supranuclear oculomotor
palsy, decreased hearing, scoliosis, sensorimotor neu-
ropathy, and amyotrophy may be observed.
16,18 On-
set of the disease is predominantly in adulthood
(third or fourth decade), but a wide range between
age 10 and 77 has been described.
18,19

Adult-onset Alexander disease (MIM #203450). Alex-
ander disease, caused by mutations in the GFAP
gene, can be inherited in an autosomal dominant
manner, but often presents as a sporadic disease with
de novo mutations in the majority. The classic form,
which predominantly affects infants and children, is
characterized by progressive psychomotor retarda-
tion, megalencephaly, seizures, and severe white mat-
ter abnormalities on brain MRI, and usually results
in death within the first decade.
In about 25%, however, the disease starts in ado-
lescence or adulthood (range 12–71 years) with a
slowly progressive neurologic syndrome consisting of
pyramidal signs with cerebellar ataxia, often with
lower brainstem signs like dysarthria, dysphonia, and
dysphagia.
20,21 Additional autonomic dysfunctions
like urinary disturbances, ocular abnormalities, and
sleep disorders have been reported with varying fre-
quencies.
20 –22 When present, palatal tremor is a
highly suggestive sign. 21 Neuroimaging shows
marked atrophy and signal intensity changes in the
medulla oblongata and upper spinal cord with or
without cerebellar atrophy and periventricular white
matter changes.
23 Adult-onset Alexander disease pre-
senting with a spastic-ataxic syndrome has recently
been reported and should thus be considered in spo-
radic adult-onset spastic-ataxia.
22,24
Spinocerebellar ataxia type 3 (SCA3)/Machado Joseph
disease (MIM #109150). The estimated prevalence of
autosomal dominant cerebellar ataxias (ADCAs) var-
ies from 0.3 to 3.0 per 100,000 worldwide.
25 SCA3,
caused by a CAG repeat expansion in the MJD1 gene
and inherited in a autosomal dominant manner, is
the most frequent SCA subtype in many popula-
tions, usually accounting for 20%–25% (but ranging
from 10% to 84% in different populations).
25,26 The
clinical phenotype is highly variable but the primary
symptoms are cerebellar ataxia and pyramidal signs,
variably associated with extrapyramidal signs or pe-
ripheral neuropathy/amyotrophy. Isolated spastic
paraplegia as a presenting sign has been described in
some patients.
27 Age at onset is usually in adulthood
(second to fifth decade). Additional bulging eyes/
eyelid retraction, ophthalmoparesis with upgaze restric-
tion, and (peri)oral fasciculations can be suggestive signs
of SCA3. Over the course of the disease, the cerebellar
syndrome will gradually predominate. Neuroimaging
shows pontocerebellar atrophy.
28
Cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (MIM #213700).
Cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis (CTX) is a treat-
able, autosomal recessive inborn error of bile acid
metabolism, which can clinically be recognized by
some rather typical non-neurologic and age-related
symptoms: diarrhea in infancy, cataract in child-
hood, and tendon xanthomas in young adulthood.
Progressive neuropsychiatric deterioration occurs in
young adulthood, with pyramidal or cerebellar signs
that are almost always present before age 30 and with
dementia in more than 50%.
29 Increased levels of
serum cholestanol and urine bile alcohols prompt
mutation analysis of the corresponding CYP271A
gene. The relevance here is the availability of treat-
ment in the form of chenodeoxycholic acid that may
arrest and possibly reverse the progression of CTX
when started early.
30
Fatty acid hydroxylase-associated neurodegeneration.
This is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative dis-
ease with brain iron accumulation, caused by muta-
tions in the FA2H gene (MIM *611026), to which
SPG35 is allelic, and which so far has been described in
less than 30 individuals. Key features are first- or
second-decade onset spasticity with ataxia or dystonia
and episodic neurologic decline. Nystagmus, strabis-
mus, or optic atrophy can occur early in the disease.
Later, intellectual impairment and seizures may de-
velop. Brain MRI shows typical iron deposition in the
globus pallidus, combined with periventricular white
matter changes, profound pontocerebellar atrophy,
mild cerebral atrophy, and thinning of the corpus
callosum.
31
Rare spastic-ataxias. The spastic-ataxias discussed
above are the ones to consider in case of a spastic-
ataxic phenotype. However, not all spastic-ataxias are
explained by these 7 genetic diseases. More rare
forms, some of which have been reported in only 1 or
a few families, are briefly discussed below for the sake
of completeness. It should be pointed out that their
true prevalence has not been established.
Spastic ataxia 1 (MIM %108600) and spastic ataxia 2
(MIM %611302). Spastic ataxia 1 (SPAX1) and spas-
tic ataxia 2 (SPAX2) are genetic diseases, for which
only a locus has been assigned so far.
Autosomal dominant SPAX1, on chromosome
12p13, has been described in 3 large, unrelated New-
foundland families with a progressive spastic-ataxia
before age 20.
32
Autosomal recessive SPAX2, on chromosome
17p13, was found in 1 consanguineous Moroccan fam-
ily with dysarthria and gait ataxia at age 14, followed by
mild spasticity with amyotrophy and fasciculations.
33
Autosomal recessive spastic ataxia 3 (MIM *609728).
SPAX3 or spastic ataxia with leukoencephalopathy
(ARSAL), caused by mutations in the MARS2 gene,
consists of a spastic-ataxia, periventricular white mat-
ter changes, and cerebellar atrophy, with an age at
onset that varies from 2 to 59 years.

#614487; formerly known as AFG3L2-associated

Autosomal dominant spinocerebellar ataxias with py-
ramidal signs. These disorders in general are charac-
terized by cerebellar ataxia as the main symptom and
usually well recognized as ataxic syndromes. Spastic-

Rare genetic and metabolic causes of spastic-ataxias.
Many other genetic and metabolic diseases can pres-
ent with a combination of spastic paraplegia and
ataxia, although a pure spastic-ataxic syndrome as the
only feature is very rare in these disorders. In such
cases, brain MRI is often extremely helpful as a hint
toward the underlying disorder (table 2). Metabolic
blood and urinary tests or targeted molecular testing
can be considered to prove one of these disorders or
to screen for some of them, when the other above-
mentioned causes of spastic-ataxic syndromes have
been excluded.
PRACTICAL APPROACH The required starting
point is the recognition of spastic-ataxia as the core
phenotype in the patient confronted with. If ac-
quired causes (table 1) are deemed unlikely or have
been excluded, a genetic cause should be suspected.
Some metabolic disorders can be well recognized by
typical neuroimaging abnormalities (table 2). The
combination of age at onset, suspected mode of
inheritance, and additional symptoms and signs
(including neuroimaging) will then already result
in a limited number of possible diagnoses (figure).
We suggest performing molecular tests for SPG7,
Friedreich ataxia, or ARSACS first, especially in
sporadic cases or when inheritance appears to be
autosomal recessive. In case of an early-onset ( /H1102115
years) spastic-ataxia with a sensorimotor polyneu-
ropathy, ARSACS would be the first to test. An age
at onset above 25 would favor LOFA and SPG7,
less frequently SCA3 and adult-onset Alexander
disease. Periventricular white matter changes on
brain imaging point toward adult-onset Alexander
disease, SPAX3 (although this cannot yet be tested
for), or one of the rarer metabolic diseases, includ-
ing fatty acid hydroxylase-associated neurodegen-
eration with the typical iron depositions in the
globus pallidus. A clear autosomal dominant pat-
tern would favor SCA3 or SPAX1 (cannot yet be
tested for), and possibly Alexander disease. Con-
comitant ocular abnormalities can also be helpful:
cataract in cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis, optic
