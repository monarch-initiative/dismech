The miR-96 mouse mutant diminuendo exhibits deafness and arrested hair cell functional and morphological differentiation. We have previously shown that several genes are markedly downregulated in the diminuendo organ of Corti; one of these is <italic>Ptprq</italic>, a gene known to be important for maturation and maintenance of hair cells. In order to study the contribution that downregulation of <italic>Ptprq</italic> makes to the diminuendo phenotype, we carried out microarrays, scanning electron microscopy and single hair cell electrophysiology to compare diminuendo mutants (heterozygous and homozygous) with mice homozygous for a functional null allele of <italic>Ptprq</italic>. In terms of both morphology and electrophysiology, the auditory phenotype of mice lacking Ptprq resembles that of diminuendo heterozygotes, while diminuendo homozygotes are more severely affected. A comparison of transcriptomes indicates there is a broad similarity between diminuendo homozygotes and Ptprq-null mice. The reduction in Ptprq observed in diminuendo mice appears to be a major contributor to the morphological, transcriptional and electrophysiological phenotype, but does not account for the complete diminuendo phenotype.</p><sec id="kwd-group1" sec-type="kwd-group" disp-level="2"><p><bold>Keywords:</bold> ear development, hereditary hearing loss, knockout and transgenic m, molecular genetics, sensory hair cells</p></sec></abstract><custom-meta-group><custom-meta><meta-name>status</meta-name><meta-value>released</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>display-pdf</meta-name><meta-value>yes</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-in-collection-domain</meta-name><meta-value>yes</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-olf</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-manuscript</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-preprint</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-journal-matter</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-scanned</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-retracted</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta><notes notes-type="article-notes"><sec id="historyarticle-meta1" sec-type="history" disp-level="2"><p>Received 2013 Nov 25; Revised 2013 Dec 16; Accepted 2013 Dec 18; Issue date 2014 Mar.</p></sec></notes></front><body><sec id="sec1" disp-level="1"><title>Introduction</title><p>The diminuendo mutant mouse exhibits deafness and arrested development of the cochlear sensory hair cells. Inner and outer hair cells (IHCs and OHCs) in homozygotes appear immature at young stages and later degenerate; by postnatal day (P)28, very few OHCs are visible. Heterozygotes display poor hearing at P15 followed by rapid deterioration until they lack any response to sound. Their hair cells also appear immature, but are still present at P28 (Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>; Kuhn <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b18" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref>). In humans, two <italic>MIR96</italic> mutations have been found to be associated with dominantly-inherited progressive hearing loss (Mencia <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b23" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>).</p><p>The diminuendo causative mutation is a single nucleotide change in the seed region of the microRNA miR-96, which disrupts its function by both loss of its proper targets and gain of novel ones (Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>). miR-96 is a regulator controlling a wide array of genes, as seen in the microarrays comparing wildtypes and homozygotes (Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>). However, a few genes were markedly downregulated in diminuendo homozygotes, including <italic>Ptprq</italic>, which is downregulated to ∼ 50% of the wildtype level (Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>).</p><p><italic>Ptprq</italic> is a phosphatidylinositol phosphatase, which in the mouse is expressed specifically in the hair bundles of cochlear and vestibular hair cells at early postnatal stages, although expression in the OHCs is transient. In mature hair cells, the staining is restricted to the base of the hair bundle (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>). Three isoforms have been identified in chick hair cells, one expressed at immature stages and two in mature hair cells (Nayak <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b26" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref>). Ptprq localisation at the base of the stereocilia is actively maintained by Myo6, and is thought to be important for hair bundle maturation and maintenance (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>; Sakaguchi <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b30" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2008</sup></xref>). Mice homozygous for a catalytically inactive form of Ptprq (Ptprq-CAT-KO), which has a shortened intracellular domain but a wildtype C-terminus, do not respond to sound. It is likely to be a functional null; no Ptprq protein is detectable in the hair cells of homozygous Ptprq–CAT-KO mice (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>). At early postnatal stages hair cells are present but the hair bundles are disorganised, and smaller and rounder in shape. At 3 months old hair cells are absent from the basal turn of the organ of Corti. The shaft connectors of vestibular hair cells are absent from Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygotes, as are vestibular evoked potentials (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b10" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2012</sup></xref>).</p><p>miR-96 is a master regulator, controlling multiple genes and coordinating maturation of the hair cells (Kuhn <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b18" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref>), and its absence would be expected to affect a wide range of genes, although the pathways it regulates are not yet known. Understanding how much the downregulation of <italic>Ptprq</italic> contributes to the diminuendo phenotype is important for understanding the overall action of miR-96. We therefore undertook a detailed morphological examination of the hair cells, electrophysiology and transcriptome of diminuendo homozygotes and heterozygotes, Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygotes and controls, to ascertain what contribution the downregulation of <italic>Ptprq</italic> makes to the diminuendo phenotype.</p></sec><sec id="sec2" disp-level="1"><title>Materials and methods</title><sec id="sec3" disp-level="2"><title>Mice</title><p>Mouse studies were carried out in accordance with UK Home Office regulations and the UK Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act of 1986 (ASPA) under a UK Home Office licence, and the study was approved by the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute's Ethical Review Committee. Mice were culled using methods approved under this licence to minimize any possibility of suffering.</p><p>Two genetically modified mouse lines were used; the diminuendo line and the Ptprq-CAT-KO line. The diminuendo phenotype is the result of an <italic>N</italic>-ethyl-<italic>N</italic>-nitrosourea (ENU) mutagenesis screen (Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>); the line was generated and has been maintained on a C3HeB/FeJ background. The Ptprq-CAT-KO allele is a targeted knock-out in which a 1061 bp fragment of Ptprq, which contains two of the exons coding for the catalytic domain, is replaced by a neomycin resistance cassette. 129/Sv stem cells were used, and the mouse line was established and has been maintained on a mixed C57BL/6J/129/Sv background (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>). Fifty-eight animals were used in this study in total.</p></sec><sec id="sec4" disp-level="2"><title>Scanning electron microscopy</title><p>Scanning electron microscopy was carried out on diminuendo homozygote, heterozygote and wildtype and Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygote and wildtype mouse inner ears at P4 (four mice of each genotype, of either sex) as described in Chen <italic>et al</italic>. (<xref rid="b4" ref-type="bibr">2013</xref>), using the OTOTO method (Hunter-Duvar, <xref rid="b12" ref-type="bibr">1978</xref>). Images were taken using an Hitachi SE-4800 microscope at 5 kV under a standard magnification (× 80) to show the whole organ of Corti and a higher magnification (× 15 k) for IHCs and the outermost row of OHCs at 10% intervals along the length of the cochlear duct from 10 to 90% of the distance from the base to apex (Fig. <xref rid="fig01" ref-type="fig">1</xref>). No obvious differences of the size of the organ of Corti were observed during dissection of the different genotypes, and all mice examined had the normal number of cochlear turns. (<xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr">2009</xref>), using male Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygous (<italic>n</italic> = 6) and wildtype (<italic>n</italic> = 6) P4 littermates collected within a 2-h time window to control for circadian changes. Total RNA (500 ng) for each sample was amplified and purified using the Illumina TotalPrep-96 RNA Amplification kit (Ambion, UK), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Biotin-labelled cRNA was then normalized to a concentration of 150 ng/uL and 1500 ng was hybridised to Illumina MouseWG-6 v2 beadarrays (Illumina, CA, USA) for 16 h (overnight) at 58 °C. Following hybridisation, beadarrays were washed and stained with streptavidin-cy3 (GE Healthcare, UK). Beadarrays were then scanned using the Beadarray reader and image data was then processed using Genome Studio software (Illumina). The data were normalised using a quantile normalisation, making the assumption that the overall intensity distributions of the arrays should be comparable (Bolstad, <xref rid="b1" ref-type="bibr">2001</xref>; Bolstad <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b2" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>). Normalised data was then analysed using the Bioconductor LUMI and LIMMA packages (Gentleman <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b8" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2004</sup></xref>; Smyth, <xref rid="b33" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2004</sup></xref>; Smyth <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b34" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2005</sup></xref>). The <italic>P</italic>-value was adjusted for multiple tests as described in Benjamini &amp; Hochberg (<xref rid="b100" ref-type="bibr">1995</xref>), and the <italic>P</italic>-value cut-off was set to 0.05. Probe data from wildtype mice was compared to probe data from homozygote mutant mice; the only difference between each pair of littermates was the genotype. Microarray data are available in the ArrayExpress database (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://www.ebi.ac.uk/arrayexpress" ext-link-type="uri">http://www.ebi.ac.uk/arrayexpress</ext-link>) under accession number E-MTAB-1785. Further analysis was carried out using the R statistical software package (Team, <xref rid="b37" ref-type="bibr">2012</xref>) and the GSEA (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) software (Mootha <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b24" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>; Subramanian <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b36" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2005</sup></xref>) on ranked microarray gene lists, using gene pathway sets downloaded from mSigDB (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp" ext-link-type="uri">http://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp</ext-link>, August 2013), Reactome (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://www.reactome.org/" ext-link-type="uri">http://www.reactome.org/</ext-link>, July 2013) and Pathway Commons (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://www.pathwaycommons.org" ext-link-type="uri">http://www.pathwaycommons.org</ext-link>, July 2013). GSEA does not accept duplicate gene IDs, so if duplicates were present in the input gene list the highest absolute value was used.</p></sec><sec id="sec6" disp-level="2"><title>cDNA creation and RTPCR</title><p>Each pair of RNA samples (homozygote and wildtype Ptprq-CAT-KO P4 littermates of either sex; fourteen mice used in total) were normalised to the same concentration before treatment with DNAse 1 (Sigma; cat.no. AMP-D1). cDNA was created using the Superscript II Reverse Transcriptase kit (Invitrogen; cat. no. 11904-018). Primer/probe sets and the Taqman Master Mix were purchased from Applied Biosystems (Master Mix: 4364340; <italic>Hprt1</italic>: Mm01318747_g1; <italic>Jag1</italic>: Mm01270190_m1; <italic>Calb2</italic>: Mm00801461_m1; <italic>Grxcr1</italic>: Mm01217856_m1; <italic>Hsd17b7</italic>: Mm00501703_m1. Primer/probe sets for <italic>Chrna1, Chrna9</italic> and <italic>Otof</italic> were manually designed using a tool supplied by Applied Biosystems). <italic>Hprt1</italic> was used as the internal control, and <italic>Jag1</italic>, which is expressed in supporting cells (Morrison <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b25" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1999</sup></xref>; Zine <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b39" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2000</sup></xref>; Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>), was used to control for sensory tissue content. Pairs were only used if their <italic>Jag1</italic> expression levels did not differ significantly (where significance was determined to be <italic>P</italic> &lt; 0.05). Quantitative PCR was run on an ABI7000 machine (Applied Biosystems). Four technical replicates were carried out per sample, and three wildtype–homozygote pairs were tested for each probe. The <inline-formula><inline-graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="ejn0039-0744-mu1.jpg"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/b689/4065360/f5ff93370706/ejn0039-0744-mu1.jpg?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?><?image-server-status NEVER_LOAD?></inline-graphic></inline-formula> test was used to determine relative expression levels for each probe (Livak &amp; Schmittgen, <xref rid="b21" ref-type="bibr">2001</xref>); the calibrator was the wildtype littermate threshold for the same probe and the internal control was <italic>Hprt</italic>. The <italic>F</italic>-test and Fligner–Killeen test were used to estimate homoscedasticity, and the Shapiro–Wilks test for normality. The Bonferroni test was used to adjust <italic>P</italic>-values to take multiple testing into account. Excel (Microsoft, Seattle, USA) and R (Team, <xref rid="b37" ref-type="bibr">2012</xref>) were used to carry out the calculations and statistical tests.</p></sec><sec id="sec7" disp-level="2"><title>Single hair cell electrophysiology</title><p>Outer hair cells from the apical coil of diminuendo homozygote, heterozygote and wildtype mice of either sex were studied in acutely dissected organs of Corti from P1 to P6, where the day of birth is P0. Twenty-four mice were used in total. Animals were killed by cervical dislocation in accordance with UK Home Office regulations. Cochleae were dissected as previously described (Johnson <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b15" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2012</sup></xref>) in normal extracellular solution (in m<sc>m</sc>): NaCl, 135; KCl, 5.8; CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 1.3; MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 0.9; NaH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, 0.7; <sc>d</sc>-glucose, 5.6; and Hepes–NaOH, 10. Sodium pyruvate (2 m<sc>m</sc>), MEM amino acids solution (50 ×, without <sc>l</sc>-glutamine) and MEM vitamins solution (100 ×) were added from concentrates (Fisher Scientific, UK). The pH was adjusted to 7.5 (osmolality ∼ 308 mmol/kg). All animals were genotyped as previously described (Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>).</p><p>Voltage recordings were performed at room temperature (22–24 °C) using an Optopatch (Cairn Research Ltd, UK) amplifier. Patch pipettes were coated with surf wax (Mr Zogs SexWax, USA) to minimise the fast capacitance transient of the patch pipette. The intracellular solution of the patch pipette (2–3 MΩ) contained (in m<sc>m</sc>): Cs-glutamate, 106; CsCl, 20; MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 3; EGTA–CsOH, 1; Na<sub>2</sub>ATP, 5; Na<sub>2</sub>GTP, 0.3; Hepes–CsOH, 5; and sodium phosphocreatine, 10 (pH 7.3). Data acquisition was controlled by pClamp software using Digidata 1440A boards (Molecular Devices, USA). Recordings were low-pass filtered at 2.5 or 5.0 kHz (8-pole Bessel), sampled at 5 or 10 kHz and stored on computer for off-line analysis (Origin – OriginLab, USA). Membrane potentials were corrected for the residual series resistance and liquid junction potential (−11 mV).</p><p>Mechanoelectrical transducer (MET) currents were elicited by stimulating the hair bundles of OHCs using a fluid jet from a pipette (tip diameter 8–10 μm) driven by a piezoelectric disc (Johnson <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b14" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref><sup>, </sup><xref rid="b15" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2012</sup></xref>). The pipette tip of the fluid jet was positioned near to the bundles to elicit a maximal transducer current. Mechanical stimuli were applied as force-steps or 50-Hz sinusoids (filtered at 0.25 kHz, 8-pole Bessel) with driving voltages of ± 40 V. MET currents were recorded with a patch pipette solution containing 1 m<sc>m</sc> EGTA as the calcium buffer, which was previously assessed using perforated patch recordings (Johnson <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b13" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2008</sup></xref>). The effect of endolymphatic Ca<sup>2+</sup> concentration [reported to be between 0.02 and 0.04 m<sc>m</sc> Ca<sup>2+</sup> – Bosher &amp; Warren, <xref rid="b3" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1978</sup></xref>; Salt <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b31" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1989</sup></xref>)] was examined by superfusing the hair bundle with 0.04 m<sc>m</sc> Ca<sup>2+</sup> (buffered with 4 m<sc>m</sc> HEDTA) using a multi-barrelled pipette positioned closed to the patched cells. During the experiments in which different extracellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> concentrations were used (0.04 m<sc>m</sc> or 1.3 m<sc>m</sc>), the fluid jet pipette was also filled with the same solution.</p><p>Statistical comparisons of means were made by one-way <sc>anova</sc> followed by the Tukey multiple comparison test with alpha = 0.05. Mean values are quoted ± SEM.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec8" disp-level="1"><title>Results</title><sec id="sec9" disp-level="2"><title>Hair bundle structure – OHCs</title><p>In the wildtype mice at P4, there was a gradient in maturity of OHCs from cochlear apex towards base with apical hair cell bundles showing a slightly more immature, rounded arrangement, a few short microvilli remaining within the cleft of the bundles, and narrower angles between the two arms of the V-shaped array. Because the MET current reverses near 0 mV (Zampini <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b38" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref>), it became outward when excitatory bundle stimulation was applied during voltage steps positive to its reversal potential (Fig. <xref rid="fig10" ref-type="fig">10</xref>A–C). At positive potentials (+ 98 mV), the larger resting transducer current (Fig. <xref rid="fig10" ref-type="fig">10</xref>A–C, arrowheads) is likely to be due to an increased open probability of the transducer channel resulting from a reduced driving force for Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx (Crawford <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b5" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1989</sup></xref>; Johnson <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b14" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref>). Note that the residual outward K<sup>+</sup> current observed at + 98 mV, just before the beginning of the MET current, was largely reduced in heterozygous and homozygous mutant OHCs, which is consistent with a failure in the normal development of the basolateral membrane currents in these diminuendo mutant cells (Kuhn <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b18" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref>). We asked whether the Ca<sup>2+</sup> sensitivity of the MET current was affected in diminuendo mutant OHCs by locally superfusing their hair bundle with a solution containing an endolymph-like concentration of Ca<sup>2+</sup> (0.04 m<sc>m</sc>; Bosher &amp; Warren, <xref rid="b3" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1978</sup></xref>). Lowering the extracellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> concentration is known to increase both the maximum MET current and its fraction activated at rest. Calcium is a permeant blocker of the transducer channel (Ricci &amp; Fettiplace, <xref rid="b28" ref-type="bibr">1998</xref>; Marcotti <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b22" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2005</sup></xref>), so the increased current amplitude in low Ca<sup>2+</sup> is caused by the partial relief of this block. Moreover, extracellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> causes adaptation and as such closes some transducer channels. Therefore, reducing Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx into the transducer channel, by either depolarizing hair cells to near the Ca<sup>2+</sup> equilibrium potential (as shown in Fig. <xref rid="fig10" ref-type="fig">10</xref>A–C) or lowering the extracellular concentration, causes an increased open probability of the channel (Crawford <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b6" ref-type="bibr"><sup>1991</sup></xref>). In diminuendo mice, both phenomena were observed because decreasing the Ca<sup>2+</sup> concentration from 1.3 to 0.04 m<sc>m</sc> increased the resting (Fig. <xref rid="fig10" ref-type="fig">10</xref>D–F) and maximal (Fig. <xref rid="fig10" ref-type="fig">10</xref>D, E and G) MET current in control and homozygous OHCs. The above results indicate that the size of the MET current, but not its Ca<sup>2+</sup> sensitivity, is affected by mutation in miR-96.</p><fig id="fig10" position="float"><?disp-level 3?><label>Fig. 10</label><caption><p>Mechanotransducer currents in diminuendo cochlear outer hair cells. We also see a strong similarity between the reduced amplitude of the MET current in diminuendo heterozygotes with that previously reported in OHCs from Ptprq-null mutant mice (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>). However, both slipping and rebound adaptation are absent or largely reduced in diminuendo heterozygotes while they were still present in <italic>Ptprq</italic>-null mutants (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>).</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec14" disp-level="1"><title>Discussion</title><p>Homozygous diminuendo mice at P4 lack the normal gradient of hair bundle development of OHCs between 0 and 80% of the cochlear length while above that in the apex the hair cells are still less mature than those at the equivalent position in wildtype littermates. This suggests developmental retardation followed by stalling at a certain point in differentiation. The hair cells appear unable to differentiate any further. Heterozygous diminuendo mice show a delay in differentiation of OHCs at P4 but there is no stalling of development at this stage; the gradient of maturity is present throughout the organ of Corti. We have previously shown that, at 4 weeks old, diminuendo heterozygote OHCs still display significantly narrower bundle span than wildtypes while supporting cells have wider apices, and hair cell density remains the same (see Supporting Information Fig. s1p and q in Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>). The OHCs of P4 Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygous mice also appear to exhibit delayed differentiation, similar to diminuendo heterozygotes but with a greater delay (Fig. <xref rid="fig03" ref-type="fig">3</xref>). The IHCs of diminuendo heterozygotes are broadly similar to wildtypes, but both diminuendo homozygote and Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygote IHCs have disorganised stereocilia bundles. However, Ptprq-CAT-KO IHCs display more hallmarks of maturity than diminuendo homozygote IHCs, such as well-organised stereocilia rows and thickening of stereocilia tips. The overall phenotype of Ptprq-CAT-KO hair cells bears a striking resemblance to that of diminuendo heterozygous mice and is not as severe as that of diminuendo homozygous mice, suggesting that the reduction in Ptprq activity is a major contributor to the diminuendo stereocilia morphological phenotype. This observation also fits with part of the electrophysiological phenotype, where heterozygous diminuendo mice resemble homozygous <italic>Ptprq</italic>-null mice (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>), with a reduced MET current amplitude compared to controls. In diminuendo homozygotes the phenotype is more severe, as normal development of the MET current amplitude appears to be arrested just after birth (P1–2), consistent with the proposed role of miR-96 in regulating the progression of the physiological and morphological differentiation of mammalian cochlear hair cells (Kuhn <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b18" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref>). Furthermore, the downregulation of <italic>Ptprq</italic> expression in the diminuendo mutants is not likely to be responsible for the adaptation defects seen in these mice, because adaptation in <italic>Ptprq</italic> mutants is near to normal (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>).</p><p>The transcriptome analyses, on the other hand, show less of a similarity between Ptprq-CAT-KO and diminuendo. This is not unexpected – Ptprq is not a regulatory factor but performs a specific function in the hair cell, while miR-96, which is a regulator, would be expected to affect the expression of many genes. However, when just the genes with a <italic>P</italic>-value &lt; 0.1 are considered, there are similarities between Ptprq-CAT-KO and diminuendo, with many of the genes affected in Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygotes being similarly regulated in diminuendo homozygotes, as seen in the heatmap (Fig. <xref rid="fig08" ref-type="fig">8</xref>C) and reflected in the correlation coefficient, <italic>r</italic> = 0.62 (Fig. <xref rid="fig08" ref-type="fig">8</xref>B). An initial exploration of pathways affected shows that some of the pathways and transcription factors implicated by the transcriptional data are common to both mutants. Of the three genes whose expression changes were confirmed by qRTPCR, however, only downregulation of <italic>Hsd17b7</italic> is seen in both Ptprq-CAT-KO and diminuendo homozygotes. <italic>Grxcr1</italic> is not significantly affected in diminuendo homozygotes, and <italic>Otof</italic> is upregulated in diminuendo homozygotes and downregulated in Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygotes (Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>; Kuhn <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b18" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref>). In addition, many of the other major molecular changes observed in diminuendo homozygotes are not reproduced in Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygotes, for example the downregulation of <italic>Ocm</italic>. It is possible that <italic>Hsd17b7</italic> is downregulated in both mutants because of a specific effect for which lack of Ptprq is responsible. <italic>Hsd17b7</italic> (hydroxysteroid (17-beta) dehydrogenase 7) is involved in cholesterol biosynthesis and is required for neuronal survival early in development; mice homozygous for a disrupted allele of <italic>Hsd17b7</italic> die at embryonic day 10.5, with increased apoptosis observed in the neural tube, dorsal root ganglia and trigeminal nerve. They also display cardiovascular defects (Shehu <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b32" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2008</sup></xref>; Jokela <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b16" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2010</sup></xref>). Mice with an ENU-induced mutation in <italic>Hsd17b7</italic> display disrupted Hedgehog signalling. The mutation is hypomorphic; it leads to most of the <italic>Hsd17b7</italic> transcripts being truncated due to a mis-splice, but low levels of wildtype transcript are still present (Stottmann <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b35" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2011</sup></xref>). Hedgehog signalling is required for inner ear development (Liu <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b20" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2002</sup></xref>; Riccomagno <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b29" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2002</sup></xref>), but any more direct link between Ptprq and Hsd17b7 remains unclear. The changes in expression of <italic>Grxcr1</italic> in Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygotes, and of <italic>Otof</italic> in both diminuendo and Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygotes, may be only indicative of a dysfunctional hair cell.</p><p>In conclusion, many of the morphological and electrophysiological features of diminuendo heterozygotes are reproduced in Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygotes, and in some features the transcriptome of Ptprq-CAT-KO homozygotes resembles that of diminuendo homozygotes. It is particularly interesting that in the P4 diminuendo cochlea there is very little difference in expression of <italic>Ptprq</italic> between heterozygotes and homozygotes; <italic>Ptprq</italic> is downregulated to ∼ 62% of the wildtype level in heterozygotes and ∼56% of the wildtype level in homozygotes (Lewis <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b19" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2009</sup></xref>), which would suggest that diminuendo heterozygotes and homozygotes should both resemble Ptprq heterozygotes, which is not the case; Ptprq-CAT-KO heterozygotes have no hair cell defects (Goodyear <italic>et al</italic>., <xref rid="b9" ref-type="bibr"><sup>2003</sup></xref>). The reduction in <italic>Ptprq</italic> seen in diminuendo mice appears to be a major contributor to the phenotype, particularly the apparent delay and eventual stalling of hair cell development observed by scanning electron microscopy and the delayed development of the MET current, but it does not account for all of the observations. The more severe phenotypes in the diminuendo homozygous mice indicate that other genes besides Ptprq are contributing to the diminuendo homozygous phenotype, and these genes may not be affected in diminuendo heterozygous or Ptprq-null mice.</p></sec><sec id="ack1" sec-type="ack" disp-level="1"><title>Acknowledgments</title><p>This work was supported by the Wellcome Trust [grant numbers 100669 (K.P.S.) and 091895 (W.M.)], Action on Hearing Loss (G41) and the MRC.
