BACKGROUND: Stiff person syndrome spectrum disorders (SPSD) are rare, disabling 
neuroimmunological conditions with no consensus diagnostic criteria, making 
diagnosis challenging. Misdiagnosis often occurs due to the limited awareness of 
atypical symptoms and presentations. This study aimed to identify key clinical 
and paraclinical features most predictive of classic SPS and SPS-plus diagnosis 
and misdiagnosis patterns.
METHODS: We conducted a retrospective case-control study at Johns Hopkins SPS 
center, analyzing patient data from 1997-2021. A total of 154 classic SPS, 45 
SPS-plus, and 66 control patients (evaluated for SPSD but given an alternative 
diagnosis) were included. Clinical assessments, autoantibody testing, 
electromyography (EMG), and other diagnostic studies were reviewed. Sensitivity, 
specificity, and diagnostic odds ratios were calculated, with logistic 
regression identifying the strongest diagnostic indicators of the SPS 
phenotypes.
RESULTS: Torso/lower extremity symptoms, hypersensitivity triggers, 
paravertebral stiffness, and gait dysfunction were common in both phenotypes. 
Classic SPS was most specifically associated with high-titer GAD65 antibodies 
(98%), cerebrospinal fluid GAD65 positivity (100%), characteristic EMG 
abnormalities (> 90%), and hyperlordosis (87%). SPS-plus specificity was highest 
for cerebellar (98.5%) and brainstem (100%) signs/symptoms. High-titer GAD65 
antibodies were the strongest independent diagnostic factor for both phenotypes. 
Misdiagnosis was most common in patients presenting with upper extremity, 
brainstem, or cerebellar involvement.
CONCLUSIONS: Recognizing key diagnostic and misdiagnosis patterns may help 
clinicians make accurate and timely diagnoses of SPSD. This could help prevent 
misdiagnosis/overdiagnosis, ensure timely treatment, and assure appropriate 
patient populations are included in future interventional SPS clinical trials. 
Further studies are needed to validate these findings and refine diagnostic 
criteria.

Stiff person syndrome spectrum disorders (SPSD) are a rare group of autoimmune neurologic disorders that most often affect the truncal and limb musculature with progressive muscle rigidity and painful spasms [1, 2]. Classic stiff person syndrome (SPS) has been associated with glutamic acid decarboxylase-65 (GAD65) antibodies, though these have also been associated with a wide variety of other clinical phenotypes, including SPS-plus (classic features of SPS with additional cerebellar and/or brainstem dysfunction), cerebellar ataxia (CA), stiff-limb syndrome (SPS symptoms isolated to one limb or trunk), progressive encephalomyelitis with rigidity and myoclonus (PERM), refractory epilepsy, and autoimmune encephalitis [3]. There may be overlap between presentations, suggesting a spectrum of disease.

Besides GAD65 antibodies, amphiphysin and glycine receptor antibodies are seen in association with SPSD [4, 5]. In addition, SPSD patients may develop other symptoms that impact them differentially (e.g., agoraphobia). Diagnosis of these conditions can be challenging, even with proposed diagnostic criteria [6, 7]. While these criteria share common features, including episodic spasms, axial stiffness, positive GAD65 antibodies, characteristic electrophysiologic findings, and exclusion of alternative etiologies, it remains unclear which of these features hold the greatest diagnostic value. Moreover, the lack of awareness for these diseases and absence of consensus diagnostic criteria leads to a diagnosis latency of several years, or misdiagnosis/overdiagnosis [8, 9]. This is particularly challenging in non-classical phenotypes due to their rarity. To aid accurate and timely diagnoses, we aimed to identify clinical and paraclinical features most predictive of SPSD by comparing confirmed cases with those who were evaluated for suspected SPSD but were ultimately found to have an alternative diagnosis. Additionally, we identified common misdiagnoses and SPSD features associated with misdiagnosis.

As part of an ongoing observational study, we reviewed 235 patients with SPSD who were treated at Johns Hopkins from 1997 to 2021, and 66 controls who were also evaluated for suspected SPSD and ultimately diagnosed with other conditions. The non-SPSD controls were used as the reference population for the sensitivity and specificity analyses (see Statistical Analysis section).

SPSD diagnosis was determined by clinical presentation, examination findings, antibody status, and electrophysiological testing. Classic SPS was defined as patients presenting with prototypical body regions involved (torso and lower > upper extremities), hallmark symptom triggers, presence of associated serum autoantibodies, and exclusion of alternative causes, as historically proposed in the literature [2, 6, 7]. SPS-plus was defined by the same findings as classic SPS, with additional brainstem and/or cerebellar signs and symptoms [7]. For the SPSD patients who were seronegative, they had to meet all predetermined clinical criteria for diagnosis including prototypical symptoms and body region involvement (e.g., torso and lower extremity), hallmark exam findings, typical symptom triggers, symptom response to gamma-aminobutyric (GABA)-ergic medications (e.g., benzodiazepines), and exclusion of alternative causes; approximately half of these patients also had characteristic SPS EMG findings.

The inclusion criterion for this study was the evaluation of suspected SPSD. Most of the 301 patients were assessed by author SDN, while 16 were evaluated by other neuromuscular or movement disorder specialists at Johns Hopkins. Among these, 66 patients were identified with non-SPSD conditions and served as controls, while 199 of the remaining 235 cases had confirmed diagnoses of classic SPS or SPS-plus phenotypes. We analyzed data on demographics, initial symptomatology, clinical history, examination features, and diagnostic testing. Sensitivity and specificity analyses were assessed for each abstracted variable in confirmed SPSD cases versus controls. Amongst the non-SPSD group, the actual diagnosis was documented where possible. For missing data on any specific variable, that patient was excluded from the corresponding univariate or multivariable analysis.


The following autoantibodies relevant to SPSD were tested as part of standard of care: GAD65 antibody, amphiphysin antibody, and glycine receptor antibody. Clinical laboratories for serological testing included Johns Hopkins, Quest Laboratories (both enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay [ELISA] method), and Mayo Clinic Laboratories (radioimmunoassay [RIA] method) for GAD65 antibody testing. High positive levels for RIA were defined as > 20nmol/L, while ELISA titers were assessed from > 1000 to > 10,000 IU/mL (based on univariate analyses- see results section). For CSF GAD65 antibodies, assays were performed at Mayo Clinic Laboratories.

Electrodiagnostic studies included EMG with evaluation for co-contraction of agonist and antagonist muscles, continuous motor unit activity (CMUA) in the paraspinal muscles, upper extremities, and lower extremities were performed in a subset of patients. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of brain and spine and other laboratory studies were obtained to rule out mimickers of SPSD.

We reviewed the Johns Hopkins SPS center’s longitudinal database for any prior misdiagnoses given to the patients with SPSD, and the results were tabulated. Similar conditions were combined into categories (e.g. “Cervical spondylosis” and “Myelopathy” into “Spondylotic myelopathy”). Controls final diagnoses are included separately in Supplement Table 1. 


Statistical analyses were performed using R (Version 4.0.03). Sensitivity and specificity were calculated for each of the individual items analyzed, relative to the final diagnosis of either classic SPS or SPS-plus, with the controls as a reference group [10]. An item was considered "highly sensitive" or "highly specific" if its value exceeded 80%. Univariate analyses were performed to calculate odds ratios for characteristics associated with classic SPS and SPS-plus diagnoses. Univariate analysis also evaluated clinical features linked to misdiagnosis, reporting odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Stepwise logistic regression was performed to create multivariable models, identifying variables with the highest likelihood ratios that best distinguished classic SPS and SPS-plus groups from controls [11]. Each variable was weighted by its logistic regression coefficient, with significance defined as p < 0.05.

The study was approved by the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Review Board (IRB00154798) as part of an ongoing longitudinal observational study. Chart reviews and participant consent followed the IRB-approved protocol.

Clinical data were available for all 199 SPSD cases (154 classic SPS, 45 SPS-plus) and 66 controls. Among SPSD patients, the mean age was 43 (±13.5), with 141 (70.8%) identifying as White and 152 (76.4%) as female. For controls, the mean age was 37 (±15.5), with 52 (78.8%) identifying as White and 41 (62.1%) as female. While no significant differences were observed in mean age or female proportion, the classic SPS and SPS-plus cohorts included a significantly higher proportion of Black patients compared to controls (Chi-square, p < 0.001).

Of the patients with classic SPS and SPS-plus, many had associated systemic autoimmunity or autoimmune endocrine disorders. Overall, 73 (36.7%) had thyroid disorders, 64 (32.1%) had diabetes, 17 (8.5%) had pernicious anemia, 16 (8.0%) had either systemic lupus erythematosus or Sjogren’s syndrome, and 34 (17.1%) had other associated autoimmunity.

Of the confirmed SPSD patient cohort, 185 (93.0%) were seropositive within the serum, and 14 (7.0%) were seronegative. The majority of patients were positive for GAD65 antibodies (169, 84.9%), with another six patients (3.0%) positive for amphiphysin antibodies, and ten (5.0%) positive for glycine receptor antibodies. No SPS-plus cases were amphiphysin antibody positive, and only one was glycine receptor positive. CSF GAD65 antibody was present in 62.5% of tested SPSD patients (40/64). Of the controls, the majority were seronegative (50, 75.8%). None of the GAD65 Ab seropositive controls had an antibody result >10,000 IU/mL on ELISA (average 527 IU/mL) or >20nmol/L (average 0.14 nmol/L).

In classic SPS, torso and lower extremity symptoms were sensitive (89.1%; 98.1%, respectively) but lacked specificity (50.0%, 13.1%, respectively). Each of these variables were associated with a diagnosis of classic SPS (OR 7.9, 95% CI 3.6–17.6; OR 7.4, 95% CI 1.9–29.1, respectively). For SPS-plus, brainstem and cerebellar symptoms were highly specific (100%, 98.5%, respectively) but not sensitive (26.7%, 24.4%, respectively). Odds ratio was not calculated for brainstem symptoms due to 100% specificity, but it was for cerebellar symptoms and these symptoms were also associated with an SPS-plus diagnosis (OR 21.0, 95% CI 2.6–170). Upper extremity symptoms were not associated with a diagnosis of either phenotype.

Hypersensitivity triggers associated with a classic SPS diagnosis included unexpected noise (OR 13.9, 95% CI 5.9–32.7), fear of open space (OR 11.8, 95% CI 4.7–29.9), exposure to cold (OR 6.8, 95% CI 3.2–14.7), stress (OR 27.0, 95% CI 8.4–86.8), and agoraphobia (OR 16.2, 95% CI 6.1–43.3). The same triggers were associated with a diagnosis of SPS-plus (Table 2). The only trigger that was highly specific was agoraphobia (86.7%), and stress as a symptom trigger was the most sensitive (96.8%). Both were associated with a diagnosis of classic SPS (OR 7.9, 95% CI 3.6–17.6; OR 7.4, 95% CI 1.9–29.1, respectively).

In classic SPS, hyperlordosis was the most specific exam finding (87.1%) but not highly sensitive (62.6%), though it was still associated with diagnosis (OR 11.9, 95% CI 5.1–27.3). Gait abnormalities and rigidity/stiffness were highly sensitive (91.4%, 88.2%, respectively) but less specific (35.9%, 68.8%, respectively). Each was associated with a classic SPS diagnosis. In SPS-plus, ocular motor dysfunction was both sensitive and specific (80.0%, 81.6% respectively) and highly associated with diagnosis (OR 17.7, 95% CI 5.7–55). Dysarthria was also highly specific (86.5%, OR 21.3, 95% CI 6.4–71). Similar to classic SPS, hyperlordosis was highly specific (87.1%, OR 14.1, 95% CI 5.1–38.7) for SPS-plus but not a sensitive exam finding (67.6%).

Univariate analysis revealed that hyperlordosis, abnormal gait, hyperreflexia, rigidity/stiffness, paravertebral muscle abnormalities, and abdominal spasms were all associated with a diagnosis of both classic SPS and SPS-plus. Ataxia, ocular motor dysfunction, and dysarthria were associated with a diagnosis of SPS-plus.

GAD65 antibody status was assessed across multiple titers based on univariate analysis results. A GAD65 antibody titer >1,000IU/mL or >20nmol/L was highly specific (98.2%, OR 59.2, 95% CI 8.0-440) for a diagnosis of classic SPS, but sensitivity decreased as the titer cutoff rose for the ELISA assay (50% at > 1,000 IU/mL, 41.4% at > 10,000 IU/mL) in classic SPS (OR 41.4, 95% CI 5.6–308). Similar results were seen in SPS-plus, with high specificity (98.2%) but reduced sensitivity above > 3,000 IU/mL (73.2%, OR 150, 95% CI 18.5–1219 to 70.7%, OR 118, 95% CI 14.7–952).

The presence of glycine receptor, amphiphysin, and CSF GAD65 antibodies were 100% specific in classic SPS cases. CSF GAD65 antibody was 100% sensitive and specific for SPS-plus.

In both phenotypes, EMG findings of agonist and antagonist co-contraction were highly specific (93.1%), as were CMUA in the paraspinal muscles (98.3%), upper extremities (98.3%), and lower extremities (96.6%). However, these findings lacked sensitivity for both phenotypes (< 80%). EMG co-contraction of agonist and antagonist was associated with a classic SPS diagnosis (OR 4.2, 95% CI 1.4–12.6), but none of the other EMG findings were significantly associated with a diagnosis of either phenotype (p > 0.05 based on univariate analysis).

Of 199 SPSD patients, 147 (73.9%) had a prior misdiagnosis. The most common misdiagnoses were neuropathy, myelopathy, movement disorder, and functional neurologic disorder. Age, race, and sex were not associated with misdiagnosis. The clinical characteristics associated with misdiagnosis were upper extremity stiffness and/or spasms (OR 3.86, 95% CI 2.63–5.88), and this association was stronger in classic SPS patients (OR 8.40, 95% CI 6.35-10.44). In SPS-plus patients, brainstem (OR 7.00, 95% CI 5.38–8.62) and cerebellar signs/symptoms (OR 3.27, 95% CI 1.83–4.71) were associated with misdiagnosis. The median time to diagnosis for SPSD patients with a prior misdiagnosis was 45.7 (IQR 18.9–85.8) months, compared to 35.1 (IQR 12.0–74.0) months in the overall SPSD cohort. Multivariable modeling identified the presence of an SPSD autoantibody (serum high titer GAD65, CSF GAD65, serum amphiphysin, serum glycine receptor antibodies [OR 513, p < 0.001]) and abnormal EMG findings (OR 12.5, p < 0.01) as the strongest association of having classic SPS (Table 3). Patient-reported spasms (OR 2.1, p = 0.28) and clinical examination findings associated with classic SPS (OR 3.0, p = 0.11) were less predictive.

Multivariable modeling identified the presence of an SPSD autoantibody (serum high titer GAD65, CSF GAD65, serum glycine receptor antibodies), brainstem/cerebellar symptoms, cranial nerve and cerebellar dysfunction on examination, as all having the strongest association with an SPS-plus diagnosis (Table 4). Although, the presence of an SPSD autoantibody (e.g., high titer GAD-65 antibody) had the strongest association with the diagnosis (OR 120, p < 0.001), followed by brainstem/cerebellar symptoms (OR 9.1, p < 0.001).

We present detailed sensitivity and specificity data from a large SPSD cohort, identifying key characteristics for diagnosis of both classic SPS and SPS-plus. Our study builds on previous research by clarifying which key clinical and paraclinical features enhance the diagnostic accuracy of SPSD and provide quantifiable data to distinguish SPSD from other neurologic conditions, which is a limitation in prior studies.

Our findings support prior observations of classic SPS presentation, with greater sensitivity for torso and lower extremity clinical presentation compared to upper extremity presentation (more common in amphiphysin antibody-associated disease) [12]. Hyperlordosis was the most specific physical exam finding, identifiable at the bedside and uncommon in SPSD mimickers. Given the diagnostic challenges and delays in care associated with misdiagnosis, recognizing this clinical feature could help facilitate proper work up, accurate diagnosis and treatment in the appropriate clinical contexts. Overall, this study provides additional support to the clinical picture of SPSD impacting the thoracolumbar musculature and highlights the importance of musculoskeletal exam findings [13, 14].

While no singular reported hallmark trigger was a 100% specific in our study (most specific was agoraphobia at 86.7%), the presence of hypersensitivity triggers in general were 100% sensitive as all patients with SPSD had at least one or more symptom triggers; unexpected noise trigger was very sensitive (88% and 90%), which may correlate with the acoustic startle reflex [8, 15]. In patients with SPS-plus, we observed the important addition of cerebellar and/or brainstem abnormalities on the neurologic exam as highly specific objective findings for an accurate diagnosis.

Interpreting elevated GAD65 antibody titers in SPSD can be challenging, as they are observed in association with other conditions such as autoimmune diabetes, though typically at much lower levels. In prior work, the titer on ELISA was 10-fold lower in type 1 diabetes than what is observed in SPSD [16]. Using > 10,000 IU/mL as a high-titer cutoff, samples below this threshold were 100% negative for immunohistochemistry, while 97% above it were positive. In our analysis, serum ELISA titers > 1,000 IU/mL to > 10,000 IU/mL showed consistent specificity for supporting a diagnosis of SPSD. Only one non-SPSD patient (diagnosed with neuropathy) exceeded 1,000 IU/mL (7,072 IU/mL) while others remained below these thresholds. These findings could suggest that ELISA titers above 1,000 IU/mL could point towards an SPSD diagnosis with other supporting clinical features/findings, as opposed to relying solely on the value of > 10,000 IU/mL. In fact, earlier studies on GAD65 antibody pathogenicity suggested a cutoff as low as 2,000 IU/mL, though these were performed with different test calibration than modern immunoassays, and so antibody titers in this range must again be interpreted in the context of the clinical presentation and other diagnostic testing (e.g., EMG, CSF, etc.) [13]. It should also be noted that there can be differences in titers based on a laboratories ELISA dilution methods [17]. Hence, collaborative studies are needed to further validate and refine ELISA titer cutoffs in SPSD; specifically testing the same samples from a patient in separate labs. Moreover, SPSD remains a diagnosis of exclusion and lab-based tests are helpful in supporting a diagnosis when the appropriate clinical syndrome is present.

Based on our findings, spinal fluid assessment for GAD65 antibodies/CSF-restricted OCBs may help with diagnostic accuracy in cases with low serum GAD65 antibody titers or inconclusive findings. Additionally, GAD65 antibody titers can be influenced by treatments. A study on serum neuronal antibodies in different intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) preparations found that GAD65 antibody titers could be falsely elevated after IVIg treatment, with ELISA concentrations ranging from 40 to 1,507 IU/mL [18]. Clinicians should recognize the potential for false-positive antibody results after IVIg treatment and avoid relying solely on serum antibody tests for diagnosis. Serial testing off IVIg can confirm the accuracy of positive results. Ultimately, SPSD is a clinical diagnosis, supported by paraclinical data and the exclusion of alternative explanations for the patient’s symptoms and exam findings.

EMG was highly specific for the hallmark findings of co-contraction of agonist and antagonist muscles, as well as CMUA, regardless of body region tested. These findings support the use of EMG when evaluating for SPSD and it is recommended, when possible, to have the same experienced clinical neurophysiologist perform the EMG as signs of SPSD can be over/misinterpreted. Additional findings not assessed in our cohort included evaluating variation of CMUA during waking and sleeping states, or in response to GABAergic medications, which may be helpful [18, 19]. Our center does not perform exteroceptive reflexes, but these can be assessed for the presence of an exaggerated response to hypersensitivity triggers [20]. The most common misdiagnoses in our cohort were myelopathy and neuropathy; therefore, obtaining EMG and MRIs of the spine are advisable in order to avoid such misdiagnoses.

In our multivariable analysis, we identified core characteristics that are applicable to clinical practice for diagnosis of both classic SPS and SPS-plus. GAD65 antibodies with > 1,000 IU/mL on ELISA or >20 nmol/L on RIA were the features most strongly associated with a diagnosis of each phenotype. In patients with classic SPS, the next most pertinent factor was hallmark EMG findings. This is notable given the many challenges in identifying the diagnosis off history and clinical examination findings alone. Objective measures such as antibody testing and electrodiagnostics help clinicians identify patients with SPSD, especially in cases where the clinical history and examination are ambiguous. However, clinicians should note that when requesting EMGs to evaluate SPSD, they must specify the assessment of co-contraction and CMUA involved muscle groups suggested by the patient's history and exam. Ideally, testing should be conducted without recent use of antispasmodic medications (e.g., benzodiazepines, baclofen). Additional factors that were shown to be important for diagnosing SPS-plus included signs and symptoms of posterior fossa involvement. In clinical practice, it is important to further characterize whether brainstem and/or cerebellar involvement exists since these regions are harbingers of future disability in SPSD [9]. Our recent work highlighted delayed diagnosis in SPS-plus due to limited awareness of non-classical phenotypes. Additionally, Chia et al. described neurological overlap syndromes in SPS-plus, including myelopathy, ataxia, cognitive dysfunction, and epilepsy, which further complicate diagnosis [15]. Clinicians should maintain a degree of suspicion for SPSD when evaluating patients exhibiting typical limb and torso signs and symptoms concomitant with atypical features, particularly with discernible brainstem and/or cerebellar signs/symptoms upon clinical examination, in order to avert misdiagnosis. Moreover, patients should exhibit both subjective and objective findings consistent with SPSD in order to reduce the risk of overdiagnosis [14].

We also found that misdiagnosis of SPSD occurs frequently. These misdiagnoses can lead to delays in diagnosis and treatment, which is especially important as patients typically require a multi-pronged approach with both symptomatic and immune-based interventions. Recent work demonstrated that earlier initiation of immunotherapy may be associated with either stabilization or improvement in the clinical course of SPSD [9]. Hence, early recognition of phenotypic presentations and obtaining appropriate diagnostic studies are vital for confirming SPSD. Clearer diagnostic criteria can help clinicians identify SPSD correctly, initiate proper treatments, and avoid unnecessary interventions when the diagnosis is not certain. Additionally, standardized, evidence-based criteria are crucial for future research, enabling the selection of homogenous study populations for studies; identifying core features most important for an SPSD diagnosis, as done in this study, are critical for inclusion criteria of future interventional clinical trials.

Limitations of this study include its retrospective in nature and small sample sizes. Although, the sample size for each group is large when considering the rarity of SPSD and when comparing other cohorts that exist in the literature. Final diagnosis was determined by one SPSD expert clinician, which could introduce rater bias. However, having a single adjudicator for diagnosis and a priori clear rating criteria ensured consistent data collection and diagnostic methodology. We did not include rarer non-classical features seen in SPSD (e.g. gastrointestinal dysmotility, retinal involvement, etc.) into the analyses due to even smaller numbers of individuals with these features in SPSD, and it is unclear if these other features would be helpful in differentiating SPSD from other conditions [21–23].

This study identified key characteristics most strongly associated with a diagnosis of classic SPS and SPS-plus, highlighting the diagnostic value of SPS autoantibodies like high-titer GAD65 antibody and typical EMG findings. Clinicians should maintain a high suspicion for SPSD, relying on both clinical and paraclinical features for accurate diagnosis while avoiding misdiagnosis in cases lacking objective findings. Future studies are needed to validate these results and to develop consensus diagnostic criteria.
