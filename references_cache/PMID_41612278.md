BACKGROUND: Asbestosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) share overlapping
clinical and pathological features; however, differences in comorbidities and
prognostic patterns between the two diseases remain poorly defined. This study
aimed to compare comorbidities and prognostic factors associated with pulmonary
fibrosis progression and mortality in asbestosis and IPF.
METHODS: This prospective cohort study included 254 patients with asbestosis and
548 patients with IPF enrolled between 2016 and 2020, with follow-up through
2022. Outcomes included progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) and all-cause
mortality. Multivariable Cox models were applied to identify independent risk
factors associated with these outcomes.
RESULTS: Patients with asbestosis exhibited a higher comorbidity burden.
Specifically, they more frequently had asthma, lung cancer, pleural disease,
cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease, and connective tissue disease, whereas
emphysema was more common in IPF (all P < 0.05). Over a median follow-up of 44
months (IQR 24-61), IPF patients had a higher cumulative incidence of endpoint
events than those with asbestosis. In asbestosis, a usual interstitial pneumonia
(UIP) pattern on HRCT predicted disease progression. In IPF, risk factors for
progression included hypoxemia, forced vital capacity (FVC) < 80%, and UIP
compared with probable UIP. Interestingly, combined emphysema was protective
against IPF progression. Mortality in asbestosis was associated with lung
cancer, pulmonary hypertension (PH), and atrial fibrillation, whereas in IPF, it
was linked to FVC < 80% and PH.
CONCLUSIONS: Asbestosis and IPF show distinct comorbidity patterns and
prognostic drivers. IPF prognosis is mainly determined by fibrosis severity,
while asbestosis mortality is driven largely by asbestos-related complications.

Abstract

study aimed to compare comorbidities and prognostic factors associated with pulmonary fibrosis progression and
mortality in asbestosis and IPF.

between 2016 and 2020, with follow-up through 2022. Outcomes included progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) and
all-cause mortality. Multivariable Cox models were applied to identify independent risk factors associated with these
outcomes.

asthma, lung cancer, pleural disease, cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease, and connective tissue disease,

Introduction
Fibrotic interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) represent a het -
erogeneous group of progressive lung scarring disorders,
among which asbestosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibro -
sis (IPF) are two distinct clinical entities. Asbestosis is a
type of pneumoconiosis caused by long-term inhalation
of asbestos fibers, resulting in diffuse fibrosis predomi -
nantly in the lower lung zones. Despite asbestos bans in
66 countries and regions [1], asbestosis continues to pose
a global health burden due to its long latency period of
20–40 years. In China, over one million people remain
at risk due to occupational asbestos exposure, and dis -
ease-related mortality is likely underestimated [ 1, 2]. IPF
primarily affects middle and old aged individuals and is
characterized by irreversible fibrosis, a median survival
of approximately five years, and a pathological hallmark
of the usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern [ 3]. The
estimated incidence of IPF ranges from 0.09 to 1.30 per
10,000 population, with a gradual annual increase [4].
The comorbidities play a critical role in shaping the
clinical course and outcomes of fibrotic ILDs. Asbestosis
is frequently linked to pleural disease, asbestos-related
malignancies [2], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
(COPD) [ 5], pulmonary hypertension (PH) [ 6], connec -
tive tissue diseases [ 7], and cardiovascular comorbidities
[8–10]. IPF is more commonly associated with COPD or
emphysema, PH, lung cancer, gastroesophageal reflux,
and obstructive sleep apnea [ 11]. Disease progression
patterns also differ. IPF is inherently progressive, with
up to 95% of patients experiencing fibrotic worsening
over time [ 12]. Asbestosis may exhibit similar two-year
progression rates compared to IPF (43% vs. 59%) [ 12],
challenging traditional assumptions regarding its more
indolent course.
Therefore, we conducted a comparative cohort study
to evaluate and contrast the comorbidity burden and key
prognostic determinants between asbestosis and IPF,
aiming to inform disease-specific risk stratification and
clinical management strategies.
Methods
Study design and participants
The prospective cohort study included patients diag -
nosed with asbestosis or IPF at Beijing Chao-Yang Hos -
pital between January 2016 and December 2020 (Fig. 1).
Asbestosis patients were confirmed by multidisciplinary
discussion using the International Labour Organization
classification [13]. While IPF diagnoses were established
according to 2011 European Respiratory Society/Ameri -
can Thoracic Society/Japanese Respiratory Society/
Latin American Thoracic Society criteria [ 14]. Final IPF/
asbestosis diagnosis was always determined by a multi -
disciplinary team (reported in the Supplemental Mate -
rial). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of
other idiopathic interstitial pneumonias or fibrosis sec -
ondary to identifiable causes; (2) silicosis, coal workers’
pneumoconiosis, or other pneumoconiosis; (3) insuf -
ficient data to assess disease progression; and (4) loss

to follow-up. Patients were followed from their initial
visit until death, loss to follow-up, or the censoring date
(November 30, 2022). The study complied with the Dec -

from the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospi -

Data collection and follow-up
All baseline data were collected from electronic medical
records, including demographics (age, sex, body mass
index [BMI]), smoking history (including smoking dura -
tion and quantity in pack-years), occupational history,
arterial blood gas analysis, pulmonary function tests,
chest imaging findings, comorbidities, and treatment.
Comorbidities were classified by organ/system (e.g. respi-
ratory, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular, malignant,
metabolic-related, and other). The Charlson Comorbid -
ity Index (CCI) was used to quantify comorbidity burden,
providing a validated estimate of mortality risk [15].
Based on the 2013 ATS/European Respiratory Soci -
ety guidelines for the diagnosis of idiopathic interstitial
pneumonias [ 16], chest HRCT patterns were classified
into UIP (including definite UIP and probable UIP), non -
specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), organizing pneu -
monia (OP), and unclassifiable interstitial pneumonia
(unclassifiable IP). For the purpose of statistical analysis,
NSIP , OP , and unclassifiable IP were collectively catego-
rized as “Other IIPs” . Owing to the lung volume preser -
vation effect, pulmonary function tests have inherent
limitations in assessing emphysema combined with lung
fibrosis. In this study, emphysema was primarily evalu -
ated through imaging examinations. Emphysema was
defined as low attenuation areas occupying ≥ 5% of the
lung volume on chest CT.
Follow-up continued through November 2022 to mini -
mize potential bias from coronavirus disease 2019 in
China. During follow-up, outpatient records (typically
every 6–12 months) and hospitalization records were
collected, and telephone interviews were conducted
when necessary to assess clinical changes. The endpoints
were defined as (1) progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF)
according to the current criteria [ 3], and (2) all-cause
mortality. Pulmonary fibrosis progression was assessed
at each follow-up visit using a 12-month retrospective
window. Progression-free survival was calculated from
the baseline until the occurrence of endpoints or the last
follow-up.
During follow-up visits, we utilized the mMRC scale to
assess the worsening of patients’ symptoms.The mMRC
scale is a self-rating tool to measure the degree of disabil -
ity that breathlessness poses on day-to-day activities on
a scale from 0 to 4 [ 17]: 0, no breathlessness except on
strenuous exercise; 1, shortness of breath when hurry -
ing on the level or walking up a slight hill; 2, walks slower
than people of same age on the level because of breath -
lessness or has to stop to catch breath when walking at
their own pace on the level; 3, stops for breath after walk-
ing 100 m or after few minutes on the level; and 4, too
breathless to leave the house, or breathless when dressing
or undressing. Previous research has demonstrated that
the mMRC score not only reflects the severity of dyspnea
in IPF patients but also serves as an indicator for health-
related quality of life (HRQoL) and overall symptom bur -
den [18].
The progression of pulmonary fibrosis in patients was
independently assessed by the thoracic radiologist and
pulmonologist in patients with asbestosis and IPF. Both
specialists were certified by the National Health Com -
mission of China for diagnosing occupational respiratory
diseases, based on national criterion that is in line with
ILO classification guidelines. In cases of disagreement
between the pulmonologist and radiologist after the ini -
tial assessment, consultation was sought from a senior
ILD expert.
Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Sta -
tistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).
Continuous variables with non-normal distributions are
expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR),
and categorical variables as frequencies and percent -
ages. Intergroup comparisons were performed using
the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and
the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical
variables.
Progression-free survival was estimated using the
Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups
were evaluated with the log-rank test. Univariable and
multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were
applied to identify predictors of disease progression
and mortality. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence
intervals (CIs) were reported. A two-sided P value < 0.05
was considered statistically significant.
Results
Baseline characteristics
A total of 802 patients were included, comprising 254
(31.67%) with asbestosis and 548 (68.33%) with IPF. The
median age was 67(IQR 62, 73)yrs, with 68.33% being
male and 58.85% current or former smokers (Table  1).
Compared to IPF patients, those with asbestosis were
older and had significantly lower proportions of males
and smokers. IPF patients presented with lower mean
arterial oxygen tension and reduced diffusing capacity for
carbon monoxide (DLCO% predicted). On chest high-
resolution computed tomography (HRCT), a definite
or probable UIP pattern was predominantly observed
in IPF patients, while only 14.17% of asbestosis patients

was administered to 30.11% of IPF patients, whereas

Comorbidities
Patients with asbestosis demonstrated significantly ele -
vated comorbidity burden compared to IPF patients, as
evidenced by higher median comorbidity counts and CCI
scores (Table S2). Asbestosis patients had a significantly
higher frequencies of asthma, benign pleural diseases
(including diffused pleural thickening, plaques, and effu -
sion), arrhythmias, heart failure, hypertension, hyper -
lipidemia, cerebrovascular disease, connective tissue
diseases, and lung cancer (all P < 0.05) (Table S1, Fig. 2 A).
In contrast, IPF patients had a significantly higher preva -
lence of emphysema compared to those with asbestosis
(P < 0.001).
IPF had a significantly higher proportion of individuals
with 1–3 comorbidities compared to those with asbes -
tosis (66.24% vs. 47.64%, P < 0.05). Conversely, the pro -
portions of asbestosis with 4–6 comorbidities and ≥ 7
comorbidities were significantly higher than those of IPF
patients (Table S2).
After categorizing comorbidities into respiratory dis -
eases, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases,
malignant tumors, metabolic-related diseases, and other
diseases, comparisons were made between the two
groups (Table S3). The prevalence of respiratory diseases
(79.13% vs. 60.04%), cardiovascular and cerebrovascular
diseases (60.24% vs. 48.54%), malignant tumors (12.60%
vs. 4.93%), and other diseases (25.20% vs. 17.52%) were
higher in asbestosis patients than in IPF patients (all
P < 0.05).
Outcomes and disease deterioration
Longitudinal follow-up data were available for 97 asbes -
tosis patients (median follow-up: 59 months; IQR: 40–73)
and 264 IPF patients (median follow-up: 38 months; IQR:
20–54) (Table S4, Figure S1). During a median follow-up
of 44 months (IQR 24–61 months), the cumulative inci -
dence of endpoint events was significantly higher in IPF
than in asbestosis. Specifically, the cumulative progres -
sion rate of pulmonary fibrosis was 69.53% versus 86.60%,
and the cumulative all-cause mortality rate was 16.39%
versus 59.33%, respectively (Table  2). IPF patients had
significantly lower overall and progression-free survival
rates than those with asbestosis, as shown in Kaplan–
Meier curves using both PPF (log-rank P < 0.001, Fig. 2B)
and mortality endpoints (log-rank P < 0.001, Fig. 2C).
Risk factors for PPF or ortality
In multivariable Cox regression analysis, definite UIP
pattern (aHR 5.36, 95% CI 1.77–16.19; P = 0.003) was
independent predictors of pulmonary fibrosis progres -
sion in asbestosis (Table  3). For IPF, independent predic-
tors of pulmonary fibrosis progression included baseline
hypoxemia (adjusted HR [aHR] 2.49, 95% CI 1.32–4.69;
P = 0.005), forced vital capacity (FVC)% predicted < 80%
(aHR 1.44, 95% CI 1.03–2.01; P = 0.033), and definite
UIP pattern (aHR 1.41, 95% CI 1.01–1.98; P = 0.045)
(Table 4). And emphysema (aHR 0.66, 95% CI 0.48–0.90;
P = 0.008) was a protective factor against progression in
IPF patients.

Discussion
This cohort study revealed distinct clinical profiles
between asbestosis and IPF. Asbestosis patients had a
broader comorbidity burden, including asthma, lung can-
cer, cardiovascular disease, and CTD, whereas emphy -
sema was more common in IPF. Both diseases carried
high risks of fibrosis progression and mortality, but via
different mechanisms. In IPF, progression was indepen -
dently associated with baseline hypoxemia, reduced lung
function (FVC% predicted < 80%), and a definite UIP pat-
tern, while in asbestosis, progression was mainly linked
to UIP . Mortality also differed: asbestosis-related deaths
were driven by asbestos-related complications such as
lung cancer, PH, and atrial fibrillation, whereas IPF mor -
tality was related to impaired lung function and PH.
Overall, fibrosis severity and asbestos-related complica -
tions were key outcome drivers.
A comparison of 22 comorbidities between asbes -
tosis and IPF revealed distinct profiles. In asbestosis,
benign pleural lesions are considered markers of asbestos
exposure [19]. Asbestos exposure may lead to the benign
pleurisy and calcification [ 20]. These manifestations,
even with low-level exposure, often present as pleural
plaques. Fibrotic bands and peribronchiolar and alveolar
fibrosis frequently coexist with pleural plaques [ 21]. The
predominant chest HRCT findings of asbestosis/UIP are
irregular reticulation and honeycombing, predominantly
in the lower lungs, which may be accompanied by pleural
thickening and plaque formation. This pleural involve -
ment represents the most significant distinguishing fea -
ture from IPF-UIP . Inhaled asbestos fibers contribute to
tumorigenesis through multiple mechanisms, with asbes-
tos exposure increasing the risk of lung carcinoma across
all histological types, with a reported prevalence of 15.5%
[19, 22–25]. Asbestosis generally has a long latency
period, and patients tend to be older. In the present
cohort, the prevalence of arrhythmias, cardiac diseases,
and cerebrovascular disease was higher in asbestosis
patients than in IPF patients, reflecting comorbidities
commonly associated with aging [ 26]. A meta-analysis of

with aging and cellular senescence [ 36–39]. The elevated
prevalence of diabetes mellitus in IPF aligns with genetic
studies suggesting a causal relationship, in which hyper -

activating lung fibroblasts [40–44].
PPF patients exhibited a higher incidence of hypox -

previous studies, coexisting emphysema may preserve
lung volume in pulmonary fibrosis [ 61–63]. Consis -

81.95% in those without emphysema ( P = 0.002), thus
supporting the notion that it likely explains a protective
effect against disease progression in IPF. Previous stud -

bined with emphysema have larger lung capacity and
higher survival rates compared to those with UIP alone
[64]. PH secondary to asbestosis or IPF is classified as
Group 3 (PH due to lung diseases and/or hypoxia), with
hypoxic vasoconstriction as a key mechanism [ 65]. PH
significantly increases mortality in IPF and is an inde -
pendent predictor [ 66–68]. The relationship between
asbestosis and PH is less well defined. Histopathological
studies, including experimental models and autopsies,
reveal severe perivascular fibrosis in early-stage asbesto -
sis, leading to hemodynamic impairment consistent with
early PH [69]. Clinically, PH prevalence is higher in pneu-
moconiosis patients than in the general population [6]. In
coal workers’ pneumoconiosis, 5-year mortality reaches
60% when mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) is ≥
20 mmHg, and 1-year mortality rises to 70% when mPAP
exceeds 30 mmHg [ 70], indicating that PH progression
correlates with adverse outcomes in pneumoconiosis.
This study compared comorbidity profiles and risk fac -
tors for pulmonary fibrosis progression and mortality
between asbestosis and IPF, revealing distinct patterns
in comorbidity burden and risk profiles. Nevertheless,
several limitations should be noted. First, the inclusion
of patients with baseline pulmonary function and chest
CT data in this study may introduce selection bias, as
it excluded those who were unable to complete pulmo -
nary function tests or leave the ward for a chest CT due
to more severe conditions. Consequently, the generaliz -
ability of our findings to critically ill patients is limited. In
addition, about one-third of IPF patients received antifi -
brotic therapy, which could have attenuated progression.
Second, the incidence of lung cancer in IPF (2.01%) was
lower than earlier reports, likely reflecting exposure to
chrysotile asbestos or delayed detection. Among 264 IPF
patients with complete follow-up, baseline lung cancer
prevalence was 2.27%, with 20.5% showing nodular find -
ings on HRCT. After a median follow-up of 38 months
(IQR 20–54), 10 patients with baseline nodules were
diagnosed with lung cancer. In addition, mesothelioma
incidence in asbestosis patients was notably low, with
0.6–30 cases per million person-years reported in west -
ern countries compared to only 1.5 per million in China
during 2008–2012 [ 71, 72], suggesting possible underre -
porting or missed diagnoses.
In conclusion, although asbestosis and IPF share clini -
cal similarities, they differ in comorbidities and prognos -
tic drivers. In asbestosis, a definite UIP pattern predicted
progression, while in IPF, hypoxemia, FVC < 80% pre -
dicted, definite UIP , asthma, and PH were risk factors.
Mortality was linked to lung cancer, PH, and atrial fibril -
lation in asbestosis, and to FVC < 80% predicted and PH
in IPF. These findings highlight the need for disease-spe -
cific risk stratification to guide management.
