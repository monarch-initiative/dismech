# A germline ETV6 mutation disrupts hematopoiesis via de novo creation of a nuclear export signal.
**Authors:** McConville M, Thomas T, Beckner R, Kroger B, Valadez C, Chook YM, Chung S, Liszczak G
**Journal:** Sci Adv (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1126/sciadv.adu4058](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adu4058)

## Content

Germline mutations in the transcriptional regulator ETV6 are a root cause of familial inherited thrombocytopenia and predispose carriers to myelodysplastic syndromes and acute leukemias. Here, we report that the ETV6 P214L mutation creates an XPO1-dependent nuclear export signal to cause protein mislocalization. Strategies to disrupt XPO1 nuclear export activity fully restore ETV6 P214L protein nuclear localization and transcription regulation activity, establishing XPO1-dependent mislocalization as a critical mechanism underscoring ETV6 P214L dysfunction. Mechanistic insight inspired the design of "humanized" ETV6 mice in which the germline P214L mutation is sufficient to elicit severe defects in thrombopoiesis and hematopoietic stem cell maintenance and survival in animals. These studies define a unique mechanism by which the ETV6 P214L mutation exerts a dominant negative effect on protein function and reveal critical mutation-dependent disruptions to hematopoiesis that underlie disease phenotypes.

The transcriptional repressor protein ETV6 plays a critical role in hematopoiesis, including hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) maintenance, thrombopoiesis, and lymphopoiesis (1, 2). Many unique ETV6 genetic alterations have been characterized as critical contributors to myeloid and lymphoid leukemogenesis (3–10). Given their pervasive nature and high penetrance, intense efforts to elucidate how ETV6 mutations lead to protein dysfunction and disease phenotypes are ongoing. To date, more than 30 cancer-associated chromosomal translocation events involving ETV6 have been reported (10). While these translocations can create chimeric transcription factors or tyrosine kinases that are critical for transformation, it is often the loss of ETV6 protein that is a key event in disease onset. Consistently, loss-of-function somatic ETV6 missense mutations are recurrently observed in myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and leukemia sequencing analyses (5, 6, 11, 12). More recently, familial inherited autosomal dominant ETV6 missense mutations have been reported, and carriers segregate with variable thrombocytopenia and an elevated risk for MDS and leukemia (13–15). These disease associations are in line with phenotypes observed in conditional Etv6 knockout mice, which demonstrate a role for ETV6 protein in adult HSC survival and megakaryocyte maturation (1, 2).

There are two “hotspots” within the ETV6 protein where somatic and germline missense mutations commonly occur (16, 17). The first hotspot is the ETS family DNA binding (ETS) domain, wherein a cluster of mutations occurs that disrupt the DNA:protein interaction (16). The second hotspot is the P214 site, which lies within a long, unstructured linker that connects the N-terminal pointed (PNT) domain to the ETS domain (13, 15). This site does not fall within a functionally annotated region of ETV6, and the mechanism whereby a P214 mutation abrogates ETV6 activity is unclear. In the case of ETV6P214L germline mutations, all carriers are heterozygous for the allele and thrombocytopenic, and approximately 30% will develop leukemia in their lifetime (17). Similar to many ETS domain mutations, including R399C and R418G, the P214L mutation causes ETV6 mislocalization to the cytoplasm (13, 15). It has therefore been hypothesized that the P214L mutation abrogates DNA binding via an allosteric mechanism and that loss of DNA binding activity causes ETV6 cytoplasmic accumulation (15). However, there is no experimental data to support this model. Furthermore, mice carrying the homologous Etv6P216L/WT or Etv6P216L/P216 allele do not exhibit overt hematopoietic defects (18). These confounding observations have raised questions about the P214L mutation and its role in ETV6 dysfunction and hematologic disease.

Here, we set out to understand how the enigmatic P214L mutation disrupts ETV6 protein localization and function. We have found that the P214L mutation creates a potent nuclear export signal (NES) and that ETV6 protein mislocalization is dependent on the activity of the nuclear export receptor, XPO1. This mechanism explains the dominant-negative ETV6P214L/WT phenotype, as wild-type and mutant ETV6 protein copies oligomerize via the PNT domain, resulting in nuclear export of both constructs. We also find that strategies to disrupt the mutant ETV6:XPO1 interaction restore ETV6 protein nuclear localization, rescue ETV6 transcription regulation activity, and restore Mendelian inheritance patterns in mice. We therefore conclude that nuclear exclusion is the primary driver of ETV6 P214L protein dysfunction. Moreover, we found that the synonymous P216L mutation in the mouse ETV6 (mETV6) protein does not create an NES due to lack of strict local sequence conservation, thus explaining the lack of hematopoietic defects in current animal models. We therefore generated Etv6P216L/WT mice with an accompanying humanizing mutation in the mutant Etv6 allele. Notably, these mice are thrombocytopenic and exhibit robust HSC defects as identified in competitive bone marrow transplant assays. In addition, lineage-specific transcriptome-wide gene set enrichment analyses specifically define HSC defects in mice, which uncover unique functions for ETV6 at different stages of hematopoietic differentiation and provide a rational explanation for why human carriers are strongly predisposed to MDS.

The P214L mutation has no impact on recombinant ETV6 behavior or DNA binding activity in biochemical assays (Fig. 1A). Consistently, AlphaFold prediction of the full-length ETV6 structure does not suggest P214 interaction with the ETS or PNT domains (Fig. 1B). To further assess potential P214L-dependent effects on ETV6 protein function, we set out to determine the minimal ETV6 protein construct required for P214L-dependent protein mislocalization in mammalian cells. Stable expression of full-length ETV6 protein in HeLa cells followed by immunofluorescence analysis with an ETV6 antibody shows clear nuclear exclusion of the mutant construct (Fig. 1C). Unexpectedly, P214L-mediated nuclear exclusion was maintained after the removal of the ETS and PNT domains from the protein (fig. S1). We note that this construct included a strong nuclear localization signal (the SV40 NLS) to compensate for the loss of the endogenous ETV6 protein nuclear import mechanism (19). Further truncation analysis identified residues 203 to 245 as the minimal ETV6 protein sequence required for P214L-dependent mislocalization of the mCherry-NLS construct (Fig. 1D). Thus, it is clear that the P214L mutation does not cause mislocalization via interference with known ETV6 activities, including DNA binding and homotypic oligomerization.

In diseases associated with an ETV6 mutation at the P214 site, patients exclusively exhibit proline substitution to a leucine residue (13, 15). To further probe the mechanism of mutation-dependent mislocalization, we interrogated the importance of residue identity at amino acid 214. We engineered single base pair substitutions within the proline 214 codon of the mCherry-ETV6203–245-NLS ectopic expression construct for subcellular localization assays in mammalian cells (Fig. 1D and fig. S2). In all cases, including proline-214 mutation to arginine and serine, mutant protein localization was indistinguishable from the wild-type ETV6 protein fragment. Moreover, the complete removal of the proline-214 codon also yields a protein construct that localizes to the nucleus (fig. S2). Therefore, the mislocalization phenotype is not due to loss of a proline at this position but rather by gain of a leucine residue. Among plausible explanations, these observations are consistent with the creation of a protein interaction motif.

Analysis of P214L-proximal protein sequence composition revealed a preponderance of bulky aliphatic residues upstream of the mutation site, including Leu205, Ile209, and Leu212 (Fig. 1E). This relatively high concentration of hydrophobic amino acids is uncommon in protein regions lacking structural order. Short leucine-rich sequences that reside within disordered protein segments were the earliest found “leucine-rich NESs” (20–22). These NESs are directly engaged by XPO1, which is a nuclear export receptor that recognizes linear sequence motifs comprising four to five bulky hydrophobic residues with specific spacing patterns (23). While the wild-type ETV6 protein has no predictable NES, the P214L mutation creates a class 1a XPO1 interaction motif from residues 205 to 214 (Fig. 1E). Consistently, the commonly used NES prediction software LocNES (24) identifies a strong NES from residues 205 to 214 in the mutant ETV6 construct and no NES in the corresponding region of the wild-type protein (fig. S3).

To test our hypothesis that the P214L mutation causes ETV6 protein mislocalization via creation of an NES, we treated live HeLa cells expressing the mCherry-ETV6 P214L203–245-NLS construct with the small-molecule XPO1 inhibitor Leptomycin B (25) and captured fluorescent images every 6 min (Fig. 1F and movie S1). Within 30 min of exposure to 5 nM Leptomycin B, the mCherry-ETV6 P214L203–245-NLS construct had completely accumulated in the nucleus in all cells observed. Microscopy image-based quantification was used to show that Leptomycin B treatment restores the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic distribution ratio of full-length ETV6 P214L protein to that of wild-type protein (Fig. 1G). This experiment demonstrates that the ETV6 P214L protein is constantly being imported into the nucleus and that nuclear import of ETV6 is far less efficient than P214L-dependent export activity. Similar results were obtained with the ETV6 P214L protein and when Leptomycin B was replaced with the chemically distinct XPO1 inhibitor Selinexor (26), which is a Food and Drug Administration–approved anticancer agent. Notably, XPO1 inhibition has no impact on the cellular localization of ETV6 proteins harboring R399C or R418G mutations, suggesting a different mechanism of mislocalization for these protein variants (fig. S4).

As an alternate approach to chemical XPO1 manipulation, we generated an ETV6 P214L construct with a second mutation, I209S, that abolishes the XPO1 interaction motif. The ETV6 I209S/P214L double mutant protein (henceforth “ETV6 P214Lnuc”) was stably expressed in HeLa cells and imaged via immunofluorescence (Fig. 1F). Consistent with chemical inhibition studies, the genetic disruption of the XPO1 interaction motif fully restores the nuclear localization of an ETV6 protein bearing the P214L mutation (Fig. 1G).

We next sought to validate and quantitatively assess the ETV6 P214L:XPO1 interaction in a highly controlled setting. A fluorescence polarization-based assay was devised in which a recombinantly expressed and purified complex of XPO1 and its obligate auxiliary component RanGTP (20–22, 27) was titrated into various synthetic, fluorescently labeled ETV6203–245 protein fragments. Consistent with cellular studies, the P214L mutant protein exhibits robust binding to XPO1, whereas binding cannot be detected with the wild-type counterpart (Fig. 1H). Notably, XPO1 engages the ETV6 P214L protein fragment with an affinity constant (261 nM) that is comparable to that of the well-established XPO1 ligand peptide PKI (65 nM) (28). Similarly, glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down studies with recombinant proteins showed a P214L-dependent interaction between the GST-XPO1:RanGTP complex and full-length ETV6 (fig. S5). Together, our results definitively demonstrate that the P214L mutation creates an XPO1 interaction motif that is responsible for ETV6 cellular mislocalization.

The heterozygous ETV6 P214L mutation is known to disrupt the localization of both mutant and wild-type ETV6 protein copies within the cell (13, 29), although the mechanistic basis for this observation is not known. Our data offer a rational hypothesis for the dominant negative phenotype wherein PNT domain-mediated oligomerization leads to export of mutant and wild-type ETV6 proteins. To test this, hemagglutinin (HA)–tagged P214L protein and Myc-tagged wild-type protein were expressed together or separately in mammalian cells, and immunofluorescence assays were performed to determine localization of each construct (Fig. 2A). While wild-type ETV6 on its own localizes to the nucleus as expected, coexpression with ETV6 P214L induces mislocalization of the wild-type protein to the cytoplasm. In this context, XPO1 inhibitors rescue the localization of both mutant and wild-type ETV6 protein constructs. We next coexpressed ETV6 wild-type protein and an ETV6 P214L protein bearing two PNT domain mutations (A93D and V112E) that abolish oligomerization activity (Fig. 2A) (30). While the oligomerization-deficient P214L protein localizes to the cytoplasm, the wild-type protein localizes to the nucleus. Therefore, PNT domain-mediated oligomerization creates complexes of mutant and wild-type ETV6 protein copies that are exported from the nucleus by XPO1, ultimately leading to the dominant-negative nature of the P214L mutation (Fig. 2B).

We have shown that strategies to disrupt the ETV6 P214L:XPO1 interface effectively restore nuclear localization of the mutant ETV6 protein. However, it is possible that the P214L mutation also affects ETV6 function in a manner that would render the protein inactive regardless of proper localization. To investigate this, we implemented an established cellular assay for ETV6 activity (16). In this assay, the introduction of active ETV6 suppresses growth of NRASG12D-transformed Ba/F3 cells (Ba/F3NRAS) (Fig. 3A). Wild-type, P214L, or P214Lnuc ETV6 constructs were stably integrated into Ba/F3NRAS cells, and growth kinetics was measured over a 7-day period (Fig. 3B). As expected, wild-type ETV6 protein localizes to the nucleus and strongly inhibits NRASG12D-mediated transformation, while the P214L variant accumulates in the cytoplasm (fig. S6A) and has no effect on Ba/F3NRAS growth rate. The ETV6 P214Lnuc protein localizes to the nucleus and suppresses Ba/F3NRAS growth in a manner that is nearly identical to that of wild-type ETV6. Notably, the P214L mutation has no impact on ETV6 protein levels in cells (fig. S6B). These results indicate that the ETV6 P214L variant can function as a suppressor of transformed Ba/F3 cell growth once nuclear localization is restored.

To further investigate the transcriptional repressor properties of the nuclear ETV6 P214L, we isolated total RNA from Ba/F3NRAS cells expressing each ETV6 variant for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) characterization. Hierarchical clustering and principal components analysis (PCA) analysis of global gene expression profiles show notable overlap between Ba/F3NRAS cells expressing the wild-type ETV6 and ETV6 P214Lnuc proteins (Fig. 3, C and D). However, very little overlap is observed between the wild-type transcriptional profile and that of the P214L variant, which closely resembles Ba/F3NRAS cells lacking ETV6. We also analyzed individual the transcript abundance of known ETV6 target genes, including glutathione-specific gamma-glutamylcyclotransferase 2 (CHAC2), ETS-related gene (ERG), matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9), and plexin-C1 (PLXNC1) (Fig. 3E). In all cases, transcript levels in wild-type and ETV6 P214Lnuc-expressing Ba/F3NRAS cells are strongly down-regulated, while ETV6 P214L fails to elicit a transcriptional response. Our transcriptional profiling studies further support the conclusion that XPO1-dependent mislocalization is the primary driver of ETV6 P214L dysfunction.

Previously reported genetically engineered mice harboring a P216L mutation in the endogenous Etv6 allele have normal platelet counts and do not exhibit an overt hematopoietic defect (18). To investigate this perplexing observation, we introduced wild-type or P216L mETV6 constructs into NIH 3T3 cells for immunofluorescence imaging-based localization analysis (Fig. 4A). Unexpectedly, mETV6 wild-type and mETV6 P216L proteins localize to the nucleus with equal efficiency. A sequence alignment comparing human ETV6 and mETV6 revealed that the P216L mutation does not create an XPO1 interaction motif (Fig. 4B). This is due to variance at a single amino acid site, the S211 position in mice, which is an isoleucine residue in the human sequence. Coincidentally, this substitution in mETV6 precisely mimics our ETV6 P214Lnuc rescue construct. We found that a humanizing mutation at the mETV6 S211 site (S211I) does not disrupt mETV6 protein nuclear localization, while the S211I/P216L double mutation causes robust mETV6 nuclear exclusion (Fig. 4A). These data explain why the previously reported Etv6P216L/WT and Etv6P216L/P216L mice do not recapitulate the human disease phenotype.

We next generated three genetically engineered mouse lines using CRISPR-Cas9 to knock-in single-point mutations (S211I or P216L) or the double mutation (S211I/P216L) at the endogenous Etv6 locus. The Etv6S211I/WT and Etv6P216L/WT single mutant mouse lines exhibited Mendelian inheritance patterns, while the Etv6S211I P216L/WT double mutant line demonstrated a significant viability defect (Fig. 4C). We also collected bone marrow from heterozygous mice to determine mETV6 localization in megakaryocytes via immunofluorescence (Fig. 4D). Consistent with our cell culture results, mETV6 protein in Etv6S211I P216L/WT mice is mislocalized to the cytoplasm in megakaryocytes, while mice harboring a single S211I or P216L mutation exhibit proper mETV6 localization to the nucleus.

To determine mutation-dependent impacts on PB cell counts, CBC analysis was performed on the genetically engineered mouse lines and wild-type littermates (Fig. 4E). As observed with ETV6-deficient human patients (17), white and red blood cell counts are consistent across each genetic background. Similarly, mice carrying the mETV6 S211I or P216L protein mutations exhibit platelet counts resembling that of wild-type mice (Etv6S211I/WT = 1140 ± 155.1 × 103/μl, Etv6P216L/WT = 1065 ± 37.82 × 103/μl, Etv6WT/WT = 1254 ± 98.61 × 103/μl). However, all Etv6S211I P216L/WT double mutant mice are thrombocytopenic with platelet counts ~60% lower than wild-type littermates (485 ± 143.8 × 103/μl). Notably, ETV6P214L/WT patients exhibit notable similar platelet deficiencies, with platelet count decreases ~70% compared to noncarrier relatives (17). The platelet defect also persisted in aged mice, with similar results observed in mice greater than 10 months old (fig. S7). Additional characterization of myeloerythroid progenitors (31) revealed a decrease in megakaryocyte progenitors in Etv6S211I P216L/WT mice (Fig. 4F and fig. S8), phenocopying the megakaryocyte maturation defect that has been described in patients (13). Thus, as our biochemical studies suggest, the humanizing S211I mutation in mice is essential to properly model human ETV6 P214L dysfunction. Given the clear thrombocytopenia phenotype, the Etv6S211I P216L/WT mouse may also prove useful in the study of additional ETV6 mutation-associated blood diseases, including MDS and leukemia.

Beyond phenocopying the megakaryocyte maturation and thrombocytopenia that characterizes patients with ETV6 mutations, the Etv6S211I P216L/WT mouse also represents a new in vivo system to study the impact that mutation-engendered ETV6 protein dysfunction has on HSCs. Previous work has shown that the complete loss of Etv6 is embryonically lethal and that the conditional inactivation of Etv6 in the hematopoietic system leads to depletion of HSCs in adult mouse bone marrow (1, 2). Given that Etv6S211I P216L/WT mice are viable, in contrast to the phenotype of the Etv6 knockout mouse, we wondered if these mice would exhibit HSC defects. Flow cytometry analysis of bone marrow hematopoietic stem and progenitor populations revealed a significant decrease in the absolute number of Lin−Sca-1−c-Kit+ (L−S−K+) cells and HSCs in our Etv6S211I P216L/WT mice compared to Etv6WT/WT littermates (Fig. 5A and fig. S9). Notably, there was no difference in the frequency of myeloid progenitor populations, suggesting that the mETV6 S211I/P216L protein alteration may specifically impact HSCs.

We next performed RNA-seq on HSCs and megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEPs) purified from Etv6WT/WT and Etv6S211I P216L/WT mice. Suggestive of a functional self-renewal defect, a gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that Etv6S211I P216L/WT HSCs exhibit an increase in signatures related to hematopoietic progenitors, cell division, and the DNA damage response while exhibiting a decrease in stem cell signatures (Fig. 5, B to D; fig. S10, A and B; and dataset S1). Similar gene signatures have been previously reported in abnormal HSCs, particularly those that have lost quiescence (32, 33). The notable reduction in stemness observed in Etv6S211I P216L/WT HSCs is consistent with other MDS predisposition syndromes that exhibit decreased HSC self-renewal and that require cooperating mutations for progression to MDS or leukemia (34, 35), providing a mechanistic link between the ETV6 P214L mutation and the high MDS predisposition in human carriers (15). An MEP GSEA was highly consistent with a thrombopoiesis defect (fig. S10, C and D, and dataset S1), with increases in myeloid differentiation-associated genes (including Elane and Alox5), decreases in platelet-associated genes (including Axl, Smad4, and Dock7), and a global decrease in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which has been implicated in platelet function (36). There was no overlap between differentially expressed genes in Etv6S211I P216L/WT HSCs and MEPs (Fig. 5E). Thus, ETV6 mutations may have distinct cell context-specific effects at different stages of hematopoietic differentiation, with loss of HSC self-renewal leading to MDS predisposition and decreased platelet-associated factors in MEPs leading to thrombocytopenia.

To test whether the presence of mETV6 S211I/P216L protein leads to a cell autonomous defect in HSC self-renewal, we performed competitive bone marrow transplant assays, wherein equal numbers of bone marrow cells from control mice and Etv6 mutant mouse lines were co-injected into lethally irradiated recipient mice, and peripheral blood (PB) donor engraftment was measured over a 16-week period (Fig. 5F). We found that Etv6S211I P216L/WT-derived bone marrow cells exhibited a defect in engraftment in recipient mice when compared to mice that received cells from Etv6S211I/WT, Etv6P216L/WT, and Etv6WT/WT littermates (Fig. 5G). After 16 weeks, mice were euthanized, and hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell populations were analyzed. Of the genotypes tested, only donor Etv6S211I P216L/WT HSCs were significantly decreased in number and self-renewal quotient compared with donor HSCs from wild-type littermates, with the average donor chimerism value dropping to ~2.4% (Fig. 5H). In addition, in donor Etv6S211I P216L/WT cells, there was a decrease in the frequency of HSCs exhibiting high levels of c-Kit expression, a population that exhibits an intrinsic megakaryocyte lineage bias (Fig. 5I) (37). Collectively, our HSC analyses demonstrate that while P214L-mediated ETV6 dysfunction does not cause strict embryonic lethality, the mutation does have profound impacts on HSC maintenance and function. The Etv6S211I P216L/WT HSC defects may also explain the high incidence rate of MDS and leukemia in human ETV6P214L/WT carriers (13, 15, 17), as discussed in detail below.

Here, we have found that the enigmatic ETV6 P214L protein mutation creates an XPO1-dependent NES and that nuclear export activity is required for mutation-associated hematopoietic defects. Mechanistic insight enabled rational design of genetic and small molecule approaches to rescue ETV6 P214L protein localization in cells and explains the dominant negative phenotype. We were also able to address the perplexing observation that the currently studied Etv6P216L/WT and Etv6P216L/P216L mice fail to recapitulate most human disease phenotypes. The design of mice expressing humanized mETV6 protein, which properly model P214L-dependent dysfunction in hematopoiesis, was inspired by our mechanistic studies. Notably, the XPO1-dependent mislocalization mechanism is distinct from and potentially more damaging than traditional loss-of-function mutations in that it can also cause mislocalization of wild-type protein copies or other nuclear proteins by forming complexes that are shuttled out of the nucleus.

The previously reported Etv6P216L/WT and Etv6P216L/P216L mice exhibit only subtle hematopoietic deficits in specific contexts (18), raising questions about the discrepancies between mouse and human phenotypes. Our humanized Etv6S211I P216L/WT mouse represents a unique, genetically accurate model of ETV6 thrombocytopenia that recapitulates the degree of thrombocytopenia and megakaryocyte progenitor defects observed in patients. Our data and model posit continuous import and export of the mutant and wild-type ETV6 protein. Given that we are able to breed heterozygous Etv6S211I P216L/WT mice, we hypothesize that there remains a low fraction of ETV6 protein activity in the nucleus of our Etv6S211I P216L/WT mice. This explains why Etv6S211I P216L/WT mice are viable, while Etv6 knockout is embryonically lethal. The viability defects observed in our Etv6S211I P216L/WT mouse suggests that only a subpopulation of heterozygous mice have sufficient nuclear ETV6 protein activity during the crucial developmental stage of yolk sac angiogenesis, which fails in Etv6 knockout mice (38).

Our data show that ETV6 regulates unique transcriptional pathways in HSCs versus MEPs. The transcriptional abnormalities observed in the Etv6S211I P216L/WT mouse are in strong alignment with the defects observed in impaired HSC self-renewal and thrombopoiesis. GSEA revealed depletion of well-annotated stem cell self-renewal signatures (3, 4, 6, 7), which also provides a rational explanation for why human carriers are strongly predisposed to MDS. Notably, ETV6 has been demonstrated to repress inflammatory response genes (39), and elevated innate inflammation is a hallmark of MDS stem cells (40). Future hematopathology studies are needed to determine whether the ETV6 P214L mutation is sufficient for transformation to full MDS with aging or to affect inflammatory response pathway genes. Alternatively, cooperating mutations may be required, as have been described in patients (15). Our work provides a much-needed model to carry out these studies. Last, we observe in humanized Etv6S211I P216L/WT mice depletion of HSCs with high expression of c-Kit, a population previously described to have a megakaryocyte lineage (37), with megakaryocytes arising directly from such HSCs (41). This raises the possibility that the megakaryocyte progenitor defect and thrombocytopenia associated with ETV6 mutations arise directly from a defect in HSCs, which would mirror a similar mechanism described for RUNX1 mutation-associated thrombocytopenia (42).

Interestingly, multiple studies have demonstrated that XPO1 inhibitors cause thrombocytopenia and defects in HSC differentiation (43, 44). Therefore, we do not anticipate that XPO1 inhibitors will be useful for the treatment of ETV6 P214L-related familial thrombocytopenia. However, it is possible that XPO1 inhibitors will be of value for the treatment of cancers harboring ETV6 P214L mutant protein, as they could reactivate ETV6 function and restore its tumor suppressor activity. Further studies will be required to explore the efficacy of this therapeutic approach.

Mutations that disrupt protein folding to unmask an otherwise inaccessible NES or induce a frameshift that creates an NES have been previously reported (45–48). The ETV6 P214L missense mutation is novel in that it directly converts the surrounding sequence into an NES. More work in this area may reveal unique mutations in other proteins and diseases that also operate via direct creation of an NES. However, the interplay between nuclear import strength and nuclear export strength for a given protein is complex and can be differentially regulated in various cell types and stages of development. Therefore, rigorous cellular and disease models, as described here for the ETV6 P214L mutation, will be essential to probe the broad nature of this mechanism. Regardless, our study demonstrates that de novo creation of an NES via a missense mutation can serve as an underlying mechanism for protein dysfunction and disease.

Bone marrow cells were isolated for analysis by flushing two femurs of adult male mice. For HSC isolations, cells were isolated by crushing the spine, femurs, tibias, and pelvic bones of mice with a mortar and pestle into PBS with 2% fetal bovine serum, followed by filtration through a 70-μm strainer. C-kit enrichment was completed using c-kit microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec) and following the standard manufacturer’s protocol.

Female C57BL/6 mice (CD45.1 Pep boy, the Jackson Laboratory) were used as recipient mice in transplant experiments. Recipient mice were lethally irradiated with two 550-cGy doses (XRAD 320 irradiator, Precision X-Ray) given at least 3 hours apart. Within 24 hours of irradiation, 5.0 × 105 donor cells and 5.0 × 105 recipient cells were transplanted via retro-orbital injection.

Blood was collected by lateral tail vein collection using capillary blood collection tubes (Greiner Bio-One MiniCollect). Donor PB chimerism was measured in recipient mice every 4 weeks. PB specimens underwent red blood cell lysis with ammonium chloride potassium (ACK) lysis buffer (Gibco) followed by staining with antibodies against CD45.1, CD45.2, Gr-1, CD11b, B220, and CD3. Complete blood counts were obtained using a Procyte Dx Hematology Analyzer (IDEXX) according to standard manufacturer’s instructions.

At 16 weeks after transplant, bone marrow cells were isolated from two femurs of recipient mice as detailed above and stained with antibodies against CD45.1, CD45.2, lineage (CD3, CD4, CD8, B220, CD11b, Gr-1, and Ter119), c-kit, sca-1, CD48, and CD150.

All statistical analysis was done using GraphPad Prism 10.0.0.
