</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abs0010"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Recurrent somatic variants in <em>SPOP</em> are cancer specific; endometrial and prostate cancers result from gain-of-function and dominant-negative effects toward BET proteins, respectively. By using clinical exome sequencing, we identified six <em>de novo</em> pathogenic missense variants in <em>SPOP</em> in seven individuals with developmental delay and/or intellectual disability, facial dysmorphisms, and congenital anomalies. Two individuals shared craniofacial dysmorphisms, including congenital microcephaly, that were strikingly different from those of the other five individuals, who had (relative) macrocephaly and hypertelorism. We measured the effect of <em>SPOP</em> variants on BET protein amounts in human Ishikawa endometrial cancer cells and patient-derived cell lines because we hypothesized that variants would lead to functional divergent effects on BET proteins. The <em>de novo</em> variants c.362G&gt;A (p.Arg121Gln) and c. 430G&gt;A (p.Asp144Asn), identified in the first two individuals, resulted in a gain of function, and conversely, the c.73A&gt;G (p.Thr25Ala), c.248A&gt;G (p.Tyr83Cys), c.395G&gt;T (p.Gly132Val), and c.412C&gt;T (p.Arg138Cys) variants resulted in a dominant-negative effect. Our findings suggest that these opposite functional effects caused by the variants in <em>SPOP</em> result in two distinct and clinically recognizable syndromic forms of intellectual disability with contrasting craniofacial dysmorphisms.</p>
<section id="kwrds0010" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> SPOP, neurodevelopmental disorder, <em>de novo</em> mutation, germ line mutation, BET protein, missense mutation, craniofacial dysmorphisms, microcephaly, macrocephaly, intellectual disabilty syndrome</p></section></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Main Text</h2>
<p id="p0010">Pathogenic variants in a considerable number of highly mutable genes lead to cancer when they occur in somatic cells, and they can lead to neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD) if occur in the germline or early in the embryonic development.<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1"><sup>1</sup></a> They frequently disrupt normal cell proliferation and/or differentiation while evading cellular death. Moreover, mutational hotspots in both somatic cell lines and germlines point toward analogous functional effects of pathogenic variants. Examples include gain-of-function variants in genes of the RAS-MAP kinase pathway, such as <em>PTPN11</em>,<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2"><sup>2</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3"><sup>3</sup></a> and loss-of-function variants in several genes of pathways that regulate chromatin remodeling, such as <em>ASXL1</em>.<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4"><sup>4</sup></a><sup>, </sup><a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5"><sup>5</sup></a><sup>, </sup><a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6"><sup>6</sup></a> Nevertheless, recognizing the clinical relevance and investigating the functional impact of <em>de novo</em> missense mutations in genes associated with NDD remains challenging. By using <em>in silico</em> and <em>in vitro</em> analyses, we examined the effect of <em>de novo</em> clustered missense <em>SPOP</em> variants identified in individuals with NDD on protein interactions.</p>
<p id="p0015"><em>SPOP</em> (MIM: <a href="https://omim.org/entry/602650" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">602650</a>) encodes the speckle-type POZ (pox virus and zinc finger protein; SPOP) protein in humans. SPOP homodimers function as a substrate adaptor of a larger cullin3-RING-based ubiquitin ligase complex that mediates the ubiquitination of target proteins; this ubiquitination usually leads to proteasomal degradation of the proteins.<a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7"><sup>7</sup></a> SPOP contains an evolutionarily conserved <em>m</em>eprin <em>a</em>nd tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-receptor associated factor (TRAF) <em>h</em>omology (MATH) domain; a bric-a-brac, tramtrack, and broad complex (BTB) domain (also known as a POZ domain); a three-box domain; and a C-terminal nuclear localization sequence.<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8"><sup>8</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9"><sup>9</sup></a> The MATH domain mediates interaction with protein-ubiquitin ligase substrates, such as BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4 proteins, which are collectively referred to as BETs.<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10"><sup>10</sup></a></p>
<p id="p0020">Somatic missense <em>SPOP</em> variants restricted to the MATH domain are frequently identified in prostate cancer<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11"><sup>11</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12"><sup>12</sup></a> and endometrial cancer.<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10"><sup>10</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13"><sup>13</sup></a> Indeed, with up to 6–15% of localized prostate tumors harboring acquired heterozygous missense <em>SPOP</em> variants<em>, SPOP</em> is the most commonly point-mutated gene in prostate cancer.<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12"><sup>12</sup></a> These missense <em>SPOP</em> variants act in a dominant-negative fashion to repress ubiquitination and degradation of oncogenic substrate proteins.<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14"><sup>14</sup></a><sup>, </sup><a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15"><sup>15</sup></a><sup>, </sup><a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16"><sup>16</sup></a><sup>, </sup><a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17"><sup>17</sup></a> In contrast, recurrent missense <em>SPOP</em> variants in endometrial cancer result in a SPOP gain of function leading to enhanced degradation of distinct protein substrates, including the BET proteins.<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10"><sup>10</sup></a></p>
<p id="p0025">Two unrelated individuals with the same <em>de novo SPOP</em> variant were identified via trio-based clinical exome sequencing. One was in a cohort of 4,749 individuals with unexplained intellectual disability (ID) ascertained by the Department of Human Genetics of Radboud University Medical Center (Nijmegen, the Netherlands), and the other was ascertained at the Department of Genetics, University Medical Center Utrecht (Utrecht, the Netherlands). A third individual was identified in a cohort of 1,133 children with severe, undiagnosed developmental disorders through the DDD research variant list on DECIPHER.<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18"><sup>18</sup></a> Additionally, four individuals with <em>SPOP</em> variants detected by clinical exome sequencing were identified via GeneMatcher.<a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19"><sup>19</sup></a> One girl with ID was referred to the Molecular Diagnostic Laboratory at the University of Chicago for clinical exome sequencing (Chicago, Illinois, USA). The other individuals were selected from a cohort of 14,183 individuals who had neurodevelopmental disorders and who underwent trio-based clinical exome sequencing at GeneDx (Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA). All legal representatives signed consent to share medical information and clinical photographs, and this study was approved by the institutional review board Commissie Mensgebonden Onderzoek Regio Arnhem-Nijmegen under CMO approval number NL36191.091.11.</p>
<p id="p0030">Six unique <em>de novo</em> missense variants in <em>SPOP</em> (GenBank: <a href="http://ncbi-n:NM_001007226.1" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NM_001007226.1</a>) were identified in peripheral blood of these seven individuals with intellectual disability: c.73A&gt;G, p.Thr25Ala; c.248A&gt;G, p.Tyr83Cys; c.362G&gt;A, p.Arg121Gln; c.395G&gt;T, p.Gly132Val; a recurrent c.412C&gt;T, p.Arg138Cys; and c.430G&gt;A, p.Asp144Asn (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>; <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Tables 1</a> and <a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">2</a>; <a href="#mmc2" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>). All variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. With the exception of p.Thr25Ala, which is positioned six amino acids before the start of the MATH domain, all variants were located within the MATH domain. Three of these variants are identical to those described in cancers of the prostate, endometrium, lung, and large intestine (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). <em>In silico</em> analyses, which we performed to assess evolutionary constraints on SPOP, showed that all affected amino acids are fully conserved down to <em>C. elegans</em> (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S1</a>). None of the variants were reported in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD),<a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20"><sup>20</sup></a> and the CADD (combined annotation-dependent depletion) score<a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21"><sup>21</sup></a> was higher than 25 for all variants. In ExAC,<a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20"><sup>20</sup></a>
<em>SPOP</em> contains fewer missense variants than expected (<em>Z</em> score = 4.74), and no loss of function variant is described (pLi = 0.99). In the DGV (Database of Genomic Variants; March 2019),<a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22"><sup>22</sup></a> no CNVs, inversions, or indels were reported. We used MetaDome<a href="#bib23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib23"><sup>23</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24"><sup>24</sup></a> to generate a tolerance landscape, which is a regional tolerance plot for genetic variation and is based on the ratio of observed missense and synonymous (<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M1" altimg="si1.gif"><mrow><msub><mrow><mi>d</mi></mrow><mrow><mi>N</mi></mrow></msub><mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">/</mo><msub><mrow><mi>d</mi></mrow><mrow><mi>S</mi></mrow></msub></mrow></math></span>) variants that are included in gnomAD r2.0<a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20"><sup>20</sup></a> and are found in the protein-coding region of <em>SPOP</em>. All <em>de novo</em> SPOP variants are located in regions that are extremely intolerant to missense variants (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S2</a>). We investigated the 3D location of the variants by using the experimentally solved 3D-conformation of the dimeric SPOP structure (PDB file 3hqi; <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>A).<a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25"><sup>25</sup></a> The structure was analyzed with the YASARA and WHAT IF Twinset.<a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26"><sup>26</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27"><sup>27</sup></a> This analysis suggested that four of the six substitutions, p.Tyr83Cys, p.Arg121Gln, p.Gly132Val, and p.Arg138Cys, would directly affect the binding of substrates to SPOP. For the p.Asp144Asn and p.Thr25Ala variants, prediction of the binding consequences was non-informative. The p.Asp144Asn substitution was not predicted to have a large effect on the local protein structure, and residue Thr 25 is too distant from the binding cleft.</p>

<figcaption><p><em>De Novo SPOP</em> Variants Produce Opposing Effects on BET Amounts</p>
<p>(A) 3D structure of an asymmetric homodimer SPOP (one monomer is shown in grey), including the MATH domain (most beta strands are in the upper part of the protein and are shown as red arrows) and the BTB domain (blue helices and three beta strands on the lower part). Except for variant p.Thr25Ala, tered residues (magenta spheres) occurred in the MATH domain. The p.Thr25Ala residue is not shown because the protein structure starts with residue 26 (orange extremity of a red beta strand). The displayed mutated residues are closely located to the binding cleft, where the MATH domain interacts with its substrates (yellow peptide). The structure shows that Gly 132 is located at the bottom of the binding cleft, where the introduction of a larger valine affects the conformation and modifies substrate interaction. Tyr 83, Arg 138, and Asp 144 can be found clustered together on the surface of the protein. The alteration of both Tyr 83 and Arg 138 into a cysteine is very likely to affect the local structure surrounding the binding cleft and, hence, disturb SPOP substrate interaction. The substitution of an Asp for an Asn at position 144 is not large enough to affect the structure in a similar way, so we cannot predict the effect of this substitution for now. Amino acid residue Arg 121 is located on the protein surface on the opposite side of the binding cleft, where its side chain supports Tyr 123. Changing this residue to a slightly smaller Gln could affect the position of Tyr 123 and change the interaction of SPOP with its substrate. Lastly, Thr 25 is even more distant from the binding cleft. This residue is not present in the PDB file, but because Gly 26 is present as the first residue of the solved protein chain, we could still infer the effect of this change. The change of a Thr into a smaller Ala is not expected to cause strong structural changes or to affect the binding site directly.</p>
<p>(B) Representative immunoblot showing that the p.Asp144Asn and p.Arg121Gln SPOP substitutions downregulate, whereas p.Thr25Ala, p.Tyr83Cys, p.Gly132Val, and p.Arg138Cys upregulate, BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4 protein amounts when compared with wild-type SPOP (SPOP-WT) in Ishikawa endometrial cancer cells.</p>
<p>(C) When proband-derived Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)-immortalized PBMC lines from individuals 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 7 were compared to cell lines from three healthy individuals (controls), the same effect as for the isogenic cells (in B) was observed. β-ACTIN was used as loading control. Molecular weights are indicated (kDa).</p></figcaption></figure><p id="p0035">Reverse deep phenotyping of the seven individuals revealed that all had intellectual disability, motor and speech delay, facial dysmorphisms, and congenital anomalies (<a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>; <a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Table S3</a>; <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>; <a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S3</a>). Besides these common features, individuals 1 and 2 shared craniofacial dysmorphisms that are strikingly different from those of individuals 3–7; in fact, when one considers the head circumference and forehead of individuals 1 and 2, their craniofacial dysmorphisms could even be said to be the opposite of the features of individuals 3–7. Individuals 1 and 2 both presented with specific features that include congenital microcephaly, hearing loss, and a recognizable facial gestalt, such as a small forehead, highly arched eyebrows, blepharophimosis, a full nasal tip, a flat philtrum, micrognathia, and a pointed chin. Between them, individuals 3–7 also shared specific facial dysmorphisms, in particular (relative) macrocephaly, a high and broad forehead, and hypertelorism. Additional overlapping phenotypic abnormalities confined to the second group of five individuals were failure to thrive and short stature (2/5), cardiovascular abnormalities (4/4), endocrine abnormalities (3/4), epilepsy (2/4), and sleep disturbance (4/5).</p>
<p id="p0040">On the basis of the previously reported opposite functional effect of somatic <em>SPOP</em> variants in prostate and endometrial cancer (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>),<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10"><sup>10</sup></a>we hypothesized that the divergent phenotype of the two groups of individuals corresponds to differential functional effects. We envisaged that the <em>de novo</em> variants, including p.Arg121Gln, that were identified in the first group of individuals would result in a gain-of-function, and conversely, the variants, namely p.Tyr83Cys, that were detected in the second group would have a dominant-negative effect. To investigate this, we measured BET protein amounts in human Ishikawa endometrial cancer cells, an isogenic model system, in which we introduced all <em>de novo SPOP</em> variants. Variants p.Arg121Gln and p.Asp144Asn resulted in reduced amounts of the BET proteins BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4, suggesting that these are gain-of-function variants comparable to those seen in endometrial cancer (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figures 2</a>B, <a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">S4</a>A, and <a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">S5</a>). In contrast, the variants p.Thr25Ala, p.Tyr83Cys, p.Gly132Val, and p.Arg138Cys resulted in an upregulation of BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4 protein amounts and are hence <em>SPOP</em> variants such as those seen in prostate cancer and are acting in a dominant-negative manner. Importantly, and in conjunction with the notion of altered protein stability, we did not observe relevant transcriptional changes in BET proteins in response to these mutants (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S4</a>B). We confirmed the nature of the variants by measuring the kinetics of protein degradation by using two variants that represent either gain-of-function or dominant-negative effects. Indeed, after inhibition of protein synthesis with cycloheximide, BET amounts remained stable upon expression of SPOP-p.Arg138Cys. In contrast, protein half-life was more dramatically reduced upon expression of SPOP-p.Asp144Asn as compared to that of wild-type SPOP (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S6</a>). Consistent with the notion that BETs are degraded through the proteasome, treatment with MG132 restored BET amounts in both contexts. Proband-derived EBV-immortalized peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) lines from individuals 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 7 were additionally obtained and cultured as previously described.<a href="#bib31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib31"><sup>31</sup></a> Notably, measurements of BET amounts in proband-derived cell lines matched those found in healthy individuals in terms of the deregulation found in the isogenic system generated in Ishikawa endometrial cancer cells (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figures 2</a>B, 2C, and <a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">S4</a>C), suggesting that the <em>de novo</em> variants are causally linked to the functional effects observed.</p>
<p id="p0045">We identified seven individuals with NDD and <em>de novoSPOP</em> missense variants that caused either reduced (individuals 1 and 2) or increased (individuals 3–7) BET amounts. Furthermore, variants resulted in growth abnormalities, including a head size spectrum ranging from microcephaly to macrocephaly and distinct recognizable facial dysmorphisms. Whereas individuals with gain-of-function variants presented with microcephaly, individuals with variants leading to BET’s functional loss had absolute macrocephaly or a head circumference (HC) in the normal centile range, though exceeding the centile for height (relative macrocephaly). Of note, individuals 5 and 6, who had the same <em>de novo</em> p.Arg138Cys variant, had absolute macrocephaly and a normal HC, respectively, suggesting interfamilial variability. So far, no true congenital macrocephaly has been observed, although birth HC measurements of individuals 5, 6, and 7 were unknown. Specifically, individuals 3 and 4, who were born with below average HC, showed postnatal ventriculomegaly, which also contributed to absolute or relative macrocephaly, respectively. Additional investigation is needed if we are to understand the intricate mechanisms involved in head growth in individuals with pathogenic <em>SPOP</em> variants; such mechanisms might be determined by cell signaling pathways that govern cell proliferation and overlap with cancer pathways.<a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32"><sup>32</sup></a> Functionally, BET proteins are involved in cell-cycle progression.<a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33"><sup>33</sup></a> In mice, BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4 were found to be downregulated in differentiating neural progenitor cells, whereas their amounts remained unaltered in proliferating neural progenitor cells.<a href="#bib34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib34"><sup>34</sup></a> In addition, in <em>Brd2</em>-deficient neuroepithelial cells, cell-cycle progression was accelerated, whereas neuronal differentiation as well as cell-cycle exit were impaired.<a href="#bib35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib35"><sup>35</sup></a> These results correlate well with the congenital microcephaly found in two individuals with a STOP gain of function resulting in fewer BET proteins and, hence, supposedly less neuronal differentiation. Conversely, more BET proteins would stimulate the latter process, which might result in macrocephaly. Lastly, a c.910C&gt;T (p.His304Tyr; GenBank: <a href="http://ncbi-n:NM_058243.2" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NM_058243.2</a>) heterozygous missense variant in <em>BRD4</em> has been described as resulting in macrocephaly and short stature, resembling features in individuals with dominant-negative variants in <em>SPOP</em>.<a href="#bib36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib36"><sup>36</sup></a> Interestingly, this <em>BRD4</em> variant might result in increased protein stability because it is in close proximity to the SPOP degron (AA292–299).</p>
<p id="p0050">Diverse and eventually mirror phenotypes caused by genetic variation in the same gene are increasingly recognized (e.g. <em>CDKN1C</em><a href="#bib37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib37"><sup>37</sup></a> and <em>RAC1</em><a href="#bib38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib38"><sup>38</sup></a>). Different explanations that have been proposed for the resultant excess or inhibition of cell proliferation and differentiation include disruption of a single neurodevelopmental step that is sensitive to gene dosage<a href="#bib39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib39"><sup>39</sup></a> and the possibility of a gene’s influencing several biologic pathways resulting in different consequences depending on temporal and cellular contexts within a genetic background.<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1"><sup>1</sup></a> In particular, the (re)occurrence of specific genetic variants in the substrate-binding MATH domain of SPOP, which results in opposite functional effects, emphasizes the key role of this domain in cell biology.</p>
<p id="p0055">In summary, <em>SPOP</em> variants have been identified in individuals with intellectual disability and in tumor samples, which suggests that <em>SPOP</em> variants occurring at different times of development, specifically in germline versus somatic tissue, result in different consequences, i.e., neurodevelopmental delay or cancer, respectively. Individuals with NDD and <em>de novo SPOP</em> variants could be differentiated on the basis of distinct craniofacial dysmorphisms and congenital anomalies, indicating the presence of diverse clinically recognizable intellectual-disability syndromes. The opposing effects of variants impairing SPOP function provide a molecular basis for the contrasting phenotypic differences.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Declaration of Interests</h2>
