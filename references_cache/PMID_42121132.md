Anti-synthetase syndrome (ASS) is an autoimmune disease characterized by serum anti-aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (ARS) antibodies and frequently associated with recurrent and refractory interstitial lung disease (ASS-ILD). Its pathogenesis remains poorly understood.

Paired peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cells from 4 patients with new-onset ASS-ILD were analyzed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), whereas paired plasma and BALF samples from 11 new-onset ASS-ILD patients underwent Pro-DIA proteomics. Ten patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and 10 healthy donors served as disease and healthy controls (HC) for the proteomics. In vitro experiments were performed to support the multi-omics findings.

Integrated scRNA-seq (117,960 PBMCs and 91,230 BALF cells) and Pro-DIA proteomics (4,629 plasma and 3,919 BALF proteins) analyses were performed. In ASS-ILD BALF, monocyte-macrophages showed significantly higher cytokine and inflammatory gene module scores compared with IPF and HCs (allP< 2e-16). The proportion of alveolus pro-inflammatory monocyte-macrophages was markedly increased (52.7% vs. 6.2% in IPF and 3.6% in HC). These subsets exhibited upregulated tRNA aminoacylation pathway and elevated multiple ARS gene expressions (adj.P< 1e-5). BALF proteomics also revealed increased ARS-related proteins and inflammatory mediators in ASS-ILD (adj.P< 0.05). In vitro, human histidyl ARS-containing immune complexes (IC) induced higher expression of MHC-I/II, CD80/CD86, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and ARS genes in primary human macrophages compared with control IC.

Our findings provide novel insights into ASS-ILD pathogenesis and suggest the underlying pathogenicity of ARS containing IC. These results underscore the role of monocyte-macrophage activation in ASS-ILD related immunopathogenesis and pave the way for targeted therapies directed at IC formation and aberrant monocyte-macrophage dynamics.

Anti-synthetase syndrome (ASS), a heterogeneous and refractory autoimmune disease, is a distinct subtype of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) [1]. Its clinical spectrum extends beyond classic myositis to involve multiple systems, characterized by interstitial lung disease (ILD), non-erosive arthritis, Raynaud’s phenomenon, and “mechanic’s hands”. Among these manifestations, pulmonary involvement is particularly prominent and serves as the critical determinant of long-term survival and quality of life [2]. Current therapeutic strategies involve glucocorticoids combined with immunosuppressants, whereas the management of ASS remains a significant challenge. A substantial proportion of patients exhibit resistance to treatments, and disease flares are common during drug tapering, leading to progressive decline in lung function and irreversible pulmonary fibrosis or respiratory failure [3]. Given the disease’s heterogeneity, clinical phenotyping and prognostic assessment are vital. A large-scale retrospective cohort studies suggest that ASS patients can be stratified into three primary phenotypes: ILD cluster, arthritis cluster, and dermatomyositis (DM) type. Survival analysis indicates that the ILD phenotype is associated with the significantly worse prognosis, the lowest five-year survival rate, and the highest risk of acute exacerbation compared to the other subtypes [4]. These findings underscore the central role of ILD in the ASS disease course and highlight the urgency of exploring the specific immune mechanisms underlying ASS-ILD. In vivo models also support the theory of pulmonary origin of anti-synthetase syndrome: C57B6/J.G7 mice immunized with Histidyl-tRNA synthetase (HARS) protein developed anti-HARS antibodies and ILD [5].

Despite studies elucidating some immunological features of ASS-ILD, basic research into its pathogenesis remains scarce. Wang et al., using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from ASS-ILD patients, found the disease is characterized by upregulated interferon responses and altered CD8 + T cell homeostasis [6]. Similarly, Wei et al., using anti-MDA5 + DM as a control, observed significant enrichment of type-II interferon pathways in ASS patients, alongside distinct metabolic abnormalities that may impact oxidative phosphorylation [7]. Monocytes in ASS patients appeared to act as key receptor-signaling cells in the TNF pathway also were noted [8]. However, peripheral blood signatures often only reflect systemic “spillover” effects and fail to accurately recapitulate the local immune microenvironment of affected organs. Proteomic studies of ASS muscle tissue reveal specific macrophage (Mφ) infiltration and tissue destruction mechanisms within the affected tissue that are not fully captured in peripheral blood [9]. Therefore, distinguishing between “peripheral circulation” and “target organ” immune dysregulation is crucial for understanding pathogenic mechanisms. Recent pathophysiological research on SARS-CoV-2 infection offers important insights, emphasizing that the immune cell landscape in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) differs significantly from that of peripheral blood, with BALF directly reflecting the inflammatory state of the alveolar microenvironment [10,11]. In the context of ASS-ILD, there is currently a lack of high-resolution multi-omics research focusing on BALF. Moreover, existing knowledge fails to explain why immune attacks specifically aggregate in the lungs or what cellular interactions within the alveolar space drive ILD. This gap severely limits the identification of precise therapeutic targets for ASS-ILD.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the disparate changes and intrinsic links between the peripheral circulation and the local lung immune landscape in ASS-ILD patients. We collected paired plasma, PBMC, and BALF samples from patients with new-onset ASS-ILD and utilized single-cell transcriptomics combined with pro-DIA proteomics to explore potential specific targets and regulatory pathways. By integrating the data from both transcriptomic and proteomic dimensions, this study demonstrates the important driving role of the monocyte-Mφ in ASS-ILD pulmonary immunopathology.

This study was designed to gain insights into the disrupted immune landscape spanning both the peripheral and pulmonary domains in individuals afflicted with ASS-ILD and to identify potential therapeutic targets. We employed scRNA-seq to examine the cellular constituents within participants’ BALF cells and PBMCs. Pro-DIA proteomics was utilized to explore the protein components of the extracellular environment within plasma and paired BALF. RT-qPCR and flow cytometry were subsequently used for further supportive validation of the findings.

We enrolled patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), who exhibit similar ILD patterns, as the disease control group [2]. All patients newly diagnosed with ASS according to the 2017 EULAR/ACR Classification Criteria [1] at the First People’s Hospital of Hangzhou between October 2024 and July 2025 were consecutively recruited. PBMC and BALF samples were collected from participants meeting the following inclusion criteria: (I) age ≥ 18 years; (II) positive for anti-ARS antibodies; and (III) chest high-resolution computed tomography findings consistent with the 2022 ATS/ERS clinical practice guidelines for ILD [12]. Similarly, patients newly diagnosed with IPF (meeting the 2022 ATS/ERS guidelines [12]) who underwent bronchoscopy were also consecutively recruited. Individuals undergoing bronchoscopy for a solitary pulmonary nodule or mass, with samples taken from the contralateral (unaffected) lung, served as healthy controls (HCs). Participants were excluded if they had: (I) a malignancy; (II) overlap syndrome; or (III) active hepatitis B/C or tuberculosis. All subjects provided written informed consent.

Regarding sample size, to minimize batch effects in Pro-DIA proteomics analysis, the number of samples in the IPF and HC groups was kept consistent with that of the ASS-ILD group.

PBMCs and plasma were isolated from whole blood using density gradient centrifugation. The isolated PBMCs were subsequently resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The collection of BALF was performed by a qualified and experienced physician with specialized training in a dedicated bronchoscopy suite. The operation adhered to the 2012 ATS clinical practice guidelines [13]. For both ASS-ILD and IPF patients, the BALF specimen was collected from the most affected lung segment, as identified on HRCT. Conversely, BALF samples from HCs were collected from the contralateral (unaffected) lung of patients. Bronchoscopy was performed under general anesthesia. Normal saline at room temperature (100–300 mL, divided into 3–5 aliquots) was instilled through the bronchoscope. A minimum of 5% of the instilled volume was retrieved. Fresh BALF was filtered through a 100 mm nylon cell strainer to remove aggregates and debris, and then centrifuged. The resulting BALF supernatant was collected and stored at -80℃ and the cell pellets were then resuspended in chilled RPMI 1640 complete medium. Both BALF cells and PBMCs intended for scRNA-seq were analyzed freshly. Plasma and BALF samples designated for proteomic were stored at -80℃ and processed in a single batch for unified analysis.

The scRNA-seq libraries were meticulously prepared utilizing the Chromium Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v4 (10x Genomics). Preparation was conducted strictly according to the manufacturer’s stipulated protocol. Subsequently, the resulting gene expression libraries were subjected to sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads to ensure comprehensive transcriptomic coverage.

To match the ethnicity, age, gender, and cross-dataset technical heterogeneity across scRNA-seq data, the datasets of HCs are selected from GSE271789 (CM1-CM10, were chosen as controls for PBMCs, ten samples), IPFs from GSE233844 (Progressive IPF patient1-10, are chosen as disease controls for PBMCs, ten samples), BALF HCs are from GSE14592 (HC1, HC2 and HC3), and BALF IPFs are from GSE271789 (IPF BALI1-BALI9, nine samples). All scRNA-seq data were aligned and quantified using Cell Ranger (v9.0.1) with the GRCh38 human reference genome, obtained from the 10× Genomics official website. The filtered feature matrixes were then imported into Seurat (v5.0.1) for quality control. All functions were executed with default parameters unless otherwise specified. Cells with 300-5,000 expressed genes and a mitochondrial percentage of < 10% (PBMCs) or < 15% (BALF) were retained. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the 2,000 most variable features identified byFindVariableFeatures. The PCA matrix was then integrated using Harmony (v0.1.1) to correct for batch effects in the single-cell gene expression data.

UMAP was employed for the visualization of single cells, using the top 20 principal components derived from the dimensionality reduction step. The initial round of unsupervised clustering was executed with a resolution of 0.5 to delineate the major cell types. To achieve a finer resolution of heterogeneity within the prominent lineages, a secondary clustering analysis was performed independently on the major populations identified as T cells, monocytes, and Mφ, utilizing an increased resolution of 0.8. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the resulting clusters were identified using theFindMarkersfunction implemented in the Seurat package. Genes were considered differentially expressed based on the criteria of |Log2FoldChange | > 1 and an adjustedP-value of < 0.05. To investigate the functional significance of these DEGs, GO pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the ClusterProfiler package (v4.6.0). Geneset enrichment scores were calculated using theAddModuleScorefunction within the Seurat package, based on previously published literature. The specific reference gene sets utilized for the calculation of these module scores are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis for monocyte–Mφ was performed with Monocle3 (v1.4.26) [11]. To integrate monocyte–Mφ data from PBMCs and BALF cells, cell trajectories were inferred with peripheral monocytes as the starting point.

Metabolic activity within the monocyte–Mφ clusters from both PBMCs and BALF cells was quantified and visualized using the scMetabolism package (v2.1.0) via the AUCell method [14].

Intercellular communication between the BALF monocyte–Mφ clusters and other BALF cellular populations was analyzed using the CellChat package (v1.1.3) [15]. The CellChatDB.human database served as the reference for ligand-receptor interaction pairs. Communication probabilities were subsequently computed to infer the detailed cellular communication networks.

Transcription factors (TFs) and gene regulatory networks were inferred from the scRNA-seq data using the pySCENIC (version 0.12.1) [16] based on Python 3.9.2. The analysis was executed in three distinct phases: co-expression module inference, regulon refinement via motif enrichment, and regulon activity quantification. Reference motif databases and corresponding genome annotations necessary for this process were sourced from the official SCENIC Resource Repository (https://github.com/aertslab/pySCENIC).

To assess the transcriptional similarity between the current BALF Mφ clusters and those implicated in pulmonary fibrosis, gene set module scores were calculated. Specifically, these fibrosis gene expression program scores were derived using the AddModuleScore function within the Seurat package, employing a fixed random seed (seed = 1993) for reproducibility. The reference gene sets were curated from distinct, previously published pulmonary fibrosis datasets [17,18,19]. For each reference, the top 50 marker genes were selected to define the module. In instances where a reference contained fewer than 50 available marker genes, all identified genes were included. This systematic approach facilitated the quantitative assessment of transcriptional profiles indicative of profibrotic Mφ phenotypes.

Pro-DIA quantitative proteomics was employed to analyze the extracellular protein environment in both plasma and BALF samples. The entire workflow comprised four sequential steps: protein extraction, SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, protein digestion, and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry, which are detailed in Supplementary method S1. Proteins expressed across the samples were systematically identified. To determine differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), |Log2FoldChange | > 1 and an adjustedP-value of < 0.05 were applied as the screening criteria. AdjustedP-values (q-values) were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR) for multiple testing. The resulting DEPs were subsequently used to conduct GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis.

The NicheNet package was utilized to integrate the DEPs identified from BALF proteomics with the DEGs from the BALF scRNA-seq monocyte-Mφ clusters. This integration aimed to explore the influence of the extracellular protein environment on target gene expression. This analysis relied on NicheNet’s pre-built databases for ligand-receptor, signal transduction, and ligand-target gene interactions. The specific details regarding these reference databases are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Based on the findings from the scRNA-seq and proteomics analyses, in vitro experiments were designed and performed. Human Mφ were stimulated with ARS antigen-antibody immune complexes and appropriate controls to provide supportive evidence for the multi-omics findings.

Derived from healthy human PBMCs (collected from HCs), human monocytes were isolated using CD14-positive selection magnetic beads. Primary human monocytes were differentiated by culturing them at a density of 5 × 10^5/mL in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 50 ng/mL Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor (M-CSF) for 7 days.

Based on evidence from in vivo models in which murine HARS fusion protein stimulation induced anti-HARS antibodies and ASS phenotypes [5], we selected HARS immune complexes as the specific ARS antigen-antibody immune complex to stimulate human Mφ. The immunodominant amino terminal fragment of human HARS was produced as a maltose binding protein (MBP) fusion protein [5,20]. This construct consisted of amino acids 1-151 of HARS linked to the carboxy-terminal end of MBP (designated MA/MBP). Hereafter designated as recombinant HARS, this bacterially expressed fusion protein was purified using amylose resin, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Sinobiological, Beijing, China). The purified protein was then assessed by SDS-PAGE, dialyzed against PBS, and finally filter sterilized prior to its use in the coating of ICs. Anti-HARS antibody used in these experiments was purchased from Abcam (Catalog number: ab249182). The IC coated by human serum albumin (HSA, Sigma A9080) and anti-HSA antibody (Sigma A0433) was selected as the control IC.

The RIA/EIA 96-well plates were coated with 100 uL 20ug/mL specific antigen in 100 μM sodium carbonate at 4℃ overnight. After coating, the plates were washed once with 200 µL of wash buffer (PBS supplemented with 10% Ultra-low IgG FBS) and then blocked with the same buffer at 37 °C for 1 h. Following blocking, the plates were incubated with 100 µL per well of 10 µg/mL relevant antibody in wash buffer at 37 °C for 2–4 h. The IC-coated plates were washed once more with 200 µL of wash buffer and were ready for Mφ stimulation.

Primary human Mφ were seeded at at density of 200k cells/well into the IC-coated plates, or into uncoated plates for non-IC-based groups (Vehicle, HARS, and Positive Control: 1 µg/mL LPS + 10 ng/mL IFN-γ). Following 8 h of stimulation, cellular RNA was extracted for RT-qPCR (the primers used in this study are detailed in Supplementary Table S3), while cells stimulated for 24 h were collected for flow cytometry using a BD FACSAria II. The following antibodies were incubated for flow cytometry analysis: CD14-FITC (Invitrogen; 11-0149-42), HLA-DR-PE (Invitrogen; 12-9956-42), HLA-A/B/C-AF700 (Invitrogen; 56-9983-42), CD80-BV510 (BD Horizon; 563084) and CD86-PECy7 (Invitrogen; 25-0862-82). Each experiment was performed for three times and the results were substantiated by repetition under a range of conditions.

Descriptive statistics were presented as numbers with percentages and means ± SDs. For normally distributed quantitative data, one-way ANOVA was applied. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and proportions; comparisons between groups were performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Gene set enrichment scores calculated by theAddModuleScorefunction were compared between groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test. For RT-qPCR results’ analysis, the relative mRNA expression levels were determined by the 2^-ΔΔCp method, the one-way ANOVA were applied and the LSD method was used for pairwise comparison. All statistical tests performed with R software (version 4.2.3), were two-tailed, and aP-value less than 0.05 was established as the criterion for statistical significance.

The flowchart of the cohort study is shown in Fig.1A, and the detailed participant characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S4. Among the 31 participants, 14 (45.1%) were male and 17 (54.9%) were female, with a mean age of 60.64 ± 11.63 years. Compared to the IPF and HC groups, ASS-ILD patients were predominantly female (90.9%) and significantly younger (bothP< 0.05). In contrast to those with IPF, ASS-ILD patients had a shorter disease duration, with fever (45.5%), Gottron’s papules (63.6%), and muscle weakness (54.5%) being more prevalent (allP< 0.01). Furthermore, ASS-ILD patients exhibited markedly elevated peripheral blood inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR) and a higher prevalence of antinuclear antibody positivity compared to the control groups(allP< 0.05), and all ASS-ILD patients were positive for one or more anti-ARS antibodies (Figures S1A). Regarding pulmonary function, both ASS-ILD and IPF patients demonstrated restrictive ventilatory defects and impaired diffusion capacity compared to the HC group (P= 0.009 and 0.047,respectively). Characteristic ILD patterns of ASS-ILD and IPF patients, such as organizing pneumonia, diffuse ground-glass opacities, and consolidations, were observed on HRCT (Figures S1A).

We integrated ASS-ILD scRNA-seq cellular profiles with publicly available datasets from disease controls and HCs, and the demographic information for the IPF patients and HCs is provided in Supplementary Table S5 and S6. Combined with ASS-ILDs’ data, a high-quality scRNA-seq dataset, consisting of 209,190 cells (117,960 cells from PBMCs and 91,230 cells from BALF), were established. Within the PBMCs, seven major cell types were successfully discerned and annotated based on established marker genes: CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, B cells, dendritic cells, and plasma cells(Fig.1B, C and F, and S2A). Similarly, in the BALF, five predominant cell types—comprising Mφ, T cells, neutrophils (NEU), classical dendritic cells (cDCs), and epithelial cells—were identified and characterized using the same methodology(Fig.1D, E and G, and S2C).

Significant differences were clearly visible among the ASS-ILD, IPF, and HC groups based on UMAP projections(Fig.1C and E, and S2B, S2D). In the PBMC compartment, the most prominent alteration observed in ASS-ILD was an expansion of the monocyte population alongside a reduction in NK cell populations. Conversely, within the BALF, we identified an enrichment of NEU and a noticeable decrease in Mφ, which deviated from the profiles of IPF and HCs(Figs.1I and S2F). Collectively, these data reveal substantial changes in the peripheral blood and lung immune cell compartments of ASS-ILD patients when compared to IPF and HCs.

The high-quality Pro-DIA proteomics pipeline successfully identified 4,629 proteins in 30 plasma samples and 3,919 proteins in 31 BALF samples, respectively. Differential expression of these proteins indicated significant variations among ASS-ILD, disease controls, and HCs(Fig.1J and K, and S2G, S2H), suggesting that both ASS-ILD and IPF possess distinct protein signatures within the extracellular environment of peripheral blood and BALF.

ASS-ILD often presents with concomitant inflammatory and fibrotic manifestations. We compared the gene expression of cytokines and chemokines in samples derived from patients, disease controls, and HCs(Fig.2A). The scRNA-seq data obtained from BALF indicated elevated levels of interleukins (IL-1β,IL-6,IL-10),IFNγ, andVEGFA, alongside a broad spectrum of chemokines (CCL2,CCL3,CCL4,CCL7,CCL8,CXCL1,CXCL2,CXCL8, andCXCL14) in ASS-ILD patients compared with IPF and HCs. Moreover, this pattern was more obvious than that observed in PBMCs, suggesting a hyper-inflammatory state primarily localized to the lung in ASS-ILD. On the other hand, IPF also exhibited increased expression of BALF chronic inflammatory factors and chemokines compared to HCs (CSF2,IL12B,CCL23,CCL18,CXCL9,CXCL11). However, an analysis of the transcriptome data from PBMCs did not reveal a significant inflammatory state when comparing the disease groups and HCs(Fig.2A).This observation suggests that the pro-inflammatory cytokines detected in the serum are more likely derived from lung tissue cells rather than from peripheral blood cells.

We next aimed to identify the inflammatory and immune response status across various cell subsets in the circulation and the lung. Cytokine and inflammatory scores, as well as adaptive and innate immune scores, were calculated for each cell cluster based on the expression of relevant cytokine, inflammatory response, adaptive immunity, and innate immunity genes, respectively, to assess their contribution to the proinflammatory state and immune responses(Fig.2B and C). Our analysis revealed significantly elevated expression of these cytokine and inflammatory genes across various immune and epithelial cells in the BALF of ASS-ILD patients, signifying an intense inflammatory response within the pulmonary environment(Fig.2B). Notably, monocyte-Mφ cluster in ASS-ILD BALF samples exhibited substantially higher cytokine and inflammatory scores compared to those in IPF and HCs, indicating their important role in driving the inflammatory response. In contrast, peripheral monocytes demonstrated markedly lower cytokine and inflammatory scores than their BALF counterparts, reflecting their lower activation level(allP< 2e-16, Fig.2B). The immune response scores showed a similar trend, with monocyte-Mφ cluster in ASS-ILD BALF exhibiting a more vigorous immune response(Fig.2C). Furthermore, other immune cells (T cells and DCs) in ASS-ILD also displayed a clear pattern of low activation peripherally but high activation in the lung(Fig.2B and C). This significant disparity underscores the localized nature of the high inflammatory and intense immune response in the lung, which is distinct from the peripheral immune landscape observed in PBMCs.

Given the substantial increase in multiple chemokine concentrations in BALF, we assessed the gene expression levels of chemokine receptors in PBMCs(Fig.2D). Monocytes demonstrated elevated expression ofCCR1andCCR2, additionally, the receptorsCCR5andCX3CR1on CD8 T cells and NK cells were substantially upregulated in ASS-ILD patients, suggesting that these cell types may migrate towards the tissue via chemotaxis. Furthermore, our examination of chemokine expressions across different cell clusters in BALF revealed that lung monocyte-Mφ in patients were likely key drivers of local inflammation(Fig.2E). They exhibited high transcriptional levels of chemokines such asCCL2,CCL8,CXCL2, andIL-1β, which may facilitate the recruitment of additional monocytes and T cells to the lungs, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response. However, the corresponding high expression of inflammatory factors was not observed in ASS-ILD’s peripheral monocytes (Figure S3A). On the other hand, alveolar monocyte-Mφ in ASS-ILD showed more pronounced expression of inflammatory differentiation marker genes, such as CD80 and CD86(Fig.2F). Similarly, based on the analysis of immune response-associated marker gene expression, pulmonary monocyte-Mφ appeared to play a critical role in the immune response in ASS-ILD, evidenced by the increased expression of TLR-like receptors and complement receptors(Fig.2G). Moreover, analysis of the peripheral immune status suggests that the immune response in ASS-ILD was also stronger in the periphery than in IPF and HCs(Fig.2C and G), hinting at a systemic autoimmune disorder in ASS.

The scRNA-seq data suggest a pulmonary pro-inflammatory and intense immune response state that is significantly stronger than the peripheral environment, a finding that is also supported by the extracellular environment analysis from proteomics. PCA analysis indicated that the protein composition in both the plasma and BALF of ASS-ILD differed significantly from that of IPF and HCs (Figure S3B). Clustering analysis of the DEPs commons to ASS-ILD versus both IPF and HCs(Fig.2H, I), followed by naming the clusters based on enriched GO-BP terms (Figure S3C, S3D), revealed that BALF samples had significantly represented clusters relevant to ASS disease characteristics: immune response-related proteins and amino acid metabolism-related proteins (Figure S3F). The plasma DEP clustering analysis also showed a cluster relevant to ASS disease characteristics—high expression of muscle component proteins—potentially linked to myositis (Figure S3E).

These findings highlight a significant discrepancy between the peripheral and pulmonary immune response and inflammatory state in ASS-ILD patients, emphasizing the localized nature of the lung response. These results emphasize the importance of focusing on BALF analyses to better understand the underlying mechanisms of ASS-ILD.

Given the pivotal role of monocyte-Mφ in the alveolar inflammation and immune response, we conducted a more detailed analysis of the monocyte–Mφ cluster within the BALF. This analysis led to the identification of 13 distinct populations, each possessing unique gene expression profiles(Fig.3A and B, S4A).

These populations could be broadly categorized into tissue-resident alveolar macrophages (AMφ) and monocyte-derived macrophage (Mφ/Mono-Mφ) subsets. The AM clusters (AMφ-CSTA, AMφ-CA2, AMφ-LSAMP, and AMφ-SCGB1A1) were defined by the robust expression of resident markers such asCSTA,PLA2G16, andIGFBP2, consistent with previously described resting AMφ populations involved in lung immune defense and repair [21]. Among these, HCs were primarily dominated by AMφ-CSTA, while IPF patients were enriched in AMφ-CA2 and AMφ-LSAMP. Notably, ASS-ILD patients showed a significantly reduced proportion of AMφ(Fig.3C, S4B). In contrast, several monocyte-derived Mφ and highly inflammatory subsets were identified, predominantly within the BALF samples of ASS-ILD patients. Specifically, the Mφ-CXCL10 cluster displayed a robust inflammatory phenotype, strongly expressing key interferon-stimulated genes and pro-inflammatory chemokines, includingCXCL10,CCL4,IFIT1, andTNFAIP6, suggesting a dominant subset driving the inflammatory response. Furthermore, clusters such as Mφ-IRAG2, Mono-Mφ-DUSP2, and Mono-Mφ-PLA2G7 likely represent specialized functional subsets, potentially involved in matrix remodeling (MMP19) or resolving inflammation (PLA2G7). The observed diversity, featuring both resident AMφ and distinct inflammatory Mono-Mφ, highlights the complex cell population shift within the BALF environment(Fig.3D).

Since we had observed that circulating monocytes exhibit elevated expression ofCCR1andCCR2(Fig.2E), supporting the hypothesis of monocyte recruitment to the pulmonary microenvironment [22], we utilized pseudotime analysis to infer the differentiation trajectory of circulating and BALF Mono-Mφ. To determine the starting point for this analysis, we first conducted a further analysis of peripheral circulating monocyte subsets (Figure S5A), which led to the identification of six distinct monocyte subtypes with different GO-BP enrichment characteristics: CD14+-HLA-DRlowMono, CD14+CD16-inflammatory Mono, CD14+CD16-interferon activated Mono, CD14+HLA+Mono, CD14+immune regulatory Mono, CD16+Mono (Figure S5F).

The composition of peripheral circulating monocyte subtypes did not show significant differences among the ASS-ILD, IPF, and HC groups (Figure S5B, S5C). The CD14+CD16-inflammatory Mono displayed a classic pro-inflammatory signature, exhibiting strong and specific high expression of cytokines and chemokines such asCCL3,IL1B,CXCL8,SIGLEC14, andRETN(Figure S5E). In contrast, the CD14+CD16-interferon activated Mono were defined by the highly specific upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes, includingGBP5,GBP1, andISG15, alongside markers likeDUSP2andCXCR4, indicating clear activation via interferon signaling. Furthermore, two clusters, CD14+HLA+Mono and CD14+-HLA-DRlowMono, were distinguished by high expression of MHC Class II genes (HLA-DQA1,HLA-DQA2,HLA-DPB1), suggesting robust antigen-presentation capability with subtle differences potentially indicating a maturational state. The CD14+immune regulatory Mono showed a unique expression pattern of genes likeCAMK1,RORA,and PROK2, which may confer specialized immunomodulatory or trafficking functions. The CD16+Mono was unequivocally defined by the exclusive high expression ofFCGR3A, along with unique transcription factors such asTCF7L2andHES4(Figure S5E, S5G).

Under pathological conditions, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) can exhibit significant migratory properties, enabling them to traffic from the bone marrow to affected tissues to exert immunosuppressive functions [23]. The distinctive features of CD14+-HLA-DRlowMono, including lower composite scores for MHC II molecules and higher scores for calprotectin (Figures S5I), closely resembled those of MDSCs. This cluster also had the highest MDSC-like score among all six clusters (Figure S5H). On the other hand, given the elevated levels of monocyte-attracting chemokines in BALF(Fig.2D)and the upregulation ofCCR1andCCR2in circulating monocytes(Fig.2E),CCR1andCCR2expression was analyzed across the peripheral circulating monocyte clusters to identify the cluster most likely to migrate to the lung. Among these, the CD14+-HLA-DRlowMono cluster exhibited the highest expression ofCCR1andCCR2(Figure S5J). Therefore, we selected the CD14+-HLA-DRlowMono as the starting point for the pseudotime analysis.

Pseudotime analysis revealed that peripheral monocytes underwent a potential differentiation trajectory toward Mono-Mφ, eventually leading to AMφ in the BALF, aligning with the theory of peripheral monocyte recruitment to inflammatory tissues(Figs.3E). Differential gene expression analysis along the monocyte–Mφ trajectory revealed 3 key stages(Fig.3F). The initial stage was marked by heightened expression of calprotectin (S100A12) and elastase (FCN1), indicating an early inflammatory response and leukocyte chemotaxis. In the second stage, marker genes and GO-BP terms related to endocytosis and lipid metabolism (APOE,FABP4) were observed. In the later stage, monocyte–Mφ expressed genes related to antigen processing and presentation (HLA-DQA1) and complement activation (C1QA,C1QB,C1QC,SERPING1), signaling a shift towards tissue remodeling and immune modulation (Figure S4C, S4D).

To decipher the specific roles of each monocyte-Mφ cluster in pulmonary inflammation and immune responses, we calculated immune, inflammatory, and cytokine scores for each group(Fig.3G and H). Our analysis revealed that the AMφ populations exhibited lower scores for both immune and inflammatory responses, while Mono-Mφ populations were relatively higher (allP< 2e-16), suggesting that these recruited monocytes may be the primary contributors to the immune-related inflammation in the lungs.

Beyond the inflammatory response, ASS-ILD patients with ILD also suffer from progressive pulmonary fibrosis, which is linked to worse prognoses. Assessing the expression of fibrosis-related genes (TGFB1,TGFBI,LGMN, andCCL18) for each cell cluster, we found elevated levels in the Mφ-FOLR2, Mφ-CXCL10, and Mono-Mφ clusters(Fig.3I). Interestingly, the inflammatory responses of these clusters were also relatively active, pointing to their potential role in fibrogenesis. Furthermore, calculating M1 and M2 phenotypic scores within the Mφ and Mono-Mφ clusters revealed that the Mφ-CXCL10 and Mo-Mφ populations predominantly exhibited an M1 phenotype, while the Mφ-FOLR2 cluster showed higher expression of M2 markers (Figure S4E).

Notably, Mφ in fibrotic niches of pulmonary fibrosis exhibit shared transcriptional programs [18,24]. To investigate this further, we compared the transcriptional profiles of these monocyte-Mφ clusters with publicly available pulmonary fibrosis datasets. Using gene set expression-based cell scores, we identified significant differences across populations(Fig.3J and S4F, S4G, S4H). This analysis indicated that the Mono-Mφ and specific Mφ populations closely resembled fibrosis Mφ phenotypes, whereas AMφs did not exhibit this phenotype. Of particular note, the Mono-Mφ clusters displayed stronger expression of fibrosis-related genes and higher phenotypic scores, suggesting their potential to mediate the chronicity of inflammation and induce tissue repair.

In summary, the migration and differentiation of monocyte-Mφ could play a notable role in the local pulmonary immunity, inflammation, and fibrosis observed in ASS-ILD. Consequently, we further explored the pathogenesis of ASS-ILD by focusing on the functional alterations within the monocyte-Mφ populations.

Based on GO-BP enrichment analysis (Figure S6A, S6B), we classified the 13 BALF monocyte-Mφ clusters into two major functional groups: the phagocytic and metabolism group and the pro-inflammatory and immune group(Fig.4A). The phagocytic and metabolism group highly expressed genes related to endocytosis, lipid, and nucleotide metabolism, whereas the pro-inflammatory and immune group exhibited higher expression of genes encoding inflammatory cytokines and chemokines(Fig.4B). In the ASS-ILD samples, the proportion of the pro-inflammatory and immune group was significantly higher (52.7%) compared to IPF and HCs (6.2% and 3.6% respectively), while the phagocytic and metabolism group was markedly reduced, indicating a distinct immune response and inflammatory reaction localized to the ASS-ILD lung(Fig.4C).

The metabolic state of monocyte-Mφ is intimately linked to their function [25], prompting us to analyze metabolic reprogramming pathways in both peripheral and pulmonary monocyte-Mφ. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant metabolic reprogramming, particularly within the BALF monocyte-Mφ across three groups. While PBMC subsets showed several pathway enrichment for nucleotide and fatty acid metabolism, the changes were most pronounced in BALF(Fig.4D). BALF samples from both ASS-ILD and IPF patients exhibited a robust hypermetabolic phenotype compared to HCs. Specifically, ASS-ILD monocyte-Mφ in BALF were characterized by pronounced enrichment in core carbon metabolism, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, and the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), associated with inflammatory and potentially hypoxic environments. Crucially, ASS-ILD monocyte-Mφ in BALF also demonstrated highly enriched scores across multiple amino acid metabolism pathways (Histidine, Arginine, Tryptophan, Cysteine, and Alanine metabolism). This substantial enhancement of amino acid processing pathways is biologically pertinent, as it may suggest a direct metabolic-immunological link with the autoantigen-ARS, which is central to the pathogenesis of ASS-ILD [1,26]. Further subgroup analysis, indicated that ASS-ILD samples differed significantly from disease controls and HCs in terms of metabolic pathway composition in both the peripheral circulation and the lung (Figure S7A, S7B). In both peripheral and BALF samples, almost all ASS-ILD monocyte-Mφ subsets showed an upregulation of amino acid catabolism, with this effect being more pronounced in the lung (Figure S7C, S7D).

Further GO-BP enrichment specifically on ASS-ILD BALF samples supported these metabolic changes in monocyte-Mφ. Intriguingly, both groups in the ASS-ILD group were significantly enriched in pathways related to tRNA aminoacylation for protein translation(Fig.4E), an enrichment completely absent in the IPF and HC groups (Figure S6C, S6D). There was only one cluster of peripheral circulating monocytes in ASS-ILD also showed a weak enrichment for tRNA amino acid metabolism terms (Figure S6E). This observation further reinforces the idea that the pathogenesis of ASS-ILD may originate in the lung and be directly related to the defined autoantigen.

We then calculated the tRNA aminoacylation geneset score for the BALF samples, which was significantly higher in the ASS-ILD group compared to IPF and HCs(P< 2e-16, Fig.4F, left panel). Within the ASS-ILD group, tRNA metabolism was generally augmented across all clusters(Fig.4F, right panel), whereas IPF and HC groups showed low tRNA metabolism scores (Figure S8A), consistent with the metabolic reprogramming calculations. Analysis of the DEGs enriched in the tRNA aminoacylation pathway revealed that multiple ARS and ARS-metabolism-related genes were upregulated(adj.P< 1e-5, Fig.4G). In the BALF proteomics data, we similarly found a high detection abundance of ARS and ARS-metabolism-related proteins in the extracellular environment(Fig.4H, S8C), while the corresponding tRNA metabolism-related proteins were not enriched in the plasma (Figure S8B, S8D). Notably, the upregulation of ARS expression was not confined to a single specific type but involved the elevated expression of multiple ARSs.

Overall, the pulmonary monocyte-Mφ in ASS-ILD exhibit definite activation and functional alterations, including changes in inflammation, antigen presentation, and metabolism. Particularly, the upregulation of ARS gene expression and function may be closely associated with the disease pathogenesis. We then investigated the underlying reasons for these changes.

Monocyte-Mφs engage in intimate crosstalk with the extracellular environment via endocytosis, cell surface, and intracellular receptors [27]. Baesd on the existence of anti-ARS antibody, we demonstrate the disease-specific upregulation of ARS metabolism in Mφ maybe linked to the Mφ activation by specific proteins, most probably antigen-antibody complexes.

We performed a NicheNet ligand-receptor interaction analysis between the DEPs in the ASS-ILD BALF samples and the DEGs in the ASS-ILD monocyte-Mφ, aiming to explore the mode by which extracellular proteins activate monocyte-Mφ. This analysis identified inflammation and immune-related proteins as the active ligands with the highest regulatory potential among the monocyte-Mφ clusters (Figure S9A). Specifically, the inflammatory mediator S100A8 showed potent regulatory activity over a broad range of pro-inflammatory and immune-related genes, inducing the expression ofCXCL8,IL1B,CCL2,TNF,VEGFA,and SERPINE1(Fig.5A). Furthermore, the chord diagram highlighted key interaction pairs where DEPs such as S100A8, A2M, and HLA-DPB1 in the extracellular space target Mφ receptors (TLR4,SIRPA) and downstream DEGs involved in inflammation and proliferation, includingEGR1,FOS, andVEGFA(Fig.5B). These findings suggest that the inflammatory state of ASS-ILD’s monocyte-Mφ in BALF was heavily influenced by specific danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and immune molecules present in the alveolar fluid. The following results support the more active antigen presentation by monocyte-Mφ in BALF from patients with ASS-ILD: In the BALF proteomic analysis, ASS-ILD patients exhibited a higher expression of FcγR1A compared to disease controls and HCs (Figure S9B, S9C). Moreover, the ASS BALF monocyte-Mφ demonstrated a higher activation level of the TLR signaling pathway, predominantly TLR4, TLR7, and TLR9 (allP< 2e-16, Fig.5C). Additionally, ASS-ILD patients had higher expression of MHC Class I/II molecules in BALF (Figure S9D).

Building upon the previous hypothesis that the specific tRNA metabolism in ASS-ILD monocyte-Mφ might be related to ARS autoantibodies, and having used NicheNet analysis to suggest the stimulation of monocyte-Mφ by extracellular proteins, we sought to further investigate the cause of ARS gene expression upregulation. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the reasons behind the increased ARS expression, we further explored the TFs associated with the upregulation of ARS gene expression. Using the pySCENIC algorithm, we found that the top active TFs in ASS-ILD’s BALF monocyte-Mφ were dominated by those in the interferon pathway (IRF1,IRF7,IRF9) and the JAK-STAT pathway (STAT1,STAT2)(Fig.5D). In contrast, the IPF group was mainly dominated by TFs related to tissue repair and chronic fibrosis(FOS,SPI1,JUND, Figure S10A, S10B). In ASS-ILD, the TFs highly correlated with ARS expression wereATF3andELF4(mean AUCs score = 0.071, 0.053, respectively), which are related to inflammation and extracellular stimulation(Fig.5E).The GO-BP and KEGG enrichment terms for their target genes included inflammatory pathways (NF-κB, TNF pathway), protein modification, and endocytosis (all adj.P< 0.001, Fig.5F and G, S10C, S10D). These results support the proposed theory that monocyte-Mφ, upon interacting with extracellular proteins, initiate inflammatory and immune pathways, leading to the upregulation of ARS expression and exhibiting a pro-inflammatory and pro-immune phenotype.

Another point of interest is the high abundance of ARS protein in the BALF(Fig.4H). The mechanism by which Mφ release highly expressed ARS into the extracellular space warrants attention. By analyzing cellular programmed death, we found that the apoptotic level of ASS-ILD monocyte-Mφ in BALF was highly increased, with higher scores observed in the Mono-Mφ and Mφ-CXCL10 clusters (bothP< 2e-16, Fig.5H and I). Other programmed death scores did not show differences among the three groups (Figure S10E, S10F).

Therefore, based on the integrated multi-omics data, we propose a hypothetical model for the abnormal monocyte-Mφ dynamics in the ASS-ILD(Fig.5J): In the BALF, monocyte-Mφ may recognize the ARS-antibody IC via Fcγ R, internalize the IC through endocytosis, and subsequently activate NF-κB via TLR signaling. This activation then could upregulate the activity of corresponding TFs, which correlates with enhanced ARS synthesis and inflammatory responses. Further metabolic reprogramming and the hypoxic environment may lead to increased monocyte-Mφ apoptosis, which, combined with local inflammation, results in the further release of autoantigen.

Monocyte-Mφ are capable of extensive communication with other immune cells, often mediated by antibody and receptor interactions on their surfaces. We performed a CellChat analysis on the pulmonary monocyte-Mφ to explore the role of cell communication in tissue damage. CellChat analysis revealed a highly interactive cellular micro-environment, where the monocyte-Mφ clusters emerged as the important source and receiver of signaling, exhibiting a higher total number and strength of interactions, particularly with cDCs(Fig.7A and B). Grouped analysis showed that in ASS-ILD, communication was stronger among monocyte-Mφ, T cells and NEU, whereas in IPF patients, the strongest communication was between epithelial cells and other immune cells (Figure S11A). Specifically, MHC-I molecules showed strong predicted interactions with T cell receptors such as CD8A and CD8B, suggesting a significant role for MHC-I-dependent cell-cell recognition in inducing local tissue injury(Fig.7C).

Consequently, we performed a further analysis of the T cell subsets within the BALF. Single-cell analysis revealed 8 distinct T cell subsets in the BALF, demonstrating a significant compositional shift in ILD patients compared to HC(Fig.7D and E, S11B). While HC T cell compartments were dominated by quiescent tissue-resident memory T (TRM T) cells, both ASS-ILD and IPF cohorts showed expansion towards activated effector subsets, notably CD8+Cytotoxic T and CD8+T Effector Memory (TEM) cells. Crucially, the ASS-ILD group was uniquely characterized by a striking and specific enrichment of the CD8+Interferon-responsive T (IFN-resp.T) cluster, suggesting an important role for chronic, IFN-driven inflammation in this disease(Fig.7F). GO enrichment of ASS-ILD T cells in BALF showed that these expanded CD8+T cell subsets were strongly associated with pathways for cell killing and the activation of the type II Interferon pathway, underscoring their cytotoxic and inflammatory potential (Figure S11C). Following the characterization of cytotoxic CD8+T cell expansion, we investigated the potential for functional exhaustion in these subsets, a common feature of chronic inflammation. Analysis of key exhaustion markers, includingPDCD1,LAG3, and the main regulatorTOX, revealed a pronounced exhaustion signature in the CD8+T cells of both ASS-ILD and IPF patients(Fig.7G, S11D), particularly within the cytotoxic and IFN-responsive clusters. This suggests that the sustained inflammatory microenvironment drives these CD8+T cells into a state of dysfunctional hyper-activation, a hallmark of chronic antigen stimulation and T cell exhaustion in the alveolar space.

The significant enrichment of NEU in the ASS-ILD patient’s BALF suggested a profound local inflammatory response. CellChat analysis between the monocyte-Mφ subsets and NEU revealed a robust signaling axis, with NEU acting as a meaningful recipient of signals from the myeloid compartment(Fig.7H). Detailed ligand-receptor analysis showed that this communication was overwhelmingly driven by the chemokine signaling pathway. Monocyte-Mφ subsets, particularly the Mφ-CXCL10 cluster, were identified as the primary source of potent attractants, including CXCL (CXCL8,CXCL5,CXCL2) and CCL(CCL7,CCL3) chemokines, which target NEU receptors CXCR1/2 and CCR1/3 (Figure S11E). This chemokine efflux was functionally supported by GO analysis of ASS-ILD NEU, which showed striking enrichment in pathways related to leukocyte chemotaxis and inflammatory response(Fig.7I). These findings collectively demonstrated that monocyte-Mφ clusters could orchestrate the recruitment and functional programming of NEU in ASS-ILD, thereby significantly amplifying the local inflammatory cascade within the alveolar space.

Through a comprehensive, integrated analysis of peripheral blood and BALF samples using scRNA-seq and Pro-DIA quantitative proteomics in patients with ASS-ILD, we have gained deeper insights into the immune pathological mechanisms of the disease. Our core finding is the marked disparity between a low-activation peripheral immune microenvironment and a highly focalized, hyperactive immune state in the lung. The alveolar inflammatory process appears to center on the dynamic dysregulation and functional reprogramming of the monocyte-Mφ lineage, which is notably characterized by enhanced tRNA aminoacylation and high expression of autoantigens.

ASS-ILD is an autoimmune disease recognized for its progressive and refractory course [1,28]. In a multi-centre ASS cohort contained 927 patients, ILD remains the leading cause of mortality (18.8%) [29]. Furthermore, 38.6% of ASS-ILD patients experienced relapse over a median follow-up of 4.3 years, with a 5-year relapse rate of 45.1% [30]. Another Chinese multi-centre cohort reported that 51.8% of ASS-ILD patients exhibit a fibrotic phenotype [31]. As Qiang et al. suggested, through HRCT analysis, that ASS is characterized by sustained inflammatory activity in imaging manifestations [32]. Therefore, it is crucial to delineate the lung pathogenesis and the reasons for recurrent inflammation to improve prognosis.

Our results indicate that the inflammation and immune responses in ASS are predominantly localized to the lung. Compared to PBMCs, the expression levels of cytokines and chemokines (IL1β,IL6,IFNγ,CCL2) were significantly elevated in the BALF, and inflammatory scores were universally upregulated across various cell subsets. Conversely, peripheral PBMCs lacked a significant state of inflammatory activation. This finding underscores that serum biomarkers likely reflect a pulmonary spillover effect rather than the primary driving factor. A similar pattern of localized inflammation has been observed in anti-MDA5 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, correlating with the central role of activated monocytes-Mφ in driving the cytokine storm [33]. Related research on fibrotic ILD, such as IPF, also shows a peripheral-pulmonary immune imbalance, where local lung lesions involve the activation of Mφ and lymphocytes, driving chronic inflammation and fibrosis [24,34]. Proteomic features in IPF lungs demonstrate the upregulation of pro-fibrotic factors (TGFβ, PDGF) and potential suppression of anti-oxidant & anti-inflammatory pathways, consistent with our findings [35]. Immunological studies focusing on ASS-ILD PBMCs and BALF have indicated that serum inflammatory markers may not adequately reflect the severity of the lung disease. Notarnicola et al. proposed that ASS-ILD patients have significantly higher BALF titers of anti-HARS-WHEP antibodies than in peripheral blood, and BALF anti-HARS-WHEP IgG reactivity correlated with poor pulmonary function [36]. In contrast, another cohort of Swedish IIM patients, including ASS cases, did not detect neutralizing autoantibodies against IFN-α or other IFNs, a finding that contrasts sharply with the anti-Type I Interferon autoantibodies characteristic of other systemic autoimmune diseases like systemic lupus erythematosus, further suggesting indistinct peripheral inflammatory features in IIM [37].

Our results highlight the substantial role of monocyte-Mφ in the immune-inflammation and fibrotic progression of ASS-ILD. Studies have shown that circulating monocytes were chemoattracted and recruited to the lung tissue in pulmonary fibrosis, subsequently differentiating into monocyte-derived Mφ and polarizing [24,38]. In certain models of pulmonary fibrosis (e.g., bleomycin-induced), newly recruited monocyte-derived Mφ are even more critical than tissue-resident Mφ in the onset and development of fibrosis [38,39]. In ASS, Mφ activation is also evident in muscle injury, with CD68+MHC-II+CD169+Mφ infiltration being a characteristic pathological feature of ASS myositis [9]. Furthermore, the observed characteristic of MDSC-like cells migration and differentiation from the periphery to the local site in ASS aligns with previous reports of the pro-inflammatory role of MDSC-like cells in systemic autoimmune diseases. For instance, in SLE, MDSC-like cells promote Th17 differentiation and exacerbate disease severity [40], while in rheumatoid arthritis, they produce proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) and induce Th17 differentiation [41].

Another key finding of our study is the intricate interplay among immunity, metabolism, and inflammation in ASS pathogenesis, positioning alveolar monocyte-Mφ as the notable link. Our results suggest that the activation of pulmonary monocyte-Mφ in ASS maybe initiated by local immune abnormalities (antigen-antibody complexes), leading to specific metabolic reprogramming (tRNA aminoacylation & ARS expression upregulation). The cell activation could mediate the upregulation of downstream inflammatory pathways (NF-κB) and cytokines (IL-1β,IL-6,VEGFA). Increased Mφ apoptosis further releases autoantigen, allowing the immune-inflammatory cycle to repeat, thereby manifesting local damage with recurrent, progressive, and fibrotic characteristics. The development of a ASS in vivo model using HARS-WHEP protein, which induced Jo-1 antibodies, ILD, and myositis in mice, is highly instructive [5,20]. The induction of auto-antibodies and subsequent tissue damage by a specific auto-antigen represent a classic immune-mediated inflammatory disease (IMID) cascade leading to multi-organ involvement [42].

Integrating our scRNA-seq data, we proposed that the monocyte-Mφ lineage as the hub of lung injury and provided preliminary validation of the immune-metabolism-inflammation pathway via HARS-containing IC stimulation. Related research supports the pathogenic role of the antigen and antibodies: in the mice model, ARS can be cleaved by trypsin B to generate immunogenic fragments that chemoattract lymphocytes, monocytes, and DCs, triggering a local immune response [43]. Studies on CD4+T cells in ASS patient BALF found that HisRS (HARS) N-terminal derived peptides induce a pro-inflammatory T cell phenotype, accompanied by the formation of pulmonary germinal center-like structures and anti-Jo-1 antibody production, suggesting that HARS may initiate an autoimmune cascade via antigen presentation in the lung micro-environment [44]. Regarding antibodies, in vivo experiments suggested that anti-ARS antibodies themselves possess chemokine-like activity, inducing the chemotaxis of CD8+T cells, CD4+T cells, DCs, and M1-like Mφ differentiated under IFNγ stimulation, thereby triggering the inflammatory response in ASS-ILD [45]. Furthermore, reports indicated that JAK inhibitors demonstrated a significant immunologic and clinical effectiveness in ASS patients, reducing key immune cell populations and downregulating immune activation pathways [46]. These researches are consistent with our result about the upregulated STAT1 and STAT2 transcription factor activity in ASS pulmonary monocyte-Mφ, and support the view that ASS is a typical antibody mediated IMID. Moreover, the superior efficacy of B cell depletion therapy compared to conventional immuno-suppressive therapy in ASS-ILD also highlights the importance of antibodies in ASS pathogenesis [47].

Another noteworthy finding from our data is that the upregulation of ARS expression in Mφ from patients with ASS-ILD was not confined to a single specific ARS but involved elevated expression of multiple ARSs, including those for which corresponding autoantibodies have not yet been reported [1,2](Fig.4G). Integrating this observation with reports of ASS patients harboring multiple ARS antibodies [48], we propose that the pathogenesis of ASS may involve selective loss of immune tolerance to different ARSs. Furthermore, active tRNA metabolism and elevated autoantigen expression likely provide the mechanistic basis for the generation of these autoantibodies which warrants further investigation.

The amplification of local injury, mediated by pulmonary monocyte-Mφ activation of CD8 + T cells and upregulation of the type II interferon pathway, is of particular importance. The role of intercellular communication in amplifying ASS-ILD tissue damage is evidenced by the interaction between monocyte-Mφ and CD8+T cells and NEU, primarily regulated by the MHC-I and chemokine pathways, leading to cytotoxic T cell expansion, exhaustion, and NEU recruitment. This aligns with the Mφ-lymphocyte axis described in fibrotic ILD immune mechanism studies [49], highlighting the unique enrichment of IFN-responsive CD8+T cells in models of chronic antigen stimulation and supporting potential strategies that inhibit signaling like CXCL8/CCR1 to alleviate tissue damage. Reports analyzing PBMCs in ASS-ILD using scRNA-seq suggested that ASS-ILD is characterized by upregulated interferon II responses and altered CD8+T cell homeostasis [7]. Our results showed that these features are even more pronounced in the lung tissue, where the inflammation and immune response are stronger, and CD8+T cells exhibit characteristics of exhaustion. The in vivo researches indicate that chronic inflammation promotes CD8+T cell exhaustion, where high levels of inhibitory molecules and cytokines in the inflammatory microenvironment persistently suppress T cell function, forcing them into an exhausted state [50]. Combined with our data, the ARS-containing IC participated continuous stimulation of monocyte-Mφ may amplify tissue damage through CD8+T cells and NEUs, leading to the sustained progression and chronicity of inflammation.

Our study faces several limitations. First, a subset of the PBMC and BALF scRNA-seq data for IPF and HCs were obtained from public datasets. Although efforts were made to match ethnicity, age, and gender, cross-dataset technical heterogeneity may influence certain group-wise comparisons. These findings should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. Second, although pseudotime analysis with peripheral monocytes as the starting point generated a differentiation trajectory consistent with monocyte recruitment and differentiation toward alveolar Mφ, the cross-sectional nature of the integrated dataset precludes definitive proof of true in vivo lineage relationships. This warrants further validation through in vivo experiments. Third, ASS involves multiple ARS auto-antibodies, and clinical features are not entirely uniform across different antibodies. Constrained by sample size, we did not distinguish between antibody types or conduct longitudinal follow-up to observe treatment response over time. More critically, even we found increased expression of multiple ARS antigens in patients with different auto-antibodies, based on current data, we cannot definitively explain why only a specific antibody appears when multiple ARSs are upregulated. Although there are reports of ASS patients with multiple ARS antibodies [48], we could only attribute this phenomenon to the selectivity or stochasticity of the loss of immune tolerance to specific ARS. Last but not least, while our conclusions are based on antigen-antibody mediated metabolic and inflammatory abnormalities, we unfortunately did not annotate B cells in the BALF of ASS-ILD patients. Thus, the precise origin of this immuno-inflammatory response remains to be clarified, necessitating more detailed sequential research.

In summary, our study reveals a significant discrepancy between the peripheral and alveolar immune microenvironments in ASS-ILD, suggesting that the monocyte-Mφ lineage serves as a potential hub for inflammation, metabolic reprogramming, and autoimmune pathology. The framework of study may elucidate the pathological basis for the refractoriness of ASS-ILD. This deepened understanding of ASS-ILD pathogenesis opens promising new avenues for identifying novel therapeutic targets and refining clinical assessment strategies.
