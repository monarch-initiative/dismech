Established Facts

Lateral meningocele syndrome (LMS) is caused by pathogenic variants in exon 33 of NOTCH3 that increase NOTCH3 cellular half-life through interruption of its regulatory PEST domain.

All previously reported patients have had multiple lateral meningoceles, and on average, these are clinically recognized by age 4.

Novel Insights

The reported 8-year-old patient shows that lateral meningoceles are not a universal finding in LMS.

Comparing this case to the 10 patients with a confirmed NOTCH3 variant better establishes the spectrum of neurological, craniofacial, musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, audiological, and cardiac manifestations associated with LMS.

Introduction

Lateral meningocele syndrome (LMS) is a rare connective tissue disorder caused by heterozygous pathogenic NOTCH3 gain–of-function variants in exon 33. Variants in this final exon of NOTCH3 interrupt the regulatory PEST domain, leading to enhanced NOTCH3 signaling due to prolonged cellular half-life [1]. To date, 20 cases of LMS have been reported, with half having received confirmatory genetic testing [2]. Individuals with LMS are expected to have developmental delay, hypotonia, dysmorphic facial features, feeding difficulties, and lateral meningoceles [3, 4]. Common findings include ligamentous laxity, scalloped vertebra, short stature, hernias (predominantly umbilical and inguinal), scoliosis, pectus deformity, cryptorchidism, congenital cardiac abnormalities, and hearing loss [3, 4]. Less common findings include proptosis, wormian bones, skin hyperextensibility, paresthesia, urinary incontinence, and keloids [4, 5]. All previous molecularly confirmed individuals with LMS had multiple lateral meningoceles before age 8 years, which are caused by weaknesses of the dura and arachnoid mater that lead to protrusion through the spinal foramina [5]. Here, we expand the spectrum of LMS by reporting an individual with a pathogenic truncating variant in exon 33 of NOTCH3 who lacks lateral meningoceles.

Case Presentation

An 8-year-old male presented to genetics with autism spectrum disorder, hypotonia, severe intellectual disability, dysmorphic facial features, epilepsy, and self-injurious behavior. He was born at 37 weeks’ gestation as part of a nonconsanguineous union to a Caucasian 34-year-old G3P2101 mother. His pregnancy was complicated by maternal hepatitis C, poorly controlled maternal type 1 diabetes, and exposure to benzodiazepines and opiates. His birth weight was 3.15 kg (25th percentile, z = −0.68), length was 53.5 cm (91st percentile, z = 1.33), and head circumference was 35 cm (43rd percentile, z = −0.39).

He had global hypotonia and an absent gag reflex at delivery, leading to a brain MRI on day of life 1 that showed small bilateral intraventricular and subdural hemorrhages with periventricular calcifications. A urine cytomegalovirus PCR was negative. Follow-up brain imaging at age 15 months found ventriculomegaly, cerebellar tonsillar ectopia, and moderate crowding at the foramen magnum, though serial imaging showed spontaneous resolution of his ventriculomegaly and crowding. His last head circumference at 5 years 6 months was within normal limits (50.5 cm, 30th percentile, z = −0.52). His most recent imaging at age 7 years was only notable for stable moderate volume loss of the white matter and moderate thinning of the corpus callosum. Following a tonic seizure at age 1 month, an EEG showed multifocal epileptiform discharges that were controlled with oral phenobarbital. His last known seizure occurred at age 5 years, but he continues to take oxcarbazepine and cannabidiol oils.

He has required multidisciplinary care throughout his life. Severe reflux led to a Nissen fundoplication and gastrostomy tube (GT) placement at age 4 months, and he remains GT dependent with severe oral aversions. Auditory brainstem response testing at age 15 months showed mild conductive hearing loss bilaterally, though he maintains functional hearing without hearing aids. He has ongoing ophthalmologic management of his nystagmus, exotropia, and bilateral hyperopic astigmatism. An echocardiogram at age 14 months showed asymmetric hypertrophy of the ventricular septum and an anomalous aortic origin of the coronary artery from the ascending aorta.

An ADOS assessment at age 6 years led to a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder. The Mullen Scales of Early Learning at that time suggested that his developmental abilities were severely delayed, ranging from expressive language at a 6-month age equivalence to fine motor skills at a 20-month age equivalence. Currently, he remains nonverbal but can communicate through gestures. He has frequent headbanging, sleep disruption, and self-injurious behaviors requiring arm cuff and helmet use.

At age 1 month, his hypotonia in combination with posteriorly rotated ears and mild micrognathia prompted a microarray, which returned as normal. Upon return to genetics care at age 7 years, he had additional dysmorphic features noted including a prominent metopic ridging, broad forehead, downslanting palpebral fissures, high-arched palate, short stature, and a mild pectus excavatum (shown in Fig. 1). An autism/ID gene panel revealed a NOTCH3 (NM_000435.3) c.6663C>G (p.Y2221*) pathogenic variant, consistent with a diagnosis of LMS.

Following his diagnosis, he had a normal renal ultrasound and an echocardiogram unchanged from his previous imaging. A spine MRI at age 7 years showed a ventral sacral extradural arachnoid cyst but no lateral meningoceles (shown in Fig. 2). His most recent height at 8 years 8 months was 121.5 cm (4th percentile, Z = −1.73), and his weight was 27.8 kg (52nd percentile, Z = 0.06).

Discussion

This case expands the phenotype for LMS, as all previously reported patients with molecularly confirmed cases had multiple lateral meningoceles before age 8 years, with the average age of identification being approximately 4 years [1, 3–11]. However, other expected characteristics of LMS are present in this patient (Fig. 1; Table 1). While 11 other genetically confirmed cases have been reported, the patient reported by Han et al. [2] was excluded due to insufficient phenotyping (defined as having less than 25% of evaluated characteristics reported).

This patient shares the dysmorphic facial features seen among other individuals with LMS, and he shares the ongoing GT dependence, failure to thrive, developmental delay, and self-injurious behavior seen in other individuals with LMS. His anomalous coronary artery origin is comparable to the abnormalities of the aorta and vena cava seen in other individuals with LMS, though his asymmetric hypertrophy of the ventricular septum is more likely related to maternal diabetes [12] rather than his LMS. His overall functioning is much lower than most other reported patients, including a 9-year-old patient with lateral meningoceles but no intellectual disability who shares this p.Y2221* variant. The larger cohort suggests that early developmental delays are nearly universal, but only one other case has had intellectual disability. The discrepancy in the reported individual’s intellectual functioning may be related to his prenatal polysubstance exposure or his concern for early childhood neglect. The additional features that cannot be explained by environmental factors suggest significant variable expressivity associated with truncating variants in exon 33 of NOTCH3.

Compared to previously reported cases, this individual establishes that patients with LMS may not have lateral meningoceles by age 8 years. However, all individuals with LMS share craniofacial abnormalities (11/11) and neonatal hypotonia (11/11). Gross motor delay was universal (11/11), but intellectual disability was rare (2/7). Neonatal feeding intolerance and failure to thrive requiring GT or NG placement were reported in all (5/5) patients with neonatal medical histories in the medical literature. Thoracolumbar lateral meningoceles (10/11), a wide spinal canal (7/11), scalloped vertebrae (7/11), scoliosis (7/11), and Wormian bones (2/9) were the most prevalent findings on head and spine imaging. Joint hypermobility (9/10) was commonly seen along with keloid scars (4/6) and skin hyperextensibility (2/10) (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000536632).

There are currently no established LMS surveillance guidelines. Cuoco et al. [13] recommend close clinical and MRI monitoring to evaluate meningocele status and potential worsening of neurogenic bladder and paresthesia. A potential approach could include a spine MRI at the time of diagnosis followed by repeat imaging occurring every 6 months to 2 years based on the size and stability of the lateral meningoceles.

Despite the lack of lateral meningoceles in this patient, we anticipate repeating spine MRIs at least every 2 years with a low threshold to obtain more frequent imaging if new neurological symptoms or behavioral changes develop. Currently proposed pathophysiology suggests that congenital weaknesses in the arachnoid and dura mater lead to protrusion of the arachnoid and dura mater through the spinal foramina, particularly over the thoracic and lumbar spine that are exposed to greater hydrostatic pressure [5]. Hydrostatic pressure proportionally increases with fluid height, implying that growth of the spine with age will increase hydrostatic pressure. Thus, meningoceles may worsen during childhood and then stabilize in late adolescence as individuals reach their adult height. More research is needed to determine the expected evolution of lateral meningoceles.

Management of LMS should include a multidisciplinary specialist team to monitor and manage developmental, musculoskeletal, ophthalmologic, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, genitourinary, and auditory problems to provide the best possible patient outcomes. Amelioration of correctable sensory issues in children at risk for developmental delay is especially critical. Early intervention therapies, such as physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy, can optimize developmental outcomes as needed in patients with developmental delay [14].

In conclusion, this individual expands the characteristic features of LMS and is the first recognized case to present without lateral meningoceles. His case confirms that lateral meningoceles are not a universal finding in LMS.
