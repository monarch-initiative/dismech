Focal cortical dysplasias (FCDs) are malformations of cortical development 
(MCDs) that are highly associated with medication-resistant epilepsy and are the 
most common cause of neocortical epilepsy in children. FCDs are a heterogeneous 
group of developmental disorders caused by germline or somatic mutations that 
occur in genes regulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway-a key pathway in neuronal 
growth and migration. Accordingly, FCDs are characterized by abnormal cortical 
lamination, cell morphology (e.g., cytomegaly), and cellular polarity. In some 
FCD subtypes, balloon cells express proteins typically seen in neuroglial 
progenitor cells. Because recurrent intractable seizures are a common feature of 
FCDs, epileptogenic electrophysiological properties are also observed in 
addition to local inflammation. Here, we will summarize the current literature 
regarding FCDs, addressing the current classification system, histopathology, 
molecular genetics, electrophysiology, and transcriptome and cell signaling 
changes.

INTRODUCTION
Focal cortical dysplasias (FCDs) are developmental malformations of the cerebral cortex that
are highly associated with medically intractable epilepsy. Originally described in separate reports
by Crome (1) and Taylor et al. (2), the clear association between FCD and medication-resistant
seizures has been ﬁrmly established over the past 20 years. Although it seems obvious with the
knowledge currently available, at the time of these descriptions, the authors postulated that these
areas of dysplastic cortex served as the possible substrate for seizures in these individuals. With
the advent of higher-resolution brain imaging techniques (e.g., 3 Tesla MRI), FCDs are now
identiﬁed more easily than in previous years and, as evidence has conﬁrmed, are clearly linked
to seizure onset (3). The actual incidence and prevalence of FCDs in the general population are
unknown, and it seems that FCDs are uncommon as incidental radiographic ﬁndings. However,
among individuals with pharmacoresistant seizures, especially children, FCDs likely account
for a majority of neocortical epilepsy cases (4, 5). Of particular interest, many cases of so-called
nonlesional neocortical epilepsy (i.e., individuals with normal MRI results) in fact result from
a small FCD that is undetectable by MRI but is found during histopathological analysis of
resected tissue. The standard treatment for intractable seizures associated with FCD is surgical
resection of the lesion; several histopathological grading systems have demonstrated the spectrum
of pathological abnormalities in FCD, suggesting that FCD is not a unitary diagnosis but a
heterogeneous group of neurodevelopmental disorders. Perhaps most exciting, in the past few
years, single causative gene mutations, both germline and somatic, have been found for FCD. The
majority of these mutations have been identiﬁed in genes encoding regulatory proteins within
the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, suggesting that aberrant mTOR pathway
signaling is a critical mechanism accounting for the histopathological features of some FCD
subtypes. These disorders have thus been grouped functionally as disorders of mTOR signaling or
mTORopathies (3).
HISTOPATHOLOGY
From the ﬁrst descriptions of FCD, it was clear that the sine qua non feature of FCD was
disorganization of the cerebral cortex cytoarchitecture, albeit of variable severity and type, in
lesions that are either microscopic or grossly apparent on pathological analysis. The normal
hexalaminar structure of the cortex is either disorganized or completely lost. FCDs exhibit some
variation in size, from a few millimeters to several centimeters in area, with some extending from
the deep white matter to layer I and others only affecting select laminae. There is often an indeter-
minate border between the actual lesion and the surrounding, structurally intact cerebral cortex,
highlighting the focality of the lesion. All FCD subtypes may exhibit some degree of abnormal
gray matter–white matter border boundaries, and heterotopic neurons are often observed in the
subcortical white matter. FCDs typically occur as single lesions, although there are case reports
of multiple FCDs in the same patient, and may be identiﬁed in any brain lobe; indeed, tubers in
tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) have recently been classiﬁed as FCDs (4), and it is common
for TSC patients to have multiple tubers. In addition, the presence of novel cell types not found
in the normal cortex, including very large and oddly shaped neurons [cytomegalic dysmorphic
neurons (CDNs)] ( Figure 1 ) as well as bizarre, massively enlarged ovoid cells [balloon cells
(BCs)] (Figure 2 ), may be recognized. Excessive numbers of astrocytes and microglia occur to a
variable extent in some specimens. There is also clear evidence for abnormal myelination in FCD
subtypes (5).

histological features (9). These investigators assessed FCD as a pathological diagnosis rather than
a descriptive term for all types of MCD. In 2012, an international task force convened by the

differentiate distinct FCD subtypes (10). A particularly novel feature of the ILAE system was
the association between FCDs and other brain lesions such as tumors, hippocampal sclerosis,
and vascular malformations. Although the ILAE classiﬁcation is wholly descriptive and does not
account for molecular genetic etiologies in FCD, it has good interrater reliability and provides
a conceptual means to interpret pathology in resected epilepsy specimens (11). The type I(a–c)
FCDs are characterized by alterations in columnar/radial (Ia) or laminar/tangential (Ib) structure,
or both (Ic). In type I FCDs, hypertrophic pyramidal neurons are seen (although they are smaller
than CDNs) outside of layer V. These changes may be visualized using NeuN or microtubule
associated protein-2 (MAP2) immunolabeling. Type Ia FCDs may show a smaller cortical ribbon
width and higher neuronal densities than Ib or Ic (6). Type I FCDs may affect small areas of
the cortex and be difﬁcult to detect even by an experienced neuropathologist. Because these
cytoarchitectural aberrations may be subtle, type I FCDs are often radiographically occult.
In contrast, type II FCDs are more often easily visualized by MRI and are characterized
by marked disruption of the laminar cortical structure and the appearance of morphologically
abnormal cell types, including CDNs or BCs. CDNs exhibit aberrant dendritic arbors, altered
polarity, inappropriate radial organization, and a markedly enlarged cell soma, all of which can
be easily detected with SMI-31 immunolabeling (6). CDNs may be found across the width of the
cortical mantle, in the deep white matter, or in more superﬁcial portions of the cortex. CDNs
may be critically important as seizure generators in FCD (see the section titled Electrophysiology
in Focal Cortical Dysplasia). In type IIb FCDs, BCs, in addition to CDNs and severe laminar
disorganization, are the deﬁning cell type. These massively enlarged, ovoid cells express markers
of both neuronal and glial lineages, as well as markers of neuroglial progenitor cells such as nestin,
Sox2, and vimentin (see below) (12). BCs typically express small atrophic processes, or none at all,
that have not clearly polarized into axons or dendrites. Loss of myelination is often observed in
type II FCDs, especially type IIb, as evidenced by the pallor seen with Luxol-Fast-Blue staining;
the transmantle sign—a tail of cells extending into the white matter often seen on MRI—is highly
speciﬁc for type IIb FCDs. Recently, cortical tubers in TSC were renamed as FCD, although this
nomenclature is speciﬁcally excluded by the ILAE classiﬁcation, and the histological features are
consistent speciﬁcally with type IIb FCDs. Similarly, hemimegalencephaly (HME), a hemispheric
malformation highly associated with seizures, often exhibits a type IIa or IIb cytopathology.
Type III FCDs are essentially type I FCDs with an additional brain lesion and are classiﬁed as IIIa
(hippocampal sclerosis), IIIb (tumor), IIIc (vascular malformation), or IIId (lesions acquired in
early life, e.g., glial scar due to infectious or ischemic injury). In the case of type II FCDs, current
belief is that the cortical disorganization is linked to the additional pathological feature, i.e.,
hippocampal sclerosis or vascular malformation. When type II FCDs are found in conjunction
with, for example, hippocampal sclerosis or a vascular malformation, this is thought to reﬂect a
distinct pathological process and is thus termed dual pathology.

Cell Lineage in Focal Cortical Dysplasia
The cerebral cortex forms between gestational weeks 8 and 20 by a highly orchestrated and elegant
process controlled by numerous regulatory genes. In brief, neuroglial progenitor cells undergo
rounds of mitosis within the telencephalic ventricular zone (VZ) to generate cells that will populate
the cerebral cortex. At deﬁned time points, progenitor cells exit the cell cycle and commit to a
neural lineage. These cells then migrate radially from the VZ, guided by radial glial cell ﬁbers, to
the nascent cortical plate (CP) to generate cortical layers. This process proceeds in an inside-out
gradient, with cells destined for layer VI arriving ﬁrst, followed by those destined for layers V, IV,
III, and II. Layer I forms as a result of the remaining preplate. In contrast, inhibitory interneurons
leave the ganglionic eminences in the ventral telencephalon and migrate along nonradial pathways
to cortical destinations. A pivotal goal of many recent studies has been to attempt to pinpoint the
gestational time point for formation of FCD using cell lineage–speciﬁc protein markers to identify
the speciﬁc populations of neuroglial progenitor cells that give rise to the CDNs and BCs.
Until the early 2000s, much of the work on FCD had been descriptive neuropathology report-
ing the expression of single or small families of proteins in small cohorts of surgically resected FCD
specimens. Subsequently, studies evaluating protein expression in a developmental context estab-
lished several important features of FCD. For example, CDNs express a number of cytoskeletal
proteins found in normal neocortical pyramidal cells, including neuroﬁlaments and microtubule
associated protein-2 (MAP2) (10). Neurons in FCD and HME also express abnormally high levels
of tau (15, 16). The expression of layer-speciﬁc marker proteins in type IIb FCDs, including OTX1
(layers V and VI) or neurochemical markers [i.e., the vesicular glutamate transporter (VGLUT)],
suggested that many CDNs derived from neuroglial progenitors in the telencephalic VZ, the
normal source of cortical pyramidal cells, and that FCD likely resulted from aberrant movement
of cells from the cortical plate during radial migration (17). CDNs may also express markers of
cellular immaturity such as nestin or MAP1B. Interestingly, morphologically normal neurons in
FCD may also express markers of immaturity (18). These data would suggest that FCDs form
during the critical period of corticogenesis occurring in human gestational weeks 8–20, when
cortical laminae are established. In support of this hypothesis, fetal MRI studies and analysis of
fetopsy specimens in TSC demonstrate that tubers may be detected as early as gestational week
20 (19, 20).

by non-cell-autonomous mechanisms. One study identiﬁed a subset of cytomegalic neurons that

enhanced cell size (cytomegaly) could be seen in cells derived from the GE as well as from the VZ.
BCs present a particular challenge in understanding the pathogenic mechanisms of FCD
formation. They are the deﬁning cell type in type IIb FCDs and are also seen in TSC tubers.
BCs are not present in the normal cerebral cortex, and their appearance thus suggests either the
birth of a new cell type unique to the pathogenic mechanism of type IIb FCDs and tubers or the
transformation of an existing cell type into a grossly distorted morphology. BCs express a het-
erogeneous and often mixed complement of lineage markers, e.g., glial and neuronal, suggesting
that these cells share some heritage with neuroglial progenitor cells. BCs express nestin, CD34,
and vimentin, proteins typically expressed in neuroglial progenitor cells (25, 26, 27), and variably
express glial ﬁbrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a glial marker, and neuroﬁlament, a neuronal
marker (28). Thus, in the same tissue specimen, BCs might express GFAP, MAP2, or both. BCs
also express doublecortin, a marker of cell immaturity (29). These observations indicate great
cell-type heterogeneity within this class of cells. BCs variably express OTX1 but do not express
either Dlx1 or Dlx2, markers of inhibitory interneurons derived from the GE (17). A notable
feature of BCs is the expression of stem cell marker proteins such as nestin, vimentin, Klf-4, Sox2,
c-myc, CRMP4, and GFAPdelta (12, 17), suggesting either a gross alteration in transcriptional
or translational expression or a stem cell phenotype. BCs express minichromosome maintenance
protein 2 (30) and CD133 (a stem cell marker) (31), suggesting that, like stem cells, BCs may
exist in a G1/S phase transition point, a time during the cell cycle at the boundary between the
G1 phase, in which the cell physically grows, and the S phase, when DNA replication is initiated.
Interestingly, although BCs express markers of cellular immaturity, they rarely express markers
of frank cell division such as proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) or Ki-67, suggesting that
FCDs are not mitotically active in a way that glial neoplasms might be; indeed, there are no reports
of enlarging FCDs. Thus, it seems that these cells are trapped in a near-stem-cell state yet are not
actively dividing. This seems plausible because two in vitro studies demonstrated that BCs could
be grown in culture (12, 32). In cortical tubers and type II FCDs, there are signiﬁcant increases
in both terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL)-positive and
caspase-3-positive BCs and CDNs compared with control specimens (33, 34), suggesting that
there may be cell turnover in FCDs and that the cellular milieu may be dynamic rather than static.

in FCDs, suggesting that the formation of FCDs occurs within the evolving cortical plate, sparing
the preplate where CR cells are generated. In addition, no study has comprehensively compared

GENE AND PROTEIN EXPRESSION PROFILES OF FOCAL
CORTICAL DYSPLASIA
Cells within FCDs display a wide variety of protein expression, and the types of proteins expressed
can provide insight into the developmental epoch in which a mutation occurred and produced the
resulting lesion. Broadly, these proteins include cytoskeletal elements, ion channels, G-protein-
coupled receptors, and secreted proteins.
Expression of Cytoskeletal Elements
Cytoskeletal proteins contribute to a number of important functions in the development of the
cerebral cortex, including neuronal migration, axon and dendrite outgrowth, and transport of
molecules within the cell (35). A common ﬁnding in tissue resections from patients with FCD is
expression of cytoskeletal elements typically found in immature neurons in the developing cortex,

proper organization of the cortex at the earliest stages of brain development.

FOCAL CORTICAL DYSPLASIA AND MTOR SIGNALING
Until 2004, investigators had few etiological clues to FCD pathogenesis. The ﬁrst insights came
from studies which demonstrated that human type IIb FCD and cortical tuber specimens resected
during epilepsy surgery exhibited enhanced activation of the mTOR signaling pathway (63, 64)
(Figure 3). These studies found enhanced phosphorylation of ribosomal S6 and 4E-BP1 proteins,
which are downstream mTOR targets. These phospho-isoforms were identiﬁed immunohis-
tochemically in CDNs and BCs with remarkable consistency, and these ﬁndings were soon
corroborated by additional groups (65, 66). These studies reached three important conclusions:
(a) Activation of mTOR signaling could distinguish type IIb FCDs from normal cerebral cortex
and TSC from perituberal cortex; ( b) the fact that TSC specimens also showed the same patterns
and that studies at that time linked TSC1 and TSC2 to the insulin-like growth factor signaling
pathway provided support for the notion that FCDs were caused by similar aberrant signaling
in that cascade; and ( c) the proﬁle of phospho-activation was distinct between FCD and TSC,
suggesting similar but not identical etiologies (e.g., that type IIb FCDs were not what had been
referred to as forme fruste, or a brain-only subtype of TSC). These observations in human tissue
specimens were strongly supported by subsequent studies in cell culture and animal models in
which knockdown or knockout of Tsc1 or Tsc2 was tightly associated with enhanced mTOR
activation, an effect that was readily blocked by mTOR inhibitors such as rapamycin (67, 68). Over
the next several years, other groups demonstrated mTOR hyperactivation in other focal cortical
malformation subtypes such as HME (65, 69) and ganglioglioma (70). Enhanced mTOR signaling
has now been identiﬁed in type IIa and IIb FCD specimens associated with somatic mutations
in mTOR regulatory genes, e.g., MTOR, DEPDC5,a n d NPRL3 (see the section titled Molecular
Genetics and Mechanisms of Lesion Formation: The Role of mTOR; Table 2 ; Figure 4 ), and
experimental manipulation of these genes indeed leads to altered cell morphology and hyperactive
mTOR signaling. A key element of hyperactive mTOR signaling is that it accounts for many of
the histological features of FCD, especially cytomegaly. In cell culture and mouse models, FCD
is linked to mutations in mTOR regulatory genes; a highly consistent and reproducible feature of
FCD in these models is cell enlargement, often to twice normal size. This effect can be blocked
or reversed by mTOR inhibitors. In addition, enhanced cell size may compromise cell motility.
Notably, several studies have now also found evidence for enhanced mTOR signaling in a
number of epilepsy-associated pathologies, including hippocampal sclerosis, type I FCDs, and
Rasmussen’s encephalitis, that have no identiﬁed link to mTOR pathway gene mutations (e.g.,
71). Of course, when mTOR activation was ﬁrst identiﬁed in type IIb FCD and HME, there was
no known molecular link to mTOR pathway gene mutations; thus, future studies may identify
pathogenic mutations in mTOR genes in these epilepsy-associated pathologies. In addition,
several recent studies have suggested that seizures alone may induce mTOR activation and may
be ameliorated by rapamycin therapy (72, 73). Thus, a critical question to be addressed in future
studies is to what extent mTOR activation is caused by the underlying molecular abnormality
versus induced by ongoing ictal discharges. This question has obvious implications for future
therapeutic development.

INFLAMMATION IN FOCAL CORTICAL DYSPLASIA
A number of studies have demonstrated clear evidence of an inﬂammatory response in resected
FCD samples (for a review, see 90). For example, numerous reactive astrocytes and activated
microglia were ﬁrst observed in cortical tubers (33) with CD68-labeled microglia and enhanced

primarily located around blood vessels (91). In both type IIb FCDs and TSC, the numbers of
inﬂammatory cells (e.g., microglia) did not correlate with any clinical features (e.g., seizure fre-
quency or duration). A subsequent study demonstrated inﬂammatory responses evident in fetal
TSC brains ranging from 23 to 38 gestational weeks (19). Fetal tubers contained macrophages,
and T-lymphocyte cells and BCs within the lesions expressed inﬂammatory response markers,
including major histocompatibility complex class I and II, Toll-like receptors (TLR) 2 and 4, and
receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) (19). Enhanced expression of IL-6 and its
receptors IL-6 receptor (IL-6R) and glycoprotein 130 (gp130) was observed in type IIb FCDs
and TSC tubers (92). These observations suggest the possible immunogenicity of BCs and the
early activation of inﬂammatory pathways in the TSC brain. In FCDs, inﬁltration of the lesions

compared to type I FCDs or control tissues. Expression of components of the complement cas-

bor mutations in a number of genes linked to mTOR signaling, i.e., PI3K, LKB1, PTEN (104),
suggesting a link between HPV and somatic gene mutations in mTOR regulatory genes in FCD
and glioma.
ELECTROPHYSIOLOGY OF FOCAL CORTICAL DYSPLASIA
The most prominent clinical presentation of FCD is medically intractable epilepsy. Often, surgical
removal of the lesion is the only option to alleviate seizures (105). Seizures typically ﬁrst appear in
early infancy or childhood, but many patients do not have their ﬁrst seizure until adulthood (106);
indeed, the ﬁrst paper describing FCDs detailed a number of patients whose initial seizure occurred
when they were adults (2).Thus, in vitro studies have attempted to deﬁne electrophysiological
abnormalities in FCD and the mechanisms by which altered brain structure leads to recurrent
seizures.
EEG and Organotypic Slice Culture Analysis of Focal Cortical Dysplasia Tissue
Numerous studies have focused on the electroencephalographic (EEG) signatures of FCDs. For
example, FCDs may exhibit high-amplitude spiking followed by postspike slow wave complexes.
Post-spike slow waves are particularly common in patients with type II FCDs and appear to
be localized to the seizure onset zone (107). Data also demonstrate a role for high-frequency
oscillations (HFOs), in the form of ripples and fast ripples, as a potential biomarker for focal
epilepsies. Ripples (80–200 Hz) and fast ripples (200–500 Hz) are recorded during intracranial
EEG, have greater speciﬁcity toward the seizure onset zone in patients with FCDs than do spikes,
and localize the seizure onset zone in MRI-negative lesions with a greater degree of precision
than do spikes (108). HFOs may be an effective predictor of seizure proclivity. This ﬁnding is
supported by evidence demonstrating that patients with type II FCDs have more seizures and are
more likely to have HFOs than patients with type I FCDs (109). In sum, HFOs may prove to be
an effective EEG biomarker for FCDs.
Organotypic slice cultures of resected lesions are a powerful tool for the examination of epilep-
togenic discharges within FCDs. These are thin slices of FCD taken from the resected specimen
and maintained in culture to investigate how individual cell types (e.g., BCs, dysplastic neurons,
normal cells) within the lesion contribute to pathological electrical discharges. In one study, treat-
ment of the slices with 4-aminopyridine, a potassium channel blocker and potent convulsant agent,
elicited prolonged depolarization and bursting action potentials similar to those observed by EEG
prior to surgical resection of FCDs (110). These electrographic ﬁring patterns were not observed
in control tissue. Interestingly, these ictal-like discharges were not attenuated by glutamate recep-
tor blockade but were decreased using the GABA
A receptor antagonist bicuculline, indicating that
the primary driving force for these pathological discharges is GABAergic rather than glutamater-
gic transmission. Similar discharges were observed only rarely in control tissue (110). Ictal-like
discharges triggered by a 4-aminopyradine bath appear to be unique to FCD tissue: A similar
study failed to produce epileptiform discharges from hippocampal sclerosis slices but was able
to produce discharges from FCD lesion slices (111). Most of these spiking patterns came from
deep within the lesion rather than near the cortical surface, which is consistent with histological

Cellular and Neurotransmitter Contributions to Abnormal Electrophysiology
in Focal Cortical Dysplasia
Dysplastic neurons are a major contributor to epileptiform discharges in the FCD network. For
example, dysplastic neurons within the lesion or in vitro reveal increased calcium inﬂux and cal-
cium currents upon stimulation, compared to normal neurons under the same conditions (115).
The morphology of dysplastic neurons also contributes to aberrant electrical discharges because
the increased cell size promotes larger cellular capacitance and lower input resistance. In Pten-( a n
inhibitory node of the mTOR pathway) knockout mice, cytomegalic neurons exhibited an overall
decrease in threshold and an increase in action potential ﬁring rates (116). In a separate study, elec-
trographic and behavioral seizures in Pten-null mice were diminished by treatment with rapamycin
(65), demonstrating a link between increased mTOR activity and abnormal electrophysiology. In
Tsc1-knockout mouse models, an increase in neuronal ﬁring and loss of excitatory/inhibitory bal-
ance is observed in hippocampal slice cultures; this, too, was corrected with rapamycin treatment
(117). It should be noted, however, that many TSC models display abnormal electrophysiological
properties (117) but lack dysmorphic/cytomegalic neurons, whereas those models that display ab-
normal cytoarchitecture (118) do not have abnormal electrophysiology, suggesting a large gap in
knowledge regarding how FCD-associated gene changes contribute to both abnormal physiology
and abnormal cellular morphology.
BCs have also been extensively examined in vitro (although no animal models have accurately
replicated a BC) and do not appear to contribute to abnormal electrical discharges. Surprisingly,
BCs lack voltage- and ligand-gated sodium and calcium currents and do not receive synaptic in-
put from surrounding neurons. Furthermore, BCs are insensitive to glutamate application (115,
119). Thus, BCs may not contribute to the epileptogenic network within the lesion and may,
in fact, provide an antiseizure effect. An important caveat in the use of slice cultures to exam-
ine FCDs is that, although the lesion drives seizures prior to surgical resection, ictal discharges
have not been observed in resected specimens without manipulation (e.g., electrical stimulation,
convulsant-containing washes, etc.); only pathological discharges have been observed under these
conditions. This indicates that a larger area of the brain or more intricate mechanisms may be at
play in generating seizures (110).

CONCLUSION
Over the past decade, major advances have occurred in our understanding of FCDs. We now
know that alterations in mTOR signaling result from single loss-of-function or gain-of-function
mutations in mTOR pathway regulatory genes. Thus, an exciting future direction will be the
establishment of clinical trials to test the efﬁcacy of mTOR inhibitors, such as sirolimus or
everolimus, for the treatment of seizures in these individuals. Critical variables in these trials
will include age at treatment initiation, dosing regimen, and treatment duration. For individuals

SUMMARY POINTS
1. FCDs are a common cause of epilepsy.
2. FCDs are caused by genetic mutations that lead to abnormal cytoarchitecture, altered
cellular function, and disorganized cortical lamination.
3. FCDs are currently classiﬁed and graded based on histopathology.
4. Many FCDs are caused by germline or somatic mutations in components of the
PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway.
5. Histological and molecular biological analysis of FCD tissue reveals that cells within a
dysplastic lesion retain an immature phenotype.
6. Retention of immature GABA receptors may contribute to hyperexcitability within
FCDs.
7. Along with genetic mutations, an inﬂammatory milieu also promotes seizures within the
malformed cortex.
FUTURE ISSUES
1. Detailed genetic analyses of FCD tissue reveal the need for a more detailed classiﬁcation
scheme of FCDs that encompasses the wide array of germline and somatic mutations
along the mTOR pathway.
2. Recent ﬁndings have demonstrated a role for mTOR regulatory proteins (e.g., DEPDC5
and NPRL3) in FCDs and epilepsy. Future studies should aim to deﬁne the pathophys-
iological consequences of these mutations on the cell and network levels.
3. The wide variety of mutations along the mTOR cascade in FCDs suggests the need for
more clinical trials to determine whether or not rapamycin and other rapalogs provide the
same efﬁcacy across FCD subtypes resulting from distinct mTOR pathway mutations.
4. These data also suggest that mTOR inhibitors may be clinically beneﬁcial in combination
with antiseizure medication or therapy postsurgery.
5. New approaches should be developed to determine how mutations in the mTOR cascade
produce MRI-negative lesions. This may lead to better imaging techniques to identify
these lesions without the use of invasive EEG techniques.
6. As deep-sequencing techniques advance, the ability to identify and determine the origin
of somatic mutations during embryogenesis will not only reveal new targets for therapy
but also help explain how somatic mutations occur during early development.
