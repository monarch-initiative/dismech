Pyruvate carboxylase (PC) deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive mitochondrial 
neurometabolic disorder of energy deficit resulting in high morbidity and 
mortality, with limited therapeutic options. The PC homotetramer has a critical 
role in gluconeogenesis, anaplerosis, neurotransmitter synthesis, and 
lipogenesis. The main biochemical and clinical findings in PC deficiency (PCD) 
include lactic acidosis, ketonuria, failure to thrive, and neurological 
dysfunction. Use of the anaplerotic agent triheptanoin on a limited number of 
individuals with PCD has had mixed results. We expand on the potential utility 
of triheptanoin in PCD by examining the clinical, biochemical, molecular, and 
health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) findings in a cohort of 12 individuals 
with PCD (eight with Type A and two each with Types B and C) treated with 
triheptanoin ranging for 6 days to about 7 years. The main endpoints were 
changes in blood lactate and HRQoL scores, but collection of useful data was 
limited to about half of subjects. An overall trend of lactate reduction with 
time on triheptanoin was noted, but with significant variability among subjects 
and only one subject reaching close to statistical significance for this 
endpoint. Parent reported HRQoL assessments with treatment showed mixed results, 
with some subjects showing no change, some improvement, and some worsening of 
overall scores. Subjects with buried amino acids in the pyruvate 
carboxyltransferase domain of PC that undergo destabilizing replacements may be 
more likely to respond (with lactate reduction or HRQoL improvement) to 
triheptanoin compared to those with replacements that disrupt tetramerization or 
subunit-subunit interface contacts. The reason for this difference is unclear 
and requires further validation. We observed significant variability but an 
overall trend of lactate reduction with time on triheptanoin and mixed parent 
reported outcome changes by HRQoL assessments for subjects with PCD on long-term 
triheptanoin. The mixed results noted with triheptanoin therapy in this study 
could be due to endpoint data limitation, variability of disease severity 
between subjects, limitation of the parent reported HRQoL tool, or subject 
genotype variability. Alternative designed trials and more study subjects with 
PCD will be needed to validate important observations from this work.

Pyruvate carboxylase (PC) deficiency (OMIM #266150) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the PC gene. The estimated incidence of isolated PC deficiency (PCD) is about 1 in 250,000 live births [1]. The PC (EC 6.4.1.1) enzyme is a biotin-containing nuclear encoded mitochondrial homotetrameric enzyme that catalyzes the ATP-dependent conversion of pyruvate to oxaloacetate. The complexity of the biochemical phenotypes observed in this condition are driven by the unique position of PC in the confluence of several pathways [2]. PC has a critical role in gluconeogenesis, anaplerosis, neurotransmitter synthesis, and lipogenesis [1]. The main clinical and biochemical findings in PCD include lactic acidosis, failure to thrive and neurological dysfunction [3,4]. Residual enzyme activity has shown no significant correlation with the clinical phenotype [4,5]. Based on the spectrum of severity, PCD has been classified into three distinct phenotypes. Type A (infantile or North American form) presents in infancy with lactic acidosis, ketosis, hyperalaninemia, hyperprolinemia, seizures, hypotonia, and developmental delay [3,6-8]. In this form, death typically occurs in infancy or early childhood [3]. Type B (severe neonatal or French form) has a more complex and severe phenotype characterized biochemically by severe neonatal lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, hyperammonemia, elevation of plasma alanine, citrulline, proline and lysine, with signs of abnormal intracellular redox balance including elevated lactate to pyruvate and acetoacetate to β-hydroxybutyrate ratios [5,7,9-11]. Clinically, these patients present with hypotonia, seizures, severely delayed psychomotor development, and in most cases, die within the first month of life [10,12]. Type C (intermittent or benign form) can have normal or mildly delayed development along with episodic lactic acidosis and hypoglycemia [12-15].

There is currently no cure or FDA-approved therapeutic option for PCD. Management of acute decompensation focuses on reversing catabolism with dextrose-containing intravenous fluids and correcting acidosis with bicarbonate. Long-term treatment strategies include supplementation with citrate and aspartate [3,16]. Biotin supplementation, although purported to improve residual enzyme activity, has proven to be of little benefit [17]. Orthotopic hepatic transplantation with living related donor was reported in an individual with severe neonatal PCD with partial improvement of the biochemical phenotype with reversal of ketoacidosis and improvement of lactic acidosis [18]. Liver transplantation with a cadaveric donor was reported in a 19-month-old with infantile form of PCD with post-transplant improvement of biochemical parameters with decreased lactate, normalization of alanine and proline, and improvement in metabolic acidosis enabling decrease in sodium citrate dosing [19].

Triheptanoin is a highly purified, synthetic medium odd-chain (C7) triglyceride that has been FDA-approved as a source of calories and fatty acids for the treatment of pediatric and adult patients with molecularly confirmed long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (LC-FAOD) [20]. Triheptanoin is first catabolized to heptanoate which enters the mitochondria and then metabolized into acetyl-CoA, propionyl-CoA, and 4- and 5-carbon ketone bodies. Acetyl-CoA, as donor of acetyl group for oxidation, can be utilized for ketogenesis, or it can enter the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Propionyl-CoA, as donor of anaplerotic propionyl group, is metabolized to succinyl-CoA with subsequent entry into the TCA cycle. Triheptanoin is proven effective and safe in the treatment of LC-FAOD [21-25]. Triheptanoin has also been tried in other conditions including glycogen storage type V (McArdle disease)[26] and adult polyglucosan body disease (APBD) [27], with generally no statistically significant improvement of the prevailing phenotype for either of these disorders such as exercise capacity in McArdle disease and gait quality or stair climbing capacity in APBD, respectively. Triheptanoin did not significantly reduce overall seizure frequency in individuals with glucose-transporter 1 deficiency syndrome (Glut1DS) who had drug-resistant epilepsy and not on ketogenic diet, but in Glut1DS patients with non-epileptic paroxysmal movement disorder, triheptanoin use resulted in major and sustainable benefit [28-31]. Triheptanoin did not prevent paroxysmal events in alternating hemiplegia of childhood (AHC) [32].

Because PCD is an energy deficit metabolic disorder, anaplerotic therapy with triheptanoin has been tried with mixed outcomes [10,33,34]. Mochel et al. 2005 described a critically ill newborn with severe (Type B) PCD (genotype unknown) with immediate improvement of liver failure and biochemical parameters following administration of triheptanoin including decreased lactate and lactate to pyruvate ratio, improved plasma ammonia and decreased citrulline [10]. They also showed increased CSF C5-ketone bodies, glutamine and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and improved myelination [10]. This provided evidence that triheptanoin was successful in bypassing the anaplerotic defect in PCD and restoring redox balance and oxaloacetate and aspartate levels. Unfortunately, this child died of an infection at 6 months of age. Breen et al. 2014 reported two patients (Patient 1, homozygous c.506G>A p.G169D in exon 6 of PC; Patient 2, homozygous c.1154_1155delGC p.R385QfsTer10 in exon 10 of PC) with neonatal PCD started on triheptanoin between the second and third week of life along with citrate, aspartate and dichloroacetate, but found no improvement in plasma or CSF lactate with continued episodic severe lactic acidosis and ketoacidosis [33]. Both patients died at 7-8 months of life. In contrast, a patient (subject P1 in this study) with mild PCD with speech delay and autism reportedly had improved development following use of triheptanoin [8].

Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) measurements of wild-type amino acid residues in proteins allows for the identification of solvent accessible vs inaccessible residues providing insight into the relationship between solvent accessibility of residues undergoing variation and pathogenicity of disease-causing missense variants (DMV) as well as the impact of change on stability, folding, subunit interaction, and function of proteins [35-37]. At least two-thirds of all human DMV are located in solvent inaccessible (“buried”) positions on proteins [37]. While buried amino acids are common among DMV, a significant number of them are enriched at or close to protein-protein interfaces, subunit-subunit interface contacts (SSIC) in multienzyme complexes, ligand-binding sites, and sites or pockets involved in enzymatic function [36,38]. For example, SASA analysis showed that >84% of residues with DMV on the α and β subunits of the E1 component of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, are solvent inaccessible with pathogenesis of 30% of those buried E1α DMV due to perturbation of SSIC [38].

To further expand on the potential utility of triheptanoin in PCD, we have undertaken a study examining the clinical, biochemical, molecular, and health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) findings in a cohort of 12 individuals with PCD treated with triheptanoin.

Written informed consent was secured for all patients (or parents were applicable). The study complied with University of Pittsburgh institutional regulations and was approved by University of Pittsburgh IRB as #PRO-08020019. This study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT01461304. Other Study ID: PRO08020019. Registered October 27, 2011, https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT01461304

The study aim was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of Dojolvi® (triheptanoin) in patients with PCD, with main study endpoints being clinically meaningful reduction of blood lactate and/or improvement of parent reported HRQoL with time on triheptanoin. This was an open label, compassionate use study in which each subject served as their own control. We collected and compared clinical data in a variety of areas including age appropriate PedsQL™ v.4 QoL questionnaires. This trial was designed to allow patients already on triheptanoin through previous studies to continue receiving the supplement. The study also allowed triheptanoin supplementation in patients with PCD who were naïve to triheptanoin. Efficacy was evaluated through standard of care clinical and laboratory parameters used to follow patients as part of their metabolic care.

From 2013 to 2020, 11 patients with confirmed PCD were treated with triheptanoin on a compassionate base following enrollment into the Dietary Therapy for Inherited Disorders of Energy Metabolism study at the University of Pittsburgh. Patients at least one month of age with a confirmed diagnosis of PCD were eligible for enrollment. Clinical information was collected until study closure in December 2020. Written informed consent was obtained from the parent/legal guardian prior to any study related procedures or study drug administration. The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board. Patient 12 (Table 1) who was not eligible to participate in the above compassionate use trial because of age, was included and treated with triheptanoin off-label after FDA approval.

Patients were provided with triheptanoin as a pharmaceutical-grade investigational product supplied by Ultragenyx Pharmaceutical, Inc. (Novato, CA, USA). Triheptanoin was supplied by Ultragenyx Pharmaceuticals, Inc. (Novato, CA, USA) as a colorless to light yellow clear oral liquid manufactured by chemical synthesis from glycerol and heptanoic acid, and manufactured in accordance with Current Good Manufacturing Practice regulations, applicable International Conference on Harmonization guidelines, and regional regulations. After FDA approval of triheptanoin for treatment of individuals with LC-FAOD, participants were switched to Dojolvi® (triheptanoin) oral liquid. Triheptanoin dosage and regimen were derived from experience in the treatment of LC-FAOD [21,39]. Age at start of triheptanoin ranged from 2 days to 13 years of life (Suppl. Table 1). Total time on triheptanoin ranged from 6 days to about 7 years (Suppl. Table 1). Triheptanoin maximum dosing goal was 2 g/kg of body weight and was gradually titrated and depending on subject tolerance of triheptanoin, the actual maximum dose ranged between 15 and 42% of total daily caloric intake (DCI) from triheptanoin (Suppl. Table 1). The remainder of the diet was modified to maintain appropriate caloric intake and balance as per recommended daily allowance.

Details of items assessed in this study are in Supplementary Table 2. The Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory™ (PedsQL™) 4.0 Generic Core Scales were used for HRQoL including physical, emotional, social, and school functioning in our study participants with the PARENT Report for Toddlers (ages 2-4 years), CHILD and the PARENT Reports for Young Children (ages 5-7 & 8-12 years), Teens (ages 13-18 years) and Young Adults (ages 18-25). The parent proxy-report was used for most of the participants. We scored reports from seven of 11 participants enrolled in the study. Assessments were completed yearly and at study closure. Scores were transformed on a scale from 0-100 with higher scores indicating better HRQoL. Plasma/serum amino acids analyses were not part of the assessments, but such data were collected from medical records collected from referring medical centers or locally. We focused assessments from outpatient visits and all values are from subjects not on total parenteral nutrition (TPN) during analysis.

We accessed ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) and collected all known missense pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants as of April 2022. A limited PubMed search review (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) was also undertaken to include variants published in the literature. Missense variants present in our patient cohort but absent in the literature or databases were also assessed. The crystal structure of Staphylococcus aureus PC homotetramer was acquired from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/; ID: 3BG5 at 2.8 Å resolution)[40]. We measured SASA on the PC homotetramer and monomer using the S. aureus PDB 3BG5 structure data in order to investigate the impact of residues with DMV in PC as before[35]. Accessibility defined as solvent inaccessible (“buried”, “i”; SASA <20%), indeterminate (blank, SASA ≥20 to ≤50%) and solvent accessible (“o”; SASA >50%) (Suppl. Table 3), and flipping from inaccessible or indeterminate to solvent accessible, with absolute SASA difference of ≥10% suggests that the specific residue is important in interface contact between subunits [38]. We used CUPSAT (http://cupsat.tu-bs.de/) to quantitatively predict the destabilizing impact of disease-causing amino acid replacements in the PC homotetramer, with ΔΔG ≤ −1.5 kcal/mol considered destabilizing [41]. We also used CLUSTAL Omega multiple sequence alignment tool (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/) to align human PC against other mammals as well as S. aureus (Suppl. Table 4).

Jupyter, a free web-based environment for statistical computing and graphics, was used for the statistical analyses using Python [42]. Linear regression was applied with results of regression tested for significance using the F-statistic test as before [43,44]. Smoothed confidence intervals (CI) for the regression analysis were displayed with scatter plots using NumPy library in Python (http://nbviewer.ipython.org/github/demotu/BMC/blob/master/notebooks/CurveFitting.ipynb). The Jupyter Notebook was utilized to generate boxplots using the numpy, scipy, and matplotlib packages [45,46].

Table 1 summarizes the clinical phenotype and genetic characteristics of the 12 subjects with PCD in this open label, compassionate use study. Seven of 12 subjects (58%) were females and five (42%) were males. Most subjects (67%, 8/12) were of type A, with types B and C represented by two patients each. Clinical onset of disease ranged from birth to 2 years of age, with initial clinical presentation of blood lactic acidosis (ranging 6-16 mM) in all, ketonuria in all, and hyperammonemia of ≥70 μM in about half (5/9) of those where ammonia was measured. Other significant initial symptoms included respiratory distress, hypoglycemia, and in one case, death at less than 1 month of age. Long-term complications included significant developmental delay, intellectual disability, and in half of cases seizures. Three subjects (P3, P10 and P11) underwent liver transplantation for their disease, with P10 and P11 undergoing transplantation before start of the triheptanoin trial, while P3 started the trial prior to transplantation. Subject data presented here including laboratory and HRQoL data were only those collected prior to transplantation and with subjects seen as outpatient and not on TPN. Among brain abnormalities in those with completed MRI, the most common intracranial abnormality was septated or nonseptated inter- or periventricular or caudothalamic groove cysts (71%, 5/7; Table 1), similar to other reported cases with PCD [10,33]. Greater than 90% (11/12) subjects had at least a missense variant in one of the two alleles, with 2 subjects (P7 and P9) as homozygotes for a missense variant. The subjects with PCD in this study are representative of other subjects trialed on triheptanoin [10,33].

Although lactate is an important diagnostic biomarker in disorders of pyruvate metabolism, gluconeogenesis, and other mitochondrial disorders (MtDs), it is prone to preanalytical error such as its stability at room temperature vs in slushed ice conditions [47,48] and given its chronic elevation in many of these conditions it does not necessarily always correlate with clinical status. Other reliable markers of lactic acidosis include alanine (Ala) and proline (Pro) as well as alanine:leucine and proline:leucine ratios. Alanine:leucine and proline:leucine ratios used in combination have been shown to be sensitive but not specific in the diagnosis of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex deficiencies (PDCDs) and other MtDs [43]. We wanted to determine if such amino acid markers and ratios could also be useful biomarkers in patients with PCD and if they could be used for monitoring clinical status in such patients. Significant linear correlation (R2=0.65) is observed between Pro:Leu and Ala:Leu ratios (Fig. 1) as also noted for PDCD [43]. The sensitivity of using these specific amino acid ratios for PC screening appears slightly lower (73%) compared to PDCD (86%) if the combination Pro:Leu ≥3.0 or Ala:Leu ≥4.0 criteria is applied as screening cutoff [43]. The Ala:Leu vs alanine plot showed less statistically significant linear correlation (R2=0.18; data not shown) consistent with neither alanine nor Ala:Leu individually being strong biomarkers for PDCD as well [43].

We evaluated whether increasing the percentage of DCI from triheptanoin or long-term maintenance on triheptanoin provided good control of lactate, alanine and/or Ala:Leu for subjects with PCD. The subjects as a group appeared to show a decreasing trend for Ala:Leu and alanine by linear regression with increasing percentage of DCI from triheptanoin, but their R2 coefficients were not statistically predictive of a linear fit (0.05 and 0.20 in Fig. 2A and 2B, respectively). Furthermore, we saw significant variability in Ala:Leu or alanine within certain subjects with the same percentage of calories from triheptanoin but with data taken at different times (see P2 and P9 data in Fig. 2A and 2B). Change in lactate with time on triheptanoin showed a similar trend of decreasing lactate for most cases with collected data but with generally better statistical significance by linear regression (P3 with R2=0.36 Fig. 3A and P4, P5, and P6 with R2=0.57, =0.58 and =1.00, respectively, Fig. 4B-D). P1 and P2 showed no statistically significant reduction in lactate with time on triheptanoin (R2=0.27 Fig 4A and R2=0.03 Fig 3C, respectively). Quarterly boxplot analysis of lactate with time on triheptanoin for subject P3 showed a decreasing trend with close to statistical significance (Fig. 2B), but not so for P2 (Fig. 2D).

This study did not evaluate HRQoL in participants less than 2 years of age (P8, P9, P10 and P12). HRQoL assessments using PedsQL™ were completed by parents only and not providers. Parents of P11 had >50% of questionnaires not completed and thus could not be scored. Subjects P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P6, and P7 were scored. The mean total PedsQL™ score was 74 (range 38-100; n=26 scores) (Suppl. Table 5). Patients classified as having PC type A had lower PedsQL™ scores (mean 60, range 38-89; n=15 scores) than those classified as having PC type C (mean 93, range 72-100; n=11 scores) (Suppl. Table 5). There were no significant differences between PedsQL™ scores and days on triheptanoin. Two of seven participants (P5 and P7) had lower scores at study closure, possibly suggesting QoL deterioration with triheptanoin, whereas five of seven participants had stable or improved scores (P1, P2, P3, P4 and P6, Fig. 5), with P2 and P3 showing improved scores with triheptanoin (Fig. 5). Subject P1 had increased speech and improved eye contact after start of triheptanoin at about 3 years of age (Patient 5 in [8]), but no significant change in parent reported HRQoL over the 3.7 years on triheptanoin (Suppl. Table 5). Because PedsQL™ scores focus on the experience during a month-long period prior to the evaluation, not surprisingly, there were more missing items, especially in the school section after/during hospitalizations and during the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic when most of our participants were out of school.

This study cohort and our database search identified 32 disease-causing PC missense variants (Fig. 6B and Suppl. Tables 3 and 4). Of the 32 missense variants, 41% (13/32) and 34% (11/32) are within the biotin carboxylase (BC) and pyruvate carboxyltransferase (PCT) domains, respectively, highlighting the importance of these two domains in disease pathogenesis in 75% of cases. Twenty (63%) disease-causing missense amino acid replacements represented amino acids that were solvent inaccessible in the homotetramer, while 9 (28%) and 3 (9%) were indeterminate for solvent accessibility and solvent inaccessible, respectively (Fig 6A). Of the buried amino acid with replacements involved in disease, three (15%) were also involved in SSIC as determined before [35]. Mature N1061 is not located in either the BC or PCT domains but rather is within the PC tetramerization (PT) domain [40]. Mature N1061 had an indeterminate SASA measurement and exhibited ~20% change in absolute SASA between PC homotetramer and monomer subunit (Suppl. Table 3), suggesting involvement in SSIC and supporting its role in PT (see Fig. 1 in [40]). Therefore, disruption of SSIC or PT may be the cause of disease pathogenesis with replacements of N1061 observed in subjects P1, P4, P6 and P8.

Triheptanoin is considered efficacious and safe in the treatment of LC-FAOD [21-25]. Triheptanoin has been tried and found safe to use but not significantly effective in other conditions including McArdle disease [26], APBD [27], and AHC [32]. Glut1DS patients with paroxysmal movement disorder showed >90% clinical improvement in non-epileptic paroxysmal episodes with triheptanoin and this was not considered a placebo effect [28]. Use of triheptanoin in PCD has been limited and shown mixed results, with restoration of redox balance as well as oxaloacetate and aspartate levels and improvement of development in some cases but no improvement of symptoms in others [8,10,33]. This study of 12 subjects with PCD also demonstrated a mixed picture of efficacy of triheptanoin, with main study endpoints being clinically meaningful reduction of blood lactate and/or improvement of parent-reported HRQoL with time on triheptanoin. As with other disorders, we found triheptanoin use to be safe with no serious adverse effect documented during the study, although adjustments of DCI from triheptanoin for some subjects were needed due to known drug tolerability and mild GI distress (Suppl. Table 1). P3 was the only subject whose reduction of lactate with time on triheptanoin was closest to statistical significance (Fig. 3A and 3B) with concurrent improvement of parent reported HRQoL noted during the same time period (Fig. 5). P2 also showed improvement of HRQoL scores (Fig. 5) but no statistically significant change in lactate with triheptanoin treatment (Fig. 3C and 3D). Subjects P4, P5 and P6 showed a general trend of lactate reduction with time on triheptanoin (Fig. 4), with the statistical strength of the linear regression fit (R2) ranging 0.57-1.00, with only three data points for P6 (Fig. 4D). However, subjects P1, P4 and P6 showed no significant improvement in HRQoL scores (Fig. 5). Although it is important to have parent reported HRQoL data from validated questionnaires, such questionnaires may still lack objectivity limiting their utility for this particular study. In the future, a prospective study that also includes provider-based objective HRQoL measurements and evaluation with validated developmental assessment tools could be invaluable.

Besides the obvious genotype-phenotype correlation of biallelic truncating or nonsense variants resulting in absent PC enzyme and consequently severe PCD, the determining factors for PCD disease severity remain unclear [6,11,49]. Mosaicism is reported to result in prolonged survival [5]. Patients with variants outside of the biotin-binding domain can be responsive to biotin supplementation [50]. We evaluated whether subject responsiveness to triheptanoin correlated with structural and functional impact of DMV as they relate to PC domain location, structural destabilization of the amino acid replacements, or amino acid replacements involving SSIC (Table 2). We found mixed response of blood lactate with time on triheptanoin in subjects (P2 and P3) with missense variants with buried and predicted destabilizing amino acid replacements in the PCT domain, but improvement of HRQoL with triheptanoin in both subjects (Table 2). In contrast, in subjects (P1 and P4 sibs, P5 and P6) with missense variants of amino acid replacements whether predicted to be destabilizing or not and involved in SSIC and/or within the PT domain, they in general showed no change of HRQoL even with some trending reduction of blood lactate with time on triheptanoin (Table 2). Interestingly, Patients 1 and 2 of Breen et al. (2014) who showed no improvement of clinical or biochemical parameters in response to triheptanoin had variants in the BC and biotin-carboxyl carrier protein (BCCP) domains of PC, respectively [33]. The genotype of the patient reported by Mochel et al. (2005) who showed prompt and stable recovery following triheptanoin is unknown [10]. Additional subjects are needed to support the observation that some buried amino acids in the PCT domain that undergo destabilizing replacements may be more likely to respond to long-term triheptanoin therapy than those with amino acid replacements that disrupt tetramerization or specific SSICs.

This study was limited by the heterogenous nature of the PCD patient population and duration of treatment with triheptanoin (Table 1 and Suppl. Table 1), but this limitation is difficult to overcome. This study was not a randomized, double-blinded cross-over with placebo control study, which could be challenging to implement for this disorder. The study had limited (sometimes n=3) and incomplete blood lactate and serum amino acids data on subjects with PCD. We limited endpoint data collection to times when subjects were followed in the outpatient setting and not during their inpatient hospital stays to limit anticipated variability in such measurements during inpatient hospital stays. The number of HRQoL questionnaires completed for each subject were limited (in some cases n=2) and they were parent reported and not provider-based ones. For future investigations, objective provider-based validated HRQoL assessments concurrent with parent-reported ones would be invaluable for capturing observable meaningful outcome changes. Furthermore, future prospective investigations should be designed to ensure a more rigorous and standardized data collection so that better conclusions can be drawn.

This is the largest cohort study evaluating the effect of long-term triheptanoin use on subjects with PCD. We enrolled eight PCD Type A subjects, two Type B, and two Type C. We limited endpoint data (e.g., lactate, amino acids, and HRQoL) to subjects presenting at outpatient settings, pre-transplantation periods when applicable, and when subjects were not on TPN in order to limit anticipated variability and data errors during inpatient care management. Despite such efforts, we still observed significant variability in endpoint data among individual subjects, but some of that could be attributable to variability of disease severity between subjects (Table 1). Although we observed a general trend in 4 of 6 subjects (namely P3, P4, P5 and P6) of reduction of lactate with time on triheptanoin, only subject P3 reached close to statistical significance for this endpoint. Parent-reported HRQoL assessments with time on triheptanoin showed mixed results, with three subjects (P1, P4 and P6) showing no change in overall scores, two subjects (P2 and P3) showing improvement of scores, while two subjects (P5 and P7) showing worsening of scores. Subjects (P2 and P3) with buried amino acids in the PCT domain that undergo destabilizing replacements may be more likely to respond (with lactate reduction or HRQoL improvement) to long-term triheptanoin therapy than those (P1 and P4 sibs, P5, and P6) with amino acid replacements that disrupt tetramerization or specific SSICs, or in other domains such as BC or BCCP noted in other patients treated with triheptanoin [33]. However, this potential genotype-dependent response to triheptanoin needs validation by further investigations on additional subjects with PCD treated with triheptanoin.
