# TENT5A-associated osteogenesis imperfecta: long-term follow-up and molecular insights.
**Authors:** Stasek S, Zaucke F, Stephan A, Etich J, Mörgelin M, Baumann U, Rehberg M, Reincke S, Semler O, Hoyer-Kuhn H
**Journal:** JBMR Plus (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1093/jbmrpl/ziaf083](https://doi.org/10.1093/jbmrpl/ziaf083)

## Content

OI is a genetically diverse disorder characterized by bone fragility and deformities, with most cases attributed to dominant mutations in collagen I-related genes. However, mutations in TENT5A, encoding a poly(A) polymerase, have recently been implicated in severe, recessive forms of OI. We reported 3 individuals from a consanguineous family with a novel homozygous TENT5A variant (c.672G>T, p.Arg224Ser). Our patients presented with severe bone fragility, generalized osteopenia, skeletal deformities, short stature, and early wheelchair dependence. Patient-derived fibroblasts demonstrated impaired collagen secretion and disorganized fibril network formation. Our findings provide new insights into the complex pathophysiology of TENT5A-associated OI and underscore the need for further research into its role in skeletal homeostasis.

OI, commonly referred to as “brittle bone disease,” represents a genetically diverse group of disorders characterized by bone fragility, frequent fractures, and skeletal deformities. 1 The majority of OI cases are attributed to dominant mutations in the COL1A1 or COL1A2 genes, which encode the α-chains of type I collagen, a principal structural component of bone. However, a subset of OI cases follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern and is linked to mutations in various genes involved in collagen post-translational modification, bone homeostasis, and mineralization. 2

Recently, Doyard and colleagues demonstrated that TENT5A mutations are associated with severe forms of OI (type XVIII), characterized by multiple spontaneous fractures of long bones and vertebrae in early childhood and severe skeletal deformities. 3 These findings underscore the critical role of TENT5A in maintaining bone integrity and its involvement in skeletal development and homeostasis. TENT5A, also known as FAM46A, encodes a nucleotidyltransferase that regulates collagen I expression post-transcriptionally via mRNA polyadenylation, which is critical for mRNA stability and thus efficient protein translation. 4 However, the exact biological function of TENT5A in bone development and maintenance is not clear yet.

Understanding the molecular mechanisms by which TENT5A regulates osteoblast function and collagen production will provide deeper insights into the pathogenesis of OI and reveal potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Here, we describe the clinical course of 3 individuals with a novel mutation in TENT5A, the eldest of whom has been under our care for over 15 yr. This study describes the phenotypic characteristics and aims to elucidate its underlying pathophysiology by analyzing fibroblasts derived from affected individuals.

Three patients from a single family, treated in our pediatric osteology outpatient clinic, underwent genetic testing. A novel variant in the TENT5A gene was identified by targeted next-generation sequencing from peripheral blood using an in-house panel for increased bone fragility. Our genetic institute assessed the population frequency of the identified TENT5A variant. This variant was not described in the following databases in healthy individuals: dbSNP, Exome Variant Server, ExAc-Browser, and gnomAD-Browser. Furthermore, in silico analysis using prediction programs (MutationTaster, PolyPhen-2, CADD, and MaxEntScan, ClinPred, REVEL) consistently predicted that this variant is likely pathogenic or pathogenic. Additionally, Sanger sequencing confirmed that both parents of patients PII-1 and PII-2 are heterozygous carriers of this variant. The combination of the variant’s absence in population databases, the consistent “pathogenic” or “likely pathogenic” predictions from multiple in silico tools, and the identification of both parents of patients PII-1 and PII-2 as heterozygous carriers, strengthens the likelihood that this variant contributes to the OI phenotype observed in this family. To gain a deeper understanding of this type of OI, we retrospectively analyzed and compiled clinical data and laboratory results and analyzed tissue samples from one of our patients.

As part of routine follow-ups in our clinic, height and weight measurements, clinical examinations, and the documentation of newly occurred fractures were performed. In the youngest patient, it was possible to measure his standing height with assistance. However, the 2 older patients were unable to stand upright, so their height was measured while seated, from foot to crown, using a tape measure. Additionally, bone density measurements were conducted at least once annually using the Lunar densitometer (GE Healthcare). Radiographs were obtained as needed, for instance, in cases of suspected fractures or to assess the correct positioning of osteosynthetic material.

Blood and urine samples were routinely collected during regular visits at our outpatient clinic. These included a standardized laboratory panel comprising blood count, kidney and liver function tests, CRP, IGF-1, IGF-BP3, and bone-specific parameters (calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, vitamin D, PTH, osteocalcin, cross-laps, and urinary desoxypyridinolin [DPD]/creatinine ratio).

Fibroblast cultures were established from skin biopsies of our oldest patient at the age of 24 yr. Control fibroblasts were kindly provided by Prof. Uwe Kornak (Institute of Human Genetics, University Medical Center Göttingen, Germany) and derived from skin biopsies of 3 independent 20-26 yr old male and female donors. Patient and control cells were cultured under standard conditions. Cells were plated at 4 × 104 cells/cm2 on plastic (RNA) or glass (immunocytochemistry) and cultured for 3 d without medium change to analyze gene expression and collagen production. Medium was supplemented with ascorbate (22 μg/mL ascorbic acid, 65 μg/mL ascorbic acid-2-phosphate).

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol, reversely transcribed into cDNA with the Omniscript RT assay and 25 ng cDNA was used for SYBR Green-based qPCR assays (Eurogentec). The expression was normalized to GAPDH; the fold change was calculated with the ΔΔCT method. 5 Detailed information about all primers used in the present study is given in Table S1.

For collagen type I staining, cells were incubated with a primary antibody specific for collagen type I (Abcam; ab34710; 1/200) in 5% normal goat serum. The primary antibody was visualized with a secondary antibody coupled to Cy3 (Jackson Laboratories). To detect collagen fibrils, cells were fixed with ice cold 50% methanol/50% acetone and incubated with CHP (3Helix; HEL-RED60; 5 μM) in 5% normal goat serum according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, coverslips were mounted in Mowiol and analyzed by fluorescence microscopy (NikonEclipse TE2000-U Microscope). For visualization, images were adjusted in brightness.

Cells were lyzed in reducing sample buffer (250 mM Tris/HCl pH 6.8, 8% SDS, 40% glycerol, 2% β-mercaptoethanol and bromophenol blue) and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Equal amounts of protein were resolved on 8% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose (VWR, Amersham Protran 0.45 NC). Blots were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with 5% milk powder in TBS-0.1% Tween 20, and then a primary antibody against collagen type 1 (Southern Biotech; 1310-01; 1/4000) or GAPDH (Millipore; MAB374; 1/1500) were applied over night at 4 °C. After application of a secondary antibody labeled with horseradish peroxidase (DAKO) specific bands were visualized by enhanced chemoluminescence (Amersham ImageQuant 800).

The structure of secreted collagen fibrils in human fibroblast supernatants was examined using negative staining and transmission electron microscopy, following the method described by Engel and Furthmayr. 6 Cells were cultured in medium supplemented with ascorbate for 3 d without medium change. Then, supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove cellular debris. Samples were snap frozen without any further treatment. Five-microliter samples of thawed supernatant were applied to carbon-coated grids for 1 min, rinsed with 2 drops of water, and stained with 2 drops of 0.75% uranyl formate. To enhance hydrophilicity, the grids underwent glow discharge at low pressure in air. Specimens were analyzed with a Philips/FEI CM 100 BioTWIN transmission electron microscope operating at an accelerating voltage of 60 kV. Images were captured using a side-mounted Olympus Veleta camera with a resolution of 2048 × 2048 pixels (2 k × 2 k). 7

The mutant protein sequence was generated by introducing the desired amino acid substitution into the WT sequence of TENT5A (UniProtKB ID: Q96IP4). Structural predictions for the mutant sequence were performed using ColabFold, an implementation of AlphaFold2 optimized for user-defined sequences. 8 The resulting predicted structure was exported as a PDB file. Protein structures were visualized using the Mol*3D viewer available at RCSB.org. 9 , 10 The WT structure was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database by querying UniProtKB ID Q96IP4 directly within Mol*3D. The predicted mutant structure was imported into Mol*3D by uploading the PDB file generated by ColabFold.

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 5.0), with experiments replicated at least 3 times. For comparisons across 3 control groups and 1 patient group, a one-way ANOVA was performed, followed by a post hoc Bonferroni multiple comparison test. p-values (p ≤ .05) were considered to be statistically significant.

A skin biopsy was collected with the informed consent of the patient PII-1. The procedure and subsequent scientific analysis were approved by the local ethics committee (AZ 13-202) and conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the WMA Declaration of Helsinki and the Department of Health and Human Services Belmont Report. The family also provided consent for the publication of patient information.

Three children with clinical signs of severe OI from the same family of Turkish origin, presented at our outpatient clinic at different ages. Genetic testing revealed the same homozygous variant in all 3 individuals in the TENT5A gene (c.672G>T, p.Arg224Ser). Previously, Doyard et al. have reported 3 TENT5A variants associated with OI, encompassing 2 missense variants (c.380A>G, p.His127Arg) and c.692A>G, p.Asp231Gly) and 1 frameshift variant (c.612_613dup, p.Ser205Tyrfs*13), (Figure S1). 3 The novel variant, c.672G>T, identified in our patient cohort has not been previously linked to OI. Sanger sequencing confirmed that both parents of patients PII-1 and PII-2 are heterozygous carriers of this variant. This TENT5A variant was absent in population databases was predicted to be pathogenic by in silico tools. A family pedigree is presented in Figure 1A. Patients PII-1 and PII-2 are siblings born to consanguineous parents in Turkey, who moved to Germany during early childhood. Both patients were diagnosed clinically with OI shortly after birth, due to the presence of multiple fractures. The 2 sisters presented at our center for the first time at the age of 12 and 4 yr. At presentation, the skeletal manifestations in both patients included thoracic deformity, scoliosis, bowing of both upper and lower extremities, short growth (Table 1) and multiple vertebral compression fractures (Figure 1, panels B-I). Neither patient had joint hypermobility. Radiographic imaging demonstrated generalized osteopenia, severe deformities of the long bones and multiple vertebral fractures. Both patients were already at this timepoint permanently wheelchair dependent. We measured serum alkaline phosphatase, osteocalcin, and beta-CTX, which were all within normal limits, indicating normal bone turnover rates. Serum calcium and phosphate levels were also normal. However, vitamin D levels were consistently low, as the patients did not adhere to their vitamin D supplementation regimen. Despite the low vitamin D, parathyroid hormone levels remained normal.

Oral bisphosphonate therapy was initiated in Turkey during early childhood for both patients. Due to incomplete documentation and relocation from Turkey, the precise age at therapy initiation is unknown. Intravenous bisphosphonate treatment with neridronate 1 mg/kgBW every 3 months was continued at our center. Intermittently, oral bisphosphonates (clodronic acid; 520 mg/d) had to be administered to our 2 patients because of extreme obesity and difficult venous access. Fracture rates, skeletal pain and bone density improved under bisphosphonate therapy in both patients (Table 1).

Both patients presented at our center with severe obesity, exhibiting BMIs of 40.2 kg/m2 (at 12 yr of age) and 29.4 kg/m2 (at 4 yr of age) at their initial visit. Due to incomplete medical records from their previous treatment facilities in Turkey, the precise onset of their obesity could not be clearly determined. However, their parents reported that the obesity developed during early childhood. Despite attempts at lifestyle modifications, the patients’ obesity persisted and progressively worsened over time. At their last evaluation before transitioning to adult care, patient PII-1 had a BMI of 63.5 kg/m2 at 27 yr old, while her sister’s BMI was 38.4 kg/m2 at 19 yr of age. Leptin deficiency was ruled out in patient PII-1 (Table 1), but unfortunately, testing was no longer possible for the sister before the transition to adult care.

Additional clinical findings included diffuse alopecia in both patients. Intellectual and social development was normal. Both patients also exhibited persistently elevated C-reactive protein levels, despite the absence of any clinical evidence of infections or inflammation.

Patient PIV-1, the nephew of the aforementioned female patients, was also diagnosed with OI at birth. He has been under our care since infancy and the patient is currently 4 yr old. Bisphosphonate therapy with 0.05 mg/kgBW zoledronic acid was initiated during the first year of life and given every 6 mo. The boy exhibited bowed deformities of the upper and lower extremities, accompanied by multiple vertebral compression fractures, but no scoliosis. (Figure 1, panels E and H). Consistent with the phenotype observed in his affected aunts, the patient demonstrated significant short growth at 3.9 yr of age (Table 1). Serum electrolytes, including calcium and phosphate, were consistently within the normal range for this patient (Table 1). Furthermore, bone-specific alkaline phosphatase and osteocalcin levels were also normal during outpatient follow-ups. However, urinary DPD levels were increased by the age of 1 yr and 7 mo, suggesting an elevated bone resorption rate at this timepoint, which might be related to microtraumata during infancy. At the initial evaluation, the patient’s serum vitamin D concentrations were within the normal range. By their final presentation at nearly 4 yr of age, the vitamin D levels had declined to subnormal levels. Parathyroid hormone levels remained within the normal reference range. The patient is currently wheelchair-dependent but does not present with obesity, with a BMI of 15.8 kg/m2 (Z-score: 0.25, age- and sex-adjusted) at the age of 4 yr. Due to the extreme obesity of his 2 aunts, we tested this patient for monogenic causes of obesity and leptin deficiency, but the tests revealed no pathological findings. Cognitive development and social behavior are age appropriate. Laboratory analyses indicated initially elevated CRP levels, without proper explanation, which subsequently decreased over time.

Analysis of the protein structure in silico revealed that Arg224 is located in a cluster with many positive charges, most likely essential for RNA binding (Figure 2A). As a mutation of the same arginine residue in TENT5b completely eliminated its transferase activity, 11 it is highly likely that also the mutation described here in TENT5A would abolish the enzyme function. Quantitative PCR analysis of cultured fibroblasts derived from patient PII-1 revealed that the expression levels of TENT5A, COL1A1 and COL1A2 are not significantly altered, despite considerable variability in expression observed in control cells (Figure 2B). Similarly, collagen type I levels in the cell-associated matrix fraction were variable but showed no substantial changes (Figure 2C). In contrast, the total amount of collagen I secreted into the culture supernatant appeared to be slightly reduced, and the protein was detected as a double band. The ratio of the band intensities was altered with an increased signal being detected in supernatants of patient cells (Figure 2D).

This observation suggests potential alterations in collagen processing and/or posttranslational modifications. Fluorescent staining with collagen type I-specific antibodies and the collagen hybridizing peptide (CHP) revealed that a significant portion of the collagen type I and CHP staining in patient cultures was localized intracellularly, suggesting that the collagen secretion process was impaired. As a consequence, control cells were able to form a well-defined, organized extracellular collagen-containing network, while TENT5A-derived cultures exhibited a less pronounced network (Figure 2E). A closer look at the fibril structure using electron microscopy revealed a strongly disorganized and aggregated collagen type I appearance in contrast to well-organized and banded fibrils secreted by the control cells (Figure 2F).

Variants in TENT5A, also known as FAM46A, have recently been identified as a cause of OI. 3 Due to the limited number of reports, little is known about the phenotype and pathomechanism of TENT5A-associated OI. To better understand and treat this form of OI, a stepwise characterization of its clinical and cellular features is essential.

Here, we describe 3 severely affected OI patients from a consanguineous family with a novel variant in the TENT5A gene. This specific mutation has not previously been linked to OI. The oldest of the 3 patients was followed at our center for over 15 yr. The patients exhibited not only a severe bone phenotype but also several unexplained endocrinological abnormalities. Furthermore, we were able to investigate fibroblasts derived from one of the patients and demonstrated that this novel TENT5A variant impairs the secretion of type I collagen, leading to a disorganized fibril network structure.

All 3 patients—2 sisters and their nephew—were diagnosed with OI shortly after birth due to multiple fractures and deformities. Genetic testing revealed the same homozygous TENT5A variant (c.672G>T, p.Arg224Ser) in all individuals. TENT5A encodes for active cytoplasmic poly(A) polymerase that, among many others, polyadenylates also several OI-relevant RNAs, including Col1a1 and Col1a2. 4 The mutation described here is predicted to be located in the active center of the polymerase and was expected to affect its enzyme activity. The expression of TENT5A was unchanged, and although we did not analyze the polymerase activity, we were able to show that the amount of transcribed collagen type I RNA was not affected and that this RNA was also translated into similar amounts of collagen type I protein. Interestingly, the complete absence of TENT5A in mice resulted in skeletal abnormalities, including multiple fractures, and it could be demonstrated that the defect in collagen type I was both quantitative and qualitative. 4 Patient-derived fibroblasts were able to produce similar amounts of collagen type I though our data suggest that the secretion and deposition into the matrix is altered. Electron microscopy revealed significant structural defects in collagen fibrils, consistent with the qualitative abnormalities observed in other non-classical OI-types. 7 Interestingly, a qualitative defect with disorganized and thinner collagen type I fibrils has also been observed in TENT5A-deficient mice. 4

Our patients presented shortly after birth with multiple fractures, severe deformities, generalized osteopenia, and typical blue sclerae. Similar to our patients, Doyard et al. described 4 cases of TENT5A-associated OI with severe limb bowing and multiple fractures of the extremities and vertebrae with a pre-natal onset. 3 However, while at least 2 of Doyard et al.’s patients eventually achieved ambulation, all 3 of our patients are wheelchair dependent. Bisphosphonate therapy led to moderate improvements in bone density and pain and bone turnover markers were normalized during the anti-resorptive therapy in the 2 older patients. Fracture rates ceased post-puberty in the 2 older sisters, allowing discontinuation of bisphosphonates at ages 17 and 18 yr, respectively.

In addition to their skeletal phenotype, the patients exhibited unexplained endocrinological and laboratory abnormalities. The 2 older sisters (PII-1 and PII-2) presented with extreme obesity, with BMIs of 63 and 38 kg/m2, which could not be explained by reduced mobility or dietary habits. The 2 older female patients began experiencing weight gain in early childhood, around 4-6 yr of age. However, precise details on the timepoint of weight gain are unavailable, as the parents could not provide exact information on weight development of their children. Given the patients’ severe obesity, we tested the older patient for genetically determined obesity. While genetic analysis revealed no clear pathogenic findings, a variant of unknown significance in the SH2B1 gene was identified. However, the same mutation was also detected in the non-obese young patient. Therefore, we assume that this variant is not the reason for the obesity in the family. Due to challenges with patient compliance and the transition to adult care, we were unable to conduct additional testing in the obese sister and in the patients’ parents and other unaffected family members.

Recent studies have implicated TENT5A as a regulator of leptin, 12 a key hormone in appetite regulation. However, bioactive leptin deficiency was excluded in two of our patients.

Our findings suggest that the TENT5A variant may play a role in metabolic regulation, though more conclusive evidence is needed.

The 2 older patients additionally displayed repeatedly elevations in C-reactive protein levels over an extended period, in the absence of clinical evidence of active infection or overt inflammation. TENT5 proteins have been shown to regulate immune responses in animal models 13 and are involved in TGF-β signaling. 14 However, CRP levels in the youngest patient were normal at the last follow-up. It is therefore possible that the combination of severe OI and extreme obesity in the older sisters contributed to a state of chronic subclinical inflammation. 15 , 16 Further studies are needed to elucidate how TENT5A contributes to regulate inflammation and immune response.

In summary, our findings provide new insights into the role of TENT5A in OI, highlighting its impact on collagen secretion and fibril organization. Clinically, the novel identified variant in TENT5a leads to a severe form of OI with perinatal fractures, severe bone deformities, short growth, and early wheelchair dependence. Furthermore, the phenotype of TENT5a-associated OI may extend beyond skeletal manifestations and TENT5A may also function as a regulator in lipid metabolism.
