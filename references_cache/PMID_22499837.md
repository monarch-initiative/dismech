BACKGROUND: Congenital toxoplasmosis is a severe, life-altering disease in the 
United States. A recently developed enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) 
distinguishes Toxoplasma gondii parasite types (II and not exclusively II 
[NE-II]) by detecting antibodies in human sera that recognize allelic peptide 
motifs of distinct parasite types.
METHODS: ELISA determined parasite serotype for 193 congenitally infected 
infants and their mothers in the National Collaborative Chicago-based Congenital 
Toxoplasmosis Study (NCCCTS), 1981-2009. Associations of parasite serotype with 
demographics, manifestations at birth, and effects of treatment were determined.
RESULTS: Serotypes II and NE-II occurred in the United States with similar 
proportions during 3 decades. For persons diagnosed before or at birth and 
treated in infancy, and persons diagnosed after 1 year of age who missed 
treatment in infancy, proportions were similar (P = .91). NE-II serotype was 
more common in hot, humid regions (P = .02) but was also present in other 
regions. NE-II serotype was associated with rural residence (P < .01), lower 
socioeconomic status (P < .001), and Hispanic ethnicity (P < .001). Prematurity 
(P = .03) and severe disease at birth (P < .01) were associated with NE-II 
serotype. Treatment with lower and higher doses of pyrimethamine with 
sulfadizine improved outcomes relative to those outcomes of persons in the 
literature who did not receive such treatment.
CONCLUSIONS: Type II and NE-II parasites cause congenital toxoplasmosis in North 
America. NE-II serotype was more prevalent in certain demographics and 
associated with prematurity and severe disease at birth. Both type II and NE-II 
infections improved with treatment.
