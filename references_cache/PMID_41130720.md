# PURA protein mislocalisation in the nucleus: mechanistic basis for transcriptional dysregulation and DNA unwinding deficits in a model of the p.L148Wfs*77 PURA variant.
**Authors:** Wang Y, Wang P, He J, Wang H, Liu S
**Journal:** J Med Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1136/jmg-2025-110818](https://doi.org/10.1136/jmg-2025-110818)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Heterozygous PURA (Purine-rich element-binding protein A) variants cause PURA syndrome, a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by hypotonia, seizures and intellectual disability. Previous studies have focused on the effect of the PURA variant in the cytoplasmic location, but nuclear mislocalisation remains to be explored.

METHODS: We identified a de novo heterozygous frameshift variant (c.442del, p.L148Wfs*77) via trio whole-exome sequencing in one child suspected of PURA syndrome due to intellectual disability. Functional analyses included structural modelling, subcellular localisation assays, RNA-seq, CUT&Tag and DNA unwinding assays.

RESULTS: The variant disrupts PURA repeats II-III, causing aberrant nuclear mislocalisation. RNA-seq revealed 688 differentially expressed genes enriched in neurodevelopmental pathways. CUT&Tag analysis revealed that PURA and Pol II exhibit enhanced binding at transcription start sites in cells expressing the variant, indicating dysregulated transcriptional engagement. Despite retained nucleic acid binding, the variant impaired DNA unwinding partly due to disrupted repeat III-mediated homodimerisation.

CONCLUSIONS: Nuclear mislocalisation of the PURA variant dysregulates transcriptional balance and impairs DNA unwinding, linking PURA's structural integrity to neurodevelopmental deficits. This highlights PURA's dual roles in cytoplasmic RNA regulation and nuclear transcription, providing mechanistic insights into PURA syndrome pathogenesis.

PURA gene variants cause PURA syndrome, a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by hypotonia, developmental delays and seizures.

Prior studies established that PURA (Purine-rich element-binding protein A) localises to the cytoplasm, binds RNA/DNA, and its loss disrupts neurodevelopmental pathways, but the impact of nuclear-localised variants remained unclear.

This study identifies a novel PURA frameshift variant (p.L148Wfs*77) that mislocalises to the nucleus, disrupting structural domains essential for DNA unwinding and homodimerisation.

It demonstrates aberrant PURA and Pol II recruitment at transcription start sites and imbalanced neurodevelopmental gene expression, linking nuclear mislocalisation to transcriptional dysregulation.

The findings suggest that therapies should target both nuclear and cytoplasmic PURA dysfunction and highlight the need to screen for localisation-altering variants in undiagnosed neurodevelopmental cases.

 The PURA gene, located on chromosome 5q31.2, encodes the Purine-rich element-binding protein A (PURA), a single-exon protein comprising 322 amino acids with three highly conserved sequence-specific repeats (PUR repeats I–III).13 As a member of the PUR family, which includes PURA, PURB and PURG, PURA is the most extensively studied and is ubiquitously expressed.4 It plays a critical role in a wide range of cellular processes, including DNA replication, RNA transport and neuronal development, with particular importance in neuronal proliferation and dendrite maturation.57 Given its essential functions in cellular regulation, variants in the PURA gene have been strongly associated with a spectrum of neurodevelopmental disorders, collectively referred to as PURA syndrome (MIM # 616158).

PURA syndrome, which was first described in 2014,2 3 is characterised by a constellation of clinical features, including moderate to severe neonatal hypotonia, feeding difficulties, respiratory challenges such as apnoea and hypoventilation, neurodevelopmental delays, motor and language impairments, learning disabilities, epileptic seizures, abnormal movements, visual problems and facial dysmorphism.23 810 The syndrome is primarily caused by de novo heterozygous variants in the PURA gene in humans. Animal models have provided further insights into the roles of PURA in neurodevelopment. For instance, homozygous deletion of PURA in mice results in phenotypes such as continuous tremors and spontaneous seizures, which partially resemble human symptoms.5 6 In contrast, heterozygous PURA deletion in mice leads to poor memory and abnormal behaviours, showing minimal overlap with the full spectrum of PURA syndrome observed in humans.11 To date, over 475 individuals worldwide have been diagnosed with PURA syndrome, all of whom carry heterozygous variants.9

PURA is a multifunctional protein that binds both single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) and RNA.12 Initially identified in HeLa cells as an ssDNA-binding factor,13 it was later characterised in mice as a transcriptional regulator that binds to the promoter region of the myelin basic protein.14 15 Recent studies have expanded our understanding of PURA’s role in RNA processing.16 17 Notably, PURA variants identified in patients with PURA syndrome have been shown to impair its co-localisation with processing bodies, disrupt protein folding and reduce RNA-binding capacity, highlighting the functional consequences of these variants.16 17 However, these studies have primarily focused on PURA variants localised to the cytoplasm, leaving the functional implications of nuclear-localised variants largely unexplored, which may exhibit distinct functional and pathological consequences. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effects of nuclear mislocalisation caused by PURA variant on cellular function, with a focus on understanding its role in the pathogenesis of PURA syndrome.

Here, we report a variant (c.442del, p.L148Wfs*77) of the PURA gene in one child suspected of PURA syndrome. This frameshift variant deletes the β-sheet and α-helix in repeats II and III, shifting protein localisation from predominantly cytoplasmic to entirely nuclear, with repeat III determining cytoplasmic localisation. Transcriptome sequencing reveals disrupted gene expression balance, particularly in neurodevelopmental pathways. CUT&Tag shows that variant of PURA was associated with increased enrichment of PURA and RNA Pol II at transcription start sites (TSS). Unwinding assay further indicates impaired DNA duplex unwinding despite retained Pol II recruitment and nucleic acid binding. The variant’s inability to accurately recognise target TSS and defective DNA unwinding likely underlies PURA syndrome pathogenesis.

Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood samples obtained from the proband and his parents, followed by trio whole-exome sequencing (Trio-WES). Exome capture was conducted using the Nano WES Human Exome V2 kit (Berry Genomics), and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. The resulting reads were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 reference genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) software. Variant annotation was performed using ANNOVAR and the Enliven Variants Annotation Interpretation System (Berry Genomics). Population frequency filtering was applied using data from gnomAD (http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) and the 1000 Genomes Project (1000G, http://browser.1000genomes.org). Variants were classified according to the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG).18

Human PURA cDNA (NM_005859.5) with a Flag-tag at the N-terminus and EGFP were cloned into the pRP[Exp]-EGFP/Puro-EF1A>3xFLAG/hPURA vector (VectorBuilder). The variant plasmid was generated using Gold Medal Mix Ver.2 (TSE102, Tsingke Biotechnology) with the above plasmid as template and the following primers: forward 5′- GAGTTCTGGTGCGCGAGAACCGCAA-3′ and reverse 5′- GCACCAGAACTCGCTTTTGAGCGCC-3′. The plasmids without EGFP and different truncations were constructed by GENEWIZ. All the constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

HEK-293 and NSC-34 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium(DMEM)/high glucose with 10% fetal bovine serum(FBS) at 37°C/5% CO2. For transfection, cells (6×10⁵/9.6 cm²) at 60–70% confluency were treated with 4 µg plasmid DNA and 10 µL PEI (HY-K2014, MedChemExpress) in 400 µL mixture (30 min room temperature (RT) incubation). Cells were harvested after 24 hours (immunofluorescence/western blot) or 48 hours (RNA-seq/CUT&Tag).

The nuclear-cytoplasmic separation was performed using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0027, Beyotime) and cell lysis buffer for western blot and immunoprecipitation (IP) (P0013, Beyotime). HEK-293 cell pellets were resuspended, vortexed and centrifuged in cytoplasmic protein extraction buffer to collect the supernatant, which contains the cytoplasmic proteins. The pellet is then resuspended in an equal volume of cell lysis buffer for western blot and IP, followed by sonication to obtain nuclear proteins. Subsequently, the protein concentration was determined using the BCA kit (23225, Thermo).

The protein lysates were separated with 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at RT for 1 hour and subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Flag (1:4000, F1804, Sigma-Aldrich), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase(GAPDH) (1:10 000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech) and histone H3 (1:10 000, 17168-1-AP, Proteintech). The membranes were then washed three times with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20(TBST) and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at RT. The target proteins were then visualised using chemiluminescence (WBKLS0500, Millipore), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the membranes were exposed to X-ray film in a darkroom.

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were analysed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPC (R323-01, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Q712-02-AA, Vazyme) on a QuantStudio TM5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as an internal control for normalisation. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean±SD.

HEK-293 cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde fixative (BL539A, Biosharp) for 15 min, followed by three washes with phosphate-buffered saline(PBS). The cells were then incubated for 1 hour at RT with a blocking buffer containing 0.3% Triton-X and 5% fetal bovine serum in PBS. After this, the cells were treated overnight at 4°C with an anti-Flag-tag antibody (1:400, 66008_3-Ig, Proteintech). Subsequently, cells were washed three times for 5 min with PBS and a Cy3 AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) secondary antibody (1:600, 715-165-150, Jackson Immunoresearch) was added for 1 hour at RT in the dark. The cells were then stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) at a concentration of 0.5 µg/mL (C1002, Beyotime Biotechnology). After staining, the cells were washed five times with PBS and were mounted in ProLong Diamond Antifade. Imaging was performed by confocal microscopy (Leica).

Three independent biological replicates of PURA-WT and PURA-p.L148Wfs*77 cells were prepared as described above. Total RNA was extracted using a Trizol reagent kit (T9424, Sigma-Aldrich). The quality of the RNA was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyser and confirmed through RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. The cDNA library was constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (7530, NEB) and subsequently sequenced using the Illumina Novaseq6000 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). The reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38/GRCh38) using HISAT2.19 The mapped reads of each sample were assembled using StringTie V.1.3.1.20 Differential expression analysis of the RNA was performed using DESeq2.21 Genes with a fold change greater than 1.5, an Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads(FPKM) value greater than 1 and an adjusted p value <0.05 were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

PURA-WT and PURA-p.L148Wfs*77 cells were prepared as described above. 100 000 cells per condition were centrifuged and processed for CUT&Tag using the CUT&Tag Assay Kit (TD904, Vazyme) with anti-Flag M2 antibody (F1804, Sigma-Aldrich), RNA Pol II antibody (ab26721, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells were incubated with ConA Beads Pro (TD904, Vazyme) for a period of 10 min, following the overnight incubation of the primary antibody at a temperature of 4°C, with the objective of allowing specific protein binding. The following day, the appropriate secondary antibody was added and the cells were washed twice to remove any unbound secondary antibodies in the supernatant. pA/G-Tnp Pro (TD904, Vazyme) was then added to the cell suspension and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour to activate the transposase enzymatic activity. The labelled DNA was then recovered using DNA Extract Beads Pro (TD904, Vazyme) and subsequently amplified by PCR using indexed P5 and P7 primers to generate the sequencing library. The final library products were sequenced on a Novaseq X Plus (Illumina) platform with PE150 mode to obtain high-throughput sequencing data.

Co-immunoprecipitation was performed using the Immunoprecipitation Kit (Protein A+G Agarose, P2197M, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were lysed in ice-cold IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. The lysates were centrifuged at 13 000×g for 5 min at 4°C to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Protein A+G beads were incubated with anti-FLAG antibody (5 µg/mL) for 1 hour at RT, then mixed with lysates for an additional 2 hours at RT. After centrifugation and three washes, bound proteins were eluted in SDS-PAGE buffer and analysed by western blot.

The protocol of the unwinding assay was performed as described before.17 22 23 Briefly, a dual-labelled oligonucleotide (FAM-CCAGGGCACTTAAAAAATTCGCCTGG-Dabcyl) was employed as the substrate. Reactions were carried out in a 50µL volume using an unwinding buffer consisting of 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 2 mM DTT, 4% (v/v) glycerol and 3 mM MgCl2. Protein concentrations ranged from 0 to 12nµM against a fixed DNA concentration of 50 nM. FAM fluorescence measurements were performed using a Victor Nivo S and were excited and recorded separately at 480 and 530 nm.

A 23-month-old male infant was admitted to the West China Second University Hospital of Sichuan University (Chengdu, China). The mother had regular obstetric examinations during her pregnancy. The mother reported prenatal chromosomal analysis for increased nuchal translucency with reportedly normal results; documentation was unavailable for verification. After birth, the infant exhibited hypotonia in both upper limbs and experienced recurrent episodes of aspiration. He presented with retrognathia, full cheeks, a high forehead, telecanthus and a depressed nasal bridge. An MRI conducted at our hospital demonstrated slight dilation of the bilateral lateral ventricles, along with a subependymal cyst in the left lateral ventricle (figure 1A). Cardiac ultrasound revealed a patent ductus arteriosus (large tubular type) and an atrial septal defect, while a subsequent examination showed no significant abnormalities. The patient currently weighs 11 kg (less than −1 SD), exhibits instability while holding his head upright, requires bilateral upper extremity support while seated, leans forward when sitting, is unable to roll over, can only grasp light objects with both hands, and may inadvertently call out ‘mama.’ The patient has undergone rehabilitation treatment for over 1 year, with unsatisfactory results.

A novel heterozygous variant (c.442del, p.L148Wfs*77) was identified in the proband on the PURA gene (NM_005859.5), which was de novo and was validated by Sanger sequencing (figure 1B and C) using primers as follows: forward-5′- AACAAGAGCCGCCTTACT-3′, reverse-5′-TCCGTAGTCGTCGATGAG-3′. According to the criteria of ACMG, the variant was categorised as a likely pathogenic variant (PVS1-S, PS2-M, PM2_P).

According to the recently published structural data of PURA from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 8CHT and 8CHW), the three repeat regions of the PURA protein have been precisely delineated: repeat I (residues 57–126), repeat II (residues 143–212) and repeat III (residues 216–280) (figure 1D). The variant was located in repeat II and resulted in a frameshift truncation of PURA (figure 1D). An attempt was made to predict the three-dimensional (3D) structure using Alphafold2, and the results indicate that L148 is part of the β-sheet of repeat II and the variant results in the loss of both the β-sheet and the α-helix of repeat II and repeat III (figure 1E).

Previous studies have shown that the PURA protein is predominantly localised in the cytoplasm, where it participates in processing-body (P-body)-associated RNA regulatory processes by binding to various mRNA molecules.16 17 To investigate whether the p.L148Wfs*77 affects the subcellular localisation of PURA, we overexpressed full-length PURA and the p.L148Wfs*77 variant in HEK-293 cells and mouse motor neuron NSC-34 cells, and the efficiency is shown in online supplemental figure S1. The results showed that PURA was primarily localised in the cytoplasm, with a minor fraction of signals in the nucleus (figure 2A,B and online supplemental figure S2a,b), suggesting that PURA may play roles in both cytoplasmic and nuclear biological processes.

Interestingly, both immunofluorescence and fractionation experiments demonstrated that p.L148Wfs*77 was almost exclusively localised in the nucleus, with almost absent signals in the cytoplasmand online supplemental figure S2a,b). Such mislocalisation could impair PURA’s functions, particularly its role in P-body-mediated RNA regulation. Moreover, the nuclear accumulation of p.L148Wfs*77 may interfere with nuclear processes, potentially exerting detrimental effects on cellular functions.

To elucidate the key domains responsible for the cellular localisation of PURA, we generated expression vectors encoding repeat I, repeat II, repeat III and repeat II+III. Our findings revealed that the expression of the repeat III domain alone, as well as the combined repeat II+III, significantly restored the normal cellular localisation of PURA and online supplemental figure S2c), indicating that repeat III is critical for the proper localisation of PURA.

To investigate the impact of PURA variant mislocalisation on cellular function, RNA sequencing was performed to analyse intracellular changes, revealing 688 DEGs (online supplemental table S1) and the heatmap was shown in figure 3A. Comparative analysis demonstrated that PURA WT exhibits two distinct gene expression peaks, whereas the p.L148Wfs*77 variant causes these peaks to converge towards an intermediate level, with an overall higher average gene expression compared with WT (figure 3B). This suggests that the p.L148Wfs*77 variant leads to the upregulation of most low-expressed genes and the downregulation of a subset of highly expressed genes in WT, thereby disrupting the balance of gene expression levels.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs identified the top 20 significantly enriched pathways (figure 3D). These pathways were predominantly associated with forebrain development, neurogenesis and nervous system development. The expression levels of selected genes highly related to these pathways were further validated by qPCR (figure 3C and online supplemental figure S3a). Additionally, 16 DEGs were found to be involved in the regulation of cyclin-dependent protein kinase activity, implying that the PURA variant may affect DNA replication, consistent with previous research findings.4 Notably, 129 DEGs were linked to transcription mediated by RNA polymerase II, suggesting that dysregulation of RNA polymerase II-mediated transcription may contribute to the pathological manifestations observed in PURA syndrome patients with nuclear mislocalisation of the variant.

To map the genome-wide occupancy of PURA, we employed the CUT&Tag (Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation) technique on HEK-293 cells transfected with either WT or the p.L148Wfs*77 variant separately (figure 4A). This approach allowed us to identify 28491 and 33391 high-confidence peaks in cells expressing WT (online supplemental table S2) and the p.L148Wfs*77 variant (online supplemental table S3) separately. The genomic distribution of these peaks, illustrated in online supplemental figure S4a, revealed a notable increase in nucleic acid-binding activity associated with the p.L148Wfs*77 variant compared with WT. Intriguingly, we observed a significant enrichment of PURA binding at TSS in the variant (33.45% p.L148Wfs*77 vs 31.47% WT) (online supplemental figure S4b). We further validate this finding by performing a merge analysis of TSS binding patterns between WT and the variant, and the resulting heatmap confirmed the increased TSS binding affinity of the p.L148Wfs*77 variant (figure 4B).

To elucidate the potential role of RNA Polymerase II (RNA Pol II) in PURA-mediated transcriptional regulation, we also mapped the genome-wide occupancy of RNA Pol II using CUT&Tag. The results demonstrated that both PURA and RNA Pol II exhibit highly similar genomic binding profiles around TSS, with the p.L148Wfs*77 variant showing increased binding to gene TSS (figure 4C). Furthermore, a strong positive correlation was observed between the binding patterns of PURA and RNA Pol II at TSS regions in both WT and p.L148Wfs*77 (figure 4D), suggesting a functional interplay between PURA and RNA Pol II in the regulation of transcription. To investigate whether this interaction is direct, we performed co-IP assays, which confirmed that PURA physically interacts with RNA Pol II (figure 4E).

Despite the increased binding of the p.L148Wfs*77 variant to TSS regions, this variant did not result in a global alteration of gene transcription. Instead, it specifically modulated the expression of 688 DEGs, with a particular impact on genes involved in neurodevelopmental pathways (figure 3D). These findings suggest that the interaction between PURA and RNA Pol II, as well as their DNA binding capabilities, is independent of the repeat II and repeat III domains of PURA. However, these domains appear to be critical for maintaining normal RNA levels of genes that are essential for neurodevelopment, highlighting their functional importance in transcriptional regulation.

The observed increase in PURA binding capacity prompted us to investigate how the PURA variant influences the expression levels of DEGs. We analysed 688 DEGs and found that 30% of them were directly bound by PURA (figure 5A), indicating that the remaining 70% of DEGs are likely downstream effects rather than direct PURA targets. Among the 30% of PURA-bound DEGs, 66% remained bound after PURA variant (figure 5A), suggesting that the p.L148Wfs*77 does not abolish binding but may impair normal function. Notably, the PURA-bound DEGs included both upregulated and downregulated genes (figure 5B), implying that PURA functions not only as a transcriptional activator but also as a repressor.

To further elucidate the functional consequences of PURA binding, we selected several key DEGs involved in neurodevelopment—AKIRIN2, DLX2, TRA2B and DLG4—and performed genomic occupancy analysis (figure 5C). These genes continued to be bound at their TSS regions even after the PURA variant, suggesting that the binding itself is not sufficient for proper transcriptional regulation. Previous studies have reported that the repeat III domains of two independent PURA proteins interact to form an intermolecular homodimer, adopting a classic PC4-like fold structure.17 23 This structure is critical for unwinding DNA duplexes, and proper secondary structure formation is essential for this activity. We hypothesised that the p.L148Wfs*77 variant might disrupt the formation of this homodimer, thereby reducing the efficiency of DNA unwinding and impairing transcriptional activation, even though the variant retains its DNA binding capability.

To further investigate the DNA unwinding activity of the PURA variants, we employed a well-established in vitro unwinding assay.17 22 23 In this assay, a double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) substrate was designed with a 5′-FAM fluorophore and a 3′-Dabcyl quencher. In its duplex state, the close proximity of the quencher to the fluorophore results in low fluorescence. However, when the DNA is unwound, the physical separation of the strands leads to increased fluorescence due to loss of quenching. Using this system, we found that the PURA WT protein efficiently unwound the DNA duplex, as indicated by a pronounced increase in fluorescence, whereas the p.L148Wfs*77 variant showed no significant unwinding activity (figure 5D). These results collectively demonstrate that the p.L148Wfs*77 significantly reduces DNA unwinding efficiency, as illustrated in the proposed model (figure 5E).

While our findings provide mechanistic insights into PURA’s role as a transcriptional activator, further studies are required to elucidate the molecular pathways involved in its function as a transcriptional repressor.

PURA, a key regulator of DNA/RNA metabolism, is implicated in PURA syndrome, characterised by neurodevelopmental deficits.24 25 Here, we identified a novel heterozygous frameshift variant (c.442del, p.L148Wfs*77) in the PURA gene in a patient suspected of PURA syndrome. This variant truncates the protein, eliminating β-sheet/α-helix structures in repeats II–III and causing aberrant nuclear accumulation. Nuclear mislocalisation disrupted gene expression balance and impaired DNA unwinding, linking structural loss to functional deficits. Our findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying PURA syndrome and highlight the functional consequences of nuclear mislocalisation of PURA.

Previous studies have established that PURA is primarily localised in the cytoplasm, where it participates in cytoplasmic stress granule dynamics,26 RNA processing16 17 and associates with fragile-X mental retardation protein in neurons.27 Our results demonstrate that the p.L148Wfs*77 variant disrupts this localisation, leading to an almost exclusive nuclear accumulation which is likely due to the loss of repeat III. The nuclear accumulation of the variant may interfere with nuclear processes while simultaneously impairing its cytoplasmic functions. This dual disruption could contribute to the pathogenesis of PURA syndrome by altering the expression of genes critical for neurodevelopment. Despite these findings in our cellular model, however, the patient did not present with any novel phenotypes. Our findings align with previous reports that PURA variants impair RNA binding capacity and disrupt protein folding.16 17 However, our study extends these observations by demonstrating that nuclear mislocalisation of PURA can also lead to dysregulation of transcriptional processes, which has not been extensively explored in previous research.

RNA-seq revealed 688 DEGs enriched in pathways related to forebrain development, neurogenesis and nervous system development, which are consistent with the neurodevelopmental deficits observed in patients with PURA syndrome.2 3 8 9 Notably, the p.L148Wfs*77 variant caused a convergence of gene expression levels, suggesting a loss of precise transcriptional control and may reflect a broader dysregulation of transcriptional networks, potentially contributing to the neurodevelopmental deficits observed in PURA syndrome.

One of the key findings of this study is the impaired DNA unwinding activity of the p.L148Wfs*77 variant. Previous studies have shown that PURA is built from two PUR repeats III of independent protein chains that interact to form an intermolecular homodimer, adopting a PC4-like fold structure that is essential for DNA unwinding.17 23 This structural arrangement is critical for PURA’s function in DNA replication and transcription regulation. The repeat III domain, in particular, facilitates dimerisation, guiding a second DNA-/RNA-binding domain (PUR repeats I-II) to additional GGN motifs on dsDNA, where further DNA unwinding events can occur.23 The loss of repeat III in the p.L148Wfs*77 variant disrupts this dimerisation, impairing DNA unwinding.

While the N-terminal repeats I and II of PURA can also promote DNA unwinding, their efficiency is highly dependent on the integrity of their secondary structures. For instance, K97E disrupts secondary structure and impairs unwinding, while R140P preserves structure and maintains activity.23 This underscores the importance of structural integrity in PURA’s function and suggests that the p.L148Wfs*77 variant disrupts both dimerisation and secondary structure, leading to impaired DNA unwinding.

PURA is traditionally thought to act as a transcription activator by contacting the purine-rich strand of promoter regions and displacing the pyrimidine-rich strand, facilitating the binding of other proteins and the activation of transcription.22 In our study, we observed both downregulated and upregulated PURA-bound genes, indicating PURA may also act as a repressor, which needs further exploration. This could include investigating potential interactions between PURA and co-repressors or chromatin-modifying complexes, as a recent study reported that PURA patients have a specific epigenetic signature.28

Despite these insights, our study has certain limitations that warrant consideration. A primary limitation is that all our functional analyses were conducted using a cellular model of the specific p.L148Wfs*77 variant. While this variant provides a clear model for understanding the consequences of disrupting the PUR-II and -III domains, the PURA syndrome spectrum encompasses a wide range of pathogenic variants (including missense, nonsense, frameshift and in-frame deletions or insertions affecting different domains). Therefore, the phenomena we observed—particularly the aberrant nuclear mislocalisation and its sequelae—may not be universally generalisable to all PURA variants. For instance, prior studies have shown that missense variants like K97E (affecting PUR-I), I206F (affecting PUR-II), F233del (affecting PUR-III) exhibit distinct cytoplasmic phenotypes without nuclear mislocalisation.17 This suggests that pathogenicity may manifest through divergent mechanisms depending on the affected protein domain. Consequently, a crucial next step for future research will be to investigate the molecular consequences of variants affecting different functional domains (PUR-I, II and III) to determine whether our findings represent a common pathomechanism. Such studies will be essential for building a comprehensive genotype-phenotype map for PURA syndrome.

Our study demonstrates that the p.L148Wfs*77 variant disrupts its subcellular localisation, leading to nuclear accumulation and impaired DNA unwinding activity. This mislocalisation results in dysregulation of gene expression, particularly in neurodevelopmental pathways, and may contribute to the pathogenesis of PURA syndrome. Our findings highlight the importance of PURA’s structural integrity for its function as both a transcriptional activator and repressor and provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying PURA syndrome. Future studies should focus on elucidating the mechanisms of PURA-mediated transcriptional repression and exploring the in vivo consequences of nuclear mislocalisation of PURA.
