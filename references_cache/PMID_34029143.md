Synaptopodin (Synpo) is an actin-associated protein in podocyte foot processes. By generating mice that completely lack Synpo, we previously showed that Synpo is dispensable for normal kidney function. However, lack of Synpo worsened adriamycin-induced nephropathy, indicating a protective role for Synpo in injured podocytes. Here, we investigated whether lack of Synpo directly impacts a genetic disease, Alport syndrome (AS), because Synpo is reduced in podocytes of affected humans and mice; whether this is merely an association or pathogenic is unknown. We used collagen type IV-α5(Col4a5) mutant mice, which model X-linked AS, showing glomerular basement membrane (GBM) abnormalities, eventual foot process effacement, and progression to end-stage kidney disease. We intercrossed mice carrying mutations inSynpoandCol4a5to produce double-mutant mice. Urine and tissue were taken at select time points to evaluate albuminuria, histopathology, and glomerular capillary wall composition and ultrastructure. Lack of Synpo inCol4a5−/Y,Col4a5−/−, orCol4a5+/−Alport mice led to the acceleration of disease progression, including more severe proteinuria and glomerulosclerosis. Absence of Synpo attenuated the shift of myosin IIA from the podocyte cell body and major processes to actin cables near the GBM in the areas of effacement. We speculate that this is mechanistically associated with enhanced loss of podocytes due to easier detachment from the GBM. We conclude thatSynpodeletion exacerbates the disease phenotype in Alport mice, revealing the podocyte actin cytoskeleton as a target for therapy in patients with AS.

NEW & NOTEWORTHYAlport syndrome (AS) is a hereditary disease of the glomerular basement with hematuria and proteinuria. Podocytes eventually exhibit foot process effacement, indicating actin cytoskeletal changes. To investigate how cytoskeletal changes impact podocytes, we generated Alport mice lacking synaptopodin, an actin-binding protein in foot processes. Analysis showed a more rapid disease progression, demonstrating that synaptopodin is protective. This suggests that the actin cytoskeleton is a target for therapy in AS and perhaps other glomerular diseases.

Alport syndrome (AS) is an inherited nephropathy characterized by progressive glomerular disease and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) associated with hearing loss and ocular abnormalities (1,2). AS is caused by mutations that affect expression and/or function of collagen type IV family protein isoforms [collagen type IV-α3 (COL4A3), collagen type IV-α4 (COL4A4), and collagen type IV-α5 (COL4A5)], which heterotrimerize and form the major collagen type IV network in the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) (3). Three major modes of inheritance have been reported in AS (4). The most common form (∼80% of patients) is X linked due to mutations inCOL4A5. The X-linked form is characterized by severely affected males showing persistent hematuria, proteinuria, and inevitable progression to ESKD. Heterozygous females have widely variable disease outcomes, with some affected females exhibiting normal kidney function and no extrarenal manifestations, whereas others develop ESKD and deafness (5,6). The autosomal recessive form (∼15%), which causes severe disease in both males and females, is caused by mutations inCOL4A3andCOL4A4, whereas the autosomal dominant form (5%) causes late-onset kidney disease in a subset of individuals carrying heterozygous mutations in eitherCOL4A3orCOL4A4(7,8). Thin basement membrane nephropathy, a condition within the spectrum of AS that does not always progress to kidney failure but is usually accompanied by hematuria, can also be caused by heterozygous mutations inCOL4A3orCOL4A4(9).

Although AS is primarily a disease of collagen type IV, there is genetic evidence that variants in other genes can impact disease severity. Examples have been reported of patients within the AS spectrum who carry at least one collagen type IV mutation with phenotypes exacerbated by coinheritance of variants inLAMA5, which encodes another GBM component (laminin-α5), andNPHS2, which encodes the slit diaphragm-associated protein podocin (10). The laminin-β2(LAMB2) missense mutation S80R, although causing nephrotic syndrome in a patient (11), was not pathogenic by itself in mice; however, even a single mutantLamb2allele increased the rate of progression to kidney failure inCol4a3−/−Alport mice (12), providing further support for the hypothesis that the progression of disease in AS is subject to the effects of genetic modifiers. Whether variants in genes that encode proteins directly associated with the actin cytoskeleton are responsible in part for the variability of AS is not clear but is the subject of this study.

Synaptopodin (Synpo) is an actin-associated protein that is important for the integrity of the actin cytoskeleton and for the regulation of podocyte migration in vitro (13). Synpo normally exists as two isoforms: neuronal Synpo-short and podocyte Synpo-long (14). We have previously demonstrated that complete lack of all Synpo isoforms caused no obvious phenotype in podocytes under physiological conditions but that lack of Synpo exacerbated kidney disease induced by acute injury with adriamycin (15).

Synpo has been reported to be downregulated in the podocytes of patients with various forms of nephrotic syndrome (16) as well as in patients with AS (17), suggesting that Synpo and the actin cytoskeleton could be involved in disease pathogenesis. However, no mutations in theSYNPOcoding region have been reported, although heterozygous variants were found in what was described as theSYNPOpromoter region in two patients with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) (18). To directly investigate the relationship between the abnormal GBM in AS and the podocyte’s actin cytoskeleton, we set out to test the effects of Synpo deficiency on podocyte injury due to AS. We crossed mice carrying null mutations inSynpoandCol4a5to produce mice deficient in one or both of these genes. We found that theSynpomutation behaved as a strong modifier that worsened kidney disease progression when homozygous. This study provides the first genetic evidence that the absence of an actin cytoskeleton-associated protein can affect the progression of kidney disease in Alport mice and reveals the podocyte actin cytoskeleton as a target for therapy in AS.

Mouse spot urine samples were collected by manual restraint. Several samples were collected at different time points (5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and other weeks of age as indicated in the figures) from the same mice, providing longitudinal data about the progression of albuminuria as an indicator of kidney disease progression. Creatinine levels were quantified by a QuantiChrom Creatinine Assay Kit (DICT-500, BioAssay Systems, Hayward, CA), and albumin levels were determined with a QuantiChrom BCG Albumin Assay Kit (DIAG-250, BioAssay Systems) according to the manufacturer’s specifications. Although 24-h collections are superior for quantifying albumin excretion rate, assaying spot urine samples for albumin-to-creatinine ratios (ACRs) is sufficient to show differences among the groups. Blood was collected by cheek puncture and centrifuged at 5,500 rpm for 5 min to collect serum. Blood urea nitrogen was assayed with a QuantiChrom Urea Assay Kit (DIUR-100, BioAssay Systems).

Kidneys were harvested at the indicated times, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and then processed and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin or with periodic acid-Schiff stain. For quantitative analysis of glomerulosclerosis, 50 full-sized periodic acid-Schiff-stained glomeruli from each specimen were assessed. Glomeruli that exhibited adhesion of the capillary tuft to Bowman’s capsule, capillary obliteration, mesangial expansion, or fibrotic crescents were defined as sclerotic. The percentage of sclerotic glomeruli was determined as previously described (20). To assess the degree of sclerotic lesions, a more in-depth quantification of glomerulosclerosis was performed using a semiquantitative scoring system of 0–4, as previously described (21): 0, normal; 1, mesangial expansion or sclerosis involving up to 25% of the tuft; 3, 50–75% sclerosis of the tuft, segmental extracapillary fibrosis or proliferation, or both; and 4, global sclerosis (>75%) or global extracapillary fibrosis or proliferation or complete collapse of the tuft.

For electron microscopic examination, portions of the kidney cortex were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde (buffered with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4). Tissues were dehydrated and embedded in plastic, and ultrathin sections were viewed by transmission electron microscopy, as previously described (22).

Sections of fresh frozen kidneys (4 μm thick) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After being washed, they were blocked in buffer (5% normal goat serum and 1% BSA in PBS) for 30 min and incubated with dilutions of the primary antibody in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-Wilms’ tumor-1 [WT1; ab89901, Abcam, 1:500; previously validated as podocyte specific (15)], mouse anti-desmin [clone D33, M076029-2, Agilent, 1:200 (23,24)], rat anti-COL4A5 (clone H53, no. 7078, Chondrex, 1:400; specificity demonstrated below), rabbit anti-mouse LAMB2 [a gift from Takako Sasaki (Oita University, Oita, Japan), 1:1,000; fails to stainLamb2−/−mouse tissue (25)], rat anti-nidogen-1 (MAB1946, Millipore Sigma, 1:1,000; stains all basement membranes), and guinea pig anti-Synpo [no. 03-GP94-IN, American Research Products, 1:500; fails to stain podocytes inSynpoknockout mice (15)].

After extensive washes with PBS, fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies were applied at 1:300 dilution for 1 h at room temperature. The secondary antibodies used were as follows: Alexa 488 goat anti-rabbit (A-11008), Alexa 488 goat anti-mouse (A-11001), Alexa 594 goat anti-rabbit (A-11037), Alexa 647 goat anti-rabbit (A-21244), and Alexa 594 goat anti-rat (A-11077) from Invitrogen as well as Alexa 488 donkey anti-guinea pig (no. 706-545-148) from Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories.

Tissues were imaged on an Olympus FV1000 confocal microscope. To quantify podocyte injury, at least 750 WT1-positive podocytes from at least 50 glomeruli from each mouse were examined for the presence of the injury marker desmin. The percentage of desmin-positive (injured) podocytes was determined by dividing the number of WT1 and desmin double-positive cells by the number of WT1-positive cells. The percentage of COL4A5-positive GBM was calculated as the length of the COL4A5-positive GBM divided by the length of the LAMB2-positive GBM.

After dissection, kidneys were cut into smaller pieces and fixed for 2 h with 4% PFA, followed by washing in PBS. Early signs of AS in humans can be detected by light microscopy as podocyte hypertrophy and tubular red blood cell casts (31), whereas at later stages, the GBM becomes segmentally thickened, with a mix of FSGS and global glomerulosclerosis, sometimes with crescents (3). To further investigate the impact of Synpo loss on AS progression, we examined renal histology. Hematoxylin and eosin as well as periodic acid-Schiff staining revealed significant pathological features inCol4a5−/−;Synpo−/−kidneys. At 8 wk of age, glomerulosclerosis and tubular protein casts appeared inCol4a5−/−;Synpo−/−mice, which progressed to ESKD with pathology that included diffuse glomerulosclerosis, crescents, and widespread protein casts at 15 wk of age (Fig. 2A). In contrast, less severe pathology was observed inCol4a5−/−;Synpo+/−littermates. Blinded quantification of glomerular lesions showed a significantly increased percentage of sclerotic glomeruli and higher glomerulosclerosis scores inCol4a5−/−;Synpo−/−mice versusCol4a5−/−;Synpo+/−mice (Fig. 2,BandC). Thus, histological analysis indicated that loss of Synpo accelerates AS progression. At 8 wk of age, transmission electron microscopy revealed mild GBM splitting and thickening, with subepithelial bulging typical of AS at the early stages inCol4a5−/−;Synpo+/−mice (Fig. 3). Compared with this,Col4a5−/−;Synpo−/−mice exhibited pathology typical of later stages, including extremely thick and electron lucent areas of the GBM (Fig. 3).Col4a5−/−;Synpo+/−mice exhibited mild foot process effacement (FPE), but, by comparison,Col4a5−/−;Synpo−/−mice showed worse FPE (Fig. 3).

FemaleCol4a5+/−heterozygous mice exhibit mosaic expression and deposition of the Col4a5 chain due to random X chromosome inactivation. They develop kidney disease variably and more slowly thanCol4a5−/Ymales andCol4a5−/−females. Although the X chromosome inactivation ratio is likely responsible for much of the observed variability among female patients, including those within the same family (32), other determinants of disease progression in heterozygous females are likely at play and remain to be discovered. To investigate the impact of changes to the actin cytoskeleton on the phenotype ofCol4a5+/−female mice, we generatedCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−females and compared histology, albuminuria levels, and the extent of COL4A5 deposition and podocyte injury with those of control littermateCol4a5+/−;Synpo+/−mice at up to 4 mo of age.

Histological analysis revealed enhanced glomerulosclerosis, protein casts, and tubular atrophy inCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−kidneys versusCol4a5+/−;Synpo+/−kidneys at 15 wk of age (Fig. 4A). Transmission electron microscopy revealed more severe GBM thickening and effacement of podocyte foot processes inCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−mice versusCol4a5+/−;Synpo+/−mice at 15 wk (Fig. 4B). Urinalysis revealed significantly increased albuminuria inCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−mice versusCol4a5+/−;Synpo+/−mice at each collection between 5 and 9 wk of age (Fig. 4C). Curiously, the average ACR ofCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−mice peaked at 8 wk of age and then decreased to a level similar to controls by 15 wk (Fig. 4C). In contrast to ACRs, at 15 wk, blood urea nitrogen levels were significantly higher inCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−mice versusCol4a5+/−;Synpo+/−mice (Fig. 4D). This increase in blood urea nitrogen is consistent with a decrease in glomerular filtration rate, which we speculate could also explain in part the decreased ACR after 8 wk (Fig. 4C). However, it is important to note here that both glomerular and tubular mechanisms can contribute to albuminuria, so understanding the basis for this finding will require further investigation.

In patients with AS, podocyte adherence to the GBM is defective, resulting in accelerated podocyte detachment and progressive podocyte depletion (34,35). This leads to FSGS-like pathological changes and eventual ESKD. To determine the relative number of resident podocytes in ourCol4a5+/−mice, WT1-positive nuclei (Fig. 5A) were counted in 15-wk-old mice. The number of WT1-positive cells per glomerulus was significantly decreased inCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−mice versusCol4a5+/−;Synpo+/−mice (Fig. 5C). This suggests that Synpo helps to maintain podocyte adhesion to the GBM after injury due to AS.

Podocytes are the only glomerular cells that produce the collagen-α3α4α5(IV) heterotrimer (36,37). To investigate the effects ofSynpomutation on the extent of deposition of COL4A5 inCol4a5+/−kidneys, we costained for COL4A5 and LAMB2. GBMs ofCol4a5+/−;Synpo+/−andCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−mice exhibited the expected mosaic pattern of COL4A5 staining due to random X chromosome inactivation (Fig. 6A). In contrast, LAMB2 deposition was observed in all GBM segments and appeared enhanced in COL4A5-negative segments (Fig. 6A), likely due to the thickened GBM. Interestingly, the percentage of the GBM that immunostained positive for COL4A5 was reduced inCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−mice versusCol4a5+/−;Synpo+/−mice (Fig. 6B).

The podocyte’s contractile actomyosin network is normally found in the cell body but not in the foot processes, which contain actin but not myosin IIA (27). However, after genetic (Cd2-associated protein andLamb2knockout) or chemical (adriamycin) injuries that disrupt the glomerular filtration barrier and cause FPE, myosin IIA-positive contractile cables are found at the basal aspect of the podocyte in the effaced areas adjacent to the GBM (27), suggesting a shift in location to that site. To investigate the molecular changes in the cytoskeleton that occur in Alport mice with and without Synpo, we stained ultrathin (200 nm) frozen kidney sections with antibodies against α-actinin-4 and myosin IIA (Fig. 7). InSynpo+/−andSynpo−/−glomeruli, myosin IIA was not detected in the α-actinin-4-positive foot processes adjacent to the GBM but was mainly found in the cell bodies and abundantly in mesangial cells (Fig. 7,CandF). Myosin IIA was detected in the effaced areas ofCol4a5−/−;Synpo+/−podocytes (Fig. 7,H–J), but myosin IIA was usually absent from the effaced areas ofCol4a5−/−;Synpo−/−podocytes (Fig. 7,L–N). To investigate if this phenomenon occurs in a different context of podocyte injury when Synpo is absent, we also stained kidneys fromSynpo+/−andSynpo−/−mice with adriamycin-induced nephropathy. We obtained similar results, as myosin IIa was readily detected in the effaced areas ofSynpo+/−podocytes but not in those ofSynpo−/−podocytes (Supplemental Fig. S2).

Our data unveil a new modifier gene,Synpo, that can contribute to the variability of the kidney disease phenotype in an AS mouse model. Why is Synpo deficiency in mice innocuous on its own but detrimental on the Alport background? Our previous study (15) revealed that Synpo is dispensable for kidney function but is essential for protecting podocytes after an injury (adriamycin-induced nephropathy) that causes FPE, a dramatic rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton and cell shape. Similarly, a differentSynpomutation had no effect on uninjured podocytes but worsened recovery from LPS-initiated podocyte injury (14). Synpo plays a protective role in the context of podocyte injury through its involvement in actin reorganization and focal adhesion dynamics (15). Yet even during kidney development, total lack of Synpo is not detrimental to glomerular function (15). This suggests that the extensive morphological changes that occur in podocytes during glomerulogenesis are not analogous to the podocyte injuries that occur in adriamycin-induced nephropathy, LPS-initiated injury, or AS.

It has been previously reported that podocyte loss underlies the progression of kidney disease in patients with AS (34). All progressive glomerular diseases in humans, including AS, are associated with an increased rate of podocyte detachment (38–40). The lack of the GBM’s normal collagen-α3α4α5(IV) network could result in impaired podocyte adhesion to the GBM, leading to accelerated podocyte detachment in AS (41,42). In this study, we showed that Synpo deficiency worsened podocyte injury and enhanced the loss of podocytes in Alport mice (Fig. 5). This serves as further evidence that Synpo protects podocytes from the effects of injury by helping them to resist detachment from the GBM.

How does Synpo protect podocytes from injury and cell detachment? Synpo is one of many actin-associated proteins that comprise the cytoskeleton within foot processes. This cytoskeleton is involved in anchoring the podocytes to the GBM via integrins and in linking adjacent foot processes to each other via the slit diaphragm (43,44). Absence of Synpo may exacerbate the reduced stability of podocyte adhesion to the abnormal GBM in AS. Podocytes respond to stress and injury by undergoing major changes in shape (45) and exhibiting FPE, a change in podocyte structure that occurs in a wide variety of human and experimental glomerulopathies with proteinuria (46). FPE, which requires the active reorganization of actin filaments, may represent a protective response by podocytes to prevent their detachment from the GBM (45). Thus, proteins regulating the plasticity of the podocyte actin cytoskeleton are of crucial importance for maintaining glomerular filtration (47).

A remarkable molecular change of the actin cytoskeleton in injured podocytes is that a network of myosin IIA-containing contractile actin cables within podocyte cell bodies and major processes relocates to the basal effaced areas (27). Our work demonstrates that in both Alport mice and those with adriamycin-induced nephropathy, absence of Synpo attenuates the relocation of myosin IIA to the areas of FPE (Fig. 7). This suggests that Synpo plays a protective role in injured podocytes by a mechanism that involves facilitating the relocation of myosin IIA to the areas of FPE. This conclusion is consistent with a study in cultured epithelial cells that showed that Synpo can physically link cell adhesion molecules to the cellular contractile apparatus, in part by binding to myosin II (48). Although more evidence is necessary to validate this mechanism in podocytes, we speculate that the contractile actin cables in the effaced areas help the podocyte hold on to and resist detachment from the GBM in AS and in other glomerular disease contexts (27).

Of particular relevance to these concepts, it has been recently shown that the NH2-terminus of inverted formin-2 (N-INF2), the formin that is frequently mutated in autosomal dominant FSGS, colocalizes with Synpo in foot processes under physiological conditions (49). N-INF2 disappeared from the effaced areas upon podocyte injury and FPE, including injury caused by AS (49). It would be interesting to investigate the link between absence of N-INF2 from the effaced areas in AS and the failure of myosin IIA to translocate inCol4a5−/−;Synpo−/−mice.

Col4a5+/−;Synpo−/−female mice showed a more rapid onset of podocyte injury versusCol4a5+/−;Synpo+/−controls. This was demonstrated by both histopathological and functional assessments (Fig. 4) as well as by an increased percentage of podocytes expressing desmin and an overall reduction in podocyte number (Fig. 5). Whether desmin is merely a marker of injured podocytes, contributes to injury, or is protective has not been investigated. One straightforward interpretation is that podocyte injury leads to desmin expression as a precursor event to detachment. If true, the increased percentage of desmin-positive podocytes (Fig. 5B) likely stems from more podocyte injury due to lack of synaptopodin, but the possibility that more podocyte detachment itself leads to more injury (and therefore more desmin) cannot be ruled out.

Although the more rapid onset of podocyte injury inCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−females was not unexpected, a surprising finding was the impact of Synpo deficiency on the mosaic deposition of collagen-α3α4α5(IV) in the GBM. We hypothesized that the detachment of podocytes expressing theCol4a5mutant X chromosome from GBM segments lacking collagen-α3α4α5(IV) would lead to an overrepresentation of podocytes expressing theCol4a5wild-type X chromosome and thus an increase in the percentage of the GBM containing collagen-α3α4α5(IV). However, quantification of collagen type IV deposition showed the opposite (Fig. 6). Based on our previous study that revealed a migration defect inSynpo−/−podocytes (15), we conclude that the overall reduced migration ofCol4a5+/−;Synpo−/−podocytes, even those expressing the wild-typeCol4a5allele, led to the observed reduction in the percentage of the GBM containing collagen-α3α4α5(IV). Additional studies into the mechanisms of podocyte migration could have important implications for devising new therapeutic approaches forCOL4A5+/−female patients with X-linked AS (50).

The calcineurin inhibitor cyclosporine A reduces proteinuria in human and canine AS (51,52), but due to limited efficacy and side effects, it is not suggested as a long-term therapy (53,54). The antiproteinuric effect of cyclosporine A is independent of its immunosuppressive function and results directly from the stabilization of the podocyte actin cytoskeleton. Calcineurin regulates podocyte actin dynamics by dephosphorylating Synpo and abrogating 14-3-3-mediated protection of Synpo from cathepsin-mediated proteolysis (55). These data suggest that the reduced Synpo abundance, which enhances destabilization of the actin cytoskeleton, is a contributing factor to the pathogenesis of AS. Indeed, Synpo abundance was reduced in the podocytes of patients with AS (17) and in Alport mouse podocytes as well, even before glomerulosclerosis was evident (Supplemental Fig. S1). Whether the reduction in Synpo abundance cooperates with concomitant reductions in other podocyte proteins to promote podocyte injury (56), or perhaps involves a mechanism dependent on transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily C member 6 trafficking (57), remains to be determined.

Despite the many activities that have been attributed to Synpo in the context of cultured cells (13,57,58), no pathogenicSYNPOmutations have been reported in patients with proteinuria. This is consistent with the lack of a phenotype in unchallengedSynpomutant mice (14,15). However, recently, unique homozygous mutations in the relatedSYNPO2gene were reported as possibly pathogenic in two families with congenital-onset nephrotic syndrome (59). Interestingly,SYNPO2is expressed in mesangial cells at a much higher level than in other glomerular cells, so the nephrotic syndrome in these families could result from a mesangial cell defect rather than from a podocyte defect.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that Synpo is crucial for the protection of kidney function in AS. Synpo and the podocyte actin cytoskeleton, including the contractile cables in the effaced areas, should be viewed as potential therapeutic targets in AS. The use of pharmacology or new CRISPR-based methods of gene modulation (60) to increase Synpo levels in podocytes could be beneficial for patients affected by a variety of podocytopathies.
