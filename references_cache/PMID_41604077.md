# Doxecitine and Doxribtimine: First Approval.
**Authors:** Blair HA
**Journal:** Paediatr Drugs (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s40272-025-00734-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40272-025-00734-1)

## Content

Doxecitine and doxribtimine (KYGEVVI®) are pyrimidine nucleosides developed by UCB for the treatment of thymidine kinase 2 deficiency (TK2d). In November 2025, doxecitine and doxribtimine received its first approval in the USA for the treatment of TK2d in adult and pediatric patients with an age of symptom onset on or before 12 years. This article summarizes the milestones in the development of doxecitine and doxribtimine leading to this first approval for TK2d.

Mitochondrial DNA depletion and deletion syndrome (MDDS) are a group of inherited, autosomal recessive disorders characterized by a severe reduction in cellular mtDNA that leads to impaired energy production [1]. MDDS are phenotypically heterogeneous and can be classified as myopathic, encephalomyopathic, hepatocerebral, or neurogastrointestinal. TK2 deficiency (TK2d) is a MDDS caused by mutations in thymidine kinase 2 (TK2) [1], a mitochondrial enzyme encoded by nuclear DNA that converts the nucleosides deoxycytidine and deoxythymidine into their corresponding monophosphates [2]. These molecules are then further phosphorylated to form deoxynucleoside triphosphates, essential building blocks for mtDNA replication [2].


TK2d is an ultra-rare, life-threatening type of MDDS that primarily affects the muscles [3]. The clinical symptoms of TK2d include severe, progressive muscle weakness (myopathy), respiratory failure, ptosis, swallowing and feeding difficulties, and early death [3]. Three clinical subtypes of TK2d have been identified based on the age at which symptoms begin [4]. Onset of TK2d at ≤ 2 years of age is typically linked to rapidly progressive myopathy, often accompanied by delayed or regressing motor milestones and early mortality [4]. Onset between > 2 and ≤ 12 years usually involves progressive proximal limb myopathy and loss of previously attained motor skills, with many patients requiring ventilatory assistance and losing the ability to walk within 10 years [2, 4]. Until now, there have been no approved treatment options for TK2d beyond supportive care. Current management largely focuses on symptomatic measures such as respiratory support (e.g., mechanical ventilation) and physical, occupational, and speech therapy, which help maintain function and quality of life but do not alter disease progression [5]. Pyrimidine nucleos(t)ide therapy offers a mechanistically targeted approach to the treatment of TK2d by restoring mitochondrial nucleotide pools, thereby improving mtDNA maintenance [4, 6].

Doxecitine and doxribtimine (KYGEVVI®) are pyrimidine nucleosides developed by UCB for the treatment of TK2d. In the USA, doxecitine and doxribtimine was granted Orphan Drug Designation on 7 July 2016 and Rare Pediatric Disease Designation on 21 October 2016 [7]. PRIority Medicine (PRIME) Designation was granted by the EMA in June 2018 and Orphan Drug Designation was granted by the EMA on 20 April 2017 [8]. Breakthrough Therapy Designation was granted by the US FDA on 8 February 2019 [9], and Priority Review Designation in February 2025 [10]. The New Drug Application for doxecitine and doxribtimine was accepted by the US FDA in February 2025 [10]. On 3 November 2025, doxecitine and doxribtimine received its first approval in the USA [11, 12], where it is indicated for the treatment of TK2d in adults and pediatric patients with an age of symptom onset on or before 12 years [13]. It is the first approved therapy for patients with TK2d.

Doxecitine and doxribtimine is available as a powder for oral solution that is mixed with water and swallowed or given through a feeding tube [13]. It is administered with food in three equal doses ≈ 6 h (± 2 h) apart. The recommended dosage of doxecitine and doxribtimine is based on body weight, with a starting dosage of 260 mg/kg/day (comprising doxecitine 130 mg and doxribtimine 130 mg), an intermediate dosage of 520 mg/kg/day (comprising doxecitine 260 mg and doxribtimine 260 mg) and a maintenance dosage of 800 mg/kg/day (comprising doxecitine 400 mg and doxribtimine 400 mg). After ≥ 2 weeks at the current dosage level, the dosage is titrated to the next level based on tolerability. Warnings and precautions regarding doxecitine and doxribtimine in the US prescribing information include those for elevated liver transaminase levels and gastrointestinal adverse reactions. Regular monitoring for and/or managing these adverse reactions (e.g., by reducing, interrupting, or discontinuing doxecitine and doxribtimine and/or initiating appropriate treatment) is recommended [13]. Local prescribing information should be consulted for further details, including instructions for preparation, storage, and administration.

Doxecitine and doxribtimine is undergoing regulatory review in the EU, where a Marketing Authorization Application was accepted in February 2025 [10].

Administration of doxecitine and doxribtimine facilitates the incorporation of the pyrimidine nucleosides deoxycytidine and deoxythymidine into skeletal muscle mtDNA, thereby promoting restoration of mtDNA copy number [13]. The exposure-response relationship and time course of the pharmacodynamic effects underlying the effectiveness and safety of doxecitine and doxribtimine have yet to be fully elucidated [13].

In phase I trials in healthy adult volunteers, plasma concentrations of deoxycytidine and deoxythymidine increased rapidly (median Tmax 1–2 h in the fasted state and 2–4 h in the fed state) and dose-dependently following oral administration of doxecitine and doxribtimine at single doses of 86.6, 173.4, and 266.6 mg/kg; these doses represent the sum of the two active ingredients present in a 1:1 ratio by weight (e.g., the 86.6 mg/kg dose consists of 43.3 mg/kg of doxecitine and 43.3 mg/kg of doxribtimine) [14]. Over this dose range, deoxycytidine demonstrated less than dose-proportional pharmacokinetics while deoxythymidine demonstrated greater than dose-proportional pharmacokinetics. When doxecitine and doxribtimine was administered with food, deoxycytidine Cmax and AUC increased by ≈ 79–96% and 137–250%, respectively, and deoxythymidine Cmax and AUC increased by 27–29% and 74–89%, respectively [14].

There is minimal or no accumulation following repeated administration of doxecitine and doxribtimine [13]. Deoxycytidine and deoxythymidine are < 10% bound to plasma proteins. Deoxycytidine and deoxythymidine undergo primary catabolic degradation via cytidine deaminase and thymidine phosphorylase, respectively, yielding their corresponding nucleobases and the 2-deoxy-α-D-ribose 1-phosphate moiety. Deoxycytidine is further metabolized to deoxyuridine, uracil, and dihydrouracil, and ultimately to β-alanine, ammonia, and CO2. The pyrimidine nucleobase of deoxythymidine, thymine, is further metabolized to dihydrothymine and subsequently to γ-amino-isobutyric acid and CO2. Following a single oral dose of doxecitine and doxribtimine 266 mg/kg under fed conditions, deoxycytidine and deoxythymidine have a mean half-life of ≈ 1 h and ≈ 5 h, respectively. Less than 1% of unchanged drug was excreted in urine following oral administration in healthy subjects [13].

Sex had no significant impact on the pharmacokinetics of deoxycytidine and deoxythymidine [13]. The effects of hepatic impairment and mild renal impairment on deoxycytidine and deoxythymidine pharmacokinetics have not been studied. However, deoxycytidine AUC was 122% and 66% higher in subjects with moderate [estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥ 30 and ≤ 59 mL/min/1.73 m2] and severe (eGFR ≥ 15 and ≤ 29 mL/min/1.73 m2) renal impairment, respectively, than in subjects with normal renal function. Deoxythymidine AUC was 447% and 14.8% higher in subjects with moderate and severe renal impairment, respectively, than in subjects with normal renal function [13]. The doxecitine and doxribtimine clinical program reflects the fact that TK2d is a rare disease and therefore does not follow the traditional clinical trial pathway. The data package within the US prescribing information was pooled from two retrospective chart reviews (Study 1; NCT03701568 and Study 2; NCT05017818), a prospective, open-label, single-arm trial (Trial 1; NCT03845712), and an expanded-access program (EAP; NCT06590493). This was compared with a database of untreated patients from comprehensive literature search and supplemented with data from untreated patients from the retrospective chart review NCT05017818.

Treatment with pyrimidine nucleos(t)ides improved survival, stabilized disease progression and improved functional outcomes in patients with TK2d, according to the results of a multicenter, retrospective chart review (Study 1; NCT03701568) [4]. None of the patients in the treated group died, while 58% of untreated patients died. Estimated reductions in the risk of death with treatment ranged from 85% to 93% [hazard ratio (HR) 0.067–0.147] for the time to death from TK2d symptom onset and ranged from 75% to 91% (HR 0.091–0.251) for the time to death from treatment initiation. Cox regression confirmed a statistically significant 95% reduction in the risk of death [odds ratio (OR) 0.044–0.047; p < 0.0001 across all matching methods]. Similar results were seen in the subgroup of patients with age at TK2d symptom onset of ≤ 12 years (n = 85): no treated patients died, while 59% of patients in the untreated group died. Estimated reductions in the risk of death with treatment were 67–89% (HR 0.111–0.333) for the time to death from symptom onset and 75–91% (HR 0.091–0.251) for the time to death from treatment initiation. Exact conditional logistic regression estimates were consistent with those of the Cox regression, with a statistically significant 92% reduction in the risk of death (OR 0.072–0.080; p = 0.0020 for 50th percentile, p = 0.0010 for all other matching methods) [4].

Medical records of children and adults with TK2d treated with pyrimidine nucleosides and/or pyrimidine nucleotide monophosphates (n = 38) were collected and compared with a control group of untreated patients with TK2d (n = 69) [4]. All treated patients had genetic confirmation of pathogenic TK2 variants. They received oral deoxycytidine and deoxythymidine and/or deoxycytidine monophosphate and deoxythymidine monophosphate at a low starting dosage which was gradually increased to a maintenance dosage of 400/400 mg/kg/day. Overall, the median age at TK2d symptom onset was ≈ 2 years and most patients (79%) had an age at symptom onset of ≤ 12 years. In the treated group, most (61%) patients started treatment before the age of 18 years and the median duration of treatment was 1.6 years (treatment exposure 74.3 patient-years) [4].

In terms of functional outcomes, no patients lost any achieved milestones after starting treatment and 65% regained ≥ 1 previously lost milestone [4]. Among those using ventilatory support after starting treatment (n = 21), five patients had a ≥ 4-h reduction in support requirements, and no patients required increased hours of ventilatory support. Before treatment, 21% (8/38) of patients used an enteral feeding tube, three of whom had feeding tubes removed after starting treatment [4].

Pyrimidine nucleos(t)ide therapy significantly decreased mortality in patients with TK2d aged ≤ 12 years at symptom onset, but not in patients aged > 12 years at symptom onset, according to pooled analyses of Study 1 (NCT03701568), Study 2 (NCT05017818), Trial 1 (NCT03845712), and an EAP (NCT06590493) [16–18]. The pooled analyses were conducted in treated patients (82 patients aged ≤ 12 years at symptom onset [16, 17] and 22 patients aged > 12 years at symptom onset [18]), with survival analyses also conducted in untreated patients from Study 1 and literature reviews (93 patients aged ≤ 12 years at symptom onset [16, 17] and 21 patients aged > 12 years at symptom onset [18]). In patients aged ≤ 12 years at symptom onset, death occurred in 4% of treated patients and 57% of untreated patients [16]. Treatment reduced the risk of death by 92–94% (HR 0.06–0.08; p = 0.0001) for the time to death from TK2d symptom onset and by 87–95% (HR 0.05–0.13; p < 0.0001) for the time to death from treatment initiation. The restricted mean survival time (RMST) estimated over 30 years after TK2d symptom onset was 29.2 years for treated patients versus 14.4 years for untreated patients. The RMST estimated over 6 years after treatment initiation was 5.8 years for treated patients versus 2.8 years for untreated patients [16].

In terms of functional outcomes, treatment with pyrimidine nucleos(t)ides reduced the frequency of developmental motor milestone loss in patients with age of symptom onset ≤ 12 years, with 75% of patients regaining ≥ 1 previously lost motor milestone and 23% of patients regaining ≥ 4 previously lost motor milestones [17]. In patients aged > 12 years at symptom onset, death occurred in 18% of treated patients and 24% of untreated patients [18], with no significant difference in the risk of death for treated versus untreated patients. The RMST estimated over 30 years after TK2d symptom onset was 27.2 years for treated patients and 24.8 years for untreated patients. The RMST estimated over 6 years after treatment initiation was 4.8 years for treated patients and 5.4 years for untreated patients. Following treatment with pyrimidine nucleos(t)ides, 33% of patients regained ≥ 1 previously lost motor milestone. No patients discontinued use of ventilatory support after treatment but 11% of patients reduced duration of ventilatory support requirements [18].

Pyrimidine nucleos(t)ide therapy was well tolerated in patients with TK2d, according to pooled analyses of Study 1 (NCT03701568), Study 2 (NCT05017818) and Trial 1 (NCT03845712) [19]. In the overall pooled safety population including patients with all ages of symptom onset (n = 67; 345.0 patient-years of exposure), 24% of patients experienced treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) that led to dose reduction, and 13% had TEAEs resulting in treatment discontinuation. Six percent of patients discontinued treatment due to serious TEAEs, and 5% experienced fatal serious TEAEs, none of which were considered treatment related. Among patients with available TEAE data (n = 50), the incidence of TEAEs was 100%. Diarrhea was the most common TEAE, occurring in 86% of patients. The proportion of patients with TEAEs leading to dose reduction was 28%. Eight percent of patients experienced treatment-related TEAEs and 56% of patients experienced serious TEAEs. The most common serious TEAEs were pneumonia (14%) and acute respiratory failure (10%); these were not considered treatment related [19].

In Study 1 (NCT03701568), TEAEs related to treatment occurred in 76% of patients and were mainly gastrointestinal in nature [4]. The overall incidence of TEAEs was 95%, most of which were of mild severity. The most common (incidence ≥ 10%) TEAEs in patients treated with pyrimidine nucleos(t)ides were diarrhea (68%), increased blood creatine kinase (24%), pyrexia (16%), increased alanine aminotransferase (ALT; 16%), increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST; 13%), dysphagia (13%), muscular weakness (13%), cough (11%), influenza-like illness (11%), pneumonia (11%), and vomiting (11%). Serious TEAEs occurred in 37% of patients; these included chronic respiratory failure (5%), femur fracture (5%), pneumonia (5%), and restrictive pulmonary disease (5%). Five percent of patients discontinued treatment due to TEAEs [4].

In Trial 1 (NCT03845712), the most common (incidence ≥ 5%) adverse reactions in patients treated with pyrimidine nucleosides (n = 47) were diarrhea (72%), abdominal pain (23%), vomiting (21%), increased ALT (21%), and increased AST (17%) [13]. Pediatric patients were more likely than adult patients to experience vomiting (28% vs 7%), increased ALT (25% vs 13%), and increased AST (22% vs 7%) [13].

In addition to Trial 1 (NCT03845712), which is ongoing, two open-label, single-arm, single-center, investigator-initiated trials are currently underway: a phase II trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of doxecitine and doxribtimine in adults (≥ 18 years) with TK2d (NCT06754098) and a phase I/II trial investigating the safety and efficacy of doxecitine and doxribtimine in children and adults with TK2d (NCT03639701).

Two separate EAPs offering treatment with doxecitine and doxribtimine are available (NCT06590493). The first (TK0113) is a company-supported program that required single-patient Investigational New Drug submissions to the USA FDA. This program began in July 2020, is still ongoing and has specific inclusion criteria, including pediatric and adult patients with a diagnosis of TK2d based on confirmed reportable variant(s) in the TK2 gene in countries where UCB has an affiliate/local safety officer; signs and symptoms compatible with TK2d disease; and risk of major disability or death resulting from TK2d. The second (TK0115) is a UCB-sponsored EAP, which began in July 2024, is still ongoing, and the inclusion criteria are pediatric and adult patients with TK2d with an age of symptom onset of ≤ 12 years.

Doxecitine and doxribtimine received its first approval in the USA on 3 November 2025 for the treatment of TK2d in adults and pediatric patients with an age of symptom onset on or before 12 years [11, 12].
