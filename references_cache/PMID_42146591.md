Autism affects males four times more often than females, yet the basis of this
sex bias remains unclear. One hypothesis is that hypomorphic variants in
X-linked genes-genes where loss-of-function alleles cause syndromic
neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) predominantly in females-produce milder,
non-syndromic phenotypes in hemizygous males. We tested this by investigating
cis-regulatory elements (CREs) of MECP2, a dosage-sensitive X-linked gene. Using
a massively parallel reporter assay in human neurons, we mapped transcription
factor binding sites within MECP2 CREs and tested autism-associated variants for
functional impact. We identified two noncoding variants that change CRE
activity, each with a male-biased phenotype. One of these, a promoter variant,
disrupts NFY binding and reduces MECP2 expression by ~30%, a magnitude that
produces autism-like phenotypes in mice. These findings suggest noncoding MECP2
variants can cause non-syndromic, male-biased autism, and provide a framework
for uncovering regulatory variants in other X-linked NDD genes that may
contribute to autism's missing heritability.

Introduction  37
Autism is a common, heritable1 neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) marked by altered social 38
interaction and restricted, repetitive behaviors or interests2.  Autism is diagnosed about four times more 39
in males than females, suggesting sex-specific factors may increase male vulnerability3. Previous work4 40
tested whether this bias is reflected in the genetic architecture of autism and other NDDs by comparing 41
the enrichment of deleterious de novo coding variants in male and female NDD probands. Surprisingly, 42
the most sex-biased genes showed an excess of de novo mutations in females, not males4. These 43
genes—DDX3X, HDAC8, WDR45, NAA10, and MECP2—are all X-linked. Of these, only MECP2 44
approached statistical significance when restricting diagnostic criteria to autism4. This likely reflects the 45

produce a milder phenotype like autism, with a male-biased penetrance. 50
To investigate this, we focused on MECP2, a dosage-sensitive gene that encodes methyl CpG 51
binding protein 2 (MeCP2). Strong loss-of-function variants in MECP2 cause a profound disorder, Rett 52
syndrome, in females10 but typically lead to severe neonatal encephalopathy and premature lethality in 53
males9. Gain-of-function MECP2 variants are often asymptomatic in females but cause profound 54
neurodevelopmental phenotypes in males11, termed MECP2 duplication syndrome (MDS). In both 55
disorders, females are protected from more severe phenotypes by mosaic expression of wild-type 56
MECP2 on their second X chromosome. This holds true even for mild hypomorphic MECP2 variants, 57
such as p.A140V, which causes intellectual disability and psychiatric symptoms in males, while female 58
carriers are healthy or minimally affected12,13. This led us to hypothesize that other mild MECP2 59
variants, such as mutations in cis-regulatory elements (CREs), might cause non-syndromic neurological 60
phenotypes like autism, particularly in males.  61
This hypothesis is supported by mouse models of the Mecp2 allelic spectrum. While Mecp2 62
null14,15 or over-expression alleles16 recapitulate Rett syndrome or MDS, respectively, mutations that 63
lead to moderate changes in Mecp2 expression levels can cause milder autism-like phenotypes. 64
Deleting a conserved intronic enhancer (“CRE 2”) decreases Mecp2 expression by 30%, causing social 65
deficits, anxiety, and hyperactivity17. Conversely, deleting a conserved upstream repressor (“CRE 6”) 66
increases gene expression by 50% and causes social defects, hypoactivity, and anxiety, and impairs 67
motor function, learning, and memory17. This indicates that there is a narrow range for MeCP2 levels in 68
healthy brain development, and that human variants in CREs that tip MECP2 expression levels out of 69
this range may cause autism in hemizygous males.  70
To begin testing this hypothesis, we took two parallel approaches: 1) test the functional impact 71
of rare MECP2 noncoding variants found in male autism probands, and 2) expand the catalog of 72
MECP2 CREs and improve their functional analysis to inform future variant discovery and 73
interpretation. To this end, we performed massively parallel reporter assays (MPRAs) in induced 74
pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neurons (iNeurons) to test autism-associated variants for altered 75
CRE activity and to determine which regions of MECP2 CREs contain active transcription factor (TF) 76
binding sites (TFBSs). We identified multiple CRE sequences with clear activator or repressor binding 77
sites and identified the most likely corresponding transcription factors (TFs). We also found autism-78
associated variants near or within these binding sites that impact CRE function. Finally, we validated a 79
promoter variant identified in a male proband with non-syndromic autism and ADHD, discovering that it 80
lowers endogenous MECP2 expression by ~30%. This study is the first to link noncoding MECP2 81
variants to male-biased, non-syndromic autism, highlighting the potential for noncoding variants in other 82
X-linked NDD genes to contribute to male-biased autism. 83

A massively parallel reporter assay to dissect MECP2 cis-regulatory elements 87
To build a catalog of MECP2 CREs in relevant human brain tissues, we examined regions of 88
open chromatin from two independent human fetal prefrontal cortex datasets18,19 (Fig. 1a). We focused 89
on the region between OPN1LW and IRAK1, as previous work has shown that a bacterial artificial 90
chromosome encompassing this region is sufficient to reproduce the endogenous MECP2 expression16, 91
demonstrating that it contains all required CREs. As expected, we identified open chromatin at the 92
MECP2 promoter, and at the two previously identified17 regulatory elements, CRE 2 and CRE 6. We 93
also found two unexplored regions of open chromatin, one in intron 2 close to CRE 2 (which we call 94
“CRE A”) and one between the promoter and CRE 6 (“CRE B”). Unlike CREs 2 and 6, these elements 95
are not conserved to mice, although they are conserved across multiple nonhuman primate species 96
(Supplementary Fig. 1a-b). 97
 CREs contain motifs that are recognized by TFs, which act in concert and with co-factors to 98
direct gene expression20. We identified hundreds of potential TF motifs in each of the CREs (Fig. 1b). 99
To test which ones actively contribute to CRE function, we designed a lentivirus MPRA to 100
systematically perturb the motif and evaluate the effect on gene expression. As MECP2 is most critical 101
in neurons15, we performed this assay in human iNeurons, using Ngn2 to drive differentiation into 102
cortical glutamatergic neurons21. To test the whole CRE sequence within the constraints of an 103
oligonucleotide library, we broke each CRE into overlapping 270 bp tiles, with a sliding window of 70 bp 104
(Fig. 1c). In each tile, we predicted all possible TF motifs, then disrupted each motif by randomly 105
shuffling its nucleotides, two independent times (Fig. 1c). Altogether, this introduced over 1,000 106
mutations in each CRE (Fig. 1d). 107
 We also included a curated set of single-nucleotide variants identified in individuals with autism, 108
derived from analyzing over 8,000 families across 14 different autism cohorts. We selected variants that 109
were: 1) present in one of the five defined CREs; 2) ultra-rare (private to a single family); 3) found only 110
in male probands; and 4) inherited from unaffected mothers. This resulted in 22 variants across the five 111
CREs (Fig. 1e). As negative controls, we also included 19 gnomAD variants that were each found in at 112
least 50 males assumed to be neurotypical (i.e., not designated as cases in neuropsychiatric studies) 113
(Supplementary Table 1). As technical controls, we included two sets of sequences previously shown to 114
have high or low activity in the iNeuron MPRA assay22.  115
We followed an established protocol for lentivirus MPRA23, performing four replicates in 116
iNeurons (Fig. 1f). We achieved a median value of ~135 barcodes per element (Supplementary Fig. 2a) 117
and observed a strong correlation across the four replicates (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Our control 118
sequences behaved as expected, with negative control sequences showing low log2(RNA/DNA) values 119
and the positive control sequences with high log2(RNA/DNA) values (Supplementary Fig. 2c). 120

Annotating active TFBSs in MECP2 CREs 124
 We first examined which shuffled TF motifs significantly impacted MPRA activity compared to 125
their matched reference, then mapped these back to the full CRE sequence. Shuffled sequences and 126
their effect sizes are provided in Supplementary Table 2. To visualize results, we plotted the average 127
effect size of each shuffle mutation across the four replicates, sorting each mutation into 50 bp bins. 128
Each motif is represented by multiple datapoints from independent shuffle mutations tested across 129
multiple tiles. A motif that reproducibly contributes to CRE activity appears as a cluster of datapoints 130
within a single bin. 131
We then defined “hotspot” regions with robust evidence of activator or repressor binding. Four 132
out of five CREs (CRE 2, 6, A, and the proximal promoter) contained at least one hotspot 133
(Supplementary Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table 3). Consistent with its positive regulatory role, the 134
proximal promoter contained an activator-binding hotspot (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The known 135
enhancer CRE 2 contained four activator-binding sites and three repressor-binding sites 136
(Supplementary Fig. 3a, Fig. 2a), and the known repressor CRE 6 contained two strong repressor-137
binding sites and one activator-binding site (Supplementary Fig. 3a-b). These data align with previous 138
findings that both activator and repressor motifs can work together to fine-tune a CRE’s regulatory 139
output24. Notably, the CTCF binding site previously identified in CRE 617 does not reach statistical 140
significance here yet has a subtle repressive effect (Supplementary Fig. 3b-c), suggesting that it retains 141
some repressor function even without its endogenous chromatin context. Finally, we evaluated the two 142
newly described CREs. One element, CRE B, had no active TFBSs (Supplementary Fig. 3a, 143
Supplementary Fig. 3d). This is consistent with reduced chromatin accessibility at CRE B in 144
differentiated neurons compared to radial glia (Fig. 1a) and suggests that CRE B may regulate MECP2 145
in neural stem cells rather than neurons. CRE A, in contrast, contained three repressor-binding sites 146
and no activator-binding sites (Supplementary Fig. 3a), consistent with a role as a repressor in neurons.  147
We then extracted the sequences underlying the TF binding hotspots and aligned them to 148
assess whether the mutations overlapped with each other and to predict which motifs were driving the 149
difference in activity. For example, in CRE 2, we focused on an activator-binding site with a large effect 150
between positions 300 and 400 (Fig. 2a), where we found the critical sequence between positions 316-151
364 (Fig. 2b). We then used an unbiased, orthogonal approach to independently test which motifs 152
contributed the most to the change in activity. Briefly, we identified nucleotide sequences (i.e., k-mers) 153
with the greatest effect on CRE activity, matched each to known motifs using TomTom25, and refined 154
iteratively to the smallest k-mer that optimized the motif match. We then prioritized motifs 155
corresponding to TFs expressed in iNeurons26,27 and the developing brain28 to focus on biologically 156
relevant candidates. This approach improved the mapping of TFBSs with strong contribution to CRE 157
activity, refining the list of candidate TFs that may mediate its regulatory activity. For the CRE 2 158

CRE 2 TFBS activity is influenced by flanking sequence context  168
The MPRA’s tiling design allowed us to investigate whether the activity of these TFBSs is 169
impacted by the context of the surrounding nucleotides. To test this, we focused on the strong activator-170
binding sites we detected in CRE 2. The shuffled sequences at the TCF3/ZNF281 motifs were tested 171
with four different flanking sequences, moving from the far 3' end of the tile (Tile 2) to the far 5' end of 172
the tile (Tile 5) (Fig. 3a). Overall, the mutations that decreased MPRA activity in one tile similarly 173
decreased activity in the other tiles, indicating that this TFBS is active regardless of the flanking 174
sequence (Fig. 3b). However, we discovered the opposite effect at the nearby activator-binding site at 175
position 500-550 (Fig. 2a). Through sequence alignment and our motif prediction strategy, we narrowed 176
the key region to the poly-adenine sequence within position 510-548 (Fig. 3c), with the best motif 177
prediction matching a ZNF362 motif (Supplementary Table 4). Here, the mutations that decreased 178
MPRA activity in Tile 5 lost their effect when the same mutation was tested in Tiles 6, 7, or 8 (Fig. 3d). 179
One possibility is that moving from Tile 5 to Tile 6 adds a 3' repressor-binding site that neutralizes the 180
upstream ZNF362 activator site. However, the nearest repressor-binding site at 650-700 (Fig. 2a) is not 181
included in the sequence until Tile 7 (Supplementary Table 2) and thus cannot explain the loss of 182
activator effect in Tile 6. The other possibility is that moving from Tile 5 to Tile 6 loses a 5' activating 183
partner. Indeed, the TCF3/ZNF281 site is included in Tile 5 but mostly excluded from Tile 6 (Fig. 3a). 184
This suggests that the factor(s) binding to the ZNF362 motif rely on a partnership with those binding to 185
the TCF3/ZNF281 motifs to drive CRE activity.  186

A male proband variant in the proximal promoter impacts MECP2 expression  219
We next investigated the architecture of the MECP2 proximal promoter, selecting a 970 bp 220
sequence spanning 1021-51 bp upstream of the transcription start site (TSS). This excludes the GC-221
rich core promoter that binds the transcription initiation machinery30. Across this ~1 kb sequence, we 222
identified a single activator-binding hotspot at MPRA coordinates 918-955 (Fig. 5a) corresponding to a 223
SP/KLF motif and an E-box motif. Motif matching identified KLF7 and ZEB1 as the strongest candidates 224
with robust expression in iNeurons (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 4).  225
We also tested seven maternally inherited variants from male autism probands, alongside two 226
gnomAD variants. These controls did not affect regulatory activity (Fig. 5c). Four of the seven autism 227
proband variants significantly changed regulatory activity in one tile, and three of these 228

5c). However, they did not change activity when tested in additional tiles, indicating that these effects 230
may be context-dependent or false positives. (Variant -7837:A:G was positioned so close to the 3' end 231
of the design that it could only be tested in one tile.) To clarify these data, we turned to the luciferase 232
reporter assay, using the entire 970 bp sequence upstream of a minimal promoter. As expected, the 233
reference sequence drove strong luciferase activity compared to an empty plasmid control (Fig. 5d). 234

To further investigate the pathogenicity of this variant, we took a closer look at the genetic and 239
phenotypic data of the proband. This variant was identified in a pedigree from the Simons Simplex 240
Collection (SSC)31 and was passed from an unaffected mother to her affected son, but not her 241
neurotypical son (Fig. 6a). We did not identify any de novo coding variants or CNVs in known autism 242
genes that might explain the affected son’s phenotype. Although the proband did inherit a missense 243
variant of uncertain significance in NAA15, this was also transmitted to his unaffected sibling 244
(Supplementary Table 5). We were surprised to find a deleterious variant so close to the core MECP2 245
promoter in a male case of non-syndromic autism, and so we carefully evaluated the extensive 246
phenotypic data collected by SSC to rule out Rett syndrome features. The proband was diagnosed with 247
autism and ADHD but had a full-scale IQ of 97 and normal verbal skills, with no motor deficits or 248
seizures (Fig. 6b)—consistent with non-syndromic autism.  249
The -7837:A:G variant affects a highly conserved residue in the proximal promoter (Fig. 6c) and 250
is predicted to disrupt a NFY binding site (Fig. 6d). NFY is a trimeric complex (with -A, -B, and -C 251
subunits) involved in nucleosome depletion and transcription initiation at promoters32,33 and regulates 252
both housekeeping34 and neuron-specific genes35. Intriguingly, NFY-B binds to the MECP2 promoter in 253
ChIP-Seq datasets from iPSCs, K562 cells, and GM12878 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a), indicating that 254
this complex may regulate MECP2 expression. To test this, we used CRISPR-Cas9 to edit this variant 255
into the native locus in iPSCs. After editing, we isolated clonal lines with or without the variant, using 256
matched controls that were subject to nucleofection and single-cell cloning but did not undergo editing. 257
We selected two variant clones and two wild-type clones to differentiate into iNeurons, collecting cells 258
after 4 weeks of neuronal differentiation. To determine whether the -7837:A:G variant impairs NFY 259
binding, we performed CUT&Tag for NFY-B across our four clones, as well as for IgG as a negative 260
control. NFY-B iNeuron peaks substantially overlapped with NFY-B peaks in the iPSC, K562, and 261
GM12878 datasets, with ~35% of peaks shared between all four cell types, and another 15% shared 262
between iNeurons and 1-2 other cell types (Supplementary Fig. 5b). The remaining peaks may be 263
neuron-specific or protocol-dependent (i.e., CUT&Tag in this study compared to ChIP-Seq). As 264
expected, the iNeuron peaks were strongly enriched for promoters (Supplementary Fig. 5c), and de 265

Discussion 280
 We explored the hypothesis that noncoding variants impacting dosage-sensitive, X-linked NDD 281
genes contribute to male-biased autism, focusing on MECP2 as initial proof-of-concept. We performed 282
a MPRA in iNeurons to define critical TFBSs in MECP2 CREs, predict their trans-acting partners, and 283
test the functional consequences of ultra-rare variants identified in male autism probands. We mapped 284
key activator- and/or repressor-binding sites for four of the five CREs we interrogated. We also 285
identified two autism-associated variants that impact CRE function, one in the proximal promoter and 286
one in the newly described CRE A repressor. Consistent with the expected male sex bias, both variants 287
were inherited from an unaffected mother. Modeling the promoter variant at the endogenous MECP2 288
locus in iNeurons revealed that it reduced RNA and protein by ~30%, a degree that is sufficient to 289
produce autism-like phenotypes in mice17. 290
 One of the goals of this study was to develop a strategy for improving the annotation of key 291
active sequences within a CRE, to narrow the search space for noncoding pathogenic variants and help 292
prioritize variants for follow-up studies. While not all proband variants overlapping an active TFBS 293
affected CRE activity, the two variants that altered activity in our assay were within or close to a strong 294
TFBS. While the CRE A variant perfectly aligned with the major CRE A repressor-binding site, the 295
promoter variant was 17 bp away from the strongest activator-binding sites. This may be explained by 296
our focus on non-syndromic autism cohorts; a variant directly impacting the activator motifs might 297
cause more features of Rett syndrome or even premature lethality in males. Overall, the orthogonal 298
dataset for TFBSs helped bolster our interpretation of the human proband variants. We also mitigated 299
false positives by focusing on variants that impacted function in more than one MPRA tile, and by 300
including variants from neurotypical gnomAD controls to define a threshold for a benign change in 301
reporter expression. These careful design considerations may help future MPRA studies prioritize the 302
most likely pathogenic variants for additional mechanistic studies. 303
 We also predicted candidate TF motifs underlying each of these activator- and repressor-304
binding sites, matched them to TFs expressed in iNeurons, and demonstrated a functional relationship 305
between selected TFs and MECP2. However, we are limited in our ability to comprehensively catalog 306
which TFs (or which combination of TFs) directly regulate MECP2 expression. This is partly because a 307
motif can be matched with many different TFs that have similar position weight matrices, especially 308
those within the same TF family36. Despite this, some of the TFs we identified are compelling 309
candidates that have been implicated in NDDs, such as KLF737. Others, such as ZNF281 and ZNF362, 310

include noncoding variants linked to non-syndromic autism in males, and establishes a generalizable 323
framework for interrogating cis-regulatory variation in X-linked NDD genes that may underlie a fraction 324
of autism’s missing heritability and male sex bias.   325

MPRA design 475
Candidate CREs were selected from fetal brain ATAC-seq datasets18,19. The sequence under 476
each peak was extended to 970 bp (CREs A, B, 2, and promoter) or 690 bp (CRE 6). Each CRE was 477
then divided into 270 bp tiles offset by 70 bp, with a 200 bp overlap. To design the motif shuffling part of 478
the library, TF motifs within each tile were identified using the MEME Suite tool FIMO43, using the CIS-479
BP v2.0 motif library. Each motif sequence was extracted, shuffled two independent times using the 480
universalmotif package (shuffle sequence function, linear shuffling, k=1), then placed back into the tile. 481
To build the human variant part of the library, the 22 PIVs were manually inserted into the relevant 482
library tiles. As controls, variants from gnomAD identified in at least 50 males from the v3.1.2 non-neuro 483
subset were included. Finally, positive and negative controls (100 sequences each) known to have high 484
or low activity in iNeuron lentiMPRA were included44.  485

LentiMPRA analysis 585
Data analysis was performed using the MPRAflow pipeline23 with minor modifications: 1) in the 586
Association workflow, the cigar value of 270M was used as the primary selection criteria to map reads 587
to the reference, and 2) in the Count workflow, merged barcode reads were trimmed to be 15 bp. Only 588
CREs with at least 10 unique barcodes were considered in downstream analysis. The log2(RNA/DNA) 589
value for each CRE across each replicate was used for all data analysis. Briefly, a one-way ANOVA 590
with Dunnett’s post hoc test was performed to compare the log2(RNA/DNA values) for each of the 591
engineered TF shuffle mutations to its matched reference sequence. A separate ANOVA with Dunnett’s 592
post hoc test was performed for each of the human variants. Each mutated nucleotide was labeled with 593
its numerical position within the entire CRE element to enable binning and comparison with other 594
shuffled sequences. TF hotspots were classified by defining bins with at least 50 shuffled nucleotides to 595
ensure adequate coverage, with 10% of the shuffles significantly decreasing or increasing activity. The 596
full data are available in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Table 6.  597
