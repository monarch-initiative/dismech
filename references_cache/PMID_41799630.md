# Incidence of cranial and ophthalmic nerve palsy and associated risk factors in tuberculous meningitis: A systematic review and meta-regression analysis.
**Authors:** Eftekhari N, Gholizadeh M, Azizi Z, Najafi S, Mohammadi R, Arzaghi M, Hemat M, Aliaskari S, Esmaeili S, Zamiri R, Salimi O, Naseri Rad F, Yarahmadi D, Deravi N
**Journal:** IBRO Neurosci Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ibneur.2026.02.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ibneur.2026.02.014)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Tuberculous meningitis (TBM) is the most severe form of extrapulmonary tuberculosis, frequently complicated by cranial nerve palsy (CNP) and ophthalmic nerve palsy (ONP), which are associated with poor prognosis. Understanding their incidence and associated risk factors is essential for timely diagnosis and improved management.

METHOD: This study conducts a systematic review and meta-analysis of research on CNP and ONP in TBM patients. Several medical databases were searched from the earliest records to April 2025. Eligible studies included cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, and baseline data from clinical trials. Two independent reviewers extracted data and assessed quality. Pooled incidence rates were estimated using random-effects models. Univariable meta-regression explored potential risk factors.

RESULT: Forty-seven diverse studies published between 1977 and 2025 from around the world with varying samples sizes were included. The pooled prevalence of CNP in TBM patients was 26.5 % (95 % CI: 22.4-31.1 %), while ONP prevalence was 23.9 % (95 % CI: 18.7-30.0 %). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial. In univariable meta-regression, hydrocephalus was associated with higher incidence of both outcomes, whereas cerebral infarction and higher CSF protein were additionally associated with CNP. These findings represent study-level associations and should be interpreted cautiously.

CONCLUSION: Around one-quarter of TBM patients experience cranial nerve palsy and approximately one-quarter develop ophthalmic nerve palsy. Hydrocephalus emerged as the most consistent correlate of both outcomes. Cerebral infarction and higher CSF protein were also associated with higher CNP incidence. Early identification and management of these complications may improve neurological outcomes, but variability across settings should be interpreted cautiously.

In 2023, Tuberculosis likely reemerged as the foremost cause of mortality from a singular infectious agent, following a three-year period during which it was supplanted by coronavirus disease (COVID-19), resulting in approximately double the fatalities attributed to HIV/AIDS. Annually, around 10 million individuals are afflicted by this disease, with the incidence increasing since 2021. In 2023, the TB incidence rate was 134, reflecting a marginal increase of 0.2 % compared to 2022 (World Health Organization, 2017, World Health Organization, 2024). Tuberculous meningitis (TBM) is a severe manifestation of extrapulmonary tuberculosis characterized by elevated rates of disability and fatality (Brancusi et al., 2012, Seddon et al., 2019).

TBM represents a significant concern, as its clinical presentation and long-term prognosis depend on the disease's progression at the time of diagnosis. The revised British Medical Research Council staging criteria classifies tuberculous meningitis into three stages: Stage I patients are alert and oriented, exhibiting no focal neurological deficits; Stage II includes individuals with reduced awareness, as evidenced by a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score ranging from 11 to 14, or minor focal symptoms like cranial nerve palsies; Stage III is assigned to those with significantly reduced awareness, indicated by a GCS score of 10 or lower, or severe neurological impairments such as hemiplegia or paraplegia (Amin et al., 2016, Sher et al., 2013, Selvaraj et al., 2020). A more advanced stage at initial evaluation corresponds to an increased chance of mortality and enduring neurological consequences. Cranial and ocular nerve impairments are predominantly observed in Stages II and III, underscoring their potential as early prognostic indicators (Sher et al., 2013, Kalita et al., 2020).

Cranial nerve palsy is a common consequence of tuberculous meningitis. Recent investigations indicate that patients with TBM are at an elevated risk of developing cranial nerve palsy compared to those with other central nervous system illnesses and healthy controls (Moghtaderi et al., 2013, Li et al., 2019, Sinha et al., 2010). Clinically, cranial nerve involvement can support the diagnosis of TBM relative to alternative causes of meningitis (Sinha et al., 2010). Severe cranial neuropathy may lead to disabling sequelae such as hearing loss and visual impairment, and is associated with unfavorable outcomes (Marais et al., 2010).

Research indicates that approximately 40 % of TBM patients experience visual impairment due to injury to the optic and oculomotor nerves, either from inflammation or physical compression (Li et al., 2019, Anupriya et al., 2010, Li et al., 2017, Wen et al., 2023).Visual impairment can arise from various reasons, including third ventricle compression of the optic chiasm, optic chiasmatic arachnoiditis, ethambutol poisoning, and optic nerve granuloma (Berger, 1994). Vision impairments caused by medication complicate the distinction between disease advancement and therapy adverse effects.

Ultimately, the treatment for TBM prioritized mortality reduction, with less emphasis on long-term consequences. Nonetheless, the WHO’s 2015 “End TB” strategy, which aims to reduce TB mortality by 95 % by 2035, has rendered the management of long-term sequelae such as optic nerve palsy increasingly significant (World Health Organization, 2023, Misra et al., 1996, Kalra and Ghose, 1985).

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to elucidate the epidemiological trends and identify significant risk variables associated with ocular nerve palsy in individuals with TBM. This research seeks to deepen our comprehension of the fundamental mechanisms, facilitating early diagnosis and informing therapeutic management techniques to improve patient prognosis through the collection and analysis of data from various investigations.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the pooled incidence and risk factors of cranial nerve palsy (CNP) and ophthalmic nerve palsy (ONP) in patients with tuberculous meningitis (TBM). The study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO. PROSPERO registration ID: CRD42025649903

A comprehensive search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception through April 2025. The strategy combined relevant Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords, including “tuberculous meningitis,” “cranial nerve palsy,” “ophthalmic nerve,” and “risk factors.” Reference lists of included articles and related systematic reviews were screened manually. No restrictions were placed on language or publication date.

We included studies (cross-sectional, cohort, or case-control studies, or baseline data from clinical trials) that met all of the following:

• Diagnosis of TBM confirmed by clinical presentation, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, and/or neuroimaging findings.

• Reported incidence of cranial nerve palsy and/or ophthalmic nerve palsy among TBM patients.

• Reported clinical or laboratory variables (risk factors) potentially associated with nerve involvement.

For RCTs (Randomized controlled Trial), only baseline characteristics prior to any randomization or intervention were extracted to avoid bias from treatment effects. For instance, in the landmark RCT by Thwaites et al (Thwaites et al., 2004). evaluating dexamethasone therapy in TBM, we included only pre-intervention baseline data for analysis

Studies were excluded if they: (World Health Organization, 2017) did not clearly define TBM diagnostic criteria; (World Health Organization, 2024) were case reports, reviews, conference abstracts, or animal studies; or (Brancusi et al., 2012) assessed nerve palsy from causes unrelated to TBM (e.g., drug side effects, co-infections).

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form. Extracted variables included bibliographic information, study design, country of origin, sample size, incidence of CNP and ONP, and reported clinical and laboratory risk factors. When specific variables were unavailable, they were coded as missing (“NA”). Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists appropriate to each study design (Munn et al., 2020). For each checklist, items were scored as Yes (World Health Organization, 2017), No/Unclear (0), and Not applicable (excluded from the denominator). We reported a “quality score” in Table 1 as the number of Yes items over the total number of applicable items. All statistical analyses were performed in R 4.5.1 using the meta and metafor packages. Because primary studies seldom stratified by CNP/ONP status, we conducted one-arm, study-level meta-analyses. Proportions (CNP, ONP, and dichotomous/continuous risk factors) were synthesized on the logit scale using the PLO measure, a random-effects framework with Paule–Mandel τ² estimation, Hartung–Knapp adjustment for random-effects confidence intervals, and a 0.5 continuity correction applied to all studies. Continuous variables (for example CSF indices, GCS, age, length of stay) were pooled as random-effects meta-means. We quantified heterogeneity with I², τ², and Cochran’s Q, and where informative we reported 95 % prediction intervals to reflect dispersion expected in new settings. A priori subgroup analyses contrasted publication period (≤2000, 2001–2010, 2011–2020, 2021–2025) and WHO region (SEARO, WPRO, EURO, EMRO). In meta-regression with WHO region as a categorical moderator, we used EMRO as the reference category (intercept) so that coefficients represent contrasts versus EMRO. Univariable meta-regression evaluated predefined clinical and radiologic moderators on the logit scale; continuous moderators were z-standardized, and multiplicity was addressed with Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate q-values. Given limited data availability for ONP, multivariable meta-regression was fitted only for CNP using prespecified severity markers (hydrocephalus, infarction, stage III). A parsimonious multivariable model retained prespecified severity markers (hydrocephalus, infarction, stage III) and, in separate specifications where power allowed, categorical contrasts for period and region. Small-study effects were explored with funnel plots plus Begg’s rank-correlation tests computed from PLO effects and variances, interpreted cautiously given high heterogeneity typical of prevalence synthesis. Robustness was examined with leave-one-out analyses (classic forest style) and Baujat diagnostics.

In this study, we examined three databases and identified 1498 articles: 1361 from Web of Science, 127 from PubMed, and 10 from Google Scholar. After eliminating the duplicate papers, 508 items remained; then, we reviewed the titles and abstracts, reducing the total to 82 articles. Ultimately, 47 publications were incorporated into the meta-analysis. There were 19 articles from China, 7 from Pakistan, 4 from Romania, 3 from Turkey, and 2 from Vietnam. Additionally, Greece, Qatar, Nepal, New Zealand, Indonesia, Iran, South Korea, and Taiwan each had 1 item. The publication year ranged from 1977 to 2025. The sample size ranged from 721 to 26. Females' percentages ranged between 18.8 % and 62.82 %. Risk-factor prevalence highlighted substantial disease burden at presentation. Approximately 36 % had hydrocephalus and 23 % had cerebral infarction; altered sensorium approached 50 %, and about 45 % were stage III at diagnosis. Other notable features included tuberculoma and seizures (each ≈ 22–23 %). Continuous markers were also deranged on average (e.g., elevated CSF protein), consistent with intense meningeal inflammation (Table 2). Heterogeneity was high for most estimates, emphasizing variability in recruitment periods, diagnostic thresholds, and imaging practices.

Time trends suggested lower CNP incidence in more recent years, declining from 56.5 % (≤ 2000) to 19.0 % (2021–2025); the omnibus test was significant (p = 0.0057). In contrast, ONP did not vary meaningfully by period (omnibus p = 0.969) (Table 3). Regional patterns were evident: CNP was highest in SEARO (34.3 %) and lower in WPRO and EURO, with a significant overall difference (p = 0.0113). ONP showed a similar geographic gradient, with higher pooled incidence in SEARO than WPRO (p = 0.014). Univariable models (logit scale) identified hydrocephalus as the most consistent correlate of both outcomes. For CNP, hydrocephalus (β ≈ 0.39; OR-like ≈ 1.47), basal meningeal exudates/enhancement (β ≈ 0.41; OR-like ≈ 1.51), cerebral infarction (β ≈ 0.32; OR-like ≈ 1.38), and CSF protein (β ≈ 0.30; OR-like ≈ 1.34) were significant; papilledema trended positive. Given data constraints and to preserve power, we fitted a parsimonious multivariable meta-regression model for CNP incidence (dependent variable: study-level logit proportion of CNP). The model retained a base set of prespecified severity markers (hydrocephalus, infarction, stage III). In this specification, coefficients attenuated and did not reach conventional significance (e.g., hydrocephalus β ≈ 0.33, p = 0.055), suggesting collinearity among severity markers and limited degrees of freedom rather than absence of effect. Models that also included time period and region were underpowered. These results emphasize that univariable signals should be interpreted as study-level associations rather than independent causal effects.

Visual inspection of funnel plots showed typical dispersion for prevalence meta-analyses with high heterogeneity. Begg’s rank-correlation tests were non-significant for both outcomes (e.g., CNP p = 0.279; ONP p = 0.501), providing no strong evidence of small-study effects, although asymmetry cannot be excluded (Figures S1–S2). Given the one-arm design and heterogeneity, these tests are interpreted cautiously.

Leave-one-out analyses showed no single study unduly shifted the pooled incidence for either CNP or ONP (Figures S3–S4). Baujat diagnostics identified a handful of influential studies contributing to heterogeneity, but their removal did not alter the overall conclusions (Figures S5–S6). Together with wide prediction intervals, these checks indicate that results are robust at the summary level yet context-dependent across settings.

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that cranial nerve palsies (CNPs) and ophthalmic nerve palsies (ONPs) are common complications of tuberculous meningitis (TBM). The pooled incidence of CNP was 26.5 % (Fig. 1; Table 2) and the pooled incidence of ONP was 23.9 % (Fig. 2; Table 2), with substantial heterogeneity across studies. In univariable meta-regression, hydrocephalus, cerebral infarction, and higher CSF protein were associated with higher CNP incidence (Table 5), and hydrocephalus was associated with higher ONP incidence (Table 5). Because the analyses are based on aggregated study-level data and substantial methodological heterogeneity, these associations should be interpreted cautiously. The existence of CNP indicates that the presence of nerve involvement is not a passive bystander effect but instead due to a complex interaction among multiple pathogenic processes that involve disruption of normal mechanical physiology and the impact of vascular ischemia on the blood supply to the nerves and the inflammatory changes that occur within the arachnoid of the brain.

Hydrocephalus was the most consistent study-level correlate of both CNP and ONP in univariable meta-regression (Table 5), and it remained borderline in the parsimonious multivariable model for CNP (Table 6). The pathophysiological mechanism is likely due to mechanical distortion of the brainstem and stretching of cranial nerves resulting from elevated intracranial pressure and ventricular system expansion (Randeni et al., 2025, Arpa et al., 2024). The abducens nerve (CN VI), which has a long course through the head, and the optic chiasm, which is located just below the expanding third ventricle, are especially susceptible to this mass effect caused by hydraulic pressure (Grisold et al., 2023). Our data also suggest that cerebral infarction is significantly associated with CNP (Table 5), whereas the corresponding association for ONP was not statistically significant. Therefore, an ischemia-related mechanism must be responsible for the association between infarction and CNP. TBM-related vasculitis damages perforating arteries that supply blood to the lower brainstem, leading to brainstem infarctions that may affect cranial nerve nuclei (Wilkhoo et al., 2025). The association between elevated CSF protein levels and the basal meningeal exudate indicates that basal meningeal exudates play an essential role in the formation of the multi-layer thick exudate located in the pontine and interpeduncular subarachnoid spaces (Table 5). That accumulation of inflammatory agents, combined with the localized inflammatory response, creates an environment in which nerve compression occurs and the blood-nerve barrier is disrupted (Skaper, 2017). This envelopment of cranial nerves results in compressive neuropathy, a form of nerve injury (Lim et al., 2014). In addition, given that elevated CSF protein remains a significant predictor of nerve injury, even after adjustment for other variables, we hypothesize that localized inflammatory responses contribute to nerve injury independent of gross structural lesions.

Across subgroups, we observed variation in incidence by publication era and WHO region (Table 3). CNP incidence declined across eras, whereas ONP did not show a clear temporal trend (Table 3, Table 4). Differences over time may reflect changes in case mix, earlier diagnosis and treatment, and evolving diagnostic practices. At the same time, improved access to high-resolution MRI after 2010 may increase detection of subtle cranial nerve enhancement and small infarcts, potentially contributing to apparent differences across eras (Wangaryattawanich et al., 2023).

In a large cohort of individuals with TBM from China, Wen et al. found a prevalence of CNP at 33.3 %, this prevalence is much greater than our pooled estimate (Table 2) (Wen et al., 2023). This difference in the two studies is likely due to a more severe baseline presentation of disease and the use of broader clinical criteria to define cranial neuropathy in their study compared to our synthesis. An Indonesian cohort that reported a CNP prevalence of 24.3 % was close to our overall estimate. The Indonesian cohort found a significant correlation between CNP and lymphocytic pleocytosis in CSF, indicating a possible immunologic component of nerve injury beyond structural factors (Al Adawiyah and Sugianto, n.d.). Sriviruthi et al. focused primarily on visual loss and optochiasmatic involvement, and their more limited focus on neuro‑ophthalmic manifestations and their stricter outcome definitions likely explain the lower overall CNP rate that they found relative to our synthesis (Sriviruthi B et al.).

Several other studies support this view that structural complications are a significant factor for the increased burden of cranial nerves in Tuberculous Meningitis. Selvaraj et al., found that the clustering of cranial nerve palsies with radiological findings of hydrocephalus and infarction further emphasized that basal exudate, high intracranial pressure, and ischemia have a dominant role in the pathophysiology of nerve paralysis (Selvaraj et al., 2020). Huang et al., demonstrated that cranial nerve infarction in TBM patients is associated with vascular inflammation and male sex, which indicates that there may be some variation in host-vascular factors as an individual risk factor for ischemic insult to brainstem nuclei and vasa nervorum (Huang et al., 2024). While we did not evaluate sex as a modifying variable, their observations coincide with the idea that inflammation of the vascular bed is the predominant pathway that connects severe meningeal inflammation and cranial neuropathy; however, on the other hand, O′Halloran. reported a 19 % incidence of cranial nerve palsy, and new cranial nerve deficits often occurred during paradoxical worsening. These data suggest that the time of assessment and the body’s response to immune-mediated treatment affect the incidence and pattern of cranial nerve paralysis seen with TBM (O'Halloran, 2025).

A major finding of this meta-analysis is the very high between-study variability (I² often >80 %; Table 2). This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in TBM case definitions, timing of outcome assessment, neuroimaging availability, and how cranial versus ophthalmic nerve palsies were defined and reported. Meta-regression identified study-level associations, but these should not be interpreted as causal or patient-level independent effects (ecological inference). Hydrocephalus and infarction may develop because of TBM. Still, they may represent how severe the disease is rather than being the only reasons why someone may develop TBM-related nerve damage.

In summary, this systematic review and meta-analysis found that cranial and ophthalmic nerve palsies were frequent complications of TBM (Fig. 1, Fig. 2; Table 2). Hydrocephalus showed the most consistent association with both outcomes (Table 5), while cerebral infarction and higher CSF protein were additionally associated with CNP at the study level (Table 5). Clinically, the presence of CNP should prompt consideration of severe basal meningitis and increased stroke risk, supporting early neuroimaging and timely management of raised intracranial pressure. Future prospective studies using standardized definitions and time points are needed to quantify patient-level risk and to evaluate interventions that may prevent long-term neuropathic sequelae.

Several biases may influence the pooled estimates and regional comparisons. First, detection bias is likely because studies varied in imaging modality and frequency, and later cohorts may have had better access to MRI and neuro-ophthalmic assessment. Second, referral bias is probable because many studies were conducted in tertiary centers, where patients may present with more advanced disease and complications. Third, because analyses relied on aggregated data, residual confounding and ecological fallacy are possible. Finally, the high heterogeneity indicates that pooled incidence should not be interpreted as an absolute clinical benchmark, but rather as a summary of diverse settings shaped by methodological and health-system factors; therefore, variability should be interpreted cautiously.

The study found that cranial and eye nerve palsies are common in TB meningitis patients due to three main risk factors: hydrocephalus, cerebral ischemia, and intense meningeal inflammation. We suggest a combined mechanism in which nerves are affected by both mechanical compression from hydrocephalus and by inflammatory or ischemic processes, such as vasculitis and exudates. Clinically, detecting CNP should alert practitioners to the possibility of severe basal meningitis and an increased risk of stroke. Based on these results, promptly managing increased intracranial pressure and using neuroimaging to identify high-risk individuals is crucial. Future research should include long-term studies using standardized criteria to determine whether targeted host therapies can reduce inflammatory nerve damage.
