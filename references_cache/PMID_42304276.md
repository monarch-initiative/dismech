# The intervention effects of resistance exercise on sarcopenia in older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
**Authors:** Li GQ, Tang SY, Luo J, Shu MY, Peng PX, Zhang XC, Liang J
**Journal:** BMC Geriatr (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12877-026-07808-w](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07808-w)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The accelerating trend of population aging has increased the prevalence of sarcopenia in older adults, which not only impairs quality of life but also places a heavy medical and economic burden on families and society.

METHODS: This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of resistance exercise on muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical function in older adults with sarcopenia, and to explore the optimal exercise regimen for this population. Literature was retrieved from Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and CNKI, with the retrieval period ranging from database inception to 31 May 2026. Eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs) focusing on resistance exercise interventions for sarcopenia in older adults were included. A total of 13 RCTs involving 546 participants (282 in the intervention group and 264 in the control group) were finally enrolled.

RESULTS: Meta-analysis showed that resistance exercise significantly improved handgrip strength, gait speed, appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI), and five-times sit-to-stand performance in older adults with sarcopenia. The pooled mean differences (MD) were 2.95 kg for handgrip strength, 0.15 m/s for gait speed, 0.25 kg/m² for ASMI, and -1.79 s for the five-times sit-to-stand test (all P<0.0001). Subgroup analysis of gait speed indicated that shorter intervention cycles (≤12 weeks), younger age (≤75 years), and a weekly training frequency of three sessions yielded larger effect sizes with higher heterogeneity; conversely, longer cycles (>12 weeks), older age (>75 years), and non-three-session weekly frequency presented lower heterogeneity. High-heterogeneity subgroups were the main source of overall heterogeneity.

CONCLUSIONS: Resistance exercise exerts significant beneficial effects on handgrip strength, ASMI, gait speed, and physical performance, as assessed by the five-times sit-to-stand test, in older adults with sarcopenia.

Sarcopenia is a progressive, systemic skeletal muscle disorder characterized by accelerated loss of muscle mass and function, associated with adverse outcomes such as falls, functional decline, frailty, and increased mortality [1]. It can be classified into two types: primary sarcopenia, caused by age-related factors, and secondary sarcopenia, resulting from other conditions such as cancer and cardiovascular diseases [2]. With advancing research, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) and the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) updated their consensus in 2019 and 2025, respectively, specifying that sarcopenia diagnosis requires meeting the dual criteria of “low muscle mass + low muscle function (strength or physical performance)”. The condition progresses through three stages: pre-sarcopenia, sarcopenia, and severe sarcopenia [3, 4]. Globally, 10%–27% of older adults are affected by sarcopenia. Currently, no approved pharmacological treatments exist for sarcopenia. Given the inevitability of aging and the growing population affected by sarcopenia, implementing appropriate interventions is crucial to alleviate the disease burden [5, 6].

Interventions for sarcopenia primarily focus on exercise and nutrition. Several studies have investigated the effects of exercise combined with nutrition in the management of sarcopenia, and systematic reviews have confirmed that exercise alone improves physical function, with or without nutritional supplementation [7, 8]. Among various exercise modalities, resistance exercise has been identified as the most effective intervention for increasing muscle mass and strength [9]. The biological mechanism involves mechanical loading activating signaling pathways, such as mTOR, thereby promoting muscle protein synthesis and enhancing neuromuscular recruitment efficiency and coordination, thereby improving muscle mass and function in a synchronous manner [10]. Consequently, authoritative bodies such as the American College of Sports Medicine recommend progressive resistance exercise as a primary strategy for maintaining and improving musculoskeletal health in adults [11]. Although the effectiveness of resistance exercise in preventing and treating sarcopenia is widely acknowledged [12], notable inconsistencies persist in the optimization and implementation of exercise prescriptions across scientific research and clinical practice. For instance, RCTs included vary widely in exercise frequency, duration, intensity, and other parameters, which directly contributes to high between-study heterogeneity.

To address these gaps, this meta-analysis synthesized evidence from RCTs examining the effects of resistance exercise on sarcopenia in older adults to achieve the following objectives: (1) quantitatively and comprehensively evaluate the effects of resistance training on the three core domains of sarcopenia; (2) perform prespecified subgroup analyses to explore potential factors influencing intervention effects and explain sources of heterogeneity; (3) integrate and derive a specific, evidence-based, and practical resistance exercise program framework based on overall effect sizes and subgroup findings, to provide direct evidence for improving muscle health and developing precise exercise prescriptions for older adults.

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The PRISMA 2020 checklist is shown in Table S1. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (Registration No.: CRD420261290429).

This study conducted a comprehensive literature search in the Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and CNKI databases from inception to 31 May 2026. The search strategy combined the following keywords: “resistance exercise/training”, “strength exercise/training”, “weight exercise/training”, “weight-bearing exercise”, “older adults”, “senior”, “sarcopenia”, “muscle loss”, “age-related muscle loss”, and “randomized controlled trial”.The literature screening process was independently conducted by two reviewers(G-Q. L and X-C. Z). First, duplicate records were removed using EndNote 20. Then, titles and abstracts of the remaining records were screened to exclude obviously irrelevant studies. Subsequently, full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and reviewed against the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third senior reviewer (J. L). No automated screening tools were used in this process. The detailed search strategies in English and Chinese are presented in Table S2.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating resistance exercise interventions for sarcopenia in older adults were included.

Participants were diagnosed with sarcopenia, aged ≥ 60 years, with no restrictions on nationality or ethnicity, and free of psychiatric disorders, major organic diseases, or conditions precluding physical exercise.

The intervention group underwent resistance exercise (using elastic bands, dumbbells, weight machines, or bodyweight exercises). The control group maintained usual activities without structured exercise.

Outcome indicators reflecting physiological changes in older adults with sarcopenia included grip strength, gait speed, appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI), and five-times sit-to-stand performance.

Studies were excluded according to the following criteria: (1) non-randomized controlled studies; (2) animal or in vitro experiments; (3) review articles, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses; (4) studies not enrolling older adults as the target population; (5) studies involving combined, multimodal, or co-interventions in the experimental group that prevented isolation of resistance exercise effects; (6) studies for which full text was unavailable; and (7) duplicate publications or studies with insufficient methodological quality, including high risk of bias, inadequate outcome reporting, or incomplete data.

Two researchers (G-Q. L and X-C. Z) independently extracted data using a double-masked method. Discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher (J. L). Extracted information included author, publication year, participant characteristics, sample size, sex, age, intervention details, and outcomes.

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (version 5.1.0) was used to assess methodological quality. Two researchers independently evaluated the following domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases. Judgments were rated as “low risk”, “unclear risk”, or “high risk”. Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed independently by two reviewers using standardized assessment tools.

In the meta-analysis, the mean (M) and standard deviation (SD) were extracted for each outcome from included studies. When outcomes were reported as standard errors (SE) or interquartile ranges (IQR), values were converted to SDs. ASMI was converted using standard SMI/ASMM formulas. RevMan 5.4.1 software was used to perform pooled effect size analysis, heterogeneity testing, sensitivity analysis, and to generate forest plots. Because all outcome indicators were continuous variables, the effect measure was calculated as the mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence interval (CI). This study adopted MD as the effect size indicator. Since MD retains the original units of measurement, MD values across different outcome indicators cannot be directly compared, so no unified grading threshold was set. Statistical significance was determined by whether the 95% confidence interval of the pooled effect size crossed the null value. The clinical implications were interpreted using the minimally clinically important difference, widely recognized in this field, and findings from previous similar meta-analyses. The I² statistic was used to assess heterogeneity among studies. When I²< 50%, indicating acceptable consistency across studies, the Mantel-Haenszel (M-H) fixed-effect model was applied for pooled analysis. When I² > 50%, suggesting substantial heterogeneity, the DerSimonian-Laird (D-L) random-effects model was used instead. Subgroup analyses were then performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. To assess the risk of reporting bias, funnel plots were used for visual inspection.

This systematic review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261290429). The full review protocol is publicly available online. No independent protocol manuscript was prepared, and no amendments were made during the review process.

A total of 1583 relevant articles were retrieved from CNKI, PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases. After removing 440 duplicates, 1143 unique records remained. A further 1058 articles were excluded during title and abstract screening, leaving 85 potentially eligible articles. Full-text screening was then performed to evaluate intervention protocols, study groups, and outcome measures. Finally, 13 studies were included in the meta-analysis, as shown in Fig. 1.

Additionally, one of the 13 included studies subdivided its intervention arm into a strength-training subgroup and a combined-training subgroup [18]; only data from the strength-training subgroup and the control group were extracted for analysis. Another study compared two resistance-exercise arms with respect to training frequency and session duration [28]. Baseline and post-intervention assessments were performed for all primary outcomes, with clear descriptions of measurement methods provided in each study.

All intervention groups followed structured resistance exercise protocols, whereas control groups received no formal exercise intervention or engaged only in routine daily activity without supervised training. All included studies reported complete intervention parameters, including intervention duration, weekly frequency, exercise intensity, and single-session duration. The intervention period ranged from 8 to 32 weeks. Training intensity was classified as moderate or submoderate. Single exercise sessions lasted 30 to 60 min, with a weekly training frequency of 2 to 4 sessions.

The risk of bias across the 13 included RCTs was evaluated using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias Tool. The distribution of bias across each domain is shown in Fig. 2. The detailed risk-of-bias judgments for each RCT are shown in Fig. 3.

The results revealed obvious variability in bias risk across domains. For selection bias, 12 studies clearly described methods for random sequence generation and were rated as low risk; only one study was judged to be at unclear risk due to insufficient reporting of randomization procedures. For allocation concealment, six studies adopted adequate measures such as central randomization or sealed envelopes and were classified as low risk. The remaining seven studies did not report allocation concealment protocols and were rated as unclear risk.

Performance bias constituted the primary source of high bias risk. Only three studies achieved effective blinding of participants and personnel and were categorized as low risk; one study was rated as unclear risk, while nine studies were judged as high risk, as blinding was not feasible given the nature of resistance exercise interventions.

For detection bias, 12 studies clearly reported blinding of outcome assessors and were rated low risk, with only one study graded as unclear risk. Regarding attrition bias, reporting bias, and other potential biases: all studies had no incomplete outcome data or additional confounding factors. One study was rated as having an unclear risk of selective reporting, while the rest were rated as having a low risk in this domain. Overall, the included studies presented acceptable methodological quality.

In summary, the included studies demonstrated good quality regarding outcome integrity and reporting standards. High performance bias, risk, and unclear allocation concealment were the main methodological limitations of the present meta-analysis.

A total of 11 studies investigating the effect of resistance exercise on handgrip strength in older adults with sarcopenia were included. The effective sample size comprised 232 participants in the intervention group and 225 in the control group. The results are shown in Fig. 4. The heterogeneity test yielded I²=33%, P=0.13, indicating low statistical heterogeneity across studies; thus, a fixed-effect model was adopted for data pooling. The overall pooled effect size was MD=2.95, 95% CI:[2.18, 3.73], P<0.00001, indicating that resistance exercise effectively improved handgrip strength compared with the control group in older adults with sarcopenia.

In this study, the heterogeneity of the gait speed outcome (I² = 73%) was high. To explore the source of heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was performed. The effects of resistance exercise on gait speed in older adult patients with sarcopenia may be limited by age group, exercise cycle, frequency, duration, intensity, and training methods. By combining the basic characteristics of the included studies, this paper found no significant differences in exercise intensity, exercise time, training methods, or other aspects of the data. Therefore, the following subgroups were set for data analysis: long cycle subgroup (> 12 weeks) and short cycle subgroup (≤ 12 weeks), younger subgroup (≤ 75 years old) and older subgroup (> 75 years old), and exercise frequency 3 times a week subgroup and exercise frequency non-3 times a week subgroup. A total of 11 studies were included to assess the effects of resistance exercise on gait speed among older adults with sarcopenia. Using the random-effects model, the pooled mean difference was 0.15 m/s (95% CI: [0.09, 0.22]), P < 0.0001. Subgroup difference tests demonstrated significant disparities between subgroups based on exercise frequency and age (P = 0.04, I²=77.4% and P = 0.02, I²=82.4%, respectively), whereas intervention duration did not lead to a significant between-subgroup difference (P = 0.32, I²=0.1%). After grouping by intervention period, the effect size for the subgroup with a long intervention period (> 12 weeks) was MD = 0.11, 95% CI: [0.01, 0.21], P = 0. 003, and the heterogeneity was I²=41%, P = 0.16. The effect size in the subgroup of short cycles (≤ 12 weeks) was MD = 0.18, 95% CI: [0.08, 0.28], P = 0.0002, and heterogeneity remained high I²=82%, P < 0.0001. When stratified by age, MD = 0.19, 95%CI: [0.11,0.28], P < 0.0001, and heterogeneity was I²=71%, P = 0.001 in the younger subgroup (≤ 75 years old). The effect size in the older adults (> 75 years) subgroup was MD = 0.08, 95%CI: [0.03,0.12], P = 0.0003, and the heterogeneity was reduced to I²=0%, P = 0.68. When stratified by weekly exercise frequency, the effect of gait speed improvement was more pronounced in the subgroup of participants who exercised 3 times per week (MD = 0.19, 95% CI: [0.10, 0.28], P < 0.0001). Still, substantial heterogeneity remained (I²=79%, P < 0.0001). The effect size for the subgroup not three times per week was MD = 0.06, 95% CI: [-0.01, 0.14], P = 0.09, and heterogeneity was significantly reduced (I²=0%, P = 0.72).

Subgroup analysis showed that exercise frequency per week and age group were the main sources of heterogeneity in this meta-analysis. The heterogeneity among studies was significantly reduced in the exercise frequency non-3 times a week subgroup and among older adults (> 75 years old). The intervention period had little effect on the heterogeneity. Although the step-speed improvement effect of short-period intervention was more pronounced, heterogeneity among studies did not improve.

To further explore the sources of heterogeneity in the effects of resistance exercise and to provide data to support the derivation and development of exercise programs, this study performed subgroup analyses of grip strength and ASMI based on the gait speed subgroup analysis. Due to the small number of included studies involving the five-times sit-to-stand test, a subgroup analysis was not performed. Overall, resistance exercise significantly improved handgrip strength MD = 2.95, 95%CI: [2.18,3.73], P<0.00001, I²=33% and ASMI MD = 0.25, 95%CI: [0.16,0.34], P<0.00001, I²=40%. Participants aged ≤ 75 years exhibited more stable improvements in both indicators, whereas high heterogeneity (79%) was observed in ASMI among those aged >75 years, with significant between‑subgroup differences (P = 0.0007), indicating considerable individual variation in muscle mass improvement in older adults. Short‑term interventions produced stable and significant improvements in both outcomes, while long‑term interventions significantly improved handgrip strength with high heterogeneity but showed no significant effect on ASMI, with significant subgroup differences for handgrip strength, P = 0.005. Training 3 times per week yielded larger effect sizes for both indicators, suggesting it may be the optimal frequency despite non‑significant between‑subgroup differences. The results of subgroup analyses were as follows (Tables S4 and S5).

To assess whether the high heterogeneity was due to a single study, this study conducted a sensitivity analysis of the gait speed outcome indicators with high heterogeneity. Each study was sequentially excluded to recalculate the combined effect size and heterogeneity and to evaluate the stability of the intervention effect of resistance exercise on gait speed in older adults with sarcopenia. The results showed (Table S6) that after excluding any single study, the range of the combined mean difference (MD) of the gait speed indicators was 0.13–0.17, the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval was all greater than 0, and the P value of the combined effect size was all < 0.05, indicating that the direction of the effect size of resistance exercise on gait speed in older adults did not reverse, and the overall stability of the meta-analysis results was good. The heterogeneity analysis showed that, except for the I² value dropping from 73% to 54% after excluding the study by Liao et al. [21]. The I² value fluctuated between 70% and 76% after excluding other studies, with no significant change, indicating that this study was the main source of heterogeneity among the gait speed indicators.

We assessed the certainty of evidence using the GRADE approach (Table S7). Evidence for handgrip strength, ASMI, and the five-times sit-to-stand test was rated as moderate-certainty evidence. Evidence for gait speed was graded as low certainty, downgraded owing to high heterogeneity, potential publication bias, and performance bias. Overall, moderate-certainty evidence supports that resistance exercise improves sarcopenia-related outcomes in older adults.

This study systematically integrated data from 546 participants across 13 RCTs to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the effects of resistance exercise on sarcopenia interventions in older adults. The meta-analysis results showed that compared with traditional care, health education, or low-intensity activities, systematic resistance training could significantly improve the three core dimensions of older adults with sarcopenia. This result provides strong evidence supporting resistance exercise as a non-pharmacological intervention for sarcopenia [29, 30]. However, the effect size for improving walking speed showed relatively high heterogeneity (I² = 73%), whereas the heterogeneity for grip strength and ASMI was relatively low (I² = 33% and 40%, respectively). The results suggest that although resistance exercise has an overall beneficial effect on various indicators, its specific effects may be influenced by other factors. To further explore the sources of heterogeneity in walking speed and to formulate a scientifically sound and safe exercise plan, we conducted a prespecified subgroup analysis. The results showed that age grouping and training frequency were the key sources of heterogeneity in walking speed. Specifically, the intervention had a greater effect on improving walking speed in the younger group than in the older group; a training frequency of 3 times per week showed a trend toward superiority over a non-3-times/week frequency. This finding elevated the study’s value from confirming effectiveness to identifying optimization strategies [31].

Gait speed is a core indicator of physical function, and its improvement is affected by muscle status, neuromuscular control, balance, joint flexibility, and exercise confidence [32]. Given the multiple factors influencing gait speed, we further conducted a subgroup analysis by intervention duration. The results showed I²=41% and P = 0.16 for the long-duration intervention, and I²=82% and P < 0.0001 for the short-duration intervention. Individual differences can easily influence the effect of short-term interventions during the “exercise adaptation period”. In contrast, long-term intervention can help stabilize the patient’s gait speed, and heterogeneity among studies is low. The necessity of long-term intervention is that, on the one hand, it provides a window for the patient’s adaptation; on the other hand, muscle growth requires long-term stimulation of muscle fibers to achieve increased fiber thickness and an increase in fiber number. Short-term training can induce neural adaptations and initial gains in muscle strength. Still, continuous training is needed to optimize complex gait patterns, improve energy utilization efficiency, and reduce the risk of joint pain [33]. A 24-week study showed that continuous improvement in gait speed was highly correlated with improvements in lower-limb muscle power, and these fine adaptations occurred during the middle and late stages of training [34].

The advantage of exercising 3 times/week may be related to the theories of “motor skill learning” and “metabolic adaptation”. More frequent training increases the likelihood of repetition and helps the central nervous system consolidate more efficient, automated movement patterns [35]. In addition, from the perspective of muscle metabolism, training three times a week can more frequently induce muscle protein synthesis to peak, thereby improving mitochondrial function and capillary density in muscle and enhancing resistance to muscle fatigue, both of which are essential for maintaining stable and continuous walking [36]. In contrast, twice-weekly training stimulates anabolism but is underpowered to drive complex remodeling of neuromuscular function.

The improvement in gait speed in the older adult group is lower than in the younger group, reflecting the physiological characteristics of the older adult population. The phenomenon of “synthetic resistance” is exacerbated with age; that is, the sensitivity of muscle tissue to anabolic signals decreases, leading to a weaker training-induced muscle hypertrophy response [37]. Older people often have more comorbidities, higher levels of inflammation, and inadequate nutritional intake, which can offset or weaken the benefits of training [38]. Baseline functional status and fear of falling will also affect their training compliance and effort level [39]. Therefore, exercise prescription for older adult patients with sarcopenia needs to pay attention not only to training parameters but also to integrating multi-dimensional strategies such as nutritional support, pain management, and psychological motivation to ensure that exercise prescription plays its role under the premise of safety.

Regarding whether resistance exercise can improve the core gait-speed index in older adults with sarcopenia, previous reviews have suggested that the improvement effect of resistance training on gait speed is limited or inconsistent [40]. This inconsistency may be due to differences in the protocol parameters across the included studies. Subgroup analysis showed that the intervention effect was stronger in the younger age group, short-cycle group, and 3-times-a-week exercise frequency group, but heterogeneity within these subgroups was high. However, the intervention effect was weak in the older adults group, the long-cycle group, and in those exercising less than 3 times per week, but heterogeneity within subgroups was low. The mixing of the two subgroups led to uncertainty in the pooled results and increased overall heterogeneity. The results showed that resistance training clearly and significantly improved gait speed under the optimized parameters. Future researchers and clinicians are reminded that the specific details of intervention programs must be fully considered when interpreting and applying the evidence.

This study confirmed that resistance training effectively improves handgrip strength, gait speed, ASMI, and five-times sit-to-stand performance in older adults with sarcopenia. Overall, the findings are comparable to most existing evidence, while some heterogeneity in outcomes remains across studies for each indicator.

In terms of handgrip strength improvement, Liu et al. [41] included 20 RCTs in their review and verified the positive intervention effect of resistance training on handgrip strength in older adults. Zhao et al. [42] also reported significant improvements in handgrip strength in the exercise group compared with the control group. These findings are generally consistent with the present study’s results and further indicate that resistance training has a clear beneficial effect on handgrip strength in sarcopenic older adults.

For the gait speed outcome, this meta-analysis demonstrated that resistance training significantly enhances gait speed in older adults with sarcopenia, and similar positive findings were reported by Villareal et al. [43]. Inconsistent findings on gait speed improvements following resistance training in sarcopenic older adults have been documented. A meta-analysis by Cheng, Fang et al. reported non-significant improvements in gait speed, with high heterogeneity (I²=97%) [44]. Similarly, Yan, Ruixiang et al. found that although resistance training significantly improved gait speed, the effect did not exceed the minimal important difference threshold (0.10 m/s), and meta-regression failed to identify significant sources of heterogeneity [45]. This phenomenon is consistent with the high heterogeneity in gait speed observed in the present study, suggesting that more high-quality, standardized trials are still required for further verification and interpretation.

In terms of physical function, the meta-analysis conducted by Liao et al. [46] found that resistance training alone, even without combined nutritional supplementation, can significantly improve muscle strength and physical function in older adults. Similarly, this study confirmed that resistance training remarkably improves five-times sit-to-stand performance. Shen et al. [6] also reported comparable positive effects, indicating that resistance training plays a fundamental role in managing physical function in older adults with sarcopenia.

This study not only systematically evaluated the overall intervention effect of resistance training on sarcopenia in older adults but also further explored the dose–response characteristics of exercise parameters through subgroup analysis. Most previous systematic reviews have focused primarily on assessing the overall effectiveness of interventions, with insufficient discussion of the regulatory effects of key parameters, such as intervention duration and weekly training frequency [9]. Existing studies have predominantly concentrated on training intensity. For instance, Schoenfeld et al. reported that high-intensity resistance training leads to greater muscle hypertrophy [47]. Nevertheless, there is no consensus on the optimal protocols for intervention duration and weekly training frequency.

Based on the subgroup analysis results, this study proposed the research hypothesis that short-term intervention combined with three training sessions per week is conducive to achieving prominent improvements in physical function among older adults. Although Binder et al. observed improvements in gait speed and physical function in long-term intervention protocols and emphasized the importance of long-term training adherence [48], more short-term studies have indicated that stable improvements in physical function tend to occur under the regular frequency of three sessions per week; insufficient training frequency only leads to gains in muscle strength rather than physical function [49]. By synthesizing population-based evidence, this study provides more generalizable guidance for selecting appropriate exercise parameters.

The essence of sarcopenia is a homeostatic imbalance caused by decreased anabolism and enhanced catabolism. As an effective form of mechanical stimulation, resistance training can improve this imbalance process at multiple levels and targets [29].

Strength improvement starts with enhancing neural adaptation, the rapid response phase of exercise intervention. During the first 4–8 weeks after the start of training, the muscle’s morphological changes are not yet apparent, but the nervous system has undergone efficient reorganization. It is mainly manifested as motor unit recruitment optimization, in which high-threshold, large-α motor units are more fully mobilized. At the same time, the firing frequency of motor units increases, the synchrony of firing is enhanced, and the unnecessary activation of antagonistic muscle groups is reduced [50, 51]. Enhanced activation and synergies within the nervous system preceded improvements in muscle mass and strength, explaining the significant gains in strength measures, such as grip strength, observed in our study over a relatively short intervention period. For older adults whose neuromuscular function has already declined, this efficient neuroadaptation has important functional compensatory significance [52].

Secondly, the increase in muscle mass depends on remodeling of protein metabolism, which requires that the intervention period be set to allow sufficient time for this mechanism. Integrins and growth factor receptors sense the mechanical load imposed by resistance training on the muscle cell membrane, thereby initiating key intracellular signal transduction [53]. The mechanical load imposed by resistance exercise increases the rate of protein synthesis by activating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathways, accompanied by satellite cell activation and fusion. It ultimately increases muscle fiber cross-sectional area [54, 55] (Fig. 15). This process typically took 8–12 weeks for the body composition measurement tool to detect significant changes, consistent with our observation that the effect size for ASMI was slightly smaller than that for grip strength.

At the same time, the activation and mobilization of satellite cells are essential for muscle fiber repair, maintenance, and hypertrophy. Sarcopenia is often accompanied by a decrease in the number and function of satellite cells [56]. Resistance training activates satellite cells in the resting state through mechanical stretch and by inducing the local release of insulin-like growth factor-1 and fibroblast growth factor [57]. These activated muscle stem cells proliferate, differentiate into myoblasts, and then fuse with existing muscle fibers by expressing specific adhesion molecules. This process plays a dual role: it provides new muscle nuclei for enlarged muscle fibers, supporting the gene transcription and protein synthesis requirements of a larger cytoplasmic volume [58]. It can produce new myofibrils and repair microdamage caused by training, thereby ensuring the structural integrity and functional plasticity of muscle fibers [59]. The successful activation of satellite cells is the decisive factor in whether resistance exercise can induce high-quality, functional muscle hypertrophy.

In conclusion, the effect of resistance exercise on sarcopenia in older adults is a multistage, multilevel process. It starts with rapid nervous system compensation, followed by remodeling of protein metabolism driven by molecular signals and muscle fiber remodeling mediated by satellite cells. Finally, it manifests as an overall improvement in muscle mass, strength, and physical function [60]. This systematic physiological framework not only provides an internal explanation for the results of this meta-analysis but also lays a theoretical foundation for developing accurate and effective resistance training programs for older adults.

To evaluate the clinical significance of resistance exercise interventions in older adults with sarcopenia, we compared the pooled effect sizes of each outcome with the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) or minimal detectable change (MDC) reported in the literature.

This meta-analysis showed that resistance exercise increased handgrip strength by 2.95 kg in older adults with sarcopenia. A systematic review by Bohannon indicated that no universal MCID threshold for handgrip strength has been established; however, studies using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, a common statistical approach to determine optimal cut-off values for clinically meaningful changes, suggest a reasonable range of 5.0–6.5 kg as a threshold for clinically meaningful change [61]. Based on this criterion, the 2.95 kg improvement in the present study did not reach the accepted MCID threshold. Nevertheless, two points should be noted. First, most studies on handgrip strength MCID focus on specific patient populations, and research specifically targeting the MCID of resistance exercise in older adults with sarcopenia remains scarce. Second, the effect size in this study exceeded the lower bound of the minimal detectable change (MDC) reported in several studies. Liu et al. reported an MDC95 of 1.64–5.87 kg in older adults with cognitive impairment [62]; Ferreira et al. reported an MDC95 of 2.21–3.50 kg in nursing home residents [63]; and Sawaya et al. reported an MDC95 of 2.4–3.2 kg in community-dwelling older Japanese adults [64]. The 2.95 kg improvement in this study exceeded the lower limits of these MDC values, indicating that the improvement was beyond measurement error and statistically reliable.

Resistance exercise increased gait speed by 0.15 m/s in older adults with sarcopenia. Multiple studies suggest that the MCID for gait speed is approximately 0.10 m/s. Tamura et al. calculated an MCID of 0.10 m/s using the anchor-based method in older patients with cardiovascular disease [65]. The 0.15 m/s improvement observed in this study significantly exceeded the MCID threshold. Notably, the pooled gait speed result showed high statistical heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis revealed that the pooled effect size remained between 0.13 and 0.17 m/s after omitting any single study, and all values exceeded the MCID threshold of 0.10 m/s, confirming that the conclusion that resistance exercise improves gait speed beyond the clinical threshold is robust. The heterogeneity may stem from differences in intervention protocols, baseline participant characteristics, and measurement methods.

Resistance exercise increased ASMI by 0.25 kg/m² in older adults with sarcopenia. No widely accepted MCID threshold for ASMI currently exists in sarcopenia research. Vlietstra et al. explicitly stated that no usable MCID value for ASMI was available in their analysis of patients with osteosarcopenia [66]. An estimated value from a related field can be used for indirect comparison. A nutritional supplementation study in older adults at risk of sarcopenia estimated the MCID for appendicular lean mass at 0.21 kg [67]. Using this reference, the 0.25 kg/m² improvement observed in this study exceeded the reference threshold.

Resistance exercise reduced the time to complete the 5-repetition sit-to-stand test by 1.79 s in older adults with sarcopenia. Interpreting this result requires distinguishing between the minimal detectable change and the minimal clinically important difference. Currently, no MCID studies for the 5-repetition sit-to-stand test have been reported in sarcopenia research, and only MDC reports are available. Yin et al. calculated the MDC of this test in older Japanese adults with sarcopenia. They found an MDC of 3.12 s in the sarcopenia group, indicating that a change of ≥ 3.12 s in the FTSST is clinically significant and can be used to assess the effectiveness of sarcopenia treatment [68]. Studies in other older adult populations have reported similar MDC values of 2.5–3.5 s [69, 70]. The 1.79s improvement observed in this study was lower than all the above MDC reference values, indicating that the magnitude of change did not exceed the minimal detectable change threshold in the sarcopenia population.

Based on the results of subgroup analysis and integration with existing literature, a framework of resistance exercise program for community-dwelling patients with sarcopenia or pre-frailty was derived. The program mainly involved the following aspects: exercise type: resistance exercises, including the use of equipment (dumbbells, kettlebells, elastic bands, etc.) or bodyweight exercises; movement mode: mainly multi-joint and compound movements. Compound movements can improve muscle activation efficiency and coordination among agonist, antagonist, and synergistic muscles, thereby more closely resembling functional tasks [71]. Exercise intensity: moderate intensity (60–75% 1RM or a score of 12–14 on the Borg 6–20 perceived exertion scale). This intensity can effectively activate neural plasticity and stimulate muscle protein synthesis. The ACSM position statement recommends this intensity range for muscle strengthening in older adults [10]. Training frequency: 3 times per week is recommended. The minimum effective frequency is > 2 times per week, and a weekly frequency of ≥ 2 times per week serves as the threshold for maintaining anabolic stimulation [72]. Training arrangement: 2–3 sets of 8–12 repetitions are performed for each movement, with 60–90 s of rest between sets. This range can effectively balance mechanical tension and metabolic stress, and induce muscle hypertrophy [34]. Intervention period: at least 12 weeks are required to sustain muscle mass and achieve significant functional improvement. Muscle structural remodeling is a gradual process that requires sufficient time to yield stable, consolidated training benefits [73]. Training principle: progressive overload must be followed. The “2-for-2” rule is adopted: if two consecutive training sessions can easily complete two more repetitions than the target number in the last set, the training load should be increased; this is a fundamental principle for the effectiveness of resistance training and a key to continuous physiological adaptation [10]. Individualized adjustment: for older adults with sarcopenia, the principle of safety first should be emphasized, and individual health status and functional baseline should be fully considered. Exercise intensity and volume should start from the minimum effective dose mentioned above [74].

The core logic of the program was as follows: to ensure the effectiveness and safety of the intervention based on moderate intensity and progressive overload; the weekly training frequency was set at 3 times per week to maximize training benefits. A minimum 12-week intervention cycle was established to ensure adequate structural adaptation of the muscles. An individualized adjustment was adopted to ensure the safety and feasibility of the protocol for older adult populations.

First, this study strictly adhered to the PRISMA guidelines to conduct a systematic and transparent literature search and screening, thereby minimizing selection bias [75]. Second, all included interventions were limited to resistance exercise, avoiding confounding effects from other intervention modalities and ensuring unbiased conclusions. Third, subgroup analysis results were further integrated to develop optimized exercise protocols, thereby enabling the research data to have practical social value. Fourth, the included studies recruited participants from Asia, Europe, South America, and Oceania, rendering the conclusions more comprehensive and generalizable. Fifth, this study comprehensively evaluated the three core diagnostic indicators of sarcopenia, providing robust analytical evidence for future research in this field.

First, the number of included studies was relatively small, limiting the possibility of comprehensive subgroup analyses across all outcome indicators. Second, the included studies adopted inconsistent diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia in older adults, which may have contributed to methodological heterogeneity. Moreover, different assessment tools were used to measure each outcome indicator, introducing additional measurement bias. For instance, muscle mass was assessed using dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry in some studies, while others adopted bioelectrical impedance analysis or computed tomography. Third, the exercise protocol derived from subgroup analysis in this study provides only a general framework and cannot be directly applied to individualized prescriptions for older adults. Fourth, several included studies lacked long‑term follow‑up data, limiting the evaluation of the sustained effects of resistance exercise on sarcopenia and related health outcomes.

Based on a pooled analysis of 13 RCTs, this study demonstrated that resistance training effectively improves handgrip strength, gait speed, ASMI, and performance on the five-times sit-to-stand test in older adults with sarcopenia. Subgroup analysis indicated that although resistance training improves gait speed, high heterogeneity across studies may be attributable to weekly exercise frequency and age stratification. Therefore, further research is warranted to explore these influencing factors in depth.

The effects of resistance exercise on sarcopenia were more pronounced in the younger age group than in the older age group. A weekly training frequency of three sessions yielded better outcomes than non-three-session weekly regimens. Short-cycle resistance exercise was superior to long-cycle intervention in improving gait speed and ASMI. In contrast, a longer intervention cycle was required to achieve significant improvements in handgrip strength. Accordingly, moderate-intensity resistance exercise with a weekly frequency of 3 sessions, a 12-week intervention cycle, and a single session duration of 40–60 min can effectively ameliorate sarcopenia-related outcomes in younger older adults. For older adults with sarcopenia, individualized exercise prescriptions should be formulated based on personal physical conditions while consistently adhering to scientific and safe training principles.
