# Corticosteroids as adjunctive therapy in acute meningitis: a narrative review.
**Authors:** Ullah N, Khan K, Haseeb A, Ahmad Z, Abbas SH, Samad M, Safdar S, Qayyum SN, Khan R, Noori S
**Journal:** Ann Med Surg (Lond) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1097/MS9.0000000000004620](https://doi.org/10.1097/MS9.0000000000004620)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Acute meningitis is a life-threatening neurological emergency with substantial global morbidity and mortality. Adjunctive corticosteroid therapy aims to mitigate the inflammatory response and improve clinical outcomes.

METHODS: We analyzed data from randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, Cochrane reviews, and international guidelines (e.g., WHO, IDSA, NICE), integrating findings from high-income and resource-limited settings. Key outcomes assessed include mortality, neurological sequelae (e.g., hearing loss, hydrocephalus), length of hospital stay, and treatment-related adverse events.

RESULTS: Corticosteroids significantly reduce mortality and neurological sequelae in acute bacterial meningitis (particularly pneumococcal), especially when administered before or with antibiotics. In tuberculous meningitis, steroids (dexamethasone or prednisolone) improve survival by 25%-30%, though long-term neurological outcomes remain inconsistent. In contrast, viral meningitis shows no clear benefit, and steroids are contraindicated in cryptococcal meningitis due to increased adverse events and worse outcomes. Limited evidence supports steroid use in parasitic meningitis (e.g., Angiostrongylus cantonensis) and non-infectious meningitis (e.g., neurosarcoidosis), where they reduce inflammation and symptom burden. Neonatal meningitis lacks sufficient evidence to support routine corticosteroid use due to potential harms.

CONCLUSION: Adjunctive corticosteroids are a valuable intervention in specific forms of meningitis, particularly bacterial and tuberculous types, where they improve survival and reduce complications. Their use must be tailored to the etiology, timing, and patient population to avoid harm. Global and regional guidelines recommend dexamethasone as part of initial empiric therapy in suspected bacterial meningitis (excluding neonates), and in all cases of TB meningitis.

Acute meningitis, an inflammation of the protective membranes (meninges) surrounding the brain and spinal cord, is a medical emergency that can rapidly become life-threatening. It is caused by a variety of pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, and can also arise from non-infectious causes[1]. Despite advances in antimicrobial therapies, and vaccines, meningitis remains a significant global health challenge with high mortality and long-term morbidity. In 2019, there were an estimated 2.5 million cases of meningitis worldwide, including about 1.6 million cases of bacterial meningitis that resulted in approximately 240 000 deaths[2]. Bacterial meningitis is the most dangerous form; it can become fatal within 24 hours of symptoms onset if not treated promptly[3]. Even with appropriate treatment, around one in six people with bacterial meningitis die, and one in five survivors suffer serious long-term complications such as neurological disabilities, hearing loss, or cognitive impairment[4].

Corticosteroids are not beneficial in viral or cryptococcal meningitis and may increase adverse outcomes in the latter.

Short-course corticosteroids are effective in parasitic eosinophilic meningitis for symptom relief and reducing intracranial pressure.

These disabilities carry heavy personal, social, and economic costs for patients and their families. The burden of meningitis is disproportionately high in low- and middle-income countries[5]. In particularly, sub-Saharan Africa’s “meningitis belt” experiences frequent epidemics of meningococcal meningitis and bears the highest rates of meningitis cases, and deaths globally[6]. Given the substantial mortality, and neurological sequelae associated with acute meningitis, there is a strong imperative to optimize all aspects of management in emergency care including the use of adjunctive therapies aimed at improving outcomes beyond what antimicrobials alone can achieve.

One such adjunctive therapy is the use of corticosteroids such as dexamethasone, prednisolone alongside standard antimicrobial treatment[7]. The rationale for corticosteroid uses in meningitis is rooted in the pathophysiology of the disease. In bacterial meningitis, the introduction of antibiotics leads to rapid lysis of bacteria in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which in turn release pro-inflammatory cell wall components. This triggers a vigorous inflammatory cascade in the subarachnoid space, mediated by cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin, and other inflammatory mediators. This results in breakdown of the blood–brain barrier, cerebral edema, increased intracranial pressure, cerebral vasculitis, and neuronal injury. Inflammation-induced swelling and impaired cerebral perfusions are major causes of death and neurological sequelae in meningitis[7]. Corticosteroids are potent anti-inflammatory agents that can attenuate this immune response. By reducing the production of inflammatory cytokines, and stabilizing the blood–brain barrier, corticosteroids theoretically reduce the secondary brain injury caused by inflammation. Animal studies of bacterial meningitis have shown that adjunctive steroids decrease meningeal inflammation and result in less neurological damage.

Numerous clinical trials and meta-analysis have evaluated the effectiveness of corticosteroids as adjunctive therapy in acute meningitis. However, their findings vary depending on the type of meningitis (bacterial vs tuberculous vs other), the patient population (adults vs children, high income vs low-income settings) and the specific outcomes measured. In some contexts, corticosteroids have clearly demonstrated benefit, leading to their incorporation into treatment guidelines, whereas in others they have shown no improvement or even potential harm.

In this narrative review, we have provided a comprehensive overview of the evidence on adjunctive corticosteroid therapy in acute meningitis. We examined the impact of corticosteroids on key clinical outcomes including mortality, neurological sequelae (such as hearing loss and other deficits), and recovery parameters (such as length of hospital stay), across the spectrum of meningitis etiologies. Meningitis remains a worldwide public health threat despite preventive vaccines for many causative pathogens. Recent studies indicate that meningitis caused approximately 2.5 million new cases globally in 2019 and was responsible for around 240 000 deaths that year[2]. The overall age adjusted mortality rate has improved over the past few decades (declining from about 7.5 per 100 000 population in 1990 to 3.3 per 100 000 in 2019), reflecting advances in vaccination such as Hib, pneumococcal, and meningococcal vaccines, and medical care[9]. Children are especially vulnerable, the burden is greatest in those under 5 years of age, who accounted for nearly half of global meningitis deaths in 2019[10]. In addition to high case-fatality rates, acute meningitis often results in serious long-term sequelae among survivors. It is estimated that roughly 20% of people who survive bacterial meningitis are left with persistent disabilities[2]. These include sensorineural hearing loss (one of the most common sequelae, particularly after Streptococcus pneumoniae or Haemophilus influenzae infection), cognitive impairment, developmental delays in children, focal neurological deficits (such as limb weakness or cranial nerve palsies), seizures or epilepsy, visual impairment, and psychological or behavioral problems. Such complications significantly impact quality of life.

The burden of meningitis is not evenly distributed worldwide. Low-resource regions, especially sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia, bear a disproportionate burden. The African meningitis belt[6] (stretching across Sahelian Africa from Senegal to Ethiopia) historically sees recurrent large-scale epidemics of meningococcal meningitis, with attack rates far exceeding those in temperate countries. Outbreaks of Neisseria meningitidis in this region have caused tens of thousands of cases in single epidemic waves[11]. Although introduction of a monovalent meningococcal A conjugate vaccine in Africa around 2010 dramatically reduced N. meningitidis serogroup A epidemics, other strains (like serogroup C, W, X) continue to cause outbreaks[12]. In these settings, limited access to prompt medical care contributes to very high case fatality rates. Even in non-epidemic periods, bacterial meningitis in low-income countries often has worse outcomes. For instance, a large trial in Malawi (where HIV co-infection is common) reported ~ 54% mortality at 6 weeks among adult meningitis patients treated with antibiotics alone – a stark contrast to the 10%–20% fatality rates seen in high-income settings[13]. Poverty, malnutrition, delayed presentation to hospital, and comorbidities such as HIV all exacerbate meningitis outcomes in developing regions. Meanwhile, in high-income countries, aggressive critical care and routine use of adjuncts like corticosteroids have improved survival over time. This geographic disparity in meningitis burden has prompted global initiatives, such as the WHO’s Defeating Meningitis by 2030 roadmap, which aim to eliminate epidemics and drastically reduce meningitis deaths and disability through improved prevention, diagnosis, and management[14]. These disparities highlight how variations in health-system resources, access to diagnostics and medications, and availability of intensive care services directly influence the effectiveness and generalizability of corticosteroid recommendations.

Local pathogen distribution and strain diversity substantially influence the outcomes and applicability of corticosteroid therapy. In sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia, recurrent epidemics of N. meningitidis (particularly serogroups C, W, and X) and high pneumococcal disease burden with variable serotypes complicate generalization of data generated in Western populations[6,12]. In contrast, high-income regions mainly report sporadic pneumococcal meningitis among older adults or immunocompromised individuals. Vaccine coverage, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and regional HIV prevalence further modify disease severity and response to adjunctive steroids. Consequently, local epidemiologic surveillance and context-specific adaptation of treatment recommendations are essential to ensure the safe and effective use of corticosteroids in diverse global settings.

The concept of using corticosteroids in acute meningitis is to dampen inflammation, while definitive antimicrobial therapy targets the infection. When a pathogen invades meninges, the body’s intense inflammatory response, to control infection, can itself cause collateral damage to brain tissue.

In bacterial meningitis, for example, components of the bacterial cell wall (like lipopolysaccharide of Gram-negatives or teichoic acids of Gram-positives) trigger production of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, etc.) by immune cells in the CSF. This leads to an influx of white blood cells and a cascade of inflammation that increases permeability of the blood-brain barrier. Protein and fluid leak into the interstitial space, causing cerebral edema, elevated intracranial pressure, and reduced cerebral blood flow. Excitatory neurotransmitters and free radicals released during inflammation can injure neurons. Furthermore, inflammation can cause vasculitis and thrombosis of cerebral blood vessels, leading to ischemic strokes in the brain[3].

Corticosteroids, as broad anti-inflammatory agents, can attenuate this response. Dexamethasone, in particular, penetrates well into the CSF and has a long half-life, making it suitable for CNS infections. It inhibits transcription of pro-inflammatory genes (via interference with NF-κB[15] and AP-1 pathways), reduces cytokine release, stabilizes endothelial tight junctions (thereby restoring blood-brain barrier integrity), and decreases the recruitment of inflammatory cells. By these mechanisms, adjunctive steroids given early in the course of meningitis can reduce intracranial pressure and neuronal damage[16]. The timing of steroid administration appears critical. Clinical and experimental data indicate that corticosteroids are most effective when given before or concurrently with the first dose of antibiotics, rather than after inflammation has fully ramped up. Early initiation, ideally before or within 20 minutes of the first antibiotic dose, is critical to blunting the inflammatory burst associated with bacterial lysis[7]. Delayed administration fails to influence cytokine dynamics and thus offers minimal neuroprotective effect. This timing-dependent mechanism explains the consistent benefit seen only in studies administering steroids pre-antibiotic.

Another rationale for steroids is the prevention of specific complications. For example, in pediatric H. influenzae meningitis, the intense inflammation in the subarachnoid space can damage the cochlea, resulting in hearing loss[17]. Dexamethasone reduces the risk of this hearing damage if given early[7]. In tuberculous meningitis (TBM), an exuberant immune response causes basilar meningeal inflammation leading to cranial neuropathies and obstructive hydrocephalus; steroids help mitigate these effects and lower the risk of these complications[18]. On the other hand, there are theoretical downsides to steroid use: immunosuppression could impair clearance of the infection or increase the risk of secondary infections, and steroids might reduce antibiotic penetration into the CSF by rapidly restoring the blood-brain barrier. Balancing these considerations, multiple trials have been undertaken to empirically determine whether the benefits of steroids outweigh the risks in various forms of meningitis.

The differential effects of corticosteroids across meningitis types stem from their modulation of the host inflammatory response. In bacterial and tuberculous meningitis, where excessive cytokine-mediated inflammation drives neuronal injury, corticosteroids mitigate damage by stabilizing the blood-brain barrier and reducing intracranial pressure. Conversely, in viral or cryptococcal meningitis, where the immune response is required for pathogen clearance, corticosteroids suppress protective immunity and may worsen outcomes.

Adjunctive corticosteroids have been most extensively studied in acute bacterial meningitis, which is the archetypal scenario for their use. Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency where N. meningitidis, S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae type b (Hib), Streptococcus agalactiae (group B strep, in neonates), Listeria monocytogenes, and other bacteria cause rapidly progressing infection. Even with antibiotics, bacterial meningitis carries a high fatality and complication rate, so numerous trials since the 1980s have tested whether steroids can improve outcomes. The evidence can be considered separately for adults and pediatric patients, as the disease and effects of steroids differ somewhat by age group.

The landmark double-blind, multicenter randomized controlled trial by de Gans and van de Beek (2002; n = 301 adults)[19] demonstrated a significant reduction in mortality demonstrated a significant reduction in mortality (7% vs. 15%) and unfavorable neurological outcomes (15% vs. 25%) when dexamethasone was administered prior to antibiotic therapy. In this study, patients received intravenous dexamethasone 10 mg every 6 hours for 4 days, administered 15–20 minutes before the first antibiotic dose. The timing of administration was essential; pre-antibiotic dosing markedly blunted the inflammatory surge that follows bacterial lysis. Subsequent Cochrane meta-analysis by Brouwer et al (24 trials, n ≈ 4000 participants)[20] further supports this benefit, particularly in pneumococcal meningitis, showing reductions in hearing loss and neurological sequelae. Across these trials, the corticosteroid regimen was typically dexamethasone 10 mg IV q6h × 4 days in adults, started concurrently with or just prior to antibiotics. These findings highlight strong recommendations by IDSA and European guidelines for the use of dexamethasone (10 mg IV every 6 hours for 4 days) initiated before or with antibiotics in suspected cases[21]. However, the benefit is less clear in low-resource settings or among immunocompromised patients. A large double-blind randomized controlled trial in Malawi (2007; n = 465 HIV-positive adults) failed to show mortality or neurological benefit[13].

Similarly, a prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trial in Vietnam (Nguyen et al; n = 435 patients) highlighted that while confirmed bacterial cases bacterial cases benefited, probable cases did not, and might even be harmed[22]. Consequently, pathogen-specific and population-specific tailoring is advocated: continue steroids for S. pneumoniae, consider for N. meningitidis, L. monocytogenes meningitis[23]. These nuanced insights highlight that while corticosteroids are standard in emergency care for bacterial meningitis in adults, effectiveness varies by etiology, comorbidities, and health care setting. Overall, in the emergency care, the standard of care in adults is to administer dexamethasone empirically alongside antibiotics for suspected acute bacterial meningitis, given the strong evidence that it improves survival and reduces neurological injury. This practice has likely contributed to outcome improvement in developed countries. Indeed, a nationwide analysis in the Netherlands found that after implementing routine adjunctive dexamethasone for adult meningitis, the rate of unfavorable outcomes from pneumococcal meningitis significantly reduced. The overall evidence quality for adjunctive corticosteroid use in adult bacterial meningitis is high, derived from multiple large, double-blind randomized controlled trials and confirmed by Cochrane meta-analyses (Level I evidence).

In emergency settings, corticosteroid therapy should begin on clinical suspicion of bacterial meningitis before microbiologic confirmation, as delayed administration diminishes efficacy. Once laboratory results identify a non-bacterial etiology, steroids should be discontinued promptly.

In pediatric patients, the use of corticosteroids especially dexamethasone has been shown to significantly reduce the risk of neurological sequelae such as hearing loss in case of acute bacterial meningitis particularly those caused by H. influenzae type b (Hib) and S. pneumoniae. Landmark double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs by Lebel et al (n = 200 children) demonstrated a clear protective effect against auditory damage when dexamethasone was administered before antibiotics[24]. Children in these studies received dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV every 6 hours for 2–4 days, started before or simultaneously with antibiotics. This schedule remains the most widely recommended pediatric regimen. Although modern vaccine programs have shifted the causative landscape, evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Brouwer et al; Ogunlesi et al; n ≈ 3800 children across 29 trials) confirms a consistent benefit in reducing hearing impairment and short-term neurological complications[20,25]. However, these studies have not identified a statistically significant mortality reduction in children, likely due to already low pediatric mortality in well-resourced health care settings.

While steroids do not appear to affect long-term developmental outcomes broadly, their neuroprotective effect, particularly regarding hearing, has led to their routine use in children older than 3 months. Guidelines from organizations like WHO and NICE recommend dexamethasone at 0.15 mg/kg IV every 6 hours for 4 days in infants and children with suspected pneumococcal or Hib meningitis[26,27]. In neonates, the use of corticosteroids is controversial. Neonatal bacterial meningitis is often caused by pathogens like group B Streptococcus, E. coli, or Klebsiella, and the inflammatory response in neonates differs from older children and adults.

A Cochrane systematic review in 2015 (Ogunlesi et al; two small RCTs, n = 132 neonates)[25] specifically examined two small trials in neonates and found no definitive evidence that steroids reduce mortality or neurological complications in this age group. In both neonatal trials, the intervention used was dexamethasone 0.25 mg/kg IV every 12 hours for 3 days, initiated after confirmation of meningitis. However, the regimen produced no consistent improvement in mortality or neurological outcomes. A study indicated a reduction in hearing loss at 4-10 weeks post-illness, but this came from a single center trial. Given insufficient data and concerns about steroids adverse effects in infants such as increased risk of gastrointestinal perforation, guidelines do not routinely recommend steroids in neonatal meningitis[28]. In neonates, available data come from two small single-center RCTs with conflicting outcomes, representing low-quality evidence. Therefore, the routine use of corticosteroids cannot be recommended until larger, well-designed studies confirm benefit

TBM, caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis infecting the meninges, is a highly lethal form of meningitis that often presents sub-acutely and predominantly affects low-income countries. TBM is characterized by a dense inflammatory exudate at the base of the brain, leading to hydrocephalus, vasculitis with cerebral infarcts, and cranial nerve palsies[29]. The disease often progresses over weeks, and even with anti-tubercular therapy, mortality is high and survivors frequently have severe neurological sequelae (e.g., paralysis, cognitive impairment). Adjunctive corticosteroids have been used in TBM for decades, and unlike in viral or fungal meningitis, in TBM they have consistently shown benefit in clinical trials.

The landmark randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial by Thwaites et al (n = 545 adolescents and adults in Vietnam) showed that adjunctive dexamethasone, when added to standard anti-tuberculous therapy, reduced 9-month mortality from 33% to 22% – a relative risk reduction of 31%[30]. Participants received dexamethasone 0.3–0.4 mg/kg/day IV for the first 2 weeks, followed by a gradual taper over 6–8 weeks, transitioning to oral prednisolone in the later phase. This survival benefit was observed across disease severity levels and in both HIV-negative and HIV-positive patients, though the effect in the latter was less statistically robust. However, corticosteroids did not significantly reduce the rate of severe neurological disability among survivors.

A 2016 Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis by Prasad et al (nine trials, n = 1337 participants[31]) consolidated these findings and confirmed that corticosteroids lower mortality by about 25% in both adults and children with TBM, without significantly affecting long-term neurological outcomes. Safety data from the Thwaites et al[30] trial revealed fewer serious adverse events in the steroid group, suggesting a protective effect against inflammation-driven complications. In children, earlier studies by Schoeman et al[31] in South Africa found that steroids reduced both mortality and neurological complications such as hydrocephalus. Collectively, these findings support the routine use of corticosteroids, typically dexamethasone or prednisolone, in TBM treatment to improve survival, even though long-term functional outcomes remain variable. Adjunctive corticosteroid therapy is universally recommended in TB meningitis, for both adults, and children, regardless of HIV status. The WHO and TB programs have advised adding steroids to the standard four-drug anti-tuberculous regimen for TBM. The typical regimen for adults is dexamethasone IV 0.3–0.4 mg/kg/day (approximately 8–12 mg IV 12-hourly) for the first 1–2 weeks, then tapering gradually over 6-8 weeks. Some protocols start with high-dose IV dexamethasone for 2–3 weeks and then switch to oral prednisolone to complete a total of 6–8 weeks of tapering doses[32]. Thus, most national TB programs recommend a 6–8-week tapering corticosteroid course, beginning with dexamethasone 0.3–0.4 mg/kg/day IV, followed by oral prednisolone. It is important that steroids in TBM are always given alongside appropriate anti-TB antimicrobial therapy. Steroids alone (without TB antibiotics) would be dangerous, and they might temporarily mask symptoms while allowing the infection to progress unchecked. In the clinical trial where some “probable meningitis” patients (likely undiagnosed TBM) received dexamethasone without TB treatment, outcomes were worse. For tuberculous meningitis, evidence quality is high, with multiple randomized trials and meta-analyses consistently demonstrating a survival advantage. However, neurological outcomes remain inconsistently improved (Level I evidence). Current WHO, IDSA, and NICE guidelines uniformly recommend adjunctive dexamethasone for bacterial and tuberculous meningitis (excluding neonates). While WHO emphasizes its role in global meningitis reduction strategies, NICE guidance specifies dose ranges and contraindications in pediatric and adult populations. Minor discrepancies exist mainly in taper duration, not in therapeutic indication.

Viral meningitis, mostly caused by enteroviruses, arboviruses, or herpesviruses, is generally a milder disease than bacterial or TB meningitis. The use of corticosteroids in viral meningitis has not been well established, and there are no recommendations on the its use as adjunctive therapy in typical viral meningitis cases[33]. Patients often recover spontaneously within 7–10 days, and long-term sequelae are less common except in certain specific viral infections. The use of steroids in viral meningitis could be counterproductive theoretically, as the immune response is needed to clear the viral infection, and immunosuppression may prolong viral shedding. There are few exceptions where clinicians might consider using corticosteroids for inflammatory aspects of viral CNS infection such as Mollaret’s meningitis, Varicella zoster meningitis, aseptic meningitis, etc. In viral meningitis, the lack of controlled trials limits the evidence base; existing data are largely observational and expert opinion, corresponding to low-level evidence. Therefore, current guidelines focus on supporting care for viral meningitis and do no enlist steroids as a recommended adjunct for viral etiologies. In emergency care, distinguishing viral from bacterial meningitis is crucial, and is often done via CSF analysis. If viral meningitis is diagnosed, one should generally avoid corticosteroids.

Fungal meningitis, particularly cryptococcal meningitis in immunocompromised patients (e.g., people with AIDS), presents a very different scenario for adjunctive therapy. Cryptococcus neoformans is the most common cause of fungal meningitis globally, often affecting HIV-infected individuals with low CD4 counts. The inflammation in cryptococcal meningitis is typically less intense than in bacterial meningitis; in fact, there is an insufficient immune response allowing the fungus to proliferate. Trials have showed that corticosteroids are not beneficial in cryptococcal meningitis, and may be harmful.

The multicenter, randomized, double-blind CryptoDex trial (2016; n = 451 HIV-positive patients with cryptococcal meningitis) investigated the adjunct use of dexamethasone in HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis[34]. In this study, patients received dexamethasone 0.4 mg/kg/day IV for 1 week, tapered progressively over 6 weeks alongside standard antifungal therapy (amphotericin B + fluconazole). By 10 weeks (the primary endpoint), the mortality was slightly higher in the dexamethasone group (47% died) compared to the placebo group (41% died), though this difference was not statistically significant (HR 1.11, 95% CI 0.84–1.47). By 6 months, the mortality gap widened: 57% vs 49% (HR 1.18, 95% CI 0.91–1.53), still not statistically significant but trending in the wrong direction. More strikingly, patients on dexamethasone had significantly worse neurological outcomes among survivors. At 10 weeks, only 13% of the dexamethasone group achieved a good neurological outcome (defined by functional status), compared to 25% of the placebo group – meaning twice as many steroid-treated patients were left with disability (OR for good outcome 0.42, 95% CI 0.25–0.69, P < 0.001). Furthermore, adverse events were more common with steroids: the dexamethasone group experienced a higher frequency of serious adverse events such as new infections (e.g., other opportunistic infections due to immune suppression), renal impairment (likely exacerbated by a combination of amphotericin toxicity plus steroids), and cardiac events. Notably, culture clearance of Cryptococcus from CSF was slower in the steroid group, suggesting that the immunosuppressive effects impeded the body’s ability to clear the fungus.

The trial was concluded with this statement, “Dexamethasone did not reduce mortality among patients with HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis and was associated with more adverse events and disability than placebo”[34]. As a result, the guidelines recommend against the routine use of corticosteroids in cryptococcal meningitis[35].

However, in treating complications of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) in cryptococcosis, corticosteroids have been used. IRIS can occur when an HIV patient with cryptococcal infection starts antiretroviral therapy, and the recovering immune system causes a paradoxical inflammatory response[36]. For cryptococcal meningitis, the evidence is strong and consistent (Level I) against corticosteroid use, as demonstrated by the large multicenter CryptoDex RCT. Given the adverse outcomes observed, the current WHO guidelines discourage corticosteroid use in active cryptococcal meningitis, except for short-term management of IRIS-related inflammation or elevated intracranial pressure. In cases of severe manifestations such as increased intracranial pressure or focal lesions, short-term steroids are sometimes administered to control inflammation. In other fungal meningitides, such as coccidioidal meningitis and candida, corticosteroids use is not recommended as adjunctive therapy in the acute treatment phase.

A variety of parasitic infections can cause meningitis or meningoencephalitis, often accompanied by eosinophilic meningitis (elevated eosinophils in CSF). These include Angiostrongylus cantonensis (rat lungworm), Gnathostoma species, Baylisascaris (raccoon roundworm), and others primarily in tropical regions. While these conditions are relatively rare, clinicians in endemic areas have used corticosteroids in their management, mainly to alleviate symptoms.

The most well-known parasitic meningitis is caused by A. cantonensis[37], which leads to eosinophilic meningitis typically after ingestion of snails or contaminated produce. Patients experience severe headaches, neck stiffness, and often radicular pain or paresthesias. There is no specific antiparasitic cure for angiostrongyliasis (the larvae generally die on their own after a few weeks), so treatment is supportive. Corticosteroids have been employed to reduce the inflammation triggered by the dying larvae in the meninges.

A randomized, placebo-controlled trial in Thailand (Chotmongkol et al; n = 60 patients with eosinophilic meningitis)[38] supports the use of prednisolone for eosinophilic meningitis as it significantly shortened the duration of headaches, and hastened recovery compared to placebo. Patients received oral prednisolone 60 mg daily for 2 weeks, producing faster headache relief and fewer repeat lumbar punctures. The median time to headache resolution was 5 days in the steroid group vs 13 days in the placebo (P < 0.0001), and fewer steroid patients had persistent headaches after 2 weeks (9% vs 46% in placebo). The study also found that the need for repeated lumbar puncture to relieve intracranial pressure was also lower in steroids. No significant adverse effects were reported from the 2-week course of prednisolone. Therefore, a short high-dose course of steroids (prednisolone 60 mg daily for 2 weeks) is recommended in severe angio-strongylus meningitis, usually in combination with analgesics, and perhaps albendazole. However, for other parasitic causes of meningitis, there are no large trials to definitively prove outcome improvement, but clinical practice has found steroids beneficial for symptoms control, and preventing damage from excessive inflammation. The evidence for parasitic and eosinophilic meningitis is limited but suggestive, based on a few small randomized and observational studies (Level III evidence). Thus, a short high-dose oral prednisolone course (≈ 60 mg daily × 14 days) remains the preferred regimen for eosinophilic meningitis.

Corticosteroids are indeed the primary treatment for non-infectious meningitis, resulting from autoimmune disorders, paraneoplastic or carcinomatous meningitis, or chemical meningitis due to medications or intrathecal interventions. For instance, neuro-sarcoidosis frequently causes chronic meningitis, and high-dose corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone 40–60 mg daily or IV methylprednisolone 1 g for 3 days in severe flares) are the first-line therapy to induce remission[39].

In summary, corticosteroids are clearly justified for bacterial meningitis (particularly pneumococcal and H. influenzae) and tuberculous meningitis. Their use is not recommended in viral and cryptococcal meningitis and should be considered optional for parasitic or aseptic autoimmune forms where inflammation, rather than infection, predominates.

Adjunctive corticosteroids in acute meningitis are generally safe when used appropriately because the safety profile varies by duration and patient population. In acute bacterial meningitis short courses (2–4 days) of dexamethasone are typically well tolerated with few potential harms include transient hyperglycemia, gastrointestinal upset, immunosuppression, and, rarely, delayed pathogen clearance. These risks are minimized through short-course therapy (≤4 days) in bacterial cases and careful monitoring in longer TBM regimens. Clinicians should monitor glucose levels, secondary infection signs, and electrolyte balance throughout therapy. In TB meningitis, longer steroid therapy is required but should be closely monitored especially in immunocompromised patients. This review is limited by the heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and health care settings among the included trials. Many data for pediatric, neonatal, and parasitic meningitis originate from single-center or small-sample studies. Publication bias and regional disparities in diagnostic capacity may also influence observed outcomes. Furthermore, resource-limited settings may lack timely access to corticosteroids or advanced neurocritical care, affecting generalizability. Future research should focus on large multicenter RCTs in underrepresented populations and on defining optimal dosing and duration in diverse clinical contexts.

Neonatal and very young infants: Existing trials are underpowered and heterogeneous, and the safety and efficacy of steroids in neonatal meningitis remain uncertain.

Low-resource and high HIV-prevalence settings: Data from Malawi, Vietnam, and other low-income regions suggest that benefits seen in high-income cohorts may not be fully reproducible; further pragmatic, context-specific RCTs are needed.

Optimal regimen and duration: The ideal dose, route, and taper length for tuberculous meningitis, and whether specific subgroups (e.g., HIV-positive, drug-resistant TB) require tailored regimens, remain areas of active investigation.

Pathogen- and biomarker-guided therapy: The role of rapid diagnostics and inflammatory or imaging biomarkers to guide initiation, continuation, or early discontinuation of steroids has not been clearly defined.

Long-term neurocognitive outcomes: Many studies focus on short-term mortality; high-quality prospective data on neurocognitive, psychiatric, and quality-of-life outcomes after adjunctive steroid use are limited.

Non-bacterial etiologies: Evidence for parasitic, autoimmune, and other non-bacterial meningitides is based primarily on small series and expert opinion; robust comparative studies are lacking.

Addressing these gaps would refine risk–benefit assessment and support more individualized use of corticosteroids across diverse clinical settings.

Emerging evidence supports more nuanced, context-sensitive application of current guidelines. While international bodies such as WHO, IDSA, ESCMID, and NICE endorse early adjunctive dexamethasone for suspected bacterial meningitis (excluding neonates) and for tuberculous meningitis, implementation should consider local epidemiology, HIV prevalence, burden of cryptococcal disease, vaccine coverage, and health-system capacity. In high-income settings with rapid diagnostics and intensive care, routine empiric dexamethasone for suspected pneumococcal meningitis is appropriate. In contrast, in resource-limited settings with high rates of unconfirmed or atypical meningitis, guidelines may need adaptation to avoid prolonged steroid use when bacterial or TB etiology is unlikely, and to prioritize rapid diagnostics and antimicrobial access. Future guideline revisions should explicitly integrate these contextual factors, include clear algorithms for when to discontinue steroids once an alternative diagnosis is established, and align with the WHO “Defeating meningitis by 2030” roadmap to ensure equitable, evidence-based care globally[21].

In conclusion, corticosteroids play a critical role in the management of acute meningitis. They significantly improve survival and reduce neurological complications in acute bacterial and tuberculous meningitis, especially when administered immediately alongside antibiotics or anti-TB therapy. However, viral meningitis is self-limiting, and in cryptococcal meningitis, where steroids increase harm, their use is not recommended. In parasitic and non-infectious meningitis, steroids may offer benefits, though evidence is limited. However, clinical decision on the use of steroids should align with updated international guidelines to ensure steroids are used where beneficial and avoided where their use is detrimental. In summary, effective corticosteroid regimens vary by etiology – from short 4-day high-dose courses in bacterial meningitis to prolonged 6–8-week tapers in TBM, whereas cryptococcal and viral meningitis generally contraindicate steroid use.
