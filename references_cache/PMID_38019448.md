# The Trouble with Trabeculation: How Genetics Can Help to Unravel a Complex and Controversial Phenotype.
**Authors:** Walsh R
**Journal:** J Cardiovasc Transl Res (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s12265-023-10459-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12265-023-10459-6)

## Content

Excessive trabeculation of the cardiac left ventricular wall is a complex phenotypic substrate associated with various physiological and pathological processes. There has been considerable conjecture as to whether hypertrabeculation contributes to disease and whether left ventricular non-compaction (LVNC) cardiomyopathy is a distinct pathology. Building on recent insights into the genetic basis of LVNC cardiomyopathy, in particular three meta-analysis studies exploring genotype-phenotype associations using different methodologies, this review examines how genetic research can advance our understanding of trabeculation. Three groups of genes implicated in LVNC are described-those associated with other cardiomyopathies, other cardiac/syndromic conditions and putatively with isolated LVNC cardiomyopathy-demonstrating how these findings can inform the underlying pathologies in LVNC patients and aid differential diagnosis and management in clinical practice despite the limited utility suggested for LVNC genetic testing in recent guidelines. The outstanding questions and future research priorities for exploring the genetics of hypertrabeculation are discussed.

The Complex Nature of Left Ventricular 
Hypertrabeculation
Left ventricular non-compaction (LVNC) cardiomyopathy 
is a complex physiological and pathological entity that has 
been the subject of considerable debate and conjecture. It is 
characterised by excessive trabeculation of the left ventricle 
wall in conjunction with left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, 
with diagnostic criteria typically requiring a ratio of non-
compact/trabecular to compact myocardium of at least 2:1

(the NC/C ratio). The contested nature of this condition is 
reflected by the fact that the American Heart Association 
refers to LVNC as a primary genetic cardiomyopathy [1 ], 
while the European Society of Cardiology considers it an 
unclassified cardiomyopathy [2].
The controversy about LVNC likely centres on three broad 
issues related to LV hypertrabeculation. Firstly, excessive tra-
beculation in itself is not necessarily a pathological substrate. 
It commonly occurs as a normal variant in apparently healthy 
individuals, with up to 15% of the population exceeding the 
diagnostic NC/C ratio when detected by sensitive cardiac 
magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging [3 ]. It can also occur 
as a physiological (and reversible) response to increased 
preload/afterload during pregnancy or in competitive ath -
letes where the extent of trabeculation can vary according 
to ancestry [4, 5]. Secondly, where hypertrabeculation does 
occur in the context of cardiac dysfunction, patients often 
are diagnosed with other distinct conditions, including car -
diomyopathies, arrhythmias, congenital heart disease and 
neuromuscular disorders. In these cases, hypertrabeculation 
is a specific phenotypic expression which, even if the most 
prominent and/or presenting symptom, does not represent 
the underlying disease. Thirdly, the extent of trabeculation 
does not seem to correlate with LV dysfunction or prognosis, 
either in population studies or cardiomyopathy cohorts [6 ]. 
For example, in the MESA (Multi-Ethnic Study of Athero-
sclerosis) cohort, excessive trabeculation was not associated 
with deterioration in LV volumes or function over a 10-year 
period [7]. Similarly, in patients with dilated cardiomyopa-
thy (DCM), NC/C mass or length had no predictive value 
for event-free survival over a 3.4-year median follow-up [8].
Nevertheless, it is clear that diagnoses of LVNC cardi-
omyopathy are regularly made within clinical practice, in 
patients with LV dysfunction and where hypertrabeculation is 
a primary phenotypic feature. The extent to which such cases 
represent a distinct cardiomyopathy condition or another 
underlying cardiac or syndromic disease remains uncertain. 
Detailed clinical phenotyping, analysis of family history and 
affected relatives and genetic diagnosis are essential tools for 
addressing this question. For the latter, the identification of 
causal genetic variants is increasingly valuable for diagnosing 
and distinguishing between cardiomyopathy subtypes, as we 
develop a more comprehensive understanding of their genetic 
architecture and, importantly, implement more robust crite-
ria for establishing gene-disease associations (e.g. through 
the ClinGen initiative to re-evaluate proposed gene-disease 
associations). Research into the genetic basis of LVNC car-
diomyopathy can therefore help to illuminate the underlying 
pathology and increase the utility of clinical genetic testing 
to aid in diagnosis and clinical management.
It was noted in a recent state-of-the-art review that the term 
“non-compaction” does not accurately reflect the processes 
underlying LV trabeculation [6]. In this article, the authors 
claimed that the compact layer of the LV wall does not form 
as a result of the compaction of existing trabeculation—rather, 
the extent of trabeculation observed in adults is determined by 
differential growth of the compact and trabecular layers during 
development. The authors therefore suggested that a term like 
“cardiomyopathy with excessive trabeculation” should be used 
instead of LVNC. However, this hypothesis does not necessar-
ily explain all the characteristics of this condition, for example 
the transient and reversible appearance of hypertrabeculation 
in athletes and during pregnancy [5, 9]. While acknowledg-
ing this debate, in the absence of an agreed new consensus 
description and for consistency with previous literature, the 
term LVNC cardiomyopathy is still used in this article.
Overview of Genetic Studies
Efforts to understand the genetic aetiology of LVNC car -
diomyopathy or LV hypertrabeculation have been ongoing 
for several decades. Initially, these investigations typically 
involved individual patients/families or small case series and 
cohort studies. Such studies faced similar limitations that 
affected all inherited cardiac (and noncardiac) conditions 
in the early era of next-generation sequencing—putative 
causal variants were often later found to be insufficiently 
rare in the population to be plausibly pathogenic or were 
subsequently recognised as providing insufficient evidence 
to demonstrate pathogenicity by more robust contemporary 
standards. For LVNC, these issues were exacerbated by the 
challenges of phenotype definition—whether the hypertra-
beculation observed in patients related to a distinct LVNC 
cardiomyopathy or simply reflected a secondary phenotype 
of another underlying cardiac or syndromic condition.
Over time, the size of clinical LVNC cardiomyopathy 
cohorts from specialised centres has expanded, affording new 
insights into genotype–phenotype correlations, e.g. a multi-
centre study of four Dutch cardiogenetics centres published 
in 2018 comprised 327 probands [10]. Recently, three meta-
analyses of different study designs have utilised this burgeon-
ing literature on LVNC genetics to yield new insights and 
clarification into its genetic architecture (Fig. 1):
1. A systematic review of 561 cases from 172 studies by 
van Waning et al. to explore genotype–phenotype cor -
relations (2019) [11]
2. A rare variant association study (RVAS) of 840 cases 
from 6 cohorts versus population controls by Mazzarotto 
et al. to identify genes with robust statistical association 
(2020) [12]
3. A systematic assessment and classification of LVNC 
gene associations by Rojanasopondist et al. that used 
the ClinGen framework to identify genes with convinc-
ing evidence (2022) [13]

The scale of genetic heterogeneity associated with LVNC 
and the number of genes implicated in this phenotype were 
revealed by these studies. The systematic review by van Wan-
ing et al. identified 80 genes that were previously reported as 
causal, although 14 were excluded on the basis of insufficient 
evidence for causality [11]. The RVAS evaluated association 
for 70 genes, but significant enrichment (with Bonferroni cor-
rection) was restricted to specific classes of rare variants in 11 
of these genes. The analysis using the ClinGen gene-disease 
framework evaluated 189 genes that had been implicated in 
LVNC in the literature (although 32 only had data from mouse 
models), with 32 genes classified as having definitive (11) or 
moderate (21) evidence of association (the typical threshold of 
this framework for defining actionable disease genes).
As case–control data was not used as evidence for the 
ClinGen-based evaluation, the RVAS analysis provides a 
largely orthogonal dataset to compare and validate the find-
ings for both of these approaches (Fig. 2). The complemen-
tarity of these two approaches, and the substantial overlap of 
the validated genes they identify, was previously highlighted 
by similar studies for DCM [14]. Of the 32 definitive/mod-
erate LVNC genes, 9 were not included in the enrichment 
study, as they were either recently described cardiomyopa-
thy genes (e.g. ALPK3) or genes primarily associated with 
syndromic conditions that would not typically be included 
in cardiomyopathy gene panels. For the 23 genes evaluated 
in both studies, 10 were enriched in cases with Bonferroni 
significance (multiple testing correction for 70 genes) and a 
further 8 were enriched with nominal significance (p < 0.05) 
(MIB1 truncating variants were enriched in LVNC cases but 
marginally outside nominal significance, p = 0.054). ACTN2 
was the only gene with significant case enrichment (of trun-
cating variants only) that only reached limited evidence in 
the ClinGen-based curation (see below for further discussion 
of this association).
From these meta-analyses, the heterogeneous list of genes 
and variant classes implicated in LVNC cardiomyopathy 
(with varying levels of confidence) can be broadly grouped 
into three sets—genes associated with other cardiomyopathy 
phenotypes (principally sarcomeric genes), genes that cause 
distinct cardiac and syndromic conditions where LVNC can 
occur (and potentially present) as one of the disease phe-
notypes and genes/variant classes that are putatively solely 
associated with isolated LVNC cardiomyopathy (Table 1).

but sub-clinical DCM/HCM phenotypic features may be pre-

tions of genes, variant classes and even individual variants 
with each cardiomyopathy subtype.
Loss-of-function or truncating variants are generally 
expected to have a distinct disease association for any par -
ticular gene as their molecular effects should be broadly 
consistent (subject to caveats about variant location with 
respect to different transcripts/exons, likelihood to cause 
nonsense-mediated decay, etc.). Truncating variants in TTN 
and MYBPC3 are primarily associated with DCM and HCM 
respectively and are the most prevalent disease genes for these 
conditions. Although patients with such variants could present 
with other phenotypic features such as LVNC (or atrial fibril-
lation [18]), the underlying molecular diagnosis is likely to be 
DCM/HCM respectively. A genotype–phenotype study by van 
Waning et al. in LVNC patients and their families supported 
this hypothesis, demonstrating, for example, that patients with 
MYBPC3 variants usually presented as LVNC with HCM and 
most of their affected relatives had HCM alone [19].
In contrast, missense variants can lead to highly diverse 
phenotypes depending on variant location and the amino 
acid biochemical change. Missense variants in MYH7 and 
genes of the thin filament (TNNT2, TNNI3, TNNC1, TPM1, 
ACTC1) can cause HCM or DCM, as well as LVNC and 
other cardiac phenotypes. Distinguishing the underlying 
disease from the phenotypic presentation is more challeng-
ing for such variants—while sarcomeric missense variants 
are often detected in patients with isolated LVNC, their 
relatives or unrelated cases with the same variant can have 
HCM/DCM. However, there is evidence to suggest that 
some missense variants in MYH7 and ACTC1 in particu-
lar may lead to more prominent or isolated LVNC pheno-
types. These genes are highly prevalent in LVNC cases 
in the meta-analysis studies described above, with ACTC1  
variants, for example, seemingly occurring at higher fre-
quencies in LVNC (2.1%) compared to similar studies 
for HCM (0.5%) and DCM (0.4%) [12]. The ACTC1:p.
Glu101Lys variant is also highly recurrent amongst LVNC 
patients, identified in 28 cases in the systematic review 
[11], although it has also been previously identified in 
HCM patients [20, 21]. MYH7 missense variants detected 
in LVNC cases cluster in the myosin head region which, 
although partially overlapping with the variant cluster 
observed in HCM patients (amino acid residues 167–931) 
[22], tend to be more concentrated towards the N-terminus 
(amino acid residues 39–415) [ 12]. MYH7 head region 
missense variants were also significantly associated with 
isolated LVNC in patients and their relatives in the geno-
type–phenotype study by van Waning et al. [19]. Further 
research will be required to assess if particular missense 
variants are solely associated with isolated LVNC, ideally 
by demonstrating presence in multiple unrelated cases and 
supported by functional validation [23].

Genetic Overlap with Other Cardiac 
and Syndromic Conditions
LVNC phenotypes have also been consistently described 
for a number of other cardiac and more broadly syndromic 
conditions with specific gene-disease associations. While 
the clinical significance of LV hypertrabeculation in these 
conditions may be unclear, or even have minimal impact, 
the identification of the underlying genetic cause could have 
important diagnostic utility in cases where LVNC is the pre-
senting phenotype.
Combined LVNC/arrhythmia phenotypes have been 
described for specific variant classes in two genes—RYR2  
and HCN4. Gain-of-function missense variants in RYR2 are 
the primary cause of catecholaminergic polymorphic ven -
tricular tachycardia (CPVT), occurring in over 50% of cases, 
a rare but potentially lethal inherited arrhythmia syndrome 
that can cause sudden cardiac death in the young. However, 
deletions of entire exons of RYR2 have been described in sev-
eral patients with phenotypes that include both CPVT and 
LVNC [24]. While deletion of RYR2 exon 3 has been most 
commonly reported, deletions of exons 2 and 19 have also 
been detected in LVNC patients [12]. Missense variants in 
HCN4 are strongly associated with complex phenotypes that 
can variably include LVNC, sinus bradycardia and aortic dila-
tion [25, 26]. For HCN4 cases where LVNC has been reported, 
variants are largely located within the transmembrane region 
of the gene where the strongest case–control association has 
been observed (3.3% vs. 0.1%, p = 4.8E–09) [12] (Fig. 3A). 
For both of these variant classes (RYR2 exons deletions and 
HCN4 transmembrane missense variants), a significant enrich-
ment was observed in patients included in LVNC case cohorts, 
indicating that genetic testing can diagnose patients that appar-
ently present with LVNC but may actually have potentially 
serious arrhythmia conditions [12].
LVNC is also frequently observed in a number of other 
syndromic conditions, with several of their causal genes clas-
sified with moderate/definitive association for LVNC using 
the ClinGen framework [13]. These include DMD (Becker/
Duchenne muscular dystrophy), DMPK (myotonic dystrophy 
1), LAMP2 (Danon disease), NKX2-5 (congenital heart dis-
ease), NNT (glucocorticoid deficiency 4), NONO (intellectual 
developmental disorder, X-linked syndromic 34), TAFAZZIN 
(Barth syndrome), TBX5 (Holt-Oram syndrome) and TMEM70 
(mitochondrial disease). Of these, LVNC cardiomyopathy 
could feasibly be the primary or presenting phenotype for 
Danon disease, glucocorticoid deficiency 4, Barth syndrome 
and Holt-Oram syndrome, indicating that genetic testing for 
LVNC could have an important role in the differential diag-
nosis of these conditions if their causal genes are included in 
test panels. These conditions may also provide the opportunity 
for further complementary research into the interplay between 
LVNC and other cardiomyopathies and any pathological role 
of hypertrabeculation in cardiac dysfunction.

PRDM16 was suggested to contribute to the cardiomyo-
pathy phenotype observed in 1p36 deletion syndrome [29], a 
common chromosomal abnormality affecting approximately 
1 in 5000 children, through fine mapping analysis that 
identified the gene within the minimal deletion region for 
cardiomyopathy phenotypes. This association was queried 
due to the incomplete correlation between phenotype and 
1p36 deletions incorporating PRDM16 and the presence of 
large PRDM16 deletions in control databases [30, 31]. How-
ever, a recent study that utilised a retrospective multicentre 
cohort, as well as a larger combined cohort incorporating 
cases from a systematic literature review, found a significant 
association between PRDM16 deletion and cardiomyopathy 
diagnosis (29.1% with PRDM16 deletion vs 10.8% without, 
p = 0.03), with LVNC being the principal cardiomyopathy 
sub-type (18/23) in patients with PRDM16 deletion [32].

PRDM16 deletion (63.3%, p = 0.06) and PRDM16 deletion 
with cardiomyopathy (82.6%, p = 0.003), findings supported 
by cardiac-specific PRDM16 knockout mice models which 
demonstrated more severe cardiac effects and higher mortal-
ity rates in female mice [32]. The association with LVNC 
is further bolstered by increasing numbers for PRDM16  
truncating variants (substitutions and small indels) identi-
fied in LVNC cases, with 14 cases now described (Table 2), 
despite the fact that such variants are highly constrained in 
the general population (Fig.  3C).
Several pathogenic missense variants in ACTN2 have 
been identified that are associated with diverse cardiomyo-
pathy and arrhythmia phenotypes (including LVNC), even 
within families carrying the same variant [33, 34]. Based on 
these missense variants alone, ACTN2 was classified as hav-
ing moderate evidence for “intrinsic cardiomyopathy” but 
only limited evidence for LVNC using the ClinGen frame-
work [13, 33]. However, the latter did not take into account 
the significant enrichment of ACTN2 truncating variants in 
LVNC cases compared to population controls [12]. Since the 
RVAS analysis, several other truncating variants have been 
described in LVNC cases, including additional patients with 
the recurrent p.Arg192* variant and patients with multi-exon 
deletions (Table 2). All of the truncating and large deletion 
variants identified in LVNC cases thus far are located in the 
N-terminal region of ACTN2 (as are most of the small number 
of truncating variants in gnomAD), which may indicate that 
variants towards the C-terminus are less tolerated (Fig. 3D).
TBX20 was classified with Limited evidence by the Clin-
Gen DCM curation but scored as Moderate for LVNC [13, 
35]. The latter classification was based on a relatively small 
amount of human genetic evidence—four reported cases from 
three studies (p.Tyr317*, p.Thr262Met, p.Val300Glyfs*27, 
p.Met224Val) [17, 36, 37]. The pathogenicity of two of these 
variants (p.Tyr317*, p.Thr262Met) was verified using patient-
derived induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes 
and demonstration of the reversal of the disease phenotype 
with genome correction of the variants [36]. Subsequently, 
six other TBX20 variants have been described in LVNC cases, 
including p.Asp278* in three affected family members (co-
occurring with the DSG2 variant p.Ser363*) [38] and the de 
novo p.Arg287Trp variant detected in a 6-year-old female 
[39]. A recent case series by Chang et al. described four addi-
tional families: (1) c.380 + 1G > C in a young girl with LVNC 
and DCM (her brother had Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) 
syndrome and LVNC and her mother had WPW only; both 
were carriers); (2) exons 7–8 deletion in a boy with congenital 
heart disease, LVNC, DCM and WPW (three relatives with 
the deletion also had LVNC only); (3) p.Lys237Thrfs*9 in a 
girl with congenital heart disease, LVNC and restrictive car-
diomyopathy (four relatives with the variant had LVNC and/
or DCM); and (4) p.Ser125* in an adult female and her father 
with LVNC [40]. TBX20 variants have also previously been 
implicated in various congenital heart disease phenotypes but 
the robustness of these associations, whether specific geno-
type–phenotype correlations exist for this gene and the com-
plexity and diversity of the observed phenotypes needs further 
systematic evaluation (Fig. 3E).

cardiomyopathy phenotypes. Early knockout mouse studies

the pathogenicity of these variants, with the ClinGen DCM

be restricted to a recessive form of DCM [42]. For LVNC, 
LDB3 also scored in the Moderate range using the ClinGen 
framework [13] and demonstrated a nominal enrichment of 
non-truncating variants in the RVAS analysis (2.2% vs 0.9%, 
p = 1.7E−03) [12]. Further studies will be needed to validate 
whether heterozygous LDB3 missense variants are indeed 
associated with LVNC cardiomyopathy.
Future Directions and Challenges
While much progress has been made in identifying and 
clarifying the genetic aetiology of LVNC cardiomyopathy, a 
number of critical issues still need to be addressed to deepen 
our understanding of this complex phenotype and maximise 
the utility of genetic testing in patients.
Cardiomyopathy Genes
As described above, most (likely) pathogenic variants 
detected in LVNC cardiomyopathy patients occur in genes 
associated with other cardiomyopathies, indicating that 
DCM or HCM may be the underlying disease in a sub-
stantial proportion of cases. LVNC has been described in 
patients carrying variants in most sarcomeric and some non-
sarcomeric cardiomyopathy genes [ 11–13]. While variants 
in sarcomeric genes have tended to predominate in published 
studies and case–control analyses, it is unclear the extent to 
which this reflects inclusion bias for the gene panels used 
in these studies and/or their high prevalence as causal genes 
for DCM and HCM. It also remains unknown whether an 
LVNC phenotype can be associated with all genetic subtypes 
of DCM/HCM (indicating a general secondary phenotype 
in cardiomyopathies) or whether the phenotype is solely or 
preferentially associated with a subset of these genotypes 
(indicating association with certain pathophysiological 
pathways).
Further research is clearly needed to clarify these issues. 
This could include large-scale cohort studies incorporating 
recently described non-sarcomeric cardiomyopathy genes 
that tend to be more rarely associated with DCM/HCM. 
For example, these could assess the extent of hypertra-
beculation in large DCM/HCM cohorts using CMR imag-
ing or perform comprehensive genetic testing in large 
LVNC cardiomyopathy cohorts that includes rarer and 
more recently associated non-sarcomeric genes like BAG3, 
FHOD3 and FLNC. Future curation efforts of published 
LVNC genotype–phenotype data should also critically 
evaluate the clinical characteristics of patients and their 
family members to better distinguish between cases with 
isolated LVNC cardiomyopathy and a likely DCM/HCM 
diagnosis with hypertrabeculation. For now, genetic test-
ing in patients diagnosed with LVNC cardiomyopathy is 
recommended to include all established DCM/HCM genes 
[33, 35, 43], although variants detected in genes not previ-
ously or robustly associated with LVNC phenotypes should 
be interpreted with caution and in the context of the clini-
cal features of the patient.

Isolated LVNC Genotypes
As described above, research into the genetic aetiology of 
LVNC has identified a number of genes and variant classes that 
appear to be solely, or at least predominantly, associated with 
an isolated LVNC cardiomyopathy phenotype. Thus far, these 
LVNC-specific genotypes have been limited to small subsets of 
patients. Further studies are needed to fully elucidate the genetic 
architecture of this specific cardiomyopathy, including further 
validation of the putative LVNC-specific genes and variant 
classes, identification of specific missense variants in sarcom-
eric genes that are distinctly associated with LVNC phenotypes 
and the discovery of novel genetic factors. Beyond the diagnos-
tic hypertrabeculation phenotype, the clinical characteristics of 
patients with these genotypes also need further exploration, to 
define the natural history of disease, associated events/outcomes 
and how these compare with more well-defined cardiomyopathy 
subtypes. Given the relative rarity of such cases in overall LVNC 
cardiomyopathy cohorts, multicentre collaborations and patient 
registries are likely to be essential for these efforts.
Differential Diagnosis with Genetic Testing
The 2022 Consensus Statement on the state of genetic test-
ing for cardiac diseases suggested limited utility for LVNC 
cardiomyopathy [43]. For diagnostic and prognostic purposes, 
it stated that genetic testing “may be useful” ( +) for LVNC, 
while it was not recommended/indicated/useful for therapeu-
tic purposes. The limited impact on diagnosis implied by the 
consensus statement is perhaps surprising, given the potential 
for differential diagnosis across the wide array of genetic con-
ditions that could present as LVNC cardiomyopathy, including 
arrhythmia phenotypes (RYR2, HCN4) and syndromic condi-
tions (LAMP2, NNT, TAFAZZIN, TBX5) (Fig. 4). By contrast,

incorporate the specifics of these associations however (i.e.

into account the context of the clinical features of the patient 
being tested, in order to minimise the uncertainty and noise 
that can be generated from large panel tests.
Conclusion
Although LVNC remains a complex and incompletely 
understood phenotype, we are developing a clearer view of 
how hypertrabeculation occurs, its role in physiological and 
pathological processes and the diverse conditions associ-
ated with LVNC-related cardiac dysfunction. Robust genetic 
analyses and large-scale studies have played a key role in 
clarifying the different pathologies than can lead to hyper -
trabeculation phenotypes and identifying specific genotypes 
that may underlie a distinct and isolated LVNC cardiomyo-
pathy subtype. Comprehensive clinical genetic testing that 
utilises these insights offers great potential for aiding diag-
nosis and clinical management in LVNC patients.
