# CXCL9 and IL-18: potential biomarkers for efficacy evaluation in refractory hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis treated with RED (ruxolitinib, emapalumab and dexamethasone).
**Authors:** Lan X, Wei N, Wang J, Wang Z
**Journal:** Ann Hematol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00277-025-06336-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00277-025-06336-8)

## Content

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a severe inflammatory disorder characterized by excessive cytokine release. More than 30% of HLH cases are refractory to frontline therapy. Unfortunately, there is no universally accepted second-line regimen, and about 30% of patients fail to respond to current salvage treatments. Moreover, evidence guiding alternative therapies and the optimal timing for switching to new treatments in refractory patients is limited. This study retrospectively analyzed the efficacy and safety of the RED regimen (ruxolitinib, emapalumab, and dexamethasone) in 15 refractory HLH patients who had failed at least two previous salvage therapies. Overall, eight (53.3%) patients achieved partial remission, and four of those eight proceeded to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Notably, pre-RED levels of C-X-C motif chemokine 9 (CXCL9) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) were significantly higher in patients who later responded to RED, suggesting that these biomarkers may predict a better response. We also observed that, for eight partial-remission patients, hemoglobin, fibrinogen, aspartate aminotransferase, calcium, and CXCL9 levels tracked well with early therapeutic responses (one to two weeks). No grade 3 or higher adverse effects were linked to the RED regimen. This comprehensive investigation of the RED approach in HLH, although small in sample size, supports the possibility that RED can serve as an effective and relatively safe salvage therapy for refractory HLH.

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare yet potentially fatal syndrome characterized by an overwhelming cytokine storm and hyperinflammation. Excessive activation of cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) and macrophages leads to massive production of inflammatory factors and can cause rapid multiorgan dysfunction. Clinically, HLH is broadly divided into primary (familial) HLH and secondary (acquired) HLH. Primary HLH is typically seen in children and is often attributed to genetic mutations (e.g., in perforin or other genes governing cytotoxic function). Nevertheless, primary HLH can occasionally remain latent until adulthood, underscoring the importance of genetic screening in suspected cases. Secondary HLH is more common in adults and is typically triggered by infections, malignancies, or autoimmune diseases. The key to its treatment is to induce remission of HLH status while actively seeking the primary disease. Often, through good control of the primary disease, HLH does not recur. For patients with refractory HLH caused by unexplained HLH, malignant tumors, and EBV infection, allogeneic HSCT may be the ultimate method to cure.

Currently, the most frequently referenced protocols for HLH therapy in clinical trials have been HLH-1994 and HLH-2004, both primarily developed for pediatric populations [ 1 ]. HLH-1994 typically includes etoposide, dexamethasone, and intrathecal methotrexate, with cyclosporine added during continuation therapy. HLH-2004 is similar to HLH-1994 but modifies certain aspects of therapy, including earlier cyclosporine and intrathecal prophylaxis. While these protocols have improved outcomes in children, a substantial fraction of adult HLH patients—some estimates exceed 30%—remain refractory, often necessitating salvage treatments [ 2 ].

For the treatment of refractory HLH, there is no standard salvage regimen, and there are just case reports or small sample sizes of clinical trials concerning salvage therapy after first-line treatment failure [ 3 ]. Therefore, effective salvage therapies for refractory HLH are needed. Salvage regimens include doxorubicin-etoposide-methylprednisolone (DEP), asparaginase-DEP (L-DEP), ruxolitinib-based DEP (R-DEP), checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., nivolumab), and other novel approaches. However, robust clinical trials remain scarce. Furthermore, the timing of introducing second-line or “alternative” regimens in HLH patients with insufficient response to frontline therapy is still uncertain [ 4– 7 ]. However, long-term and high-dose methylprednisolone in the DEP regimen may cause some side effects, such as gastrointestinal hemorrhage, infection, hypertension, and diabetes, among others [ 5 ]. Worse more, traditional chemotherapy drugs may increase the incidence of side effects and para tumors in patients.

As we mentioned before, HLH was characterized by the excessive production of cytokines, including interferon-γ (INF-γ) and interleukins 2, 6, and 10 (IL-2, IL-6, and IL-10) [ 8 ]. Some target drugs of cytokines have been used in HLH. JAK1/2 inhibitor ruxolitinib, which has already widely used in the treatment of HLH [ 5 , 6 , 9 ], has been proven can suppress the overaction of macrophage in HLH by mouse model [ 10 ] and showed a good therapeutic effect in children and adults HLH [ 6 , 9 ]. Emapalumab, a monoclonal antibody that noncompetitively inhibits IFN-γ, has been approved for the treatment of pediatric and adult patients with primary HLH who refractory, recurrent, or progressive disease or intolerance to HLH therapy [ 11 , 12 ]. However, as multiple cytokines play a role in the hyperinflammatory cytokine storm of HLH, targeting a single cytokine may not be sufficient to ameliorate the tissue inflammation and organ dysfunction that accompany the disorder. Because multiple cytokines drive HLH’s “cytokine storm,” combining agents with different immunomodulatory targets could yield synergistic efficacy. Hence, we designed and retrospectively analyzed the efficacy and safety of a combined regimen of Ruxolitinib + Emapalumab + low-dose Dexamethasone (RED) in 15 patients with highly refractory HLH from Beijing Friendship Hospital. Most of the patients had received current first-line and second-line rescue treatments, and their HLH status did not show significant improvement or HLH recurred after a temporary remission. This study investigates the clinical efficacy and adverse reactions of the combination of ruxolitinib and emapalumab in the treatment of refractory HLH under a small dose of dexamethasone background.

We retrospectively reviewed records of 15 refractory HLH patients who have received RED regimen. All these 15 patients fulfilled at least five of the eight HLH-2004 criteria for HLH (fever > 38.5℃, continue 7 days; splenomegaly; cytopenia affecting at least two of three lineages: hemoglobin < 90 g/L (or < 100 g/L in infants aged < 4 weeks), platelets < 100 × 109/L, and absolute neutrophil count < 1.0 × 109/L; hypertriglyceridemia (> 265 mg/dl) and/or hypofibrinogenemia (< 150 mg/dl); hemophagocytosis evident in bone marrow, spleen, lymph nodes, or liver; low or absent natural killer cell cytotoxicity, serum ferritin > 500 ng/mL; elevated soluble IL-2 receptor alpha (soluble CD25). All patients underwent comprehensive screening for rheumatic immune disease, malignancies, and infections. Thirteen were adults (> 18 years old), and two were adolescents (16 and 17 years old). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Friendship Hospital and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Because standardized definitions of “refractory HLH” vary, in prior studies, it is accepted that the absence of response to standard treatment at 2 to 3 weeks is often a sign of refractory HLH. However, in this study, all these patients have either tried the second-line salvage therapy or disease progression quickly, 2 to 3 weeks may be too long to evaluate the disease, so we defined refractory HLH as patients who did not achieve partial response after 1 or 2 salvage line HLH therapy or with a rapid disease progression, which at least 2 professional clinicians had assessed.

To assess the clinical outcomes of the RED regimen, laboratory markers and clinical symptoms were serially accessed before and after 1 week and 2 weeks of the treatment, including fever, splenomegaly, cytopenia affecting at least two lineages, AST, ALT, triglycerides, fibrinogen, ferritin, natural killer (NK)-cell cytotoxic activity, sCD25, levels of cytokines, including TNF-alpha, IL-6, ST2, IL-10, CD163, IFN-gamma, IL-1RA, IL-17/IL-17 A, IL-8, CXCL9, IL-18. An association with EBV was evaluated by EBV DNA real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis. Completing rheumatic immunity, tumor, and infection-related screening have been tested in unknown cause HLH patients. All the patients have received bone marrow aspirate, flow cytometry, and chromosome test, and 8 patients received whole exome (WES) or whole gene sequencing (WGS).

Treatment strategies were based on RED protocols, Ruxolitinib was used at least 10 mg, twice a day for adults and 5 mg, twice a day for children, whose dose would increase when the treatment effect was not good. The dose of emapalumab was started at 1 mg/kg, which could be increased to 2 mg/kg if unsatisfactory improvement in clinical condition happened. According to the instruction of emapalumab, patients who are not receiving baseline dexamethasone treatment, begin at a daily dose of 5 to 10 mg/m2. Still, in this study, all patients were treated with high-dose and long-term glucocorticoids, including dexamethasone and methylprednisolone, and some cases showed a deficient of platelet and fibrinogen, which may increase the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding, so we tapered the dose of dexamethasone to 5 mg. Treatment responses were evaluated after 1 and 2 weeks of treatment. Patients exhibiting a complete or partial response at 1 or 2 weeks were given HSCT if possible or given maintenance therapy with low-dose steroids and ruxolitinib for 2 to 4 more weeks, while patients who did not achieve a complete or partial response were considered for continuation received RED regimen. Four patients who showed a PR were treated with allogeneic HCT, three using Bu/Cy + VP16 regimen, including busulfan (0.8 mg/kg q6h for 3 days), cyclophosphamide (1 g/kg for 2 days), VP-16 (10 mg/kg, for 1 day), and anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG, Thymoglobulin®, 8 mg/kg for 3 days), and one used TT + VP-16/Bu/Cy regimen, including thiotepa (5 mg/kg, for 1 day), busulfan (0.8 mg/kg q6h for 3 days), cyclophosphamide (1 g/kg for 2 days), VP-16 (10 mg/kg, for 1 day), and anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG, Thymoglobulin®, 8 mg/kg for 3 days).

As we mentioned above, all quantifiable signs and laboratory abnormalities included in the diagnostic criteria were collected to assess therapeutic response. A complete response (CR) was defined as normalization of all the parameters of HLH, including sCD25, ferritin, triglyceride, hemoglobin, neutrophil counts, platelet counts, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). A partial response (PR) was defined as at least a 25% improvement in 2 or more quantifiable symptoms and laboratory tests: sCD25 was more than 1.5-fold decreased; ferritin and triglyceride decreased at least 25%; for patients with an initial neutrophil count of < 0.5 × 109/L, a PR was defined as an increase by at least 100% to > 0.5 × 109/L; while for patients with a neutrophil count of 0.5 to 2.0 × 109/L, an increase by at least 100% to > 2.0 × 109/L was considered a PR; and for patients with ALT < 400 U/L, PR was defined as an ALT decrease of at least 50%. A 50% worsening in two or more symptoms or laboratory abnormalities was considered a progressive disease (PD), and failure to fulfill any of these criteria was considered a stable disease (SD). Other observational indicators included Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-DNA, total bilirubin, and ultrasonic spleen thickness. Adverse events were monitored daily, focusing on cytopenias, infections, and bleeding. Infusion-related reactions and other possible toxicities of ruxolitinib or emapalumab were also assessed.

SPSS 27.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY) and Prism 10 were used for statistical analysis. All data were represented by means ± standard deviations and minimal with maximal. Comparison of multiple samples between groups was performed by T-test and one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data and comparison for not normally distributed data between multiple samples and groups was performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. P < 0.05 was considered a significant difference and P < 0.01 was considered very significant.

Fifteen patients (10 males, 5 females) with a median age of 33 years (range, 16–51) were included. Thirteen were > 18 years old, two were adolescents. The main clinical features of these 15 patients are summarized in Table 1. All these patients started with fever, which was greater than 38.5 ℃, lasting for more than 7 days. For cytopenia of 3 lineages in the peripheral blood, all patients showed a decrease in hemoglobin levels, and 13 patients had thrombocytopenia, however, only 46.7% of patients had neutropenia. 13 patients presented splenomegaly, including one with severe splenomegaly and one with splenectomy. Compared with splenomegaly, there were only four patients showed hepatomegaly. 11 patients had hypofibrinogenemia and hypertriglyceridemia, which comprised three cases that showed hypertriglyceridemia between 2.25 mmol/L and 3.0 mmol/L. All these patients increased levels of ferritin and decreased albumin levels, contained 5 patients’ ALBs were less than 30 g/L. As important diagnostic indicators, soluble CD25, NK cell activity, and bone marrow aspiration were done in all patients, 10 patients showed a high sCD25 level, 9 patients presented a low NK cell activity and 13 cases had hemophagocytosis in the bone marrow.

The underlying disease of HLH included 9 cases of EBV-associated HLH, 2 cases were due to adult-onset still disease, 1 case was other than EBV infection-associated HLH, two cases of unknown cause HLH, and one case of primary HLH combined with EBV infection (EBV-associated pHLH). Seven patients had been treated with the HLH-94 regimen and 12 patients had received the DEP-baseline regimen with or without other drugs. 14 patients had received no less than one regimen and did not achieve at least PR. The only patient who received the RED regimen directly due to rapid progression of the HLH. The time interval from initial treatment to RED was 68 days, ranging from 12 to 648 days.

Of the 15 patients, 8 achieved PR after RED, while 7 showed progressive disease (PD). We compared multiple baseline cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, ST2, IL-10, CD163, IFN-γ, IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA), IL-17/IL-17 A, IL-8, CXCL9, and IL-18, between the “responder” (PR) and “non-responder” (PD) groups. Intriguingly, only baseline CXCL9 and IL-18 were significantly higher in patients who went on to respond (Fig. 1). Other clinical or laboratory parameters did not differ significantly at baseline, suggesting that extremely elevated CXCL9 and IL-18 may predict better response to ruxolitinib + emapalumab–based therapy. These findings are also consistent with the etiology related to the inflammatory cytokine storm.

By using the RED regimen, the overall response rate of these 15 refractory HLH patients was 53.3% (8/15 patients), with all in partial response. The peak body temperature of these 8 PR patients decreased rapidly after the start of the RED regimen. The time of recovery to normal body temperature ranged from 24 h to 5 days. There were 5 cases of EBV-associated HLH, 1 case of EBV-associated pHLH and 2 cases of HLH caused by autoimmune disease achieved PR. Seven patients were nonresponsive to the treatment. In addition, 4 of the partial response patients with EBV-HLH received allogeneic HSCT and 2 patients survived. Another two EBV-associated patients who achieved PR could not accept HSCT, one because of cardiac coronary dilation and the other because of bone marrow necrosis, which may be both due to chronic active EB virus (CAEBV) infection, which suggests that we should accelerate transplant preparation in EBV-positive HLH patients.

On the other hand, for indicators of HLH diagnostic criteria, even though the body temperature, level of Hb, Fbg and ferritin has improved in 2 weeks of therapy, only body temperature, Fbg and ferritin have shown significant improvement in both one and two weeks, which emphasized the importance of detecting the change of Fbg, ferritin and body temperature to assess the disease progression and therapeutic effect at the early stage of treatment (Fig. 2). As we mentioned before, splenomegaly was observed in 13 patients, and hepatomegaly was observed in 4 patients with ultrasound examination. Due to the short treatment time of two weeks, no relief was observed in the symptoms of hepatosplenomegaly. All 15 patients underwent bone marrow aspirate, flow cytometry, and chromosome analysis. Fourteen had evaluable samples (one patient had severe EBV-induced marrow necrosis). Among these 14, 9 had phenotypically abnormal lymphocytes (7 with aberrant NK cells, 2 with aberrant T cells), and 7 had complex karyotypes.

8 patients received WGS/WES, all existing gene mutations related to immunodeficiency, including Omenn syndrome, combined cellular and humoral immune defects with granulomas, Alpha/beta T-cell lymphopenia with gamma/delta T-cell expansion, severe cytomegalovirus infection, and autoimmunity. 3 patients showed mutation of chronic granulomatous disease 1, autosomal recessive. However, the function of these mutated sites in HLH was unclear. Only one primary HLH patients showed HLH related gene mutation clearly (Table 4).

All patients had cytopenia before the RED regimen, among which 13 patients had cytopenia in 2 or more lineages. All patients have received bone marrow biopsy before the treatment. In bone marrow, 11 patients showed active hyperplasia while 2 cases showed myelosuppression because of salvage chemotherapy. For these 2 patients, neutrophil in 1 patient, platelet in 1 patient, and hemoglobin in both patients were lower at 1 week after treatment than before the RED regimen. The last patient showed bone marrow necrosis because of the infection of CAEBV. The level of three lineages of blood was continuously reduced during 1 and 2 weeks of RED treatment.

Before using emapalumab, we detected the infection of active mycobacterium, histoplasma capsulatum, salmonella, or leishmania, and excluded the evidence of latent tuberculosis and HIV antibody serological test positive. During the treatment, we detect the body temperature and C-reactive protein to monitor the infection. In Table 2, there were no statistical differences in CRP before and 1 or 2 weeks after RED treatment, which means that, under appropriate monitoring, the use of RED would not increase the risk of infection for HLH patients.

Before the RED treatment, PT was normal in all patients; activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) was prolonged for more than 10 s in 1 patient; Fbg level was lower than 1.0 g/L in 4 patients and the platelet level was lower than 50 × 109/L in 8 patients. Appropriate supportive treatment has been provided, including OB test for three days, infusion of platelet, preventive hemostatic therapy, and use of proton pump inhibitors (PPI). However, there was still 1 case of grade 2 lower gastrointestinal bleeding after therapy, which had thrombocytopenia (PLT 15 × 109/L) and abnormality in the coagulation indicators (Fbg 1.49 g/L).

In our study, we did not find any adverse reactions related to the RED treatment, including common infusion reactions, fever, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and other symptoms. Among these 15 patients, two patients had underlying diseases such as hypertension, and taking antihypertensive drugs on time could maintain blood pressure at normal levels.

As we mentioned before, HLH is often rapidly fatal if the cytokine storm is not curtailed early. Although the HLH-94 (and later HLH-2004) protocols have long served as frontline therapy—particularly in pediatric HLH—these regimens do not yield durable remission in approximately 30% of patients, and the salvage armamentarium remains limited [ 2 ]. Therefore, designing salvage regimens to enable more patients to achieve remission has become an important research subject in HLH, especially in adults HLH. In recent years, more and more scholars have begun to explore the ways out of HLH-94 treatment, including new regimens composed of traditional chemotherapy drugs and new targeted therapies to control the status of HLH, such as DEP [ 4 ], L-DEP, RU-DEP [ 5 ], CD52 monoclonal antibodies, emapalumab [ 11 , 12 ], and ruxolitinib [ 6 , 9 ]. However, the response rate of current salvage regimens was around 40–70%, the alternative time point of therapy for these no-responded patients is not clear. The therapeutic strategies of HLH mainly involves short-term control of inflammatory cytokines and long-term treatment of the primary disease. For different diseases causes, primary HLH is mainly relieved through allogeneic HSCT. In secondary HLH patients, the prognosis of tumor and EBV associated patients is often worse than that of rheumatic immune and other than EBV infected patients, which also explains why most of the patients were EBV-associated HLH in this study. For this part of patients, allogeneic HSCT is the ultimate cure. The ability to effectively control hemophagocytic status in the short term and achieve better remission before allogeneic HSCT directly determines the success rate of the later treatment. In our study, an EBV-HLH patients, who did not respond to HLH-94, DEP and L-DEP regimen, even though we used RED treatment and achieved partial relief after 2 weeks of timely treatment, because of long-term HLH active status and high burden EBV infection, which caused bone marrow necrosis, the patient also lost the opportunity to receive allogeneic HSCT. Enhance, the time of change treatment and the biomarkers which could sensitively predict the therapeutic effect are much more important for refractory HLH patients.

As we all know, emapalumab has achieved big success in primary HLH. But there is little study about its efficiency in secondary HLH. This is the first study to evaluation the clinical effect of ruxolitinib combined with emapalumab under small dose dexamethasone in HLH. For the RED regimen, firstly, there are a lot of research in mouse model and clinical trials have proved the role of ruxolitinib in primary and second HLH, and ruxolitinib can reverse glucocorticoid resistance [ 13– 15 ]. Secondly, as a new FDA approved HLH treatment drug, emapalumab can directly decrease the level of IFN-γ in HLH [ 16 ]. However, this new kind of target drug also increased economic burden, combination with other drugs can improve the clinical efficacy of single drug, reduce expense and the side effects caused by high-dose of emapalumab, such as infections. Finally, low-dose glucocorticoids can not only maintain and regulate the immune environment, but also reduce the side effects of high-dose glucocorticoids such as hyperglycemia, hypertension and gastrointestinal bleeding, especially in HLH patients whose thrombocytopenia and hypofibrinogenemia are very common. Here, we explored a three-drug combination: Ruxolitinib (a JAK1/2 inhibitor), Emapalumab (IFN-γ blockade), and Dexamethasone (low-dose) in 15 patients with highly refractory HLH. Despite the severity of disease and multiple salvage failures, the overall response rate was 53.3%. Four partial responders proceeded to HSCT, and half of those transplanted survived. This experience suggests that RED may be a promising second- or third-line regimen—particularly valuable as a bridge to HSCT for EBV-driven or familial HLH.

There is no international recommended guideline for refractory HLH, especially for patients who has received more than twice salvage regimens. The appropriate time point to alternative treatment in patients who were nonresponsive to a standard or current salvage HLH regimen should be investigated in further clinical trials. International HLH academic views reported that the liver function, hemogram, serum ferritin, sCD25, and sCD163 were main signs of HLH active status. The newly treated patients did not achieved PR or CR after 2 to 3 weeks of first-line treatment, salvage therapy should be considered [ 17 , 18 ]. The clinical research in our center also reported that most deaths in the active stage of HLH occurred in 4 weeks after treatment initiation. In the present study, because all the patients were failed to frontline and salvage therapy or progressed quickly, it’s too long to wait for 2 to 3 weeks to evaluated therapeutic effect, we defined refractory HLH as patients who did not achieve partial response after 1 or 2 salvage line HLH therapy. For the therapeutic indicators of refractory HLH patients, we compared the responded group and non-responded group, we found that the level of CXCL9 and IL-18 showed a significant difference before RED regimen, which suggested patients with high level of CXCL9 and IL-18 had more possibility benefited from RED. This is the first study reported the value of CXCL9 and IL-18 in target drugs of HLH. And by comparing other clinical parameters during therapy, we also found that the number of WBC, neutrophils, Fbg, the levels of AST, and ferritin were significantly improved after 2 weeks of treatment, suggesting the importance of live function in evaluating therapeutic effect. Moreover, the body temperature, Fbg and ferritin have sensitively shown improvement in the early stage of treatment. We also compared the level of multiple cytokines, unfortunately, we did not find the difference of CD163 before and after therapy. However, the level of CXCL9 showed a statistic difference at later (2 weeks after) RED treatment. As Franco Locatelli reported that CXCL9 levels decreased about 30% of the baseline level on day 5 of emapalumab treatment and that low CXCL9 levels were associated with a response at the end of treatment [ 11 ]. Even though CXCL9 didn’t reduce so quickly, but it has been a stable therapeutic biomarker which can evaluate the responsive rate in single and combination treatment of emapalumab. Therefore, assess the condition of patients after 2 weeks of RED regimen might be necessary to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy and determine whether altered the treatment regimen.

The interesting thing is that, by analysis the electrolyte level, we found that Ca2+ was also a predictor to evaluate the therapeutic response. Even though the electrolyte of HLH patients were not stable, which need to treat in time, it’s the first time to found that the change of Ca2+ may also reflect the status of treatment in HLH. The mechanism of changes in serum Ca2+ concentration still needs to be clarified, and possible reasons include the characteristic of HLH patients leaded abnormal tissue function, which reducing absorption of Ca2+ in the gastrointestinal or inhibition and resistance of parathyroid hormone secretion. A large amount of cytokines released can also affect the transfer of Ca2+ by influencing calcium channels, calcium pumps, and cell membrane permeability through various pathways [ 19 ]. However, clinical physicians generally ignore the significance of serum Ca2+ concentration in HLH and usually mistakenly believe it is intracranial hemorrhage or lesions. Therefore, exploring the mechanism of serum Ca2+ concentration in HLH is necessary to evaluate therapeutic responsive rate and predict prognosis.

In this study, we reported RED is a very effective treatment, with extraordinary performance in refractory patients, and we are envisioning the possibility of advancing the protocol in future research efforts so that the RED protocol can be articulated as soon as a patient’s first-line treatment is ineffective, thus getting more time and opportunity for the subsequent treatment. By comparing the changes in various indicators before treatment, 1 week and 2 weeks after treatment, we found that some markers may provide indications of possible remission in the early stages of treatment, while others can only gradually approach remission after completing 2 weeks of treatment. Therefore, it is still necessary to quickly evaluate the efficacy of patients through different indicators and change the rescue plan or increase the dosage of the rescue plan at the appropriate time. Limitations of this study include the small sample size, retrospective design, and heterogeneity in prior treatments. Larger, prospective trials of RED (or similar combinations) are needed to define optimal dosing, duration, and selection criteria. The timing of switching from frontline therapy to RED also deserves formal evaluation. Meanwhile, given the severity and rapid progression of refractory HLH, adopting an early switch for non-responders (e.g., by 1–2 weeks if no partial response is observed) could potentially improve outcomes.

In this retrospective study of 15 patients with highly refractory HLH, the combination of ruxolitinib, emapalumab, and low-dose dexamethasone (RED) demonstrated a 53.3% response rate. Patients with very high CXCL-9 and IL-18 at baseline appeared more likely to respond. RED was generally safe, without severe infectious complications or other high-grade toxicities, and enabled four patients to proceed to allogeneic HSCT. These findings support further prospective evaluation of this triple combination as a salvage option in refractory HLH. Close monitoring of fibrinogen, ferritin, CXCL-9, and IL-18 may guide early assessment of response and the timely addition or escalation of immunomodulatory therapies.
