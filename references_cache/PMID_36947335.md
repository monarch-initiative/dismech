# Multiple Immune Defects in Two Patients with Novel DOCK2 Mutations Result in Recurrent Multiple Infection Including Live Attenuated Virus Vaccine.
**Authors:** Li W, Sun Y, Yu L, Chen R, Gan R, Qiu L, Sun G, Chen J, Zhou L, Ding Y, Du H, Shu Z, Zhang Z, Tang X, Chen Y, Zhao X, Zhao Q, An Y
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-023-01466-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-023-01466-y)

## Content

The dedicator of cytokinesis 2(DOCK2) protein, an atypical guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEFs), is a member of the DOCKA protein subfamily. DOCK2 protein deficiency is characterized by early-onset lymphopenia, recurrent infections, and lymphocyte dysfunction, which was classified as combined immune deficiency with neutrophil abnormalities as well. The only cure is hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Here, we report two patients harboring four novel DOCK2 mutations associated with recurrent infections including live attenuated vaccine-related infections. The patient's condition was partially alleviated by symptomatic treatment or intravenous immunoglobulin. We also confirmed defects in thymic T cell output and T cell proliferation, as well as aberrant skewing of T/B cell subset TCR-Vβ repertoires. In addition, we noted neutrophil defects, the weakening of actin polymerization, and BCR internalization under TCR/BCR activation. Finally, we found that the DOCK2 protein affected antibody affinity although with normal total serum immunoglobulin. The results reported herein expand the clinical phenotype, the pathogenic DOCK2 mutation database, and the immune characteristics of DOCK2-deficient patients.

Dedicator of cytokinesis 2 (DOCK2), a member of the DOCKA subfamily within the DOCK family, is expressed abundantly in hematopoietic and lymphoid tissues, particularly in T, B, NK, and monocyte. DOCK2 is a major guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEFs) required for activation of GTPases [1]. Activation of GTPases is critical for the development and activation of the immune system to resist pathogenic microorganisms [2]. Thus, DOCK2 plays an important role in development, migration, and activation of immune cells.

In 2015, five unrelated children with DOCK2 mutations were identified [3]. Until recently, only 16 DOCK2-deficient patients have been reported in the literature, and all had a severe phenotype characterized by early-onset of recurrent, invasive viral, and bacterial infections associated with T and B cell lymphopenia [3–10]. Phosphorylated DOCK2 is required for recruitment of Rac GTPase to the cell membrane, and the activated Rac participates in the regulation of various cellular functional activities [11]. Also, DOCK2 is required for activation of antifungal signaling in macrophages [12]. Production of interferon (IFN)-alpha and -lambda by peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from DOCK2-deficient patients falls upon ex vivo exposure to herpes simplex virus 1 or vesicular stomatitis virus [13]. Overall, the phenotype of human DOCK2 deficiency is reminiscent of observations in DOCK2-/-mice [14]. However, the small number of reported cases means that the clinical and molecular manifestations are not fully clear.

Here, we report DOCK2 defects in two children from China. Both carried a compound heterozygous mutation of the DOCK2 gene. Our patients suffered from multiple infection including live attenuated virus vaccine. In addition, we analyzed the patients’ immunophenotypes in detail. We present a comprehensive analysis of the effects of DOCK2 protein on immune cells and provide a theoretical basis for further research into DOCK2. The data presented herein expand the phenotype of DOCK2 deficiency.

Diagnosis of DOCK2 deficiency was based on clinical features, targeted gene panel sequencing, and expression of the DOCK2 protein. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All study participants and healthy controls (HC), or their guardians, provided written informed consent to participate. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University.

Patient 1 was diagnosed using MyGenostics I030: Immune System Overall Solution V4 analysis. This gene panel includes 423 genes according to IUIS classification. Patient 2 used whole genome sequencing of Beijing Zhiin Orient Company.

Heparinized peripheral blood samples were obtained from patients and their parents. Cells were precipitated by dextran, and residual red blood cells were lysed in hypotonic saline. The remaining cells were granulocytes, which were washed in Krebs ringer phosphate buffer (KRG; pH 7.3; 120 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 1.7 mM KH2PO4, 8.3 mM NaH2PO4, and 10 mM glucose), resuspended in KRG supplemented with 1 mm Ca2+, and kept on ice until use.

The production of ROS was determined to use chemiluminescence (CL) and analyzed by a microplate reader (synergy H1 multi-mode reader, BioTek, USA) [15]. Neutrophils (3 × 106) were suspended in 1 mL KRG solution. In white 96-well plates (Costar), prepare the mixture of KRG 138 µL, luminol (Sigma) 20 µL, and 2µL SOD (50 U/ml) (S8410); add 20 µL of cell suspensions; put it on the microplate reader; preheat at 37 ℃ for 5 min; add 20 µL of stimulant ionomycin (Cayman); and detect endogenous ROS. The exogenous ROS was detected with 130 µL KRG, 20 µL isoluminol (A8264), 10 µL HRP(4U/ml) (Calbiochem), 20 µL cells, and finally 20 µL stimulants PMA. Use prism software to make a line chart and analyze the data. CL values are expressed as relative fluorescence intensity units.

H2O2 was detected by dihydrorhodamine (DHR)-1, 2, 3 flow cytometry. Briefly, blood samples from patients and their parents were incubated with PMA and DHR for 20 min at 37 °C. Next, the samples were added to an erythrocyte lysis solution (TIANGEN China) for 15 min to remove erythrocytes. Finally, cells were analyzed by a FACS Canto II flow cytometer. The results are expressed as a stimulation index (SI = geometric mean fluorescence intensity of PMA-incubated neutrophils/geometric mean fluorescence intensity of PBS-incubated neutrophils).

Neutrophils from patients and their parents were stimulated (or not) with PMA and cultured in black 96-well plates for a predetermined time. Next, SYTOX green (2.5 μM) was added for 5 min, 30 min, or 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 h. 0.1%Triton X-100 and KRG + solutions were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. The specific method has been described previously [16].

DNA was extracted from whole blood using standard protocols, and T cell receptor rearrangement excision circles (TRECs) and Kappa deletion element recombination circles (KRECs) were quantified by real-time PCR. The reference gene was RNase P. All RT qPCR analyses met the quality requirements of a similar slope and an R2 value > 0.99.

Peripheral mononuclear lymphocytes (2 × 106) were prepared from patient 2. The cells were lyzed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) containing phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride(PMSF) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC). Protein expression was examined by western blotting with an anti-DOCK2 (Abcam) rabbit polyclonal antibody.

PBMCs were isolated from whole blood and labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimide (CFSE). T cells were stimulated with phytohemagglutinin (PHA) (Sigma), and B cells were stimulated by CpG (InvivoGen) combined with fab2 (Jackson), for 72 h at 37 °C/5% CO2. Proliferation was measured in a flow cytometer.

CD4 + T cells and B cells were isolated from PBMCs using an isolation kit (Stem Cell Technologies) according to standard protocols. The selected cells were suspended in PBS solution containing 2% FBS and dropped onto polylysine-coated slides. The CD4 + T cells were stimulated by mouse anti-human CD3 (10 μg/ml; BioLegend) and mouse anti-human CD28 (10 μg/ml; BioLegend) at 37 °C. Stimulation was stopped by addition of PFA at 0, 5, 10, and 20 min. B cells were stimulated for different times with monobiotinylated goat-anti-human IgM + IgG Fab2 (Jackson Laboratories). Phalloidin-iFluor 488 (1:140) (Abcam) was used to stain F-actin, and DAPI (Beyotime) was used to stain nuclei. The mean fluorescence intensity of each protein was measured by the NIS-Elements AR 3.2 software.

Each T cell receptor (TCR) Vβ fragment was amplified with primers specific for 23 Vβ, and a 5 ′fam-labeled CB probe, as previously described [4, 11]. The PCR products were sequenced by Sangong Biotechnology Co. (Shanghai, China). Gene mapper v3.5 was used to analyze the data, and TCR Vβ diversity was evaluated using a scoring system, as described previously [17] (complexity score = (sum of all the peak heights/sum of the major peak heights) × number of major peaks. Major peaks were defined as those with amplitudes of at least 10% of the sum of all the peak heights).

Briefly, 150 µL of whole blood was divided into three aliquots to analyze circulating Th1 cells, Th2 cells, and Th17 cells, as described previously [18].

A human pertussis IgG antibody kit (BYabscience 30,881) was used for affinity testing. Briefly, five holes were allocated to each sample; 50 μl of sample was added to each hole and incubated at 37 °C for 50 min. The holes were washed five times with PBST buffer (PBS solution containing 0.05% tween), followed by addition of 100 μl of CH4N2S (0, 0.5, 1, 2, or 4 mol/L) at room temperature for 15 min. After washing three times, 50 μl of HRP (BYabscience 30,881) was added to each hole at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by washing five times. The OD value of each hole was measured in a microplate meter (Biotek synergy H1) at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Full-length wild-type (WT) DOCK2 cDNA was purchased from Youzeng Biotechnology, Changsha, China. Mutant DOCK2 cDNA was constructed by PCR mutagenesis and subcloned into the 7.1-pCMV-3 × Flag vector. Using the WT as a template, various missense and deletion mutants of DOCK2 were introduced by PCR. The mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The WT and mutant plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells. Cells were collected 24 h later, and proteins were extracted and analyzed by western blotting.

For detection of CD107A expression on NK cells, we resuscitated PBMCs frozen from patients and healthy controls and cultured them in a 37 ℃, 5% CO2 incubator for 2 h. Then, the stimulation group was co-cultured with K562 cells for 4 h. The cells were taken out for staining with CD3-Percp (300,326 Biolegend), CD56-PE (318,306 Biolegend), and CD107a-APC (328,620 Biolegend) at room temperature for 30 min. Then, the cell was analyzed by flow cytometer. For detection of IL-17A/IFN production, the frozen-stored PBMC of patients and healthy controls were resuscitated and cultured in a 37 ℃, 5% CO2 incubator for 4 h. Then, the stimulation group was activated with 1 μg/ml PMA (P8139 Sigma Aldrich), ionomycin (Inhion Invivogen) and Golgi stop (554,724 BD) for 4 h. The cells were taken out and incubated with CD3-Percp-cy5.5 (317,336 Biolegend) and CD8-BV510 (344,732 Biolegend), 4 ℃ for 30 min. After taking out the cells, they were fixed and punched (BD), and then stained with CD17A-PE (512,306 Biolegend) and IFN-γ-APC (502,512 Biolegend) for 1 h, then analyzed by flow cytometer.

Statistical significance was assessed using a Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test. All analyses were performed using the Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). P ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Patient 1 was a 5-year-old boy from a non-consanguineous family in China. He contracted pneumonia when he was 2 months old, which was confirmed by chest computer tomography(CT), manifested as fever and cough without definite pathogen. He suffered from perianal abscess at 3 months of age. A rash appeared all over the body at the age of 1 year and dark purple subcutaneous nodules appeared in the limbs at the age of 2 years, pathological examination of the nodules revealed CD4 + T cell infiltration (Fig. 1A). He contracted chickenpox 1 month after vaccination, which improved with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and acyclovir treatment. About 2 months later, 1 week after rotavirus vaccination, the patient suffered recurrent diarrhea secondary to rotavirus enteritis (based on examination of stool pathogens). Symptomatic treatment controlled his symptoms. He suffered recurrent attacks of pneumonia (about 5 to 6 times per year, each lasting about 1 week), confirmed by chest CT or X-ray. During hospitalization, his history included four episodes of otitis media and two of conjunctivitis. He hospitalized again due to fever and cough at 4.5 years of age, and a sputum culture revealed Hemophilus influenza. However, the patient did not receive regular IVIG replacement therapy or sulfonamides to prevent infection (due to poor adherence). He suffered pneumonia every 2–3 months. Patient 2 was a 4-year-old boy from a non-consanguineous family in China. After he was inoculated with the BCG vaccine after birth, he developed nodular redness. At the age of 2 months, he developed pneumonia confirmed by chest CT, with fever and cough. The pathogen of pneumonia has not been detected. He got the hand-foot-mouth disease at 1 year of age manifested as fever, oral herpes, and mouth ulcers, accompanied by drowsiness and irritability, after receiving the inactive the hand-foot-mouth vaccine, which should be a fortuitous connection. He was discharged after IVIG and symptomatic therapy. In addition, he suffered a bout of viral encephalitis at the age of 3 years characterized by seizure. The pathogen was unknown, but the encephalitis caused by herpes simplex virus and EB virus were excluded by CSF Q-PCR analysis. Brain MRI revealed possible medial temporal lobe sclerosis. The main manifestation was recurrent (2–3 times a year) upper respiratory tract infections, characterized by fever and cough; symptomatic treatment partially alleviates the symptom. Streptococcus pneumoniae at 3.6 years of age was identified by sputum culture. He has suffered two bouts of pneumonia caused by Mycoplasma pneumoniae at the age of 3.3 and 4 years, which were confirmed by PCR of sputum. Azithromycin and IVIG treatment were effective. The patient did not receive regular IVIG replacement therapy or sulfonamides to prevent infection. He has not suffered from pneumonia for nearly a year, and his growth has not been affected. (height, 110 cm; weight, 22 kg).

The DOCK2 protein belongs to the DOCKA subfamily and is expressed particularly by immune cells [19]. DOCK2, an atypical form of GEF, comprises a DOCK homology region (DHR-1) and a DHR-2 domain [20]. The DHR-1 domain binds to phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-triphosphate (PIP3), thereby mediating the GTP–GDP exchange required for Rac activation via the DHR-2 domain [21]. DOCK2 translocates from the cytoplasm to the cell membrane, upon which the DHR-2 domain activates Rac1/2 [22] which then binds to effector molecules and remodels the cytoskeleton, thereby regulating the development [23, 24], migration [25, 26], and proliferation of immune cells [27]. Its important role in the immune system means that patients with DOCK2 deficiency are characterized by early-onset severe viral infections, T cell lymphopenia, and functional defects in T, B, and NK cells [4]. DOCK2 also plays a role in protecting the host from enteric bacterial infections [28, 29] and is also associated with the proliferation of cancer cells [30, 31]. In the absence of HSCT, patients with DOCK2 deficiency often die from recurrent respiratory and digestive tract infections. Both of our patients onset at an early age and alive. They remained young and had not had a HSCT at this time. Recurrent respiratory infection and encephalitis were the main manifestations in patient 2, which were managed by symptomatic treatment. Patient 1 had more severe infections, including pneumonia, infectious diarrhea, and otitis media; these were partially alleviated by IVIG, although the administrations were irregular. Vaccine-associated infections in DOCK2 deficiency patients were firstly been reported by Dobbs K. et al. Our study confirmed this detection which P1 developed a vaccine strain related to varicella and rotavirus enteritis, suggesting that patients with DOCK2 deficiency should not be given live attenuated virus vaccines, at least chickenpox, rotavirus.

T cell defects are a hallmark of DOCK2-deficient patients [3]. Both patients reported herein showed a marked reduction in CD4 + T cells and naive CD4 + T cells, which is consistent with the decreased TRECs. We also found that TCR diversity was skewed, suggesting that T cell development was impaired, despite normal total T cell counts. Several studies show that DOCK2 affects the development of T cells. DOCK2 regulates colonization of the fetal thymus directly via chemokine-mediated recruitment of T lymphocyte progenitor cells [32], or indirectly through CD8 + TCR graft-facilitating cells(FCs) [33], a group of cells similar to plasmacytoid dendritic cell precursors. Also, DOCK2 deficiency can trigger the apoptosis of DP thymocytes [34]. Mitochondrial defects in DOCK2-deficient T cells also contribute to T cell lymphopenia [6]. In addition to affecting T cell development, DOCK2 deficiency disrupts the proliferation of T cells. DOCK2 is essential for TCR-mediated Rac activation and immunological synapse formation [14]; therefore, antigen-induced translocation of the TCR and lipid rafts to the APC interface is severely impaired in DOCK2-deficient T cells [34], resulting in a significant reduction in antigen-specific T cell proliferation. Also, TCR-mediated remodeling of the actin cytoskeleton was impaired in both patients. Both had an increased number of Th17 cells and a decreased number of Th1 cells, which might be related to higher levels of IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 [35, 36], and lower levels of IFN-α, IFN-γ, and IL-6 [13, 37]. These are also consistent with the description of Dobbs K. et al. [3]

B cell numbers and serum IgG levels in DOCK2-deficient patients are low to normal, although previous reports suggest high levels of IgM [7]. In our patients, levels of total IgG and IgG subclasses were normal. However, the specific antibody production was undetectable in patient 2 which is the same as the previous report. In addition, we firstly uncover the affinity of IgG for pertussis toxin in patient 1 was lower than the age-matched control, suggesting that the DOCK2 protein might be involved in the maturation of antibody affinity in germinal centers, which needs more data for confirmation. KRECs are cyclic DNA segments formed in B cells during maturation in the bone marrow (i.e., during V(D)J recombination) [38]. In patient 1, the absolute number of naive B cells was in the normal range, but the KRECs level was significantly decreased. For patient 2, naive B cell and transitional B cell numbers were low, while the KRECs just mildly decreased. These data indicate that the maturation of DOCK2-/- B cells might be disrupted, although few studies have examined how the DOCK2 protein affects the development of B cells [8, 23]. A previous mice study showed that in the absence of Rac1 and Rac2, B cell development is arrested at an IgD- transitional B cell stage and that transitional B cells are unable to migrate in response to chemokines and cannot enter the splenic white pulp [39]. DOCK2-deficient B cells show an almost complete absence of BCR-mediated Rac activation in the mouse model [40], which results in defects in formation of F-actin and internalization of BCR. Also, DOCK2 defects inhibit the activation of WASP and accelerate its degradation, resulting in reduced actin accumulation and early activation of B cells [23].

Neutrophil dysfunction in DOCK2-deficient patients has been reported recently [9]. Neutrophil chemotaxis and ROS production require activation of Rac small GTPases [41]. Here, we found both patients showed reduced ROS production. Previous publications report reduced formation of NETs by DOCK2-deficient mouse neutrophils [42]; however, NET production by patients 1 and 2 was comparable with that by HC. We also noted mild impaired respiratory burst activity in neutrophils from these two patients. Rac-GTP is part of the assembled NADPH oxidase complex; therefore, impaired activation of Rac in the absence of DOCK2 may lead to reduced NADPH oxidase activity. These functional changes in neutrophils are associated with defective F-actin recruitment, whereas the formation of NETs requires rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton [43].

In previous literatures, all patients who survived have undergone HSCT, so HSCT was the first choice for patients with DOCK2 deficiency. Besides, the gene therapy might be another choice in the future. For our patients, HSCT was a reasonable consideration because of the recurrent pneumonia and upper respiratory in such a young age.

Here, we describe the clinical manifestations, gene/protein expression, and immunological phenotype of two patients with novel DOCK2 Bi-allelic mutations. These data expand the existing clinical phenotype of DOCK2-deficient patients. At the same time, we report defects in B cell development, reduced antibody affinity, and changes in the number of Th1/Th17 cells, all of which suggest DOCK2 is broadly involved in the immune system. DOCK2 defect patients mainly manifested severe infection, regulatory immunoglobulin replacement, infection prophylaxis, and prohibition of live-attenuate vaccine as well HSCT were needed for better outcome.
