# Mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor suppresses ferroptosis to alleviate depressive-like behaviors via the PERK/ATF4 signaling pathway.
**Authors:** Dang R, Wang P, Li W, Wang M, Chen Y, Xiang Y, Fan L, Ren Y, He Y, Xie P
**Journal:** Redox Biol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.redox.2026.104303](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.redox.2026.104303)

## Content

Ferroptosis is being increasingly implicated in the pathophysiology of major depressive disorder (MDD). Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress is closely linked to ferroptosis, yet the endogenous regulators connecting ER dysfunction to ferroptosis during chronic stress remain unclear. Here, we report that the expression of mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF), an ER stress-inducible protein, was upregulated in the hippocampus (Hip) of male mice subjected to chronic social defeat stress (CSDS). Hippocampal MANF overexpression alleviated depressive-like behaviors and suppressed ferroptosis, whereas MANF knockdown promoted stress susceptibility and facilitated ferroptosis. Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) mimicked the protective effects of MANF overexpression and rescued MANF deficiency-induced phenotypes. Mechanistically, MANF inhibited PERK/ATF4 signaling, and overexpression of PERK or ATF4 partially counteracted MANF-mediated protection, whereas their knockdown rescued MANF deficiency-induced phenotypes. Furthermore, ATF4 increased the promoter activity of glutathione-specific γ-glutamylcyclotransferase 1 (CHAC1) in an ATF4-binding-site-dependent manner, thereby impairing the SLC7A11/GPX4 anti-ferroptotic defense axis and driving ferroptosis. These findings identify hippocampal MANF as an endogenous protective factor that suppresses stress-induced ferroptosis through the PERK/ATF4 pathway and position MANF as a potential therapeutic target for MDD.

Major depressive disorder (MDD), characterized by enduring low mood, anhedonia and loss of energy, is a prevalent, severely disabling psychiatric disorder [1,2]. Although conventional monoamine antidepressants are commonly used, they have notable limitations, including delayed onset of efficacy and treatment resistance in approximately one-third of patients [3]. These limitations underscore the urgency of unraveling the pathophysiological mechanisms of MDD and discovering new therapeutic targets beyond the monoaminergic system.

Accumulating evidence implicates ferroptosis in the pathophysiology of depression [4,5]. Ferroptosis is defined as a distinct form of regulated, iron-dependent cell death driven by lipid peroxidation [6]. It fundamentally differs from other types of cell death, such as apoptosis, autophagy and pyroptosis, in terms of its physiological, morphological and genetic features [7]. Dysregulation of iron and lipid metabolism has been observed in patients with depression [8,9]. Furthermore, our previous study demonstrated the downregulation of the expression of the central ferroptosis regulators glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) in mice subjected to CSDS, supporting the involvement of ferroptosis in depression-related models [10]. In addition, pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis with ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) ameliorated behavioral deficits induced by chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) [11]. The hippocampus (Hip), a brain area vital for emotion regulation, is particularly vulnerable to ferroptosis [12]. Quantitative proteomics revealed that protein alterations in the Hip of CUMS mice were primarily enriched in ferroptosis-associated pathways [13]. Moreover, ferroptosis has intricate interactions with the pathological mechanisms of depression, suggesting that it may serve as a critical node in the pathophysiological network underlying MDD [14]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms that govern ferroptosis may provide novel therapeutic strategies for depression.

Recent studies have linked endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, triggered by the accumulation of misfolded proteins, to ferroptosis [15]. To promote ER homeostasis, cells respond by triggering the unfolded protein response (UPR). However, under excessive or prolonged stress, the UPR is overwhelmed and shifts to a maladaptive state, ultimately triggering cell death [16]. The UPR is orchestrated by three sensors: protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK), activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) and inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α) [17]. These sensors detect ER stress and activate adaptive UPR programs that promote protein folding and reduce protein translation. Activated PERK phosphorylates eIF2α, leading to the upregulation of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) expression. Activated IRE1α mediates X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) mRNA splicing, while ATF6 promotes ER chaperone-related adaptive responses [18]. In addition, UPR signaling has been implicated in the regulation of ferroptosis during ER stress [19]. Notably, the PERK/ATF4 signaling arm can promote ferroptosis through the transcriptional upregulation of glutathione-specific γ-glutamylcyclotransferase 1 (CHAC1), a downstream effector of ATF4 that promotes ferroptosis by depleting intracellular glutathione (GSH) and thereby impairing GPX4-mediated detoxification of lipid peroxides [20,21].

MANF is an evolutionarily conserved protein that was initially identified as a secreted neurotrophic factor (NTF) that promotes midbrain dopaminergic neuron survival in vitro [22]. Although structurally distinct from classical NTFs such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [[23], [24], [25]], it functions primarily as an ER stress-responsive protein that is upregulated upon stress [[26], [27], [28]] and has been reported to interact with glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), also known as binding immunoglobulin protein (BiP), and other UPR-related proteins to promote ER homeostasis [[29], [30], [31]]. Accumulating studies have demonstrated the protective functions of MANF in Parkinson's disease (PD), ischemic stroke and retinopathy [[32], [33], [34]]. Despite evidence of elevated MANF in the serum of individuals with MDD [35], knowledge regarding its role in MDD remains limited, and the potential link between MANF-modulated ER homeostasis and ferroptosis is unknown.

This study sought to elucidate the role and underlying mechanism of MANF in MDD. We first performed an integrative transcriptomic analysis across multiple depression datasets and our own CSDS model, identifying MANF as the most consistently upregulated candidate gene. We then demonstrated that hippocampal MANF overexpression suppressed ferroptosis and alleviated depressive-like behaviors in mice, whereas its knockdown exacerbated ferroptosis and enhanced stress susceptibility. Fer-1 rescue experiments revealed that ferroptosis is a critical downstream effector of MANF. Furthermore, bidirectional pathway-intervention experiments supported an important functional contribution of PERK/ATF4 suppression to MANF-mediated antidepressant and anti-ferroptotic protection, with ATF4 activating CHAC1 promoter activity in an ATF4-binding-site-dependent manner. Taken together, these results demonstrate that MANF alleviates depressive-like behaviors in mice by inhibiting hippocampal ferroptosis via the PERK/ATF4 signaling pathway.

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China). Retired male CD1 breeders aged 16‒24 weeks were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). The animals were housed under standard conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. Experimental protocols associated with animals were approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. 2017013, approved October 30, 2017) and Institutional Animal Care and Use of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. IACUC-CQMU-2025-0506, approved July 29, 2025) and were carried out in strict compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

The CSDS method followed an established protocol [10,36]. Briefly, aggressive male CD1 mice were chosen before CSDS using the criteria reported in our previous study [10]. For 10 consecutive days, the experimental mice underwent daily encounters with novel CD1 aggressors for 5‒10 min. Following direct contact, both experimental and CD1 mice were kept together but isolated using a perforated transparent plastic divider for 24 h until the next day, which allowed visual, olfactory and auditory contact, mimicking persistent psychological stress. Control mice were paired in partitioned cages with daily divider changes.

SSDS represents a modified CSDS protocol for evaluating stress susceptibility in mice [37]. In short, the mice underwent three 5-min encounters with a novel CD1 mouse, with each session separated by a 15-min break. One day following the final confrontation, the subjects were evaluated using behavioral tests.

The ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (2 mg/kg, HY-100579, MedChemExpress) or vehicle was administered once daily by intraperitoneal injection for 10 consecutive days according to the CSDS and SSDS + AAV-shMANF experimental timelines. All injections were completed before behavioral testing.

The SIT comprised two 2.5-min phases and was conducted as previously described [10]. In the “No Target” phase, mice explored an arena with an empty box. In the subsequent “Target” phase, a novel CD1 mouse was confined to the box. Interaction zone time was quantified using EthoVision XT 13.0 (Noldus, Netherlands). On the basis of the social interaction ratio (“Target”/“No Target” interaction time), the mice were categorized as susceptible (social interaction ratio <1) or resilient (social interaction ratio ≥1).

To test for anhedonia, the mice were acclimated to two bottles (water and 1% sucrose solution) for 24 h, with the bottle positions alternated every 12 h. After 12 h of water deprivation, the mice were subjected to a 12-h test with free access to both bottles. Sucrose preference (%) = [sucrose consumption/(sucrose consumption + water consumption)] × 100%.

Following an established protocol [10], the mice were subjected to the TST for 6 min. Immobility time was quantified during the last 4 min.

Following an established protocol [10], the mice were placed in a cylinder (15 cm diameter, 30 cm in height) filled to a 15 cm depth with water maintained at 23 ± 1 °C for 6 min. Immobility time was quantified during the last 4 min.

The OFT was conducted to evaluate locomotor activity according to a published protocol [10]. During the 5-min session, the mice were allowed to freely explore the arena (44 × 44 × 30 cm). The total distance traveled was tracked and analyzed.

After being anesthetized, the mice were transcardially perfused with PBS and then with 4% PFA. After removal, the brain tissues were fixed in 4% PFA overnight and then dehydrated in a 30% sucrose solution. The brains were serially cryosectioned into 30-μm coronal slices. These slices were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-NeuN (MAB377A5; Sigma‒Aldrich), anti-GFAP (ab53554; Abcam), anti-Iba-1 (ab5076; Abcam), anti-MANF (ab316935; for MANF expression analysis; Abcam), and anti-MANF (ab67271; for co-immunofluorescence staining; Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, then incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature except for anti-NeuN (MAB377A5), which is directly conjugated to Alexa Fluor 555. Images were captured with a confocal microscope (STELLARIS 5 SR; Leica, Germany) and a fluorescence microscope (VS200; Olympus, Japan). Fluorescence intensity analyses were performed using ImageJ software.

Coronal brain slices (10 μm) were stained with PI solution (G1021; Servicebio) in the dark for 20 min and imaged using a confocal microscope (STELLARIS 5 SR; Leica, Germany). The fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software.

Hippocampal levels of Fe2+, malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) were quantified with assay kits (Fe2+: ab83366; Abcam; MDA, SOD, and GSH-Px: A003, A001 and A005; Jiancheng Inc., respectively) in accordance with the manufacturers’ guidelines.

The hippocampal level of 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) was monitored with an ELISA (JYM1109Mo; Wuhan Jiyinmei Biotech, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Hippocampal RNA was prepared with TRIzol reagent and converted to cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (Takara). RT‒qPCR was carried out on a LightCycler instrument (Roche, Switzerland) with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (RR820A, Takara). GAPDH was used for normalization, and the relative mRNA expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

The protocol was performed as previously outlined [10]. Protein samples (30 μg) were separated by 8% or 10% SDS‒PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes. Following blocking with 5% BSA or nonfat milk in TBST for 2 h, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-PERK (24390-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-phospho-PERK (3179; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-IRE1α (3294; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-phospho-IRE1α (AP0878; ABclonal), anti-ATF4 (10835-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-ATF6 (24169-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-MANF (ab67271; Abcam), anti-NRF2 (16396-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-ACSL4 (ab155282; Abcam), anti-SLC7A11 (A13685; ABclonal), anti-CHAC1 (15207-1-AP; Proteintech), anti–HO–1 (10701-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-GPX4 (ab125066; Abcam), anti-GAPDH (ab8245; Abcam), and anti-β-ACTIN (66009-1-Ig; Proteintech), overnight at 4 °C and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h. Protein detection was performed with an enhanced chemiluminescence kit, and band densities were assessed via ImageJ software.

Following isoflurane anesthesia, the mice were positioned on a stereotaxic apparatus. Using a microsyringe pump, viruses were bilaterally infused into the dorsal Hip (AP −1.70 mm, ML ±1.25 mm, DV −1.50 mm) and ventral Hip (AP −3.15 mm, ML ±2.94 mm, DV −3.62 mm and −2.50 mm), relative to bregma. To minimize viral backflow, the injection pipette remained positioned for 10 min post-infusion. The following viruses were used: AAV2/8-CMV-MANF-3×FLAG-EGFP-WPRE (titer 1.77×1013 vg/mL), AAV2/9-U6-shPERK-EGFP-WPRE (titer 5.53×1012 vg/mL), AAV2/8-U6-shATF4-EGFP-WPRE (titer 5.87×1012 vg/mL) and its control virus. These viruses were produced by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China). AAV2/9-U6-shMANF-EGFP (titer 1.70×1012vg/mL) and its control virus were obtained from Hanbio (Shanghai, China). AAV2/9-hSyn-PERK-3×FLAG-W3SLpA (titer 5.25×1012 vg/mL), AAV2/9-CMV-ATF4-3×FLAG-T2A-EGFP-pA (titer 3.11×1012 vg/mL) and its control virus were obtained from Taitool Bioscience (Shanghai, China). For all co-injection experiments, the dose of each experimental AAV was kept constant across the corresponding single- and dual-vector groups, and matched control AAV vectors were supplemented according to viral titers in single-vector groups.

TEM was conducted following a previously described protocol [10]. After anesthesia, the mice were perfused with saline and 4% PFA. Hip tissues (1 mm3) were immediately dissected and postfixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. After being washed, the samples were treated with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through graded ethanol and acetone series, and embedded in EPON 812 for polymerization at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (80 nm) were cut and stained with lead citrate prior to imaging on a JEM-1400 PLUS TEM operated at 80 kV.

Hippocampal total RNA was extracted using an MJZol Total RNA Extraction Kit (Majorbio, Shanghai, China). Library construction and paired-end sequencing were performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform by Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd. Raw reads were filtered using fastp (version 0.21.0), aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) by HISAT2 (version 2.2.1), and quantified using featureCounts (version 2.0.1). DEGs were identified using edgeR (version 4.2.1) with the criteria of |log2 fold change|≥ 0.585 and FDR <0.05.

Untargeted lipidomics was performed by Biotree Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Lipids were extracted from hippocampal tissues using MTBE/MeOH (5:1, v/v) containing isotope-labeled internal standards and analyzed on a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer with separation on a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 column. The raw data were converted using ProteoWizard and processed with XCMS. The resulting peak area matrix was filtered, imputed and normalized to the total ion current. Lipid annotation was performed against the LipidBlast 2022 database. Differential lipid species were identified using VIP >1 and P < 0.05.

Wild-type and ATF4-binding-site-mutant CHAC1 promoter sequences were subcloned and inserted into the pGL3-Basic vector. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with reporter constructs, an ATF4 expression vector or an empty vector and a Renilla luciferase plasmid. After 48 h, firefly luciferase activity was quantified using a dual-luciferase assay kit (Promega, USA) and normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

All data are presented as the mean ± SEM and were statistically analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. The choice of statistical tests was determined according to data distribution and experimental design. Normally distributed data were analyzed using unpaired Student's t tests to compare two groups, and one- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test for comparisons among multiple groups. Nonnormally distributed data were analyzed by the Mann-Whitney test for comparisons between two groups, or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc comparison for comparisons among multiple groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Given that chronic stress is thought to be fundamental to the etiology of MDD [38,39], we established a CSDS model to mimic several core symptoms of depression [40]. Following the 10-day modeling paradigm, the mice underwent behavioral assessments (Fig. 1E). Compared with unstressed control and resilient mice, susceptible mice exhibited a reduced social interaction ratio, decreased sucrose preference and increased immobility time (Fig. 1F–I). However, locomotor activity, as estimated by total distance traveled, remained unchanged (Fig. 1J).

We subsequently quantified MANF protein levels in depression-related brain regions. Western blot analysis revealed a robust increase in MANF levels specifically in the Hip of susceptible mice, with no such change detected in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) among the groups (Fig. 1K–M). Immunofluorescence corroborated this hippocampal-specific upregulation across the CA1, CA3 and DG subregions in the CSDS model (Fig. 1N). Co-immunofluorescence staining with cell type-specific markers showed MANF immunoreactivity in NeuN-positive neurons, GFAP-positive astrocytes and Iba-1-positive microglia in the Hip (Fig. 1O).

Given the established role of ferroptosis in depression [4], we next examined whether hippocampal ferroptosis was induced in CSDS-susceptible mice. Western blot analysis revealed significant downregulation of NRF2, SLC7A11, HO-1 and GPX4, and upregulation of ACSL4 in SUS mice compared with CON and RES mice (Fig. 2A–F). Fe2+ levels were significantly elevated in the SUS group (Fig. 2G), accompanied by increased MDA and 4-HNE levels and reduced GSH-Px activity (Fig. 2H–J). To further characterize ferroptosis-associated phospholipid peroxidation, we performed untargeted lipidomics on hippocampal tissue. The heatmap of representative oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine (OxPE) species revealed a broadly elevated OxPE profile in SUS mice (Fig. 2K). OxPE(18:0/20:4+2O), a representative arachidonic acid-containing ferroptosis-associated OxPE species, was selected for quantitative presentation and was significantly increased in SUS mice (Fig. 2L). The broader pattern of OxPE elevation was further supported by a heatmap of detected OxPE species, quantification of total 20:4-containing OxPEs and analysis of OxPE(18:0/20:4+4O) (Fig. S1A–C). Furthermore, PI staining revealed increased PI-positive cell injury in the Hip of SUS mice compared with CON and RES mice (Fig. 2M and N). Given that ATF4 overexpression increased CHAC1 protein levels in vivo, we next examined whether CHAC1 is an ATF4-responsive transcriptional target. Integration of ATF4 target gene data from five public TF-target databases, including ChIP-Atlas, GTRD, KnockTF, TRRUST and TFLink, revealed CHAC1 as a candidate target supported by all five datasets (Fig. S3). Dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that ATF4 overexpression significantly increased CHAC1-WT promoter activity, whereas mutation of the predicted ATF4-binding motifs abolished this effect, suggesting that ATF4 activates CHAC1 promoter activity in an ATF4-binding-site-dependent manner (Fig. 12N and O). Taken together, these results demonstrate that downstream ATF4 activation can functionally oppose the antidepressant and anti-ferroptotic effects of MANF, with ATF4-responsive activation of CHAC1 promoter activity representing a key downstream mechanism.

Given that MANF knockdown increased p-PERK and ATF4 levels and promoted stress susceptibility, we next examined whether PERK/ATF4 signaling contributes to MANF deficiency-induced stress susceptibility and ferroptosis. AAV-shPERK or AAV-shATF4 was co-injected with AAV-shMANF into the Hip before SSDS exposure (Fig. S4A, Fig. S5A). The viral injection sites and AAV-shATF4 knockdown efficiency were confirmed by fluorescence microscopy and Western blotting, respectively (Fig. S5B and C). AAV-shMANF-treated mice exposed to SSDS exhibited depressive-like behaviors, including social avoidance, anhedonia and despair. Co-injection of AAV-shPERK largely rescued these behavioral deficits without affecting locomotion, whereas AAV-shPERK treatment alone had no effect on behavior compared with the AAV-EGFP control group (Fig. S4B–G). Similarly, co-injection of AAV-shATF4 largely reversed the MANF silencing-induced deficits in social interaction, sucrose preference and immobility, while locomotor activity remained unaffected (Fig. S5D–I).

At the molecular level, PERK knockdown attenuated the MANF silencing-induced upregulation of p-PERK and ATF4 without altering MANF expression and restored the MANF knockdown-induced downregulation of NRF2, HO-1 and GPX4 expression (Fig. S4H–N). Consistent with these findings, AAV-shATF4 reduced ATF4 levels without affecting p-PERK or MANF levels and similarly restored NRF2, HO-1 and GPX4 expression in AAV-shMANF-treated mice (Fig. S5J–P). Together, these complementary loss-of-function experiments support an important role for hippocampal PERK/ATF4 signaling in MANF deficiency-induced stress susceptibility and ferroptosis under subthreshold stress.

Our results demonstrated that MANF, an ER stress-inducible protein, bidirectionally modulates depressive-like behaviors in male mice. By integrating four public transcriptomic datasets with our CSDS RNA-seq data and RT‒qPCR validation, MANF was identified as the most robustly upregulated gene among the validated candidates in CSDS-exposed mice. Hippocampal MANF overexpression suppressed ferroptosis and alleviated CSDS-induced depressive-like behaviors, whereas MANF deficiency exacerbated lipid peroxidation, promoted ferroptosis-related injury and facilitated stress susceptibility. PERK or ATF4 overexpression in MANF-overexpressing mice, together with PERK or ATF4 knockdown in MANF-deficient mice under subthreshold stress, collectively supported PERK/ATF4 signaling as a key downstream pathway of MANF-mediated protection. Moreover, ATF4-responsive upregulation of CHAC1 linked PERK/ATF4 activation to impaired SLC7A11/GPX4 anti-ferroptotic defense, potentially through CHAC1-mediated disruption of GSH homeostasis. Together, these findings establish hippocampal MANF as an endogenous protective factor against stress-induced ferroptosis and depressive-like behaviors.

Accumulating evidence indicates that ferroptosis contributes to the pathophysiology of depression [5,42,43]. Iron accumulation has been reported in multiple brain regions in patients with MDD and in depression-related animal models [44,45]. Iron dysregulation may affect 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) metabolism and thereby contribute to depression-related pathology [46,47]. Several ferroptosis-targeting compounds also exert antidepressant effects in animal models [11,48]. In the present study, we characterized hippocampal ferroptosis using a multiparameter approach. These analyses included untargeted lipidomic profiling of OxPE species, Fe2+ quantification, lipid peroxidation and antioxidant enzyme measurements including MDA, 4-HNE and GSH-Px activity, assessment of ferroptosis-regulatory protein expression including NRF2/HO-1, SLC7A11/GPX4 and ACSL4, mitochondrial morphology evaluation by TEM, Fer-1 pharmacological rescue and PI-positive cell injury. Lipidomic analysis revealed a broadly elevated OxPE profile in CSDS-susceptible mice, as reflected by the heatmap of detected OxPE species and the increased total abundance of 20:4-containing OxPEs. Among the OxPE species, OxPE (18:0/20:4+2O), a dioxygenated product of PE (18:0/20:4), was notably elevated. Previous redox lipidomics studies have identified PE (18:0/20:4)-derived hydroperoxy products, particularly PE (18:0/20:4-OOH), as representative ferroptosis-associated phospholipid peroxidation signals [49,50]. Although our lipidomics annotation does not resolve the exact hydroperoxy isomer PE (18:0/20:4-OOH), the detected OxPE(18:0/20:4+2O) species should be interpreted as a related oxygenated PE (18:0/20:4) species rather than as direct structural identification of PE (18:0/20:4-OOH) [51]. Both AAV-MANF overexpression and Fer-1 treatment attenuated the elevated OxPE profile and reduced the total abundance of 20:4-containing OxPEs, thereby supporting a functional link between MANF-mediated protection and the suppression of ferroptosis-associated phospholipid peroxidation. Together with broad OxPE accumulation, Fe2+ elevation, increased MDA and 4-HNE levels, reduced GSH-Px activity, altered ferroptosis-regulatory protein expression, characteristic mitochondrial alterations on TEM, Fer-1-mediated rescue and PI-positive cell injury, these findings provide convergent in vivo evidence for hippocampal ferroptosis under chronic stress.

In line with clinical findings showing elevated serum MANF levels in patients with MDD [35], CSDS triggered the upregulation of hippocampal MANF in our model, as confirmed by Western blotting and immunofluorescence. This elevation was not observed in the mPFC, suggesting a region-selective response to chronic stress in our model. The Hip is particularly sensitive to chronic stress owing to its high metabolic demand and redox vulnerability, which may render it prone to stress-induced ER dysfunction [12,16,52]. MANF contains specific regulatory elements (ERSE-I and ERSE-II) within its promoter that drive transcriptional induction during UPR activation [26,27]. This stress-induced increase may therefore represent an endogenous compensatory response to chronic stress-induced ER dysfunction, consistent with the protective effects observed after MANF overexpression. Previous work has reported MANF expression in neurons, astrocytes and microglia, and our co-immunofluorescence staining showed MANF immunoreactivity in these hippocampal cell types [53]. Functionally, MANF overexpression suppressed ferroptosis and alleviated CSDS-induced depressive-like behaviors, whereas MANF knockdown facilitated ferroptosis and stress susceptibility in mice, supporting the endogenous neuroprotective role of hippocampal MANF against chronic stress-induced ferroptosis.

We further explored how MANF modulates ferroptosis under chronic stress. MANF is an ER-localized stress-responsive protein that participates in ER proteostasis partly through its interaction with GRP78/BiP [31]. In our study, MANF gain- and loss-of-function manipulations predominantly affected the PERK/ATF4 branch, whereas IRE1α and ATF6 were not consistently altered, supporting a preferential role for MANF in modulating PERK/ATF4 signaling in this model. This pattern may reflect the contextual dominance of PERK/ATF4 signaling under chronic stress rather than intrinsic pathway specificity of MANF, as MANF has been reported to also interact with IRE1α [30]. However, the precise molecular interface linking MANF to the predominant PERK/ATF4 response observed in this study remains to be defined. Among the three UPR branches, the PERK/ATF4 pathway is closely connected to the integrated stress response and has been repeatedly implicated in ferroptosis regulation [20,54]. Under sustained PERK/ATF4 activation during chronic stress, ATF4 may promote maladaptive transcriptional outputs, including CHAC1 induction [21], thereby weakening the SLC7A11/GPX4 anti-ferroptotic defense and increasing ferroptosis vulnerability. This may help explain why the ferroptosis-suppressive effects of MANF in our model were linked mainly to inhibition of the PERK/ATF4 pathway.

Among these ATF4-linked maladaptive outputs, CHAC1 emerged as a candidate downstream effector linking ATF4 activation to ferroptosis vulnerability in our model. CHAC1 is a γ-glutamylcyclotransferase that catalyzes intracellular GSH degradation [21]. Given that GSH is required for GPX4-mediated detoxification of lipid hydroperoxides, ATF4-driven CHAC1 induction may compromise the SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 anti-ferroptotic defense axis. In our in vivo experiments, CHAC1 expression was upregulated by PERK or ATF4 overexpression and reduced by MANF overexpression. Integration of ATF4 target gene data from five public TF-target databases identified CHAC1 as an ATF4-associated candidate, and dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that ATF4 increased CHAC1 promoter activity in an ATF4-binding-site-dependent manner, consistent with previous evidence that CHAC1 is regulated by ATF4/ATF3 downstream of the UPR [21,55]. These findings support ATF4-responsive activation of CHAC1 promoter activity. Together, these data support an ATF4–CHAC1–GSH mechanism that may impair SLC7A11/GPX4 anti-ferroptotic defense and amplify lipid peroxidation. ATF4 can also affect redox balance in a context-dependent manner. Under adaptive stress conditions, ATF4 may contribute to antioxidant gene expression [56], whereas under sustained PERK/ATF4 activation as observed in our CSDS model, the induction of pro-ferroptotic outputs such as CHAC1 induction may predominate over NRF2/HO-1-associated redox-defense protein expression. Consistent with this interpretation, MANF-mediated suppression of PERK/ATF4 was accompanied by restoration of NRF2, HO-1, SLC7A11 and GPX4 protein levels, whereas PERK or ATF4 overexpression attenuated these MANF-mediated effects. The changes in NRF2 protein levels and HO-1 expression observed across experimental conditions should be interpreted as redox-defense-associated protein readouts rather than evidence of direct ATF4-mediated NRF2 regulation. NRF2 is an important anti-ferroptotic regulator that limits lipid peroxidation, and HO-1 is commonly used as a downstream redox-defense marker related to NRF2 [57,58]. However, nuclear NRF2 accumulation was not directly measured, and direct transcriptional regulation of NRF2 by ATF4 was not established in the present study.

These pathway-intervention experiments further strengthened the functional link between MANF and PERK/ATF4 signaling. PERK overexpression in AAV-MANF-treated CSDS mice partially counteracted the antidepressant and anti-ferroptotic effects of MANF despite sustained MANF overexpression. This was accompanied by reactivation of PERK/ATF4 signaling, increased CHAC1 expression, attenuation of MANF-induced upregulation of NRF2/HO-1 and SLC7A11/GPX4, worsening of ferroptosis-related biochemical indices, and increased PI-positive cell injury. ATF4 overexpression produced similar effects without altering p-PERK levels, suggesting that ATF4 is a functionally important downstream mediator. Conversely, PERK or ATF4 knockdown largely reversed MANF deficiency-induced depressive-like behaviors and restored NRF2, HO-1 and GPX4 expression under subthreshold stress. Together, these gain-of-function override and loss-of-function rescue experiments support an important functional contribution of PERK/ATF4 suppression to the antidepressant and ferroptosis-suppressive effects of MANF. Notably, PERK or ATF4 overexpression attenuated, but did not completely eliminate MANF-mediated protection. This partial reversal suggests that PERK/ATF4 suppression is a key downstream mechanism, while additional MANF-related mechanisms may also contribute. These may include PERK/ATF4-independent regulation of ER homeostasis, the secreted neurotrophic functions of MANF or glial contributions to hippocampal iron and redox balance.

The pathway-intervention experiments in this study employed dual-AAV co-injection to simultaneously manipulate two molecular targets within the same hippocampal region. The dose of each experimental AAV was kept constant across groups, and matched control AAV vectors were supplemented in single-vector groups to balance viral titers. Nevertheless, potential confounding factors inherent to dual-AAV paradigms, including viral load-related effects, co-infection-related cytotoxicity, and off-target effects, cannot be completely excluded. Open field locomotor activity did not differ among injection groups, arguing against overt nonspecific behavioral toxicity. These methodological factors should be considered when interpreting the pathway-intervention results. The apparent selectivity of MANF toward the PERK/ATF4 branch should be viewed as functional output selectivity rather than direct or exclusive targeting of PERK. MANF has been reported to interact with the ER-luminal domain of IRE1α, indicating that it is not restricted to a single UPR sensor [30]. In the present CSDS model, MANF overexpression selectively reduced p-PERK and ATF4 without markedly affecting p-IRE1α/IRE1α or ATF6, which may reflect the temporal predominance of PERK/ATF4 signaling under chronic stress and its closer connection to ferroptosis-related metabolic programs, potentially involving CHAC1-mediated GSH degradation [20,59,60]. Furthermore, ER-stress-independent mechanisms of MANF cannot be excluded. Given that MANF is both a secreted neurotrophic factor and an ER-localized cytoprotective protein [22,24,30], extracellular MANF signaling, neuronal survival pathways, or glia-mediated regulation of iron and redox homeostasis may also contribute to its ferroptosis-suppressive effects and should be explored in future studies.

The Fer-1 rescue experiments provide complementary pharmacological evidence linking MANF to ferroptosis regulation. Fer-1 largely mimicked the antidepressant and anti-ferroptotic effects of AAV-MANF overexpression in CSDS mice and rescued depressive-like phenotypes in MANF-deficient mice under subthreshold stress. The parallel outcomes of genetic and pharmacological rescue, including the restoration of NRF2/HO-1 and SLC7A11/GPX4 defense, reduction of Fe2+ accumulation, MDA and 4-HNE levels, recovery of GSH-Px activity, suppression of OxPE accumulation and attenuation of PI-positive cell injury, support ferroptosis as a critical downstream process linking the MANF/PERK/ATF4 pathway to depressive-like behaviors. Notably, ACSL4 expression increased under CSDS but was not consistently altered by MANF manipulation across experimental conditions, suggesting that MANF does not primarily suppress ferroptosis by acting through ACSL4-mediated lipid remodeling. Instead, MANF appears to preserve the SLC7A11/GPX4 defense axis, potentially by limiting ATF4/CHAC1-mediated disruption of GSH homeostasis. Together, these findings support the hippocampal MANF/PERK/ATF4 signaling axis as a stress-responsive mechanism linking ER stress to ferroptosis in depression, with CHAC1 acting as an ATF4-responsive downstream effector.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the CSDS paradigm is largely restricted to male mice because it relies on male-specific social aggression, which limits the generalizability of our findings to females. Second, although our data support the involvement of PERK/ATF4 signaling downstream of MANF, the upstream molecular interface linking MANF, GRP78/BiP and UPR sensors remains incompletely defined, and the potential contribution of ER-stress-independent MANF actions was not dissected. Third, while our lipidomics and multiparameter ferroptosis assessments provide convergent evidence for hippocampal ferroptosis, direct lipid ROS measurement using C11-BODIPY in hippocampal slices or isolated hippocampal cells would further strengthen ferroptosis characterization. Fourth, the ATF4–CHAC1 relationship was supported by multi-database TF-target integration and dual-luciferase reporter assays; however, ChIP-qPCR confirmation of ATF4 occupancy at the endogenous CHAC1 promoter, CHAC1 loss-of-function rescue, direct assessment of GSH homeostasis and nuclear NRF2 protein measurement would provide additional mechanistic precision. Fifth, clinical translation will require brain-targeted delivery strategies capable of efficiently delivering MANF or MANF-encoding therapeutics across the blood–brain barrier. Recent extracellular vesicle-based MANF mRNA delivery represents a potentially promising direction [61], although brain delivery efficiency and regional targeting specificity remain to be optimized.

This study identifies hippocampal MANF as an endogenous protective factor against chronic stress-induced ferroptosis and depressive-like behaviors. Under chronic stress, MANF expression may be upregulated as part of a compensatory response to ER dysfunction, whereas MANF deficiency enhances ferroptosis and stress susceptibility. Bidirectional pathway-intervention experiments support an important functional contribution of PERK/ATF4 suppression to MANF-mediated protection. Mechanistically, ATF4-responsive upregulation of CHAC1 links sustained ER stress to potential disruption of GSH homeostasis, impairment of the SLC7A11/GPX4 defense axis and amplification of lipid peroxidation, providing a molecular basis for how unresolved ER stress promotes ferroptosis vulnerability. These findings support the hippocampal MANF/PERK/ATF4 axis as a candidate therapeutic pathway at the intersection of ER stress and ferroptosis in MDD.
