# Autoimmune myelofibrosis with a TLR8 gain-of-function defect.
**Authors:** Qu S, Zhang P, Xu Z, Qin T, Li B, Xiao Z
**Journal:** Leuk Res Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.lrr.2026.100587](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lrr.2026.100587)

## Content

We report a case of a 25-year-old male with recurrent oral ulcers, upper respiratory tract infections, and an 8-year history of chronic neutropenia. Previously diagnosed with Behçet's disease, the patient had undergone comprehensive hematological evaluations at multiple tertiary institutions, yet the etiology of his persistent neutropenia remained undetermined. Whole-exome sequencing revealed a TLR8-G572D mutation, leading to a diagnosis of autoimmune myelofibrosis (AIMF) secondary to TLR8 gain-of-function (GOF). While concurrent Behçet's disease and neutropenia may occur clinically, the underlying pathogenesis is often neglected. TLR8-GOF screening should be emphasized in young patients with such presentations. To our knowledge, this is the first reported association between TLR8-GOF mutation and AIMF, expanding the phenotypic spectrum of TLR8-related disorders.

TLR8 gain-of-function (GOF) mutations constitute a recently recognized group of disorders characterized by overlapping features of bone marrow failure and inborn errors of immunity [1]. Reported manifestations include immune-mediated neutropenia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and autoinflammatory phenotypes [2,3]. Herein, we describe a case of a patient with a TLR8-GOF mutation who developed Behçet's disease and autoimmune myelofibrosis (AIMF), thereby expanding the documented phenotypic spectrum of this condition.

A 25-year-old male patient presented with recurrent oral ulcers and upper respiratory tract infections, and an 8-year history of chronic neutropenia. He had been previously diagnosed with Behçet's disease and was treated with a combination of tofacitinib (5 mg twice daily) and thalidomide (50 mg daily). Despite comprehensive hematological evaluations conducted at several tertiary medical institutions, the cause of his persistent neutropenia remained unclear. Initial laboratory tests revealed severe neutropenia (white blood cell count 2.2×10⁹/L, absolute neutrophil count 0.13×10⁹/L) and anemia (hemoglobin 107 g/L). The bone marrow (BM) biopsy showed extremely hypercellularity (>90%) with marked lymphoid hyperplasia presenting as focal or scattered increases (Fig. 1A, arrow). The granulocytic series was decreased in proportion (Fig. 1B), while CD3+ T-cell hyperplasia predominated, exhibiting focal infiltration (Fig. 1C, arrow). B-lymphocyte proliferation was generally normal (Fig. 1D), with increased polymorphic plasma cells (Fig. 1E). Additionally, sinusoidal dilation and transformation into small vessels were observed (Fig. 1F, arrows), accompanied by grade 2 reticulin fibrosis (Fig. 1G). Whole-exome sequencing identified a TLR8-G572D mutation with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 100% (Panel D, arrow). Based on the clinical presentation, BM pathological findings, and molecular testing, the diagnosis of AIMF associated with TLR8-GOF mutation was confirmed. For the concomitant iron-deficiency anemia, the patient received iron supplementation for 3 months, achieving hemoglobin normalization (>130 g/L). TLR8 is a gene located on the X chromosome, encoding Toll-like receptor protein 8 (TLR8)—a pattern recognition receptor that senses microbial single-stranded RNA degradation products [4]. TLR8-GOF mutation is a relatively new disease entity, which can be caused by both germline variants and somatic (mosaic) variants. Aluri et al. reported TLR8 variants in six male patients, all diagnosed with refractory chronic neutropenia. Somatic TLR8 variants were identified in five patients, with mosaicism levels below 30%. Mosaicism was also detected in skin-derived fibroblasts from three individuals. In one case, a TLR8-G572D mutation was identified to be of germline origin. All variants were classified as GOF mutations in the TLR8 protein [2]. The core features of TLR8-GOF include neutropenia, recurrent infections, T-cell lymphoproliferation, and B-cell defects; some cases may also progress to bone marrow failure. The pathogenesis of neutropenia in TLR8-GOF involves both compromised granulopoiesis and accelerated neutrophil destruction, mediated through three principal mechanisms: (i) antineutrophil antibody-mediated clearance, (ii) FasL-dependent cytotoxic apoptosis, and (iii) cytokine-induced myelosuppression [1]. Although a TLR8-G572D mutation was detected in our patient, unfortunately, genetic testing of the parents for hereditary TLR8 mutations could not be performed, even though the VAF was highly suggestive of a germline mutation rather than a somatic event.

AIMF is a distinct clinicopathological entity characterized by diffuse bone marrow fibrosis occurring in the context of autoimmune conditions, and is generally associated with a benign clinical course. Typical bone marrow features comprise hypercellularity with erythroid and megakaryocytic hyperplasia, mild reticulin fibrosis, intrasinusoidal hematopoiesis, T-cell–predominant lymphoid aggregates, and mild polytypic plasmacytosis [5]. AIMF is categorized as either secondary or primary, depending on whether it is associated with a well-defined autoimmune disorder. In the present case, the patient’s known history of Behçet’s disease, along with bone marrow biopsy findings consistent with classic AIMF pathology, supports a diagnosis of secondary AIMF. The identified TLR8-GOF mutation led to a pro-inflammatory state, characterized by T-cell activation, elevated serum cytokine levels, and impaired B-cell maturation. Furthermore, myeloid cells derived from patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells demonstrated heightened responsiveness to TLR8 stimulation [2]. We thus propose that the TLR8-GOF mutation may represent a potential etiological factor contributing to Behçet’s disease in this individual.

While concurrent Behçet's disease and neutropenia may occur clinically, the underlying pathogenesis is often neglected. TLR8-GOF screening should be emphasized in young patients with such presentations. To our knowledge, this is the first reported association between TLR8-GOF mutation and AIMF, expanding the phenotypic spectrum of TLR8-related disorders.
