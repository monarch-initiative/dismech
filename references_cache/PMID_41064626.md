INTRODUCTION: Mutations of the OFD1 gene cause oral-facial-digital syndrome type 
1 (OFD 1) and variations of the related ciliopathies Joubert syndrome and 
primary ciliary dyskinesia. OFD 1 manifests with skeletal, CNS, and renal 
abnormalities with prevalence estimated between 1 in 50,000 and 1 in 250,000. 
Cilia help regulate responses to mechanical forces in the renal tubules, and 
polycystic kidney disease (PKD) resulting from defective cilia is the primary 
determinant of morbidity in OFD 1. PKD associated with OFD 1 is rare before 
adulthood but may increase markedly with age, progressing to end-stage renal 
disease (ESRD) in 80% of cases.
CASE PRESENTATION: We report an 18-year-old female with congenital ciliopathy 
presenting with declining renal function and an increase of serum creatinine to 
1.7 mg/dL. A 24-h urine collection yielded 0.8 g of creatinine and 500 mg of 
total protein. Imaging was conducted and genetic studies were repeated as her 
early childhood results were not available. MRI revealed numerous bilateral 
renal cysts consistent with progression of ciliopathy-associated PKD. Genetic 
testing confirmed the presence of a novel c.1332del frameshift mutation in the 
OFD1 gene, prematurely truncating the OFD1 protein. Modifications to diet and 
hydration to preserve renal function and delay progression to ESRD were 
initiated.
CONCLUSION: This case of unusually early renal decline highlights the challenge 
of treating OFD 1. There are no currently approved medications and management 
consists of supportive measures to delay progression. Further research to better 
characterize and identify treatments for OFD 1 and related ciliopathies is 
warranted.

Ciliopathies are congenital disorders caused by defects in proteins essential for ciliary function. Cilia are hair-like eukaryotic cell projections classified as primary (non-motile) or motile. Motile cilia coordinate fluid flow for signal transduction and pathogen clearance, while both types transmit extracellular signals intracellularly. Impaired ciliary function in ciliopathies affects both embryological development and mature organs, notably the renal system. Dysfunctional cilia lead to renal cysts and cystic kidney disease with high morbidity [1, 2].

Ciliopathies include disorders such as autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD), OFD syndrome, Joubert syndrome, and Bardet-Biedl syndrome, caused by mutations in nearly 200 different genes. OFD1 gene mutations, located on the X-chromosome, are linked to OFD syndrome, Joubert Syndrome 10 (JBS10), and primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD). These mutations disrupt cilia structure and function, leading to developmental defects and organ dysfunction. OFD 1 syndrome, inherited in an X-dominant, male-lethal manner, manifests with renal cysts, skeletal malformations, and central nervous system abnormalities. Variable expressivity is partly due to differences in X-chromosome inactivation, and molecular genetic testing is essential for diagnosis [3].

Renal involvement in OFD1 syndrome often progresses to end-stage renal disease, with a study [4] reporting a 69% prevalence of polycystic kidney disease (PKD) in adults with OFD1 mutations, 82% of whom developed end-stage renal disease. Only 6% of patients with OFD 1 mutations manifest with PKD by age 18. Management focuses on prolonging kidney health, with routine monitoring and supportive care. Treatment remains limited, though advances in genetic therapies for ciliopathies may emerge. Here, we describe a novel frameshift mutation in OFD1 syndrome, presenting with progressively worsening renal function at 18 years of age [4, 5].

An 18-year-old female with OFD 1 syndrome associated stage 2 chronic kidney disease (CKD) presented to nephrology clinic for evaluation of a worsening serum creatinine (sCR) level. In addition to renal disease, her medical history was significant for hypertension, chronic otitis media with bilateral low frequency hearing loss, vitamin D deficiency, and iron deficiency anemia. She had been born with associated skeletal features of OFD 1, a congenital cleft palate and syndactyly surgically repaired in her early childhood. At that time, the patient had undergone genetic testing which resulted in a diagnosis of OFD 1 syndrome, but the specific genetic results were unavailable.

At the time of her initial presentation, her medications included amlodipine for hypertension. She had no prior history of tobacco, alcohol, or illicit drug use, and no known allergies. Her vital signs were within normal limits with the exception of a mildly elevated blood pressure of 131/87.

The patient was referred to our clinic by her primary care physician because her sCR increased from her presumed baseline of 0.9 mg/dL measured 3 years previously, to 1.7 mg/dL as illustrated in Figure 1. Her cystatin C similarly raised to 1.84 mg/L. Vitamin D 25-OH D3 levels 6 months prior to her presentation were 28.5 ng/mL but had improved to 43.5 ng/mL prior to her initial nephrology visit. Over the same time frame, PTH increased from 48 pg/mL to 90 pg/mL. At this time, her urine was negative for hemoglobin and red blood cells. Her estimated glomerular filtration rate was 39, and she was diagnosed with stage 3B CKD.

Renal function was monitored closely. Despite modifications to diet and hydration, renal function continued to decline as indicated by progressively increasing sCR and cystatin C.

Over the following 2 months, sCR and CysC increased further to 1.8 mg/dL and 1.97 mg/L, respectively. Her estimated glomerular filtration rate increased slightly to 42. Urinalysis revealed clinical albuminuria progressing from 378 mg/L to 423 mg/L. The albumin/creatinine ratio increased from 378 mg/g to 470 mg/g. A 24-h urine collection yielded 0.8 g of creatinine and 500 mg of total protein. Albumin urine electrophoresis was recorded at 74%. Liver function tests remained within normal range. CBC results consistent with mild microcytic anemia were obtained which was consistent with the patient’s baseline dating back 6 years. The patient’s serum kappa free light chains measured 30.82 mg/L. PTH increased to 97 pg/mL. Trace hemoglobinuria was identified. The patient’s renal sCR, CysC, BUN, and urine microalbumin/creatinine ratio are summarized in Figure 1.

The differential diagnosis at the time included ADPKD, ARPKD, and OFD1-related ciliopathies. As the genetic report from the patient’s early childhood was no longer available, the patient was amenable to genetic testing to confirm the OFD 1 diagnosis and assess for other genetic causes of renal pathology, including ADPKD, ARPKD, and the other less frequent ciliopathies implicated in cystic renal disease. Our analysis of her worsening kidney function at the initial presentation was most consistent with sequelae of OFD 1, JBS10, or a related ciliopathy.

As the patient’s worsening renal function was understood to be related to a cystic pathologic process, an abdominal MRI angiogram was ordered. Additionally, to assess for CNS malformations consistent with several ciliopathies including OFD 1 syndrome and Joubert syndrome, an MRI brain with and without contrast was ordered. Repeat labs, including urinalysis, urine chemistry, CBC, and blood chemistry, were ordered to further assess for declining renal function.

Abdominal MRI revealed multiple renal cysts bilaterally without significant increase in renal size. No hydronephrosis, solid masses, or renal artery vasculitis were noted. Additionally, multiple extra-renal cysts were found in the tail of the pancreas and in the liver along the portal venous branches (Fig. 2). The findings were most consistent with CKD secondary to either ADPKD or OFD1-related ciliopathy.

MRI revealed numerous bilateral renal cysts consistent with progression of ciliopathy-associated PKD.

The MRI brain revealed right frontal white matter foci of gray matter signal, suggestive of heterotopia and possible bilateral frontal lobe cortical thickening, which was not entirely well-characterized due to susceptibility artifact. The findings were proposed to reflect cortical dysplasia versus volume averaging. The molar tooth sign associated with classical JBS was not identified.

Genetic testing conducted was Renasight by Natera, which screens 385 genes associated with CKD. A heterozygous deletion resulting in a frameshift mutation in exon 13 of the OFD1 gene of the X-chromosome was identified, which was considered likely pathogenic and strongly associated with oral-facial-digital syndrome type 1. The frameshift caused by this deletion introduces a stop codon 26 amino acids downstream, prematurely terminating transcription of the OFD 1 protein (Fig. 3). A ClinVar search for this mutation (c.1332del [p.Lys444Asnfs*26]) confirmed the same genetic variant identified during the patient’s early childhood testing and revealed no other recorded cases, indicating that this is a novel mutation. Additionally, the genetic screening determined that the patient did not have a known ADPKD1 or ADPKD2 mutation. Confirmation of the OFD 1 mutation, in conjunction with finding no mutation in either the ADPKD1 or ADPKD2 genes and in light of the MRI finding of non-enlarged kidneys, rendered ADPKD unlikely. Further differentiation between ARPKD and OFD 1 via biopsy was considered, but given the risk of further insult to the kidneys, the decision was made to not proceed with biopsy as the differentiation would not have changed clinical management.

Treatment of the patient’s condition remained supportive, and the patient was counseled that she will likely require kidney transplant in the future as renal function declines. She was placed on ergocalciferol for vitamin D supplementation with plans to start an SGLT2 inhibitor. She was encouraged to seek a second opinion from genetics and neurologic specialists with expertise in ciliopathy-related conditions. Over the course of 5 months in our service, the patient’s OFD 1-associated PKD has been relatively stable as assessed by sCR, and the patient is being seen for routine follow-ups.

This patient’s case is notable as, while renal cysts are present in about 50% of patients with OFD 1, only 6% of individuals with OFD 1 mutations show PKD by age 18 and the c.1332del frameshift mutation appears to be a novel variant [4]. While her renal disease was previously mild, kidney function deteriorated unexpectedly rapidly, highlighting the potential for early progressive worsening in congenital ciliopathies.

Mutations in the OFD1 gene are associated with OFD 1 syndrome, JBS10, and PCD. However, to date, females with OFD1 mutations have been shown to present with phenotypes consistent with OFD 1 syndrome but not JBS10 or PCD. Additionally, the absence of the molar tooth sign on MRI makes JBS10 less likely. PCD, a motile ciliopathy characterized by impaired mucociliary clearance and respiratory disease, is improbable given the patient’s predominant renal and skeletal symptoms. Moreover, the genetic findings are consistent with the observation that mutations causing OFD 1 syndrome typically occur within the first 17 exons of the transcript, whereas those linked to JBS10 and PCD are generally found later in the transcript or involve missense mutations rather than frameshift mutations [5]. Other deletions nearby on exon 13 causing similar termination of the protein have been shown to cause OFD 1 syndrome [5].

The truncated OFD1 protein likely impairs ciliary function, contributing to cyst development and renal decline. Management of cystic disease is key to reducing morbidity and mortality in OFD 1 and related ciliopathies. Despite progress in understanding the genetic defects responsible for ciliopathies, there is a significant gap in translating this knowledge into effective treatments and no medications targeting cilia directly. Currently, tolvaptan, approved for ADPKD, is the only drug with proven efficacy in ciliopathy-related renal disease [3, 6].

Tolvaptan is also being evaluated for ARPKD and may show promise for other ciliopathies including OFD 1 [7, 8]. SGLT2 inhibitors have been effective in slowing kidney decline in CKD, though data on their use in PKD is lacking [9]. Finerenone, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, is being assessed for CKD in children, but efficacy for ciliopathy-related CKD has not been independently studied [7, 10]. mRNA-based gene therapy holds potential for future treatment, as seen in a Phase 1 trial targeting DNAI1 mutations in PCD [11]. Gene therapy is in early stages and will depend on deeper understanding of the relationship between genetics and disease.

Currently, OFD 1 syndrome is managed supportively, with careful monitoring of renal function, blood pressure, and cystic disease through ultrasound. Genetic evaluation is recommended for female relatives as they may be asymptomatic but at risk of renal disease, particularly during pregnancy [12].

This case study highlights the progression of OFD 1 renal disease in a single patient and should not be generalized to all patients with OFD 1 or other ciliopathies.

This case illustrates the complexity of managing renal disease in patients with OFD 1 syndrome. The absence of targeted therapies for ciliopathies and dearth of medical therapies more generally for PKD highlights the need for further research. Thorough characterization of the myriad manifestations of genetic defects in ciliary proteins is needed to inform the direction of future therapeutic developments. Genetic testing plays a crucial role in diagnosing and differentiating OFD 1 from other closely related ciliopathies, and early recognition of renal involvement can inform the long-term management strategy to prolong kidney function and improve patient outcomes. Supportive management aimed at delaying renal deterioration remains the cornerstone of care. Renal function monitoring of patients with OFD 1 syndrome and related ciliopathies should include blood pressure control, frequent evaluation of sCR and cystatin C, and renal imaging.
