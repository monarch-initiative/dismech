Abstract
Aim Diphtheria, caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae, is still a major global health concern. This study aims to inves-
tigate the resurgence of diphtheria.
Methods We conducted a narrative review on Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar with no limitation on publication 
date for pertinent articles published in English that were succinctly discussed under several headings.
Results Diphtheria epidemiology has changed considerably since the 1970s, but outbreaks still occur due to gaps in 
vaccination, infrastructure failures, and civil unrest, particularly in countries like Venezuela, Yemen, and Bangladesh. The 
COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated these challenges, particularly in low-resource regions like Nigeria. According to 
recent estimates, diphtheria epidemics are on the rise, especially in low-resource areas with poor sanitation and immu-
nization rates. Antimicrobial resistance, the pathogenicity of disease-producing bacteria, and behavioral variables are 
some factors triggering this return. Transmission occurs via droplet spread for respiratory/oropharyngeal diphtheria and 
through direct person-to-person contact for the cutaneous form of diphtheria with pathogenesis involving toxin produc-
tion leading to local and systemic effects. However, underreporting and low coverage persist, especially in Africa and 
Southeast Asia, leading to higher fatality rates. The resurgence of diphtheria brings to light more significant public health 
consequences, such as pressure on healthcare systems and socioeconomic effects. Maintaining robust immunization 
strategies and strengthening public health systems is crucial for global control, in addition to a comprehensive approach 
to combat the diphtheria resurgence, including assessing vaccine coverage rates, identifying barriers to vaccination, 
and improving immunization uptake, particularly in at-risk populations. Laboratory diagnosis is crucial for confirming 
cases, and preventive measures include immunization programs, early antibiotic administration, and outbreak control 
strategies. Management involves prompt administration of antitoxin and antibiotic therapy, patient isolation, and sup -
portive care. Comprehensive global standards that highlight the value of vaccination, bolster the healthcare system, and 
implement measures to encourage vaccine uptake are required to address these issues. To maintain sustainable control 
of diphtheria epidemics and safeguard public health worldwide, long-term policies should include boosting funding for 
immunization programs, improving disease surveillance, and building collaborations.
Conclusion Long-term policies including boosting funding for immunization programs, improving disease surveillance, 
and building collaborations are expedient.

1 Introduction
The respiratory tract infection called diphtheria is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Corynebacterium diphtheria 
that mostly affects the pharynx and tonsils. The bacterium’s fundamental rate of reproduction is predicted to be between 
1.7 and 4.3.2 [1]. Although treatable if diagnosed early, diphtheria can cause serious side effects such as heart difficulties, 
respiratory failure, and even death, with a case-fatality ratio among untreated, never-vaccinated cases of 28.8–29.2%. It is 
still a health issue in low-resource nations, especially among those with low immunization rates, coverage, and unsatisfac-
tory sanitation [1, 2]. Throughout most of human history, including the first part of the twentieth century, diphtheria was a 
dreadful plague that killed a large number of children [3]. Since the introduction of vaccinations in the 1940s, particularly the 
diphtheria toxoid vaccine, the number of diphtheria cases has declined dramatically. Before the vaccine’s widespread use, 
cases exceeded one million annually. The development of the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP) vaccine in the 1950s further 
reduced incidence significantly [4]. The evolution of diphtheria vaccination has seen the transition to more comprehensive 
vaccines, such as Td (tetanus-diphtheria) for booster doses and Penta (DTP-Hib-HepB) and Hexa (DTP-Hib-HepB-IPV) vaccines, 
providing broader protection against multiple pathogens. These developments were critical in reducing diphtheria cases 
to just 6582 between 2013 and 2017, highlighting the importance of ongoing vaccination efforts [4, 5]. However, it is still a 
problem in areas with low vaccination rates. Reports of its reappearance have sparked concern recently throughout the world, 
emphasizing the significance of continuing vaccination efforts [2, 6]. There have been several reports of diphtheria epidemics 
in Europe, America, Southeast Asia, and Africa, and children who are either partially or not vaccinated are at higher risk [6].
Diphtheria, a global public health concern, is resurging due to many factors, including antimicrobial resistance patterns, 
virulence factors of the disease-causing strains, and behavioral factors [7, 8]. Despite the availability of vaccines and successful 
eradication efforts, recent reports indicate a troubling resurgence of cases worldwide with a high burden in Africa, as seen 
in Nigeria between 2022 and 2024 [9]. This raises questions about the effectiveness of current prevention and control meas-
ures and the potential emergence of new strains that may evade existing immunity. The sporadic resurgence of diphtheria 
necessitates urgent attention to prevention and control strategies. This includes assessing vaccine coverage rates, identify-
ing barriers to vaccination, and enhancing immunization uptake in at-risk populations [1, 10]. A comprehensive review of 
diphtheria, its laboratory diagnosis and prevention strategies is expedient. This narrative review can inform evidence-based 
strategies to combat the disease’s re-emergence and recent advances in prevention and management of the disease resur-
gence to prevent further transmission. Thus, this study aims to investigate the resurgence of diphtheria, epidemiological 
trends, clinical manifestations, prevention strategies, barriers impacting vaccine coverage, and public health implications, 
and to provide evidence-based recommendations for comprehensive prevention and control.
2  Method
We conducted a comprehensive narrative review on the resurgence of diphtheria, global epidemiological trends, clini-
cal manifestations, preventions and public health implications of pertinent literature from PubMed, Scopus, and Google 
Scholar databases to address the aim of the study. The search keywords are “Diphtheria, ” “Corynebacterium diphtheria, ” “DTP , ” 
“Resurgence, ” and “Immunization” to identify relevant literature with no publication date limit to capture the historical and 
epidemiological trends of diphtheria, with priority given to recent publication for the currency of relevant data. Original 
research, literature review articles, both systematic and meta-analyses, perspectives, commentaries and letters to the edi-
tor were added so far; they address the aim of the study. Grey articles contributing to addressing the aim of the study were 
added, as well as additional articles from the search of the included article’s reference lists. All articles not addressing the 
aim of the study, not available in their entirety, and not written in English were excluded. Owing to the aim and scope of the 
study, J.O. and O.J.O. extracted relevant data from the searches, and discrepancies were resolved by the third author (P .O.O.), 
which was comprehensively narrated under appropriate headings through a synthesis approach.

3.1  Epidemiology

increase in vaccination coverage for infants in developing countries. This led to a significant reduction in reported cases

the Newly Independent States of the former Soviet Union (NIS), and Thailand, reported a high number of cases of diph-
theria between 1982 and 1994 [11]. The former Soviet Union experienced a resurgence of the diphtheria pandemic by 
the end of the 1980s, with 47,000 reported cases between 1990 and 1995 and 1700 mortalities in 1994 [12]. The resur -

to reduced vaccination coverage, led to the formation of the European Laboratory Working Group on Diphtheria due to 
the disease’s resurgence to epidemic levels. Since 2000, most outbreaks have been sporadic, and small but larger ones 
have occurred, often due to infrastructure failures and civil unrest [13]. Modifications have also been observed in the 
epidemiological profile of diphtheria, particularly regarding demographics and fatality rates. Adults 20 years of age and 
older accounted for just 17% of instances reported before 1980; however, in the following years, this percentage rose to 
36%. Furthermore, there has been a great deal of variation in the recorded case fatality ratio for respiratory diphtheria, 
which has ranged from 0 to 69%. Notably, as therapeutic methods have advanced, so too have mortality rates fallen 
over time. For example, the average case fatality ratio fell from 52% in the 1880s to 7% in the 1940s and 1950s. However, 
subsequent outbreaks have brought attention to ongoing issues, especially in places with low resources where case 
fatality ratios are still much higher [14].
There has been a worrying global trend in the number of diphtheria cases recorded in recent years. The number of 
cases recorded since 2000 has been declining, and between 2006 and 2013, it leveled off at around 4,300–5,700 cases 
per year. The number of reported cases has been more variable since then, with a substantial increase reaching 8,819 in 
2017—the largest number in a single year since 2004. There have been reports of diphtheria epidemics in several nations 
between 2016 and 2019, including Venezuela, Yemen, Haiti, and Bangladesh. Numerous epidemics occurred in places 
of social unrest and war or among people who were already at risk [4 , 13, 14]. 16,611 cases were reported to the WHO 
in 2018. Underreporting remains a challenge, particularly in African, Eastern Mediterranean, and Asian nations,  where 
diphtheria cases are often under-acknowledged [11]. The WHO South-East Asia area is especially concerning because it 
regularly reports the bulk of the annual global incidence of diphtheria. More than half of all cases worldwide are thought 
to have originated in India, which has recorded a disproportionately high number of cases [13]. The COVID-19 pandemic 
has further disrupted vaccination campaigns, contributing to the recent resurgence of diphtheria in developing countries. 
As of January 14, 2024, Nigeria, Guinea, Niger, Mauritania, and South Africa have reported a total of 27,991 suspected 
cases of diphtheria, with 828 deaths. Nigeria remains the most severely affected, accounting for 80.1% of cases and 72.1% 
of deaths [15]. The pandemic’s impact on healthcare systems, vaccine supply chains, and immunization campaigns has 
exacerbated the situation, leaving populations more vulnerable to diphtheria and other preventable diseases [9 , 16].
3.2  Transmission, pathogenesis, and clinical manifestation
Diphtheria is usually transmitted from one person to another via intimate physical and respiratory exposure through 
droplet spread. Respiratory droplets from sneezing, coughing, or talking carry bacteria with them, leading to the forma-
tion of pseudo-membrane. After infection, the disease takes two to five days to establish. Patients who get no treatment 
are contagious for two to three weeks; when antibiotics are administered, patients often become noninfectious in one 
day. Animals may potentially harbor diphtheria in addition to humans with infections of the skin and asymptomatic 
carriers [17]. In addition to respiratory droplet spread, there has been an increasing recognition of cutaneous diphtheria 
transmission, which can occur through direct person-to-person contact, particularly in cases involving toxigenic C. diph-
theriae. This mode of transmission is of particular concern in overcrowded or unhygienic living conditions, as reported 
among refugees and asylum seekers [18]. Usually, C. diphtheriae does not penetrate deeper into the body; instead, it 
remains at these entry points and multiplies on the mucous membrane’s surface. Infected tissues exhibit inflammatory 
symptoms, and as a result of the diphtheria toxin’s harmful effects, edema and necroses eventually appear. Addition-
ally, the toxin causes inflammation in deeper capillaries, which causes fibrin to be secreted into the injured epithelium 
[8]. The broken epithelial cells’ constituent parts, germs, and released fibrin protein combine to produce the so-called 
pseudomembrane, a grayish or yellowish-white covering. Patients may have bleeding from capillary damage brought 
on by inflammatory processes, as well as a dirty brown pseudomembrane color and somewhat putrid breath due to 
erythrocyte degradation. The body’s tissues absorb the toxins generated at the membrane location and get them through 
the bloodstream. C. diphtheriae’s pathogenesis occurs in two different stages: invasion and toxicogenic. Invasion involves 
the colonization of local tissues, preceding the proliferation of bacteria. Toxigenesis kills eucaryotic cells and tissues by 
blocking protein synthesis [19]. Since toxigenesis is preceded by a unique invasive phase; C. diphtheriae’s virulence cannot 
be solely attributable to its toxigenicity. Bacteremia, septic arthritis, endocarditis, cutaneous lesions, and mild to severe

exudative pharyngitis can all be brought on by non-toxin-producing C. diphtheriae strains [20]. The extracellular protein 
of C. diphtheriae, known as diphtheria toxin, prevents the creation of new proteins and destroys vulnerable cells. With 
a lethal dose of approximately 0.1 μg per kilogram of body weight, it is incredibly potent. Because the toxin is labile, it 
can be transformed into a toxoid by prolonged storage, incubation at 37 °C for four to six weeks, formalin treatment, 
and acid pH treatment [17, 21].
Clinical manifestations can be local or systemic, with local signs including pseudomembrane production at the coloni-
zation site and sore throat. Diphtheria can be classified by anatomic site into respiratory (pharyngeal, tonsillar, laryngeal, 
and nasal) and non-respiratory (cutaneous and other mucus membranes) diseases. Pharyngeal and tonsillar diphtheria 
are the most common sites, with gradual symptoms including malaise, sore throat, anorexia, and low-grade fever [17]. 
A bluish-white membrane forms and extends within 2 to 3 days, causing respiratory obstruction. Severe disease may 
result in severe prostration, pallor, rapid pulse, stupor, and coma, with death occurring within 6 to 10 days. Laryngeal 
diphtheria can involve only this site and can lead to airway obstruction, coma, and death. Anterior nasal diphtheria is 
mild and can be terminated quickly with diphtheria antitoxin and antibiotic therapy. Cutaneous diphtheria can manifest 
as a scaling rash or ulcers, while any chronic skin lesion may harbor C. diphtheria [22]. Diphtheria also affects other sites, 
including the conjunctiva, vulvovaginal area, and external auditory canal. The toxin, which can cause complications 
such as myocarditis, neuritis, and respiratory failure, can affect distant organs and tissues [23]. Myocarditis can lead to 
abnormal cardiac rhythms and heart failure, with a significant risk of fatality. Neuritis affects motor nerves and typically 
resolves completely, but it can cause paralysis of muscles controlling eye movement and respiration, potentially leading 
to respiratory failure. Other complications may include otitis media, airway obstruction leading to respiratory insuffi-
ciency, and secondary pneumonia. The overall case fatality ratio for diphtheria ranges from 5 to 10%, with neurological 
complications observed in approximately three-quarters of severe cases [8, 22]. Cutaneous diphtheria is more prevalent 
in tropical and subtropical regions and presents as ulcerative lesions on the lower legs, feet, and hands, characterized 
by a pseudomembrane covering and slow healing. Systemic infections caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae can lead 
to severe conditions such as bacteremia, endocarditis, and abscess formation, particularly in adults. These infections, 
though rare, can be life-threatening and require prompt diagnosis and treatment [24].
3.3  Laboratory diagnosis
The primary methods used in the laboratory diagnosis of diphtheria are culture and staining methods. Culture is still the 
gold standard, usually used for specimens from parts of the pharynx that are discolored. Aerobes such as Corynebacterium 
diphtheriae, which are facultative, grow best at 37 °C on blood-containing media and show either minimal hemolysis or 
weak beta hemolysis. C. diphtheriae and C. ulcerans can be distinguished from other Corynebacterium species (such as 
diphtheroids) that typically inhabit the nasopharynx and skin when cultured on a selective medium containing tellurite 
[17, 22]. Identification is further aided by staining techniques, such as Gram staining and Albert’s stain, which highlight 
distinctive morphological traits and metachromatic granules (Fig.  1). As a critical diagnostic tool, the Elek test is also 
essential for identifying the synthesis of exotoxins by toxic bacteria. When used together with clinical presentation, these 
laboratory techniques are important for verifying diphtheria cases and enabling the prompt beginning of presumed 
therapy [11, 25]. A positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test result targeting the diphtheria tox gene and the identifi-
cation of C. diphtheriae through cultures derived from specimens obtained from close contacts are two forms of evidence 
that can support the presumptive diagnosis in cases where antibiotic treatment started before obtaining specimens 
from a suspected case of diphtheria and culture results yielded negative findings for C. diphtheria. Other approaches 
include the sequencing of whole genomes for epidemiological research and MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, which are 
accessible only at specific reference laboratories [3, 11].
3.4  Prevention, outbreak control, and management
The mainstays of preventive measures for diphtheria are immunization programs and early antibiotic administration to 
those close to confirmed cases. Immunization is essential, especially with DTP (diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis) or DTaP 
(acellular pertussis) vaccines, whose recommended schedules differ according to age and nation. Booster dosages sup-
port immune maintenance, particularly in adults and adolescents [ 3]. However, because of socioeconomic considera -
tions and cultural views, problems like delayed vaccination and inadequate coverage continue, especially in low- and 
middle-income nations. To achieve herd immunity, combination vaccinations against pertussis, tetanus, and diphtheria 
are essential [26]. The WHO recommends the use of combination vaccines that include protection against diphtheria,

tetanus, and pertussis (DTP). These vaccines are tailored for different age groups, with routine immunization for infants 
and children at ages 2 months, 4 months, 6 months, 15–18 months, and a booster dose at 4–6 years. For adolescents, a 
booster dose of the DTP-containing vaccine (Tdap) is recommended at 11–12 years of age to maintain protection. For 
adults, regular booster doses every 10 years are recommended. WHO also provides catch-up vaccination guidance for 
children and adolescents who miss their recommended doses. In cases of vaccine contraindications, alternative vaccina-
tion schedules are advised. For individuals under 7 years old with contraindications, a non-pertussis-containing vaccine 
may be used, while for those aged 7 and older, the Td vaccine is recommended. WHO has issued temporary guidance 
for vaccine providers due to 2024 U.S. Td vaccine supply constraints, advising them to use Tdap as an alternative to Td 
for most situations, including tetanus boosters for wound management [27]. Although immunization is effective in 
avoiding symptoms due to toxins, it does not prevent non-toxic Corynebacterium species from colonizing the body. This 
emphasizes the significance of ongoing monitoring and all-encompassing preventive measures [11, 28]. Important steps 
to take during diphtheria epidemics include recording confirmed cases and isolating close contacts. Prioritize the safety 
of healthcare workers by making sure they receive a diphtheria vaccination and by implementing stringent infection 
control procedures like PPE use, patient isolation, and strict cleanliness standards. Managing cases of diphtheria requires 
ongoing training and awareness campaigns to improve the safety of society as well as healthcare workers [29, 30].
Diphtheria antitoxin (DAT), which is quickly administered to neutralize circulating diphtheria toxin and slow the dis-
ease’s course, is part of the management strategy for the disease. To minimize the risk of hypersensitive reactions, DAT 
should be given as soon as a clinical diagnosis is made, preferably intravenously. In extreme cases or in people who are 
sensitive to penicillin, antibiotic therapy—such as penicillin V, erythromycin, or azithromycin—is advised to eradicate the 
bacteria [31, 32]. The extremely contagious nature of diphtheria makes patient seclusion in an isolation unit imperative. 
Liquid nourishment, soft foods, and extended bed rest may be necessary for recovery, particularly in cases of diphtheritic 
myocarditis. Antitoxin therapy, administered intravenously for faster therapeutic blood levels, has been demonstrated to 
lower mortality by reducing cardiovascular toxicity. To stop the release of toxins, expedite healing, and stop additional 
transmission, early antibiotic therapy is essential. To prevent skin diphtheria, wounds must be thoroughly cleaned and 
disinfected [33, 34].

require significant global collaboration and investment [39].
3.6  Public health implications
There are still serious health risks associated with the reemergence of diphtheria amidst other infectious diseases such 
as COVID-19, Ebola, monkeypox, cholera, tuberculosis, and Lassa fever. The loss of human life is the main cause of con-
cern since it increases the burden of disease, rates of morbidity, and mortality. Healthcare systems are under pressure 
from these outbreaks, which drives up expenses and workload [56]. These issues are exacerbated by indirect impacts, 
such as a decline in the workforce brought on by illness and absenteeism. Because these illnesses are contagious, they 
spread over time and increase social expenses. Furthermore, the spread of diseases like diphtheria and related ones can 
cause problems for several industries, including transportation, health, agriculture, and tourism, which can affect supply 
chains and trade both domestically and internationally [3 ]. Sustaining and improving orthodox methods of diphtheria 
control, such as vaccination and surveillance, are critical to mitigating these risks. Vaccination remains the most effec -
tive method of prevention, and strengthening routine immunization programs, especially in low- and middle-income 
countries (LMICs), is essential to achieving high coverage rates. Surveillance systems also need to be enhanced to ensure

effects of future pandemics, it is crucial to strengthen health systems’ resilience and ensure that vaccination programs can 
continue even in the face of disruptions. This includes investing in healthcare infrastructure, improving vaccine supply 
chains, and ensuring that healthcare workers are adequately trained and equipped to manage multiple public health 
emergencies simultaneously [60, 61].
With increasing population mobility and globalization, developing countries may be at risk of diphtheria reappear -
ance from developed and developing nations. Epidemics could occur, especially in LMICs, among individuals who lack 
immunity to diphtheria and immunization programs that do not provide booster doses as a result of inadequate routine 
immunization [62]. Children who are particularly sensitive to epidemics have been left unprotected by prior vaccination 
shortages. The problem is made worse by inadequate reporting systems and restricted diagnostic capabilities, and during 
an epidemic, the lack of particular medicines like diphtheria antitoxin could have disastrous consequences. To address 
these issues, improving access to essential treatments like diphtheria antitoxin and ensuring that diagnostic capabili-
ties are strengthened will be critical in managing future outbreaks. On the other hand, cutaneous infections may have a 
natural booster effect that helps to slow down the organism’s progress [12]. However, relying on such natural boosting 
effects is not a sustainable strategy for diphtheria control. Vaccination and consistent surveillance efforts are necessary 
to prevent widespread outbreaks and ensure long-term disease control.
3.7  Recommendation for way out
The current spike in diphtheria outbreaks highlights the critical need for comprehensive international guidelines to 
reduce deaths and limit transmission in the short- and long term. It is essential to implement short-term actions to stop 
the current outbreak and prevent additional morbidity and mortality. Priorities for initiatives should include raising public 
awareness and educating the public, guaranteeing high clinical suspicion and efficient case management, refining the 
medical supply chain, strengthening laboratory diagnostic capabilities, and expanding vaccination coverage against 
diphtheria [62]. Public awareness campaigns that emphasize early symptoms, rapid healthcare seeking, and the value 
of vaccination should be carried out through a variety of media platforms and community involvement initiatives. These 
programs should be modelled after the effective COVID-19 awareness campaigns. To identify and manage cases promptly, 
enhanced clinical evaluation is essential. Treatment is started based on the clinical diagnosis while waiting for confirming 
test results. For efficient clinical treatment and timely case referral, healthcare delivery systems must be strengthened, 
particularly in rural areas. Furthermore, it is crucial to guarantee sufficient accessibility to diphtheria antitoxin and other 
vital medical supplies in every area to enhance patient outcomes and lower mortality rates [7 ].
Global guidelines for the prevention of diphtheria place a strong emphasis on the value of immunization, especially 
for young children. Three doses of the pentavalent vaccine, given at specified intervals during infancy, are advised by the 
childhood vaccination schedule to provide sufficient protection against diphtheria and other diseases that can be pre -
vented by vaccination. It is recommended that healthcare personnel who are at an increased risk of coming into contact 
with cases of diphtheria get vaccinated against the illness [28, 63]. To avert future outbreaks and sustainably manage 
the spread of diphtheria, long-term plans are necessary. Increasing funding for immunization programs, strengthening 
the infrastructure for disease surveillance to facilitate timely case reporting and data dissemination, enhancing access to 
healthcare services, especially in underserved areas, and putting laws into place to encourage vaccine uptake are some 
of these strategies. Policies from the government and organizations should encourage vaccination compliance. Examples 
of such policies include mandating immunization records for school enrollment and enforcing comparable regulations

4  Conclusion
This study provides a comprehensive overview of the resurgence of diphtheria, highlighting its epidemiology, trans-
mission, clinical manifestations, laboratory diagnosis, prevention strategies, vaccine coverage rates, and public health 
implications. Despite global progress in reducing diphtheria cases through vaccination efforts, recent outbreaks in low-
resource settings, including Nigeria, highlight the ongoing threat posed by this infectious disease. Effective prevention 
and control measures are essential, including maintaining high vaccination coverage rates, particularly among children, 
and addressing barriers to vaccination access and uptake. Improved laboratory diagnosis, early antibiotic administration, 
and prompt case management are crucial for reducing morbidity and mortality associated with diphtheria. Strengthen-
ing healthcare infrastructure, enhancing disease surveillance systems, and ensuring the availability of essential medi-
cal supplies are also essential components of outbreak control efforts. Public awareness campaigns and educational 
initiatives are crucial for increasing awareness of diphtheria symptoms, promoting vaccination, and encouraging early 
healthcare-seeking behaviour.
