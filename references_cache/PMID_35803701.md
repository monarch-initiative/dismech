BACKGROUND: Lowe syndrome (LS) is an X linked disease caused by pathogenic
variants in the OCRL gene that impacts approximately 1 in 500 000 children.
Classic features include congenital cataract, cognitive/behavioural impairment
and renal tubulopathy.
METHODS: This study is a retrospective review of clinical features reported by
family based survey conducted by Lowe Syndrome Association. Frequency of
non-ocular clinical feature(s) of LS and their age of onset was summarised. An
LS-specific therapy effectiveness scale was used to assess the response to the
administered treatment. Expression of OCRL and relevant neuropeptides was
measured in postmortem human brain by qPCR. Gene expression in the mouse brain
was determined by reanalysis of publicly available bulk and single cell RNA
sequencing.
RESULTS: A total of 137 individuals (1 female, 89.1% white, median age 14 years
(range 0.8-56)) were included in the study. Short stature (height <3rd
percentile) was noted in 81% (n=111) individuals, and 15% (n=20) received growth
hormone therapy. Undescended testis was reported in 47% (n=64), and median age
of onset of puberty was 15 years. Additional features were dental problems
(n=77, 56%), bone fractures (n=63, 46%), hypophosphataemia (n=60, 44%),
developmental delay and behavioural issues. OCRL is expressed in human and mouse
hypothalami, and in hypothalamic cell clusters expressing Ghrh, Sst, Oxt, Pomc
and pituitary cells expressing Gh and Prl.
CONCLUSIONS: There is a wide spectrum of the clinical phenotype of LS. Some of
the features may be partly driven by the loss of function of OCRL in the
hypothalamus and the pituitary.

Lowe syndrome (LS, OMIM # 309000), is a rare X-linked multisystemic disorder that was first described in 1952.1 It is characterized by congenital cataracts, glaucoma, cognitive/behavioural impairment, growth failure, bone disease, and renal tubular dysfunction that may lead to slowly progressive renal failure. Following the initial report, several cases were described with similar characteristics, albeit with a range of phenotypic spectrum. An X-linked mode of inheritance was postulated based on the predominance of clinical features in boys and minor features such as micropunctate lenticular opacities in heterozygous females without major systemic features.2 Subsequently, the genetic locus was mapped to Xq24-26 using restriction length polymorphism and linkage analysis3 and eventually defined at Xq26.1.The OCRL protein was shown to be highly homologous to inositol phosphatase and LS was postulated to be the first known genetic disorder of inositol metabolism.4 The identification of the genetic defect led to the recognition of the wide spectrum of clinical features – two unrelated individuals with the same variant, or individuals within a family with the same variant(s) could have different phenotypes.5
6

The complete sequence of the OCRL gene was described by Nussbaum et al in 1997.7 The encoded polyphosphate 5-phosphatase is a multi-domain protein with an N-terminal PH domain, a central 5-phosphatase domain and a C-terminal ASH and RhoGAP-like domains.8
9 OCRL preferentially converts phophatidyl 4,5-bisphophate (PIP2) involved in cytoskeletal-plasma membrane adhesion, to phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate.4 Subcellularly, it localizes to multiple compartments in the endocytic network (early endosomes, clathrin-coated pits, Golgi apparatus and the basal body), where it is proposed to play a role in the maturation of polarized epithelial cells, and cytokinesis.10
11 Fibroblasts of patients with LS show defects in the number and length of the primary cilia, and knock-down of Ocrl in zebrafish shows defects akin to ciliopathies.12
13 Cellular experiments indicate that Ocrl localizes to the base of primary cilium, may be involved in ciliogenesis, as well as vesicle trafficking by interaction with GTPase Rab8 and other endosomal proteins.12 It is possible that a combination of deregulation of the PIP2-dependent processes and/or ciliary function are responsible for the pleiotropic phenotype of LS. It is not yet known why the loss of function of OCRL results in clinical features confined to certain organ systems.

Due to the rare occurrence of the disease, the characterization of patients with LS is limited. The aim of this study was to describe and expand the clinical phenotype based on the data from a retrospective cross-sectional survey of parents/guardians of individuals with LS. The focus of this report is on the caretaker description of the non-ocular features of the disease. Additionally, OCRL expression was assessed in unaffected post-mortem human as well as mouse tissues to postulate on the potential mechanisms that may contribute to the features of the disease.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Columbia University Irving Medical Center. A comprehensive voluntary survey of 1) anthropometric and biochemical parameters, 2) clinical manifestations, and 3) the family’s perception of the treatment efficacy of children with LS was collected by the Lowe Syndrome Association (LSA) between 2008 and 2010.14
15 An LS-specific clinical effectiveness score based on the feedback of families of children with LS was used to define the adequacy of the therapy for each clinical feature on a scale of 1-5, with 1 being the least and 5 being the most successful. A score of ≥ 4 was considered an effective therapy for this report. Behavioural topographies devised by Cressey et al16 were used to classify the reported behavioural features. The normal age of attainment of milestones from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)17 was used to contextualize the age of achieved milestones for individuals with LS.

Appropriate summary statistics based on the distribution of the data were used for each of the clinical features. Ages reported in whole numbers after 2 years of age were converted to mid-year point for analyses. The SAS Program for the 2000 CDC growth charts18 was used to derive the height and BMI percentiles and z-scores using self-reported height, weight and age of the child. Analysis was conducted in R version 4.319 and figures were produced using the visualization package ggplot2. Data analysis was undertaken from July–November 2021.

Human hypothalami were dissected from flash frozen post-mortem brain from an early adolescent male who died secondary to sepsis, and a male in late 60s who died of cardiovascular disease, both without any apparent neuropathology. Total RNA was extracted using Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA) and reverse transcription was done using the Applied Biosystems High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Quantitative real time PCR was performed using a Roche LightCycler 480 (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) and the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix qPCR kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Transcript measurements were normalized to β-actin or GAPDH mRNA levels. Primer sequences used are summarized in Supplemental Table 1. Reactions were done in triplicate in 10 μL reaction volume. After a 5-minute hotstart at 95°C, 45 cycles were run with 95°C denaturation for 10 seconds, annealing at 60°C for 10 seconds, and extension at 72°C for 10 seconds followed by acquisition. Melting curves were calculated for each run and the product was run on a 2% agarose gel to ensure only one product was amplified per reaction. Crossing points were determined by the Roche software using the second derivative maximum technique. Standard curves using serial dilutions were run for each primer pair.

Gene expression in mouse brain tissues was assessed using previously published bulk and single cell RNA sequencing data obtained from NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO): 1) region specific bulk RNA sequencing data for murine brain from gene expression atlas of energy balance brain regions (accession GSE178290)20; 2) single cell gene expression data for mouse arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus (ARH, accession GSE90806)21; and 3) single cell gene expression data for mouse pituitary (accession GSE120410).22 Methods for the analysis are included in the supplement.

A total of 137 unique individuals with a median age of 14 years (IQR 7-21) at the time of survey were included in the study. One of the subjects was a female, who could not be distinguished in the data, as gender was not obtained in the survey.14 Diagnosis was made at a median age of 0.5 years (IQR 0.01-1.7) by a geneticist, nephrologist, ophthalmologist, primary care physician, and by a researcher in 2 cases using clinical features (n=114), genetic variant (n=79, reported variants [n=24] in Supplemental Table 2), skin biopsy for inositol polyphosphate 5-phosphatase activity (< 10% of enzymatic function, n=118). Multiple affected individuals were present in 22 families, and 13 had living affected siblings. Most individuals included in this report identified themselves as White (n=123, 89.8%). Cataracts were seen in all patients with the age of onset within 2 months of life in 98.5% individuals. A quarter of the carrier mothers (n=30, 21.9%) were diagnosed with cataract at a median age of 29 years (IQR 26-34.7). The aggregate number of subjects where each clinical feature was reported, and the age of onset are shown in Figure 2.

The birth weight (mean 3.36 ± 0.59 kg) and length (mean 0.52 ± 0.03 m) were normal. At the time of the survey, 117 males (85.4%) had short stature, defined as stature < 3rd percentile for age. A small fraction (n=20, 14.8%) reported using recombinant growth hormone therapy for 0.1 – 9 years. The plateau of the composite growth curve was at 20 years compared to the normal expectation of 16–18 years for males. Overweight (BMI > 85th percentile) and obesity (BMI > 95th percentile for age) were present in 48 (35.0%) of the affected individuals. Severe obesity (BMI > 120% of 95th percentile, or ≥ 40 kg/m2) was seen in 10 (7.3%) of the surveyed individuals (Figure 3).

Undescended testis was noted in 64 males (46.7%) diagnosed at a median age of 0.3 years (IQR 0.01-2.3) and was surgically treated in 36 males. Age of puberty by parental or clinical evaluation was reported in 52 males by assessment of pubic hair with the median of 15 years (IQR 13.0-16.0) and voice change at 16 years (IQR 14.0-17.0). Sixty percent (n=23) of the 38 males who responded noted engaging in masturbation. A small fraction (n=13) answered the question regarding ejaculation, 77% of which (n=10) responded positively.

Low phosphorus (n=57, 41.6%), low Vitamin D levels (n=51,37.2%) and bone fractures (n=63, 46.0%) were common (Table 1). Fractures were reported in long bones (upper or lower extremity, n=63), hip (n=7) and spine (n=1), as well as in joints, including knee or ankles (n=16), feet or toes (n=6) and wrist/hand or fingers (n=17). Dental problems such as dental cysts, gingivitis, dental surgery of the primary or secondary teeth for either correction or treatment of dental disease, were reported in 56.2% (n=77) of the individuals. Of the 39 subjects with scoliosis, 10 were using a brace, 2 required surgery and the rest received conservative treatment. Aside from the mineral deficiencies listed in Table 1, other noted deficiencies include carnitine deficiency (n=30, 21.9%) and low magnesium (n=5, 3.6%).

Kidney calcification was reported in 55.4% (n=76) at a median age of 3 years (IQR 0.9-10.0), and renal stones in 21.9 % (n=30). The therapy for this included hydration (n=76), changes in diet including low salt, calcium, and oxalate (n=54), and therapy with polycitra, bicarbonate, phosphate (n=30) and ultrasound treatment (n=15). A small fraction of individuals (n = 25) were taking drug therapy for the treatment of calcification, of which 60% (n = 15) reported it as effective. Acidosis was reported in the 72.3% (n=99) of individuals, treated with citrates (n=53), sodium bicarbonate (n=41), and in a smaller proportion with sodium phosphate and other therapies, and 90% of those treated noted it to be effective. Hypercalciuria without calcification was most commonly treated with potassium citrate (n=16) and chlorothiazide/ hydrochlorothiazide (n=13). Hypophosphatemia was treated with potassium or sodium phosphate supplementation (n=43), and hypokalemia with citric acid/sodium citrate (n=20), potassium phosphate (n=19) or potassium chloride (n=11). Vitamin D supplementation with calcitriol was given in 31 individuals and 4 subjects reported high levels of Vitamin D between 13-19 years of age. Anaemia was reported in 56 individuals (40.9%) at a median age of 5 years (IQR 1-15) and treated with iron supplements in 40 individuals, Epogen therapy in 12, and 1 subject received a blood transfusion.

In this cohort, majority of the subjects could eat table foods with the family using a spoon (n=107, 78.1%) and fork (n=70, 51.1%), or finger foods (n=79, 57.7%). Some of the subjects were able to prepare meals independently (n=13) and others were able to assist in meal preparation (n=35). Tube feeding was required in a small number of subjects by nasogastric tube (n=13) or gastrostomy tube (n=10). Constipation was noted in 82 subjects with median onset at 0.6 years (IQR 0.4-3) and the routine therapies for constipation used were considered effective in 70% of the affected individuals. Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) was reported in 36 subjects with median age of onset at 2.2 years (IQR 0.1-16.5). The treatment of GERD varied from changes in mode of feeding (n=5), H2 receptor antagonists (n=9), proton pump inhibitor (n=11), motility enhancers (n=5), antacids (n=4), and surgery (n=3) and was considered effective in 45.0% who reported it. Umbilical hernia was noted in 27 individuals at birth, resolved spontaneously in 61.5% and needed surgical correction in 4 subjects.

Seizures were reported in 44.5% (n=61), with the median age of first seizure at 6.5 years (IQR 2-10). A variable spectrum of seizure type was seen – generalized tonic clonic (n=39), generalized absence (n=18), partial simple (n=17), partial complex (n=15), partial with secondary generalization (n=9), atonic (n=6), myoclonic (n=4), and a small number of other types. Seventy-seven percent of the affected individuals reported effectiveness of anti-seizure medications. Sixty-four individuals reported using a wheelchair by a median age of 3 years (IQR 1.9-10.2) of whom over half (n=47) were using it more than 50% of the time. Other mobility equipment included braces (n=44) and standing frame (n=41). Most of the patients received physical therapy (n=112, 81.7%) and occupational therapy (n=80, 58.4%) starting at a median age of 0.5 year (IQR 0.2-1.3).

Median age and IQR of attainment of milestones for cognitive, communication and language, social and emotional, and motor development are listed in Supplemental Table 3. The age of attainment of these milestones in normal children listed by the CDC are provided as a context, but no statistical comparison was done as reference does not include age range. Common behavioural issues noted by the parents/guardians and their age of onset is reported in Table 2. Majority of individuals received early childhood education at home by therapist and/or teacher (n=108, 70%). Many attended preschools for children with disabilities (n=88, 64.2%), and some a regular preschool (n=39, 28.5%). The grade school varied from an inclusive school for children with all abilities (n=66, 48.2%), for children with physical disabilities (n=44, 32.1%), visual disability (n=36, 26.3%), multiple disabilities (n=80, 58.4%) and those with behavioural issues (n=36, 26.3%). The academic setting varied from general education (n=79, 57.7%), special education (n=86, 62.8%) to residential (n=10, 7.3%) school.

A high expression of OCRL was noted in different parts of the brain, with the highest relative expression noted in the human hypothalamus (Figure 4 a). In the bulk RNA sequencing of 5-week-old mice brain (C57BL/6, GSE178290), high expression of Ocrl was noted in the cortex, as well as regions involved in the regulation of hypothalamic pituitary axes and weight regulation -paraventricular hypothalamus, lateral hypothalamus, and the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus (ARH). Similar expression was noted in the developing embryonic hypothalamus (Embryonic day 14.5) (Figure 4 b). In the single cell RNA sequencing from the mouse ARH (GSE90806), expression of Ocrl was seen in cell clusters expressing pro-opiomelanocortin (Pomc; involved in weight regulation), Oxytocin (Oxt; involved in behaviour and weight regulation), Growth hormone releasing hormone (Ghrh, involved in growth) and Somatostatin (Sst, involved in behaviour) (Figure 4 c, and supplemental Figure 1). In the reanalysis of single cell RNA-sequencing data of the mouse pituitary (GSE120410), Ocrl expression was seen in a fraction of somatotropes (expressing Gh), lactotropes (expressing Prl), melanotropes (expressing Pomc, post-translationally cleaved to α-MSH and ACTH peptides) as well as some pituitary progenitor cells (Figure 4 d).

This report summarizes the clinical features from a large cohort of patients with LS based on a family survey. Assessment by a survey instrument that can be distributed widely is a useful way to collect data from many subjects in a rare condition like LS. The summarization of the ophthalmological features in this cohort has been previously reported.23 Detailed reports are also available on the renal and the neurological abnormalities seen in individuals with LS, albeit in smaller cohorts.24-26 This report expands on the endocrine and behavioural manifestations of LS.

Short stature has been previously reported in case reports and series of patients with LS, as well as in the mouse model.27 In a cross-sectional study of 23 subjects (ages 4 months–31 years) where LS was defined using phenotypic findings prior to the availability of genetic sequencing, Charnas et al described normal length at birth, but height/length below 3rd percentile in 78% of the subjects. Based on the 4 adult patients in this cohort, they noted a delay in the bone age in the older boys, lagging behind the height age, and continued height gain into adulthood well after the expected plateau between 16-18 years of age.28 Our study expands on this by demonstrating short stature in over 85% of the individuals with LS with lower length/stature noted after 1 year of age. The composite growth curve in our study, based on a larger sample, is consistent with prior observations. Data on the bone age were not available for this study. In contrast to the observation by Charnas et al of weight ≤ 3rd percentile, we observed a larger number of individuals with overweight and obesity. These may reflect the temporal trends of obesity in children and adults over the past few decades; may indicate the wider phenotypic spectrum in a larger cohort; could be related to the use of neuropsychiatric medication – some of which can result in weight gain; or suggest hitherto unknown role of OCRL in body weight regulation. Prior case reports have noted undescended testis, delayed puberty and infertility in boys, and postulated the role of dysfunction in the hypothalamic pituitary gonadal axis in LS.25
29 Our findings are consistent with these reports of undescended testis and delayed puberty, whereas data on infertility were not available in the study cohort.

OCRL is highly expressed in the human hypothalamus, pituitary, and other endocrine tissues in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) atlas.30 The co-expression of neuropeptides with OCRL in the both human and mouse hypothalamus demonstrated in this study may suggest functional association. In addition, expression of Ocrl was noted in cell clusters of mouse ARH and the mouse pituitary known for the regulation of neuropeptides involved in growth, (height and weight) and gonadal/pubertal function. OCRL localizes to several cellular compartments–including the trans-golgi network, endoplasmic reticulum, as well as the primary cilium.8 In cells such as neurons, where the primary cilia play a modulatory role in developmental and neurochemical signalling pathways,31 OCRL may mediate endocytic flux of relevant neuropeptides. It is conceivable that short stature in subjects with LS may be due, at least partly, to dysregulation of the hypothalamic or pituitary-associated trophic factors that can be amenable to therapy with recombinant growth hormone. Growth hormone therapy is known to improve neurodevelopmental outcomes in individuals with Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS),32-34 and it is possible that similar benefit may be seen in individuals with LS with appropriate replacement therapy. Further, there may be a role of OCRL in the gonadal and/or pubertal development leading to undescended testis and/or delayed pubertal development that needs further investigation. While Ocrl was co-expressed in the cell clusters expressing POMC, a neuropeptide important in the weight regulatory pathway in the mouse ARH as well as other regions of the mouse brain involved in weight regulation, the proportion of individuals with obesity in this cohort was not different from population prevalence in both children and adults. Disproportionately higher level of subcutaneous adipose tissue resulting in children who appeared obese for their length has been noted since the first description of LS by Lowe et al.1 Pathogenic variants in another inositol phosphatase, INPP5E, leads to MORM syndrome, a ciliopathy characterized by intellectual disability, central obesity, retinal dystrophy and micropenis.35 Similarly, obesity has been noted in a patient with Joubert syndrome caused by truncating mutation in INPP5E.36 Both INPP5E and OCRL belong to inositol phosphatase class of proteins, and it is possible there are functional similarities in their role in phosphoinostide-3-kinase (PI3K) and mTOR related pathways and in ciliary function relevant to energy homeostasis and metabolic health37-39 that remains to be explored.

Children with LS were delayed in achieving milestones. The specific maladaptive behaviours seen in this study align with the prior reports of temper outbursts, complex repetitive behaviours (stereotypy), stubbornness, negativism, and obsessions/unusual preoccupations seen in individuals with LS.40
41 Self-injurious behaviour, aggression, hyperactivity, lack of social engagement and characteristics of autism spectrum disorders have been described in genetic syndromes associated with intellectual disability such as Smith-Magenis syndrome, PWS, Rett, Cornelia de Lange, Fragile X, Angelman and Cri du Chat syndrome amongst others, although the spectrum may vary in each condition.42
43 As an example, individuals with PWS tend to require adherence to routine and often have difficulty in modulating emotions. In contrast, physical and verbal aggression towards others is more prominent in LS with the outbursts seen daily or weekly. Further, the disruptive features occur at an older age in PWS compared to LS.16 In normal children, temper outbursts are most commonly seen under 5 years of age.44 Using age equivalent scores from Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scale -II in a cohort of 17 individuals with LS, Cressey et al demonstrated that half of the subjects had a developmental age under 5 years, although their chronological age was ≥ 8 years. Hence, they postulated that the frustration intolerance prominent in LS manifesting in the temper outbursts, may be triggered by the inability to meet physiological (such as hunger, thirst, tiredness), emotional or social needs and reflective of the developmental rather than chronological age. The inability to tolerate frustration in normal children and in older paediatric psychiatric inpatients, is thought to be due to underdevelopment of cognitive mechanisms which control and regulate emotions and behaviour known as executive functions.16 Whether these are determined by the biological changes from the genetic defect, intellectual disability, or an interaction between biological/developmental consequences of genetic difference and environmental factors remains to be identified. Prior studies have noted a tendency towards self-injury related to inserting objects into body parts in LS, perhaps related to visual difficulties.40
42 This was not commonly reported in this cohort. Neuroradiological studies using MRI have shown non-specific abnormalities in brain development in LS, including delayed myelination and the presence of small cystic lesions in white matter.45 The relationship of these findings with developmental and neurological consequences, and the elucidation of the underlying molecular mechanisms of OCRL is the next step towards developing effective management strategies.

This study is limited by the mode of ascertainment of the data. The survey methodology has the advantage of being able to sample a wide array of subjects across geographical borders and environments, making it especially useful for a rare disease, but may be compromised by the absence of direct observational data. The survey did not use standardized psychometric instruments; therefore, data collection may be biased by possible variable response of caregivers. Nevertheless, the findings on this survey are comparable to previous reports on subjects with LS.24-26 The tissue co-expression data noted in this study provide clues toward cell types and subpopulations where the function of OCRL can be studied, but requires further mechanistic studies to implicate causation. In this study, OCRL expression was not ascertained in the bone or the kidney, which limits the assessment of tissue expression in these organ systems. The mouse model for LS partially recapitulates the phenotype for renal features as well as decreased length, however, the mice lack significant neurological/behavioral phenotype.46 It is possible that in vivo studies in zebrafish13 and in vitro studies with patient-specific iPSC-derived cells47 may provide insight into the functional changes associated with the clinical features in order to guide therapies in future.
