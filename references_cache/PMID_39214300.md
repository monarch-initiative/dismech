# Interaction between the TBC1D24 TLDc domain and the KIBRA C2 domain is disrupted by two epilepsy-associated TBC1D24 missense variants.
**Authors:** Tona R, Inagaki S, Ishibashi Y, Faridi R, Yousaf R, Roux I, Wilson E, Fenollar-Ferrer C, Chien WW, Belyantseva IA, Friedman TB
**Journal:** J Biol Chem (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jbc.2024.107725](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbc.2024.107725)

## Content

Mutations of human TBC1D24 are associated with deafness, epilepsy, or DOORS syndrome (deafness, onychodystrophy, osteodystrophy, cognitive disability, and seizures). The causal relationships between TBC1D24 variants and the different clinical phenotypes are not understood. Our hypothesis is that phenotypic heterogeneity of missense mutations of TBC1D24 results, in part, from perturbed binding of different protein partners. To discover novel protein partners of TBC1D24, we conducted yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screen using mouse full-length TBC1D24 as bait. Kidney and brain protein (KIBRA), a scaffold protein encoded by Wwc1, was identified as a partner of TBC1D24. KIBRA functions in the Hippo signaling pathway and is important for human cognition and memory. The TBC1D24 TLDc domain binds to KIBRA full-length and to its C2 domain, confirmed by Y2H assays. No interaction was detected with Y2H assays between the KIBRA C2 domain and TLDc domains of NCOA7, MEAK7, and OXR1. Moreover, the C2 domains of other WWC family proteins do not interact with the TLDc domain of TBC1D24, demonstrating specificity. The mRNAs encoding TBC1D24 and KIBRA proteins in mouse are coexpressed at least in a subset of hippocampal cells indicating availability to interact in vivo. As two epilepsy-associated recessive variants (Gly511Arg and Ala515Val) in the TLDc domain of human TBC1D24 disrupt the interaction with the human KIBRA C2 domain, this study reveals a pathogenic mechanism of TBC1D24-associated epilepsy, linking the TBC1D24 and KIBRA pathways. The interaction of TBC1D24-KIBRA is physiologically meaningful and necessary to reduce the risk of epilepsy.

TBC1D24, TBC1 domain family member 24, is a member of a family of genes encoding a Tre2/Bub2/Cdc16 (TBC) domain-containing proteins (1). TBC1D24 is unique among TBC family members as it also has a TLDc modular domain [TBC, lysin motif (LysM), domain catalytic] (2, 3). Pathogenic variants of human TBC1D24 are associated with three disorders that range broadly in severity and include either deafness (4, 5, 6), epilepsy with or without a hearing loss (7, 8, 9, 10, 11), or DOORS syndrome characterized by deafness, onychodystrophy (nail dystrophy), osteodystrophy (abnormal bone growth), cognitive disability, and seizures (12, 13, 14). Although nonsense and frameshift mutations of TBC1D24 are associated with more severe clinical presentations, overall, the genotype-phenotype relationship of TBC1D24 variants is puzzling (7, 9, 13, 14, 15). Variants causing each of the three classes of TBC1D24-associated disorders are not clustered either by location in the gene or regions of the protein (7). Greater understanding of the WT biological functions of TBC1D24 and its interactions with other potential protein partners are needed to develop treatments for these disorders, especially for the unremitting infantile myoclonic epilepsy, which is often refractory to available antiseizure drugs (13, 16).

Among alternative mRNAs transcribed from the human TBC1D24 gene (5), the longest full-length (FL) isoform encodes 559 amino acid residues including the N-terminal TBC domain and a C-terminal TLDc domain (RefSeq: NP_001186036.1). Pathogenic missense variants of human TBC1D24 associated with disorders such as deafness, epilepsy, or DOORS syndrome are reported in both domains (Table S1) (9). In general, TBC domain-containing proteins regulate membrane trafficking by accelerating GTP hydrolysis to inactivate a specific Rab-GTPase (17, 18), while some TLDc domain-containing mammalian proteins, such as oxidation resistance 1 (OXR1), act to reduce oxidative stress in neuronal cells (3, 19). Mouse and human develop neurological deficits associated with a loss of WT OXR1 function and have a reduced ability to respond to oxidative stress (19, 20). Additionally, assembly of mouse vacuolar H+-ATPases (V-ATPases) is regulated by the TLDc domain-containing proteins nuclear receptor coactivator 7 (NCOA7) and OXR1 (21, 22, 23). However, no interaction was detected between mouse V-ATPase and the purified TLDc domain of TBC1D24 and MTOR associated protein, eak-7 homolog (MEAK7) (21), leaving the function of the TLDc domain of TBC1D24 unresolved (24).

Several approaches have been used to study the biological roles of TBC1D24 and to explore how variants cause different disorders. Mouse models of Tbc1d24 were engineered with mutations that correspond to human disease-causing variants of TBC1D24. These mouse models show a range of phenotypes from embryonic lethality to fatal seizures (9, 15) while other Tbc1d24 mouse models do not recapitulate human deafness for reasons that are not understood (9, 15). TBC1D24 mRNA is expressed ubiquitously in mammalian tissues (GTExPortal, https://www.gtexportal.org, dbGaP Accession phs000424.v8.p2 on July 10, 2024). Immunogold electron microscopy images of mouse brain reveal TBC1D24 protein at synapses and adjacent to clathrin-coated vesicles, suggesting that TBC1D24 has a role in vesicle trafficking in neuronal signal transmission (9, 15). Consistent with this assumption, mutant alleles of skywalker (sky), the TBC1D24 ortholog of Drosophila melanogaster, increase endosomal trafficking of synaptic vesicles at neuromuscular junction boutons thereby escalating neurotransmitter release (25). The phenotype of biallelic sky mutations varies from embryonic lethality (sky1) to paralysis and death of the larvae (sky2) (25, 26, 27).

Currently, three proteins have been reported to be binding partners of the TBC domain of TBC1D24 orthologues: ADP-ribosylation factor 6 (ARF6) (8), Ras-associated protein Rab35 (RAB35) (26), and disheveled 2 (28). While these studies provide important insights into TBC1D24 function, the clinically heterogeneous neuropathogenic disorders caused by mutations of TBC1D24 remain unexplained. Particularly enigmatic are the missense mutations of the TLDc domain (Table S1), as the cellular function of the TLDc domain of TBC1D24 remains unknown (3).

The functions of a protein can depend upon its interactions with other biomolecules to form macromolecular complexes (29, 30). We hypothesized that the three clinically distinct neuropathogenic phenotypes associated with a variety of missense mutations of TBC1D24 result, in part, from binding impediments of different protein partners. Identifying the partners of TBC1D24 would provide a greater understanding of the biological cellular networks in which it functions. To identify potential protein partner(s) of TBC1D24, we used yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screens (31, 32) with mouse TBC1D24 FL protein (mTBC1D24 FL) as bait. Our screening results identified mouse kidney and brain protein (KIBRA) encoded by the Wwc1 gene (WW and C2 domain containing 1) as a partner. KIBRA has multiple functions; among them is the ability to modulate synaptic plasticity (33). In cognitively normal Caucasian cohorts, an intronic and two common coding single-nucleotide polymorphisms in WWC1 encoding KIBRA are significantly associated, although with a small effect size, on retrieval performance of experience, referred to as episodic memory (34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39). Additionally, de novo “gene-disrupting” variants of KIBRA are associated with an increased risk of Tourette syndrome (40). KIBRA is also involved with metastasis suppression (41), and the Hippo signaling pathway (42), which modulates organ size and regeneration (43). In mouse, loss of KIBRA decreases exosome secretion due to a lack of stabilization of RAB27A which controls exosome secretion in neuronal cell lines (44). KIBRA is a hub that interacts with numerous different proteins (45), among them are synaptopodin, dendrin (46, 47, 48), histone H3 (49) and PALS1-associated tight junction protein, PATJ (46) that bind to its WW domains, glutamic-rich region and PDZ-binding motif, respectively. Recently, in studies of the ancestry of Hominidae, WWC1 was identified as one of a group of seven genes positively selected in the accelerated evolution of body size, transforming growth factor-β, and Wnt signaling (50).

Here, we report that the TLDc domain of TBC1D24 binds to the C2 domain of KIBRA in both human and mouse. TBC1D24 and KIBRA are coexpressed in the same cells of the mouse hippocampus, suggesting these proteins can interact in vivo in a cellular complex. The interaction between TBC1D24 and KIBRA is disrupted by the reported human epilepsy-associated recessive variants Gly511Arg and Ala515Val, which are located in the TLDc domain (7, 51). The findings from this study help to shed new light on the functions of TBC1D24 and the pathophysiology behind its mutations.

We hypothesized that previously elusive functions of TBC1D24 would be revealed by identifying the protein partners with which it forms molecular complexes. Next, nanoscale pull-down (NanoSPD 2.0) assays were performed to test whether this interaction can also occur in live human cells (Figs. 1C and S1) (52). For NanoSPD assays, three expression plasmids were used; (i) a myosin 10 motor heavy meromyosin (HMM) that was fused in frame at the C terminus to an enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP)-Nanotrap, referred to here as MYO10-HMM-Nanotrap, which binds to EGFP but not to mCherry (30), (ii) an EGFP-tagged mTBC1D24 FL, and (iii) a mCherry-tagged mKIBRA FL. When all three plasmids were cotransfected and expressed in HeLa cells, the MYO10-HMM-Nanotrap cotransported EGFP-fused bait protein along actin filaments to the tips of filopodia. If the bait and prey proteins interact, the prey will also be transported to filopodia tips. Robust accumulation of both EGFP and mCherry fluorescence was observed as a yellow signal at the filopodial tips of these cells (Fig. S1), indicating that the MYO10-HMM-Nanotrap transported the EGFP-tagged mTBC1D24 FL bound to mKIBRA FL (Fig. 1, C–E). A control experiment using MYO10-HMM-Nanobody, mCherry-tagged mKIBRA FL and EGFP alone (i.e. EGFP without bait protein) showed EGFP at the filopodial tips, while the mCherry fluorescence remained in the cytoplasm. The control data indicate that mCherry itself does not bind to MYO10-HMM-Nanobody (Fig. 1C bottom). When EGFP-tagged mTBC1D24 FL was expressed with the MYO10-HMM-Nanobody and mCherry-tagged mKIBRA FL, the fluorescence intensity of EGFP-tagged mTBC1D24 FL at filopodial tips was significantly higher than the control experiment using EGFP alone (Fig. 1, D and E).

To pinpoint the specific region of TBC1D24–KIBRA interaction, Y2H assays were performed with various domain combinations of these two proteins (Fig. 2A). Yeast cotransformed with mTBC1D24 FL plus mKIBRA FL, mTBC1D24 TLDc domain plus mKIBRA FL, mTBC1D24 FL plus mKIBRA C2, and mTBC1D24 TLDc domain plus mKIBRA C2 domain all grew on the Y2H selection medium. However, the mTBC1D24 TBC domain plus mKIBRA FL or mTBC1D24 TBC domain plus the mKIBRA C2 domain did not grow. In yeast, the TBC domain of mTBC1D24 alone does not interact with either FL or just the C2 domain of mKIBRA (Fig. 2B). There are reports that human KIBRA (hKIBRA) forms dimers in mammalian cells and in yeast (53). X-ray crystal structures of hKIBRA C2 domain show that it forms a parallel dimer using a disulfide bond of Cys771 residue of each C2 domain (54). Following on from these reported observations, we conducted Y2H assays to test whether mKIBRA forms homodimers in yeast. mKIBRA FL WT or mKIBRA FL with a Cys772Ala substitution corresponding to Cys771Ala of hKIBRA (Fig. S2) was used as both bait and prey. As shown in Figure 3A, yeast cotransformed with mKIBRA FL WT used as bait and prey grew on the selection media, indicating mKIBRA FL WT forms homodimers. Yeast cotransformed with mKIBRA FL Cys772Ala substitution used as bait and prey also grew on selection media indicating that mKIBRA FL Cys772Ala also forms homodimers. Additionally, yeast cotransformed with mKIBRA FL WT and Cys772Ala as bait and prey, respectively, also grew on selection media. Similarly, mKIBRA C2 domain WT or the C2 domain with Cys772Ala substitutions were used as bait and prey. Y2H assay showed that yeast cotransformed with any of these bait and prey combinations did not grow on selection media. These results indicate that mKIBRA FL can form homodimers in yeast without an intermolecular disulfide bond of Cys772 of the C2 domain. On the other hand, the C2 domain alone of mKIBRA exists as monomer in yeast. hKIBRA C2 domain alone exists as mixture of dimer and monomer in solution regardless of the WT or Cys771Ala substitution in vitro (54). Next, we performed Y2H assays using mTBC1D24 FL and the TLDc domain as bait and mKIBRA FL and C2 domain as prey. Yeast cotransformed with mTBC1D24 FL and the TLDc domain with mKIBRA WT or the C2 domain grew on selection media (Fig. 3B). These results indicate that both mTBC1D24 FL and the TLDc domain alone can interact with mKIBRA FL and the mKIBRA C2 domain in yeast.

There are five human (h) and five mouse (m) TLDc domain-containing proteins, TBC1D24, NCOA7, MEAK7, and TBC/LysM-associated domain containing 2 (mTLDC2) and OXR1. The amino acid sequence identity of these TLDc domain-containing proteins range from 22% to 30% and 37% to 44% similarity compared to that of mTBC1D24 (Table S4 and Fig. S3, A and B). In particular, human NCOA7-AS (Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 7OBP), zebrafish OXR2 (PDB ID: 4ACJ), and fruit fly TBC1D24 (PDB ID: 6R82) have very similar three-dimensional protein structures. The next question we addressed is whether the other TLDc domain-containing proteins bind to the C2 domain of KIBRA. To evaluate the specificity of the interaction, Y2H assays were conducted using each of the four other mouse TLDc domain containing proteins as bait and mKIBRA C2 domain as prey (Fig. 4). Only yeast cotransformed with mTBC1D24 TLDc domain and mKIBRA C2 domain grew on the selection media, indicating that the four other TLDc domain-containing proteins do not interact with mKIBRA C2 domain. The genotype-phenotype relationships of human TBC1D24 disorders and their functional bases are largely unexplored. We hypothesized that a subset of pathogenic variants of TBC1D24 perturbs discrete physical interactions with partners that can explain, in part, the spectrum of disorders. Here, we report a novel interaction of the TBC1D24 TLDc domain with the KIBRA C2 domain. To evaluate the physiological relevance of this TBC1D24-KIBRA interaction, we tested whether this partnership is disrupted by a subset of human pathogenic missense mutations located in the TBC1D24 TLDc domain. Two neighboring epilepsy-associated variants Gly511Arg and Ala515Val of hTBC1D24 (8, 51) located in the TLDc domain (Fig. 8A) disrupted the interaction between hTBC1D24 and hKIBRA. The mTBC1D24 Ala517Val substitution, corresponding to hTBC1D24 Ala515Val, abolishes the interaction of the mTBC1D24 TLDc domain with the mKIBRA C2 domain. These data indicate that the Ala515 residue, which is located on the surface of the TLDc domain of human TBC1D24, is essential for the interaction with the C2 domain of hKIBRA. The recessive Ala515Val variant is robustly associated with epilepsy as it is segregating in a large Italian kindred of 14 individuals of whom eight are epileptic (Table S5) (7) and initially genetically linked to polymorphic genetic markers in a 3.4 cM interval of chromosome 16p13, which is the location of the gene encoding hTBC1D24 (7, 72, 73). The eight epileptic individuals were then shown to be compound heterozygous for Asp147His, a reported loss-of-function variant, and an Ala515Val substitution of hTBC1D24 (7). Detailed clinical data for the affected members reveal afebrile familial infantile myoclonic epilepsy that persists into adulthood with seizure severity reduced, but not eliminated, by treatment with valproate (73).

The Gly511 residue of hTBC1D24 is located in the same linker region as Ala515, which connects two β-sheets of the predicted protein structure of hTBC1D24 (Fig. 7B). The Gly511Arg substitution in the TLDc domain of hTBC1D24 appears to have a positive but weakened interaction with the KIBRA C2 domain. The Gly511Arg variant of hTBC1D24 was described in a single person in compound heterozygosity with Thr182Met (Table S5) who has Rolandic epilepsy and exercise-induced dystonia (51), which is a mild disorder compared to the severe form of epilepsy observed in the large family segregating the Ala515Val variant (7). Whether an apparently weakened interaction between hTBC1D24 TLDc domain and hKIBRA C2 domain causes mild epilepsy requires further investigation. For example, if additional missense variants of the TLDc domain would be found in patients who show a range of seizure disorders that vary directly with interaction strengths between TBC1D24 and KIBRA, then this would represent evidence supporting causation. Interaction strength quantification will require a more direct assay using biophysical affinity measurements of purified proteins, not just Y2H assay by varying 3-AT concentration, which, nevertheless, do offer an encouraging first approximation.

The activity of mouse V-ATPases can be controlled by its interaction with some TLDc domain-containing proteins (20, 21, 22). The mNCOA7 TLDc binds to mouse V-ATPase (mV-ATPase) (21). The six amino acid residues Gly815, Ser817, Gly845, Gly896, Leu926, and Glu938 of the mNCOA7 TLDc domain disturb the interaction with mV-ATPase. These residues are well conserved among TLDc domains of TLDc domain-containing proteins (Fig. S11). Mutation of Gly815 to Ala of mNCOA7 disrupts the interaction between mNCOA7 and mV-ATPase. mNCOA7 Gly815 corresponds to Ser376 of hTBC1D24 which has an epilepsy-associated variant Ser376Arg. In the case of TBC1D24, substitutions of this amino acid do not affect the interaction of mTBC1D24 with mKIBRA. Gly896 of mNCOA7 is located in a loop region between two β-sheets, corresponding to the loop region of hTBC1D24 in which the epilepsy-associated variants Gly511 and Gly515 are located. The protein stability of this region of TBC1D24 is affected by the size of side chains of amino acid as shown in Figure 8 and Table S4, and the substitution of Ala at Gly515 has no influence on the interaction of mTBC1D24 with mKIBRA. Additionally, no interaction was detected between purified TLDc proteins of TBC1D24 and MEAK7 with V-ATPase (21). These results suggest that the interaction of the TLDc domain of TBC1D24 and the C2 domain of KIBRA has a different function compared to the interaction of the TLDc domain of mNCOA7 with mV-ATPase. An amino acid sequence comparison shows several differences between the TLDc domain of TBC1D24 and the other TLDc domain-containing proteins (Fig. S11). One difference is a large distinctive region in TBC1D24 (amino acids 450–498 in mTBC1D24 in Fig. 4A). Y2H assays indicated that the deletion of this distinctive region did not alter the interaction between mTBC1D24 and mKIBRA (Fig. S4). As the loss of a functional interaction between TBC1D24 and KIBRA results in epilepsy, future experiments will investigate the interaction between TBC1D24 TLDc domain and KIBRA C2 domain at the atomic level.

In mouse, TBC1D24 and KIBRA are both expressed in a subset of the same cells of the hippocampus, consistent with the view that TBC1D24 and KIBRA can work together in the same pathway. It will be of interest using a mouse model to further explore the hypothesis that KIBRA interacts with TBC1D24 to prevent seizures. A mouse model could be engineered with the Ala517Val, orthologous to human Ala515Val of TBC1D24. A seizure phenotype for this mouse is realistic. A S324Tfs∗3 mutant mouse we engineered has a seizure phenotype recapitulating the epilepsy phenotype of affected members of a human family segregating the same S324Tfs∗3 variant (9). However, enthusiasm for such an outcome is tempered by our observations that some human TBC1D24 dominant and recessive variants associated with deafness result in normal hearing when the corresponding variants are engineered in mouse (15). The other epilepsy-associated variant Gly511Arg of hTBC1D24 may not be suitable for a mouse model. While hTBC1D24 Gly511Arg moderately disrupts the interaction with hKIBRA, the corresponding variant Gly513Arg of mTBC1D24 has no effect in Y2H assays on the interaction with mKIBRA (Figs. 8 and 9). Even though the amino acid sequences of TBC1D24 in mouse and human have 94% similarity (Table S3 and Figs. S2 and S11) there could be structural divergence responsible for the different effects of the corresponding substitution in the two species.

In summary, the data in this study expand the existing KIBRA scaffold complex of approximately 20 different proteins (45, 74) to include TBC1D24 and perhaps its protein partners. Here, we identified a previously unrecognized interaction between the TLDc domain of TBC1D24 and the C2 domain of KIBRA. This interaction wires together two individual scaffold proteins into a single more complex network. Future investigations could focus on understanding the cellular behavior of this complex (45, 71, 75). In the two assays we used, one human epilepsy-associated missense variant of TBC1D24 entirely abrogates the interaction between TBC1D24 and KIBRA and another variant appears to weaken it. Perhaps, a selective advantage of the role of this complex is to reduce the risk of seizures. Our findings provide the impetus for further studies of the biological functions of vertebrate TBC1D24 complexed with KIBRA and the eventual rational design of new and innovative antiseizure therapeutics.
