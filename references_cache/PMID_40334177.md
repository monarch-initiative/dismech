# Biopsy-Confirmed Small Vessel Primary CNS Vasculitis: Clinical Features and Impact of Early Intensive Treatment on Remission.
**Authors:** Reddy SP, LaHue SC, Goglin S, Chung SA, Poole S, Bove R, Pekmezci M, Tihan T, Gelfand JM
**Journal:** Neurol Neuroimmunol Neuroinflamm (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1212/NXI.0000000000200397](https://doi.org/10.1212/NXI.0000000000200397)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: This single-center retrospective cohort study sought to characterize the clinical spectrum of small vessel predominant primary CNS vasculitis (sv-PCNSV) and to investigate the impact of early intensive immunosuppressive therapy on remission.

METHODS: We analyzed data of patients diagnosed with biopsy-proven sv-PCNSV at our institution between 2009 and 2023. "Early intensive treatment" (EIT) was defined by cyclophosphamide therapy within 3 months of immunosuppressive treatment initiation. Patients in the "escalation treatment" (ESC) group initially received glucocorticoids, either as monotherapy or in conjunction with azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or methotrexate.

RESULTS: Twenty-six patients (50% female) met the study criteria, including 7 with amyloid-beta-related angiitis (ABRA). The median age at onset was 55.5 years (range 20-82), and headache (76.9%) and altered mental status (61.5%) were common presenting symptoms. Neuroimaging commonly showed bihemispheric T2/FLAIR lesions (77%) and abnormal gadolinium enhancement (88.5%), but intracranial vascular irregularities indicating large or medium vessel involvement were rare (11.5%). Among patients with non-ABRA sv-PCNSV (n = 19), some demonstrated spinal cord involvement (15.8%) and others exhibited isolated unihemispheric disease (21.1%). Although CSF testing (n = 23) often demonstrated mild pleocytosis, a notable minority of patients (17.4%) had a normal CSF analysis. Six patients (23.1%) underwent repeat brain biopsy because of initial nondiagnostic findings. Remission was achieved in all patients in the EIT group (n = 12/12), in contrast to 78.6% of patients in the ESC group (n = 11/14). Time to remission was significantly shorter among patients in the EIT group compared with the ESC group (median 5 vs 19 months, hazard ratio = 0.24, 95% CI [0.10-0.63], p < 0.005). Most patients achieving remission continued maintenance therapy, with an overall relapse rate of 19%.

DISCUSSION: This study highlights the considerable clinical heterogeneity in sv-PCNSV, a rare but serious condition. Early, aggressive treatment with cyclophosphamide is associated with a shorter time to remission, and further validation in prospective studies is warranted.

Primary central nervous system vasculitis (PCNSV), also referred to as primary angiitis of the central nervous system, is a rare autoimmune disorder characterized by destructive inflammation of blood vessels within the brain or spinal cord.1,2 Diagnosis requires either neuropathologic evidence of vasculitis on CNS biopsy or angiographic abnormalities consistent with vasculitis, and rigorous exclusion of alternate etiologies and secondary causes of CNS vasculitis (such as CNS infection, CNS involvement of systemic vasculitis, or malignancy).1,3 This broad diagnostic framework includes patients with predominantly large and/or medium vessel involvement detectable on conventional angiography, as well as those with involvement of small vessels that are below the resolution of angiography and thus only detectable on CNS biopsy.

Adult PCNSV cohort studies have tended to include all patients regardless of vessel-size involvement, and as a result, patients with small vessel predominant disease often comprise a minority in these cohorts.4-10 Analogous to systemic vasculitis syndromes, there are notable differences in PCNSV based on affected vessel size, including a higher likelihood of cognitive impairment, seizures, abnormalities on CSF testing, and gadolinium enhancement on MRI among patients with small vessel predominant PCNSV (sv-PCSNV).5,7,10

Lumping large vessel (lv-PCNSV) and small vessel phenotypes into a single diagnostic entity may limit our understanding of these distinct ends of the clinical spectrum. For example, several experts recommend early intensive treatment (EIT) with cyclophosphamide, based on observational data across the larger umbrella of PCNSV and, in part, from inference from clinical trial data in select systemic vasculitis syndromes.11,12 However, the optimal treatment of sv-PCNSV is uncertain and clinically variable,13,14 and some have considered glucocorticoid monotherapy as a reasonable first-line treatment option based on cohort studies suggesting a less aggressive clinical course for sv-PCNSV.6,7 The decision to pursue “early intensive” treatment with more potent therapies or to begin with lower intensity agents and “escalate” only if necessary is often highly individualized, weighing the potential for better long-term outcomes against the risk of more significant side effects. However, considering the paucity of sv-PCNSV–specific data and the real-world barriers that often further limit the use of cyclophosphamide in clinical practice—clinician discomfort, lack of expertise prescribing such medication, difficulty accessing care toxicity, contraindications, and patient tolerability, among others—further information is needed to inform these treatment decisions.

This single-center retrospective study sought to provide a detailed characterization of the clinicopathologic features and the impact of EIT with cyclophosphamide on time to remission among patients with biopsy-proven sv-PCNSV.

The University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) Medical Center electronic medical record system and the UCSF neuropathology database were queried with relevant ICD-10 codes and pertinent keywords to identify patients with sv-PCNSV from 2009 to 2023 (eMethods 1). A diagnosis of definite sv-PCNSV required CNS biopsy with neuropathologic evidence of vasculitis15 and exclusion of secondary etiologies.1,3 A subset of patients included in this study were diagnosed with amyloid-beta–related angiitis (ABRA), a subtype of sv-PCNSV defined by vasculitis affecting amyloid-beta–laden vessels in patients with comorbid cerebral amyloid angiopathy.16 Records were reviewed to exclude potential cases of CNS vasculitis secondary to infection, malignancy, or systemic autoimmune disease, mirroring criteria proposed by the French PCSNV Registry.9 In cases of uncertain diagnosis, the clinical records were adjudicated by 2 neurologists (S.P.R. and J.M.G.) and a neuropathologist (T.T.) to reach a consensus.

The analyses aimed to incorporate the clinical heterogeneity inherent to this real-world clinical data set. All patients received glucocorticoids at the time of treatment initiation, most commonly pulse-dose IV methylprednisolone (1,000 mg daily for 3–5 days) followed by extended oral steroid tapers. This was considered the starting time point for the time-to-remission analysis, even if immunosuppressive treatment was initiated empirically before definitive diagnosis. Two instances of brief empiric glucocorticoid exposure (<5 days, without ongoing immunosuppression) were not considered treatment initiation for the purposes of this analysis. Cumulative glucocorticoid exposure was calculated for each patient after the first remission and throughout the follow-up period (eMethods 2). Functional status was defined by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) abstracted from clinical record review at the time of treatment initiation and at one-year and two-year follow-up intervals.

Patients were stratified into 2 treatment groups. The EIT group consisted of patients who received IV or oral cyclophosphamide within 3 months of treatment initiation for vasculitis and who continued cyclophosphamide treatment for at least 3 months. This time frame for cyclophosphamide initiation was selected to account for heterogeneity in access to care and potential logistical barriers in starting cyclophosphamide. The “escalation treatment” (ESC) group consisted of patients who were initially treated with glucocorticoids with or without azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or methotrexate.

Remission was defined as (1) clinical stability or improvement (no new or worsening neurologic symptoms potentially attributable to PCNSV) and (2) no evidence of new or worsening diffusion abnormalities, hemorrhage, gadolinium enhancement, or other MRI lesions. Because patients with adequate treatment response may continue to demonstrate small, persistent areas of enhancement (including vessel-wall enhancement) for months or years, complete resolution of enhancement was not required to define remission; however, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed to determine whether adjusting this definition of remission affected the results of the analysis.14 Relapse was defined as imaging worsening consistent with vasculitis (new T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), enhancing, or hemorrhagic lesion) with clinical correlation, after a patient was previously deemed to be in remission. All patients received at least one clinical evaluation from a neurologist or rheumatologist within 6 months of treatment initiation (eTable 1 provides detailed information regarding follow-up intervals for each treatment group).

Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact test, and continuous variables were compared using a 2-sided t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as applicable. Time to remission based on the initial treatment strategy was investigated using Kaplan-Meier curves, log-rank testing, and univariable Cox proportional hazard modeling; multivariable Cox proportional hazard analyses were deliberately avoided, given the limited sample size. No patients were censored before the three-year follow-up interval for this remission analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using Python (version 3.12.4), and all p values were 2-sided with significance defined as p < 0.05.

The study protocol was approved by the UCSF Institutional Review Board (#10-00526).

Anonymized data not published within this article will be made available by request from any qualified investigator.

Twenty-six patients (50% female) with biopsy-proven sv-PCNSV met the inclusion criteria for this study. The median age at symptom onset was 55.5 years (range 20–82 years). Seven patients (26.9%) were diagnosed with ABRA, and these individuals were, on average, older at the time of diagnosis compared with those who had non-amyloid pathology (73.9 vs 50.3 years, p < 0.001). Patients were first evaluated by a clinician at a median of 1 month after symptom onset (interquartile range, IQR 0–2.4 months), with a definitive biopsy-supported diagnosis obtained 4.6 months after symptom onset (mean 10 months; IQR 2–14 months). The most common symptoms at the time of the first clinical presentation were headache (76.9%), altered mental status (61.5%), seizures (38.5%), and focal weakness (34.6%, eTable 2). Common concurrent medical conditions included hypertension (42.3%), hyperlipidemia (23.1%), and diabetes (23.1%, eTable 3).

Most patients demonstrated abnormal leptomeningeal or parenchymal enhancement on postcontrast T1 brain MRI (88.5%, Figure 1), although compared with patients with ABRA (n = 7), patients with non-amyloid disease (n = 19) were significantly more likely to have parenchymal enhancement (78.9% vs 28.6%, p = 0.03, Table 1). T2/FLAIR abnormalities on brain MRI most frequently involved both cerebral hemispheres (77.0%) while isolated unihemispheric disease was observed in 5 patients (19.2%) including one patient with ABRA (Table 1). Among patients with non-amyloid sv-PCNSV (n = 19), cerebellar or brainstem involvement occured in 11 patients (57.9%) and included enhancing parenchymal lesions (n = 4, 21.1%), punctate DWI abnormalities (n = 4, 21.1%), and leptomeningeal enhancement (n = 3, 15.8%). In addition, 1 patient demonstrated cerebrellar hemorrhagic lesions without supratentorial involvement. Three patients with non-amyloid sv-PCSNV had coexisting spinal cord involvement (15.8%): 2 with enhancing spinal cord parenchymal lesions and one with isolated spinal cord leptomeningeal enhancement without parenchymal disease.

All patients underwent intracranial vessel imaging (n = 26), including CT angiography (61.5%), MR angiography (65.4%), and/or digital subtraction angiography (11.5%), and intracranial vascular irregularities were only observed in 3 patients (11.5%, none with ABRA). Nearly half of the patients demonstrated foci of abnormal diffusion (consistent with acute or subacute ischemia); however, these were often punctate and were uncommon during the initial presentation. Indeed, clinical ischemic stroke was a presenting symptom in only 11.5% of these patients with sv-PCNSV.

At initial presentation, serum leukocytosis was present in 6 of 26 patients (23.1%) and an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate was present in 6 of 22 patients (27.2%). Antinuclear antibodies were detected at low titer (1:40, speckled pattern) in one patient and at high titer (1:320, speckled pattern) in another, but neither patient met clinical criteria for systemic lupus erythematosus nor another systemic autoimmune disease. No patients demonstrated serum eosinophilia or antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA).

CSF testing results were available for 23 patients, and 15 (65.2%) exhibited CSF pleocytosis (median CSF WBC = 13, total range 0–1,140, Table 1). CSF oligoclonal bands (OCBs) were present in 7 patients (30.4%) at an average of 9 months after symptom onset and were accompanied by CSF pleocytosis in all instances. Of the 4 patients with CSF OCBs who underwent repeat examination, CSF OCBs and elevated IgG index resolved in 3 patients who achieved remission but persisted in one patient with ongoing active disease. Notably, 4 patients (17.4%) had a normal CSF profile on pretreatment testing, including a normal IgG index and no OCBs.

Six patients (23.1%) underwent repeat brain biopsy because of initial nondiagnostic findings. Patients who received glucocorticoids within 2 weeks of biopsy had a numerically higher rate of nondiagnostic biopsies (42.9% vs 15.8%, p = 0.29). Among both non-ABRA patients (n = 19) and those with ABRA (n = 7), the most common neuropathologic subtype of CNS vasculitis was lymphocytic (68.4% and 57.1%, respectively, Figure 2), followed by granulomatous (21.1% and 42.9%, respectively). Two patients with non-ABRA sv-PCNSV (10.5%) were found to have the acute necrotizing subtype on neuropathology.

The median follow-up was 49 months after treatment initiation (range 14–93 months) and 58 months after initial presentation (range 15–152 months). Among the 12 patients included in the EIT group, cyclophosphamide treatment regimens included dosing based on the CYCLOPS clinical trial in ANCA vasculitis (n = 6, starting dose typically 15 mg/kg IV pulses every 2 weeks for 6 weeks and then 15 mg/kg IV pulses every 3 weeks, with a maximum dose per pulse of 1.2 g and dose reductions as indicated per protocol based on age and kidney function),17 monthly IV infusions (n = 4, starting dose typically 750 mg/m2 body surface area), or daily oral dosing (n = 2). The ESC group consisted of 14 patients who were initially treated with prednisone, with or without azathioprine, methotrexate, or mycophenolate mofetil (detailed treatment information available in eAppendix 1).

Baseline characteristics were similar between the 2 treatment groups, including age, sex, neuropathologic vasculitis subtype on biopsy, and time to treatment initiation (Table 2). All patients in the EIT group achieved remission, compared with 11 patients in the ESC group (100% vs 78.6%, p = 0.22). The median time to remission was 14 months shorter in the EIT group compared with the ESC group (Figure 4; median time to remission 5 months vs 19 months after treatment initiation; hazard ratio = 0.24, 95% CI [0.10–0.63], p < 0.005). At treatment initiation, 33.3% of the EIT group and 57.1% of the ESC group had good functional status (mRS score <3, p = 0.27, Figure 5). After 2 years, a decrease in the mRS score (indicating functional improvement) was observed in 50% of the EIT group compared with 35.7% of the ESC group (p = 0.69), resulting in 66.6% of EIT patients and 42.9% of ESC patients demonstrating good functional status (p = 0.27).

These assessments occurred at an average of 12.0 months (range 9.4–14.0 months) and 23.7 months (range 19.5–27.7 months) after treatment initiation for the one-year and two-year follow-up periods, respectively.

The mean cumulative glucocorticoid exposure at the time of remission (or last follow-up, if remission was not achieved) was 13.4 g in the EIT group and 17.5 g in the ESC group (p = 0.36, Table 2). Two patients in the ESC group developed new-onset diabetes mellitus during the treatment period. Treatment-related adverse events requiring hospitalization included infection (ESC: 5 patients, EIT: 3 patients), gastrointestinal hemorrhage (EIT: 1 patient), and steroid-associated osteonecrosis of the femur (ESC: 1 patient).

Among the ESC group, reasons for not receiving cyclophosphamide at the time of initial treatment included diagnostic uncertainty resulting in empiric immunosuppression before definitive neuropathologic diagnosis (n = 7), poor functional status (n = 2), shared decision making/patient preference (n = 2), and pregnancy (n = 1). Two patients in the ESC group received only a single dose of IV pulse cyclophosphamide before discontinuation, one because of lack of access to a local infusion center and another because of liver toxicity. Five patients in the ESC group eventually received cyclophosphamide at a median of 9 months after initial immunosuppression treatment initiation.

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that several important assumptions did not seem to significantly affect the results of the time-to-remission treatment analysis, including (1) analyzing time to remission from diagnosis, rather than from treatment initiation, (2) recategorizing 2 instances of brief empiric glucocorticoid exposures (<5 days, without ongoing immunosuppression) as the starting point of treatment initiation, and (3) redefining remission to require complete resolution of all abnormal enhancement (eTable 4).

The 3 patients who did not achieve remission in the ESC group included 2 patients with spinal parenchymal lesions who continued to have active disease despite multiple treatment approaches and one patient who transitioned to hospice shortly after diagnosis (eAppendix 1).

Of those achieving remission (n = 22), 19 received maintenance immunosuppression with azathioprine (n = 9), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF, n = 7), prednisone (n = 2), or methotrexate (n = 1). Four patients who achieved remission experienced relapse (18.2%), including 2 patients with relapsing unihemispheric disease (eAppendix 1). Chronic neurologic symptoms were common, including cognitive dysfunction (92.3%), chronic motor deficits (73.1%), inability to ambulate without assistance (38.5%), migraines (23.1%), and epilepsy (11.5%).

In addition to illustrating the broad clinicopathologic spectrum among patients with sv-PCNSV, this study found that EIT with cyclophosphamide was associated with a shorter time to remission.

The rationale for EIT in PCNSV is influenced by the treatment approach for certain systemic vasculitis syndromes, which consists of an induction phase aimed at achieving remission followed by maintenance therapy to prevent relapse.18,19 While patients who received EIT in this study achieved remission significantly sooner, the positive trend toward a better functional outcome with EIT was not statistically significant. Previous PCNSV treatment studies include a Mayo Clinic cohort, which found that treatment with cyclophosphamide did not seem to affect initial treatment response or disability at the last follow-up (n = 191, 37.2% diagnosed by biopsy).7 However, patients who received cyclophosphamide in the Mayo Clinic cohort had a higher degree of baseline disability and a higher likelihood of angiographically diagnosed PCNSV (e.g., lv-PCNSV) compared with those who received other treatments, limiting generalizability to sv-PCNSV.7 In another large PCNSV cohort, the multicenter French PCNSV Registry (n = 112, 29% diagnosed by biopsy), induction with cyclophosphamide was common (79%) and most patients achieved remission (95%), potentially suggesting a benefit from cyclophosphamide-based induction.9 Although further investigation is needed to confirm the benefit of EIT on functional outcomes, the convincing disparity in time to remission observed in this study suggests that ESC approaches, such as induction with just glucocorticoids as a monotherapy, should not be considered first-line therapy for sv-PCSNV.

Diagnostic delays were common in our cohort and reflective of real-world challenges. These included the imperfect yield of CNS biopsy, the potential impact of empiric immunosuppression on diagnostic yield, the need to carefully exclude alternate diagnoses, and the wide catchment areas of our tertiary care institution (often resulting in multiple local evaluations before referral). Acknowledging these challenges, these findings underscore the critical need for timely and accurate diagnosis by CNS biopsy when sv-PCNSV is suspected because EIT can affect the disease course. As in this study, various factors may limit or delay cyclophosphamide use after the diagnosis is confirmed, including lack of access to infusion centers and concerns about adverse effects (particularly in older patients such as those with ABRA). Some of these concerns may be less pronounced with newer, more targeted immunosuppressive agents. For example, randomized trials support anti-IL6 receptor therapy in giant cell arteritis and anti-CD20 therapy in ANCA-associated vasculitis, although more data are needed to determine whether they are effective as induction agents in sv-PCNSV.20-22 Furthermore, the cumulative glucocorticoid exposure observed in this study was substantial, and future prospective studies should also assess strategies to mitigate the impact of glucocorticoid toxicity.23 For example, randomized trial data in ANCA-associated vasculitis support the use of adjunctive avacopan, a C5a receptor inhibitor, to limit the use of cumulative glucocorticoid exposure during induction.24,25 While the use of avacopan in PCNSV is not yet reported, complement inhibition represents an intriguing therapeutic candidate for future study, particularly in light of CSF proteomics data suggesting a role for complement overactivation in some patients with sv-PCNSV.26

We used a clinical and radiographic definition of remission in this study that can be easily applied to future studies and in clinical practice. Among the limited subset of patients who underwent repeat CSF testing, remission was also accompanied by the resolution of CSF abnormalities, suggesting that repeat CSF analysis can help assess treatment response among patients with abnormal baseline CSF testing. This is in contrast to other neuroinflammatory disorders such as multiple sclerosis in which, at least with current standard disease-modifying treatments, oligoclonal bands tend to persist chronically even with disease-modifying immunosuppression.27,28 After remission, relapses did occur in a minority of patients, consistent with previously published PCNSV cohorts. Maintenance therapy after induction of remission is likely warranted, as supported by the French PCNSV Registry, which found that maintenance therapy was associated with prolonged remission and good functional status.9,29 In clinical practice, prolonged prednisone tapers and traditional steroid-sparing agents are most commonly used.7,9,11

Clinically, patients with sv-PCNSV in our series generally presented with headache, altered mental status, seizure and/or focal neurologic deficits, bihemispheric T2/FLAIR lesions with abnormal enhancement on neuroimaging, and mild CSF pleocytosis. However, none of these signs were particularly specific or sensitive for sv-PCNSV because some patients in this study lacked abnormal enhancement or CSF abnormalities. There were also distinct phenotypic subtypes such as relapsing unihemispheric disease,30,31 isolated cerebellar disease, and parenchymal spinal cord disease,8,32 mirroring other cohorts and further highlighting the diversity of the sv-PCNSV clinical spectrum. Although our understanding of the pathogenesis of PCNSV is limited, this clinicopathologic heterogeneity further suggests that PCNSV likely encompasses various forms of immune dysregulation converging on CNS-specific vascular injury. Diversity in the underlying pathogenesis and antigenic targets may explain why certain phenotypes—such as parenchymal spinal cord disease or relapsing unihemispheric disease—may behave in a more aggressive manner and thus require unique treatment considerations.30-32

While grouping diverse clinical phenotypes and neuropathologic subtypes under the diagnostic umbrella of PCNSV has facilitated large and critically important cohort studies, recognizing distinct phenotypes as separate diseases with unique underlying pathophysiologies may be more appropriate as our understanding evolves.4,7-10 For instance, the infrequent occurrence of stroke-like presentations and intracranial vascular irregularities in this cohort adds to the growing body of literature that “angiographically proven” lv-PCNSV and “biopsy-proven” sv-PCNSV are perhaps distinct disease entities or, at the very least, different ends of a disease spectrum with limited clinical overlap.5,10,13,33 By contrast, within the most well-defined subtype of sv-PCNSV, ABRA, in which the amyloid-beta deposits are presumably central to the pathologic immune response, there is still a profound degree of clinical and neuropathologic heterogeneity. Yet, despite this heterogeneity, further subtyping this entity may be impractical and of limited clinical utility. Assessing these tradeoffs of “lumping vs splitting” PCNSV definitions remains an important consideration for future research.

Limitations of this study include the nonstandard time frames for repeat imaging and clinical assessments, which may affect the determination of time to remission between patients. However, the frequency of clinical assessments was similar between both treatment groups (eTable 1), and the magnitude of the effect size implies that any minor differences in follow-up patterns were unlikely to affect the difference in time to remission significantly. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the results are also limited by confounding factors that could concurrently affect the choice of immunosuppressive treatment and the response to treatment. In addition, the presenting symptoms, chronic sequelae, and functional outcomes were extracted from the medical record rather than collected in a prospective standardized manner and thus are subject to biases inherent to retrospective research. Similarly, information regarding adverse events was likely incomplete, particularly given that many patients at our tertiary care center also receive care at local hospitals and urgent care centers. Acknowledging these limitations, the rigorous inclusion criteria prioritized biopsy-supported sv-PCNSV cases, minimizing confounding from nonvasculitic mimics or other neuroinflammatory conditions. The results of this study support the role of EIT with cyclophosphamide and warrant further validation through larger cohorts and randomized control trials for this rare but serious neurologic condition.
