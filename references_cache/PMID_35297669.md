ADP-ribosylation is a highly dynamic posttranslational modification frequently studied in stress response pathways with recent attention given to its role in response to viral infection. Notably, the alphaviruses encode catalytically active macrodomains capable of ADP-ribosylhydrolase (ARH) activities, implying a role in remodeling the cellular ADP-ribosylome. This report decouples mono- and poly-ARH contributions to macrodomain function using a newly engineered Sindbis virus (SINV) mutant with attenuated poly-ARH activity. Our findings indicate that viral poly-ARH activity is uniquely required for high titer replication in mammalian systems. Despite translating incoming genomic RNA as efficiently as WT virus, mutant viruses have a reduced capacity to establish productive infection, offering a more complete understanding of the kinetics and role of the alphavirus macrodomain with important implications for broader ADP-ribosyltransferase biology.

IMPORTANCEViral macrodomains have drawn attention in recent years due to their high degree of conservation in several virus families (e.g., coronaviruses and alphaviruses) and their potential druggability. These domains erase mono- or poly-ADP-ribose, posttranslational modifications written by host poly-ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) proteins, from undetermined host or viral proteins to enhance replication. Prior work determined that efficient alphavirus replication requires catalytically active macrodomains; however, which form of the modification requires removal and from which protein(s) had not been determined. Here, we present evidence for the specific requirement of poly-ARH activity to ensure efficient productive infection and virus replication.

KEYWORDS:Sindbis virus, alphavirus, ADP-ribosylation, PARP/ARTD, virus replication, RNA synthesis

Viral infection induces significant remodeling of the cellular landscape, with viral proteins promoting a favorable replication environment and the host responding in kind with multipronged defenses. Posttranslational modifications (PTMs) constitute a critical layer of defense, repurposing extant machinery to quickly respond to environmental cues and initiate or communicate signaling cascades (1). Importantly, PTMs are modular, fast-acting, and reversible processes, features that make them particularly useful for responding to dynamic stressors like viruses. This report focuses on the role of ADP-ribosylation, a modular NAD (NAD+) derivative, and its associated ADP-ribosyltransferase Diphtheria-toxin like (ARTD) protein family, (also referred to as the poly-ADP-ribose polymerase, or PARP, family) in the context of Sindbis virus (SINV) infection.

SINV, our model virus, belongs to theAlphavirusgenus of theTogaviridaefamily of (+)-sense RNA viruses and is known to cause severe inflammation and lacks clinically approved direct acting antivirals (2–7). Previous work determined that alphavirus, rubivirus, coronavirus (CoV), and hepatitis E virus nonstructural proteins encode catalytically active and functionally important macrodomains that reproducibly remove ADP-ribose (ADPr) from protein substrates to promote virulence (8–19). The conservation of these viral domains implies that host ADP-ribosyltransferase (ART) signaling contributes to antiviral programs, in yet undescribed ways, which the viruses must counteract. Genomic analysis of the ARTD family has indicated several iterations of gene duplication and divergent evolution, with some members undergoing positive selection (20).

Although viral macrodomains descend from ancestral macroD-type macrodomains, a family limited to mono-ADP-ribosylhydrolase (ARH) activity, they have also been shown to be capable of poly-ARH activityin vitro(19). Recent work has suggested that alphavirus macrodomains are functionally limited to mono-ARH activity, with little attention paid to its role as a poly-ARH (13–15,21,22). Moreover, previously published SINV and chikungunya virus (CHIKV) mutants (N10A/D10A, N24A, G32S/A/E, & Y114A) used to dissect macrodomain function disrupt the mono-ADP-ribose (MAR) binding pockets (13–15,23). Importantly, MAR binding is critical to poly-ARH activity because of the mechanism of action employed for ADPr removal (18,19). Like Terminal ADP-ribose Protein Glycohydrolase 1 (TARG1), viral macrodomains were found to remove poly-ADP-ribose (PAR) chains from the base acidic linkage, rather than ratcheting along the PAR chain as an exo-poly-ARH, which would remove the terminal ADPr moieties in succession similar to Poly-ADP-ribose Glycohydrolase (PARG)-like macrodomains (15,19,24–26). As such, mutations that affect the cleavage of the protein-proximal ADP-ribose moiety would concomitantly affect the catabolism of longer species dependent on the same cleavage mechanism. As it stands, any conclusions that alphavirus mutant attenuation is specifically due to mono-ARH inactivation assumes that poly-ARH activity is negligible. However, this has not been demonstrated. There remains much to be learned about ADPr-mediated antiviral mechanisms employed by mammalian hosts and corresponding viral countermeasures, and this area requires further study. In this work we design a SINV macrodomain mutant that is deficient in poly-ARH activity yet retains mono-ARH activity. Our studies reveal the important contribution of poly-ARH activity to macrodomain function and show that while initial translation events do not require a functional macrodomain, that poly-ARH activity is important for the initiation of productive SINV replication and the synthesis of viral RNA.

We set out to distinguish the specific contributions of mono-ARH and poly-ARH activities to attenuation observed in SINV mutants with abrogated ARH activity by designing a new mutant with attenuated poly-ARH activity (APH) that perturbs a PAR binding motif unique to poly-ARH activity and is predicted to have intact mono-ARH activity. We designed the APH mutant using publicly available crystal structures of the CHIKV macrodomain in complex with various ligands (apo, MAR, RNA, 2’,5′-oligoadenylate; PDB: 3GPG, 3GPO, 3GPQ, 4TU0, respectively) to predict residues unique to PAR binding (18). Despite the high degree of homology between viral and cellular macroD-type macrodomains, we recognized a positively charged and hydrophobic PAR binding motif unique to poly-ARH-competent macrodomains (Fig. 1). We hypothesized that this short linear motif (SLiM), SINV nsP3 residues 37–41 (I-Y-K-R-W), could be integral to PAR binding as this alpha helix is more chemically similar to the equivalent sequence of the human TARG1 protein (F-K-K-K-F), which has both mono-ARH and poly-ARH activities, rather than human macroD2 (I-H-R-A-A), which is limited to mono-ARH activity.

Notably, TARG1 proteins are functionally similar to viral macrodomains in that they remove full-length, intact PAR chains as well as MAR from acidic residues (19,26,27). To generate APH, we made three amino acid substitutions (R35A, K39A, R40A) in what we thought could be a PAR-binding SLiM and cloned these mutations into the Toto1101 SINV infectious clone and a macrodomain bacterial expression vector expressing N-terminal hexaHis/SUMO and C-terminal 3XFlag epitopes for biochemical characterization. We included R35A in our design of APH despite most alphaviruses maintaining a G in that position, reasoning that if a positively charged SLiM helps with binding PAR, this position is proximal enough that it may also contribute to PAR binding.

We biochemically assayed WT and mutant SINV macrodomains, including the APH and catalytically dead N10A mutant, for MAR binding and mono-ARH/poly-ARH catalytic activities. We did not assay PAR binding because uniform PAR chains are difficult to acquire for quantitative biochemistry. Ultimately, we were interested in reducing PAR removal without affecting mono-ARH activity because this would allow us to narrow down the contributing PARPs and possible mechanisms necessary for antiviral activity. That is, MAR and PAR effect different mechanisms of action, with MAR disrupting binding interfaces and PAR mediating protein-protein contacts (28–34). We used poly-ARH activity to infer the effects of our engineered mutations on PAR binding. MAR binding was assessed with a thermal shift assay (TSA) and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). We observed that mutant apoprotein melting temperatures (Tms) are slightly lower than for the WT domain, and only WT and APH macrodomainTms shift with ligand. This suggests that while N10A fails to bind MAR, WT and APH remain MAR-binding competent (Fig. 2A). Using ITC to quantify the strength of interaction, we determined the binding dissociation constants (Kd) for both WT and APH macrodomains to be approximately 1.5 μM (Fig. 2B).

Next, we compared mono-ARH and poly-ARH activities for WT, N10A, and APH mutants with established protocols (19). For mono-ARH and poly-ARH assays,32P-labeled NAD+(NAD+[32P]) was incubated with either PARP12 or PARP1 catalytic domains, respectively. The auto-modified proteins were desalted and incubated with equimolar concentrations of individual recombinant macrodomains (500 nM), including various host macrodomains as controls. After size separation using denaturing SDS-PAGE, we detected automodified/radiolabeled PARP1 and PARP12 catalytic domains with conventional densitometry. As the modification is removed, the signal is reduced and the protein returns to an unmodified state except for PARG, which leaves a single detectable MAR residue on PARylated species. We found that although WT and APH macrodomains were equivalently capable of removing MAR from PARP12 (∼50% of total signal), the APH mutant had attenuated poly-ARH activity, comparable to the N10A mutant (∼15–25% of WT) (Fig. 2CandD).

Curiously, we observed poly-ARH activity for the severe acute respiratory syndrome CoV (SARS-CoV-1) macrodomain (Mac1, amino acids 1001–1180 of nsp3), which lacks this positively charged SLiM and has been suggested to be limited to mono-ARH activity (Fig. 2CandD) (11,16). However, our data agree with other work that suggests the SARS-CoV-1 macrodomain is poly-ARH competent (17,19). We speculate that amino acid residues in a different locus may compensate for the lack of a positively charged SLiM, or that additional accessory protein contacts are important, like is seen with the hepatitis E virus macrodomain, which gains poly-ARH activity when expressed as a fusion protein with its proximal RNA helicase domain (19).

As inflammation, e.g., arthritis, is a hallmark of alphavirus infection, we used innate immune intact cell lines to more closely mimic natural infection and assess the impact of attenuated macrodomain function on virus replication and innate immune stimulation. For our studies we used: (1) THP-1, a monocyte cell line, differentiated toward a macrophage-like fate (M0) using phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate; (2) a patient-derived fibroblast cell line with defective STAT1 (35,36) (STAT1−/−HF); and (3) a version of STAT1−/−HF reconstituted with V5-tagged STAT1 (V5HF).

Infection of M0 cells with N10A and APH mutant viruses resulted in attenuated replication, and treatment with a nonspecific ARTD inhibitor, 3-amino-benzamide (3AB), rescued mutant replication to wild-type replication levels (Fig. 3A). Moreover, infection with either mutant resulted in reduced innate immune stimulation profiles, as determined by IFN (IFN-β), ISG (ISG15) and inflammatory cytokine (MCP-1, IL-6) production (Fig. 3B).

Similar to M0 infection, we saw that V5HF cells were more readily infected by WT virus than with either mutant, as determined by infectious titer and percentage of cells positive for double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), a by-product of virus replication (Fig. 3C). To determine if the effects of IFN signaling were critical to mutant attenuation, we compared N10A and APH replication to WT SINV in STAT1−/−HF cells, which do not respond effectively to IFN. Notably, macrodomain mutant attenuation persisted in the STAT1−/−background, indicating that the phenotype is STAT1-independent, and thus not likely dependent on IFN-based antiviral effects (Fig. 3D). We then evaluated the capacity of WT and mutant viruses to establish infection using an infectious center assay and determined that mutant infection produces comparably fewer infectious centers than WT virus (approximately 10-fold fewer), suggesting a reduced capacity to initiate a productive infection (Fig. 3E).

To pinpoint the step in the virus replication cycle impaired by macrodomain attenuation, we first assessed incoming viral genome translation. To do this we utilized temperature sensitive Toto1101 (containing an nsP4 double mutant G154E/G325E, previously called ts6/ts110), encoding nano luciferase (NLuc) in nsP3 (tsNLuc) (Fig. 4A, top) (37–41). This temperature sensitive system permits a decoupling of the initial viral translation events from replication as these viruses only replicate efficiently at lower permissive temperatures (28°C), but not at higher restrictive temperatures (40°C). We infected V5HF cells with temperature sensitive virus and measured luciferase signal across a 5 h time course, failing to observe a difference in nonstructural protein production at the restrictive temperature (Fig. 4B). We validated temperature restriction by quantifying viral genome copies and infectious titers using qRT-PCR and plaque assay, respectively, and confirmed minimal virus replication at the nonpermissive temperature (data not shown). Similarly, transfection of the viral mRNA and incubation at the restrictive temperature corroborated the infection findings since initial translation events were not dependent on poly-ARH activity as measured 24 h posttransfection (Fig. 4C). These data suggest that early infection events of entry and genome translation are unaffected by macrodomain attenuation.

To determine whether the specific infectivity of virions or intracellular replication kinetics were affected by poly-ARH deficiency, we transfected mRNA encoding a fluorescent reporter replicon system (SINrep5/mNeonGreen, SR5mNG) to decouple viral entry and spread from productive replication within a cell (42) (Fig. 4A, bottom). We found that the mutant replicons (SR5mNG/N10A and SR5mNG/APH) were attenuated as early as 8 h posttransfection. Importantly, the mutant replicons established productive infection less frequently and failed to achieve comparable levels of infection as the WT replicon as determined by the median fluorescence intensity of mNG in the cells (Fig. 4D). We interpret these data to signify that the macrodomain is critical for optimal specific infectivity of the viral genomic RNA, and that mutation reduces the capacity of the genomic RNA to establish a productive replication complex and generate the subgenomic RNA required to produce the fluorescent reporter protein in this system. Cumulatively, these data indicate that SINV macrodomain attenuation results in intracellular defects in viral replication rather than viral particle defects, though we have not ruled out additional effects on viral particle egress.

Given that we fail to see a defect in the initial genomic translation and see reduced translation of the subgenomic transcript, these data suggest an impairment in the initiation of genome replication and viral RNA production. To this end, we decided to assess the effects of macrodomain attenuation on the production of different viral RNA species. After initial viral translation, the viral products form a replication complex and begin viral RNA synthesis, producing minus-strand RNA that is used as a template for plus-strand RNA synthesis and resulting in duplex/dsRNA formation. Temporal control of minus-strand synthesis results in excess genomic plus-strand and subgenomic RNA (sgRNA) species. To determine whether poly-ARH activity is required for a specific synthesis step, we infected V5HF cells and multiplexed dsRNA immunostaining (using antibody J2) and single moleculefluorescentinsituhybridization (smFISH), detecting plus- and minus-strand RNA, over an 8 h time course with probes targeting the nsP1 and nsP2 viral polyprotein-encoding regions of the genome (Fig. 5A). By quantifying the level of genomic RNA and dsRNA in cells within the population over time, we paint a refined portrait of the replication dynamics, assessing how poly-ARH disruption affects the production of different viral RNA species.

We found that poly-ARH attenuation results in fewer dsRNA+ cells from the outset of infection, with APH mutant infection initiating replication slightly later than WT virus and achieving overall lower levels of dsRNA and genomic plus-strand RNA (Fig. 5BandC). In agreement with recently published data, with either WT or APH SINV infection we were unable to detect free minus-strand, which is only accessible to smFISH probes upon stringent denaturation of dsRNA (43). SINV macrodomain poly-ARH activity is thus critical for efficient genomic RNA replication initiation.

Taken altogether, the results of this study indicate that host-mediated ADP-ribosylation results in a cellular environment that inhibits the establishment of SINV replication within the cell at a step after translation of the incoming genomic RNA. Using a SINV macrodomain mutant that retains mono-ARH activity, but is deficient in poly-ARH activity, coupled with single cell imaging techniques, we establish that without macrodomain poly-ARH function, fewer cells are productively infected, and levels of RNA replication are delayed. The result is lower levels of dsRNA and plus-strand RNA within an infected cell. While alphavirus macrodomain mono-ARH activity had generally been assumed to be important, our studies reveal an important requirement for macrodomain poly-ARH activity in the establishment of a highly permissive cellular environment.

Many studies have characterized viral macro ARH domains, with a focus on mono-ARH activities (11,14–16,19,21). This was mostly because it was assumed that because viral macrodomains descend from macroD-type macrodomains, their primary role should be de-MARylation, despite biochemical evidence that they could catabolize PAR as well (19,21). For CoVs, mono-ADP-ribosylating PARPs, PARP12 and PARP14, were even identified as integral host factors that the viral macrodomains antagonized (16). This had not, however, been shown to be the case for the alphaviruses.

We show with loss-of-function experiments that the SINV viral macrodomain poly-ARH activities were explicitly critical to macrodomain function. Though there was prior interest in determining which proteins were ADP-ribosylated during infection and which proteins might have this modification removed during alphavirus infection, the ADP-ribosylome of infected cells has yet to be surveyed. However, it has been speculated that G3BP1, a host factor critical to SINV and CHIKV replication that is known to be PARylated under various stress conditions, could be one such factor (44). Future studies to determine the ADP-ribosylome of infected cells would shed light on this area of interest.

In this study, we distinguish mono-ARH from poly-ARH catalytic contributions to SINV macrodomain function and link previously observed replication defects and attenuation to a loss of poly-ARH activity rather than the loss of mono-ARH activity. We determined that mutant virus replication attenuation manifests upon replication initiation, reducing both the number of cells that produce dsRNA and free plus-strand RNA as well as the total amount of RNA produced. Moreover, we show that there is no decrease in viral protein production prior to dsRNA amplification, though we did not assess polyprotein processing, which could affect viral replication complex formation. By affecting minus-strand synthesis, as indicated by lower levels of dsRNA in cells, the number of replication centers and the amount of template available for plus-strand synthesis are significantly reduced. Similarly, these defects affect plus-strand RNA synthesis in kind, resulting in fewer full-length viral genomes available for packaging and effectively reducing virion production.

Although we present the APH SINV triple mutant with specifically reduced poly-ARH activity, in data not shown we found that the double mutant K39A, K40A was able to attenuate Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV) replication to similar levels as the D10A mutant (equivalent to SINV N10A). The attenuation of this VEEV APH mutant emphasizes the importance of this SLiM (I-F/Y/W-R/K-R/K-F/Y/W), located at nsP3 residues 37–41, for alphavirus macrodomain PAR binding. That this SLiM is so critical to alphavirus poly-ARH activity and absent in poly-ARH deficient macroD-type macrodomains argues that poly-ARH activity recently emerged as a result of convergent evolution. It is possible that evolutionary molecular mimicry of a motif seen in the distantly relatedTARG1gene conferred poly-ARH activity to alphavirus macrodomains while other viral macrodomains may have done so through alternative mechanisms, such as hepatitis E virus, which gained poly-ARH activity through fusion of a proximal RNA helicase domain (19).

A model proposed recently (44) suggests that G3BP1 PARylation is integral to stress granule formation and alphavirus macrodomains resolve this stress response by catabolizing integral PAR signals, thereby freeing up sequestered host translation factors. Our data support their notion that PAR removal is required by the alphaviruses, further distinguishing them from the CoV macrodomains. Whether or not G3BP1 is the only protein whose modification state can be affected by alphavirus macrodomain expression remains to be seen.

Ultimately, this work contributes to our understanding of alphavirus macrodomain function and draws attention to the poly-ARTs that may be involved in molecular conflict with viral macrodomains. While we refrain from claiming that macrodomain function is exclusive to poly-ARH activity, we maintain that poly-ARH activity is an important function of the alphavirus macrodomains needed for efficient viral RNA synthesis.

P0 virus was generated essentially as described before by electroporating 8 × 106BHK-21 cells with ∼5μg viral RNA produced with the mMessage mMachine SP6 kit (Invitrogen, cat no. AM1340), and cleaned up using the RNeasy minikit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturers' protocols (48). As titers of the P0 virus stocks were ∼107plaque forming units (PFU)/mL, we prepared a P1 stock for use in experiments with titers ∼109PFU/mL. P1 passaging was done in BHK-21 cells, collecting the viral sup at 24 hpi after infecting (multiplicity of infection, MOI 0.1) with the P0 stock. Viral RNA (vRNA) sequencing was done by collecting 300 μL of both the P0 and P1 viral stocks and using the Zymo Research quick-RNA viral kit to isolate and purify vRNA. We used the vRNA to synthesis cDNA using the SuperScript IV kit (Invitrogen, cat no. 18090200), and proceeded to PCR the macrodomain region using Phusion HF reagents and appropriate primers (Forward: 5′-AATTGCGTGATTTCGTCCG-3′; Reverse: 5′-TGCTTGAAGGGAAGAAAGGG-3′). We used 30 amplification cycles as previously described (49,50) and sent the amplified PCR products for sanger sequencing. Temperature sensitive virus was cloned to include previously reported temperature sensitive mutations (ts6, G154E, and ts110, G324E) with a total of 3 nucleotide mutations to reduce the chance of reversion during experiments. We prepared ts stocks by electroporation of BHK-21 cells as above followed by incubation at 28°C for 3 d post electroporation before harvesting the P0 stock. P1 stocks were produced by infecting BHK-21 cells (MOI 1) with the P0 stock; cells were allowed to produce virus for 4 days at 28°C before harvesting the P1 virus stock. Sequencing confirmed there was no reversion in the mutant virus stocks.

Virus stocks or samples were serially diluted 10-fold in PBS containing 1% FBS and 200 μL was used to infect 1 × 105BHK-21 cells seeded in 12-well formats the day before. After 1 h of rocking at 37°C the wells were overlaid with DMEM containing 5% FBS, 1xNEAA, 1% Pen/Strep, and 1% methyl cellulose. Infected cells were then fixed with an equal volume of 8% PFA (4% PFA final concentration) 2 d postinfection and stained with crystal violet for visual enumeration. Temperature sensitive virus plaque assays were prepared in much the same way with the following exceptions: (1) 2 × 105BHK-21 cells were seeded, (2) cells were incubated at 28°C, and (3) cells were fixed 4 d postinfection. Avicel (2.25%) was sometimes substituted for the methyl cellulose in the overlay.

The Toto1101 macrodomain was cloned using standard techniques from the viral constructs into a pET28a bacterial expression vector engineered to encode an N-terminal His6-SMT3 tag (N-terminal hexahistidine tag fused to the yeast ubiquitin-like protein SMT3, a homolog of human SUMO-1 that helps express properly folded proteins) (51). We also cloned the SARS-CoV-1 macrodomain into the bacterial expression vector (52,53). SINV constructs were expressed in the Rosetta (DE3) bacterial strain, whereas the SARS-CoV-1 macrodomain was expressed in the OverExpress C41 (DE3) pLysS bacterial strain (Lucigen). SINV macrodomains-encoding Rosetta (DE3) cells were grown in Luria broth (LB) to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of ∼0.600, the culture was then cooled on ice and induced with 500 μM IPTG (isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside), shaking in the incubator for 4 h at 25°C. Harvested cell pellets were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored until purification (usually within ∼3 d). These conditions failed to produce soluble SARS-CoV-1 macrodomain, so they were grown with an alternative protocol to yield reasonable amounts of protein. The OverExpress C41 (DE3) pLysS cells harboring the SARS-CoV-1 macrodomain were grown in terrific broth (TB) to an OD600∼0.500, cooled on ice and induced with 50 μM IPTG, shaking in the incubator for ∼20 h at 16°C, as previously described (54). After harvesting, typically, these pellets were processed immediately rather than flash frozen. Cell pellets were resuspended in 20 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 0.1% IGEPAL, 1 mM PMSF, pH 8.2, supplemented with benzonase and were sonicated using the Sonic Dismembrator 550 from ThermoFisher (sound setting 10 for 15s pulses, 4 times over 30 min). Lysates were then clarified by centrifugation at 19,000 ×gfor 60 min and the supernatants were Nickel-nitriloacetic acid (Ni-NTA, ThermoFisher HisPur Ni-NTA Resin, catalog no .88222) column-purified according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Recombinant proteins were washed on-column with 20 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 1 mM TCEP, pH 8.2 and eluted in 20 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, 1 mM TCEP, pH 8.2. After the first elution step, the His6-SMT3 tag was cleaved with Ulp1 by overnight dialysis into 20 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8.2. The next day this protein was reloaded onto the same Ni-NTA column to remove uncleaved protein and cleaved tag. Protein in the flow through fraction was concentrated and buffer exchanged into 50 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.4. Protein was stored at −80°C with 10% glycerol added to the formulation, or with 50% glycerol for −20°C storage.

Recombinant protein was dialyzed into 10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4. SYPRO orange fluorescence was used as an indicator of the folding state of the sample protein as the temperature was incrementally raised. The shape of the melting curve was used to determine the melting point of the protein with ligand (Tm) and the melting point of the apoprotein (To), where ligand binding shifts the melting temperature of the complex (ΔTm= Tm-To). SYPRO dye was diluted 25-fold in dialysis buffer and diluted 10-fold further when mixed with recombinant protein diluted to 20 μM. This SYPRO-recombinant macrodomain mix was then mixed 1:1 with either dialysis buffer or 4 mM mono-ADP-ribose (MAR) to make a final reaction volume of 20 μL. Samples were then melted and analyzed with the Bio-RAD CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System.

For ITC, viral macrodomain MAR binding was measured with a GE MicroCal auto-ITC200. Experiments were carried out at 25°C in 10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.0. The protein concentration in the cell was 20 μM and the MAR concentration in the syringe was 300 μM. Isothermal titration was performed with 19 injections of 2 μL 300 μM MAR, dissolved in the aforementioned buffer. Ligand was injected into 0.4 mL of macrodomain in the chamber every 150 s. Data for raw ITC and thermodynamic curves, each from one experiment, were downloaded after analysis using Affinimeter software and plotted using GraphPad Prism (8.0). The raw data were processed by subtracting the volumetric heat changes from the experimental enthalpy changes (ΔH). We used the processed data to calculate the binding affinities of WT, N10A, and APH SINV macrodomains, though the N10A mutant failed to produce a spectrum above noise. Titration curves were fitted assuming a simple binding model and the equilibrium constant (Kd) and stoichiometry were determined.

Radioactive MARylated-PARP12 catalytic domain was prepared by incubating 0.25 μM commercial PARP12 (abcam, cat no. ab193474) with mono-ADP-ribosylating buffer (MARB) consisting of 20 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4 supplemented with 1μCi [32P]NAD+(PerkinElmer) per sample for densitometry detection. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature (RT) for 30 min; total reaction volume was 160 μL.
