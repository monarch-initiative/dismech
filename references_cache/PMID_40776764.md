# Outcomes after carotid revascularization for symptomatic carotid artery web: A multi-institutional cohort study.
**Authors:** Bounajem MT, Liang A, Trang A, El Baba B, Bielinski TM, Sangwon K, Zhang Y, Wiggan D, Grin E, Gajjar A, Pasarikovski CR, Yang VX, Agid R, Levitt M, Anderson M, Meyer RM, Cherian J, Howard B, Hendrix P, Abecassis IJ, Srinivasan V, El Naamani K, Gooch MR, Nossek E, Grandhi R
**Journal:** Interv Neuroradiol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1177/15910199251365529](https://doi.org/10.1177/15910199251365529)

## Content

Patient demographics, comorbidities, clinical presentation, treatment methodology, and outcomes at follow up were collected.ResultsAmong the 71 included patients (mean age 52.4 years, 44 (62.4%) female), 23 (32.4%) underwent CEA, and 48 (67.6%) underwent CAS. Median mRS at follow up was 1, and carotid artery stenting and endarterectomy demonstrated similar rates of postoperative complication and recurrent stroke rates.ConclusionOur results suggest that endarterectomy and stenting are safe and efficacious for minimizing subsequent stroke risk in patients who have experienced stroke or TIA from carotid webbing, warranting further studies to determine the optimal timing and method of treatment.

The median preintervention modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score was 2. Perioperatively, one patient (1.41%) experienced stroke and none experienced myocardial infarction. During mean 14.8 (±29.2)-month follow up, one patient experienced asymptomatic recurrent stroke (1.41%). Median mRS at follow up was 1, and carotid artery stenting and endarterectomy demonstrated similar rates of postoperative complication and recurrent stroke rates.

Our results suggest that endarterectomy and stenting are safe and efficacious for minimizing subsequent stroke risk in patients who have experienced stroke or TIA from carotid webbing, warranting further studies to determine the optimal timing and method of treatment.

Keywords: Carotid web, carotid revascularization, carotid artery stenting, endarterectomy, stroke, outcome

A carotid artery web (CaW) is diagnosed radiographically as a thin intraluminal filling defect that most commonly protrudes off the posterior wall of the internal carotid artery (ICA) bulb; CaWs are informally described as abnormal shelf-like projections in the ICA.1,2 CaWs are believed to be a variant of intimal fibromuscular dysplasia, and although an embryological origin of CaW development has been proposed, they have not yet been identified in the pediatric population.3–5 The alteration in hemodynamics created by CaWs, including areas of flow stagnation and turbulence, results in greater thrombogenicity and a higher risk for intracranial embolism.6,7 Unlike classical carotid stenosis, the degree of vessel narrowing caused by the CaW is not necessarily severe in patients presenting with symptomatic strokes or transient ischemic attacks (TIAs),
8
 and frequently occurs in female patients with minimal risk factors for carotid disease.
9

Patients with CaW have historically been treated nonoperatively with antiplatelet agents, but recent reports have identified a significantly greater risk of recurrent stroke in patients with symptomatic CaW than was previously reported.1,10,11 Carotid revascularization via carotid artery stenting (CAS) or carotid endarterectomy (CEA) can be pursued,12–14 but there is significant skepticism regarding the role of surgical and interventional treatments for CaW.
15
 Although isolated series have examined each treatment modality, there are currently few medium- to large-scale multicenter series in the existing body of literature. We performed a multicenter analysis of patients with symptomatic CaW who underwent CAS or CEA to examine the safety and efficacy of treatment and understand long-term clinical outcomes.

Adult patients age 18 years or older with symptomatic CaW, defined as having had an acute ischemic stroke (AIS) or TIA, who subsequently underwent CAS or CEA between January 2022 and December 2023 across nine institutions were recorded in a central registry. Institutional review board approval was granted at each institution with a waiver of informed consent.

Patient characteristics and clinical history, including age, sex, comorbidities, tobacco and oral contraceptive use, modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, clinical presentation, and time from presentation to intervention, were recorded. Treatment and postoperative details consisting of antiplatelet and anticoagulation use, type of intervention, perioperative myocardial infarction (MI) or stroke, new neurological deficit, mRS on discharge, hospital length of stay (LOS), and discharge disposition were included. Of note, the choice between CEA and CAS was based on both treating physician preference and patient preference, and only symptomatic CaWs treated with one of these interventions were included. Follow-up duration, mRS, new stroke, new neurological deficit, and repeat imaging results were obtained.

Results were summarized with descriptive statistics. Safety was assessed by new stroke or deficits or other complications. The efficacy of treatment was determined by a lack of stenosis on follow-up imaging.

In total, 71 patients met the criteria for inclusion. The mean ± SD age of the included patients was 52.4 ± 11.4 years, and 44 (62.4%) patients were female (Table 1). Patient comorbidities included hyperlipidemia (42, 59.2%), hypertension (33, 46.5%), diabetes mellitus (15, 21.1%), coronary artery disease (9, 12.7%), and atrial fibrillation (5, 7%). Current or prior smoking was reported by 26 (36.6%) patients, and 8 (11.3%) reported current or prior oral contraceptive use. Prior diagnosis of fibromuscular dysplasia was reported in 2 (2.82%) patients and prior diagnosis of connective tissue disorder in 1 (1.41%).

Most patients (59, 83.1%) presented with a stroke on magnetic resonance imaging, and the remaining 16.9% presented with a TIA. Of those presenting with a stroke, the median National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score on presentation was 8.5 (interquartile range (IQR) 3–16.5). The median preoperative mRS was 2 (IQR 1–3). On average, patients had experienced 1.12 ± 0.7 strokes or TIAs before intervention, with a mean delay of 115 ± 224 days between the index event and intervention. Ten patients (14.1%) underwent transcranial Doppler ultrasound with emboli detection studies, one of whom was positive for emboli (10%).

CAS was undertaken in 48 patients (67.6%) and CEA in 23 (32.4%). One patient (1.41%) experienced a perioperative stroke, and none experienced a perioperative MI (Table 2). Notably, in the CAS group, 58.3% (28/48) of patients started on medical therapy prior to the stenting: 4 on aspirin (14.3%), 1 on apixaban (3.6%), 2 on warfarin (7.1%), 9 on aspirin and ticagrelor (32.1%), 10 on aspirin and clopidogrel (35.7%), and 2 on aspirin and apixaban (7.1%). A total of 5 patients on medical therapy had an additional infarct prior to stenting (5/28 patients, 17.8%). One patient (1.41%) experienced a nonflow-limiting cervical ICA dissection during CAS distal to the stent. New postoperative deficits after CEA were found in 2 patients (2.82%): one had marginal mandibular neuropraxia and the other had hypoglossal paresis, both of which resolved at follow up. The median mRS on discharge was 1 (IQR 0–2). The mean hospital LOS was 5.38 ± 6.54 days, with a mean follow-up duration of 14.8 ± 29.2 months (Table 2). Perioperative antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy was reported for 44 of the 48 patients after stenting: 25 on aspirin and clopidogrel (56.8%), 14 on aspirin and ticagrelor (31.8%), 3 on clopidogrel and apixaban (6.8%), 1 on ticagrelor and apixaban (2.3%), and 1 on aspirin and warfarin (2.3%). Four of the 48 patients were assumed to be on medical therapy, but the exact regimen could not be identified from their respective institution. On follow-up, 56.3% of patients underwent postintervention imaging in the form of digital subtraction angiography, computed tomography angiography, or magnetic resonance angiography. New symptomatic ipsilateral stroke presenting with speech difficulties was experienced by 1 patient (1.41%) during the follow-up period. The median mRS score at the latest follow up was 1 (IQR 0–2).

A 74-year-old previously healthy man presented with left facial droop, left hemibody weakness, and right gaze preference (NIHSS score 9); he was found to have a right middle cerebral artery M1 segment occlusion (Figure 1(a) and (b)). He received thrombolysis with alteplase (tPA), and subsequently underwent endovascular thrombectomy with thrombolysis in cerebral infarction score 3 reperfusion. He recovered well, and the cause of his stroke was determined to be an ipsilateral cervical ICA CaW after an otherwise-negative cryptogenic stroke workup (Figure 1(c)). He was medically treated with aspirin (81 mg) daily and atorvastatin (40 mg) daily. He was offered either CEA or CAS for definitive treatment of his CaW and elected to undergo CEA to avoid long-term dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT). He underwent CEA 305 days after his initial stroke, with postoperative ultrasound demonstrating no residual stenosis. He did experience numbness of his right jaw postoperatively because of retraction on the marginal mandibular nerve, but this resolved completely by his 6-month follow-up visit. He was otherwise asymptomatic and experienced no recurrent strokes.

A 60-year-old woman with a history of dysautonomia, migraines, and chronic hypoxemic respiratory failure initially presented with right arm weakness, numbness, and aphasia; she was found to have experienced a left frontal middle cerebral artery territory ischemic stroke. Her symptoms had resolved by the time she was initially evaluated (NIHSS 0), so she did not receive tPA. Her imaging revealed no large-vessel occlusion but was notable for a right ascending cervical ICA partially thrombosed pseudoaneurysm. On secondary review, bilateral CaW were also identified (Figure 2(a) and (b)) and further evaluated via a diagnostic cerebral angiogram (Figure 2(c) and (d)). She was treated with daily aspirin (325 mg) and clopidogrel (75 mg) for 21 days, followed by aspirin monotherapy. Her work-up was negative for any other cause for the stroke, and she elected to proceed with CAS for definitive treatment of her CaW. She was underwent left-sided CAS 227 days after her initial presentation. A 10–8 × 40mm Xact stent (Abbott, Chicago, IL) was placed across the CaW under distal embolic protection with a NAV6 device (Abbott) (Figure 2(e)). A small dissection was noted distal to the stent on postplacement angiogram, but it was nonflow-limiting so no intervention was pursued (Figure 2(f)). She tolerated the procedure well and was discharged on postoperative day 1. She was maintained on aspirin (325 mg) and clopidogrel (75 mg) daily for 6 months before being transitioned to aspirin monotherapy. At latest follow up, she has remained stroke free and has undergone carotid ultrasound demonstrating no stenosis or occlusion along the course of the stent.

The choice between operative or interventional treatment of CaW has been a matter of debate among clinicians. In a 2019 survey of neurointerventionalists, neurologists and neuroradiologists, 63% of respondents recommended nonoperative treatment for CaW in the setting of an AIS,
15
 with notably only 19% of neurointerventionalists recommending CAS and the rest recommending medical (aspirin or DAPT) or no treatment. In the setting of a CaW with recurrent AIS, a greater proportion of respondents recommended stenting or endarterectomy (65%), but surprisingly 21% of neurointerventionalists still recommended nonoperative treatment. This highlights the tendency for physicians, including interventionalists, to recommend medical therapies for symptomatic CaW, even in the context of multiple recurrent strokes. Despite this, the pathophysiology of CaW-associated strokes suggests the consideration for more aggressive intervention.

Potential etiologies of CaWs include congenital causes secondary to developmental anomalies of the brachiocephalic system and genetic predispositions with noted associations with African American race and female sex.
16
 Histologically, CaWs are described as lesions with focal intimal hyperplasia, which is distinctly different from fibromuscular dysplasia that involves medial hyperplasia.
2
 In extension, turbulent blood flow and subsequent stasis, which promote thrombogenesis, are thought to be the pathophysiology of embolic strokes in the setting of CaWs. However, hesitancy remains among practitioners to consider procedural treatments for symptomatic CaW.

In addressing this concern, our multi-institutional study, with an average follow up of 14.8 months, demonstrates that CEA and CAS for treatment of CaW can be safe with acceptable clinical outcomes. The combined perioperative stroke rate in this study was 1.41% (1/71). Notably, no recurrent strokes were observed in patients undergoing CEA, and one recurrent symptomatic stroke occurred in the CAS cohort. This one identified stroke was identified via CTA imaging within the first 30 days of follow up after the patient had speech difficulties, but the patient's mRS score remained 1 and the patient had minimal in-stent stenosis.

Recent evidence has suggested a role for early procedural intervention instead of medical management (i.e. antiplatelet therapy) for symptomatic CaW. The long-term risk for stroke recurrence has been well described in a 2022 meta-analysis study by Patel et al.
1
 Across 14 studies with a total of 282 patients with symptomatic CaW, with 131 undergoing medical management, the cumulative rate of recurrent stroke increased from 11.2% at 1 year, 26.4% at 5 years, and 39.2% at 10 years following the index stroke. Factors such as older age, male sex, and the presence of preexisting conditions (i.e., hypertension, diabetes, and atrial fibrillation) were associated with a higher rate of recurrent strokes. Notably, higher rates of mortality and severe disability were associated with early stroke recurrences (≤ 30 days), occurring in 5.7% of medically managed patients. In contrast, revascularization was pursued in 52.2% of patients, consisting of stenting in 87 patients (57.6%) and CEA in 64 patients (42.4%). Among the patients undergoing revascularization, no recurrent strokes were observed over the course of follow up ranging from 3 to 60 months.

Further supporting the need for revascularization, a recent 2025 meta-analysis of 17 studies reported that the recurrent stroke rate for 281 patients undergoing medical management was 32% whereas that for 282 patients undergoing intervention (CAS and CEA) was 0%.
17
 Thus, the interventional arm showed a significantly lower risk of recurrent ischemic stroke (relative risk 0.11, P < 0.0001). Notably, included studies by Haussen et al. (24 patients) and Rodriguez-Castro et al. (7 patients) both reported recurrent stroke rates in their medical arms as high as 37.5% and 44.5%, respectively, and no further recurrent strokes after symptomatic patients underwent revascularization.8,18 Another series described by Olindo et al.
19
 demonstrated a recurrent stroke risk of 4.4% at 30 days in medically managed patients with symptomatic CaW, which gradually increased to 27.3% at 5-year follow up. By comparison, patients treated with carotid revascularization experienced no recurrence after intervention (median follow-up 39.8 months).
19
 Overall, our results demonstrate similar favorability towards treatment as only 1 of the 71 patients experienced a post-stent stroke, and that patient maintained a mRS score of 1.

Currently, only a couple of prospective studies describe symptomatic patients with CaW. Choi et al.
20
 described a series of 7 patients with symptomatic CaW. Five patients (71.4%) experienced recurrent stroke despite antiplatelet therapy. Additionally, the CAROWEB registry is the largest ongoing study to date, including 244 patients with symptomatic CaW collected prospectively and retrospectively from 14 comprehensive French stroke centers within the past 5 years. The authors reported a low stroke recurrence rate of 2.62% at 3-month follow up, but longer-term follow up is required to re-evaluate this risk.

Although prospective studies are ongoing, the need for early intervention in symptomatic CaW is clearly outlined across multiple prior studies. There is a varied range in the time to treat, which likely is due to the delay in diagnosis. In our study, patients with symptomatic CaW had a mean mRS score of 1 at discharge, which compared favorably with the mean mRS score of 2 on initial presentation . Because intervention is thought to be primarily prophylactic, the improved mRS score is likely attributable to the longer mean time to treatment (114 days), which may allow patients to recover from their initial stroke symptoms. The difficulty in diagnosis of CaW resulting from the modest level of internal carotid luminal stenosis could explain the delated treatment interval in the study.
21
 The degree of stenosis created by the CaW was <50% in the series by Haussen et al.,
8
 the mean stenosis in the retrospective study by Olindo et al.
19
 was similarly low at 36%, and the degree of stenosis observed in the prospective CAROWEB registry was 41.6%.
22
 There have been case reports attributing the mild degree of stenosis as the direct cause of delayed diagnosis and treatment of CaW. For example, Miranda et al.
23
 reported the case of a 47-year-old woman who experienced multiple ischemic strokes that were presumed to be cryptogenic because of negative vascular imaging. A carotid ultrasound demonstrated what was believed to be a small atheromatous plaque and therefore an unlikely cause of the recurrent strokes. On formal angiography, however, this defect was determined to be a CaW and indeed the underlying cause of her strokes. The difficulty in diagnosis of a CaW, as well as the attribution of the index stroke to other causes, may contribute to delayed treatment of symptomatic CaWs. In our series, the average latency from index event to intervention was 115 ± 224 days. The observation that the average patient had more than 1 stroke before intervention also highlights the difficulty in prompt diagnosis and treatment for CaW.

Limitations of our study include (1) the lack of a nonoperative control group for comparison, (2) the retrospective nature of this study, and (3) a potential selection bias of treatment determined by the treating physician. Although the mean ages of the CAS and CEA groups were similar, there are potential confounding factors influencing the choice of treatment such as the degree and number of comorbidities each patient had, whether they could tolerate anesthesia, and patient preference. It would be beneficial to consider a prospective observational study in the United States to compare outcomes with the CAROWEB registry in France, and eventually a randomized trial comparing procedural treatments of CaW with maximal medical management. Because this was a retrospective multi-institutional chart review, our study is limited by the availability of all patient data reported, such as the lack of the specific perioperative medical treatment regimen for four patients. Overall, this was a broad study including patients from multiple institutions, but results may not be generalizable to institutions that follow other treatment plans or have patients with other presentations.

In one of the largest multi-institutional series to date, we demonstrate that CEA and CAS can be safe and effective for treating symptomatic CaW, with a low perioperative stroke rate (1.41%) and no MI. In this cohort, there was just 1 recurrent stroke during an average 14.8-month follow up. These findings support intervention over medical management and highlight the need for randomized controlled trials.
